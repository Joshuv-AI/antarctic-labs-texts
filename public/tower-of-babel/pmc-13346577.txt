Integrative analysis of single-cell and transcriptomic data with RT-qPCR validation explores TRPM4-associated gene signatures in osteoarthritis synovium
Authors: Yunxiu Chen, Daxin Guo, Jiawang Zhu, Zhiqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, disabling condition whose pathogenesis remains unclear. TRPM4 is closely associated with OA, but its specific roles and regulatory networks within different cell subsets remain to be elucidated. This study integrates single-cell and transcriptomic sequencing data to systematically analyse the cell-specific expression patterns, key downstream molecules, and regulatory networks of TRPM4, with the aim of identifying new therapeutic targets for OA. OA-related datasets were obtained from public databases. Key cells were identified at the single-cell level, and analyses including enriched pathways and cell communication were conducted. Candidate genes were screened by cross-referencing differentially expressed genes (DEGs), key module genes from high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) analysis related to key cells, and TRPM4-related DEGs. We then employed machine learning algorithms and expression levels to screen key genes. Subsequently, analyses such as functional enrichment, immune infiltration analysis, construction of molecular regulatory networks, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) were executed. Human Leukocyte Antigen-DR Alpha Chain (HLA-DRA) cells were the key cells, and the enriched pathways they participated in mainly included “FMO oxidizes nucleophiles”, and frequent cell–cell interactions were observed between HLA-DRA cells and synovial subintimal fibroblasts (SSF) in OA. Later, 2 key genes (FOSL2 and S100A11) were obtained. S100A11 was up-regulated, while FOSL2 was down-regulated in the OA group, and RT-qPCR showed consistent results (p < 0.05). In addition, FOSL2 and S100A11 shared 14 enriched pathways, such as the p53 pathway and oxidative phosphorylation, and there were infiltration differences of 8 types of immune cells between OA and control samples (p < 0.05). The miRNA–mRNA network (e.g., has-miR-6134-S100A11) was constructed. This study suggested that TRPM4-associated HLA-DRA+ antigen-presenting cells may be associated with osteoarthritis, and, based on transcriptomic correlation analysis and limited qPCR validation, identified S100A11 and FOSL2 as candidate biomarkers associated with TRPM4. These findings provided preliminary, hypothesis-generating clues regarding the cellular heterogeneity and immunometabolic pathways in which TRPM4 may be involved in osteoarthritis; however, its specific role remained to be validated by subsequent functional experiments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52134-1.

Introduction

OA is a degenerative joint disorder characterized by articular cartilage degradation and chronic synovial inflammation, representing one of the leading causes of disability among musculoskeletal diseases globally1. Clinically, OA presents with joint pain, swelling, restricted mobility, and impaired function, severely affecting patients’ quality of life. With the ongoing aging of the population, the prevalence of OA continues to rise, placing an increasing burden on healthcare systems worldwide2. Current treatments primarily focus on alleviating symptoms and improving function, such as physical therapy, pharmacological interventions (e.g., non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and intra-articular hyaluronic acid injections), and joint replacement surgery for advanced cases3. However, these approaches primarily provide symptomatic relief and fail to address the underlying pathological progression of the disease. Furthermore, limitations like adverse effects and individual variability highlight the urgent need for novel early diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, shifting the treatment paradigm from symptomatic management to mechanism-based interventions.

Transient Receptor Potential Melastatin 4 (TRPM4) is a calcium-activated, non-selective monovalent cation channel expressed in various tissues, including the heart, immune system, nervous system, kidneys, and pancreas4. TRPM4 plays a critical role in regulating membrane depolarization, intracellular ionic homeostasis, immune cell activation, and oxidative stress responses5. Previous studies have demonstrated that the TRPM4 channel acts as a key regulator of calcium influx in mast cells6. Upon activation, mast cells release pro-inflammatory mediators such as histamine, IL-1, and TNF-α, which can directly drive synovial inflammation and cartilage degradation7. In addition, sustained activation of TRPM4 leads to excessive sodium influx and membrane depolarization, ultimately inducing necrotic cell death characterized by sodium overload8,9. This mechanism may play an important role in the functional regulation of synovial immune cells. Notably, inhibition of the Sur1-Trpm4 channel has been shown to reduce the release of TNF-α and NF-κB10. Although previous studies have reported TRPM4 expression in human chondrocytes and its association with cartilage damage severity11, the central role of synovial tissue in the OA inflammatory cascade necessitates further elucidation of TRPM4 expression patterns and regulatory networks across different synovial cell populations. However, the precise function and regulatory mechanisms of TRPM4 in OA synovium, particularly its cell type specific expression and biological roles, remain largely unclear and warrant further investigation.

With the advancement of high-throughput omics technologies, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for resolving cellular heterogeneity and capturing transcriptomic profiles at the single-cell level12. Raut et al. constructed a multi-tissue single-cell atlas, revealing a significant enrichment of inflammation- and pain-associated macrophages in the synovium of OA13. Using multiple machine learning algorithms, Wu et al. identified HLA-DRA+ antigen-presenting cells (macrophages and dendritic cells) as a key synovial cell population in OA and demonstrated that the Dectin-1/Syk/NF-κB pathway regulates macrophage M1 polarization14. Although these studies have characterized the immune microenvironment of OA synovium centered on HLA-DRA+ cells, the expression patterns of TRPM4-related genes within this microenvironment and their potential pathogenic mechanisms remain insufficiently and systematically investigated.

In light of this, this study, bulk transcriptomic and scRNA-seq datasets from OA-related public databases were integrated to assess the cell-type-specific expression patterns of TRPM4 within OA tissues. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with TRPM4 expression were identified, followed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. Additionally, cell–cell communication analysis was performed, and upstream regulators of TRPM4 were predicted. This study aims to clarify the mechanisms through which TRPM4 contributes to OA pathogenesis. The findings not only enhance the molecular understanding of OA but also provide further evidence supporting TRPM4 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target, laying the groundwork for future translational research.

Exploring the interactions, functions, and TF regulatory network of HLA-DRA cells

Cell-to-cell communication analysis highlighted frequent interactions between HLA-DRA cells and synovial subintimal fibroblasts (SSF) in OA (Fig. 3a), with an increased strength of interactions between HLA-DRA cells and synovial intimal fibroblasts (SIF) (Fig. 3b). This suggested that HLA-DRA+ cells might have been involved in the structural remodelling of synovial tissue and the maintenance of the inflammatory microenvironment by regulating different fibroblast subpopulations. In OA samples, the most frequent ligand–receptor interaction between HLA-DRA cells and SSF was TNF-TNFRSF1A (Fig. 3c). The ligand–receptor pair with the highest communication probability targeting HLA-DRA cells was MIF-(CD74 + CD44) (Fig. 3d), suggesting potential differences in functions related to inflammation and immunity. These findings highlight distinct signaling mechanisms of angiogenesis and immunity within the disease context. Further secondary dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis of HLA-DRA cells revealed 3 subclusters: B cells, Inflammation-Associated Fibroblasts (IFB), and Macrophages (Fig. 3e). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that B cells and IFB were primarily enriched in pathways related to “FMO oxidizes nucleophiles”, while macrophages showed significant enrichment in pathways like “The fatty acid cycling model”, suggesting their roles in immune regulation and metabolic processes (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Table 1). The top 5 key regulators in B cells were RUNX3, ETV3, IRF7, NFE2L2, and MAFF; in macrophages, they were MAFB, SPI1, FLI1, FOXO1, and ELK4; and in IFB, they were HOXA13, GLI3, SOX5, NR2F2, and DLX3 (Fig. 3g). The median expression level of the TRPM4 gene in OA samples was 3.185, and TRPM4 expression in LEP was significantly lower than in HEP (Fig. 4d). S100A11 and FOSL2 as key genes in patients with OA

LASSO regression analysis was conducted, and when lambda.min was 0.00044, the model error was minimized, identifying this as the optimal model. Three characteristic genes (FOSL2, CXCL3, S100A11) were selected (Fig. 5a,b). The expression levels of these characteristic genes were verified, showing significant differences in S100A11 and FOSL2 expression in both the GSE55235 (Fig. 5c) and GSE89408 datasets (Fig. 5d), with consistent trends (p < 0.05). Specifically, S100A11 was upregulated, while FOSL2 was downregulated in the OA group. Similarly, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that S100A11 was upregulated and FOSL2 downregulated in the OA group (Fig. 5e, Table 1). Functional characterization of S100A11 and FOSL2

The logistic regression model based on S100A11 and FOSL2 (Fig. 6a) was able to accurately distinguish osteoarthritis samples from control samples in the GSE55235 dataset, and the model’s AUC values exceeded 0.7 in both the GSE55235 and GSE89408 datasets (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, due to the small sample size, there was a risk of overfitting, and further validation would be required using datasets with larger sample sizes. Further analysis revealed that FOSL2 had the highest expression in the nucleus, suggesting its nuclear function (Fig. 6b). S100A11 exhibited the highest scores both in the nucleus and extracellular space, implying its potential roles in both locations (Fig. 6c). The GGI network analysis showed that the two key genes interacted with 20 genes, each with distinct biological functions. For instance, both S100A11 and FOSL2 were involved in the response to cadherin binding (Fig. 6d). GSEA results indicated that FOSL2 was associated with 24 KEGG pathways (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Table 4), while S100A11 was linked to 21 KEGG pathways (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Table 5). Notably, FOSL2 and S100A11 shared 14 enriched pathways, including the p53 pathway and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). The p53 pathway regulates cellular stress and apoptosis, while OXPHOS abnormalities are closely linked to chondrocyte metabolic dysregulation. The association between 2 key genes and the immune microenvironment in OA

Given the key role of immune function in OA pathogenesis, immune infiltration analysis was performed after excluding five samples (p > 0.05). M2 macrophages comprised the largest proportion of immune cells in OA samples (Fig. 7a). Comparison between OA and controls revealed significant infiltration differences in four immune cell types, including resting CD4 memory T cells (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7b, Supplementary Table 6). Correlation analysis showed that resting CD4 memory T cells exhibited the strongest positive correlation with activated NK cells (cor = 0.84, p < 0.001), while resting mast cells were most negatively correlated with activated mast cells (cor = − 0.92, p < 0.001) (Fig. 7c). We hypothesized that mast cell activation is closely linked to T-cell-mediated immune responses, forming a specific immune regulatory network in OA-associated synovial inflammation. Moreover, FOSL2 demonstrated a strong positive correlation with resting CD4 memory T cells (cor = 0.75, p < 0.001). S100A11 exhibited a strong negative correlation with resting CD4 memory T cells (cor = − 0.69, p < 0.001), and FOSL2 was most negatively correlated with resting mast cells (cor = − 0.77, p < 0.001) (Fig. 7d). Discussion

OA is a degenerative joint disease characterized by cartilage degeneration, bone remodeling, and synovial inflammation. It predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly individuals, and its prevalence increases with an aging population15. Dysfunction of TRPM4 is associated with various diseases; its abnormal expression in OA may promote disease progression by regulating chondrocyte, inflammatory, and stress signaling pathways. Elucidating its cell-specific expression and downstream networks is crucial for understanding the mechanisms of OA and identifying new therapeutic targets Accordingly, this study integrated bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets and employed comprehensive bioinformatics approaches to identify key osteoarthritis-related genes and cell populations, followed by in-depth analyses including functional enrichment, intercellular communication network characterization, and regulatory network prediction.

Among the DEGs, two critical regulators were identified: FOSL2 (FOS-like antigen 2) and S100A11 (S100 calcium-binding protein A11). As a member of the FOS family of transcription factors, FOSL2 can be activated by various stress stimuli, including ultraviolet radiation and chemotherapeutic agents, and participates in oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling by modulating DNA repair pathways16. Emerging evidence suggests that FOSL2 may serve as a hub gene in OA, positioning it as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target17. Recent studies have identified FOSL2 as a DEG significantly enriched in the osteoclast differentiation pathway in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with OA18. This suggests that FOSL2 may contribute to OA pathogenesis by modulating osteoclast differentiation and bone remodeling.

In addition to FOSL2, S100A11 plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of OA. A member of the S100 family of calcium-binding proteins, S100A11 binds Ca2+ and interacts with target proteins such as vimentin and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), regulating cytoskeletal reorganization, adhesion, and migration19. S100A11 is also involved in cell proliferation and differentiation and can activate the TLR4/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, promoting the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α from monocytes and macrophages20. Notably, elevated inflammation and reduced cartilage protection represent early disruptions in joint homeostasis, which could potentially trigger the onset of OA. The sustained high expression of S100A11 throughout disease progression further supports its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in OA21.

RT-qPCR confirmed that S100A11 was upregulated and FOSL2 downregulated in OA synovial tissues, consistent with the bioinformatics results. These two genes were identified as TRPM4-related candidate biomarkers through integrative transcriptomic analysis and limited qPCR validation. Their altered expression offers preliminary clues for future functional studies, but their biological roles and therapeutic potential require further experimental validation.

GSEA revealed that both FOSL2 and S100A11 were significantly enriched in pathways, including the p53 signaling pathway and OXPHOS, suggesting their involvement in key pathogenic mechanisms underlying OA. Research has found that p53 regulates ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death—by modulating the p53/SLC7A11/GPX4 signaling axis, which plays a role in cartilage damage and inflammation in OA22. p53 regulates chondrocyte growth, differentiation, and metabolism. In OA, reduced p53 expression in damaged cartilage correlates with decreased thymidine uptake, indicating altered proliferation. An elevated Bcl-2/p53 ratio increases chondrocyte metabolic activity, likely as a compensatory survival mechanism under stress23. Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibition of TRPM4 within the p53 signaling pathway markedly attenuates its ionic currents and aberrantly enhances calcium signaling24, a process that is likely mediated by the calcium-binding regulator S100A11, thereby disrupting calcium-dependent homeostasis in chondrocytes. In parallel, PI3K has been reported to upregulate FOSL2 expression via the MAPK signaling pathway18. We speculate that FOSL2 and S100A11 may synergistically amplify aberrant TRPM4 signaling, leading to calcium dysregulation, extracellular matrix degradation, and joint degeneration. This hypothesis is based on transcriptomic correlation and limited validation. Future functional experiments (e.g., TRPM4 inhibition or knockout, S100A11/FOSL2 manipulation, calcium imaging, and matrix metabolism assessment) are needed to verify their therapeutic potential in OA.

Single-cell analysis showed that HLA-DRA+ cells in the synovium are mainly macrophages and dendritic cells, which are key cellular populations in OA, consistent with previous single-cell transcriptomic studies14. HLA-DRa encodes the α chain of MHC class II and is primarily expressed in antigen-presenting cells (APCs), where it presents antigens to CD4+ T cells and coordinates adaptive immune responses25. In OA, this process may become pathologically amplified: HLA-DRA+ synovial APCs present cartilage-derived autoantigens to T cells, triggering local inflammation and accelerating cartilage degradation and synovitis26.

Further single-cell analysis showed that TRPM4 has relatively high average expression in HLA-DRA+ cells, suggesting it may regulate synovial APC function. Previous studies indicate that TRPM4 deficiency impairs calcium signaling and phagocytosis in macrophages27 and reduces dendritic cell migration28. Given that macrophage activation and dendritic cell infiltration are key features of OA synovium, along with high TRPM4 expression in HLA-DRA+ cells, we speculate that TRPM4 may influence APC antigen-presenting efficiency and local inflammation by regulating calcium-dependent functions, thus contributing to OA immunopathology. However, TRPM4 expression in HLA-DRA+ cells has not been validated at the protein level in this study, and the cited functional evidence comes mainly from other disease models. Therefore, the precise role of TRPM4 in synovial APCs in OA requires further clarification through gene-intervention approaches and dedicated functional experiments.

This study integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, transcriptomics, and experimental validation to explore the role of TRPM4 in OA pathogenesis. The identification of HLA-DRA+ cells provides a novel marker for immune stratification of OA. Future assessment of their synovial infiltration by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry may help evaluate patient inflammatory status. FOSL2 and S100A11 exhibited stable differential expression across multiple datasets, suggesting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers. Their expression levels in synovial fluid or peripheral blood could be further correlated with disease severity. Moreover, the TRPM4-FOSL2/S100A11 signaling axis may offer candidate targets for novel OA therapies. However, these conclusions are based primarily on bioinformatics analyses and validation using limited clinical samples. Therefore, the specific biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of these molecules in OA require further elucidation through gene knockout experiments, animal models, and functional studies.

Although this study offers insights into potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for OA, several limitations exist. The small sample size of the scRNA-seq dataset may reduce statistical power for identifying key cell populations and regulatory networks. Bulk transcriptomic and clinical samples are limited and confined to synovial tissue, missing molecular changes in cartilage and subchondral bone. Clinical heterogeneity, batch effects, and selection bias from public databases may affect robustness and generalizability. The analysis lacks protein-level and functional validation, so the precise roles of key genes and cell populations remain unclear. Without healthy synovial scRNA-seq data, the diagnostic value of HLA-DRA+ cells needs further confirmation using multi-tissue samples and independent cohorts. Future studies should include other OA-related tissues (cartilage, bone, synovial fluid) and establish large-scale, multicenter cohorts to improve sample representativeness. Strict standardization of experimental procedures, quality control, and random sampling are needed to minimize bias. Additional experiments such as gene knockout, animal models, and protein validation should elucidate mechanistic roles and support clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the potential association between TRPM4 and osteoarthritis through an integrative analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing, transcriptomic datasets, and RT–qPCR validation. The results identified HLA-DRA+ cells as a key OA-associated cellular population. Based on transcriptomic correlation analysis together with limited qPCR validation, FOSL2 (downregulated) and S100A11 (upregulated) were identified as TRPM4-related candidate biomarkers, and their differential expression showed consistent trends across multiple datasets. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these two genes were co-enriched in 14 signaling pathways, including the p53 signaling pathway and oxidative phosphorylation, and were associated with differential infiltration of eight immune cell types, such as M2 macrophages. Collectively, these findings suggest that TRPM4 may participate in the pathogenesis of OA through HLA-DRA+ cells and the FOSL2/S100A11-related regulatory network. These results provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating insights for future investigations into the molecular mechanisms and candidate biomarkers of OA, which will require further experimental validation.

Methods

Data collection

scRNA-seq datasets related to OA were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE152805 dataset (platform: GPL20301) provided transcriptomic data from synovial tissue samples of 3 patients with OA (Supplementary Table 7). The GSE55235 dataset (platform: GPL96) included 10 OA synovial tissue samples and 10 control samples (Supplementary Table 8). Additionally, the GSE89408 dataset (platform: GPL11154) consisted of 22 OA synovial tissue samples and 28 control samples (Supplementary Table 9). The research workflow is presented in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Data quality control and cell annotation

The “PercentageFeatureSet” function in the “Seurat” R package (v 5.1.0)29 was used to analyze the GSE152805 dataset. To ensure data integrity, low-quality cells and genes were filtered out using the following criteria: nFeature_RNA < 7500, 500 < nCount_RNA < 100,000, and percentage.mt < 20%. After quality control, low-quality cells were excluded. The “PercentageFeatureSet” function in the “Seurat” R package29 was employed to identify highly variable genes (HVGs), with the “LabelPoints” function in the “LabelPoints” R package used to display the top 10 genes with the highest variation. Data were normalized using the ScaleData function, followed by PCA with ElbowPlot to determine PCA scores. Principal components (PCs) with genes showing smaller enrichment p values (p < 0.05) were retained. The cells in the GSE152805 dataset were classified using the “Seurat” R package (v 5.1.0) with a resolution threshold of 0.8. The cell clusters were annotated based on marker genes from reference datasets30. A Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot was created using the “Seurat” R package (v 5.1.0) to display the annotated cell clusters.

Identification of key cells

To explore the distribution and expression patterns of the TRPM gene in the annotated cell clusters, bubble and UMAP plots were generated using the “Seurat” R package (v 5.1.0)29. These visualizations revealed the expression pattern of TRPM4 in the annotated cells and its distribution within these cells. Cells with the highest TRPM4 expression were identified as key cells.

hdWGCNA

To identify key module genes associated with the key cells, the “hdWGCNA” R package (v 0.4.3)31 was used to perform high-dimensional Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (hdWGCNA) on the key cells across all samples in the GSE152805 dataset. The Metacell gene expression matrix was constructed using the MetacellsByGroups function. A parameter scan with TestSoftPowers determined the optimal soft power threshold (β) that achieved a scale-free topology fit (R2 > 0.85). The weighted co-expression network was built using ConstructNetwork, followed by hierarchical clustering of samples based on TOM with a minimum gene threshold of 30, generating a dendrogram to identify co-expression modules. Module eigengenes were calculated for each module. The ModuleConnectivity function computed the connectivity of module genes, and the top 10 genes were listed by k-Module Membership values. A correlation matrix between module genes and key cells was then calculated, and genes with |cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05 were selected.

Analysis of cell-to-cell communication

Cell-to-cell communication analysis was performed using the “CellChat” R package (v 1.6.1)32 to investigate interactions between annotated cell types in the OA samples from the GSE152805 dataset. This analysis generated cell–cell communication networks, determined the expression levels of receptors and ligands, and highlighted the number and strength of interactions, as well as the intensity of receptors and ligands.

Key cells function enrichment and construction of transcription factor (TF) regulatory network

The “Seurat” R package (v 5.1.0)29 was used to re-cluster the key cells into different subclusters (resolution = 0.1)30. Functional enrichment analysis of the different subclusters in key cells was conducted using the “ReactomeGSA” R package (v 1.16.1)33 on the GSE152805 dataset. Subsequently, pySCENIC software was used to analyze and predict transcription factors and their target genes in each subcluster of key cells to construct a gene regulatory network. The aucell method of the “SCENIC” R package (v 1.3.1)34 was applied to calculate the specificity scores of 179 TFs in each subcluster. The top 5 specific TFs in each molecular subcluster of key cells were selected as key regulators based on their score rankings. Cytoscape software (v 3.9.1)29 was used to construct a network of different subclusters of key cells and their corresponding key regulators.

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs1) between OA and control samples in the GSE55235 dataset were identified using the “limma” R package (v 3.58.1)29. The filtering criteria were p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5. The top 10 up- and down-regulated DEGs1, ranked by log2FC values, were visualized on a volcano plot using the “ggplot2” R package (v 3.5.1)35. The expression trends of DEGs1 were further visualized in a heatmap using the “ComplexHeatmap” R package (v 2.10.8)36.

Expression analysis of TRPM4

The Wilcoxon test was used to compare TRPM4 expression between OA and control samples from GSE55235 (p < 0.05). OA samples were then divided into high-expression (HEP) and low-expression (LEP) groups based on the median TRPM4 expression, and the Wilcoxon test was applied again to compare TRPM4 expression between HEP and LEP (p < 0.05). Boxplot visualization was performed using the “ggplot2” R package (v 3.5.1)37. DEGs2 between HEP and LEP in OA samples were identified using the “limma” R package (v 3.58.1)35. The filtering conditions were p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5. Volcano plots and heatmaps were visualized using the same methods as described in 2.7.

Ascertainment and analysis of candidate genes

To identify candidate genes, the “ggvenn” R package (v 1.73)31 was used to intersect the DEGs1, DEGs2, and module genes from hdWGCNA, resulting in the identification of candidate genes. Subsequently, the “clusterProfiler” R package (v 4.10.1)37 was employed to conduct KEGG38–40 and GO analyses of the candidate genes (p < 0.05).

Machine learning and expression level validation

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was conducted using the “glmnet” R package (v 4.1.8)41 to identify candidate genes across all samples in the GSE55235 dataset. After conducting sixfold cross-validation, a LASSO regression plot was generated, and the lambda min value was selected as the optimal model for gene selection. The Wilcoxon test was applied to assess the expression levels of the identified genes in OA and control samples from both the GSE55235 and GSE89408 datasets. Genes with significant expression differences (p < 0.05) and consistent trends across both datasets were selected as key genes.

Construction of the logistic model

To evaluate the classification performance of the key genes, a logistic model was constructed using the key genes across all GSE55235 samples. The “caret” R package (v 6.0.94)42 was used to build a confusion matrix. To further validate the model’s generalisation ability and stability, an independent GSE89408 dataset was used as an external test set for blind validation, with predictions and evaluations strictly adhering to the model parameters and classification thresholds determined from the training set. Furthermore, ROC curves were plotted using the “pROC” package (v 1.18.5)43.

Analysis of subcellular localization, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and GeneMANIA analysis

Subcellular localization information (confidence score > 5) for the key genes was retrieved from the GeneCards database, and a gene–gene interaction (GGI) network was constructed using the GeneMANIA database to reveal interconnections among these genes. To explore the signaling pathways associated with the key genes, the “psych” R package (v 2.4.3)44 was utilized to identify Spearman correlations between the key genes and other genes, ranking these dependencies from high to low across all GSE55235 samples. The “h.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols” gene set was downloaded from MSigDB as the background. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package (v 4.10.1)45, enriching the ranked genes within this background set, with criteria set at p < 0.05, FDR < 0.25, and |NES|> 1. The top 10 enriched pathways were visualized.

Immune infiltration analysis

To investigate immune cell infiltration in OA and control samples, immune infiltration analysis was conducted on the GSE55235 dataset. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm from the “IOBR” R package (v 0.1.0)46, we calculated the infiltration scores for 22 types of immune cells in each sample and excluded samples with p values greater than 0.05. The Wilcoxon test was then performed to compare infiltration proportions between OA and control samples, identifying immune cell types with significant differences (p < 0.05). To quantify differences in cell composition between the two groups, Cohen’s d effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each immune cell type using a bootstrap method with 1000 replications. Correlation analysis was conducted using the “psych” R package (v 2.4.3)44 to examine relationships among differential immune cells and between key genes and differential immune cells, with significant correlations defined as |cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05.

Molecular regulatory networks

To explore the molecular regulatory network involved in disease progression, the miRDB database (https://www.mirdb.org/) was used to predict the target microRNAs (miRNAs) that regulate the key genes. A miRNA–mRNA network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (v 3.9.1)47.

RT-qPCR

Human subject research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, relevant laws, and institutional guidelines, with approval from the Tianjin Hospital Ethics Committee (approval No. 2020YLS30, dated July 15, 2020). Participants’ privacy rights were respected, and informed consent was obtained for all experiments.

To validate the expression profiles of key genes in clinical specimens, RT-qPCR analysis was performed. Ten synovial tissue samples (five from patients with OA and five from normal controls) were collected from Tianjin Hospital (convenience sampling) (Supplementary Table 10). Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients meeting the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for knee osteoarthritis; (2) Presence of typical clinical manifestations (such as progressive limitation of movement and flexion deformity); (3) Radiographic findings indicative of characteristic osteoarthritic changes; (4) Age ≥ 55 years. Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of other types of joint diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, gouty arthritis, psoriatic arthritis); (2) Presence of severe comorbidities (e.g., severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, or malignancy); (3) History of medication or interventions within the previous 12 months that may influence the study parameters. Primers used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 11. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles. The 2−ΔΔCT method was employed to calculate relative mRNA quantification. The RT-qPCR results were exported to Excel and then imported into GraphPad Prism 10 (https://www.graphpad.com/) for statistical analysis and visualization (p < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was performed using R (v 4.3.1). The Wilcoxon test was applied to evaluate differences, while the T-test was used for statistical comparisons in the qRT-PCR analysis (p < 0.05).
