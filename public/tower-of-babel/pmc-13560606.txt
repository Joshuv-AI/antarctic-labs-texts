Admission‐Time Clinicolaboratory Phenotypes for Early Differentiation of Severe Fever With Thrombocytopenia Syndrome and Hemorrhagic Fever With Renal Syndrome
Authors: Ruize Ma, Yanli Xu, Shuwen Ding, Ruihua Zhang, Jingxia Wang, Zhouling Jiang, Ling Lin, Zhihai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly differentiation of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) and hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) remains difficult in endemic settings. In this single‐center retrospective comparative study, we analyzed admission‐time demographic, clinical, and laboratory data from 330 hospitalized patients with laboratory‐confirmed SFTS (n = 220) or HFRS (n = 110) admitted between May 2023 and November 2025. Compared with HFRS, SFTS showed an older age distribution, female predominance, more frequent neurological symptoms, and a predominantly hematologic phenotype with modest hepatic differences, whereas HFRS showed more prominent urinary manifestations and greater inflammatory and renal abnormalities. Abnormality‐based and normalized organ‐domain burden analyses supported the same phenotypic separation, with hematologic, renal, and inflammatory domains showing the clearest exploratory differences. Two differentiation models were developed as proof‐of‐concept tools using the same complete‐case data set and internally evaluated by full‐process nested cross‐validation. The basic and extended models achieved out‐of‐fold AUCs of 0.975 and 0.971, respectively; the paired difference was −0.004 (95% CI, −0.014 to 0.005; p = 0.404). Adding CRP, albumin, and LDH did not provide consistent incremental predictive value. These single‐center findings provide a proof‐of‐concept framework requiring independent external validation and recalibration before potential clinical application.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF) caused by Dabie bandavirus (DBV, formerly known as SFTS virus [SFTSV]) [1, 2, 3]. First discovered in China [1], SFTS has subsequently been reported in several Asian countries and other regions, highlighting its increasing geographic relevance as an emerging infectious disease [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) is another life‐threatening VHF caused by orthohantaviruses and transmitted mainly through exposure to infected rodents or their excreta [12, 13, 14]. Hantaan virus (HTNV) and Seoul virus (SEOV) are the principal etiologic agents of human HFRS in China [15, 16]. China accounts for a substantial proportion of the global HFRS burden, and both HFRS and SFTS are highly prevalent in rural and hilly endemic areas [14, 17, 18]. Clinically, these two diseases often present with overlapping features. Both may manifest as acute fever, fatigue, hemorrhagic manifestations, gastrointestinal symptoms, thrombocytopenia, and other nonspecific findings, making early differentiation challenging at presentation, particularly before etiologic confirmation is available [13, 18, 19, 20].

Early differentiation between SFTS and HFRS is clinically meaningful. Compared with HFRS, SFTS is often associated with higher mortality and may require earlier recognition for timely supportive care, infection control, and referral decisions [19, 20, 21, 22]. This challenge is particularly relevant in primary care facilities, county hospitals, and other resource‐limited endemic settings, where confirmatory etiologic testing may be delayed or unavailable [23]. Previous studies have compared epidemiologic features, clinical manifestations, routine laboratory indicators, and admission‐based differentiation models for distinguishing SFTS from HFRS [19, 20, 22, 24, 25]. These studies have identified variables such as age, sex, neurological symptoms, leukopenia, creatinine, coagulation‐related parameters, and other routinely available biomarkers as potentially useful differentiators.

However, many previous studies have primarily focused on individual discriminatory variables or relatively simple differentiation models, whereas clinicians usually interpret suspected cases through combined patterns of demographic features, symptom clusters, and routine laboratory abnormalities rather than any single marker in isolation [26, 27]. What remains less clear is how these previously identified clinical and laboratory findings combine into an overall admission‐time phenotype, and whether broader laboratory panels provide meaningful improvement beyond a simpler set of routinely available variables. To address these questions, we compared the overall pattern of abnormalities across six biological domains and evaluated a basic admission model alongside an extended laboratory model.

In the present study, we conducted a retrospective comparative analysis of admission‐time data from hospitalized patients with laboratory‐confirmed SFTS and HFRS. We aimed to characterize admission‐time demographic, clinical, and laboratory differences between SFTS and HFRS; organize these differences into abnormality‐based organ‐domain profiles; and subsequently develop, internally validate, and compare a basic admission model and an extended laboratory model to determine whether additional laboratory information provides incremental value. We hypothesized that, despite their overlapping syndromic presentations, SFTS and HFRS would exhibit distinct admission‐time clinicolaboratory phenotypes that could provide a clinically interpretable basis for early differentiation in endemic settings. Because this was a single‐center study without an independent validation cohort, the models were considered internally validated proof‐of‐concept models rather than instruments intended for immediate clinical deployment.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single‐center retrospective comparative study was conducted at Yantai Qishan Hospital between May 2023 and November 2025. Consecutive hospitalized patients screened for HFRS or SFTS during the study period were retrospectively reviewed. A total of 653 records were initially screened, including 232 HFRS records and 421 SFTS records. After application of the predefined eligibility criteria, 330 patients were included in the final analysis.

Admission‐time data were defined as the first available demographic, clinical, and laboratory information obtained at admission. For repeated admissions from the same disease episode, only the first eligible hospitalization was retained. The study was designed to characterize admission‐time clinicolaboratory phenotypes and evaluate internally validated differentiation models rather than to establish immediately deployable diagnostic tools.

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted within an inter‐institutional collaborative research framework led by Beijing Ditan Hospital, Capital Medical University. Yantai Qishan Hospital served as the participating and data‐contributing site, where patients were hospitalized and clinical data were collected. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Ditan Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. DTEC‐KY2022‐022‐01), and by the Ethics Committee of Yantai Qishan Hospital (Approval No. 202513). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective study using archived, de‐identified clinical data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Diagnostic Confirmation, Eligibility, and Data Collection

Diagnoses of SFTS and HFRS were confirmed according to the national diagnostic criteria used in routine clinical practice. SFTS was defined as a clinically compatible acute febrile illness confirmed by the detection of SFTSV RNA in serum samples. HFRS was defined as a clinically compatible syndrome with laboratory confirmation by either positive serum hantavirus‐specific IgM antibody or seroconversion of hantavirus‐specific IgG antibody. The same institutional diagnostic pathway was applied throughout the study period, supporting consistent case ascertainment across the cohort.

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 16 years; (2) hospitalization at Yantai Qishan Hospital during the study period; (3) laboratory‐confirmed SFTS or HFRS according to the above diagnostic criteria; and (4) availability of sufficient admission‐time demographic, clinical, and laboratory data for the prespecified analyses. Exclusion criteria were grouped as follows: (1) diagnostic ineligibility, including uncertain diagnosis, non‐laboratory‐confirmed disease, duplicate records, or repeated admissions from the same disease episode; (2) coexisting infections, clinical conditions, or treatments that could substantially affect the key study laboratory indicators, including other laboratory‐confirmed infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, hematological disease, malignancy under active radiotherapy or chemotherapy, use of immunosuppressive drugs, chronic kidney disease, recent blood transfusion within the previous 2 weeks, and other conditions judged likely to materially affect the key laboratory indicators included in the study; and (3) excessive missing clinical information or missing critical admission‐time variables required for the prespecified analyses.

Because some symptoms and signs were originally documented as free text, four trained researchers coded the clinical variables using a unified data dictionary. For the final analytic data set, each coded record underwent secondary source‐data verification by a second researcher. Discrepancies in data entry or clinical coding identified during this review were checked against the original medical records and corrected before analysis. Disagreements regarding clinical classification were resolved by reference to relevant expert consensus statements and clinical guidelines. Formal inter‐rater reliability statistics (e.g., Cohen's κ) were not calculated because the secondary review was implemented as a source‐data quality‐control procedure rather than a prospectively designed independent reliability assessment. The finalized data set used for statistical analysis incorporated all corrections identified through this source‐data verification process.

Neurological symptoms included limb tremor, altered consciousness, somnolence, delirium, seizures, coma, encephalitis, or encephalopathy. Meningeal signs were not prespecified as a separate component of this composite and were not analyzed separately. Pulmonary abnormalities included imaging abnormalities, pneumonia, or pulmonary rales. Urinary symptoms included oliguria, anuria, or reduced urine output. Bleeding manifestations included petechiae or ecchymoses, hematochezia, hematuria, or hemoptysis. Dyspnea was coded separately from pulmonary abnormalities. Because a uniform retrospective neurological severity scale was unavailable, neurological involvement was analyzed as a binary composite variable.

Laboratory Abnormality Definitions and Organ‐Domain Burden Analyses

Institutional laboratory reference intervals reported on the original hospital laboratory reports were used as the basis for the abnormality definitions in the final analyses. For each laboratory indicator, the corresponding institutional lower or upper reference limit was applied according to the direction of abnormality specified in Supporting Information S2: Table 1. The relevant analytical platforms and reference intervals remained unchanged throughout the study period. These definitions were used consistently to construct the binary laboratory‐abnormality indicators and all subsequent organ‐domain burden measures.

In total, 17 laboratory indicators were grouped into 6 domains: hematologic, inflammatory, hepatic, renal, coagulation, and systemic tissue injury. For descriptive phenotype characterization, the domain abnormality count was defined as the number of abnormal observed indicators within each biological domain. Normalized domain abnormality burden was calculated as the proportion of abnormal indicators among nonmissing indicators within each biological domain. These measures were used to summarize and compare the distribution of admission‐time abnormalities across biological domains. They were not incorporated into the differentiation models as composite scores and were not intended as standalone diagnostic or prognostic tools. Variables, units, institutional reference intervals, directional abnormality definitions, and domain assignments are provided in Supporting Information S2: Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as number (percentage). Comparisons between HFRS and SFTS were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied separately within related families of analyses. These families comprised demographics and clinical course, symptoms and signs, each laboratory section, adjusted symptom models, individual laboratory‐abnormality comparisons, organ‐domain burden comparisons, laboratory‐marker correlations, temporal‐distribution tests, and domain–outcome associations for each outcome. All tests were two‐sided. For analyses subject to multiple‐comparison correction, an FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Each prespecified symptom or sign was evaluated in a separate mean‐bias‐reduced logistic‐regression model, with diagnosis coded as SFTS = 1 and HFRS = 0. Each model was adjusted for age, sex, and the onset‐to‐admission interval. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and the adjusted symptom models constituted a separate FDR analysis family. Relationships among laboratory variables were assessed using Spearman correlation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using standardized laboratory variables to explore unsupervised patterns of admission‐time laboratory phenotype separation between the two diseases. The first two principal components were visualized using patient‐level score plots, and variable contributions were examined using PCA loading plots.

Missing values were not imputed. Descriptive analyses used the available observations for each variable. Variable‐specific availability and missingness are reported in Supporting Information S2: Table 7. All variables required for the final basic and extended models and all 17 laboratory indicators used in the organ‐domain analyses were complete; all 22 prespecified symptom and sign variables were also complete. Consequently, both differentiation models were developed and evaluated in the full cohort of 330 patients, and no patient was excluded from either model because of missing predictor data. The temporal distributions of SFTS and HFRS cases were summarized by calendar year and season.

The phenotypic separation of each normalized domain burden between SFTS and HFRS was summarized using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the curve (AUC). The raw AUC treated SFTS as the positive class. For descriptive interpretation of separation, AUC values below 0.50 were re‐expressed as 1 − AUC, and the diagnosis with the higher normalized burden was identified. No optimal threshold, sensitivity, specificity, or clinical cut‐off was derived from these domain analyses. Exploratory associations between normalized domain burden and ICU admission or in‐hospital death were evaluated using full‐cohort Firth logistic‐regression models adjusted for diagnosis, age, sex, and the onset‐to‐admission interval. ORs were reported per 1‐SD higher normalized domain burden. FDR adjustment was applied separately across the six domain models for each outcome. These exploratory, hypothesis‐generating analyses were intended to describe phenotypic separation and concurrent outcome associations rather than to establish diagnostic cut‐offs, causal effects, or validated prognostic performance.

Model Development and Internal Validation

Two multivariable logistic‐regression models were developed as proof‐of‐concept differentiation models to distinguish SFTS from HFRS: a basic admission model and an extended laboratory model. Diagnosis was coded as SFTS = 1 and HFRS = 0. Candidate predictors were prespecified from clinically relevant demographic, clinical, and routine laboratory variables and selected using LASSO logistic regression with the one‐standard‐error rule. Age, sex, and the onset‐to‐admission interval were retained a priori and were not penalized. The candidate predictor pools for the basic and extended models are detailed in Supporting Information S2: Table 3. Both models were developed and evaluated using the same complete‐case data set to ensure direct comparability. Because the objective was diagnostic differentiation rather than estimation of a causal exposure effect, propensity‐score matching and inverse‐probability weighting were not used, and all eligible patients were retained. No oversampling, undersampling, or class weighting was applied. Fold allocation was stratified by diagnosis in both the inner and outer cross‐validation loops.

The full‐cohort basic admission model retained age, female sex, onset‐to‐admission interval, neurological symptoms, urinary symptoms, white blood cell count, and creatinine. The full‐cohort extended laboratory model retained these predictors and additionally included C‐reactive protein, albumin, and lactate dehydrogenase. Predictor selection was repeated independently within each outer training set during nested cross‐validation, as described below.

Model complexity was descriptively summarized using the number of observations in the smaller diagnostic group per fixed predictor. This conservative ratio was not interpreted as the conventional event‐per‐variable measure. Sample‐size adequacy was additionally assessed using a post hoc Riley framework [28]. Variance inflation factors and formal diagnostics for complete or quasi‐complete separation were examined. Firth penalized‐likelihood logistic regression was used as a sensitivity analysis to assess coefficient stability in the presence of sparse data or unstable maximum‐likelihood estimates.

The primary internal‐validation procedure was diagnosis‐stratified nested 10‐fold cross‐validation, with 10 outer folds and 10 inner folds [29]. Within each outer training set, the LASSO tuning parameter was selected using inner 10‐fold cross‐validation, and predictor selection was repeated using the one‐standard‐error rule. The selected predictors were then refitted using ordinary logistic regression in the outer training set and applied to the corresponding held‐out fold. Predictions from the outer validation folds were combined to generate one patient‐level out‐of‐fold prediction for each model.

Discrimination was evaluated using ROC analysis and AUC. Overall prediction error was assessed using the Brier score and log loss, while calibration was evaluated using the calibration intercept, calibration slope, and calibration plots. Grouped Hosmer–Lemeshow tests using 10 equal‐sized groups of patient‐level out‐of‐fold predictions were additionally reported as supplementary calibration checks. Because these tests depend on the grouping scheme and may be unstable when grouped expected counts are small, they were not used as the primary calibration criterion. CIs for the out‐of‐fold performance measures were estimated using 2000 diagnosis‐stratified patient‐level bootstrap resamples.

The difference in out‐of‐fold AUC between the two models was summarized using a 95% CI derived from 2000 paired patient‐level bootstrap resamples, and statistical significance was assessed using the paired DeLong test [30]. The difference in out‐of‐fold Brier score was evaluated using 2000 paired patient‐level bootstrap resamples. The out‐of‐fold log‐loss difference, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were assessed using 1000 paired outcome‐stratified patient‐level bootstrap resamples. Bootstrap percentile CIs were treated as the primary uncertainty estimates for NRI and IDI, which were considered exploratory incremental metrics. Potential clinical utility was evaluated using decision‐curve analysis based exclusively on out‐of‐fold predictions [31]. At a fixed probability threshold of 0.50, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy were calculated, with 95% CIs estimated using 1000 diagnosis‐stratified patient‐level bootstrap resamples. An exploratory nomogram was derived from the full‐cohort basic admission model solely to illustrate the relative contributions of its predictors and was not intended for individual risk estimation or use as a validated clinical score.

In addition, the complete model‐development process, including LASSO tuning, predictor selection, and model refitting, was repeated in 1000 diagnosis‐stratified bootstrap samples to assess predictor‐selection frequency, model‐size stability, and maximum‐likelihood convergence. The fixed basic and extended model structures were additionally refitted among patients admitted within ≤ 5 days and ≤ 7 days after symptom onset, and maximum‐likelihood and Firth‐estimation stability was reassessed. These analyses were treated as time‐restricted sensitivity analyses rather than temporal or external validation cohorts. Detailed results are presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure 3 and Supporting Information S2: Table 2. No independent geographic or temporal validation cohort was available. Accordingly, all reported model‐performance estimates represent internal validation within the study population and do not establish transportability across institutions, endemic regions, or clinical settings.

Software

All data preprocessing, statistical analyses, modeling, internal validation, and figure and table generation were performed using R version 4.6.0.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Cohort Construction

A total of 330 hospitalized patients with laboratory‐confirmed SFTS or HFRS were included in the comparative cohort, comprising 110 patients with HFRS and 220 patients with SFTS. The study flow is shown in Figure 1. The two proof‐of‐concept models were developed and compared using the same complete‐case data set of 330 patients, whereas descriptive analyses used the available observations for each variable. Admission dates ranged from May 2023 to November 2025. The distributions of cases differed by admission year and season (both FDR‐adjusted p < 0.001), with SFTS cases occurring predominantly in summer and HFRS cases more frequently in autumn and winter.

In‐hospital clinical course and outcomes are summarized descriptively in Table 1. Patients with SFTS had more frequent ICU admission than those with HFRS (28.2% vs. 11.8%; FDR‐adjusted p = 0.001) and substantially higher in‐hospital mortality (57.3% vs. 7.3%; FDR‐adjusted p < 0.001). The median length of hospital stay was shorter in SFTS than in HFRS (9.0 [7.0–13.0] vs. 12.0 [8.0–14.0] days; FDR‐adjusted p = 0.011).

Neurological symptoms were more common in SFTS, whereas appetite loss, abdominal distension, urinary symptoms, facial or neck flushing, and conjunctival injection or edema were more common in HFRS after FDR correction. No clear between‐group differences remained for dyspnea, pulmonary abnormalities, or abdominal pain after multiple‐comparison correction. In adjusted analyses, neurological symptoms showed a positive diagnostic association with SFTS (adjusted OR, 7.94; 95% CI, 3.76–16.79), whereas urinary symptoms (adjusted OR, 0.07; 95% CI, 0.03–0.18), facial or neck flushing (adjusted OR, 0.06; 95% CI, 0.02–0.19), conjunctival injection or edema (adjusted OR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.09–0.60), and appetite loss (adjusted OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.23–0.73) showed patterns favoring HFRS. Admission‐Time Laboratory Phenotypes

The admission‐time laboratory findings are summarized in Table 2 and Figure 3. Compared with patients with HFRS, those with SFTS had lower white blood cell, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, eosinophil, and basophil counts, whereas platelet counts were lower in HFRS (all FDR‐adjusted p < 0.001). HFRS was also characterized by higher C‐reactive protein and procalcitonin levels, whereas SFTS had higher aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase levels. Albumin was lower in HFRS.

Model Development and Internal Validation

The full‐cohort basic admission model included age, female sex, onset‐to‐admission interval, neurological symptoms, urinary symptoms, white blood cell count, and creatinine, whereas the extended laboratory model additionally included C‐reactive protein, albumin, and lactate dehydrogenase (Figure 5A and Supporting Information S2: Table 3). In the full‐cohort models, female sex and neurological symptoms showed positive diagnostic associations with SFTS, whereas urinary symptoms, higher white blood cell count, and higher creatinine showed patterns favoring HFRS. Among the additional laboratory variables, lactate dehydrogenase showed a coefficient‐level diagnostic association after adjustment, whereas C‐reactive protein and albumin did not; however, this isolated association did not translate into consistent improvement in model‐level performance.

The basic admission model included age, female sex, onset‐to‐admission interval, neurological symptoms, urinary symptoms, white blood cell count, and creatinine. The extended laboratory model retained the same core variables and additionally included C‐reactive protein, albumin, and lactate dehydrogenase. Additional internal validation results are summarized in Supporting Information S2: Table 3.

The minority‐class observations‐per‐predictor ratios were 15.7 for the basic model and 11.0 for the extended model; these values were not interpreted as conventional event‐per‐variable ratios. Post hoc Riley‐based calculations indicated a minimum required sample size of 342 patients, slightly exceeding the complete‐case cohort of 330. Variance inflation factors did not indicate problematic multicollinearity, and no formal complete or quasi‐complete separation was detected in the full cohort. Nevertheless, several maximum‐likelihood estimates were extreme, prompting Firth and bootstrap sensitivity analyses. Full‐cohort LASSO tuning and Firth coefficient‐stability analyses are shown in Supporting Information S1: Figures 6 and 8, respectively.

In full‐process nested cross‐validation, the basic model achieved an out‐of‐fold AUC of 0.975 (95% CI, 0.952–0.992), compared with 0.971 (95% CI, 0.945–0.991) for the extended model (Figure 5B). The extended‐minus‐basic AUC difference was −0.004 (95% paired‐bootstrap CI, −0.014 to 0.005; paired DeLong p = 0.404). The out‐of‐fold Brier scores were 0.044 and 0.043, respectively, with an extended‐minus‐basic difference of −0.0011 (95% paired‐bootstrap CI, −0.0079 to 0.0052; paired‐bootstrap p = 0.759). The corresponding log losses were 0.188 and 0.262, and the extended model showed a higher log loss, with a difference of 0.0738 (95% outcome‐stratified paired‐bootstrap CI, 0.0052–0.1653; outcome‐stratified paired‐bootstrap p = 0.042), favoring the basic model.

Calibration intercepts were 0.118 for the basic model and 0.092 for the extended model, with calibration slopes of 0.611 and 0.429, respectively (Figure 5C). Calibration slopes below 1 suggested that predicted probabilities were too extreme, particularly for the extended model. Supplementary grouped Hosmer–Lemeshow tests based on out‐of‐fold predictions were statistically significant for both models (Supporting Information S2: Table 3). Because grouped calibration tests are sensitive to grouping schemes and sparse expected counts, particularly for the extended model, these results were interpreted as supplementary calibration checks rather than primary evidence of calibration performance. Although the continuous NRI numerically favored the extended model (0.8182; 95% CI, 0.6182–1.0002; p = 0.002), the IDI estimate was small (0.0154; bootstrap percentile 95% CI, 0.0002–0.0319). These exploratory reclassification findings were not accompanied by clear or consistent improvement in AUC, Brier score, log loss, calibration metrics, or decision‐curve net benefit. Decision‐curve analysis showed no consistent net‐benefit advantage for the extended model across the evaluated thresholds (Figure 5D). Overall, the extended model did not demonstrate consistent incremental predictive value over the more parsimonious basic model.

In the observed cohort, at the fixed probability threshold of 0.50, the basic model had a sensitivity of 96.8%, specificity of 90.9%, positive predictive value of 95.5%, negative predictive value of 93.5%, and accuracy of 94.8%. Corresponding values for the extended model were 96.4%, 90.9%, 95.5%, 92.6%, and 94.5%, respectively; bootstrap CIs are provided in Supporting Information S2: Table 3. All 1000 full‐process bootstrap repetitions converged for both models, and bootstrap predictor‐selection frequencies are shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure 7. The median final parameter counts, including the intercept, were 8 (IQR, 8–9) for the basic model and 11 (IQR, 10–12) for the extended model. An exploratory nomogram illustrating predictor contributions from the basic model is shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure 9; this visualization was not intended for individual risk estimation or use as a clinical scoring system.

In the time‐restricted analyses among patients admitted within 5 or 7 days after symptom onset, several maximum‐likelihood estimates were extreme and accompanied by very wide CIs. The extended Firth model in the ≤ 7‐day subset did not meet the predefined criteria for coefficient reporting because not all profile‐likelihood CIs converged. These findings indicated reduced model stability in early‐presenting subsets and were interpreted as sensitivity analyses of coefficient stability rather than independent temporal validation or evidence of reliable early‐stage performance.

Discussion

In this retrospective comparative cohort of hospitalized patients, we found that SFTS and HFRS exhibited distinct admission‐time clinicolaboratory phenotypes despite substantial overlap in their early clinical presentations. SFTS was characterized predominantly by a hematologic phenotype, accompanied by neurological involvement and modest hepatic abnormalities, whereas HFRS showed a more prominent inflammatory–renal phenotype with urinary manifestations. These findings suggest that the two diseases represent distinct patterns of systemic involvement at presentation rather than clinically indistinguishable hemorrhagic fever syndromes. Importantly, the observed differences were evident at admission, indicating that early disease characterization may benefit from recognizing the dominant biological patterns underlying each infection rather than relying on isolated laboratory abnormalities.

Our findings are consistent with previous comparative studies [19, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Previous studies, including those by Wang et al. [20] and Xue et al. [32], identified older age, female sex, neurological symptoms, leukopenia, and relatively preserved renal function as important characteristics distinguishing SFTS from HFRS. The consistency of these features across different cohorts suggests that they represent reproducible components of the early disease phenotype. However, previous studies have mainly focused on identifying individual clinical or laboratory characteristics, whereas the present study further places these reproducible features into the context of broader biological patterns.

The hematologic predominance observed in SFTS represents one of the most consistent features distinguishing it from HFRS. In our cohort, leukocyte‐lineage abnormalities constituted the major axis separating SFTS from HFRS, whereas hepatic abnormalities showed only modest between‐group differences. This pattern suggests that hepatic involvement in SFTS may represent accompanying systemic injury rather than the primary discriminatory feature. The prominent hematologic phenotype of SFTS is consistent with previous observations describing leukopenia, lymphopenia, and immune dysregulation during SFTSV infection. These abnormalities may reflect the complex interaction between viral infection, host immune responses, and systemic inflammatory activation during SFTSV infection. In addition, the higher frequency of neurological manifestations in SFTS further supports the concept that this infection may involve broader systemic immune and inflammatory responses beyond hematologic abnormalities alone [36, 37, 38].

In contrast, HFRS demonstrated a distinct inflammatory–renal phenotype. The predominance of inflammatory activation and renal involvement observed in our cohort reflects the characteristic clinical spectrum of hantavirus infection, in which endothelial dysfunction, immune activation, vascular permeability changes, and renal involvement contribute to disease manifestations. The presence of urinary abnormalities together with elevated renal markers provides a clinically recognizable pattern that differs from the hematologic predominance observed in SFTS [33, 34, 39].

These findings also highlight why early differentiation between SFTS and HFRS should not rely on individual biomarkers alone. Several abnormalities, including thrombocytopenia and tissue‐injury markers, were common to both diseases and therefore provided limited discriminatory information. Instead, the relative predominance of abnormalities across biological domains appeared to define the two phenotypes: hematologic abnormalities characterized the SFTS pattern, whereas inflammatory and renal abnormalities characterized the HFRS pattern. This phenotype‐oriented interpretation may provide a more clinically meaningful framework than isolated laboratory thresholds because it reflects the coordinated response of multiple biological systems during early infection.

The supplementary domain analyses further suggest that the biological features distinguishing SFTS from HFRS are not completely identical to those associated with subsequent clinical deterioration. For example, hematologic abnormalities were highly characteristic of SFTS, whereas inflammatory and renal abnormalities showed stronger relationships with severe outcomes. This suggests that the biological features defining disease identity and those reflecting disease progression may represent partially different processes, with inflammatory and organ‐injury pathways potentially playing a greater role after the initial disease phenotype has been established.

Therefore, the primary contribution of this study is not the development of a new diagnostic algorithm, but the characterization of the biological patterns that underlie early clinical differentiation between two overlapping hemorrhagic fever syndromes. The model analyses in this study should therefore be interpreted as a quantitative assessment of whether these dominant admission‐time phenotypic differences could be captured using routinely available variables, rather than as the development of an immediately deployable diagnostic instrument. The basic model captured most of the information contained in these phenotype differences, whereas additional laboratory complexity did not provide consistent incremental value. External validation and recalibration remain necessary before potential clinical implementation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective study relied on admission‐time data and therefore could not characterize longitudinal disease trajectories or dynamic changes after presentation. Second, some clinical variables were derived from retrospective free‐text documentation. Although structured coding and secondary verification were performed, residual misclassification related to retrospective documentation cannot be completely excluded and may have affected symptom prevalence estimates and model stability. Third, abnormality‐based analyses depended on institutional laboratory reference intervals, and their transportability across different analytical platforms requires further confirmation. Fourth, the study cohort consisted of hospitalized patients from a single center and may therefore have been enriched for clinically recognized and moderate‐to‐severe presentations. This spectrum restriction may have accentuated phenotypic differences between SFTS and HFRS and influenced apparent discrimination, prevalence‐dependent operating characteristics, calibration, and transportability in populations with different referral patterns or disease spectra. In addition, differences in epidemiology, healthcare settings, diagnostic practices, and co‐endemic infections across regions may influence disease presentation and model performance. The observed 2:1 SFTS‐to‐HFRS case distribution may have influenced estimate precision and prevalence‐dependent operating characteristics; accordingly, PPV and NPV should be interpreted in the context of the observed case mix. In addition, the differing admission‐year and seasonal distributions may have contributed to the observed case mix, and residual temporal confounding cannot be excluded. Because the available cohort fell slightly below the Riley‐based minimum sample‐size estimate, residual overfitting cannot be completely excluded, particularly for the extended model. Although formal separation was not detected in the full cohort, sparse‐data‐related instability remained possible in early‐presenting subsets, particularly when model estimates were imprecise. Accordingly, the models should not be considered specifically validated for early‐presenting patients, and their reliability in this subgroup remains uncertain. Most importantly, no independent geographic, temporal, or epidemiologically distinct external validation cohort was available. The models should therefore be regarded as single‐center, internally validated proof‐of‐concept tools rather than clinically deployable diagnostic instruments. Their transportability to other institutions and endemic regions remains unconfirmed, and their performance in outpatient, mild, or atypical cases has not been established. Independent external validation and recalibration are therefore required before potential clinical implementation. The organ‐domain measures should likewise be interpreted as exploratory phenotype summaries rather than validated diagnostic or prognostic scores.

In conclusion, SFTS and HFRS exhibited distinct clinicolaboratory phenotypes at admission. SFTS was characterized primarily by a hematologic phenotype with neurological involvement and modest hepatic abnormalities, whereas HFRS showed a more prominent inflammatory–renal phenotype with urinary manifestations. These findings suggest that the early distinction between these two hemorrhagic fever syndromes is better understood through patterns of biological involvement rather than isolated biomarkers. The internally validated models provided quantitative support for these phenotype differences, but independent external validation and recalibration are required before clinical application.
