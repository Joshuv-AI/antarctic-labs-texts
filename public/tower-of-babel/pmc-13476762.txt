Weight and HbA1c trajectories following initiation of continuous glucose monitoring in adults with type 1 diabetes: a cohort study using group-based multi-trajectory analysis
Authors: Sofia Pazmino, Stefanie Schmid, Nele Steenackers, Ricarda Sandig, Amar van Laar, Jantje Weiskorn, Jolien De Meulemeester, Julia K. Mader, Sara Charleer, Frank Wosch, Katja S.C. Gollisch, Dieter Erath, Christophe De Block, Jonathan Rosen, Carmen Hurtado del Pozo, Carel W. le Roux, Frank Nobels, Chantal Mathieu, Pieter Gillard, Stefanie Lanzinger, Bart Van der Schueren, Nicole Prinz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundContinuous glucose monitoring (CGM) improves glycaemic management in adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D), but may be associated with weight gain. We assessed changes in body weight and HbA1c after CGM initiation and characterized weight–glycemia trajectories.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 4178 adults with T1D initiating CGM from the DPV registry (DPV-CGM; n = 2104) and a pooled Belgian cohort (BE-CGM; n = 2074). Outcomes over 24 months were evaluated using paired t-tests and group-based multi-trajectory modelling. In DPV, CGM initiators were compared with matched controls without CGM (n = 2104; matched for sex, age, diabetes duration, baseline weight, and HbA1c).FindingsAfter 24 months, mean body weight increased by 1.8 kg [95% CI: 1.5; 2.0] in DPV-CGM and 1.0 kg [0.8; 1.2] in the BE-CGM cohort, while HbA1c decreased by −0.2% [−0.1; −0.2] and −0.1% [−0.03; −0.1], respectively (all p < 0.0001). In DPV-controls, weight increased by 1.2 kg [0.9; 1.5] (p < 0.01) and HbA1c decreased by 0.06% [−0.001; −0.11] (p = 0.05). Three trajectories were identified. The first showed modest weight gain with HbA1c improvement: 147/2104 (7.0%) DPV-CGM vs 99/2104 (4.7%) DPV-controls, and 411/2074 (19.8%) in BE-CGM. The second, largest group, showed stable weight and HbA1c: 1773/2104 (83.2%) DPV-CGM vs 1612/2104 (74.7%) controls in DPV-controls, and 1447/2074 (69.8%) in BE-CGM. The third, an unfavourable trajectory characterized by substantial weight gain (≈8 kg) with stable HbA1c, included 184/2104 (9.9%) DPV-CGM vs 393/2104 (20.5%) DPV-controls, and 216/2074 (10.4%) in BE-CGM (ꭓ2 p < 0.01).InterpretationCGM initiation was associated with modest weight gain and improved HbA1c. Compared with matched controls, CGM users were less likely to belong to the unfavourable weight gain with stable HbA1c trajectory, which occurred in 9.9% vs 20.5%, respectively.FundingInnovative Medicines Initiative2 Joint Undertaking under grant agreement No. 875534.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The prevalence of overweight and obesity is also rising in people living with type 1 diabetes, mirroring the general population. Thus, weight management has become a priority alongside improving glycaemic control.

A literature search was performed on PubMed for this study on continuous glucose monitoring in type 1 diabetes with the search terms ‘continuous glucose monitoring’ (MESH) OR ‘CGM’ OR ‘glucose monitor∗’ OR ‘glucose sens∗’ OR ‘glucose track∗’ (Title/Abstract) AND ‘Diabetes Mellitus, type 1’ (MESH) OR ‘type 1 diabetes’ OR ‘insulin-dependent diabetes’ OR ‘IDDM’ OR ‘juvenile-onset diabetes’ (Title/Abstract), yielding 6873 articles between January 1, 2000 and March 31, 2026. Studies actually performed with adults with type 1 diabetes illustrate that continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has improved diabetes management as it replaces “snapshot” glucose checks with continuous, actionable feedback about what's happening between meals, overnight, and during activity. However, evidence from both real-world data and clinical trials have raised concern about weight gain. This study aims to clarify individual-level responses by using multi-trajectory modelling in a large cohort of adults with type 1 diabetes who have initiated CGM.

Added value of this study

This study puts the average modest increase in weight observed after CGM initiation in people with type 1 diabetes into perspective by showing that only a small distinct subgroup of around 10% of CGM initiators experience substantial weight gain, while the proportion of people gaining substantial weight within the same 24-month timeframe was double (20.5%) in a matched control group without CGM. In addition, the vast majority of CGM initiators had a stable weight.

Implications of all the available evidence

CGM initiation is associated with substantial weight gain only in a small subset of people living with type 1 diabetes with high baseline BMI and weight gain during the first six months. Thus, weight management strategies should be focused on patients belonging to that group and CGM seems to support better weight management in routine care as the proportion of people gaining weight was higher in the matched control group not using CGM. This supports the use of CGM as part of broader strategies aimed at preventing weight gain in people with type 1 diabetes.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the management of type 1 diabetes has seen important treatment and technological advancements that have reduced the risk of long-term complications. For example, intensive therapy and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems with automated insulin delivery technologies that have simplified and improved diabetes management.1, 2, 3, 4 Particularly, current CGM systems have shown high effectiveness by providing real-time glucose data, which has helped improve glycaemic regulation and reduce the incidence of hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia.5,6 CGM has also demonstrated efficacy in increasing the time that blood glucose levels are within the normal range and to reduce fluctuation of blood glucose levels,7,8 which are likely beneficial for preventing diabetes-related complications such as retinopathy, nephropathy and cardiovascular disease.9, 10, 11

From real-world data, we have seen on average 0.4 [95% CI: 0.3–0.5] body mass index (BMI) unit increase after CGM initiation.12 Likely, this is due to intensification of insulin treatment as seen in the DCCT were patients treated intensively gained an average of 1.5 BMI unit among men and 1.8 BMI unit among women. The weight gain was most rapid during the first year of intensified therapy.13 Weight gain correlates inversely with improvement of glycaemic regulation,3,13,14 Weight gain occurring with insulin intensification can result in insulin resistance with a further increase in insulin need.15 For individuals living with both type 1 diabetes and overweight or obesity, a dual burden coincides with people being at risk of both obesity and diabetes complications, further compromising their overall health and quality of life.16,17 In this context, new technologies should lead to improvement of glycaemic control without increasing the likelihood of undesirable weight gain. Here, we aimed at evaluating how the introduction of CGM in individuals with type 1 diabetes influences weight and glycaemic control over time at an individual level with multicentre real-world data. To estimate if this event occurs only with CGM, we have also used as comparator a matched-control group without CGM.

Methods

Study design

In this retrospective cohort study, data was sourced from the German/Austrian/Luxembourgian/Swiss Diabetes Patient Follow-up (DPV) Registry (DPV cohort),18,19 and from the Belgian Flash Glucose Monitoring Study for Diabetes (FUTURE) trial20 and Reimbursement Study of Continuous Glucose Monitoring in Belgium (RESCUE) trial.21 Data from both Belgian trials were pooled, analysed, and presented as a joint cohort (BE-CGM cohort). DPV and BE-CGM cohorts were analysed locally, due to data accessibility. Ethical approval for the DPV initiative has been provided by the ethics committee of Ulm, Germany and each review board of the participating clinics has approved the anonymized data collection. The protocol from both FUTURE and RESCUE study trails were approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospitals of Leuven (Leuven, Belgium), which acted as the pertinent coordinating institutional review board.

Participants

From both cohorts, all adults (≥18 years) with type 1 diabetes (disease duration ≥6 months), newly started with CGM between on pump (CSII) or multiple daily injections (MDI) and with at least 2 years follow-up, and a. historical control group from the DPV cohort were used for analysis. The DPV control group consisted of individuals without CGM with a random start date and propensity score matching for age, diabetes duration, baseline weight and HbA1c, and sex (exact match). For trajectory analysis, baseline data on weight and HbA1c needed to be complete with at least two follow-up measurements recorded over 24 months. Further details about the data sources and eligibility criteria have been previously published18, 19, 20, 21 and can also be found in Supplementary Material.

Procedures and outcomes

In order to assess the change in weight (kg) and HbA1c levels (% and mmol/mol) up to 24 months after CGM initiation, baseline parameters such as diabetes duration (years), BMI (kg/m2), total daily insulin dose, insulin use per kg, insulin delivery system and demographic characteristics of the participants were analysed. Furthermore, the evolution over time of weight and HbA1c was also assessed. All outcomes were compared between CGM initiation and control group.

Statistical analysis

Baseline clinical characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables or as percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons across cohorts (DPV-control, DPV-CGM and BE) were made using one-way ANOVA from summary data. Paired t-tests were applied to evaluate significant changes in weight and HbA1c between baseline and 24 months. Chi-square with post-hoc Bonferroni correction was applied for differences between group prevalence. A p-value <0.05 has been considered statistically significant.

Group-based multi-trajectory analysis

Trajectory modelling techniques allow us to determine subgroups within a population and are helpful in understanding intra- and inter-individual outcome variability over time. The group-based multi-trajectory model (GBTM) method was used in this study for the classification of individuals with type 1 diabetes from the different data sources (DPV and BE cohorts), based on longitudinal patterns in the variables of weight and HbA1c levels. This method has been described previously.19,22,23 Briefly, it allows for the simultaneous identification of potential latent clusters following distinct trajectories across multiple indicators of an outcome of interest, providing a comprehensive representation of developmental patterns of relevant treatment parameters over time. This approach is an application of finite mixture modelling. The group-based multi-trajectory model's assumption is that the population cohort consists of J latent groups (j = 1, …, J). Each group j follows a unique trajectory modelled as a distinct polynomial function of time, with model parameters estimated using maximum likelihood.

In this modelling approach, each trajectory group is defined by a set of outcomes across K multiple variables (K = 2, in this case). To identify the final multi-trajectory model, trajectories for each outcome were estimated separately using varying numbers of groups. Determining the optimal number of trajectory groups and the appropriate polynomial order was achieved by using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and applying a minimum group size of ≥5% of participants per group. Average posterior probabilities of subgroup membership were considered when greater than 0.7 for each subgroup. The selection process was then conducted using a forward classification approach. For the current analysis, a one-group model was initially generated and then the maximum number of logical subgroups was gradually adjusted. Finally, the definitive number of multivariate trajectory groups across outcomes and the optimal polynomial order for each outcome were determined.

The clinical characteristics of the different cohorts and the identified trajectory subgroups were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range or as percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Chi-square tests were performed for comparisons across trajectory subgroups with Holms-Bonferroni correction for multiplicity. Confidence intervals of 95% were considered.

Meta-analysis of group-based trajectories across cohorts

Pooled proportions for the bi-variate trajectories were estimated while appropriately accounting for the correlation between groups originating from the same cohort. For each cohort, the number of individuals per group and total sample size were first transformed into logit-scaled proportions. To correctly model the statistical dependence between groups within the same cohort, a multivariate random-effects meta-analysis was fitted, specifying a hierarchical random-effects structure with cohort-level random intercepts nested within groups. Group was included as a fixed moderator to obtain separate pooled estimates for each group. This model simultaneously estimates group-specific pooled logit proportions while incorporating the shared within-cohort variance. Final pooled proportions and confidence intervals were obtained by back-transforming the group-specific fixed-effect estimates from the logit scale to the proportion scale.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis

Multinomial logistic regression analysis was carried out to determine the association of certain covariates with group membership, using the largest subgroup as the reference. The model applied included the following covariates at baseline: age, sex, diabetes duration (calendar) year of CGM initiation or date of inclusion to the analysis for the control group, weight, HbA1c, and total insulin dose.

Cross-lagged panel analysis

To examine the directionality and temporal dynamics of the association between weight and HbA1c, a cross-lagged panel model was performed. This analysis was applied using longitudinal data collected from individuals with type 1 diabetes after CGM initiation and up to 24 months after, to estimate the bidirectional effects between the variables across different time points, while also predicting within-variable changes over time in the whole group as well as per trajectory subgroup.

Evaluation of missingness and normality

The pattern of missingness was investigated both visually and by quantifying the proportion of missing data across variables. Normality was first assessed visually using histograms to evaluate overall distribution shape. Additionally, departures from normality were formally evaluated using distribution-based tests, specifically the Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Cramér-von Mises, and Anderson-Darling tests.

Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates that no systematic differences were observed between observed and missing values with missing data ranging from 7.9 to −10.3%. The overall structure was consistent with data that are potentially missing at random (MAR).

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 and SAS version 9.4 (PROC TRAJ macro). Complete case analysis was used.

Role of funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Overall, baseline clinical characteristics were mostly comparable between all cohorts (Table 1). For the DPV registry, none of the matched variables (sex, age, disease duration, baseline weight and HbA1c) nor pe-baseline weight and HbA1c show significant differences, suggesting successful matching and comparability. Individuals with type 1 diabetes in the Belgian (BE) cohort were on average older and had a longer disease duration. In all cohorts, most people used multiple daily injections (Table 1).

When evaluating the differences over time, there was a mean weight increase of 1.0 or 1.8 kg and a decrease in HbA1c levels of 0.1% (1.4 mmol/mol)or 0.2% after 24 months of CGM for BE and DPV-CGM, respectively (Table 1 and Supplemental Figure S2). This was reflected in a corresponding significant increase in BMI of 0.3 kg/m2 (BE) and 0.6 kg/m2 (DPV-CGM). In the DPV control group (without CGM) the mean weight increase was 1.2 kg with a 95% CI of 0.9–1.5 kg, while for HbA1c the change was −0.06% with 95% CI of −0.001 to 0.11%. Insulin dose per kilogram of body weight remained relatively stable in the DPV cohort over time, while the BE cohort experienced a slight but statistically significant reduction (Table 1).

Group-based multi-trajectory model and associated baseline factors

By applying GBTM, three distinct bi-variate trajectories of weight and HbA1c change were identified after CGM initiation (Fig. 1 and Table 2). The first subgroup (DPV-control: 4.7% [n = 99]; DPV-CGM: 7.0% [n = 147], BE: 19.8% [n = 411]) to be further referred as some weight gain with HbA1c decrease (DPV-control: +2.9 kg [95% CI: +1.8; +4.0], −2.4% [95% CI: −2.1; −2.8]; DPV-CGM: +3.8 kg [95% CI: +2.5; +5.1], −2.3% [95% CI: [−2.0; −2.6]; BE: +1.3 kg [95% CI: [+0.9; +1.7], −1.1% [95% CI: −1.0; −1.1]). The second subgroup (DPV-control: 74.7% [n = 1612]; DPV-CGM: 83.2% [n = 1773]; BE: 69.8% [n = 1447]) to be referred as stable weight and HbA1c (DPV-control: −0.5 kg [95% CI: −0.3; −0.8], +0.1% [95% CI: −0.2; +0.5]; DPV-CGM: +0.6 kg [95% CI: +0.4; +0.8], −0.01% [95% CI: −0.05; +0.03]; BE: −0.3 kg [95% CI: −0.1; −0.5], +0.2% [95% CI: +0.17; +0.24]). Finally, the third subgroup (DPV-control: 20.5% [n = 393]; DPV-CGM: 9.9% [n = 184]; BE: 10.4% [n = 216]) to be referred as weight gain with stable HbA1c (DPV-control: +6.9 kg [95% CI: +6.4; +7.3], −0.2% [95% CI: −0.07; −0.3]; DPV-CGM: +11.1 kg [95% CI: +10.2; +12.1], +0.01% [95% CI: −0.14; +0.16]; BE: +8.2 kg [95% CI: +7.6; +8.8], −0.01% [95% CI: −0.14; +0.11]). Significant proportional differences (p < 0.01) between subgroups for all three cohorts were seen. The highest weight gain with stable HbA1c subgroup was found in the control group: 20.5% [n = 393] (p < 0.01).

Pooled proportions for all 3 cohorts estimated 8.9% [95% CI: 4.7–16.2%] for the some weight gain and HbA1c decrease subgroup, 77.4% [95% CI: 63.5–87.1%] for the stable weight and HbA1c subgroup and 12.1% [95% CI: 6.5–21.3%] for the weight gain with stable HbA1c subgroup. When pooling the two CGM cohorts, the subgroups are as follows: 12.0% [95% CI: 5.6–23.9%] for the some weight gain and HbA1c decrease subgroup, 77.8% [95% CI: 60.5–89.0%] for the stable weight and HbA1c subgroup and 9.6% [95% CI: 4.4–19.6%] for the weight gain with stable HbA1c subgroup.

In the multinomial logistic regression, those with an increase of 1 kg above the average weight gain (DPV-CGM: +0.55 kg, BE: +0.51 kg, DPV-control: +0.4 kg) in the first six months after CGM initiation were almost twice more likely to belong to the weight gain with stable HbA1c subgroup (OR: 1.85 [95% CI: 1.70–2.01] (DPV-CGM), 1.91 [95% CI: 1.75–2.07] (BE), 1.98 [95% CI: 1.87–2.09] (DPV control + CGM)) compared to the stable weight and HbA1c subgroup. For glycaemic control those with an increase of 1% HbA1c above the average (DPV-CGM: −0.12%, BE: −0.17%, DPV-control: −0.11%) had around 95% less chances of belonging to the some weight gain with HbA1c decrease subgroup (OR: 0.04 [95% CI: 0.03–0.06] (DPV-CGM), 0.02 [95% CI: 0.01–0.02] (BE), 0.05 [95% CI: 0.03–0.05] (DPV control + CGM)) compared to the stable weight and HbA1c subgroup. Furthermore, in a very comparable population within DPV -matched controls-having initiated CGM gives you almost 3 times more chances of belonging to the some weight gain with HbA1c decrease subgroup (2.80 [95% CI: 1.56–5.04] -DPV control + CGM) therefore receiving an average HbA1c improvement of −2.4% (Supplementary Figure S4 and Fig. 1). These models were a good fit to the data with a high Nagelkerke pseudo R2 (DPV-CGM: 0.61, BE: 0.66, DPV control + CGM: 0.58).

Cross-lagged panel modelling

After performing a cross-lagged panel analysis on weight and HbA1c in each trajectory group, we found that each variable highly predicts its own change for the next timepoint, making the prediction of individual trajectories relevant. Also, it was observed that there was small or no interrelationship between weight and HbA1c levels across timepoints in the subgroups with the exception of the weight gain with stable HbA1c subgroup of the DPV-CGM cohort where HbA1c at 12 months was a predictor of the weight at 24 months with a gain of almost 1 kg for each percentage of HbA1c higher than the mean (Supplemental Figure S4).

Discussion

The present study provides new insights into the divergence in both weight and glycaemic evolution in adults with type 1 diabetes following CGM initiation. After 24 months with and without CGM, we observed modest but significant improvements in HbA1c and limited overall weight gain. Some individuals -CGM and their matched controls—experienced notable weight gain despite overall stable HbA1c. However, the proportion of people in the group with this unfavourable trajectory was substantially lower in the cohorts where CGM was initiated when compared to matched controls. This suggest that CGM may be a valuable tool in overall strategy to prevent excessive weight gain in people with type 1 diabetes.

Using GBTM, we identified three distinct subgroups with divergent glycaemic and weight trajectories, offering a more granular understanding of the impact of overall T1D evolution and more specifically, after CGM initiation: 1) some weight gain and HbA1c decrease, 2) stable weight and HbA1c, and 3) weight gain with stable HbA1c. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to reveal that CGM initiation in adults with type 1 diabetes is usually not accompanied by weight gain. In fact, only ≈10% of the CGM populations were in a subgroup experiencing substantial weight gain, compared to 20.5% in the control group without CGM.

The subgroup (9.9–20.5%) who experienced significant weight gain (BE: +8.2 kg, DPV-CGM: +11.1 kg, DPV-control: +8.2 kg) without accompanying glycaemic benefit after 24 months suggests that CGM use does not increase the likelihood of gaining weight. On the contrary, initiation of CGM may be part of the overall strategy to try to prevent excessive weight gain while maintaining glycaemic control. Our study provides evidence for differential weight gain in people living with type 1 diabetes. Individuals in the unfavourable subgroup with weight gain with stable HbA1c had the highest baseline weight (DPV-CGM: 1.76 kg, BE-CGM: 0.97 kg above cohort level) and the highest proportion of participants in the BMI >30 kg/m2 category. Moreover, when looking for early predictors for belonging to this group, individuals at a higher weight at baseline had further weight gain. This observation raises clinical concerns regarding the risk of developing “double diabetes”, a condition exhibiting features such as more pronounced insulin resistance and increasing central adiposity, thus combining characteristics of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes16 and further exacerbating the already high risk of developing cardiovascular11 and other complications associated with type 1 diabetes.9,10 Moreover, as a clinical red flag, the change in both HbA1c and weight within the first six months is highly predictive of the future trajectory. For people gaining approximately 1.5 kg in the first six months this almost doubles their chances of future significant weight gain (between 8.2 and 11.1 kg after 2 years) -weight gain with stable HbA1c subgroup-. Furthermore, we saw that in this group between year 1 and year 2, HbA1c control influenced weight gain, by every 1% increase, 1 kg was gained in the DPV-CGM cohort, pointing to a dynamic interplay between glucose control and weight gain. This “at-risk” population, would likely benefit the most from timely clinical adaptations like considering adjunctive therapy or early referral for surgical interventions to ensure the best glycaemic and metabolic/weight control.

Conversely, a second subgroup demonstrated substantial HbA1c improvements without significant weight gain. These individuals were generally younger with shorter diabetes duration, a higher baseline HbA1c and lower weight at baseline when compared to the other subgroups; all traits favouring a more beneficial response to tight glucose monitoring. In the DPV cohort, the population having initiated CGM were three-times more likely of belonging to the some weight gain with HbA1c decrease than their matched counterparts in the control group. This suggests that starting CGM in this population may help enhance their glucose control without risk of excessive weight gain. On the other hand, behavioural adaptations such as greater dietary awareness, improved insulin titration strategies, and increased physical activity24 may help explain the observed pattern in this subgroup.

In contrast to the concerns that CGM might exacerbate weight gain,12 GBTM allowed us to better understand individual responses to CGM initiation and compare them to a control group without CGM initiation. While other studies have shown a trend of increasing BMI (0.40, 95% CI: 0.3–0.5 kg/m2, representing approximately 1.1 kg (95% CI: 0.9–1.4) of weight gain after CGM initiation for a person with a height of 1.7 m) within two years after CGM initiation,12 our results suggest that for the majority (69.8%–83.2%) of users weight and HbA1c remained relatively stable over two years. Furthermore, the results from the control groups suggest that substantial weight gain within a two-year period may be a more general phenomenon that is experienced by a subgroup of people living with type 1 diabetes. These findings are in contrast with Tayside/Fife (Scotland, UK) where the control group showed no significant BMI changes over time.12 However, their control group was not matched for any baseline characteristics but only same year of CGM initiation.12 Our results do align with previous short-term studies showing small or inconsistent effects of CGM on weight, and smaller HbA1c reductions when used without advanced insulin delivery systems.8,25, 26, 27 For instance, in a clinical trial evaluating CGM use in adults with type 1 diabetes on multiple daily injections, the mean reduction in HbA1c levels was 1.0% after 6 months of follow-up.8 A more recent RCT reported similar results, finding a 0.8% reduction in mean HbA1c in individuals with type 1 diabetes, also 6 months after CGM start.28 Even though a statistically significant improvement in glycaemic control was also observed in the current study, the average reduction after 24 months of CGM start was small (0.07–0.15%). However, the biggest difference was made in the first 6 months (0.12–0.17%). Moreover, other studies have shown no change in HbA1c in people living with type 1 diabetes after using CGM,6,7 reinforcing the fact that CGM itself is not the sole factor driving glycaemic improvements in individuals with type 1 diabetes or that the findings seen in randomized controlled trials may attenuate over time and particularly in a real-world -not so tightly controlled-setting. This is further supported by our observation that 4.7% of people in the DPV control group without CGM also experienced substantial reductions in HbA1c.

This study used robust, longitudinal real-world data from multiple cohorts, allowing for a more comprehensive analysis of weight and glycaemic trajectories. Although the follow-up period of up to 24 months does allow for a valuable longitudinal assessment of the outcomes with and without CGM initiation, longer-follow ups might be needed to assess stability of changes as well as their association with future complications. Another strength of this study is the use of GBMT modelling, which demonstrates how averages and aggregated data can mask heterogeneous responses. On the other hand, the retrospective design of the study presents limitations, such as potential unmeasured/unavailable confounding factors that could mediate the metabolic changes observed and variability in CGM adherence. In particular the absence of information on social determinants of health could be considered a limitation, considering that these could substantially impact the trajectories observed in both the CGM cohorts and the DPV control group. Our findings should be viewed in the context of predominantly European cohorts with relatively limited ethnic variation and similar health-care structures, which may influence generalizability. Furthermore, it is important to highlight that the majority of individuals evaluated in this study were applying multiple daily injections as insulin delivery method and pumps rarely included hybrid closed-loop systems (<2%). Hybrid closed-loop systems have shown better glycaemic control beyond what can be achieved with multiple daily injections of insulin plus CGM.29,30 Hence, it is important as future work to evaluate weight and glucose control in individuals with hybrid closed-loop systems, especially since they are rapidly becoming standard of care in type 1 diabetes.4

This study has identified subgroups for people living with type 1 diabetes that may allow to personalize interventions tailored to metabolic and behavioural profiles. Future research on the dual burden of type 1 diabetes and obesity should focus on incorporating longer-term follow-up assessments to confirm if these weight and glycaemic trajectories persist or evolve in the long run. Additionally, subgroup analyses based on CGM adherence, physical activity levels, and psychological factors may provide further insights into individualized responses. Investigating metabolic pathways associated with weight changes in people living with type 1 diabetes could also help elucidate potential biological mechanisms underlying these findings.

In conclusion, our study reveals that most individuals with type 1 diabetes remained stable in both weight and glycaemic control up to 24 months with or without CGM use. While some experienced notable weight gain with stable HbA1c, another subgroup of individuals demonstrated substantial glycaemic improvement with minimal weight gain. These findings indicate that although weight gain in general is a concern for people living with type 1 diabetes, CGM as a tool can be used in diabetes care without major implications on weight for the vast majority of people and might even limit weight gain. Instead, the study highlights the interpersonal variability in metabolic responses in people living with type 1 diabetes and the need to investigate the impact of treatments and tools such as CGM in different subgroups.
