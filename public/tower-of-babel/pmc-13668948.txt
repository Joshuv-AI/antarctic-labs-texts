1,3-Dioxolane derived nucleos(t)ides: synthetic strategies, current position, and future promise in antiviral and anticancer drug discovery
Authors: Meghana Gudi, Jeremy Estes, Yugandhar Kothapalli, Stefan G Sarafianos, Uma S Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo date, altered nucleos(t)ides offer significant potential in antiviral and anticancer drug discovery. In the past several decades, numerous modified nucleos(t)ides have received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for the treatment of viral infections and several cancers. Modifying the base or sugar of a nucleos(t)ide confers specificity and selectivity for viral RNA/DNA polymerases and highly dividing cancer cells. Ribose- and carbocyclic-based nucleosides are well established in medicinal chemistry, yielding many preclinical leads and clinical candidates with therapeutic potential. The insertion of a 1,3-dioxolane ring in place of the ribose sugar represents a unique strategy in nucleoside design to target viral DNA polymerases and rapidly proliferating cancer cells. To date, only a few 1,3-dioxolane-derived nucleos(t)ides (DDNs) have been progressed for clinical development. The chemical space of DDNs remains insufficiently explored. Substantial efforts are still required to develop novel molecules within this class, which may open new avenues for the discovery of potent antiviral and anticancer agents. This review is focused on the synthesis and development of selective DDNs and their potential in preclinical/clinical development. Keywords: Dioxolane-derived nucleos(t)ides (DDNs), Antivirals, Anticancer, Drug Discovery

Introduction

1,3-Dioxolanes, heterocyclic acetal cyclopentyl rings, offer a potential replacement of the sugar scaffold in nucleosides to construct either D- or L-dioxolane-derived nucleos(t)ides (DDNs) [1, 2]. These molecules both with D- (natural) and L- (unnatural) configuration lack the crucial 3ʹ-OH necessary to propagate DNA chain elongation (Fig. 1). Additionally, replacing a ribose or 2ʹ-deoxy-ribose sugar with a dioxolane ring in nucleosides, generates specificity and selectivity for viral polymerases without causing significant cytotoxicity to human cells. DDNs may act as both viral polymerase inhibitors as well as viral DNA chain terminators. Mechanistically, the incorporation of either D- or L- dioxolane-derived nucleotide into an elongating viral DNA chain ends up with chain termination, which ultimately inhibits viral growth [3–5]. Oxathiolanes [Fig. 1(ii)], similar to dioxolanes, though with a 3′ sulfur in lieu of an oxygen, also have potent antiviral activity [6]. Lamivudine (3TC) and emtricitabine (FTC), derived from oxathiolane sugars, are FDA-approved drugs for the treatment of HBV and HIV infections, respectively [6].

Compared to the 2ʹ-deoxyribose nucleosides, 3′-OH is absent in both oxathiolane- and dioxolane-derived nucleosides. B represents nucleobase A adenine, G guanine, C cytosine, U uracil; and T thymine

In the past, dioxolane nucleos(t)ides have shown potent antiviral and anticancer activity. Our antiviral drug discovery group in the College of Pharmacy at the University of Georgia, and several others have previously synthesized numerous dioxolane analogs; some of these analogs have shown promising antiviral and anticancer activity [1, 7–13]. D-DOT (1, Fig. 2), a dioxolane thymidine nucleoside, and its prodrugs have exhibited potent anti-HIV activity [14]. Amdoxovir (DAPD, 2), a diaminopurine dioxolane nucleoside, is a potent HIV replication inhibitor [3]. Bromovinyl uracil L-BHDU (3) and vinyl uracil L-HDVD (4) are dioxolane nucleosides with strong activity against herpesviruses [4, 15]. L-BHDU showed activity primarily against varicella-zoster virus (VZV) [15], whereas L-HDVD exhibited broad-spectrum antiviral activity against Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), and herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) [4]. Troxacitabine (5), an L-dioxolane pyrimidine analog, has shown promising anticancer activities across numerous cancers, and has also exhibited anti-HIV and anti-HBV activity[2, 16, 17]. Among these molecules, DAPD and troxacitabine advanced into clinical development. Currently, POM-L-BHDU-MP [7,18–20] (a phosphate ester prodrug of L-BHDU) as well as L-HDVD [4] are under active investigation in preclinical and clinical studies for the treatment of herpesvirus infections.

Structures of potent D- and L-dioxolane-derived nucleoside analogs

There remains a significant need to develop highly efficacious and selective dioxolane nucleosides as antiviral agents. Achieving this goal will likely require additional structural alterations, either on the dioxolane ring or on the nucleobase. Such modifications increase molecule complexity and often confer improved selectivity toward specific viruses. Additionally, replacing nitrogen with carbon in the nucleobase can have a profound effect on antiviral activity. This substitution removes a potential hydrogen-bond acceptor or donor site, which can substantially alter the molecule’s binding properties and overall profile. Inspired by these modified-base strategies, 7-deazapurines and other altered-base nucleos(t)ides have attracted considerable attention as promising antiviral agents [2]. For example, tubercidin (6, Fig. 3), a 7-deazaadenosine analog, and its derivatives exhibit nanomolar potency against hepatitis C virus (HCV), as well as notable anticancer activity [21, 22]. 3-Deaza-2ʹ-deoxyguanosine (7), another deazapurine analog, demonstrated antiviral activity against both RNA and DNA viruses [2].

Structures of deazapurine nucleosides with potent antiviral activity

Furthermore, the strategic insertion of a D-dioxolane ring in combination with a 2,6-diaminopurine base led to the development of amdoxovir (2) [8]. Subsequent replacement of the purine with a pyrimidine base yielded DOT (1), which exhibited potent anti-HIV activity [13]. The incorporation of a L-dioxolane with cytosine base produced troxacitabine (5), a highly effective anticancer agent [23]. These examples illustrate how structural modifications at the 2ʹ- and 4ʹ-positions of DDNs, together with changes to the nitrogenous purine and pyrimidine bases, can open promising new pathways for developing novel antiviral and anticancer agents. Although significant medicinal chemistry efforts are currently underway, further research is still needed. This review focuses on the synthesis, biological activity, preclinical studies, and clinical development of selected DDNs (Fig. 2). It aims to provide a valuable resource for medicinal chemists, virologists, and chemical biologists interested in revisiting these analogs and exploring their therapeutic potential in the antiviral and anticancer fields.

D-DOT (1)

The chemical name of D-DOT is (-)-β-D-(2 R,4 R)-dioxolanylthymine, and it is a thymidine-based nucleoside analogue. D-DOT features a D-dioxolane ring in place of the furanose ring, forming a distinctive dioxolane-cyclopentyl-like structure. D-DOT has shown promising antiviral activity against HIV-1 while exhibiting no significant cytotoxicity in preclinical studies [24, 25]. The antiviral potency of D-DOT as a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) was assessed through a series of biochemical assays, where its inhibitory activity was compared with that of clinically approved NRTIs [25]. In initial cell-based studies, D-DOT showed promising anti-HIV-1 activity in the ATH8 cell line and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), with notably lower cytotoxicity than the standard drug azidothymidine (zidovudine, AZT) [16, 26]. Following these encouraging initial results, D-DOT was advanced as a preclinical candidate. To support further biological evaluation of D-DOT, several synthetic approaches were developed. In parallel, various prodrug strategies were explored to improve its antiviral profile. These included amino acid esters [13], short- and long-chain lipid phosphate derivatives [13], and phosphoramidate prodrugs [27], all of which were synthesized and tested for antiviral activity. Although none of these prodrugs demonstrated superior antiviral potency than the parent nucleoside (D-DOT), the modifications may still offer advantages in pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of D-DOT, including improved oral bioavailability and enhanced cellular uptake.

Saponification of the methyl ester 10 was carried out by using aqueous lithium hydroxide (LiOH) in a 4:1 THF/water mixture to produce the carboxylic acid intermediate 11. Antiviral activity of D-DOT (1)

The racemic (±)-DOT was initially screened against wild-type (WT) HIV-1 in ATH8 cells by measuring cytopathic effect, yielding an EC50 of 20 µM (Table 1), compared to 0.5 µM for the reference antiviral AZT [28]. Although racemic (±)-DOT was significantly less potent than AZT, its substantially lower cytotoxicity made it an interesting candidate for further antiviral evaluation. Subsequent testing in PBM cells revealed much greater potency of racemic (±)-DOT, with an EC50 of 0.09 µM and no detectable cytotoxicity up to CC50 > 100 µM (in Vero cells). In the same assay, AZT showed an EC50 of 0.009 µM and racemic (±)-DOT was found to be approximately 10-fold less potent [16]. Since racemic (±)-DOT comprises both D- and L-isomers, a key goal was to identify which isomer was primarily responsible for the antiviral activity, Chu et al re-evaluated the pure chiral D-DOT and revealed its EC50 of 0.3 μM in PBM cells as well as in ATH8 cells [16, 26]. Importantly, D-DOT retained strong antiviral activity against the lamivudine-resistant M184V mutant (EC50 = 0.088 µM) [16, 30]. It also showed activity against AZT-resistant strains 9 F with EC50 values of 4.9 and an EC50 of 0.2 µM against AZT-sensitive 10 strain without cytotoxicity up to CC50 > 100 µM.

Additionally, D-DOT retained its antiviral profile against NRTI-resistant HIV-1 reverse transcriptases (HIV-RT) with K65R (EC50 = 0.21 µM), L74V (EC50 = 0.33 µM), T215Y (EC50 = 0.27 µM), M184V/Q151M (EC50 = 0.23 µM) and D67N/K70R/T215Y/K219Q (EC50 = 0.49 µM) mutations (Table 1) [24]. In repeated screening against the HIV-1 LAI strain in PBM cells, D-DOT maintained its antiviral potency of EC50 = 0.68 µM. Additionally, in Vero cells, D-DOT expressed moderate antiviral activity (EC50 = 24.6 μM) against severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) without any significant cytotoxicity (CC50 > 100 µM) [31].

Molecular modeling studies of D-DOT with wild-type as well as with mutated HIV RT (HIV reverse transcriptase) strains have been examined [24, 30]. Docking studies revealed that D-DOT triphosphate (DOT-TP) maintains a series of hydrogen-bonding interactions with the HIV-1 RT binding site. The bonding between the DOT-TP and adjacent amino acid residues (Lys65, Lys73, Arg72, Tyr115 and/or Gln151) played crucial roles in stabilizing the enzyme-inhibitor complex. Particularly in the case of AZT-resistant mutant, Asp113 and Ala114 demonstrated promising binding affinity to DOT-TP. The dioxolane ring consistently appear to adopt a 3ʹ -exo conformation contrary to the endo conformation adopted by most natural deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs) interacting with HIV RT. The 3ʹ oxygen atom also appeared to express stronger interactions in the binding pocket of mutant HIV RT strains.

Several prodrug strategies have been implemented to improve the antiviral profile of D-DOT. Specifically, the 5ʹ-O-aliphatic and amino acid ester prodrugs, including phosphoramidate prodrugs of D-DOT, were synthesized [13]. However, the application of prodrug strategies to D-DOT did not result in a significant enhancement of antiviral activity; nevertheless, such approaches may offer potential advantages in improving the PK profile of D-DOT. In preclinical development, the PK profile of D-DOT was evaluated in rhesus monkeys [32]. In a single-dose 33.3 mg/Kg D-DOT was administered via intravenous and oral route. The PK studies demonstrated that D-DOT was more bioavailable than AZT and expressed an average serum half-life of 2.16 h. Also, a rapid absorption of D-DOT was observed and was almost completely absorbed by the rate constant of 2.7 h−1. The average renal and systemic clearance of D-DOT were 0.18- and 0.36-liter/h/kg, respectively. Within 8 h, 4–18% of the administered D-DOT (either by oral or IV route) was excreted through urine as its glucuronide conjugate, indicating an efficient systemic clearance.

The mechanism of action studies of D-DOT suggest that it converts into active DOT-TP and acts as a viral DNA chain-terminator [25]. It was also examined that D-DOT exclusively phosphorylated by human thymidine kinase-1 (TK-1) but not by other pyrimidine nucleoside kinases, including the mitochondrial thymidine kinase (TK-2) [33]. Interestingly, DOT was not a substrate for the host mitochondrial enzyme (TK-2), indicating that it lacks mitochondrial toxicity [33]. The biochemical studies suggest that compared to other approved nucleoside analogs, D-DOT exhibits greater resilience against thymidine analog-associated mutations (TAMs), as well as the M184V and K65R mutations, which are commonly observed in HIV RT and are known to compromise the efficacy of NRTI-based therapies [25]. The potent antiviral activity of D-DOT against lamivudine- and zidovudine-resistant HIV-1 mutants, and its excellent PK profile, suggest that D-DOT and related analogs may hold significant potential for preclinical/clinical development as a promising candidate for the treatment of drug-resistant HIV infections. To date, the development of D-DOT has been halted; however, further evaluation of various phosphate ester and phospholipid prodrug strategies remain warranted. These approaches may enhance its PK profile, including its activity against HIV. Furthermore, structural modification at the 5-position of D-DOT may yield new analogs with potential antiviral activity against emerging viruses.

Amdoxovir (DAPD, 2)

The chemical name of amdoxovir is [(2 R,4 R)-4-(2,6-diaminopurin-9-yl)-1,3-dioxolan-2-yl] methanol [34]. It is also known as 1-β-D-2,6-diaminopurine dioxolane (DAPD, 2). DAPD is an NRTI with potent activity against wild-type HIV-1 and it maintains its activity against key reverse transcriptase or thymidine kinase mutants [8]. It is a 6-amino prodrug, which in vivo converts to (-)-β-D-dioxolane guanine (DXG, 39) [3]. Due to the limited solubility of DXG, the prodrug DAPD was synthesized to enhance its solubility [3]. Antiviral data highlight its utility as a standalone or in combination with other approved NRTIs, including enfuvirtide [35], zidovudine [36], inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMP) inhibitors mycophenolic acid (MPA), and ribavirin (RBV) [37] against wild-type and drug-resistant HIV variants. While DAPD was advanced to Phase II clinical trials, it was subsequently withdrawn from further development due to safety concerns [9, 38].

Belleau et al [39] has reported the first synthetic protocol for racemic (±)-DAPD. The synthesis began with the condensation of 2-chloroacetaldehyde 40 with glycerol 41 to afford the racemic 2-chloromethyl-4-hydroxymethyldioxolane intermediate 42 (Scheme 5). Compound 42 was treated with potassium benzoate in the presence of the phase-transfer catalyst 18-crown-6 ether in DMF to afford benzoate ester 43. Hydroxy of 43 was oxidized with pyridinium dichromate in DMF to give carboxylic acid intermediate 44.

Compound 47 was treated with a methanolic ammonia solution under pressure at 110 °C to afford the final racemic (±)-DAPD (48).

The chiral pure synthesis of D-DAPD was achieved by Chu et al, starting from 1,6-anhydro-D-mannose and involving 7 steps to construct the chiral pure D-dioxolane acetate intermediate (49) [26]. Acetate 49 was coupled with silylated purine 50 in the presence of TMSOTf in DCM to produce nucleoside 51. TBDPS of 51 was deprotected by treatment with tetrabutylammonium fluoride solution (TBAF or n-Bu4NF) in THF to afford compound 52, which, upon treatment with methanolic ammonia under pressure, afforded the final chiral pure D-DAPD (Scheme 6) [34]. This reported synthesis was lengthy and involved several protections, deprotection, and oxidation steps, and was not suitable for the scalable synthesis of chiral D-DAPD.

Antiviral activity of DAPD (2) and DXG (39)

Preliminary in vitro antiviral evaluation of DXG and its 6-amino prodrug DAPD showed potent anti-HIV activity against both wild-type and HIV RT mutants [3, 42]. DAPD was also found to be effective against wild-type and drug-resistant hepatitis B virus (HBV) variants [42, 43]. In initial screening, DAPD showed an EC50 of 0.7 µM in PBM cells (PBMC), with no cytotoxicity up to CC50 > 100 µM [34]. After this exciting finding, the antiviral potency of DAPD and DXG was thoroughly studied in various cell lines, and DAPD exhibited 12.5, 4.0, and 0.97 µM activity in MT2, PBMC, and CBMC cells, respectively (Table 2). DXG demonstrated greater antiviral potency than DAPD in MT2, PBMC, and CBMC cells.

In extensive in vitro cytotoxicity studies, DAPD and DXG exhibited no detectable toxicity in human T-cell lines at concentrations exceeding 100–1000 µM (CC50), and also no mitochondrial toxicity was detected [3]. In contrast, the FDA-approved antiretroviral drug AZT demonstrated measurable toxicity in the same cell lines [3]. The data suggest that DXG is equipotent to FDA-approved lamivudine and approximately 5–10-fold less potent than AZT. Notably, further antiviral evaluations demonstrated that both DAPD and DXG retained antiviral activity against multiple 3TC- and AZT-resistant mutants and maintained efficacy against a panel of recombinant HIV-1 strains harboring resistance to 3TC and AZT [42]. Interestingly, recombinant viruses and clinical HIV-1 isolates from patients treated with NRTIs and/or non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) remained susceptible to inhibition by DXG [44]. Against the HXB-2 and NL4-3 viral isolates derived from clinically treated patients, DXG demonstrated in vitro EC50 of 0.5 and 1.6 µM, respectively, in MT-2 cells (Table 2). In resistance studies, novel S68Δ deletion in HIV RT displayed 10-30 fold resistance to DAPD [45] and HIV RT with K65R [46], L74V, and/or Q151M were also found less susceptible to both DAPD and DXG [47]. Mechanistically, DAPD is deaminated by adenosine deaminase to yield the guanosine dioxolane analogue (DXG) [3]. However, the rate of DAPD deamination is markedly slower than that of the endogenous adenosine nucleoside. After deamination, DXG undergoes phosphorylation steps to form DXG-monophosphate (DXG-MP), DXG-diphosphate (DXG-DP), and DXG-triphosphate (DXG-TP). DXG-TP competes with the natural substrate 2ʹ-deoxyguanosine triphosphate (dGTP) for binding to HIV RT and effectively incorporates into elongating viral DNA, thereby causing chain termination [42]. The PK studies of DAPD and DXG were performed in the woodchuck (Marmota monax) animal model. The DAPD was administered at 20 mg/kg intravenously (IV) and orally, and the concentrations of nucleosides DAPD and DXG in plasma and urine were determined by HPLC. In intravenous administration, the half-life (T1/2) time of DAPD was found to be 6.7 h in comparison to DXG (T1/2 = 17.5 h), which suggests DXG has greater retention in the body than DAPD [48]. The mean total clearance (CLT) of DAPD was 0.33 ± 0.14 liter h/kg, and the steady state volume of distribution (Vss) of DAPD was found to be 1.76 ± 0.65 L/kg. The oral bioavailability of DAPD was measured in a range of 3.7 to 8.2%; however, the availability of DXG following the oral administration of DAPD was 10.5–53% [48].

Considering its favorable safety profile and potent antiviral activity against multiple HIV RT mutants, including drug-resistant variants, DAPD was selected for further preclinical and clinical development. In addition, DAPD was evaluated in combination therapies to develop effective treatments for HIV. In vitro, DAPD in combination with enfuvirtide (T-20) demonstrated synergistic antiviral activity against both sensitive and resistant clinical isolates, with significant additive effects and improved EC50 values [35]. Notably, in vitro, a combination of MPA and RBV, inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase inhibitors, reduces the EC50 of DXG by 10-fold. Also, the combination of MPA, RBV, and DAPD was effective against a mutant virus (K65R/Q151M) fully resistant to inhibition by DAPD [37]. Furthermore, in clinical trials, the combination of AZT and DAPD demonstrated synergistic efficacy, resulting in greater reductions in viral load than DAPD monotherapy, while enabling a lower effective AZT dose when combined (clinical trial: NCT00432016) [36]. In clinical trial, DAPD was evaluated at doses of 200, 300, and 500 mg twice daily. Data revealed that, in week 12, HIV-1 RNA levels decreased by a mean of 1.5 log10 copies/mL in the 300 mg group, and by 0.75 log10 copies/mL in the 500 mg group. However, five subjects demonstrated lens opacities during the study [49]. Additionally, preliminary toxicity assessments of DAPD revealed obstructive nephropathy resulting from drug crystallization within the renal tubules in some animals, and lens opacities were observed in both animal and human studies. Consequently, DAPD was discontinued from further clinical development due to these toxicities (clinical trial identifiers NCT01737359 and NCT01738555) [49]. Subsequently, several 6-amino-modified prodrug strategies targeting DAPD were explored; however, none progressed to clinical phase [8].

L-BHDU (3)

5-((E)-2-bromovinyl)-1-((2S,4S)-2-(hydroxymethyl)-1,3-dioxolan-4-yl)pyrimidine-2,4(1H,3H)-dione, also known as L-BHDU, is an unnatural L-dioxolane nucleoside that exhibits potent antiviral activity against VZV and herpes simplex virus (HSV) [15, 50]. The VZV causes chickenpox on primary infection and zoster (shingles) on reactivation from latency [51]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that approximately 1 million cases of zoster occur annually in the United States (U.S.) [52]. People over the age of 50, organ transplant patients, and individuals living with immunocompromised conditions or infection with HIV are at higher risk of VZV infection and its reactivation. Acyclovir (ACV), valacyclovir (VACV), and famciclovir (FCV) are FDA-approved drugs for the treatment of VZV infections [53]. Another drug, brivudine (BVDU), has been approved in Europe to treat VZV infections and expressed strong antiherpetic activity [54]. Despite its potency against VZV and HSV, BVDU has raised safety concerns. In the liver, BVDU is metabolized to bromovinyluracil (BVU), which inhibits the action of the dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) enzyme. DPD is required for the conversion of thymidine to uracil, and drug 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) is also catabolized via DPD [55]. Therefore, the use of BVDU reveals harmful interaction with 5-FU in cancer-treating patients. Similar to BVDU, L-BHDU belongs to a series of E-5-bromovinyl analogs in which an L-dioxolane ring replaces the ribose ring [7]. L-BHDU demonstrated potent antiherpetic activity against VZV [7, 15]. L-BHDU maintains both in vitro and in vivo antiviral potency against VZV in several cell lines including human retinal pigment epithelial cell line (ARPE-19), primary human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs), skin organ culture (SOC) and in SCID-Hu mice with skin xenografts [15]. Additionally, L-BHDU was found to be safer and has not expressed any drug-drug interactions for the metabolism of 5-FU [15]. Currently, L-BHDU and its phosphate ester prodrug POM-L-BHDU-MP are under preclinical and clinical investigation to treat shingles, oral and genital herpes [7, 18].

The initial synthesis of L-BHDU was accomplished by Choi et al [50, 56] via protected-L-gulofuranose 63. Considering this, the conversion of 87 to 88 was revisited, and key intermediate 88 was synthesized by the conversion of acid 87 with manganese(II) acetate [Mn(OAc)2] in the presence of (diacetoxyiodo)benzene and acetic acid in 2-methyl-THF [57]. Preliminary L-BHDU demonstrated promising in vitro antiviral activity against VZV. In initial screening, L-BHDU exhibited a potent antiviral profile, with an EC50 of 0.07 µM and a selectivity index (SI) > 1428 [50]. To confirm the antiviral potency, L-BHDU was screened against various viral strains in several cell lines and was found to be potent in all evaluations. L-BHDU showed EC50 values of 0.22 and 0.24 µM against VZV-BAC-Luc in HFF cells and SOC, respectively, with a high SI (Table 3).

In repeated screening assays using ARPE-19 cells, L-BHDU maintained its antiviral profile, with an EC50 of 0.26 µM and a SI greater than 3878 against the VZV-ORF57-Luc strain [7]. The L-valyl prodrug of L-BHDU (92) demonstrated enhanced antiviral potency, exhibiting an EC50 of 0.03 µM against VZV-BAC-Luc in HFF cells with no observed cytotoxicity (CC₅₀ > 200 µM). Additionally, it showed an EC50 of 0.03 µM against the VZV-ORF57-Luc strain in ARPE-19 cells, with a high SI [7, 15]. Notably, the L-valyl prodrug of L-BHDU retained its antiviral activity compared to the parent nucleoside. Owing to this advantageous mechanism, POM-L-BHDU-MP was selected for further preclinical and clinical development.

In various altered cells and VZV strain evaluations, L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP have shown the best antiviral profiles against VZV-ORF57-Luc (Table 4). To examine L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP against viral mutants, these molecules were screened against VZV TK and TS (thymidylate synthase) mutants using standard cidofovir (CDV) and acyclovir (ACV) as positive controls [61]. The EC50 of both L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP against cell-associated VZV were comparable or slightly higher than those obtained with cell-free VZV (Table 4). L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP were more potent against VZV-ORF57-Luc than CDV or ACV (Table 4). However, CDV demonstrated antiviral activity against VZV-ORF57-∆TK and VZV-ORF57-∆TKTS, whereas ACV did not, owing to its dependence on phosphorylation by TK. L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP exhibited EC50 of 0.028 µM and 0.0012 µM, respectively, against VZV-ORF57-∆TS strain, and prodrug 96 surpassed antiviral activity compared to standard CDV and ACV. Additionally, L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP were found to be not active against VZV-ORF57-∆TK or VZV-ORF57-∆TKTS [7].

L-BHDU and its L-valyl prodrug, 92, were evaluated in vivo using the SCID-Hu mouse model implanted with VZV-infected human fetal skin xenografts (VZV-BAC-Luc) [62, 63]. L-BHDU was administered orally at doses of 8, 15, and 150 mg/kg/day, while L-valyl-L-BHDU was given at doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg/day. Control groups included mice treated with 0.4% CMC (vehicle), acyclovir (120 mg/kg/day), or valacyclovir (120 or 200 mg/kg/day). In vivo, mice treated with L-BHDU and L-valyl-L-BHDU showed reduced viral growth; a 150 mg/kg/daily dose of L-BHDU and a 30 mg/kg/daily dose of L-valyl-L-BHDU were effective in mimicking viral growth compared with the vehicle group [15]. There is no significant in vivo toxicity observed in L-BHDU and L-valyl-L-BHDU treated mice compared to the control group. L-BHDU also expressed a dose-dependent tissue distribution in plasma, heart, spleen, liver, kidney, xenograft skin (human), and mouse skin [15]. The in vitro and in vivo data revealed that L-BHDU is more active against VZV than ACV and VACV and offer preclinical and clinical development for the treatment of herpes zoster. Additionally, catabolic studies proved that L-BHDU does not affect the metabolism of 5-FU. It was concluded that L-BHDU is an unnatural L-dioxolane analog and is not a substrate for the thymidine phosphorylases (TPase) [64] to catabolize into BVU base, which mimics the metabolism of 5-FU. Hence, the cancer-treating patients are safe with the co-treatment of 5-FU and L-BHDU [15].

The PK profiles of L-BHDU and POM-L-BHDU-MP were evaluated for stability in liver microsomes, gastric and intestinal juices. It was found that POM-L-BHDU-MP was stable in both gastric and intestinal juice [7]. However, in liver homogenate examinations, POM-L-BHDU-MP rapidly converted to L-BHDU and L-BHDU-monophosphate (L-BHDU-MP). POM-L-BHDU-MP was incubated with homogenized mouse liver and was found to be unstable and converted to L-BHDU and L-BHDU-MP [7].

To determine the oral availability of POM-L-BHDU-MP compared to L-BHDU, a single equimolar dose of POM-L-BHDU-MP or L-BHDU was administered by oral gavage to two groups of BALB/c mice. The AUC value for L-BHDU in POM-L-BHDU-MP-treated mice was approximately 2.2-fold more than that of L-BHDU-treated mice. Additionally, Cmax for L-BHDU was significantly higher after administration of prodrug POM-L-BHDU-MP than L-BHDU (Table 5) [7]. Therefore, it was concluded that POM-L-BHDU-MP exhibits better oral absorption/availability relative to L-BHDU and is a promising candidate for further drug development against VZV infection. Currently, POM-L-BHDU-MP is undergoing toxicological and extended PK analysis and may be progressed to clinical development.

L-HDVD (4)

Chemically, L-HDVD, is known as L-[(2S,4S-2-(hydroxymethyl)-1,3-dioxolan-4-yl]5-vinylpyrimidine-2,4(1H,3H)-dione. It is an L-dioxolane analog that, in vitro, has shown effective and selective antiviral activity against EBV, KSHV, and HSV 1 [4]. EBV is a DNA virus that establishes life-long infection and causes mononucleosis, which is a considerable cause of morbidity in adolescents and young adults [65]. Both EBV and KSHV infections show tropism for lymphocytes and increase the risk of several cancers and autoimmune diseases [66, 67]. A recent definitive epidemiology study proves that individuals with EBV infection are 32 times more at risk for multiple sclerosis (MS) [68]. MS is a leading disease that impacts more than 2.8 million people worldwide. Notably, immunocompromised persons with cancer, HIV infected, and organ transplant patients have a higher risk of infections and reactivation of EBV and KSHV [69]. Infections of HSV 1 and 2 are a significant cause of oral and genital herpes and necessitate the discovery of new antivirals [70, 71]. In vitro, L-HDVD and its L-valyl prodrug (97) showed potent activity against EBV and KSHV and were more active than the FDA-approved ganciclovir and ACV [4]. It was speculated that L-HDVD selectively interacts with viral DNA polymerase, and its triphosphate (L-HDVD-TP) is anticipated to function as a competitive inhibitor with natural substrate dTTP (2ʹ-deoxy thymidine triphosphate). L-HDVD-TP may act as a DNA chain terminator by incorporating into the elongating viral DNA and blocking further extension due to its lack of the required 3ʹ-OH [4]. L-valyl-L-HDVD may demonstrate better oral availability and cellular uptake, which, after ester hydrolysis, produces L-HDVD. ACV and BVDU have demonstrated specificity to EBV TK, converting it to its active phosphate form [72]. This finding is significant, as EBV TK exhibits a narrower substrate specificity for nucleoside analogs compared to alpha (α)-herpesvirus TK, making it a challenging target for nucleoside-based antivirals [4]. In vitro antiviral data suggest that L-HDVD and L-valyl-L-HDVD have potential for preclinical and clinical development to treat EBV and KSHV infections and may facilitate in the cure of their associated cancers.

Antiviral activity of L-HDVD (4)

L-HDVD was tested against various gammaherpesviruses and herpesviruses using reference FDA-approved ACV [74], BVDU [54], CDV [75, 76], and ganciclovir (GCV) [77] as controls. By using qPCR antiviral assay, L-HDVD and its valyl prodrug (97) were evaluated against EBV and KSHV in P3HR-1 and BCBL-1 cell lines, respectively [4]. L-HDVD demonstrated a potent activity with an EC50 of 0.01 µM, and L-valyl-L-HDVD expressed an EC50 of 0.02 µM against EBV (Table 6).

It is important to note that L-HDVD and its L-valyl prodrug demonstrated comparable antiviral efficacy across all tested viruses. Both compounds also exhibited low, to no cytotoxicity in all tested cell lines and displayed high SI values. Viral TK activates L-HDVD that subsequently targets viral DNA polymerase [4]. Nucleoside analogs may also be activated by mitochondrial TK-2, which can result in host mitochondrial toxicity [78]. This is an issue associated with nucleoside-based drugs because the inhibition of dThd phosphorylation by mitochondrial TK-2 catalysis can lead to mitochondrial toxicity [79]. Therefore, L-HDVD was compared with BVDU for the inhibition of dThd phosphorylation. During the study, L-HDVD showed an approximately 500-fold decrease in its ability to inhibit TK-2–mediated dThd phosphorylation [4]. It indicates that L-HDVD has a substantially lower affinity for TK-2 and is unlikely to contribute to host mitochondrial toxicity. Additionally, L-HDVD showed stronger substrate activity with HSV-1 TK than with VZV TK, which explains why it was more active against HSV-1 than VZV [4]. After establishing the affinity of L-HDVD for viral TK, additional mutation studies were conducted using MHV-68 TK at higher L-HDVD concentrations. The studies revealed three distinct mutations in the TK gene of MHV-68 isolates that emerged following selection for L-HDVD resistance. Notably, no L-HDVD associated mutations or resistance were observed in the DNA polymerase or in the protein kinases of gamma herpesviruses [4].

Furthermore, L-HDVD in vivo evaluation was performed in BALB/c mice infected with the MHV-68 using BVDU as the standard drug [4]. Notably, MHV-68 is closely related to murine gammaherpesvirus and encodes a TK with significant homology to TKs of both EBV and KSHV [80, 81]. L-HDVD was orally administered at 200 mg/kg body weight once daily for five consecutive days, beginning on the day of infection. Alternatively, L-valyl-L-HDVD was administered at 100 mg/kg twice daily for five consecutive days. After a 6- and 12-day treatment of L-HDVD and L-valyl-L-HDVD, viral DNA loads in lung, spleen, and mediastinal lymph nodes (MLNs) were determined by qPCR. The data revealed that viral DNA loads were efficiently reduced after 6- and 12-day treatment of both L-HDVD and L-valyl-L-HDVD. Interestingly, in MLNs, neither L-HDVD nor BVDU affected viral load compared with untreated mice. No in vivo toxicities were observed for either L-HDVD or its prodrug. L-HDVD demonstrated robust inhibition of MHV-68 replication and effectively delayed the establishment of viral latency in the spleen [4]. These data indicate that L-HDVD, or its prodrug, offers substantial potential for the therapeutic management of EBV- and KSHV-associated diseases and for preventing or delaying the onset of virus-associated cancers, particularly in immunocompromised individuals.

Troxacitabine (5)

Troxacitabine (BCH 4556, or SPD 758), originally known as L-(-)-dioxolane-cytidine or [(-)-OddC] [5]. It is the first L-dioxolane nucleoside analog that showed promising anticancer activity [23]. Originally, L-enantiomers of dioxolane- or oxathiolane-derived nucleosides were believed to be inactive drugs because they were not recognized by cellular enzymes [2]. However, the discovery of lamivudine [82], an oxathiolane-derived molecule, proved that L-dioxolane derived nucleoside analogs may have therapeutic potential [83]. Later, troxacitabine was invented as a potent anticancer agent [5, 84]. Initially, in preclinical studies, troxacitabine expressed broad-spectrum anticancer activity against solid tumors, multidrug-resistant (MDR) tumors [85], hematological malignancies [86], and chemoresistant tumors [5, 87]. Mechanistically, it undergoes sequential conversion into mono-, di-, and triphosphate forms and is subsequently incorporated into the elongating DNA chain, leading to DNA chain termination [5, 88]. Additionally, troxacitabine is not the substrate for cytidine deaminase (CDA or dCD), which is beneficial compared to other deoxycytidine nucleoside analogs [5]. It was progressed to phase II clinical trial to treat refractory lymphoproliferative neoplasms or multiple myeloma [89] as well as advanced cancer of the pancreas and gastrointestinal tumors [90]. Results from a phase II clinical study indicated that troxacitabine monotherapy provided limited clinical benefit in patients with advanced lymphoproliferative disorders or multiple myeloma. However, modification of the troxacitabine dosing regimen, particularly in combination therapy, may enhance its clinical utility in the treatment of hematologic malignancies. Currently, the phosphoramidate prodrug of troxacitabine is under investigation for the treatment of liver cancer [91].

Initially, troxacitabine was synthesized from protected-L-gulofuranose. Protected-L-gulofuranose was converted to intermediate 66 (synthesis described in Scheme 8) [56]. Compound 66 was converted to crucial acetate intermediate 105 via a sequence of protection, deprotection, oxidation, and acetylation (Scheme 16) [92]. Compound 105 was coupled with the silylated N-acetylated cytosine 106 via HMDS in the presence of TMSOTf in DCM to produce the desired β-coupled compound 107. Finally, treatment of 107 with a methanolic ammonia solution affords the final troxacitabine (5).

In these cell lines IC50 of troxacitabine was evaluated using standard cytarabine (Ara-C) [94]. Notably, troxacitabine’s inability to undergo conversion to an inactive uracil metabolite confers a significant advantage over other approved nucleoside analogs. Mechanism of action studies proved that troxacitabine incorporates into the elongating DNA chain, and due to the lack of 3ʹ-OH, acts as a chain terminator [5]. In vivo, troxacitabine was tested in nude mice bearing HepG2 and DU-145 xenografts. It was found that troxacitabine effectively reduces the tumor growth within 15 days of treatment and was safe at a 50 mg/Kg dose [5]. Additionally, troxacitabine inhibited the growth of colon tumors and prolonged the survival of mice bearing p338 leukemia cells [5]. Troxacitabine has shown broad tumor cytotoxicity and was found effective against human tumor colony-forming unit (HTCFU) [84]. It was further advanced to the phase I/II clinical trial to treat refractory acute myeloid leukemia [95]. However, troxacitabine was later dropped from the clinical trials due to its limited efficacy against various cancers compared to other approved nucleosides. In 2005, troxacitabine was granted orphan drug designation (ODD) by the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to treat acute myeloid leukemia (AML) but was withdrawn in 2008 [96]. Currently, to enhance the PK and anticancer profile of troxacitabine, various prodrug strategies are underway, and recently, a phosphoramidate prodrug of troxacitabine was introduced as a clinical candidate for the treatment of liver cancer [91].

Conclusions

Dioxolane-derived nucleos(t)ides (DDNs) are actively investigated as promising scaffolds in antiviral and anticancer drug discovery and development. These molecules have demonstrated potent therapeutic efficacy against a range of viruses. However, so far, none of these DDNs have received FDA approval for treatment. Currently, POM-L-BHDU-MP has only been advanced for the treatment of shingles, oral, and genital herpes. The replacement of the ribose ring of nucleosides with either the natural (D-form) or the unnatural (L-form) 1,3-dioxolane ring generates altered nucleosides specific to virulent viruses and highly dividing cancer cells. The unique features of DDNs place them in a special niche in medical chemistry for their exploration against emerging viruses and cancers. DDNs, as therapeutics, interact with DNA polymerase and inhibit DNA chain elongation in highly dividing cancerous cells and viruses. Furthermore, a detailed SAR exploration of both D- and L-dioxolane-derived nucleosides remains warranted, particularly through alternative base modification strategies. In future studies, structural modifications at the 2ʹ- and 4ʹ-positions of DDNs may offer significant opportunities for the development of new antiviral agents. This review focuses on the synthesis and preclinical/clinical development of DDNs in the antiviral and anticancer fields and timely highlights two molecules, POM-L-BHDU-MP and L-HDVD, with therapeutic potential for treating infections caused by VZV, HSV, EBV, and KSHV. We hope that this report will spark some interest in the synthesis of modified DDNs and the development of new molecules of this class as antiviral and anticancer agents.
