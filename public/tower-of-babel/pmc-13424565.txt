Comparison of 2% lidocaine with epinephrine and 4% articaine with epinephrine in local anesthesia of maxillary molars affected by molar incisor hypomineralization: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mai Haidar, Nabih Raslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of local anesthesia is essential for pain control during the treatment of MIH-affected molars. This study aimed to compare injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy between 2% lidocaine with epinephrine (LE) and 4% articaine with epinephrine (AE) in the MIH-affected maxillary molars of pediatric patients. Twenty-six children aged 6 to 12 years requiring restorative treatment of MIH-affected maxillary molars were enrolled. Participants randomly received one of the two anesthetic solutions (LE or AE) at the first visit; the other solution was administered at the second visit. Injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy were evaluated subjectively using the Wong-Baker Faces® pain rating (WBF) scale and behaviorally using the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability (FLACC) scale. No statistically significant difference was found between LE and AE in injection-related pain on either scale (P > 0.05). Similarly, no statistically significant difference was recorded between the two solutions in the anesthetic efficacy for MIH-affected maxillary molars, according to both scales (P > 0.05). These findings indicate that both LE and AE provide comparable clinical outcomes, supporting the use of either solution as a local anesthetic for MIH-affected maxillary molars in pediatric patients.

Introduction

Molar–Incisor Hypomineralization (MIH) is defined as a qualitative enamel defect affecting at least one permanent first molar and may also involve the permanent incisors1. It represents a major challenge in dentistry due to its variable clinical manifestations, diverse treatment options, and the pain during treatment of MIH-affected teeth2–4.

In this context, the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) has recommended the need for pain control in these cases5. Local anesthesia is one of the recommended methods6,7, however, evidence on its efficacy in MIH-affected teeth remains conflicting. One study reported a high anesthesia failure rate of up to 74.1% in MIH-affected mandibular molars8, whereas two other studies showed high success rates9,10. These discrepancies may be due to various factors, including tooth characteristics, the type of anesthetic used, and the anesthesia technique used8–10.

LE and AE are among the most commonly used anesthetic solutions in pediatric dentistry11. Studies comparing these solutions in children have reported variable results regarding both injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy12,13. In MIH-affected mandibular molars, both solutions have been shown to be effective9, supporting their potential use in this challenging cases.

To date, no clinical data are available on injection-related pain with LE versus AE in the maxilla of children with MIH, nor on their efficacy in MIH-affected maxillary molars. Furthermore, the pharmacological differences between the two solutions may lead to clinical performance variations when used in the maxilla. This emphasizes the need for a comparative study of these two solutions in MIH-affected maxillary molars to guide practitioners toward the most suitable solution.

In light of the above, this study aims to compare injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy of LE versus AE in MIH-affected maxillary molars of pediatric patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a cross-over, triple-blind, randomized controlled trial. It was designed and reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting of Trials (CONSORT) guidelines14.

Ethical approved was obtained from the University Research Ethics Board (Approval No. 1969), and all procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Registry No. NCT05585112; First registration date: 15/10/2022).

The clinical phase was conducted in the Department of Pediatric Dentistry between October 2022 and May 2023. A flowchart summarizing the study design is presented in Fig. 1.

The sample size was calculated using the G * power software (version 3.1.9.7), with an alpha error of 5% and a power of 95%. The odds ratio (OR) of 1.69 was obtained from a previous systematic review16. Therefore, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 19 participants. Nevertheless, the final sample consisted of 26 patients, in anticipation of the expected high dropout rate due to the common difficulties in adhering to treatment appointments in our population.

Participants

The study sample included children recruited from patients attending the Department of Pediatric Dentistry at the University.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: the child had to be aged from 6 to 12 years, weigh at least 21 kg, be healthy according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA 1), and have no allergies to LE or AE. Additionally, the child should not have taken any analgesics or sedatives prior to either the first or second session. Both of the child’s first permanent maxillary molars had to be decayed, with a score of 4 (underlying dark shadow from dentin with or without localized enamel breakdown), 5 (distinct cavity with visible dentin), or 6 (extensive distinct cavity with visible dentin) according to the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS II)17. Furthermore, both of these molars had to be affected by MIH and have the same score on the Ghanim et al. index – either score 2 (white, creamy, yellow or brown opacities) or score 3 (post-eruptive breakdown)18.

Exclusion criteria involved children with uncooperative behavior according to the Frankl behavior classification19. Additionally, children who had an infection/inflammation at the injection site or irreversible pulpitis in any of the MIH-affected maxillary molars were excluded.

Randomization and blinding

The solution used in the first visit was determined by a random table generated by the Research Randomizer website (https://www.randomizer.org/).

Regarding the molar side for the first visit, a concealed card was drawn from a box containing 26 cards (13 right and 13 left).

Both the random table and the box of cards were kept in the office of the Vice Dean for Scientific Affairs at the Faculty of Dentistry to ensure allocation concealment.

Randomization and the concealed card drawing were performed by an independent assistant who was not involved in the administration of anesthesia, the dental treatment, or the pain assessment.

The study employed a triple-blind design, where the child, evaluators, and investigator were unaware of the solution used in each visit to avoid bias and ensure objective results. To achieve blinding, the independent assistant removed the label from the anesthesia ampoule and assembled the syringe out of sight.

Outcomes and evaluation tools

The primary outcomes were the comparison between LE and AE in terms of injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy in MIH-affected maxillary molars. These outcomes were assessed using both the WBF scale and the FLACC scale.

The WBF is a subjective tool consisting of a series of faces ranging from a happy face (score 0) to a crying face (score 10), as shown in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2The WBF scale.

The WBF scale.

The FLACC behavioral scale evaluates five categories (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability), with a total score ranging from 0 to 1020.

The secondary outcomes included two components. First, anesthesia efficacy in MIH-affected maxillary molars, regardless of the anesthetic solution used, was evaluated subjectively using the WBF scale and was considered effective at scores of 0, 2, or 421. Second, the relationship between caries characteristics and anesthesia efficacy in these molars was investigated. The caries characteristics -depth and extension- were determined based on photographs taken after complete caries removal.

Procedures

In the first visit, written informed consent was obtained from the child’s legal guardians. An experienced pediatric dentist, not involved in the study, recorded demographic data (gender, age and weight), and assessed the molars to determine the MIH score using Ghanim’s index. The same dentist then determined the child’s cooperation score and explained the WBF scale to the child in a simple manner.

The independent assistant determined the solution as lidocaine HCl 2% with 1:80,000 epinephrine (Lidocaine 2% E-80, New Stetic S.A., Antioquia, Colombia) or articaine HCl 4% with 1:100,000 epinephrine. (Artheek®, New Stetic S.A., Antioquia, Colombia) according to the random table, then removed the label from the anesthetic ampoule. He also drew a card to determine the location of the molar (right or left). After that, the syringe and its components (anesthetic ampoule and 27-gauge needle) were assembled.

The investigator, a pediatric dentist, performed local anesthesia of the molar using a supraperiosteal injection. After negative aspiration, 1.5 ml of the solution was deposited slowly11,22. During anesthesia and subsequent dental procedures, verbal communication and positive reinforcement techniques were applied23. At the end of the injection, the child indicated their pain on the WBF scale. Immediately afterward, a palatal infiltration injection was performed using the remaining volume from the same ampoule.

After a five-minute waiting period24, the MIH-affected molar was isolated using a rubber dam, and the caries along the MIH-affected enamel attached to it were removed. The child was then asked to indicate their level of pain using the WBF scale. Subsequently, several photographs of the molar were taken, and the tooth was restored using composite resin (Charisma®, Heraeus Kulzer, Hanau, Germany).

If signs of unacceptable pain appear during caries removal, the procedure is stopped. The child is then asked to indicate their pain score on the WBF scale, and an additional dose is administered, making sure not to exceed the Maximum Recommended Dose (MRD) of the solution used. The MRD for LE and AE is 7 mg/kg11. The procedure is resumed after a 5-minute wait.

In case pain persisted after administering an additional dose of local anesthetic, the affected tooth will be covered with a temporary glass-ionomer cement (GIC) restoration (Fuji IX, GC)3, the definitive restoration with composite will be completed in a subsequent visit, and the patient will be excluded from the study.

The child was monitored for side effects during the session by the investigator and for 24 hours afterward by their parents. Any observed side effects were recorded in the child’ records.

One week later, the MIH-affected contralateral maxillary molar was treated following the same protocol, but using the other solution. All anesthetic injections and treatments throughout the study were conducted by a single investigator.

The child’s behavior during anesthesia and caries removal was recorded on video using two cameras: one recorded the child’s facial expressions, and the other focused on body movements. Two independent evaluators later assessed the videos. Intra- rater reliability was assessed by re-evaluated the videos two weeks after the initial assessment, and inter- rater reliability by comparing the assessments of both evaluators. The duration of caries removal for each tooth was measured using the video recordings, with the actual time recorded from the start of caries removal until its completion.

To investigate the relationship between caries characteristics and anesthesia efficacy, two experienced dentists (with more than 30 years of clinical experience) estimated the depth and extension of caries in MIH-affected molars through photographs taken after caries removal. The molars were then classified into two types9: type 1 with dentinal caries on a single surface, shown in Fig. 3, and type 2 with deep caries and/or caries on at least two surfaces, shown in Fig. 4. The intra-rater for caries characteristics and the inter-rater reliability were assessed.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analysis was performed for all variables. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was used to assess intra- and inter- rater reliability. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to compare the two solutions in terms of injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy. Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ) test was used to assess the strength and direction of the association between the two scales. The Chi-square test was used to investigate the relationship between these characteristics and anesthesia efficacy. Two exploratory analyses were conducted: the Chi-square test was used to analyze the distribution of caries characteristics between the two solutions, and a paired t-test was used to compare the duration of caries removal between the two solutions. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A moderate positive correlation between the WBF and FLACC scales was found for both injection-related pain and anesthetic efficacy (Spearman’s ρ = 0.480 and 0.488, respectively; P < 0.05).

According to the WBF scale, local anesthesia was effective in 43 out of 52 MIH-affected maxillary molars. In contrast, anesthesia failure was observed in 9 molars from 8 children, resulting in a failure rate of 17.31%. Notably, all children received a single ampoule (1.8 ml of solution) per session, with no additional anesthesia dose required. No side effects were observed in any participants during the procedures or in the 24 h afterward.

The intra-rater reliability for caries characteristics was = 1.00 for the first evaluator (perfect agreement), and = 0.96 for the second evaluator (almost perfect agreement). The inter-rater reliability between the two evaluators was also = 0.96 (almost perfect agreement). Following the classification of molars based on caries characteristics, 20 molars were identified as type 1 and 32 as type 2. Anesthesia was effective in 17 out of 20 type 1 molars and in 26 out of 32 type 2 molars. Overall, there was no significant relationship between caries characteristics and anesthesia efficacy in MIH- affected maxillary (P = 0.728). An unanticipated pulp exposure occurred in one molar classified as type 2.

The exploratory analyses showed that distribution of molars according to caries characteristics did not differ between LE and AE (Type 1: 11 vs. 9; Type 2: 15 vs. 17; P = 0.569). The mean duration of caries removal was 4.70 ± 2.22 min for LE and 4.60 ± 2.14 min for AE, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.115), suggesting that these factors did not significantly affect the comparison of anesthetic efficacy between the two solutions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comparison between LE and AE in the context of local anesthesia of MIH-affected maxillary molars in children.

Children aged 6–12 years were included, as this is the typical period represents for MIH treatment3 and is used in clinical studies evaluating local anesthesia efficacy in MIH-affected molars4,9. Previous comparisons of LE and AE in pediatric patients have also focused on this age group13,25,26.

Tooth hypersensitivity was not evaluated in the present study, as the Ghanim et al. index used to determine the MIH score does not include sensitivity evaluation18. In addition, the treatment plan in the study sample was based on the presence of caries in the tooth, not on sensitivity, making its assessment irrelevant to the treatment decision. Furthermore, performing a sensitivity test could increase the child’s perception of pain during subsequent procedures; therefore, this test was avoided to ensure accuracy and impartiality of the results, in accordance with the AAPD policy on ethical responsibilities in pediatric care27.

Composite resin was chosen for treating molars in this study, as it is recommended for MIH-affected molars with breakdown, according to the MIH Treatment Need Index (MIH-TNI)28. It is also suitable for molars with demarcated opacities and caries, in line with the AAPD guidelines5.

The WBF (subjective) scale and the FLACC (behavioral) scale were selected because they are commonly used and reliable tools in pediatric dentistry research for the 6–12-year age group9,13,20,25,26. Combining these scales provides a more comprehensive view of the pain and reduces bias.

One ampoule of the anesthetic solution was administered to all children, in accordance with AAPD recommendations for using the lowest effective and safe dose11. This dose is widely used in pediatric dentistry studies12,13,22 and has demonstrated efficacy and safety in children with MIH-affected teeth9.

Topical anesthetic was not used because its effectiveness is limited to a depth of 2–3 mm29, whereas the supraperiosteal injection exceeds this depth24. Additionally, its unpleasant taste may cause discomfort and, in some cases, crying in children29.

Regarding injection-related pain, no significant difference was observed between AE and LE. This can be explained by the fact that the majority of cases reported mild pain or no pain, which reduced the possibility of detecting any statistical differences between the two solutions. This finding is in agreement with a previous study13. In contrast, Massignan et al. found a significant difference on the Faces Pain Scale-Revised (FPS-R)12. This discrepancy may be due to the methodological differences between the two studies.

The similar anesthetic efficacy of LE and AE in MIH-affected maxillary molars may be explained by the porosity and trabecular nature of the maxillary bone, which facilitates the diffusion of both solutions30. With regard to epinephrine concentration, 1:80,000 was used with 2% lidocaine and 1:100,000 with 4% articaine in this study, as these are the most commonly used commercial formulations in pediatric dentistry studies9,25,26,31,32. The literature indicates that varying epinephrine concentrations (1:50,000, 1:80,000, 1:100,000, and 1:200,000) used with 2% lidocaine do not produce statistically significant differences in anesthetic efficacy33–35. Our findings are consistent with Kolli et al.25. In the same context, the WBF scale results came from the study by Mittal et al.26. However, a discrepancy was noted with the Modified Behavior Pain Scale (MBPS), which showed a significant difference between the two solutions26. This difference may be explained by the fact that Mittal et al. assessed behavior directly, whereas the present study used video observation, which offers higher reliability and repeatability36. Our results also agree with the FLACC scale findings of Bahrololoomi and Maghsoudi22. However, the WBF scale result differed, possibly due to their use of two injections of LE versus one injection of AE, while in our study, all children received two injections of both solutions.

The success of local anesthesia in the 43 MIH-affected maxillary molars may be attributed to decreased or absence sensitivity in some of these teeth3. Moreover, the presence of dentin caries can stimulate tertiary dentin formation, which may reduce sensitivity if present37. This result is consistent with previous studies that evaluated anesthesia efficacy in MIH-affected teeth4,8–10. Conversely, our findings differ from those of a questionnaire study involving general dentists38, which might result from differences in dentists’ clinical experience or patient characteristics.

Regarding the failed cases, one molar experienced a pulpal exposure during deep caries removal, and this case was retained to avoid bias. Two molars belonged to a child who selected face “8” on the WBF scale in the first visit and face “6” in the second, which may suggest a low pain threshold. In another case, the child indicated face “10”, despite the FLACC assessment showing only mild pain, which may suggest the influence of emotional factors on pain perception. The remaining five molars, all children pointed to face “6”, probably failure due to the unsuccessful supraperiosteal injection, which has a 73–100% success rate in the first permanent maxillary molars39–43. Conversely, a higher failure rate was recorded in a previous study on MIH-affected molars, possibly due to not separating the MIH-affected maxillary and mandibular molars in the analysis9.

The observed 17.3% anesthesia failure rate highlights the need for strategies to ensure adequate pain control in MIH-affected teeth. This may include supplemental injections4,8, alternative anesthetic techniques10, or preemptive analgesics44 to ensure successful completion of dental procedures while maintaining patient comfort. Awareness of this failure rate also helps dentists better plan treatment and anticipate potential challenges when managing MIH-affected maxillary molars.

No additional dose of local anesthetic was required, even in the single case of severe pain recorded on the FLACC scale, where the child selected of face “4” on the WBF scale, supporting the clinical decision not to repeat the anesthesia. These results agree with the study by Özgül et al.4. Conversely, Dixit and Joshi found that 51.8% of MIH-molars needed repeat anesthesia8 but their study involved supraperiosteal injections for MIH-mandibular molars. Additionally, this result differs from Cabasse et al.10 who noted that one MIH-affected molar required additional anesthesia; however, their sample included molars requiring endodontic treatment.

No side effects were reported in the present study with either LE or AE, which is in agreement with the study of Kolli et al.25 and, also the study of Haidar & Raslan9.

Caries characteristics did not significantly affect anesthesia efficacy. This may be due to inflammatory changes in the pulpal tissue of MIH-affected teeth -affecting anesthesia efficacy- which may present even in the absence of caries45,46. This finding is consistent with a previous study conducted on MIH- affected mandibular molars9.

This study provides clinical evidence for comparing LE and AE in MIH-affected maxillary molars and also offers a simple and inexpensive approach to pain management. The use of a crossover design minimized inter-individual variability, while standardized anesthesia conditions and MIH score enabled a reliable comparison between the two solutions. Furthermore, the consistent results of the two pain scales, along with adherence to CONSORT guidelines, supported the accuracy and reliability of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered in this study. First, children with negative behavior were excluded due to the difficulty in managing MIH-teeth treatment, which may limit the generalizability of the results to this behavioral group, and highlight challenges in applying these findings to all pediatric patients with MIH. Second, previous dental treatment experiences of the participants were not directly assessed, which may influence their pain response and behavior during anesthesia and treatment, potentially affecting the observed outcomes. Third, the wide age range and the variability in MIH scores, including molars with opacities and post-eruptive breakdown, may affect the outcome related to anesthesia efficacy in MIH-affected maxillary molars, regardless of the anesthetic solution used, and should be recognized as factors affecting the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, the use of commercially available local anesthetic formulations (LE and AE) limits the ability to isolate the individual effects of each anesthetic agent. Therefore, future studies are recommended to address these limitations to improve both the accuracy of assessing anesthesia efficacy and the applicability of the results to a broader population of children with MIH.

Despite these limitations, LE and AE demonstrated comparable clinical outcomes in the context of local anesthesia for MIH-affected maxillary molars. These findings support the use of either solution as a local anesthetic in managing such complex dental cases.
