Research on Activation Mechanisms In Situ during Rapid Desorption of Mercury-Absorbing Activated Coke by the Heating Method
Authors: Donghai An, Jiawei Zhao, Yanna Chen, Chunyan Bai, Xingxing Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe adsorption of active coke is a good method to control mercury in coal-fired power plants, but spent powdered activated coke (SPAC) will cause secondary pollution and waste of resources if it is not properly treated. The purpose of this study was to explore the desorption performance of SPAC when heated in a drop-tube reactor under different atmospheres. The carbon consumption was 0.13%, 0.24%, 0.17%, 0.16%, and 0.14%, under desorption conditions in N2, O2, H2O, CO2, and SFG (N2 + 12% CO2 + 6% H2O + 6% O2) atmospheres, respectively. The readsorption performances of PAC–N2, PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, PAC–O2, and PAC–SFG were 40%, 72%, 77%, 58%, and 86%, respectively. In particular, compared with the original PAC, the physicochemical properties of PAC–SFG (optimum conditions) changed greatly; the surface area of PAC–SFG and the pore volume increased by 14.1% and 11.6%, respectively, and the average pore diameter decreased by 0.203 nm. Furthermore, the total acid content of PAC–SFG was 0.505, which was greater than those of the other samples. Additionally, the desorption and activation mechanisms in situ were determined under the optimum reaction conditions. The results showed that the activator gas reacted with C to form new functional groups, Lewis acid sites, and developed pore structures, which could increase Hg0 adsorption efficiency from 66% to 86% after the adsorption/desorption cycles. This study provides a novel idea for the reuse of SPAC, and this research is expected to provide valuable guidance for Hg0 removal and SPAC activated in situ during rapid desorption processes in future large-scale engineering applications.

Introduction

Owing to its high toxicity, mercury has attracted worldwide attention, and coal combustion is the greatest pollution source of Hg0 emissions.1−3 In order to protect human health and the environment, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued the Mercury and Air Toxics Standards (MATS) to limit mercury pollution from power plants in December 2011.4 In China, an increasing amount of attention has been paid to mercury pollution, with the mercury emission standard dropping from 30 μg/m3 to 3 μg/m3 by 2020.5 Therefore, China is facing huge challenges in reducing mercury emissions.

During combustion, mercury in flue gas is present as particulate-bound mercury (Hgp), oxidized mercury (Hg2+), and elemental mercury (Hg0).6−8 Hg2+ can be removed by wet flue gas desulfurization (WFGD) systems because it tends to be soluble in water.9,10 Hgp can be effectively captured by dust collectors.11−13 However, Hg0 is released into the atmosphere because it is extremely difficult to collect using existing air pollution control devices. Furthermore, Hg0 is slowly oxidized and solubilized in rainwater and transported to soil, rivers, and lakes, where it is transformed into more toxic organic species. Therefore, the control of mercury in coal-fired power plants is an urgent problem.14−17

In previous studies, there have been many methods for Hg0 capture, such as sorbent injection,18−20 catalytic oxidation,21−24 electrocatalytic oxidation,25 and photochemical oxidation.26−28 Activated carbon injection is the most effective technique for Hg0 removal from combustion flue gases because the adsorbent has an excellent specific surface area, developed pore structure, and good removal performance for a variety of pollutants. However, the operating cost limits the use of activated carbon for Hg0 removal. Hence, the development of more economical and effective sorbents is critical for both scientific and practical concerns.29−32 Activated coke (AC) is obtained by lignite pyrolysis, and it is considered to be one of the ideal adsorbents for Hg0 control in industry, because it contains various oxygen functional groups, developed pore structure, and excellent mechanical strength. More importantly, the AC price is lower than that of activated carbon.33−35 The active sites of the AC will be occupied by Hg0 in the adsorption process and lose its activity. Normally, the spent AC tends to be stacked or landfilled, resulting in a waste of resources and readily producing secondary pollution.36−38 In order to avoid these problems, the regeneration method has been shown to increase the number of times activated coke can be used, which can save costs for Hg0 control using AC.39

The purpose of this paper is to explore the regeneration method of spent powdered activated coke (SPAC), and the application potential of a drop-tube reactor for in situ activation during the rapid desorption process has received little attention and has not been an area of active research. The activation mechanism of SPAC was explored under different atmospheres of N2, H2O, CO2, O2, and SFG. Additionally, the desorption properties of SPAC activated in situ were examined in the drop-tube reactor at high temperatures, with the expectation that the PAC would develop a higher adsorption capacity for Hg0. Furthermore, changes to the PAC basic physicochemical properties and particle size, NH3-TPD, and carbon consumption were investigated. As expected, this research provides an energy and natural resource conservation procedure for the control of Hg0 by powdered activated coke in coal-fired power plants.

Experimental Section

The main experimental system is shown in Figure 1, consisting of a drop-tube device, mercury detection system, and temperature control system. In order to avoid condensation of water vapor and mercury on the walls, the pipes should be heated. The simulant gases were controlled by mass flow controllers (MFCs) accurately.

Desorption and adsorption system of samples.

The mercury detection system: the Thermo Fisher Mercury Continuous Emissions Monitoring System was used to measure the concentration of Hg0, which can measure the mercury concentration in different forms in the flue gas. The Hg0 concentrations throughout the empty reactor showed less than 2% variation compared with the Hg0 concentrations through the bypass, indicating that the initial concentration was consistent before adsorption.

The Hg0-adsorption system: a bubbling fluidized bed reactor was selected to adsorb Hg0 to avoid the resistances of a fixed-bed. The reactor was designed with a quartz filter inside a quartz pipe, with an inner diameter of 30 mm and a height of 1.2 m. The bore was evenly distributed in the quartz filter with a diameter of 0.2 mm, which was formed by a laser.

Material Preparation

Zhundong lignite (Changji City, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China) was used to prepare the PAC under coal-fired hot gas conditions in a drop-tube reactor, which has been proven as a feasible and rapid method for obtaining PAC for mercury removal.40 The proximate and ultimate analyses of the PAC are listed in Table 1. The Hg0-adsorption performance of the PAC (particle size of 85 μm) was explored by using a bubbling fluidized bed reactor adsorption system with temperature-controlled heating equipment for adsorption. The permeation tube within a U-type glass tube was placed in water bath. In the adsorption test, the flow of N2 as carrier gas and the temperature of the water bath were 1 L/min and 60 °C, respectively, to provide a constant feed of Hg0 (100 ± 2 μm/Nm3). 25 g of the prepared PAC was added to the bubbling fluidized bed reactor, which was placed in the center of the temperature-controlled tube furnace. The adsorption temperature was subsequently increased from room temperature to 80 °C and then maintained. The total flow and the adsorption time were 10 L/min and 300 min, respectively. Regeneration of Spent PAC

After the adsorption, the adsorbent loses its activity; regeneration experiments were considered to be vital for a cost-effective process in Hg0 treatment. The thermal regeneration of the SPAC was carried out in a drop-tube reactor. The regeneration system consists of gas, feeder, regeneration, collector, and mercury detection systems, which are shown in Figure 1. The regeneration time was changed with the length of the heating area. A stainless steel container (heating zone and internal diameter are 2 and 80 mm, respectively) was selected as the reactor, which can be easily replaced. The desorption properties of SPAC were investigated in N2, 12% CO2, 8% H2O, 6% O2, and SFG. Using N2 as the carrier gas, a heated water bubbler was used to generate water vapor. The desorption properties of SPAC were investigated in N2 as a contrast. The regeneration temperature was 950 °C, regeneration time was 10 s, and total gas flow was 10 L/min. The SPAC was collected in a container at the bottom of the drop-tube reactor through a water-cooled sampling tube. After filtering, the Hg0-containing desorbed gas was measured by a Thermo Fisher Mercury Continuous Emissions Monitoring System.

Characterization

The surface areas and pore properties of all the samples were determined by N2 adsorption at −196 °C on the instrument (Quantachrome, Boynton Beach, FL, USA), and pore size, pore volume, and average pore diameter were calculated by the QSDFT method. Scanning electron microscopy (DESKV, Denton Vacuum, Cherry Hill, NJ, USA) was used to characterize the surface morphology of all the samples, the surface element composition and functional groups were characterized with X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) using a photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with Al Kα radiation. A Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer was used to record parameters of the samples on a Shimadzu IRAffinity-1 in the 400–4,000 cm–1 range. The particle size distribution was measured by using a laser particle size analyzer (Beckman, LS13320, SUA).

Results and Discussion

Characterization of the Samples

SEM Analysis

The scanning electron microscopy characterization provides a convenient approach to investigate the morphology of the samples. Inspecting the original PAC, PAC–O2 (O2 atmosphere desorption), PAC–N2 (N2 desorption), PAC–CO2 (CO2 atmosphere desorption), PAC–H2O (H2O desorption), and PAC–SFG (simulated flue gas atmosphere desorption) using SEM revealed the morphological changes of the samples. The micromorphology of the original PAC is shown in Figure 3a, which reveals numerous pore structures distributed on the surface of the PAC. After desorption under the N2 atmosphere, the pore structure collapsed on the surface of PAC–N2, possibly because of the decomposition of the surface functional groups. There were also ablative traces on the surface of the PAC–O2, but it contained a substantial number of pores, which could react with the carbon in the PAC. The surface of the PAC–CO2 had a new fine pore structure, and the number of pore structure of the PAC–H2O surface was further increased, indicating that CO2 and H2O can promote the formation of pore.41,42 The surface of the PAC–SFG demonstrated no obvious ablation and contained numerous pore structure, which could have been caused by chemical changes in the different gas components on the surface of the PAC. By comprehensively comparing the surface morphology of the PAC under different desorption conditions, it was found that O2, CO2, H2O(g), and SFG could promote the formation of pores.

N2 Adsorption Isotherm Analysis

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of the original PAC, PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–H2O, PAC–CO2, and PAC–O2 are shown in Figure 4a, according to the classification of Brunauer et al., all the samples belong to type I isotherms,43 and the isotherms show the Langmuir behavior of rising adsorption in the low-pressure range (10–6 < P/P0 < 0.05) and a hysteresis loop, indicating primarily microporous, and to a small degree, mesoporous structures for the samples. The mesopores provide an internal diffusion channel for the Hg0 adsorption on the surface of the samples.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms (a) for PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–H2O, PAC–CO2, original PAC, and PAC–O2 and their pore size distributions (b).

Subsequently, quenched solid-state density functional theory (QSDFT) was used to calculate the pore size distributions of the samples, and the results are shown in Figure 4b. The micropores of the samples primarily had diameters of <2 nm. According to the adsorption theory, pore sizes that are 1.7–3 times the kinetic diameter of mercury (0.32 nm) adsorb mercury the best compared with other pore sizes.44 Therefore, the micropores with an ideal diameter on the surface of the samples are conducive to the adsorption of Hg0. Many peaks of PAC in the range of 0–2 nm indicate that PAC has numerous microporous structures. The peaks of PAC–N2 at 0–2 nm were significantly smaller than those of PAC, indicating that the pore structure of PAC was destroyed under the N2 atmosphere. The peaks of PAC–O2 at 0–2 nm slightly decreased, indicating that desorption under an O2 atmosphere could cause the pore size to increase. The peaks of PAC–CO2 and PAC–H2O at 0–2 nm were similar and increased compared with the original PAC, indicating that CO2 and H2O could promote the formation of the micropore structure. The peaks of PAC–SFG at 0–2 nm increased, indicating that the regenerated PAC surface can promote the formation of micropores and mesopores under an SFG atmosphere.

The pore structure characteristic parameters obtained from the BET measurements are presented in Table 2. The results show that the surface areas of the original PAC, PAC–O2, PAC–N2, PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG were approximately 348.2 m2/g, 295.6 m2/g, 262.1 m2/g, 362.8 m2/g, 378.4 m2/g, and 397.6 m2/g, respectively. After regeneration under N2 and O2, the surface areas were 15.1% and 24.7% less than the original PAC, respectively. However, the surface areas of PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG increased by 4.1%, 8.6%, and 14.1%, respectively. The pore volumes of PAC–O2 and PAC–N2 decreased by 7.7% and 14.9%, respectively, while those of PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG increased by 6.6%, 8.2%, and 11.6%, respectively. The average pore diameters of PAC–O2 and PAC–N2 increased by 0.016 and 0.018 nm, respectively, while the average diameters of PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG decreased by 0.092, 0.056, and 0.203 nm, respectively. The results show that different desorption atmospheres have a significant influence on the physical properties of the PAC and indirectly affect the adsorption efficiency of Hg0.

The above results signify that the pore wall could decompose under N2 and high temperatures, resulting in a decrease in the micropores and the surface area of PAC–N2. Although O2 was used as an accelerant, the specific surface area was reduced; however, the pore diameter was slightly increased, indicating that O2 elongated the pore diameter of the PAC at this concentration. H2O and CO2 can react with carbon in the desorption process rapidly, and the two activated atmospheres increased the micropore structure; meanwhile, this reaction can produce a large specific surface area of PAC–H2O and PAC–CO2. Under the desorption atmosphere of SFG, the microstructure of the micropores was further increased. Therefore, the rapid desorption of SPAC under the activation atmosphere can promote the physical adsorption performance of Hg0, especially under an SFG atmosphere.

XPS analysis was employed to reveal the chemical valence states of the PAC.45 The scanning spectra and spectra of O 1s are illustrated in Figures 5 and 6, respectively. The surface atomic concentrations of the PAC samples are summarized in Table 3. Oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of the samples have a significant effect on the adsorption of pollutant molecules, and the contents of the original PAC, PAC–O2, PAC–N2, PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG are shown in Figure 5. The spectral peaks of C and O indicate that the contents of O 1s were 8.01%, 7.78%, 5.51%, 6.65%, 5.91%, and 7.89%, respectively. The oxygen content of PAC–N2 decreased sharply, mainly due to surface functional group decomposition. The contents of PAC–CO2 and PAC–H2O were similar, and both were lower than the PAC, indicating that desorption under the atmospheres of CO2 and H2O(g) was not conducive to the formation of oxygen-containing functional groups.

The oxygen content of the oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of the sample was analyzed. The O 1s XPS spectra are shown in Figure 6a–f, and three peaks at approximately 531.6–531.9 eV, 532.6–533.2 eV, and 533.6–534.4 eV were observed in the samples, which could be attributed to the oxygen atoms in C=O (ketone, lactone, and quinone groups), C–O (hydroxyl, ester, amide, and anhydride groups), and C=O (carbonyl groups), respectively.46−48 The contents of different oxygen species are listed in Table 3, determined by calculating the peak area. The surface C=O contents of the original PAC, PAC–O2, PAC–N2, PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG were 44.35%, 43.56%, 42.72%, 44.62%, and 45.73%, respectively, and the C–O contents were 47.25%, 55.65%, 56.64%, 57.28%, 55.38%, 54.27%, and 52.75%, respectively. There were distinct significant changes in the concentrations of the O species in the samples under different atmospheres. The ratios (C=O/C–O groups) increased from 0.376 to 0.393, 0.426, and 0.454 after regeneration with H2O, CO2, and SFG atmospheres; however, the ratios decreased from 0.376 to 0.364 and 0.254 after regeneration with O2 and N2. The C=O functional group contents on the surfaces of PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG were higher than that of the original PAC, indicating that the chemical reaction of H2O, CO2, and SFG–AC occurred during the desorption process, which promoted the formation of C=O functional groups. Based on previous research, the ketones, lactones, carbonyl, or quinone groups (C=O) have been shown to promote Hg0 removal;49,50 hence, the SPACs regenerated under H2O, CO2, and SFG atmospheres have good Hg0 readsorption properties because more active sites are formed on the surface of the PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, and PAC–SFG.

NH3-TPD Analysis

Hg0 is considered to be a Lewis base51 and readily adsorbs to Lewis acid sites on the PAC. The adsorption of NH3 on the surface of PAC plays an important role in determining Lewis acid sites.52 As shown in Figure 7, the NH3-TPD profiles can be fitted to two main characteristic peaks according to the test results. The peaks at the temperatures of 180 and 430 °C can be assigned to chemically adsorbed NH3 at weak and strong acid sites, respectively. The acid content of each peak and the total acid content were summed and are listed in Table 4. The original PAC with more Lewis acid sites had a total acid content of 0.494 mmol/g. After the Hg0 adsorption, the weakly acidic site contents decreased from 0.118 to 0.079 mmol/g and that of the strong acid sites decreased from 0.376 to 0.328 mmol/g, so the total acid content decreased from 0.494 to 0.407 mmol/g.

According to these results, Hg0 may react with the functional group on the PAC to form two types of mercury oxide. Compared with the original PAC, the contents on the weak acid sites on PAC–N2, CO2, H2O, and O2 were 0.071, 0.091, 0.085, and 0.078 mmol/g, respectively. However, the content for the weak acid sites on the PAC–SFG was 0.121 mmol/g. The results show that spent PAC regenerated under SFG could promote the formation of weak acid sites. The contents on the strong acid sites of PAC–N2 (0.354 mmol/g) and PAC–O2 (0.353 mmol/g) were less than that of the original PAC, and the spent PAC regenerated under N2 and O2 was not conducive to the formation of strong acid sites. PAC–SFG (0.384 mmol/g), PAC–CO2 (0.403 mmol/g), and PAC–H2O (0.406 mmol/g) have stronger acid sites than the original PAC, because in the desorption process, the activation of CO2, O2, and SFG plays a substantial role in their formation.

In particular, CO2 and H2O have the greatest promoting effect. The total acid contents of PAC–SFG, PAC–CO2, and PAC–H2O were 0.505, 0.494, and 0.491 mmol/g, respectively, similar to that of the original PAC. However, the total acid contents of PAC–N2 and PAC–O2 were less than that of the original PAC. Hence, the spent PAC regenerated under SFG, CO2, and H2O(g) promoted Hg0 chemical adsorption on the PAC.

FTIR Analysis

Figure 8 shows the FTIR spectra of the original PAC, PAC–H2O, PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–O2, and PAC–CO2 in the range of 400–4,000 cm–1. It was found that the peaks at 3,600–3,200 cm–1 are the O–H stretching vibrations of alcohol, phenol, and water; in the range of 2,300–2,400 cm–1, there were the characteristic peaks of physical adsorption of CO2.53 The C–H stretching vibrations of −CH2 (2,875 cm–1) and −CH3 (2,960 cm–1) were located in the range of 3,000–2,800 cm–1. The characteristic peaks representing stretching vibrations of carbonyl (C=O), lactone (O–C=O), and benzene (C=C) groups were at 1,700–1,395 cm–1.54 The peaks at 1,200–1,000 cm–1 were mainly attributed to the C–O stretching vibrations of anhydride and ether.55 The different regeneration gases have different effects on the detailed absorption peaks of functional group. The peak representing the surface functional groups (e.g., C–O–C and −OH) in the range of 500–1,500 cm–1 changed noticeably compared with other ranges of wavenumbers.56

FTIR spectra of original PAC, PAC–H2O, PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–O2, and PAC–CO2.

After desorption in different atmospheres, when the peaks of functional groups tended to change, the main peaks of carbonyl, quinone, and lactone were considered, which promoted Hg0 removal. The peak intensity of these groups showed significant reduction with a desorption atmosphere of N2 and O2. In the N2 atmosphere, the functional groups on the PAC were decomposed, and in the O2 atmosphere, the functional groups reacted acutely with the oxygen. In addition, the groups tended to increase for PAC–H2O, PAC–SFG, and PAC–CO2 compared with the original PAC. The main reason may be that C on the PAC reacted with CO2, H2O, and SFG, which led to the redistribution of functional groups being rebuilt. Namely, the diversity of the functional groups in the activated coke was well preserved when the activation agent was added into the regeneration atmosphere.

Effect of Different Atmospheres on Carbon Consumption

The carbon consumption of PAC under different regeneration atmospheres was further explored, and the mass loss of PAC before and after desorption was used to calculate the carbon consumption. The results in Table 5 show that the total carbon consumption after each regeneration process was 0.13%, 0.24%, 0.17%, 0.16%, and 0.14% under N2, O2, H2O, CO2, and SFG atmospheres, respectively. From these results, under N2, the carbon consumption was less than those for other desorption methods, which was explored in previous work; hence, the rapid desorption method can reduce carbon consumption. Under O2, the carbon consumption was higher than those under other conditions, which may be due to the carbon being burned with O2. The reactions of H2O and CO2 with PAC were similar, so the carbon consumption rates were comparable. The SFG atmosphere contained many atmospheres with activation properties, and the reactions were diverse, so the carbon consumption rate was close to that of the N2 atmosphere. Therefore, the spent PAC was regenerated using this method under an SFG atmosphere, reducing carbon consumption, saving energy, and reducing the desorption time.

Particle Size Distribution

In the regeneration process, the particle size change is another important factor that was taken into account. The particle size distribution and content of the original PAC, PAC–H2O, PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–O2, and PAC–CO2 are shown in Figure 9. The samples show that the particle sizes of PAC–H2O, PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–O2, and PAC–CO2 changed slightly compared with those of the original PAC, as shown in Figure 9a. With further analysis of the superposition of the different size ranges, the volume fraction of the original PAC in the range of 0.357–52.62 μm was 9.13%, and those for PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, PAC–N2, PAC–O2 and PAC–SFG were 12.69%, 10.44%, 10.82%, 13.03%, and 12.41%, respectively, and the results are shown in Figure 9b, indicating that the particles were slightly worn during desorption and evolved into activated coke with small particle sizes under different desorption atmospheres. In the range of 52.62–121.82 μm, the volume fractions were 81.58%, 77.19%, 79.01%, 79.84%, 76.68%, and 77.43%, respectively.

Particle size distribution (a) and the volume fraction (b) of original PAC, PAC–H2O, PAC–SFG, PAC–N2, PAC–O2, and PAC–CO2.

The particle size reduction was the greatest under the O2 atmospheres, which may be caused by the combustion of O2 on the activated coke surface. After regeneration under CO2 and H2O, the PAC–CO2 and PAC–H2O particles in the size range of 52.62–121.82 μm decreased by 4.39% and 2.58% respectively, indicating that CO2 and H2O reacted with the surface of the PAC, causing the wear of the particle size. The PAC decomposed during desorption under N2, and the particle sizes decreased by 1.74%. In particular, the particles of the sample regenerated under the SFG desorption atmosphere decreased by 4.15%. Subsequently, there was no significant increase or decrease in the range of 121.82–400 μm in any of the samples. The above results indicate that different atmospheres promote the particle size to decrease differently. The particle size change phenomenon was caused by carbon consumption and decomposition of surface functional groups of the activated coke. In future applications, the particle wear should be controlled during the regeneration process.

Readsorption Efficiencies of Samples

As shown in Figure 10, the removal efficiencies of the original PAC, PAC–N2, PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, PAC–O2, and PAC–SFG are 66%, 40%, 72%, 77%, 58%, and 86%, respectively. Compared with the original PAC, the Hg0 readsorption efficiencies of PAC–N2 and PAC–O2 generally decreased, indicating that desorption under N2 and O2 negatively impacted the Hg0 readsorption efficiencies of the PAC because desorption could destroy the micropore structure and reduce chemically active sites. However, the Hg0 adsorption efficiencies of PAC–CO2 and PAC–H2O were higher than those of original PAC under the same adsorption conditions. Hence, the SPAC desorbed under CO2 and H2O could promote the removal of Hg0. The results showed that CO2 and H2O as activators promoted an increase in the micropore structure and specific surface area of the SPAC. In addition, the regeneration atmosphere increased the content of the active functional groups conducive to Hg0 adsorption. Specifically, the active functional groups, micropores, and specific surface area of PAC-SFG were optimized because the regeneration atmosphere reacted with PAC, leading to physical and chemical properties favorable to Hg0 adsorption. Therefore, it may be indicated that using SFG desorption is an effective method to increase physicochemical properties of SPAC and may cause enhanced Hg0 removal efficiencies compared with other regeneration atmospheres.

Desorption Mechanism

Physical and chemical adsorption of Hg0 occurred in the PAC micropore structures and active sites of the surface functional groups. In the process of rapid desorption at high temperatures, Hg0 that was physically adsorbed moved violently, and the van der Waals force changed to infinitely small, volatilizing Hg0 from the micropore structure quickly. Hg0 was released from the active sites by breaking the chemical bonds. At the same time, the physicochemical properties of the PAC changed under individual desorption atmospheres differently. The possible influencing mechanisms of the different atmospheres on the PAC activated in situ at high temperatures during the desorption process were further considered.

Under the N2 atmosphere, the functional groups on the PAC surface decomposed at high temperatures, generating CO2 and CO, collapsing the pore structure, and decreasing the pore structure content. In addition to that, the functional groups on the surface were decomposed under high-temperature conditions; in particular, the amount of carbonyl and quinone groups that promoted Hg0 adsorption decreased greatly. Furthermore, the adsorption Hg0 site on the surface of the PAC was reduced.

Under the O2 desorption atmosphere, O2 acted as a combustion-supporting agent and burned with carbon on the surface of the PAC, so the pore structure of PAC changed and part of the micropore structure evolved into larger sizes. Meanwhile, O2 reacted with carbon atoms on the PAC surface to form unsaturated bonds such as the C=O group, which partially decomposed at high temperatures and generated CO and CO2 gas.

Under an atmosphere of CO2 and H2O, the micropore structures increased, indicating that both atmospheres produced heterogeneous reactions on the surface of the PAC, which acted as openings on the surface. The two atmospheres formed pressure on the surface and then reacted with carbon, producing more unsaturated chains and C=O. In summary, N2 reduced the micropore structures and functional groups, and O2 caused a large amount of ablation of the PAC but could promote the formation of a small amount of C=O. Both H2O and CO2 promoted the development of micropore structures and C=O functional groups during the desorption process. Therefore, the PAC desorbed in the SFG exhibited better physicochemical properties and Hg0 readsorption than those in other desorption atmospheres.

Conclusions

Hg0 adsorption efficiency of PAC was explored by bubbling a fluidized bed reactor for 300 min, which presented excellent results (average efficiency of 91%). Then, the desorption properties and activated in situ mechanisms during rapid desorption of SPAC were systematically explored under different atmospheres. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

The method that was carried out in a drop-tube reactor exhibited a short desorption time (10 s) to achieve complete regeneration at a temperature of 950 °C. Under a N2 atmosphere, the functional groups and the pore structure contents decreased on the surface of the PAC. A part of micropore structure evolved into macropores or mesopores, and unsaturated bonds, such as the C = O group, were slightly reduced. In addition, both H2O(g) and CO2 promoted the formation of micropore structures and C = O functional groups during the desorption process, which were optimized under an SFG atmosphere.

The readsorption efficiency was subsequently investigated, and the removal efficiencies of the original PAC, PAC–N2, PAC–CO2, PAC–H2O, PAC–O2, and PAC–SFG were 66%, 40%, 72%, 77%, 58%, and 86%, respectively. These results indicate that the readsorption performance of PAC–N2 decreased significantly, while the readsorption efficiency of PAC–O2 was slightly reduced. The physicochemical properties that promote Hg0 removal with PAC–H2O and PAC–CO2 increased, indicating that these two atmospheres play an active role in desorption. Further analysis revealed that the carbon consumption losses after each desorption process were 0.13%, 0.24%, 0.17%, 0.16%, and 0.14% under N2, O2, H2O, CO2, and SFG atmospheres, respectively.

This study provides an ecofriendly industrial application basis for mercury control in coal-fired power plants. In addition, the spent activated coke was activated in situ during the rapid desorption process by the heating method, potentially providing valuable information for future engineering applications.
