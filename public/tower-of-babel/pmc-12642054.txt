Association of novel obesity indexes with cognitive impairment: a cross-sectional study of a low-income population in rural China in 2014
Authors: Dandan Guo, Juan Hao, Tao Li, Yuting Lu, Fuchen Liu, Jun Tu, Jun Ma, Xianjia Ning, Jinghua Wang, Yiming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment and dementia present significant global health challenges, particularly in low-income and rural populations. As the prevalence of cognitive impairment continues to rise, early identification of risk factors is essential to prevent further cognitive impairment and mitigate the associated socioeconomic burden. This study aims to explore the associations between novel obesity indices—conicity index (C-index), relative fat mass (RFM), and Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI)—and cognitive impairment in a low-income rural population in Tianjin, China.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 1,132 participants aged 60 years and older from rural Tianjin, China. Data were gathered through face-to-face interviews, covering socio-demographic and clinical information. Physical measurements were taken, including height, weight, and waist circumference. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). The C-index, RFM, and CVAI were calculated using standard formulas and categorized into quartiles. Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the associations between obesity indices and cognitive impairment, adjusting for potential confounders. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were utilized to explore the nonlinear relationship between RFM and cognitive impairment. Subgroup analyses were performed based on gender and age groups.ResultsThe study found that 47.7% of the participants exhibited cognitive impairment. Univariate analysis revealed significant associations between cognitive impairment and several factors, including gender, age, waist circumference, smoking history, alcohol consumption, diabetes mellitus, and fasting blood glucose (FBG). Multivariate analysis indicated a significant association between RFM and cognitive impairment, with individuals in the third quartile of RFM having 59% higher odds of prevalent cognitive impairment compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.05–2.41, p = 0.028), and demonstrated a U-shaped association (p = 0.005). Higher RFM was protective in men (OR = 0.94, p = 0.003) but not in women and no age-specific effects emerged.ConclusionThis study underscores the significant role of relative fat mass in cognitive health, revealing a nonlinear relationship in which both high and low levels of RFM are linked to an increased risk of cognitive impairment. Background

Cognitive impairment and dementia represent significant global health challenges. According to the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors (GBD) study, more than 56.9 million people worldwide are affected by Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, marking a staggering 160.8% increase since 1990. These conditions contribute to 36.3 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally [1]. In Asia alone, approximately 4 million individuals develop dementia annually, accounting for 40% of new cases worldwide [2]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a condition that lies between normal aging and dementia, serves as an early predictor of dementia, with 6–12% of adults worldwide estimated to suffer from MCI [3–5]. In China, the prevalence of MCI and dementia among adults aged 60 and older is reported to be 15.5% and 6.0%, respectively, imposing a substantial economic burden [6]. Therefore, early identification of risk factors for cognitive impairment is crucial for mitigating the risk of dementia and alleviating the associated socioeconomic impact.

Obesity is an increasingly prevalent issue in China, which now has the largest population of overweight and obese individuals globally, with approximately half of Chinese adults classified as overweight or obese [7]. Traditional obesity indicators, such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC), have shown inconsistent relationships with cognitive function. Some studies suggest that higher obesity markers are associated with poorer cognitive performance, while others indicate a protective effect [8–11]. This inconsistency is partly due to the limitations of BMI, which does not differentiate between muscle and fat mass, and fails to account for regional fat distribution. Similarly, WC, though indicative of central obesity, does not effectively assess overall fat distribution [12].

Novel obesity indices, such as the conicity index (C-index), relative fat mass (RFM), and Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), offer more precise assessments of obesity. The C-index increases with abdominal fat accumulation and assesses both total and regional adiposity [13, 14]. RFM, a simple and reliable estimator of body fat, provides a more accurate reflection of total body fat percentage than BMI [14, 15]. CVAI is a sex-specific formula incorporating waist circumference, BMI, triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) to quantify visceral adipose dysfunction in Chinese adults. Validated across multiple provinces in China, this index is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease and stroke [16–19]. However, the relationships between these novel obesity indices and cognitive function remain unclear [20, 21].

Despite recent advances, significant gaps and controversies persist in understanding the relationship between obesity indices and cognitive impairment. Studies have produced contradictory findings regarding the impact of obesity on cognitive function. For example, while some research suggests a positive association between higher obesity markers and cognitive impairment, others propose a protective effect [8–11]. Furthermore, the role of novel obesity indices such as C-index, RFM, and CVAI in cognitive impairment remains underexplored, with limited studies yielding mixed results [20, 21]. Specifically, while some studies indicate that a higher C-index is associated with poorer cognitive function, others find no significant association [20, 21]. The correlation between RFM and cognitive dysfunction also remains ambiguous, with few studies addressing this relationship directly [22, 23]. Although the CVAI has been shown to associate significantly with cardiovascular disease and stroke, no study has yet examined its relationship with cognitive impairment.In this study, we selected relative fat mass index (RFM), C-index, and China visceral adiposity index (CVAI) to evaluate the relationship between obesity and cognitive function. The selection of these indicators was based on their applicability in the Chinese population, correlation with cognitive health studies, and data availability. Although other obesity indicators also have potential, our choice aims to maximize the relevance and feasibility of the research while maintaining exploratory nature.

This study aims to investigate the associations between novel obesity indices (C-index, RFM, and CVAI) and cognitive impairment in a low-income rural population in Tianjin, China.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional analysis is part of the Tianjin Brain Study. The study design has been described previously [24]. In briefly, participants were recruited from Yangjinzhuang, a township including 18 villages in Tianjin, China by cluster sampling. 95% of the residents in this town are low-income rural residents. This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2014, and individuals aged 60 and above without visual or auditory impairments were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria included a history of myocardial infarction, stroke, congenital hypospadias, psychiatric disorders, and incomplete Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores or obesity indices [25, 26].

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. The written informed consent form has been signed by the participant himself/herself (if able to write) or his/her legal guardian (if unable to write).

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained researchers, which was described previously [26]. Socio-demographic and clinical data including name, sex, age, education lifestyle and self-reported physician diagnoses of diabetes and hypertension were obtained through interviewer-administered questionnaires. Physical examinations were performed to measure height, weight, and waist circumference (WC), with participants wearing light clothing during the assessments. Trained nurses collected fasting venous blood samples for laboratory measurement of fasting blood glucose (FBG), TG, total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). All laboratory measurements were tested in the central laboratory of Tianjin Jizhou People’s Hospital. These biochemical parameters were subsequently used for CVAI calculation and other analyses.

Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, the use of antihypertensive drugs, or a history of hypertension. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, the use of diabetes medication, or a self-reported history of diabetes.

Obesity indices calculation

Three novel obesity indices were calculated: the C-index, RFM, and CVAI. These indices were computed using standard formulas and categorized into quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4) [21, 27].

The C-index was determined by the formula: C-index = WC/[0.109 × √(weight/height)]. RFM was calculated using the formula: RFM = 64 – (20 × height/waist circumference) + (12 × gender), where gender is 0 for men and 1 for women. CVAI was calculated according to a previously validated formula specific to the Chinese population. Males: CVAI = − 267.93 + 0.68 × age (years) + 0.03 × BMI (kg/m2) + 4.00 × WC (cm) + 22.00 × log10(TG [mmol/L]) − 16.32 × HDL-C (mmol/L); Females: CVAI = − 187.32 + 1.71 × age (years) + 4.32 × BMI (kg/m2) + 1.12 × WC (cm) + 39.76 × log10(TG [mmol/L]) − 11.66 × HDL-C (mmol/L) [27].

Cognitive function assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE, a widely recommended tool in China for screening cognitive deficits [28, 29]. The MMSE includes items on memory, orientation, language, attention, and calculation, with a total score of 30 [30]. A nationwide, systematic, longitudinal survey revealed that MMSE scores are influenced by age, education, and other demographic variables. In a representative Chinese elderly cohort, the optimal cut-off points for cognitive impairment was defined as an MMSE score below 18 for illiterate individuals, below 21 for those with 6 or fewer years of education, and below 25 for those with more than 6 years of education [31]. These thresholds are widely adopted in China to account for the education sensitivity of the MMSE. Participants scoring below the corresponding threshold were classified as having cognitive impairment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD) and compared using t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square tests. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between obesity indices and cognitive impairment.

Covariate selection and coding. Candidate covariates were identified a priori based on biological plausibility and previous literature, and retained if associated with cognitive impairment at P < 0.05 in univariate analyses. The following variables were considered: age (60–69 years [reference] vs. ≥70 years), sex (female [reference] vs. male), smoking status (never [reference], current, former), alcohol consumption (never [reference], current, former), and diabetes (no [reference] vs. yes). In the main models (RFM as a continuous variable and in quartiles), we adjusted for age group, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and diabetes. In subgroup analyses, the adjustment set was tailored to maintain model stability and avoid overparameterization in smaller strata. To prevent overadjustment, anthropometric measures that constitute or may mediate RFM (e.g., BMI, waist circumference) were not included simultaneously in the models. Because education was already incorporated into the definition of cognitive impairment through education-specific MMSE thresholds, it was not additionally included as a covariate to avoid redundancy with the outcome classification.

The strength of association between obesity indices and cognitive impairment was expressed as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to explore the dose-response relationship between obesity indices and cognitive impairment, with the number of knots determined by evaluating the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) of models with 3, 4, and 5 knots. The central thick line in the RCS plots represented the adjusted OR, and the shaded areas indicated the 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on gender (male, female) and age groups (60–69 years, ≥ 70 years) to further investigate the associations.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0, with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. R version 4.4.0 was used to create the RCS plots.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study included 1,132 participants, with 520 males (45.9%) and 612 females (54.1%). The mean age of the participants was 67.48 ± 6.32 years. Among them, 68.4% were aged between 60 and 69 years, and 31.6% were 70 years or older. Education levels varied significantly, with 28.2% of participants having no formal education, 56.7% having 1–6 years of education, and 15.1% having more than 6 years of education.

In terms of BMI, 42.3% of the participants had a normal weight, 37.6% were overweight, and 17.2% were classified as obese. Smoking habits differed markedly between genders, with 51.0% of males being current smokers compared to only 6.5% of females. Additionally, alcohol consumption was much higher in males, with 47.7% being current drinkers compared to just 2.1% of females. Hypertension was reported in 45.9% of the participants, and diabetes was reported in 17.5% of the participants. Cognitive impairment was found in 47.7% of the study population, with a higher prevalence in females (59.0%) compared to males (34.4%; Table 1).

Specifically, the prevalence of cognitive impairment was higher among female participants, and individuals aged 70 years or older exhibited a significantly greater prevalence of cognitive impairment than those younger than 70. Smokers and individuals with a history of alcohol consumption had lower rates of cognitive impairment compared to non-smokers and non-drinkers (p < 0.001 for both). Additionally, diabetes mellitus and elevated FBG levels were more common among participants with cognitive impairment (p = 0.010 and p = 0.043, respectively). No significant associations were observed between traditional obesity indices, such as BMI and CVAI, and cognitive impairment.

Multivariate analysis of RFM and cognitive impairment

After adjusting for smoking history, alcohol consumption, age subgroups, and diabetes, multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed a significant association between RFM and cognitive impairment. Participants in the third quartile of RFM (Q3) had 59% higher odds of prevalent cognitive impairment compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.05–2.41, p = 0.028). Additionally, current smokers and former smokers were less likely to have cognitive impairment compared to never smokers (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.41–0.85, p = 0.005 for current smokers; OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.24–0.69, p < 0.001 for former smokers). Advanced age (≥ 70 years) was also associated with a significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment (OR = 3.67, 95% CI: 2.77–4.86, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis by gender and age

Subgroup analyses explored the associations between RFM and cognitive impairment across different gender and age groups. In males, a negative association was observed between RFM and cognitive impairment, with a 6% reduction in risk for each unit increase in RFM (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98, p = 0.003). However, no statistically significant associations were found between RFM and cognitive impairment in females, the 60–69 years age group, or the ≥ 70 years age group (all p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

This research aimed to elucidate the correlations between novel obesity indices, including the C-index, RFM, and the CVAI, and cognitive function, particularly within a demographic often underrepresented in scientific studies. Among the study population, 47.7% of participants were found to have cognitive impairment, underscoring the significant public health burden of cognitive impairment in rural, low-income populations.

A significant association was observed between RFM and cognitive impairment. Specifically, individuals in the third quartile of RFM (Q3) had a 59% higher risk of cognitive impairment compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1). This relationship remained significant even after adjusting for potential confounders such as smoking history, alcohol consumption, age, and diabetes. Notably, a nonlinear relationship was identified between RFM and cognitive impairment, indicating that both extremely high and low RFM values are associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. This finding emphasizes the importance of maintaining RFM within an optimal range to reduce the risk of cognitive impairment.

Subgroup analysis revealed a gender-specific association between RFM and cognitive impairment. In males, higher RFM was negatively associated with cognitive impairment, suggesting that lower RFM increases the risk of cognitive impairment. However, no significant association was found in females, indicating a potential gender difference in how body fat distribution affects cognitive health. Additionally, no statistically significant associations were found between RFM and cognitive impairment in the 60–69 years and ≥ 70 years age groups.

The high prevalence of cognitive impairment observed in this study is consistent with other research investigating cognitive impairment in elderly populations. Previous studies have reported similar associations between obesity indices and cognitive impairment; however, our study provides novel insights by focusing on a low-income rural Chinese population and utilizing novel obesity indices such as RFM. For instance, Norris et al. found a bidirectional relationship between adiposity and cognitive function in the UK Biobank cohort, suggesting that higher levels of body fat were associated with poorer cognitive performance over time [8]. Zeng et al. reported that elevated visceral adiposity index was linked to improved cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly Chinese participants, highlighting a complex relationship between fat distribution and cognitive health [9]. A retrospective cohort study involving two million people found that higher BMI was associated with a lower risk of dementia, suggesting a potential protective effect of higher body weight in older adults [10]. Additionally, the relationship between obesity and cognitive impairment has been attributed to obesity-related metabolic disturbances, such as insulin resistance and inflammation, which contribute significantly to cognitive deterioration [11]. Emerging evidence indicates that incorporating the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a surrogate of insulin resistance, with RFM significantly improves cardiovascular risk prediction and may thereby circumvent several risk factors for cognitive impairment [32].

The association between RFM and cognitive impairment observed in this study has been explored in previous research, though with varying conclusions. Our study adds a novel dimension to understanding how body fat distribution affects cognitive health, particularly in a rural Chinese population. Suthahar et al. demonstrated that RFM is a reliable indicator of adiposity and found significant associations between RFM and cardiovascular diseases, such as heart failure and coronary artery disease, suggesting that higher RFM may have adverse health effects [15]. Zhu et al. identified a positive correlation between RFM and the risk of depression, indicating that higher RFM could be linked to mental health issues, which may indirectly affect cognitive function [33]. Another study showed that RFM is predictive of various chronic conditions, including atrial fibrillation and metabolic syndrome, both of which are risk factors for cognitive impairment [22]. Additionally, elevated RFM has been associated with an increased risk of stroke in Chinese patients with metabolic syndrome, further implicating high RFM as a potential risk factor for neurological disorders [27, 34].

In contrast to the predominantly linear associations reported in previous studies, our research identified a significant nonlinear relationship between RFM and cognitive impairment, where both high and low RFM values were associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. Previous studies have provided preliminary evidence for a non-linear relationship between obesity indices and cognitive function; however, investigations focusing on RFM remain scarce, rendering the present findings particularly noteworthy. Morys et al. investigated the relationship between midlife obesity and cognitive impairment, finding a complex interaction where both underweight and obesity were associated with adverse cognitive outcomes, hinting at a possible nonlinear association [35]. A study by Zeng et al. demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between visceral adiposity index (VAI) and cognitive function, suggesting that moderate levels of visceral fat might be protective, while very high or very low levels were detrimental [9]. Stevens et al. discussed the limitations of traditional obesity measures like BMI, emphasizing the need for more nuanced indicators that could capture the nonlinear effects of adiposity on health outcomes, including cognitive function [12]. The global epidemic of metabolic syndrome and its components, including obesity, has complex relationships with various health outcomes, including cognitive impairment [36]. Liu et al. reported that higher relative fat mass (RFM) was associated with poorer cognitive performance in older men, with the association becoming particularly pronounced when RFM exceeded 35.78 [23]. Our study’s identification of a nonlinear relationship between RFM and cognitive impairment aligns with previous studies, but our research specifically demonstrates that both extremely high and low levels of RFM increase the risk of cognitive impairment, highlighting the complexity of the relationship between body fat and cognitive health. This finding is innovative. Defining the protective RFM range highlights the intricate relationship between adiposity and cognitive health, underscoring that an optimal body-fat level is essential for preserving cognitive function. Several mechanisms may account for the observed associations. Excess adipose tissue, particularly visceral fat, secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines that stimulate macrophages and monocytes to generate reactive oxygen species, thereby promoting oxidative stress. This cascade can precipitate chronic neuroinflammation, neuronal degeneration and ultimately cognitive impairment [37–39]. Conversely, lipopenic obesity in older adults is often associated with malnutrition and a concomitant decline in protective adipokines. When subcutaneous adipose tissue is diminished, inadequate nutritional intake and depleted energy reserves ensue, resulting in reduced secretion of anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective adipokines such as adiponectin and leptin. This deficiency exacerbates neuroinflammation and impairs hippocampal synaptic plasticity, ultimately precipitating cognitive impairment [40, 41].

The gender-specific association between RFM and cognitive impairment identified in this study contributes to the understanding of how body fat distribution affects cognitive health differently in males and females. Previous studies have demonstrated that the effect of traditional obesity indices—such as BMI—on cognitive impairment is significantly moderated by sex, with pronounced associations observed in obese men and underweight women [42, 43]. More recently, emerging anthropometric indicators have also exhibited clear sex-specific patterns. Specifically, Huang et al. reported that metabolic syndrome, CI, and the abdominal volume index (AVI) were linked to poorer cognitive performance in older adults, and these associations were substantially stronger in men than in women [20]. Traditional and novel measures of central obesity have shown different impacts on cognitive performance in older multi-ethnic Asians with type 2 diabetes, with gender-specific differences noted in the associations [21]. Elevated visceral adiposity index has been linked to improved cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly Chinese, with the effect more pronounced in males than females [9]. Higher levels of body fat have had a more detrimental impact on cognitive health in males compared to females [8]. Liu et al. demonstrated that elevated RFM was significantly associated with poorer cognitive performance in older men, whereas no such relationship was observed in women [23]. The current study’s finding that higher RFM is negatively associated with cognitive impairment in males but not in females is consistent with the results of Huang et al. and Norris et al., which both indicated stronger associations between obesity and cognitive function in males. Sex-specific findings may be influenced by hormonal differences that differentially affect fat distribution and metabolic health in men and women. In addition, men and women may exhibit distinct sensitivities to the metabolic and inflammatory consequences of obesity, thereby accounting for the observed associations.

The age-specific findings of this study, which indicate no significant associations between RFM and cognitive impairment in the 60–69 years and ≥ 70 years age groups, contrast with some prior research that has explored the relationship between obesity indices and cognitive function across different age groups. Previous research on age-specific associations has indicated that higher BMI in midlife is associated with a lower risk of dementia in older age, suggesting a potential protective effect of higher body weight in the elderly [10]. Cognitive impairment and dementia progression have been more strongly associated with obesity in younger elderly populations compared to those aged 75 and older [3]. The prevalence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia increases with age, but the impact of obesity on cognitive function varies across different age groups [6]. Moreover, a meta-analysis found significant age-related differences in the prevalence and risk factors of cognitive impairment, indicating that the relationship between obesity and cognitive function might be influenced by age [5]. The current study’s findings that RFM is not significantly associated with cognitive impairment in either the 60–69 years or ≥ 70 years age groups differ from the protective effect of higher BMI in older age and the age-related differences observed in previous studies. This discrepancy may be due to the focus on RFM rather than traditional obesity measures like BMI. Our study provides a more precise assessment of body fat and its impact on cognitive function, suggesting that RFM may not have the same age-specific effects as BMI.

The lack of significant age-specific associations between RFM and cognitive impairment in our study could be due to several factors. One possibility is that the metabolic and inflammatory processes associated with body fat have different impacts at different stages of aging. Younger elderly individuals (60–69 years) may experience more pronounced metabolic benefits from maintaining higher RFM, while these benefits might diminish or reverse in older age due to cumulative health issues and decreased metabolic resilience. Additionally, the rural, low-income setting of our study population may introduce unique lifestyle and environmental factors that influence the relationship between RFM and cognitive health differently than in more urban or higher-income populations. Moreover, our analyses did not examine the effects of lifestyle factors such as diet and physical activity on cognition. Additionally, the reduced sample size in stratified subgroups diminished statistical power, which may have attenuated the observed age-specific associations.

Notably, while this study found no significant association between RFM and cognitive impairment in both the 60–69 and ≥ 70 age groups, these negative findings may not reflect true null effects but rather suggest that age moderates the obesity-cognitive relationship more complexly than initially hypothesized. First, the sharp reduction in post-homogeneous sample size compromised statistical power, potentially missing weak yet clinically significant effects. Second, as age advances, drivers of cognitive impairment shift from reversible metabolic risks to irreversible neurodegeneration and comorbidities, diminishing the relative weight of body fat metrics. In other words, in the “super-aged” phase, RFM’s cognitive effects might be overshadowed by stronger factors like cerebrovascular disease, sarcopenia, or inflammatory aging. Third, our binary age categorization may have obscured finer threshold effects. Future research could employ continuous age-interaction terms or segmented regression models to validate nonlinear relationships between RFM and cognitive trajectories across larger samples.

Limitations

There were several limitations in the present study. First, this study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between RFM and cognitive impairment. Because this is a cross-sectional analysis, reverse causation cannot be excluded (e.g., prodromal cognitive impairment may reduce physical activity and alter body composition), and selection/survivor bias may also operate in an elderly rural cohort. Prospective follow-up within the Tianjin Brain Study will enable repeated assessments of adiposity indices and cognitive function to clarify temporality. Second, the study population was limited to a low-income rural population in Tianjin, China, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations, such as urban residents or those from different socioeconomic backgrounds or geographic regions. Future research should include diverse populations from various regions and socioeconomic statuses to improve the generalizability of the results. Multi-center, and longitudinal studies across urban and rural settings are needed to validate these associations. Third, the use of the MMSE as the sole tool for assessing cognitive function may be inadequate. Although the scale is easy to administer in rural areas, it is less sensitive to mild cognitive impairment and to specific domains such as executive function and processing speed, which may result in underestimation or misclassification of cognitive impairment. Notably, we applied education-stratified MMSE thresholds to partially mitigate education-related misclassification; however, this approach cannot substitute for multi-domain assessments. While education-stratified thresholds reduce some heterogeneity, education- and literacy-related biases (e.g., floor effects and language-dependent items) may still persist. Given that reliance on a single screening tool may introduce nondifferential misclassification, the observed associations are likely conservative. Accordingly, future follow-up will incorporate literacy-independent, multi-domain cognitive tests (e.g., MoCA-Basic, verbal fluency, digit symbol coding, Trail Making, clock drawing, and word-list learning/recall) to improve sensitivity to MCI and to better characterize domain-specific deficits. Fourth, several potential confounders—such as dietary patterns, physical activity, sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and genetic susceptibility (e.g., APOE ε4)—were not measured in the present study. Although our multivariable models adjusted for established covariates available in this dataset (e.g., age, sex, smoking, alcohol use, diabetes/fasting glucose), residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. The net direction of such bias is uncertain because some omitted factors may be associated with both adiposity and cognitive impairment. In addition, to avoid overadjustment, we did not adjust for anthropometric measures that constitute or may mediate RFM. In forthcoming longitudinal waves, we will collect a broader set of confounders using pragmatic instruments suitable for rural settings (e.g., a brief FFQ or 24-hour dietary recall; IPAQ-Short for physical activity; PSQI for sleep; PHQ-9 or GDS-15 for depressive symptoms; and APOE ε4 genotyping) and will consider causal-inference approaches (e.g., DAG-informed confounder selection, inverse-probability weighting/marginal structural models, and quantitative bias analysis) to better isolate the association between RFM and cognitive impairment. Moreover, some of the data, such as smoking and alcohol consumption histories, were self-reported. Self-reported data are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, which can lead to inaccurate reporting and affect the study results. Utilizing objective measures and verifying self-reported data through medical records or biochemical markers would improve data accuracy and reliability.

Conclusion

This study investigates the associations between novel obesity indices (C-index, RFM, and CVAI) and cognitive impairment in a low-income rural population in Tianjin, China. The key findings indicate that RFM is significantly associated with cognitive impairment, particularly highlighting a nonlinear relationship where both high and low RFM levels increase the risk of cognitive impairment. Additionally, gender-specific differences were observed, with higher RFM being negatively associated with cognitive impairment in males but not in females. No significant age-specific associations were found between RFM and cognitive impairment in the 60–69 years and ≥ 70 years age groups. This study highlights the significant role of relative fat mass in cognitive health and the importance of personalized and targeted health strategies for different populations. By addressing the nonlinear and gender-specific associations between RFM and cognitive impairment, healthcare providers and policymakers can develop more effective interventions to enhance cognitive function and overall well-being in rural, low-income communities.

Findings may not generalize beyond similar low-income rural Chinese populations, and replication in multi-center cohorts with diverse socioeconomic and ethnic backgrounds is warranted.
