Neuromorphic Computing with Microfluidic Memristors
Nico C. X. Stuhlmüller, René van Roij, Marjolein Dijkstra

Abstract
Conical microfluidic channels filled with electrolytes exhibit volatile memristive behavior, offering a promising foundation for energy-efficient, neuromorphic computing. Here, we integrate these iontronic channels as additional nonlinear element in nonlinear Shinriki-inspired oscillators and demonstrate that they exhibit alternating chaotic and non-chaotic dynamics across a broad frequency range. Exploiting this behavior, we construct XOR and NAND gates by coupling three “Memriki” oscillators, and we further realize the full set of standard logic gates through combinations of NAND gates. Our results establish a new paradigm for iontronic computing and open avenues for scalable, low-power logical operations in microfluidic and bio-inspired systems.



I
Introduction

The era we live in has been proposed the
Silicon age

Chabal 2001
, defined by semiconductor transistors that have revolutionized data processing, accelerating it by several orders of magnitude. This technological advancement has enabled groundbreaking inventions, including computers, the internet, and, more recently, artificial intelligence (AI).
However, advancements in AI are rapidly approaching a critical power bottleneck
Burr et al. 2016
. A key factor driving this challenge is the high energy cost caused by Ohmic losses of frequent data transfer between physically separated processing and storage units in conventional computing architectures, a limitation known as the von Neumann bottleneck
Rajendran et al. 2019
.
AI power consumption is currently doubling every four to six months
Tripp et al. 2024
, which would surpass global energy production by the 2030s.
This trend highlights the pressing need for novel computing paradigms. To enhance energy efficiency and meet the increasing demands for computing power in the era of AI and big data, a fundamentally different approach is required.

Inspired by the most energy-efficient computer known – the biological brain – the field of neuromorphic computing emerged
Marković et al. 2020
. The human brain, an intricate biological neural network comprising approximately 10
11
neurons and 10
15
synapses, operates with a remarkable efficiency at just
20\,\mathrm{W}
of power.
A key subfield of neuromorphic computing focuses on developing devices that mimic the computational functions of the brain. One such device is the memristor, first theoretically proposed by Chua in 1971 as the fourth basic circuit element
Chua 1971
.
Memristors act as memory elements, with a resistance that depends on past voltage or current, allowing them to retain information. In 2008, Strukov
et al.
experimentally demonstrated a solid-state memristor, paving the way for its use as an artificial synapse in neuromorphic systems
Strukov et al. 2008
, with present-day applications in for instance crossbar arrays
Xia and Yang 2019
;
Jeon et al. 2024
. However, silicon-based technologies typically rely on a single type of information carrier: electrons (or their absence (holes)). In contrast, the biological brain utilizes ions dissolved in water as information carriers, allowing for a diverse range of soluble information carriers, such as various species of ions and small molecules. A key distinction between solid-state electronics and ion-mediated biological systems lies in their energy efficiency, with biological systems operating orders of magnitude more efficiently, albeit at much lower clock frequencies.
This contrast has led to the emergence of iontronics, a subfield of neuromorphic computing focused on developing electronic circuit elements where ions dissolved in water serve as information carriers
Chun and Chung 2015
. Just as solid-state memristors have been widely explored, researchers have pursued iontronic analogs
Xu et al. 2024
. Memristive effects in charged conical nanopores were first observed in 2010 and 2012
Feng et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2012
, paving the way for the development of diverse nanofluidic memristors based on various mechanisms. These include electric double layer polarization
Wang et al. 2012
;
Kamsma et al. 2024
;
Emmerich et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
, salinity gradients yielding negative differential resistance behavior
Leong et al. 2020
, electro-wetting
Smirnov et al. 2011
;
Powell et al. 2011
, structural and conformational changes of nanopores
Tuszynski et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2024
, the ionic analogue
Robin et al. 2023
of electronic Coulomb blockade
Bose et al. 2015
, mixtures of and interfaces between ionic liquids and water
Sheng et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2019
, and specific polyelectrolyte-ion interactions
Xiong et al. 2023
.

The next step towards neuromorphic computing is integrating iontronic devices into electric circuits. Key questions include: Can these networks emulate neural functions, perform Boolean logic operations, or execute simple machine learning tasks
van Doremaele et al. 2024
?
While the use of iontronic elements in large-scale networks is still in its infancy, initial progress has been made in implementing basic logic gates such as
AND
,
OR

Sabbagh et al. 2023
,
NAND
and
NOR

Zhang et al. 2024
, and material implication
IMP

Emmerich et al. 2024
gates. However, a complete set of logic gates based on (memristive) iontronics has yet to be realized.

In this work, we bridge iontronics and memristive computing by constructing logic gates using microfluidic memristors
Kamsma et al. 2023a
.
In addition to microfluidic memristors, we base our logic gates on Shinriki-inspired oscillators
Shinriki et al. 1981
.
Shinriki oscillators are simple electric circuits, first studied by Shinriki
et al.
, that can show chaotic behavior depending on the exact system parameters.
As the human brain operates at the edge of chaos
Kitzbichler et al. 2009
;
Chua 2013
, we designed our circuits to inherit this property.

We first provide a brief introduction to volatile microfluidic memristors as descibeed in Ref.
Kamsma et al. 2023a
, and
then explore their integration into electric circuits that form Shinriki-inspired (non-linear) oscillators
Shinriki et al. 1981
. Finally, we demonstrate how these oscillators can be combined to realize the full set of logic gates, thereby advancing the use of iontronics for neuromorphic and Boolean logic applications.

III
Characterization of a Single Memriki oscillator

To leverage the volatile behavior of the memristors of Section II, we incorporate two of them into a nonlinear electrical circuit with an intrinsic oscillatory timescale similar to that of the memristors.
The circuit design, shown in Fig.
2
, is heavily inspired by the well-known Shinriki-oscillator
Shinriki et al. 1981
, albeit with a slightly modified topology and with iontronic memristors
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
rather than diodes.
We propose the name
Memriki oscillator
for this circuit. The circuit, driven by a sinusoidal source potential
U_{in}
with frequency
f
, consists of a negative differential resistance
\mathrm{NDR}
, two anti-parallel memristors in series with a (weakly damped) LRC oscillator with eigen frequency
1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{o}C_{o}})
, and a resistor
R_{1}
and capacitor
C_{1}
that are both parallel to the memristor-oscillator part. Based on the original work of Shinriki
et al
.
Shinriki et al. 1981
, the

\mathrm{NDR}
is an active element with a characteristic function given by
R(U)\,\mathrm{g_{0}}=(U/\,\mathrm{V})^{3}-3U/\,\mathrm{V}
. This implies that it behaves as an active amplifier rather than a passive resistor within the
\pm 1\,\mathrm{V}
voltage range around
0\,\mathrm{V}
. The parallel resistor and capacitor, with
C_{1}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{g_{0}s}
and
g_{0}R_{1}
of order unity, can serve as tunable parameters that significantly alter the dynamics of the circuit.
The electric specifications for the oscillatory part of the circuit read
C_{o}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{g_{0}s}
,
L_{o}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}/\mathrm{g_{0}}
and
R_{o}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{g_{0}}^{-1}
, ensuring that
R_{o}\ll R_{1}
. As a result, the eigen period of the weakly damped oscillator is approximately
2\pi\sqrt{L_{o}C_{o}}\approx 6\,\mathrm{ms}
, which is comparable to the memory time of the two memristors,
\tau\approx 5\,\text{ms}
.
While we describe and denote all circuit elements (except for the memristors) as conventional electrical components, we note that microfluidic counterparts exist for each of them
Stojanović et al. 2019
;
Gallardo Hevia et al. 2022
. This enables a purely microfluidic realization of this Memriki oscillator.

The dynamic response of the Memriki oscillator varies dramatically depending on the precise values of the resistor
R_{1}
and the driving frequency
f
.
For instance, it can exhibit behaviors ranging from simple driven oscillations to chaos to subharmonic oscillations, as illustrated in
Fig.

3
. A comprehensive study of the circuit’s full dynamics across a broader parameter space is beyond the scope of this paper. Instead, we focus here on the coupling of multiple Memriki oscillators.

The most fundamental computations involve combining two inputs to produce a single output.

Combining gates

The
NAND

In particular, an

\overline{\overline{U_{\mathrm{in}}^{1}\cdot U_{\mathrm{in}}^{1}}\cdot\overline{U_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}\cdot U_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}}}=\overline{\overline{U_{\mathrm{in}}^{1}}\cdot\overline{U_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}}}=\overline{\overline{U_{\mathrm{in}}^{1}}}+\overline{\overline{U_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}}}=U_{\mathrm{in}}^{1}+U_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}

To test the repeatability and reproducibility of the gates we need to verify whether they perform as expected over many cycles of the (weakly noisy) input and output sequences.
For our continuous system, with output voltages spanning the full range between zero and one volt when the input signal switches, we need to define boundaries separating high- and low-level signals during specific time intervals. Here we set the time interval as the duration between the switching of the input signals and consider an output larger than
0.66\,\mathrm{V}
as a digital 1 and below
0.33\,\mathrm{V}
a digital 0.
To evaluate the pre-gates, we connect them to the coupling circuit as described above to ensure they function as proper gates.
Using these definitions, we tested 100 cycles of all possible input combinations and obtained the following accuracies. Individual
XOR
-gates operate with accuracy of
\geq 99\%
,
NAND

\geq 99\%
, the combined
OR
-gate
\geq 99\%

VI
Conclusion

We designed microfluidic iontronic circuits, mainly based on the combination of volatile memristors and Shinriki-like oscillators, that can perform the non-linear
XOR
classification task and act as the functional complete
NAND
logic gate.
We showed that these
NAND
-gates are robust and can be combined to form other gates, especially
OR
and
AND
. A key feature of these circuits is their exploitation of noise, here set at the level of
3\,\mathrm{mV}
on the scale of
1\,\mathrm{V}
for the signals. This noise level allows for self-excitation of the oscillators from a quiescent (0) to an oscillating (1) state, a feature that stems from the intrinsic non-linearity of the “Memriki” oscillators from which our circuits were composed. We hope that our findings will inspire future explorations of experimental realizations of memristive iontronic devices. While we have not investigated the energy efficiency of our circuits and logic gates in any detail yet, we do not expect their current state to be more energy efficient than present-day silicon technology. In this sense, this work serves as a proof of concept for a potentially versatile iontronic platform for computing, based on the same aqueous electrolyte as mammalian brains.

Here,
g_{0}\approx 4.2\,\mathrm{pS}
is the zero-field conductance,
\Delta g\approx-3.59
is the asymmetry parameter, and
\mathrm{Pe}
is the Peclet number, which varies linearly with the applied voltage
U
as
\mathrm{Pe}(U)\approx 16U/\mathrm{V}
for our parameter set (exact expressions provided below). Using the short-hand notation
\Delta R=R_{b}-R_{t}
, and performing the integral, one obtains the explicit static channel conductance

\displaystyle g_{\infty}(U)=g_{0}\left(1+\Delta g\frac{R_{t}}{\Delta R}F(U)\right)

(5)

\displaystyle F(U)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\mathrm{Pe}R_{t}^{2}}{\Delta RR_{b}}}A(U)}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{Pe}\frac{R_{t}}{R_{b}}}-1}-\left(\frac{R_{b}}{\Delta R}\ln\frac{R_{t}}{R_{b}}+1\right)

(6)

\displaystyle A(U)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{Pe}R_{t}}{\Delta R}}-\frac{R_{b}}{R_{t}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{Pe}R_{t}^{2}}{\Delta RR_{b}}}+\frac{\Delta R}{R_{t}}+B(U)

(7)

\displaystyle B(U)=\frac{-\mathrm{Pe}R_{t}}{\Delta R}\left(\mathrm{Ei}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pe}R_{t}}{\Delta R}\right)-\mathrm{Ei}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pe}R_{t}^{2}}{\Delta RR_{b}}\right)\right),

(8)

where
\mathrm{Ei}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-t}/t\mathrm{d}t
is the exponential integral function.

We stress that this explicit expression for
g_{\infty}(U)
stems from an (approximate) analytic solution to the fully microscopic Poisson-Nernst-Planck-Stokes equations in the long-channel limit. The dependence of the conductance on
U
is a direct consequence of electro-osmotic flow, characterized by the dimensionless parameter
\mathrm{Pe}(U)
. This effect relies on the presence of a nonzero surface charge
\sigma
(and hence a nonzero zeta potential
\psi
on the channel walls), as becomes explicit by the analytic relation
\mathrm{Pe}(U)=\frac{-eR_{b}}{k_{B}TR_{t}w}U
, where
w=\frac{eD\eta}{k_{B}T\varepsilon\psi}\approx-9.6
represents the ratio of ionic mobility
D/k_{B}T
to osmotic mobility
\varepsilon\psi/(e\eta)
. Here
D=1.75\,\mathrm{nm}^{2}/\mathrm{ms}
is the diffusion coefficient of the monovalent cations and anions in water,
k_{B}
denotes the Boltzmann constant,
T=293.15\,\mathrm{K}
is the absolute temperature, and
\varepsilon=80.2\epsilon_{0}\approx 7.1\times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{F/m}
is the dielectric constant, and
\eta=1.01\,\mathrm{mPas}
is the shear viscosity of water.
We define the steady zero-field conductance as
g_{0}=\frac{\pi R_{t}R_{b}}{L}\frac{2\rho_{b}e^{2}D}{k_{B}T}
, where
\rho_{b}
is the ionic bulk concentration, set to
10\,\mathrm{mM}
.
The asymmetry parameter is given by

\Delta g=\frac{-2w\Delta R}{R_{b}\mathrm{Du}}\approx-3.59
,
where the Dukhin number is
\mathrm{Du}=\sigma/(2\rho_{b}R_{t})\approx-0.25
, with
\sigma=-0.0015\,e/\mathrm{nm}^{2}
the surface charge density on the channel walls. The corresponding zeta potential is
\psi=\frac{2k_{B}T}{e}\mathrm{asinh}(2\pi\lambda_{D}\lambda_{B}\sigma/e)\approx-10\,\mathrm{mV}
, where
\lambda_{D}=\sqrt{\varepsilon k_{B}T/(2e^{2}\rho_{b})}\approx 30\,\mathrm{nm}
is the Debye length and
\lambda_{B}=e^{2}/(4\pi\varepsilon k_{B}T)\approx 0.71\,\mathrm{nm}
is the Bjerrum length.

The characteristic time scale of this conical channel, which is the time scale to build up (or break down) the static voltage-dependent salt concentration profile
\rho_{s}(x;U)
in the channel, was derived to be the diffusion time
\tau=L^{2}/12D
, valid in the long-channel limit
L\gg R_{b}
. For the present parameters this leads to
\tau\approx 5\,\mathrm{ms}
.
