Abstract
Computation of confidence sets is central to data science and machine learning, serving as the workhorse of A/B testing and underpinning the operation and analysis of reinforcement learning algorithms [ 6 ] . Among all valid confidence sets for the multinomial parameter, minimum-volume confidence sets (MVCs) are optimal in that they minimize average volume [ 12 ] , but they are defined as level sets of an exact p p -value that is discontinuous and difficult to compute. Rather than attempting to characterize the geometry of MVCs directly, this paper studies a fundamental and practically motivated decision problem: given two observed multinomial outcomes, can one certify whether their MVCs intersect? We present a certified, tolerance-aware algorithm for this intersection problem. The method exploits the fact that likelihood ordering induces halfspace constraints in log-odds coordinates, enabling adaptive geometric partitioning of parameter space and computable lower and upper bounds on p p -values over each cell. For three categories, this yields an efficient and provably sound algorithm that either certifies intersection, certifies disjointness, or returns an indeterminate result when the decision lies within a prescribed margin. We further show how the approach extends to higher dimensions. The results demonstrate that, despite their irregular geometry, MVCs admit reliable certified decision procedures for core tasks in A/B testing.



I
Introduction

Confidence sets, regions, and intervals are fundamental tools in data science, statistical inference, and machine learning, capturing a range of plausible beliefs of the parameters of a model. For simplicity of computation and analysis, most approaches to construct confidence sets rely on approximation or bounds that are loose in the small sample regime
[
2
,
3
,
12
]
. While these approaches are often optimal asymptotically, tighter confidence sets in the small sample regime can reduce sample complexity in A/B testing, reinforcement learning algorithms, and other problems in applied data science
[
11
,
5
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

For categorical data, constructing tight confidence sets for the multinomial parameter is a long-studied problem. Recent work
[
12
]
, building on classical dualities between hypothesis testing and confidence sets
[
14
,
4
,
1
]
, showed that confidence sets defined as level sets of the exact
p
-value attain a minimum-volume optimality property when averaged over empirical outcomes. These
minimum-volume confidence sets
(MVCs) are therefore statistically optimal in a precise sense.

Despite their optimality, MVCs present significant computational challenges. Membership testing for a single parameter value requires evaluating an exact
p
-value defined as a sum over multinomial outcomes, a task that scales poorly with sample size and dimension
[
13
]
. Moreover, because the
p
-value is a discontinuous function of the parameter, MVCs exhibit highly irregular geometry: they are generally nonconvex, may be disconnected, and do not admit simple geometric characterizations
[
7
]
. These features make it difficult to use MVCs directly in even basic inference tasks.

This paper focuses on one such task that is central to A/B testing: given two observed multinomial outcomes, decide whether their corresponding MVCs intersect. An intersection certifies that there exists a parameter value consistent with both outcomes at a given confidence level, while disjointness implies a statistically significant difference between the underlying distributions. Naive approaches based on gridding or continuous relaxations fail to provide guarantees due to the discontinuous and fragmented structure of MVCs.
Figure
1
illustrates this difficulty in a simple multinomial example with three categories. The asymptotic chi-square (Likelihood-ratio/Wilks) confidence regions
[
15
]
, shown in Figure
1(a)
, are disjoint and would suggest a statistically significant difference between the two empirical outcomes.
In contrast, the exact minimum-volume confidence sets defined via p-value membership, shown in Figure
1(b)
, do intersect, correctly certifying the existence of a parameter value consistent with both observations at the prescribed confidence level. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of asymptotic approximations in the small-sample regime and motivates the need for exact, algorithmically tractable methods for reasoning about intersections of MVCs.

Our contribution is a certified decision procedure for this intersection problem. Rather than attempting to compute MVCs explicitly, we exploit a key structural property: likelihood comparisons between multinomial outcomes become linear inequalities in log-odds coordinates. This observation enables us to partition parameter space into geometric cells on which tight lower and upper bounds on the exact
p
-value can be computed. Using adaptive refinement, we obtain an algorithm that either certifies intersection, certifies disjointness, or reports uncertainty when the decision lies within a prescribed tolerance. We present the method in detail for the case of three categories, then extend to general
k
dimensions.

II
Notation and Basic Definitions

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. samples of a categorical random variable that takes one of
k
possible values from a finite number of categories
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
. The empirical distribution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
is the relative proportion of occurrences of each element of
\mathcal{X}
in
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}=[\nicefrac{{n_{1}}}{{n}},\dots,\nicefrac{{n_{k}}}{{n}}]
, where
{n}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{{\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{j}=x_{i}\}}}
. Let
\Delta_{k,n}
denote the discrete simplex from
n
samples over
k
categories:

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,n}\ :=\ \left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\{0,\ \nicefrac{{1}}{{n}},\ \nicefrac{{2}}{{n}},\ \dots,\ 1\}^{k}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{p}_{i}=1\right\},

and define
m=|\Delta_{k,n}|={n+k-1\choose k-1}
.
Denote the continuous simplex as
\Delta_{k}=\left\{\boldsymbol{p}\in[0,1]^{k}:\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\right\}
. We use
\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{k,n})
to denote the power set of
\Delta_{k,n}
, and
\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{k})
to denote the set of Lebesgue measurable subsets of
\Delta_{k}
. For any
\mathcal{S}\subset\Delta_{k,n}
we write
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\mathcal{S})
as shorthand for
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}^{n}:\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}(X)\in\mathcal{S}\right\}\right)
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\cdot)
denotes the probability measure under the multinomial parameter
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}
. Lastly, let
\mathbb{R}^{k+}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:x_{i}>0,i=1,\dots,k\}
.

Definition 1
.

(Confidence set) Let
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}):\Delta_{k,n}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{k})
be a set valued function that maps an observed empirical distribution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
to a subset of the
k
-simplex.
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})
is a
confidence set
at confidence level
1-\alpha
if the following holds:

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\not\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\right)\ \leq\ \alpha.

(1)

Definition 2
.

(
p
-value) Fix an outcome
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
. The

p
-value as a function of the null hypothesis
\boldsymbol{p}
is given by:

\displaystyle\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})\ =\ \sum_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\Delta_{k,n}:\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}})\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}\right).

(2)

For a fixed outcome
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
, we write
\rho(\boldsymbol{p})
for simplicity.

Proposition 1
.

(Minimum volume confidence set (MVCs)
[
12
]
).
The MVCs are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\star}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\ :=\ \big\{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}\ :\ \rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\alpha\big\},

and satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\Delta_{k,n}}\mathrm{vol}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\star}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\right)\leq\sum_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\Delta_{k,n}}\mathrm{vol}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\right)

for any confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
; here
\mathrm{vol}(\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue measure. A proof can be found in
[
12
]
.

III
AB-Testing Intersection Problem

In this paper we focus exclusively on a single computational task motivated by AB testing:
given two observed multinomial outcomes, decide whether their minimum-volume confidence sets
(MVCs) intersect.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(A)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(B)}\in\Delta_{k,n}
denote two observed empirical
distributions from
n
i.i.d. categorical samples
over
k
categories. For a confidence level
1-\alpha
, recall from Section II that the MVC
associated with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
is

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\star}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\;=\;\{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}:\ \rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\alpha\}.

Our goal is to decide whether

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\star}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(A)})\ \cap\ \mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\star}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(B)})\ \neq\ \emptyset.

(3)

Equivalently, define
\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p}):=\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(A)}}(\boldsymbol{p})
and

\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p}):=\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(B)}}(\boldsymbol{p})
. Then (
3
) holds iff

\max_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}}\ \min\{\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p}),\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\}\;\geq\;\alpha.

We emphasize that we do
not
seek to compute or visualize MVC boundaries like
[
7
]
; we only seek
a reliable yes/no answer to (
3
).

In finite-precision computation, decisions can be numerically delicate when the optimum is near the threshold
\alpha
. We therefore introduce a
decision margin

\tau>0
and target a
certified decision with a buffer around
\alpha
.

Definition 3

(Robust intersection decision)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\tau>0
. We say that the MVCs
robustly intersect
if there exists

\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}
such that

\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\alpha+\tau\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\alpha+\tau.

We say that the MVCs are
robustly disjoint
if for all
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}
,

\min\{\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p}),\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\}\leq\alpha-\tau.

Our algorithm will either return

•

INTERSECT
(certifying robust intersection)

•

DISJOINT
(certifying robust disjointness), or

•

UNCERTAIN
when the instance cannot be resolved within the chosen tolerances.

In addition to
\tau
, the algorithm takes a
geometric refinement tolerance

\varepsilon>0
controlling the maximum cell diameter in an
adaptive partition.

IV
Certified Intersection Test for
k=3

We first present the method cleanly for
k=3
, where the simplex is two-dimensional and the
algorithm is especially effective. Section
V
then generalizes the approach to
arbitrary
k
.

IV-A

Log-odds coordinates and outcome probabilities

For
k=3
we write
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\in\Delta_{3}^{\circ}
, , all coordinates are
strictly positive so that log-odds coordinates are finite. Define log-odds coordinates

(u,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by

u=\log\frac{p_{1}}{p_{3}},\qquad v=\log\frac{p_{2}}{p_{3}},

with inverse map

p_{1}(u,v)=\frac{e^{u}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}},\quad p_{2}(u,v)=\frac{e^{v}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}},\quad p_{3}(u,v)=\frac{1}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}}.

(4)

For any count vector
r=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
with
r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}=n
, define the multinomial
coefficient

\kappa(r)=\frac{n!}{r_{1}!\,r_{2}!\,r_{3}!}.

Then the probability of observing
r
under parameter
\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)
is

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\;=\;\kappa(r)\,p_{1}(u,v)^{r_{1}}\,p_{2}(u,v)^{r_{2}}\,p_{3}(u,v)^{r_{3}}.

Taking logs and using (
4
) gives

\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)=\log\kappa(r)\;+\;r_{1}u+r_{2}v\;-\;n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v}).

(5)

In the presence of zero counts, the intersection decision is understood as allowing
\boldsymbol{p}
to lie on the boundary of
\Delta_{k}
. As shown in Section
V-E
, this can be reduced to a finite collection of lower-dimensional multinomial distributions on the faces of the simplex.

IV-B

Likelihood-ordering halfspaces

Fix an observed outcome
\hat{r}=(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},\hat{r}_{3})
(equivalently

\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}=\hat{r}/n\in\Delta_{3,n}
). Recall that the p-value is

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\sum_{r:\ \mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r).

A key fact is that the likelihood-ordering comparison
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r})

is
linear
in
(u,v)
.

Lemma 1

(Halfspace form in
(u,v)
)
.

For any
r
and fixed
\hat{r}
, the inequality
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r})
is
equivalent to

(r_{1}-\hat{r}_{1})u+(r_{2}-\hat{r}_{2})v\;\leq\;\log\kappa(\hat{r})-\log\kappa(r).

(6)

Proof.

Subtract (
5
) for
r
and for
\hat{r}
. The shared term
-n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})
cancels.
Exponentiating yields (
6
).
∎

Thus, for fixed
\hat{r}
, each
r
defines a halfspace in
(u,v)
, and the set of
(u,v)
where

r
is in the p-value tail (i.e. has probability no larger than
\hat{r}
) is a halfspace boundary.

IV-C

A compact search domain

The coordinates
(u,v)
range over
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, so we restrict the search to a compact region without
losing any robust decision. The following crude bound is sufficient.

Lemma 2

(A universal upper bound on the p-value)
.

Let
m=|\Delta_{3,n}|
. For any observed
\hat{r}
and any
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{3}
,

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})\ \leq\ m\,\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}).

Proof.

The tail set in the p-value definition contains at most
m
outcomes; each term in the tail sum
is
\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})
by definition. Summing yields the claim. A detailed proof is provided in Appendix
VI-A
.
∎

By Lemma
2
, if
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})<(\alpha-\tau)/m
then

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})<\alpha-\tau
. Therefore any
\boldsymbol{p}
that could witness robust intersection
(Definition
3
) must satisfy
both

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}^{(A)})\geq(\alpha-\tau)/m\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}^{(B)})\geq(\alpha-\tau)/m.

In
(u,v)
-coordinates, each constraint is a superlevel set of the concave function

(u,v)\mapsto\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\cdot)
, hence convex and bounded. Let
\mathcal{W}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be any
compact convex set containing the intersection of these two superlevel sets. In practice, a
simple bounding box around that intersection suffices; the algorithm below only requires that

\mathcal{W}
contain all potential robust witnesses.

IV-D

Cellwise lower bounds on multinomial probabilities

We partition
\mathcal{W}
into triangles. Let
T\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a triangle with vertices

w^{(1)},w^{(2)},w^{(3)}\in\mathcal{W}
, where
w^{(j)}=(u^{(j)},v^{(j)})
.

For any outcome
r
, define
f_{r}(u,v):=\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)
. From (
5
),

f_{r}
is concave in
(u,v)
because it is affine minus
n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})
(and
\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})

is convex). Concavity implies a simple, computable lower bound on
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)
over
T
.

Lemma 3

(Vertex minimum for concave log-probabilities)
.

For any outcome
r
and any triangle
T
with vertices
\{w^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{3}
,

\min_{(u,v)\in T}\ f_{r}(u,v)\ =\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}f_{r}(w^{(j)}).

Consequently, the quantity

\underline{P}_{T}(r)\ :=\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(w^{(j)})}(r)

satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\geq\underline{P}_{T}(r)
for all
(u,v)\in T
.

Proof.

Any
(u,v)\in T
is a convex combination of the vertices. Concavity gives

f_{r}(u,v)\geq\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}f_{r}(w^{(j)})\geq\min_{j}f_{r}(w^{(j)})
. Please refer to Appendix
VI-C
for details.
∎

IV-E

Certified p-value bounds on a triangle

Fix an observed outcome
\hat{r}
. For each
r
, define the linear function

g_{r;\hat{r}}(u,v):=(r_{1}-\hat{r}_{1})u+(r_{2}-\hat{r}_{2})v-\big(\log\kappa(\hat{r})-\log\kappa(r)\big).

By Lemma
1
,
r
is in the p-value tail at
(u,v)
iff
g_{r;\hat{r}}(u,v)\leq 0
.
Since
g_{r;\hat{r}}
is linear, membership can be certified over a triangle by checking vertices.

Define the
definitely-in-tail

\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})
and
definitely-out-of-tail

\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})
index sets for triangle
T
:

\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r}):=\{r:\ g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(j)})\leq 0\ \text{for all }j=1,2,3\},\qquad\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r}):=\{r:\ g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(j)})>0\ \text{for all }j=1,2,3\}.

All remaining outcomes are
ambiguous
on
T
.

Proposition 2

(Certified p-value interval on a triangle)
.

Let
\hat{r}
be fixed and let
T
be a triangle. Define

\underline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r}):=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{T}(r),\qquad\overline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r}):=1-\sum_{r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{T}(r),

(7)

where
\underline{P}_{T}(r)
is from Lemma
3
.
Then for all
(u,v)\in T
,

\underline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r})\ \leq\ \rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))\ \leq\ \overline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r}).

Proof.

If
r\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})
then
g_{r;\hat{r}}\leq 0
at all vertices, hence everywhere on
T
, so
r

belongs to the tail for all
(u,v)\in T
. Therefore the p-value tail sum includes all

r\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})
, and Lemma
3
lower-bounds each probability by

\underline{P}_{T}(r)
, yielding the lower bound.

If
r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})
then
g_{r;\hat{r}}>0
at all vertices, hence everywhere on
T
, so
r
is
excluded from the tail for all
(u,v)\in T
. Thus the p-value is at most the total probability
mass of outcomes not in
\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})
. Using that
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\geq\underline{P}_{T}(r)
for
each
r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})
and
\sum_{r}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)=1
yields the upper bound in (
7
).
∎

IV-G

Correctness guarantee

Theorem 1

(Soundness of Algorithm
1
)
.

Let
m=|\Delta_{3,n}|
and define

S_{A}:=\{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r}^{(A)})\geq(\alpha-\tau)/m\},\quad S_{B}:=\{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r}^{(B)})\geq(\alpha-\tau)/m\}.

Assume the compact search domain
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
\mathcal{W}\supseteq S_{A}\cap S_{B}
.
Then Algorithm
1
is sound in the following sense:

•

If it returns
INTERSECT
, then the MVCs robustly intersect (Definition
3
).

•

If it returns
DISJOINT
, then the MVCs are robustly disjoint (Definition
3
).

If it returns
UNCERTAIN
, then the true decision is within a
\tau
-neighborhood of the
threshold on at least one triangle of diameter at most
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

On any triangle
T
, Proposition
2
bounds each p-value over all points in
T
,
hence
\underline{M}_{T}\leq\min\{\rho_{A},\rho_{B}\}\leq\overline{M}_{T}
on
T
.

If
\underline{M}_{T}\geq\alpha+\tau
for some processed
T
, then for all
\boldsymbol{p}\in T
,

\min\{\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p}),\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\}\geq\alpha+\tau
, so a robust witness exists and the MVCs robustly intersect. If the queue empties with no unresolved triangles, then for every triangle in the (refined) triangulation of
\mathcal{W}
we have
\overline{M}_{T}<\alpha-\tau
, hence
\min\{\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p}),\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\}<\alpha-\tau
for all
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{W}
.
Now consider any
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{3}
with coordinates
(u,v)\notin\mathcal{W}
. Since
\mathcal{W}\supseteq S_{A}\cap S_{B}
, we have
(u,v)\notin S_{A}
or
(u,v)\notin S_{B}
.
If
(u,v)\notin S_{A}
, then
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}^{(A)})<(\alpha-\tau)/m
, and by Lemma
2
,

\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p})<\alpha-\tau
. Similarly for
B
. In either case,
\min\{\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p}),\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\}\leq\alpha-\tau
holds for all
\boldsymbol{p}\notin\mathcal{W}
. Combining with the bound on
\mathcal{W}
proves robust disjointness over all
\Delta_{3}
.
∎

IV-H

Computational complexity

The naive per-triangle cost is
O(m)
outcomes times a small constant, where

m=|\Delta_{3,n}|=\binom{n+2}{2}=O(n^{2})
. In practice, substantial speedups are obtained by:
(i) precomputing
\log\kappa(r)
for all
r
once; (ii) caching
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(w^{(j)})}(r)
across
triangles that share vertices; and (iii) early pruning using
\overline{M}_{T}<\alpha-\tau
.

V
Extension to general
k

We now generalize the construction to arbitrary
k
, highlighting what changes and what remains
identical.

V-A

Log-odds coordinates in dimension
k-1

For
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}^{\circ}
, define
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k-1}
by

u_{i}=\log\frac{p_{i}}{p_{k}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,k-1,

with inverse map

p_{i}(u)=\frac{e^{u_{i}}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}e^{u_{j}}},\ i\leq k-1,\qquad p_{k}(u)=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}e^{u_{j}}}.

For any outcome
r=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
with
\sum_{i}r_{i}=n
, let

\kappa(r)=\frac{n!}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}!}
. Then

\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u)}(r)=\log\kappa(r)\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}r_{i}u_{i}\;-\;n\log\!\Big(1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}e^{u_{j}}\Big).

V-B

Halfspace structure and simplex cells

Fix an observed
\hat{r}
. Exactly as in Lemma
1
, for any
r
,

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u)}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u)}(\hat{r})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(r_{i}-\hat{r}_{i})u_{i}\;\leq\;\log\kappa(\hat{r})-\log\kappa(r),

which is a halfspace in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k-1}
.

To keep vertex checks inexpensive in higher dimension, we recommend partitioning the compact
search region
\mathcal{W}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k-1}
into
simplices
. A simplex in dimension
d=k-1
has only

d+1=k
vertices (as opposed to
2^{d}
vertices for a hypercube).

V-C

Certified p-value bounds on a simplex cell

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k-1}
be a simplex with vertices
\{w^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{k}
. Define the linear
functions

g_{r;\hat{r}}(u):=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(r_{i}-\hat{r}_{i})u_{i}-\big(\log\kappa(\hat{r})-\log\kappa(r)\big).

As before, define

\mathcal{I}_{S}(\hat{r}):=\{r:\ g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(j)})\leq 0\ \text{for all }j\},\qquad\mathcal{O}_{S}(\hat{r}):=\{r:\ g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(j)})>0\ \text{for all }j\}.

The function
u\mapsto\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u)}(r)
is concave in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k-1}
, so the vertex-minimum
lower bound generalizes directly:

\underline{P}_{S}(r):=\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(w^{(j)})}(r)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u)}(r)\geq\underline{P}_{S}(r)\ \ \forall u\in S.

Hence the p-value bounds in Proposition
2
hold verbatim with
T
replaced
by
S
:

\underline{\rho}_{S}(\hat{r})=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}_{S}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{S}(r),\qquad\overline{\rho}_{S}(\hat{r})=1-\sum_{r\in\mathcal{O}_{S}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{S}(r).

V-D

Algorithm and scaling discussion

Algorithm
1
generalizes by replacing triangles with
(k-1)
-simplices and using a
shape-regular refinement rule (e.g. newest-vertex bisection). The
INTERSECT
/
DISJOINT

soundness remains unchanged.

The main difference for
k>3
is computational scaling:

m=|\Delta_{k,n}|=\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}
grows as
n^{k-1}
for fixed
k
, and adaptive refinement may
require many cells in dimension
k-1
in worst cases. Nonetheless, for modest
k
(as is common in
categorical AB testing), the method remains practical because
(i) simplex cells use only
k
vertices;
(ii) vertex-probability evaluations can be cached aggressively; and
(iii) many cells prune early via
\overline{M}_{S}<\alpha-\tau
.

V-E

Handling zero-count categories via face decomposition

The development so far implicitly assumes
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}^{\circ}
, i.e.,
p_{i}>0
for all

i
, so that log-odds coordinates are finite. In practice, observed multinomial outcomes
often contain zero counts, and any correct intersection test must account for boundary
faces of the simplex. We show that this can be handled cleanly by a finite decomposition
over lower-dimensional faces.

Let
\hat{r}^{(A)},\hat{r}^{(B)}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
be the observed outcomes. Define the index sets

S_{A}:=\{i:\hat{r}^{(A)}_{i}>0\},\qquad S_{B}:=\{i:\hat{r}^{(B)}_{i}>0\},\qquad S_{0}:=S_{A}\cup S_{B}.

Lemma 4

(Zero-count exclusion)
.

If
\hat{r}_{i}>0
for some outcome
\hat{r}
and
p_{i}=0
, then

\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})=0
.

Proof.

If
p_{i}=0
and
\hat{r}_{i}>0
, then
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})=0
. The p-value tail

\{r:\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})\}
contains only outcomes of probability zero,
hence the p-value sum equals zero.
∎

Lemma
4
implies that any parameter
\boldsymbol{p}
satisfying

\rho_{A}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\alpha
and
\rho_{B}(\boldsymbol{p})\geq\alpha
must satisfy
p_{i}>0
for all
i\in S_{0}
.
Consequently, only categories with zero count in
both
outcomes may be set to zero in a potential intersection witness. We provide an example of such case in Appendix
VI-E
.

Let

Z:=\{1,\ldots,k\}\setminus S_{0}

denote the set of jointly-zero categories. For any subset
T\subseteq Z
, define the face

\Delta_{k}^{(T)}:=\{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}:\ p_{i}=0\ \forall i\in T\}.

On this face, the multinomial distribution reduces to a
(k-|T|)
-category multinomial
over the remaining coordinates, with the same sample size
n
. The corresponding reduced
outcomes are obtained by deleting the coordinates in
T
from
\hat{r}^{(A)}
and

\hat{r}^{(B)}
.

Therefore, deciding whether the MVCs intersect reduces to checking robust intersection on
each face
\Delta_{k}^{(T)}
,
T\subseteq Z
, in the reduced dimension. We provide an example of such case in Appendix
VI-F
. A general algorithm that extends to
k
-dimension with zero-count handling can be find in Algorithm
2
.

VI
Summary

This paper studied a fundamental computational problem associated with minimum-volume confidence sets for the multinomial parameter: deciding whether the confidence sets corresponding to two observed outcomes intersect. Rather than attempting to compute or visualize MVCs directly, we developed a certified decision procedure that exploits the geometric structure induced by likelihood ordering in log-odds coordinates.

For the case of three categories, we presented an adaptive partitioning algorithm that computes rigorous lower and upper bounds on the exact
p
-value over each cell, yielding provably sound certificates of intersection or disjointness up to a user-specified tolerance. The method naturally accommodates the discontinuity and nonconvexity of MVCs by reasoning over regions on which the ordering of likelihoods is fixed. We further showed how the approach extends to higher dimensions and how boundary cases arising from zero-count categories can be handled cleanly by decomposing the simplex into a finite collection of lower-dimensional faces.

Detailed Proof of Lemma

Restatement of Lemma
2
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
, and let
\Delta_{k,n}
denote the set of all empirical
distributions (equivalently, count vectors divided by
n
) arising from
n
i.i.d. samples over
k
categories. Let
m:=|\Delta_{k,n}|=\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}
. Fix any observed
outcome
\hat{r}
(or equivalently
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}=\hat{r}/n\in\Delta_{k,n}
). Then for every
multinomial parameter
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}
,

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p})\ \leq\ m\,\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}).

Equivalently, writing
\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})
for the same p-value,

\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})\ \leq\ m\,\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}).

Proof.

We begin by unpacking the definition of the (exact) p-value used throughout the paper.
Fix
\hat{r}
and
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{k}
. Recall that the p-value associated with
\hat{r}
under
the null hypothesis
\boldsymbol{p}
is

\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})\ :=\ \sum_{r\in\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r),\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p}):=\Big\{r\in\Delta_{k,n}:\ \mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})\Big\}.

(8)

Here
\Delta_{k,n}
indexes all possible empirical outcomes from
n
draws (equivalently,
all count vectors
r=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with
\sum_{i}r_{i}=n
), and
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r)
is
the multinomial probability of observing that outcome under
\boldsymbol{p}
.

The proof is a direct counting-and-bounding argument.

We first show that the tail set contains at most
m
outcomes.
By definition,
\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})\subseteq\Delta_{k,n}
. Since

|\Delta_{k,n}|=m
, we have the trivial cardinality bound

|\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})|\ \leq\ |\Delta_{k,n}|\ =\ m.

(9)

Then we show that each summand in the p-value is bounded by
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})
.
For any
r\in\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})
, the defining property of
\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})

gives

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r)\ \leq\ \mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}).

(10)

This holds for every
r
included in the p-value sum (
8
).

No we bound the total tail probability mass.
Using (
8
), (
10
), and the fact that there are

|\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})|
terms in the sum, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{r\in\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(r)\ \leq\ \sum_{r\in\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})\ =\ |\mathcal{T}_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})|\,\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}).

(11)

Finally apply the cardinality bound (
9
) to (
11
):

\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})\ \leq\ m\,\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r}),

which is exactly the desired inequality.
∎

VI-B

Geometric interpretation of the superlevel-set restriction

We now justify the geometric statement used following Lemma
2
, namely
that the constraint

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})\ \geq\ \frac{\alpha-\tau}{m}

defines a convex and bounded region in log-odds coordinates.

We start by expressing the constraint as a superlevel set.
Fix an observed outcome
\hat{r}
and consider the function

f(u,v)\ :=\ \log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r}),

where
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the log-odds coordinates defined by

p_{1}=\frac{e^{u}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}},\qquad p_{2}=\frac{e^{v}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}},\qquad p_{3}=\frac{1}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}}.

The inequality

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r})\ \geq\ \frac{\alpha-\tau}{m}

is equivalent, after taking logarithms on both sides, to

f(u,v)\ \geq\ \log(\alpha-\tau)-\log m.

(12)

The set of points
(u,v)
satisfying (
12
) is therefore the

superlevel set
of the function
f
, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}\ :=\ \{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ f(u,v)\geq c\},\qquad c:=\log(\alpha-\tau)-\log m.

We now show the concavity of the log-likelihood function.
Recall from (
5
) in the main text that

f(u,v)=\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v}).

The first three terms are affine functions of
(u,v)
.
The final term
-\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})
is the negative of a convex function, since

\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Therefore
f(u,v)
is a concave function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
A standard result from convex analysis states that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

is concave, then for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the superlevel set

\{x:\ f(x)\geq c\}

is a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Applying this result to
f(u,v)
shows that the set
\mathcal{S}
defined above
is convex.

We now show that
\mathcal{S}
is bounded.
As
\|(u,v)\|\to\infty
, at least one of
u
or
v
tends to
+\infty
or
-\infty
.
In all such cases, the term
-n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})
dominates and tends to
-\infty
,
while the linear terms
\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v
grow at most linearly.
Consequently,

\lim_{\|(u,v)\|\to\infty}f(u,v)=-\infty.

This implies that for any finite constant
c
, the inequality
f(u,v)\geq c

can only hold within a bounded region of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Hence the superlevel set
\mathcal{S}
is bounded.

Therefore, the constraint
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})\geq(\alpha-\tau)/m
defines, in
(u,v)
-coordinates,
a bounded and convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Applying this argument separately to
\hat{r}^{(A)}
and
\hat{r}^{(B)}
, we conclude
that the intersection of the two corresponding superlevel sets is also convex and
bounded. Any compact convex set
\mathcal{W}
containing this intersection may
therefore be used as the search domain in Algorithm
1
.

Detailed Proof of Lemma
3

Restatement of Lemma
3
.

Fix
k=3
and
n\geq 1
. For any outcome
r=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
with
r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}=n
, define

f_{r}(u,v)\ :=\ \log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r),

where
\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)
is given by the softmax map (
4
). Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

be any (closed) triangle with vertices
w^{(1)},w^{(2)},w^{(3)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

\min_{(u,v)\in T}f_{r}(u,v)\ =\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}f_{r}\!\left(w^{(j)}\right).

Consequently, the quantity

\underline{P}_{T}(r)\ :=\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(w^{(j)})}(r)

satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\geq\underline{P}_{T}(r)
for all
(u,v)\in T
.

Proof.

We proceed in three steps. First we verify concavity of
f_{r}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Second we
use a general fact about concave functions on convex polytopes: the minimum over a polytope is attained at an extreme point (vertex). Third we specialize to triangles and conclude the probability lower bound.

We first show that
f_{r}(u,v)
is concave in
(u,v)
.
Recall the multinomial probability in log-odds coordinates (cf. (
5
)):

f_{r}(u,v)=\log\kappa(r)\;+\;r_{1}u+r_{2}v\;-\;n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v}),

(13)

where
\kappa(r)=\frac{n!}{r_{1}!\,r_{2}!\,r_{3}!}
is constant with respect to
(u,v)
.
The first three terms on the right-hand side of (
13
) are affine functions of

(u,v)
, hence are both convex and concave. The remaining term

(u,v)\ \mapsto\ \log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})

is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(this is the standard “log-sum-exp” function). Multiplying a
convex function by
-n<0
yields a concave function. Therefore the sum of the affine terms
and the concave term
-n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})
is concave, proving that
f_{r}
is concave on

\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Then we show that the minimum of a concave function over a triangle occurs at a vertex.
The triangle
T=\mathrm{conv}\{w^{(1)},w^{(2)},w^{(3)}\}
is a compact convex set. We will show
that for any
(u,v)\in T
,

f_{r}(u,v)\ \geq\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}f_{r}\!\left(w^{(j)}\right).

(14)

Since
T
is compact and
f_{r}
is continuous (as a sum of continuous functions),
f_{r}

attains its minimum on
T
by the extreme value theorem. Inequality (
14
)
then implies that the minimum value cannot be smaller than the minimum over the vertices,
hence the minimum must be achieved at (at least) one vertex.

To establish (
14
), let
(u,v)\in T
be arbitrary. By definition of
convex hull, there exist coefficients
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\geq 0
with

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}=1
such that

(u,v)\ =\ \lambda_{1}w^{(1)}+\lambda_{2}w^{(2)}+\lambda_{3}w^{(3)}.

Concavity of
f_{r}
means precisely that for any such convex combination,

f_{r}\!\left(\lambda_{1}w^{(1)}+\lambda_{2}w^{(2)}+\lambda_{3}w^{(3)}\right)\ \geq\ \lambda_{1}f_{r}(w^{(1)})+\lambda_{2}f_{r}(w^{(2)})+\lambda_{3}f_{r}(w^{(3)}).

(15)

Using the representation of
(u,v)
and applying (
15
) gives

f_{r}(u,v)\ \geq\ \lambda_{1}f_{r}(w^{(1)})+\lambda_{2}f_{r}(w^{(2)})+\lambda_{3}f_{r}(w^{(3)}).

Finally, since
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\geq 0
and sum to
1
, the right-hand side is a
convex combination of the three numbers
\{f_{r}(w^{(1)}),f_{r}(w^{(2)}),f_{r}(w^{(3)})\}
and
therefore cannot be smaller than their minimum. Formally,

\lambda_{1}f_{r}(w^{(1)})+\lambda_{2}f_{r}(w^{(2)})+\lambda_{3}f_{r}(w^{(3)})\ \geq\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}f_{r}(w^{(j)}).

Combining the two inequalities yields (
14
).

We want to now conclude the equality of minima.
Let
j^{\star}\in\arg\min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}f_{r}(w^{(j)})
. Since
w^{(j^{\star})}\in T
, we have

\min_{(u,v)\in T}f_{r}(u,v)\ \leq\ f_{r}\!\left(w^{(j^{\star})}\right)\ =\ \min_{j}f_{r}(w^{(j)}).

On the other hand, inequality (
14
) holds for all
(u,v)\in T
, so
taking the minimum over
(u,v)\in T
on both sides gives

\min_{(u,v)\in T}f_{r}(u,v)\ \geq\ \min_{j}f_{r}(w^{(j)}).

Together these two inequalities imply

\min_{(u,v)\in T}f_{r}(u,v)\ =\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}f_{r}(w^{(j)}),

proving the first claim.

Step 4: Translate the result to probabilities.
Exponentiation preserves order, hence from

f_{r}(u,v)\geq\min_{j}f_{r}(w^{(j)})
we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\ =\ e^{f_{r}(u,v)}\ \geq\ \exp\!\Big(\min_{j}f_{r}(w^{(j)})\Big)\ =\ \min_{j}\exp(f_{r}(w^{(j)}))\ =\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(w^{(j)})}(r).

This is exactly
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\geq\underline{P}_{T}(r)
for all
(u,v)\in T
, completing the proof.
∎

Generalization to
k>3
: The same argument holds verbatim in dimension
d=k-1
when
T
is replaced by a simplex

S\subset\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
with vertices
\{w^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{k}
, since every point in a
simplex is a convex combination of its vertices and
\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u)}(r)
remains concave in

u
.

Restatement of Proposition

Fix an observed outcome
\hat{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{3}

\hat{r}_{1}+\hat{r}_{2}+\hat{r}_{3}=n

\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}=\hat{r}/n\in\Delta_{3,n}

T

(u,v)\in T

We start by characterizing the halfspace tail membership.

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g_{r;\hat{r}}(u,v)\leq 0.

(u,v)

(17)

Now consider the vertices for “definitely-out-of-tail” outcomes.
Now consider any

g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(j)})>0

for all vertices. The same convex-combination argument implies that for every
(u,v)\in T
,

g_{r;\hat{r}}(u,v)=\lambda_{1}g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(1)})+\lambda_{2}g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(2)})+\lambda_{3}g_{r;\hat{r}}(w^{(3)})\ >\ 0,

and hence, by (
16
),

r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)>\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r})

(18)

for all
(u,v)\in T
. In particular, such
r
is excluded from the p-value tail

everywhere
on
T
.

We then derive a uniform lower bound on probabilities over
T
.
By Lemma
3
, for each outcome
r
and for all
(u,v)\in T
,

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\ \geq\ \underline{P}_{T}(r)\ :=\ \min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(w^{(j)})}(r).

(19)

Lower bound on the p-value as follow:
Let
\mathcal{T}(u,v)
denote the p-value tail set at
(u,v)
:

\mathcal{T}(u,v):=\{r:\ \mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(\hat{r})\}.

By (
17
), every
r\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})
lies in
\mathcal{T}(u,v)

for all
(u,v)\in T
, hence

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{T}(u,v)}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\ \geq\ \sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r).

Applying the uniform probability lower bound (
19
) term-by-term yields

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))\ \geq\ \sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{T}(r)\ =\ \underline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r}).

Since
(u,v)\in T
was arbitrary, this proves the lower bound uniformly over the triangle:

\underline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r})\ \leq\ \rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))\qquad\forall(u,v)\in T.

Similarly, we can upper bound the p-value.
By (
18
), every
r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})
is
not
in the tail

\mathcal{T}(u,v)
for any
(u,v)\in T
. Therefore the p-value tail is contained in the
complement of
\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})
:

\mathcal{T}(u,v)\ \subseteq\ \Delta_{3,n}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r}).

Hence

\displaystyle\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{T}(u,v)}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\ \leq\ \sum_{r\in\Delta_{3,n}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)=1-\sum_{r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r).

(20)

Applying the uniform lower bound (
19
) to each
r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})

gives

\sum_{r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}(u,v)}(r)\ \geq\ \sum_{r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{T}(r),

and substituting into (
20
) yields

\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))\ \leq\ 1-\sum_{r\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(\hat{r})}\underline{P}_{T}(r)\ =\ \overline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r}).

Again, since
(u,v)\in T

Combining the two bonds establishs that for all

\underline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r})\ \leq\ \rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}}(\boldsymbol{p}(u,v))\ \leq\ \overline{\rho}_{T}(\hat{r}),

∎

Outcomes in
\mathcal{I}_{T}(\hat{r})

VI-E

Example of Zero-count exclusion.

Consider
k=3
categories and
n=5
samples. Suppose the observed outcome is
\hat{r}=(2,2,1)
, so that category 3 appears at least once. Now consider a parameter
\boldsymbol{p}=(1/2,\,1/2,\,0)
assigning zero probability to category 3. Then

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})=\frac{5!}{2!\,2!\,1!}(1/2)^{2}(1/2)^{2}(0)^{1}=0.

Since
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})=0
, the p-value tail consists only of outcomes of probability
zero, and hence
\rho_{\hat{r}}(\boldsymbol{p})=0
. In particular,
\boldsymbol{p}
cannot belong to the MVC at
any confidence level
\alpha>0
.

By contrast, if
\hat{r}=(2,3,0)
and
\boldsymbol{p}=(1/2,\,1/2,\,0)
, then
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\hat{r})>0
and the p-value is not forced to vanish. This illustrates why only categories with zero count in
both
outcomes may be set to zero when searching for intersection witnesses. When a category
i
has zero count in both observed outcomes, setting
p_{i}=0
does not invalidate either likelihood. In this case the multinomial model
collapses
onto a lower-dimensional face of the simplex, corresponding to a reduced multinomial over the remaining categories. Importantly, this collapse is
EXACT
rather than approximate: the distribution of outcomes conditional on
p_{i}=0
is precisely a multinomial with one fewer
category and the same sample size
n
.

From the perspective of the p-value, this means that restricting
\boldsymbol{p}
to a face
\Delta_{k}^{(T)}
(for
T\subseteq Z
) induces the same p-value ordering as that obtained by deleting the zeroed coordinates from both the parameter and the observed outcome. As a
result, testing MVC intersection on a face
\Delta_{k}^{(T)}
is equivalent to running the same certified intersection test on the reduced outcomes in dimension
k-|T|
.

Algorithm
2
exploits this structure by enumerating all faces corresponding to jointly-zero categories and applying the interior algorithm on each reduced problem. In this way, boundary behavior is handled exactly, without approximation or special-case logic, while preserving the same soundness guarantees as in the interior.

VI-G

Bounding box construction for
W
in
(u,v)
-coordinates

We provide two ways to derive a bound for
W
, one is closed-form and the other is analytical.
Throughout we start with
k=3
, with log-odds coordinates

u=\log\frac{p_{1}}{p_{3}},\qquad v=\log\frac{p_{2}}{p_{3}},

so that

p_{1}(u,v)=\frac{e^{u}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}},\quad p_{2}(u,v)=\frac{e^{v}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}},\quad p_{3}(u,v)=\frac{1}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}}.

For an observed count vector
\hat{r}=(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},\hat{r}_{3})
with
\sum_{i}\hat{r}_{i}=n
, define

f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\triangleq\log P_{p(u,v)}(\hat{r})=\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-n\log\!\bigl(1+e^{u}+e^{v}\bigr),

where
\kappa(\hat{r})=\frac{n!}{\hat{r}_{1}!\hat{r}_{2}!\hat{r}_{3}!}
. For a fixed threshold
t>0
, the superlevel set

S_{\hat{r}}(t)\triangleq\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;P_{p(u,v)}(\hat{r})\geq t\}=\{(u,v):\;f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\geq\log t\}

is convex (indeed, compact), since
f_{\hat{r}}
is concave in
(u,v)
.

In the certified intersection procedure we require a compact search domain containing all possible witnesses. A convenient sufficient condition is

W\subseteq S_{\hat{r}^{(A)}}(t)\cap S_{\hat{r}^{(B)}}(t),\qquad t=\frac{\alpha-\tau}{m},\quad m=\lvert\Delta_{3,n}\rvert.

An axis-aligned bounding box for
S_{\hat{r}}(t)
(or for the intersection of two such sets) requires computing coordinate extrema such as

u_{\max}=\sup\{u:\exists v\text{ s.t. }f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\geq\log t\},\qquad v_{\max}=\sup\{v:\exists u\text{ s.t. }f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\geq\log t\},

and analogously
u_{\min},v_{\min}
. Boundary points satisfy the level-set equation

\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-n\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})=\log t.

Rearranging gives

1+e^{u}+e^{v}=\exp\!\left(\frac{\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-\log t}{n}\right),

which mixes
e^{u}
,
e^{v}
, and an exponential of an affine form in
(u,v)
. Solving for one variable given the other leads to a transcendental equation; no algebraic closed form exists in general. The same obstruction persists when bounding the intersection

S_{\hat{r}^{(A)}}(t)\cap S_{\hat{r}^{(B)}}(t),

whose extrema arise from convex programs with boundary conditions of the same form.

Although the boundary itself is not closed-form, each univariate slice admits a closed-form maximizer. Fix
u
and consider
v\mapsto f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)
. Differentiation yields

\frac{\partial f_{\hat{r}}}{\partial v}(u,v)=\hat{r}_{2}-n\frac{e^{v}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}}.

Setting this derivative to zero gives

\frac{e^{v}}{1+e^{u}+e^{v}}=\frac{\hat{r}_{2}}{n}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{v}=\frac{\hat{r}_{2}}{n-\hat{r}_{2}}(1+e^{u}),

and hence the unique slice maximizer

v^{*}(u)=\log\!\left(\frac{\hat{r}_{2}(1+e^{u})}{n-\hat{r}_{2}}\right).

By concavity, for fixed
u
the superlevel set

\{v:\;f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\geq\log t\}

is either empty or an interval

[v_{-}(u),v_{+}(u)],

with endpoints satisfying
f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)=\log t
. These endpoints have no closed form but can be computed robustly by one-dimensional bracketing and bisection. The analogous statements hold for
u^{*}(v)
obtained by symmetry.

If a purely closed-form (but very loose) bound is desired, one may use the inequality

1+e^{u}+e^{v}\leq 3e^{\max\{0,u,v\}},

which implies

\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})\leq\log 3+\max\{0,u,v\}.

Substituting into
f_{\hat{r}}
yields

f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\geq\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-n\log 3-n\max\{0,u,v\}.

Thus any
(u,v)
satisfying

\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-n\log 3-n\max\{0,u,v\}\geq\log t

is guaranteed to lie in
S_{\hat{r}}(t)
. This condition is piecewise linear and can be unfolded into explicit linear inequalities by considering the cases
\max\{0,u,v\}=0
,
u
, or
v
.

Conversely, using the lower bound

\log(1+e^{u}+e^{v})\geq\max\{0,u,v\},

we obtain the implication

f_{\hat{r}}(u,v)\leq\log\kappa(\hat{r})+\hat{r}_{1}u+\hat{r}_{2}v-n\max\{0,u,v\}.

Whenever the right-hand side is strictly less than
\log t
, the point
(u,v)
is certainly outside
S_{\hat{r}}(t)
. This yields a closed-form (but conservative) exclusion region, from which an explicit axis-aligned outer box can be derived.
