Pre-operative prediction of difficult tracheal intubation using an explainable multi-pose shared encoder vision transformer: a prospective observational study
Authors: Serkan Telli, Süleyman Camgöz, Naciye Nur Arslan, Durmuş Özdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDifficult tracheal intubation remains poorly predicted by conventional bedside airway tests. Facial photographs analysed by deep learning have been proposed as an alternative, and multi-pose acquisition protocols are increasingly advocated; however, whether acquiring more poses actually improves prediction has not been tested.MethodsIn this prospective, single-centre observational study, standardised pre-operative facial photographs were obtained in seven poses from 250 patients undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia with orotracheal intubation. Difficult intubation was defined pragmatically as two or more intubation attempts and/or use of an alternative airway device. A multi-pose shared-encoder Vision Transformer was trained and evaluated under stratified five-fold cross-validation for every one of the 127 possible pose combinations and compared with conventional bedside tests. To quantify post-selection optimism, the pose-selection step was itself nested within the resampling: it was repeated using the training folds only and evaluated in the withheld fold. The calibration of the preferred configuration was assessed under a separate held-out scheme.ResultsDifficult intubation occurred in 70 of 250 patients (28.0%). Conventional bedside tests discriminated poorly: the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.66 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.73) for a neck circumference ≥ 400 mm, and the confidence interval of every other test included 0.50. Across all 127 combinations, discrimination was unrelated to the number of poses acquired (Spearman’s rho = 0.09, P = 0.34): the seven-pose model (mean AUC 0.666) performed no better than the average single-pose model (0.660). Inter-fold variability fell markedly once more than one pose was acquired (median SD of AUC 0.170 for single-pose versus 0.098 for multi-pose combinations; P < 0.001) and did not fall further thereafter. After this pose-number analysis, upper lip bite plus lateral neutral position was selected as a pragmatic two-pose candidate on the basis of discrimination, stability, anatomical complementarity, and acquisition burden. It ranked first among the 21 two-pose combinations and tied third overall under the four-criterion ranking, achieving a mean AUC of 0.688 (95% CI 0.594 to 0.781), with pooled out-of-fold sensitivity 67.1% and specificity 71.1%; its confidence interval overlapped that of neck circumference.ConclusionsAcquiring additional facial poses did not improve the prediction of difficult tracheal intubation. A second, anatomically complementary pose stabilised an otherwise erratic estimate, but further poses produced no observed gain in discrimination or stability. The internally cross-validated performance of the pragmatic two-pose candidate was modest and was not demonstrably superior to neck circumference. Background

Difficult tracheal intubation remains a major cause of airway-related morbidity and mortality, particularly when unanticipated [1]. Failure to identify patients at risk may lead to repeated attempts, delayed oxygenation, and, in the worst cases, catastrophic outcomes including hypoxaemia, brain injury, and death [2]. Accurate pre-operative identification is therefore critical for preparing alternative devices and strategies.

Conventional bedside tests such as the modified Mallampati score, thyromental distance, sternomental distance, mouth opening, and upper lip bite test have limited sensitivity and positive predictive value individually, and remain imperfect even when combined [3, 4]. This is biologically plausible, as difficult intubation reflects a multidimensional phenotype shaped by mandibular geometry, perioral anatomy, submental soft tissue distribution, cervical contour, and neck mobility [4]. An integrative approach capturing these interacting features may be better aligned with airway difficulty than isolated bedside thresholds.

Recent advances in computer vision have made such an approach feasible [5, 6]. Since the early work showing that facial analysis could classify intubation difficulty from photographs [7], several groups have demonstrated that pre-operative facial imaging can extract clinically relevant airway information [8–12]. These studies collectively suggest that airway difficulty is encoded in broader facial and cervical morphological patterns beyond manually measured distances.

Despite this progress, key limitations persist. Most published models use convolutional neural networks (CNN) or landmark-based strategies that may incompletely capture the relationships between anatomically distant but clinically related regions [8, 10]. Many have used Cormack–Lehane grade as the target, although laryngoscopic grading has recognised inter-observer variability, and a favourable view does not guarantee successful intubation [9, 10]. Explainability has been inconsistently addressed, limiting clinician confidence [9, 12].

We developed and prospectively evaluated an explainable multi-pose shared-encoder Vision Transformer model (MP-SEViT) for the pre-operative prediction of difficult tracheal intubation. Specifically, we aimed to determine whether anatomically complementary facial and cervical views improved prediction beyond conventional bedside tests and single-image approaches, using a pragmatic clinical definition of difficult intubation based on two or more attempts and/or the need for an alternative airway device. In addition, occlusion-based explainability analysis was performed to visualise the anatomical regions driving model predictions.

We hypothesised that combining anatomically complementary facial poses would improve discrimination relative to single-pose models and conventional bedside airway tests, and that discrimination would increase with the number of poses acquired. Both hypotheses were tested against all 127 possible pose combinations.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Anaesthesiology and Reanimation of a tertiary care centre. The study protocol was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kütahya Health Sciences University (Kütahya, Türkiye; Decision No. 2025/05–35; approved on 7 April 2025) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06961240; registered on 29 April 2025). Written informed consent, including consent for the acquisition and analysis of facial photographs in both print and electronic forms, was obtained from all participants before enrolment. A data analysis and statistical plan was written and filed with the Kütahya Health Sciences University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee before data were accessed. This study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD + AI) statement [13] and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [14] for observational studies; completed checklists are provided as Supplementary File 1 and Supplementary File 2.

Participants

Adults aged 18 years or older with ASA physical status I–III scheduled for elective surgery requiring orotracheal intubation under general anaesthesia between June and November 2025 were prospectively enrolled. Exclusion criteria were emergency surgery, pregnancy, history of head and neck surgery or trauma altering airway anatomy, previous facial surgery causing significant external change, and facial obstruction by dense beard to a degree that could impair image-based analysis. The study was conducted in a 610-bed public tertiary academic referral hospital affiliated with Kütahya Health Sciences University, serving a predominantly Turkish population in western Anatolia and performing approximately 10 000 operations under general anaesthesia each year. Eligible patients were approached consecutively in the pre-operative holding area on days on which an investigator was present; therefore, recruitment was consecutive within investigator availability rather than fully consecutive across the study period. No patient was selectively approached or omitted on the basis of anticipated airway difficulty. Because emergency surgery, obstetric patients, and non-operating-room anaesthesia were not eligible, the cohort represents an elective adult surgical population, and the spectrum of airway difficulty may differ from that encountered in unselected or emergency practice; this is acknowledged as a source of spectrum bias.

Pre-operative clinical assessment and image acquisition

All patients underwent routine pre-operative airway assessment by consultant or senior specialist anaesthetists with at least five years of clinical experience. Demographic data, including age, sex, and body mass index, were recorded. Conventional bedside airway predictors were also documented, including the modified Mallampati score, thyromental distance, sternomental distance, interincisor distance, and neck circumference.

For routine patient-safety planning, patients were considered at increased risk of difficult tracheal intubation if one or more of the following were present: modified Mallampati class ≥ III, thyromental distance ≤ 60 mm, sternomental distance ≤ 120 mm, interincisor distance ≤ 30 mm, or neck circumference ≥ 400 mm.

Standardised image acquisition was performed in the pre-operative waiting area under controlled conditions. Images were obtained against a homogeneous background at a fixed distance of 100 cm under standard lighting using a single mobile device (iPhone 8; 12 MP, f/1.8, 28 mm, autofocus). For each patient, seven photographs were obtained to capture complementary facial and cervical views: frontal neutral (mouth closed), maximal mouth opening, tongue protrusion, head extension, upper lip bite position, lateral neutral, and lateral flexion. Representative poses are shown in Fig. 2. Before inclusion in the dataset, image clarity and positional accuracy were independently checked by two investigators.

Clinical intubation protocol and outcome definition

To standardise the ascertainment of the primary outcome, a predefined clinical intubation protocol was followed for all patients (Fig. 3). The artificial intelligence model was not deployed in real time during clinical care, and no model output was available to the anaesthetist at the bedside. The objective was to ensure that the anaesthetist was not influenced by the AI output during routine airway assessment and intubation, and that the AI accurately reflected the anaesthetist’s intubation experience without introducing bias. All image-based predictions were generated offline only after completion of patient recruitment and outcome collection.

Plan B: stepwise escalation to advanced or alternative airway techniques (stylet or bougie, levering laryngoscopes, videolaryngoscopy) following an unsuccessful first attempt, with each subsequent attempt involving a deliberate strategy change. Plan C: rescue oxygenation if intubation fails or oxygenation becomes suboptimal, including face-mask ventilation and consideration of a second-generation supraglottic airway device

Intubation management followed a structured institutional protocol (Fig. 3). After standardised induction (midazolam 0.02 mg/kg, fentanyl 1 µg/kg, propofol 2 mg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg), direct laryngoscopy with a Macintosh blade was performed in the sniffing position. Optimisation measures included confirmation of adequate neuromuscular blockade, external laryngeal manipulation, and appropriate blade selection. If the first attempt failed, mask ventilation was re-established and the conditions were re-optimised before escalation, which could include technique modification, bougie or stylet use, and videolaryngoscopy. Each subsequent attempt reflected a deliberate strategy change. If intubation was not achieved within three attempts, management was transferred to a more senior anaesthetist.

The primary outcome was difficult tracheal intubation, operationally defined as the need for two or more intubation attempts and/or the use of an alternative airway device (videolaryngoscope, stylet, or bougie). This pragmatic endpoint was chosen to capture clinically meaningful failure of the initial intubation strategy, rather than relying solely on laryngoscopic grading.

An attempt was defined as a single insertion of the laryngoscope blade into the patient’s oral cavity. When an adjunct (bougie or stylet) was introduced without the withdrawal of the blade, it was recorded as a single attempt with adjunct use; such patients met the composite endpoint through the “alternative airway device” criterion rather than through repeated attempts.

Image preprocessing and model architecture

All images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels (RGB) and normalised to the 0–1 range. No data augmentation was applied to ensure that the results reflected performance on the original clinical images. We developed a multi-input Vision Transformer-based model that jointly processes photographs from different facial and head positions [15], designated the Multi-Pose Shared-Encoder Vision Transformer (MP-SEViT).

The model operates in three stages (Fig. 4). First, each image is divided into non-overlapping 16 × 16 patches, each linearly projected into a 64-dimensional feature vector with learnable positional embeddings. Second, the patch representations are processed by a shared Transformer encoder (eight layers, four attention heads, dropout 0.1), whose weights are applied identically to all input poses to ensure that anatomically corresponding information from different views is represented within a consistent latent space. Third, the pose embeddings are concatenated into a K × 64 feature vector and passed to a multilayer perceptron classifier (Dense[128] → Dense[64] → Dense [1]), with a sigmoid output for binary classification.

Training used the Adam optimiser (learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁴; batch size 16; maximum 20 epochs) with binary cross-entropy loss. Early stopping based on the validation AUC (patience = 5) and learning-rate reduction on plateau were applied. The decision threshold for each validation fold was selected using the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) [16]. All 127 possible pose combinations (K = 1 to K = 7) were evaluated. All data, including facial images, were stored and analysed on a local encrypted workstation without external network access. Model development used Python 3.9 and TensorFlow 2.10.1 on a workstation with an Intel Core i7-11800 H processor, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 Laptop GPU.

Model validation strategy

All analyses were performed at the patient level to prevent data leakage. The seven images obtained from each patient were treated as a single unit, and images from the same patient were not allowed to appear in both training and validation sets. The dataset was divided using patient-level stratified group five-fold cross-validation, with the class distribution of non-difficult and difficult intubation preserved across folds [15]. In each iteration, approximately 80% of patients were used for training and 20% for validation (Fig. 5). Each fold was trained independently and evaluated only on previously unseen validation patients. The final performance was reported as the mean (SD) across the five folds.

Pose-combination ranking

All 127 non-empty pose combinations (K = 1 to K = 7) were trained and evaluated under the identical cross-validation scheme. First, we examined the relationship between the pose number and discrimination and inter-fold stability across all 127 combinations without selecting a particular combination. Because discrimination showed no association with K and the reduction in inter-fold variability was already complete at K = 2, two poses were treated as the pragmatic acquisition ceiling for a clinically feasible protocol. Second, the 21 two-pose combinations were ranked on four equally weighted criteria: mean cross-validated AUC, mean F1-score, mean accuracy, and inter-fold stability (SD of AUC across the five folds, lower values preferred). Upper lip bite plus lateral neutral had the best mean rank within the two-pose subset and was carried forward as the pragmatic candidate after also considering anatomical complementarity and the workload of acquiring additional images. Supplementary File 5 preserves the global ranking of all 127 combinations: a four-pose combination was ranked first overall, whereas upper lip bite plus lateral neutral tied for rank 3 overall and ranked fifteenth on mean AUC alone. Therefore, the two-pose designation was a clinical-efficiency choice made after analysis, not a claim that this pair was the overall statistical winner or that it was pre-specified.

Selection-free analysis of pose number

To determine whether discrimination depends on the number of poses acquired, independently of the particular combination chosen, all 127 combinations were grouped by the number of poses (K) and summarised (Table 4). The association between K and mean AUC was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation across all 127 combinations, and inter-fold stability was compared between single-pose and multi-pose combinations. Because no combination was selected in this analysis, it was not affected by post-selection optimism.

Correction for pose-selection optimism

Because the preferred configuration was identified among 127 candidates using the same cross-validation folds from which its performance was subsequently reported, that estimate is subject to post-selection optimism. We therefore nested the selection step within the resampling procedure. For each of the five folds in turn, the four-criterion ranking described above was recomputed using only the remaining four folds; the combination preferred on those four folds was then evaluated in the fold that had been withheld. Because every model had already been fitted separately within each fold using only that fold’s training partition, the withheld fold contributed to neither the fitting nor the selection of the model evaluated in it. Selection-corrected performance is reported as the mean (SD) of the five held-out AUCs, together with the combination preferred in each fold, which characterises the stability of the selection procedure itself (Table 6). This analysis re-uses the per-fold results already generated for all 127 combinations (Supplementary File 3) and required no model re-training.

Explainability analysis

Occlusion-based explainability analysis was performed to identify the anatomical regions that contributed most strongly to the model predictions [17]. Occlusion maps were used to visualise the spatial areas that most influenced the model output and to describe their anatomical distribution across the face and neck.

Missing data

Patients with an incomplete photographic set or a missing intubation outcome were excluded before analysis (Fig. 1). The resulting analytic dataset of 250 patients was complete for every variable used in model development, in the bedside test comparisons, and in the baseline characteristics table; no variable contained a missing value and no imputation was performed. Demographic and airway data were not collected from patients who declined consent, as this was not covered by the ethics approval; a formal comparison of enrolled and non-enrolled patients was therefore not possible, and this is acknowledged as a potential source of selection bias.

Data quality

Before analysis, every clinical variable was screened against the range of anatomical plausibility, and related measurements were checked for internal consistency — for example, the sternomental distance must exceed the thyromental distance in every participant. This identified transcription errors in three patients: two implausible neck circumferences, and one patient in whom the thyromental and sternomental distances had been transposed. All three were corrected against the original source records. No value was altered on statistical grounds, and none of the corrected variables was used by the image-based model. The corrected dataset is provided in full as Supplementary File 4.

Sample size

The required sample size was estimated using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test framework, which is statistically equivalent to testing whether the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) exceeds chance-level discrimination (AUC = 0.50). Based on published image-based difficult intubation prediction studies reporting AUC values in the range of 0.60–0.86 [9, 11, 12], a conservative medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50, corresponding to an expected AUC of approximately 0.64) was assumed. With a two-sided α of 0.05, 80% power, and an allocation ratio of 3:1 (non-difficult to difficult) based on an anticipated difficult intubation prevalence of approximately 25%, G*Power (version 3.1.9.6) estimated a minimum requirement of 178 patients (45 difficult, 133 non-difficult; actual power = 0.80). However, because the study employed patient-level stratified group five-fold cross-validation of a deep learning model — requiring a sufficient number of difficult intubation cases in each validation fold for stable and generalisable performance estimation — a target enrolment of 250 patients was set. This higher target also accounted for potential incomplete data or image quality issues.

We acknowledge that this calculation addresses the precision of a discrimination estimate rather than the data requirements of a deep learning model. With 70 outcome events, the number of events per learnable parameter is far below the conventional recommendations for model development. Therefore, model capacity was deliberately constrained — a single shared encoder with eight layers and a 64-dimensional embedding, no data augmentation, and early stopping — but this does not eliminate the risk of overfitting, and the sample size remains a principal limitation of the study.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because all continuous variables deviated from normality, between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test; results are presented as mean (SD). Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test for expected cell counts ≥ 5 and Fisher’s exact test otherwise; results are presented as n (%).

For conventional bedside tests, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was computed from the underlying continuous or ordinal variable and is therefore independent of any threshold; 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained using the method of DeLong. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and the F1-score were computed at the clinical cut-off conventionally applied to each test.

For the deep learning model, performance was summarised across the five validation folds as mean (SD), and the 95% CI for the mean AUC was obtained from the t distribution with four degrees of freedom. Because the five folds are disjoint and every patient appears in exactly one validation fold, patient-level classifications were also pooled across folds to yield out-of-fold estimates of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and F1-score over the complete cohort of 250 patients, with Wilson score 95% CIs. As the decision threshold was selected within each fold using the Youden index, these threshold-dependent metrics are optimistic; the AUC, which is threshold-independent, was therefore treated as the primary performance metric.

The association between the number of poses and discrimination was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation across all 127 combinations, and inter-fold stability (the SD of AUC across folds) was compared between single-pose and multi-pose combinations using the Mann–Whitney U test. Because the reported model estimate is the average of five independently fitted cross-validation models rather than a single fitted model, a formal pairwise comparison of AUCs (for example, the DeLong test) was not applicable; therefore, the discrimination of the model and bedside tests was compared by inspection of their 95% CIs, and no formal claim of superiority was made.

Analyses were performed using Python 3.9 (SciPy 1.10, scikit-learn 1.2) and SPSS version 26.0. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Calibration

Assessment of calibration requires a predicted probability for every patient, which the primary cross-validation summaries do not provide. Therefore, out-of-sample predicted probabilities for the preferred two-pose configuration were obtained under a held-out evaluation scheme (200 patients for five-fold cross-validation and 50 reserved as a test set), so that every one of the 250 probabilities was generated on data not used to train the model that produced it. Discrimination under this scheme (out-of-sample AUC 0.66, 95% CI 0.58–0.74) was consistent with the primary cross-validated estimate (0.688) and the selection-corrected estimate (0.614). Calibration was summarised by the Brier score — compared with the non-informative reference value of prevalence × (1 − prevalence) = 0.202 — by logistic calibration intercept and slope (logit[outcome] = intercept + slope × logit[predicted probability]; Wald 95% CIs), and by a flexible calibration plot (Fig. 6). Bootstrap 95% CIs for the Brier score used 5000 resamples.

Results

Demographic characteristics and class distribution

Of the 518 patients assessed for eligibility, 250 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

The demographic characteristics and conventional airway assessment parameters are presented in Table 1. Difficult tracheal intubation, as defined by the composite endpoint, occurred in 70 of 250 patients (28.0%). Of these, 33 (47.1%) required two or more intubation attempts, and 68 (97.1%) required an alternative airway device. Thirty-one patients (44.3%) met both criteria; 37 (52.9%) required an alternative device at the first attempt without a repeat attempt, and 2 (2.9%) required repeated attempts without an adjunct. A Cormack–Lehane grade III–IV view was observed in 39 of 250 patients (15.6%).

Sex distribution differed between the groups: male sex was more common in the difficult intubation group (43/70, 61.4%) than in the non-difficult group (59/180, 32.8%; P < 0.001). In contrast, Mallampati classes III–IV (15.7% versus 11.7%; P = 0.39) and upper lip bite test class III (14.3% versus 7.2%; P = 0.08) did not differ significantly (Table 1).

Among the continuous variables, there were no significant between-group differences in body mass index (P = 0.56), thyromental distance (P = 0.46), sternomental distance (P = 0.49), or interincisor distance (P = 0.80). In contrast, neck circumference was greater in the difficult intubation group (mean (SD), 398.0 (39.1) mm) than in the non-difficult intubation group (374.5 (37.7) mm; P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Model performance: multi-pose analysis

Performance metrics for all 127 pose combinations are provided in Supplementary File 3 and are summarised by the number of poses acquired in Table 4; Fig. 7; 95% confidence intervals for the AUC of each of the 127 combinations are provided in Supplementary File 6. Cross-validated discrimination did not improve as poses were added. The mean AUC was 0.660 across the seven single-pose models and lay between 0.666 and 0.678 for every multi-pose group, and there was no association between the number of poses and discrimination across the 127 combinations (Spearman’s rho = 0.09, P = 0.34). The single seven-pose model (mean AUC 0.666) performed no better than the average single-pose model. The only change observed with the number of poses was stability: the median inter-fold standard deviation of AUC fell from 0.170 for single-pose models to 0.098 for multi-pose combinations (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test comparing single-pose with multi-pose combinations); this reduction was already complete at two poses (median SD 0.098 across the 21 two-pose combinations) and did not continue as further poses were added (Spearman’s rho = − 0.08, P = 0.41 within K ≥ 2). Therefore, a second pose stabilised an otherwise erratic estimate, whereas a third and subsequent poses added neither discrimination nor stability.

Calibration

The post hoc internal calibration assessment of the preferred two-pose model is shown in Fig. 6. The Brier score was 0.196 (95% CI 0.175–0.218), only marginally lower than the non-informative reference value of 0.202, obtained by always predicting the cohort prevalence, indicating limited incremental probabilistic accuracy beyond prevalence-only prediction. Predicted probabilities spanned a narrow range (0.21–0.36) and were concentrated around the cohort prevalence (mean predicted probability, 0.30 versus observed event frequency 0.28). The calibration slope was 2.31 (95% CI 0.77–3.84), and the calibration intercept was 0.99 (95% CI − 0.29 to 2.27). A slope above unity suggests under-dispersed predictions, with estimated probabilities concentrated too closely around the mean risk.

Explainability analysis

Occlusion-based explainability analysis was performed on the preferred two-pose configuration (upper lip bite plus lateral neutral, K = 2); representative maps are presented in Fig. 8. In the lateral neutral pose, the regions contributing most to the model output corresponded to the mandibular angle and anterior neck contour. In the upper lip bite pose, the highest-contributing regions were located in the perioral and submandibular areas. These spatial patterns were consistent across participants when occlusion maps were averaged over the mean face template and were consistent with anatomical regions conventionally examined during airway assessment. Because these maps were generated from the same fitted models whose performance is reported above, they describe where the model attended and are not independent evidence that these regions determine airway difficulty.

Discussion

This study had two principal findings. First, conventional bedside airway tests discriminated poorly in this cohort: no test achieved an AUC above 0.66, and the 95% confidence interval of every test other than neck circumference included 0.50. This is consistent with the systematic evidence that bedside airway examination has limited predictive value in apparently normal adults [3, 4]. Second, the preferred two-pose configuration of the multi-pose facial Vision Transformer showed modest discrimination, with a mean cross-validated AUC of 0.688 (95% CI 0.594 to 0.781), which decreased to 0.614 (95% CI 0.408 to 0.820) after the pose-selection step was itself cross-validated — a reduction of 0.074 AUC points. The confidence intervals of the model and of neck circumference overlapped, and we therefore make no claim that the model outperforms bedside assessment. The most informative result of this study was not the performance of any single configuration, but the shape of the relationship between the number of poses acquired and the behaviour of the model.

Multi-pose acquisition has been advocated on the assumption that additional views carry additional anatomical information [18–20]. Because every one of the 127 possible pose combinations was trained and evaluated under an identical protocol, we were able to test this assumption without selecting any combination, and therefore without post-selection bias. Discrimination was flat: mean AUC was 0.660 across the seven single-pose models and lay between 0.666 and 0.678 for every multi-pose group, with no association between the number of poses and AUC across the 127 combinations (Spearman’s rho = 0.09, P = 0.34). The full seven-pose model was no better than the average single pose. What changed was reliability. The inter-fold standard deviation of AUC almost halved between the single-pose and multi-pose models (median 0.170 to 0.098; P < 0.001) — a reduction already complete at two poses — and then stopped falling. Therefore, a single facial view yields an estimate that is not merely weak but erratic, and the practical value of a second, anatomically complementary view is that it stabilises that estimate rather than sharpens it. Beyond two poses, additional images appear redundant, adding neither discriminative information nor further reduction in variance. This has a direct consequence for study design: protocols requiring five, six, or seven standardised views impose a real cost on the pre-operative workflow, and our data suggest that this cost buys nothing.

The second implication is cautionary. The preferred two-pose configuration was chosen after reviewing all 127 combinations, and its reported mean AUC of 0.688 is therefore vulnerable to post-selection optimism. The current reporting guidance for prediction models requires such development choices to be considered when performance is interpreted [13]. Therefore, we regard the identity of the optimal pose pair as an open question to be resolved through anatomical reasoning and prospective validation rather than through further analysis of the same dataset. The upper lip bite plus lateral neutral pose pair remains a pragmatic candidate because it ranked first among the 21 two-pose combinations and samples two anatomically distinct regions. However, the occlusion maps were generated from this same post hoc selected model and therefore cannot independently corroborate the choice, particularly because the pair was not selected in any individual fold, and the selection-corrected estimate was compatible with chance performance. Therefore, we advance this configuration only as a hypothesis for prospective testing, rather than as an optimal combination established by the present data.

Our discrimination was lower than that reported by most earlier image-based studies. Tavolara et al. reported an AUC of 0.71 using an ensemble of deep learning models applied to frontal face images [8]; García-García et al. reported median AUCs of 0.75 to 0.77 from pre-operative photographs [10]; Xia et al. reported an AUROC of 0.78 (95% CI 0.73 to 0.83) for difficult videolaryngoscopy [11]; Kim et al. reported AUCs of 0.81 to 0.88 [12]; and Hayasaka et al. reported an AUC of 0.86 [9]. We make no claim of superiority over these approaches, and we do not interpret our lower figure as evidence that the present model is inferior, because the studies are not directly comparable.

Three differences are material. First, the reference standard differs. Most previous studies predicted a laryngoscopic view — such as Cormack–Lehane grade III–IV or a difficult videolaryngoscopic view — whereas we predicted failure of the initial intubation strategy, a behavioural composite. Had we used Cormack–Lehane grade III or IV alone, our event rate would have been 15.6% rather than 28.0%, and the prediction task would have been materially different. Second, several earlier models were developed using retrospectively assembled or curated image sets, in which image quality and pose may have been implicitly selected; our photographs were acquired prospectively in a pre-operative holding area under routine conditions. Third, performance in this literature is seldom accompanied by confidence intervals, and model, feature, or pose selection performed during development is not always transparently addressed. In the present study, the preferred two-pose model was identified post hoc among multiple candidates; consequently, its mean AUC of 0.688 may be optimistic and should not be directly compared with estimates obtained under different development and validation procedures.

One observation reinforces the need for cautious interpretation. Tavolara et al. reported AUCs of 0.60 and 0.47 for conventional bedside tests in their cohort [8], which closely matched the 0.47–0.66 range observed in the present study. The relative consistency of these bedside baselines, contrasted with the wider variation among image-based model estimates, may reflect differences in case mix, outcome definitions, model-development procedures, and validation strategies rather than true differences in predictive capability.

Among the conventional variables examined, neck circumference was the only bedside parameter to show a clear signal above the others (AUC 0.66; 95% CI 0.58 to 0.73). This is plausible because neck circumference captures more than a single linear measurement; it may also reflect submental soft tissue burden, anterior neck fullness, and broader changes in upper airway geometry. Recent studies have similarly suggested that neck-related morphometric variables retain clinical value even when traditional bedside tests perform inconsistently [21, 22]. It is sobering, however, that male sex alone discriminated as well as every bedside test other than neck circumference (AUC 0.64; 95% CI 0.58 to 0.71). Male sex was more frequent in the difficult intubation group (61.4% versus 32.8%; P < 0.001), a direction consistent with the greater neck circumference and submental soft tissue mass typically observed in men. Given the modest sample size and the absence of a multivariable analysis, this association should be regarded as cohort-specific and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of an independent risk factor. It nonetheless underlines how little discriminative information the classical bedside tests carried in this cohort.

The 28.0% incidence of difficult intubation in our cohort is higher than most previously reported figures [22]; this reflects our pragmatic endpoint definition rather than a true excess of anatomically challenging airways. We defined difficult intubation as two or more intubation attempts and/or the use of an alternative airway device because, in routine practice, what ultimately changes management is failure of the initial strategy rather than the laryngoscopic view itself [1, 23]. Had we used a Cormack–Lehane grade III or IV alone, the incidence would have fallen to 15.6% — in line with published prevalence estimates, but, in our view, less clinically actionable. It is important to be explicit about how the composite behaved: most events were driven by the alternative-device criterion rather than by repeated attempts (68 of 70 versus 33 of 70), and 37 patients met the endpoint through use of an adjunct at the first attempt without any repeated attempt. This reflects contemporary practice, in which a videolaryngoscope or bougie is deployed early rather than after repeated failed attempts [1], and it makes the endpoint sensitive to local escalation thresholds and to individual operator behaviour. The endpoint therefore captures meaningful escalation of airway management rather than a purely anatomical descriptor, but it is not a behaviour-independent reference standard, and this is a limitation of the outcome definition itself.

From an architectural perspective, the choice of a Vision Transformer with a shared encoder rather than a conventional convolutional neural network was motivated by the hypothesis that morphological information relevant to difficult intubation arises not only from local facial features but also from global spatial relationships between anatomically distant regions — for example, the interplay between chin profile, neck fullness, and mouth opening. The self-attention mechanism inherent to the Transformer architecture is well suited to modelling such long-range dependencies [15], and the shared-encoder design ensures that all poses are represented within a consistent feature space. Occlusion-based explainability analysis [17] highlighted the mandibular contour, perioral region, and anterior neck profile, which are regions consistent with known airway anatomy. However, because the maps were generated from the same fitted, post hoc selected configuration, they did not independently validate either the selected pose pair or the clinical relevance of the highlighted regions. Given the modest and imprecise selection-corrected discrimination, the maps should be interpreted as descriptive visualisations of model behaviour rather than as evidence that the model learned clinically valid morphology or avoided spurious image features.

This study has several strengths: a prospective design with a registered protocol, a standardised image-acquisition protocol, a clinically pragmatic endpoint, patient-level cross-validation with all images from each patient assigned to the same fold, evaluation of every possible pose combination rather than a convenient subset, and transparent acknowledgement of the post-selection optimism associated with the post hoc choice of the preferred two-pose candidate.

Several limitations must be recognised. First, this was a single-centre study of elective adult surgical patients without external validation; emergency, obstetric, and non-operating-room populations were not represented, and the spectrum of airway difficulty may therefore differ from that of unselected practice. The cohort was drawn from a single, predominantly Turkish population and imaged with a single camera under standardised conditions; generalisability to other populations, acquisition devices, and healthcare systems therefore remains untested. No formal subgroup-performance or model-fairness analysis was performed, and differential discrimination or calibration across demographic and phenotypic subgroups — including groups defined by sex, age, skin tone, or ethnicity — and across imaging devices cannot be excluded. Future multicentre validation should prospectively assess subgroup-specific discrimination and calibration in sufficiently diverse populations and imaging conditions. Second, patients were approached consecutively only on days when an investigator was present, and no data were collected on those who declined consent; therefore, selection bias cannot be excluded. Third, although the sample exceeded our a priori power calculation, 70 outcome events remain few for a Transformer-based model, and the risk of overfitting is not eliminated by the deliberate constraints placed on model capacity. Fourth, the selection-corrected estimate, while more honest than the naive one, is itself imprecise (mean AUC 0.614; 95% CI 0.408 to 0.820) and is compatible with performance no better than chance. Fifth, the decision threshold was selected within each fold using the Youden index [16], so threshold-dependent metrics — accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values — remain optimistic; the same applies, to a lesser degree, to the use of validation-fold AUC for early stopping. Sixth, we did not fit a multivariable model combining image-derived and clinical predictors, and we therefore cannot state whether the image model adds information beyond neck circumference and sex. Finally, conventional bedside findings were known to the anaesthetist performing intubation as part of routine care; although the model was evaluated offline, clinician awareness of those findings may have influenced the threshold for escalation and hence the outcome itself.

Conclusions

Conventional bedside airway tests were poorly discriminative in this cohort, and a multi-pose image-based Vision Transformer achieved only modest discrimination after correction for pose-selection optimism (mean AUC 0.614; 95% CI 0.408 to 0.820). The principal finding is that acquiring more facial poses does not improve prediction: across all 127 combinations, discrimination was unrelated to the number of poses, whereas a single pose yielded unstable estimates that were resolved by adding a second, anatomically complementary view. Two poses therefore appear to represent a practical ceiling rather than a starting point. However, which two poses should be used could not be determined reliably from these data and should be pre-specified before prospective validation. The upper lip bite plus lateral neutral pair remains one anatomically motivated candidate because it samples mandibular protrusion, perioral soft tissue, sagittal mandibular contour, and anterior neck fullness. The model’s occlusion maps attended to these same regions, but, deriving from the same fit, they are consistent with this choice rather than independent support for it. These findings are hypothesis-generating and do not support clinical deployment; external, multicentre validation of a pre-specified two-pose protocol is the necessary next step.
