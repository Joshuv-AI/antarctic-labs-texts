Barriers and facilitators to implementing antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) in a Chinese tertiary hospital: a qualitative study based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)
Authors: Jiao Chen, Junli Yang, Ming Li, Yuxing Yan, Yutong Fu, Xinchen Li, Yun Lu, Yue Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough current evidence supports the effectiveness of antimicrobial stewardship (AMS), its sustained and effective implementation in tertiary hospitals remains challenging. This study aims to explore the facilitative and barrier factors influencing AMS implementation in a Chinese tertiary hospital, thereby providing insights to support the ongoing and effective promotion of AMS within tertiary healthcare institutions. MethodsBased on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), semi-structured interviews were conducted with 28 key stakeholders, including 15 physicians, 5 hospital managers, 4 pharmacists, 2 nurses, and 2 microbiologists. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged across at least two consecutive interviews. The interview data were analyzed using directed content analysis to systematically code and categorize factors. Textual data were coded and categorized with NVivo 15 software.ResultsThis study identified 34 facilitative factors, 41 barrier factors, and 1 mixed determinant across 30 CFIR constructs. The distribution of these factors across the CFIR domains is uneven, with a marked concentration observed within the inner setting domain. Key facilitative factors include the establishment of a closed-loop management system, national policy support, multidisciplinary collaboration, and integration of clinical pharmacists into clinical departments. In contrast, major barrier factors include limited clinical adaptability of AMS programs, ambiguous role definition, insufficient available resources, insufficient relative priority, lagging policy management, challenges in interdepartmental collaboration, limited role perception among nurses, and limited knowledge of antibacterial agents among frontline physicians.ConclusionBy synthesizing the identified facilitative and barrier factors, this study suggests that sustainable AMS advancement in tertiary hospitals requires multi-dimensional alignment. While national policy and institutional measures facilitate AMS implementation, its long-term delivery still faces multiple challenges. These findings identify key constraints and provide empirical evidence for optimizing AMS in similar healthcare settings to support its long-term success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13263-0.

Background

Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) represents a significant threat to human health and has emerged as one of the foremost challenges in global public health [1]. Projections indicate that by 2050, AMR could result in approximately 39 million deaths and incur economic losses amounting to up to 412 billion dollars annually [2]. The primary driver of AMR is the inappropriate use of antimicrobial agents [3–5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), during the Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, around 75% of hospitalized patients received antimicrobial treatment, yet only 8% genuinely required it [6]. In the United States, nearly half of patients suffering from respiratory tract infections were prescribed unnecessary antimicrobial medications [7]. In England, the rate of inappropriate use of antimicrobial agents for respiratory diseases reached an alarming 69.2% [8]. About 76% of patients in Africa received inappropriate antimicrobial treatments [9]. In China, it was found that 51.4% of outpatient prescriptions for antimicrobial agents were considered inappropriate [10].

Research suggests that Antimicrobial Stewardship (AMS) has been established as one of the most effective strategies for promoting rational use of antimicrobial agents while minimizing bacterial resistance without compromising patient safety [11, 12]. WHO defines AMS as a coherent set of integrated actions which promote the responsible and appropriate use of antimicrobial agents to help improve patient outcomes across the continuum of care [13]. To combat the threat of AMR, various countries and regions have progressively initiated AMS actions. For example, the European Union, the United States, and India have prioritized this critical issue by formulating national action strategies, creating national monitoring networks to track trends in drug resistance [14–16], and establishing dedicated working groups. Since 2011, China has also introduced and implemented several AMS actions in medical institutions [17]. However, AMS in China has not yet evolved into a sustainable and systematic operational framework. There is a lack of continuous professional capacity-building programs in healthcare institutions, as well as an absence of national professional bodies dedicated to promoting AMS, evaluating its implementation, and measuring outcomes. Furthermore, the execution of AMS initiatives and related professional education and training remain relatively weak [18]. A survey study reveals that among 116 randomly selected tertiary hospitals across China, the implementation of AMS programs has not been fully achieved, only 50% have adopted prescription pre-authorization, along with mechanisms for prescription review and feedback, while approximately 25.8% of these hospitals have not established a formal infection control department as required by national policy [19]. It is evident that AMS efforts in China still require transformation into sustained and effective practices at the hospital level.

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) serves as the most widely utilized theoretical framework within implementation science, enabling a comprehensive and systematic exploration of the factors influencing the implementation process of policies or interventions [20]. The framework was originally proposed by Damschroder et al. in 2009 (original CFIR) [21] and updated to CFIR 2.0 in 2022 [22]. CFIR integrates the core contents of multiple fields in implementation research, covering five dimensions: innovation, inner setting, outer setting, individuals, and implementation process. The new version of CFIR remains largely unchanged in the five core areas, with the main changes reflected in three aspects: the individuals domain is subdivided into two sub-domains, “roles” and “characteristics”, guiding statements are added for each domain to enhance operability, the number of constructs has expanded from 39 to 48, and 19 sub-group constructs have been added. Notably, each component in the new CFIR can be mapped to the original version, ensuring the consistency of framework application.

The multi-dimensional nature of CFIR makes it particularly suitable for analyzing the implementation issues of AMS programs, with its five domains precisely aligning with the multiple levels of policy, organization, team, and individual involved in the implementation of AMS in medical institutions. Therefore, this study, guided by the new version of CFIR framework, selected a tertiary hospital in Southwest China as the research site and adopted qualitative research methods to systematically identify and analyze key facilitative and barrier factors affecting the implementation of AMS. The study clarifies the multi-level factors influencing the implementation of AMS, aiming to provide empirical evidence for optimizing the implementation strategies of AMS in tertiary hospitals.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is part of a research project investigating the current status and influencing factors of AMS in a tertiary hospital in Southwest China. The project employs a mixed-methods design, and the present study represents its qualitative component. We used a qualitative descriptive design to systematically investigate factors influencing the implementation of AMS in a tertiary hospital. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders. The CFIR served as the main guiding framework throughout the research process, from interview protocol development to data coding and analysis. The quantitative component of the project analyzes antimicrobial use data to explore prescribing patterns and trends.

Participants and setting

In China, public hospitals are divided into three levels, each with three grades, and tertiary Grade A represents the highest level of medical services [23]. This study was conducted in a tertiary Grade A hospital located in Guizhou Province, Southwestern China—an economically less developed region compared to eastern and coastal areas, with relatively limited healthcare resources. The study hospital serves as a regional medical center with approximately 3,500 beds and is one of the highest-level medical institutions in the province. However, like many hospitals in less developed regions, this hospital also faces resource constraints. In terms of AMS implementation, the hospital has established an AMS committee led by the pharmacy department, with participation from medical administration, infection control, the microbiology laboratory, the nursing department, and clinical departments. The hospital operates under national antimicrobial stewardship policies and is subject to regular national assessments, which set specific antimicrobial use targets—including inpatient antimicrobial use intensity ≤ 40 DDDs per 100 bed-days, antimicrobial prescription rate ≤ 60%, and microbiological specimen submission rate for restricted-grade agents ≥ 80% [24].

Purposive sampling, guided by the principle of maximum variation [25], was employed to ensure diversity across key dimensions relevant to AMS implementation. The sample included heads of the medical administration department, the medical quality control department, and the hospital infection management department, physicians from departments with high antimicrobial usage, clinical pharmacists, as well as nurses and microbiologists from relevant departments. Sampling considered variations in professional titles and AMS participation roles (i.e., managers responsible for policy development and frontline implementers responsible for clinical execution).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) employment at the hospital for one year or more. (2) intermediate or senior professional title for physicians, pharmacists, nurses and microbiologists (no title restrictions applied to administrators). (3) willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria included: (1) being engaged in external training or assignments during the data collection period, as their absence from the hospital setting would limit their direct, contemporaneous experience with the hospital’s AMS implementation. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with informed and voluntary participants between June and July 2025. Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved—defined as the point at which no new themes emerged across at least two consecutive interviews [26].

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with healthcare providers (hospital manager, physician, nurse, pharmacist, and microbiologist) by one interviewer (JC) and two note-takers (YXY and XCL). The interview guide was initially developed based on the CFIR in Additional File (1). Prior to formal data collection, pilot interviews were conducted with one physician and one administrator to test and refine the guide. The final version of the interview guide was included in the Additional File (2). Data were collected through in-depth, one-on-one interviews with healthcare providers from the study hospital, which utilized a combination of online and face-to-face formats. Before conducting the interview, written informed consent was obtained from each participant. All interviews were conducted in a quiet and private setting and audio-recorded. Each interview lasted between 30 and 60 min. Verbatim transcription was completed within 24 h after each interview. Audio files were initially transcribed using iFlyTek software, followed by manual checking to ensure verbatim accuracy. Key points from each transcript were then verified with participants to prevent misinterpretation. All transcripts were anonymized using identifiers (M for hospital managers, N for nurse, MB for microbiologist, PY for physicians, and PR for pharmacists), retaining only the dialogue text for subsequent analysis. To ensure data accuracy and consistency, two researchers (YTF and YL) independently reviewed the transcripts.

Data analysis

This study employed directed content analysis [27], guided by the CFIR. The specific process is as follows: (1) Importing files: Transcribed interview texts were imported into NVivo 15 software (QSR International). The researcher then proofread the transcribed text data to ensure the accuracy of the data. (2) Building a coding framework based on CFIR: The coding manual obtained from the CFIR official website (https://cfirguide.org/) was used, and a node structure was created step by step based on the CFIR domains and their constituent elements, from parent nodes to child nodes and grandchild nodes. (3) Coding process: The researcher analyzed the text sentence by sentence, taking the key words and sentences in the interview content related to the above-mentioned node structure as the basic analysis units and coding them into the corresponding nodes of the CFIR framework. If some content was found to be unsuitable for the existing node structure during the coding process, adjustments were made and new nodes were added. (4) Theme rating: Each theme was classified as a facilitator, barrier, or mixed determinant based on its impact on AMS implementation. Facilitators were defined as factors that supported or enhanced implementation. Barriers were defined as factors that hindered or complicated it. Mixed determinants are defined as factors that exhibited both facilitative and inhibitive effects—promoting implementation under certain conditions while constraining it under others. The entire process was independently conducted by two researchers for coding and rating. In case of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion, and if no consensus was reached, a third expert was consulted. All code names, definitions, and ratings were discussed and finalized through research team meetings. It was ensured that the content of each node accurately reflected the domains of the CFIR framework.

All quoted excerpts underwent strict forward and backward translation: Professional translators first translated the Chinese quotes into English, and then independently back-translated them into Chinese for cross-verification. Each quote was only labeled with the participant’s role and study code (such as M1, PY1) to prevent identification.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team was led by Dr. YC, a qualitative research expert, and included one university researcher (JLY, female), five postgraduate students (JC, XCL, YXY, YL, YTF, fourth female, one male), and one senior pharmacist (ML, male). Prior to data collection, Dr. YC conducted systematic training for all team members, covering interview techniques and textual data management. All interviews were conducted by three researchers (JC, YXY, XCL) based on the semi-structured interview guide, with no prior contact between interviewers and participants. To foster an environment conducive to open and honest responses, several measures were implemented. Interviews were conducted in private, quiet settings with only the interviewer and participant present. Before each interview, researchers explicitly explained the study’s academic purpose, emphasizing that the data would be used solely for research, would remain strictly confidential and anonymized, and would not be linked to participants’ performance evaluations or professional standing. All participants completed the interview, and no participant withdrew from the study.

We recognized that researchers’ professional backgrounds and perspectives could influence both data collection and interpretation. To address this, the team held regular reflexive meetings and documented analytic memos to examine potential bias throughout the research process, alongside independent double-coding and consensus discussions to enhance analytic accuracy and reliability. This study strictly adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [28].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval Certificate No. 2025 (205)). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of the study, confirming their consent to participate. The research strictly adheres to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study is not a clinical trial. Before conducting interviews, researchers collected demographic information and verified the signed consent forms. To ensure privacy, all data were anonymized using unique identification codes to maintain confidentiality.

The influencing factors of the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship

Through CFIR-guided systematic coding, this study identified 34 facilitative factors, 41 barrier factors, and 1 mixed determinant across 30 CFIR constructs, spanning the innovation domain (5 constructs), outer setting domain (3 constructs), inner setting domain (10 constructs), individuals domain (6 constructs), and implementation process domain (6 constructs). Innovation characteristics

According to the CFIR framework, innovation refers to the “thing” being implemented. In this study, innovation specifically denotes the AMS programs and supporting measures developed by the hospital to address the threat of AMR, including core components such as the antimicrobial grading system, establishment of management assessment indicators, prescription review, and feedback mechanisms. Within this dimension, we identified 6 facilitators and 10 barriers spanning key constructs including innovation source, evidence-base, adaptability, complexity, and innovation design, as summarized in Table 2.

Facilitators

Participants reported that the hospital’s AMS management program was primarily developed based on national policies and requirements, which lent it credibility and authority. The core measures were described as maturity and operational simplicity, laying a solid foundation for subsequent implementation. As a manager and a pharmacist commented:

This antimicrobial management program has been in place for over a decade. The management program is highly mature and aligns with international best practices. (M4) The formulation of the hospital’s AMS management program is primarily based on policy documents issued by national health authorities—this is the most fundamental and important reference. (PR2)

This antimicrobial management program has been in place for over a decade. The management program is highly mature and aligns with international best practices. (M4)

The formulation of the hospital’s AMS management program is primarily based on policy documents issued by national health authorities—this is the most fundamental and important reference. (PR2)

Barriers

A significant barrier lies in the design limitations of the current hospital AMS program, with limited clinical adaptability emerging as the primary design constraint. Participants indicated that existing AMS requirements, measures, and assessment criteria do not fully align with clinical realities. As one physician pointed out:

It is reasonable to require pre-treatment microbiological specimen submission. However, it takes three to five days to obtain culture results after specimen submission. In many clinical situations, patients’ conditions do not allow such a long wait—this creates an inherent contradiction. (PY15)

It is reasonable to require pre-treatment microbiological specimen submission. However, it takes three to five days to obtain culture results after specimen submission. In many clinical situations, patients’ conditions do not allow such a long wait—this creates an inherent contradiction. (PY15)

Ambiguous role delineation represents another major design limitation that further undermines implementation effectiveness. Due to unclear boundaries of responsibility, managers, nurses, and pharmacists face challenges in effectively regulating and enforcing AMS measures, resulting in weakened oversight and reduced program efficiency. As one manager stated:

National regulations stipulate that preventive medication should not exceed 24 or 48 hours. If we strictly adhere to these guidelines, who will assume responsibility if a patient becomes infected? (M2)

National regulations stipulate that preventive medication should not exceed 24 or 48 hours. If we strictly adhere to these guidelines, who will assume responsibility if a patient becomes infected? (M2)

In addition, factors such as the complex and time-consuming expert consultation process for special-grade antimicrobial agents and the lack of credibility and authority in assessment criteria reflect shortcomings at the program design level, further exacerbating implementation difficulties.

Outer setting

Facilitators

Facilitators and barriers within the outer setting domain are summarized in Table 3. Most respondents reported that national policy support was a core facilitator driving AMS implementation. Closely related to this, the DDDs targets in the National Tertiary Public Hospital Performance Assessment, which prompted hospitals to integrate AMS indicators into daily management and continuous improvement. One physician explained the positive role of DDDs assessment:

Using DDDs quantitative assessment to control antimicrobial use serves two purposes: on one hand, it standardizes physicians’ prescribing practices and prevents harm to patients from prolonged indiscriminate use; on the other hand, DDDs indicators create horizontal comparisons between hospitals, which can promote continuous internal improvement. Ultimately, the goal is to make treatment more standardized. (PY11)

Using DDDs quantitative assessment to control antimicrobial use serves two purposes: on one hand, it standardizes physicians’ prescribing practices and prevents harm to patients from prolonged indiscriminate use; on the other hand, DDDs indicators create horizontal comparisons between hospitals, which can promote continuous internal improvement. Ultimately, the goal is to make treatment more standardized. (PY11)

Additionally, external communication and collaboration also supported AMS implementation. The hospital invited external experts for exchanges and dispatched staff to other institutions for learning, drawing on advanced experiences from other organizations. A manager provided an example:

Some infection prevention and control measures in our hospital, such as the fluorescent marking method, were developed after learning from exchanges with other hospitals. (M1)

Some infection prevention and control measures in our hospital, such as the fluorescent marking method, were developed after learning from exchanges with other hospitals. (M1)

Barriers

Lagging management policies were widely reported by respondents as a major barrier. One physician noted that existing regulatory requirements were disconnected from clinical realities:

The complexity and severity of infections among our patient population are far higher than in other comparable institutions, so the level and volume of antibiotic use are relatively high. When there are many critically ill patients, it’s really not feasible to fully comply with national requirements. (PY8)

The complexity and severity of infections among our patient population are far higher than in other comparable institutions, so the level and volume of antibiotic use are relatively high. When there are many critically ill patients, it’s really not feasible to fully comply with national requirements. (PY8)

This issue was also reflected in the antibacterial agents list. A pharmacist pointed out:

One major problem now is that the 50-agent limit for antimicrobial formularies is insufficient. Many new antimicrobial agents have emerged, but because the policy threshold hasn’t been adjusted and essential medicines cannot be phased out, these new drugs cannot be added to the hospital formulary. (PR1)

One major problem now is that the 50-agent limit for antimicrobial formularies is insufficient. Many new antimicrobial agents have emerged, but because the policy threshold hasn’t been adjusted and essential medicines cannot be phased out, these new drugs cannot be added to the hospital formulary. (PR1)

The overlapping policies and coordination difficulties were important external barriers to AMS implementation. A physician described the clinical decision-making dilemma under multiple assessment requirements:

National policy includes average length of hospital stay in hospital performance assessments. The hospital asks us to cut our length of stay. Our department used to be 10 days, and now we’re expected to get it down to 9. If we use lower-level antibiotics, patients might not get better for ten days or two weeks, and we’ll definitely go over the target. So sometimes we just have to start with higher-level antibiotics. (PY3)

Poor collaboration with external medical institutions was another barrier mentioned. Respondents indicated that some primary care institutions had inadequate antimicrobial use supervision, and patients referred upward from these facilities had often received inappropriate treatment. A physician noted:

Some grassroots or community hospitals overuse antibiotics—for instance, combining multiple antimicrobial agents for upper respiratory tract infections, which typically don’t require such treatment. (PY10)

Inner setting

Facilitators

Facilitators and barriers within the inner setting are summarized in Table 4. Respondents generally perceived multidisciplinary collaboration as a key facilitator for AMS implementation. The hospital established an AMS management team involving the pharmacy department, medical administration, infection control, clinical departments, and the microbiology laboratory. Regular consultations were conducted to enhance the precision of antimicrobial selection. As a pharmacist noted:

Hospital-wide consultations (multidisciplinary collaboration) significantly support the rational use of antibacterial agents, as specialists from various disciplines can provide targeted recommendations. (PR2) Information infrastructure also provided technical support for AMS implementation. The hospital adopted PDA (Personal Digital Assistant) scanning technology to enable traceability and control of medication administration processes, and introduced advanced diagnostic tools such as mNGS. A nurse and a microbiologist explained:

Hospital-wide consultations (multidisciplinary collaboration) significantly support the rational use of antibacterial agents, as specialists from various disciplines can provide targeted recommendations. (PR2)

When executing medication orders, we must scan with a PDA, and the time of administration is recorded to the minute. If an order is inappropriate, the system will not execute it. It also cannot be executed if there is a network issue or a problem with the medication source that prevents scanning. The hospital incorporated indicators such as DDDs and pre-treatment specimen submission rates into monthly medical quality evaluations, linking them directly to departmental performance. A pharmacist stated:

The monthly medical quality evaluation includes antimicrobial indicators, such as prescription audits and DDD target achievement. We set targets for each clinical department and deduct points based on the degree of deviation. The quality control score is directly linked to departmental performance. (PR2)

The issue of resistance is indeed severe, especially among ICU patients. We see a lot of resistant cases, and sometimes choosing an antibacterial agents is really difficult. We honestly don’t know which one to select. (PY2)

Barriers

Participants identified insufficient available resources and insufficient relative priority as the most prominent barriers to AMS program implementation in the hospital. This was primarily reflected in the limited human and time resources allocated to the AMS program, alongside low prioritization in resource allocation and implementation planning. As one pharmacist and a manager explained:

The hospital primarily focuses on physicians. The pharmacy department is often regarded as a cost center since it does not generate direct revenue, which restricts staffing levels. No hospital would turn down additional clinical pharmacists if given the opportunity. Discussion

This qualitative study was guided by the CFIR, systematically exploring the facilitative and barrier factors in the implementation process of the AMS program in a tertiary hospital in Southwest of China. We maintained continuous reflections on attitudes, assumptions, and positions during the interview processes and analyses to ensure our understanding authentically reflected participants’ perspectives. Based on the CFIR system coding, multiple-dimensional factors affecting the implementation of AMS were identified, covering five domains: innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individual characteristics, and implementation process. The main facilitative factors include program design based on national policies, a closed-loop management system integrating prescription authority assessment and real-time review, a multidisciplinary collaboration mechanism, and the deployment of clinical pharmacists. The barrier factors include limited clinical applicability of the AMS program, ambiguous role definition, insufficient available resources, inadequate relative priority, difficulties in cross-departmental collaboration, lagging policy updates, limited role perception among nurses, and insufficient knowledge acquisition by frontline physicians.

Innovation characteristics

Current AMS programs in this hospital are relatively mature and operational simplicity. However, their sustained advancement remains constrained by two core barriers: limited clinical adaptability and ambiguous role delineation. This finding contrasts with Hashad et al.‘s study in the United Arab Emirates, which identified the complexity of antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) themselves as a primary implementation barrier [29]. In the present study, the challenge does not stem from program complexity, but rather from the mismatch between program design and clinical context—specifically, within China’s hierarchical medical system, where patients are triaged based on disease severity and treatment difficulty across different levels of healthcare institutions [30]. Tertiary hospitals admit a higher proportion of critically ill patients, and existing AMS programs struggle to effectively adapt to their complex clinical profiles. The pre-treatment microbiological specimen submission rate, as a key AMS implementation indicator, can effectively improve treatment precision [31]. However, due to long culture cycles and delayed result feedback in this hospital, it often fails to provide timely decision-making support for initial treatment in critically ill patients, consistent with previous research [32]. Under assessment pressure, formalistic specimen submission occurs during implementation, deviating from the goal of rational drug use.

The second barrier factor concerns ambiguous role delineation within multidisciplinary teams. Consistent with prior studies, physicians—bearing primary accountability for patient safety—often adopt defensive prescribing behaviors, such as extending treatment durations or increasing dosages, to mitigate perceived risks of infection recurrence [29, 33]. However, due to the absence of formalized intervention authority and shared responsibility mechanisms in the program design, clinical pharmacists and administrative departments struggle to effectively regulate such practices. Nursing staff face similar role perception challenges. Although nurses have been formally integrated into the hospital’s AMS framework, this study found that some nurses continue to perceive themselves primarily as “order executors” with limited awareness of their responsibilities in medication communication and therapeutic feedback. Yet international evidence suggests that nurses play an active role in antimicrobial stewardship by influencing prescribing practices, advocating for timely patient treatment, and safeguarding patient safety [34]. When nurses’ understanding of their role remains incomplete, their capacity to generate positive impact is correspondingly diminished.

Based on the above findings, two optimization strategies are proposed from a program design perspective. First, to address limited clinical adaptability, the hospital could introduce rapid pathogen detection technologies to shorten testing turnaround times and provide timely decision support for critically ill patients. Additionally, establishing exemption pathways for exceptional clinical scenarios would enhance program flexibility. Second, to address ambiguous role delineation, the responsibilities and decision-making boundaries of each professional group within the multidisciplinary team should be explicitly defined through program design. For physicians, the scope of prescribing autonomy should be clarified—specifying under what circumstances deviation from standard protocols is clinically justified and how such decisions should be documented and reviewed. For clinical pharmacists, institutionalizing risk-sharing agreements would formally authorize them to adjust prescriptions under defined conditions, transforming intervention from informal consultation into structured practice. For nurses, the program design should articulate their roles in medication monitoring, therapeutic feedback, and specimen collection quality control, transitioning them from passive “order executors” to active participants in the antimicrobial management loop. For administrators and infection control departments, their responsibilities in implementation oversight, cross-departmental coordination, and policy enforcement should be clearly defined—including authority to intervene in inappropriate prescribing, mechanisms for addressing implementation gaps, and accountability for facilitating multidisciplinary collaboration. For microbiologist, their role in ensuring diagnostic quality, timeliness of result reporting, and clinical communication should be specified. Through these design-level clarifications, clear accountability boundaries across all professional groups can be established, enabling authentic multidisciplinary collaboration and maximizing the unique contributions of each discipline, thereby enhancing AMS implementation effectiveness.

Outer setting

At the outer setting level, national policy support constitutes a fundamental driver for AMS programs implementation. Nevertheless, lagging policy management presents significant impediments. Although national health authorities have established clear benchmarks—such as limiting the variety of antimicrobial agents procured by tertiary general hospitals to no more than 50 varieties—these regulations increasingly demonstrate inflexibility in dynamic clinical contexts [24]. As microbial resistance patterns evolve and novel antibacterial agents emerge, hospitals face difficulties in updating varieties due to fixed upper limits, preventing timely inclusion of clinically valuable new drugs and thereby constraining therapeutic options for complex infections [2, 35]. Additionally, poor collaboration with external medical institutions was identified as a factor constraining AMS implementation. Some participants noted that coordination with primary healthcare institutions was suboptimal: patients referred from lower-level hospitals often had a history of inappropriate antimicrobial use, which created challenges for subsequent antimicrobial management in the tertiary hospital and, to some extent, disrupted the continuity of AMS. Similar issues have been raised in the existing literature. Alhassan et al. [36] highlighted that there are notable deficiencies in information transfer and collaborative linkages between different healthcare institutions, with fragmented communication during interfacility transfers being a systemic driver of antimicrobial resistance transmission. Ellingson et al. [37] also identified deficiencies in cross-institutional collaboration mechanisms: although most hospitals had established interfacility transfer communication protocols, standardization of communication remained inadequate in practice, with fewer than half consistently sharing critical information with receiving institutions.

Inner setting

Within the inner setting, insufficient available resources and insufficient relative priority emerged as two interconnected barriers to AMS implementation. The low priority afforded to AMS was reflected in the limited allocation of human and time resources. Specifically, the hospital tends to prioritize clinical departments, while pharmacy departments are positioned as “cost centers” within the current operational system, receiving limited human resource investment. Meanwhile, AMS team members typically fulfill dual clinical and managerial roles, leaving limited capacity and time for dedicated AMS programs. This reflects that AMS has not yet been accorded sufficient priority within the hospital’s overall strategy and daily operations [38]. Compared with the study by Kapatsa et al. [39] in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the two contexts exhibit different patterns of resource constraints. In SSA, the challenge lies in fundamental resource scarcity, where even basic laboratory diagnostic capacity and drug supplies are difficult to secure. In contrast, resource constraints in this study manifest primarily as insufficient prioritization. Insufficient institutional support in terms of resources and time may erode healthcare professionals’ motivation and long-term engagement in stewardship initiatives [40, 41]. The underlying reason is that the long-term public health benefits and social value contributed by AMS are difficult to fully measure and reflect within the existing evaluation and compensation systems, leading to a decision-making logic oriented toward short-term clinical benefits often overriding the long-term goals of AMS [42, 43]. This systemic bias in value assessment further permeates the prescribing behavior of individual physicians. As shown by Broom et al., physicians’ perception of direct clinical risk to individual patients often dominates their prescribing decisions, rather than population-based long-term resistance risks. This is particularly evident in certain specialties (e.g., surgery, respiratory, and hematology), resulting in AMS not being sufficiently prioritized within specialty-specific clinical agendas [44]. At the level of interdepartmental collaboration, while multidisciplinary teams have been formally established, meaningful cooperation continues to face deep-seated structural challenges. Clinical departments typically dominate therapeutic decision-making, whereas supportive units such as pharmacy, nursing and infection control occupy subordinate roles. This asymmetry in authority and professional influence can give rise to communication barriers and collaborative inefficiencies, which is consistent with the findings of Wang Xiaomin et al. in China [42].

It is therefore recommended that the hospital elevate AMS to a core component of institutional quality and patient safety strategy. The hospital leadership must increase its involvement by demonstrating both strategic commitment and active participation in AMS programs. Beyond symbolic endorsement, senior executives should integrate AMS performance reviews into routine management practices, such as hospital-wide ward rounds and medical quality control assurance meetings, thereby transforming policy commitments into tangible governance actions. Second, dedicated positions and protected time should be allocated to AMS team members. Building upon current management structures, the hospital should strengthen administrative incentives by increasing the weight of AMS-related indicators in performance evaluations and closely linking these metrics to departmental funding allocations and individual physicians’ career progression, including promotion and professional title assessments. Furthermore, a standardized, systematized interdepartmental collaboration process should be established, with clearly defined response timelines and role responsibilities for key AMS programs. Regular cross-disciplinary seminars should be institutionalized to foster shared understanding through case-based discussions and locally generated data, promoting a common clinical language and reducing professional silos and implementation resistance. Finally, the hospital HIS should be further optimized to enable real-time monitoring and intelligent regulation of antibiotic use, ensuring greater accuracy, appropriateness, and efficiency in prescribing practices.

Characteristics of individuals

Regarding individual characteristics, physicians’ strong sense of prescribing autonomy, limited knowledge of antibacterial agents, and entrenched prescribing habits constitute major barriers. Research indicates that antimicrobial prescription decisions largely depend on physicians’ existing knowledge, training, and accumulated experience [45, 46]. Some frontline physicians often lack sufficient understanding knowledge of antibacterial agents and relevant guidelines, which significantly impacts prescription rationality. Under conditions of knowledge deficit, physicians tend to default to familiar antibiotics based on habitual patterns, without rigorously assessing alignment with current susceptibility profiles or clinical indications [47]. These ingrained behaviors are resistant to rapid change [48, 49]. Additionally, a strong sense of prescribing autonomy further hinders AMS program implementation. In actual practice, physicians typically value their professional autonomy, preferring to choose medications based on personal clinical judgment, leading to neglect of treatment guidelines and AMS recommendations. Although knowledge enhancement alone does not guarantee behavioral modification, educational interventions tailored to local prescribing contexts and incorporating active behavior change strategies show greater potential to reshape attitudes and improve prescribing practices [50]. It is therefore recommended to comprehensively upgrade the antimicrobial knowledge base of frontline physicians and address maladaptive prescribing patterns. This can be achieved by refining institutional training frameworks—retaining regular sessions while leveraging online platforms, case-based learning, and interactive workshops to increase relevance and applicability. Additionally, strengthening clinical decision support through the establishment of regional antimicrobial resistance surveillance networks and bacterial spectrum databases can provide evidence-based prescribing references [32].

Notably, patient-related factors also pose significant barriers to AMS implementation, primarily manifested as patients’ inappropriate medication demands and poor patient compliance. Some respondents noted that patients may hold misconceptions about antimicrobial agents and may pressure physicians to prescribe specific agents without clinical justification, thereby undermining AMS effectiveness [51, 52]. Additionally, patient acceptance of microbiological testing remains low, particularly for invasive procedures such as blood draws. For self-collected specimens like sputum or urine, patients often fail to adhere to collection instructions, resulting in decreased submission rates and compromised diagnostic accuracy, which may lead to inappropriate antimicrobial use [53]. International literature indicates that by enhancing patients’ understanding of appropriate antibiotic use and related risks, physicians and patients can jointly form consistent treatment expectations and improve the implementation of AMS [54]. Therefore, it is recommended to guide patient expectations appropriately, and strengthen structured patient medication education. Visual educational materials and medication guidance discussions can help patients understand the principles of rational antimicrobial use, reduce inappropriate requests driven by cognitive biases, and encourage them to provide valuable medical history information during consultations, fostering positive interaction.

Implementation processes

In terms of implementation processes, implementation gaps, limited implementation effectiveness, and weakened management capacity exist in bridging AMS programs and actual clinical practice, a finding consistent with prior research [38]. Notably, the ongoing pilot program for ward-based clinical pharmacists at the sampled hospital offers a promising partial solution. These embedded pharmacists participate in daily rounds, deliver individualized medication counseling, monitor antibiotic use, provide feedback, and conduct targeted training. Interview data indicate that these interventions have significantly reduced inappropriate prescribing in pilot clinical departments. Research by Anup et al. shows that clinical pharmacist-led AMS interventions can significantly reduce antibiotic prescriptions, improve policy compliance, and decrease treatment days with restricted antibacterial agents [55]. However, a systematic review points out that the current shortage of human resources is a common barrier to AMS implementation [56]. This study finds that within the existing operational systems of Chinese hospitals, the root of this barrier lies in the relatively low priority of pharmacy departments in resource allocation. Thus, at the hospital level, it is recommended to progressively expand the deployment of clinical pharmacists to departments with high antimicrobial consumption, optimizing resource utilization under current constraints. Concurrently, accelerating the development of the clinical pharmacist workforce is crucial. Internal job retraining programs and direct recruitment mechanisms should be explored by the hospital to increase staffing levels, provided regulatory frameworks permit.

Dynamic interactions across CFIR domains

The factors discussed above are not independent but interrelated, and collectively shape implementation outcomes.

National policies provide the foundation for hospital AMS program design. However, delays in policy updates—such as the slow revision of antimicrobial formularies and restricted access to new antibiotics—result in AMS programs with limited clinical adaptability. When these programs are implemented in tertiary hospitals that care for a higher proportion of critically ill patients, a misalignment emerges between policy requirements and clinical needs. Simultaneously, the professional culture that prioritizes patient safety leads clinicians to favor patient safety when policy requirements conflict with patient interests. Consequently, some physicians gradually develop prescribing behaviors such as extending treatment durations or increasing dosages. Meanwhile, due to their limited managerial authority, managers and clinical pharmacists exhibit weakened oversight and fail to effectively regulate physicians’ prescribing practices. Nurses—due to their limited role perception in AMS—demonstrate low initiative in participating in medication communication and fail to fully exert their influence on physician prescribing. These factors collectively hinder the implementation of management measures, ultimately resulting in execution deviations. Such deviations do not stem from resistance to management objectives, but from the misalignment among policy updates, program design, clinical needs, and individual perceptions. When these elements are misaligned, implementation effectiveness is inevitably compromised.

Conclusion

Systematically advancing AMS programs is widely recognized as an essential strategy for promoting rational antimicrobial use and mitigating the global threat of bacterial resistance. This study, grounded in the CFIR, conducted a systematic analysis of key factors influencing the implementation of AMS in a Chinese tertiary hospital. Findings indicate that while national policy support and institutional measures—such as the clinical pharmacists in clinical department—have yielded positive outcomes, the implementation process continues to encounter significant barriers. These include limited clinical adaptability, ambiguous role delineation, insufficient available resources, insufficient relative priority, limited knowledge of antibacterial agents and behavioral inertia among healthcare providers regarding appropriate antibacterial agents use, inefficiencies in interdepartmental collaboration, and limited role perception among nurses. These insights not only identify critical challenges hindering the effective and sustained implementation of AMS in this setting but also offer empirical evidence to inform AMS optimization efforts in regions with comparable healthcare systems and socioeconomic conditions, thereby supporting the long-term success of stewardship initiatives.

Limitations

Several limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings and their transferability, especially considering the heterogeneity of healthcare systems across China. First, as this study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in Guizhou Province—an economically less developed region of China—the identified barriers and facilitators may reflect institution-specific factors such as staffing levels, information systems, and organizational routines shaped by local resource constraints. Thus, the transferability of our findings is greater for tertiary hospitals in similar resource-limited contexts, particularly in less developed regions. Caution is needed when extrapolating to well-resourced tertiary hospitals in economically developed areas, or to primary care and community settings. Future multi-center studies across diverse regions and hospital types are needed to validate these findings and to better understand how contextual factors shape AMS implementation in Chinese diverse healthcare system. Integrating qualitative insights with quantitative data (e.g., prescription and chart reviews) would further strengthen the evidence base and support robust data triangulation.

Second, potential limitations related to researcher roles and data collection should be acknowledged. Although all interviews were conducted by external researchers with no prior relationship to participants, the hospital setting itself may have influenced responses. Within their professional environment, they may have been more conscious of their institutional roles and workplace hierarchies. This could have led them to moderate critical comments or offer socially acceptable answers when discussing sensitive topics such as management practices. To mitigate these issues, we emphasized strict data confidentiality and anonymity, explicitly stating that responses would not be linked to performance evaluations. Despite these efforts, the potential impacts of social desirability bias could not be fully eliminated.
