Prevalence of Capitellar Osteochondritis Dissecans and Medial Epicondylar Abnormalities in Female Youth Baseball Players: A Large-Scale Ultrasonographic Screening Study
Authors: Masaru Munemori, Daisuke Momma, Kyosuke Numaguchi, Keiko Goto, Eiji Kondo, Nobuo Adachi, Norimasa Iwasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Capitellar osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) and medial epicondylar apophyseal abnormalities are recognized throwing-related elbow injuries in youth baseball. However, most epidemiological data have been derived from male athletes and the prevalence of these conditions among female youth baseball players has not been well characterized.Purpose:To determine the prevalence of capitellar OCD and medial epicondylar abnormalities among female youth baseball players using large-scale ultrasonographic screening data and to compare these findings with those of male players.Study Design:Cross-sectional study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:The authors analyzed a prospectively collected regional screening database of 13,483 youth baseball players (13,130 male, 353 female) collected between 2014 and 2025. Standardized field-based ultrasonography was used to assess the capitellum and medial epicondyle. Player position and arm dominance were recorded. Sex-based prevalence and position-related differences (female subgroup) were compared using prespecified tests.Results:Capitellar OCD was identified in 2.4% of male players (313/13,130) and 0.57% of females (2/353), showing no significant sex-based difference (P = .09). The prevalence of medial epicondylar abnormalities was 24.0% in male players (3147/13,130) and 16.4% in female players (58/353; P < .001). In female athletes, all cases involved the dominant arm, and prevalence was higher among pitchers/catchers (28.9%) than among infielders/outfielders (11.6%) (P < .01).Conclusion:Capitellar OCD was observed in female youth baseball players, with no significant difference in prevalence compared with male players. Medial epicondylar abnormalities occurred at a lower rate in female players, but were still observed, particularly among pitchers and catchers and exclusively in the dominant throwing arm. These findings indicate that female athletes are also at risk for throwing-related elbow conditions and support the inclusion of female players in routine screening and workload management programs.Keywords: osteochondritis dissecans, elbow, ultrasonography, screening, baseball, female athletes

Throwing-related elbow injuries, particularly capitellar osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) and medial epicondylar apophyseal abnormalities, are recognized concerns in youth baseball and are associated with repetitive valgus loading across the immature elbow.12,21 Early detection is important because delayed recognition may result in prolongation of symptoms or long-term functional limitations.6,23

While large-scale epidemiological and ultrasonographic studies have been conducted in youth baseball populations, the majority of available data are based on male players because of their larger representation in competitive baseball and research cohorts.9,12,15,20,21 Although female participation in baseball remains lower than male participation, female youth baseball players represent an established but understudied athletic population, and data describing elbow injury prevalence in this group remain limited.4,19,22

Previous biomechanical and developmental studies have suggested that female athletes may differ from their male counterparts in throwing exposure, training environment, and upper-extremity strength characteristics, which could influence valgus loading patterns and skeletal adaptation during growth.3,13 Therefore, whether elbow injury patterns established in male athletes can be directly generalized to female players remains unclear, and sex-specific epidemiological data are needed.

The purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence of capitellar OCD and medial epicondylar abnormalities in female youth baseball players using a large-scale ultrasonographic screening program, and to compare these findings with those of male players. We hypothesized that female players would demonstrate lower prevalences of both conditions compared with male athletes, reflecting differences in throwing load and skeletal response.

Methods

Study Population

This cross-sectional study analyzed data from a large regional youth baseball elbow screening program conducted annually between 2014 and 2025. The program included field-based ultrasonographic screening of the elbow for youth baseball players across multiple sites within the region. A total of 13,483 players (13,130 male and 353 female) aged 8 to 18 years were evaluated. The recorded age represented the age at the time of screening rather than at the onset of any lesion. Age distribution data were available for all 353 female players, with a mean age of 13.02 ± 2.53 years (range, 8-18 years). Individual-level age data for male players were not consistently available across all screening years in the registry used for this analysis. The inclusion criteria were all elementary, junior high, and high school baseball players who belonged to a school or club baseball team and participated in the official screening during the study period. The screening cohort did not include softball players. Players with incomplete ultrasonographic data or unclear diagnostic findings were excluded.

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents or guardians prior to participation.

Ultrasonographic Evaluation

Bilateral elbow ultrasonography (US) was performed by 3 orthopaedic surgeons (M.M., D.M., and K.N.) specializing in upper extremity and sports medicine, using a 10-MHz linear transducer (Viamo sv7; Canon), following previously established field-based screening protocols.2,20,21 Participants were examined in a seated position. For the anterior view, the elbow was fully extended to evaluate the humeral capitellum. For the posteromedial view, the posterior capitellum was assessed with the elbow in full flexion, and the medial epicondyle was evaluated with the elbow flexed to approximately 90°.

Capitellar OCD was defined as irregularity or discontinuity of the subchondral bone surface on ultrasonography obtained from the anterior and posterior views, consistent with validated US-based screening and imaging studies among adolescent baseball players.6,12,23 Medial epicondylar abnormalities were defined as traction-related apophyseal changes and classified as either apophyseal protrusion or physeal fragmentation/separation.9,15,16 For the purposes of epidemiological analysis in this large screening cohort, these subtypes were collectively analyzed as “medial epicondylar abnormalities,” as detailed subtype information was not uniformly obtainable across all participants.

For each participant, the presence or absence of OCD and medial epicondylar abnormalities was recorded. Additional information, including age, screening year, playing position, and throwing side or handedness (partially missing), was collected for female players.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the prevalence of capitellar OCD and medial epicondylar abnormalities and the comparison of these prevalence rates between male and female players. Secondary analyses in female players included descriptive assessment of lesion involvement relative to the dominant throwing arm and position-based differences in the prevalence of medial epicondylar abnormalities.

Statistical Analysis

Prevalence estimates were calculated with corresponding 95% CIs using the Wilson method. Between-sex comparisons were assessed using the chi-square test, with Fisher exact test applied for OCD because of the small number of affected female cases. Prevalence ratios (female vs male), along with 95% CIs, were calculated to quantify sex-based differences in lesion rates. A 2-sided P value of <.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using EZR Version 1.52 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical interface for R (Version 4.0.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). 7

Results

Sex-Based Comparison of Prevalence

A total of 13,483 youth baseball players were included in the final analysis after exclusion of cases with incomplete ultrasonographic data (13,130 male, 353 female). Capitellar OCD was identified in 315 players (2.3%). The prevalence of OCD was 2.4% in male players (313/13,130) and 0.6% in female players (2/353). This difference was not significant (P = .09). Medial epicondylar abnormalities were identified in 3205 players (23.8%). The prevalence was significantly higher in male players (24.0%; 3147/13,130) than in female players (16.4%; 58/353) (P < .001) (Table 1).

Position-Based Differences in Female Players

Data on playing position were available for 270 of 353 female players (Table 3). The prevalence of medial epicondylar abnormalities was 26.9% (21/78) in pitchers, 36.8% (7/19) in catchers, 14.3% (12/84) in infielders, and 9.0% (8/89) in outfielders. Grouped by throwing load–related positions, pitchers and catchers demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence (28.9%; 28/97) compared with infielders and outfielders (11.6%; 20/173) (P < .01). Capitellar OCD was uncommon across all playing positions, occurring in 1.3% of pitchers, 0% of catchers, 0% of infielders, and 1.1% of outfielders. These findings indicate that medial epicondylar abnormalities are more common in players with greater repetitive valgus stress exposure.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the prevalence of capitellar OCD and medial epicondylar abnormalities in female youth baseball players based on a large-scale, standardized ultrasonographic screening program. We analyzed screening data from 13,483 youth players, including 353 female athletes, and clarified the prevalence of elbow abnormalities in female players. Capitellar OCD was identified in 0.6% of female athletes (2/353), demonstrating that the condition occurs in female players, although at a lower frequency than in their male counterparts, and is clearly not exclusive to male athletes. Medial epicondylar abnormalities were observed in 16.4% of female athletes (58/353), particularly among pitchers and catchers. All medial epicondylar abnormalities involved the dominant throwing arm, consistent with a load-related pattern of occurrence.

Large-scale ultrasonographic screening studies in youth baseball have primarily focused on male athletes, reflecting the higher male participation in organized baseball programs.11,15 Capitellar OCD prevalence in male youth cohorts has generally been reported as approximately 1.4% to 3.4% depending on age range and screening methodology.8,11,12,15 In the present study, capitellar OCD was observed in female players, indicating that female baseball players are not exempt from this condition. Medial epicondylar abnormalities have been widely reported among male youth baseball players, particularly in pitchers and catchers, due to repetitive valgus loading of the elbow during throwing. 15 Our findings similarly demonstrated that medial epicondylar abnormalities in female athletes were concentrated in pitchers and catchers and exclusively affected the dominant arm, supporting a load-dependent injury mechanism that appears consistent across sexes.5,10,14

Sex-based biomechanical differences may help explain the lower overall prevalence of medial epicondylar abnormalities among female athletes. Female pitchers have been reported to demonstrate lower ball velocity and lower elbow varus moment compared with male pitchers, suggesting reduced overall valgus loading demands during pitching. 13 In addition, the coordination of pelvic and trunk rotation has been shown to influence how throwing forces are transmitted through the upper limb. 17 Studies of female pitchers have further noted subtle differences in pelvic–thoracic rotation patterns compared with males, indicating variations in how the kinetic chain is utilized during pitching. 1 Taken together, these observations suggest that female athletes may generally adopt pitching mechanics that result in lower cumulative medial elbow stress, even though the fundamental throwing motion and injury mechanism are shared across sexes. Differences in throwing exposure may also contribute to the observed sex-based disparity. Although detailed workload surveillance in female baseball remains limited, female youth baseball players often train and compete in settings with fewer high-intensity pitching opportunities compared with male athletes. 22 However, increased pitch volume and throwing velocity are well-established predictors of medial elbow injury in youth baseball, regardless of sex.10,23 Therefore, when female pitchers assume primary pitching or catching roles and accumulate greater throwing loads, the risk of medial elbow injury likely increases in a manner similar to that observed in male athletes. Thus, the mechanism of injury appears to be conserved across sexes, with the magnitude of exposure (rather than the nature of the motion) likely accounting for the difference in prevalence. In addition, earlier skeletal maturation in girls (earlier appearance/fusion of elbow ossification centers) may shorten the period of physeal vulnerability, offering a complementary explanation for the lower prevalence observed in female athletes. 18

These findings have direct clinical relevance. Capitellar OCD and medial epicondylar abnormalities were identified in female youth baseball players, particularly among pitchers and catchers, who experience greater throwing loads, indicating that female athletes should not be considered at inherently lower risk. Routine ultrasonographic screening and workload management strategies (eg, pitch count monitoring, scheduled rest, and instruction in efficient throwing mechanics) should thus be considered in female baseball programs, particularly for players in high-exposure positions. The large sample size and standardized screening protocol used in this study strengthen the reliability of these observations and provide a population-based foundation for developing sex-informed prevention and training strategies. Rather than assuming that female athletes are protected from valgus stress–related elbow injuries, clinicians and coaches should recognize that the underlying mechanism is conserved across sexes and that injury risk is primarily determined by cumulative load and positional demands.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, individual player identifiers were unavailable, preventing confirmation of repeated participation across screening years. Second, detailed workload factors (eg, pitch count, inning volume, competitive level, and cumulative pitching experience) were not collected, limiting the ability to assess dose-response relationships. Third, ultrasonography allows the detection of morphological abnormalities but does not evaluate symptoms or functional status, so the clinical significance of some findings remains uncertain. Fourth, interobserver and intraobserver reliability for ultrasonographic interpretation was not assessed in this screening database. Although examinations were performed by orthopaedic surgeons using a previously established field-based screening protocol, variability in image acquisition and interpretation may have influenced diagnostic classification. Fifth, interpretation of age-related patterns requires caution: the 2 OCD cases were newly identified at screening but almost certainly represented preexisting lesions rather than new onset at 15 and 17 years old. In addition, although girls typically reach skeletal maturity earlier than boys, 18 the maturational stage could not be adjusted for because individual age data were unavailable for male players. Finally, this was a cross-sectional study, and causal inferences cannot be made. Prospective studies with workload monitoring and longitudinal follow-up are needed to clarify progression and clinical relevance.

Conclusion

Capitellar OCD was observed in female youth baseball players, with no significant difference in prevalence compared with male players. Medial epicondylar abnormalities were less frequent in female players but were observed particularly among pitchers and catchers and exclusively on the dominant throwing arm. These findings indicate that female baseball players are also at risk for throwing-related elbow conditions and support their inclusion in routine screening and workload management programs.
