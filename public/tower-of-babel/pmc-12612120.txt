Discovery of a novel small molecule degrader of wild type and mutant estrogen receptors using DNA encoded libraries
Authors: Anil Kumar Devakrishnan, Chandrashekhar Madasu, Yong Wang, Ramkumar Modukuri, Kurt M Bohren, Kevin R MacKenzie, Damian W Young, Suzanne A W Fuqua, Martin M Matzuk, Murugesan Palaniappan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstrogen receptor α (ERα) variants with mutations in the ligand binding domain (LBD) are less sensitive than wild type to standard-of-care drugs that bind ERα directly. To identify novel small-molecule drugs that target ERα mutants, we screened our multibillion-compound DNA-encoded libraries against ERα LBD variants. CDD-1274, which was highly enriched with all three variants, blocked spontaneous coactivator peptide recruitment to mutant ERα LBDs and inhibited estradiol-driven proliferative markers in several ER-positive breast cancer cell lines, but not in ER-negative breast cancer cells. We demonstrated that CDD-1274 induced proteasomal degradation of ERα variants in breast cancer cell lines and caused Y537S ERα degradation more effectively than elacestrant in a palbociclib-resistant cell line. These findings establish that CDD-1274 potently blocks ligand-dependent and ligand-independent ER signaling in endocrine-resistant breast cancer cells and could be further optimized for developing a new class of ERα degraders for endocrine therapy-resistant breast cancer.Subject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery

Introduction

Estrogen receptor α (ERα) is an important therapeutic target in 70% of ER-positive breast cancers1. Patients with ERα-positive breast cancer typically respond well to endocrine therapy, which attenuates ERα signaling either by blocking the production of estrogens with aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole and letrozole), or by blocking the activity of estrogens via competitive binding of ERα antagonists such as tamoxifen or fulvestrant (a selective ERα degrader, SERD)2–6. Although estrogen-disrupting therapies are effective in both adjuvant and metastatic settings, patients often relapse after prolonged endocrine therapy, which remains a major clinical problem7,8. Numerous mechanisms of endocrine resistance have been identified, including altered activity of ERα coactivators, loss of ERα expression, and growth factor receptor-driven mitogenic pathways9–16. However, recent large-scale genomic sequencing has also shown that activating somatic mutations in the ERα gene (ESR1) that map to the ligand-binding domain (LBD) are frequently found in endocrine-resistant metastatic breast tumors but rarely in primary breast tumors17–21. Of these, Y537S and D538G mutations are found in (~30%) patients with metastatic breast cancer22.

The effects of Y537S and D538G mutations on ERα activity have been extensively investigated in biochemical, biophysical, cell-based, and xenograft studies17,19,23–25. These gain-of-function mutations cause ligand-independent ERα activation, which produces high transcriptional activity, even in the absence of estradiol17,19. Structural studies have shown that Y537S stabilizes an agonist conformation in the absence of estradiol by forming a hydrogen bond between S537 and D351, which cannot be formed in WT ERα23,26. These mutations also weaken the binding of the ERα antagonists tamoxifen and fulvestrant, which allows cells to resist these drugs22,27. As a result, these mutations constantly induce transcriptional gene activities that are linked to aggressive diseases24,28 and are directly implicated in the dysregulation of ERα signaling that affects disease pathology. These findings emphasize the need for novel antagonists or SERDs that can effectively block the signaling by mutant ERα. Recently, elacestrant has been approved by the FDA for the management of advanced breast cancer, including ESR1 mutants with disease progression following more than one line of endocrine therapy29. Elacestrant is the first orally available small-molecule drug in the SERD class29. Several SERDs are also in various stages of clinical development3,30 but selective, potent chemical scaffolds for antagonizing or degrading constitutively active mutant ERα remain elusive.

The DNA-encoded library (DEL) platform uses combinatorial chemical synthesis and next-generation DNA sequencing to provide high-throughput, cost-effective affinity-based hit discovery31–35. Small molecules are tagged with unique DNA sequences that encode the structure of each small molecule and serve as molecular “barcodes” to allow the identification of ‘hits’ that enrich in affinity selection pulldowns with targets33,36–39. Billions of drug-like small molecules can be simultaneously screened as a single mixture to bind to a protein target of interest, and hits can be identified by reading the output of DNA sequencing. Hits are then synthesized from DNA and validated by biochemical and functional studies40–44. DEL selection technology has discovered novel, potent, and selective compounds for various target classes33,40,42,43,45–48. Here, we applied our collection of several billion DEL compounds to identify the chemical entities that bind to both wild-type and mutant ERα LBDs. Our large-scale small-molecule screen discovers a novel class of ERα degrader that disables both WT and drug-resistant mutant ERα signaling in breast cancer cells. We further demonstrated that CDD-1274 induces WT and Y537S ERα degradation through the 26S proteasome pathway. More importantly, our small molecule (CDD-1274) effectively degrades Y537S ERα in a palbociclib-resistant cell line model in which elacestrant fails to significantly reduce Y537S ERα levels. Collectively, these data suggest that CDD-1274 is a promising tool compound for further development into drug-like properties for preclinical and clinical studies.

Results

Affinity selection of DNA-encoded chemical libraries against WT and mutant ERα LBDs

To screen our large in-house DELs, we expressed human WT ERα LBD (298-544aa) and the Y537S or D538G mutants along with an N-terminal 6His tag followed by a TEV cleavage site in a bacterial expression system25. The soluble proteins were purified using high-performance immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), anion exchange, and size exclusion chromatography (SEC) to obtain more than 90% pure WT, Y537S and D538G LBDs (Supplementary Fig. S1A). To test whether our purified proteins (WT, Y537S, and D538G LBDs of ERα) are active in a biochemical assay, we employed a coactivator recruitment assay using fluorescein-labeled SRC3 coactivator peptide. Unlike WT, Y537S and D538G ERα LBDs spontaneously recruited SRC3 peptide in the absence of estradiol (E2), and treatment with E2 further enhanced coactivator recruitment. However, in the WT ERα LBD, coactivator recruitment occurred only in the presence of E2 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). These biochemical assays established that our purified ERα LBD proteins are highly active, a prerequisite for our DEL screening process. Our biochemical data are consistent with those of other published studies23,25,49. Next, we performed a capture test with our purified proteins because the His tag of the target is used to pull down the protein during the affinity-based DEL selection process. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1C–E, 25 μl of Ni-NTA magnetic beads successfully captured all proteins from WT, Y537S, and D538G LBDs, which is also a prerequisite for DEL campaigns.

To identify potent and novel chemical matters that preferentially bind to LBDs of ERα variants, we used a DEL platform that contains more than 4.3 billion drug-like small molecules35,41,50,51. A pool of 31 DELs encompassing more than 4.39 billion unique small molecules was used in the screen against various forms of the ERα LBDs, including apo WT, WT + E2, apo Y537S, and apo D538G. His-tagged human ERα LBD variants were incubated with the library pool for 45 min before targets (any bound hits) were pulled down by their affinity tags using His-select nickel affinity resin. The complexes were washed to eliminate nonbinders, and bound DEL molecules were eluted by heat denaturation of the protein. This affinity selection process was repeated using the eluted library members and fresh proteins to further enrich the small molecule binder population. An independent affinity selection was also carried out in parallel without any target to serve as a non-target control (NTC) to identify any binders that enrich with the nickel affinity resin. After four rounds of selection, the final eluted fraction was quantified by qPCR using the library forward and reverse primers. The enriched molecules were PCR amplified using Illumina P5 and P7 adapters and then sequenced to determine the identities of the small molecule binders (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Selection data analysis and hit identification

The significance of DEL hit enrichment was determined by a normalized Z-score metric calculated from sequencing read counts as previously described by our group52. Many hits from several DELs were very strongly enriched, showing counts >1000 and Z-scores > 50, consistent with the deep, ‘druggable’ ERα binding pocket. Most of the high Z-score hits with WT ERα LBD were depleted in the selection that included E2, suggesting that they compete with the native ligand. Many hits discriminated between the LBD variants, and some behaved similarly to tamoxifen, showing strong enrichment with WT but poor enrichment with either mutant (Supplementary Table S1). However, in one DEL (BCM DEL 21), the top hit enriched with the Y537S mutant LBD (Fig. 1A, 21a) was also strongly enriched with the WT and D538G mutant LBDs (Supplementary Fig. S3). The top hit was almost completely depleted in the WT + E2 condition, indicating that hit 21a competes with estradiol (Supplementary Fig. S4). Therefore, we prioritized the synthesis of DEL hit 21a, called CDD-1274 hereafter (Fig. 1a, 21a; the BB1-BB2-BB3 tri-synthon that was best enriched with Y537S LBD) and 21-di, hereafter referred to as CDD-1272 (Fig. 1A, 21-di; the di-synthon BB2 and BB3) for off-DNA synthesis. The scheme for off-DNA chemical synthesis of DEL hit compounds is depicted in Supplementary Fig. S5. Briefly, the selected hit CDD-1274 was achieved by the reductive amination of commercially available amine 3 with aldehyde 6 to yield the desired target compound, CDD-1274 (14%). Similarly, compound 6 reacted with methyl amine (CH3–NH2) to produce truncated CDD-1272 (80%). The NMR spectra of CDD-1272, compound 6, and CDD-1274 are shown in Supplementary Figs. S6–S10.

Validation of WT and mutant ERα LBDs hits

To functionally validate the DEL selection hits, CDD-1272 and CDD-1274 were initially tested in a biochemical assay that monitored coactivator recruitment to mutant ERα LBDs. Previous studies have shown that constitutively active mutants Y537S and D538G induce an agonist conformation that spontaneously recruits coactivator proteins22,23,25. To test whether CDD-1272 and CDD-1274 displaced spontaneous coactivator recruitment to mutants, we conducted an SRC3 peptide recruitment assay with Y537S and D538G recombinant ERα LBD proteins. The SRC3 peptide is extensively used in coactivator recruitment assays since SRC3 is abundant in breast cancer cells and has a high affinity for ERα22,23,25. As shown in Figs. 1B and 1C, CDD-1272 and CDD-1274 disrupted spontaneous SRC3 coactivator peptide recruitment to the mutant LBDs, suggesting that they act as antagonists. 4-Hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) was used as a positive control. Our biochemical assay also suggests that CDD-1272 and CDD-1274 may compete with the SRC3 coactivator peptide off the Y537S and D538G LBDs, leading to active to inactive conformational changes in mutant LBDs. To determine the biochemical potency of CDD-1272 and CDD-1274 on coactivator binding to Y537S and D538G mutant ER LBDs, they were titrated into constant amounts of mutant ERα LBDs and SRC3 coactivator peptide. As shown in Fig. 1D and E, coactivator peptide recruitment was disrupted in a concentration-dependent manner for both Y537S and D538G ERα LBDs. Notably, CDD-1274 had an IC50 value of 1 µM range for both Y537S and D538G LBDs. As expected, di-synthon (21-di, building blocks 2 and 3) CDD-1272 was less potent (IC50 ~ 18 µM) than tri-synthon (CDD-1274). Therefore, we tested CDD-1274 in a series of cell-based studies using WT and mutant breast cancer cells.

CDD-1274 degrades WT and Y537S ERα in T47D breast cancer cells

Small molecules can attenuate ERα function as functional antagonists or degraders. We probed the effects of CDD-1274 by measuring the levels of ERα and cell proliferative markers. We used T47D cells as the parental cell line and employed a CRISPR knock-in ESR1 mutant that stably expressed ERαY537S as described previously53. Because coactivator recruitment is required for ERα activity, we hypothesized that the displacement of the SRC3 peptide by CDD-1274 would inhibit ERα signaling in cells. To test this, WT and Y537S mutant cells were treated with CDD-1274 for 24 h at different concentrations and immunoblots were performed for ERα and cell proliferation markers. CDD-1274 treatment decreased ERα protein levels in both WT and Y537S mutant cell types (Fig. 2A and B), suggesting that it might function as an ERα degrader. Concordantly, CDD-1274 treatment also led to a concomitant dose-proportional reduction in the expression of the cell proliferative markers cyclin D1 and survivin, as well as the canonical ERα target gene progesterone receptor (PR) in WT and Y537S T47D cells (Fig. 2A and B). Further, to evaluate the ERα degradation, we employed a time course study with CDD-1274. Treatment of WT and Y537S mutant cells with CDD-1274 led to the time-dependent degradation of ERα in both WT and Y537S T47D cells. Of note, we observed WT and Y537 mutant ERα degradation within 6 h and complete loss of ERα at 48 h after CDD-1274 treatment (Fig. 2C and D). Consistent with the ERα degradation profile, CDD-1274 also caused a time-dependent reduction in PR and proliferative markers (cyclin D1 and survivin) in WT and Y537S T47D cells (Fig. 2C and D). Importantly, these effects were very similar in both WT and Y537S cell types, suggesting that, unlike tamoxifen, the effects of CDD-1274 on ERα are not ablated by the Y537S mutation. It is worth noting that CDD-1274 was a direct hit from our DEL screen and was strongly enriched in both WT and mutants. Consistent with the observation in the DEL screen, CDD-1274 potently degraded WT and Y537S mutant ERα levels with similar potency in T47D breast cancer cells.

To interrogate the potential effect of CDD-1274 on transcription of ERα target genes, T47D WT and Y537S mutant cells were treated with CDD-1274 in the presence or absence of E2 for 24 h. Transcripts for ERα (ESR1), progesterone receptor (PR), and c-MYC were analyzed by qPCR. In the absence of E2, CDD-1274 treatment markedly decreased the basal levels of ESR1, PGR, and c-MYC mRNAs in both WT and Y537S mutant cell types. In the presence of E2, CDD-1274 did not significantly alter ESR1 or PGR mRNA levels in WT cells but did decrease these transcript levels in Y537S cells. CDD-1274 abolished the E2-induced increase in c-MYC mRNA levels in WT cells and decreased c-MYC mRNA levels in Y537S cells, suggesting that CDD-1274 blocks a subset of E2-induced genes at the transcriptional level (Supplementary Fig. S11).

To further confirm the antagonistic activity of CDD-1274 in ERα signaling, we performed immunoblotting for ERα target gene expression and cell proliferative markers in the presence and absence of E2. Consistent with previous reports, we observed that E2 induced the expression of cyclin D1, c-MYC, survivin, E2F1, and PR in WT T47D cells (Fig. 2E). However, Y537S mutant cells expressed high levels of these proteins in the absence of E2 (Fig. 2F), implying that the Y537S mutation induces ERα target gene expression in a ligand-independent manner, which is consistent with previous results54. WT ERα levels decreased with E2 treatment, but Y537S ERα levels did not (Fig. 2E and F). Importantly, CDD-1274 completely blocked E2-induced expression of cyclin D1, c-MYC, survivin, E2F1 and PR in WT cells, and greatly decreased their expression in Y537S cells with or without E2 treatment. Furthermore, CDD-1274 also induced the accumulation of cleaved PARP, a marker of apoptosis, in WT and Y537S cells (Fig. 2E and F). Together, these data lead us to conclude that CDD-1274 effectively antagonizes and degrades both WT and Y537S ERα and blocks ligand-dependent and ligand-independent ERα signaling in WT and Y537S mutant cells. These properties are similar to those reported for elacestrant, a non-steroidal small molecule drug approved by the FDA in 2023 for the treatment of ER-positive, HER2-negative, ESR1-mutated advanced or metastatic breast cancer, which induces ERα degradation in ER+ breast cancer cells55. Therefore, we assessed CDD-1274 activity relative to that of elacestrant in T47D WT and Y537S mutant cells. Treatment of T47D WT and Y537S cell lines with CDD-1274 led to dose-dependent degradation of WT and Y537S ERα, whereas elacestrant effectively degraded only WT ERα but not Y537S ERα (Supplementary Fig. S12 and Fig. 2G), suggesting that the Y537S ERα is more sensitive to CDD-1274 than to elacestrant. However, CDD-1274 and elacestrant caused a substantial decrease in cell proliferative markers in a dose-dependent manner in both T47D WT and Y537S mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S12 and Fig. 2G). Of note, the effects of CDD-1274 on cyclin D1 and survivin appeared to be greater at 5 μM than those of elacestrant in T47D Y537S mutant cells (Fig. 2G). However, elacestrant greatly reduced c-MYC protein levels compared with CDD-1274 in T47D Y537S mutant cells (Fig. 2G). These results suggest that CDD-1274 and elacestrant do not act by identical mechanisms of action in T47D Y537S cells.

Previous studies have suggested that lasofoxifene can inhibit Y537S mutant activity, thereby reducing primary tumor growth and metastases more effectively than fulvestrant56. Therefore, we also compared the activity of lasofoxifene with CDD-1274 in T47D WT and Y537S mutant cells. As expected, CDD-1274 treatment induced concentration-dependent degradation of WT and Y537S mutant ERα, whereas lasofoxifene caused a significant reduction in WT ERα but not in Y537S mutant ERα (Supplementary Fig. S13). Notably, lower concentrations of lasofoxifene induced ERα protein levels in WT and Y537S mutant, consistent with selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) properties. CDD-1274 and lasofoxifene substantially reduced the expression of cell proliferative markers (survivin and cyclin D1) in the T47D WT and Y537S mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S13).

CDD-1274 degrades WT ERα and constitutively active ERα mutants and blocks ER signaling in MCF-7 cells

To assess CDD-1274 antagonist activity in the mutant context, we engineered Y537S and D538G mutations in the ESR1 gene of MCF-7 cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology53. To detect changes in ERα degradation, we assessed the effects of CDD-1274 on WT, Y537S and D538G MCF-7 cells. As shown in Fig. 3A–C, CDD-1274 markedly decreased the levels of WT, Y537S and D538G ERαs, as was seen in T47D cells (Fig. 2). Consistent with the ERα degradation profile, CDD-1274 also decreased both E2-driven and mutation-driven cell proliferative markers such as cyclin D1, survivin and E2F1. Similarly, we also detected a significant loss in the expression of the ERα target gene TFF1, GREB1, and PR protein levels. Furthermore, CDD-1274 increased the levels of c-PARP in both the mutant cell lines (Fig. 3A–C). Notably, E2-induced c-MYC level was blocked by CDD-1274 in WT and D538G cells but not in Y537S MCF-7 cells. However, we also observed that acute treatment with CDD-1274 completely blocked E2-induced c-MYC level in Y537S MCF-7 cells (unpublished data). Together, these data demonstrate that our DEL selection hit CDD-1274 suppresses E2-dependent and independent ERα signaling in WT, Y537S and D538G cells.

CDD-1274 decreases levels of ERα and its targets in palbociclib-resistant WT and Y537S MCF-7 cells

Drugs that inhibit CDK4/6 (palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib) have revolutionized treatment for metastatic ER+ breast cancer57. However, resistance to these CDK4/6 inhibitors inevitably develops58,59 and there are currently no standard next-line treatments for palbociclib-resistant metastatic ER+ breast cancer. Because ER signaling is maintained in the CDK4/6 resistant setting, we developed palbociclib-resistant WT and Y537S MCF-7 cell lines by culturing them with palbociclib for 12 months as previously described28. We assessed the ability of CDD-1274 and elacestrant to decrease ER signaling in these cells. The cells were treated with either CDD-1274 or elacestrant and cultured in the presence or absence of E2 for 24 h. CDD-1274 markedly degraded both WT and Y537S ERα proteins and blocked constitutive and E2-induced cyclin D1, survivin and TFF1 expression in palbociclib-resistant WT and Y537S MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S14), consistent with the desired ERα degrader profile. Elascestrant degraded ERα in the WT, but not in the Y537S mutant ERα in palbociclib-resistant MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S14), which is consistent with previous reports60. Elascestrant blocked E2-induced cyclin D1, survivin, c-MYC and TFF1 protein expression in palbociclib-resistant WT MCF-7 cells but not in Y537S MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S14). To further confirm our findings, we generated a long-term estrogen-deprived (LTED) model by culturing MCF-7 cells in hormone-deprived media for six months as described previously28. These cells are relatively growth resistant to tamoxifen and fulvestrant, and as shown in Fig. 3E, CDD-1274 dramatically decreased the levels of ERα and E2-induced ERα target gene, TFF1 and proliferative markers (cyclin D1, survivin and E2F1). However, elacestrant treatment increased ERα levels but did not affect the levels of cyclin D1, survivin, E2F1 and TFF1 in the presence of E2, suggesting that the two compounds have different modes of action.

CDD-1274 effects are restricted to ER-positive cancer cells

Next, we tested the off-target effects of CDD-1274 in normal breast cells and ER-negative breast cancer cells. MCF-10A normal mammary epithelial cells were treated with several concentrations of CDD-1274 for 24 h, and the effects on cyclin D1 protein level was determined by immunoblotting. As shown in Fig. 4A, CDD-1274 did not alter the cyclin D1 level. To further evaluate its target specificity, we assessed the impact of CDD-1274 on other subtypes, such as HER2+ (HCC1954) and triple-negative (MDA-MB-231) breast cancer cells. CDD-1274 did not cause any significant change in the cell proliferative markers (Fig. 4B and C). To further validate the mechanism of action of CDD-1274, we treated VCaP prostate cancer cells that express androgen receptors. CDD-1274 treatment did not alter the expression of androgen receptor and proliferative markers (Fig. 4D), further confirming that the observed effects of CDD-1274 in ER+ tumor cells were mediated through ERα and were not due to off-target effects. To probe for cell-type selective activity, we tested CDD-1274 in endometrial cells, where the estrogenic effects of tamoxifen induce uterotrophic activity. Despite being primarily an antagonist, 4OH-tamoxifen is known to induce ERα signaling (partial agonism) in Ishikawa endometrial carcinoma cells in the absence of E261. We tested CDD-1274 activity in these cells to determine whether it has the potential to act as an agonist. Treatment with CDD-1274 for 24 h at a range of concentrations showed that CDD-1274 decreased ERα levels and inhibited cell proliferative markers (cyclin D1 and survivin) (Fig. 4E), demonstrating that CDD-1274 has no inherent ERα agonist activity in endometrial cancer cells. As expected, 4OH-tamoxifen increased ERα protein levels in Ishikawa endometrial carcinoma cells (Fig. 4E). The ability of CDD-1274 to antagonize ERα was further evaluated by determining its effects on the expression of cell proliferative markers. As expected, E2 induced cyclin D1 and survivin expression; CDD-1274 completely blocked basal and E2-stimulated cyclin D1 and survivin expression (Fig. 4F), suggesting that CDD-1274 acts as a pure antagonist in both breast and endometrial cancer cells.

CDD-1274 inhibits ERα and its target gene expression in T47D cells after 72 h washout

To distinguish between transient and prolonged inhibition by CDD-1274, T47D WT and Y537S mutant cells were treated with CDD-1274 in the presence and absence of E2 for 24 h, followed by compound washout and replacement of the fresh medium without CDD-1274 or E2. Cells were collected and lysed at 24, 48, and 72 h after washout for Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, CDD-1274 induced decreases in WT and Y537S mutant ERα levels that were sustained for up to 72 h after washout. Analysis of the blots for ERα target gene levels showed that E2-induced c-MYC and PR protein levels were also maintained up to 72 h after removal of E2 from the culture medium, but not in survivin levels. Remarkably, CDD-1274 potently blocked basal and E2-induced cyclin D1, c-MYC and PR expression in WT T47D cells (Fig. 5A) and blocked constitutive cyclin D1, c-MYC, and PR expression in Y537S T47D cells (Fig. 5B), suggesting a sustained ERα degrader antagonist profile after the removal of CDD-1274 from the culture medium.

CDD-1274 docks to antagonist ERα WT and mutant structures

Our selection data indicated that CDD-1274 competes with estradiol (Supplementary Fig.S4). To understand how CDD-1274 binds, we docked CDD-1274 with antagonist-liganded structures of WT (1err.pdb), Y537S (7uj8.pdb), and D538G (4q50.pdb) ERα LBDs. The major difference among the starting structures is the loop near the ligand-binding pocket. In wild-type ERα, the loop was oriented away from the pocket compared to the Y537S and D538G complexes, presenting a more open pocket. We removed the original ligands and docked the CDD-1274 ligand with Maestro Suite 13.4 (Schrodinger, LLC). Docked structures were chosen based on their scores, compound occupancy, and binding modes. The clusters of top poses for all three LBD variants orient CDD-1274 BB3 deep into the binding pocket away from the solvent and expose the DEL DNA attachment site to the solvent (Supplementary Fig. S15A–C). These poses would support the LBD pulldown of 21a (Fig. 1A) without steric interference from the DNA tag or linker. CDD-1274 poses with all three LBD variants are in contact with helix 3 (A350, C354, L384) and helix 11 (K525) in similar deep ligand-binding pockets23. Interestingly, the hydroxyl group of CDD-1274 forms a hydrogen bond with S530 in ERα-LBD WT, but with K529 in ERα-LBD Y537S and D538G.

CDD-1274 treatment induces proteasomal degradation of WT and Y537S ERα in T47D cells

Selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs) such as fulvestrant bind to ERα, impair receptor dimerization, and accelerate proteasome-mediated degradation62. We tested whether the proteasome inhibitor, MG132, alters the effects of CDD-1274 on the degradation of WT and Y537S mutant ERα. In WT T47D cells, treatment with CDD-1274 or fulvestrant decreased WT ERα levels; this decrease was completely blocked by the addition of MG132 (Fig. 6A). More importantly, treatment with CDD-1274 decreased the levels of Y537S mutant ERα more strongly than treatment with fulvestrant, and both effects were blocked by MG132 treatment (Fig. 6B). These findings indicate that CDD-1274-induced decreases in ERα protein levels resulted primarily from increased proteasome-mediated turnover. We noted that CDD-1274 degraded Y537S ERα to a greater extent than fulvestrant, but the relative effect was reversed for WT ERα. Our data are consistent with previous results showing that the Y537S mutant requires significantly higher levels of fulvestrant than WT ERα to fully inhibit its activity22. These data showed that CDD-1274 effectively accelerated ERα degradation through the proteasomal degradation pathway.

Discussion

Mutations with gain-of-function in the LBD of ERα are a major driver for the development of endocrine resistance and are associated with poor survival in patients with metastatic ER-positive breast cancer. Activating somatic mutations Y537S or D538G drive ligand-independent activities in cell-based studies, and these mutant receptors are less sensitive to current ERα targeting agents in the clinic. Consequently, there is a critical need to develop next-generation small-molecule ERα antagonists or degraders that can disable aberrant activities of mutant ERα signaling. By leveraging the DEL hit discovery campaign, we have identified and characterized a novel molecule, CDD-1274, as an antagonist and degrader of both WT and mutant ERα proteins.

DEL technology is widely used in drug discovery pursuits, especially for the identification of small molecules that are capable of modulating therapeutically relevant targets34. Unlike high-throughput screening (HTS), this technology offers rapid screening of a massive number of small molecules and quick identification of hits using DNA barcodes in the target of interest. Because of these advantages, both pharmaceutical companies and academia are implementing this platform.

Recently, we have established DEL technology with more than 70 libraries51 that we have employed to identify novel and potent small molecule inhibitors for various biological target classes including kinases40–43,46. In this study, DEL selection successfully identified a novel and potent small-molecule ERα degrader for WT and clinically relevant mutants, indicating the robustness of our platform. We selected the compound that was the most highly enriched among all three LBDs, CDD-1274, for characterization and validation in biochemical and cell-based studies.

Single point mutations in the ERα LBD enable ligand-independent activation and weaken the binding of tamoxifen and fulvestrant, allowing breast cancer cells to resist the effects of these standard-of-care therapeutics22. To identify chemical matter that bound strongly to both WT and mutant ERαs, we used DEL technology to perform parallel selections with WT, Y537S, and D538G LBDs and pursued hits that were strongly enriched with all three LBDs. Our biochemical data showed that DEL hit CDD-1274 disrupted SRC3 peptide recruitment by mutant LBDs, consistent with an antagonist mode of action. Many of our DEL hits discriminated between these LBDs, so parallel selections were critical to ensure that we focused on a ‘pan-ERα’ hit. We note that it would also be possible to pursue a mutant-selective hit, which could be used as a tool compound.

Our cell-based studies showed that CDD-1274 treatment decreased WT and mutant ERα protein levels. The addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 rescued CDD-1274-induced depletion of both WT and Y537S ERα, establishing that CDD-1274 is a selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD). Our findings were consistent with previous studies that demonstrated estradiol (E2)- induced degradation of the ERα expression, and this downregulation was prevented by inhibiting the proteasome with MG13263,64. Our data also suggest that E2 and CDD-1274-induced degradation of ERα may proceed through the proteasome pathway in MCF-7 LTED cells. In several cell lines, CDD-1274 is a highly effective degrader of Y537S ERα, and in palbociclib-resistant Y537S mutant MCF-7 cells, it induced Y537S ERα degradation better than elacestrant. Since the ability to cause ERα degradation is likely to contribute strongly to its impact on breast cancer cells, optimization of this compound for the clinic will need to emphasize cell-based assays over its affinity for the LBD.

ERα plays a major role in ER+ breast cancer cell growth by regulating cell cycle proteins, including cyclin D1, E2F1, and c-MYC65,66. Moreover, CDK4/6 inhibitor-resistant ER+ breast cancer cells rely on ERα signaling to drive tumor cell growth. CDD-1274 treatment resulted in the downregulation of cyclin D1, E2F1, and c-MYC in multiple breast cancer cell line models, including palbociclib-resistant (WT and Y537S) and LTED cells, demonstrating that CDD-1274 inhibits cell proliferation markers in many different ER+ breast cancer cell lines and anti-estrogen resistance models. Critically, these effects were not observed in MCF-10A normal mammary epithelial cells, HER2+, triple-negative (MDA-MB-231) breast cancer cells, and AR+ prostate cancer cells; therefore, we inferred that the effects of CDD-1274 in ER+ cells are mediated largely through ERα. These findings are aligned with the properties of other SERDs in clinical trials3,30.

Because CDD-1274 can antagonize and degrade WT and mutant ERα, either mode of action may contribute to the observed effects. Our transcriptional studies in WT and Y537S T47D cells showed that CDD-1274 treatment decreased mRNA levels of ERα target genes such as PGR and c-MYC, consistent with a recent study demonstrating that oral ERα inhibitor AZD9496 decreases mRNA levels of ERα-activated genes in a dose-dependent manner in both in vitro and in vivo models67. Since ERα protein levels were not completely ablated in our 24 h treatment studies, we expect that antagonism may contribute to these transcriptional changes, especially in the presence of estradiol. Several oral SERDs have recently been evaluated in early- and late-phase clinical trials for metastatic ER-positive breast cancer3. Future studies will focus on comparing the performance of CDD-1274 and of optimized variants with these compounds. Partial ERα agonism activity in the uterus can increase the risk of endometrial cancer68, and drugs that act as ERα antagonists in breast cancer cells can act as partial agonizts. The observation that CDD-1274 does not elicit any agonism in Ishikawa endometrial carcinoma cells, but 4-OHT does, suggests that CDD-1274 acts as a complete antagonist in both breast and endometrial cancer cells. These data are consistent with those of other SERDs in the clinical trials3,30.

Endocrine therapy combined with cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i, e.g., palbociclib) is the standard first-line therapy for most patients with ER+ and human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2)- advanced breast cancer. However, resistance to CDK4/6i is inevitable, and novel therapy for patients who relapse after treatment is urgently required69. Our results showed that CDD-1274 strongly decreased ERα levels, cell proliferative markers, and ERα target gene expression in palbociclib-resistant WT and Y537S mutant MCF-7 cells. The marked effects of CDD-1274 in Y537S cells contrasted with the minimal effects of elacestrant, which did not induce Y537S ERα degradation or decrease cyclin D1 and TFF1 levels, suggesting that CDD-1274 may have clinical benefits over elacestrant in palbociclib-resistant Y537S mutant cells. Together, our data suggest that CDD-1274 may be used in the early or late stages of ER+ breast cancers, warranting further investigation using an in vivo model.

A recent study demonstrated dose-dependent imlunestrant-mediated ERα degradation that was sustained for up to 72 h after imlunestrant washout70. Our washout experiments with CDD-1274 showed that both WT and Y537S ERα protein levels remained suppressed even 72 h after the washout of CDD-1274 from the culture medium, presumably because of the continual degradation mediated by CDD-1274 that remained inside the cells. We expect that this behavior will favorably impact the dosing regimen and therapeutic window of CDD-1274 in vivo. Taken together, our cell-based studies provide a strong rationale for optimizing the drug-like properties and cellular potency of CDD-1274 through medicinal chemistry aimed at generating lead compounds for endocrine-resistant metastatic breast cancer.

In summary, our multibillion small molecule DEL screens against LBDs of ERα variants have rapidly identified a potent novel ERα mutant degrader. CDD-1274 is a direct hit from our screen that potently degraded both WT and clinically relevant ERα mutants. Moreover, we observed a sustained ERα degradation profile, even after the removal of CDD-1274 from the culture medium. We also found that this degradation is mediated by the 26S proteasome pathway. Furthermore, CDD-1274 effectively blocked ligand-dependent and ligand-independent ERα signaling in WT and Y537S mutant cells (Fig. 6C). More importantly, when compared with elacestrant, CDD-1274 showed enhanced efficacy and strong suppression of the ERα signaling pathway in Y537S mutant breast cancer cells, including palbociclib-resistant cells, highlighting its widespread therapeutic potential for endocrine-resistant breast cancer. Thus, further optimization of CDD-1274 through traditional medicinal chemistry will offer drug-like molecules for developing a new class of ERα degraders and warrant assessment of their efficacy and safety in preclinical and clinical studies.

Protein expression and purification

The human ERα LBD wild-type, Y537S, and D538G with an N-terminal 6xHis-tag were expressed and purified from the E. coli BL21(DE3) strain. Cells were cultured in lysogeny broth medium at 37 °C, and protein overexpression was induced by 0.5 mM IPTG for 6 h at 26 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 5 mM βME and Complete EDTA-free proteinase inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics), and disrupted by a microfluidizer set at 13,000–18,000 psi. The supernatant was loaded onto a pre-equilibrated HisTrap FF column (GE Healthcare, Boston, MA) and eluted with an imidazole gradient using FPLC (GE Healthcare). The purity of the ERα LBD in each fraction was visualized by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) followed by Coomassie Blue staining. Protein fractions that were >75% pure were pooled and buffer-exchanged with buffer B (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 50 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 2 mM βME using an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit (Millipore Sigma). LBDs were further purified on a HiPrep SP FF cation exchange column, concentrated, and subjected to size-exclusion chromatography on a SuperdexTM 75 Increase 10/300 GL gel filtration column (GE, Amersham Biosciences, USA) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Protein purity was monitored by SDS–PAGE and fractions were pooled and concentrated in gel filtration buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM β-mercaptoethanol and 10% glycerol), aliquoted, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for DEL selection and biochemical assays.

DNA-encoded chemical library affinity selection with WT and mutants

DEL selection was performed as previously described46. Briefly, 31 different DELs (total 4.39 billion unique molecules) were combined and incubated with 1 μM WT ERα LBD, or 1 μM WT ERα LBD with estradiol (10 μM) or 0.2 μM WT ERα LBD or 0.2 μM Y537S ERα LBD, or D538G ERα LBD in 150 μL of selection buffer (20 mM HEPES, 134 mM potassium acetate, 8 mM sodium acetate, 4 mM sodium chloride, 0.8 mM magnesium acetate, 1 mg/ml sheared salmon sperm DNA, 1 mM TCEP, 5 mM imidazole, 0.02% Tween-20, pH 7.2). The same library pool without a target was incubated under the same conditions as the non-target control (NTC) to assess the background binding of DEL molecules to the affinity resin. One microliter of the library molecules was used for quantification by quantitative PCR (qPCR). Ni-NTA magnetic beads (25 μL) were prewashed with selection buffer, and the target protein–library mixture was added for affinity selection. The magnetic beads were washed with 500 μL selection buffer to remove unbound DEL molecules. Target-bound compounds were eluted by incubation at 80 °C for 10 min. One microliter of elution material was again set aside for quantification by qPCR, and the entire remaining volume of the sample was subjected to an additional three rounds of affinity selection with the fresh target as described above. After four rounds of selection, DEL molecules were quantified using qPCR, which served as a quality control measure. qPCR was performed on each sample of recovered DEL material after each round of selection to monitor the total number of remaining DNA tags. This information was used to guide both the selection process and sequencing preparation. An appropriate number of PCR cycles was used to amplify DNA, and the added DNA sequences were compatible with Illumina sequencing flow cells. The PCR product was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP SPRI beads (Agencourt, Danvers, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and then quantified on an Agilent BioAnalyzer (Santa Clara, CA) using a high-sensitivity DNA kit. The final amplicon concentration for each sample was pooled to obtain a concentration of 4 nM. The final concentration of 1.8 pM library pool samples was loaded onto an Illumina Next-Seq 500 sequencer (San Diego, CA) at the Genomic and RNA Profiling Core Facility at Baylor College of Medicine. The selection output data was processed using in-house data pipelines and code.

A higher Z-score indicated more significant enrichment in the selection. The normalized Z-score was calculated by modeling the selection data as a binomial distribution52. Homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence assay

Coactivator recruitment was measured using a time-resolved fluorescence resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) assay. This assay measures TR-FRET from a terbium-labeled anti-His antibody, which is used to label His-tagged wild-type ERα LBD, Y537S, or D538G LBDs, to a fluorescein-labeled coactivator peptide in a homogenous mix-and-read format. This assay was performed using either an agonist (estradiol), an antagonist (4-hydroxytamoxifen), or hit compounds. His-tagged ERα LBD variants were incubated with terbium-labeled anti-His antibody, followed by the addition of estradiol, 4-hydroxytamoxifen or hit compounds, and the fluorescein- SRC3 coactivator peptide. A SpectraMax M5e plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) or BMG LAB TECH Clariostar Plus was used with an integration delay of 50 µs and an integration time of 300 µs. Excitation of the donor (terbium cryptate) at 340 nm leads to both direct emission and FRET; fluorescence emission from the cryptate donor at 620 nM and from the fluorescein acceptor at 520 nM was recorded ratiometrically (acceptor/donor). The measurement of this ratio provided a normalized signal that was independent of the optical properties of the medium in which the interaction was studied. Binding data were analyzed using the global fitting procedure for one-site receptor-specific binding and non-specific binding sites provided by GraphPad Prism 7. Inhibition data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 7 using the 4-parameter logistic equation in log space, resulting in IC50 values.

Cell Culture

The Palbo-R cell lines were maintained in 1 µmol/L palbociclib. MCF-7 LTED cells were maintained in phenol red-free MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. # 51200-035) with 10% charcoal-stripped FBS (Fisher Scientific; 12676-029), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 1% L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. # 25030-081) and 1% NEAA. Western blot analysis was performed as previously described by our group71. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer (Boston Bioproducts, Cat. # BP115) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. # A32953). After removal of debris by centrifugation, protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; #23227). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on gradient gels (4–15%; Invitrogen; Cat. # NW04120BOX, NW04122BOX), followed by transfer onto nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad). Blocking was performed on the membrane before incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was then washed with TBST (0.1% Tween 20 in TBS) and incubated with an HRP-linked secondary antibody. After the third wash, the target proteins were visualized using the Super Signal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. 34096) using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The antibodies used in the immunoblots were anti-ERα (Invitrogen, Cat. # MA1-27107), anti-cyclin D1 (Invitrogen, Cat. # MA5-42676), anti-survivin (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Cat. # 2808S), anti-E2F1 (CST, Cat. # 3742), anti-TFF1 (CST, Cat. # 15571S), anti-cleaved PARP (CST, Cat. # 5625S), anti-PR A/B (CST, Cat. # 8757S), anti-c-MYC (CST, Cat. # 5605S), anti-GREB1 (Sigma, Cat. # MABS62), anti-CDK2 (CST, Cat. # 2546T) anti-β-actin (SCB; Cat. # SC-4778) and anti-GAPDH (SCB; Cat. # SC-32233).

Docking studies

The ERα wild-type (PDB ID: 1ERR), Y537S (PDB ID: 7UJ8) and D538G (PDB ID: 4Q50) structures were curated from RCSB.org in a ligand-bound state. The structures were individually refined using homology modeling in Maestro Suite 13.4 (Schrodinger, LLC). Ligands bound to the receptor structures were extracted using PyMOL (TM) 2.5.4 and subsequently utilized to validate docking studies. For docking analysis, ERα LBDs (wild-type, Y537S, and D538G) and ligands (CDD-1274, estradiol, 4-hydroxy tamoxifen, and raloxifene) were energy-minimized and prepared using the Maestro Suite with the OPLS4 force field. A grid was generated around the ERα binding site based on the coordinates of the bound ligands, and docking was performed. The interaction between the ligands and ERα was analyzed using the Maestro suite. Surface models of ERα bound to proteins were generated using PyMOL software. Standard ERα ligands were used as a control for docking studies.

Statistical analysis

All biochemical and cell-based experiments were performed in two or three biological replicates. In the quantification experiments (HTRF and qPCR), each biological replicate had two to four technical replicates, depending on the experiments. Statistical differences (P values) among the groups were determined using multiple t-tests. The P-value at p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For qRT-PCR experiments, data are represented as the mean of triplicate experiments and standard error of the mean. For biochemical experiments, data are presented as the mean of triplicate measurements and standard deviation (SD) of the mean. GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for the statistical analysis and graph generation.
