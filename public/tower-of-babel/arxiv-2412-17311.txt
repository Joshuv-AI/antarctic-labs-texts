1.
Introduction

Let
F
be a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic
0
and
G=\GL(n,F)
. For
g\in G
, we let
g^{\top}
denote the transpose of the matrix
g
, and
w_{0}
to be the matrix with anti-diagonal entries equal to one. Let
\tau:G\rightarrow G
be the map
\tau(g)=w_{0}g^{\top}w_{0}
. Clearly
\tau
is an anti-automorphism of
G
such that
\tau^{2}=1
. We call
\tau
the
standard involution
on
G
. Let
(\pi,V)
be an irreducible smooth complex representation of
G
. We write
(\pi^{\vee},V^{\vee})
for the smooth dual or the contragredient of
(\pi,V)
. For
\beta
an anti-automorphism of
G
such that
\beta^{2}=1
, we let
\pi^{\beta}
to be the twisted representation defined by

\pi^{\beta}(g)=\pi(\beta(g^{-1})).

The following is an old result of Gelfand and Kazhdan.

Theorem 1.1

(Gelfand-Kazhdan)
.

Let
\tau
be the standard involution on
G
. Then

\pi^{\tau}\simeq\pi^{\vee}.

We refer the reader to Theorem 2 in
[
4
]
for a proof of the above result.

If
\beta
is any anti-automorphism of
G
such that
\beta^{2}=1
, and satisfies
\pi^{\beta}\simeq\pi^{\vee}
, then we call
\beta
a
dualizing involution
. The above result of Gelfand and Kazhdan says that the standard involution
\tau
on
G
is a dualizing involution.

Let
r\geq 2
be a positive integer. For
n\geq 2
, let
\widetilde{G}
be the
r
-fold metaplectic cover of
G
(see Chapter 0 in
[
7
]
for the general definition). It is well known that (see Proposition 3.1 in
[
8
]
) the standard involution
\tau
on
G
has at least one lift to the metaplectic group. A natural and an interesting question that arises is whether the lifts of the standard involution are themselves dualizing involutions. In the case when
\widetilde{G}
is the
2
-fold metaplectic cover of
G=\GL(2,F)
, it is known that any lift of
\tau
to
\widetilde{G}
is dualizing (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
).

In this paper, we address this question when
\widetilde{G}
is the
n
-fold metaplectic cover of
G=\GL(2,F)
(explained later) for
n\geq 2
. It follows from Proposition 1 in
[
6
]
that there are many lifts of the standard involution to
\widetilde{G}
. In particular, for each
\alpha\in F^{\times}
, we have a lift
\sigma_{\alpha}
(see Section
4
for the definition) of
\tau
to
\widetilde{G}
. We show that the involutions

are dualizing if and only if
n=2
. To be precise, we prove

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\pi
be any irreducible admissible genuine representation of
\widetilde{G}
. For
\alpha\in F^{\times}
, let
\sigma_{\alpha}
be the lift of
\tau
to
\widetilde{G}
. Then

\pi^{\sigma_{\alpha}}\simeq\pi^{\vee}

if and only if
n=2
.

The above result raises the question, if a similar result holds for the
r
-fold covering
\widetilde{G}
of
G=\GL(n)
for
r,n\geq 2
. To be precise, a lift of the standard involution
\tau
of
G
to
\widetilde{G}
is a dualizing involution if and only if

. We hope to address some of these questions in future.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the basic properties of the Hilbert symbol and use it to establish some more properties that we need. We give the definition of the metaplectic group that we work with and discuss its topology. We recall a result of Harish-Chandra about the character of an admissible representation and give references for analogous results in the setting of metaplectic groups. We prove some basic properties of the character that we need. We also mention a few results of Kable about the lifts of an automorphism. In Section
3

4

2.1.
n
-th order Hilbert Symbol and its properties

Let
F
be a non-Archimedean local field of characterisic zero and
F^{\times}
be the group of non-zero elements in
F
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{n}=\{x\in F^{\times}\mid x^{n}=1\}
be the subset of
n
-th roots of unity in
F^{\times}
. Suppose also that
|\mu_{n}|=n
. We let

\langle\,,\,\rangle:F^{\times}\times F^{\times}\rightarrow\mu_{n}

denote the
n
-th order Hilbert symbol. In this section, we recall some fundamental properties of the
n
-th order Hilbert symbol and use it to derive some simple consequences that we need.

The following basic properties (see
[
7
]
) of the Hilbert symbol are well known. We record them in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
a,b,c\in F^{\times}
. The Hilbert symbol satisfies

1)

\langle ab,c\rangle=\langle a,c\rangle\langle b,c\rangle
.

2)

\langle a,b\rangle\langle b,a\rangle=1
.

3)

\langle a,1-a\rangle=1
, if
1-a\in F^{\times}
.

4)

\{a\in F^{\times}\mid\langle a,x\rangle=1
for all
x\in F^{\times}\}=(F^{\times})^{n}
, where
(F^{\times})^{n}=\{a^{n}\mid a\in F^{\times}\}
.

The following properties of the Hilbert symbol can be derived easily from properties
1)
to
4)
mentioned in Proposition
2.1
above.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
a,b,c\in F^{\times}
, the Hilbert symbol satisfies

(i)

\langle a,bc\rangle=\langle a,b\rangle\langle a,c\rangle
.

(ii)

\langle a^{m},b\rangle=\langle a,b\rangle^{m}=\langle a,b^{m}\rangle
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

\langle 1-a,a\rangle=1
if
a\neq 1
, and
\langle a,1\rangle=\langle 1,a\rangle=1
.

(iv)

\langle b,a\rangle=\langle a,b\rangle^{-1}=\langle a^{-1},b\rangle=\langle a,b^{-1}\rangle
.

(v)

\langle-a,a\rangle=\langle a,-a\rangle=\langle a,a^{2}\rangle=1
.

(vi)

If
\langle a,x\rangle=1
for all
x\in F^{\times},
then
a\in(F^{\times})^{n}
. In other words, if
a\notin(F^{\times})^{n}
, there exists
x_{0}\in F^{\times}
such that
\langle a,x_{0}\rangle\neq 1
.

Proof.

(i)

Using properties 1) and 2) in Proposition
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\langle a,bc\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle bc,a\rangle^{-1}

\displaystyle=\langle b,a\rangle^{-1}\langle c,a\rangle^{-1}

\displaystyle=\langle a,b\rangle\langle a,c\rangle.

(ii)

It follows from (i) and property 1).

(iii)

We know that
\langle 1-a,a\rangle\langle a,1-a\rangle=1
, so the result follows from property 3). By (i), we have

\langle a,1\rangle=\langle a,1\cdot 1\rangle=\langle a,1\rangle\langle a,1\rangle.

Hence
\langle a,1\rangle=1
. Consequently,
\langle 1,a\rangle=\langle a,1\rangle^{-1}=1
.

(iv)

Since
\langle a,b\rangle\langle b,a\rangle=1
, we obtain
\langle b,a\rangle=\langle a,b\rangle^{-1}
. Using (iii) and property 1), we have

\langle a,b\rangle\langle a^{-1},b\rangle=\langle aa^{-1},b\rangle=\langle 1,a\rangle=1.

Similarly

\langle a,b\rangle\langle a,b^{-1}\rangle=\langle a,bb^{-1}\rangle=\langle a,1\rangle=1.

(v)

If
a=1
, we are done using (iii). For
a\neq 1
, observe that
-a=\frac{1-a}{1-a^{-1}}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle a,-a\rangle

\displaystyle=\left<a,\frac{1-a}{1-a^{-1}}\right>

\displaystyle=\langle a,1-a\rangle\langle a,(1-a^{-1})^{-1}\rangle

(using (i))

\displaystyle=\langle a,1-a\rangle\langle a^{-1},1-a^{-1}\rangle

(using (iv))

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{(using property 3))}.

Thus, we also have
\langle a,a^{2}\rangle=\langle a,-a\rangle\langle a,-a\rangle=1
.

(vi)

Follows from property 4).

∎

2.2.
The Metaplectic Group

Let
F
be a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic zero and
G=\GL(2,F)
. For
n\geq 2
, let
\mu_{n}\subset F^{\times}
be the subset of
n
-th roots of unity in
F
. Suppose also that
|\mu_{n}|=n
. Throughout, we write
\widetilde{G}=\widetilde{\GL}(2,F)
for the
n
-fold metaplectic cover of
G
. In this section, we recall the definition of
\widetilde{G}
and a few basic facts about its topology.

Throughout, we write
\mathfrak{o}
for the ring of integers in
F
,
\mathfrak{p}
for the unique maximal ideal in
\mathfrak{o}
,
\varpi
for the generator of
\mathfrak{p}
and
k_{F}
for the finite residue field. We write
ord
for the valuation on
F
.

The first explicit construction of the
n
-fold metaplectic cover of
G=\GL(2,F)
was given by Kubota in
[
9
]
by concretely describing a
2
-cocyle on
G
taking values in
\mu_{n}
. For
g_{1},g_{2},m\in G
, the following simpler version of the Kubota cocycle
c:G\times G\rightarrow\mu_{n}
defined as

c(g_{1},g_{2})=\left<\frac{X(g_{1}g_{2})}{X(g_{1})},\frac{X(g_{1}g_{2})}{X(g_{2})\Delta(g_{1})}\right>,

where

X(m)=\begin{cases}m_{21}&\text{if}\;m_{21}\neq 0\\
m_{22}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\textrm{ and }\Delta(g_{1})=\det(g_{1})

was given by Kazhdan and Patterson in
[
8
]
. Throughout this paper, we take
\widetilde{G}=\widetilde{\GL}(2,F)
to be the central extension of
G
by
\mu_{n}
determined by the
2
-cocycle
c
. Since
G
,
\mu_{n}
are locally compact groups, Mackey’s theorem (see Theorem 2 in
[
11
]
) implies that
\widetilde{G}
is a locally compact topological group and defines a topological central extension of
G
by
\mu_{n}
. We call the group
\widetilde{G}
constructed above as “the” metaplectic group.

It can be shown that the topology on
\widetilde{G}
has a neighborhood base at the identity consisting of compact open subgroups (see Lemma 3 in
[
6
]
). Before we give the construction of this basis, we recall a few preliminaries.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow G
be the projection of
\widetilde{G}
onto
G
. A map
\ell:G\rightarrow\widetilde{G}
is called a
section
if
p\circ\ell=1_{G}
. Given a subgroup
H
of
G
, we say that
\widetilde{G}

splits
over
H
if there exists a homomorphism
h:H\rightarrow\widetilde{G}
such that
p\circ h=1_{H}
.

Let
\ell:G\rightarrow\widetilde{G}
be the map
\ell(g)=(g,1)
. Then
\ell
is a section and is called the natural or preferred section. For
g=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in G
, define
s:G\rightarrow\mu_{n}
as

s(g)=\begin{cases}\langle c,d\Delta(g)\rangle,&\textrm{ if }cd\neq 0\textrm{ and $ord(c)$ is odd}\\
\hfil 1,&\textrm{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Let
K_{0}=\GL(2,\mathfrak{o})
be the maximal compact subgroup in
G
, and for
\lambda\geq 1
, let
K_{\lambda}=1+\varpi^{\lambda}\M(n,\mathfrak{o})
. It is known that
\{K_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\geq 0}
is a neighborhood base at the identity element in
G
consisting of compact open subgroups. We can use this base to define a neighborhood base at the identity in
\widetilde{G}
. For a suitable choice of
\lambda_{0}
, it can be shown that
\widetilde{G}
splits over
K_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
(see Proposition 2.8 in
[
3
]
, Theorem 2 in
[
9
]
). Define
\kappa:K_{\lambda_{0}}\rightarrow\widetilde{G}
as
\kappa(k)=(k,s(k))
. Let
K_{\lambda_{0}}^{*}=\kappa(K_{\lambda_{0}})
and for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
,
K^{*}_{\lambda}=K_{\lambda_{0}}^{*}\cap p^{-1}(K_{\lambda})
. It can be shown that
\{K^{*}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}}
is a neighborhood base at the identity in
\widetilde{G}
.

2.3.
Character of an admissible representation

Let
F
be a non-Archimedean local field of characteristic
0
and
G=G(F)
be a connected reductive algebraic group defined over
F
. We let
(\pi,V)
be an irreducible smooth complex representation of
G
. It can be shown that such representations are always admissible. For an admissible representation
(\pi,V)
, we can define a suitable notion of a character. Before we proceed further, we set up some notation and recall an important result of Harish-Chandra.

Throughout we let
G=G(F)
and
(\pi,V)
to be an irreducible smooth representation of
G
. We let
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
to be the space of all locally constant complex valued functions on
G
with compact support. For
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
, we let
\pi(f):V\rightarrow V
denote the linear operator given by

\pi(f)v=\int_{G}f(g)\pi(g)vdg,\quad v\in V,

where the integral is with respect to a Haar measure
\mu_{G}
on
G
which we fix throughout. If
(\pi,V)
is an admissible representation, it can be shown that the trace of the operator
\pi(f)
is finite for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
. The resulting linear functional

\Theta_{\pi}:C^{\infty}_{c}(G)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

given by

\Theta_{\pi}(f)=\tr(\pi(f))

is called the
distribution character
of
\pi
. It determines the irreducible representation
\pi
up to equivalence, i.e., if
\Theta_{\pi_{1}}(f)=\Theta_{\pi_{2}}(f)
,
\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
, then
\pi_{1}\simeq\pi_{2}
.

We now state a theorem of Harish-Chandra which is used to define the character of the representation
\pi
.

Let
G_{\reg}
be the subset of regular semi-simple elements in
G
. It can be shown that
G_{\reg}
is an open dense subset of
G
whose complement has measure zero. The following is a deep result of Harish-Chandra.

Theorem 2.3

(Harish-Chandra)
.

There exists a locally integrable complex valued function
\Theta_{\pi}
on
G
such that
\Theta_{\pi}|_{G_{\reg}}
is a locally constant function on
G_{\reg}
and satisfies

\Theta_{\pi}(f)=\int_{G}f(g)\Theta_{\pi}(g)dg,\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G).

Also, for
x\in G_{\reg},y\in G
, we have

\Theta_{\pi}(yxy^{-1})=\Theta_{\pi}(x).

We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for a proof of the above result. The locally constant function
\Theta_{\pi}
on
G_{\reg}
in the above theorem is called the
character
of
\pi
.

Let
\widetilde{G}
be a locally compact topological central extension of
G
by
\mu_{n}
. Let
\xi:\mu_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be any injective character of
\mu_{n}
. Let
(\pi,V)
be a representation of
\widetilde{G}
.
\pi
is called a
genuine
representation, if for
\epsilon\in\mu_{n}
,
g\in\widetilde{G}
, we have

\pi(\epsilon g)=\xi(\epsilon)\pi(g).

The above result of Harish-Chandra also holds in this setting. To be more precise, it can be shown that the distribution character of an irreducible admissible genuine representation
\pi
of
\widetilde{G}
is represented by a locally integrable function
\Theta_{\pi}
on
\widetilde{G}
which is locally constant on
\widetilde{G}_{\reg}=p^{-1}(G_{\reg})
(here
p:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow G
is the projection) and satisfies

\Theta_{\pi}(y^{-1}xy)=\Theta_{\pi}(x),\textrm{ for }x\in\widetilde{G}_{\reg},y\in\widetilde{G}.

We refer the reader to Theorem 4.3.2 and Corollary 4.3.3 in
[
10
]
where results about character theory for metaplectic groups are discussed. See also Theorem I.5.1 in
[
7
]
where the result is only mentioned.

Before we proceed further, we set up some notation and record a few properties of the character. Throughout we write
\mu=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}
for the Haar measure on
\widetilde{G}
. For
K
a compact open subgroup of
\widetilde{G}
, we let
e_{K}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{G})
to be the function

e_{K}(g)=\begin{cases}\mu(K)^{-1},&g\in K\\
0,&g\notin K\end{cases}.

For
g\in\widetilde{G}
and
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{G})
, the natural action of
\widetilde{G}
on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{G})
is denoted as
g.f
where

(g\cdot f)(x):=f(g^{-1}x).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(\pi,V)
be an irreducible admissible genuine representation of
\widetilde{G}.
Let
z
be an element in the center of
\widetilde{G}
and
\omega_{\pi}
be the central character of
\pi
. Then, the following statements hold:

1)

\pi(z\cdot f)=\omega_{\pi}(z)\pi(f),
for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{G}).

2)

\Theta_{\pi}(zg)=\omega_{\pi}(z)\Theta_{\pi}(g),
for all
g\in\widetilde{G}_{\reg}.

Proof.

Let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{G})
. We have,

(2.1)

\pi(z\cdot f)=\int_{\widetilde{G}}(z\cdot f)(g)\pi(g)dg=\int_{\widetilde{G}}f(z^{-1}g)\pi(g)dg.

By applying the change of variable
g\mapsto zg
in (
2.1
) and using
\pi(z)=\omega_{\pi}(z)1_{V},
we get

\pi(z\cdot f)=\omega_{\pi}(z)\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{G}}f(g)\pi(g)dg=\omega_{\pi}(z)\pi(f).

This proves 1). For
2)
, using the definition of the distribution character
\Theta_{\pi}
we have,

\Theta_{\pi}(z\cdot f)=\tr(\pi(z\cdot f))=\omega_{\pi}(z)\tr(\pi(f))=\omega_{\pi}(z)\Theta_{\pi}(f).

Thus we get

\displaystyle\Theta_{\pi}(z\cdot f)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\pi}(z)\Theta_{\pi}(f)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{G}}f(g)\omega_{\pi}(z)\Theta_{\pi}(g)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{G}}(z\cdot f)(g)\Theta_{\pi}(g)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{G}}f(z^{-1}g)\Theta_{\pi}(g)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{G}}f(g)\Theta_{\pi}(zg)dg.

It follows that

\Theta_{\pi}(zg)=\omega_{\pi}(z)\Theta_{\pi}(g).

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(\pi,V)
be an irreducible admissible genuine representation of
\widetilde{G}.
Then

\Theta_{\pi}(f)\neq 0

for some

.

Proof.

Let
0\neq u\in V
. Since

such that

\pi(e_{K})u=\int_{\widetilde{G}}e_{K}(g)\pi(g)udg=\mu(K)^{-1}\int_{K}\pi(g)udg=\mu(K)^{-1}\int_{K}udg=u.

It follows that
\pi(e_{K})

\pi(e_{K})\neq 0.

\displaystyle\pi(k)v_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{G}}e_{K}(g)\pi(kg)vdg

\displaystyle=\mu(K)^{-1}\int_{K}\pi(kg)vdg

\displaystyle=\mu(K)^{-1}\displaystyle\int_{K}\pi(g)vdg\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{ ($g\mapsto k^{-1}g$)}

2.4.
Some results about lifts of an automorphism of
G
to
\widetilde{G}

We recall a few results from
[
6
]
which we need in proving our main result. Let
\widetilde{G}
be a central extension of a group
G

A
. Let
p:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow G
be the projection map,
s:G\rightarrow\widetilde{G}
be a section of

and
\tau
be the 2-cocycle representing the class of this central extension in
\coh^{2}(G,A)
with respect to the section

. If

is an automorphism (resp. anti-automorphism) of

\tilde{f}:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow\widetilde{G}

p(\tilde{f}(g))=f(p(g)),\forall g\in\widetilde{G}.

Let
\mathcal{L}(f)
denote the set of all lifts of

\Aut(G)

f[\sigma]=[\sigma\circ(f^{-1}\times f^{-1})]
for any 2-cocycle
\sigma
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The set
\mathcal{L}(f)
is precisely described in terms of this action by the following:

1)

The set
\mathcal{L}(f)
is non-empty if and only if
f[\tau]=[\tau]
.

2)

If
\mathcal{L}(f)
is non-empty, then
\mathcal{L}(f)
is a principal homogeneous space for the group
\Hom(G,A)
under the action

(\phi.\tilde{f})(g)=\phi(p(g))\tilde{f}(g).

We also need the following result (see Corollary 1 in
[
6
]
for a proof) which discusses the continuity properties of the lift in the case when
G=\GL(m,F)
. We state it below for clarity.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
F
be a non-Archimedean local field and suppose that the group of
n^{th}
roots of unity in
F
has order
n
. Let
\langle\,,\,\rangle
be the
n^{th}
order Hilbert symbol on
F
and
\widetilde{\GL}(m)
the corresponding metaplectic group. Then the lift of any topological automorphism of
\GL(m)
to
\widetilde{\GL}(m)
is also a topological automorphism.

3.
Lift of the standard involution

Let
G=\GL(2,F)
and
\widetilde{G}=\widetilde{\GL}(2,F)
be the
n
-fold metaplectic cover of
G
. Let
\tau
be the standard involution on
G
. In this section, we define a lift
\sigma
of

\tau
and show that it is an involutive anti-automorphism of
\widetilde{G}
. We also discuss an important property of this lift (see Theorem
3.9
) which is crucial in proving the main result of this paper.

For
\lambda\in F^{\times}
and
g=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in G
, we let
u(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda&0\\
0&-\lambda\end{bmatrix}
and
\Delta(g)=\det(g)
. It is easy to see that

\tau(g)=w_{0}g^{\top}w_{0}=u(\Delta(g))g^{-1}u(1).

For
\epsilon\in\mu_{n}
, we denote the element
(I_{2},\epsilon)\in\widetilde{G}
as
\epsilon
. Let
\tilde{u}(\lambda)=(u(\lambda),1)
and
\tilde{z}(\lambda)=(\lambda I_{2},1)
where
I_{2}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix. We extend
\Delta
to
\widetilde{G}
by
\Delta((g,\epsilon))=\Delta(g)
.
For
h=(g,\epsilon)\in\widetilde{G}
define

(3.1)

\sigma(h)=\tilde{u}(\Delta(h))h^{-1}\tilde{u}(1).

Before discussing the properties of
\sigma
, we first prove a proposition which is used to study the properties of

\sigma
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in F^{\times}
, and
h\in\widetilde{G}
. Then

(i)

h\tilde{z}(\lambda)=\langle\Delta(h),\lambda\rangle\;\tilde{z}(\lambda)h
.

(ii)

\tilde{z}(\lambda_{1})\tilde{z}(\lambda_{2})=\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle\tilde{z}(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})
.

(iii)

\tilde{u}(\lambda_{1})\tilde{u}(\lambda_{2})=\langle\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2}\rangle\tilde{z}(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})
.

(iv)

\tilde{u}(\lambda)^{-1}=\tilde{u}(\lambda^{-1})
.

(v)

\tilde{u}(\lambda_{1})\tilde{z}(\lambda_{2})=\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle\tilde{u}(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})
.

Proof.

(i)

Let
h=\left(\begin{bmatrix}[r]a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix},\epsilon\right)
. It is easy to see that

\tilde{z}(\lambda)h=\Bigg(\begin{bmatrix}[r]a\lambda&b\lambda\\
c\lambda&d\lambda\end{bmatrix},c\left(\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\right)\epsilon\Bigg),

and

h\tilde{z}(\lambda)=\Bigg(\begin{bmatrix}[r]a\lambda&b\lambda\\
c\lambda&d\lambda\end{bmatrix},c\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}\right)\epsilon\Bigg).

Note that
c\left(\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{cases}\langle\lambda,c\rangle,&c\neq 0\\
\langle\lambda,d\rangle,&c=0.\end{cases}

Therefore

\displaystyle\langle\Delta(h),\lambda\rangle c\left(\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\langle\Delta(h),\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda,c\rangle,&c\neq 0\\
\langle\Delta(h),\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda,d\rangle,&c=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\langle\lambda,\Delta(h)^{-1}c\rangle,&c\neq 0\\
\langle\lambda,\Delta(h)^{-1}d\rangle,&c=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=c\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}\right).

The result follows.

(ii)

Since
c(z(\lambda_{1}),z(\lambda_{2}))=c(\lambda_{1}I_{2},\lambda_{2}I_{2})=\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle
, we have

\tilde{z}(\lambda_{1})\tilde{z}(\lambda_{2})=(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}I_{2},\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle)=\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle\tilde{z}(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}).

(iii)

Using

c(u(\lambda_{1}),u(\lambda_{2}))=c\left(\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&-\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\langle\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2}\rangle,

we get

\tilde{u}(\lambda_{1})\tilde{u}(\lambda_{2})=(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}I_{2},\langle\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2}\rangle)=\langle\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2}\rangle\tilde{z}(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}).

(iv)

We have
\tilde{u}(\lambda)=(u(\lambda),1)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(\lambda)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\big(u(\lambda)^{-1},c(u(\lambda),u(\lambda)^{-1})^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(u(\lambda)^{-1},c(u(\lambda),u(\lambda^{-1}))^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(u(\lambda^{-1}),\langle-\lambda,\lambda\rangle^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(u(\lambda^{-1}),1\big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\lambda^{-1}).

(v)

It is easy to see that,

c(u(\lambda_{1}),z(\lambda_{2}))=c\left(\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\langle\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}^{-1}\rangle=\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle.

Thus we have,

\tilde{u}(\lambda_{1})\tilde{z}(\lambda_{2})=\Bigg(\begin{bmatrix}[r]\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix},\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle\Bigg)=\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle\tilde{u}(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}).

∎

Let
\sigma:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow\widetilde{G}
be defined as in (
3.1
). We record some properties satisfied by
\sigma
in the proposition below.

Lemma 3.2
.

The map
\sigma
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\sigma
is an anti-automorphism.

(2)

\sigma(\epsilon)=\epsilon^{-1}
for any
\epsilon\in\mu_{n}
.

(3)

\sigma
is an involution.

(4)

\sigma(h^{-1})=\sigma(h)^{-1}
for all
h\in\widetilde{G}
.

(5)

\sigma
is a lift of
\tau
.

Proof.

(1)

We use properties (i), (iii), and (v) from Proposition
3.1
. For
h_{1},h_{2}\in\widetilde{G}
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(h_{1}h_{2})

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\Delta(h_{1}h_{2}))(h_{1}h_{2})^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\Delta(h_{2})\Delta(h_{1}))(h_{1}h_{2})^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=\left<\Delta(h_{1}),\Delta(h_{2})\right>\tilde{u}(\Delta(h_{2}))\tilde{z}(\Delta(h_{1}))h_{2}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\Delta(h_{2}))h_{2}^{-1}\tilde{z}(\Delta(h_{1}))h_{1}^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\Delta(h_{2}))h_{2}^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)\tilde{u}(\Delta(h_{1}))h_{1}^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=\sigma(h_{2})\sigma(h_{1}).

(2)

Let
1\neq\epsilon\in\mu_{n}
be any non-trivial element in
\mu_{n}
. Identifying
\epsilon
with (
I_{2},\epsilon
), we obtain
\Delta(\epsilon)=1
. We have

\displaystyle\sigma(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\Delta(\epsilon))\epsilon^{-1}\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=(u(\Delta(\epsilon)),1)(I_{2},\epsilon)^{-1}(u(1),1)

\displaystyle=\left(u(1),1\right)\left(I_{2},\epsilon^{-1}\right)\left(u(1),1\right)

\displaystyle=\left(u(1),\epsilon^{-1}\right)\left(u(1),1\right)

(since
c\left(u(1),I_{2}\right)=1
)

\displaystyle=(I_{2},\epsilon^{-1})

(since
c\left(u(1),u(1)\right)=1
)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-1}.

(3)

For
h=(g,\epsilon)\in\widetilde{G}
, observe that
\Delta(\sigma(h))=\Delta(u(\Delta(g))g^{-1}u(1))=\Delta(h)
. Using the properties (i)-(iv) mentioned in Proposition
3.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma((\sigma(h)))

\displaystyle=\sigma(\tilde{u}(\Delta(h))h^{-1}\tilde{u}(1))

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\Delta(h))\tilde{u}(1)h\tilde{u}(\Delta(h)^{-1})\tilde{u}(1)

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta(h),-1\rangle\tilde{z}(\Delta(h))h\langle\Delta(h)^{-1},-1\rangle\tilde{z}(\Delta(h)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta(h),\Delta(h)\rangle h\tilde{z}(\Delta(h))\tilde{z}(\Delta(h)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta(h),\Delta(h)\rangle h\langle\Delta(h),\Delta(h)^{-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=h.

(4)

It follows directly from the fact that
\sigma
is an anti-automorphism.

(5)

It is enough to consider the case when
h=(g,1)
, with
g\in G
. Indeed, if we have
\sigma(g,1)=(x,\xi)
, then
\sigma(g,\epsilon)=(x,\epsilon^{-1}\xi)
, using (1) and (2). This implies that
x=(p\circ\sigma)(g,\epsilon)=(p\circ\sigma)(g,1)
. To show that
\sigma
is a lift of
\tau
, it is enough to show that
(p\circ\sigma)(h)=(\tau\circ p)(h)
for
h=(g,1)\in\widetilde{G}
.
We now evaluate
\sigma
, depending on whether
c
is non-zero or zero. If
c\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(h)

\displaystyle=\sigma(g,1)

\displaystyle=(u(\Delta(g)),1)(g^{-1},1)(u(1),1)

\displaystyle=(u(\Delta(g))g^{-1},\left<\Delta(g),c\right>)(u(1),1)

\displaystyle=(u(\Delta(g)g^{-1}u(1),\left<\Delta(g),c\right>)

\displaystyle=(\tau(g),\left<\Delta(g),c\right>),

and if
c=0
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(h)

\displaystyle=\sigma(g,1)

\displaystyle=(u(ad),1)(g^{-1},\left<a,d\right>)(u(1),1)

\displaystyle=(u(ad)g^{-1},\left<d,a\right>\left<d,d\right>\left<a,d\right>)(u(1),1)

\displaystyle=(u(ad)g^{-1},\left<d,d\right>)(u(1),1)

\displaystyle=(u(ad)g^{-1}u(1),\left<d,d\right>\left<d,-1\right>)

\displaystyle=(\tau(g),1).

In both the cases, we see that
(p\circ\sigma)(h)=\tau(g)=(\tau\circ p)(h)
. Hence the result follows.

Remark 3.3
.

We have used the following computations in part (5) of Lemma

Lemma 3.4
.

, let

(2)

Using the cocycle property, we have for

(3.2)

c(g_{1}g_{2},g_{3})c(g_{1},g_{2})=c(g_{1},g_{2}g_{3})c(g_{2},g_{3}).

and
g_{3}=xgx^{-1}
in (

(3.3)

\displaystyle c(xg^{-1}x^{-1},xgx^{-1})=

\displaystyle c(x,x^{-1})c(g^{-1}x^{-1},xgx^{-1})c(x,g^{-1}x^{-1})^{-1}.

) again with

g_{3}=gx^{-1}
and then again with

\displaystyle c(g^{-1}x^{-1},xgx^{-1})=

\displaystyle c(g^{-1},gx^{-1})c(g^{-1}x^{-1},x)c(x,gx^{-1})^{-1}\vskip 2.84544pt

(3.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[c(g^{-1},g)c(g,x^{-1})^{-1}]c(g^{-1}x^{-1},x)c(x,gx^{-1})^{-1}.

Now, (1) follows by (

) and (

Proof of (2) is a direct application of (

Theorem 3.9
.

For
h=(g,\eta)\in\widetilde{G},
we have

\sigma(h)=c(g^{-1},g)^{-2}\eta^{-2}zhz^{-1},

for some
z\in\widetilde{G}.

Proof.

Let
g\in G
and set
h=(g,1).
By Lemma
3.6
, it is enough to show that
h\in S
. Suppose that
g=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\alpha\end{bmatrix}
, for some
\alpha\in F^{\times}.
Take
u=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
, and write
\widetilde{u}=(u,1).
Since
\tau(g)=g,
by (3) of Remark
3.3
, we get
\sigma(h)=h.
It is easy to see that
c(ug,u^{-1})=1=c(u,u^{-1})
and
c(u,g)=\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{2}=1.
Hence,
\widetilde{u}h\widetilde{u}^{-1}=h.
Observe that
c(g^{-1},g)=\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle.
Thus,

\sigma(h)=c(g^{-1},g)^{-2}\widetilde{u}h\widetilde{u}^{-1}.

Suppose that
g
is not a scalar matrix. Let
g_{1},

g_{2}
and
g_{3}
be as in Lemma
3.7
. Using the rational canonical form, it follows that
g
is conjugate to a matrix of the type either
g_{1},

g_{2},
or
g_{3}
. We discuss these cases separately.

Case (1)
: Assume that
g
is conjugate to
g_{1},
where
g_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&v\\
1&w\end{bmatrix}
. Suppose
g=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{bmatrix}
. Since
g
is conjugate to
g_{1},
we have

v=-\Delta(g)=-\det(g)\quad\text{and}\quad w=\tr(g)=\alpha+\delta.

Also,
\gamma\neq 0
by Lemma
3.5
. Consider
x=\begin{bmatrix}0&v\gamma^{-1}\\
1&\delta\gamma^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
. It is easy to verify that
xgx^{-1}=g_{1}.
By Lemma

3.1.
Other lifts of the standard involution and their properties

In this section, we define other lifts of the standard involution and show that they also satisfy analogous properties like the lift
\sigma
of
\tau
.

Lemma 3.12
.

For each
\alpha\in F^{\times}
and
h\in\widetilde{G}
, let

(3.14)

\sigma_{\alpha}(h)=\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle\sigma(h).

Then

(1)

\sigma_{\alpha}
is an anti-automorphism.

(2)

\sigma_{\alpha}
is an involution.

(3)

\sigma_{\alpha}
is a lift of
\tau
.

Proof.

(1)

For
g,h\in\widetilde{G}
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}(gh)

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(gh)\rangle\sigma(gh)

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(g)\Delta(h)\rangle\sigma(h)\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(g)\rangle\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle\sigma(h)\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle\sigma(h)\langle\alpha,\Delta(g)\rangle\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\alpha}(h)\sigma_{\alpha}(g).

(2)

Let
\sigma_{\alpha}(h)=y
. We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}(\sigma_{\alpha}(h))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(y)\rangle\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle\sigma(\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle\sigma(h))

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle\langle\alpha,\Delta(h)\rangle^{-1}\sigma(\sigma(h))

\displaystyle=h.

(3)

Let
h=(g,\epsilon)\in\widetilde{G}
. Suppose that
\sigma((g,1))=(\tau(g),\xi)
. We have

\tau(p(h))=\tau(p((g,\epsilon)))=\tau(g)

and

p(\sigma(h))=p(\sigma((g,\epsilon)))=p((\tau(g),\epsilon^{-1}\xi))=\tau(g).

Thus
\sigma_{\alpha}
is a lift of
\tau
.

∎

Remark 3.13
.

We have used the following observation in part (2) of Lemma

Theorem 3.14
.

Let

. We have

\sigma_{\alpha}(h)=c(g^{-1},g)^{-2}\eta^{-2}zhz^{-1},

for some

Suppose
