GCFR: graph contrastive fault representation for robust diagnosis in power communication networks
Authors: Baohao Chen, Guosheng Lu, Yifeng Zhu, Chengyu Liu, Bingdong Qin, Jianxue Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work addresses fault diagnosis in power communication networks (PCNs) under three practical challenges–limited labels, evolving topology, and noisy alarms–by proposing GCFR, a graph-contrastive fault representation framework. GCFR learns robust node embeddings via lightweight, semantics-preserving multi-view augmentations (feature masking, edge dropping, and mild topology perturbations) and a mini-batch InfoNCE objective, followed by a small classification head for deployment-friendly inference. To withstand structural drift, we incorporate a time-consistency regularizer that stabilizes representations across successive snapshots. We further enhance robustness with domain-aware alarm-propagation features and a lightweight wavelet denoising module that mitigates timestamp jitter, missing events, and spurious alarms. For reliable decision-making in operations, we provide approximate uncertainty quantification and selective prediction, enabling explicit control of the coverage–risk trade-off. Experiments on two benchmark graphs (GridGraph and Texas2000) and an anonymized real-world slice show that GCFR maintains strong recall with scarce labels and consistently outperforms strong supervised and signal-processing baselines under topology perturbations and alarm noise. Overall, GCFR offers a low-overhead, deployment-oriented solution that couples label efficiency with calibrated, risk-aware predictions for PCN fault diagnosis.Keywords: Graph neural networks, Power communication networks, Fault diagnosis, Contrastive learning, Graph data augmentationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Modern smart grids rely on interdependent power communication networks (PCNs) as specialized infrastructures for real-time monitoring and control1,2. PCNs provide critical services such as protection signal transmission, control command delivery, and data exchange scheduling that underpin stable grid operations3. Due to this tight coupling, faults in the PCN may propagate to the physical grid layer, potentially triggering system-wide disruptions. As the scale and complexity of PCNs continue to grow, accurate and timely fault diagnosis has become essential to ensure grid reliability and operational safety4,5.

A variety of fault diagnosis methods have been proposed for PCNs, ranging from early rule-based systems and expert knowledge inference6 to data-driven machine learning techniques7. Traditional approaches, such as expert systems, rely on handcrafted rules and domain knowledge, which are difficult to maintain and scale in evolving network environments8,9. With the rise of big data and automation, machine learning algorithms like decision trees, support vector machines, and neural networks have been applied to analyze alarm patterns10. More recently, graph neural networks (GNNs) have shown promise in modeling the topological and feature information of PCNs11,12. Although these methods demonstrate strong performance under certain conditions, they face limitations when applied to dynamic, large-scale networks with scarce labeled data13.

In practical settings, PCN fault diagnosis faces several inherent challenges. Beyond dynamic topology and label scarcity, alarm streams often exhibit timestamp jitter, missing bursts, and spurious cascades, which degrade learned representations and destabilize thresholds. Meanwhile, distribution shifts across regions and periods further stress generalization. These realities call for models that are label-efficient, robust under temporal churn and alarm noise, and calibrated enough to offer controllable recall–precision trade-offs in operation.

To address these issues, we propose GCFR, which couples multi-view graph augmentations with a mini-batch InfoNCE objective to learn robust node embeddings, followed by a lightweight classifier for deployment-friendly inference. Beyond the base design, GCFR adds a temporal-consistency regularizer to stabilize representations under topology evolution; domain-aware alarm-propagation features and a lightweight DWT denoiser to mitigate timestamp jitter, missing events, and spurious alarms; and approximate uncertainty quantification with selective prediction to calibrate decisions and explicitly trade coverage for reliability. These additions require minimal changes to training and modest runtime overhead while improving robustness and operational control.

Related work

Fault diagnosis in power communication networks

Power communication networks (PCNs) serve as the backbone infrastructure for modern smart grids, enabling real-time data exchange for control, monitoring, and protection tasks14. As the scale and complexity of PCNs increase, fault events–such as equipment failure or link disconnection–pose significant risks to grid reliability15,16. Therefore, rapid and accurate fault diagnosis in PCNs is critical to maintaining system stability and minimizing economic losses17. Early research primarily relied on traditional methods such as expert systems and rule-based inference. For instance, Gao et al.18 proposed a codebook-based fault diagnosis model that maps alarm patterns to root causes using Boolean logic and prior knowledge. Similarly, Ma et al.19 developed an expert-system-driven approach that utilizes a predefined knowledge base to infer fault sources via rule matching. While effective in structured environments, these methods are difficult to scale and maintain, especially as network configurations evolve.

To address the limitations of static rule-driven methods, data-driven techniques have gained attention. Machine learning algorithms such as decision trees20, fuzzy neural networks21, and object-oriented Bayesian networks22 have been applied to automatically extract patterns from alarm data and perform classification. More recently, knowledge-graph-enhanced approaches have emerged. For example, Zhao and Jia23 proposed an unsupervised deep learning method that combines a feature extractor with structured domain knowledge to diagnose rotating machinery faults. These advancements mark a paradigm shift from manual diagnosis toward intelligent fault identification that integrates domain semantics, graph structures, and learning-based inference. Nevertheless, the robustness and adaptability of these methods remain challenges when applied to large-scale, partially observable, or dynamically changing PCNs, which motivates further exploration of graph-based learning frameworks.

Graph neural networks in power communication networks

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have shown great promise in fault diagnosis tasks within power communication networks (PCNs), owing to their ability to capture both topological structure and node-level feature information. As PCNs can be naturally modeled as graphs, with nodes representing devices and edges indicating communication or control relationships, GNNs offer an effective means to learn expressive node representations for fault identification. Recent studies have applied various GNN architectures such as graph convolutional networks (GCNs)24, graph attention networks (GATs)25, and meta-learning-based models26 to extract hierarchical and relational features in communication networks. For instance, Chen et al.27 proposed a GCN-based framework for power distribution systems, achieving robust fault localization even under measurement noise and missing data. Similarly, Long et al.28 developed an adaptive GAT model based on meta-learning to improve generalization under few-shot scenarios. These studies demonstrate the applicability and effectiveness of GNNs in real-world diagnosis systems.

In addition to supervised GNN models, self-supervised graph contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful alternative for improving representation robustness without requiring labeled data. Methods such as Deep Graph Infomax (DGI)29, GRACE30, and GCA31 leverage graph augmentations (e.g., node masking, edge perturbation) to generate multiple views of the same graph and encourage alignment of their representations through contrastive objectives. These techniques are especially beneficial for fault diagnosis in PCNs, where labeled fault samples are often scarce and topology may vary over time. The NSMFD method32 serves as a representative GNN-based approach tailored for PCNs, utilizing neighborhood sampling and multi-hop aggregation to classify faulty nodes. However, its reliance on single-view supervision limits its adaptability. In contrast, our proposed GCFR model integrates graph augmentation and contrastive learning to enhance representation discrimination and fault detection performance across diverse PCN scenarios.

Graph data augmentation strategies

To improve the discriminative capacity of node representations, we design three graph data augmentation strategies that apply controlled perturbations to the original graph structure, generating different views for contrastive learning. These strategies include node masking, edge dropping, and topology perturbation. By injecting randomness into the input while preserving essential graph characteristics, the model is encouraged to learn robust and informative node embeddings that capture subtle structural and feature-level differences useful for fault detection. The details of each augmentation strategy are described below.

Topology perturbation

Topology perturbation introduces structural variation by randomly modifying the graph’s edge configuration. For example, a new edge can be added between two previously unconnected nodes, or an existing edge endpoint can be replaced with another randomly selected node. These operations lead to minor topological differences, forming a new view with distinct local structures. By altering the connection patterns, this strategy provides additional perspectives for contrastive learning, enabling the model to distinguish faulty nodes based on structural changes. The perturbation is carefully constrained to small-scale modifications to avoid destroying essential properties of the graph.

In summary, the proposed augmentation strategies apply lightweight random perturbations to node attributes and graph topology, generating diverse yet semantically consistent views of the original graph. Node masking simulates feature incompleteness, edge dropping alters local structures, and topology perturbation introduces alternative connection patterns. These augmented views form the foundation for contrastive learning, enabling the model to capture discriminative patterns that distinguish faulty nodes from normal ones.

Experiments

Experimental setup

We conduct experiments on two benchmark datasets of power communication networks: GridGraph33 and Texas200034. The GridGraph dataset contains 446 nodes and 597 edges, with each node represented by an 8-dimensional feature vector. The Texas2000 dataset is larger, consisting of 2000 nodes and 2675 edges, and each node is associated with a 10-dimensional feature vector. In both datasets, nodes are labeled as either normal or faulty, which enables the formulation of a node-level binary classification task for fault detection.

Each dataset is randomly split into training and validation sets with a ratio of 8:2, where the validation set is used for hyperparameter tuning and performance evaluation. To ensure fair comparisons among different methods, all experiments are conducted under a consistent software and hardware environment: CentOS 7.9 operating system, NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU with 32GB memory, Python 3.9, and PyTorch 2.0.

For implementation, we adopt a mini-batch training strategy. Specifically, we use a batch size of 64 for GridGraph and 128 for Texas2000 to accommodate their different graph sizes. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001. We train for up to 100 epochs and apply early stopping to prevent overfitting. The embedding dimension is 128, and other hyperparameters are tuned based on the validation set.

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of fault detection, we adopt multiple metrics, including accuracy, F1-score, recall, false alarm rate (FAR), and false negative rate (FNR)35. Specifically, accuracy and F1-score reflect the overall classification quality, recall measures the proportion of faulty nodes correctly detected, FAR indicates the rate of normal nodes misclassified as faulty, and FNR quantifies the proportion of faulty nodes mistakenly predicted as normal. Ideally, higher accuracy, F1-score, and recall are preferred, while lower FAR and FNR indicate better fault detection reliability.

At , GCFR achieves F1 of 0.948 versus 0.914 for Supervised-only and 0.930 for NSMFD, together with a lower FNR of 0.051. By , GCFR recovers to F1 of 0.971 and FNR of 0.036, approaching the full-label regime. In all cases, the standard deviations are small, suggesting stable training behavior across seeds under partial labels.

These results support three observations (cf. Table 4). First, contrastive learning provides strong label efficiency: most of the full-label performance is recovered by to , especially in recall and FNR, which are critical for fault diagnosis. Second, the gains are largest in low-label regimes and on the more heterogeneous Texas2000 graph, consistent with the expectation that representation learning from unlabeled structure mitigates overfitting to scarce labels. Third, restricting all methods to identical labeled budgets yields a fair comparison; GCFR’s consistent advantage indicates that the learned representations transfer well even when the supervised signal is weak. From a deployment perspective, these findings suggest that effective operation is possible with modest annotation efforts, while preserving the computational profile of the baseline pipeline because only the label budget is varied.

As shown in Table 5, the static GCFR degrades gracefully as churn increases, but adding temporal consistency systematically recovers both F1 and FNR with a small constant overhead (about 4–6 percent wall-time and about 1 percent peak memory). The temporal encoder variant GCFR–TGAT provides slightly higher accuracy than TempReg in most cases, but the improvement margin is small and comes with a noticeable constant-factor overhead (about 16–19 percent time and 10–12 percent memory per epoch; see Table 5). Given the reliability–efficiency objective of PCN fault diagnosis, the lightweight consistency regularization attains a better trade-off: it preserves the baseline pipeline and code path, requires tuning only one extra weight, and yields most of the temporal gains at a fraction of the cost.

Two observations follow. First, temporal drift primarily hurts recall by disrupting neighborhood semantics; regularizing consecutive embeddings stabilizes node representations across evolving topologies and reduces missed detections. Second, while time-encoding encoders can push the upper bound slightly further, their constant-factor overhead and added engineering complexity do not justify replacing the default GCN in our deployment setting. We therefore keep GCN as the default encoder and enable temporal consistency as a plug-in for deployments where topology churn exceeds a few percent per window.

Domain-aware alarm features and denoising robustness

We enrich node attributes with domain-aware alarm propagation descriptors computed from SCADA/NMS logs within fixed windows : (1) propagation speed (median hop time), (2) fan-out (unique downstream alarms), (3) local burstiness (Fano factor of inter-arrival times), (4) persistence (fraction persisting across consecutive windows), and (5) counts of common 2/3-hop motifs. These features are appended to the original inputs; the encoder and training objective remain unchanged.

Motivated by HVDC protection studies that denoise and characterize transient signals with discrete wavelet transforms (DWT) prior to ANN/LSTM classifiers40–42, we add a lightweight DWT-based denoiser to alarm time series. Concretely, we apply a Daubechies-4 wavelet, level-3 decomposition, and soft-thresholding with the universal threshold estimated from the median absolute deviation at the finest scale. For comparison, we also include a simple exponential moving average (EMA) smoother (span 5).

Instead of replicating full protection schemes (which depend on domain-specific relays and nonpublic code), we implement two practical baselines aligned with the preprocessing in40–42: (i) DWT+ANN–two-layer MLP (hidden 128, ReLU, dropout 0.2) on wavelet summary statistics (energy per subband), and (ii) DWT+LSTM–one-layer LSTM (hidden 128) on the denoised sequences followed by a linear head. All methods use identical train/val/test splits, augmentations, and schedules where applicable. We report mean ± std over three seeds.

We then test robustness under alarm noise and system dynamics. The “mild noise” regime applies timestamp jitter s, 5% missing, and 3% spurious alarms; the “heavy noise” regime uses s, 10% missing, and 5% spurious.

Across Tables 9, 10, domain-aware propagation features consistently reduce missed detections with negligible overhead. Wavelet denoising further stabilizes the features under timestamp jitter, missing, and spurious alarms, aligning with findings in HVDC protection research40–42. The GCFR variants outperform DWT+ANN and DWT+LSTM baselines in both clean and noisy settings; the gap widens under heavy noise, indicating complementarity between structure-aware representation learning and signal denoising. From a deployment view, we recommend enabling alarm propagation features by default and adding the DWT denoiser when alarm streams are noisy or coarsely time-stamped.

Uncertainty quantification for reliable diagnostics

We quantify predictive uncertainty for fault diagnostics using three lightweight, practical approximations: (i) Monte Carlo (MC) dropout at test time with stochastic forward passes; (ii) a small deep ensemble with independently trained GCNs; and (iii) temperature scaling learned on the validation set. The core training pipeline remains unchanged. Reported results are mean ± std over three seeds. Inference-time overhead is given relative to the single deterministic model.

Beyond calibration, predictive entropy enables risk-controlled deployment via selective prediction. We report performance at two coverage targets (keep the most confident 95% or 90% of test samples; abstain on the rest and escalate to operators).

Table 11 shows that all three approximations substantially improve calibration (lower ECE/NLL) while maintaining or slightly improving F1/FNR. Temperature scaling is nearly free to deploy and already halves ECE on both datasets. MC dropout and deep ensembles provide further gains, with ensembles offering the best calibration at a moderate inference cost. Table 12 demonstrates a practical reliability control: by abstaining on the most uncertain 5–10% samples, FNR drops markedly (e.g., 0.079 0.066 at 95% coverage on GridGraph), which is desirable in operational settings where missed faults are costly. In summary, calibration modules can be layered on GCFR without retraining or changes to the encoder, enabling risk-aware decision thresholds and escalation policies at deployment.

On GridGraph, moving from no augmentation to M+E+T reduces FNR by 0.016 (0.095 0.079); On Texas2000, the reduction is 0.009 (0.044 0.035), consistent with the larger graph being more tolerant to moderate perturbations. The near-additive behavior aligns with the single-factor sensitivity trends reported earlier: Masking and edge dropping contribute most of the benefit, while topology perturbation adds a consistent but smaller improvement. For deployment, if augmentation must be simplified, M+E is a near-optimal two-component choice; Otherwise, keeping all three at their default strengths yields the best reliability profile with minimal additional complexity.

Overall performance. Attention-based aggregation delivers modest yet consistent gains across both graphs. Computational overhead. GCFR–GAT adds a constant-factor cost due to per-edge scoring and neighborhood softmax, increasing per-epoch wall-time by roughly 12–14% and peak memory by 7–9%, while the rest of the pipeline remains unchanged. This overhead is non-negligible for repeated retraining and large-graph inference.

Rationale for retaining GCN as the default. First, reliability at lower cost: the GCN-based GCFR already achieves a strong false-negative profile (low FNR), especially on Texas2000; GAT further lowers FNR only marginally, yielding diminishing returns under typical deployment budgets. Second, robustness–efficiency under homophily and label scarcity: many operational sub-networks exhibit moderate homophily and regular neighborhoods, where uniform aggregation is a strong inductive bias; in such regimes, the observed GAT gains (no more than 0.5–0.8 percentage points on F1/Recall in our runs) do not justify the extra overhead, and the variance-reducing, compute-efficient GCN is a safer default for periodic retraining and online scoring. Third, simplicity and scalability: the GCN encoder matches our near-linear training complexity in graph size and a lower memory footprint, simplifying scaling to larger PCNs and facilitating roll-outs on commodity GPUs. Accordingly, we retain GCN as the default encoder and position GAT as an optional plug-in for scenarios characterized by noisy or heterogeneous neighborhoods, where its modest gains justify the additional cost.

As seen in Table 24, GCFR shares the same asymptotic order as standard GCN-based methods; its constant factors come from processing two augmented views and the projection head, while the in-batch similarity term is minor on sparse PCNs. Among variants, SimCLR-style is nearly cost-neutral; temporal consistency and DWT-based denoising add small constant factors; BYOL-style and attention encoders increase cost due to additional computation (momentum target/predictor or attention mixing); deeper GCNs scale almost linearly and may suffer from over-smoothing. At inference (Table 25), temperature scaling provides near-free calibration, while small Bayesian approximations (ensembles or MC dropout) trade compute for improved calibration and selective prediction.

Conclusion

This work presented GCFR, a deployment-oriented framework for fault diagnosis in power communication networks that learns robust node representations via multi-view graph contrast and a lightweight classifier. Beyond the base design, we incorporated a temporal-consistency regularizer to stabilize embeddings under topology evolution, domain-aware alarm-propagation features, and a lightweight DWT denoiser to withstand timestamp jitter, missing events, and spurious cascades, as well as approximate uncertainty calibration with selective prediction to explicitly control the coverage–risk trade-off. Comprehensive experiments on two benchmark graphs and an anonymized real-world slice show that GCFR maintains strong recall under scarce labels, improves robustness to topology churn and alarm noise, and consistently outperforms strong supervised and signal-processing baselines with modest training and inference overhead.

We focus on practical aspects that facilitate broader adoption. Future work includes expanding case studies to additional regions and seasons, enriching visualization and interpretability tools for operator training, packaging GCFR with preset configurations and monitoring dashboards, and conducting systematic latency/energy profiling across diverse hardware backends. We also plan to broaden comparisons with recent graph encoders and self-supervised objectives, and to run user studies on workflow integration and alert-triage ergonomics.
