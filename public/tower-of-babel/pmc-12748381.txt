Exploring the causal relationship between gut microbiota, circulating inflammatory proteins, and colorectal neuroendocrine tumors: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jie Cui, Tie-Jun Wang, Yue-Chen Zhao, Xuan-Peng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gut microbiota may influence the occurrence and development of colorectal neuroendocrine tumors (CR-NETs), with inflammatory mediators potentially playing an important intermediary role. However, the current research evidence is insufficient to support this view. In this study, we extracted SNP data from different Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) summary statistics. Using bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), two-step MR, and multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) methods, we explored the causal relationship between gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs. Our study found that five types of gut microbiota (family Acidaminococcaceae, family Porphyromonadaceae, genus Oscillospira, genus Paraprevotella, genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group) and five inflammatory mediators (C-C motif chemokine Ligand 4, C-C motif chemokine Ligand 23, TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand, C-X-C motif chemokine Ligand 5, Monocyte chemoattractant protein 2) have a causal relationship with CR-NETs. Additionally, C-C motif chemokine Ligand 23 (CCL23) mediated the causal effect of family Porphyromonadaceae on CR-NETs (with a mediation proportion of 6.3%), while C-C motif chemokine Ligand 4 (CCL4) mediated the causal effect of genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group on CR-NETs (with a mediation proportion of 4.7%). There is a causal relationship between gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins in the development of CR-NETs, with CCL23 and CCL4 possibly playing a mediating role.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03207-6.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are highly heterogeneous tumors originating from neuroendocrine cells and peptidergic neurons, capable of synthesizing and secreting monoamine substances. The gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, and lungs are common sites for NETs, although they can occur in neuroendocrine tissues widely distributed throughout the body [1, 2]. NETs account for approximately 0.5% of all cancers, and their incidence has gradually increased over the past few decades, with a crude incidence rate now reaching about 2 per 100,000 people [3]. Based on their main clinical features and endocrine characteristics, NETs can be divided into functional and non-functional neuroendocrine tumors [4]. Functional NETs produce various hormones, such as serotonin, leading to a series of symptoms known as carcinoid syndrome (CS) [5]. Colorectal neuroendocrine tumors (CR-NETs) originate from neuroendocrine cells in the intestine. Colonic neuroendocrine tumors typically present as larger masses and are often diagnosed with local lymph node or distant metastases. In contrast, rectal neuroendocrine tumors usually appear as small, localized, non-functional masses with rare metastasis [6].

The human gut is colonized by a high density of microorganisms, which form a symbiotic relationship with humans under normal conditions and play important roles in regulating metabolic functions and maintaining immune homeostasis [7]. When the gut microbiota becomes imbalanced, with significant changes in the proportions of various species, the gut microbiota may interact with the tumor microenvironment, affecting tumor differentiation and progression [8]. Studies have shown that gut microorganisms can promote cancer development by activating pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive pathways. For example, gut microorganisms can be recognized by Toll-like receptors, activating downstream NF-κB signaling pathways and producing pro-inflammatory factors [9]. Furthermore, studies indicate that intestinal endocrine cells may proliferate and undergo tumor transformation due to excessive stimulation from chronic inflammation, with TNF-α and IL-2 closely associated with susceptibility to CR-NETs [10]. CR-NETs exhibit a highly vascularized state and can express various cytokine receptors, serving as markers of their malignancy [11]. Therefore, we hypothesize that there may be a potential causal relationship between gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs, and that gut microbiota may promote the occurrence of CR-NETs through specific inflammatory proteins.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological research method that uses genetic variations, typically single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables to analyze the potential causal relationship between exposures and outcomes, thus avoiding the effects of reverse causality and confounding factors [12, 13]. Therefore, compared to traditional observational studies, the results of MR studies are more reliable. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have used MR methods to reveal potential causal relationships between gut microbiota and various diseases, providing new insights into the mechanisms of disease occurrence [14]. In this study, we aim to determine whether there is a causal relationship between gut microbiota and CR-NETs, and to assess whether inflammatory proteins mediate this process and their extent of influence. We utilized the summary statistics from the most extensive genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs, conducting bidirectional MR analysis and mediation analysis. Through these analyses, we explored the potential causal relationships among these variables, providing theoretical foundations for the early diagnosis and treatment of CR-NETs.

Materials and methods

Study design

We utilized summary statistics from GWAS to investigate the genetic associations between gut microbiota and CR-NETs, primarily focusing on individuals of European ancestry. Since all initial studies obtained ethical approval and all participants provided informed consent, further ethical review for this study was not required. This MR study consists of two main aspects: (1) exploring the potential causal relationships between 211 types of gut microbiota and CR-NETs through bidirectional two-sample MR analysis; (2) assessing the mediating effect of inflammatory proteins between gut microbiota and CR-NETs through a two-step MR approach followed by multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) analysis. In the process of Mendelian randomization research, the selection of instrumental variables (IVs) must satisfy three key assumptions: (1) IVs are strongly associated with the exposure; (2) IVs are not associated with any confounding factors that influence both the exposure and the outcome; (3) IVs affect the outcome only through the exposure [15] (Fig. 1). The writing of this study follows the guidelines of “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR)” [16].

Data sources

The SNP data related to human gut microbiota were obtained from the summary statistics of the GWAS conducted by the MiBioGen consortium. To our knowledge, this study represents the largest and most comprehensive genome-wide meta-analysis to date on the relationship between human autosomal genetic variations and gut microbiota [17]. It included 16s rRNA gene sequencing and genotyping data from 18,340 individuals across 24 cohorts, with the vast majority being of European ancestry (N = 13,266). Additionally, this analysis identified genetic loci affecting the relative abundance (microbiota quantitative trait loci, mbQTL) or presence (microbiota binary trait loci, mbBTL) of microbial taxa through microbiome-wide association studies (mbTL), ultimately providing data on 211 gut microbiota, including 131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 classes, and 9 phyla.

The genetic data of circulating inflammatory proteins are derived from another GWAS summary statistics [18], which is a meta-analysis involving 11 cohorts and 14,824 individuals of European ancestry. This study conducted a genome-wide protein quantitative trait locus (pQTL) analysis on 91 circulating inflammatory proteins measured using the Olink Target Inflammation panel. Additionally, the GWAS catalog provides the data for each circulating inflammatory protein (accession numbers from GCST90274758 to GCST90274848).

The GWAS summary statistics for CR-NETs were provided by the FinnGen consortium (version 10), including 314,544 individuals of European ancestry (351 cases and 314,193 controls). FinnGen combines samples collected by Finnish biobanks and data from Finnish health registries to support genetic research on various diseases [19]. In the models used by FinnGen, covariates such as age, sex, 10 Principal Components, Finngen chip version 1 or 2, and legacy genotyping batch are included to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results. Colonoscopy and biopsy, supplemented by endoscopic ultrasound (primarily for rectal lesions), are the main methods for diagnosing CR-NETs. Additionally, the detection of specific markers such as serum chromogranin A (CgA) levels is also valuable [20]. CR-NETs are defined based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O), with specific codes 8240/3, 8246/3, and 8249/3. Selection of instrumental variables

In the MR analysis, we selected SNPs as instrumental variables for subsequent analysis. To obtain SNPs significantly associated with the exposure, we conducted further screening. For SNPs related to the gut microbiota, due to the limited number of SNPs, we set a relatively loose threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5 [21]. For selecting SNPs for the 91 circulating inflammatory proteins, we set a threshold of P < 5 × 10− 6. When performing reverse MR, we used a threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5. Subsequently, we removed SNPs with linkage disequilibrium, setting specific conditions of R2 < 0.001 and Kb = 10,000 kb. Finally, we excluded palindromic sequences with intermediate allele frequencies. Notably, to assess the strength of the selected instrumental variables, we calculated the F-statistic. SNPs with F < 10 were considered weak instruments. The specific calculation formula is: F = R2 (N-K-1)/[K (1-R2)], where R2 is the proportion of variance in the exposure explained by the instrumental variables, N is the sample size of the exposure study, and K is the number of SNPs [22].

Statistical analysis

Two-sample Mendelian randomization

In this study, we used a bidirectional two-sample MR approach to evaluate the causal relationships between gut microbiota, 91 circulating inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs. For exposures with only a single SNP, we used the Wald ratio method for analysis. For exposures with multiple SNPs, we used the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary analysis method, supplemented by MR-Egger, Weighted median, Simple mode, and Weighted mode methods. The IVW method provides the most accurate estimate of the overall causal effect by combining the causal effect ratios of multiple genetic variants in a weighted manner [23]. The MR-Egger method not only provides an estimate of the causal effect but also tests for horizontal pleiotropy through the intercept bias [24]. The Weighted median method can provide a reliable estimate of the causal effect when at least half of the SNPs are valid [25]. Compared to the previous methods, the Simple mode and Weighted mode methods relax the assumptions about instrumental variables but may have reduced testing efficiency [26]. We considered the results to be statistically significant only when the IVW method’s P-value was less than 0.05 and the direction of the results was consistent across all MR methods.

Mediation analysis

The overall impact of gut microbiota on CR-NETs can be divided into two aspects: direct effects and indirect (mediated) effects. First, we used two-sample MR analysis to assess the causal relationship between gut microbiota and CR-NETs, referred to as the total effect (β). Subsequently, using a two-step MR approach, we identified inflammatory proteins that might mediate the relationship between gut microbiota and CR-NETs. Finally, considering the influence of inflammatory proteins, we obtained the direct effect of gut microbiota on CR-NETs (β3) through MVMR analysis. During the analysis, we also calculated β1, the MR effect of gut microbiota on inflammatory proteins, and β2, the MR effect of inflammatory proteins on CR-NETs, determined using the MVMR method. The indirect effect was obtained by multiplying β1 and β2 [27]. Additionally, we divided the indirect effect by the total effect to visually represent the magnitude of the mediation effect.

Sensitivity analysis

We applied various sensitivity analysis methods. Cochran’s Q test was used to assess the heterogeneity of each SNP [28], and the presence or absence of heterogeneity determined the choice between the random effects model or the fixed effects model in the IVW method. Additionally, we conducted horizontal pleiotropy analysis using MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO methods. The MR-PRESSO method also corrects for horizontal pleiotropy by removing outliers [29]. We visualized the MR results by plotting forest plots and funnel plots. Furthermore, we performed leave-one-out analysis, excluding each SNP one at a time and calculating the overall effect after exclusion to determine the influence of individual instrumental variables [30].

To address the issue of multiple comparisons in MR analysis, we used the Bonferroni correction method [31]. Only the two-sided P-values that passed the Bonferroni correction were considered statistically significant, with the threshold for gut microbiota set at 0.00024 (0.05/211) and the threshold for inflammatory proteins set at 0.00055 (0.05/91). P-values less than 0.05 but greater than the Bonferroni correction threshold were considered suggestive of an association. For MR analysis results where the outcome was a binary variable, the results were expressed using odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals. For MR analysis results where the outcome was a continuous variable, the results were expressed using β values. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.3 with the “TwoSampleMR (version 0.6.4)”, “MVMR (version 0.4)”, “MRPRESSO (version 1.0)”, and “RMediation (version 1.2.2)” packages.

Results

Instrumental variables for exposures

The number of included SNPs varied depending on the selected exposure. For gut microbiota, after removing certain unknown taxa, a total of 2,092 SNPs from 195 taxa were included, with a median F-statistic of 21.1 (range 16.9–88.4). For inflammatory proteins, a total of 1,596 SNPs from 91 inflammatory proteins were included, with a median F-statistic of 22.9 (range 20.8-2740.3). In the reverse MR analysis from gut microbiota to CR-NETs, a total of 1,520 SNPs were included, with a median F-statistic of 20.9 (range 19.7–23.3). In the reverse MR analysis from inflammatory proteins to CR-NETs, a total of 1001 SNPs were included, with a median F-statistic of 20.9 (range 19.7–24.5). We can see that the F-statistics for all instrumental variables were greater than 10, effectively avoiding bias that might arise from weak instrumental variables. Detailed information about the included SNPs can be found in Supplementary Tables 1, 2, 3, 4.

Causal relationship between gut microbiota and CR-NETs

Through the analysis using various MR methods (Table 2), we identified five gut microbiota that may be associated with CR-NETs. The results based on the IVW analysis method (Fig. 2A) showed that family Acidaminococcaceae (OR = 2.454, 95%CI = 1.076–5.597, P = 0.033), family Porphyromonadaceae (OR = 4.034, 95%CI = 1.391–11.696, P = 0.010), genus Oscillospira (OR = 2.410, 95%CI = 1.104–5.262, P = 0.027), and genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group (OR = 2.697, 95%CI = 1.278–5.692, P = 0.009) were associated with an increased risk of CR-NETs. On the other hand, genus Paraprevotella (OR = 0.608, 95%CI = 0.373–0.990, P = 0.045) appeared to have a protective effect (Fig. 3). Relevant scatter plots are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. After applying the Bonferroni correction, the aforementioned results were not statistically significant.

Causal relationship between inflammatory proteins and CR-NETs

Multiple MR methods (Table 3) provided evidence for a potential causal relationship between five inflammatory proteins and CR-NETs. IVW analysis indicated (Fig. 2B) that C-C motif chemokine Ligand 4 (CCL4, OR = 1.459, 95%CI = 1.119–1.904, P = 0.005), C-C motif chemokine Ligand 23 (CCL23, OR = 1.490, 95%CI = 1.064–2.086, P = 0.020), and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL, OR = 1.360, 95%CI = 1.032–1.794, P = 0.029) were associated with an increased risk of CR-NETs. Conversely, C-X-C motif chemokine Ligand 5 (CXCL5, OR = 0.696, 95%CI = 0.495–0.978, P = 0.037) and Monocyte chemoattractant protein 2 (MCP-2, OR = 0.806, 95%CI = 0.653–0.994, P = 0.044) appeared to have a protective effect against CR-NETs. Relevant scatter plots are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. However, after applying the Bonferroni correction, no statistically significant associations were found.

Causal relationship between gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins

For the gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins initially found to be associated with CR-NETs, we conducted further MR analysis to identify potentially mediating inflammatory proteins. The results based on the IVW method indicated that family Porphyromonadaceae was positively associated with CCL23 (Beta = 0.207, P = 0.011). Additionally, genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group was positively associated with CCL4 (Beta = 0.142, P = 0.020) (Supplementary Table 9). After applying the Bonferroni correction (threshold of 0.05/25), these results were suggestive of an association.

Analysis of mediation effects

We further conducted MVMR analysis, including family Porphyromonadaceae and CCL23, adjusting their causal effects on CR-NETs to: family Porphyromonadaceae (Beta = 0.912, OR = 2.490, 95%CI = 1.114–5.565, P = 0.026), and CCL23 (Beta = 0.426, OR = 1.532, 95%CI = 1.124–2.087, P = 0.007). For genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group and CCL4, the adjusted results were: genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group (Beta = 0.893, OR = 2.442, 95%CI = 1.434–4.160, P = 0.001), and CCL4 (Beta = 0.332, OR = 1.394, 95%CI = 1.141–1.704, P = 0.001). When CCL23 was considered a mediator, its mediation effect was 0.088 (95%CI = 1.01–1.222), accounting for 6.3% of the total effect. When CCL4 was considered a mediator, its mediation effect was 0.047 (95%CI = 1.005–1.111), accounting for 4.7% of the total effect (Table 4; Fig. 4). This study suggests that the overall impact of gut microbiota on CR-NETs comprises both a direct effect and an indirect effect mediated through CCL23 and CCL4. Specifically, 6.3% of the total effect of gut microbiota on CR-NETs can be attributed to CCL23, while 4.7% is mediated by CCL4. These findings emphasize the significant role of CCL23 and CCL4 in the causal pathway, suggesting that gut microbiota may affect CR-NETs by modulating inflammatory responses and signaling pathways. Although these indirect effects represent a small proportion of the total impact, they still indicate a meaningful contribution to the overall regulation.

Reverse MR analysis

In the reverse MR analysis between gut microbiota and CR-NETs, none of the five positive results from the forward analysis showed reverse causality. However, we found that genetically correlated CR-NETs were positively associated with the abundance of genus Eubacterium brachy group and class Gammaproteobacteria, and negatively associated with the abundance of phylum Bacteroidetes, class Bacteroidia, and order Bacteroidales (Supplementary Table 10). Sensitivity analysis did not reveal any evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Table 11). In the reverse MR analysis between inflammatory proteins and CR-NETs, we did not find any positive results.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to use MR methods to explore the causal relationships between gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs. In our study, we identified potential causal associations between five types of gut microbiota, including the family Porphyromonadaceae, and CR-NETs. Additionally, we found indicative causal associations between five inflammatory proteins and CR-NETs. Subsequent mediation analysis confirmed the mediating roles of CCL23 and CCL4 between gut microbiota and CR-NETs. Specifically, the family Porphyromonadaceae may increase the risk of CR-NETs by elevating serum CCL23 levels, and the genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group may increase the risk of CR-NETs by elevating serum CCL4 levels. Therefore, we speculate that the CC chemokine family may play an important role in the pathogenesis of CR-NETs.

Gut microbiota plays an irreplaceable role in maintaining normal physiological activities in humans. Studies have shown that the colon and rectum harbor the largest quantity of microbiota, approximately 3 × 10¹³ cells [32]. These microorganisms interact with the colorectal epithelium to regulate various physiological processes, such as energy balance, metabolic regulation, and immune maintenance [33]. When the proportion of different groups within the gut microbiota changes, it may adversely affect the host. For example, gut microbiota may contribute to cancer development by forming carcinogens and triggering inflammatory responses [32]. Hu et al. conducted deep shotgun metagenomics and untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics analyses on two cohorts, including patients with rectal neuroendocrine tumors (R-NETs), and found disproportionate gut microbiota and the accumulation of certain lipid or lipid-like molecules in R-NETs patients [34]. Massironi et al. examined 20 specimens from gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors using fluorescent in situ hybridization and confocal microscopy. They found that 15 of the specimens contained bacteria, and the distribution of bacteria varied among individuals [35]. Mohamed et al. analyzed the microbiomes of patients with different subgroups of gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs) and found that poorly differentiated GEP-NETs showed significant enrichment in certain bacterial categories, such as Enterobacter hormaechei, Bacteroides fragilis, and Trichosporon asahii, compared to well-differentiated GEP-NETs [8]. This partially overlaps with our findings, as we identified the family Porphyromonadaceae (belonging to the order Bacteroidales) as a risk factor for CR-NETs. Furthermore, a Mendelian randomization study explored the causal relationship between gut microbiota and CS. The results indicated that the genus Anaerofilum had a protective effect on CS, while the family Coriobacteriaceae, along with the genus Enterorhabdus, Ruminiclostridium6, Veillonella, and Holdemanella, demonstrated a detrimental effect on CS [36].

In this study, we found that CCL4, CCL23, and TRAIL might be risk factors for CR-NETs, while CXCL5 and MCP-2 might be protective factors for CR-NETs. Both CCL4 and CCL23 belong to the CC chemokine family (N-terminal CC domain), and as part of cell-to-cell communication, CC chemokines can influence the function of the tumor microenvironment [37]. Research indicates that CCL4 (also known as MIP-1β) not only recruits certain regulatory T cells and tumor-promoting macrophages but also acts on fibroblasts, thereby promoting tumor occurrence and progression [38]. In NETs, immune cell infiltration induced by various chemokines, including CCL4, is closely related to tumor progression. For example, some pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs) with high macrophage and neutrophil content show elevated levels of CCL4 [39]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway regulates the tumor immune microenvironment by inducing the expression of the inhibitory factor ATF3, which suppresses CCL4 production and subsequently limits T cell recruitment [40]. CCL23 (also known as MPIF-1, MIP-3, or CKβ8) is produced by eosinophils, monocyte-derived dendritic cells, and others. Research suggests that CCL23 may contribute to tumor angiogenesis by activating the CCR1 receptor on vascular endothelial cells, thereby enhancing VEGF activity and upregulating MMP-2 expression [37, 41, 42]. Additionally, CCL23 may promote the development of an immunosuppressive microenvironment by depleting T-cell phenotypes [43]. TRAIL (also known as Apo2 ligand) is a member of the TNF family and can induce apoptosis by binding to its receptors, thus playing an immune surveillance role against tumor development [44]. It is noteworthy that TRAIL can also indirectly promote tumor occurrence and development. In some tumor cells, TRAIL can promote tumor cell growth and survival by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway [45]. CXC chemokines, as heparin-binding proteins, regulate angiogenesis and metastasis in cancer. CXCL5 can promote tumor development by attracting neutrophils [46], but Speetjens et al. found that low expression of CXCL5 is associated with poor prognosis in colorectal cancer patients, possibly due to increased recruitment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes by CXCL5 [47]. This might explain our finding that CXCL5 acts as a protective factor for CR-NETs. MCP-2 (also known as CCL8) also belongs to the CC chemokine family [48]. Although we found that MCP-2 might be a protective factor for CR-NETs, current research primarily focuses on MCP-2’s role in promoting tumor progression and metastasis [49]. Therefore, the role of MCP-2 in CR-NETs requires further study.

Through mediation analysis, we have preliminarily identified two possible mediators between the gut microbiota and CR-NETs: the family Porphyromonadaceae may promote the occurrence of CR-NETs through CCL23, and the genus Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group may promote the occurrence of CR-NETs through CCL4. This undoubtedly provides genetic evidence for further exploration of the pathogenesis of CR-NETs. At the same time, this study also preliminarily indicates that the CC chemokine family may play an important role in CR-NETs, although there are few studies directly investigating the role of the CC chemokine family in CR-NETs. Yang et al. found that in the early stages of colitis-associated cancer (CAC), monocytic-like macrophages (MLM) can aggregate through the CCL2 signaling pathway. Blocking the CCL2 signaling pathway not only inhibits the enrichment of MLM but also significantly inhibits tumor growth [50]. Therefore, to validate our speculative findings, further experimental research should be conducted, including in vitro functional assays and animal model experiments, to explore the specific pathogenic mechanisms and functional roles of CCL23 and CCL4 in CR-NETs.

This study explores the relationship between gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs from a genetic perspective using GWAS data, effectively avoiding the biases common in traditional observational studies. Additionally, we ensured the reliability of the results and increased the credibility of the conclusions by employing various sensitivity analysis methods. However, this study also has certain limitations. Firstly, the classification of gut microbiota in this study is based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, which, although common, cannot provide more detailed species-level information. Secondly, The rarity of CR-NETs limits the feasibility of accessing large-scale and diverse GWAS studies, preventing us from further validating the analytical results using a validation cohort. To address this challenge, future efforts should focus on fostering multicenter collaborations or developing global databases to acquire larger datasets. Thirdly, our results did not withstand the strict test of Bonferroni correction, which may not only result in the omission of some true associations but also affect the stability of the obtained findings. Although Bonferroni correction may obscure some biologically significant associations, thereby overlooking potential causal relationships. Moreover, this study focuses on exploring the existence of relevant causal relationships, and MR analysis itself is hypothesis-driven and not affected by multiple comparisons [51]. Lastly, our study is mainly based on populations of European ancestry, and genetic differences between European and non-European populations may influence gut microbiota composition, dietary habits, and inflammatory responses, which somewhat limits the generalizability of the findings. In conclusion, our research pioneers the study of the gut microbiota-inflammatory protein-CR-NETs axis. More studies are needed in the future to discover potential mediators, expand on the related mechanisms, and provide a solid theoretical foundation for the research of various therapeutic approaches.

Conclusion

This study explores the potential causal relationship between gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and CR-NETs, and preliminarily identifies the mediating roles of CCL23 and CCL4 between gut microbiota and CR-NETs. This provides new insights into the pathogenesis of CR-NETs. Additionally, our findings offer possible directions for the development of targeted therapies against CR-NETs.
