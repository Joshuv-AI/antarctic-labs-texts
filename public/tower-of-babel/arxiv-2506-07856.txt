Stability of Mean-Field Variational Inference
Shunan Sheng, Bohan Wu, Alberto González-Sanz, Marcel Nutz

Abstract
Mean-field variational inference (MFVI) is a widely used method for approximating high-dimensional probability distributions by product measures. This paper studies the stability properties of the mean-field approximation when the target distribution varies within the class of strongly log-concave measures. We establish dimension-free Lipschitz continuity of the MFVI optimizer with respect to the target distribution, measured in the 2-Wasserstein distance, with Lipschitz constant inversely proportional to the log-concavity parameter. Under additional regularity conditions, we further show that the MFVI optimizer depends differentiably on the target potential and characterize the derivative by a partial differential equation. Methodologically, we follow a novel approach to MFVI via linearized optimal transport: the non-convex MFVI problem is lifted to a convex optimization over transport maps with a fixed base measure, enabling the use of calculus of variations and functional analysis. We discuss several applications of our results to robust Bayesian inference and empirical Bayes, including a quantitative Bernstein–von Mises theorem for MFVI, as well as to distributed stochastic control.

Mean-Field; Variational Inference; Optimal Transport

Introduction

Given a probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, i.e.,

\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

where
Z
denotes the normalizing constant, the mean-field variational inference (MFVI) problem is to find the best approximation of
\pi
within the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
of product measures in the sense of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence,

\nu^{*}\in\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}H\left(\nu\mid\pi\right).

(MFVI)

While
H\left(\cdot\mid\pi\right)
is a strictly convex functional, the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
of product measures is not convex in the usual sense. Indeed, (
MFVI
) generally admits more than one optimizer. Lacker, Mukherjee and Yeung
[
55
]
resolved this issue when the target measure
\pi
is
\alpha
-log-concave, i.e., the potential
V
is
\alpha
-strongly-convex. Using that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
is
displacement
convex (geodesically convex in the optimal transport geometry), their seminal work shows that (
MFVI
) then admits a unique optimizer
\nu^{*}
with several desirable properties (see also
Theorem

1.1
below), in particular the approximation bound

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu^{*},\pi)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{3}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\int|\partial_{ij}V(x)|^{2}\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)

where
\mathcal{W}_{2}
denotes the
2
-Wasserstein metric.
The present paper establishes
stability
of the optimizer
\nu^{*}
under perturbations of the target
\pi
within the class of log-concave target measures.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first quantitative stability results for MFVI.

MFVI has gained popularity in large-scale Bayesian applications
[
51
,
75
,
13
]
due to its computational tractability, especially when sampling from the posterior distribution
\pi
is required for downstream tasks. Unlike traditional Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods
[
47
,
37
,
68
]
which can be prohibitive in high dimensions, MFVI offers a computationally scalable alternative, especially when the posterior admits a complex graphical structure
[
75
]
. Although MFVI relies on sampling from an approximate posterior, these samples often provide sufficiently accurate inference for practical applications such as genomics
[
18
,
77
]
, generative modeling
[
53
]
, or language modeling
[
14
]
.
Stability plays a crucial role in the area of
robust Bayesian analysis
, where the goal is to quantify the sensitivity of posterior inference under perturbations of the model or prior
[
10
,
45
,
40
]
. As MFVI is increasingly used in place of exact posterior inference for large-scale and complex models, understanding its stability is essential for evaluating the robustness of statistical decisions and analysis made based on the variational approximation.

The assumption of log-concavity is standard in the literature on log-concave sampling
[
25
]
which is the foundation for several recent theoretical developments in variational inference
[
49
,
55
,
2
]
. The classical application of MFVI in statistical physics—here more commonly known as the
mean-field approximation
—often assumes that the target measure
\pi
is a discrete Gibbs measure supported on the discrete hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{d}

[
67
,
65
,
6
]
. A parallel line of work has developed quantitative error bounds for mean-field approximations using a “gradient complexity” framework, both for models defined on the discrete hypercube
[
23
,
6
,
33
,
4
]
and for those supported on general compact spaces
[
80
,
62
,
3
]
. Although the compact support setting is of independent interest, we do not consider it in this paper as the MFVI optimizer is often not unique in that setting (as noted in
[
62
]
), precluding stability in the sense shown here.

Next, we discuss our methodology and main results—in this order, as the statement of the second main result uses an object best presented as part of the methodology.

Methodology

We follow the novel approach to MFVI based on
linearized
optimal transport
[
78
,
50
]
. Fixing the
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian
\rho
as reference measure, we can parametrize
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by the unique optimal transport map
T
from
\rho
to
\nu
. Optimal transport maps (for the quadratic transport cost) are precisely the gradients of convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the push-forward
\nu
of
\rho
under the map
T
is a product measure if and only if
T(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=(T_{1}(u_{1}),\ldots,T_{d}(u_{d}))
where each
T_{i}
is the gradient of a convex function on
\mathbb{R}
. As a result, we can recast the MFVI problem as an optimization over such maps which naturally
form a convex set
. The KL divergence
H\left(\cdot\mid\pi\right)
translates to the strictly convex functional

\mathcal{F}_{V}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log(T_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i}))\rho_{1}(\mathrm{d}u_{i})+\int V(T_{1}(u_{1}),\ldots,T_{d}(u_{d}))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

(1)

up to a constant (see
Lemma

4.1
for details). In summary, the non-convex MFVI problem is lifted to a convex problem over transport maps. While novel in the present context, this seems to be a natural and powerful approach to MFVI. For instance, the uniqueness of the MFVI optimizer (already proved in
[
55
]
) is immediate from this perspective. All our results are derived using the “lifted” MFVI problem whose convexity enables powerful tools of calculus of variations and functional analysis.

The use of optimal transport theory is certainly not unprecedented in studying the MFVI problem. As mentioned,
[
55
]
used the Wasserstein geometry
[
66
,
58
]
to show uniqueness; this is a curved geometry in contrast to the flat geometry of our lifted problem.
More closely related is the recent work
[
50
]
, developing a polynomial-time algorithm for solving MFVI. The authors also translate the variational problem into an optimization over transport maps. Specifically, they fix a finite-dimensional set of transport maps and compute an approximate solution of the MFVI problem using gradient descent on that set. In the present work, we introduce an infinite-dimensional optimization problem on a Gaussian Sobolev space and show that it is equivalent to the original variational problem.
Another related paper is
[
2
]
, showing the convergence of the Coordinate Ascent Variational Inference (CAVI) algorithm, an algorithm that is commonly used to find the optimizer. The analysis in
[
2
]
focuses on the tangent space at the MFVI optimizer or the CAVI iterates, and does not apply when the target measure is itself varying as in our problem.
The identification of probability measures with their corresponding transport maps also plays a central role in rank-based inference. When the base measure is the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, the optimal transport map coincides with the quantile function of the associated probability measure. In higher dimensions,
[
24
,
46
,
38
,
29
]
and others used optimal transport maps to define multivariate analogues of quantile functions and conduct rank-based inference.

Results

We study the stability of the MFVI procedure: how sensitive is
\nu^{*}
to perturbations of the potential
V
of the target
\pi
? Our first main result (
Theorem

2.1
) shows that when
\pi,\tilde{\pi}
are
\alpha
-log-concave, the corresponding MFVI optimizers
\nu^{*},\tilde{\nu}^{*}
satisfy the dimension-free Lipschitz stability bound

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu^{*},\tilde{\nu}^{*})\leq\frac{\|\nabla V-\nabla\tilde{V}\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}}{\alpha}.

(2)

This can be seen as a mean-field analogue of classical transportation–information inequalities (e.g.,
[
31
,
42
,
4
]
) with
\|\nabla V-\nabla\tilde{V}\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}
taking the place of the relative Fisher information between
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
. We also provide a Lipschitz result for the optimal KL divergence achieved by
\nu^{*}
, or equivalently the maximal evidence lower bound (ELBO); see
Theorem

2.3
. Recognizing that the right-hand side of (
2
) is not directly accessible if
\nu^{*}
is unknown,
Corollary

2.4
complements
Theorem

2.1
by giving upper bounds that are explicit in terms of the given data.

The bound (
2
) is based on the variational first-order condition of optimality for the lifted MFVI problem: at the optimizer, the first variation of the functional (
1
) in any admissible direction vanishes. We prove (
2
) by using this fact at both optimizers,
\nu^{*}
and
\tilde{\nu}^{*}
.

Our second main result (
Theorem

2.5
) establishes the differentiability of the MFVI optimizer as a function of the potential
V
and characterizes the derivative. To that end, we use the lifted problem where derivatives can be defined in the usual sense thanks to the flat geometry. Specifically, consider a family of potentials
\{V_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
that are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,
\alpha
-convex and
\beta
-smooth, and assume that the gradient
\nabla V_{\theta}
is Fréchet differentiable in
\theta
(in a suitable
L^{2}
space) with derivative denoted by
\partial_{\theta}\nabla V_{\theta}
. We show that the optimizer
T^{\theta}
of the lifted functional (
1
) is differentiable in
\theta
and that the derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta}
can be characterized as the solution to a partial differential equation.

For applications, identifying the derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta}
is useful to quantify the sensitivity of the MFVI optimizer under perturbations of the target measure. This is relevant for analyzing the robustness of downstream statistical procedures that rely on the variational approximation
[
81
,
82
]
.

The proof of
Theorem

2.5
builds on a second-order analysis of the functional (
1
) whose form precludes a straightforward application of the implicit function theorem. Instead, we construct the derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta}
via the Lax–Milgram theorem in the space
(\mathcal{H}^{1}(\rho_{1}))^{d}
, where
\rho_{1}
is the one-dimensional standard Gaussian measure. In this argument,
\alpha
-convexity of the potentials is exploited to guarantee coercivity of the bilinear form. Another key ingredient for the proof of
Theorem

2.5
is a novel
L^{p}
estimate for the difference
T^{\theta}-T^{\theta_{0}}
where
p>2
(
Proposition

5.2
). As a by-product, this estimate also leads to a stability bound for
\nu^{*}
in the
p
-Wasserstein metric for
p>2
; see
Proposition

2.9
.

Several applications in statistical inference and stochastic control are presented. On the strength of our theoretical results, we quantify the
robustness of variational Bayesian inference
in several situations including high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression and the prior swapping problem (
Section

3.1
). In our likely most impactful statistical application, we derive a novel
quantitative Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorem
that bounds the Wasserstein distance between the MFVI optimizer and a mean-field analogue of the Laplace approximation of the posterior (
Section

3.2
). This is the first quantitative BvM theorem for MFVI in a high-dimensional setting. Separately, our results on MFVI imply a quantitative stability result for the optimal value of
distributed stochastic control
problems (
Section

3.3
).
Last but not least, the established stability of the MFVI optimizer is a crucial auxiliary result in forthcoming work on
structured variational inference
where a given target measure is approximated by distributions following a prescribed dependence structure rather than having independent marginals as in the present mean-field case.

Notation and Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the subspace of measures with finite second moment. For
\nu,\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the 2-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\tilde{\nu})
is defined via

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\tilde{\nu})=\inf\int\|x-y\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y),

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
\gamma
of
(\nu,\tilde{\nu})
, i.e., all
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with marginals
(\nu,\tilde{\nu})
.
The Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between
\nu,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as
H\left(\nu\mid\pi\right)=\int\log\left(\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu}{\,\mathrm{d}\pi}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu
if
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
(denoted
\nu\ll\pi
) and
H\left(\nu\mid\pi\right)=\infty
otherwise. Here,
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu}{\,\mathrm{d}\pi}
denotes the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\nu
with respect to
\pi
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{d}
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
\nu\ll\mathcal{L}_{d}
, we use
\nu
to denote both the measure and its density with respect to
\mathcal{L}_{d}
. The (negative) differential entropy is then defined as
H(\nu)=\int\log\nu(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
if
\nu\ll\mathcal{L}_{d}
and
\infty
otherwise.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of all product probability measures
\nu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{i}

\nu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}

. Similarly, for a vector

and

, we denote by

the vector with entries
(x_{i})_{i\neq j}
. We write the integral of

with respect to
\pi

\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi
or
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(X)]
, depending on the context (here

is the identity function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). Unless otherwise specified, all integrals are taken over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a measurable function

We denote by

d

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}=\left(\int\|f(u)\|^{p}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right)^{1/p}

We write

We recall that a map
f:\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}

(\mathcal{B}_{i},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}_{i}})

For a space

A probability measure

|\mathcal{R}(\tilde{V})-\mathcal{R}(V)|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\beta d}}{\alpha}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha^{2}}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}^{2}+\|\tilde{V}-V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}.

If
\tilde{V},V
are normalized such that
\int V\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}^{*}=\int\tilde{V}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}^{*}
, then

|\mathcal{R}(\tilde{V})-\mathcal{R}(V)|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\beta d}+1}{\alpha}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha^{2}}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}^{2}.

(8)

In contrast to
Theorem

2.1
, the right-hand side in
Theorem

2.3
contains the dimension-dependent expression
\sqrt{d}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}
. Noting that
\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}
is generally expected to scale like
\sqrt{d}
, terms on the right-hand side of (
8
) then scale like
d
. At the same time, both
\mathcal{R}(V)
and
\mathcal{R}(\tilde{V})
also scale like
d
, suggesting that the factor
\sqrt{d}
in (
8
) is natural (and unavoidable). For example, when
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
and
\tilde{\pi}=\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
for
\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}>0
, the left-hand side of (
8
) becomes
\frac{d}{2}\left|\log\sigma^{2}-\log\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\right|
, while
\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}=\sqrt{d}\tilde{\sigma}\left|1/\sigma^{2}-1/\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\right|
.

Theorem

2.3
is helpful to establish the consistency of the learned prior in variational empirical Bayes procedures (similarly as in
[
61
, Theorem 3]
). In particular,
Theorem

2.3
suggests that any log-concave prior lying within a ball of certain size around the ELBO-maximizing prior yields a consistent estimate of the true prior. In a different application,
Theorem

2.3
yields a stability result for distributed stochastic control with respect to the utility function; see
Section

3.3
.

2.1.3
Explicit Lipschitz Bounds

We present an explicit version of the Lipschitz stability bound for the MFVI optimizer. In comparison with
Theorem

Lemma

.

In applications, priors are often parametrized by a set
\Theta

to be a subset of Euclidean space for concreteness.

2

2.2
Methodology: The Lifted MFVI Problem

The purpose of this subsection is twofold: first, to introduce a lifting approach to the MFVI problem, which we consider the main methodological contribution. Second, to introduce notions needed to state the differentiability result in the subsequent
Section

2.3
. A key challenge in the analysis of the MFVI optimizer is that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
is not convex in the usual sense.
[
55
]
made use of the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
is displacement convex to obtain the uniqueness in
Theorem

1.1
. In the present work, a key innovation is to use
linearized
optimal transport: Fix the reference measure
\rho=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
. Since for each measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there is a unique optimal transport map
T_{\rho\to\mu}
, we can parametrize such measures
\mu
by their optimal transport maps, and this turns out to be particularly useful for the set
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
. In fact, for
\mu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
, the components
T_{i}
of
T_{\rho\to\mu}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{d})
are the optimal transport maps from
\rho_{1}
to the marginals
\mu_{i}
. Each such transport problem being scalar, the map
T_{i}
has a simple form, namely,
T_{i}=Q_{i}\circ\Phi
where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
Q_{i}
is the quantile function of
\mu_{i}
.

Recall that the space
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathcal{W}_{2})
can be endowed with a formal Riemannian structure
[
66
]
. The tangent space of the submanifold
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
at the base measure
\rho
is identified (see
[
54
, Section 3]
for details) as

{\rm Tan}_{\rho}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}{\rm Tan}_{\rho_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=(L^{2}(\rho_{1}))^{d},

(10)

where
{\rm Tan}_{\rho_{1}}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=\overline{\left\{\varphi^{\prime}:\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\right\}}^{L^{2}(\rho_{1})}=L^{2}(\rho_{1})
.
Once we represent each
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
via the optimal transport map
T_{\rho\to\mu}\in{\rm Tan}_{\rho}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
, (
MFVI
) becomes a convex optimization problem over optimal transport maps, or more explicitly, the set of maps
T(u)=(T_{1}(u_{1}),\ldots,T_{d}(u_{d}))
such that each
T_{i}
is the gradient of a convex function on
\mathbb{R}
. Specifically, since we know that the MFVI optimizer is absolutely continuous and the map
T_{\rho\to\mu}
is the gradient of a strictly convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
when
\mu
is absolutely continuous, (
MFVI
) becomes a minimization over the convex cone

\mathcal{H}_{+}:=\left\{(T_{1},\dots,T_{d})\in\mathcal{H}(\rho):T_{i}^{\prime}>0\text{ a.e.}\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}(\rho),

(11)

where
\mathcal{H}(\rho):=(\mathcal{H}^{1}(\rho_{1}))^{d}\subset{\rm Tan}_{\rho}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\rho_{1})
is the Gaussian Sobolev space over
\mathbb{R}
(see
Section

1.1
).
We show in
Lemma

4.1
that (
MFVI
) is equivalent to

\min_{(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}}\mathcal{F}_{V}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}),

(L-MFVI)

where
\mathcal{F}_{V}
is the convex functional on
\mathcal{H}(\rho)
defined as

\mathcal{F}_{V}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}):=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log(T_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i}))\rho_{1}(\mathrm{d}u_{i})+\int V(T_{1}(u_{1}),\ldots,T_{d}(u_{d}))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

(12)

if
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}
, and
\infty
otherwise. We call (
L-MFVI
) the
lifted problem
of (
MFVI
).
Theorem

2.1
will be derived by analyzing the first-order optimality condition of the convex problem (
L-MFVI
).

2.3
Differentiability of the MFVI Optimizer

Our second main result studies the derivative of the mean-field approximation with respect to a perturbation of the target potential. More precisely, we consider potentials
\{V_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
depending suitably on a parameter
\theta
and show that the optimizer
T^{\theta}
of (
L-MFVI
) for the target measure
\pi_{\theta}\propto e^{-V_{\theta}}
is differentiable in
\theta
. Moreover, we characterize the derivative as the solution to a partial differential equation. We assume that
\Theta
is an interval, but note (see
Remark

2.6
) that this includes the case of a parameter varying along a segment in a high-dimensional space.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq\beta
. Let
\{V_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}\subset\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a family of
\alpha
-strongly
convex and
\beta
-smooth functions indexed by an open interval
\Theta
. Let
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
and assume that

(i)

V_{\theta}\to V_{\theta_{0}}
pointwise as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
;

(ii)

for some
\varepsilon>0
, the mapping

\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto\nabla V_{\theta}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\varepsilon)\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d}))

is well-defined and Fréchet differentiable at
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
with derivative
\partial_{\theta}\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x\mapsto\partial_{\theta}\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}(x)
is Lipschitz.

(iii)

we have

\|\nabla V_{\theta}-\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\varepsilon)\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d}))}\leq C_{\theta_{0}}|\theta-\theta_{0}|\quad\text{for some }p>2.

(13)

Then
\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto T^{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}(\rho)

is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
. The derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}\in\mathcal{H}(\rho)
can be characterized as the unique solution
S\in\mathcal{H}(\rho)
of the equation

\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}(S,R)=-\int\left\langle\partial_{\theta}\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}\circ T^{\theta_{0}}(u),R(u)\right\rangle\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\quad\text{for every}\,\,R\in\mathcal{H}(\rho),

(14)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}:\mathcal{H}(\rho)\times\mathcal{H}(\rho)\to\mathbb{R}
is the bilinear operator given by

\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}(S,R)=\int\langle[\nabla^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}(T^{\theta_{0}}(u))]S(u),R(u)\rangle\rho(\mathrm{d}u)+\int\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{S_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})R_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})}{((T^{\theta_{0}}_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i}))^{2}}\rho(\mathrm{d}u).

(15)

Remark

2.8
below elaborates on the meaning of the characterization (
14
) and the bilinear operator (
15
). Before that, some comments on the conditions of
Theorem

2.5
are in order.

Remark 2.6
.

(a)

The assumptions in
Theorem

2.5
can be understood as follows. On the one hand, the Fréchet differentiability of
\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto\nabla V_{\theta}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\varepsilon)\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d}))
implies that
\partial_{\theta}\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}\in L^{2}(\nu_{\theta_{0}}^{*})
; see
Lemma

6.3
. This ensures that the right-hand side of (
14
) is finite for all
R\in\mathcal{H}(\rho)
. On the other hand, the
\alpha
-convexity and
\beta
-smoothness condition of
\{V_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
ensures that the bilinear operator
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}
is well-behaved, so that the Lax–Milgram theorem allows us to infer the existence and uniqueness of the derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}
.

(b)

The Lipschitz condition (
13
) can be replaced by
\|\nabla V_{\theta}-\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}\|_{L^{p}(\nu_{\theta_{0}}^{*})}\leq C_{\theta_{0}}|\theta-\theta_{0}|
. However, (
13
) has the advantage of being verifiable without access to the MFVI optimizer
\nu_{\theta_{0}}^{*}
. Indeed, given query access to
\nabla V_{\theta}
, one can sample from
\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\varepsilon)\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})
and verify assumption (
13
) for any pair of parameters
\theta,\theta_{0}
.

(c)

While we assumed that
\Theta
is an interval, our argument is applicable to multivariate (or even infinite-dimensional) parameters. E.g., for
\theta,\theta_{0}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we can consider the line segment
\{(1-t)\theta+t\theta_{0}:t\in(-\delta,1+\delta)\}
for some
\delta>0
and accordingly define an auxiliary parameter space
\tilde{\Theta}:=(-\delta,1+\delta)
. See
Example

3.5
for an illustration based on a Huber contamination model in robust statistics.

(d)

The differentiability of
\theta\mapsto T^{\theta}
also holds in
L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, as
\mathcal{H}(\rho)
is contained in the local Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
15
, Proposition 1.5.2]
, Morrey’s inequality
[
16
, Theorem 9.1.2]
yields that
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is embedded in
L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the map
\theta\mapsto T^{\theta}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is Fréchet differentiable at any
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
.

The characterization of the Fréchet derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}
in (
14
) is related to
[
41
, Theorem 1.2]
which studies the differentiability of the optimal transport map with respect to a parameter when the reference and target measures are both supported on bounded, convex sets and admit smooth densities. In that setting, the derivative is characterized as the classical solution to a second-order elliptic partial differential equation, in contrast to the weak formulation provided in
Theorem

2.5
.

Solving for
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}
yields a first-order approximation to the MFVI optimizer
\nu^{*}_{\theta}
. Define
\tilde{\nu}_{\theta}:=\left(T^{\theta_{0}}+(\theta-\theta_{0})\,\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}\right)_{\#}\rho
. Then,

2.5
implies that

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{\nu}_{\theta},\nu^{*}_{\theta})=o(|\theta-\theta_{0}|)
, allowing one to compute the MFVI optimizer only at
\theta_{0}
and adjust for small perturbations in
\theta
via a first-order correction. This has potential applications, e.g, in transfer learning. Finally, we remark that under sufficient regularity, the classical formulation in (
18
) enables computation of
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}
by solving a system of ordinary differential equations.

As mentioned in
Section

2.2
, the component
T_{i}^{\theta}
of
T^{\theta}
is the optimal transport map from
\rho_{i}
to
\nu_{\theta,i}^{*}
and has the form
T_{i}^{\theta}=Q_{i}^{\theta}\circ\Phi
where
\Phi
is the cdf of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
Q_{i}^{\theta}
is the quantile function of the
i
-th marginal
\nu_{\theta,i}^{*}
; that is,
Q_{i}^{\theta}
is the optimal transport map from

to
\nu_{\theta,i}^{*}
. Writing

\|T^{\theta}-T^{\theta_{0}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\|Q^{\theta}-Q^{\theta_{0}}\|_{(L^{2}([0,1]))^{d}}
. As a consequence,
Theorem

2.5
yields the following result.

Corollary 2.7
.

In the setting of
Theorem

2.5
, let
Q_{i}^{\theta}
denote the quantile function of the

. Then the map

.

Remark 2.8
.

We can understand the characterization (
14
) as the weak solution concept for a more straightforward (but technically inconvenient) variational condition, (
18
) below. Indeed, the bilinear operator
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}
in (
15
) arises naturally as the second-order derivative of the lifted MFVI problem. To see this, suppose that
T^{\theta_{0}}
is smooth and each
(T^{\theta_{0}}_{i})^{\prime}
is bounded away from zero and infinity. Define the operator
\Gamma_{i}:\Theta\times\mathcal{H}(\rho)\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\rho)
by

\Gamma_{i}(\theta,T)(u_{i})=-\frac{(T_{i})^{\prime\prime}(u_{i})}{\left((T_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i})\right)^{2}}-\frac{u_{i}}{(T_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\partial_{i}V_{\theta}\left(T(u)\right)\,\rho_{-i}(\mathrm{d}u_{-i}),\quad i\in[d].

The first-order optimality condition of (
L-MFVI
) entails that

\Gamma_{i}(\theta_{0},T^{\theta_{0}})=0,\quad\forall\,i\in[d].

(16)

Taking the directional derivative of
\Gamma_{i}
at
T^{\theta_{0}}
along a smooth direction
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{d})\in(\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))^{d}
and using integration by parts, we deduce that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\langle D_{T}\Gamma_{i}(\theta_{0},T^{\theta_{0}})(S),R_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho_{1})}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}(S,R)\quad\mbox{for all }S,R\in(\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))^{d}.

(17)

Comparing (
17
) to the characterization (
14
) in

, we observe that the Fréchet derivative
\partial_{\theta}T^{\theta_{0}}
is formally the weak solution to the following system of equations,

D_{T}\Gamma_{i}(\theta,T^{\theta_{0}})(S)(u_{i})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\partial_{\theta}\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}\circ T^{\theta_{0}}(u_{i},u_{-i})\rho_{-i}(\mathrm{d}u_{-i}),\quad\forall\,u_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i\in[d].

(18)

While this formal derivation motivates our result, our proof takes a different route. Indeed, working with (
16
) would require pointwise control of the first and second derivatives of

, which seems challenging to obtain. As highlighted by the relation (
17
), we may instead work with the bilinear operator
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{0}}
which is better-behaved in our setting (see the proof of

2.5
). The main technical obstacle in our approach is that functions in
\mathcal{H}(\rho)
are only defined

estimate on the difference

; see

5.2
. Further comments on the proof strategy are deferred to

Remarkably, the following

for

is that the bound depends explicitly on the dimension

Proposition 2.9
.

In the setting of
Theorem

2.5
, for any

,

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\nu_{\theta},\nu_{\theta_{0}})\leq d^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\frac{\alpha+\sqrt{d}\beta}{\alpha^{2}}\|\nabla V_{\theta}(T^{\theta})-\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}(T^{\theta})\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}.

3
Applications

In this section, we present several applications of our main results. In
Section

3.1
, we quantify the sensitivity of variational Bayes in the context of Bayesian linear regression, prior swapping, and when priors belong to a
\varepsilon
-contamination class. In
Section

3.2
, we establish a quantitative Bernstein–von Mises theorem for MFVI which states that the variational posterior is well-approximated by a Gaussian when the sample size is large. Finally, in
Section

3.3
, we provide an application to distributed stochastic control, quantifying the stability of the optimal value with respect to the utility function.

3.1
Variational Bayesian Robustness

Our first application is in the context of Bayesian statistics. Suppose we observe a collection of data points
\mathbf{x}_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
generated from a model with Lebesgue density
\rmp(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\mathbf{z}_{d})
, conditional on latent variables
\mathbf{z}_{d}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We place a prior distribution
\rmp(\mathbf{z}_{d})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
on the latent variables. Together, the prior and likelihood define a joint distribution over
(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{d})
with potential
V(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{d})=-\log\rmp(\mathbf{z}_{d})-\log\rmp(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\mathbf{z}_{d})
. The central object in Bayesian inference is the posterior distribution, given by the conditional law of
\mathbf{z}_{d}
given
\mathbf{x}_{n}
:

\pi\left(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{d}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n}\right)=\frac{\exp\left(-V\left(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{d}\right)\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-V\left(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{d}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{d}}.

When
\mathbf{z}_{d}
is high-dimensional, posterior sampling methods such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) can be computationally prohibitive. An empirically successful alternative is to approximate the posterior distribution
\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{n})
using MFVI
[
75
,
13
]
.

Many probabilistic models have the form
V_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{d})
, where
\theta\in\Theta
parametrizes either the prior or the likelihood. This setting induces a family of posterior distributions
\{\pi_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
, with the corresponding MFVIs
\{\nu_{\theta}^{*}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
.

We analyze the sensitivity in Wasserstein distance of
\nu_{\theta}^{*}
to perturbations of
\theta
around some reference point
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
. By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality
[
73
, Theorem 1.14]
, a bound in Wasserstein distance controls the fluctuation of the posterior means of Lipschitz statistics, including linear statistics
\varphi(x)=\langle\gamma,x\rangle
for some vector
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
or the norm
\varphi(x)=\|x\|_{1}
(see
Corollary

3.4
for an example).

3.1.1
Variational Empirical Bayes

Empirical Bayes
[
8
]
replaces an unknown parameter
\theta_{0}
in a probabilistic model by a frequentist estimate
\hat{\theta}
. In this context, our stability bound quantifies how the estimation uncertainty of
\hat{\theta}
propagates to the uncertainty of statistical inference based on
\nu_{\hat{\theta}}^{*}
. We illustrate this in the examples of a linear regression model.

Example 3.1

(Bayesian Linear Regression)
.

Suppose we observe data points
\{(y_{i},X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{X}^{\top}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
. Consider the model

y=\bm{X}\beta+\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\tau^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{n}\right).

Here
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the coefficients of interest. Assume that
\beta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mu
where
\mu(\mathrm{d}\beta_{1})\propto\exp\left(-v(\beta_{1})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}
for some
v:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. Set
\bm{A}:=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X},\mathbf{w}:=\bm{X}^{\top}y
. The posterior density of
\beta
given
y
and
\bm{X}
has the form
\pi_{\tau}\left(\mathrm{d}\beta\right)\propto\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\beta)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\beta
, where

V_{\tau}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v(\beta_{i})+\frac{\tau}{2}\beta^{T}\bm{A}\beta-\tau\mathbf{w}^{T}\beta.

MFVI is frequently used to approximate the posterior of high-dimensional linear models, both in statistical methodology
[
18
,
77
]
and in statistical theory
[
62
,
61
,
55
]
. In practice, however, the unknown precision parameter
\tau
is typically replaced by an estimate
\hat{\tau}
. The following result shows that the MFVI optimizer is Lipschitz stable in
\mathcal{W}_{2}
with respect to the estimation error in
\hat{\tau}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume that
v
is
\alpha_{0}
-convex for some
\alpha_{0}\geq 0
and that
\bm{A}\succeq\alpha_{1}\mathrm{I}_{d}
for some
\alpha_{1}>0
. For
\tau>0
, let
\nu_{\tau}^{*}
be the MFVI optimizer for target
\pi_{\tau}
. Then

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}}^{*},\nu_{\tau}^{*})\leq\frac{\|\bm{A}\beta-\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*}_{\hat{\tau}})}}{\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}}|\hat{\tau}-\tau|

(19)

and the full approximation error satisfies

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}}^{*},\pi_{\tau})\leq\frac{\|\bm{A}\beta-\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*}_{\hat{\tau}})}}{\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}}|\hat{\tau}-\tau|+\frac{\sqrt{2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}|\bm{A}_{ij}|^{2}}}{\left(\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}\right)^{3/2}}.

Proof.

In view of
\nabla^{2}V_{\hat{\tau}}\succeq\alpha_{1}\hat{\tau}+\alpha_{0}
and
\nabla^{2}V_{\tau}\succeq\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}
, the posteriors
\pi_{\hat{\tau}}
and
\pi_{\tau}
are log-concave, with log-concavity constants
\alpha_{1}\hat{\tau}+\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}
, respectively.
Theorem

2.1
thus yields

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}}^{*},\nu_{\tau}^{*})\leq\frac{\|\nabla V_{\hat{\tau}}-\nabla V_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}})}}{\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}}=\frac{\|\bm{A}\beta-\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}})}}{\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}}|\hat{\tau}-\tau|.

The bound on full approximation error follows by the triangle inequality
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}}^{*},\pi_{\tau})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\hat{\tau}}^{*},\nu_{\tau}^{*})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\tau}^{*},\pi_{\tau})
and the bound for
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\tau}^{*},\pi_{\tau})
given in
[
55
, Theorem 1.1]
.
∎

Corollary

3.2
guarantees that
\nu_{\hat{\tau}}^{*}
is close to
\nu_{\tau}^{*}
when
\hat{\tau}
is close to
\tau
. The Lipschitz constant in (
19
) may be interpreted as the posterior predictive mean squared error (MSE) of
\mathbf{w}
under the variational posterior
\nu_{\hat{\tau}}
. If
\hat{\tau}
is a consistent estimate of
\tau
, one can empirically quantify the local sensitivity of
\nu_{\tau}^{*}
at
\tau=\hat{\tau}
by drawing Monte Carlo samples
\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(m)}
from
\nu_{\hat{\tau}}
and evaluating the bound

\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\bm{A}\beta^{(j)}-\mathbf{w}\|^{2}}}{\sqrt{m}(\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0})}
.
A large bound suggests that
\hat{\nu}_{\tau}
is highly sensitive to the estimation error in
\hat{\tau}
, thus more data should be used to obtain a more accurate estimate of
\hat{\tau}
for reliable downstream inference. We remark that
Corollary

3.2
remains valid even when
\alpha_{0}
is negative, provided that
\alpha_{1}\tau+\alpha_{0}>0
.

3.1.2
Robustness under Perturbation in Prior

Our second application concerns the classical notion of Bayesian robustness in statistics
[
9
]
and econometrics
[
39
]
. Two main approaches thereof are
global robustness
, which examines the range of posterior means as the prior varies over a family of distributions
[
9
]
, and
local robustness
, which quantifies the sensitivity of posterior functionals to infinitesimal changes in the hyperparameter
[
44
]
. Within the local robustness framework,
[
43
]
investigates the sensitivity of the posterior mean, while
[
45
]
quantifies the sensitivity of the full posterior distribution under perturbations of the prior, using the total variation metric.
[
40
]
study local prior robustness in the context of MFVI, deriving sensitivity measures that quantify the effect of model perturbations on the variational posterior mean. In our setting, a Lipschitz bound of the form
Corollary

2.4
, namely
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\tilde{\theta}}^{*},\nu_{\theta}^{*})\leq L\|\tilde{\theta}-\theta\|
, implies a uniform bound of
\ell L
on the local sensitivity of
\ell
-Lipschitz posterior means with respect to
\theta
.

Example 3.3

(Variational Bayes with Prior Swapping)
.

In the prior swapping problem
[
64
]
, the variational posterior
\tilde{\nu}^{*}
is computed under a surrogate prior
\tilde{\rmp}
, while the desirable target is the variational posterior
\nu^{*}
associated with the prior
\rmp
. The surrogate prior
\tilde{\rmp}
is often chosen for computational tractability—for instance,
\tilde{\rmp}
might be conjugate to the likelihood, yielding a posterior with known parametric form
[
30
]
, or one that provides closed-form updates in coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI)
[
76
]
.

Existing approaches to prior swapping typically require estimating the likelihood ratio between
\tilde{\rmp}
and
\rmp
, a procedure that becomes computationally expensive in high dimensions when the normalizing constant of
\rmp
is unknown, e.g., when the prior is specified via an energy-based model
[
57
]
. Using the stability bound of
Theorem

2.1
, we propose an alternative approach to prior swapping that avoids directly computing the density ratio.

Corollary 3.4
.

Assume that
\log\rmp(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\cdot)
is
\alpha_{n,d}
-concave for some
\alpha_{n,d}\in\mathbb{R}
, and that the priors
\rmp
and
\tilde{\rmp}
are
\tilde{\alpha}_{d}
-log-concave for some
\tilde{\alpha}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha_{n,d}+\tilde{\alpha}_{d}>0
. Let
\nu^{*}
and
\tilde{\nu}^{*}
denote the MFVI optimizers for the posteriors under likelihood
\rmp(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\cdot)
and priors
\rmp
and
\tilde{\rmp}
, respectively. Then

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*},\nu^{*})\leq\frac{\|\nabla\log\tilde{\rmp}-\nabla\log\rmp\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}}{\alpha_{n,d}+\tilde{\alpha}_{d}}.

(20)

In particular, any
\ell
-Lipschitz statistic
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{*}}\left[\varphi(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}^{*}}\left[\varphi(X)\right]\right|\leq\frac{\ell\|\nabla\log\tilde{\rmp}-\nabla\log\rmp\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}}{\alpha_{n,d}+\tilde{\alpha}_{d}}.

(21)

Proof.

The bound (
20
) is a direct consequence of
Theorem

2.1
while (
21
) follows from (
20
) via the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula
[
74
, Equation (5.11)]
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\tilde{\nu}^{*},\nu^{*})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*},\nu^{*})
.
∎

It is often easy to evaluate the gradient of the log-prior even when it is not easy to compute the normalizing constants. Given a statistic
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of interest, the bound (
21
) provides an
uncertainty region
for
\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{*}}\left[\varphi(X)\right]
given samples from the surrogate posterior
\tilde{\nu}^{*}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{*}}\left[\varphi(X)\right]\in\big[\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}^{*}}\left[\varphi(X)\right]-\delta,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}^{*}}\left[\varphi(X)\right]+\delta\big],\quad\delta:=\frac{\beta\|\nabla\log\tilde{\rmp}-\nabla\log\rmp\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}}{\alpha_{n,d}+\tilde{\alpha}_{d}}.

Example 3.5

(Robustness for
\epsilon
-contamination class)
.

To assess the influence of priors on posterior inference, the global robustness approach considers the range of posteriors over a family of priors. Here we focus on a particularly appealing class, the

-contamination class, commonly used in robust Bayesian analysis
[

,

. Given a reference prior

Assume that

\alpha_{n,d}
-concave for some
\alpha_{n,d}>0
, and that
\rmp_{\epsilon}
is
\alpha_{\epsilon}
-log-concave for some

continuous in

Theorem

implies the stability bound

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\epsilon}^{*},\nu^{*})\leq\frac{\|\nabla\log\rmp_{\epsilon}-\nabla\log\rmp\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}}{\alpha_{n,d}+\alpha_{\epsilon}}=\left\|\frac{\rmq}{\rmp_{\epsilon}}\left(\nabla\log\rmq-\nabla\log\rmp\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha_{n,d}+\alpha_{\epsilon}}.

As

, the local sensitivity

(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{n,d})^{-1}\left\|\frac{\rmq}{\rmp}(\nabla\log\rmq-\nabla\log\rmp)\right\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}.

3.2
Quantitative Bernstein–von Mises Theorem for Mean-Field Variational Posterior

The well-known Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorem characterizes the asymptotic stability of the posterior for finite-dimensional parameter spaces (
[
72
, Section 10]
; see also
[
60
]
and the references therein). It tells us that, when the sample size is large, the posterior distribution is approximately a Gaussian centered at the true parameter. Recent works have extended BvM to semiparametric models
[
11
]
, variational inference
[
79
]
, misspecified models
[
60
]
and models with growing dimensions
[
52
]
.

The main result of this subsection is a quantitative BvM theorem for MFVI. We state two versions; version (i) assumes smoothness on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whereas (ii) imposes a smoothness condition only on a ball, and a growth condition outside the ball.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\pi_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)\propto\exp\left(-nf_{n}(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x
where
f_{n}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and
\alpha_{n}
-strongly convex. Let
x_{n}^{*}=\argmin f_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}^{*}:=\mathcal{N}\left(x_{n}^{*},D_{n}^{-1}\right)
, where
D_{n}
is the diagonal part
3
3

3

By the

of a matrix

, we mean the diagonal matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with the same diagonal as
A
.
of the matrix
n\nabla^{2}f_{n}(x_{n}^{*})
. Let
\nu_{n}^{*}
be the MFVI optimizer for target
\pi_{n}
.

(i)

If
f_{n}
is
b_{n}
-smooth, the MFVI optimizer satisfies

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\nu_{n}^{*},\gamma_{n}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{4b_{n}^{2}d}{\alpha_{n}^{3}n}.

(22)

(ii)

If
\nabla^{2}f(x_{n}^{*})\preceq b_{n}\mathrm{I}_{d}
and there exist

and
s_{n}>\sqrt{(d+2)/(n\alpha_{n}-\tau_{n})}

on the Euclidean ball

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\nu_{n}^{*},\gamma_{n}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{\ell_{n}^{2}\left(d^{2}+2d\right)}{3\alpha_{n}^{4}n^{2}}+\left(\frac{nb_{n}}{2}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{C(d+2)s_{n}^{d}}{\Gamma(d/2)\alpha_{n}^{2}(n\alpha_{n}-\tau_{n})}\exp\left(-\frac{(n\alpha_{n}-\tau_{n})s_{n}^{2}}{2}\right).

(23)

We remark that
\gamma_{n}^{*}
is the mean-field analogue of Laplace approximation
[
71
]
.
Theorem

3.6
establishes a non-asymptotic BvM theorem for MFVI in the Wasserstein distance. It applies to log-concave and log-smooth models in the form (

). Under a relaxed smoothness condition in an

[
79
]
and the total variation bounds based on Laplace approximations when

[
52
]

\gamma_{n}^{*}=\mathcal{N}(\beta_{n}^{*},D_{n}^{-1})

\beta_{n}^{*}
is the unique solution
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the equation

\nabla_{\beta}V_{\Lambda}(\beta)=-\tau\mathbf{w}+\tau\bm{A}\beta+\nabla v(\beta)=0\quad\mbox{where}\quad\nabla v(\beta):=\left(v^{\prime}(\beta_{1}),\ldots,v^{\prime}(\beta_{d})\right)^{\top}

and
D_{n}
is the diagonal part of the matrix
\bm{A}+\nabla^{2}v(\beta_{n}^{*})
. Then

\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\nu_{\tau}^{*},\gamma_{n}^{*}\right)\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{d(\tau b_{n}+b_{0})^{2}}{n(\tau\alpha_{n}+\alpha_{0})^{3}}}.

Corollary

3.8
addresses the non-contracting regime, where the posterior law of
\beta
may not contract strongly around the true regression vector
[
22
,
62
]
. In this setting, accurate quantification of the posterior uncertainty is a difficult but important task. In our result, the approximate normality of the MFVI optimizer holds on the strength of the curvature assumption on the prior, with the condition
b_{0}\geq\alpha_{0}>-\tau\alpha_{n}
ensuring that the prior does not overly distort the Gaussian structure of the likelihood.

3.3
Distributed Stochastic Control

In this application, we show how our stability result for the MFVI problem implies a stability result for distributed (a.k.a. decentralized) stochastic control. Fix a time horizon
T\in(0,\infty)
and a utility function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
which is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, concave, and
\beta
-smooth. Consider the classic problem of drift control under quadratic cost,

\sup_{(\alpha,X)\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{T})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\alpha(t,X_{t})\|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\right],

(29)

where the function
\alpha
controls the dynamics of the state process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
according to the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\,\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{t}=\alpha_{i}(t,X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\,\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t},\quad X^{i}_{0}=0,\quad i\in[d]

(30)

driven by independent Brownian motions
B^{i}
. This can be interpreted as a cooperative game (also known as social planner problem): each agent
i\in[d]
controls their process
X^{i}
while aiming to optimize the reward
g(X_{T})
depending on the processes of all agents. Each agent is subject to a quadratic cost and idiosyncratic noise. Popular special cases are utility functions
g
that depend on
X_{T}
only through the mean
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}X^{i}_{T}
, giving rise to a so-called mean-field control problem (here “mean-field” has a different meaning compared to the MFVI problem). See, e.g.,
[
20
,
19
]
and the references therein.

Before proceeding, let us detail the mathematical formalization of (
29
), which uses the so-called weak formulation to address the solvability of (
30
) while keeping
\alpha
as general as possible. The supremum in (
29
) is taken over pairs
(\alpha,X)
where
\alpha:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a measurable function and
X
is a weak solution of the system (
30
) on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
with independent Brownian motions
B^{i}
and
\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha(t,X_{t})|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
a.s. We denote by
\mathcal{A}
the set of all such
(\alpha,X)
.

The problem (
29
) is coupled in the sense that each agent’s control is a function of the states of all agents. We are interested in approximating (
29
) by distributed controls, meaning that each agent’s control is a function only of their own state. Thus, we define
\mathcal{A}_{\rm dist}
as the set of all
(\alpha,X)\in\mathcal{A}
where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
is of the distributed form

\alpha_{i}(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\hat{\alpha}_{i}(t,x_{i}),\quad i\in[d],

(31)

for some measurable functions
\hat{\alpha}_{i}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and moreover
\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, meaning that the components
X^{i}_{t}
are independent.
4
4

4

Independence already follows from (
31
) and the SDE (
30
) if the SDE satisfies uniqueness in law, but uniqueness is not guaranteed for irregular
\alpha
.
The resulting control problem is

\mathcal{V}_{\rm dist}(g):=\sup_{(\alpha,X)\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm dist}}\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{T})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\hat{\alpha}_{i}(t,X^{i}_{t})\right|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

(32)

When the dimension
d
is large, distributed controls are significantly more tractable as they only depend on a one-dimensional state, hence have been of recent interest in mean-field reinforcement learning (e.g.,
[
21
]
) and other areas. We refer to
[
59
,
63
]
for extensive references on distributed control.

The distributed control problem (
32
) can be reformulated, via Girsanov’s theorem, as the MFVI problem

\mathcal{M}(g):=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}\left(\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu-H\left(\mu\mid\gamma_{T}\right)\right)=-\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}H\left(\mu\mid\pi\right)+\log Z

(33)

where
\gamma_{T}:=\mathcal{N}(0,T\mathrm{I}_{d})
and
\pi(\mathrm{d}x):=Z^{-1}e^{g(x)}\gamma_{T}(\mathrm{d}x)
with
Z\in(0,\infty)
being the normalizing constant. See
[
55
, Equation (2.12)]
. In particular,
\mathcal{V}_{\rm dist}(g)=\mathcal{M}(g)
. As a consequence, our stability result for the MFVI problem implies stability for the distributed control problem with respect to the utility function
g
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
g,\tilde{g}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, concave and
\beta
-smooth. Let
\nu^{*}
be the MFVI optimizer of (
33
) for
g
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{V}_{\rm dist}(\tilde{g})-\mathcal{V}_{\rm dist}(g)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{(\beta T^{2}+T)d}\|\nabla\tilde{g}-\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta T^{2}+T}{2}\|\nabla\tilde{g}-\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}^{2}+\|\tilde{g}-g\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}.

Proof.

Note that the potential function of
\pi
is
V(x)=-g(x)+\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2T}
. In the notation of (
7
), we then have

\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu-H\left(\mu\mid\gamma_{T}\right)=\ELBO(\mu,V)
and hence
\mathcal{M}(g)=\mathcal{R}(V)
. As
g
is concave,
V
is
1/T
-convex and
(\beta+1/T)
-smooth. In view of
\mathcal{V}_{\rm dist}(\cdot)=\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
, the claim follows from
Theorem

2.3
.
∎

Lemma 4.1

(Equivalence of (

Recall the space

from (

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}H\left(\mu\mid\pi\right)=\inf_{(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}}\mathcal{F}_{V}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})+C

(34)

with the constant

\nu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{i}\in\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}H\left(\nu\mid\pi\right)

T(\nu):=(T_{1}(\nu),\dots,T_{d}(\nu))\in\argmin_{(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}}\mathcal{F}_{V}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}),

(35)

In particular, there is a unique optimizer of (

and characterized by

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

Since
\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\nabla^{2}V\succeq\alpha\mathrm{I}_{d}

to (

sufficiently large and

, for all

. Therefore,
\nu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
and
H(\nu^{*})<\infty
. Brenier’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
73
, Theorem 2.32]
,
[
36
, Theorem 1.2]
or
[
27
]
) and Caffarelli’s contraction theorem (see
[
17

and
[

) yield
5
5

5

In fact, we only use the one-dimensional special cases of those general results, which are straightforward from the explicit form of
T_{i}

[
69
, Section 2.1]
.
that the optimal transport map
T^{V}=(T_{1}^{V},\ldots,T_{d}^{V})

\nu^{*}
satisfies

,

Therefore,

\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}H\left(\nu\mid\pi\right)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}}H\left(\nu\mid\pi\right)=\inf_{T\in\mathcal{H}_{+}}H\left(T_{\sharp}\rho\mid\pi\right).

For any
T\in\mathcal{H}_{+}
with

\displaystyle H\left(T_{\sharp}\rho\mid\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\,\mathrm{d}T_{\sharp}\rho}{\,\mathrm{d}\pi}(x)T_{\sharp}\rho(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int\log T_{\sharp}\rho(x)T_{\sharp}\rho(\mathrm{d}x)+\int V(x)T_{\sharp}\rho(\mathrm{d}x)+\log Z.

By the Monge–Ampère equation

\det(\nabla T(u))T_{\sharp}\rho(T(u))=\rho(u)

\int\log T_{\sharp}\rho(x)T_{\sharp}\rho(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\log\rho(u)\rho(\mathrm{d}u)-\int\log\det(\nabla T(u))\rho(\mathrm{d}u).

As

\displaystyle H\left(T_{\sharp}\rho\mid\pi\right)

\displaystyle=-\int\log\det(\nabla T(u))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)+\int V(T(u))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)+\int\log\rho(u)\rho(\mathrm{d}u)+\log Z

\displaystyle=-\int\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log T_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})\rho(\mathrm{d}u)+\int V(T_{1}(u_{1}),\ldots,T_{d}(u_{d}))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+H(\rho)+\log Z

\inf_{T\in\mathcal{H}_{+},\,H(T_{\sharp}\rho)<\infty}\mathcal{F}_{V}(T)=\inf_{T\in\mathcal{H}_{+}}\mathcal{F}_{V}(T).

Indeed, let

4.2
Proof of
Theorem

2.1

Let
V,\tilde{V}
be potentials as in
Theorem

2.1
. Fixing
T=(T_{1},\dots,T_{d})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}
, we define the linear functional
\mathcal{L}_{T,V}:\mathcal{H}(\rho)\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\rho)\ni R=(R_{1},\dots,R_{d})\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{T,V}(R)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int\left\{\partial_{i}V(T(u))R_{i}(u_{i})-\frac{R_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})}{(T_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i})}\right\}\rho(\mathrm{d}u).

(36)

Thus
\mathcal{L}_{T,V}
is the first variation of
\mathcal{F}_{V}
at
T
in the direction of
R
.

Roughly speaking, the next lemma states that the first variation of
\mathcal{F}_{V}
at the optimizer
T^{V}
in the direction
T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}
is zero. Since
1/T_{i}^{\prime}
may not belong to
\mathcal{H}(\rho)
, we cannot directly take
R=T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}
; the integral of
R_{i}^{\prime}/T_{i}^{\prime}
in (
36
) could be infinite. Instead, we use a truncation argument. Given
M>0
, we define
R^{M}=(R_{1}^{M},\dots,R_{d}^{M})
as a truncation of
T^{\tilde{V}}_{i}-T^{V}
,

R_{i}^{M}(u_{i}):=\begin{cases}T^{\tilde{V}}_{i}(u_{i})-T^{V}_{i}(u_{i})&{\rm if}\ u_{i}\in[-M,M],\\
T^{\tilde{V}}_{i}(M)-T^{V}_{i}(M)&{\rm if}\ u_{i}\geq M,\\
T^{\tilde{V}}_{i}(-M)-T^{V}_{i}(-M)&{\rm if}\ u_{i}\leq-M.\end{cases}

Then Caffarelli’s contraction theorem
[
17
,
26
]
yields that
|(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}(u_{i})|\leq\max\{\alpha^{-1/2},{\tilde{\alpha}}^{-1/2}\}
for
u_{i}\in[-M,M]
while
(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}=0
outside
[-M,M]
.
We can now state the rigorous version of the first-order condition.

Lemma 4.2
.

In the setting of
Theorem

2.1
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{T^{V},V}(R^{M})=0
for all
M>0
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality,
\alpha\leq\tilde{\alpha}
. Observe that

\int|\partial_{i}V(T(u))|^{2}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\leq\int\|\nabla V(x)\|^{2}\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty

as
\nu^{*}
is
\alpha
-log-concave and the growth condition (
5
) holds. On the other hand,
|(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}|
is bounded by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}
on
[-M,M]
and vanishes elsewhere. Thus, the Monge–Ampère equation
(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}(u_{i})(\nu_{i}^{*}\circ T_{i}^{V}(u_{i}))=\rho_{1}(u_{i})
, together with the fact that
\nu_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, yield

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}}{(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{-M}^{M}\frac{1}{(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}=\int_{-M}^{M}\frac{\nu_{i}^{*}\circ T_{i}^{V}}{\rho_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}=\int_{-M}^{M}\nu_{i}^{*}\circ T_{i}^{V}(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}u_{i}<\infty.

Moreover, integration by parts
[
15
, Theorem 5.1.2]
and the Monge–Ampère equation yield

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}}{(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{\nu^{*}_{i}\circ T_{i}^{V}(u_{i})}{\rho_{1}(u_{i})}\rho_{1}(\mathrm{d}u_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{(R_{i}^{M})^{\prime}}e^{\log(\nu^{*}_{i}\circ T_{i}^{V})}\,\mathrm{d}u_{i}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}R_{i}^{M}(\nu^{*}_{i}\circ T_{i}^{V})(\log(\nu^{*}_{i}\circ T_{i}^{V}))^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}u_{i}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}R_{i}^{M}(\nu^{*}_{i}\circ T_{i}^{V})((\log(\nu^{*}_{i}))^{\prime}\circ T_{i}^{V})(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}u_{i}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}R_{i}^{M}((\log(\nu^{*}_{i}))^{\prime}\circ T_{i}^{V})\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{1},\quad\forall i\in[d].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T^{V},V}(R^{M})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int R_{i}^{M}(u_{i})\left\{\partial_{i}V(T^{V}(u))+(\log(\nu^{*}_{i}))^{\prime}\circ T_{i}^{V}(u_{i})\right\}\rho(\mathrm{d}u).

(37)

Finally, the fixed-point equation (
4

\log(\nu^{*}_{i})(x_{i})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}V(x_{i},x_{-i})d\nu^{*}_{-i}(x_{-i})-\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\int V(x)d\nu^{*}_{-j}(x_{-i})}d\nu^{*}_{i}(x_{i})\right)

and thus

(\log(\nu^{*}_{i}))^{\prime}\circ T_{i}^{V}(u_{i})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\partial_{i}V(T^{V}(u))\rho_{-i}(\mathrm{d}u_{-i}),\quad\forall u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

Plugging this into (
37
) yields
\mathcal{L}_{T^{V},V}(R^{M})=0
.
∎

We can now state the proof of our first main result.

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
.

On the one hand,

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\,\,\mathcal{L}_{T^{\tilde{V}},V}(R^{M})-\mathcal{L}_{T^{V},V}(R^{M})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int\left\{(\partial_{i}V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-\partial_{i}V(T^{V}(u)))R_{i}^{M}(u_{i})+\frac{R_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})((T^{\tilde{V}}_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i})-(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}(u_{i}))}{(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}(u_{i})(T^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}(u_{i})}\right\}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

\mathcal{L}_{T^{\tilde{V}},\tilde{V}}(R^{M})=\mathcal{L}_{T^{V},V}(R^{M})=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T^{\tilde{V}},V}(R^{M})-\mathcal{L}_{T^{V},V}(R^{M})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int\left\{(\partial_{i}V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-\partial_{i}{\tilde{V}}(T^{\tilde{V}}(u)))R^{M}_{i}(u_{i})\right\}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\leq\|R^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\|\nabla V\circ T^{\tilde{V}}-\nabla\tilde{V}\circ T^{\tilde{V}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\|R^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\|\nabla V-\nabla\tilde{V}\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}.

Combining the displays, we have

\displaystyle\|R^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\|\nabla V-\nabla\tilde{V}\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int(\partial_{i}V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-\partial_{i}V(T^{V}(u)))R_{i}^{M}(u_{i})\rho(\mathrm{d}u).

\|T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}\|_{\mathcal{H}(\rho)}=\left(\|T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\|(T^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}-(T^{V})^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\right)^{1/2}\leq\alpha^{-1}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}.

(43)

In particular, we record for ease of reference the bound

\|(T^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}-(T^{V})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leq\alpha^{-1}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}.

(44)

Remark 4.4
.

In the proof of
Theorem

2.1
,
\alpha
-log-concavity is applied to infer (

). This step remains valid if
\alpha
-log-concavity is relaxed to
\alpha
-monotonicity with respect to

, meaning that

\int\langle\nabla V(S(u))-\nabla V(T^{V}(u)),S(u)-T^{V}(u)\rangle\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\geq\alpha\|S-T^{V}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}

for all

, where

denotes the minimizer of (

). This observation may suggest an avenue to go beyond the

-log-concave setting. The above monotonicity is in the spirit of the log-Sobolev inequality for measures which is often used as an alternative to log-concavity in establishing convergence results in the literature of non-log-concave sampling
[
25
]
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3

Next, we prove our bound for the optimal reward function.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

Note that the
\beta
-log-smoothness assumption implies (
5
). By
Lemma

4.1
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}(\tilde{V})-\mathcal{R}(V)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{V}}(T^{\tilde{V}})-\mathcal{F}_{V}(T^{V})\right|

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left|-\int\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\log((T_{i}^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}(u_{i}))-\log((T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}(u_{i}))\right)\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right|}_{A}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left|\int\left(\tilde{V}(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-V(T^{V}(u))\right)\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right|}_{B}.

As
\tilde{V},V
are
\beta
-smooth,
(T_{i}^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime},(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}\geq 1/\sqrt{\beta}
a.e. by Caffarelli’s contraction theorem. We deduce

\left|\log((T_{i}^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}(u_{i}))-\log((T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}(u_{i}))\right|\leq\sqrt{\beta}\left|(T_{i}^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}(u_{i})-(T_{i}^{V})^{\prime}(u_{i})\right|

and thus

A\leq\sqrt{\beta d}\|(T^{\tilde{V}})^{\prime}-(T^{V})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

(45)

To bound

B\leq\left|\int\tilde{V}(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right|+\left|\int V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-V(T^{V}(u))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right|.

For the first term on the right-hand side, Jensen’s inequality gives

\left|\int\tilde{V}(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right|\leq\|\tilde{V}-V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}.

(46)

On the other hand, by
\beta
-smoothness and
[
7
, Theorem 5.8]
,

\left|V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-V(T^{V}(u))\right|\leq\left|\langle\nabla V(T^{V}(u)),T^{\tilde{V}}(u)-T^{V}(u)\rangle\right|+\frac{\beta}{2}\|T^{\tilde{V}}(u)-T^{V}(u)\|^{2},\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{*}}\left[\Delta V(X)-\langle\nabla V(X),\nabla V(X)\rangle\right]=0
(see, e.g., the proof of
[
25
, Lemma 4.0.1]
), we have

\int\|\nabla V(T^{V}(u))\|^{2}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)=\int\|\nabla V(x)\|^{2}\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\Delta V(x)\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\beta d.

(47)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int\left|\langle\nabla V(T^{V}(u)),T^{\tilde{V}}(u)-T^{V}(u)\rangle\right|\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\|\nabla V(T^{V}(u))\|^{2}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right)^{1/2}\|T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\beta d}\|T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

In summary,

\left|\int\left(V(T^{\tilde{V}}(u))-V(T^{V}(u))\right)\rho(\mathrm{d}u)\right|\leq\sqrt{\beta d}\|T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|T^{\tilde{V}}-T^{V}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}.

(48)

Collecting (
45
), (
46
), (
48
) and applying (
41
) and (
44
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}(\tilde{V})-\mathcal{R}(V)\right|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\beta d}}{\alpha}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha^{2}}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}^{2}+\|\tilde{V}-V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}.

(49)

When
V,\tilde{V}
are normalized, meaning that
\int V\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}^{*}=\int\tilde{V}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}^{*}
, then as
\tilde{\nu}^{*}
is
\alpha
-log-concave, applying the Poincaré inequality
[

, Corollary 4.8.2]
to the last term in (

\left|\mathcal{R}(\tilde{V})-\mathcal{R}(V)\right|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\beta d}+1}{\alpha}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha^{2}}\|\nabla\tilde{V}-\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\nu}^{*})}^{2}.\qed

.

The

Integrating our assumption that

It remains to bound

below, we know that

9

\displaystyle\leq C_{\theta,d}\int\|x\|^{p}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_{\theta}}{2}\|x-x^{*}_{\theta}\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thus, substituting (

Whereas for (ii), when

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{*}_{\theta}}[e^{\|X\|}]\leq e^{\|x^{*}_{\theta}\|}\int\exp(\|x-x^{*}_{\theta}\|)C_{\theta,d}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_{\theta}}{2}\|x-x^{*}_{\theta}\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

Therefore, we obtain

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\tilde{\theta}}^{*},\nu_{\theta}^{*})\leq\frac{LC_{\theta,d}^{1/2}}{\tilde{\alpha}_{\theta}}\sqrt{S(d)e^{\|x^{*}_{\theta}\|}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{r-\frac{\alpha_{\theta}}{2}r^{2}}r^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}\|\tilde{\theta}-\theta\|,

(55)

completing the proof.

Together with the first claim, this shows the boundedness of the linear functional

∎

Next, we derive a bilinear relation that will be the foundation for the proof of

.
Lemma

Using (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T^{\theta},V_{\theta_{0}}}(R)-\mathcal{L}_{T^{\theta_{0}},V_{\theta_{0}}}(R)

On the other hand, the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T^{\theta},V_{\theta_{0}}}(R)-\mathcal{L}_{T^{\theta},V_{\theta}}(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int\left\{(\partial_{i}V_{\theta_{0}}(T^{\theta}(u))-\partial_{i}V_{\theta}(T^{\theta}(u)))R_{i}(u_{i})\right\}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle=\int\left\langle\nabla V_{\theta_{0}}(T^{\theta}(u))-\nabla V_{\theta}(T^{\theta}(u)),R(u)\right\rangle\rho(\mathrm{d}u).

Set

for brevity. Combining (

\displaystyle\int\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ij}V_{\theta_{0}}(\bar{T}^{j,\theta}(u))S_{i}(u_{i})R_{j}(u_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{S_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})R_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})}{(T^{\theta_{0}}_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i})(T^{\theta}_{i})^{\prime}(u_{i})}\rho(\mathrm{d}u)

and showing that the resulting left-hand side converges to the bilinear operator

Remark 5.4
.

For some of the calculations below, it will be convenient to use the following norm for a vector-valued function

This norm satisfies
\|f\|_{\tilde{L}^{p}(\rho)}^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|f_{i}\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}^{p}
. It differs from
\|f\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}
by using the

instead of the Euclidean 2-norm, but is of course equivalent. We note that

\int|\left\langle f,g\right\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leq\|f\|_{\tilde{L}^{p}(\rho)}\|g\|_{\tilde{L}^{q}(\rho)}

, by using Hölder’s inequality both on

and for the integrals.
Furthermore,

(a)

(b)

if

d^{\frac{p-2}{2p}}\|f\|_{\tilde{L}^{p}(\rho)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}\leq\|f\|_{\tilde{L}^{p}(\rho)}
;

(c)

for

, we have
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\|f\|_{\tilde{L}^{2}(\rho)}
.

Here (a) follows from the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{p}\leq(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2})^{p/2}\leq d^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{p}
for

when

while (b) follows from

for
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
when

.

Step 1: Proof of (

6
Technical Lemmas

In this section, we provide several properties of
\alpha
-log-concave measures and their MFVI optimizer that were used in the main proofs. The last result of the section,
Lemma

6.6
, provides a calculation used in the proof of
Theorem

.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,
\alpha
-log-concave and
\beta
-log-smooth with potential function
V
. Let
\nu^{*}
be the MFVI optimizer for target
\pi
and
x^{*}:=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)
. Then

\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(85)

where

C:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{(\beta-\alpha)d}{\alpha}\left[2H\left(\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})\mid\pi\right)+d\right]\right).

(86)

Moreover,

\int\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu^{*}\leq\frac{4H\left(\mu\mid\pi\right)+2d}{\alpha},\quad\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}.

(87)

Remark 6.2
.

The stated constant
C
in (
86
) depends on
x^{*}
. This dependence can be eliminated by replacing
\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})
with
\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})
. In fact, the proof shows that the bound (
85
) remains valid if
\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})
is replaced by any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
.

Proof of
Lemma

6.1
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x^{*}=0
.
Then by strong convexity and smoothness,

V(0)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x\|^{2}\leq V(x)\leq V(0)+\frac{\beta}{2}\|x\|^{2}.

From (
4
), we know that

\nu_{i}^{*}(x_{i})=Z_{i}\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}V(x_{i},x_{-i})\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right),

where
Z_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}V(x_{i},x_{-i})\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\nu_{i}^{*}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|x\|^{2}\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|x\|^{2}\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|x_{-i}\|^{2}\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right)}{\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|x_{-i}\|^{2}\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|x_{-i}\|^{2}\nu_{-i}^{*}(\mathrm{d}x_{-i})\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\|x\|^{2}\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}x_{i}^{2}\right).

As
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
, we conclude

\nu^{*}(x)\leq\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{(\beta-\alpha)d}{2}\int\|x\|^{2}\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x\|^{2}\right).

(88)

It remains to bound the second moment,
\int\|x\|^{2}\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)
. Using the triangle inequality, Talagrand’s transportation inequality
[
5
, Corollary 9.3.2]
, the optimality of
\nu
, and finally
[
32
, Proposition 1]
, we have for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}
the bound

\displaystyle\int\|x\|^{2}d\nu^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\nu^{*},\delta_{0})

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\nu^{*},\pi)+\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\pi,\delta_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}H\left(\nu^{*}\mid\pi\right)+2\int\|x\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}H\left(\mu\mid\pi\right)+2\int\|x\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}H\left(\mu\mid\pi\right)+\frac{2d}{\alpha}.

This is the claim (
87
). Inserting that result into (
88
) and specializing to
\mu=\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})
gives

\nu^{*}(x)\leq\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{(\beta-\alpha)d}{\alpha}\left[2H\left(\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})\mid\pi\right)+d\right]\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x\|^{2}\right),

which is the claim (
85
).
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,
\alpha
-log-concave and
\beta
-log-smooth with potential function
V
. Let
\nu^{*}
be the MFVI optimizer for target
\pi
and
x^{*}:=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)
. We have

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\nu^{*})}\leq C_{\alpha,\beta,d,x^{*},\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{N}(0,(1+\varepsilon)\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d}))}

for any
\varepsilon>0
and any measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, where

C_{\alpha,\beta,d,x^{*},\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{(\beta-\alpha)d}{\alpha}\left[2H\left(\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})\mid\pi\right)+d\right]+\frac{(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\alpha}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\|x^{*}\|^{2}\right).

(89)

Proof.

Using
Lemma

6.1
and the fact that
\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\|x\|^{2}-\frac{1+\varepsilon^{-1}}{1+\varepsilon}\|x^{*}\|^{2}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
(which follows from Young’s inequality),

\nu^{*}(\mathrm{d}x)\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\exp\left(\frac{(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\alpha}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\|x^{*}\|^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\|x\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
C
is specified in (
86
). Thus

\displaystyle\int|f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu^{*}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int|f(x)|^{2}C\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha,\beta,d,x^{*},\varepsilon}\int|f(x)|^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\|x\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

as desired.
∎

The following fact is standard.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
f,f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\alpha
-strongly convex. Let
x_{n}^{*}
(resp.
x^{*}
) be the minimizer of
f_{n}
(resp.
f
). If
f_{n}\to f
pointwise, then
x_{n}^{*}\to x^{*}
and
f_{n}(x_{n}^{*})\to f(x^{*})
.

Proof.

Pointwise convergence of finite convex functions implies Gamma convergence
[
28
, Example 5.13]
. Moreover, uniform strong convexity and pointwise convergence together imply equi-coercivity. For a Gamma convergent sequence of equi-coercive functions with unique minimizers, the minimizers and the minimum values converge
[
28
, Corollary 7.24]
.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\{\pi_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a family of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,
\alpha
-log-concave and
\beta
-log-smooth probability measures with potential functions
\{V_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
and
x^{*}_{\theta}:=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}V_{\theta}(x)
. Denote by
C_{\alpha,\beta,d,x_{\theta}^{*},\varepsilon}
the constant specified in
Lemma

6.3
corresponding to
\theta
. For any
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
M_{\alpha,\beta,d,x_{\theta_{0}}^{*},\varepsilon}
such that if
V_{\theta}\to V_{\theta_{0}}
pointwise as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
, then

\limsup_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}C_{\alpha,\beta,d,x_{\theta}^{*},\varepsilon}\leq M_{\alpha,\beta,d,x_{\theta_{0}}^{*},\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Let
V_{\theta}\to V_{\theta_{0}}
pointwise as
\theta\to\theta_{0}
, then
x_{\theta}^{*}\to x_{\theta_{0}}^{*}
and
V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})\to V_{\theta_{0}}(x_{\theta_{0}}^{*})
by
Lemma

6.4
. Since
V_{\theta}
is
\alpha
-convex and
\beta
-smooth,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}\leq V_{\theta}(x)-V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})\leq\frac{\beta}{2}\|x-x_{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}.

Thus, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}(x_{\theta}^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})}[V_{\theta}(X)]\leq V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}(x_{\theta}^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})}\|X-x_{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}=V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})+\frac{d\beta}{2\alpha}

and

\int e^{-V_{\theta}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq e^{-V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})}\int e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=C_{\alpha,d}e^{-V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})}.

In view of
V_{\theta}(x_{\theta}^{*})\to V_{\theta_{0}}(x_{\theta_{0}}^{*})
, it follows that

\limsup_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}H\left(\mathcal{N}(x_{\theta}^{*},\alpha^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d})\mid\pi_{\theta}\right)<\infty.

In view of (
89
) and
x_{\theta}^{*}\to x_{\theta_{0}}^{*}
, this shows the claim.
∎

Finally, we state an identity that was used in the proof of
Theorem

3.6
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
s>\sqrt{(d+2)/\alpha}
. Then

I(\alpha,s):=\int_{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y\|\geq s\}}\|y\|^{2}\,\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\|y\|^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\leq\,S(d)\frac{(d+2)s^{d}}{2\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha s^{2}}{2}\right),

where
S(d)
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

By passing to spherical coordinates,

I(\alpha,s)=S(d)\int_{s}^{\infty}r^{2+d-1}\,\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,r^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r.

The change of variable
u=\frac{\alpha}{2}\,r^{2}
gives

r^{d+1}\,\mathrm{d}r=\left(\sqrt{\frac{2u}{\alpha}}\right)^{d+1}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}u}{\alpha\sqrt{\frac{2u}{\alpha}}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{2u}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u

and thus

I(\alpha,s)=S(d)\,\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\frac{\alpha s^{2}}{2}}^{\infty}u^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\exp(-u)\,\mathrm{d}u.

By
[
35
, Theorem 4.4.3]
, as
\alpha s^{2}>d+2
, the incomplete gamma function is bounded by

\int_{\frac{\alpha s^{2}}{2}}^{\infty}u^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\exp(-u)\,\mathrm{d}u\leq\frac{d+2}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha s^{2}}{2}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha s^{2}}{2}\right)

and the claim follows.
∎
