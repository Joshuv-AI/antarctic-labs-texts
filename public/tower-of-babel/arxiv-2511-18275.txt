Variations of the Hardy Z-Function and the Montgomery Pair Correlation Conjecture
Yochay Jerby

Abstract
. In 1973, Montgomery formulated the Pair Correlation Conjecture , asserting that the nontrivial zeros of Hardy’s Z Z -function exhibit the same local spacing statistics as the eigenvalues of large Hermitian matrices drawn from the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE). However, Z ⁡ ( t ) Z(t) is a fixed, deterministic analytic function, and the mechanism by which its zeros replicate the statistical behavior of random matrices has remained elusive. In this paper, assuming the Riemann Hypothesis (RH), we prove Montgomery’s Pair Correlation Conjecture for the zeros of Z ⁡ ( t ) Z(t) . In recent work, for each N ∈ ℕ N\in\mathbb{N} , we introduced the finite-dimensional space 𝒵 N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) of analytic sections Z N ​ ( t , a ¯ ) = cos ⁡ ( θ ⁡ ( t ) ) + ∑ k = 1 N a k k + 1 ​ cos ⁡ ( θ ⁡ ( t ) − t ​ log ⁡ ( k + 1 ) ) , a ¯ = ( a 1 , … , a N ) ∈ ℝ N , Z_{N}(t;\overline{a})=\cos(\theta(t))+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_{k}}{\sqrt{k+1}}\,\cos\!\bigl(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1)\bigr),\qquad\overline{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}, where θ ⁡ ( t ) = ℑ ⁡ log ⁡ Γ ⁡ ( 1 4 + i ​ t 2 ) − t 2 ​ log ​ π \theta(t)=\Im\log\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{it}{2}\right)-\tfrac{t}{2}\log\pi is the Riemann–Siegel theta function, with Z N ​ ( t , 1 ¯ ) Z_{N}(t;\overline{1}) approximating Z ⁡ ( t ) Z(t) on [ 2 ​ N , 2 ​ N + 2 ] [2N,2N+2] . Within 𝒵 N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) we now define the “real hall” domain ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) , consisting of sections whose zeros in the associated critical rectangle over [ 2 ​ N , 2 ​ N + 2 ] [2N,2N+2] are real, simple, and remain so along any homotopy from the core section Z 0 ​ ( t ) = cos ⁡ θ ⁡ ( t ) Z_{0}(t)=\cos\theta(t) . This domain plays the role of the random matrix ensemble in our setting. Equipping ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) with a probability measure μ N \mu_{N} on ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) with a smooth, strictly positive density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the coefficient space, restricted to ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) , we obtain a natural probabilistic model for the zeros of the sections. On ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) we construct a Skorokhod-type stochastic differential equation with reflection at its discriminant boundary, and show that the induced dynamics of the zeros t j ​ ( a ¯ ) t_{j}(\overline{a}) are, after unfolding and whitening, equivalent in law to Dyson Brownian motion with β = 2 \beta=2 for a one-dimensional Coulomb gas. As a consequence of universality theory, for every Schwartz test function f f , the expected local pair–correlation functional P ​ C N ​ ( f , a ¯ ) PC_{N}(f;\overline{a}) of the zeros of a μ N \mu_{N} -random section in [ 2 ​ N , 2 ​ N + 2 ] [2N,2N+2] converges, as N → ∞ N\to\infty , to the GUE sine–kernel law. To pass from this ensemble result to the genuine Hardy Z Z -function on the whole critical line, we apply Selberg’s probabilistic theory of the argument S ⁡ ( t ) S(t) to prove that the sequence of pair–correlation observables P ​ C N ​ ( f , 1 ¯ ) PC_{N}(f;\overline{1}) of Z N ​ ( t , 1 ¯ ) Z_{N}(t;\overline{1}) on different windows [ 2 ​ N , 2 ​ N + 2 ] [2N,2N+2] behaves asymptotically like a decorrelated sample drawn from the GUE-distributed ensembles on ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) , for N ∈ ℕ N\in\mathbb{N} . This quenched limit upgrades the averaged GUE law on ℛ ​ ℋ N ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}) to a deterministic statement for Z ⁡ ( t ) Z(t) , and yields the GUE pair–correlation law for the nontrivial zeros of ζ ⁡ ( s ) \zeta(s) , as anticipated by Montgomery.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Montgomery Pair Correlation Conjecture and Random Matrix Theory

The Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
, defined for
\Re(s)>1
by the absolutely convergent series

\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}},

(1)

and extended analytically to the complex plane with a simple pole at
s=1
, lies at the heart of modern analytic number theory. Its nontrivial zeros, conjectured by Riemann to lie on the critical line
\Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}
, encode deep information about the distribution of prime numbers. Understanding their fine-scale structure remains one of the most important and enduring open problems in mathematics, with far-reaching implications for number theory, algebra, cryptography, and quantum physics, see
[
5
,
57
]
and references therein.

A pioneering observation concerning the zeros of
\zeta(s)
was made in the 1970s by Hugh Montgomery
[
43
]
, who analyzed the pairwise statistics of the imaginary parts of the nontrivial zeros. Letting the nontrivial zeros be
\rho_{n}=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma_{n}
, ordered by increasing
\gamma_{n}
, Montgomery considered the distribution of the normalized spacings

s_{n,m}(T)\;:=\;\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma_{n}-\gamma_{m})\,

(2)

with
0<\gamma_{n},\gamma_{m}\leq T
, in the limit as
\gamma_{n},\gamma_{m}\to\infty
. He conjectured that the local statistics of these spacings resemble those of the eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices drawn from the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE). The resulting
Pair Correlation Conjecture
(PCC) asserts that the limiting pair correlation function is given by

g(x)=1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{2},

(3)

so that for any Schwartz test function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, one expects

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\left(1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{2}\right)\,dx,

(4)

where

N(T)\sim\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}

(5)

denotes the number of nontrivial zeros up to height
T
. This striking conjecture reveals a deep correspondence between the fine-scale distribution of the zeta zeros and the spectral statistics of random matrices, and has profound implications for the connection between number theory and quantum chaotic systems.

Since then, extensive numerical investigations, most notably by Odlyzko, have provided overwhelming support for the PCC
[
49
,
47
,
48
]
. The conjecture has become one of the central pieces of evidence linking the theory of the Riemann zeta function with random matrix theory (RMT). However, despite the empirical success of this connection, a rigorous explanation for
why
the zeros of
\zeta(s)
should statistically mimic eigenvalues from GUE remained elusive. The prevailing approaches developed so far to explain the appearance of GUE-type statistics in the zeros of the Riemann zeta function have relied on various modelling frameworks: either probabilistic analogies from random matrix theory
[
39
,
38
,
21
]
, semiclassical trace formulas inspired by quantum chaos
[
2
,
4
]
, or deep but abstract spectral constructions such as those arising from noncommutative geometry
[
9
]
.
The work of Rudnick and Sarnak
[
55
]
establishes a foundational framework for analyzing the statistical behavior of zeros across general families of
L
-functions. Assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH), their results provide compelling evidence that GUE-type correlations occur across families of
L
-functions, significantly generalizing Montgomery’s original conjecture, see Remark
10.1
.

While these perspectives provide powerful heuristic and statistical insight, they do not derive the GUE behavior from any mechanism internal to the analytic structure of the zeta function itself. That is, so far, no known argument explains how the classical intrinsic properties of
\zeta(s)
could account for the emergence of random matrix statistics.

At the heart of the difficulty lies a fundamental conceptual disparity: the Riemann zeta function is a completely rigid and deterministic object, while random matrix models are inherently probabilistic, built from ensembles whose statistics emerge through averaging. That a fixed arithmetic function could exhibit the same fine-scale statistical behavior as large random matrices raises profound questions. Despite the power of heuristic frameworks, ranging from trace formulas to analogies with quantum chaos, none provides an explanation rooted within the analytic structure of
\zeta(s)
itself.

Identifying such an internal mechanism is one of the central open problems at the interface of number theory and mathematical physics, and is the subject of this work. Our approach is to regard Montgomery’s conjecture as the combination of the following two questions:

(1)

The microscopic question.

Identify a natural analytic ensemble intrinsically associated with
Z(t)
, analogous to the matrix ensembles of RMT, and show that the average local (microscopic) statistics of its zeros coincide with those of GUE.

(2)

The macroscopic question.

Assuming
Z(t)
can be related to such analytic ensembles, in what sense does it globally (macroscopically) behave like a “random” or “typical” element of these spaces, despite being a purely deterministic function?

1.2.
The Microscopic Question - Dyson Brownian Motion on Zeros of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})

Recall that the Hardy
Z
-function is the real function defined by

Z(t)=e^{i\theta(t)}\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)

(6)

where

\theta(t)=\text{arg}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{it}{2}\right)\right)-\frac{t}{2}\log(\pi).

(7)

is the Riemann-Siegel
\theta
-function
[
16
,
31
]
. The Riemann Hypothesis (RH) is equivalent to the statement that all non-trivial zeros of
Z(t)
are real.
In a series of recent works
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
, we introduced the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
of analytic sections

Z_{N}(t;\overline{a})\;=\;\cos(\theta(t))+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_{k}}{\sqrt{k+1}}\,\cos\bigl(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1)\bigr),

(8)

parametrized by real coefficients
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and refer to

Z_{0}(t):=Z_{N}(t;\bar{0})=\cos(\theta(t))

(9)

as the core function of
Z(t)
. Consider:

Definition
.

Let
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
denote the subset of all functions
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})

for which there exists a continuous homotopy
a:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
a(0)=\bar{0}
and
a(1)=\bar{a}
such that,
for every
s\in[0,1]
, the zeros of
Z_{N}(t;a(s))
in the critical rectangle

2N\leq\Re t\leq 2N+2,\qquad-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Im t\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

(10)

remain real and simple, and the number of zeros in the rectangle stays constant along the homotopy.

The boundary
\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is described in terms of the analytical discriminants introduced in
[
37
]
, see Remark
3.2

Z(t)=Z_{N(t)}(t;\overline{1})+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{t}}\right),

(11)

where
N(t)=\left[\frac{t}{2}\right]
. Although, a priori, the error term could contain essential information about the zeros, we showed in

,
37
]
that, unlike the classical Riemann–Siegel formula, the approximation (
11

Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R}),

(12)

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Our first main result in this work is:

Theorem A

(GUE law for the ensemble
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
)
.

As
N\to\infty
, the average pair
correlation of the zeros of sections

Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, taken with respect to
any probability measure
\mu_{N}
on

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
with a smooth, strictly positive density
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the coefficient space, restricted
to
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, converges to the GUE distribution. That is, for any Schwartz test
function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\!\left[\frac{1}{M_{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{j},t_{k}\in[2N,2N+2]\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log N}{2\pi}\bigl(t_{j}(\bar{a})-t_{k}(\bar{a})\bigr)\right)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\left(1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{2}\right)\,dx,

(13)

where
M_{N}\sim\tfrac{1}{\pi}\log\!\bigl(\tfrac{N}{\pi}\bigr)
is the
number of zeros
\{t_{j}(\bar{a})\}
of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})
in
[2N,2N+2]
.

Theorem
A
provides the first purely analytic framework in which random matrix behavior emerges intrinsically from the theory of the
Z
-function itself. To illustrate this phenomenon, Fig.

P_{\mathrm{GUE}}(s)=\frac{32}{\pi^{2}}s^{2}e^{-4s^{2}/\pi},

P_{\mathrm{GUE}}(s)

Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})

The key mechanism underlying Theorem
A
is the introduction of a Skorokhod-type stochastic differential equation (SDE) flow on the real hall
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, with reflection at the boundary, which drives a diffusion of the section parameters
\overline{a}(t)
while keeping the flow inside
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. This SDE induces, via the analytic dependence of zeros on the coefficients, a coupled system of SDEs for the deformed zeros
t_{n}(\overline{a}(t))
.

This construction is directly analogous to the classical random matrix setting, where one considers an SDE on the space of Hermitian matrices and studies the induced dynamics of the eigenvalues. In that case the eigenvalue process is Dyson Brownian motion (DBM) with parameter
\beta=2
, and a central result of Dyson
[
15
]
shows that DBM is ergodic with the GUE eigenvalue distribution as its unique invariant measure, with convergence to equilibrium as time tends to infinity. The proof of Theorem
A
rests on showing that the induced zero-dynamics on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, after unfolding and whitening, share the same asymptotic local properties as DBM.

To pass from the obtained structural DBM on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
to the full GUE sine–kernel law, we appeal to the modern universality theory for Dyson Brownian motion and related log–gases. Whereas Dyson’s original work
[
15
]
showed that DBM is ergodic and converges in law to the global GUE equilibrium for a specific matrix Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, later developments, due to Erdős, Schlein, Yau, Bourgade and others, established that the local correlation functions of DBM at fixed energy are universal. That is, for a wide class of initial distributions and potentials, the eigenvalues rapidly acquire GUE local statistics essentially independent of the initial law, see
[
1
,
6
,
13
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
40
,
50
]
. Applying these universality results to the zero–dynamics on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, once coupled at the microscopic scale to a suitable DBM, we obtain that the local pair–correlation functionals
PC_{N}(f;\bar{a})
of
\mu_{N}
–random sections converge to the sine–kernel law stated in Theorem
A
. Consequently, the ensemble of zeros of a
\mu_{N}
-random section in
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
exhibits the same GUE sine–kernel pair–correlation behavior in the window
[2N,2N+2]
, resolving the microscopic question.

1.3.
The Macroscopic Question - the Ergodicity of the Pair-Correlations of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})

Theorem
A
establishes GUE pair–correlation at the level of the real hall ensemble

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, but by itself this does not yet say anything about any

specific
element of the ensemble.
In fact, in view of Theorem
A
, the Pair–Correlation Conjecture can now be reformulated as a question about the realization of the Hardy function within the real halls
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Namely, whether the GUE universality established for the ensemble on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is in fact realized by the specific, fixed analytic function
Z(t)
itself. In light of the approximation (
11
), we regard
Z(t)
on each window
[2N,2N+2]
as being represented by the canonical section

Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, so that the problem becomes one of understanding the statistical behavior of the deterministic sequence of sections
\{Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
inside the ensembles
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Before considering the function
Z(t)
itself, we introduce a randomized analogue
defined on the whole real line.
For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let

Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})

be chosen independently according to the Gaussian measure
\mu_{N}

restricted to
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.
We define the
randomized
Z
–function

Z(t;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\})
by setting

Z(t;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\})=Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)}),

(15)

for
t\in[2N,2N+2]
, so that
Z(t;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\})
coincides with the section

Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})
on each corresponding window. Theorem
B
upgrades the averaged GUE result for sections

Z_{N}(t;\overline{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
on individual windows

[2N,2N+2]
to a genuine GUE pair–correlation law for a single realization of a
randomized
Z
–function on the entire real line.

Theorem B

(GUE Pair–Correlation for randomized
Z
–functions)
.

Let
Z(t;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\})
be a randomized
Z
–function as above.
Then the real zeros of
Z(t;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\})
obey the
Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) pair–correlation law.
That is, for every Schwartz test function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{N(T)}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\!\left(1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{\!2}\right)dx,

(16)

where
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
denote the real zeros of

Z(t;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\})
, and

N(T)\sim\tfrac{T}{2\pi}\log\!\tfrac{T}{2\pi}

is their counting function.

Finally, the following theorem provides an affirmative answer and constitutes a proof of Montgomery’s original conjecture. It is proven by showing that the Hardy

Z
–function constructed from the deterministic sections

\overline{a}^{(N)}=\overline{1}
reproduces, in the limit, essentially the same
pair–correlation statistics as the randomized

Z
–functions considered in Theorem
B
.

Theorem C

(Montgomery Pair–Correlation Theorem for Hardy’s
Z
–function)
.

Assuming the Riemann Hypothesis, the nontrivial zeros of Hardy’s
Z
–function
obey the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) pair–correlation law.
Equivalently, Montgomery’s Pair–Correlation Conjecture holds.
That is, for every Schwartz test function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{N(T)}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\!\left(1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{\!2}\right)dx,

(17)

where
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
denote the real zeros of
Z(t)
,
and
N(T)\sim\tfrac{T}{2\pi}\log\!\tfrac{T}{2\pi}
is their counting function.

In the classical random matrix setting, a single
Hermitian matrix need not exhibit GUE–like statistics at all. For example, a diagonal
matrix with integer entries has a completely rigid spectrum with evenly spaced eigenvalues,
far from the sine–kernel behavior. An analogous phenomenon occurs in our analytic
framework, where the core section
Z_{0}(t)=\cos\theta(t)\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
has zeros
on each window
[2N,2N+2]
that are essentially uniformly spaced, with no visible level
repulsion. Thus, in order to prove Theorem
C
for the Hardy
Z
–function itself,
we must show that
Z(t)
, represented on each window by the canonical section

Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})
, lies on the “random” side of the ensemble, similar to the randomized sections of Theorem
B
, rather than behaving
like such singular, highly structured cases.

This is achieved by combining Selberg’s probabilistic theory of the argument function

[
58
,
59
,
61
,
60
]
,

S(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arg\zeta\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+it\Bigr),

(18)

with the microscopic universality established in Theorem
A
. We refer to

\mathrm{PC}_{N}(f;\overline{a}):=\frac{1}{M_{N}}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{j}(\overline{a}),\,t_{k}(\overline{a})\in[2N,2N+2]\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log N}{2\pi}\bigl(t_{j}(\overline{a})-t_{k}(\overline{a})\bigr)\right),

(19)

as the local pair–correlation functional of
Z_{N}(t;\overline{a})
in the window
[2N,2N+2]
.
Assuming RH, we first use the approximation

Z(t)\approx Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\qquad t\in[2N,2N+2],

(20)

to express the global Montgomery pair–correlation sum of the Hardy
Z
–function,

PC^{Z}(f;T):=\frac{1}{N(T)}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right),

(21)

as an average of local pair–correlation
functionals
PC_{N}(f;\overline{1})
over successive windows.

Selberg’s
L^{2}
and covariance
estimates for
S(t)
and its increments then imply that, on the scale of these windows,
the fluctuations of
S(t)
behave like a Gaussian process with rapidly decaying correlations
between well–separated windows. Using Selberg’s theory, we prove in Theorem
9.1
that for every fixed Schwartz test function
f
, the sequence
\{PC_{N}(f;\overline{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
has uniformly bounded variance and
satisfies the weak decorrelation condition

\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(PC_{N}(f;\overline{1}),PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\overline{1})\right)\to 0,

(22)

as
|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty
. This gives a law of large numbers–type result for the
sequence
\{PC_{N}(f;\overline{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
: after suitable normalization, their average
converges to its ensemble mean as
N\to\infty
.

Combining this macroscopic decorrelation with the microscopic GUE universality of
Theorem
A
,
we conclude that the deterministic canonical sections
Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})
have the same
limiting pair–correlation statistics as a “typical” random section in
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
In this sense the sequence
\{Z_{N}(t;\overline{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
behaves ergodically inside the
ensembles, and the global pair–correlation of the zeros of Hardy’s
Z
–function coincides
with the GUE law, proving Montgomery’s conjecture.

2.
Review of GUE Eigenvalue Statistics and Universality for Dyson’s 1D Coulomb Gas Model

In this section, we review fundamental concepts from the theory of GUE eigenvalue statistics and Dyson’s one-dimensional Coulomb gas model, see
[
43
,
15
,
50
,
1
]
for comprehensive treatments. While these constructions are classical within the framework of random matrix theory, they serve as benchmark analogues for the deformations studied in our variation space of
Z(t)
. This section thus also provides the technical groundwork necessary for developing the stochastic mechanisms that emerge in our analytic number theory framework in the subsequent sections.

2.1.
Random Matrices and 1D Coulomb Gas

Let

\mathcal{H}_{M}\;=\;\Bigl\{\,H\in\operatorname{Mat}_{M}(\mathbb{C})\;\bigm|\;H_{ji}=\overline{H_{ij}}\Bigr\}

(26)

be the real vector space of Hermitian
M\times M
matrices, equipped with the inner product

\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(AB).

(27)

We say that
H
is a random
M\times M
Hermitian matrix drawn from the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble, if its entries are defined as follows:

(1)

The diagonal entries
H_{ii}
are independent normally distributed real Gaussians
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
with mean zero and variance
1
.

(2)

The off-diagonal entries
H_{ij}
for
i<j
are independent complex Gaussians with real and imaginary parts
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right)
.

The resulting probability density on the space of Hermitian matrices is

P(H)=(2\pi)^{-M^{2}/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{Tr}(H^{2})\right),

(28)

defined with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the independent real and imaginary parts of the matrix entries.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M}
of
H
are real and can be studied via a change of variables from matrix entries to eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Integrating over the Haar measure on the unitary group gives the joint probability density function (JPDF) for the eigenvalues:

P(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M})=\frac{1}{\hat{Z}_{M}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq M}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}^{2}\right),

(29)

where the normalization constant is given explicitly by

\hat{Z}_{M}=(2\pi)^{M/2}\,\prod_{j=1}^{M}j!.

(30)

The expression (
29
) has a natural interpretation in terms of statistical mechanics. Define the associated energy functional

E(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M})=-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq M}\log|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}^{2}.

(31)

Then the JPDF takes the form of a Boltzmann distribution at inverse temperature
\beta=2
:

P(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M})=\frac{1}{Z_{M}}\,e^{-\beta\,E(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M})}.

(32)

This viewpoint, introduced by Dyson
[
15
]
, reveals a deep analogy with statistical physics, according to which the eigenvalues of a GUE matrix behave like a one-dimensional Coulomb gas, a system of
M
identical charged particles confined to the real line, repelling one another via a logarithmic potential and held in place by an external quadratic potential. The repulsion term
-\log|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|
corresponds to the two-dimensional Coulomb interaction, since
\log|x|
is the Green’s function of the Laplacian in two dimensions, while the confining term
\tfrac{1}{4}\sum\lambda_{i}^{2}
, equivalently, the quadratic weight in (
29
), prevents the spectrum from escaping to infinity. The interplay between repulsion and confinement gives an equilibrium in which the eigenvalues are neither rigid nor independent, but exhibit universal statistical correlations characteristic of GUE statistics, such as the sine kernel (
3
), appearing in Montgomery’s pair correlation conjecture.

2.2.
Itô’s Calculus and Dyson Brownian Motion for Hermitian Matrices

Let
H(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{M}
evolve from
H(0)
by

dH(t)=dB(t),

(33)

where
B(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{M}
is a Hermitian matrix Brownian motion (BM) with independent
increments, Hermitian symmetry
dB_{ji}(t)=\overline{dB_{ij}(t)}
, and quadratic
variation

\mathbb{E}\!\left[dB_{ij}(t)\,\overline{dB_{kl}(t)}\right]=\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\,dt,\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\left[dB_{ij}(t)\,dB_{kl}(t)\right]=0,

(34)

for all
i\leq j,\;k\leq l
. Equivalently, the diagonal entries are real standard BMs and the off-diagonals have
independent real/imaginary parts with variance
dt/2
. From (
34
),
for all
1\leq i,j\leq M
,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\,\bigl|H_{ij}(t)-H_{ij}(0)\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]=t.

(35)

For each fixed
t
let

H(t)=U(t)\,\Lambda(t)\,U(t)^{\dagger},\qquad\Lambda(t)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{M}(t)\bigr)

(36)

be an eigen-decomposition with
U(t)
unitary. If the ordered eigenvalues of
H\in\mathcal{H}_{M}
are

\lambda_{1}(H)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{M}(H)
, call the spectrum

simple
when the inequalities are strict.

\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\mathrm{simp}}\;=\;\bigl\{H\in\mathcal{H}_{M}\;\bigm|\;\lambda_{1}(H)<\cdots<\lambda_{M}(H)\bigr\}.

(37)

Its complement

\Sigma_{\mathrm{deg}}=\mathcal{H}_{M}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\mathrm{simp}}

has (real) codimension
\geq 2
and Lebesgue measure
0
.
On
\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\mathrm{simp}}
the ordered eigenvalues are

smooth
functions; at points of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{deg}}
they are
only Lipschitz and lack derivatives.

For a twice continuously differentiable scalar function
F:\mathcal{H}_{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
Itô’s formula for
\mathcal{H}_{M}
–valued semimartingales reads

dF(H_{t})=\;\bigl\langle\nabla F(H_{t}),\,dH_{t}\bigr\rangle\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl\langle dH_{t},\;\nabla^{2}F(H_{t})[\,dH_{t}\,]\bigr\rangle

(38)

where the second inner product uses the fact that

(dB_{t})^{2}=dt
. Let us set

F(H)=\lambda_{n}(H).

(39)

Let
v_{n}(H)
be the normalised eigenvector of
H
for
\lambda_{n}(H)
.
Classical Rayleigh–Schrödinger theory gives the following deterministic perturbation formulas

\nabla\lambda_{n}(H)=P_{n},\qquad\nabla^{2}\lambda_{n}(H)[X]=2\!\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle v_{m},Xv_{n}\rangle}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}}\,\langle v_{n},Xv_{m}\rangle,

(40)

where

P_{n}:=v_{n}v_{n}^{\!\dagger}
, is the rank-one noise operator.
Inserting (
40
) into
Itô’s formula (
38
) gives

d\lambda_{n}(t)=\Bigl\langle P_{n},\,dB_{t}\Bigr\rangle+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|\,\langle v_{m},dB_{t}v_{n}\rangle|^{2}}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}}.

(41)

The two components of (
41
) satisfy:

(1)

The diagonal pieces

d\widetilde{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}:=\Bigl\langle P_{n},\,dB_{t}\Bigr\rangle=\langle v_{n},dB_{t}v_{n}\rangle

(42)

are independent standard Brownian motions thanks to the covariance
choice, see Remark
4.1
below for further details.

(2)

For
m\neq n
the off–diagonal bracket

\big\langle v_{m},\,dB_{t}\,v_{n}\big\rangle

is a centred complex Gaussian increment whose conditional second moment is given by

\mathbf{E}\!\Bigl[\,\bigl|\!\bigl\langle v_{m},\,dB_{t}\,v_{n}\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,\bigm|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\Bigr]\;=\;dt,

(43)

where

\mathcal{F}_{t}\;:=\;\sigma\!\bigl(\,H_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\bigr)

(44)

is the natural filtration of the matrix process
H_{t}
, see Remark
6.1
for further details.

We have thus obtained Dyson Brownian motion (DBM) coupled system of SDE

d\lambda_{n}(t)=d\widetilde{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(t)-\lambda_{m}(t)}\,dt,\quad n=1,\dots,M.

(45)

For the eigenvalue vector

\lambda(t)=(\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{M}(t))

the coupled SDE (
45
) induces the following Fokker–Planck equation for its density

p_{t}(\lambda)
:

\partial_{t}p_{t}(\lambda)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\partial_{\lambda_{n}}^{2}p_{t}(\lambda)\;-\;\sum_{n=1}^{M}\partial_{\lambda_{n}}\!\left[\left(\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m}}\right)p_{t}(\lambda)\right].

(46)

If
H(0)=c\cdot I
, for
c\in\mathbb{R}
, then for each fixed
t>0
the law of
H(t)
is Gaussian with variance
t
, and the eigenvalue density is

p_{t}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{M,t}}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq M}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2t}\sum_{k=1}^{M}(\lambda_{k}-c)^{2}\right),

(47)

with
Z_{M,t}=(2\pi t)^{M/2}\prod_{j=1}^{M}j!
.
In particular, after recentring and unfolding by the instantaneous density, the bulk correlation statistics are known to be given by the GUE sine kernel.

Remark 2.1

(Collisions occur with probability zero)
.

Because the repulsion term blows up as

\lambda_{n}\to\lambda_{m}
, the eigenvalues cannot cross and the collision set is polar.
Formally, starting from a simple spectrum,

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{there exists }t>0\text{ such that }\lambda_{n}(t)=\lambda_{m}(t)\right)=0,\qquad\text{for }n\neq m.

(48)

so the DBM path remains in the smooth chamber
\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\mathrm{simp}}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Thus the derivation above is valid almost surely and the matrix Itô
lemma provides a rigorous bridge from Brownian motion on

\mathcal{H}_{M}
to the Dyson SDE without ever encountering
non-differentiable points.

2.3.
Modern universality theory for DBM

Dyson’s original analysis of (
45
) identifies (
47
) as the unique invariant density and shows that, for the matrix Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics started from a Gaussian law, the eigenvalue process is ergodic and converges in distribution to the global GUE equilibrium
[
15
]
. In recent years this picture has been vastly extended by the universality theory for Dyson Brownian motion and related log–gases. It is now known that, for a very broad class of initial eigenvalue configurations and confining potentials, the short–time evolution of (
45
) drives the system to GUE local statistics at fixed energy, essentially independent of the microscopic details of the initial law. In particular, the bulk
k
–point correlation functions and gap distributions converge to those of the sine–kernel determinantal process after a relaxation time that is much shorter than the global equilibration time. See, for example, the works of Erdős–Schlein–Yau and Bourgade–Erdős–Yau
[
1
,
6
,
13
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
40
,
50
]
.

In particular, let

\lambda^{(N)}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{(N)}_{M_{N}}

denote the eigenvalues in a fixed bulk window for an
M_{N}\times M_{N}
log–gas or, equivalently, the solution of (
45
) at a fixed time
t=t_{N}>0
, with initial law
\mu_{N}
satisfying the usual regularity and rigidity assumptions. Define the eigenvalue pair–correlation functional

PC_{N}^{\mathrm{eig}}(f)\;:=\;\frac{1}{M_{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k:\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log M_{N}}{2\pi}\,\bigl(\lambda^{(N)}_{j}-\lambda^{(N)}_{k}\bigr)\right),

(49)

where
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is a Schwartz function. Then universality theory for Dyson Brownian motion and log–gases asserts that, for any admissible sequence of initial measures
\mu_{N}
and suitable times
t_{N}
, one has

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigl[PC_{N}^{\mathrm{eig}}(f)\bigr]\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\Bigl(1-\Bigl(\tfrac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\,dx.

(50)

In words, once a DBM flow has been identified, the unfolded local pair–correlation of eigenvalues converges, in expectation and for any reasonable initial law
\mu_{N}
, to the GUE sine–kernel functional. This is precisely the mechanism we shall use in Theorem
A
. After unfolding and coupling the zero–dynamics on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
to (
45
), we may apply (
50
) with
\mu_{N}
any probability measure on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
with smooth, strictly positive density, yielding the GUE pair–correlation limit for
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigl[PC_{N}(f;\bar{a})\bigr]
.

3.
The Skorokhod SDE on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
and the Induced SDEs on zeros

In our previous works
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
, we introduced a novel analytic framework for the Hardy
Z
-function, based on an approximate functional equation (AFE) with exponentially decaying error. We proved that

Z(t)=\cos(\theta(t))+\sum_{k=1}^{N(t)}a^{\mathrm{acc}}_{k}(N(t))\frac{\cos(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1))}{\sqrt{k+1}}+O\left(e^{-\omega t}\right),

(51)

where
N(t)=\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor
, and the coefficients
a^{\mathrm{acc}}_{k}(N)
are given explicitly by

a^{\mathrm{acc}}_{k}(N):=\sum_{n=k}^{N}\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\binom{n}{k},

(52)

with
\omega>0
a fixed constant.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
to be the
N
-th variation space of the Hardy
Z
-function, whose elements are generalized sections of the form

Z_{N}(t;\overline{a})=\cos(\theta(t))+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_{k}}{\sqrt{k+1}}\cos\left(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1)\right),

(53)

with
\overline{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This space
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
includes two distinguished elements:

(1)

The core approximation
Z_{0}(t)=\cos(\theta(t))
corresponding to
\overline{a}=\overline{0}
, whose zeros
t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are fully understood and are given as the unique solutions of

\theta(t_{n})=\pi\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(54)

(2)

The function
Z_{N}^{\mathrm{acc}}(t):=Z_{N}(t;\overline{a}^{\mathrm{acc}}(N))
, where
\overline{a}^{\mathrm{acc}}\approx\overline{1}
as defined in (
52
), which provides a remarkably accurate approximation to
Z(t)
in the range
2N\leq t\leq 2N+2
, as guaranteed by (
51
).

This leads us to define:

Definition 3.1

(The
N
-th real hall)
.

Let
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
denote the subset of all functions
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})

for which there exists a continuous homotopy
a:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
a(0)=\bar{0}
and
a(1)=\bar{a}
such that,
for every
s\in[0,1]
, the zeros of
Z_{N}(t;a(s))
in the critical rectangle

2N\leq\Re t\leq 2N+2,\qquad-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Im t\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

(55)

remain real and simple, and the number of zeros in the rectangle stays constant along the homotopy.

Remark 3.1

(
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
and the RH)
.

The Riemann Hypothesis can essentially be considered as equivalent to showing
Z_{N}(t;\overline{a}^{\mathrm{acc}}(N))\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
for all
N>0
. It should be noted that this central feature, which enables the construction of our

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
as a meaningful object, stands in sharp contrast to the
classical Riemann–Siegel formula, whose main truncation up to

\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{t/2\pi}\,\bigr\rfloor
is known to violate the
Riemann Hypothesis infinitely often. The various distinctions between our approximation
(
51
) and the setting of the classical Riemann–Siegel formula are discussed in detail
in
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
, to which we refer the reader for further background.

As discussed in Section
2
, when the entries of a Hermitian matrix

are further assumed to evolve stochastically under matrix-valued Brownian motion, Itô’s lemma gives the DBM system of stochastic differential equations for the eigenvalues (
45
). We now introduce a parallel stochastic framework in our setting of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 3.2

(The geometry of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
)
.

Let

\mathcal{P}_{N}:=\{\,t\in\mathbb{C}:\;2N\leq\Re t\leq 2N+2,\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Im t\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\}.

(56)

be the critical
N
-th rectangle. The boundary
\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
decomposes into two natural pieces:

\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\;=\;\Sigma_{N}\,\cup\,\Gamma_{N},

(57)

where

\Sigma_{N}:=\bigl\{\,\bar{a}:\ Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})=0\text{ and }\partial_{t}Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})=0\text{ for some }t\in\mathcal{P}_{N}\,\bigr\}.

(58)

is the
discriminant (collision) set
of parameters at which two, or more, zeros in
\mathcal{P}_{N}
coalesce, introduced and studied in
[
37
]
, and

\Gamma_{N}:=\bigl\{\,\bar{a}:\ Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})=0\ \text{for some }t\in\partial\mathcal{P}_{N},\bigr\}.

(59)

is the
entry/exit set
of parameters at which a real zero lies on the boundary of the rectangle, so the number of zeros inside
\mathcal{P}_{N}
changes by crossing
\Gamma_{N}
. In particular, the regular part of
\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is a smooth hypersurface and the singular locus of higher multiplicities

\partial\mathcal{RH}^{sing}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is a real–analytic subvariety of codimension at least
2
.

The local geometry of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
was studied in
[
36
,
37
]
. When negative coordinates
a_{i}<0
are allowed, additional zeros may “arrive from infinity” by crossing the horizontal sides
\Im t=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}
. In the positive cone
a\geq 0
, a Rouché’s theorem argument shows that entry/exit can occur only at the endpoints
t=2N,\,2N+2
of the real segment inside the rectangle
\mathcal{P}_{N}
. More concretely, if
a:[0,1]\to(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{N}
is a homotopy with
a(0)=\bar{0}
and
a(1)=\bar{a}
, then zeros can leave the real line along the homotopy if and only if a collision between two consecutive real zeros occurs, that is if
a(s)\in\Sigma_{N}
for some
s\in[0,1]
. In such a case the pair of zeros can depart off the real axis together. Hence, in the positive cone
a\geq 0
, no complex zeros can come from “infinity”, therefore the number of zeros in
\mathcal{P}_{N}
can change only via crossings at the real endpoints of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
.

To ensure that the stochastic process remains inside
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
for all time, we introduce a reflecting term along the boundary
\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}
. This leads naturally to a reflected stochastic differential equation, known as a
Skorokhod SDE
, which describes the evolution of a process that behaves like Brownian motion in the interior of a domain but is
reflected
at the boundary to prevent it from exiting, see
[
62
,
63
,
66
,
41
,
29
]
.

More precisely, if
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an open domain with smooth boundary
\partial D
, then a reflected Brownian motion in
D
is a continuous process
A_{t}
satisfying

dA_{t}=dB_{t}+dL_{t},

(60)

where
B_{t}
is standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
L_{t}
is a finite-variation process supported on the boundary
\partial D
that pushes
A_{t}
inward whenever it hits the boundary. The vector
dL_{t}
points in the direction of the inward normal to
\partial D
at the point
A_{t}
and increases only when
A_{t}
is on the boundary.

When
D
has a smooth boundary, this reflection is well-defined and pushes the process back into the domain in the direction of the inward normal vector.
However, in many applications, including queueing theory, interacting particle systems, and stochastic networks, one encounters domains with non-smooth boundaries, such as polyhedral, convex, or Lipschitz domains, or boundaries with singularities of lower codimension. The analysis of Skorokhod problems has been extended to such settings by various authors, establishing existence, uniqueness, and regularity of reflected processes even when the boundary is non-smooth or includes singular strata, see for instance
[
56
,
14
,
68
,
11
,
7
,
24
]
. This extended theory applies to our setting where the domain of interest is
\mathcal{RH}_{N}\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. We thus have, by direct application of the multidimensional Itô formula (
38
) to
t_{n}(A_{t})
:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A_{t}
evolve as reflected Brownian motion in
\mathcal{RH}_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, satisfying the Skorokhod SDE:

dA_{t}=dB_{t}+dL_{t},

(61)

where
B_{t}
is standard Brownian motion in
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
, and
L_{t}
is a finite-variation process pushing
A_{t}
inward when it reaches the boundary
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}
, where the reflection vector is defined almost always along the inward normal to
\mathcal{D}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{N}^{sing}
. Then,

dt_{n}(A_{t})=\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),dB_{t}\rangle+\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),dL_{t}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle dB_{t},\nabla^{2}t_{n}(A_{t})[dB_{t}]\rangle.

(62)

where
t_{n}(A_{t})
is the
n
-th real zero of
Z_{N}(t;A_{t})
within the window
[2N,2N+2]
.

We will show in the following sections that the SDE system for the zeros
t_{n}(A_{t})
in (
62
) satisfies the following properties:

(1)

The first–order martingale terms
\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,dB_{t}\rangle
provide the leading diffusive noise and become asymptotically independent.

(2)

The Itô second–order correction
\tfrac{1}{2}\,\langle dB_{t},\,\nabla^{2}t_{n}(A_{t})[\,dB_{t}\,]\rangle
generates a Coulomb–type repulsion, in direct analogy with random matrix theory.

(3)

The Skorokhod reflection term keeps the process in
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
and prevents collisions of zeros, but its contribution to the resulting SDE is negligible.

Consequently, the system (
62
) reduces to Dyson Brownian motion (
45
) for the GUE eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}(H_{t})
. Hence, in the limit
N\to\infty
, the zeros
t_{n}(A_{t})
exhibit GUE–type local statistics. In practice we work with the normalized form (
130
) of (
62
), introduced in Section
4
.

Remark 3.3

(RMT analogy of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
)
.

The open chamber structure of

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),

(63)

where the zeros of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})
remain real and simple, has a natural analogue in random matrix theory. The complex Ginibre ensemble
\mathrm{Gin}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
consists of all
M\times M
complex matrices with independent Gaussian entries, see
[
22
]
. The subset of Hermitian matrices
\mathcal{H}_{M}
forms a real-algebraic submanifold of codimension
M^{2}
. Within this Hermitian chamber, the eigenvalues are always real, generically simple, and vary smoothly with the matrix entries. This is precisely the setting where the classical Dyson Brownian Motion (DBM) is defined. Let us denote

\mathcal{CP}_{M}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{A\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}\mid p_{A}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}[\lambda]\right\}

(64)

as the set of complex matrices whose characteristic polynomial
p_{A}(\lambda)
is real. Thus, just as
\mathcal{H}_{M}
forms a spectral chamber within the larger space
\mathcal{CP}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
, bounded by the discriminant locus where eigenvalues leave the real line or coalesce, the region
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
in our model defines a spectral chamber within the full parameter space
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, bounded by collisions of real zeros of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})
. In both cases, the analytic control of eigenvalues or zeros, and the associated variational calculus, is valid only within this open stratum.

Remark 3.4

(Analytic ensembles of analytic functions)
.

In view of the above Remark
3.3
the probabilistic structure on the
A
-variation space
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is related in spirit
to the Gaussian analytic ensembles studied by Sodin and Tsirelson and in later
works on random complex zeroes and Gaussian analytic functions

[
26
,
45
,
64
,
65
]
. In their setting,
one considers Gaussian analytic, or entire, functions and regards the zero set
in
\mathbb{C}
as a random point field. The resulting local statistics of
zeros are of Ginibre type as after appropriate scaling they are shown to fall into the
same universality class as the complex Ginibre ensemble
\mathrm{Gin}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
, i.e. the two–dimensional Coulomb gas with logarithmic interaction.

There is, however, a fundamental difference in scope. For Gaussian analytic
functions, a typical realization has zeros spread throughout
\mathbb{C}
, and
no constraint is imposed that all zeros in a given region lie on the real line.
There is in particular no finite–dimensional parameter domain on which the
zeros in a fixed window are constrained to be real, simple, and ordered.

By contrast, the real hall
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is defined precisely so
that for each section
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})
in
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
all zeros in
the window
[2N,2N+2]
are real, simple, and correctly ordered. In this sense,

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
encodes a finite–dimensional, windowed version of
the Riemann Hypothesis for the Hardy
Z
–function. Equipping

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
with a smooth probability measure on the
coefficients and constructing a reflected diffusion on this domain yields a
random zero process on the real line with one–dimensional log–gas which is of DBM dynamics. Theorem
A
shows that the local statistics
of these real zeros fall into the GUE universality class, whereas the Sodin–Tsirelson
Gaussian analytic ensembles exhibit Ginibre–type statistics of complex zeros in the plane.

4.
The Quadratic Co-Variation Matrix of the First-Order Noises

Recall that a family
\{\beta^{(n)}_{t}\}_{n=1}^{M}
is said to consist of
independent standard Brownian motions
if for any
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}
and measurable sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,(\beta^{(n)}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\beta^{(n)}_{t_{k}})\in A,\;(\beta^{(m)}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\beta^{(m)}_{t_{k}})\in B\,\Bigr)=\\
=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,(\beta^{(n)}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\beta^{(n)}_{t_{k}})\in A\,\Bigr)\,\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,(\beta^{(m)}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\beta^{(m)}_{t_{k}})\in B\,\Bigr).

(65)

Let
X_{t}=(X^{(1)}_{t},\ldots,X^{(M)}_{t})
be a continuous vector
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-local martingale, and let

[X]_{t}\;:=\;\bigl([X^{(i)},X^{(j)}]_{t}\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq M}

(66)

be its quadratic covariation matrix. According to Lévy’s characterization if
X_{0}=0
and

[X]_{t}\;=\;t\,I_{M}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

(67)

then
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(M)}
are independent standard Brownian motions, see Theorem 8.6.1 of
[
70
]
and Theorem 3.6 of
[
52
]
. The following remark shows that in the RMT case, independence follows from the orthonormality of the eigenvectors
v_{n}
:

Remark 4.1

(Independence of first-order terms — RMT case)
.

Consider the family

\widetilde{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle v_{n}(s),\,dB_{s}\,v_{n}(s)\big\rangle,

(68)

where
v_{n}(s)
is the eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{n}(s)
, as in (
42
).
Assume
B_{t}
is a Hermitian matrix Brownian motion with covariance

d\bigl[(B_{s})_{ij},\,\overline{(B_{s})_{k\ell}}\bigr]\;=\;\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}\,ds,\qquad d\bigl[(B_{s})_{ij},\,(B_{s})_{k\ell}\bigr]\;=\;0.

(69)

Then

\displaystyle d\bigl[\widetilde{\beta}^{(n)},\widetilde{\beta}^{(m)}\bigr]_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}\overline{v_{n,i}(s)}\,v_{n,j}(s)\,\overline{v_{m,k}(s)}\,v_{m,\ell}(s)\;d\bigl[(B_{s})_{ij},\,\overline{(B_{s})_{k\ell}}\bigr]

(70)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\overline{(v_{n}(s)v_{n}(s)^{\!*})_{ij}}\,(v_{m}(s)v_{m}(s)^{\!*})_{ij}\,ds=\bigl|\langle v_{n}(s),v_{m}(s)\rangle\bigr|^{2}ds=\delta_{nm}\,ds.

(71)

Integrating gives

\bigl[\widetilde{\beta}^{(n)},\widetilde{\beta}^{(m)}\bigr]_{t}\;=\;t\,\delta_{nm}.

(72)

Hence, by Lévy’s characterization
\widetilde{\beta}^{(n)}
are independent standard Brownian motions for
n=1,...,M
.

The first-order stochastic variation of the zeros
t_{n}(A_{t})
in (
62
) is

d\beta^{(n)}_{t}:=\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,dB_{t}\rangle,

(73)

the projection of the parameter Brownian motion
dB_{t}
onto the gradient
\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})
. Our aim is to show that the processes
\beta^{(n)}_{t}
form a family of asymptotically independent standard Brownian motions. In contrast to the RMT case of Remark
4.1
, where independence follows from the orthonormality of the
v_{n}
, the situation in our setting
\mathcal{RH}_{N}\subset\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is more subtle. First we record the bracket structure.

Proposition 4.1
.

The first-order noises

\beta_{t}^{(n)}:=\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\,dB_{s}\big\rangle

(74)

form a continuous vector local martingale with quadratic covariations given by

[\beta^{(n)},\beta^{(m)}]_{t}\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\,\nabla t_{m}(A_{s})\big\rangle\,ds.

(75)

Proof.

Since the map
s\mapsto\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})
is predictable and locally square-integrable, the stochastic Itô integral

\beta_{t}^{(n)}=\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\,dB_{s}\big\rangle

(76)

is a continuous local martingale. Write
B_{s}=(B^{(1)}_{s},\dots,B^{(N)}_{s})
with independent coordinates, and expand

d\beta^{(n)}_{s}=\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\,dB_{s}\big\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{a_{i}}t_{n}(A_{s})\,dB^{(i)}_{s}.

(77)

By Itô’s multiplication rule,

dB^{(i)}_{s}\,dB^{(j)}_{s}=\delta_{ij}\,ds,

(78)

so

d\beta^{(n)}_{s}\,d\beta^{(m)}_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{a_{i}}t_{n}(A_{s})\,\partial_{a_{i}}t_{m}(A_{s})\,ds.

(79)

Therefore, by the definition of quadratic covariation for continuous semimartingales,

[\beta^{(n)},\beta^{(m)}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}d\beta^{(n)}_{s}\,d\beta^{(m)}_{s}=\\
=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{a_{i}}t_{n}(A_{s})\,\partial_{a_{i}}t_{m}(A_{s})\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\,\nabla t_{m}(A_{s})\big\rangle\,ds.

(80)

∎

Contrary to the RMT case in (
72
), in our case one does not have vanishing of the quadratic covariation

[\beta^{(n)},\beta^{(m)}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\nabla t_{m}(A_{s})\rangle\,ds,

(81)

since, in general, the gradients
\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a})
and
\nabla t_{m}(\bar{a})
are not almost always orthogonal. The following formulas present the point-wise inner product of
\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a})
and
\nabla t_{m}(\bar{a})
:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

\psi_{n,k}(\bar{a}):=\theta(t_{n}(\bar{a}))-t_{n}(\bar{a})\log(k+1).

(82)

Then the following holds:

(1)

For
n=m
, we have

\langle\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a}),\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a})\rangle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\prime}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\frac{1}{2(k+1)}+\frac{\cos(2\psi_{n,k}(\bar{a}))}{2(k+1)}\right].

(83)

(2)

For
n\neq m
, we have

\langle\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a}),\nabla t_{m}(\bar{a})\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\cos(\psi_{n,k}(\bar{a})-\psi_{m,k}(\bar{a}))+\cos(\psi_{n,k}(\bar{a})+\psi_{m,k}(\bar{a}))}{2(k+1)Z_{N}^{\prime}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})Z_{N}^{\prime}(t_{m}(\bar{a});\bar{a})}.

(84)

Proof.

Let

F(t,\bar{a})=Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}).

(85)

F\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a}),\bar{a}\bigr)=0.

(86)

\partial_{t}F\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a}),\bar{a}\bigr)\,\partial_{a_{k}}t_{n}(\bar{a})\;+\;\partial_{a_{k}}F\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a}),\bar{a}\bigr)=0.

(87)

\partial_{a_{k}}F(t,\bar{a})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\,\cos\!\bigl(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1)\bigr).

(88)

\partial_{a_{k}}t_{n}(\bar{a})=-\,\frac{\partial_{a_{k}}F\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a}),\bar{a}\bigr)}{\partial_{t}F\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a}),\bar{a}\bigr)}=-\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\prime}\!\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)\sqrt{k+1}}\,\cos\!\bigl(\theta(t_{n}(\bar{a}))-t_{n}(\bar{a})\log(k+1)\bigr).

(89)

Hence

\displaystyle\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a}),\,\nabla t_{m}(\bar{a})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\partial_{a_{k}}t_{n}(\bar{a})\,\partial_{a_{k}}t_{m}(\bar{a})

(90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\prime}\!\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)\,Z_{N}^{\prime}\!\bigl(t_{m}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\cos\psi_{n,k}(\bar{a})\,\cos\psi_{m,k}(\bar{a})}{\,k+1\,},

(91)

and using the standard trigonometric identity for the product of cosines gives the required formulas.
∎

We have:

Proposition 4.3
.

Fix
T>0
and let
A_{t}\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
be the reflected Brownian motion
with continuous paths. For
t_{n},t_{m}\in[2N,2N+2]
set

S_{n}(A_{t})\;:=\;\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\cos\bigl(2\psi_{n,k}(A_{t})\bigr)}{2(k+1)},\qquad S_{n,m}(A_{t})\;:=\;\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\cos\bigl(\psi_{n,k}(A_{t})\pm\psi_{m,k}(A_{t})\bigr)}{2(k+1)}.

(92)

Then, almost surely, there exists a finite constant
C_{T}(\omega)
, depending on the
sample path
\{A_{s}:0\leq s\leq T\}
, such that for all
0\leq t\leq T
,

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{S_{n}(A_{s})}{Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{s});A_{s}\bigr)^{2}}\,ds\right|\;\leq\;C_{T}(\omega)\int_{0}^{t}|S_{n}(A_{s})|\,ds,

(93)

and

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{S_{n,m}(A_{s})}{Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{s});A_{s}\bigr)\,Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{m}(A_{s});A_{s}\bigr)}\,ds\right|\;\leq\;C_{T}(\omega)\int_{0}^{t}|S_{n,m}(A_{s})|\,ds.

(94)

Proof.

Because
A_{t}
has continuous paths, the image

K_{T}:=\{A_{s}:0\leq s\leq T\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

(95)

is compact. On
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
all zeros are simple and depend smoothly on the
parameters, so
a\mapsto t_{n}(a)
is continuous on
K_{T}
. Define the compact set

\Gamma_{T}^{(n)}:=\{(t_{n}(a),a):a\in K_{T}\}\subset[2N,2N+2]\times K_{T}.

(96)

The map
(t,a)\mapsto\partial_{t}Z_{N}(t;a)
is continuous and it never vanishes on

\Gamma_{T}^{(n)}
due to the simplicity of the zeros. By compactness there exists

\delta_{T}^{(n)}>0
with

\inf_{(t,a)\in\Gamma_{T}^{(n)}}\bigl|\partial_{t}Z_{N}(t;a)\bigr|=\inf_{a\in K_{T}}\bigl|Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(a);a\bigr)\bigr|\;\geq\;\delta_{T}^{(n)}.

(97)

Hence, for all
0\leq s\leq T
,

\frac{1}{\bigl|Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{s});A_{s}\bigr)\bigr|^{2}}\leq(\delta_{T}^{(n)})^{-2},\qquad\frac{1}{\bigl|Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{s});A_{s}\bigr)\,Z^{\prime}_{N}\!\bigl(t_{m}(A_{s});A_{s}\bigr)\bigr|}\leq(\delta_{T}^{(n)}\delta_{T}^{(m)})^{-1}.

(98)

Let
C_{T}(\omega):=\max_{1\leq r\leq M}(\delta_{T}^{(r)})^{-2}
. Pulling these
pathwise bounds inside the integrals gives (
93
)–(
94
).
∎

We have:

Theorem 4.1

(Oscillatory averages are small)
.

Let
A_{t}\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
be the reflected Brownian motion. Fix
T>0
. Then there exists a finite random constant
C_{T}(\omega)

0\leq t\leq T
and all

(99)

(100)

for
n\neq m
.

Proof.

Introduce the Dirichlet polynomial

D(x):=\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{e^{-ix\log(k+1)}}{2(k+1)}.

(101)

We have the representations

S_{n}(A_{s}):=\Re\Big(e^{\,2i\theta(t_{n}(A_{s}))}\,D\bigl(2t_{n}(A_{s})\bigr)\Big),

(102)

S_{n,m}(A_{s}):=\Re\Big(e^{\,i(\theta(t_{n}(A_{s}))\pm\theta(t_{m}(A_{s})))}\,D\bigl(t_{n}(A_{s})\pm t_{m}(A_{s})\bigr)\Big).

(103)

Fix
n
and set
X_{s}:=t_{n}(A_{s})

X_{s}
is continuous and takes values

I_{N}=[2N,2N+2]
. By the occupation–time formula we have, for any Borel function
f(x)
,

\int_{0}^{t}f\!\bigl(X_{s}\bigr)\,ds=\int_{I_{N}}f(x)\,L_{t}^{x}(X_{s})\,dx,

(104)

where
L_{t}^{x}(X_{s})
denotes the local time of
X_{s}

x
up to time

[

]
.
Since
X_{s}
is

I_{N}
is compact, its local time is jointly continuous. In

C_{T}^{(n)}(\omega):=\sup_{x\in I_{N}}L_{T}^{x}(X)<\infty

(105)

almost-surely. Using (

|\Re z|\leq|z|
, and (

\int_{0}^{t}\left|S_{n}(A_{s})\right|ds\ \leq\ \int_{I_{N}}|D(2x)|\,L_{t}^{x}(X)\,dx.

(106)

|I_{N}|=2

\int_{I_{N}}|D(2x)|\,L_{t}^{x}(X)\,dx\leq\\
\ \leq\ \Big(\!\int_{I_{N}}|D(2x)|^{2}dx\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\!\int_{I_{N}}L_{t}^{x}(X)^{2}dx\Big)^{1/2}\ \leq\ \sqrt{2}\,C_{T}^{(n)}(\omega)\,\Big(\!\int_{I_{N}}|D(2x)|^{2}dx\Big)^{1/2}.

(107)

One has the following mean-value theorem for Dirichlet polynomials,

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\Big|\sum_{m\leq N}a_{m}m^{-ix}\Big|^{2}dx=(\beta-\alpha)\sum_{m\leq N}|a_{m}|^{2}+O\Big(\sum_{m\leq N}m\,|a_{m}|^{2}\Big),

[

[

(109)

108
) on
I_{N}

\int_{I_{N}}|D(2x)|^{2}dx\ \ll\ 1+\log N.

(110)

Combining (

C_{T}(\omega):=C_{T}^{(n)}(\omega)

For the off–diagonal case, define
Y_{s}^{\pm}:=t_{n}(A_{s})\pm t_{m}(A_{s})

\int_{0}^{t}\left|\sum_{k\leq N}\frac{\cos\bigl(\psi_{n,k}(A_{s})\pm\psi_{m,k}(A_{s})\bigr)}{2(k+1)}\right|ds\ \leq\ \sqrt{4}\,C_{T}^{(\pm)}(\omega)\,\Big(\!\int_{J_{\pm}}|D(z)|^{2}dz\Big)^{1/2},

Set

G_{nn}(\bar{a})=\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}_{N}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})^{2}}\Big(\frac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{n}(\bar{a})\Big),\quad G_{nm}(\bar{a})=\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}_{N}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})\,Z^{\prime}_{N}(t_{m}(\bar{a});\bar{a})}\,S_{n,m}(\bar{a}),

Theorem 4.2

(Asymptotics of the quadratic covariation)
.

Fix

\beta^{(n)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),dB_{s}\rangle

Consequently, for

\frac{[\beta^{(n)},\beta^{(m)}]_{t}}{\sqrt{[\beta^{(n)}]_{t}\,[\beta^{(m)}]_{t}}}=O_{\omega}\!\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log N}}\Big)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0,

where

A_{s}
for

Proof.

Proposition

(123)

\rho_{nm}(A_{t})\;:=\;\frac{\big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,\nabla t_{m}(A_{t})\big\rangle}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|\big\|\nabla t_{m}(A_{t})\big\|}.

(124)

(125)

Set

\widetilde{X}(t)\;=\;X(0)\;+\;\int_{0}^{t}D(s)^{-1}\,dX_{s}

(126)

Equivalently, in coordinate-wise form,

d\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)\;=\;\frac{1}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\,dX_{n}(t),

(127)

for each
n\in I_{N}
. In particular, instead of the first-order noises

\beta^{(n)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\Big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{s}),\,dB_{s}\Big\rangle

(128)

considered so far, let us define the following:

Definition 4.1

(Normalized first-order noises)
.

we refer to

\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\Big\langle\frac{\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})\|},\,dB_{s}\Big\rangle.

(129)

as the standardized
n
-th first-order noises

Applying (
127
) to (
62
) we have:

Corollary 4.4

(The diagonal normalization of the SDE)
.

For each
n\in I_{N}
the following SDE holds

d\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)=d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}+\frac{1}{2\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\langle dB_{t},\nabla^{2}t_{n}(A_{t})[dB_{t}]\rangle+\frac{1}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\,\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,dL_{t}\rangle.

(130)

The following remarks discusses the interpretation of the normalization (
127
) in terms of the one-dimensional Lamperti transform:

Remark 4.2

(One-dimensional Lamperti transform)
.

The Lamperti transform is a change of variables that whitens state–dependent noise. That is, it
trivializes the diffusion by turning the possibly varying diffusion coefficient into the constant
value, see
[
12
,
44
]
and references therein. Equivalently, after the transform the quadratic variation of the state becomes exactly
t
,
so the stochastic driver is a standard Brownian motion in the new coordinate. In particular, our process
\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)
of (
127
) can be viewed as a one-dimensional Lamperti transform of
X(t)
with respect to
\sigma_{n}(t)=\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|
.

Classical DBM (
45
) is an idealized stochastic model of one–dimensional
particles with logarithmic repulsion and independent Brownian noise. In applications, however,
one rarely obtains DBM exactly. Universality theory for DBM asserts that
a large class of perturbations do not change local bulk statistics and after a short time, gaps have the GUE/sine–kernel law, see
[
17
,
18
,
6
,
40
,
13
,
20
]
.
The proof of Theorem
A
thus proceeds as follows:

(1)

In the following Section
5
we prove that the normalized first-order noises
\hat{\beta}^{(n)}
are a family of asymptotically independent standard Brownian motions.

(2)

In Section
6
we analyze
\frac{1}{2\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\langle dB_{t},\nabla^{2}t_{n}(A_{t})[dB_{t}]\rangle
, the Itô second–order term, and show that it produces a Coulomb–type repulsion with state–dependent metric weights, namely the singular drift of the form
\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{G_{nm}(X)}{X_{n}-X_{m}}
.

(3)

In Section
7
we show that our SDE (
130
) fits into the
DBM + small error
class and hence, relying on the universality machinery, the local bulk correlation functions and gap statistics of (
130
) agree with those of DBM and converge to the GUE sine–kernel law.

5.
Normalized First-Order Noises are Asymptotically Independent Brownian Motions

In this section we prove that, in the large
N
limit, the normalized
one–dimensional noises
\big(\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\big)_{n\in I_{N}}

behave as a family of asymptotically independent standard Brownian motions.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
T>0
and let

\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\Big\langle\frac{\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})\|},\,dB_{s}\Big\rangle

(131)

be the normalized first-order noises. Then for all
0\leq t\leq T
,

[\hat{\beta}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}^{(n)}]_{t}=t,

(132)

and, for
n\neq m
,

[\hat{\beta}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}^{(m)}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{G_{nm}(A_{s})}{\sqrt{G_{nn}(A_{s})\,G_{mm}(A_{s})}}\,ds=O_{\omega}\!\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{\log N}}\Big).

(133)

Proof.

For the diagonal case
n=m
, by the Itô isometry,

d[\hat{\beta}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}^{(n)}]_{s}=\Big\langle\frac{\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})\|},\,\frac{\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{s})\|}\Big\rangle ds=ds,

(134)

hence
[\hat{\beta}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}^{(n)}]_{t}=t
. For the off–diagonal brackets
n\neq m
, we have

d[\hat{\beta}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}^{(m)}]_{s}=\Big\langle\frac{\nabla t_{n}}{\|\nabla t_{n}\|},\,\frac{\nabla t_{m}}{\|\nabla t_{m}\|}\Big\rangle(A_{s})\,ds=\rho_{nm}(A_{s})\,ds,

(135)

with the correlation coefficient

\rho_{nm}(\bar{a})=\frac{G_{nm}(\bar{a})}{\sqrt{G_{nn}(\bar{a})\,G_{mm}(\bar{a})}}=\frac{S_{n,m}(\bar{a})}{\sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{n}(\bar{a})\bigr)\bigl(\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{m}(\bar{a})\bigr)}},

(136)

where
G_{nm}
are as defined in (
113
). Note that since, by definition,

\rho_{nm}(\bar{a})=\frac{\langle\nabla t_{n}(\bar{a}),\nabla t_{m}(\bar{a})\rangle}{\|\nabla t_{n}(a)\|\,\|\nabla t_{m}(a)\|},

(137)

then

|\rho_{nm}(a)|\leq 1,

(138)

by Cauchy–Schwarz.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
and split the time interval into

E^{bad}_{t}:=\Bigl\{s\in[0,t]:\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{n}(A_{s})<\tfrac{H_{N}}{4}\ \text{ or }\ \tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{m}(A_{s})<\tfrac{H_{N}}{4}\Bigr\},

(139)

and let

\qquad E_{t}^{good}=[0,t]\setminus E^{bad}_{t}

(140)

be the complement. On
E_{t}^{good}
we have

\sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{n}\bigr)\bigl(\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{m}\bigr)}\geq\tfrac{H_{N}}{4},

(141)

hence

|\rho_{nm}(A_{s})|\leq\frac{4}{H_{N}}\,|S_{n,m}(A_{s})|,

(142)

for
s\in E_{t}^{good}
. On the other hand, note that

\Bigl\{\,\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{n}(A_{s})<\tfrac{H_{N}}{4}\,\Bigr\}\subseteq\Bigl\{\,|S_{n}(A_{s})|\geq\tfrac{H_{N}}{4}\,\Bigr\}.

(143)

Applying Markov’s inequality

\bigl|\{\,s\in[0,t]:f(s)\geq a\,\}\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,ds.

(144)

with
f(s):=|S_{n}(A_{s})|
and
a=H_{N}/4
we hence obtain

\Bigl|\Bigl\{\,s\in[0,t]:\tfrac{H_{N}}{2}+S_{n}(A_{s})<\tfrac{H_{N}}{4}\,\Bigr\}\Bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{4}{H_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}|S_{n}(A_{s})|\,ds.

(145)

Using the union bound for
E_{t}^{bad}

we thus get

|E^{bad}_{t}|\ \leq\ \frac{4}{H_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}|S_{n}(A_{s})|\,ds+\frac{4}{H_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}|S_{m}(A_{s})|\,ds.

(146)

In particular, since by Theorem
4.1

\int_{0}^{t}|S_{n}(A_{s})|\,ds,\int_{0}^{t}|S_{m}(A_{s})|\,ds\ll_{\omega}\sqrt{1+\log N}

(147)

uniformly for
0\leq t\leq T
, we get

|E^{bad}_{t}|\ \ll_{\omega}\ \frac{\sqrt{1+\log N}}{\log N}\;=\;O_{\omega}\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log N}}\Bigr).

(148)

Integrating (
137
) and using Theorem
4.1
and (
138
),(
142
),(
148
) gives

\displaystyle\big|[\hat{\beta}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}^{(m)}]_{t}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E^{bad}_{t}}|\rho_{nm}(A_{s})|\,ds+\int_{E_{t}^{good}}\frac{4}{H_{N}}\,|S_{n,m}(A_{s})|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq|E^{bad}_{t}|+\frac{4}{H_{N}}\!\int_{0}^{t}\!|S_{n,m}(A_{s})|\,ds\ \ll_{\omega}\ \frac{\sqrt{1+\log N}}{\log N}\ =\ O_{\omega}\!\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log N}}\Big),

as required.
∎

Set
\hat{\beta}^{I_{N}}:=(\hat{\beta}^{(n)})_{n\in I_{N}}
. By Theorem
5.1
,
\hat{\beta}^{I_{N}}
is a continuous local martingale with quadratic covariation

[\hat{\beta}^{I_{N}},\hat{\beta}^{I_{N}}]_{t}\;=\;t\,I_{|I_{N}|}\;+\;E_{N}(t),

(149)

with

\|E_{N}(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{\|E_{N}(t)x\|}{\|x\|}\;=\;O_{\omega}\!\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{\log N}}\Big),

(150)

for
0\leq t\leq T
. Hence, by (
149
) we have

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|[\hat{\beta}^{I_{N}},\hat{\beta}^{I_{N}}]_{t}-t\,I_{|I_{N}|}\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|E_{N}(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\omega}\frac{T}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

(151)

Remark 5.1
.

The explicit
t
in
\|E_{N}(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O_{\omega}(t/\sqrt{\log N})
is harmless since
0\leq t\leq T
gives
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|E_{N}(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O_{\omega}(T/\sqrt{\log N})
, and for any fixed
k
–block
M_{N}^{k}
this uniform control suffices for tightness and Brownian identification in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

According to Lévy’s characterization theorem, a continuous local martingale whose
bracket is exactly
tI
is Brownian. In our case, (
151
) shows that the bracket is almost

tI
, up to a small uniform error on
[0,T]
.
Thus we appeal to a functional central limit theorem (CLT) to pass from almost
tI
to the independent Brownian limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
. We refer the reader to
[
33
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the limit-theorem tools used in this section.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
I_{N}
be the set of indices of the zeros
t_{n}(\bar{a})
in the window
[2N,2N+2]
.
For each
N
, let
I_{N}^{k}\subset I_{N}
be any set of
k
consecutive indices and define the
\mathbb{R}^{k}
–valued standardized martingale

M_{N}^{k}(t)\;:=\;\big(\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\big)_{n\in I_{N}^{k}},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

(152)

Definition 5.1

(Tightness)
.

We say that the family
M^{k}_{N}
is
tight in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact set

K_{\varepsilon}\subset C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that

\inf_{N}\ \mathbb{P}\big(M^{k}_{N}\in K_{\varepsilon}\big)\ \geq\ 1-\varepsilon.

(153)

According to Prokhorov’s theorem, tightness of the family
M_{N}^{k}
in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
ensures the existence of a subsequence converging in distribution in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, see Theorem 3.5 of
[
33
]
. We have:

Proposition 5.2

(Tightness of the standardized
k
–block)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
T>0
. The sequence
\{M_{N}^{k}\}_{N}
is tight in

C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Proof.

According to the Aldous–Rebolledo tightness criterion for martingales
if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(AR1)

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\ \mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{tr}\,[M^{k}_{N},M^{k}_{N}]_{T}\;<\;\infty,

(154)

(AR2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\ \sup_{N}\ \sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\leq T\\
0\leq\theta\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\,\big\|[M_{N}^{k},M^{k}_{N}]_{\tau+\theta}-[M^{k}_{N},M^{k}_{N}]_{\tau}\big\|\;=\;0,

(155)

then the family
\{M_{N}^{k}\}_{N}
is tight in

C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, see Theorem 4.5 of
[
33
]
.

By Theorem
5.1
we have, a.s.
and uniformly on
[0,T]
,

[M_{N}^{k},M_{N}^{k}]_{t}\;=\;t\,I_{k}+E_{N,k}(t),\qquad\|E_{N,k}(t)\|_{op}\ \leq\ C_{T}(\omega)\,\frac{t}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

(156)

We thus get

\mathrm{tr}\,[M_{N}^{k},M_{N}^{k}]_{T}=kT+\mathrm{tr}\,E_{N,k}(T)\leq kT+k\,\|E_{N,k}(T)\|_{op}\leq kT+k\,C_{T}(\omega)\,\frac{T}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

(157)

Taking expectations thus gives

\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{tr}\,[M_{N}^{k},M_{N}^{k}]_{T}<\infty
which establishes (AR1). For (AR2), let
\tau\leq T
be a bounded stopping time and
0\leq\theta\leq\delta
.
Using (
156
) we get

\displaystyle\big\|[M_{N}^{k},M^{k}_{N}]_{\tau+\theta}-[M^{k}_{N},M^{k}_{N}]_{\tau}\big\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\big\|\,\theta\,I_{k}+E_{N,k}(\tau+\theta)-E_{N,k}(\tau)\big\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\theta+\|E_{N,k}(\tau+\theta)-E_{N,k}(\tau)\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\theta+2\,\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|E_{N,k}(t)\|_{op}\ \leq\ \theta+2\,C_{T}(\omega)\,\frac{T}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

(158)

Taking expectations and then the sup over
N,\tau,\theta\leq\delta
gives

\sup_{N}\ \sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\leq T\\
0\leq\theta\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\big\|[M_{N}^{k},M^{k}_{N}]_{\tau+\theta}-[M^{k}_{N},M^{k}_{N}]_{\tau}\big\|_{op}\ \leq\ \delta+2\,\mathbf{E}\,C_{T}(\omega)\,\frac{T}{\sqrt{\log N}}\ \leq\ \delta+o(1),

(159)

and hence the left-hand side tends to
0
as
\delta\downarrow 0
, uniformly in
N
which establishes (AR2). Therefore, by the Aldous–Rebolledo criterion stated above,
\{M_{N}^{k}\}_{N}
is tight in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.
∎

In particular, we have

Theorem 5.1

(Asymptotic independence of
\hat{\beta}^{(n)}
)
.

Fix
T>0
and let
I_{N}
be the set of indices of the zeros
t_{n}(\bar{a})
in the window
[2N,2N+2]
.
Then, the standardized first–order noises

\bigl(\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\bigr)_{n\in I_{N}}
are asymptotically independent standard Brownian motions on
[0,T]
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a block
I_{N}^{k}\subset I_{N}
with
|I_{N}^{k}|=k
, and define

M_{N}^{k}
as above. Theorem
5.1
implies that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big\|\bigl[M_{N}^{k},M_{N}^{k}\bigr]_{t}-t\,I_{k}\big\|_{op}\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\ \mathbb{P}\ }\;0.

(160)

By Proposition
5.2
, the
sequence
\{M_{N}^{k}\}
is tight in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Hence every
subsequence admits a further subsequence that converges in law to some
continuous process
M^{k}
in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. By the convergence of
brackets (
160
), this limit process
M^{k}
satisfies

[M^{k},M^{k}]_{t}\;=\;t\,I_{k},

(161)

for
0\leq t\leq T
. Since
M^{k}
is a continuous local martingale with bracket
t\,I_{k}
, Lévy’s
characterization implies that
M^{k}
is a
k
–dimensional standard Brownian motion, that is, its coordinates are independent standard Brownian motions.

Therefore every sub-sequence of the family
\{M_{N}^{k}\}
has a further sub-sequence converging
to
M^{k}
, and by uniqueness of the limit the whole sequence converges to

M_{N}^{k}\;\Rightarrow\;M^{k}\quad\text{in }C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{k}).

(162)

Because
k
and the finite block
I_{N}^{k}
were arbitrary, this means that for any
fixed finite set of indices inside
I_{N}
, the vector

\{\hat{\beta}^{(n)}\}_{n\in I_{N}^{k}}
converges jointly to independent standard
Brownian motions on
[0,T]
as

∎

6.
Zeros of Hadamard Product and Coulomb-Type Repulsion

If
f
is entire of order
1
, Hadamard’s factorization theorem states that

f(z)\;=\;e^{A+Bz}\prod_{n}\!\left(1-\frac{z}{z_{n}}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{z}{z_{n}}\right),

(163)

where
\{z_{n}\}
are the zeros of
f
with multiplicity and
A,B\in\mathbb{C}
are some constants. It is known that the Hadamard factorization of the function

\Xi(t):=\xi(\tfrac{1}{2}+it),

(164)

where

\xi(s)\;:=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,s(s-1)\,\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{s}{2}\big)\,\zeta(s),

(165)

with
\mathrm{ord}(\xi)=1
is given by

\Xi(t)\;=\;\Xi(0)\prod_{n}\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{t_{n}^{2}}\right),

(166)

where
\{\pm t_{n}\}
are the ordinates of the nontrivial zeros of
\Xi(t)
, see
[
16

67
]

\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)=\pi^{-1/4}\big|\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{it}{2})\big|\,e^{i\theta(t)}

(167)

at

\Xi(t)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(t^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\pi^{-1/4}\,\bigl|\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{it}{2}\big)\bigr|\,Z(t).

(168)

In particular, from (

Z(t)\;=\;\widetilde{C}(t)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{t_{n}^{2}}\right),

(169)

\widetilde{C}(t)\;:=\;\frac{2\,\pi^{1/4}}{\,t^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\,}\,\frac{\Xi(0)}{\bigl|\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{it}{2}\big)\bigr|}\,.

(170)

(Hadamard factorization for sections)

Lemma 6.1
.

\frac{\partial^{2}t_{n}}{\partial a_{i}\partial a_{j}}(a)=\\

for a smooth

We wish to understand how the zeros

Proposition 6.2
.

\frac{\partial^{2}t_{n}}{\partial a_{i}\partial a_{j}}(\bar{a})=\\

where the product runs over all zeros of

(180)

Evaluating at

survives. For the final double sum, note that

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq\ell\\
k\neq m\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{k}(\bar{a})}\right)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ell\neq n\text{ and }m\neq n.

(181)

Equivalently, the inner term survives iff

\ell=n

contribute. Collecting the surviving terms gives

+(\partial_{t}C_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr))\,\frac{\partial_{a_{i}}t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\,\prod_{k\neq n}\!\left(1-\frac{t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{k}(\bar{a})}\right)+\\

k\neq n\end{subarray}}\!\left(1-\frac{t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{k}(\bar{a})}\right)\\
-\,C_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)\,\frac{\partial_{a_{i}}t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\,\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{t_{m}(\bar{a})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq n\\
k\neq m\end{subarray}}\!\left(1-\frac{t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{k}(\bar{a})}\right).

(182)

Since

Z_{N}^{\prime}\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)=-C_{N}\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)\frac{1}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\prod_{k\neq n}\!\left(1-\frac{t_{n}(\bar{a})}{t_{k}(\bar{a})}\right),

(183)

-\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\prime}\bigl(t_{n}(a);a\bigr)}\,\partial_{t}\partial_{a_{i}}Z_{N}\bigl(t_{n}(a);a\bigr)=\sum_{\ell\neq n}\frac{\partial_{a_{i}}t_{\ell}(\bar{a})}{t_{n}(\bar{a})-t_{\ell}(\bar{a})}+\\
+\left[\frac{\partial_{t}C_{N}}{C_{N}}\!\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)\,+\frac{1}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\,+\,\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{t_{n}(\bar{a})-t_{m}(\bar{a})}\right]\partial_{a_{i}}t_{n}(\bar{a})-\frac{\partial_{a_{i}}C_{N}}{C_{N}}\!\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr).

(184)

Write

Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})=\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\right)\,\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}(t;\bar{a})\qquad;\qquad\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}(t;\bar{a}):=C_{N}(t;\bar{a})\!\!\prod_{m\neq n}\!\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{m}(\bar{a})}\right).

(185)

Then

Z_{N}^{\prime}(t;\bar{a})=-\frac{1}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}(t;a)+\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\right)\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}^{\prime}(t;\bar{a})

(186)

Z_{N}^{\prime\prime}(t;\bar{a})=-\frac{2}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\,\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}^{\prime}(t;\bar{a})+\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\right)\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}^{\prime\prime}(t;\bar{a}).

(187)

Evaluating at

Z_{N}^{\prime}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})=-\frac{1}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})\qquad;\qquad Z_{N}^{\prime\prime}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a})=-\frac{2}{t_{n}(\bar{a})}\,\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}^{\prime}(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}),

(188)

hence

\frac{Z_{N}^{\prime\prime}}{Z_{N}^{\prime}}\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)=2\,\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}^{\prime}}{\widetilde{Z}_{N,n}}\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a});\bar{a}\bigr)=2\left(\frac{\partial_{t}C_{N}}{C_{N}}\bigl(t_{n}(\bar{a})\bigr)+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{t_{n}(\bar{a})-t_{m}(\bar{a})}\right).

(189)

Substituting (

\frac{\partial^{2}t_{n}}{\partial a_{i}\partial a_{j}}(\bar{a})=\\

(190)

as required. ∎

We thus have

Proposition 6.3
.

\frac{1}{2}\big\langle dB_{t},\nabla^{2}t_{n}(A_{t})[dB_{t}]\big\rangle=\\

(191)

Proof.

By Proposition

\frac{1}{2}\big\langle dB_{t},\nabla^{2}t_{n}(A_{t})[dB_{t}]\big\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}t_{n}}{\partial a_{i}\partial a_{j}}(A_{t})\,dB_{t,i}\,dB_{t,j}.

(193)

Insert (

(194)

The extra terms contribute

Remark 6.1

(Comparison with the RMT case)
.

In the RMT case the
Rayleigh–Schrödinger (second–order) term takes the form

\frac{1}{2}\,\big\langle dH_{t},\;\nabla^{2}\lambda_{n}(H_{t})[dH_{t}]\big\rangle\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\big|\langle v_{m}(t),\,dB_{t}\,v_{n}(t)\rangle\big|^{2}}{\lambda_{n}(t)-\lambda_{m}(t)}\,,

(196)

where
\lambda_{n}
and
v_{n}
are the eigenvalues and orthonormal
eigenvectors, as in (
41
). Because the entries of
dB_{t}
have covariance

\mathbf{E}\,[dB_{ab}\,dB_{cd}]=\tfrac{1}{\beta}(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}+\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc})\,dt
,
one has the variance identity

\Big\langle v_{m},\;dB_{t}\,v_{n}\Big\rangle=\sum_{a,b}\,\overline{v_{m,a}}\,(dB_{t})_{ab}\,v_{n,b},

(197)

and hence

\Big|\big\langle v_{m},\;dB_{t}\,v_{n}\big\rangle\Big|^{2}=\sum_{a,b}\sum_{c,d}\overline{v_{m,a}}\,v_{n,b}\,v_{m,c}\,\overline{v_{n,d}}\,(dB_{t})_{ab}\,\overline{(dB_{t})_{cd}}.

(198)

Assume
B_{t}
is the standard matrix Brownian motion for the unitary ensemble,
so that (entrywise, with indices running over
1,\dots,N
)

\mathbf{E}\!\left[(dB_{t})_{ab}\,\overline{(dB_{t})_{cd}}\right]=\,\big(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}+\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}\big)\,dt.

(199)

Taking the conditional expectation (conditioning on the past
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,
so the vectors
v_{m},v_{n}
are deterministic) yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\!\left[\Big|\big\langle v_{m},\;dB_{t}\,v_{n}\big\rangle\Big|^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}\sum_{c,d}\overline{v_{m,a}}\,v_{n,b}\,v_{m,c}\,\overline{v_{n,d}}\;\mathbf{E}\!\left[(dB_{t})_{ab}\,\overline{(dB_{t})_{cd}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}\sum_{c,d}\overline{v_{m,a}}\,v_{n,b}\,v_{m,c}\,\overline{v_{n,d}}\,\big(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}+\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}\big)\,dt

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{a}\overline{v_{m,a}}v_{m,a}\right)\left(\sum_{b}v_{n,b}\overline{v_{n,b}}\right)dt+\left|\sum_{a}\overline{v_{m,a}}\,v_{n,a}\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\,\langle v_{m},v_{m}\rangle\,\langle v_{n},v_{n}\rangle\,dt+\,|\langle v_{m},v_{n}\rangle|^{2}dt.

(200)

With orthonormal eigenvectors
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
, the
second term vanishes for
m\neq n
, and the first term equals
dt
.
Hence, for
m\neq n
,

\mathbf{E}\!\left[\Big|\big\langle v_{m},\;dB_{t}\,v_{n}\big\rangle\Big|^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\;=\;\,dt.

(201)

and therefore immediately

\frac{1}{2}\,\big\langle dH_{t},\;\nabla^{2}\lambda_{n}(H_{t})[dH_{t}]\big\rangle\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{dt}{\lambda_{n}(t)-\lambda_{m}(t)}\,.

(202)

Thus, in the matrix case the Coulomb (logarithmic) drift with prefactor

1/(N)

drops out directly
from the quadratic variation,
thanks to the entrywise isotropy and the exact orthonormality of the
eigenvectors.

Substituting (
191
) of Proposition
6.3
to the normalized SDE (

Corollary 6.4

(The diagonal normalization of the SDE)
.

For each
n\in I_{N}
the following SDE holds

d\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)=d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\;+\;\sum_{m\neq n}\left[\frac{\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A_{t})}{\;\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m}(t)\;}\ +r_{nm}^{gap}(t)\right]dt+\frac{1}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\,\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,dL_{t}\rangle+\\
+\left[\frac{1}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}\Big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,\nabla_{a}\log C_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{t});A_{t}\bigr)\Big\rangle-\frac{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}{t_{n}(A_{t})}\right]\,dt

(203)

where

\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(1+\frac{\big\|\nabla t_{m}(A_{t})\big\|}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\right)\rho_{nm}(A_{t}),

(204)

and the gap remainder is

r^{\mathrm{gap}}_{nm}(t)=\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A_{t})\!\left[\frac{1}{\phi_{nm}(A_{t})\big(X_{n}(t)-X_{m}(t)\big)}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m}(t)}\right],

(205)

with

\phi_{nm}(A):=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}+\tfrac{1}{\big\|\nabla t_{m}(A_{t})\big\|}\right).

(206)

Proof.

Writing (

d\widetilde{X}_{n}=d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\big\|\nabla t_{m}(A_{t})\big\|\rho_{nm}(A_{t})}{X_{n}-X_{m}}\,dt+\frac{1}{\big\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\big\|}\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,dL_{t}\rangle+\\
+\left[\frac{1}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}\Big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,\nabla_{a}\log C_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{t});A_{t}\bigr)\Big\rangle-\frac{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}{t_{n}(A_{t})}\right]\,dt.

(207)

Note that for each

\frac{\big\|\nabla t_{m}(A_{t})\big\|\rho_{nm}}{X_{n}-X_{m}}=\frac{\widetilde{c}_{nm}}{\phi_{nm}(A)\,(X_{n}-X_{m})}=\frac{\widetilde{c}_{nm}}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}+\widetilde{c}_{nm}\!\left[\frac{1}{\phi_{nm}(A)\,(X_{n}-X_{m})}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}\right],

(208)

which is exactly (

7.
Universality and Reduction of the SDE to the Classical DBM

7.1.
Definition of the drifts
b^{reg}_{n},b^{err}_{n},b_{n}^{Sko}
and
b^{1-body}_{n}

The following result gives the asymptotic size of the number of zeros of an element of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
inside the
window
[2N,2N+2]
:

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
I_{N}
be the index set of zeros of
Z_{N}(t,\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
inside the
window
[2N,2N+2]
. Then

|I_{N}|\;\sim\frac{1}{\pi}\,\log N.

(209)

Proof.

Since the number of zeros of
Z_{N}(t,\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
inside the
window
[2N,2N+2]
is independent of
\bar{a}
let us consider
Z_{0}(t)=Z_{N}(t;\bar{0})=cos(\theta(t))
where the Riemann-Siegel theta function (
7
) satisfies

\theta(t)\;=\;\frac{t}{2}\log\frac{t}{2\pi}-\frac{t}{2}-\frac{\pi}{8}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{t}\right).

(210)

The number of zeros of
Z_{0}(t)
in the interval
[N,N+2]
is asymptotic to

|I_{N}|\;\sim\;\frac{1}{\pi}\bigl(\theta(2N+2)-\theta(2N)\bigr)\sim\;\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\theta^{\prime}(2N)\;\sim\;\frac{1}{\pi}\log N,

(211)

where the last approximation follows from
\theta^{\prime}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\log(t/2\pi)+O(1/t)
.
∎

In view of Proposition
7.1
The average spacing between consecutive zeros in
I_{N}
satisfies

h_{N}:\;=\;\frac{2}{|I_{N}|}\;\sim\;\frac{\pi}{\log N}.

(212)

In particular, the natural microscopic scale of zero gaps in
[2N,2N+2]
is of order
1/\log N
. Let us fix a smooth cut-off function
\chi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
0\leq\chi\leq 1
,
\chi\equiv 1
on
[0,1]
and
\chi\equiv 0
on
[2,\infty)
. For a any
\delta>0
set

\chi_{N}(\Delta;\delta):=\chi\!\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{\delta h_{N}}\right).

(213)

Let us define:

Definition 7.1

(The drifts
b_{n}^{\mathrm{err}},b_{n}^{\mathrm{reg}},b_{n}^{\mathrm{Sko}}
and
b_{n}^{\mathrm{1\text{-}body}}
)
.

For
n\in I_{N}
and
\delta>0
define:

(1)

The error drift:

b^{\mathrm{err}}_{n}(t;\delta):=\sum_{m\neq n}\chi_{N}\!\big(\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m};\delta\big)\left[\frac{\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A_{t})-1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}+r^{\mathrm{gap}}_{nm}(t)\right].

(214)

(2)

The regular drift:

b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(t;\delta):=\sum_{m\neq n}\Big(1-\chi_{N}\!\big(\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m}(t)\,;\,\delta\big)\Big)\left[\frac{\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A_{t})-1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m}(t)}+r^{\mathrm{gap}}_{nm}(t)\right].

(215)

(3)

The Skorokhod drift:

b_{n}^{\mathrm{Sko}}(t):=\frac{1}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}\,\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,dL_{t}\rangle.

(216)

(4)

The one-body drift:

b_{n}^{\mathrm{1\text{-}body}}(t):=\frac{1}{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}\Big\langle\nabla t_{n}(A_{t}),\,\nabla_{a}\log C_{N}\!\bigl(t_{n}(A_{t});A_{t}\bigr)\Big\rangle-\frac{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}{t_{n}(A_{t})}.

(217)

The SDE (
203
) can hence be expressed in terms of the drifts
b^{reg}_{n},b^{err}_{n},b_{n}^{Sko}
and
b^{1-body}_{n}
as follows

d\widetilde{X}_{n}=d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}\,dt+\left[b^{reg}_{n}+b^{err}_{n}+b_{n}^{Sko}+b^{1-body}_{n}\right]dt.

(218)

Our goal in the remainder of this section is to show that the additional drift terms

b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n},b^{\mathrm{err}}_{n},b_{n}^{\mathrm{Sko}}
and
b^{\mathrm{1\text{-}body}}_{n}

do not affect the limiting local statistics of (
218
).
Consequently, (
218
) exhibits the same bulk local GUE statistics as
Dyson Brownian motion (
45
) itself.
The essential work lies in controlling the error and regular drifts
b^{\mathrm{err}}_{n},b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}
, while the Skorokhod drift
b^{\mathrm{Sko}}_{n}
and the one-body drift
b^{\mathrm{1\text{-}body}}_{n}
are more immediate and their contribution can be dealt with directly, as will be explained below.
The proof proceeds by controlling these drift contributions and showing that they can be reduced to the framework of established universality theory

[
18
,
6
,
40
]
.

7.2.
The regular drift
b^{reg}_{n}
does not affect the limit statistics

We first show that
b_{n}^{reg}
is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz away from collisions, placing it in the admissible class of drifts in the universality framework.

Proposition 7.2

(Admissibility of
b^{\mathrm{reg}}
for universality)
.

Fix
S>0
and
\delta\in(0,1]
. Then there exist constants

C_{S},L_{S,\delta},N_{0}<\infty
such that for all

N\geq N_{0}
:

(i)

The following bound holds

\sup_{t\in[0,S]}\ \sup_{n\in I_{N}}\ \big|\,b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(t;\delta)\,\big|\;\leq\;C_{S}\,\frac{1}{\delta h_{N}}\,\big(1+\log|I_{N}|\big).

(219)

(ii)

Let

\mathcal{D}_{\delta}:=\Big\{(\widetilde{X}_{k})_{k\in I_{N}}\ :\ |\widetilde{X}_{k+1}-\widetilde{X}_{k}|\geq\delta\,h_{N}\ \text{for all }k\in I_{N}\Big\}.

(220)

For every
t\in[0,S]
the map

(\widetilde{X}_{k})_{k\in I_{N}}\mapsto b^{\mathrm{reg}}(t;\delta)
is
C^{\infty}

on
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
, and for all configurations

\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
, the following
\ell_{1}
Lipschitz condition holds

\sum_{n\in I_{N}}\big|\,b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(\widetilde{X};t;\delta)-b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(\widetilde{Y};t;\delta)\,\big|\ \leq\ L_{S,\delta}\,\frac{1+\log|I_{N}|}{(\delta h_{N})^{2}}\,\sum_{k\in I_{N}}|\widetilde{X}_{k}-\widetilde{Y}_{k}|.

(221)

Proof.

(i) Write
b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}=T_{1,n}+T_{2,n}
with

T_{1,n}(t):=\sum_{m\neq n}\Big(1-\chi_{N}\!\big(\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m};\delta\big)\Big)\frac{\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A_{t})-1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}

(222)

T_{2,n}(t):=\sum_{m\neq n}\Big(1-\chi_{N}\!\big(\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m};\delta\big)\Big)\,r^{\mathrm{gap}}_{nm}(t),

(223)

By construction,
1-\chi_{N}(\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m};\delta)
vanishes unless

|\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}|\geq\delta h_{N}
, up to a harmless smooth
transition. Hence, in the relevant region

|\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{n\pm j}|\;\geq\;\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}|\widetilde{X}_{n+k+1}-\widetilde{X}_{n+k}|\;\geq\;j\,\delta h_{N}.

(224)

On the fixed window
[0,S]
the coefficients
\widetilde{c}_{nm}(A_{t})
are
uniformly bounded, hence

|T_{1,n}(t)|\;\leq\;C_{1}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1-\chi_{N}\!\big(\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m};\delta\big)}{|\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}|}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{1}}{\delta h_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{|I_{N}|}\frac{2}{j}\;\leq\;2C_{1}\,\frac{1}{\delta h_{N}}\,\big(1+\log|I_{N}|\big).

(225)

Recall that

r^{\mathrm{gap}}_{nm}=\widetilde{c}_{nm}\left[\frac{1}{\phi_{nm}(A_{t})\,(X_{n}-X_{m})}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}\right].

(226)

When
1-\chi_{N}\neq 0
, both denominators are
\geq c\,\delta h_{N}
and

\phi_{nm},\widetilde{c}_{nm}
are uniformly bounded on
[0,S]
. Hence

|r^{\mathrm{gap}}_{nm}(t)|\leq C_{2}/|\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}|.

(227)

Therefore,

7.3.
Whitening to standard independent Brownian motion noise

We now explain how Theorem
5.1
allows to replace the asymptotically independent standardized first-order noises
\hat{\beta}=(\hat{\beta}^{(n)})_{n\in I_{N}}
in the SDE (
251
), which have covariance
matrix
\rho(A_{t})
, by a vector of fully independent standard Brownian motions

B=(B^{(n)})_{n\in I_{N}}
.

Theorem 7.1

(Whitening of noise to standard Brownian)
.

Let
\hat{\beta}=(\hat{\beta}^{(n)})_{n\in I_{N}}
denote the driving martingales
with covariance
\rho(A_{t})
, and let
\Sigma_{t}:=\rho(A_{t})
. Consider the coupled SDE
systems on
I_{N}
:

\displaystyle d\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{m}(t)}\,dt+\left[b^{\mathrm{err},\Sigma}_{n}+b_{n}^{Sko}+b_{n}^{1-body}\right]\,dt,

(253)

\displaystyle d\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=dB^{(n)}_{t}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{m}(t)}\,dt+\left[b^{\mathrm{err},I}_{n}+b_{n}^{Sko}+b_{n}^{1-body}\right]\,dt,

(254)

where
B=(B^{(n)})_{n\in I_{N}}
is a standard
|I_{N}|
–dimensional Brownian motion.
Then for any bounded Lipschitz local observable
\Phi
,

\Big|\mathbb{E}\,\Phi(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)})-\mathbb{E}\,\Phi(\widetilde{X}^{(I)})\Big|\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;0.

(255)

In particular, one may replace the drivers
\hat{\beta}
by independent
Brownian motions
B
on
I_{N}
without affecting local statistics.

Proof.

Recall that
\hat{\beta}_{t}\;=\;\bigl(\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\bigr)_{n\in I_{N}}
form a continuous martingale with covariance structure

d[\hat{\beta}^{(i)},\hat{\beta}^{(j)}]_{t}\;=\;\rho_{ij}(A_{t})\,dt,\qquad i,j\in I_{N},

(256)

and denote by
\Sigma_{t}:=\rho(A_{t})
the corresponding matrix. By the Martingale Representation Theorem there exists a standard
|I_{N}|
-dimensional Brownian motion

B_{t}=\bigl(B^{(n)}_{t}\bigr)_{n\in I_{N}}

(257)

such that

d\hat{\beta}_{t}\;=\;\Theta_{t}\,dB_{t},

(258)

for some matrix
\Theta_{t}
. In fact,

\Theta_{t}=\Sigma_{t}^{1/2},

(259)

must hold, where
\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}
is the positive semidefinite square root of the symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma
. Indeed, if we define

\hat{\beta}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}\,dB_{s},

(260)

then Itô’s isometry gives

[\hat{\beta},\hat{\beta}]_{t}\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}I(\Sigma_{s}^{1/2})^{\top}\,ds\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{s}\,ds,

(261)

as required. Let

\Delta_{t}:=\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{t}-\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{t}.

(262)

Then

d\Delta_{t}=(\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}-I)\,dB_{t}+\big(F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{t})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{t})\big)\,dt,

(263)

where

F(x)=\left(\sum_{m\neq n}(x_{n}-x_{m})^{-1}\right)_{n\in I_{N}},

(264)

denotes the Dyson drift vector. In particular,
\Delta_{t}
is itself an Itô process. Set

M_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}(\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I)\,dB_{s}\quad;\quad D_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\big(F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{s})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{s})\big)\,ds.

(265)

For the martingale part, Itô’s isometry gives

\mathbb{E}\|M_{t}\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds.

(266)

For the drift part, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\|D_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\left(\int_{0}^{t}1ds\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{s})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{s})\|^{2}ds\right)=t\,\int_{0}^{t}\|F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{s})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{s})\|^{2}ds,

(267)

and therefore taking expectations

\mathbb{E}\|D_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;t\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{s})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{s})\|^{2}ds.

(268)

Combining these bounds and using
the parallelogram inequality
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}+2\|b\|^{2}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;2\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds+2t\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{s})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{s})\|^{2}ds.

(269)

Next, by the Lipschitz continuity of
F
on configurations with no collisions,

\|F(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{s})-F(\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{s})\|\;\leq\;L\,\|\Delta_{s}\|,

(270)

for some random Lipschitz constant
L=L(S)
.
Thus, for all
0\leq t\leq S
,

\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds+SL^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{s}\|^{2}\,ds.

(271)

We further have

\sup_{u\leq t}\|\Delta_{u}\|^{2}\;\leq\;2\sup_{u\leq t}\|M_{u}\|^{2}+2\sup_{u\leq t}\|D_{u}\|^{2}.

(272)

For the martingale part, Doob’s
L^{2}
-inequality
with
p=2
gives

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\leq t}\|M_{u}\|^{2}\Big]\;\leq\;4\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds.

(273)

From (
270
) we get

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\leq t}\|D_{u}\|^{2}\Big]\;\leq\;SL^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{v\leq s}\|\Delta_{v}\|^{2}\Big]\,ds.

(274)

Set

H(t):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\leq t}\|\Delta_{u}\|^{2}\Big].

(275)

Combining (
273
) and (
274
), we arrive at the inequality

H(t)\;\leq\;4\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds+SL^{2}\int_{0}^{t}H(s)\,ds.

(276)

Recall that according to Grönwall’s inequality if for
H:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
a continuous function there exists a constant
\beta\geq 0
and a nonnegative, locally integrable non-decreasing function

A:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

H(t)\;\leq\;A(t)\;+\;\beta\int_{0}^{t}H(s)\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

(277)

Then

H(t)\;\leq\;A(t)e^{\beta t}.

(278)

Taking
A(t)=4\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds
hence gives

H(t)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq S}\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}\Big]\leq\;C_{S}\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds,

(279)

for some constant
C_{S}>0
depending only of the time horizon
S>0
. By Corollary 5.3, we have

\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{S}\|\Sigma_{s}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,ds\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;0.

(280)

Together with (
279
) this gives

H(t)=\sup_{t\leq S}\|\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)}_{t}-\widetilde{X}^{(I)}_{t}\|\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\;0.

(281)

Thus, for any bounded Lipschitz local observable function
\Phi
,

\Big|\mathbb{E}\,\Phi(\widetilde{X}^{(\Sigma)})-\mathbb{E}\,\Phi(\widetilde{X}^{(I)})\Big|\;\leq\;L\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\sup_{t\leq S}\|\Delta_{t}\|\big]\;\to 0,

(282)

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant, as required.
∎

7.4.
Negligible occupation time near small gaps

Define the nearest–neighbour gap

\Delta_{n}(t):=\widetilde{X}_{n+1}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{n}(t).

(283)

Our aim is to prove the following occupation time result which is central to the proof of Proposition
7.7
:

Theorem 7.2

(Occupation time near small gaps)
.

For any
S>0
there exists a constant
C_{S}<\infty
, such that for every
n\in I_{N}
and every

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{S}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta_{n}(t)\leq\delta\,h_{N}\}}\,dt\right]\;\leq\;C_{S}\,\delta.

(284)

We have

Proposition 7.4
.

The gaps
\Delta_{n}(t)
satisfy the following Bessel-type SDE:

d\Delta_{n}(t)=dM_{n}(t)+\frac{2}{\Delta_{n}(t)}\,dt+H_{n}(t)\,dt.

(285)

where the martingales are given by

dM_{n}(t):=d\hat{\beta}^{(n+1)}_{t}-d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}.

(286)

and

H_{n}(t):=\Gamma_{n}(t)+\big(b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n+1}(t)+b^{err}_{n+1}(t)+b^{Sko}_{n+1}(t)+b_{n+1}^{1-body}(t)\big)-\big(b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(t)+b^{err}_{n}(t)+b^{Sko}_{n}(t)+b_{n}^{1-body}(t)\big).

(287)

with

\Gamma_{n}(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2})\neq(n,\,n+1)\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m_{1}}(t)}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m_{2}}(t)}\right).

(288)

Proof.

Subtracting the corresponding equations (
130
) from each other, we
obtain the following SDE for
\Delta_{n}
:

d\Delta_{n}=\big(d\hat{\beta}^{(n+1)}_{t}-d\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}\big)+\Bigg[\sum_{m\neq n+1}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}-\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}\Bigg]dt+\\
+\big(b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n+1}(t)+b^{err}_{n+1}(t)+b^{Sko}_{n+1}(t)+b_{n+1}^{1-body}(t)\big)-\big(b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(t)+b^{err}_{n}(t)+b^{Sko}_{n}(t)+b_{n}^{1-body}(t)\big).

(289)

Split off the two nearest–neighbour terms in the interaction sums:

\sum_{m\neq n+1}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}=\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}-\widetilde{X}_{n}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\neq n,\,n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}-\widetilde{X}_{m}},

(290)

\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}=\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{n+1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\neq n,\,n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}.

(291)

Subtracting gives the canonical cancellation

\Bigg[\sum_{m\neq n+1}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}-\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}-\widetilde{X}_{m}}\Bigg]=\frac{2}{\Delta_{n}}+\Gamma_{n}(t),

(292)

where the remainder of the interaction drift is

\Gamma_{n}(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2})\neq(n,\,n+1)\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m_{1}}(t)}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{m_{2}}(t)}\right).

(293)

Collect the remaining non–singular drift pieces into

H_{n}(t):=\Gamma_{n}(t)+\big(b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n+1}(t)+b^{err}_{n+1}(t)+b^{Sko}_{n+1}(t)+b_{n+1}^{1-body}(t)\big)-\big(b^{\mathrm{reg}}_{n}(t)+b^{err}_{n}(t)+b^{Sko}_{n}(t)+b_{n}^{1-body}(t)\big).

(294)

Inserting (
293
)–(
294
) into (
289
) gives the required result.
∎

For
a,\varepsilon>0
define the convex
C^{1}
hinge-function
\phi_{a}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\phi_{a,\varepsilon}(x)\;:=\;\begin{cases}a-x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&0\leq x\leq a\\
\dfrac{(a+\varepsilon-x)^{2}}{2\varepsilon}&a\leq x<a+\varepsilon,\\[5.16663pt]
0&x\geq a+\varepsilon.\end{cases}

(295)

We have:

Proposition 7.5
.

For any
a>0
the following holds:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{S}\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta_{n}\leq a\}}\,dt\right]\;\leq\;\frac{a}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(\Delta_{n}(0))\big]\;+\;\frac{a}{4}\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{S}\phi^{\prime\prime}(\Delta_{n})\,v_{n}(t)\,dt\\
\;+\;\frac{a}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{S}|H_{n}(t)|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta_{n}\leq a+\varepsilon\}}\,dt,

(296)

where

v_{n}(t):=2\bigl(1-\rho_{n+1,n}(A_{t})\bigr).

(297)

Proof.

Itô’s formula applied to
t\mapsto\phi(\Delta_{n}(t))
gives

\phi(\Delta_{n}(S))-\phi(\Delta_{n}(0))=\int_{0}^{S}\phi^{\prime}(\Delta_{n})\,d\Delta_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{S}\phi^{\prime\prime}(\Delta_{n})\,d[\Delta_{n},\Delta_{n}]_{t}.

(298)

Recall that

d[\hat{\beta}^{(i)},\hat{\beta}^{(j)}]_{t}=\rho_{ij}(A_{t})\,dt,

(299)

with
\rho_{ii}\equiv 1
. Since
M_{n}(t)=\hat{\beta}^{(n+1)}_{t}-\hat{\beta}^{(n)}_{t}

Proposition 7.6

(

(1) Recall the definition

Proof.

Recall that for two probability measures

\mu

\nu

\mathrm{Ent}(\mu\!\mid\!\nu):=\int\log\!\left(M\right)\,d\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[log(M)]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[M\cdot\log(M)],

(358)

whenever
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
and

is the Radon-Nikodym derivative. The Pinsker inequality then asserts that

\|\mu-\nu\|_{1}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{Ent}(\mu\!\mid\!\nu)},

(359)

see Lemma 17.3.2 of
[

In our setting, the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the Girsanov’s tilted measure

(367)

Since by Proposition

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\int_{0}^{S}\|\theta_{t}\|^{2}\,dt=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left(\!\int_{0}^{S}\sum_{n\in I_{N}}\big|b^{\mathrm{err}}_{n}(t)\big|^{2}\,dt\right)^{2}\right]\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\ \\
\ }0,

(368)

we also have by (

\mathrm{Ent}(\mathbb{Q}\!\mid\!\mathbb{P})\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\ \ \mathbb{P}\ \ }0

(369)

Applying (

(370)

Let

Corollary 7.9

(GUE for
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
)
.

As

, the average pair

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\!\left[\frac{1}{M_{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{j},t_{k}\in[2N,2N+2]\\

where

\{t_{j}(\bar{a})\}

in
[2N,2N+2]
.

Proof.

By the SDE representation (

(376)

(378)

The following remark further clarifies the negligible role of the Skorokhod term in the SDE (

Remark 7.1

(Negligibility of the Skorokhod term)
.

By definition, the Skorokhod reflection term

acts only on the boundary

\partial\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})=\bigcup_{n<m}\{\,\widetilde{X}_{n}=\widetilde{X}_{m}\,\}.

(381)

However, the Coulomb drift term

repels trajectories from the boundary. In the limiting DBM, the collision set is polar, i.e.

\mathbb{P}\big(\{\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)=\widetilde{X}_{m}(t)\ \text{for some }t>0\}\big)=0,

(382)

for

implies that the contribution of

to bounded bulk observables tends to zero as

2.1
.

A similar argument applies to the one–body term

\tfrac{\|\nabla t_{n}(A_{t})\|}{t_{n}(A_{t})}
of (

t_{n}(A_{t})
of order

grows only logarithmically, cf. (

.

8.
Pair-Correlations of Randomized
Z
-functions are GUE

8.1.
Annealed vs. Quenched Point-of-View

Strictly speaking, being GUE is a property of an ensemble, that is, a probability
distribution on the space of Hermitian matrices
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, or, in our setting, on sections
Z_{N}(t;a)
in
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
,
with Gaussian law on coefficients as proven in Theorem
A
. The GUE sine-kernel law refers to the limiting
pair-correlation of eigenvalues, or zeros, under this distribution. For a single
deterministic element, such as a fixed Hermitian matrix
H
, a section
Z_{N}(t;a)
, or,
Z(t)
as in the Montgomery pair-correlation conjecture, one can only ask whether its configuration of eigenvalues or zeros resembles typical GUE behaviour. In random matrix theory
this distinction appears as annealed versus quenched universality, where
annealed results concern averages over the ensemble, while quenched results discuss to which extent
individual elements of the space exhibit GUE-type local statistics.

In the context of the Montgomery pair–correlation conjecture, our interest now lies in
quenched questions. Mainly, assuming RH, whether the specific deterministic section

Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})\approx Z(t)
already exhibits GUE–type local statistics without any
ensemble averaging within the

\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. Our viewpoint is that the Hardy function
Z(t)

already contains the required averaging intrinsically within itself,
when interpreted through its approximations in the
A
–variation spaces.
For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, recall that
Z(t)
admits the finite approximation (
11
)

Z(t)\;\approx\;Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\,\cos\!\bigl(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1)\bigr)\;\in\;\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R}),

(383)

valid on the local window
[2N,2N+2]
.
Assuming the Riemann Hypothesis, we have
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
see
[
35
]
.

Remark 8.1

(On the choice of window boundaries)
.

It should be noted at this point that the specific choice of the window
[2N,2N+2]
in the definition of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is non-essential. One may instead consider more
flexible intervals of the form

[\,2N-\alpha,\,2N+2+\beta\,],\qquad\alpha,\beta\geq 0,

(384)

provided that the endpoints remain within the same logarithmic scale of

2N
, so the approximate functional equation
Z(t)\approx Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
remains valid. In particular, in Theorem A the fixed boundaries
[2N,2N+2]
were chosen merely for
convenience, to establish the GUE nature of the
space
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.
When dealing with the true Hardy function
Z(t)
, one might worry that some
zeros of
Z_{N}(t;1)
near the boundary could drift slightly outside the
prescribed window.
However, for large
N
the bulk of zeros in each window remains well inside
its interior, and any such boundary effects can be safely absorbed by a small
thickening of the interval via a sufficent increase of
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Consequently, the precise placement of the window boundaries is immaterial to
the asymptotic behaviour of the pair–correlation functionals.

In the random matrix setting, the statement that the Hermitian matrices are
“GUE–distributed” can be understood in two complementary senses.
First, in the ensemble sense, one considers the expectation of observables
with respect to the Gaussian measure on the full space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, and the
GUE sine–kernel law arises as the limit of the ensemble, averaged
pair correlation of eigenvalues.
The analogue of this viewpoint in our framework is precisely the result of
Theorem
A
, which establishes the GUE law for the ensemble of sections
in the real chamber of the
A
–variation space
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.

Second, an equivalent viewpoint is through Monte–Carlo realization.
Once it is known that a given space is GUE, if one draws a sufficiently large number of independent Hermitian matrices

H^{(1)},H^{(2)},\ldots,H^{(N)}
of increasing size from the GUE distribution and computes, for each,
the empirical pair correlation of eigenvalues, then the average of these samples
converges to the same GUE law. In this sense, a Monte–Carlo simulation provides a finite approximation to the ensemble average,
and the universality of the GUE law expresses the stability of the limiting statistics
under such sampling procedures, with accuracy improving as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
It is precisely this second, dynamical view-point that becomes relevant in our analytic interpretation
of the pair–correlation conjecture, where the sequence of sections
\{Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

is viewed as a deterministic sequence of “samples”
traversing the analytic ensemble
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. We first consider a randomized version of this question for section
\{Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\bar{a}^{(N)}
chosen independently according to Gaussian measure
\mu_{N}
restricted to
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.

8.2.
The Pair-Correlation conjecture for Random Sequences

Let us make the following definitions:

Definition 8.1

(Local and global pair–correlation functionals)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be a Schwartz test function.

(i)

We refer to

\mathrm{PC}_{N}(f;\bar{a}):=\frac{1}{M_{N}}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{j}(\bar{a}),\,t_{k}(\bar{a})\in[2N,2N+2]\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log N}{2\pi}\bigl(t_{j}(\bar{a})-t_{k}(\bar{a})\bigr)\right),

(385)

as the local pair-correlation functional of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
in the window
[2N,2N+2]
.

(ii)

Assume
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
for every
N
, we refer to to

PC^{approx}(f;\left\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\right\},T):=\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}M_{N}\,\mathrm{PC}_{N}\!\bigl(f;\bar{a}^{(N)}\bigr),

(386)

where
N(T)\sim\tfrac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}
, as the approximate pair-correlation function of the sequence
\left\{Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(iii)

Let

\Gamma_{T}=\bigcup_{2N\leq T}\bigl\{t_{j}(\bar{a}^{(N)})\in[2N,2N+2]\bigr\}.

(387)

be the union of all zeros of the sections

\{Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\}_{2N\leq T}
lying in
[0,T]
. We refer to

PC^{gen}(f;\left\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\right\},T):=\frac{1}{N(T)}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{T}\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right),

(388)

as the genuine global pair–correlation functional of the sequence
\left\{Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(iv)

We refer to

PC^{Z}(f;T):=\frac{1}{N(T)}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right),

(389)

where the sum runs of zeros of
Z(t)
, as the pair-correlation functional of the Hardy
Z
-function.

By definition, the pair–correlation conjecture asserts that, asymptotically,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{Z}(f;T)=\mathrm{GUE}(f),

(390)

where

\mathrm{GUE}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\!\left(1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{\!2}\right)\!dx.

(391)

Moreover, in view of our results in
[
35
]
we have

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{Z}(f;T)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{gen}(f;\bar{1},T),

(392)

Hence, for us the pair-correlation conjecture is essentially henceforth

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{gen}(f;\bar{1},T)=\mathrm{GUE}(f).

(393)

Let us note the following remark:

Remark 8.2

(The point
\bar{1}
versus the accelerated coefficients
\bar{a}^{\mathrm{acc}}_{N}
)
.

While
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
was chosen for convenience and clarity,
one may, for greater numerical accuracy, replace
\bar{1}
throughout this
section by the accelerated coefficient vector
\bar{a}^{\mathrm{acc}}_{N}

defined in (
52
).
According to (
51
), this choice provides a highly accurate
approximation of the full Hardy function
Z(t)
, and all results presented
herein remain valid under this substitution.
In particular, for sufficiently large
T
, the zeros of

Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{\mathrm{acc}}_{N})
are effectively indistinguishable from those
of
Z(t)
.

The following result shows that the approximate global pair-correlation is asymptotically similar to the genuine pair-correlation:

Proposition 8.1

(
PC^{approx}
approximates
PC^{gen}
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be a Schwartz test function. Then, asymptotically the approximate and genuine pair-correlations coincide

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{gen}(f;\left\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\right\},T)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{approx}(f;\left\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\right\},T),

(394)

for any sequence
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Let us first assume
supp(f)\subset[-C,C]
. Partition
[0,T]
into half–open windows
I_{N}:=[2N,2N+2)
.
Decompose the genuine global sum into intra–window and inter–window parts:

\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{gen}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)=\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{T}\cap I_{N}\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)\;+\\
+\;E_{\mathrm{cross}}(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T).

(395)

The proof is based on showing the following:

(1)

The intra-window main sum asymptotically coincides with
PC^{approx}
,

\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{T}\cap I_{N}\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)=\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{approx}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right).

(396)

(2)

The inter-window sum is asymptotically vanishing:

|E_{\mathrm{cross}}(f;T)|=O\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right).

(397)

For (1) write

s_{N}=\frac{\log N}{2\pi},\qquad s_{T}=\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}.

(398)

Since
\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset[-C,C]
,
a contributing pair must satisfy
s_{T}|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\leq C
, and hence

|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\ll\frac{1}{\log T}
. By the mean–value theorem,

\Big|f\!\big(s_{T}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\big)-f\!\big(s_{N}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\big)\Big|\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\,|s_{T}-s_{N}|\ll\frac{\log(T/N)}{\log T}.

(399)

Next, the number of pairs of zeros in
I_{N}
satisfying
|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\ll 1/\log T
is

O\!\left(M_{N}\cdot\frac{\log N}{\log T}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{(\log N)^{2}}{\log T}\right),

(400)

since
I_{N}
contains
M_{N}=O(\log N)
zeros, and the local zero density is also
O(\log N)
.
Therefore, the per–window rescaling error is

O\!\left(\frac{(\log N)^{2}}{\log T}\right)\times O\!\left(\frac{\log(T/N)}{\log T}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{(\log N)^{2}\,\log(T/N)}{(\log T)^{2}}\right).

(401)

Summing over
2N\leq T
and dividing by
N(T)=O(T\log T)
yields the normalized
rescaling error

\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}O\!\left(\frac{(\log N)^{2}\,\log(T/N)}{(\log T)^{2}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{T\cdot(\log T)^{2}}{T(\log T)^{2}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right),

(402)

since the average of
(\log N)^{2}
over
1\leq N\leq T
is
O((\log T)^{2})

and the average of
\log(T/N)
is
O(1)
. Hence

\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{T}\cap I_{N}\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)=\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{approx}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right),

(403)

which gives (1).
For (2), as before, a pair from distinct windows contributes only if

|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\ll 1/\log T
. Hence, pairs form non-adjacent windows do not contribute. For
adjacent windows
I_{N}
and
I_{N+1}
, both zeros must lie in

O(1/\log T)
–neighbourhoods of the common boundary
2N+2
. Each side contributes

O(1)
zeros, hence
O(1)
pairs per boundary, so with
O(T)
boundaries

|E_{\mathrm{cross}}(f;T)|=O\!\left(\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{N(T)}\,T\right)=O\!\left(\frac{T}{T\log T}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right)\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\;0.

(404)

Combining the steps (1) and (2) gives

\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{gen}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)=\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{approx}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right),

(405)

and hence

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{gen}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{approx}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr).

(406)

∎

We will need the following weighted variation of the strong law of large numbers:

Proposition 8.2

(Weighted strong law of large numbers)
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be independent random variables with
\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}]=0
and

\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}^{2}]<\infty
.
Let
(w_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be positive weights and define

W_{N}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\quad\text{with}\quad W_{N}\to\infty.

(407)

Assume that

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\frac{w_{N}^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(Y_{N})}{W_{N}^{2}}<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\max_{k\leq N}\frac{w_{k}}{W_{N}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(408)

Then

\frac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}Y_{k}\xrightarrow[\mathrm{a.s.}]{}0.

(409)

Proof.

Define

Z_{N}:=\frac{w_{N}}{W_{N}}\,Y_{N},\qquad N\geq 1.

(410)

Then
(Z_{n})
are independent, centered, and satisfy

\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}^{2}]=\frac{w_{N}^{2}}{W_{N}^{2}}\,\mathrm{Var}(Y_{N}).

(411)

By the assumption (
408
), we have

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}^{2}]<\infty.

(412)

Hence, by the classical Kolmogorov convergence criterion,
the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Z_{n}
converges almost surely. Let
S_{m}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}Z_{k}
.
Since
S_{m}
converges a.s., the classical Kronecker’s lemma
[
8
]
gives

\frac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}Z_{k}\xrightarrow[\mathrm{a.s.}]{}0.

(413)

But from (
410
),

\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}Z_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}Y_{k},

(414)

and therefore

\frac{1}{W_{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}Y_{k}\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

(415)

∎

The following result is Theorem
B
which shows that if
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is chosen randomly then the pair-correlation of the sequence is asymptotically GUE.

Theorem 8.1

(GUE for random chains)
.

For each
N
, assume
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})
is drawn independently from the Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then, with probability
1
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{gen}(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T)=\mathrm{GUE}(f).

(416)

Proof.

Write
X_{N}:=\mathrm{PC}_{N}\!\bigl(f;\bar{a}^{(N)}\bigr)
.
By Theorem
A
we have

m_{N}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{N}]\ \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\mathrm{GUE}(f).

(417)

Since
f
is bounded, each term in the local pair–correlation sum satisfies

|f|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
.
As there are
M_{N}=O(\log N)
zeros in the window
[2N,2N+2]
, we obtain

|X_{N}|\ll\|f\|_{\infty}\,\log N,\qquad\mathrm{Var}(X_{N})=O\!\left((\log N)^{2}\right).

(418)

Clearly

\max_{2N\leq T}\frac{M_{N}}{N(T)}\to 0

(419)

because
M_{N}=O(\log N)
while
N(T)=O(T\log T)
.

Consider the centred independent variables

Y_{N}:=X_{N}-m_{N}
with
\mathbb{E}[Y_{N}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[Y_{N}^{2}]<\infty
.
Let us take
w_{N}=M_{N}
and
W_{T}=\sum_{2N\leq T}w_{N}
. We have

\frac{w_{N}^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(Y_{N})}{W_{N}^{2}}=O\!\left(\frac{(\log N)^{4}}{(N\log N)^{2}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{(\log N)^{2}}{N^{2}}\right),

(420)

and
\sum_{N}(\log N)^{2}/N^{2}<\infty
.
Hence, the conditions of the weighted strong law of large numbers of Proposition
8.2
are satisfied, and thus

\frac{1}{W(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}w_{N}Y_{N}\ \xrightarrow[\text{a.s.}]{}\ 0.

(421)

Since
W(T)\sim N(T)
, we obtain

\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{gen}}(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T)=\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}w_{N}X_{N}=\frac{1}{N(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}w_{N}m_{N}\;+\;o(1)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(422)

Recall that according to the Toeplitz summation theorem if
(a_{n})
is any sequence with
a_{n}\to A
and
(p_{n,k})
is a triangular array of nonnegative numbers such that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{n,k}=1
for each
n
and
\max_{k}p_{n,k}\to 0
,
then the weighted averages

b_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{n,k}a_{k}

(423)

also converge to the same limit
A
, see
[
19
]
. Let us take
a_{N}=m_{N}
, and define

p_{T,N}:=\frac{w_{N}}{W(T)},

(424)

for
2N\leq T
.
These satisfy
p_{T,N}\geq 0
,
\sum_{2N\leq T}p_{T,N}=1
, and

\max_{2N\leq T}p_{T,N}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
Hence, by the Toeplitz summation theorem,

\frac{1}{W(T)}\sum_{2N\leq T}w_{N}m_{N}\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\;\mathrm{GUE}(f).

(425)

Finally, since
W(T)\sim N(T)
, we may replace
W(T)
by
N(T)
in the normalization without changing the limit.
Substituting (
425
) into (
422
) yields

\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{gen}}(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T)\;\xrightarrow[\mathrm{a.s.}]{}\;\mathrm{GUE}(f),

(426)

which completes the proof.
∎

9.
The Proof of the Pair Correlation Conjecture for the Hardy Function

9.1.
From Ensemble Universality to the Pair Correlation of the Hardy Function

In this section we explain how the GUE universality established in Theorem A
extends from the variational ensemble
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
to the actual Hardy
function
Z(t)
. Once the ensemble statistics are known to coincide with the GUE law,
the remaining task is to show that the canonical section
Z_{N}(t;1)
behaves, over large disjoint windows, like a typical randomized member
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})
of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.

It should be noted that not every element of the ensemble displays GUE–like spacing. For instance,
the identity matrix in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
or the core section
Z_{0}(t)=\cos(\theta(t))
both admit perfectly ordered, evenly spaced zeros within each window.
From the viewpoint of the one–dimensional Coulomb gas analogy, these correspond to “crystalline” zero–temperature configurations in which the repulsive forces between neighboring charges exactly balance.
Such rigid structures are dynamically unstable and under small perturbations or under Dyson Brownian motion they “melt” and evolve toward the universal GUE equilibrium, as shown for instance in the universality results of
[
18
,
6
]
.

To ensure that the deterministic sections
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
display the same effective randomness as the ensemble, one needs a mechanism enforcing statistical independence between distant spectral windows.
Theorem
9.1
provides precisely this mechanism as it establishes that the local pair–correlation functionals
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
decorrelate across disjoint intervals, ensuring that the zeros of the Hardy function exhibit the same macroscopic independence that underlies GUE universality.

Theorem 9.1

(Decorrelation of
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
)
.

For every fixed Schwartz test function
f
, the sequence
\{PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
has uniformly bounded variance and
satisfies the weak decorrelation condition

\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(PC_{N}(f;\bar{1}),PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\bar{1})\right)\to 0,

(427)

as
|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty
.

The truncations
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
and the full Hardy function
Z(t)
share
essentially the same zeros on the interval
I_{N}=[2N,2N+2]
,
since the remainder of the approximate formula (
51
) contributes only an exponentially small perturbation to their zero locations, see Remark
8.2
.
It is therefore legitimate to replace the analysis of the zeros of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
by the classical,
well–established theory of the zeros of
Z(t)
itself.

Remark 9.1

(Statistical–physics interpretation)
.

Although each
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
is a single scalar quantity, it aggregates the
pairwise interactions of
M_{N}\!\to\!\infty
zeros of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
within the
window
I_{N}=[2N,2N+2]
. In the context of
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
, these zeros can be viewed as originating from the perfectly structured
configuration of
Z_{0}(t)=\cos(\theta(t))
, whose zeros in
I_{N}
are evenly spaced,
and are progressively perturbed by the oscillatory components

\cos(\theta(t)-t\log(k+1))/\sqrt{k+1}
for
k=1,\dots,N
.
Each new frequency
\log(k+1)
introduces an incommensurable phase, adding
a new layer of microscopic irregularity to the zero configuration.
As
N
grows, the cumulative effect of these quasi–independent perturbations
renders the fluctuations of distinct zeros effectively chaotic,
so that the global functional
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
behaves as the macroscopic sum of
infinitely many weakly correlated contributions.

The proof of Theorem
9.1
is based on Selberg’s theory on the fluctuations of the argument function

S(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arg\zeta\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+it\right),

(428)

see
[
58
,
59
,
61
,
60
]
. Selberg’s results describe the local oscillations of
Z(t)
as an almost Gaussian process
with rapidly decaying correlations on disjoint intervals.
This analytic structure gives a precise counterpart to the
statistical–physics picture discussed above. While in the Coulomb–gas model
decorrelation arises from thermal agitation or Dyson Brownian motion,
here it emerges from the intrinsic pseudo–randomness of the argument increments of
\zeta(s)
.
The proof of Theorem
9.1
therefore rests on Selberg’s covariance estimates for
S^{\prime}(t)
,
which formalize the near–independence of the underlying oscillations and ensure that the
local functionals
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
behave as asymptotically independent variables.
The rest of this section develops this argument in detail.

(1)

In subsection
9.2
,
we review Selberg’s statistical theory for the argument function

S(t)=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\arg\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
,
describing its Gaussian fluctuations and the decay of correlations in its derivative
S^{\prime}(t)
.

(2)

In subsection
9.3
,
we reinterpret Selberg’s
L^{2}
correlation estimates in the language of stationary Gaussian processes,
and recall the strong–mixing coefficient
\alpha(u)
together with the

\alpha
–covariance bound (
443
),
which quantify the asymptotic independence of disjoint segments of the process, following
[
27
]
.

(3)

In subsection
9.4
,
we reformulate the local pair–correlation statistic
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})

as a smooth bounded functional
F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N}})

of the restriction of the derivative process
S^{\prime}(t)

to the window
I_{N}=[2N,2N+2]
.
Using Selberg’s covariance estimates, we show that
S^{\prime}(t)

is a strongly mixing process, implying asymptotic independence
of such local functionals across distant intervals.
A uniform bound on
\|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\|_{\infty}

then allows the application of the

\alpha
–covariance bound (
443
),
establishing the weak decorrelation property required in Theorem
9.1
.
For completeness, we also indicate an alternative proof based on
the covariance identity for Gaussian functionals,
derived from Selberg’s empirical covariance kernel.

(4)

Finally, in subsection
9.5
,
we combine the decorrelation of
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
established in Theorem
9.1

with the ensemble universality result of Theorem
A

and the GUE law for randomized sections proved in Theorem
B
,
to complete the proof of Theorem
C
.

9.2.
Selberg’s Statistical Theory of
S(t)

The statistical fluctuations of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
are encoded in the function

S(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arg\zeta\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+it\right),

(429)

called the argument of the Riemann zeta function. Here
\arg\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
denotes the increment of an arbitrary continuous branch of
\arg\zeta(s)
along the broken line starting at
2
, where the argument is zero, going vertically to
2+it
and then reaching
\tfrac{1}{2}+it
horizontally. At a zero
\gamma
one takes the symmetric limit

S(\gamma)=\lim_{\delta\to+0}\frac{1}{2}\bigl(S(\gamma+\delta)+S(\gamma-\delta)\bigr).

(430)

The function
S(t)
is a slowly varying and highly irregular function. Modulo integers, the function
S(t)
coincides with the principal value of the argument of
\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
. However, in the definition of
S(t)
one uses a continuous, unwrapped, branch of the argument obtained by analytic continuation along the path from
s=2
to
\tfrac{1}{2}+it
, prescribed above. As a result, each time
Z(t)=e^{i\theta(t)}\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
crosses a zero
\gamma
, the function
\arg\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
changes discontinuously by
\pm\pi
, hence
S(t)
jumps by
\pm 1
.
The sign of this jump is determined by the sign of the derivative,

\Delta S\left(\gamma\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\Delta(\arg Z)=\operatorname{sgn}\,\bigl(Z^{\prime}\left(\gamma\right)\bigr).

(431)

If
Z^{\prime}(\gamma)>0
the curve
\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
crosses the real axis counter-clockwise and
S(t)
increases by
+1
, and if
Z^{\prime}(\gamma)<0
it crosses clockwise and
S(t)
decreases by
1
.

A simple model illustrates this mechanism by noting that the core function
Z_{0}(t)
has zeros at the points
\theta(t_{n})=(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi
, with

Z_{0}^{\prime}(t_{n})=-\theta^{\prime}(t_{n})\sin\,\left(\theta(t_{n})\right)=(-1)^{n}\,\theta^{\prime}(t_{n}),

(432)

so the sign of
Z_{0}^{\prime}(t_{n})
alternates as
(-1)^{n}
.
Hence a corresponding argument function for the core function

S^{\mathrm{core}}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arg\bigl(e^{-i\theta(t)}Z_{0}(t)\bigr)

(433)

would produce the perfectly alternating pattern of jumps

\Delta S^{\mathrm{core}}(t_{n})=(-1)^{n}.

(434)

However, for the true zeta function one has

S(t)=-\frac{\theta(t)}{\pi}+\frac{1}{\pi}\arg Z(t).

(435)

Since the zeros of
Z(t)
occur slightly off the ideal positions
\theta(t)=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi
, their signs
Z^{\prime}(\gamma_{n})
are not perfectly alternating, producing the irregular
\pm 1
jumps in
S(t)
, which can accumulate in a non-trivial manner.

The function
S(t)
first appeared implicitly in Riemann’s 1859 memoir
[
53
]
and
was made explicit by von Mangoldt in his derivation of the Riemann-von Mangoldt formula,

N(T)=\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}-\frac{T}{2\pi}+\frac{7}{8}+S(T)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\right).

(436)

Here
N(T)
denotes the number of nontrivial zeros of
Z(t)
with
0<t\leq T
.
The main terms describes the smooth asymptotic density of zeros,
while the remainder
S(T)
captures their fine irregularities, we refer the reader to the classical texts
[
16
,
67
]
.
Classically, one proves that
S(T)=O(\log T)
,
and, on average, its size is much smaller.

In a series of seminal papers
[
58
,
59
,
61
,
60
]
,
Selberg developed a probabilistic theory of the Riemann zeta function,
in which the argument function

S(t)
is treated as a centered Gaussian process with

\mathrm{Var}\,S(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\log\log t+O(1).

(437)

Its derivative,

S^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{1/2}}\sin(t\log n),

(438)

has mean zero and satisfies the energy bound

\int_{T}^{2T}S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\,dt=O(T\log T),

(439)

together with the cross–correlation estimate

\int_{T}^{2T}S^{\prime}(t)\,S^{\prime}(t+u)\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{T}{\log T}\right),

(440)

for
u\geq 1
.

9.3.
Ergodic Properties of
S^{\prime}(t)
and the Proof of Theorem
9.1

Selberg’s asymptotic formula (
440
) can be expressed as the following covariance relation

\mathrm{Cov}_{T}\,\bigl(S^{\prime}(t),S^{\prime}(t+u)\bigr):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{T}^{2T}\!S^{\prime}(t)S^{\prime}(t+u)\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log T}\right),

(441)

which expresses the rapid decay of correlations in the derivative process
S^{\prime}(t)
.
In particular, it implies that the values of
S^{\prime}(t)
and
S^{\prime}(t+u)
become asymptotically uncorrelated as
T\to\infty
,
indicating that
S^{\prime}(t)
behaves as a strongly mixing stationary process on large scales.

To make this precise, recall that a real process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is stationary if the joint law of

(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})
depends only on the differences
t_{i}-t_{j}
.
For such a process, let

\alpha(u):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}^{0},\,B\in\mathcal{F}_{u}^{+\infty}}\bigl|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\bigr|

(442)

denote the strong–mixing coefficient, see
[
54
]
.
For any bounded measurable functions
F_{1},F_{2}
one has the following
\alpha
-bound

|\mathrm{Cov}(F_{1}(X_{t}),F_{2}(X_{t+u}))|\;\leq\;4\,\|F_{1}\|_{\infty}\|F_{2}\|_{\infty}\,\alpha(u),

(443)

so that
\alpha(u)
uniformly bounds all covariances of bounded functionals of the process, see Theorem 17.2.1 of
[
27
]
.

The process is said to be strongly mixing if
\alpha(u)\to 0
as
u\to\infty
.
In the special case of a stationary Gaussian process,
a fundamental theorem of Ibragimov
[
28
]
asserts that
the decay of correlations is
equivalent
to strong mixing, that is,

R(u):=\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+u})\xrightarrow[u\to\infty]{}0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha(u)\xrightarrow[u\to\infty]{}0.

(444)

Hence, combining (
443
) with (
444
) shows that
the vanishing of the covariance at large lags
implies asymptotic independence of all smooth functionals

f(X_{t})
and
g(X_{t+u})
as
u\to\infty
.
In summary, the logical chain

R(u)\xrightarrow[u\to\infty]{}0\;\Longrightarrow\;\alpha(u)\xrightarrow[u\to\infty]{}0\;\Longrightarrow\;\mathrm{Cov}\bigl(F_{1}(X_{t}),F_{2}(X_{t+u})\bigr)\xrightarrow[u\to\infty]{}0

(445)

encapsulates the general mechanism of the mixing theory:
the decay of the covariance function
R(u)
implies the decay of the strong–mixing coefficient
\alpha(u)
,
which in turn controls the asymptotic independence of bounded functionals through the
\alpha
–bound (
443
).
In our case, Selberg’s estimate (
441
) already provides the decay of
R(u)
,
and for smooth functionals of a Gaussian process the corresponding covariance bound
follows immediately by integrating against
R(u)
itself.
We will make this direct argument explicit in the next subsections.

9.4.
The Pair-Correlation
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
as a Functional of
S^{\prime}(t)

Selberg’s asymptotic bound (
441
)
implies that for every fixed lag
u
, the mean covariance

\mathrm{Cov}_{T}(S^{\prime}(t),S^{\prime}(t+u))
vanishes as
T\to\infty
.
Thus the limiting covariance function

R(u):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{Cov}_{T}(S^{\prime}(t),S^{\prime}(t+u))

(446)

exists and is identically zero for all finite
u
.
Consequently,

\lim_{u\to\infty}R(u)=0

holds trivially, and the process
S^{\prime}(t)
is decorrelated—
indeed, strongly mixing—at every finite separation. From the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula (
436
) one has, for any
u>0
,

N(t+u)-N(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\int_{t}^{t+u}\!\log\!\frac{s}{2\pi}\,ds+\!\int_{t}^{t+u}\!S^{\prime}(s)\,ds+O\!\left(\frac{u}{t^{2}}\right).

(447)

The first integral represents the smooth mean density of zeros,
while the second encodes the local oscillatory fluctuations through
S^{\prime}(s)
.
Hence, the local configuration of zeros in any short interval
[t,t+u]

is entirely determined by the integral of
S^{\prime}(s)
over that region. In particular, the local pair–correlation functional

PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})=\frac{1}{M_{N}}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in I_{N}\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log N}{2\pi}(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right),

(448)

depends only on the relative spacings of zeros within
I_{N}=[2N,2N+2]

and may therefore be viewed as a smooth bounded functional
of the restriction of
S^{\prime}(t)
to that interval:

PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})=F_{f,N}\bigl(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N}}\bigr),\qquad F_{f,N}:\,L^{\infty}(I_{N})\to\mathbb{R}.

(449)

Since
S^{\prime}(t)
is strongly mixing,
the values of such functionals on disjoint intervals are asymptotically independent.
Hence, by the general theoretic logical chain (
445
), obtaining a uniform bound on
\|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\|_{\infty}
would imply via (
443
) the decorrelation

\mathrm{Cov}\bigl(PC_{N}(f;\bar{1}),PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\bar{1})\bigr)\to 0,\qquad|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty,

(450)

as required in Theorem
9.1
.
The naive estimate

|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})|\;\ll\;\|f\|_{\infty}\log N

(451)

arises from the fact that the double sum in
PC_{N}
contains

\binom{M_{N}}{2}
pairwise contributions of size at most
\|f\|_{\infty}
but is normalized by only one factor of
M_{N}
. However, (
451
) is actually an overestimation of the true scale of the functional, since it implicitly assumes that every zero interacts with every other zero in the window.
In reality, because
f
is or rapidly decaying,
each zero interacts with only
O(1)
neighbouring zeros,
so the effective number of contributing pairs is
O(M_{N})
rather than
O(M_{N}^{2})
. The following gives the required estimate:

Proposition 9.1

(Uniform boundedness of the pair–correlation functional)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be a bounded test function with compact support

\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset[-R,R]
. Then there exists a constant
C(f)>0
, depending only on
f
,
such that

\|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;C(f)

(452)

for all sufficiently large
N
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset[-R,R]
, a term

f\!\big(\tfrac{\log N}{2\pi}(t_{j}-t_{k})\big)
is nonzero only when

|t_{j}-t_{k}|\;\leq\;\frac{2\pi R}{\log N}.

The average spacing between consecutive zeros in
[2N,2N+2]
satisfies

\Delta t\sim\frac{2\pi}{\log N}
, so each zero
t_{j}
can have
at most
O(R)
neighboring zeros within that range.
Hence, for each
t_{j}
, the number of indices
k\neq j
with

f\!\big(\tfrac{\log N}{2\pi}(t_{j}-t_{k})\big)\neq 0
is bounded by a constant
C_{R}

independent of
N
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M_{N}}\sum_{j}\sum_{|k-j|\leq C_{R}}\big|f\!\left(\tfrac{\log N}{2\pi}(t_{j}-t_{k})\right)\big|

(453)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M_{N}}\sum_{j}C_{R}\,\|f\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;C_{R}\,\|f\|_{\infty}.

(454)

The constant
C_{R}
depends only on the support width of
f
and not on
N
.
Consequently,

\|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\|_{\infty}\leq C(f):=C_{R}\|f\|_{\infty},

(455)

uniformly for all
N
, completing the proof.
∎

Let us thus summarize the proof of Theorem
9.1
outlined above:

Proof of Theorem
9.1
.

Selberg’s covariance estimate (
441
) shows that the derivative process
S^{\prime}(t)
is stationary and satisfies
R(u)\to 0
as
u\to\infty
, implying asymptotic decorrelation of its values at distant points.
Since the local pair–correlation functional
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
can be written as a smooth bounded functional
F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N}})
of the restriction of
S^{\prime}(t)
to the window
I_{N}
, see (
449
), the general chain (
445
) ensures that vanishing of
R(u)
yields vanishing covariance of such functionals across disjoint windows.
The remaining ingredient is the uniform bound on
\|PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\|_{\infty}
established in Proposition
9.1
, which provides the uniform Lipschitz control required for the covariance estimate (
443
).
Combining these ingredients gives

\mathrm{Cov}\!\bigl(PC_{N}(f;\bar{1}),PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\bar{1})\bigr)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty,

proving the weak decorrelation condition (
427
).
∎

The following remark outlines an alternative proof of Theorem
9.1
, obtained directly from Selberg’s covariance estimates, without appealing to the general
\alpha
–mixing theory:

Remark 9.2

(Alternative Proof of Theorem
9.1
)
.

A proof of Theorem
9.1
Since

PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})=F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N}})

(456)

depends only on the restriction of
the Gaussian process
S^{\prime}(t)
to
I_{N}=[2N,2N+2]
,
one may apply the following covariance identity for Gaussian functionals, given in Prop. 6.2.1 of
[
46
]
,

\mathrm{Cov}\!\bigl(F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N}}),F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N^{\prime}}})\bigr)=\iint_{I_{N}\times I_{N^{\prime}}}\!\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[\partial_{t}F_{f,N}\,\partial_{t^{\prime}}F_{f,N^{\prime}}\right]R_{T}(t,t^{\prime})\,dt\,dt^{\prime},

(457)

where

R_{T}(t,t^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{T}\!\int_{T}^{2T}\!S^{\prime}(s+t)S^{\prime}(s+t^{\prime})\,ds

(458)

is Selberg’s empirical covariance kernel.
By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\bigl|\mathrm{Cov}\!\bigl(F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N}}),F_{f,N}(S^{\prime}|_{I_{N^{\prime}}})\bigr)\bigr|\;\leq\;\|\partial_{t}F_{f,N}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|\partial_{t^{\prime}}F_{f,N^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}}\!\iint_{I_{N}\times I_{N^{\prime}}}\!|R_{T}(t,t^{\prime})|\,dt\,dt^{\prime}.

(459)

Selberg’s bound

\mathrm{Cov}_{T}(S^{\prime}(t),S^{\prime}(t+u))=O((\log T)^{-1})

implies that
R_{T}(t,t^{\prime})\to 0
uniformly as
|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty
,
so any family of local functionals with uniformly bounded Fréchet norms

\|\partial_{t}F_{f,N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{f}

satisfies

\mathrm{Cov}\,\bigl(PC_{N}(f;\bar{1}),PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\bar{1})\bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;0,\qquad|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty.

(460)

Hence, A bound of the form

\|\partial_{t}F_{f,N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{f}
on the norm of the Fréchet derivative would, similarly to Proposition
9.1
ensure that the contribution of
R_{T}(t,t^{\prime})
in (
457
)
is dominated by the vanishing covariance of the process itself,
thereby leading to the same asymptotic independence of the functionals (
427
).
This route could be viewed as a more direct “functional–analytic” derivation of covariance decay based solely on Selberg’s Gaussian representation of
S^{\prime}(t)
.

9.5.
Proof of the Pair–Correlation Conjecture and Discussion

We are now in position to conclude the proof of Montgomery’s pair correlation conjecture, which is Theorem
C
of the introduction:

Theorem 9.2

(Montgomery Pair–Correlation Theorem for Hardy’s
Z
–function)
.

Assuming the Riemann Hypothesis, the nontrivial zeros of Hardy’s
Z
–function
obey the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) pair–correlation law.
Equivalently, Montgomery’s Pair–Correlation Conjecture holds.
That is, for every Schwartz test function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{N(T)}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f\!\left(\frac{\log(T/2\pi)}{2\pi}\,(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\!\left(1-\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{\!2}\right)dx,

(461)

where
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
denote the real zeros of
Z(t)
,
and
N(T)\sim\tfrac{T}{2\pi}\log\!\tfrac{T}{2\pi}
is their counting function.

Proof.

By Theorem
9.1
, the covariance between pair–correlation functionals on distant windows satisfies

\mathrm{Cov}\!\bigl(PC_{N}(f;\bar{1}),PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\bar{1})\bigr)\;\to\;0\qquad\text{as}\qquad|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty.

(462)

Hence the sequence
\{PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\}_{N\geq 1}
forms a stationary, strongly–mixing process with uniformly bounded variance.
By the ergodic theorem for mixing sequences, see
[
27
,
3
]
,
the global average

PC_{T}(f;\bar{1}):=\frac{1}{T}\!\!\sum_{2N\leq T}PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})

(463)

converges in
L^{2}
to its ergodic mean

PC_{T}(f;\bar{1})\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{L^{2}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{erg}}\!\bigl[PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\bigr],

(464)

implying that the deterministic
time average along the
N
coincides asymptotically with
the expectation over the randomized ensemble

PC_{N}(f;\bar{a}^{(N)})
.
By Theorem
B
, the expectation of the corresponding randomized model satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\!\bigl[PC_{N}(f;\bar{a}^{(N)})\bigr]=GUE(f)+o(1).

(465)

Selberg’s mixing ensures that time averages of the deterministic process

PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
coincide asymptotically with ensemble averages of its randomized counterpart.
Hence,

\lim_{T\to\infty}PC_{T}(f;\bar{1})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}[PC_{N}(f;\bar{a}^{(N)})]=GUE(f).

(466)

This establishes Montgomery’s pair–correlation law for Hardy’s
Z
–function.
∎

Montgomery’s original analysis of the pair correlation of zeta zeros
was based on the explicit formula, which connects the nontrivial zeros of

\zeta(s)
to the distribution of prime numbers, see
[
43
]
.
In his seminal work, Montgomery established the pair–correlation formula
only for the highly restricted class of test functions whose Fourier transform
satisfies
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{f})\subset[-1,1]
.
Beyond this limited range, extending this approach to the general case remains entirely open,
as it would require deep new information on correlations between distinct primes.
The following remark outlines the main ideas of Montgomery’s approach,
the reason for this restriction, and its relation to conjectures on prime correlations.

Remark 9.3

(Montgomery’s original approach and primes)
.

Let

\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)

(467)

be Chebyshev’s function.
The explicit formula gives, for
\Re(s)>1
,

-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{\rho}\frac{1}{s-\rho}+O(1),

(468)

where the sum is over the nontrivial zeros
\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma
.
Montgomery considered the normalized pair correlation sum

F(\alpha):=\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\log T\right)^{-1}\sum_{0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T}T^{i\alpha(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})}\,w(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}),

(469)

where
w(u)=(4+u^{2})^{-1}
is a smooth weight.
Formally,
F(\alpha)
is the Fourier transform of the pair correlation density.

By inserting the explicit formula (
468
) for

-\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
into the double–sum representation
of
F(\alpha)
, and after expanding the resulting Dirichlet series
and applying the approximate functional equation to truncate and
symmetrize the sums, one arrives at expressions of the form

F(\alpha)\;\approx\;\frac{1}{\log^{2}T}\sum_{m,n\leq T}\frac{\Lambda(m)\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt{mn}}\,\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{i\alpha}V\!\left(\frac{mn}{T}\right),

(470)

where
V
is a smooth cutoff.
The diagonal terms with
m=n
can be evaluated directly using the Prime Number Theorem:

\frac{1}{\log^{2}T}\sum_{n\leq T}\frac{\Lambda(n)^{2}}{n}\sim 1.

These diagonal contributions dominate when the Fourier transform

\widehat{f}
of the test function is supported in
[-1,1]
,
and yield the GUE prediction

F(\alpha)=1+o(1),\qquad|\alpha|<1.

(471)

However, the off–diagonal part
m\neq n
gives rise to the oscillatory terms

\frac{1}{\log^{2}T}\!\sum_{m\neq n\leq T}\!\frac{\Lambda(m)\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt{mn}}\!\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{i\alpha}V\!\left(\frac{mn}{T}\right).

(472)

For
|\alpha|>1
, these terms no longer cancel,
since the Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\alpha)

probes correlations between distinct primes.
Writing
n=m+h
and expanding the convolution of the von Mangoldt function
for small fixed even shifts
h
, one is naturally led to the correlation sum

\sum_{m\leq T}\Lambda(m)\Lambda(m+h)\;\approx\;\mathfrak{S}(h)\,T,

(473)

where
\mathfrak{S}(h)
is the Hardy–Littlewood singular series.
This product encapsulates the local correlations of primes modulo
p
,
and is defined by

\mathfrak{S}(h)=\prod_{p>2}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\nu_{p}(h)}{(p-1)^{2}}\right),\qquad\nu_{p}(h)=\begin{cases}1,&p\mid h,\\[4.0pt]
2,&p\nmid h.\end{cases}

(474)

In this expression, each Euler factor reflects the local density of
prime pairs
(p,p+h)
modulo
p
.
The validity of (
473
) for all fixed even
h

is precisely the Hardy–Littlewood prime–pair conjecture

[
25
]
,
which predicts the asymptotic density of prime pairs

\pi_{2}(x;h):=\#\{p\leq x:\,p,\,p+h\ \text{prime}\}\;\sim\;2\,\mathfrak{S}(h)\,\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2}}.

(475)

Thus, the extension of Montgomery’s formula for
F(\alpha)
beyond
|\alpha|\leq 1

depends on understanding the joint distribution of distinct primes,
and is therefore conditional on the Hardy–Littlewood conjectures.

This explains why Montgomery could only prove the PCC under the special restriction of test functions with
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{f})\subset[-1,1]
, as
within this range, the off–diagonal correlations are absent,
and only the diagonal, single-prime, contribution is required,
which follows from the Prime Number Theorem alone.
For
|\alpha|>1
, the analysis of (
472
)
requires detailed control of prime pairs (
473
),
which lies far beyond current methods.

By contrast, the present variational–probabilistic approach
does not rely on any arithmetic input.
Rather than tracing zeros back to the primes via the explicit formula,
the pair–correlation law emerges in Theorem
A

as a universal statistical property of the variational ensemble itself.
The local Gaussian fluctuations of the derivative process
S^{\prime}(t)

govern the mixing and decorrelation of the functionals
PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})

on disjoint intervals, and this behaviour follows directly from Selberg’s
probabilistic theory of
S(t)
via Theorem
9.2
, without invoking any open conjectures
about correlations among the primes.

However, it is known that the resolution of the pair–correlation conjecture
has far–reaching implications for the distribution of prime numbers.
Through the explicit formula, which links the nontrivial zeros of
\zeta(s)

to the oscillations of the prime counting function

[
16
,
43
]
,
the pair–correlation law directly translates into information about
the average behaviour of prime pairs.
In particular, it implies asymptotic formulas for the mean frequency
of primes separated by a given gap
h
, in the spirit of the
Hardy–Littlewood prime–pair conjecture

[
25
,
23
]
.
Thus, while the analytic proof of the pair–correlation law
requires no arithmetic hypotheses, its validity reflects,
through the explicit formula, a deep regularity in the collective
distribution of the primes themselves.

10.
Summary and Concluding Remarks

In 1973 Montgomery introduced the Pair–Correlation Conjecture
[
43
]
which states that the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta–function, behave statistically like the eigenvalues of a random Hermitian matrix
drawn from GUE. Our approach is that Montgomery’s conjecture actually conceales two sperate questions:

(1)

The microscopic question:

What does it mean that a single, deterministic function such as
Z(t)

behaves “as if” it were drawn from an ensemble?
Is there a natural analytic ensemble intrinsically associated with
Z(t)
,
and if such an ensemble exists, why should its local, microscopic, statistics
be expected to coincide with those of GUE?

(2)

The macroscopic question:

In what precise sense can the deterministic Hardy function
Z(t)
itself
exhibit random–matrix behavior?
If the zeros of
Z(t)
are fixed and non-random,
how can they nevertheless obey statistical, ergodic, or probabilistic laws
when viewed at the macroscopic, global, level?

For the microscopic question, we introduced in Section
3
a variational framework in which the Hardy
Z
–function is realized as a canonical section
of a high–dimensional analytic manifold of real functions, the
A–variational space
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.
Within this space, we defined the sub-domain
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
, to which we refer as the “real hall”. Each element
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}
corresponds to a deformation of the core function

Z_{0}(t)=\cos(\theta(t))
parametrized by a finite vector of coefficients
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
, preserving the real zeros of
Z_{0}(t)
within
[2N,2N+2]
.
The definition of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is largely enabled by our previous works on the approximate functional equation with exponentially decaying error
[
36
,
37
,
35
]
where the variational approach was first developed.

Theorem
A
establishes that, as
N\to\infty
,
the expected pair–correlation functional

PC_{N}(f;\bar{a})
over the variational ensemble
\mathcal{RH}_{N}

coincides with the GUE law.
Thus, the ensemble of sections in the real hall
\mathcal{RH}_{N}

provides the analytic counterpart of the random–matrix ensemble
\mathcal{H}_{N}
,
and the GUE correlation law emerges naturally as the universal equilibrium
of this variational system, thereby resolving the microscopic question. The proof of Theorem
A
is based on the construction of a
Skorokhod–type stochastic differential equation on
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
,
with reflection at the boundary,
and on showing that the induced system of SDEs governing the motion of zeros
asymptotically coincides with Dyson’s Brownian motion in the large
N
limit.

For the macroscopic question, we first note that under the Riemann Hypothesis,
the canonical section
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
corresponding to Hardy’s real
Z
–function
is itself an element of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}
, as shown in
[
35
]
.
The remaining task is therefore to justify that the local pair–correlation sequence

\{PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
associated with these special, deterministic sections

Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
exhibits the same statistical behavior as the sequence

\{PC_{N}(f;\bar{a}^{(N)})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
,
where
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})

are drawn randomly with respect to the ensemble measure
\mu_{N}
.
For such randomized sequences, Theorem
8.1
establishes that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{PC}^{\mathrm{gen}}\!\bigl(f;\{\bar{a}^{(N)}\},T\bigr)=\mathrm{GUE}(f),

(476)

showing that the ensemble average already reproduces the GUE pair–correlation law.

Using Selberg’s probabilistic theory of the argument function
S(t)
,
we demonstrated in Theorem
9.1
that the fluctuations of its derivative
S^{\prime}(t)

form a genuinely stochastic field whose covariance decays across distant intervals.
This covariance decay implies that the local pair–correlation functionals

PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})
and
PC_{N^{\prime}}(f;\bar{1})
become asymptotically independent as
|N-N^{\prime}|\to\infty
.
Hence, the effective randomness of Hardy’s function
Z(t)
arises from the Gaussian
fluctuations of its argument field, and the sequence

\{PC_{N}(f;\bar{1})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
becomes statistically indistinguishable
from a sequence

\{PC_{N}(f;\bar{a}^{(N)})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
coming from randomized sections, for the purposes of showing global GUE behaviour.

The combination of the ensemble GUE law for
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
established in Theorem
A
,
together with the macroscopic decorrelation derived from Selberg’s theory in Theorem
9.1
,
establishes the full proof of the Pair–Correlation Conjecture in Theorem
9.2
.
Our result therefore shows that the zeros of Hardy’s function
Z(t)

follow the same universal statistics as the eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices.
This universality arises because both systems are governed by the same structural principles such as
Gaussian mixing, ergodicity, self–averaging of local fluctuations,
and their realization as elements of ensembles evolving under Dyson–type Brownian motion. We conclude this work with several remarks on open problems and further directions:

Remark 10.1

(The Rudnick-Sarnak Conjecture)
.

In the automorphic setting, one considers
L
–functions
L(s,\pi)

associated with automorphic representations
\pi
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
,
whose analytic continuations and functional equations generalize those of
\zeta(s)
.
Rudnick and Sarnak
[
55
]
extended Montgomery’s framework to this general case,
showing that, under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH),
the
n
–level correlation functions of zeros of
L(s,\pi)
agree with GUE for test functions whose Fourier transform
satisfies
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{f})\subset[-1,1]
.
They further conjectured that the same GUE law should hold
without any restriction on the support of
\widehat{f}
,
for every fixed automorphic
L
–function, in the high–energy or
global regime where
t\to\infty
and averaging is taken over long intervals on the critical line.

In this global regime, the Rudnick–Sarnak conjecture may therefore be viewed as
a generalized pair–correlation conjecture for automorphic
L
–functions,
asserting that the local spacing statistics of zeros of any fixed
L(s,\pi)

coincide with those of eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices. As in Montgomery’s case, described in Remark
9.3
, their proof under
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{f})\subset[-1,1]
captures only the
“diagonal” part of the explicit formula, corresponding to the coincident prime powers
p=q
,
and leaves open the “off–diagonal” correlations
p\neq q
.
In the automorphic case these terms involve joint correlations of Hecke eigenvalues, or Satake parameters,
\alpha_{\pi}(p,j)
and
\alpha_{\pi}(q,k)
at distinct primes,
generalizing the classical Hardy–Littlewood conjecture on prime pairs.
The lack of any known control over these off–diagonal terms constitutes the same
analytic barrier that limited Montgomery’s original proof for
\zeta(s)
,
and the full GUE law for individual automorphic
L
–functions
remains conjectural for this reason.

Our methods, based on the variational structure of the Hardy function,
are naturally adaptable to the global regime of general automorphic
L
–functions.
For each such
L(s)
one may define a corresponding analytic ensemble

Z_{N}(t;\chi,\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\chi),

(477)

constructed from the truncated approximate functional equation of
L(s,\chi)

and parameterized by coefficients
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})

that play the same role as in the
\zeta
–case.
We expect that analogues of Theorems
A
–
C

hold in this broader context,
implying that the ensemble averages of the pair–correlation functionals
over
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\chi)
satisfy the GUE law.
In this way, our variational and probabilistic framework appears to naturally generalize
to extend beyond the classical zeta function,
providing a parallel route toward a proof of the
Rudnick–Sarnak generalization of Montgomery’s Pair–Correlation Conjecture,
in an analogous manner to the approach developed in this work.
The analytic mechanism underlying Theorems
A
and
9.1
,
the emergence of GUE statistics from the variational ensemble and
the decorrelation induced by Selberg-type Gaussian fluctuations,
should carry over to general automorphic
L
–functions,
suggesting a unified explanation of the expected GUE universality
throughout the automorphic spectrum as well.

Moreover, while the present work deals primarily with the pair–correlation case corresponding to Montgomery’s original conjecture, the reduction to Dyson Brownian motion suggests that the same variational–stochastic machinery should extend naturally to general
k
–level correlation functions as anticipated in the Rudnick–Sarnak framework.

Remark 10.2

(The Katz–Sarnak Philosophy)
.

Beyond the Rudnick–Sarnak analyses of individual
L
–functions, one has the far-reaching Katz–Sarnak philosophy
[
38
]
,
which concerns the collective behavior of entire families
of automorphic
L
–functions.
The central principle asserts that the local statistics of zeros near
the critical point
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
when averaged over a natural family of
L
–functions,
mirror the eigenvalue statistics of one of the three classical compact groups:
the unitary group
U(N)
, the orthogonal group
O(N)
, or
the symplectic group
USp(N)
.
The particular symmetry type is determined by the arithmetic
properties of the family, notably the sign of its functional equation.
Thus, while individual automorphic
L
–functions are expected to exhibit
GUE–type behavior at high energies, or the “global regime”,
entire families display in the low–lying region distinct
universality classes corresponding to GOE, GUE, or GSE.
In the function–field setting, this philosophy was rigorously established by Katz and Sarnak, who showed that
the zeros of
L
–functions correspond to eigenvalues of Frobenius matrices acting on étale cohomology,
and that as the size of the finite field grows, these matrices become equidistributed in the corresponding
classical compact groups, thereby proving the expected symmetry types in this setting.

In the number–field setting, this principle was formulated in the late 1990s
and supported by a series of analytic results on low–lying zeros.
Iwaniec, Luo, and Sarnak
[
32
]
proved one and two level
density theorems for families of automorphic
L
–functions
(such as Dirichlet, modular, or Maass form
L
–functions),
under the assumption that the Fourier transform of the test function
has restricted support
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{f})\subset[-1,1]
.
Their results confirmed the predicted random–matrix symmetry types
in this limited range, giving analytic evidence for the
Katz–Sarnak conjectures in number fields.
Extending these results beyond the small–support regime remains open
and is believed to require entirely new analytic input,
since the off–diagonal prime correlations that appear in this regime
are as difficult to control as in Montgomery’s original setting.

Extensive numerical computations in the number–field case,
notably by Odlyzko, Rubinstein, Miller, Dueñez, and collaborators,
have since provided striking empirical confirmation of the
Katz–Sarnak predictions, showing that families such as quadratic Dirichlet
L
–functions,
elliptic curve
L
–functions, and modular form families
exhibit spacing distributions indistinguishable from those of the
predicted random–matrix ensembles.
Nevertheless, a general proof of the Katz–Sarnak universality laws
for automorphic
L
–functions over
\mathbb{Q}
remains out of reach
and stands as one of the deepest open problems in modern analytic number theory.

In view of our present results, it is natural to ask whether the variational–probabilistic
framework developed in this work could provide an analytic realization
of the Katz–Sarnak philosophy.
For an individual automorphic representation
\pi
,
the corresponding Hardy representative
Z_{\pi}(t)

plays exactly the same analytic role as
Z(t)
in the Riemann case.
By truncating the approximate functional equation of
L(s,\pi)
,
one obtains a finite–dimensional analytic space
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\pi)
,
whose elements
Z_{N}(t;\pi,\bar{a})
serve as local sections parametrized by a finite vector
\bar{a}
.
Within a window
[2N,2N+2]
,
Brownian motion on the coefficients
\bar{a}
induces Dyson–type dynamics on the zeros,
with Dyson index
\beta=2
.
This is expected to reproduce the GUE law for the local statistics of zeros of each individual
L(s,\pi)

in the global regime
t\to\infty
, as mentioned in Remark
10.1
.

When one studies families of automorphic forms,
the situation changes qualitatively.
The functional equations of family members impose algebraic couplings between
their Hardy representatives,
typically of the form

Z_{\tilde{\pi}}(t)=\varepsilon_{\pi}\,Z_{\pi}(-t),

(478)

where
\varepsilon_{\pi}=\pm 1
is the root number.
In the variational setting, these relations are expected to translate into symmetry constraints
on the stochastic flows governing the coupled Brownian motions
on the spaces
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\pi)
.
These constraints deform the GUE dynamics (
\beta=2
)
into orthogonal (
\beta=1
) or symplectic (
\beta=4
) types,
according to the parity of
\varepsilon_{\pi}
and in families where the root numbers
\varepsilon_{\pi}
vary randomly,
the ensemble remains of unitary type (
\beta=2
).
Thus, the three Dyson ensembles of the Katz–Sarnak classification
are expected to arise as invariant submanifolds of a single underlying analytic system,
governed directly by the functional equations of the
L
–functions.

Remark 10.3

(Relation to the Hilbert–Pólya Philosophy)
.

The variational framework developed in this work bears a deep conceptual connection to the
Hilbert–Pólya philosophy, which postulates that the nontrivial zeros of
\zeta(s)

correspond to the eigenvalues of a self–adjoint operator
H
on a suitable Hilbert space,
so that the Riemann Hypothesis would follow from the spectral reality of
H
, see
[
69
]
.
In that envisioned setting, the zeros would arise as the spectrum of a Hermitian system,
and the observed GUE correlations would reflect the universal statistics of Hermitian eigenvalues.

In our approach this picture is realized analytically, but with the direction of reasoning reversed.
Instead of seeking an unknown self–adjoint operator whose spectrum reproduces the zeros,
we construct a finite–dimensional analytic manifold of real functions,
the real hall
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
,
which plays the role of the Hermitian ensemble
\mathcal{H}_{N}
in random matrix theory.
Each section
Z_{N}(t;\bar{a})\in\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})

serves as a finite–dimensional analytic representative of a matrix,
and its zeros
\{\gamma_{j}(\bar{a})\}
act as its eigenvalues.
In this sense, the restriction of
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
to a fixed interval
[2N,2N+2]

is naturally interpreted as the characteristic polynomial
of the corresponding spectral operator.
Under the Riemann Hypothesis, the canonical section
Z_{N}(t;\bar{1})
associated with
Hardy’s
Z
–function belongs to this real hall, providing the concrete realization of
the Hilbert–Pólya vision within an analytic function space.

Thus, rather than proving the Riemann Hypothesis by constructing an operator,
the present framework realizes
Z(t)
itself, locally in each window
[2N,2N+2]
,
as an analytic analogue of such an operator within
\mathcal{RH}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.
The variational geometry of this space, together with the induced Dyson–type
dynamics of its zeros, reproduces the statistical features expected from a
Hermitian spectral theory, thereby turning the Hilbert–Pólya hypothesis
from a postulate about operators into a concrete property of analytic function ensembles.
