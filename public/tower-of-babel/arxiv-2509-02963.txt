Combinatorics behind discriminants of polynomial systems
Vladislav Pokidkin

Abstract
In the 1970s, Kouchnirenko, Bernstein, and Khovanskii noticed that the geometry of a generic system of polynomial equations is determined by the geometry of its Newton polytopes. In the 1990s, Gelfand, Kapranov, Zelevinsky, and Sturmfels extended this observation to discriminants and resultants of generic polynomials. Particularly, well-known open questions about the irreducibility of discriminants and sets of solutions of such systems lead to questions about the corresponding geometric property of tuples of polytopes: Minkowski’s linear independence. To address these questions, we encode Minkowski’s linear independence into a finite matroid and characterize its bases, circuits, and cyclics. The obtained combinatorial results are used in the subsequent work to describe components of discriminants for generic square polynomial systems.

1
Minkowski matroids

1.1
Definition and rank function of Minkowski matroids

Theorem 1.1
.

(Minkowski)

A tuple of subspaces
(L_{1},...,L_{n})
is linearly independent
if and only if there is a linearly independent set of vectors
\{\upsilon_{i}\}_{i\in\overline{1,n}}

such that
\upsilon_{i}\in L_{i}
for all indexes
i
.

The proofs are valid for arbitrary fields
[
Est10
,
Kho16
]
.
For a tuple
\mathsf{n}
, denote by
\mathcal{I}
the set of all
linearly independent subtuples.

Proposition 1.2
.

\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{n})=(\mathsf{n},\mathcal{I})

is a matroid.

Proof.

It’s clear that the hereditary property holds. So, we need to check
the augmentation property. Choose two subtuples
\mathsf{k},\mathsf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}

such that
\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k}^{\prime})<\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k}).
By
Theorem
1.1
, the subtuples
are linearly independent if and only if there are linearly independent
sets of vectors
\{\upsilon_{L}\in L,\;L\in\mathsf{k}\}
and
\{\upsilon_{L^{\prime}}\in L^{\prime},\;L^{\prime}\in\mathsf{k}^{\prime}\}
.
Since
\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k}^{\prime})<\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k}),
there
is a vector
\upsilon_{\tilde{L}}\in\mathsf{k}\backslash\mathsf{k}^{\prime}

such that the set of vectors
\{\upsilon_{L^{\prime}}\in L^{\prime},\;L^{\prime}\in\mathsf{k}^{\prime}\}\cup\{\upsilon_{\tilde{L}}\}

is linearly independent. Hence, the subtuple
\mathsf{k}^{\prime}\cup\{\tilde{L}\}

is linearly independent by Theorem
1.1
,
and
\mathsf{k}^{\prime}\cup\{\tilde{L}\}\in\mathcal{I}.

∎

We call matroids from Proposition
1.2

Minkowski matroids
. In particular, similar matroids appeared
in the study of tropical resultants (Remark 2.25 in
[
JY13
]
).

Example 1.3
.

1) The Minkowski matroid of a linearly independent tuple is a Boolean
algebra.

2) Every representable matroid is a Minkowski matroid. Use a representation
to generate a tuple of one-dimensional subspaces and to get the correspondence.

Proposition 1.4
.

The rank function
for a Minkowski matroid
(\mathsf{n},\mathcal{I})
equals

rk(\mathsf{k})=max\{\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{h})\,|\;\mathsf{h}\text{ is a linearly independent subtuple of }\mathsf{k}\},

where
\mathsf{k}\subseteq\mathsf{n}
.

Proof.

The rank function for a Minkowski matroid on a subtuple
\mathsf{k}

coincides with a maximal chain of linearly independent vectors
\{\upsilon_{L}\in L,\;L\in\mathsf{h}\}

for some subtuple
\mathsf{h}\subseteq\mathsf{k}
. Since each vector

\upsilon_{L}
lies in its own subspace
L
, the cardinality of

\mathsf{h}
equals the rank of
\mathsf{k}
.
∎

Example 1.5
.

Consider the tuple
\mathsf{n}=(L_{1},L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2})

such that
L_{1}=L_{1}^{\prime}\subset L_{2},

dim\,L_{1}=1,

dim\,L_{2}=2
.
Then,
rk(\mathsf{n})=2,
because we can choose no more than two
linearly independent vectors
e_{1}\in L_{1}
and
e_{2}\in L_{2}

from two different subspaces
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
(or
L_{1}^{\prime}
and

L_{2}
).

Remark 1.6
.

1) A tuple is linearly independent if and only if it satisfies the
Hall-Rado condition
[
Mir71
]
:
rk(\mathsf{k})\geq\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k})

for any subtuple
\mathsf{k}
.

2) Matroids provide optimal solutions of the greedy algorithm

]
.
By Proposition
1.2
, we can apply
matroid algorithms in the study of polynomial systems.

1.2
Quotient tuples and contractions of Minkowski matroids

Definition 1.7
.

For tuples of vector subspaces
\mathsf{n}
and
\mathscr{\mathsf{k}}

from a vector space
V,
the
quotient tuple
is defined by

\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k}=\pi(\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{k})
for the
projection
\pi:V\rightarrow V/\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle
.

Lemma 1.8
.

1) Defect of the quotient tuple equals

\delta(\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k})=\delta(\mathsf{n}\cup\mathsf{k})-\delta(\mathsf{k}).

2) If tuples
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{n}\cup\mathsf{k}
are zero-defect,
then the quotient
\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k}
has zero defect.

Proof.

1) Direct calculations:

\displaystyle dim_{V/\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle}\,\langle\pi(\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=dim_{V}\,\pi^{-1}\langle\pi(\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{k})\rangle-dim_{V}\,Ker\,\pi=dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\cup\mathsf{k}\rangle-dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle,

\displaystyle\delta(\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k})

\displaystyle=dim_{V/\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle}\,\langle\pi(\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{k})\rangle-\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k})=\delta(\mathsf{n}\cup\mathsf{k})-\delta(\mathsf{k}).

2) Use the first statement:
\delta(\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k})=\delta(\mathsf{n}\cup\mathsf{k})-\delta(\mathsf{k})=0.

∎

Example 1.9
.

1) Choose the tuple
\mathsf{n}
from Example
1.5
.
For the subtuple
\mathsf{k}=(L_{1},L_{2})
, the quotient
\mathsf{k}/(L_{1}^{\prime})=(0,L_{2}/L_{1})

can be computed in two different ways:

\displaystyle\text{by definition: }\delta(\mathsf{k}/(L_{1}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\delta((0,L_{2}/L_{1}))=dim\,\text{$\langle(0,L_{2}/L_{1})\rangle-2=-1$},

\displaystyle\text{by Lemma \ref{Lemma. Quotient tuples}: }\delta(\mathsf{k}/(L_{1}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\delta(\mathsf{n})-\delta((L_{1}^{\prime}))=(dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle-3)-(dim\,L_{1}^{\prime}-1)=-1.

2) Notice that
\delta(\mathsf{k})=\delta((L_{1}))=0
. Then
\delta((L_{2}/L_{1}))=\delta(\mathsf{k}/(L_{1}))=\delta(\mathsf{k})-\delta((L_{1}))=0.

Lemma 1.10
.

1) For tuples
\mathsf{k}\subset\mathsf{n},

the following holds:
\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle/\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle=\langle\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k}\rangle
.

2) For tuples
\mathsf{h}\subset\mathsf{k}\subset\mathsf{n}
, the
quotients are equal:
\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k}=\frac{\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{h}}{\mathsf{k}/\mathsf{h}}.

Definition 1.11
.

The
contraction
of a matroid
(E,\mathcal{I})
by a linearly
independent set
I\in\mathcal{I}
is the matroid
(E\backslash I,\{J\backslash I\,|\;I\subseteq J\in\mathcal{I}\}).

Proposition 1.12
.

For
a tuple
\mathsf{n}
and its BK-subtuple
\mathsf{k}
, the Minkowski
matroid of the quotient tuple
\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k})

equals the contraction of
\mathsf{k}
in the matroid
\mathsf{M}(\mathsf{n})
.

Proof.

Consider a subtuple
\mathsf{h}\subseteq\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{k}

such that the quotient
\mathsf{h}/\mathsf{k}
is an independent
subtuple in the tuple
\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k}
. It’s enough to show
that the union
\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}
is an independent tuple
of
\mathsf{n}
. Since the tuples
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{h}/\mathsf{k}

are linearly independent, there are linearly independent sets of vectors

\{\upsilon_{N}\in N,\;N\in\mathsf{k}\}
and
\{\upsilon^{\prime}_{L}\in L,\;L\in\mathsf{h}/\mathsf{k}\}
.
Choose arbitrary vectors
\{\upsilon{}_{L}\in L,\;L\in\mathsf{h}\backslash\mathsf{k}\}

such that
\upsilon_{L}/\mathsf{k}=\upsilon^{\prime}_{L}
. Then the set of
vectors
\{\upsilon_{N}\in N,\;N\in\mathsf{k}\}\cup\{\upsilon{}_{L}\in L,\;L\in\mathsf{h}\backslash\mathsf{k}\}

is linearly independent, the union subtuple
\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}

is linearly independent, and the Minkowski matroid for the quotient

\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{k}
is the contraction of the subtuple
\mathsf{k}

in the Minkowski matroid of
\mathsf{n}
.
∎

Remark 1.13
.

For a polynomial system with a fixed tuple of Newton polytopes, the

subsystem led to the notion of the quotient tuple.

1.3
Defects of circuits for Minkowski matroids

We start with one lemma about essential tuples. Sturmfels introduced
essential tuples in the study of resultants
[
Stu94
]
.
Examples of essential tuples are irreducible tuples, circuits, and
minimal by inclusion subtuples with minimal defect.

Lemma 1.14
.

For
any subspace
L
from an essential tuple
\mathsf{n}
, it holds:

L\subseteq\langle\mathsf{n}\backslash L\rangle
.

Proof.

If the dimension
dim\,L/\langle\mathsf{n}\backslash L\rangle>0

is positive, then
dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle>dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\backslash L\rangle
.
However, it means that the defect of the subtuple
\mathsf{n}\backslash L

does not exceed the defect of
\mathsf{n}
, and the tuple
\mathsf{n}

can not be essential by definition.
∎

Lemma 1.15
.

Circuits of a Minkowski
matroid have negative defect.

Proof.

For a circuit, every proper subtuple is linearly independent and has
a non-negative defect. By definition, linearly dependent tuples contain
a subtuple with a negative defect. Hence, there is only one option
for circuits: the defect of the whole circuit is negative.
∎

Lemma 1.16
.

For an element
L
of a circuit

\mathsf{c}
, the subtuple
\mathsf{c}\backslash L
is a BK-tuple.

Proof.

By definition of a circuit, the subtuple
\mathsf{c}\backslash L

is linearly independent. We need to show that the tuple
\mathsf{c}\backslash L

has zero defect. Lemma
1.14

implies that the dimensions of tuples
\mathsf{c}
and
\mathsf{c}\backslash L

are equal. By Lemma
1.15
, the
dimension of the tuple
\mathsf{c}
is less than the cardinality

\mathfrak{c(\mathsf{c}})
. Since the tuple
\mathsf{c}\backslash L

is linearly independent, the dimension of
\mathsf{c}\backslash L

is no less than
\mathfrak{c(\mathsf{c}})-1
. Therefore, the dimension
of the tuple
\mathsf{c}\backslash L
equals
\mathfrak{c(\mathsf{c}})-1
,
and the tuple
\mathsf{c}\backslash L
is BK.
∎

Theorem 1.17
.

Circuits
of a Minkowski matroid have the defect
-1
.

Proof.

\delta(\mathsf{c})=dim\,\langle\mathsf{c}\rangle-\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{c})\overset{\text{Lemma }\ref{Lemma. Inclusion of an element into the complement for essential tuples}}{=}dim\,\langle\mathsf{c}\backslash N\rangle-\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{c})\overset{\text{Lemma }\ref{Lemma. c without L is BK}}{=}(\mathfrak{c(\mathsf{c}})-1)-\mathfrak{c(\mathsf{c}})=-1.

∎

Example 1.18
.

Choose the tuple
\mathsf{n}
from Example
1.5
.
The subtuple
(L_{1},L_{1}^{\prime})
is a circuit, and it has the defect

\delta((L_{1},L_{1}^{\prime}))=dim\,\langle(L_{1},L_{1}^{\prime})\rangle-2=-1.

Remark 1.19
.

1) Notice that loops in Minkowski matroids have the defect
-1
as
well.

2) For a linearly dependent tuple of Newton polytopes, Theorem
1.17

is applied in
[

]
to show that the mixed

1.4
Defects of bases for Minkowski matroids

Proposition 1.20
.

[
Oxl11
]

Let
\mathsf{k}
be an independent set in a matroid, and let
L

be an element such that the union
\mathsf{k}\cup L
is dependent.
Then there is a unique circuit contained in the set
\mathsf{k}\cup L
,
and this circuit contains
L
.

Lemma 1.21
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be an independent
set in a Minkowski matroid, and let
L
be an element such that the
union
\mathsf{k}\cup L
is dependent. Then the dimensions of tuples

\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{k}\cup L
are equal.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.20
, there is a circuit

\mathsf{c}\subseteq\mathsf{k}\cup L
, and this circuit contains

L
. Lemma
1.14

implies
dim\,\langle\mathsf{c}\rangle=dim\,\langle\mathsf{c}\backslash L\rangle
.
Hence, the dimensions are equal:
dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle=dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\cup L\rangle.

∎

Recall the basis-exchange property for matroids: if bases
\mathsf{b}

and
\mathsf{b}^{\prime}
are distinct, and
L\in\mathsf{b}\backslash\mathsf{b}^{\prime}
,
then there is an element
L^{\prime}\in\mathsf{b}^{\prime}\backslash\mathsf{b}
such
that
(\mathsf{b}\cup L^{\prime})\backslash L
is a basis.

Lemma 1.22
.

Basis change does not change the
defect:
\delta((\mathsf{b}\cup L^{\prime})\backslash L)=\delta(\mathsf{b})
.

Proof.

Use Lemma
1.21
twice:
\delta((\mathsf{b}\cup L^{\prime})\backslash L)=dim\,\langle(\mathsf{b}\cup L^{\prime})\backslash L\rangle-\mathfrak{c}((\mathsf{b}\cup L^{\prime})\backslash L)=

=dim\,\langle\mathsf{b}\cup L^{\prime}\rangle-\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{b})=dim\,\langle\mathsf{b}\rangle-\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{b})=\delta(\mathsf{b}).

∎

Lemma 1.23
.

For
a basis subtuple
\mathsf{b}
from a tuple
\mathsf{n}
, it holds:

\langle\mathsf{b}\rangle=\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle
.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose there is a strict inclusion
\langle\mathsf{b}\rangle\subset\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle
.
It means the quotient tuple
\mathsf{n}/\mathsf{b}
contains a subspace

L^{\prime}
of positive dimension. The subspace
L^{\prime}
has a preimage subspace

L
from the complement subtuple
\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{b}

such that
L^{\prime}=L/\mathsf{b}
. Then the tuple
\mathsf{b}\cup L
is
linearly independent, and the tuple
\mathsf{b}
can not be a basis.
∎

Theorem 1.24
.

Bases
of a Minkowski matroid have the same non-negative defect.

Proof.

№1. By Lemma
1.22
, the basis exchange
property preserves defect. Since all bases are linked with each other
by a sequence of basis changes, it means that all bases have the same
defect. The defect is non-negative, because bases are linearly independent.
∎

Proof.

№2. By Proposition
1.4
,
the cardinality of a basis is the Minkowski rank of the tuple. By
Lemma
1.23
,
the dimension of the linear span of a basis is the dimension of the
linear span of the tuple. Hence, the defects of bases are determined
by the tuple and are the same.
∎

Example 1.25
.

The tuple
\mathsf{n}
from Example
1.5
has two
bases
(L_{1},L_{2})
and
(L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2})
of zero defect. Hence,
bases are BK-tuples. Also, the Minkowski rank equals the dimension:

rk(\mathsf{n})=dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle=2.
Notice that the
element

is a coloop.

Corollary 1.26
.

The Minkowski
rank of a linearly dependent tuple equals the dimension if and only
if bases are BK-tuples.

Proof.

For a tuple
\mathsf{n}
, all bases have the same defect by Theorem

1.24
.
Choose a basis
\mathsf{b}
.

\boxed{\Rightarrow}

\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{b})\overset{\text{Proposition }\ref{Proposition. Rank of Minkowski matroids}}{=}rk(\mathsf{n})=dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle\overset{\text{Lemma }\ref{Lemma. The linear span of a basis subtuple equals the linear span of the tuple}}{=}dim\,\langle\mathsf{b}\rangle.

\boxed{\Leftarrow}

rk(\mathsf{n})\overset{\text{Proposition }\ref{Proposition. Rank of Minkowski matroids}}{=}\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{b})\overset{\mathsf{b}\text{ - BK}}{=}dim\,\langle\mathsf{b}\rangle\overset{\text{Lemma }\ref{Lemma. The linear span of a basis subtuple equals the linear span of the tuple}}{=}dim\,\langle\mathsf{n}\rangle.

∎

Remark 1.27
.

For a tuple of Newton polytopes and the generic polynomial system
with a non-empty set of solutions, the defect of Minkowski bases equals
the expected dimension of the set of solutions for the system.

1.5
The unique BK-subtuple in a basis tuple

Lemma 1.28
.

In
a linearly independent tuple, the union and intersection of BK-subtuples
are BK-subtuples.

Proof.

For BK-subtuples
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{h}
, the defects are
positive,
\delta(\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h})\geq 0
and
\delta(\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h})\geq 0
,
by definition of a linearly independent tuple. At the same time, the
following holds:

\displaystyle dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle+dim\,\langle\mathsf{h}\rangle-dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle\cap\langle\mathsf{h}\rangle\overset{\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h}\text{ - BK}}{\leq}\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k})+\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{h})-dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h}\rangle\overset{\delta(\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h})\geq 0}{\leq}\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}).

Hence, the subtuple
\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}
has zero defect. Similarly,
we have
\delta(\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h})\geq 0,
and

dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h}\rangle\leq dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\rangle+dim\,\langle\mathsf{h}\rangle-dim\,\langle\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}\rangle\overset{\mathsf{k},\,\mathsf{h},\,\mathsf{k}\cup\mathsf{h}\text{ - BK}}{=}\mathfrak{c}(\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h}).

Therefore, the subtuple
\mathsf{k}\cap\mathsf{h}
is BK.
∎

Lemma 1.29
.

Each basis subtuple
of a linearly dependent tuple contains a BK-subtuple.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{n}
be a linearly dependent tuple and
\mathsf{b}

be its basis subtuple. For every element
L\in\mathsf{n}\backslash\mathsf{b}
,

2
Minkowski cyclic subtuples

In a matroid,
flats
are maximal subsets among sets of its
rank. Bonin and Mier showed that the set of cyclic flats forms an
arbitrary lattice
[
BM08
]
. The join of cyclic flats
is the closure of the union, and the meet of cyclic flats is the maximal
cyclic flat inside the intersection. The lattice of cyclic flats can
be an alternative cryptomorphic definition for matroids.

This section characterizes cyclic subtuples in Minkowski matroids.
Also, we show that cyclic subtuples coincide with essential subtuples.
In particular, it means Minkowski cyclic flats have minimal defects
among sets of the same rank.

3
BK-tuples

For a BK-tuple of Newton polytopes, the Kouchnirenko-Bernstein theorem
guarantees a non-empty and finite number of solutions for the generic
polynomial system. It explains our choice of the prefix BK.

3.1
Distributive lattice of BK-subtuples

A reducible BK-tuple contains a proper BK-subtuple by definition.
If the BK-subtuple is reducible, we repeat the process until we choose
an irreducible BK-subtuple. Hence, every reducible BK-tuple contains
some set of irreducible BK-subtuples.

Proposition 3.1
.

For a linearly
independent tuple, BK-subtuples form a distributive lattice.

Proof.

By Lemma
1.28
,
the union and intersection of BK-subtuples are BK-subtuples. Hence,
BK-subtuples form an order lattice by inclusion. This lattice is distributive
since the union and intersection satisfy the distributive law.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Edelman established that distributive lattices are antimatroids
[
Ede80
]
.
It means that every basis of a Minkowski matroid is equipped with
the antimatroid structure.

Definition 3.3
.

An
order ideal
of a poset
P
is a subposet
I
of
P

such that if
\beta\in I
and
\alpha\leq\beta
, then
\alpha\in I
.
For an element
\beta
from a poset, the
principal order
ideal
(\beta)
is the order ideal of all elements that are not
greater than
\beta.

In 1937, Birkhoff proved
[
Bir37
]
that every finite
distributive lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of order ideals
of some poset
P.
Hence, every BK-tuple
\mathsf{n}
corresponds
to some poset
P_{\mathsf{n}}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For any finite poset
P,

there is a BK-tuple
\mathsf{n}
such that
P_{\mathsf{n}}=P.

Proof.

Let’s build a tuple
\mathsf{n}
such that its poset
P_{\mathscr{\mathsf{n}}}

equals the poset
P
over
n
elements.

The incidence algebra
I(P)
(see
[
Sta11
]
)
is isomorphic to the subalgebra of all upper matrices
L=\{(m_{ij})\in Mat_{n,n}\,|\;m_{ij}=0

if
i\nleq j,\;i,j\in P\}.
The algebra of upper matrices admits
the decomposition on columns as a vector space
L=\underset{j\in P}{\oplus}L_{j},

where
L_{j}=\langle m_{ij}\,|\;i\leq j\rangle.
By the construction,
every order ideal
I\subseteq P
corresponds to the BK-subtuple
\mathsf{k}_{I}=(L_{i},\,i\in I)
,
and vice versa. Hence, the lattice of order ideals of

is isomorphic
to the lattice of BK-subtuples for the built BK-tuple
\mathsf{n}
,
and the posets coincide:
P_{\mathsf{n}}=P.

∎

Proposition 3.8

In

4
Realizable polymatroids

Minkowski matroids are closely related to polymatroids, introduced
by Edmond in 1970. Notice that Minkowski matroids differ from natural
matroids of polymatroids
[
BCF23
]
.

A
polymatroid

P
is a pair
(E,rk_{P})
of a finite set

E
and a rank function
rk_{P}:2^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
which is submodular, monotone, and normalized (
rk_{P}(\varnothing)=0
).

A
flat
of a polymatroid
P
is a subset
F\subseteq E

that is maximal among sets of its rank. The set of all flats forms
a lattice
\mathcal{L}_{P}
by inclusion. The intersection of flats
is a flat. The
closure

cl_{P}(I)
of a set
I
is the
intersection of all flats containing
I
.

The
defect
of a set
I\subseteq E
is the number
\delta(I)=rk_{P}(I)-|I|
.

A polymatroid
P
is
realizable
over a field
\mathbb{K}

if there is a
realization
via a tuple of vector subspaces

(L_{1},...,L_{n})
over the field
\mathbb{K}
and the rank function

rk_{P}(I)=dim\,L_{I}
,
L_{I}=\underset{i\in I}{\sum}L_{i}
.

4.1
Dual realization of a polymatroid

Let
V^{\lor}=\mathrm{Hom}(V,\mathbb{K})
be the dual space for a
finite-dimensional vector space
V
over a field
\mathbb{K}
. For
a set
S
from the space
V
, its
orthogonal complement

is called the dual subspace
S^{\perp}
of all linear functions,
taking zero value on each element of
S
.

A subspace tuple
(L_{1},...,L_{n})
from the vector space
V
represents
a polymatroid
P
with the rank function
rk_{P}(I)=dim\,L_{I}
.
We can build the orthogonal configuration
(L_{1}^{\perp},...,L_{n}^{\perp})

in the dual space
V^{\lor}
, choose the rank function
rk_{P^{\perp}}(I)=codim\,L_{I}^{\perp},

L_{I}^{\perp}=\underset{i\in I}{\cap}L_{i}^{\perp}
, and get a polymatroid

P^{\perp}.
Sometimes its lattice of flats is called the
intersection
lattice
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The polymatroids
P
and
P^{\perp}
are the same.

For a realizable polymatroid
P,
the pair of a tuple of vector subspaces

(L_{1}^{\perp},...,L_{n}^{\perp})
and the codimension rank function
is the
dual realization
. The sign
\perp
indicates that
we consider the dual realization. Since we have only one polymatroid

P=P^{\perp}
and two realizations, the lattices of flats are the
same,
\mathcal{L}_{P}=\mathcal{L}_{P^{\perp}}=\mathcal{L}
.

Notice that for the inclusion of flats
F^{\prime}\subset F
, we have the
same inclusion for the corresponding subspaces
L_{F^{\prime}}\subset L_{F}

in the space
V
, and the inverse inclusion
L_{F^{\prime}}^{\perp}\supset L_{F}^{\perp}

for the dual realization in the dual space
V^{\vee}
.

4.2
Polymatroid partition of the dual vector space

For a realizable polymatroid
P
, consider a dual realization
(L_{1}^{\perp},...,L_{n}^{\perp})

in a dual space
V^{\vee}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For a point
x\in V^{\lor},

the set
I=\{i\in[n]\,|\;L_{i}^{\perp}\ni x\}
is flat.

Proof.

Notice that
x\in L_{I}^{\perp}=\underset{i\in I}{\cap}L_{i}^{\perp}
.
By contradiction, if the set
I
is not flat, there is an element

j\in F\backslash I,
where
F=cl(I).
Then, the point
x
doesn’t
lie in the subspaces
x\notin L_{j}^{\perp}
and
x\notin L_{F}^{\perp}
.
We get the contradiction since
L_{I}^{\perp}\neq L_{F}^{\perp}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

For
a tuple of subspaces
(L_{1}^{\perp},...,L_{n}^{\perp})
from a vector
space
V^{\lor}
over an infinite field, there is a partition of
the space
V^{\lor}
on constructible sets
B_{F}
, enumerated by
the lattice of flats
\mathcal{L}
of the polymatroid
P^{\perp}
,

V^{\lor}=\underset{F\in\mathcal{L}}{\sqcup}B_{F}
.

Proof.

Since every point of the dual space
V^{\vee}
corresponds to a unique
flat in the lattice of flats
\mathcal{L}
of the polymatroid
P
,
Lemma
4.2
provides a set-theoretic
map
\gamma:V^{\vee}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
. This map defines a partition
of the dual space via the disjoint union
V^{\lor}=\underset{F\in\mathcal{L}}{\sqcup}B_{F},

where
B_{F}=\gamma^{-1}(F)
for a flat
F
. Notice that every set

B_{F}
is a constructible set:
B_{F}=L_{F}^{\perp}\backslash\underset{F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\backslash(F)}{\cup}L_{F^{\prime}}^{\perp},

where
(F)
is the principal order ideal in the lattice of flats

\mathcal{L}
. If the field is finite, some sets
B_{F}
can be
empty. However, for an infinite field, the map
\gamma
defines a
partition of the dual space.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The polymatroid partition of the dual vector space was motivated by
the Esterov conjecture about irreducibility of discriminants
[
Est19
,
Pok25a
]
.
The conjecture is crucial for the general description of discriminants
for BK-tuples of Newton polytopes
[
Pok25
]
.

4.3
Polymatroids with a distributive lattice of flats

Proposition 4.5
.

(Polishchuk,
Positseleski,
[
PP05
]
)
For a tuple of subspaces
from a vector space
V
, the following are equivalent:

a) the lattice of polymatroid flats is distributive;

b) there is a direct sum decomposition
V=\underset{\alpha\in I}{\oplus}V_{\alpha}

of the vector space
V
such that each subspace from the tuple is
a sum of subspaces
V_{\alpha}
;

c) there exists a basis of the vector space
V
such that each subspace
from the tuple is generated by some set of basis vectors.

Definition 4.6
.

A
BK-polymatroid
is a polymatroid with a distributive lattice
of flats.

BK-polymatroids are automatically realizable, and their lattices of
flats can be arbitrary finite distributive lattices by Proposition

4.8
.

Corollary 4.7
.

BK-polymatroids admit realizations via tuples of coordinate subspaces.

Proposition 4.8
.

Every
BK-tuple corresponds to a BK-polymatroid.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.12
,
every BK-tuple admits a unique partition into subtuples, encoded by
a poset
P
. The tuple of subspaces
(\langle\mathsf{k}_{(\alpha)}\rangle,\;\alpha\in P)

has the distributive lattice of flats by the construction and Proposition

3.1
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

For a BK-tuple
of subspaces, it is possible to choose a basis in the ambient vector
space such that the linear span of every BK-subtuple is a coordinate
subspace.

Remark 4.10
.

For a BK-tuple of Newton polytopes, it is possible to choose coordinates
such that every BK-subtuple of Newton polytopes corresponds to a square
polynomial subsystem.
