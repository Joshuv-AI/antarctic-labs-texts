The Role of c-Abl in Alzheimer’s Disease: Guilty or not Guilty?
Authors: Wei Li, Xianning Wang, Jiannan Liu, Yurou Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder. Extracellular senile plaques composed of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) containing the hyperphosphorylated tau protein, excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and neuroinflammation are crucial contributing factors to the pathological mechanisms of AD. The nonreceptor tyrosine kinase c-Abl plays a complex dual role in AD through the regulation of signaling pathways such as oxidative stress, DNA repair, and apoptosis. c-Abl mitigates early neuronal damage by activating antioxidant enzymes and potentially promoting homologous recombination (HR) repair. However, its aberrant activation is associated with Aβ plaque formation, tau phosphorylation, neuronal cell death, and synaptic dysfunction. Its synergistic interaction with Aβ and tau exacerbates the neurodegenerative pathology. This article provides a systematic review of the molecular mechanisms of c-Abl in AD, including its dual role in oxidative stress, synergistic regulation of neuronal function with Aβ and the tau protein, involvement in the maintenance of genomic stability, and potential as a therapeutic target.Graphical abstractDual roles of c-Abl in Alzheimer’s disease. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive age-related neurodegenerative disorder characterized by gradual memory loss and cognitive decline (Qiu et al. 2009; Rossor 1993). The typical pathological features of the AD brain include senile plaques formed by the deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ), neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) caused by the hyperphosphorylation of the tau protein, inflammation, neuronal dysfunction, and brain atrophy (Merlo et al. 2021; Reitz and Mayeux 2014; Rojo et al. 2008; Morishima-Kawashima and Ihara 2002). The main theories suggest that Aβ plaque deposition and tau protein hyperphosphorylation in neurons are the primary mechanisms contributing to AD pathogenesis(Liu et al. 2019).

Mammalian nonreceptor tyrosine kinase c-Abl is a cellular homolog of the v-abl oncogene of the Abelson murine leukemia virus (Ozanne et al. 1982). It is widely expressed in various mammalian tissue cells and has been shown to regulate the cell cycle, proliferation, adhesion, migration, apoptosis, cytoskeletal reorganization, and responses to oxidative stress and DNA damage(Hantschel and Superti-Furga 2004; Pendergast 2002; Whang et al. 2000; Colicelli 2010; Meltser et al. 2011; Wang 2014). Under physiological conditions, c-Abl kinase activity remains at a low level and shuttles between the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments, performing distinct functions to maintain normal cellular homeostasis(Taagepera et al. 1998). However, in AD, both the expression and kinase activity of c-Abl are abnormally elevated, mainly because of oxidative stress and abnormal Aβ deposition(Alvarez et al. 2004). c-Abl not only participates in Aβ oligomer (AβO)-induced synaptic toxicity but also exacerbates neurodegeneration through multiple mechanisms, including Aβ generation, tau protein phosphorylation, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dynamics dysregulation, and epigenetic silencing(Vargas et al. 2014; Gutierrez et al. 2019; Mannan et al. 2025; Derkinderen et al. 2005; Schlatterer et al. 2011; Zhou et al. 2017; Gonzalez-Zuniga et al. 2014; Li et al. 2025). However, some reports suggest that Aβ plaque deposition and tau phosphorylation in AD patients may not be the cause of AD pathogenesis but rather a consequence of oxidative stress, which serves as a primary line of antioxidant defense(Castellani et al. 2006; Morris et al. 2018; Cuajungco et al. 2000). Oxidative stress is among the earliest events in AD and plays a crucial regulatory role in its onset, progression, and pathogenesis(Perry et al. 2002; Dumont et al. 2009). As a key regulator of intracellular oxidative stress levels, c-Abl plays a dual role. It can mitigate early neuronal damage by activating antioxidant enzymes and potentially enhancing homologous recombination (HR) repair(Cao et al. 2003a, b; Shimizu et al. 2009), and can promote the ubiquitination and degradation of antioxidant enzymes as well as the pro-apoptotic effects of p73 and p53, thus accelerating neuronal apoptosis(Cao et al. 2003d; Cancino et al. 2008; Lee et al. 2008). Here, we review the role of c-Abl in Aβ plaques, tau phosphorylation, oxidative stress, and HR repair to provide a thorough understanding of its role in AD (Fig. 1).

c-Abl, Aβ, and Tau Phosphorylation

Aβ is a peptide resulting from the sequential cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β-secretase (β-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1, BACE1) and γ-secretase(Esch et al. 1990; DeTure and Dickson 2019; Flammang et al. 2012; Vassar et al. 1999). Although the variability of γ-secretase cleavage generates Aβ peptides of varying lengths, Aβ40 and Aβ42 are the most abundant, with Aβ42 demonstrating significantly greater neurotoxicity than Aβ40 does(Masters et al. 2015; Findeis 2007). In regard to the pathological mechanisms of AD, the Aβ cascade hypothesis has been the leading theory for an extended period(Hardy and Higgins 1992). According to this theory, the disrupted balance between Aβ production and clearance is the initiating factor of AD pathogenesis, triggering a cascade of events, including tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal death(Hardy and Higgins 1992; Hardy and Selkoe 2002; Hampel et al. 2021).

A large body of research has demonstrated that in AD, c-Abl kinase activity is elevated and may affect the pathological progression of the disease through synergistic toxicity with Aβ and phosphorylation of tau (Reichenstein et al. 2021; Cancino et al. 2008; Lebouvier et al. 2009). AβOs colocalize with the neuron erythropoietin-producing hepatocellular A4 (EphA4) receptor tyrosine kinase, inducing EphA4 phosphorylation (activation)(Vargas et al. 2014; Fu et al. 2014). EphA4 induces c-Abl activation through phosphorylation at the Y412 site(Vargas et al. 2014, 2018). Activated c-Abl promotes actin cytoskeletal remodeling and the loss of dendritic spines(Jones et al. 2004; Gutierrez et al. 2023). It can also phosphorylate its downstream effector histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) at the Y222 site(Gonzalez-Zuniga et al. 2014). HDAC2 is an epigenetic regulator that suppresses gene expression through histone deacetylation; its overexpression impairs synaptic plasticity and cognitive function(Guan et al. 2009; Jin et al. 2023). This phosphorylation enhances protein stability and transcriptional repression activity, leading to suppressed synaptic gene expression(Gonzalez-Zuniga et al. 2014). Alternatively, c-Abl may directly phosphorylate p73 at Y99, p63 at Y149, and Yap1 at Y357, thus activating their transcriptional activity and promoting neuronal apoptosis(Codelia et al. 2010; Gonfloni et al. 2009; Jacobs et al. 2004; Levy et al. 2008).

c-Abl is involved in the regulation of Aβ production. On the one hand, c-Abl may directly phosphorylate APP at the Y682 site, which enhances the interaction between APP and BACE1, an enzyme that catalyzes the proteolytic processing of APP and promotes the processing of APP via the amyloidogenic pathway, leading to increased Aβ production(Zambrano et al. 2001; Perkinton et al. 2004; Bazzari and Bazzari 2022). In AD transgenic mouse (e.g., APPsw/PSEN1ΔE9 or Tg2576) models, treatment with c-Abl inhibitors (such as imatinib, nilotinib, or neurotinib) reduces Aβ production and deposition and ameliorates cognitive dysfunction(Barbera et al. 2021; Estrada et al. 2016; Cancino et al. 2008; Leon et al. 2023). This effect is partially attributed to decreased tyrosine phosphorylation of APP upon c-Abl inhibition, which might shift APP processing toward the nonamyloidogenic pathway(Haass et al. 1992). On the other hand, c-Abl indirectly regulates the production of Aβ through hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF-1α). HIF-1α is the active subunit of HIF-1, and its protein stability is tightly regulated by oxygen concentration(Jaakkola et al. 2001; Wenger and Gassmann 1997; Chun et al. 2002). HIF-1α and Aβ mutually regulate each other in a feedback loop. Aβ directly induces HIF-1α DNA binding, and HIF-1α is more highly expressed in AD transgenic mice than in age-matched controls(Soucek et al. 2003). In turn, HIF-1α transcriptionally upregulates BACE1 and nontranscriptionally activates γ-secretase for Aβ production(Sil et al. 2020, 2021; Alexander et al. 2022). Moreover, c-Abl kinase enhances HIF-1α protein stability by competitively inhibiting E3 ligase cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor-1, thus preventing HIF-1α degradation(Mayro et al. 2023) and indirectly promoting Aβ accumulation.

Another important pathway through which c-Abl contributes to the pathology of AD involves promoting the excessive phosphorylation of the tau protein and the formation of NFTs(Derkinderen et al. 2005; Choudhury et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2007). Tau is a microtubule-associated protein that maintains microtubule assembly and integrity under normal physiological conditions(Frappier et al. 1994; El Mammeri et al. 2022). In AD, tau is abnormally phosphorylated, which causes it to lose its microtubule binding ability, form paired helical filaments and eventually aggregate into NFTs(Cowan et al. 2010; Lebouvier et al. 2009; Brotzakis et al. 2021; Alonso Adel et al. 2006; Acosta et al. 2022). c-Abl directly phosphorylates the Y394 residue of tau(Derkinderen et al. 2005; Tremblay et al. 2010). Additionally, it phosphorylates CDK5 at Y15 and promotes tau phosphorylation in AD transgenic mice(Cancino et al. 2011). Alternatively, c-Abl may phosphorylate GSK-3β at Y216(Ren et al. 2018), thus activating GSK-3β, which subsequently phosphorylates tau(Lauretti et al. 2020). These phosphorylation events not only impair the microtubule binding ability of tau but also increase its aggregation propensity(Lovestone et al. 1994; Rekha et al. 2025; Rankin et al. 2007). Notably, c-Abl inhibitors such as STI571 have been shown to reduce tau phosphorylation and aggregation, thus improving synaptic function and cognitive performance in APPsw/PSEN1ΔE9 mouse models of AD (Cancino et al. 2008, 2011). These findings further highlight the pivotal role of c-Abl in tau pathology.

Although numerous experimental studies have indicated that Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation are associated with the pathology of AD, a substantial body of research suggests that Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation may represent a manifestation of the body’s antioxidant response(Castellani et al. 2006; Cuajungco et al. 2000; Smith et al. 2002a; Surguchov et al. 2023). Increased levels of Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation are correlated with reduced oxidative damage in organisms. For instance, (a), both in vitro and in vivo experiments have demonstrated that neurons increase the production of Aβ protein in response to oxidative stress and that this increase in Aβ deposition is associated with the subsequent alleviation of oxidative stress(Nunomura et al. 2001); (b), most neuronal loss in AD occurs prior to NFT deposition (Gomez-Isla et al. 1997; Kril et al. 2002). During this period, neuronal loss is primarily caused by high levels of oxidative stress, whereas subsequent NFT deposition actually reduces oxidative stress levels. Although oxidative stress affects tau protein expression, leading to its aggregation and the formation of NFTs, this process enables these neurons to survive for decades(Smith et al. 2002b). Additionally, a significant proportion of cognitively intact elderly individuals exhibit Aβ burdens comparable to those of AD patients(Chetelat et al. 2013; Morris et al. 2018). On the basis of these findings, many scholars argue that Aβ is not the driving factor of pathological progression but rather a product of pathological oxidative processes with antioxidant functions. In normal aging, the production and deposition of Aβ protein successfully counteract age-related redox imbalance. However, in AD patients with severe chronic redox imbalance, even high levels of Aβ protein are insufficient to maintain homeostasis. Research has also revealed that nilotinib, a c-Abl kinase inhibitor, does not exhibit neuroprotective effects in human neuronal cell (SH-SY5Y) models. It fails to significantly alter APP protein expression or modify the expression of genes involved in amyloid processing and mitochondrial function(Srivastava et al. 2024). Accumulating evidence points to a neuroprotective role of APP, as APP itself or its soluble extracellular fragment, APPsα, can promote neuronal survival(Hefter and Draguhn 2017). On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that c-Abl should not be simplistically characterized as the primary culprit or central driver of AD. If the hypothesis that Aβ imbalance (between generation and clearance) is the primary trigger of AD holds true(Tarasoff-Conway et al. 2015), the interaction between c-Abl and Aβ could form a feedback loop, exacerbating AD progression. Assuming that Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation act as antioxidant defenses for the body, c-Abl kinase activity might initially promote Aβ expression or tau phosphorylation to enhance their protective functions. However, when oxidative damage exceeds controllable thresholds, the role of c-Abl kinase may shift toward promoting neuronal apoptosis.

c-Abl and Oxidative Stress

Neurons are particularly sensitive to oxidative stress because of their high metabolic demands, high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids, and relatively low levels of antioxidant enzymes(Niedzielska et al. 2016; Cobley et al. 2018). In AD patients, oxidative damage not only increases in severity but also persists chronically. On the basis of these findings, many researchers propose that the exacerbation of oxidative damage is an early event in AD that may further promote mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal cell death(Perry et al. 2002; Lovell and Markesbery 2007). Therefore, a thorough understanding of oxidative stress regulatory mechanisms could provide comprehensive insight into the pathogenesis of AD. Oxidative stress can activate cytoplasmic c-Abl, and its kinase activity increases with increasing H2O2 concentration and prolonged exposure time (Sun et al. 2000a, b). When the reactive oxygen species (ROS) inhibitor N-acetylcysteine is applied, c-Abl kinase activity is suppressed.

c-Abl can regulate cellular resistance to oxidative stress. The primary enzymatic mechanisms for regulating intracellular H2O2 levels involve catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and peroxiredoxin1 (Prx1), which collectively convert H2O2 into H2O and O2(Rengasamy and Johns 1994; Brigelius-Flohe and Flohe 2020; Liochev and Fridovich 2007; Kim and Jang 2019). c-Abl and Abl-related gene (Arg) proteins can phosphorylate the Y231 and Y386 sites of CAT, thus increasing its catalytic activity(Cao et al. 2003b). Additionally, c-Abl and Arg interact with GPX1 and phosphorylate its Y96 site, thus enhancing the enzymatic activity of GPX1(Cao et al. 2003a). Thus, c-Abl regulates intracellular ROS levels via CAT and GPX1. When both c-Abl and its homolog Arg are deleted, intracellular ROS levels increase significantly, increasing the sensitivity of cells to H2O2-induced apoptosis(Cao et al. 2003b).

In addition to stimulating the activity of CAT, c-Abl and Arg also promote the degradation of CAT. Studies have shown that mouse cells deficient in c-Abl and Arg exhibit increased stability of CAT. c-Abl and Arg phosphorylate CAT at the Y231 and Y386 sites, causing it to be ubiquitinated and degraded by the 26 S proteasome(Cao et al. 2003d). c-Abl and Arg have dual regulatory effects on CAT activity. At low H2O2 concentrations (0.25–1.0 mM), c-Abl and Arg activate and phosphorylate CAT and GPX1, thus reducing intracellular ROS levels and exerting antiapoptotic effects. In contrast, under high H2O2 concentrations (2.0 mM), they dissociate from CAT, leading to a decrease in the catalytic activity of CAT. This might occur via two mechanisms: (a) tyrosine phosphatase dephosphorylates the CAT residues Y231-P and/or Y386-P, and (b) phosphorylated CAT undergoes ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, leading to an increase in ROS levels and initiating an apoptotic response(Cao et al. 2003c, d; Ito et al. 2001). c-Abl may serve as a molecular switch modulating the antiapoptotic function of CAT. By impairing the ability of CAT to degrade H2O2, c-Abl initiates apoptosis when H2O2 levels exceed a controllable threshold. Under oxidative stress conditions, PKCδ can be activated and phosphorylate c-Abl(Sun et al. 2000b). In turn, phosphorylated c-Abl further activates PKCδ by phosphorylating it at the Y512 residue(Yuan et al. 1998b). These two proteins mutually activate each other and colocalize to the mitochondria, leading to the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, the release of cytochrome c, the activation of caspase-3, and ultimately the induction of neuronal cell death(Sun et al. 2000a; Kumar et al. 2001). Activated c-Abl also directly phosphorylates dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), a known regulator of mitochondrial dynamics, at residues Y266, Y368, and Y449. Among these, Y266 might be the major phosphorylation site(Zhou et al. 2017). This activity enhances the GTPase activity of Drp1, promoting Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fragmentation and neuronal cell death(Zhou et al. 2017).

In addition to acting as an upstream molecule to regulate intracellular ROS levels by regulating the activities of CAT and GPX1, c-Abl can also form a feedback loop with Prx1 to further control ROS levels. Prx1 interacts with the SH3 domain of c-Abl to inhibit its catalytic activity and is considered a physiological inhibitor of c-Abl(Wen and Van Etten 1997). In turn, c-Abl can inhibit the expression of Prx1. In c-Abl knockout osteoblasts, the expression of Prx1 and its upstream transcriptional regulator nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) increases(Li et al. 2004). Therefore, a feedback loop may exist to regulate c-Abl activity in the stress response. When exposed to oxidative stress, PKCδ phosphorylates the S40 site of Nrf2, leading to its dissociation from Keap1(Buelna-Chontal et al. 2014; Niture et al. 2014; Ulasov et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2021). This process enhances the stability of the cytoplasmic Nrf2 protein and facilitates its translocation into the nucleus, where it modulates the expression of antioxidant response genes by binding to antioxidant response elements (Liu et al. 2022; Fao et al. 2019). Nrf2 subsequently promotes the expression of cytoprotective genes such as Prx1, GSH, HO-1 and NQO1(Saha et al. 2020; Ma 2013). Prx1 forms a feedback loop with c-Abl to inhibit excessive c-Abl activity, thus preventing cellular damage. Hematopoietic cell-specific protein 1-associated protein X-1 (HAX-1), a novel mitochondrial antiapoptotic protein that regulates oxidative stress-induced apoptosis, exerts its protective effect by activating c-Abl to eliminate intracellular ROS(Dong et al. 2022). HAX-1 interacts with c-Abl, and its binding is significantly enhanced under oxidative stress or ionizing radiation. Notably, HAX-1 promotes c-Abl activation under oxidative stress. However, when c-Abl is overactivated, HAX-1 mediates the binding of c-Abl to its E3 ligase, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation(Dong et al. 2022). This feedback mechanism maintains c-Abl activity at an optimal level, achieving self-regulation and ensuring the dynamic equilibrium of intracellular ROS levels.

c-Abl and HR Repair

As terminally differentiated cells, mature neurons largely lose their proliferative capacity and must maintain genomic stability throughout their lifespan. However, as a high-oxygen-consuming organ, the brain continuously generates large amounts of ROS, resulting in a significantly greater DNA damage burden in neurons than in other tissues(Madabhushi et al. 2015; Suberbielle et al. 2013). Oxidative base damage is the most common form of DNA lesions observed in AD. 8-Hydroxyguanine (8-OHG), a biomarker of oxidative DNA damage, emerges as an early event in AD pathogenesis and precedes obvious neuropathological changes(Markesbery and Lovell 2006; Nunomura et al. 2001). Notably, DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) represent the most cytotoxic type of DNA damage(Scott and Pandita 2006; Provasek et al. 2022). Fast but highly error-prone nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) and high-fidelity HR repair are the two main repair pathways for DSBs(Symington and Gautier 2011; Xu and Xu 2020). HR is the most precise mechanism for repairing DSBs, and both deficient and exacerbated HR activity can lead to genomic instability. Studies have shown that in the brain tissues of AD mouse models and AD patients, the levels of DSB markers (e.g., γ-H2AX) are elevated, whereas the expression or function of HR repair markers (e.g., Rad51 and BRCA1) is impaired(Shanbhag et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2018; Suberbielle et al. 2015). These findings suggest that HR repair defects play a critical role in the pathological process of AD.

The traditional view holds that HR is primarily active during the DNA replication phase (S/G2), whereas neurons, as terminally differentiated cells, rely mainly on NHEJ to repair DSBs. However, emerging data indicate that the HR mechanism persists in neurons. As a key HR repair protein, Rad52 is recruited to DSBs in neurons in a nascent mRNA-dependent manner, suggesting the existence of a novel RNA template-dependent HR pathway in these cells(Welty et al. 2018). Additionally, BRCA1 expression is reduced in the brains of AD patients and human APP transgenic mice. Knocking out BRCA1 in the dentate gyrus neurons of wild-type mice results in increased DSBs, neuronal atrophy, impaired synaptic plasticity, and learning/memory deficits(Mano et al. 2017). These findings demonstrate that BRCA1 is essential for repairing DSBs in central neurons. Importantly, these changes occur without significant neuronal loss, suggesting that the synaptic dysfunction caused by HR deficiency precedes cell death. Finally, in both AD mouse models and the brains of AD patients, the protein expression level of Sirtuin 6 is reduced(Jung et al. 2016). Sirtuin 6 is a member of the mammalian antiaging sirtuin family, also known as a longevity gene, and is involved in HR repair(Hou et al. 2020; Affandi et al. 2024; Mao et al. 2012). By cooperating with the chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 4 to promote chromatin relaxation and DNA repair or by stabilizing HR repair factors such as BRCA1, it helps to repair DNA damage and maintain genomic stability(Hou et al. 2020).

c-Abl serves as both an effector of DNA damage and a regulator of the repair process, finely modulating HR repair through multiple mechanisms. Rad51 and Rad52 play critical roles in HR repair following DNA damage, and their activity is regulated by c-Abl-mediated phosphorylation(Yuan et al. 1998a; Kitao and Yuan 2002). Specifically, activated c-Abl phosphorylates Rad51 at Y54 and Y315(Yuan et al. 1998a). This phosphorylation by c-Abl kinase occurs sequentially, with phosphorylation at Y315 preceding and stimulating phosphorylation at Y54(Popova et al. 2009). Notably, these two phosphorylation sites have opposite effects. Y54 phosphorylation inhibits the DNA binding ability of Rad51 and impairs ATP-dependent DNA strand exchange reactions, thus suppressing HR repair(Yuan et al. 1998a). In contrast, Y315 phosphorylation enhances the chromosomal binding capacity of Rad51 and promotes Rad51–Rad52 complex formation, facilitating HR repair(Shimizu et al. 2009; Chen et al. 1999). However, some studies have presented opposing findings, suggesting that phosphorylation at Y54 significantly increases the DNA strand exchange activity of Rad51, promotes the formation of nucleoprotein filaments, and allows it to efficiently compete with single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) for binding to protein replication protein A(Subramanyam et al. 2016). They proposed that this is the primary site through which c-Abl regulates Rad51 function. The discrepancies in the functional description of Y54 phosphorylation may stem from differences in experimental methods and phosphorylation mimicry strategies (antibody-based detection of endogenous phosphorylation versus p-carboxymethyl-L-phenylalanine mediated artificial mimicry that avoids cross-reactivity), species variations, or the stage of DNA damage being studied. c-Abl can also modulate the interactions of Rad51 with p53 and other proteins, such as BRCA1, or it can directly interact with BRCA1(Scully et al. 1997; Foray et al. 2002). Upon activation, c-Abl interacts with Rad52 and phosphorylates its Y104 residue, which promotes the nuclear accumulation of Rad52(Kitao and Yuan 2002; Honda et al. 2011). This phosphorylation enhances the annealing activity of Rad52 on ssDNA, thus facilitating DNA recombination and repair. However, the Rad52 Y104F mutant fails to accumulate in the nucleus(Honda et al. 2011). Thus, c-Abl regulates HR repair by phosphorylating Rad51 at multiple specific tyrosine residues and Rad52 at the Y104 site.

In neurons, the DNA repair function of c-Abl is particularly critical, as terminally differentiated cells must prioritize repair over apoptosis. Under physiological conditions, moderate c-Abl phosphorylation enhances the DNA-binding capacity and recombinase activity of Rad51 or Rad52, thus maintaining genomic stability. However, in the persistent oxidative stress environment of AD, the hyperphosphorylation of c-Abl may lead to the formation of abnormal Rad51 polymers and impair its nuclear focus formation ability, severely compromising HR efficiency. This finding aligns with the reduction in Rad51 foci observed in APP/PSEN1 mice reported by Yu et al. (Yu et al. 2018). A decrease in HR efficiency leads to persistent DSBs and the accumulation of γ-H2AX foci, thus promoting mutation accumulation and epigenetic dysregulation. These effects ultimately result in increased genomic instability in neurons, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive decline. Alternatively, when DNA damage becomes irreparable, c-Abl triggers programmed cell death through the phosphorylation of proapoptotic proteins such as p73 and Yap1.

Conclusions and Outlook

As a complex neurodegenerative disorder, AD involves multiple pathological processes. The nonreceptor tyrosine kinase c-Abl plays a dual role in the pathological process of AD (Fig. 1). The homeostasis of c-Abl is closely related to cellular oxidative stress levels, genomic stability, and neuronal survival, making it both a key regulatory factor in neuronal protection and a potential driver of neurodegenerative pathology. Most current studies regard c-Abl as a promoter of AD disease, mainly because c-Abl inhibitors (such as imatinib and nilotinib) have been confirmed to alleviate Aβ deposition, tau phosphorylation, impaired synaptic plasticity, and cognitive dysfunction in AD models(Lonskaya et al. 2015; Cancino et al. 2008). However, some studies have shown that the effect of STI571 on Aβ production does not involve Abl kinase(Netzer et al. 2003). In addition, the downstream regulatory factor HIF-1α of c-Abl plays a dual role in AD, as HIF-1α can suppress neuronal death induced by Aβ and oxidative stress(Chai et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2021; Merelli et al. 2018). Low concentrations of Aβ exert neuroprotective effects by activating HIF-1α, thus alleviating more severe neuronal damage. Moreover, HIF-1α overexpression alone is sufficient to confer neuronal resistance to Aβ toxicity(Soucek et al. 2003). Aβ peptides are not entirely detrimental; they can also perform various physiological functions in the central nervous system(Cai et al. 2023; Pearson and Peers 2006; Giuffrida et al. 2009). In normal brains, BACE1 is highly expressed at both the mRNA and protein levels, and its absence leads to cognitive impairment in young adult mouse models of traumatic brain injury(Cole and Vassar 2007; Mannix et al. 2011). Despite being successful at reducing the Aβ load, treatments targeting Aβ reduction have failed to reverse deficits in memory and cognitive symptoms(Canter et al. 2016; Huang et al. 2020). Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation may be protective responses of the body against oxidative stress(Smith et al. 2002a). Therefore, the role of c-Abl in AD cannot be simply categorized as either ‘promoting’ or ‘inhibiting’. Instead, it is context dependent, dynamically shifting between neuroprotective and detrimental effects on the basis of the cellular microenvironment. For example, postsynaptic density 95 (PSD95) is among the most abundant proteins at excitatory postsynaptic sites in the central nervous system and is involved in synaptic maturation and functional regulation(El-Husseini et al. 2000; Kim and Sheng 2004; Yusifov et al. 2021). c-Abl phosphorylates PSD95 at the Y533 site, and mutation of this site reduces its clustering ability at postsynaptic sites(Perez de Arce et al. 2010). c-Abl regulates the postsynaptic clustering of PSD95 through tyrosine phosphorylation, thus modulating synapse formation and function. When the dynamic activity of c-Abl kinase is dysregulated, excessive or insufficient phosphorylation of PSD95 might lead to synaptic dysfunction. As is the case in HR repair, the phosphorylation of Rad51 at Y54 and Y315 results in distinct functions, and even the role of Y54 phosphorylation remains controversial(Yuan et al. 1998a; Shimizu et al. 2009). This finding not only further elucidates the complex functions of c-Abl but also suggests its potential role as a “molecular switch” in maintaining the dynamic equilibrium of HR repair. During the early stage of DSBs, phosphorylation at Y54 inhibits the premature activation of Rad51, ensuring proper repair timing; in later stages, phosphorylation at Y315 promotes Rad51 assembly and function, enabling efficient HR. If c-Abl is abnormally activated, dysregulated phosphorylation at these two sites may lead to HR deficiency or excessive recombination, exacerbating the pathological progression of AD. The complicated role of c-Abl in AD reflects the diversity and dynamics of the AD pathogenesis mechanism. Future research should focus on the role of c-Abl in different pathological stages of AD, especially the regulatory mechanism under varying levels of oxidative stress.

In summary, c-Abl plays a dual role in AD, functioning as both a “villain” (e.g., by promoting Aβ plaque formation and tau hyperphosphorylation, thereby exacerbating neurodegenerative pathology) and a “savior” (e.g., by activating antioxidant enzymes and facilitating HR repair, thus ameliorating neuronal damage). To reconcile these opposing functions, potential strategies include spatiotemporally specific inhibition of c-Abl or combination therapies. This duality provides critical scientific insights into the pathogenesis of AD and offers potential for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. As research progresses, we may unravel the full mysteries of c-Abl in AD and identify new pathways to combat this devastating disease.
