Therapeutic Potential of Nanocurcumin in Mitigating Duloxetine‐Induced Hepatic Damage: Biochemical and Histopathological Insights
Authors: Hassanali Abedi, Maryam Jalali Jahromi, Zahra Mohammadzade, Bahareh Ebrahimi, Pegah Abdollahzadeh, Arash Hassannezhad, Farhad Sheikhnia, Hossein Kargar Jahromi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDuloxetine, a serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, commonly prescribed for depression and chronic pain, has been implicated in hepatotoxicity, primarily driven by oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. This has led to growing interest in naturally derived antioxidant compounds as potential protective strategies. The aim of this study was to evaluate the hepatoprotective potential of nanocurcumin against duloxetine‐induced liver injury in a controlled animal model. Male Wistar rats were divided into eight experimental groups, control, Solvent, curcumin 200 mg/kg, nanocurcumin 200 mg/kg, duloxetine 60 mg/kg, and treatment groups that simultaneously received duloxetine and nanocurcumin. Biochemical assays measured liver function enzymes (ALT, AST), oxidative stress markers (MDA, TOS), and antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPx). Proinflammatory cytokine levels, TNF‐α were also measured and liver tissue was evaluated histologically. Duloxetine administration significantly increased liver enzymes, oxidative stress markers, and TNF‐α levels, along with histopathological evidence of liver injury. Concomitant treatment with nanocurcumin reduced these effects and attenuated these changes, as evidenced by normalization of biochemical indices, enhanced antioxidant defense, suppression of proinflammatory cytokines, and preservation of liver structure. Nanocurcumin exhibits potent hepatoprotective effects against duloxetine‐induced liver injury, likely through enhancement of antioxidant capacity and modulation of inflammatory responses. These findings support its potential as an adjunctive therapy to duloxetine to mitigate hepatic side effects.Keywords: duloxetine hydrochloride, liver injury, nanomedicine, oxidative stress

Introduction

Depression is recognized as a common chronic disease worldwide that significantly impairs normal functioning and quality of life. It is estimated that approximately 300 million people worldwide suffer from depression, making it a leading cause of global disability [1]. Duloxetine, marketed under the brand name Cymbalta, is an antidepressant medication classified as a serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor. This medication is used in the treatment of major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, and stress urinary incontinence [2]. Studies in humans and animals show that this drug, especially at high doses, can induce hepatotoxicity. The mechanism of liver damage caused by duloxetine includes increased oxidative stress, decreased antioxidant enzyme activity in liver tissue, and histopathological changes such as hepatocyte necrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration [3, 4, 5, 6].

Turmeric (Curcuma longa) and its derivative curcumin have been used for thousands of years in the treatment of various diseases due to their effectiveness, cost‐effectiveness, and rich antioxidant content. Curcumin (Diferuloyl methane) is the main biologically active polyphenolic compound in turmeric, found mainly in the roots and rhizomes of the plant [7]. Furthermore, studies show that curcumin is well‐tolerated, even at high doses (up to 12 g in humans). Previous research has confirmed the therapeutic potential of curcumin as an antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, antioxidant, antihypertensive, anti‐obesity, anti‐inflammatory, and immunomodulator agent. Several studies have also highlighted the protective effects of curcumin in cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and gastric ulcers [8].

Curcumin has received a lot of significant attention for its hepatoprotective properties [9, 10]. Its multifaceted mechanisms include antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐apoptotic actions that mitigate liver injury caused by various insults [11, 12]. Previous studies have shown that curcumin can modulate key signaling pathways such as NF‐κB, Nrf2/Keap1, and TGF‐β/Smad, thereby reducing oxidative stress, suppressing inflammatory cytokines, and regulating apoptotic mediators like Bax, Bcl‐2, and caspase‐3 [13, 14]. Studies have demonstrated its efficacy in preventing liver fibrosis, steatosis, and cholestasis, and in enhancing liver enzyme profiles in models of drug‐induced hepatotoxicity and metabolic liver diseases [11, 15, 16, 17]. Furthermore, nanoformulations of curcumin have shown improved bioavailability and therapeutic outcomes, making them a promising candidate for hepatoprotection.

Despite Curcumin broad's medicinal benefits, its hydrophobic nature (insoluble in aqueous media), low tissue absorption and distribution, coupled with its rapid metabolism and subsequent inactivation in vivo, pose significant challenges and limitations to its therapeutic application [18, 19]. Various approaches have been employed to overcome these limitations, including the use of structural analogs of curcumin [20], liposomal curcumin [21], curcumin‐phospholipid complexes [22], and finally nanocurcumin formulations [19].

The use of nanoparticles in drug delivery of various agents, particularly for especially curcumin, has attracted the attention of researchers due to increased drug bioavailability and the possibility of targeted drug delivery. Studies show that the use of biodegradable nanoparticles for drug delivery of water‐insoluble drugs such as curcumin increases the stability and aqueous solubility of the drug in the body [19]. The characteristics of an ideal nanocarrier include easy preparation, small size, biodegradability, non‐toxicity, and ease of use [19, 23, 24].

Given the increasing global reliance on duloxetine for the management of depression and anxiety, concerns have been raised about its potential hepatotoxic effects, particularly at higher therapeutic doses. Identifying protective interventions against such liver injury is crucial to improve patient safety and treatment effectiveness. Nanocurcumin has emerged as a promising candidate due to its potent antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties. However, its hepatoprotective potential specifically in the context of duloxetine‐induced liver injury has not yet been investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate nanocurcumin in reducing duloxetine‐associated liver injury by assessing biochemical, oxidative stress, and histopathological markers in a controlled animal model. The present study will assess biochemical markers of liver function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), and total bilirubin. Additionally, we investigated the impact of nanocurcumin on oxidative stress markers, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), total oxidant status (TOS), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), in duloxetine‐treated rats.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Curcumin, duloxetine, olive oil, formalin (10%), Masson's trichrome stain, and hematoxylin‐eosin (H&E) stain were procured from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA). Nanocurcumin (SinaCurcumin) was obtained from Exir Nano Sina (Iran). Ketamine and xylazine were purchased from Alfasan (Netherlands). All chemicals and reagents used were of analytical grade.

Physiochemical Characterization of Nanoparticles

The physicochemical properties of the nanocurcumin formulation (Exir Nano Sina, Iran) used in this study were evaluated prior to experimentation. The hydrodynamic particle size and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (nanoPartica SZ‐100V2, HORIBA). Briefly, the nanocurcumin suspension was diluted with olive oil to an appropriate concentration and analyzed at 25°C. The average particle size was reported as the mean hydrodynamic diameter, and the zeta potential was measured to assess the surface charge and colloidal stability of the nanoparticles. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Animal Preparation

A total of 64 adult male Wistar rats were used in this experimental study. The rats were acclimatized for 1 week in the animal breeding facility at Jahrom University of Medical Sciences. During the study, animals had unlimited access to food and water and were maintained on a 12‐h light/dark cycle, at a temperature of 20°C−25°C, and a humidity of 50%−55%. The inclusion criterion was adult male Wistar rats with an average weight of 180−200 g, and the exclusion criterion was animal death during the study.

Experimental Groups and Treatment Protocol

The doses of duloxetine and nanocurcumin used in this study were selected based on previous preclinical studies and human equivalent dose calculations. The duloxetine dose of 60 mg/kg/day was chosen to induce hepatotoxicity in rats, as previously established by Vuppalanchi et al. [25] and Mishra et al. [5]. These studies reported significant liver injury at this dose.

Nanocurcumin doses (100, 150, and 200 mg/kg) were selected based on previous studies [26, 27, 28] that demonstrated hepatoprotective effects without toxicity. Sankar et al. [29] reported that 200 mg/kg nanocurcumin was safe and effective in improving heavy metal‐induced liver injury in mice. Similarly, Sadat‐Hosseini and Roshan [26] used 200 mg/kg nanocurcumin in combination with exercise training. The HED for the highest dose of nanocurcumin (200 mg/kg in mice) is approximately 32 mg/kg in humans (based on Km conversion). This is below the established safe limit for curcumin in humans, which has been reported to be well tolerated at doses up to 12 grams per day (approximately 170 mg/kg for a 70 kg adult). Therefore, the doses of nanocurcumin used in this study are within the safe and pharmacologically active range. Consistent with prior reports, nanomicelles containing curcumin were used in this study. These spherical nanomicelles are composed of surfactant molecules with hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, which increase the solubility of curcumin in water [26].

All experimental compounds (curcumin, nanocurcumin, and duloxetine) were dissolved in olive oil prior to administration by gavage. Based on previous studies showing that olive oil, at the volume used in this study (1 cc/day), had no significant effect on liver function markers, oxidative stress parameters, or inflammatory cytokine levels in mice [30, 31], no separate vehicle control group receiving olive oil alone was included in this study. However, since all treatment groups received the same olive oil vehicle, any potential vehicle effect would be consistent across groups.

The animals were randomly divided into eight groups of eight:

Control group: This group received no treatment during the 28‐day experiment.

Solvent group: This group received olive oil during the 28‐day experiment.

Experimental group 1 (Cur200): This group received 200 mg/kg body weight of curcumin dissolved in olive oil daily by gavage for 28 days.

Experimental group 2 (NCur200): This group received 200 mg/kg body weight of nanocurcumin dissolved in olive oil by gavage daily for 28 days.

Experimental group 3 (D60): This group received 60 mg/kg body weight of duloxetine dissolved in olive oil by gavage daily for 28 days.

Experimental group 4 (D60 + NCur100): This group received 60 mg/kg body weight of duloxetine along with 100 mg/kg body weight of nanocurcumin by gavage daily for 28 days.

Experimental group 5 (D60 + NCur150): This group received 60 mg/kg body weight of duloxetine along with 150 mg/kg body weight of nanocurcumin by gavage daily for 28 days.

Experimental group 6 (D60 + NCur200): This group received 60 mg/kg body weight of duloxetine along with 200 mg/kg body weight of nanocurcumin by gavage daily for 28 days.

Blood and Tissue Collection

At the end of the treatment period, the animals were weighed and anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg) [32]. Blood was drawn from the heart using a 5 cc syringe. Serum was obtained by centrifugation (15 min at 3000 rpm) and stored at −20°C for subsequent measurements.

Histopathological Analysis

Following blood collection, the animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide gas. Liver tissue was then quickly removed and placed in 10% formalin. After tissue processing, 10 sections were selected from each liver and stained with Masson's trichrome and H&E. Following staining, five microscopic fields from each sample section were randomly selected, and pathological changes in the liver were studied. Finally, the animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide gas.

Liver Function Assessment

Serum ALT (code: BXC0215), AST (code: BXC0205), ALP, TP (code: BXC0173), Alb (code: BXC0222), and total bilirubin (code: BXC0192) were measured using spectrophotometry with commercial kits (Biorexfars, Iran).

Oxidative Stress Assessment

MDA (Lot n.: E.0156Ra), TAC (Lot n.: E.0181Ra), TOS (Lot n.: E.1512Ra), GSH‐PX (Lot n.: E.0814Ra), and SOD (Lot n.: E.0168Ra) were measured using spectrophotometry and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Crystal Day, China).

TNF‐α Measurement

Tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) (Lot n.: E.0764Ra) was measured using ELISA (Crystal Day, China).

Statistical Analysis

The results were analyzed using SPSS software version 21 (Chicago, IL, USA) and Tukey's statistical test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. The Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test was used to check the normality of data distribution.

Results

Nanocurcumin Ameliorated Liver Function

The physicochemical characteristics of the nanocurcumin formulation were evaluated using DLS and zeta potential analysis. The nanoparticles exhibited a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 10.4 ± 3 nm, confirming their nanoscale size distribution. In addition, the measured zeta potential was −12.2 ± 2 mV, indicating adequate surface charge and colloidal stability of the nanoparticle suspension (Figure 1).

Physiochemical characterization of nanoparticles (A) DLS result and (B) zeta potential.

The obtained particle size was within the expected range for nanocurcumin the zeta potential results further suggest that the formulation maintained satisfactory dispersion stability during the experimental period. These findings confirm the suitability of the nanocurcumin formulation used in the present study.

The mean serum concentrations of ALT, AST, ALP, and total bilirubin in the group receiving duloxetine alone at a dose of 60 mg/kg (experimental group 3; D60) showed a significant increase compared to the control, curcumin (experimental group 1; Cur200), and nanocurcumin (experimental group 2; NCur200) groups. In contrast, the groups receiving duloxetine plus nanocurcumin at concentrations of 100, 150, and 200 mg/kg, exhibited a significant dose‐dependent decrease in these markers compared to the D60 group (Figure 2).

Liver enzyme activities and protein levels after nanocurcumin treatment in rats with duloxetine‐induced liver injury. Cu, curcumin; D, duloxetine; NCu, nanocurcumin.

Serum Alb and TP concentrations in the D60 group showed a significant decrease compared to the control, Cur200, and NCur200 groups. Co‐administration of duloxetine with nanocurcumin at doses of 100, 150, and 200 mg/kg demonstrated an increasing trend in serum TP concentration relative to the D60 group. For Alb, a significant increase in the mean serum concentration was observed only in the group receiving duloxetine plus nanocurcumin at a dose of 200 mg/kg (D60 + NCur200) compared to the D60 group (Figure 2).

Nanocurcumin Reduced Duloxetine‐Induced Oxidative Stress

Consistent with our hypothesis, co‐administration of nanocurcumin attenuated the oxidative stress induced by duloxetine (60 mg/kg). The mean serum MDA concentration in the group receiving duloxetine plus nanocurcumin (100, 150, and 200 mg/kg) decreased significantly compared to the D60 group with increasing nanocurcumin concentration in a dose‐dependent manner. Additionally, the Cur200 and NCur200 groups showed lower TOS levels compared to the control group, while duloxetine elevated TOS levels. Co‐administration of nanocurcumin (100, 150, and 200 mg/kg) with duloxetine reduced TOS levels, with the most effective dose being 200 mg/kg (Figure 3).

Oxidative stress markers and blood TNF‐α after nanocurcumin treatment in rats with duloxetine‐induced liver injury. Cu, curcumin; D, duloxetine; NCu, nanocurcumin.

The Cur200 and NCur200 groups showed significant increases in SOD and GPx activities, while the groups receiving duloxetine alone and duloxetine plus nanocurcumin showed a significant decrease compared to the control group. All groups receiving duloxetine or duloxetine plus nanocurcumin showed significant reductions relative to the NCur200 and Cur200 groups. All groups receiving duloxetine plus nanocurcumin showed a significant increase compared to the D60 group, and the greatest effect was observed with a dose of 200 mg/kg of nanocurcumin. Furthermore, all groups receiving duloxetine plus nanocurcumin showed a significant increase in SOD and GPx activities compared to the D60 group, with the greatest effect observed at the 200 mg/kg dose of nanocurcumin.

Additionally, TAC levels in the D60 group showed a significant decrease compared to the control, Cur200, and NCur200 groups. Only the D60 + NCur200 group exhibited a significant increase in TAC levels relative to the D60 group (Figure 3).

The most effective dose of nanocurcumin was 200 mg/kg, which produced the greatest improvement in duloxetine‐induced alterations. With the exception of TAC, the mean serum concentrations of all parameters in the Cur200 and NCur200 groups did not differ significantly from those of the control group (Figure 3).

Nanocurcumin Decreased TNF‐α Levels

The Cur200 and NCur200 groups showed a significant decrease in TNF‐α levels, while the groups receiving duloxetine alone or duloxetine plus nanocurcumin showed a significant increase compared to the control group. Furthermore, all groups receiving duloxetine plus nanocurcumin exhibited a significant reduction in TNF‐α levels compared to the duloxetine‐only group. Among the nanocurcumin‐treated groups, the greatest reduction was observed at the 200 mg/kg dose.

Nanocurcumin Mitigated Duloxetine‐Induced Histopathological Changes

Histological analysis of liver tissue (Figure 4) revealed significant differences in pathological features among treatment groups. The duloxetine (60 mg/kg) group showed tissue destruction and inflammatory cell infiltration, especially around the portal triads and within the liver parenchyma. Co‐administration of nanocurcumin with duloxetine demonstrated a dose‐dependent improvement in these changes. The group treated with 100 mg/kg nanocurcumin showed significant infiltration of inflammatory cells, which was moderately reduced at the 150 mg/kg dose. At the highest concentration (200 mg/kg), nanocurcumin significantly reduced tissue damage, suppressed inflammatory responses, and restored liver morphology to a near‐normal state. These findings highlight the hepatoprotective efficacy of nanocurcumin in ameliorating duloxetine‐induced structural damage.

Discussion

The results of the present study demonstrated that duloxetine administration led to an increase in ALT, AST, ALP, total bilirubin, TNF‐α, MDA, and TOS levels and a decrease in ALB, TP, and TAC levels. Oxidative stress occurs when there is an imbalance between oxidants, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), and antioxidants, leading to excessive production of ROS. This imbalance causes the accumulation of oxidized antioxidants and loss of their function. Normally, cells maintain a mild pro‐oxidant state, resulting in baseline oxidative stress. Enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidants help maintain a reducing environment within cells. When this redox balance is disrupted, it can lead to toxic effects, damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, and potentially induce cell death, apoptosis, or necrosis [33].

Given the detrimental effects of duloxetine, which increases the liver enzymes ALT, AST, and ALP [5], it is likely that duloxetine likely produces free radicals that cause oxidative stress. These metabolites have a high affinity for binding to glutathione, a powerful antioxidant, and thus reduce its levels in vivo. This process leads to damage to hepatocytes and a significant increase in serum enzymes. These findings are consistent with those of Mishra et al. [5], who reported that administration of 10 mg/kg duloxetine to rats for 21 days decreased SOD and GPx activities, increased lipid peroxidation and MDA levels, and elevated ALT, AST, and ALP. The mechanism of underlying this effect involves the metabolism of orally active duloxetine by Cytochrome P450 1A2 to hydroxylated and epoxide metabolites. These oxidative stress‐inducing metabolites have a strong affinity for glutathione and consequently reduce its levels in vivo [5].

Administration of 100 mg/kg nanocurcumin to rats for 14 days ameliorated the arsenic‐induced harmful effects on liver tissue and enzymes. Nanocurcumin increased the activity of antioxidant enzymes SOD, catalase, and GPx and reduced lipid peroxidation and MDA levels. In addition, nanocurcumin was more effective than pure curcumin due to its enhanced solubility, bioavailability, and stability in vivo. The same study also demonstrated the protective effects of curcumin against neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, immunotoxicity, bone diseases, and hepatotoxicity [29]. Consistent with these reports, various studies have shown that nanocurcumin has hepatoprotective properties against heavy metals such as cadmium, copper, chromium, and lead. Nanocurcumin exerts its hepatoprotective effects by preventing lipid peroxidation induced by heavy metals and maintaining the balance of antioxidant system [15].

Studies by Wernicke et al. [34] and Liu et al. [3] investigating duloxetine‐induced liver damage have shown that the toxic properties of the naphthyl ring, along with mitochondrial dysfunction and the induction of steatosis and cholestasis, can generate intracellular ROS and reduce glutathione levels, leading to liver cell damage. Consistent with these findings, research conducted by Vuppalanchi et al. [4] in patients with depression who received 60 mg of duloxetine daily for 2 months showed that duloxetine caused hepatotoxicity in these patients. Duloxetine increased serum levels of ALT and AST liver enzymes in these subjects through mechanisms including cholestasis and tissue changes such as hepatocellular necrosis, Kupffer cell accumulation, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Although isolated case reports have also described ALT and bilirubin elevations following duloxetine administration, the precise mechanisms remain incompletely defined [6].

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the anti‐inflammatory and hepatoprotective properties of curcumin. For instance, Morovvati et al. [35] reported that curcumin reduced adrenalectomy‐induced liver injury through antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory mechanisms. These mechanisms included the inhibition of interleukin‐1 and TNF‐α secretion. These findings are consistent with our observation that nanocurcumin reduced TNF‐α levels. However, it is important to acknowledge that our study did not include direct molecular analyses (e.g., Western blotting, RT‐PCR, or immunohistochemistry) to confirm modulation of specific pathways such as Nrf2/Keap1 or NF‐κB. Therefore, the mechanistic interpretations discussed above are based on previously published literature rather than experimental validation in the present study.

Other studies have also shown that concomitant use of curcumin with oxidative agents can prevent the harmful effects on the liver. Khodaparast et al. [16] showed that co‐administration of cyclophosphamide and curcumin could reduce cyclophosphamide‐induced free radical damage on liver tissue. Sadat‐Hoseini and Roshan [26] showed that the use of nanocurcumin supplements combined with endurance training could partially mitigate the negative effects of doxorubicin on the balance of the oxidant‐antioxidant system and the caspase‐independent programmed cell death pathway in liver tissue through via their antioxidant properties, further supporting the potential utility of nanocurcumin formulations.

These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of nanocurcumin as a hepatoprotective agent against duloxetine‐induced toxicity. The observed protective effects are likely mediated through multiple mechanisms, including enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defenses, suppression of oxidative stress pathways, and modulation of proinflammatory cytokine production. The nanoparticle formulation of nanocurcumin significantly improves its bioavailability and intracellular uptake, thereby enhancing its ability to scavenge ROS and restore the redox balance disrupted by duloxetine metabolites. Furthermore, the increased activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and glutathione peroxidase, along with decreased lipid peroxidation markers such as MDA and TOS, indicates potent antioxidant activity. At the same time, the significant reduction in TNF‐α levels reflects the anti‐inflammatory role, which complements its oxidative stress‐reducing function. These combined effects contribute to the preservation of histological integrity observed in liver tissue, especially at the 200 mg/kg dose of nanocurcumin. Overall, this study supports the hypothesis that nanocurcumin confers hepatoprotection through a multimodal biochemical and cellular approach, making it a promising adjunct in reducing drug‐induced liver injury. However, it is important to acknowledge that the present study did not include direct molecular analyses (e.g., Western blotting, RT‐PCR, or immunohistochemistry). Therefore, the mechanistic interpretations discussed above are inferred from biochemical and histopathological findings in conjunction with previously published literature, rather than being experimentally validated in this study.
