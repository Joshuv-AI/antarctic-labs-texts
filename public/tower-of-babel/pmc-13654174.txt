A deep learning-based model combining quantitative parameters of initial chest computed tomography and clinical variables for the prediction of posttraumatic acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Wenzhao Zhang, Xu Hu, Ke Ning, Huayan Xu, Jianqun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) frequently complicates severe trauma, and early risk stratification for this condition remains challenging. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a combined prediction model integrating deep learning-based quantitative parameters of initial chest computed tomography (CT) with clinical variables for the early prediction of posttraumatic ARDS, with a focus on automation and interpretability for potential integration with emergency workflows.MethodsA total of 173 patients with trauma admitted to West China Hospital of Sichuan University from January to June 2024 were retrospectively screened, and 105 patients were finally enrolled (34 in the ARDS group and 71 in the nonARDS group). Clinical data were collected, and quantitative parameters (e.g., opacity score, opacity volume, opacity percentage, highattenuation opacity volume, and mean CT value) were automatically extracted with deep learning-based CT quantitative analysis software. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with ARDS. The final multivariate model was expressed as follows: logit(ARDS risk) = −4.623 + [0.142 × Injury Severity Score (ISS)] + [0.247 × procalcitonin (PCT)] + (0.113 × bilateral lung opacity percentage). Model stability was evaluated via bootstrap resampling (1,000 repetitions), and the predictive performance was assessed according to the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsCompared with the non-ARDS group, the ARDS group had a higher ISS (40 vs. 25, P<0.001), lactate level (3.9 vs. 1.9 mmol/L, P<0.001), and PCT level (3.2 vs. 0.2 ng/mL, P<0.001), as well as a lower Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score (12 vs. 15, P<0.001) and SpO2 (94.8% vs. 96.7%, P=0.038). The ARDS group also had a significantly higher bilateral lung opacity volume (186.3 vs. 56.7 mL, P=0.001), opacity percentage (5.2% vs. 1.8%, P=0.002), and high-attenuation opacity volume (45.8 vs. 9.0 mL, P=0.001). Multivariate analysis identified the independent predictors of ARDS to be ISS [odds ratio (OR) =1.15; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.06–1.24; P<0.001], PCT (OR =1.28; 95% CI: 1.04–1.58; P=0.02), and bilateral lung opacity percentage (OR =1.12; 95% CI: 1.04–1.21; P=0.004). The combined model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.832–0.952). At the optimal cutoff of 0.385, the sensitivity and specificity were 82.4% and 85.9%, respectively. Bootstrap internal validation confirmed good model stability (mean AUC =0.878; 95% CI: 0.821–0.934).ConclusionsQuantitative parameters of initial chest CT within 24 hours after trauma, especially bilateral lung opacity percentage, are associated with the development of ARDS in patients with trauma and may serve as objective imaging biomarkers for early risk assessment. The prediction model combining ISS score and PCT with automated CT quantitative parameters demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC =0.892) and clinical operability and can be automated within minutes in a research setting. Further implementation studies are needed before the model can be implemented after the initial CT scan in clinical settings; nonetheless, the model may serve as a decision support tool and as a component in implementing early lung-protective strategies.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a common complication following severe trauma and a leading cause of death among patients with trauma. The syndrome is characterized by diffuse lung injury, and the related pathophysiological mechanisms including alveolar-capillary barrier disruption, pulmonary edema, and uncontrolled inflammation (1,2).

The incidence of trauma-associated ARDS ranges from 12% to 28%, and the mortality rate is 30–45% (3,4). Trauma is the third most common cause of ARDS, after pneumonia and sepsis (3). Recent studies have demonstrated that deep learning frameworks can accurately segment lung lesions and predict ARDS (1), and machine learning tools have been developed for ARDS detection in critically ill patients (2).

Procalcitonin (PCT) levels predict ARDS in patients with trauma (5). Tignanelli et al. (6) reported the independent predictors of ARDS to be pulmonary contusion [odds ratio (OR) =1.42], flail chest (OR =2.60), and multiple rib fractures (OR =1.64), suggesting a dose–response relationship between chest injury severity and ARDS risk. Similarly, a nomogram-based prediction model for ARDS in patients with severe traumatic brain injury was developed, incorporating clinical variables to facilitate individualized risk assessment (7).

ARDS profoundly affects the long-term outcomes of patients. Crucially, the early identification of high-risk patients creates a narrow yet decisive therapeutic window. Prompt adoption of lung-protective ventilation (8), restrictive transfusion (9), and goal-directed fluid management (10) has been shown to reduce the incidence of ARDS and improve patient prognosis.

ARDS prediction tools include the Injury Severity Score (ISS), Abbreviated Injury Scale for the thorax (AIS thorax), and Lung Injury Prediction Score (LIPS), each with inherent limitations. The ISS assesses multiregional injuries but has limited weighting for chest injuries (11), and its reported area under the curve (AUC) is 0.66–0.72 (12,13). The AIS thorax primarily reflects anatomical injuries but lacks information on pulmonary parenchymal function (14), with an AUC of approximately 0.68 (15).

Recent advances in deep learning technology have enabled the automated quantification of pulmonary contusions. Sarkar et al. (16) developed an auto-Lung Contusion Index (LCI) method using volumetric segmentation, which exhibited strong correlation with the AIS score. Machine learning approaches consisting of balanced random forest algorithms have demonstrated excellent performance for early ARDS prediction in patients with polytrauma (17). However, the bulk of these models either rely on manual or semi-automated lesion delineation, limiting their realtime applicability, or they employ “black-box” algorithms that hinder clinical interpretation and adoption. A fully automated, transparent model built on routinely available data is still lacking.

Posttraumatic ARDS follows a distinct temporal pattern. Primary lung injury (pulmonary contusion and laceration) can be visualized on computed tomography (CT) within hours after injury (18,19), and secondary lung injury typically manifests 24–72 hours later (20,21). Thus, completing the initial CT scan within 24 hours postinjury is crucial for accurately informing treatment decisions (22,23).

Pulmonary parenchymal abnormalities on initial CT reflect the primary injury burden and are associated with subsequent ARDS risk. In one study, radiomic scores derived from initial CT predicted ARDS with an AUC of 0.79, outperforming the ISS (AUC =0.66) and AIS thorax (AUC =0.68) (24). Other research reported that CT volume index score correlates with hospital stay, ventilation duration, and ARDS incidence (25). Deep learning algorithms have been applied to automatic quantification of pulmonary contusions. Lee et al. (26) found that pulmonary contusion volume ratio predicts pneumonia with high accuracy. A multicenter study by Zhou et al. (1) confirmed that end-to-end deep learning models can satisfactorily predict ARDS in critically ill patients.

Despite these advances, few studies have translated imaging findings into a bedside-ready risk score that seamlessly combines deep learning outputs with simple clinical variables. In our study, we directly addressed this deficiency by constructing a parsimonious logistic regression model that can be automatically calculated immediately after the initial CT scan, generating a probability score that can guide early preventive interventions.

Conventional CT interpretation is dependent on subjective visual assessment and is marred by interobserver variability, limiting the standardized application of imaging information (27). The Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) has established standardized methods for radiomic feature extraction to improve reproducibility (28).

Deep learning-based CT quantitative analysis software enables automatic lung and lobe segmentation, outputting parameters including opacity volume, opacity percentage, high-attenuation opacity volume, and mean CT value. Ground-glass opacity (GGO) is defined as increased CT attenuation that does not obscure underlying vessels, whereas consolidation obscures underlying vessels. In the study by Homayounieh et al. on patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pneumonia (29), deep learning-based quantitative features predicted disease severity and outcomes with high accuracy. Chaganti et al. (30) validated the correlation between quantitative parameters and clinical severity. Jacob et al. (31) and Salisbury et al. (32) confirmed that CT quantitative parameters are significantly associated with pulmonary function decline and mortality in patients with interstitial lung disease. Meanwhile, Mets et al. (33) incorporated CT-based quantitative metrics into assessment systems for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

However, the predictive value of this technology for ARDS for patients who have experienced trauma remains to be validated. Moreover, a fully automated, transparent model built on routinely available data is still lacking. In our study, we attempted to address these issues by constructing a parsimonious logistic regression model that can be calculated immediately after the initial CT scan, generating a probability score that may assist early preventive interventions. More specifically, the aim of this work was to determine the value of initial CT quantitative parameters obtained within 24 hours after injury in predicting ARDS to thus provide a reference for the early clinical identification of highrisk patients. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1279/rc).

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2024-2551). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study, and all data were deidentified prior to analysis.

Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and study flow

Consecutive patients with trauma admitted to the Emergency Department of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, from January 2024 to June 2024 due to motor vehicle accidents, falls from height, blunt trauma, or crush injuries were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were (I) initial non-contrast chest CT scan completed within 24 hours after injury and (II) complete clinical data. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) no chest CT examination or incomplete imaging data; (II) initial CT scan performed beyond 24 hours after injury; (III) missing key laboratory variables; (IV) pre-existing pulmonary infection, interstitial lung disease, or prior chest surgery; and (V) death within 24 hours. A total of 173 trauma patients were initially screened, among whom 105 were ultimately enrolled, including 34 in the ARDS group and 71 in the non-ARDS group. Clinical data collection

The following clinical data were collected from the electronic medical record system: demographic data, including age, sex, and cause of injury; vital signs, including body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and oxygen saturation (SpO2); laboratory test findings, including arterial blood gas analysis [pH, base excess, lactate, arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), and arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2)], complete blood count (white blood cell count and neutrophil percentage), PCT level, and coagulation function (prothrombin time and international normalized ratio); and trauma scores, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) (range, 3–15; lower scores indicate more severe consciousness impairment) and ISS (range, 0–75; higher scores indicate more severe injury). Clinical outcomes included mechanical ventilation use, duration of mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, length of ICU stay, and 28-day mortality.

Diagnostic criteria of ARDS

ARDS was diagnosed according to the Berlin Definition (34); all of the following criteria needed to be met for a positive diagnosis: (I) onset within 1 week of a known insult; (II) bilateral opacities (ground glass or consolidation) on chest imaging not fully explained by effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules; (III) respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload; and (IV) PaO2/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ≤300 mmHg with positive end-expiratory pressure or continuous positive airway pressure ≥5 cmH2O.

CT scanning protocol

All patients underwent non-contrast chest CT within 24 hours after injury. The scanning equipment was a 128-slice dual-source CT device (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The scanning parameters were as follows: a tube voltage of 100 kV [body mass index (BMI) <25 kg/m2] or 120 kV (BMI ≥25 kg/m2); automatic tube current modulation (CARE Dose 4D), a quality reference of 100 mAs; a collimation of 128 mm × 0.6 mm, a pitch of 0.9–1.2, a gantry rotation time of 0.5 s, a scan range from the lung apices to the lung bases, iterative reconstruction (ADMIRE, with strength level of 2), a slice thickness of 1.0 mm, B70f (lung window) and B30f (mediastinal window) convolution kernels, and a matrix size of 512×512. Patients were placed in the supine position and scanned at end-inspiration breath-hold; for patients unable to cooperate, fast scanning during quiet breathing was performed.

Quantitative analysis of CT images

CT images were exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format and imported into prototype deep learning-based CT pneumonia analysis software (Siemens Healthineers) for quantitative analysis (Figure 2). The software uses a multiscale deep reinforcement learning algorithm to automatically detect anatomical landmarks (carina and sternal tip). Lung segmentation masks were generated with a deep image-to-image network (DI2IN). A densely connected U-network (DenseUNet) with anisotropic kernels was employed for semantic segmentation of intrapulmonary lesions (GGOs and consolidations). The software was validated in a multicenter study (29). The training set included 1,371 chest CT examinations, and the validation set included 1,000 chest CT examinations, with data from multiple centers and different CT scanners. All automatically generated segmentation masks were reviewed bythree radiologists with 5–10 years of experience in thoracic imaging, ensuring clinical oversight of the automated measurements; only 2 cases (1.9%) required manual correction in this study. The software automatically output the following quantitative parameters (29): opacity score (0–4 per lobe), a semiquantitative score based on the percentage of lobar involvement, summed to obtain the total bilateral lung opacity score (range, 0–20); lung volume (mL), the total volume of bilateral lungs or individual lobes; opacity volume (mL), the absolute volume of ground-glass and consolidative opacities; opacity percentage (%), the ratio of opacity volume to lung volume, identified as the best feature for distinguishing disease severity; high-attenuation opacity volume (mL), the volume of lesions with a CT attenuation ≥−200 Hounsfield units (HU) corresponding to consolidation; high-attenuation opacity percentage, the ratio of high-attenuation opacity volume to lung volume; and mean and standard deviation CT value (HU). Based on prior studies that have established the prognostic value of CT-derived volumetric metrics in traumatic and inflammatory lung injury (24-26,29), the following parameters were analyzed: total bilateral lung opacity score, total bilateral lung opacity volume, bilateral lung opacity percentage, bilateral lung high-attenuation opacity volume, bilateral lung mean CT value, and mean CT value of opacity regions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared with the independent samples t-test. Nonnormally distributed variables are expressed as the median and interquartile range and were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test. The Levene test was used to assess the homogeneity of variances. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared with the χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Univariate logistic regression was performed to screen variables potentially associated with ARDS (those with P<0.10 were entered into multivariate analysis). Multivariate analysis was performed via forward stepwise selection with the Akaike information criterion as the selection criterion, and ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

To further assess the stability and reliability of the model, bootstrap resampling (1,000 repetitions) was performed for internal validation of the multivariate logistic regression model. For each bootstrap sample, the model was refitted and the AUC was calculated. The mean AUC and 95% CI of the 1,000 repetitions were ultimately reported. This method effectively evaluates the sensitivity of the model to sampling variability and is recommended for validating models with small sample sizes (35). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to evaluate predictive performance, and the AUC with 95% CI was calculated. The combined prediction factor was constructed based on the regression coefficients. The optimal cutoff value was determined according to the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and likelihood ratios were calculated. The ARDS group consisted of 34 patients, and three independent variables were finally included in the multivariate model. The ratio of events to variables was 11.3:1, which met the basic requirement for logistic regression analysis (generally required ≥10:1) (36). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Data distribution and description of statistical methods

Before intergroup comparisons, normality tests (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance tests (Levene test) were performed for all continuous variables. The results showed that age, SpO2, and bilateral lung mean CT value were normally distributed with homogeneous variances (P>0.05). ISS, GCS, lactate, PCT, opacity score, opacity volume, opacity percentage, and high-attenuation opacity volume were not normally distributed (P<0.05).

Comparison of clinical characteristics

There were no statistically significant differences between the ARDS and non-ARDS groups in terms of general data such as age or sex (P>0.05). Regarding vital signs, the ARDS group had a lower SpO2 than did the non-ARDS group (P=0.038), while there were no significant differences in other vital signs between the two groups (P>0.05). In terms of trauma scores, the ARDS group, as compared to the nonARDS group, had a higher ISS than did the non-ARDS group (P<0.001) and a lower GCS score (P<0.001). Regarding laboratory findings, both lactate and PCT levels were higher in the ARDS group than in the nonARDS group (both P values <0.001). Detailed data are shown in Table 1.

Predictive model performance and bootstrap validation

Based on the multivariate logistic regression results, a combined prediction model was constructed. ROC curve analysis (Figure 3) showed that the combined model predicted ARDS with an AUC of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.832–0.952). The optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index was 0.385. At this cutoff, the combined model had a sensitivity of 82.4% (95% CI: 65.5–93.2%), a specificity of 85.9% (95% CI: 75.6–93.0%), a positive predictive value of 73.7% (95% CI: 58.0–85.8%), and a negative predictive value of 90.8% (95% CI: 81.5–96.2%). The positive likelihood ratio was 5.85, and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.20. The AUC values for individual predictors were as follow: ISS, 0.825 (95% CI: 0.748–0.902), PCT, 0.802 (95% CI: 0.718–0.886), and bilateral lung opacity percentage, 0.788 (95% CI: 0.698–0.878). The differences in AUC between the combined model and each individual predictor were all statistically significant (all P values <0.05). Internal validation with bootstrap resampling (1,000 repetitions) showed that the mean AUC of the 1,000 bootstrap samples was 0.878 (95% CI: 0.821–0.934), close to the original AUC (0.892), with no statistically significant difference (P=0.34, based on the proportion of bootstrap replicates with an AUC more extreme than the observed difference). The standard deviation of the bootstrap AUC was 0.029, indicating that the model performance was relatively stable across different samples with a low risk of overfitting. The calibration curve of the combined model showed good agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: χ2=8.24; P=0.31).

Discussion

This study found that quantitative parameters from initial chest CT scans within 24 hours after trauma, especially bilateral lung opacity percentage, may be associated with the development of ARDS. The combined model incorporating ISS, PCT, and opacity percentage yielded an AUC of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.832–0.952), outperforming any single predictor. This AUC value is higher than that previously reported for ISS (0.66–0.72) (12,13) and AIS thorax (~0.68) (15), suggesting that incorporating imaging quantitative parameters can significantly improve predictive performance.

The pathophysiological basis of this finding lies in the “two-hit” mechanism of posttraumatic ARDS. The first hit (0–24 hours after injury) mainly consists of pulmonary contusion, hemorrhage, and local inflammatory responses. Animal model studies have confirmed that alveolar capillary rupture and inflammatory cell infiltration can be observed early after pulmonary contusion (20), which manifests as GGOs and consolidation on CT. The second hit (24–72 hours) is triggered by systemic inflammatory response syndrome, fat embolism, and massive transfusion. A study has shown that neutrophils begin to accumulate in the lungs after trauma, peaking at 24–48 hours (21), leading to more extensive diffuse injury in an already vulnerable lung. In our study, the median bilateral lung opacity percentage in the ARDS group was significantly higher than that in the non-ARDS group (P=0.002). These data indicate that pulmonary parenchymal abnormalities detected on the initial CT scan can quantitatively reflect the primary injury burden and are involved a dose-response relationship with subsequent ARDS risk.

Compared with traditional manual interpretation, the deep learning-based quantitative analysis technology used in this study has several significant advantages. First, the technology enables fully automated processing. Only 1.9% (n=2) of the cases in this study required manual correction, close to the rate reported by Homayounieh et al. (29) (2/241, 0.8%), indicating high segmentation accuracy. Second, standardized output eliminates interobserver variability. Studies have noted that the agreement among different radiologists for assessing pulmonary lesions is limited (27), whereas deep learning models provide continuous, reproducible quantitative parameters. The IBSI has since established guidelines to improve reproducibility across different software platforms (28). Third, the technology can uncover subtle features that are difficult to discern visually. In our study, there was no significant difference in mean CT attenuation between the ARDS and non-ARDS groups (P=0.479), but the opacity percentage differed significantly (P=0.002), which is consistent with the finding by Röhrich et al. (24), who found that radiomics features can capture microheterogeneity of lung parenchyma, with predictive performance significantly better than that of traditional scores.

The three predictors in our model had similar OR values, suggesting that systemic injury burden, inflammatory response intensity, and local lung injury contribute equally to ARDS risk. The large-scale study by Tignanelli et al. (6) also confirmed that chest injury, massive transfusion, and sepsis are independent predictors of ARDS, with OR values consistent with our own, supporting the scientific validity of multidimensional combined prediction strategies. In the proposed clinical workflow for early posttraumatic ARDS risk stratification, the deep learning software serves as an assistive tool that provides standardized quantitative measurements, which are then reviewed by the attending radiologist or emergency physician. The model does not replace clinical judgment but rather augments it by offering objective, reproducible risk estimates within minutes of CT acquisition. As it relates to clinical translation, the proposed model could be operationalized as follows: upon completion of the initial non-contrast chest CT, the deep learning software automatically segments the lungs and calculates the opacity percentage within seconds. Simultaneously, ISS and PCT values are extracted from the trauma evaluation and laboratory system. A simple equation then generates a predicted probability, which is immediately displayed in the electronic health record. If the probability score exceeds 0.385, a clinical decision support alert recommends initiating lung-protective ventilation (tidal volume ≤6 mL/kg predicted body weight), restrictive transfusion triggers, and early consultation for ICU admission. Such a workflow has the potential to reduce the incidence of ARDS and improve resource utilization in busy trauma centers.

Early risk stratification through our model may enable clinicians to act before ARDS fully manifests, although the clinical benefit of such early action remains to be prospectively demonstrated. This early identification could facilitate timely implementation of lung-protective ventilation—which has been shown to reduce mortality among patients with ARDS and may prevent progression in at-risk patients—restrictive transfusion practices to minimize transfusion-related lung injury, and goal-directed fluid management to increase the number of ventilator-free days. Additionally, the model’s high negative predictive value (90.8%) suggests that it may help avoid unnecessary intensive monitoring and interventions in low-risk patients. Although the model provides objective, reproducible risk estimates within minutes of CT acquisition, it is not intended to be a replacement for clinical judgment; rather, it may augment such decision-making, potentially reducing inter-clinician variability and supporting more standardized critical care decisions. We emphasize that these potential benefits remain to be validated in future prospective studies that directly assess the impact of model-guided interventions on patient outcomes. Multimodal artificial intelligence frameworks incorporating such tools into electronic health records could further minimize mortality through timely and informed interventions for high-risk patients.

Our model achieved an AUC of 0.892, superior to that of ISS (0.825) and the AIS thorax (~0.68) (15). Compared with traditional clinical scores, our model has significantly higher predictive performance. Sarkar et al. (16) developed an auto-LCI method using volumetric segmentation of pulmonary contusions, which showed strong correlation with the AIS score and demonstrated potential for predicting ARDS. Machine learning approaches incorporated balanced random forest algorithms have also shown excellent predictive performance for ARDS in patients with polytrauma (17). Compared with the radiomic model devised by Röhrich et al. (24) (AUC =0.79), our model incorporated clinical variables (ISS and PCT), which increased the AUC by approximately 0.10, supporting the incremental value of a multimodal fusion strategy. Crucially, by using a simple logistic regression rather than a complex machine learning ensemble, we preserve full model transparency, which facilitates regulatory approval and clinical acceptance.

The sample size of this study was limited (34 cases of ARDS). Although it met the minimum requirement for the ratio of events to variables in logistic regression (11.3:1; generally required ≥10:1) (36), it was insufficient for subgroup analyses. Bootstrap internal validation (1,000 repetitions) produced a mean AUC close to the original AUC, with a small standard deviation, indicating good internal stability and low risk of overfitting, consistent with the recommendation by Steyerberg et al. (35) for the internal validation of prediction models. Regarding image quality, the incidence of respiratory motion artifacts in patients with trauma is considerable (28). Approximately 26% of the initial CT scans in this study had moderate-to-severe motion artifacts, but the predictive performance of the quantitative features was not significantly affected. The study by Homayounieh et al. (29) similarly found no significant difference in AUC between CT scans with and without artifacts, suggesting that deep learning algorithms possess a degree of robustness to mild-to-moderate motion artifacts.

Limitations

This study involved several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the single-center, retrospective design might have introduced selection bias, and the findings may not be generalizable to other institutions with different patient populations or CT protocols. Second, the limited sample size (ARDS group: n=34), while sufficient for the three-variable logistic model (events per variable ratio 11.3:1), precludes subgroup analyses and the development of more complex machine learning models. Third, the CT quantitative software employed is a noncommercial research prototype; although validated in previous multicenter studies (29), it is not yet widely available, and consistency across different software platforms needs to be evaluated; indeed, the ISBI has noted that outputs from different radiomics software may differ substantially (28). CT acquisition parameters—including scanner vendor, tube voltage, reconstruction kernel, and slice thickness—can influence quantitative measurements and radiomic feature reproducibility. Our study used a single CT platform (Siemens SOMATOM Definition Flash) at a single institution; therefore, the generalizability of our opacity percentage thresholds to other scanner platforms and protocols remains to be further validated. Fourth, external validation in independent cohorts is needed before clinical implementation. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory, and the model requires further validation in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts.

Future multicenter prospective studies will be used to externally validate our model, and decision curve analysis will be incorporated to formally evaluate its net clinical benefit and thus its suitability to bedside implementation. Dynamic CT monitoring should also be explored to compare the trajectories of CT quantitative parameters at different time points after injury. For instance, as Lee et al. (26) suggested that dynamic changes in the pulmonary contusion volume ratio may have greater predictive value than a single measurement. Additionally, integrating CT quantitative parameters, clinical variables, and inflammatory markers may further improve predictive performance, and embedding the model into hospital information systems as a clinical decision support tool could facilitate real-time risk alerts and earlier lung-protective interventions, ultimately reducing ICU length of stay and ARDS-related mortality.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that deep learning-based quantitative parameters from initial chest CT scans, especially bilateral lung opacity percentage, are associated with the development of ARDS in patients with trauma and may serve as objective imaging biomarkers for early risk assessment. The prediction model, which combines ISS and PCT level with fully automated CT quantitative parameters, achieved robust discriminative ability (AUC =0.892) with a high negative predictive value. This suggests it is a transparent tool that can facilitate early risk stratification in the emergency setting, pending further validation in implementation studies. Bootstrap internal validation confirmed good model stability. However, given the limited sample size, these results should be considered exploratory, and the clinical value of the modelmust be further validated in multicenter, prospective cohorts with direct measurement of clinical outcomes following model-guided interventions.
