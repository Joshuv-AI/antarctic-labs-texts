“Because we’re the experts”: centering the expertise of people who use drugs to identify and propose solutions for overdose prevention
Authors: Erika J. Bailey, Mikaela Becker, Jess Morrow, Rick Schaefer, Sonya Angeles, Patrick Kemp, Zachary Crews, Katy Mijal, Joella Striebel, Caitlin J. Conway, Emily Claypool, David W. Seal, Elizabeth M. Salisbury-Afshar, Ryan P. Westergaard, Rachel E. Gicquelais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople who use drugs possess valuable expertise, knowledge, needs, and preferences as part of their lived experience, which should be incorporated into overdose-focused public health programs and policies.MethodsWe formed a community advisory board composed of 16 clients from four syringe services programs (SSPs) in Wisconsin for an overdose prevention research study to inform project priorities. Over 6 meetings spanning 10 months, the board engaged in a root cause analysis facilitated by SSP staff and University researchers using a semi-structured assessment technique from the Community Anti-Drug Coalitions of America. The board then identified solutions to each root cause and rated solutions on two dimensions: impact and effort required to implement. Similar solutions were combined by averaging effort and impact scores respectively. After the activity concluded, we gathered members’ perceptions of the activity using a brief, open-ended questionnaire.ResultsMembers identified nine root causes of overdose that spanned individual (e.g., solitary drug use), structural (e.g., inaccessible or suboptimal substance use treatment programs), and policy (e.g., criminalization of drug use) drivers of overdose occurrence and fatality risk. Substance use stigma was a cross-cutting, fundamental driver of all root causes. The board proposed 28 solutions responsive to these root causes, including changing policies, implementing whole-person focused harm reduction and treatment programs, and incorporating people with lived/living experience of drug use into the design and implementation of public health programs. Members felt the activity was meaningful and effectively facilitated, but highlighted that several solutions were unable to be acted upon in the context of the research study.ConclusionThis work reinforces a need for multi-dimensional, comprehensive interventions, programs, and policies to address the overdose crisis. Collectively, new and continued overdose prevention programming should broaden access to and use of harm reduction tools (e.g., naloxone, drug checking) and substance use and mental health treatments as well as reform policies that negatively impact the health of people who use drugs. Our findings underscore the need to incorporate the expertise of directly impacted populations into future research and program planning focused on overdose prevention by partnering with people who use drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01368-9.

Background

Drug overdose is the leading cause of death among United States (U.S.) adults aged 18–44 [1]. In 2024, there was a significant decline in overdose deaths [2, 3]. This decline is hypothesized to be from increased availability of naloxone and treatment for substance use disorders, changes in the drug supply and population of people using drugs, and waning effects of the COVID-19 pandemic [4–6]. However, complexities of the drug overdose crisis continue to emerge and threaten this progress, such as new adulterants in the drug supply (e.g., xylazine, nitazenes) [7, 8]. Further, high-profile, rigorously conducted trials of community-based interventions have failed to show an effect on overdose mortality [9, 10]. Continued progress in reducing overdose mortality therefore demands that comprehensive, feasible, and novel solutions are developed, tested, and rapidly implemented in communities. Meaningful partnerships with people with lived and living experience of drug use, who are directly impacted by the overdose crisis, are an indispensable yet underacknowledged component of the research and translation process [11, 12]. Indeed, people who use drugs (PWUD) have conceptualized and implemented many of the most impactful, effective harm reduction programs and tools available to date [11].

PWUD have been increasingly engaged to inform programming and research in recent decades [11, 13–16]. For example, drug user unions have developed new models of community-academic partnerships that ensure research is community-driven [11, 16] and community advisory boards have influenced the development of culturally informed overdose prevention interventions [13]. Further, major federal funders have publicly recognized the importance of community involvement in substance use research [17], and community engagement is a required part of the research approach for several funded research networks in the National Institutes of Health Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) Initiative [18]. Despite recent advances, significant heterogeneity remains in the degree to which PWUD are engaged in creating, guiding, or refining public health programs and research on drug overdose [12]. The role of PWUD in research is often limited to their participation as research ‘subjects’, falling short of recognizing them as partners and experts in their own right [12, 15, 19]. For example, PWUD often play an essential role in recruiting difficult to reach populations and provide highly sensitive information on illicit behaviors via surveys or interviews [20, 21] but receive limited remuneration and few opportunities to benefit from research findings directly through timely community-level changes [12, 15]. This dynamic reflects entrenched power imbalances between PWUD and researchers and leaves those with the most first-hand knowledge out of key conversations, particularly during the crucial phase of selecting research topics and defining research questions and objectives that will have the greatest community impact [12, 15].

Researchers have used many formats of partnership to successfully incorporate perspectives of lived and living experience into research design and implementation, such as community advisory boards (CABs), community-based participatory research (CBPR) processes, and as members of research teams. CBPR engages people with lived or living experience who are most directly impacted by a problem [22] by establishing a reciprocal relationship between PWUD and researchers. Further, it requires a continuous commitment to impacted communities throughout all phases of the research process (e.g., developing research questions and study protocols, collecting data, interpreting results, and disseminating findings). In the context of the ongoing North American overdose crisis, CBPR approaches have been extended by recent calls for the democratization of drug use research, which demand increased focus on PWUD involvement and dissemination [23, 24]. These approaches embody the principles of harm reduction, namely, ensuring that PWUD “routinely have a real voice in the creation of programs and policies designed to serve them,” and affirming that PWUD are the “primary agents” in reducing harms of drug use [25]. Meaningful partnerships created by these approaches are necessary if research on overdose is to create lasting and impactful community-based change [12, 15, 19].

CABs are one strategy academics have used to partner with PWUD in research, especially in the context of drug use and HIV [26]. In research, CABs may consist of community members with lived or living experience and are convened to provide feedback on specific elements of a project. In alignment with CBPR approaches, engagement with PWUD via CABs can shift power from academic institutions to community members, build capacity, improve the quality of research conducted, and democratize dissemination efforts [13, 27, 28]. An important criticism of CABs is that they are often set up after a research study has been funded, which can diminish opportunities for communities to truly influence research design in accordance with CBPR principles [11]. This limitation underscores the importance of intentionally designed CAB activities and processes to maximize the CAB’s capacity to impact research.

In this manuscript, we describe results from two activities conducted with a CAB composed of 16 PWUD that was convened to inform the design and implementation of a harm reduction-oriented overdose prevention intervention. Specifically, we report on results of a semi-structured root-cause analysis that used the Community Anti-Drug Coalitions of America (CADCA)’s community needs assessment methods [16] to identify primary drivers of overdose in CAB members’ communities. Second, we describe solutions responsive to these root causes that were elicited using Nominal Group Technique [30]. After the activity concluded, we collected and summarized CAB members’ perspectives about the importance of CAB participation as well as successes and challenges related to the activities to inform our ongoing interactions with our CAB.

Methods

This manuscript reports on two CAB activities conducted for the University of Wisconsin-Madison’s Exploration of Novel Harm Reduction Approaches to Increase Client Engagement (ENHANCE) Project, which is one of 10 overdose prevention studies in the National Institute of Health’s HEAL Initiative Research on Interventions for Stability and Engagement Network. The study is guided by the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) Framework, which describes the process and determinants of implementing new interventions [31]. The two CAB activities reported here were part of the EPIS “Exploration” phase and sought to assess the needs of the target population (i.e., PWUD) for a forthcoming overdose prevention intervention we will test in later project years.

ENHANCE occurs in partnership with Vivent Health, a community-based organization that operates a network of 10 syringe services programs (SSPs) in Wisconsin. In 2022, the ENHANCE Project CAB, named the Community Leadership Team, was established to advise on study development, implementation, and dissemination activities. The CAB has 16 members who are clients from the four participating study sites, which are Vivent Health SSPs located in Green Bay, La Crosse, Madison, and Milwaukee, Wisconsin. Further details on the ENHANCE Project protocol are described elsewhere [32]. Typically, 8–12 members attend each meeting.

CAB recruitment and meeting structure

Members of the ENHANCE Project CAB (hereafter referred to as ‘members’) were recruited by Vivent Health SSP staff in each site, who identified 3 to 4 clients who use drugs who were able and willing to serve as members. As described previously [32], the CAB met for approximately 90 min every other month. In-person meetings were facilitated at each of the four sites by a local staff member. Facilitators also joined a remote meeting link (via Zoom) to enable a hybrid meeting structure with opportunity for cross-site interaction. Meals were provided during the meetings and members were compensated $50 cash per meeting.

Prior to each CAB meeting, members of the study team convened to create a facilitation guide to ensure consistency in discussion topics across sites. The guide contained open-ended questions, probing questions for gathering additional relevant information, and space for facilitator notes. A companion participant guide was also created, so that members could follow along with the meeting plan. The four facilitators convened virtually with the study team to review the facilitator guide before each meeting and discuss the purpose of each question, how to implement activities, anticipated challenges, and to answer questions.

At the beginning of each meeting, a project staff member reviewed the agenda and shared any introductory information with attendees before moving each local site into a Zoom “breakout room.” Facilitators then used the facilitation guide to discuss focal topics or complete activities. A virtual note-taker was placed in each breakout room to capture a summary of the discussion in each site. During the last 10 min of the meeting, breakout rooms were closed and each of the four note-takers summarized key points from each site’s discussion.

Problem analysis to identify root causes of overdose

During meetings in 2024, the CAB completed a root cause analysis activity to inform priorities for an overdose prevention intervention that will be pilot tested later in the study. The activity used a semi-structured root-cause analysis technique for community assessments developed by the Community Anti-Drug Coalitions of America (CADCA) [29]. The CADCA method includes development of a problem statement followed by facilitated discussion about why the problem occurs in society followed by probing questions to identify why the problem occurs within a specific community or location.

The study team first developed a problem statement to identify root causes of overdose from the perspective of CAB members and a meeting facilitation guide containing the questions and directions described below. At the first meeting, members were provided an overview of the activity and its purpose. Facilitators described the legal and funding-related limitations of the research project but encouraged members to think broadly for the initial stages of brainstorming. Members began a discussion around the following problem statement, posed as a question by facilitators: “Why do overdoses keep happening?” Members in each site were provided several minutes to silently reflect and write ideas down on post-it notes and then share their post-its aloud in a round-robin style. As members shared, facilitators collected post-it notes and grouped together similar ideas. Local meeting facilitators then asked probing questions such as, “why do you think that is?” and “why might that be a problem here?” to identify locally relevant issues.

After the meeting, one member of the research team collected and reviewed all post-it notes and paraphrased them using a virtual Padlet to allow for further synthesis. Post-its with highly overlapping or identical content were placed on the same “card” on the Padlet. Cards focused on the same topics (e.g., mental health, substance use disorder treatment) were then grouped together into the same section of the Padlet. The staff member then developed an initial draft of text summarizing the content of the cards in each section. This summary text is termed a “root cause” in this manuscript. After this initial draft was generated, all study team members, including each site’s facilitators, reviewed and edited the Padlet, resulting in a revised draft of root causes. At the following CAB meeting, facilitators asked members for feedback on the root causes, including whether they were comprehensive and reflective of the discussion and thoughts about anything that was missing, incorrect, or overlooked. Ultimately, the CAB verbally reached consensus on the root causes after making minor edits.

Developing solutions to each root cause of overdose

Over the next three meetings, facilitators solicited solutions to each of the nine root cause using Nominal Group Technique [30] modified to fit a 90-minute meeting format. At the beginning of each meeting, members reviewed a handout describing six strategies to effect community change outlined by CADCA (i.e., provide information, build skills, change access or barriers, change consequences or incentives, change physical design of the environment, change policies) to help aid brainstorming [33]. Within each site, facilitators then presented each problem and members were asked to silently reflect and write down solutions they thought would best address the problem on a provided worksheet. Each member then verbally shared one idea at a time with their local group in a round-robin style until all solutions were proposed. The solutions were compiled on whiteboards or poster paper by the local facilitator. Members voted on the group’s top three ideas and ranked each on two dimensions, impact on overdose risk and effort required to implement, on a scale of 1 (low) to 5 (high). The facilitation guide for these sessions consisted of a list of strategies to effect community change and directions to solicit solutions for each of the three problem statements covered per meeting (e.g., prompted the facilitator to read the first problem statement aloud to members, provide time for silent reflection and preparation of individual responses, and described processes to share and rank solutions).

After each session, each of the four facilitators shared the top three solutions and corresponding scores with the research team for each of the nine root causes (i.e., a maximum of 108 solutions could be submitted). However, local sites often proposed identical solutions, which were combined by the study team by averaging effort and impact scores. This resulted in a list of 6–12 solutions for each root cause, amounting to a total of 85 solutions. After all solutions were generated, the research team used generative artificial intelligence to summarize the 85 solutions into a one-page list for the CAB to review for comprehensiveness at the next meeting. The research team provided minimal edits to the summary prior to review by the CAB. Ultimately, the CAB verbally reached consensus on the list of solutions after making minor edits at the subsequent meeting.

For the present manuscript, the research team standardized impact and effort scores for each of the 85 solutions. First, identical solutions offered in response to more than one problem statement were grouped together. Next, conceptually similar ideas that mentioned identical or overlapping tools, resources, or strategies (e.g., improving naloxone access and education) were grouped together, amounting to a total of 28 categories of solutions. Within each category, z-scores for effort and impact were averaged. The averaged z-scores were used by the research team to place each of the 28 categories of solutions into one of four groups based on whether they were above or below the average effort and impact score. The four groups were: moderate impact and low effort; moderate impact and high effort; high impact and low effort; and high impact and high effort.

Perspectives on participation in the CAB and feedback on the activity

The study team drafted a list of open-ended questions (Supplementary Methods) to gather members’ perspectives on participating in the CAB and the activities described above. The study team introduced the opportunity to submit responses during a CAB meeting. Members could subsequently submit responses over a two-week period via text, email, voice memo, handwritten responses, or in-person to staff at Vivent Health, who dictated responses. Responses to each question were compiled in a document that listed each question and the responses read by three project staff. Using this document, one member of the research team drafted a summary of three areas, (1) members’ reflections on the importance of participating in the CAB, (2) what they appreciated about the activities, and (3) what they found challenging about the activities. The other two project staff subsequently reviewed and edited the summary. The summary was then reviewed with the entire research team to inform future refinements of CAB meeting processes and topics.

Results

Root causes of overdose

Members identified nine root causes of overdose, which represent their perspectives on the reasons why overdose risk remains pervasive among PWUD (Fig. 1). Below, we describe each root cause elicited by the CADCA process [29]. Root causes are organized by numbering, but each was equally important to the CAB and discussed in similar depth.

Root cause 1. Treatment & recovery programs are insufficient and inaccessible

Members discussed several challenges around stopping opioid use, including withdrawal symptoms and the sentiment that many people are using enough to function in daily life rather than to get high. Though members recognized the clinical evidence behind medications for opioid use disorders (MOUD), several described adverse experiences with buprenorphine, such as withdrawal symptoms or a lack of perceived effectiveness. While members favored methadone, they perceived it was harder to access. They did not discuss naltrexone. Members expressed difficulties connecting to treatment after adverse events (e.g., an overdose or visit to the emergency room).

Members also found it difficult to know what programs or treatments were available, what is covered by insurance, and what is required during MOUD program participation. Members who had received treatment described several challenges, including stigmatizing attitudes or interactions, punitive approaches, and a lack of person-centeredness. Abstinence-based treatments and self-help programs were particularly challenging; members noted that they lacked support during periods of continued use or relapse and feared being dismissed from treatment or support groups. Several experimental programs were viewed positively, including ketamine or psychedelic treatments and approaches not requiring abstinence.

Root cause 2. Life circumstances can influence overdose risk

Members identified unemployment, homelessness, incarceration, social isolation, and other structural factors that negatively affected their emotional state, motivation, and quality of life. Exclusionary housing and employment policies were particularly harmful to members with a criminal record. Barriers to escaping poverty, homelessness, and other hardships led to a sense of hopelessness and apathy about stopping drug use. These feelings increased overdose risk behaviors, including buying from a new dealer, using more than is typical, or not testing their drugs. Housing situations interacted with overdose in complex ways. Some members reported that certain cohabitated living settings, like stable tent cities, created a sense of community that led people to look out for one another, including by spotting one another’s drug use. Conversely, stable housing and employment created a need to hide drug use, leading to more solitary consumption.

Root cause 3. Untreated mental illness can influence behavior

Members expressed several concerns that described how mental health conditions contribute to increased overdose risk, such as using drugs to alleviate emotional pain, apathy toward risk reduction, and suicidality. These feelings of hopelessness and defeat echoed sentiments expressed in Root Cause #2 in response to suboptimal housing and other social conditions, and similarly led to apathy about stopping drug use or decreasing risk. Members discussed using drugs to cope with mental health conditions due to the difficulty of accessing mental health treatment. They also discussed the complexities of mental health in the context of an overdose, particularly difficulty distinguishing whether an overdose was accidental, intentional, or something in between.

Root cause 4. The criminal legal system has not fully adopted harm reduction principles

Members shared feelings of distrust, frustration, and anger with the criminal legal system because of mistreatment they experienced. Negative interactions with law enforcement led them to fear carrying harm reduction tools such as naloxone, drug test strips, and sterile syringes. While carrying naloxone, syringes, and test strips is legal in Wisconsin, these items were perceived to give officers potential probable cause for searching a vehicle or person, which was not worth the risk for some members.

Members were dissatisfied with the treatments available for substance use disorders during and after incarceration. They identified incarceration as a missed opportunity for treatment, leading to a return to drug use and elevated risk of overdose after release. Members felt law enforcement did not belong at the scene of an overdose as they had experienced discrimination (e.g., expressing apathy about survival of a person overdosing, use of stigmatizing terms) and criminal-legal consequences at the scene of an overdose. This led to avoidance or reticence to call 911.

Root cause 5. People frequently use drugs while alone

Members engaged in solitary drug use to conceal use from others despite acknowledging the risks associated with not having a bystander available to respond to an overdose. Members also used drugs alone when they were trying to avoid withdrawal during daily responsibilities (e.g., work). Newly available overdose response technologies that provide supervision of drug use by phone or apps and if needed, facilitate an overdose response (e.g., the Never Use Alone and SafeSpot telephone hotlines) were generally not used. Several members did not know about them. Substance use stigma also led to difficulty finding a trusted person to supervise their drug use within their peer network (i.e., a spotter). Instead of spotting specific drug use events, members said their most trusted confidants knew they were always using and checked in periodically. Finally, members were concerned about the potential repercussions of using overdose response technologies. For example, members were concerned about the consequences of law enforcement responding to the scene of an overdose or being taken to a hospital, where they feared being unable to pay for healthcare or anticipated being stigmatized by healthcare providers.

Root cause 6. Limited options to get harm reduction education or supplies

Across our Wisconsin-based study sites, SSPs were the primary harm reduction service accessed and available. Members appreciated SSPs but noted their limited operating hours and members’ lack of transportation as barriers to accessing SSPs. They knew peers who did not use SSPs because they did not want to be seen using services and identified as a PWUD, despite the fact that Vivent Health offers many services beyond the SSP. Members perceived PWUD who do not use available SSPs had limited access to education about risk reduction, awareness of what drugs might be circulating, or where to get sterile supplies, and perceived a need to build awareness of SSPs and other harm reduction programs among PWUD. They also perceived a need to build awareness of harm reduction programs and consensus on their effectiveness among the general public to reduce substance use stigma.

Root cause 7. Naloxone is still underutilized

Members identified naloxone as a critically important tool but identified gaps in access to naloxone for PWUD who do not use SSPs. They noted that naloxone can be difficult to find for free and that some locations for dispensing were suboptimal, including naloxone vending machines located near a law enforcement office. Several had experience with or worried about being stigmatized for carrying naloxone by the general public or law enforcement. Members also felt the general public had little knowledge about naloxone and how to respond to an overdose due to stigmatizing attitudes, apathy, and a lack of compassion among people unaffected by substance use or overdose.

Root Cause 8. Unstable and unregulated drug supply poses serious threats

Members were concerned by the unregulated illicit drug market. Members noted that while they expected fentanyl in the drug supply, inconsistent drug potency made it difficult to decide how much was safe to use. Further, they distrusted the drug supply due to novel contaminants such as xylazine. Members perceived that dealers could not be trusted to share information they knew about potential contamination, though some members stated dealers may also be unaware.

Root cause 9. Drug checking is inaccessible and often criminalized

The unregulated, unpredictable illicit drug supply led members to recognize the importance of test strips and other drug checking technologies. While members recognized a need to test drugs for emerging adulterants, they assumed their supply was always contaminated with fentanyl. Fentanyl test strips that only confirm presence vs. absence of fentanyl were therefore not useful to them. Members also had concerns that carrying test strips would give law enforcement probable cause to search them and about having them confiscated. While some used test strips regularly, others expressed greater enthusiasm for drug checking technologies that provide a more comprehensive description of all drugs in a tested sample (e.g., Fourier-transform infrared [FTIR] spectroscopy, mail-in gas chromatograph mass spectrometer testing). Unfortunately, access to point-of-care comprehensive testing was limited in Wisconsin.

Solutions to root causes of overdose

A total of 85 solutions were identified by members as potential responses to the nine root causes of overdose (Supplemental File 1). Of the 85 originally offered ideas, only four ideas were rated with an impact score less than three (out of five), indicating nearly all solutions could be impactful to the community. Therefore, we describe solutions in categories of high vs. moderate impact and high vs. low effort. After synthesizing the 85 solutions into categories, a total of 28 solutions for overdose prevention remained (Fig. 2).

High impact, low effort solutions

Of the 28 solutions, 7 fell into the ‘high impact, low effort’ category, indicating a perception that these were feasible solutions that if implemented, would have high benefit to communities. Three solutions focused on treatment systems and access, (1) improving access to public or other transportation, particularly to and from substance use disorder treatment, (2) including people with lived and living experience as treatment program frontline staff, and (3) improving quality and management of sober living homes and recovery residences. Another two solutions described improvements to harm reduction programming, including (4) implementing community-wide naloxone access, awareness, and training and (5) ensuring access to safer use supplies (e.g., syringes and other drug use equipment, naloxone, test strips) anytime, anywhere using mail-order and vending machines. Members also identified a need to (6) establish drug user unions and (7) improve app and internet-based options for community resource navigation (e.g., 211 Wisconsin, which provides online and telephone-based support for finding food pantries, utility assistance, substance use services, housing, mental health and domestic violence support, and other local services).

High impact, high effort solutions

Eleven solutions were considered “high impact, high effort,” indicating they would require substantial investment of effort but yield considerable benefit. Members suggested several harm reduction-oriented solutions that would expand or amend service models to reduce overdose risk, including (1) establishing a safe drug supply (e.g., through a government-regulated supply of drugs available via prescription or for purchase), (2) promoting and expanding existing harm reduction programs (e.g., SSPs), and (3) establishing overdose prevention centers (also called safe or supervised consumption sites). They also proposed legal reforms, including (4) implementing community-based overdose and crisis response teams (i.e., not involving law enforcement), (5) decriminalizing drugs and drug use, and (6) reforming background check and drug screening policies. Suggested improvements to treatment and social service systems included: (7) expanding non-abstinence based treatment and recovery support, (8) integrating substance use and mental health treatment programs, and (9) funding and co-locating social services (e.g., for housing and employment). Finally, members proposed (10) eliminating stigma and (11) inviting PWUD to help make decisions about Opioid Settlement Fund spending.

Moderate impact, low effort Solutions

Six “moderate impact, low effort” solutions include feasible strategies that may be less beneficial to communities than those discussed above. These included the following expansions or changes to harm reduction services: (1) leveraging pharmacies to provide naloxone and test strips, (2) creating community-based and “pop-up” harm reduction initiatives, (3) increasing education on test strips, and (4) increasing advertising and use of overdose response technologies that provide spotting. Members also suggested (5) codifying comprehensive protection from paraphernalia laws (i.e., for syringes, pipes, etc.) for harm reduction clients and (6) improving access to education and opportunities for advancement for PWUD.

Moderate impact, high effort solutions

Finally, four “moderate impact, high effort” solutions would require substantial effort to achieve more modest results. These included a variety of ideas to expand harm reduction services, reform policy, and behavioral solutions, including: (1) providing comprehensive, point-of-care drug checking (e.g., FTIR spectroscopy), (2) reforming the Good Samaritan Law in Wisconsin, (3) increasing harm reduction support and strategies within the criminal legal system and (4) boycotting the unsafe drug supply.

CAB member perspectives on participation and the activity

Five CAB members provided reflections. Four provided written responses, with two members collaborating to produce a single written response; a fifth CAB member provided their reflections during a telephone call with project staff. Regarding reflections on the importance of CAB participation, members discussed the benefits of being present both when research is designed and as solutions are tested and implemented. They expressed feelings of pride, gratitude, and purpose from having their ideas heard by the research team. They hoped that insights from the activities would be considered for future research and that research teams would increasingly incorporate perspectives from people with lived and living experience of drug use. When asked why PWUD should be included in research in this way, CAB members 1 and 2 shared the following written response:Because we’re the experts; yet so often neglected, misunderstood, shamed, and incarcerated; basically thrown away by society. Members appreciated several aspects of the activities that encouraged meaningful participation, including consistent CAB membership and empathetic facilitators, which built comfort with discussing sensitive topics. Members also recognized the benefits of having a diversity of perspectives, demographics, and experiences in CAB members to facilitate identification of a broad variety of effective solutions. Facilitators’ use of active listening and probing questions resulted in open dialogue and accurate summaries of members' perspectives at the conclusion of the activity. Regarding challenging aspects of the activities, members disliked narrowing the list of potential solutions and ranking on effort and impact. This aspect of the activity left them feeling discouraged after realizing some ideas were infeasible to implement in the current project. CAB Member 3 stated, “After having the ability to have our most extreme ideas heard, respected, and listed as potential solutions, it was difficult having to essentially cross them off the list.” At the same time, the process led them to identify barriers to actualizing systemic change in their community. For example, they recognized that advocating for policy changes was necessary to achieve their most impactful ideas, such as opening overdose prevention centers or improving access to drug checking.

Discussion

Our CAB identified nine root causes of overdose and proposed a variety of responsive solutions that addressed both proximal drivers of fatal and nonfatal overdose (e.g., solitary drug use, lack of comprehensive drug checking services) as well as structural and policy barriers (e.g., an inability to meet basic needs such as housing, the criminalization of drug use). In alignment with the recognition of stigma as a fundamental cause of health inequities [34], substance use stigma emerged as a clear, cross-cutting issue impacting each root cause and the likelihood of success of all strategies identified by the CAB [35]. Below we describe key takeaways of our findings in greater depth.

Several of the root causes identified by our CAB related to expanding harm reduction programs, including SSPs. Members noted that safer drug use supplies and naloxone distribution were not yet optimized to meet community demand. This corroborates prior literature that the number of SSPs [36] and naloxone distribution sites have increased [37], but programs remain underfunded [38]. Further, community naloxone distribution may benefit from a concerted effort to distribute naloxone to the people most likely to witness overdoses – PWUD [39]. Members also noted concerns about solitary drug use and suggested establishing overdose prevention centers would be highly impactful, though these are unfortunately only available in a handful of U.S. cities currently [40, 41]. Another strategy, increasing use of remote monitoring hotlines, apps, and other technologies, called overdose response technologies [42, 43], was viewed as moderately impactful. Taken together, these points suggest further research is needed to understand what factors influence the decision to set up a spotter or use an overdose response technology amidst the unpredictable drug supply. Members also noted the lack of available comprehensive drug checking locally, beyond test strips that provide only presence/absence results for fentanyl and xylazine, corroborating findings from a North American survey of drug checking services [44].

Several of the root causes and solutions identified by the CAB referenced evidence-based practices that were valued by members, but perceived to be inaccessible or requiring improvement based on members’ personal experiences. For example, despite appreciating the available, effective MOUD therapies, members reported challenges related to low initial doses and accessing healthcare due to stigma. Several members reported experiencing distressing withdrawal symptoms, particularly with buprenorphine, that impacted treatment success or interest in attempting this modality again. These experiences may indicate a need to revisit buprenorphine initiation dosing to maximize the effectiveness of long-term treatment retention based on data collected since the introduction of fentanyl to the illicit drug supply [45]. Prior qualitative research suggests methadone dosing may face similar concerns around breakthrough withdrawal symptoms [46]. Interestingly, naltrexone was not discussed by members, likely because difficulties starting naltrexone treatment [47] may diminish its use relative to available opioid agonists.

Members described complex socio-economic and individual-level factors that raised risk for overdose. For example, though stable housing may contribute to decreases in overall risk of non-fatal and fatal overdose [48, 49], it might simultaneously remove the consistent spotting support members reported receiving in tent cities. Also, while employment may increase financial security, solitary drug use to avoid withdrawal during the work day may contribute to fatal overdose risk. Fear and stigma were driving forces behind limited adoption of overdose risk reduction behaviors, particularly fear of law enforcement. Police encounters and perceived vulnerability to arrest tend to inhibit the use of sterile supplies or SSPs [50–54], but these patterns must be further dissected to fully understand the experiences described by members and their impact on use of harm reduction supplies and services. Importantly, a lack of access to housing, employment, and other basic needs as well as mental healthcare fueled a sense of hopelessness and distress that impacted drug use and overdose risk.

It was notable that < 5% of solutions identified to root causes of overdose were rated as low impact by our CAB, highlighting the importance of pursuing a comprehensive, multi-pronged response to the U.S. overdose crisis. When asked to reflect on the process, members were disappointed that many proposed solutions were beyond the scope of the project and/or difficult to implement. It is unsurprising that our activity elicited both actionable solutions and those beyond the scope of the research project, as facilitators encouraged brainstorming but stated that all the ideas generated here would not be actionable in a single research project at the beginning of the activity. This tension between the large number of solutions versus what can be achieved in a single project points to structural limitations of how grant funds can be applied and the complex, multi-level challenges in addressing the overdose crisis that the CAB outlined. The compressed timelines of research grant cycles often pressure researchers to concretize research questions and study designs before CABs can fully articulate local priorities, resulting in studies that are community-engaged in implementation yet still conceptually predetermined by previously written grants. Our CAB’s name, the Community Leadership Team, highlights our aspiration to fully share autonomy and authority over research aims and projects with members. However, this requires continued effort and financial investment to build capacity of researchers and members to collaboratively identify actionable issues and develop appropriate solutions. We are committed to disseminating the CAB’s ideas to broad audiences through the project website [56], local coalitions and community partners, and to academic audiences. Ultimately, the work described here provides an agenda for practice and policy reform that could be enacted by local communities using public funds such as Opioid Settlements. Within the context of future research, this activity provided the research team and community an opportunity to develop a partnership and shared agenda that can inform future research questions, in line with CBPR principles [22]. The subset of members who provided feedback suggested they felt strongly connected with the purpose of the work and appreciated the effective facilitation and distillation of the main points of discussion over multiple meetings, echoing facilitators of previously effective CAB-researcher partnerships [41].

While barriers to involving PWUD in research and planning do exist, we found that most barriers were easily overcome by implementing several best practices for CAB meetings. First, we host meetings in locations safe for members to go to (i.e., SSPs) and facilitators are SSP and study team staff that have built rapport and trust with members prior to inviting them to be part of the CAB. We also offer flexibility where possible, such as by allowing members to virtually participate or bring children to meetings with them if needed. We also compensate members for each meeting they attend (via cash or direct cash transfer based on their preference) and provide food at meetings. We extended this flexibility to our request for CAB member feedback by allowing members to send feedback in any format (i.e., hand-written, text message, email, voice memo, or in-person). Additionally, we wrote the present research grant with our SSP partner organization and compensated them via a grant subaward, ensuring alignment between the research team and community partner helping to recruit and conduct the research.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, while all members were asked to provide feedback on their experience and feedback was accepted in any format (written, verbal, voice memo), only five members provided feedback as brief, written comments (4 members in 3 responses) or verbally (1 member). This limited our ability to conduct a thematic qualitative analysis and we do not expect data saturation was achieved. Second, while we applied published methods to generate root causes and identify responsive solutions (i.e., CADCA and Nominal Group Technique), we adjusted our approach slightly from the published methods and improved processes meeting-to-meeting where possible, introducing some variation over time. For example, after receiving feedback from facilitators that our first meeting involving the generation of solutions felt rushed, we allowed more time for group discussion in subsequent meetings, potentially resulting in variation in solutions across root causes. Further, impact and effort ratings were based on members’ perspectives, which offer valuable insights but may differ from ratings among those ultimately implementing new public health programs. While CAB members reviewed and endorsed a summary of the list of solutions, they did not review our summary of effort and impact, as z-scores were calculated and ranked for the present manuscript, after the CAB activity concluded. Similarly, the process of collapsing similar solutions proposed by each study site was done through consensus of the research team and SSP staff after CAB meetings, introducing some subjectivity in our approach. Lastly, while most members have remained consistent over time, some members may not have been present at every meeting for the activity described, resulting in variability in who participated in the activities at each session. We additionally invite new members to join over time when a standing member stops attending.

Conclusions

Collectively, our results demonstrate a need for expanded, person-centered harm reduction services that meet individuals where they are at and meaningfully incorporate members of affected communities at every step. In conclusion, we leave the reader with a member’s summary of why community-engaged research is critical to reducing fatal and nonfatal overdoses in the present U.S. overdose crisis:“PWUD bring valuable insight that can’t be found in textbooks or academia…When we include these voices in studies, it’s not just ticking boxes: it’s making sure the research is relevant and grounded in reality. Together, we can create more ethical and effective solutions that truly serve our communities.” -CAB Member 3.
