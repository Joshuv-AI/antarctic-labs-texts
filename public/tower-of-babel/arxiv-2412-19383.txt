1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Quantum Difference Equation

Enumerative algebraic geometry (quantum K-theory) of Nakajima varieties is governed by quantum difference equations (QDE)
[
25
]
which have the following form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbold{z}q^{\mathcal{L}},\mathbold{a},q)\mathcal{L}={\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q),\ \ \ \mathcal{L}\in\mathcal{Z}\,,

where
\mathcal{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{l}
is the lattice of tautological line bundles on a Nakajima variety
X
and
l
denotes the number of vertices in the corresponding quiver. The variables
\mathbold{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{l})
and
\mathbold{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
denote the Kähler and the equivariant parameters, respectively. The shift of the Kähler variables is of the form

\mathbold{z}q^{\mathcal{L}}=(z_{1}q^{c_{1}},\dots,z_{l}q^{c_{l}}),

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
are integers determined by the expansion

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=L_{1}^{c_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes L_{l}^{c_{l}},

in the basis of the lattice
\mathcal{Z}
given by the tautological line bundles
L_{i}

Let
\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
be the fundamental solution matrix of (
1.1
) given by a power series in
\mathbold{z}
and uniquely determined by the normalization

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\sum\limits_{d\in C}\Psi_{d}(\mathbold{a},q)\mathbold{z}^{d}\in K_{T}(X)[[\mathbold{z}]],

where
C\subset\mathcal{Z}
is a cone determined by a choice of stability condition for
X
.

The matrix
\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
provides the
capping operator
– the fundamental object in enumerative geometry which can be defined as the partition function of quasimaps with relative and non-singular boundary conditions, see Section 7.4 in
[
26
]
for definitions.

Difference connection (
1.1
) is flat, i.e., for any two line bundles
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
we have

(1.4)

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)={\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z}q^{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\mathbold{a},q){\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)={\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\mathbold{z}q^{\mathcal{L}_{2}},\mathbold{a},q){\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q).

Explicit formulae for
{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
in terms of representation theory of quantum groups were obtained in
[
25
]
. An alternative description of these operators in terms of the elliptic stable envelope classes was also obtained in
[
16
,
17
]
. In any chosen basis of the equivariant
K
-theory
K_{T}(X)
the operators
{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
are represented by matrices with coefficients given by rational functions in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
.

1.2.
Quantum K-theory

It follows from the definition of the shift operators
{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
, namely, from the properness of the relative quasimap moduli space, that they do not have poles in
q
, see Section 8.1 in
[
26
]
. In particular, these operators have well-defined specializations at
q=1
.

Let us consider the following operators

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=\left.{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)\right|_{q=1}.

From (
1.4
) we see that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})

i.e. these operators commute

(1.6)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a}),\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})]=0,\ \ \ \forall\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

In
[
27
,
18
]
we showed that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
are the operators of quantum multiplication by
\mathcal{L}
in the equivariant quantum K-theory ring
QK_{T}(X)
. This ring is commutative which agrees with (
1.6
).

An interesting problem is to describe the joint set of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the operators
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
. It is conjectured that the joint spectrum of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
,
\mathcal{L}\in\mathcal{Z}
is simple
1
1

1

This conjecture is known for type
A
-flag varieties
X=T^{*}(G/P)
, since in this case the classical multiplication by generic
\mathcal{L}
has simple spectrum. It is also known for
X=Hilb^{n}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
, via cohomological limit
[
24
]
. We expect that it can also be proved for affine type
A
quiver varieties by carefull analysis of Bethe roots, e.g.,
[
29
]
.
, which implies that they generate the quantum K-theory ring
QK_{T}(X)
.

1.3.
Eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
and Bethe Ansatz

The above-mentioned eigenvalue problem also arises naturally in the theory of quantum integrable spin chains
[
27
,
18
]
. For any quiver variety
X
there is a quantum group
\mathcal{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{X})
which acts on its equivariant K-theory
K_{T}(X)
, see Section 3 of
[
25
]
for the construction. This action identifies
K_{T}(X)
with the quantum Hilbert space of a certain XXZ-type spin chain. In this setting, the algebra of commuting Hamiltonians of the spin chain is identified with the algebra generated by operators of quantum multiplication by the K-theory classes, i.e., with the commutative algebra
QK_{T}(X)
. In particular, the operators
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
represent certain Hamiltonians of the corresponding XXZ spin chain. Namely,
the operators of quantum multiplication by line bundles

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
appear as the “top” coefficients of the Baxter
Q
-operators of the spin chain
[
27
]
. Describing the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of these Hamiltonians is a classical problem in quantum mechanics.

The algebraic Bethe Ansatz
[
11
]
is a method used in the theory of integrable models to diagonalize spin chain Hamiltonians. Let
\mathcal{V}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,l
be the set of tautological bundles over a Nakajima variety
X
. Let
\mathbold{x}=\{x_{i,j}\}
denote the collection of the Grothendieck roots of these vector bundles, so that in K-theory we have:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{i}=x_{i,1}+\dots+x_{i,r_{i}},\ \ r_{i}=\textrm{rk}{\mathcal{V}_{i}}.

The tautological line bundles are given by
L_{i}=\det\mathcal{V}_{i}=x_{i,1}\cdot\dots\cdot x_{i,r_{i}}
.
By (
1.2
) every line bundle
\mathcal{L}
can be represented as a certain product of the Grothendieck roots

(1.8)

\mathcal{L}=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{r_{i}}x_{i,j}\right)^{c_{i}}\,.

In a nutshell, the algebraic Bethe Ansatz asserts
[
2
]
that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
are given by the same product

(1.9)

\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbold{a},\mathbold{z})=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{r_{i}}x_{i,j}\right)^{c_{i}}\,,

where
x_{i,j}
are now certain functions of
\mathbold{z}
and
\mathbold{a}
determined as the roots of the algebraic equations, known as the Bethe equations:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}(\mathbold{x},\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=0.

Equations (
1.10
) are constructed explicitly from the underlying quiver
[
2
]
. We recall this construction in Section
3
. In essence, each solution to the Bethe equations (
1.10
) provides
x_{i,j}
as specific functions of the parameters
\mathbold{z}
and
\mathbold{a}
. Substituting those functions into (
1.9
) gives an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
.

1.4.
The Case
q^{p}=1

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\zeta_{p}\in\mathbb{C}
be a primitive
p
-th root of unity
2
2

2

At this point
p
does not have to be prime, that would be required later in Section
5
.
.
For a line bundle
\mathcal{L}
we consider the operator
{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
. By iterating (
1.4
)
p
-times we have:

(1.11)

{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)={\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z}q^{(p-1)\mathcal{L}},\mathbold{a},q){\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z}q^{(p-2)\mathcal{L}},\mathbold{a},q)\cdots{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z}q^{\mathcal{L}},\mathbold{a},q){\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q).

We denote its value at
q=\zeta_{p}
by:

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})={\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\zeta_{p})\,.

It is evident from (
1.4
) that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}\mathcal{L}_{2},\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1},\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2},\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2},\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1},\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a}).

In particular, these operators commute

[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a}),\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})]=0,\ \ \ \forall\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}.

It is, therefore, natural to study the joint set of eigenvalues for these operators. Surprisingly, this problem has not been considered yet.

In this paper we prove that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
can be obtained from the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a}),\lambda_{2}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a}),\dots\}
be the set of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
then the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}\,\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
are given by the set

\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p}),\lambda_{2}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p}),\dots\}

where

\lambda_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})

is the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}(z,a)
in which all Kähler variables
\mathbold{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{l})
and equivariant variables
\mathbold{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
are substituted by
\mathbold{z}^{p}=(z^{p}_{1},\dots,z^{p}_{l})
and
\mathbold{a}^{p}=(a^{p}_{1},\dots,a^{p}_{m})
respectively.

As we explain in the previous subsection, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
can be determined from the Bethe equations. In this way, the spectrum of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
is also controlled by these equations.

1.5.
The Intertwiner

Theorem
1.1
is a corollary of the following pole cancellation property of the fundamental solution matrix
\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
. The coefficients of the power series expansion (
1.3
) have poles in
q
located at the roots of unity.
In particular,
\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
is singular at
q=\zeta_{p}
.

Let
\Psi(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}})
be the fundamental solution matrix in which all Kähler and equivariant parameters are raised to power
p
while the variable
q
is raised to the power
p^{2}
. We prove the following pole-cancellation property.

Theorem 1.2
.

The operator

\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)\Psi(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}})^{-1}

has no poles in
q
located at primitive complex
p
-th roots of unity.

Let us define

(1.13)

\mathsf{F}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\zeta_{p})=\Psi(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)\Psi(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}})^{-1}\Big|_{q=\zeta_{p}}

It can be shown from the difference equation (
1.1
) that

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\zeta_{p})\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})\mathsf{F}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\zeta_{p})^{-1}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})\,,

where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})
denotes the operator of quantum multiplication (
1.5
) in which all variables are raised to power
p
. The isospectrality Theorem
1.1
thus follows as an obvious corollary to (
1.14
).

The operator (
1.13
) is a complex field analog of the Frobenius intertwiner
[
30
]
which is defined over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. We note, however, a difference – the intertwiner constructed in
[
30
]
does not have poles at
p
-adic roots of unity of order
p^{s}
for any
s
, while (
1.13
) is only defined at the roots of the order
p
.

1.6.
p
-Curvature and Frobenius

The concept of
p
-curvature originated in Grothendieck’s unpublished work from the 1960s and was subsequently developed further by Katz
[
14
,
15
]
. The
p
-curvature plays an important role in the theory of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) as well as holonomic PDEs, establishing a connection between the existence of algebraic fundamental solutions and their behavior under reduction modulo a prime
p
. Specifically, if an algebraic solutions exist, then for almost all primes the reduction of the ODE modulo
p

exhibits zero
p
-curvature. The converse, however, remains an open question and is known as the Grothendieck-Katz conjecture.

Recently, Jae Hee Lee gave an enumerative interpretation of the
p
-curvature operators
[
20
]
. In
[
12
,
31
]
a new class of operators was defined in the study of quantum cohomology modulo a prime
p
, which are known as
quantum Steenrod operations
. In his work, Jae Hee Lee showed that the quantum Steenrod operations coincide with the
p
-curvature of quantum connection for a large class of symplectic resolutions, including Nakajima varieties with isolated torus fixed points under the assumption of simplicity of the quantum multiplication. In
[
3
]
the agreement between the
p
-curvature and quantum Steenrod operations has also been shown for hypertoric varieties. Most recently, in
[
4
]
Bai and Lee discussed quantum Adams operations which are the K-theoretic generalizations of the quantum Steenrod operations, and are studied in this paper in the setting of quasimaps.

In Section
5
we show that our operators
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
(
1.12
) provide a proper
K
-theoretic generalization of the
p
-curvature once all its parameters are specialized to their
p
-adic values. Namely, we consider an extension of the
p
-adic field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
where
\pi
solves the equation
\pi^{p-1}=-p
. The ideal
(\pi)
in the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\pi]\subset\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
of this field is maximal with the quotient field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\pi]/(\pi)=\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Using this property, we analyze operator
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
near
\zeta_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
given by a primitive
p
-th root of unity, and then reduce it modulo
(\pi)
to the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. We show that under this reduction to
\mathbb{F}_{p}
the operator
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{p}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
specializes precisely to the
p
-curvature of the quantum connection on
X
. Our main isospectrality Theorem
1.1
then reduces to a result describing the eigenvalues of the
p
-curvature:

Theorem 1.3
.

The
p
-curvature
C_{p}(\nabla_{i})
of the quantum connection for a Nakajima variety

\nabla_{i}=z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}-s\,C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u}),\ \ \ i=1,\dots,l,

and the Frobenius twist of the quantum multiplication by the divisor
(s^{p}-s)C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})^{(1)}
have the same spectrum over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

We refer to Section
5
and Theorems
5.4
and
5.5
for notations and details.

We note also that this theorem was recently proven by Etingof and Varchenko
[
9
,
10
]
, using a very different approach – they introduced and studied a large family of differential operators, called
periodic pencils
, which include among many other examples, the quantum connections of Nakajima varieties. Theorem
1.3
is deduced in
[
9
]
from various strong properties of the periodic pencils and semiclassical analysis.

z\mapsto z^{p},\qquad a_{1}\mapsto a_{1}^{p},\qquad a_{2}\mapsto a_{2}^{p},\qquad\hbar\mapsto\hbar^{p}\,.

This illustrates the statement of Theorem
1.1
in this simple example.

Finally, we also note that the relation in the quantum K-theory ring (
1.15

X
. Solving this quadratic equation for

gives two eigenvalues of matrix (

2.
Asymptotics of Vertex Functions

The quantum difference equation of a Nakajima variety
X
has a natural basis of solutions given by the
K
-theory components of the vertex function
[
1
]
. These functions can be viewed as generalizations of the classical
q
-hypergeometric series – the components of the vertex function of the simplest Nakajima variety
X=T^{*}\mathbb{P}^{n}
are exactly the
q
-hypergeometric series, see
[
6
,
8
]
for explicit examples.

Similarly to the
q
-hypergeometric functions, the vertex functions have natural integral representations of Mellin-Barnes type, see Section 3 of
[
1
]
. The integral representations can be used for computing the asymptotics of the vertex functions as
q\longrightarrow 1
, or, more generally, as
q\longrightarrow\zeta
, where
\zeta
is a root of unity, using the method of steepest descent.

The saddle point equations for the steepest descend method appearing at
q\longrightarrow 1
are precisely the Bethe equations, e.g., see Proposition 4.2 in
[
27
]
. In this Section we compute the corresponding saddle point equations for
q\longrightarrow\zeta
and show that they are given by the same Bethe equations with all variables raised to the power
p
, where
p
is the order of the root of unity
\zeta
, see
Corollary
3.2
.

2.1.
Integral representations of the vertex functions

Let
X
be a Nakajima quiver variety associated to an oriented quiver
Q
.
For a vertex
i\in Q
let
\mathcal{V}_{i}
be the
i
-th tautological bundle and
\mathcal{W}_{i}
be the framing tautological bundle over
X
(i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{i}
is a trivial bundle). We recall that the K-theory class

(2.1)

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i\longrightarrow j}\,\mathcal{V}^{*}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{j}+\bigoplus_{i\in Q}\mathcal{W}^{*}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{i}-\sum\limits_{i\in Q}\,\mathcal{V}_{i}^{*}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{i}

where the first sum is over the edges of the quiver
Q

connecting vertices
i
and
j
,
is called the canonical polarization of
X
. This K-theory class represents a “half” of the tangent bundle, in the sense that:

(2.2)

\displaystyle TX=P+\hbar^{-1}\,P^{*}.

Let
L_{i}=\det\mathcal{V}_{i}
,
i\in Q
denote the set of tautological line bundles over
X
. We have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\det P=\bigotimes_{i\in Q}\,L^{n_{i}}_{i},\ \ \ n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Following the notation of
[
2
]
we denote by

\mathbold{z}_{\#}=\mathbold{z}(-\hbar^{1/2})^{-\det P}

the set of shifted Kähler variables.
More precisely, in components these shifts are equal:

z_{\#,i}=z_{i}(-\hbar^{1/2})^{-n_{i}},\ \ \ i\in Q.

Let
\varphi(x,q)
denote the
q
-analog of the reciprocal Gamma function:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\varphi(x,q)=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-xq^{i})

We extend this function to Laurent polynomials
\sum_{i}n_{i}a_{i}
,
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
by the rule (omitting the second argument of
\varphi
for brevity)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\Phi(\sum_{i}n_{i}x_{i})=\prod_{i}\varphi(x_{i})^{n_{i}}.

Using (
2.1
) and (
1.7
) we can represent the polarization
P
by a Laurent polynomial
in the Grothendieck roots of the tautological bundles
\mathbold{x}
and the equivariant parameters
\mathbold{a}
, so that

P=\sum_{w\in N(P)}\,w,

where
N(P)
denotes the Newton polygon of
P
and
w
are monomials in the variables
\mathbold{x}
and
\mathbold{a}
.

Let
\tau\in K_{T}(X)
be a K-theory class represented by a Laurent polynomial in the Grothendieck roots
\tau(\mathbold{x})
. We recall that the components of the vertex function of a Nakajima variety with descendant
\tau
have the following integral representation:

(2.6)

\displaystyle V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z})=\int_{\gamma_{i}}\Phi((q-\hbar)P)e(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{x})\,\tau(\mathbold{x})\,\prod\limits_{a,b}\dfrac{dx_{a,b}}{x_{a,b}}\,,

where
V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z})
denotes the
i
-th component of the vertex function in the basis of the torus fixed points,

e(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{x})=\prod\limits_{i\in Q}\,\exp\left(\dfrac{\log(z_{\#,i})\log(L_{i})}{\log(q)}\right),

the function
\Phi((q-\hbar)P)
is constructed from the polynomial
(q-\hbar)P
by rule (
2.5
), and
\gamma_{i}
is the contour defined by (A.12) in
[
1
]
.

2.2.
Integrand of (
2.6
) near roots of unity.

The infinite product (
2.4
) converges for
|q|<1
. Thus, the integrand of (
2.6
) is well defined only for
|q|<1
. However, when
q
approaches
1\in\mathbb{C}
, the divergent part can be separated as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

The integrand of (
2.6
) has the following form

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Phi((q-\hbar)P)e(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{x})=\exp\left(-\dfrac{Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{1-q}\right)\cdot\star

where
\star
stands for a function of all parameters regular at
q=1
and

(2.8)

\displaystyle Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})=\sum\limits_{w\in N(P)}\,({\rm Li}_{2}(w)-{\rm Li}_{2}(\hbar w))+\sum\limits_{i\in Q}\,\log(z_{\#,i})\log(L_{i})\,,

where
{\rm Li}_{2}(w)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\frac{x^{m}}{m^{2}}
denotes the dilogarithm function.

The function
Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})
is known as the
Yang-Yang
function in the literature on integrable systems
[
22
,
23
]
.

Proof.

We have

(2.9)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\varphi(qw)}{\varphi(\hbar w)}=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1-qwq^{i}}{1-\hbar wq^{i}}=\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(q^{m}-\hbar^{m})w^{m}}{m(1-q^{m})}\right)

Note that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(q^{m}-\hbar^{m})w^{m}}{m(1-q^{m})}\right)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{1-q}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(1-\hbar^{m})w^{m}}{m^{2}}\right)\cdot\star

where

\star=\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{w^{m}}{m}\left(\dfrac{q^{m}-\hbar^{m}}{1-q^{m}}-\dfrac{1-\hbar^{m}}{m(1-q)}\right)\right).

Also note that
\star
is regular at
q=1
:

\left.\star\right|_{q=1}=\exp\left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{m+1+m\hbar^{m}-\hbar^{m}}{2m^{2}}w^{m}\right)

Thus, we may write:

\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(q^{m}-\hbar^{m})w^{m}}{m(1-q^{m})}\right)\sim\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{1-q}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(1-\hbar^{m})w^{m}}{m^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{\mathrm{Li}_{2}(w)-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(\hbar w)}{1-q}\right)

where
\sim
denotes equality modulo terms regular at
q=1
. Similarly,

\dfrac{1}{\log(q)}\sim-\dfrac{1}{1-q}

and hence

e(z)\sim\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{1-q}\sum\limits_{i\in Q}\log(z_{\#,i})\log(L_{i})\right)

Combining these results for all factors gives the Yang-Yang function (
2.8
).
∎

More generally, let
\zeta_{p}
be a
p
-th primitive complex root of unity of order

. The divergent part of (

Proposition 2.2
.

The integrand of (
2.6

(2.11)

\displaystyle\Phi((q-\hbar)P)e(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{x})=\exp\left(-\dfrac{Y(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}^{p})}{(1-q^{p})p}\right)\cdot\star

where
Y(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}^{p})
denotes the Yang-Yang function
(
2.8
) in which all the variables are raised to the power
p
and
\star
denotes a function regular at
q=\zeta_{p}
.

Proof.

We note that as
q\longrightarrow\zeta_{p}
the divergent terms in the sum of (
2.9
) correspond to the summands with
m
divisible by
p
. Separating these terms we obtain

\dfrac{\varphi(qw)}{\varphi(\hbar w)}=\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(q^{pm}-\hbar^{pm})w^{pm}}{pm(1-q^{pm})}\right)\cdot\star

where
\star
stands for a factor regular at
q=\zeta_{p}
.
Further, as in (
2.10
) we obtain

\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(q^{pm}-\hbar^{pm})w^{pm}}{pm(1-q^{pm})}\right)\sim\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{(1-q^{p})p}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(1-\hbar^{pm})w^{pm}}{m^{2}}\right)

where
\sim
now denotes equality up to multiples regular at
q=\zeta_{p}
. Next,

e(z)=\prod\limits_{i\in Q}\,\exp\left(\dfrac{\log(z_{\#,i}^{p})\log(L_{i}^{p})}{p\log(q^{p})}\right)

From which we see that

e(z)\sim\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{(1-q^{p})p}\,\sum_{i\in Q}\,\log(z_{\#,i}^{p})\log(L_{i}^{p})\right)

Combining these results for all the factors proves the Proposition.

2.3.
Asymptotics of vertex functions via steepest descend method

By Proposition
2.1
as
q\longrightarrow 1
the descendent vertex functions are given by the integrals

V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\int_{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{(1-q)}\right)\,\cdot\star\cdot\dfrac{d\mathbold{x}}{\mathbold{x}}

where
\star
denotes terms regular at
q=1
. Using the steepest descent method with small parameter
\epsilon=1-q
, we find that

(2.12)

V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\exp\Big(-\dfrac{\lambda_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})}{1-q}\Big)\cdot\star

where

(2.13)

\lambda_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a}))

is the value of the Yang-Yang function at its critical point
\mathbold{x}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})
on the contour
\gamma
and
\star
denotes terms regular at
q=1
.

The critical points of the Yang-Yang function are the solutions to equations

(2.14)

\mathfrak{B}_{i,k}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x}):=x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}\,Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})=0,\ \ i\in Q,\ \ k=1,\dots,\textrm{rk}(\mathcal{V}_{i}).

In Section
3
we recall an explicit form of these equations, in particular, we recall that (
2.14
) is equivalent to a system of algebraic equations also known as “Bethe equations” in the theory of integrable systems.

Next, assume that
q
approaches a complex root of unity
\zeta_{p}

of order
p
. By Proposition
2.2
, the descendant vertex functions are given by the integrals

V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\int_{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{Y(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}^{p})}{(1-q^{p})p}\right)\,\cdot\star\cdot\dfrac{d\mathbold{x}}{\mathbold{x}}

where
\star
is a function regular at
q=\zeta_{p}
.
In this case, the critical points for the steepest descend method are given by solutions to the following equations

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}Y(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}^{p})=p(x_{i,k})^{p}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial(x_{i,k})^{p}}Y(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}^{p})=0

i.e., the critical point equations are

(2.15)

\mathfrak{B}_{i,k}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}^{p})=0,\ \ i\in Q,\ \ k=1,\dots,\textrm{rk}(\mathcal{V}_{i}).

with the same
\mathfrak{B}_{i,k}
as in (
2.14
).

Note that if
\mathbold{x}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})

solves the critical point equations (
2.15
). Combining this together, we find that near

the vertex functions have the following behaviour:

V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{\lambda_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})}{(1-q^{p})p}\right)\cdot\star\,,

where
\star
denotes a power series in
\mathbold{z}
with coefficients which do not have poles at
q=\zeta_{p}
. Note that

\lambda(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})=Y(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},\mathbold{x}_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p}))

is the critical value function (
2.13

-th power
\mathbold{z}\longrightarrow\mathbold{z}^{p}
,
\mathbold{a}\longrightarrow\mathbold{a}^{p}

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
V^{(\tau)}_{i}\left(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}}\right)
be the vertex functions in which all Kähler parameters
\mathbold{z}
and all equivariant parameters
\mathbold{a}
are raised to power
p
, and
q
is raised to power
p^{2}
. This function has the following form:

V_{i}^{(\tau)}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}})=\exp\left(-\dfrac{\lambda_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})}{(1-q^{p})p}\right)\cdot\star

where

\star
is a power series in
\mathbold{z}
with coefficients which do not have poles at
q=\zeta_{p}
.

Proof.

Note that when
q\longrightarrow\zeta_{p}
we have
q^{p^{2}}\longrightarrow 1
. Thus, by (
2.12
) we have :

V_{i}^{(\tau)}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}})\longrightarrow\exp\left(-\dfrac{\lambda_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p})}{(1-q^{p^{2}})}\right)\cdot\star

The Corollary holds since

\dfrac{1}{1-q^{p^{2}}}=\dfrac{1}{(1-q^{p})(1+q^{p}+\dots+q^{p(p-1)})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q\longrightarrow\zeta_{p}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\dfrac{1}{(1-q^{p})p}

∎

We recall that the coefficients of the power series
V^{(\tau)}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)
have poles in

located at the roots of unity. The last two corollaries imply that the coefficients of the power series

\dfrac{V_{i}^{(\tau)}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p^{2}})}{V^{(\tau^{\prime})}_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)}

do not have poles at

3.
Bethe Equations

3.1.
Bethe Equations for Yang-Yang Functions

The critical points of the Yang-Yang functions
Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})
are determined by the equations

(3.1)

\displaystyle x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}=0,\ \ i\in Q,\ \ k=1,\dots,\textrm{rk}(\mathcal{V}_{i}).

In the theory of integrable spin chains these equations appear as
Bethe Ansatz equations
.
These equations can be written in the following convenient form. Let us define the following function

\hat{a}(x)=x^{1/2}-x^{-1/2}

and extend it by linearity to Laurent polynomials with integral coefficients by the rule

\hat{a}\left(\sum_{i}\,m_{i}x_{i}\right)=\prod_{i}\,\hat{a}(x_{i})^{m_{i}},

where
x_{i}
denote monomials in the equivariant parameters and
x_{i,k}
.

Let
TX
be the K-theory class of the tangent space (
2.2
), written as a Laurent polynomial in Grothendieck roots
\mathbold{x}=\{x_{i,j}\}
and the equivariant parameters for the canonical choice of polarization (
2.1
). The following description of the Bethe equations was obtained in
[
2
]
:

Proposition 3.1

(Proposition 9,
[
2
]
)
.

Bethe Ansatz equations (
3.1
) have the following form

(3.2)

\displaystyle\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}TX\right)=z_{i},\ \ i\in Q,\ \ k=1,\dots,rk(\mathcal{V}_{i}).

Proof.

For the Yang-Yang function (
2.8
) we write

Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})=Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})+Y_{2}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})

with

Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})=\sum\limits_{w\in N(P)}\,({\rm Li}_{2}(w)-{\rm Li}_{2}(\hbar w)),\ \ \ Y_{2}(\mathbold{x},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{z})=\sum\limits_{i\in Q}\,\log(z_{\#,i})\log(L_{i})

where
L_{i}=\det\mathcal{V}_{i}=\prod_{j}\,{x_{i,j}}
.
Let

W(w)={\rm Li}_{2}(w)-{\rm Li}_{2}(\hbar w)

be a summand of
Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{x})
. Since
w
is a monomial in the Grothendieck roots
x_{i,k}
we compute

(3.3)

\displaystyle w\dfrac{\partial W(w)}{\partial x_{i,k}}=\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\dfrac{(1-\hbar^{m})w^{m}}{m}=-\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\log\left(\dfrac{1-w}{1-\hbar w}\right).

A straightforward calculation gives

(3.4)

\displaystyle\log\left(\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}(w+\frac{1}{\hbar w})\right)\right)=\dfrac{x_{i,k}}{w}\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\log(-\hbar^{1/2})+\dfrac{x_{i,k}}{w}\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\log\left(\frac{1-w}{1-\hbar w}\right).

Combining (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) we get

(3.5)

\displaystyle x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial W(w)}{\partial x_{i,k}}=\dfrac{x_{i,k}}{w}\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\log(-\hbar^{-1/2})-\log\left(\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}(w+\frac{1}{\hbar w})\right)\right)\,.

Wring (
2.3
) as

\det P=\prod\limits_{i\in Q}\left(\prod_{k}x_{i,k}\right)^{n_{i}},

we see that

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial\det P}{\partial x_{i,k}}=n_{i}\det P,

but from
\det P=\prod_{w\in N(P)}w
we obtain

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial\det P}{\partial x_{i,k}}=\det P\sum_{w\in N(P)}\dfrac{x_{i,k}}{w}\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}.

Comparing the last two expressions we obtain

(3.6)

\displaystyle n_{i}=\sum_{w\in N(P)}\dfrac{x_{i,k}}{w}\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\,.

From (
2.2
) for the virtual tangent space we have

TX=\sum\limits_{w\in N(P)}\,\left(w+\dfrac{1}{\hbar w}\right).

Since

Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})=\sum\limits_{w\in N(P)}\,W(w)

summing (
3.5
) over
w
gives

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}=\log(-\hbar^{-1/2})\left(\sum\limits_{w}\dfrac{x_{i,k}}{w}\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x_{i,k}}\right)-\log\left(\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}TX\right)\right)

or, by (
3.6
) we have

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}=\log(-\hbar^{-1/2})n_{i}-\log\left(\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}TX\right)\right).

Note also that

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y_{2}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}=\log(z_{i,\#})=\log(z_{i})-\log(-\hbar^{1/2})n_{i}.

Overall we obtain

x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}=x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y_{1}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}+x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial Y_{2}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},\mathbold{x})}{\partial x_{i,k}}=-\log\left(\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}TX\right)\right)+\log(z_{i}).

Therefore the Bethe equations (
3.2
) are

-\log\left(\hat{a}\left(x_{i,k}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,k}}TX\right)\right)+\log(z_{i})=0,

which, after exponentiation finishes the proof of the Proposition.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The transformation
\mathbold{a}\mapsto\mathbold{a}^{p}
,
\mathbold{z}\mapsto\mathbold{z}^{p}
,
q\mapsto q^{p^{2}}

may not look symmetric. This visible asymmetry may have the following simple interpretation in terms of
q
-quantized coordinate rings: let is consider an algebra of
q
-commuting coordinates
X
,
Y
with relations

XY=qYX

The Frobenius transformation acts on the coordinates by
X\longrightarrow X^{p}
,
Y\longrightarrow Y^{p}
, which are now subject to relations

X^{p}Y^{p}=q^{p^{2}}Y^{p}X^{p}

which means that
q\longrightarrow q^{p^{2}}
under the Frobenius. In this context,
q
-deformations of Frobenius structures were also investigated by M. Kontsevich, as communicated to the second author during his visit to IHES in the Summer of 2025.

Remark 4.5
.

Since the analysis of the preceding sections relies on the integral representations of vertex functions from
[
1
]
we would like to comment on these integral formulae.

The vertex functions of Nakajima quiver varieties, come with natural power series representations:

(4.14)

\displaystyle V(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a})=\sum_{\mathbold{d}\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\textrm{eff}}}{\mathbold{z}^{\mathbold{d}}}I({q^{\mathbold{d}}},q,\mathbold{a})

where the sum runs over the cone of effective curves in
X
and
I(\mathbold{x},\mathbold{a})
is a certain product of
q
-gamma functions constructed from the underlying quiver in a combinatorial way. These power series representations come from computing the vertex function via equivariant localization, i.e., the coefficients represent the contributions of the torus fixed points on the quasimap moduli spaces. The purpose of the integral formula of
[
1
]
is to merely represent this power series as a summation over the residues of a global function.
Thus, it may be more natural to derive the asymptotic behavior of the vertex function directly from the series representation (
4.14
) using the multidimensional Laplace method for sums. According to this method, the asymptotics are governed by the critical points of the phase function
\log I(\mathbold{x},q,\mathbold{a})
as
q\longrightarrow\zeta_{p}
. The resulting computations are equivalent to the critical-point analysis of the the preceding sections.

The analytic aspects of the integral representations of vertex functions have been studied extensively in the literature on integrable systems. Halanäs and Ruijsenaars
[
13
]
(see section 2) constructed an analytic formula for the eigenfunction of the Ruijsenaars-Macdonald operators which in our geometric language corresponds to the vertex functions for the cotangent bundle to the complete flag variety. The infinite products in the integrand can be analytically continued to the double sine functions.
More generally, the correspondence between integrability of the Rujisenaars system and geometry of quiver varieties was addressed in
[
19
]
(see Theorem 4.8).

5.

In this final section, we discuss a reduction of the isospectrality Theorem

First, we recall that over

5.1.
Quantum connection

The quantum differential equation for a Nakajima variety

has the form:

(5.1)

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}\tilde{\Psi}_{coh}(z)=0,\qquad\nabla_{i}=z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}-s\,C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u}),\ \ \ i=1,\dots,l,

denote the equivariant parameters coming from torus action on

and

denotes the equivariant parameter corresponding to the action of the torus

on the source of the stable maps

\nabla=(\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{l})

For Nakajima varieties, the three-point functions associated with quantum multiplication by divisors receive no contributions from the orbifold points and are integral-valued, i.e., they lie in

where the sum is taken over the positive roots of the Lie algebra corresponding to the quiver

. Positivity is defined by the choice of stability condition

and

act in the stable basis of the cohomology of

with integral coefficients. Expanding the rational coefficients in (

in the stable basis lie in

(5.3)

\displaystyle(C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u}))_{a,b}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbold{u}][[\mathbold{z}]]\cap\mathbb{Q}(\mathbold{u},\mathbold{z}).

Let
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
be the field of

-adic numbers,
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\subset\mathbb{Q}_{p}
be the ring of integers.
The above integrality allows for base change to

and

We would like to emphasize the following point. The integrality of quantum multiplication by divisors in the cohomological stable basis allows us to associate to connection

:

(5.8)

\tilde{\Psi}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\tilde{\Psi}_{coh}(z)+O(\epsilon)

We write (
5.7
) as

\Big({\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}q^{z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}}\Big)\tilde{\Psi}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)=\tilde{\Psi}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)

Substituting (
5.5
), (
5.6
) and (
5.8
) into this equation, we obtain the quantum differential equation (
5.1
) as the linear term in the
\epsilon
-expansion.

5.3.
Extension
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)

Let
p
be a prime number and let
|\cdot|_{p}
denote the multiplicative
p
-adic norm

|p|_{p}=\dfrac{1}{p}.

We consider an extension
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
where
\pi
denotes a root of the equation
\pi^{p-1}=-p
.
Clearly, the
p
-adic norm of
\pi
equals:

(5.9)

|\pi|_{p}=\dfrac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}<1.

The field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
contains all
p
th roots of unity
\zeta_{p}
, which are of the form

(5.10)

\zeta_{p}=1+b\pi+O(\pi^{2})\,,\qquad b=0,1,\dots,p-1.

In the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\pi]\subset\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
the ideal
(\pi)
is maximal with the residue field

(5.11)

\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\pi]/(\pi)=\mathbb{F}_{p}.

Thanks to the relation
\pi^{p-1}=-p
the
p
-adic expansions in
\pi
may acquire additional terms which do not appear in the expansions over
\mathbb{C}
as the following Lemma demonstrates:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two
N\times N
matrices with
|\alpha_{i,j}|_{p}\leq 1
,
|\beta_{i,j}|_{p}\leq 1
. Then

(1+\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)^{p}=1+\pi^{p}(\alpha^{p}-\alpha)+O(\pi^{p+1})

Proof.

We get

(5.12)

\displaystyle(1+\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k}(\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)^{k}=1+p(\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)+\frac{p(p-1)}{2}(\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)^{2}

(5.13)

\displaystyle+\dots+(\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)^{p}\,.

Recall that
p=-\pi^{p-1}
. Since
|\alpha_{i,j}|_{p}\leq 1
, and
|\beta_{i,j}|_{p}\leq 1
we see that the lowest term
\pi^{p}
in the
p
-adic norm appear in the second and the last term of the sum (
5.13
):

(5.14)

(1+\pi\alpha+\pi^{2}\beta)^{p}=1-\pi^{p}\alpha+\pi^{p}\alpha^{p}+O(\pi^{p+1})\,,

where in the second term
-\pi^{p}\alpha=p\pi\alpha
.
∎

5.4.
p
-curvature

The
p
-curvature of connection
\nabla_{i}
is defined in components by

(5.15)

C_{p}(\nabla_{i})=\nabla_{i}^{p}-\nabla_{i}\pmod{p}

Modulo
p
all derivatives in (
5.15
) cancel out and the
p
-curvature is a linear operator.

We assume that the cohomological equivariant parameters have integral
p
-adic values values
\mathbold{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{p}
. Then, from (
5.3
) we obtain

(5.16)

\displaystyle(C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u}))_{a,b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[\mathbold{z}]]\cap\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\mathbold{z}).

Thus reductions modulo
p
for coefficients of such power are well defined and we obtain

C_{p}(\nabla_{i})\in Mat_{N}(\mathbb{F}_{p}(z)[s]).

An interesting problem is to determine the spectrum of this operator.

Remark 5.2
.

The connection (
5.1
) is sometimes called ‘logarithmic connection’ in order to distinguish it from the following connection

(5.17)

\tilde{\nabla}_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}-\frac{s}{z_{i}}\,C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u}),\hskip 22.99988pti=1,\dots,l.

In terms of
\tilde{\nabla}_{i}
the
p
-curvature has a shorter expression due to the following Lemma

Lemma 5.3
.

The following holds modulo
p

(5.18)

\left(\nabla_{i}\right)^{p}-\nabla_{i}=z_{i}^{p}\tilde{\nabla}_{i}^{p}\pmod{p}

Proof.

First, let us assume that the connection term in (
5.17
) is trivial
C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})=0
. Then for a section
f
(we suppress index
i
for brevity) we get

(z\tilde{\nabla})^{p}f(z)=(z\partial)\dots(z\partial)f(z)

where one acts
z\partial=z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
on
f
successively
p
times. Let

(5.19)

\left(z\partial\right)^{n}f(z)=zf^{\prime}(z)+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}a_{n,k}z^{k}f^{(k)}(z)+z^{n}f^{(n)}(z)\,.

One can immediately observe the following recursive relation between the coefficients
a_{n,k}

a_{n+1,k}=a_{n,k-1}+k\cdot a_{n,k}\,,

This recursive relation is solved by Stirling numbers of the second kind – the number of ways to partition a set of
n
objects into
k
non-empty subsets:

a_{n,k}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}j^{n}\,.

When
n=p
is prime by the little Fermat theorem
j^{p}\equiv j
mod
p
so

a_{p,k}\equiv\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}j\,.

The latter expression is equal to zero thanks to the following observation for
k>1

0=\frac{d}{dx}(1-x)^{k}|_{x=1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}j\,.

Thus
a_{p,k}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for
1<k<p
and

(5.20)

\left(z\partial\right)^{p}f(z)\equiv zf^{\prime}(z)+z^{p}\partial^{p}f(z)\pmod{p}\,.

The complete statement of the Lemma for the nontrivial connection follows after twisting the equation (
5.19
)
with the following operator

(5.21)

\exp\left[s\int^{z}\frac{C(y,u)}{y}dy\right]\cdot\left(z\partial\right)^{n}\cdot\exp\left[-s\int^{z}\frac{C(y,u)}{y}dy\right]=(z\tilde{\nabla})^{n}

and similarly on its right hand side before reducing both expressions modulo
p
. All contributions involving coefficient
a_{n,k}
will remain trivial after the reduction.
∎

5.5.
Expansions at roots of unity

From (
5.4
) we see that the quantum differential equation appears from the expansion of the
q
-difference equation when
|q-1|<1
in the complex norm. In this Section, we consider a similar expansion in the
p
-adic norm. A new feature of this approach is that
q
may be assumed to be close to a
p
-th root of unity. We show that in the vicinity of these points the quantum difference connections reduce to the corresponding
p
-curvatures.

Assume that
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
is a
p
-th root of unity. By (
5.10
), without loss of generality, we may assume

(5.22)

q=1+\pi+O(\pi^{2})\,.

We also assume that

(5.23)

a_{i}=q^{su_{i}}=1+\pi su_{i}+O(\pi^{2}),\quad i=1,\dots,l.

for
u_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and a formal variable
s
as above. The expansions (
5.22
) and (
5.23
) are the
p
-adic analogs of (
5.4
) where
\pi
is considered ‘small’ in the
p
-adic norm (
5.9
).

Using the shift operator, we can write the inverse of the iterated product (
1.11
) as

(5.24)

{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}^{p}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}q^{pz_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}}=\left(\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}q^{z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}}\right)^{p}\,.

As in (
5.5
) in the order up to
\pi
one gets

\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}q^{z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}}=1+\pi\nabla_{i}(z)+O(\pi^{2})\,.

Next, thanks to Lemma
5.1
for
\alpha=\nabla_{i}
we get

\left(\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}q^{z_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}}\right)^{p}=1+\pi^{p}\left(\nabla_{i}^{p}-\nabla_{i}\right)+O(\pi^{p+1})\,,

or

(5.25)

\frac{{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}^{p}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}-1}{\pi^{p}}\equiv\left(\nabla_{i}^{p}-\nabla_{i}\right)\pmod{\pi}.

Note that by (
5.11
), this precisely gives the
p
-curvature:

(5.26)

\frac{{\bf M}_{\mathcal{L}^{p}_{i}}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{a},q)^{-1}-1}{\pi^{p}}\equiv C_{p}(\nabla_{i})\pmod{\pi}\,.

The above analysis demonstrates that (
5.24
) considered over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\pi)
is the correct
q
-difference generalization of the
p
-curvature: it reduces to the
p
-curvature in the first nontrivial term of the
\pi
-expansion around a
p
-th root of unity.

Next let us consider the same expansion for
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p})^{-1}
.
As in Lemma
5.1
we have

a^{p}_{i}=(1+\pi su_{i}+O(\pi^{2}))^{p}=1+\pi^{p}(s^{p}u_{i}^{p}-su_{i})+O(\pi^{p+1}).

Since we assume
u_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, it follows that
u^{p}_{i}=u_{i}+O(\pi^{p-1})
(since
u_{i}^{p}=u_{i}\pmod{p}
) we also have

a^{p}_{i}=(1+\pi su_{i}+O(\pi^{2}))^{p}=1+\pi^{p}(s^{p}-s)u_{i}^{p}+O(\pi^{p+1}).

Using this expansion, from (
5.5
) we find:

\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p})^{-1}=1-C_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{u}^{p})\pi^{p}(s^{p}-s)+O(\pi^{p+1}).

In other words,

(5.27)

\frac{\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{a}^{p},q^{p})^{-1}-1}{\pi^{p}}\equiv(s-s^{p})C_{i}(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{u}^{p})\pmod{\pi}\,.

5.6.
The Isospectrality Theorem

Let us summarize the discussion of the previous sections. Let
C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})
be the operator of quantum multiplication by the divisor
c_{1}(L_{i})
in the equivariant quantum cohomology of a Nakajima variety. We denote by the same symbol
C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})
the matrix of this operator in the cohomological stable basis. Let us specialize the equivariant parameters so that
\mathbold{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{p}
.
Thanks to (
5.16
), this matrix has a well defined reduction mod
p
which gives a
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-connection. The
p
-curvature of this connection is

(5.28)

C_{p}(\nabla_{i})=(\nabla_{i})^{p}-\nabla_{i}\in Mat_{N}(\mathbb{F}_{p}(\mathbold{z})[s]).

Let
(s-s^{p})C(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{u}^{p})
be the operator obtained from
C(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})
via substitution
\mathbold{z}^{p}=(z_{1}^{p},\dots,z_{l}^{p})
and
\mathbold{u}^{p}=(u_{1}^{p},\dots,u_{m}^{p})
and multiplication by polynomial
s-s^{p}
. Modulo
p
we obtain the following matrix

(5.29)

(s-s^{p})C(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{u}^{p})\in Mat_{N}(\mathbb{F}_{p}(\mathbold{z})[s]).

Theorem 5.4
.

Matrices (
5.28
) and (
5.29
) have equal sets of the eigenvalues.

Proof.

The operator in (
5.28
) corresponds to the right-hand side of (
5.26
), while the operator in (
5.29
) is defined by the right-hand side of (
5.27
). Congurences modulo
\pi
imply congruences modulo
p
since
\pi^{p-1}=-p
. Finally, by Theorem
4.2
, the left-hand sides of (
5.26
) and (
5.27
) share the same spectrum.
∎

For a matrix
A=(a_{i,j})
let
A^{(1)}
denote its Frobenius twist, i.e., the matrix obtained from
A
by raising all matrix elements to the
p
-th power
A^{(1)}=(a^{p}_{i,j})
. Clearly, over a field of characteristic
p
, we have

C(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})^{(1)}=C(\mathbold{z}^{p},\mathbold{u}^{p})

We then can reformulate the last theorem in the form in which it was formulated in
[
9
]
:

Theorem 5.5

(
[
9
]
)
.

The spectra of the periodic pencil
(s-s^{p})C(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})^{(1)}
and the
p
-curvature
C_{p}(\nabla_{i})
are isomorphic over field of characteristic
p
.

Finally, we note that the spectrum of the quantum operators
C_{i}(\mathbold{z},\mathbold{u})
has an explicit description in terms of Bethe Ansatz
[
2
]
. The last theorem thus fully determines the spectrum of the
p
-curvature.
