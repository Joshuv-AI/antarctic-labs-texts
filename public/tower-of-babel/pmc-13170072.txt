Second‐Language Learning Facilitates Non‐Adjacent Dependency Learning: Effects Moderated by Specific Language
Authors: Helen Shiyang Lu, Toben H Mintz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStatistical learning allows language learners to implicitly track regularities in input. Prior studies have suggested that second language (L2) learning affects statistical learning, but the nature of this relationship remains unclear. Does L2 learning broadly enhance sensitivity to statistical structure, selectively tune learners to patterns emphasized in the learned language, or both? We tested English‐speaking adults enrolled in introductory Mandarin or Spanish courses, along with English monolingual controls, on two statistical learning tasks: a tonal word segmentation task and a non‐adjacent dependency (NAD) learning task. Participants completed both tasks at the beginning of instruction and again after two academic terms. All groups performed above chance in the tonal task, but none showed significant improvement over time, including Mandarin learners. In contrast, only Spanish learners demonstrated increased sensitivity to NADs over time. These findings suggest that statistical learning is not uniformly boosted by L2 experience. Instead, L2 exposure may selectively tune learners' sensitivity to relational patterns that are emphasized in their linguistic experience. More broadly, the results highlight how the structure of linguistic experience can shape statistical learning mechanisms.Keywords: Statistical learning, Second‐language learning, Language experience, Non‐adjacent dependency

Introduction

Statistical learning (SL) refers to the ability to implicitly extract regularities from structured input through exposure. Across development, learners track transitional probabilities, distributional patterns, and long‐distance dependencies in speech and other modalities (e.g., Aslin, Saffran, & Newport, 1998; Bulf, Johnson, & Valenza, 2011; Choi, Batterink, Black, Paller, & Werker, 2020; Emberson, Conway, & Christiansen, 2011; Lu & Mintz, 2021; Lu & Mintz, 2023; Monroy, Gerson, & Hunnius, 2017; Romberg & Saffran, 2013; Saffran, Aslin, & Newport, 1996; Saffran, Johnson, Aslin, & Newport, 1999; Stahl, Romberg, Roseberry, Golinkoff, & Hirsh‐Pasek, 2014; Turk‐Browne, Jungé, & Scholl, 2005). Sensitivity to statistical structure supports multiple aspects of language acquisition, including word segmentation, phonotactic learning, and morphosyntactic development. Moreover, individual differences in SL have been linked to variability in language comprehension and literacy outcomes in both first‐ and second‐language contexts (e.g., Godfroid & Kim, 2021; Misyak & Christiansen, 2012).

One of the most important insights from recent research is that SL is not a fixed ability that operates in a vacuum. Rather, SL appears to be shaped by learners' prior experience and expectations. A review of studies on bilingualism and artificial language learning suggests that experience with multiple linguistic systems can modulate how learners encode and prioritize distributional information (Weiss, Schwob, & Lebkuecher, 2020). For example, bilingual adults show graded advantages in learning new statistical patterns as a function of their language dominance (Onnis, Chun, & Lou‐Magnuson, 2018). Findings like these raise important questions: if SL mechanisms are experience‐sensitive, to what extent do new experiences recalibrate learners' sensitivity, and what are the downstream consequences for subsequent processing?

Second‐language (L2) learning provides a particularly informative test case for examining the plasticity of SL. Unlike early native language acquisition, which reflects prolonged exposure at a stage where learners' processing history is still sparse, L2 learning introduces substantial new regularities later in development and often over a compressed time scale. If SL mechanisms remain experience‐sensitive beyond childhood, L2 experience should offer an opportunity to observe how sensitivity to distributional structure adapts to changing linguistic input. Some recent evidence supports this possibility. For example, children with extensive exposure to a non‐native language through passive input exhibit enhanced sensitivity to non‐adjacent dependencies (NADs) compared to their peers (Dumont, Belenger, Destrebecqz, & Kissine, 2025). NADs are a type of structure where two elements are related despite being separated by intervening items. They are commonly found in natural languages, such as in subject‐verb agreement and in vowel harmony, and are more difficult to learn than adjacent dependencies due to the presence of intervening items (Gómez, 2002; Lany, Gómez, & Gerken, 2007; Wang & Mintz, 2018). Findings like Dumont, Belenger, Destrebecqz, & KissineDumont et al.'s (2025) suggest that sustained experience with structured linguistic input from a new language can reshape learners' sensitivity to statistical regularities. These findings motivate examining L2 experience as a window into how SL mechanisms remain dynamically tuned by linguistic experience throughout development.

Broad improvements in SL

One possibility is that experience with a second language leads to a general enhancement of SL ability. From this perspective, managing multiple linguistic systems may strengthen the learners' ability to track distributional regularities more broadly. Consistent with this view, some studies report advantages for bilinguals in artificial grammar learning, word segmentation, and cross‐situational word learning (e.g., Bartolotti, Marian, Schroeder, & Shook, 2011; Escudero, Mulak, Fu, & Singh, 2016; Onnis et al., 2018; Poepsel & Weiss, 2016; Wang & Saffran, 2014). Accounts such as the structural sensitivity hypothesis propose that bilingual experience may heighten learners' attention to the dimensions along which languages vary, thereby enhancing learners' readiness to reorganize language input and discover novel patterns (Kuo & Anderson, 2012). Under a general enhancement account, learners with L2 experience should show broadly improved performance across different types of SL tasks.

However, evidence for a global bilingual advantage in SL remains mixed. For example, one study with 5‐ to 13‐year‐old monolingual and bilingual children found no group differences in two nonlinguistic SL tasks (Yim & Rudoy, 2013). Similarly, a recent review emphasized that bilingual effects in SL are nuanced and sensitive to task demands, input structure, and learner experience (Weiss et al., 2020).

One factor that may contribute to these mixed findings is that SL is a multifaceted construct that operates over different representational levels and structural formats (e.g., Arciuli, 2017; Frost, Armstrong, & Christiansen, 2019; Siegelman, Bogaerts, Christiansen, & Frost, 2017). For example, learners can track adjacent transitional probabilities, NADs, and other distributional patterns, and performance across these tasks is often only weakly correlated (e.g., Conway & Christiansen, 2005; Siegelman & Frost, 2015). As a result, evidence for, or against, bilingual advantages based on a single SL task may provide an incomplete picture of how experience shapes learning. Even within the linguistic domain, different types of regularities place distinct demands on learners' perceptual and attentional systems. In particular, adjacent dependency learning is often characterized as relatively automatic and strongly supported by bottom‐up associative processes, whereas learning NADs typically requires greater top‐down attentional control and working memory resources (Conway, 2020). Consistent with this view, adjacent relations are usually acquired rapidly from continuous input, while NADs are more fragile and often benefit from additional cues such as variability or perceptual similarity (see Wilson et al., 2020, for a review). These differences suggest that performance on a single SL task may capture only a subset of the mechanisms through which experience shapes learning. In the present study, we, therefore, examine two types of linguistic regularities, adjacent tonal word segmentation and NAD learning, to provide a more fine‐grained assessment of how L2 experience relates to sensitivity to different types of statistical structure.

Selective changes in SL

Beyond the possibility of general enhancement, L2 experience may induce experience‐dependent attunement to particular dimensions or structural relations. First‐language acquisition is known to reshape perceptual and distributional sensitivities early in development, biasing learners toward cues that are informative or reliable in their input (e.g., Thiessen & Saffran, 2007; Werker & Tees, 1984). Importantly, similar recalibration processes may continue as learners acquire additional languages. Under this view, L2 learning does not uniformly improve SL ability. Rather, it selectively increases learners' sensitivity to features or dependencies that are functionally relevant in the learned language.

Second‐language experience may alter the weighting of relevant cues. When learners acquire a language in which a previously noncontrastive cue becomes lexically meaningful, they may allocate greater attention to that cue in subsequent processing. For example, in tonal languages such as Mandarin, pitch contour carries lexical contrast. Even when a novel tonal system differs from the learner's L2 in its specific inventory, experience treating pitch as lexically informative may increase the likelihood that pitch‐based regularities are encoded and tracked in new input. Consistent with this possibility, Potter, Wang, and Saffran (2017) found that learners of Mandarin showed improvement in tonal SL following classroom exposure compared to naive controls, suggesting that sensitivity to pitch‐based regularities could be recalibrated through L2 experience.

In addition, second‐language experience may highlight certain temporal relations that are otherwise difficult to detect. NADs are generally more computationally demanding than adjacent relations because learners must maintain representations across intervening material while discounting more local cues. Moreover, the possible NAD relations increase exponentially as input length increases, adding additional challenges to processing. However, not all NADs are equally difficult to learn. A growing body of work suggests that sensitivity to NADs is facilitated when the dependencies are perceptually similar (Gebhart, Newport, & Aslin, 2009; Grama, Kerkhoff, & Wijnen, 2016; Martinez‐Alvarez, Gervain, Koulaguina, Pons, & de Diego‐Balaguer, 2023; Weyers, Männel, & Mueller, 2022) or when learners have prior experience with structurally related patterns (Lany et al., 2007; Lany & Gómez, 2008; Ling et al., 2024; LaCross, 2015; Zettersten, Potter, & Saffran, 2020).

One mechanism that may support this facilitation is developmental scaffolding, whereby prior experience with simpler or more local structure supports the acquisition of more complex dependencies. Learners often show improved performance on nonadjacent relations following exposure to structurally related patterns, suggesting that earlier‐acquired representations can bootstrap the encoding of long‐distance structure (Lany et al., 2007; Lany & Gómez, 2008; Ling et al., 2024; Zettersten et al., 2020). Indeed, Conway (2020) argues that attention and working memory can modulate or gate learning and are especially important for extracting global or long‐distance structure. Consequently, learners' prior experience, and the extent to which it directs attention toward particular dimensions or relational patterns, may shape whether and how fast NADs can be learned.

Emerging developmental evidence suggests that some classes of NADs in natural language may be privileged compared to others. For example, a meta‐analysis showed that harmony‐based dependencies (e.g., long‐distance vowel similarity within a word) often exhibit relatively early sensitivity (Sundara, Zhou, Breiss, Katsuda, & Steffman, 2022), potentially because they provide perceptually coherent cues that facilitate the detection of long‐distance structure. More broadly, experience with such perceptually supported dependencies may do more than simply ease learning of those specific patterns. It may also highlight the relevance of distal temporal relations more generally, thereby directing learners' attention to nonadjacent structure in subsequent input. Consistent with this possibility, Lu and Mintz (2021) found that 9‐month‐old infants exposed to nonadjacent repetition patterns in visual sequences subsequently showed learning of novel NADs, suggesting that early experience can tune attention to distal structure. This interpretation is consistent with the proposal that successful learning of NADs depends critically on the ability to orient attention across temporally separated elements. Because dependent units occur at distant moments in time, learners must engage temporally guided attention to relate them, and this requirement becomes more pronounced for structurally complex dependencies (de Diego‐Balaguer, Martinez‐Alvarez, & Pons, 2016). Extending this logic to second‐language learning, sustained experience with an L2 that frequently instantiates perceptually similar long‐distance relations may increase learners' sensitivity to temporally nonadjacent relations in novel input.

Importantly, these accounts are not mutually exclusive. Second‐language experience may both strengthen learners' general sensitivity to statistical structure and selectively tune attention toward dimensions and dependency types that are particularly prominent in the learned language. Disentangling these possibilities, therefore, requires examining multiple forms of SL within the same learners and comparing across different language experiences. To this end, we focused on learners of Mandarin and Spanish, two languages that differ in the types of cues and structural relations that are highly informative during processing. Mandarin provides sustained experience with lexically contrastive pitch, whereas Spanish frequently requires learners to relate morphosyntactic information across intervening material. By jointly assessing adjacent tonal segmentation and NAD learning in these groups alongside monolingual controls, the present study provides a more comprehensive test of how linguistic experience shapes the SL system. This approach allows us to evaluate whether L2 experience is associated primarily with broad enhancement, experience‐dependent tuning, or a combination of both. To test these predictions, we conducted a longitudinal online study in which participants completed both SL tasks at multiple time points during classroom language exposure.

Methods

Participants and test administration design

Participants were recruited through Prolific (www.prolific.com), a crowdsourcing platform, as well as through flyers posted on university campuses in the Greater Los Angeles Area and outreach to language course instructors. The Prolific posting was restricted to college students in the United States. The language course instructors who assisted with recruitment were teaching introductory Spanish or Mandarin courses at community colleges or universities in the United States. Contact information for these instructors was obtained through publicly available school registrar records and departmental websites. A total of 503 Spanish course instructors and 278 Mandarin course instructors were contacted. The discrepancy in numbers was largely due to the greater availability of Spanish courses compared to Mandarin courses in US institutions.

Participants completed a brief screening survey to determine their eligibility for the study. Individuals who self‐identified as bilingual, reported fluency in a language other than English, or had learned a second language within the past 3 years were excluded. We received 2018 screening survey responses, from which 532 participants met the initial inclusion criteria and were invited to participate in the study. Of these, some participants were enrolled in introductory Spanish or Mandarin courses at their home institutions (i.e., L2 learners), while others were not actively learning a second language (i.e., control participants). Participants were compensated with $20 or course credit per session, with the option to participate in up to three sessions. They provided informed consent at the beginning of each session.

The study was originally designed with two testing sessions, with a contingency plan to add a third if one semester of classroom learning was insufficient to detect reliable changes. Session 1 was conducted during the first 2 weeks of the language course for L2 learners and within 2 weeks of the study invitation for controls. Session 2 was scheduled near the end of the first semester or quarter of language learning (within 14 days before or 7 days after the last day of instruction). Control participants were yoked to L2 learners' test timing, matched on the elapsed time between Session 1 and Session 2.

After data collection, however, we observed that Session 2 coincided with final exams and other end‐of‐term commitments. Performance declined across groups, and no improvements were detected. While the initial rationale for adding a third session was to extend the learning period beyond a single semester, these results suggested that end‐of‐term scheduling was an additional likely confound. Accordingly, we implemented Session 3 in the following semester/quarter.

Session 3 was scheduled between 28 and 14 days before the last day of the second semester or quarter, deliberately avoiding overlap with final exams and other end‐of‐term activities. Controls were again yoked to L2 learners' timing, matched on the elapsed time between Session 1 and Session 3. All participants who completed Session 1 were invited to participate in Session 3.

A total of 532 participants were initially invited to participate in the study. In Session 1, 294 participants completed the study, with 44 excluded for inconsistencies in reported language background or course enrollment, resulting in a final sample of 250 participants: 89 Mandarin learners, 80 Spanish learners, and 81 controls. In Session 3, 96 participants completed the study, but 20 were excluded due to incomplete responses or discrepancies in reported language course enrollment, yielding a final sample of 76 participants: 24 Mandarin learners, 23 Spanish learners, and 29 controls.

Stimuli

Each session consisted of the same two SL tasks—a tonal word segmentation task and an NAD learning task.

Tonal word segmentation task

In this task, the constructed artificial language featured two levels: syllables and tones. Nine syllables were created for this task—ta, tu, ti, da, du, di, ba, bi, and gu. For the tonal component, three tonal contours (i.e., rising, level, falling) were combined with three initial pitch levels (i.e., high, mid, low), resulting in nine distinct tones. We used the same pitch ranges as Wang and Saffran's (2014) study, with the high tone ranging from 200 to 330 Hz, the mid tone ranging from 150 to 250 Hz, and the low tone ranging from 50 to 200 Hz. The nine specific tones were crafted by reviewing tonal languages, where pitch variations at the syllabic level differentiate meanings (Wang and Saffran, 2014).

The syllables and tones were paired such that each syllable had a unique tone, maintaining consistency across the training and test phases to provide redundant cues for word segmentation. Table 1 shows the pairing of syllables and tones, which remained consistent across participants.

We first generated nine pure tones from specified pitch start and end points in Praat (Boersma, 2001). Then, a female speaker without tonal language fluency but with perfect pitch and musical training listened to the synthesized tones and sang the syllables with the specified tones. The syllables were recorded individually at 44.1 kHz, and edited to match in duration (i.e., 500 ms) and amplitude while maintaining pitch accuracy.

Training

The nine syllables were randomly combined to form three trisyllabic words for each participant (e.g., tadugu, bidatu, tibadi). Thirty repetitions of the three trisyllabic words were concatenated into a continuous speech stream such that no adjacent words were the same. There were 10 ms of silence between syllables, but no additional silences between words. The resulting training stream lasted 45.9 s. The transitional probabilities between both the syllables and the tones provided consistent information regarding word boundaries: 0.5 between words and 1 within words.

Test trial

The three trisyllabic words were used in test trials. In addition, three nonwords were created by reversing the syllables (and their corresponding tones) within a word. For example, if tadugu was a word for one participant, the corresponding nonword would be guduta, ensuring a transitional probability of 1 within a word and 0 within a nonword. Nine test pairs were generated by exhaustively pairing each of the words with each of the nonwords.

Catch trial

To ensure attentiveness in this online study, catch trials were included, where participants were asked to compare the familiarity of novel trisyllabic words and part‐words. Six novel toned syllables (i.e., pi, ku, ro, do, pa, and go) were created for the novel words. They were matched in duration and amplitude to the training syllables, and they were randomly combined to form eight novel trisyllabic words. Additionally, eight part‐words were created by selecting three consecutive syllables that had crossed word boundaries during training (e.g., if words were tadugu, bidatu, tibadi, examples of part‐words could be dugubi and gutiba). Because part‐words were composed of syllables heard during training, while novel words were not, catch trials allowed us to identify participants who were inattentive to the task.

NAD learning task

Fifteen monosyllabic artificial words—bep, bok, des, dob, feep, foom, foop, ghan, gop, ghep, kag, meb, mak, pag, poog—were recorded by a female speaker at 44.1 kHz. The recordings were then shortened to 250 ms using the lengthen function in Praat (Boersma, 2001). To enhance intelligibility, 83 ms of silence was added to the end of each word, extending the length to 333 ms per word. This speech rate was chosen because prior research found it to facilitate NAD learning in speech in the absence of other cues (Wang, Zevin, & Mintz, 2019).

Training

Six of the artificial words were used to form three NADs: a_b, c_d, and e_f. The assignment of actual words to these letters was randomized for each participant to ensure that any group‐level learning effects were not due to specific word pairings. That is, for one participant, the letter a might represent the word bep, while for another, it might represent foom. The remaining nine words were inserted in the medial position to form nine unique NAD trigrams: a[X1,X2,X3]b, c[X4,X5,X6]d, and e[X7,X8,X9]f. Note that each NAD was associated with a unique subset of the nine medial words (X1−X9); this constraint allowed for critical design features of test items, described below.

Test trial

Eighteen novel grammatical and 18 novel ungrammatical trigrams were generated for the test phase. The grammatical trigrams were formed by combining a trained NAD and a middle item that had never occurred with that NAD during training. Exhaustively combining these two components resulted in 18 novel grammatical trigrams (see Table 2). An ungrammatical trigram was formed by substituting the final word of a grammatical trigram with a different word that had occurred in the final position of another trained trigram, ensuring that the middle word had never occurred with either the first or final word during training (e.g., aX4f, where a_f broke the NAD and X4 had never occurred with either a or f). In this way, the adjacent transitional probabilities were all zeros within both grammatical and ungrammatical trigrams, and the two types of trigrams only differed in whether they adhered to or violated the trained NADs.

Catch trial

Eight novel words were created specifically for the catch trials (i.e., fod, ghen, goob, keb, koog, mep, seeg, soog). A catch trigram combined three novel words pseudo‐randomly, such that each of the eight words occurred exactly once in each trigram position (first, middle, and last) within a given testing phase. Therefore, a testing phase contained eight catch trigrams.

Procedure

Given that participants were recruited from across the country, all sessions were conducted online through Pavlovia (pavlovia.org). The order of the tasks was randomized across participants, and each participant received the same task order across sessions.

To minimize interference, participants were explicitly told that the two tasks were unrelated, and they watched a hand‐drawing video for 5 min in between. Although this interval was shorter than the 30–40 min used in Potter et al.'s (2017) study, previous work showed that adults successfully learned two statistical structures when they received explicit instructions that two structures were present (Gebhart, Aslin, & Newport, 2009). Based on this evidence, we judged that the combination of the 5‐min video and the explicit instruction would be sufficient to reduce interference.

In addition to completing the two tasks, participants also answered a short language course questionnaire during each session. The questionnaire asked about their enrollment, the course length, the modality of instruction, whether they learned other languages, and their intention to continue learning the language.

Tonal word segmentation task

This task followed the design used in a prior study of L2 learning with tonal materials (Potter et al., 2017), allowing for a direct comparison with that work.

The task had one training phase and one testing phase. In the training, the 45.9‐s training stream was repeated 13 times, resulting in a total of 10 min of exposure. Then, participants completed 18 test trials and eight catch trials in the testing phase.

Each test trial presented two sequences separated by a 500‐ms silence (i.e., one word and one nonword). After hearing both sequences, participants selected which sequence sounded more familiar. Each of the nine pairs of word and nonword was presented twice, once with the word first and once with the nonword first, totaling 18 test trials. This differs from the classic study with adults, which contrasted words with both nonwords and part‐words (sequences spanning word boundaries; Saffran et al., 1996). In the present design, we focused on the word versus nonword comparison. Despite this seemingly easier contrast, prior work has shown that monolingual English learners often fail to show reliable learning in tonal contexts, performing at chance even when the probabilities differ maximally (Potter et al., 2017; Wang and Saffran, 2014). Therefore, we focused on the word versus nonword comparison to reduce the risk of floor effects. If participants correctly segmented the words, they would find the words more familiar than the nonwords.

Each catch trial presented a novel word and a part‐word, with a 500‐ms silence in between them. The eight novel words and eight part‐words were randomly paired to form the eight catch trials. Half of them had the novel word presented first, and the other half had the part‐word presented first. If participants were paying attention, they should find the part‐words more familiar than the novel words.

The order of test trials and catch trials was pseudo‐randomized for each participant, such that no more than two consecutive trials presented the same type of sequence first in a pair.

NAD learning task

The task was structured into three blocks, each consisting of a 144‐s training phase followed by a test phase (following the design of Wang et al., 2019). Unlike the tonal SL task, which followed prior study designs that used a two‐alternative forced choice (2AFC) protocol, here we used a 5‐point rating scale for the NAD task. While syllable‐based segmentation tasks typically yield robust effects in binary choice paradigms like 2AFC, the detection of NAD learning may rely more heavily on the sensitivity of the offline task. Recent work demonstrates that rating tasks can be more sensitive than 2AFC paradigms specifically in the context of NAD learning (Lu & Mintz, 2026). Rating tasks can allow for the expression of gradient knowledge and levels of certainty that may be obscured by the binary nature of a 2AFC task. This sensitivity may be especially important in the design of this task, where grammatical and ungrammatical items are identical in all respects except for their adherence to the NAD structure.

Each training phase contained 16 repetitions of the nine NAD trigrams, resulting in a total of 144. The words were randomly concatenated into a continuous speech stream such that adjacent trigrams had no words in common. Since each word lasted 333 ms, a trigram's duration was approximately 1 s, resulting in a 144‐s speech stream for each training phase. Participants were instructed that they would listen to a sequence of novel words and later be tested on what they heard. They then passively listened to the training speech stream while viewing a blank screen.

A test phase directly followed each training phase. Participants heard one grammatical trigram, one ungrammatical trigram, or one catch trigram in a given trial. After hearing the trigram, participants responded to the question, “Do you think that you heard this sequence in the previous section?” using a 5‐point scale: “Definitely yes,” “Maybe yes,” “Not sure,” “Maybe not,” and “Definitely not.”

Each test phase contained six unique grammatical trigrams, six unique ungrammatical trigrams, and eight unique catch trigrams, resulting in a total of 20 trials. A new set of catch trigrams was generated for each test block. Across the three blocks, all of the 18 possible grammatical and 18 possible ungrammatical trigrams were presented exactly once. The order of trigram presentation within a testing phase was pseudo‐randomized, ensuring that no more than two consecutive trials presented the same type of trigrams (grammatical, ungrammatical, or catch).

Analysis

For each session, we began by removing participants who did not pass the catch trials. In the tonal task, participants were excluded if they failed four or more of the eight catch trials—defined as choosing the novel item instead of the part‐word more often than chance (chance level: 50%). A total of 10 participants in Session 1 (four English controls, one Mandarin learner, and five Spanish learners) and a total of three participants in Session 3 (two English controls and one Spanish learner) were excluded from further data analysis in the tonal task.

In the NAD task, we defined correctly answering a catch trial as selecting a rating lower than “not sure” (i.e., selecting “definitely not” or “maybe not”) for the catch trigram. If a participant answered less than half of the catch trials correctly within a test block, then data from that block were removed. If a participant failed the catch trials in all three blocks, then their data were removed for further analysis. A total of 29 participants in Session 1 (15 English controls, 6 Mandarin learners, and 8 Spanish learners) and a total of seven participants in Session 3 (three English controls, two Mandarin learners, and two Spanish learners) were removed in the NAD task due to failing all three blocks of catch trials.

For analyses comparing performance across sessions, we included only those participants who contributed usable data for at least one task in both sessions.

We conducted statistical analyses for both tasks using Bayesian mixed‐effects models via the brms package (Bürkner, 2017), applying a logit link function. For the tonal task, we used a binary logistic regression model with Language Group (a between‐subjects factor: English [reference level], Spanish, and Mandarin) as a fixed effect and included a random intercept for participant. For the NAD task, data from each session were analyzed using an ordinal logistic regression model. This model included fixed effects for Grammaticality (a within‐subjects factor: grammatical vs. ungrammatical, sum‐coded), Language Group (a between‐subjects factor: English [reference level], Spanish, and Mandarin), and their interaction. To account for individual variability in performance, we included a random intercept for participant. To examine whether participants' learning changed over the course of the session or was influenced by participants' L2 proficiency, we also ran exploratory models including block or self‐reported L2 proficiency (at Session 3) as fixed effects. These models did not significantly improve model fit compared to the simpler models reported here. Therefore, we report the more parsimonious models, and full model results of these exploratory analyses can be accessed on our OSF repository: https://osf.io/f495m/files/5gfpw.

For both tasks, when analyzing changes across sessions, we incorporated Session (within‐subjects: Session 1 vs. Session 3, sum‐coded) as an additional fixed effect, along with its interactions with other predictors already described. To regularize estimates, we used weakly informative priors for the fixed effects. Specifically, we placed normal priors centered at zero with a standard deviation of 1 on the log‐odds scale, which assumes effects are likely small but allows for a wide range of plausible values.

Results are presented as posterior estimates (β) along with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) for the predictors of interest. In Bayesian terms, if zero falls outside the 95% UI for a slope estimate or if one falls outside the 95% UI for an odds‐ratio estimate, this is taken as evidence that the predictor likely has a meaningful effect on the outcome. When appropriate, we carried out follow‐up comparisons using the emmeans package (Lenth, 2025). Complete model outputs are available on the project's OSF repository.

Results

Session 1

Tonal word segmentation task

We retained data from 77 English control participants, 88 Mandarin learners, and 75 Spanish learners for the tonal task. Discussion

This study investigated how second language (L2) learning shapes adults' ability to track statistical regularities in novel linguistic input. Building on accounts that view SL as experience‐sensitive, we asked whether L2 experience is associated with broad changes in learners' sensitivity to statistical structure, selective retuning to particular dimensions and dependency types, or a combination of both. To address this question, we tested English‐speaking adults enrolled in introductory Mandarin or Spanish courses, as well as English monolingual controls, on two SL tasks: a tonal word segmentation task and an NAD learning task. Participants completed both tasks at the start of their L2 instruction (Session 1) and again after approximately two courses of language learning (Session 3).

Tonal word segmentation

In this task, participants were asked to segment continuous speech streams in which word boundaries were defined by transitional probabilities between toned syllables. Across both sessions, all groups performed well above chance, suggesting that adults possess a general ability to extract statistical structure from tonal input. However, no group showed reliable improvement over time, and Mandarin learners did not differ from the other groups at either session.

This finding diverges from earlier studies that reported at‐chance performance in English‐speaking monolinguals who had no prior exposure to tone languages (Potter et al., 2017; Wang and Saffran, 2014). Notably, our study and theirs used similar procedures such that transitional probabilities between syllables and tones provided redundant segmentation cues. While it is well established that adults can segment continuous syllable streams (see Hartshorne et al., 2019, for example), the addition of tonal information may have disrupted their participants' ability to track the syllable‐based regularities, potentially due to the lack of familiarity with tonal input and difficulty in encoding the tonal information. In our sample, many participants were based in California, where ambient exposure to tonal languages such as Mandarin, Cantonese, or Vietnamese is relatively common. Prior work suggests that even passive exposure to diverse language environments can influence language learning outcomes (Bice & Kroll, 2019), which may explain the elevated baseline performance in our sample.

It is also worth noting that minor methodological differences in foil construction existed between these studies. While Potter et al. (2017) utilized nonword foils created by combining syllables from different words, our study, following Wang and Saffran (2014), used reversed‐word foils. However, given that both previous studies reported similar at‐chance performance in monolinguals despite these differing foil types, it is unlikely that the specific construction of nonwords accounted for the elevated performance observed in our sample.

Despite the elevated baseline performance in the tonal task, we did not observe improvement across sessions for any group, including the Mandarin learners. These results contrast with prior research suggesting that extensive experience with tone languages can heighten sensitivity to tonal regularities (Potter et al., 2017). One likely explanation is that participants in our study had already approached near‐ceiling performance in Session 1 (e.g., 69% correct in our English controls vs. 54% in Potter et al.'s control participants), reducing the opportunity to observe growth.

Additionally, the design of the tonal task may not have been optimal for capturing fine‐grained learning differences. The tonal regularities were highly redundant and tightly aligned with syllable structure, potentially enabling learners to succeed without relying on the kind of tonal tracking that would emerge through Mandarin learning. More importantly, the structure of the task may not have aligned well with the kind of tone representations reinforced in Mandarin instruction. Mandarin uses four distinct lexical tones and, therefore, only 16 different tone‐to‐tone transitional probabilities. Tracking transitional probabilities across tones may, therefore, be less informative or useful in natural Mandarin input.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while adults can segment tonal input, the specific structure of the task may not have been sensitive enough to capture the effects of L2 experience. Future work should consider using more complex tonal structures or tasks that better align with the linguistic input provided in L2 instruction.

NAD learning

At Session 1, all groups, including monolingual controls, showed some evidence of distinguishing grammatical from ungrammatical NAD sequences. Although only the English controls and Spanish learners showed consistent differences in their ratings, the model also estimated a numerically positive difference for Mandarin learners' ratings for grammatical versus ungrammatical trigrams. Still, Mandarin learners' performance at this initial time point was numerically lower than that of the other two groups, suggesting they may have begun the study at a slight disadvantage. This overall success nonetheless aligns with previous findings that adults can learn NADs from continuous speech input, even in the absence of additional cues such as prosody or pauses (Wang et al., 2019). These results confirm that NADs are tractable for adult learners under the right input conditions and highlight the reliability of NAD learning in linguistic input.

By Session 3, however, a divergence emerged across groups: only the Spanish learners continued to show reliable sensitivity to NADs. The clear grammaticality distinction observed in Session 1 was no longer evident for the English controls. This reduction in performance may be due, in part, to reduced sample sizes and increased noise in the data at Session 3, which limits the precision of group‐level estimates. Critically, while follow‐up contrasts identified reliable NAD learning only in the Spanish group at this time point, we did not find a significant three‐way interaction among language group, session, and grammaticality. This suggests that we lacked sufficient evidence to establish a qualitative difference between groups, and these findings should be interpreted as suggestive of a selective enhancement rather than a definitive group‐level divergence. We will return to the implications of the lack of interaction, as well as the associated challenges of sample size and attrition, later in this section. Nevertheless, the fact that Spanish learners maintained, and even strengthened, their sensitivity to NADs under the same sampling constraints suggests that their performance pattern may reflect a developmental trajectory rather than solely a statistical artifact. This pattern provides at least preliminary support for the interpretation that NAD learning can be influenced by experience with particular properties of a learned language.

In the case of Spanish, learners are frequently exposed to morphosyntactic structures that instantiate NADs: for example, gender and number agreement between determiners, nouns, and adjectives that are separated by intervening words (e.g., l a cas a mi a , “the.sg.f house.sg.f my.sg.f, ” my house; l os perr os lind os, “the.pl.m dog.pl.m pretty.pl.m,” the pretty dogs). These dependencies are highly frequent and consistently marked in Spanish input, often requiring learners to relate perceptually similar morphosyntactic information across intervening material. Sustained experience with such patterns may increase learners' attention to nonadjacent temporal relations, thereby supporting the detection of NADs in the artificial language. On the other hand, Mandarin lacks the same kind of morphologically driven, perceptually similar NADs that are common in Spanish. As a result, Mandarin learners may not have developed analogous expectations about long‐distance relationships between elements, and thus did not receive the same benefit in the NAD task. However, it is important to acknowledge that Spanish and Mandarin differ in many ways, and with the current data, we cannot pinpoint a specific characteristic in Spanish that makes it more beneficial for NAD learning than Mandarin.

A related consideration is that the artificial words used in the NAD task might resemble Spanish phonotactics more closely than Mandarin phonotactics. This could be a potential caveat in our findings. However, the artificial words were most similar to English phonotactics (e.g., Spanish does not have many consonant‐vowel‐consonant structures in the language), and our participants were all native English speakers. Thus, if phonotactic familiarity played a role, participants would likely rely on their English phonotactic knowledge when processing these words.

The lack of a significant three‐way interaction among language group, session, and grammaticality indicates that we lacked sufficient evidence for a definitive qualitative difference between groups. While our follow‐up contrasts identified reliable learning only in the Spanish group at Session 3, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that L2 learning exerts more general effects on SL, particularly given that the Mandarin group also showed numerical improvement across sessions. The difficulty in identifying an interaction may partly stem from the high attrition rates observed between Session 1 (N=250) and Session 3 (N=76), which significantly reduced our statistical power. This attrition highlights the inherent challenges of longitudinal, online research. Although our recruitment spanned multiple institutions across the United States, participants (especially those in the language learning groups) were primarily incentivized through monetary compensation rather than consistent course credit or in‐person supervision. These factors may have led to lower long‐term motivation and increased noise in our Session 3 data. Future work should utilize larger samples and perhaps more direct classroom integration or in‐person testing environments to maintain the power necessary to definitively establish group‐level divergences in complex learning trajectories.

Beyond the specific properties of the L2, it is important to consider the role of individual learner backgrounds and instructional contexts. To ensure group comparability from the start, we only included participants who were English monolinguals with no recent (within 3 years) second‐language learning experience. In addition to the SL tasks, we also asked participants to rate their proficiency in the learned language on a scale of 1–10, where a higher number represents greater proficiency, at Session 3. Overall, we found no significant differences between Spanish learners' and Mandarin learners' listening (p=.314) and speaking proficiency (p=.317). While we observed that Spanish learners reported higher reading proficiency than Mandarin learners at Session 3 (p=.030), likely due to orthographic similarities between English and Spanish, the groups were well‐matched in the auditory domains most relevant to our tasks. Exploratory models incorporating self‐reported proficiency did not significantly improve model fit, suggesting these differences were unlikely to drive our results.

A related limitation involves the specific instructional contexts of our participants. Because we recruited across multiple institutions (which was necessitated by the difficulty of attaining a sufficiently large Mandarin sample at a single site), and because instructors participated on a voluntary basis to pass on the study information to their students, we were unable to formally evaluate specific pedagogical methods or curricular content. It remains possible that variation in instructional approach or the specific linguistic structures prioritized in different courses could influence SL trajectories. Future research should aim to incorporate more direct measures of classroom practice or utilize single‐site cohorts where the curriculum could be more tightly controlled. This would allow us to investigate not just which language is being learned, but how specific modes of instruction might interact with SL mechanisms. To provide transparency regarding the data we did collect, we provided Supplementary Material on our OSF repository (https://osf.io/f495m/files/tgy5d) regarding instructional hours, delivery mode, participants' self‐reported proficiency scores, and ambient language exposure.

The divergence of improvement in task performance observed in Session 3 (i.e., tonal word segmentation vs. NAD learning) may also reflect differences in task difficulty across the two SL measures. The tonal task appeared relatively easy for this sample, with all groups performing well above chance at Session 1, which likely reduced the opportunity to detect growth over time. In contrast, NAD learning is typically more demanding, requiring learners to maintain representations across intervening elements and to suppress more local transitional cues. The sustained, and even enhanced, sensitivity observed in the Spanish learners may reflect not only a language‐specific tuning of statistical sensitivity, but also the fact that this task provided greater room for experience‐related differences to emerge.

Overall, our results align better with the claim that second‐language experience does not uniformly enhance SL capacity, but instead shapes what learners are most attuned to. At the same time, the absence of robust group differences in the tonal task leaves open the possibility that L2 experience may also exert broader influences under different task conditions or with more sensitive measures. While the acquisition of any new language involves learning to track unfamiliar patterns, improvements may be most apparent when the regularities tested align with dimensions that are repeatedly emphasized in the learner's experience. Together, these findings are consistent with a structure‐sensitive tuning account of SL, whereby learners become more efficient at extracting regularities that match the forms and dependencies they encounter in their linguistic input.

Much of the literature has emphasized the role of SL as a predictor of language outcomes. For example, children's and adults' SL has been linked to reading, grammar, and pragmatic abilities (e.g., Arciuli & Simpson, 2012; Frost, Siegelman, Narkiss, & Afek, 2013; Lukics & Lukacs, 2025; Misyak & Christiansen, 2012), and Lee (2023) found that Korean‐speaking adult L2 learners of English showed a marginal correlation between SL task performance and their processing of long‐distance dependencies in English. Together, these studies highlight how SL can scaffold language outcomes.

The reverse direction (i.e., whether language learning experience influences SL) has been less frequently studied, and most evidence comes from bilingual populations. For example, Aguasvivas, Cespón, and Carreiras (2024) reported that Spanish–Basque and Spanish–English bilinguals outperformed Spanish monolinguals on a demanding word‐referent mapping task, though no group differences were found for segmentation or NAD learning. Similarly, Bulgarelli, Bosch, and Weiss (2019) failed to find a consistent bilingual advantage in visual SL. Together, these findings suggest that bilingualism may sometimes confer benefits for SL, especially in tasks with high memory or attentional demands, but not consistently across domains.

Our study provides a novel contribution by focusing on learners acquiring their first L2, rather than on lifelong bilinguals. Unlike bilinguals, these learners were at the early stages of formal instruction, offering a clearer test of how new language experience can tune SL mechanisms. The pattern of results observed in our study, where only Spanish learners showed improvement in NAD sensitivity, suggests that even limited L2 exposure can shape SL, particularly when the L2 repeatedly emphasizes relational patterns that are relevant to the task. Importantly, this interpretation is consistent with experience‐dependent tuning of attention and expectations. Future work can build on this and identify which specific properties of L2 input most strongly drive this tuning and whether similar effects extend to other domains of language learning.
