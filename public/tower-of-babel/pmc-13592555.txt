Microscale matrix defects suppress tension-dependent protrusions and stall collective cell migration
Authors: Hannah Zmuda, Diego Barra Avila, Ping-Hsien Lee, Christopher Walter, Amit Pathak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDuring development, wound repair, and disease, epithelia must detect and respond to subtle extracellular defects to maintain coordinated migration. We show that collectively migrating epithelia undergo large-scale spatiotemporal stalling in response to laser-ablated micro-defects in the presence of collagen type IV. When the filopodia of leading-edge cells encounter micro-defects, the resulting local cytoskeletal disruption propagates to the follower cells, producing multicellular stalling over length scales much larger than the original defect. Extracellular changes in matrix stiffness, collagen type, and osmolarity regulate cell stiffness and membrane tension, which, in turn, control protrusive activity and stall migration. Through these extracellular variations, we found that stiffer cells and lower membrane tension suppress protrusions in leader cells, which enhances multicellular stalling through intercellular propagation of cytoskeletal disruption. This work advances the biophysical understanding of cell migration by showing that collagen-IV, softer matrices, and hypertonic media enhance cellular sensing of extracellular defects and wounds.

INTRODUCTION

Wound healing and epithelium regeneration are critical biological processes for tissue homeostasis. When the epithelium does not properly heal, the tissue and organ cannot properly function, often leading to fibrosis,1 cancer,2 and chronic inflammatory diseases.3–5 For epithelial cells to repair the injury and regenerate the tissue, subcellular processes and multicellular migration must be executed in a coordinated fashion.3 During this time, the cells must interact with extracellular matrix (ECM) environments defined by specific chemo-mechanical properties, such as stiffness, protein types, and osmolarity.

The ECM is a biological scaffold that supports epithelial growth and provides mechanical support during tissue regeneration. Cells during wound healing and regeneration encounter two primary ECM structures: the basement membrane (BM) and the interstitial matrix (IM). The BM is a dense, sheet-like structure primarily composed of laminins, proteoglycans, nidogens, and collagen type IV (Col-IV).6,7 The BM enhances the adherence of epithelial cells8 and acts as a tissue barrier for organs due to the mechanical stability provided by the network-forming Col-IV.9 The IM sits directly below the BM layer, composed of fibrillar collagens, such as collagen type I (Col-I), elastin, fibronectin, growth factors, and stromal cells.6,10,11 Cells sense mechanical cues of the ECM via focal adhesions (FAs), actomyosin contractility, and downstream transcription and translation.12,13 Previous work has also shown that wound softening via FAK inhibition14 and epithelial softening in the presence of a BM15 enhance wound healing and collective migration. However, it is unclear how the different individual ECM proteins influence migratory responses of epithelial cell collectives during wound closure events.

When cells generate rapid wound closure, the resulting regeneration is largely owed to active cell migration rather than proliferation.16 Specifically, these wound healing processes are enabled by migrating epithelial cells that rely on the extension of protrusions from the leading-edge cells (lamellipodia and filopodia crawling)17,18 and actin purse-string mechanisms.19–24 Classic scratch wound assays17,25,26 have revealed these fundamental mechanisms of collective gap closure; however, the gaps can vary from tens to several hundred microns in size. To understand how these physical gaps influence wound healing, 2D micro-patterns have been used to study the evolution of epithelial closure and cell mechanics over non-adherent areas.14,18,20–22,27–29 However, it is unclear whether micron-size gaps in leading edge protrusions can cause multicellular dysfunction in collective cell migration.

In this study, we demonstrate the importance of investigating different ECM proteins in collective cell migration, especially in complex environments such as wound healing. When a micro-defect is introduced on polyacrylamide hydrogels coated with Col-IV, epithelial migration stalls for a significant amount of time. During this stalling, multicellular actin fibers initially align parallel with the defect and eventually collapse. This “defect-sensing” phenomenon is not observed when cells interact with Col-I or stiffer ECMs. Instead, the cells can migrate over the microscale defect (μdefect) with no disruption in collective cell migration or actin architecture reorganization. Using this system, we discovered that cytoskeletal disruptions at the leading edge can propagate across cells and that membrane tension and cell stiffness influence collective defect sensing in different ways. Our findings illustrate the importance of understanding how breaks in the ECM biomechanically impact collective cellular response and connect the roles of ECM stiffness, protein type, and osmolarity in regulating cell migration. Altogether, we reveal mechanisms of cytoskeletal dynamics and cell migration that are broadly relevant during the fundamental regulation of collective cell migration and physiological processes in wound healing and regeneration.

RESULTS

Micro-defects on soft collagen-IV surfaces stall collective cell migration

To understand how collectively migrating epithelia sense and respond to μdefects on soft BM-like surfaces, we seeded human mammary epithelial cells (MCF10As) onto 10-kPa hydrogels composed of polyacrylamide (PA) supplemented with 15 mM 2-aminoethyl methacrylate30 to ensure continuous collagen-IV coating (Video S1; Figure 1A). After seeding epithelial monolayers, we performed laser ablation (see STAR Methods) to make precise μdefects that broke through the ECM protein layer and the superficial PA gel surface, as confirmed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and bright-field imaging (Figures 1B and 1C, respectively). These laser-ablated μdefects typically ranged from 4 to 6 μm in width (Figure 1D) and were ~250 μm in length (Figure S4A), and they had a uniform depth (Figure S14).

After allowing monolayer expansion on Col-IV-coated gels, we found that cell migration halted in the presence of a μdefect but continued seamlessly in the control conditions with continuous substrates (Figure 1E; Video S2). Upon quantification of this defect sensing, we found that it took several hours (~8 h on average) for the leading edge to cross over such defects, compared to controls where the leading edge migrated within ~1.5 h over an imaginary line of equivalent dimensions (Figure 1F). These results demonstrate how ECM surface defects of only a few microns in width can cause multicellular stalling.

To quantify the stalling behavior during cell migration, we used particle image velocimetry (PIV) through MATLAB (PIVLab) and masking through ilastik.31 We found that the collective migratory speed was not affected by the stalling (Figures 1G and 1H) around the μdefect. To understand whether directed collective cell migration is impacted by the Col-IV μdefect, we measure the direction autocorrelation, defined as the alignment of cell trajectory along the unit vector pointed in the direction normal to the defect (Figure S1B). We found that the Col-IV μdefect significantly disrupted the collective trajectory of the MCF10A cells through a reduction in the direction autocorrelation (Figures 1I and S2B) after the cells came in contact with the Col-IV μdefect (Figure 1J). As the cells interacted with the μdefect, they diverged in the direction parallel along the μdefect. Similarly, the collective order parameter, measuring the degree of coordinated movement (Figure S1C), decreased as the cells reached the μdefect, while cells in the control condition were able to maintain ordered migration (Figure 1O) and decreased in cells after encountering the μdefect (Figure 1P). These results demonstrate how the presence of a micron-size gap on Col-IV leads to a reduction in directional and coordinated migration in MCF10A collectives. Because epithelial cells are critical mediators of wound healing,3 we repeated these experiments with MDCK cells (Figures 1M and 1N) and primary zebrafish keratinocytes obtained from excised fish scales (Figures 1O and 1P), and we confirmed this defect sensitivity and stalling for both cell lines. Overall, these results demonstrate that superficial ECM μdefects cause multicellular migratory stalling that lasts over several hours, which occurs due to the disruption of the directional order of collective migration.

F-actin disruption initiated at the micro-defect propagates across cells

To understand how cell migration begins to stall upon encountering a μdefect on Col-IV gels, we performed fixed F-actin imaging to understand the shape of the cytoskeleton during stalling. When we observe fixed cells in continuous substrates, we find that the actin fibers are distinct (Figure 2A) and aligned in all directions (Figure S3Ai). In stalled MCF10A cells, we found that the actin fibers are aligned parallel with the μdefect (Figures 2B and S3Aii) and that the cells are unable to reach and rather increase in height compared to the control (xz plane in Figures 2A and 2B). Upon stalling of cell migration at the defect, actin fibers formed parallel with the defect (±90°), while the cells in the control condition pointed in the normal direction about the μdefect (0°) (Figures 2D and 2C, respectively). To assess whether this actin fiber reorientation along the μdefect varies within the cell monolayer, we calculated the order parameter of the actin fibers using Alignment by Fourier Transform (AFT)32 and found a more ordered actin network in the stalled leading-edge cells around the defect compared to follower cells or control surfaces without a μdefect (Figure 2E). We also found a reduction in fluorescence intensity of F-actin expression in cells stalled around the μdefect, compared to controls (Figure 2F). Our results demonstrate that cellular stalling around extracellular physical μdefects is enabled by reorientation and reduction of F-actin fibers along the μdefect.

To understand whether this disruption of protrusive actin fibers propagates across cells and deeper into the epithelial monolayer, we performed live F-actin imaging (using SiR-actin) and analyzed the actin fiber architecture. We observed that the actin cytoskeleton began to collapse when cells reached the μdefect (Video S3) and actin fibers became less filamentous over time in both leading cells (Figure 2Gi, from left to right) and follower cells (Figure 2Gii). Since migratory stalling resulted in actin fibers aligning parallel to the defect (Figure 2B), we wondered whether such cytoskeletal rearrangement propagates to neighboring actin fibers. To do this, we applied direction autocorrelation vector tracking with the unit vector in the normal direction of the μdefect to quantify the correlation of actin fiber directionality, which we refer to as actin alignment. The heatmaps demonstrate that as the cells stall, the cytoskeleton reorients in the opposite direction of migration (Figure 2Gi) and this propagates from the leading-edge cell to other cells that follow 150 μm behind the μdefect (Figure 2Gii). Although the velocity of the actin movement is unchanged by the presence of the μdefect (Figures 2H and 2I), the degree of alignment of the actin fibers is lower in the areas directly in contact with the μdefect (Figure 2J) compared to actin in follower cells 100–200 μm behind the leading edge (follower/rear regions) (Figure 2K). To understand how this cytoskeletal disruption at the leading edge propagates spatially rearward, we measured the actin alignment and movement based on the distance from the leading edge. We define actin alignment as the directional autocorrelation of actin fibers over time and actin movement as the velocity of the actin fibers over time. While the actin movement does not vary based on the spatial location in the stalling monolayer (Figures 2L and 2M), the alignment of the fibers within the cytoskeleton decreases as the collective stalls (Figure 2N) with larger disruption at the leading edge (Figure 2O). These results reveal that the spatiotemporal rate of actin disruption caused by the ECM μdefect propagates across multicellular sheets, taking ~2 h to travel ~200 μm into the monolayer.

Next, we asked whether a similar spatiotemporal propagation of actin disruption occurs when initiated by generating defect within the cells as opposed to those in the ECM. To this end, we performed laser ablation on the lamella region of the leader cells (Figure 2P) and showed (using SiR-actin) that the actin cytoskeleton at the leading edge collapsed immediately, reducing the actin alignment in the normal direction of the ablated lamellipodia (Figure 2Q). Follower cells were initially able to maintain stress fibers after laser ablation of leader cell actin (Figure 2R); however, the cohesion of actin fibers in followers reduced ~40 min after lamella ablation (Figure 2R), resembling the spatiotemporal propagation of actin disruption caused by Col-IV μdefects. Together, these results demonstrate that disruption of the F-actin network at the leading edge can propagate across multiple cells to disrupt collective cell migration.

Collagen type I maintains collective cell migration and actin fibers across ECM defects

Thus far, we have shown that μdefects on ECM surfaces with Col-IV (BM-like) cause wide-ranging subcellular and multicellular disruptions (Figures 1 and 2). Since epithelia are often in contact with tissue layers rich in Col-I that lies underneath the BM,33 we next asked how this change in collagen type would affect the observed migratory stalling. By imaging the structure of the collagen coatings on PA gels using SEM, we found that the surface of Col-IV had shorter fibers and a more uniform surface coating (Figure 3A), compared to the longer fibers on the Col-I coating (Figure 3B). Here, the ablated regions of the gel were devoid of collagen altogether and μdefect dimensions were unaffected by collagen coating type (Figures S4B and S4C; non-colorized regions in Figures 3A and 3B). Upon repeating the cell migration experiments, we found that MCF10A monolayers seeded on Col-I-coated 10 kPa PA gels did not stall at the μdefect (Figure 3C; Video S4), unlike cells seeded on Col-IV. The migration distance of leading-edge cells did not change between Col-I with a μdefect (Figure 3D) and control conditions (Figure 3E). Additionally, the presence of μdefects on Col-I hydrogels did not alter migratory speed (Figures 3F and 3G), direction autocorrelation (Figures 3H and 3I), or order parameter (Figures 3J and 3K). These quantifications confirm that the Col-I μdefect does not impact collective migration across defects. Upon repeating these experiments with additional cell types, we found that primary zebrafish keratinocytes migrated over the μdefects on Col-I-coated substrates similar to MCF10As (Figures S4F and S4G). However, MDCKs did stall (unlike MCF10A) at the μdefect with Col-I (Figures S4D and S4E), which could be attributed to their more cohesive characteristics compared to MCF10As.34 By contrast, upon depleting cell-cell adhesion protein α-catenin in MCF10A cells, these cells did not stall on Col-IV soft gels and behaved largely similar to control cells on Col-I (Figure S5). These results demonstrate that the more fibrillar Col-I suppresses stalling, while Col-IV of shorter fibers promotes stalling. We also found that combining the two types of collagen prevents stalling, which indicates that the presence of some long fibers dominates the migratory response across defects (Figure S6A).

On the cytoskeletal level, cells on Col-I were isotropic and had actin alignment in all directions, both on the control (Figure 3L) and μdefect (Figure 3M) conditions. Cells at the μdefect maintained flat lamellipodia and contact with the substrate (Figure 3M, xz plane) similar to cells on Col-I in the control condition (Figure 3L), and actin fibers were oriented in all directions in both the control (Figures 3N and S7A) and the defect (Figures 3O and S7B) conditions in fixed imaging. We also found that the order of actin fibers (Figure 3P) and F-actin intensity (Figure S4H) remained unaffected by the presence of a μdefect on Col-I. Over time, the actin stress fibers maintained their shape with little disruption in actin fiber alignment (Figure 3Ri). Cells 100 μm behind the leading edge were also able to maintain higher alignment as the cells migrated over the μdefect (Figure 3Rii). The actin movement and alignment were maintained over time, even upon encountering the μdefect (Figures 3S, 3T, and 3U). However, cells behind the leading edge were able to maintain more cohesive actin fiber structure (Figure 3V).

To understand the variations in the length of actin fibers, we used SiR-actin-labeled MCF10A and manually chose distinct actin stress fibers, as illustrated by the arrows in Figure S9A. We found that cells on Col-I at the μdefect were able to maintain actin fiber length as the leading cells migrated over the μdefect (Figure S9B). The stress fiber length in cells on Col-IV significantly reduced over time, indicating a collapse in the actin network that initiates at the μdefect and propagates to follower cells (Figures S9C and S9D). Cells on Col-I maintained actin stress fiber length within the monolayer (Figure S9D), thus allowing them to migrate over the μdefect on Col-I. To better understand the biophysical cellular and extracellular contributions to this epithelial stalling on Col-IV, we next investigated the factors implicated in the formation of intracellular actin network and protrusions.

Filopodial extensions and cellular softening enable cell migration over defects

During collective cell migration, the leading edge utilizes different protrusive processes, such as filopodia and lamellipodia, to explore its environment. Filopodia are actin-rich finger-like protrusions at the very front of the leading cell and have been specifically attributed to helping cells probe the ECM.35,36 Formation of filopodia and protrusions depends on actin polymerization, bundling, and network crosslinking. However, it remains unclear how collagen type impacts actin network formation and cellular protrusions, such as lamellipodia and filopodia.

To understand whether collagen type affects filopodia structure, we used FiloQuant37 to measure filopodia length in fixed phalloidin images. Filopodia on Col-I were significantly longer compared to cells seeded on Col-IV both at the μdefect (Figure 4A) and control conditions (Figure 4B). Furthermore, SEM imaging revealed that cells stalled at a μdefect on Col-I had longer filopodia and were able to reach across the μdefect (Figure 4C). By contrast, filopodia on Col-IV were shorter and were unable to reach over the μdefect (Figure 4D). On Col-I, we observed filopodia extending past the μdefect width (Figure S10A). However, on Col-IV, filopodia were unable to reach across the defect and shorter cellular protrusions were blocked at the leading edge (Figure S10B). Additional images on Col-I indicate that longer filopodia were generated after encountering the defect (Figure S10C) because filopodia in homogeneous regions were relatively shorter (Figure S10D; Figure 4A).

When the cells stalled at the Col-IV μdefect, we found that the actin stress fiber orientation, stress fiber length, and filopodial length all reduced. It is known that higher formation of actin stress fibers is connected to higher cell stiffness.38 To understand the role of cell stiffness in collagen-dependent defect sensing, we generated stiffness maps of migrating cells using atomic force microscopy (AFM). By measuring a 100 × 100-μm scan area (Figure 4E), and by choosing areas with only the cell based on the real height of the probe (Figures 4Fii and 4Gii), we found that the cells stalled at the Col-IV μdefect were significantly stiffer than the cells migrating over the Col-I μdefect or stalled at the Col-I μdefect (Figure 4H). When compared to control conditions (without defects), we found that there was no difference in cell stiffness on Col-IV vs. Col-I (Figure S11). Because the filopodia length and stiffness drastically changed in cells at the Col-IV μdefects but not Col-I, we next asked how actin-modulating molecules played a role in defect sensing.

While Rac1 generates forward protrusions, RhoA is responsible for rear-edge retraction (Figure 4I) over the migratory cycle.39 To understand the difference in protrusive and Rac1-dependent signal, we measured the expression of the Arp2/3 branching protein in cells. We found that ArpC2, the antibody for Arp2/3 complex, had a higher expression in the cells on Col-I μdefect (Figure 4Ji) as compared to cells on Col-IV μdefect (Figure 4Jii). When we compared the front and rear regions of interest (ROIs) on Col-I (Figure S1D), ArpC2 expression was higher behind the leading edge (rear ROI) (Figure 4K), indicating that actin crosslinking behind the leading edge could be responsible for aiding collective migration over the μdefect on Col-I. When we compared other actin-modulating proteins, CIP4 for Cdc42 and filopodia formation (Figure S12A) and α-actinin for actin stabilization (Figure S12C), we found no difference in expression between the cells on Col-I and Col-IV at μdefects (Figures S7D and S7F, respectively). These results demonstrate how actin branching and polymerization may play a role in collagen-dependent defect-sensing.

To better understand the individual role of each actin modulator, we chemically inhibited Arp2/3, Cdc42/Rac1, and actin polymerization. Chemical inhibitors CK666 (Arp2/3 inhibitor), ML141 (Cdc42/Rac1 inhibitor), and latrunculin A (actin polymerization) were added to the monolayer setups before laser ablation. Comparing the μdefect on Col-I and Col-IV without inhibitors, the stall time was similar with CK666, demonstrating that Arp2/3 is necessary for stall time on Col-IV μdefects, but not for Col-I μdefects (Figure 4L). Only ML141 significantly increased the stall time in cells both on Col-I and Col-IV μdefects (Figure 4L), demonstrating how Rac1 and filopodia formation via Cdc42 is necessary for defect sensing. Although latrunculin A slightly increased stalling, there was no difference in the stall time on Col-I and Col-IV, demonstrating the critical role of filopodia to allow cells to cross the μdefect on Col-I (Figure 4L). CK666 caused a reduction in the direction autocorrelation for both collagen types when the leading edge first reached the μdefect, but they were able to recover soon after (Figure 4M). ML141 (Figure 4N) and latrunculin A (Figure 4O) both showed a reduction in direction autocorrelation after cells came in contact with the μdefect. In summation, these results further support the idea that the maintenance of the actin architecture and protrusion dynamics is critical for collagen-dependent defect sensing by collectively migrating epithelia.

Focal adhesions are suppressed by Col-IV, while actin-myosin activity modestly influences defect sensing

The actin network is anchored by stable FAs, which are formed by a collection of proteins that bind to the transmembrane protein integrin to facilitate cellular adhesion to the surrounding substrates. The FA is then able to transmit the external forces to the actin network by connecting actin fibers and actin-modulating proteins.13 We asked whether Col-IV μdefects disrupt FA formation during collective stalling. To better understand the role of cell-ECM adhesions, we imaged paxillin because of its known role in stable FA formation. We found that cells on Col-I (Figure 5A) had more FA puncta perpendicular to the μdefect compared to cells on Col-IV (Figure 5B).40 Cells overcoming the μdefect on Col-I had larger paxillin puncta area compared to stalled cells on Col-IV (Figure 5C).We found the total expression of the paxillin to be unchanged based on collagen type (Figure 5D), indicating that the morphological features of the paxillin puncta better indicate the collective stalling behavior. To understand the role of cell membrane adhesion receptors in the formation of cell-ECM adhesions for the two collagen types, we imaged integrin α2. We found that integrin α2 was expressed within the ROIs on both collagen types (Figures 5F and 5G). Integrin α2 puncta on Col-I were both larger in area (Figure 5G) and integrated density (Figure 5H). This suggests that the integrin α2 subunit expression can aid in defect sensing. Combined, these results display how the physical and biochemical expression of cell-ECM adhesions can induce collective stalling on Col-IV and not on Col-I.

After cell-ECM adhesions are established, myosin motor heads bind to the F-actin stress fibers and apply force to move the cell body. Because of the change in actin fiber orientation and dynamics, we wondered how actomyosin contractility was impacted by the presence of a μdefect. We used myosin ATPase inhibitor (blebbistatin), a phosphatase inhibitor (calyculin A),41 and constitutively active (CA) RhoA in MCF10A cell lines to understand how actomyosin activity plays a role in collagen-dependent defect sensing. Blebbistatin-treated MCF10A cells stalled at the μdefect for a longer duration (Figure 5I) and showed lower directional autocorrelation (Figure 5J) compared to the untreated controls. Given that blebbistatin is known to reduce FA formation and actin network development,42 these results further support the importance of actin and FA formation in overcoming μdefects. Cells with enhanced actin-myosin activity (calyculin A and CA RhoA) did not stall at the defect for a significant amount of time (Figure 5J), and they maintained relatively high directionality in collective migration (Figures 5K and 5L). To further understand the role of contractility during defect sensing, we measured the degree of colocalization of non-muscle myosin 2A (NM2A) fibers over F-actin fibers using the ImageJ plug-in JaCop (Figure S13E),43 and we found no difference in actin-myosin colocalization between cells seeded on Col-I compared to Col-IV (Figures S13E–S13G). These results further support that cells’ defect sensing depends more on the formation of FA and actin network than on active myosin-based contractility.

Stiffer substrates allow cells to overcome μdefects on both collagen types I and IV

According to the results thus far, higher filopodial length, robust actin network development, prominent FA formation, and increased cell-ECM receptor expression all allow cells to overcome μdefects on Col-I compared to Col-IV. Next, we asked whether stiffer ECMs, which are known to increase cellular motility, increase filopodia stability,36,44 enhance adhesion dynamics,45,46 and increase cellular forces, would alter collagen-dependent defect sensing. Through AFM force mapping measurements, we characterized a physiologically relevant range of PA gel stiffness, with the softest gels around 2 kPa, control gels around 10 kPa, and stiff gels around 42 kPa (Figure 6A). When we characterized the μdefect, we found that soft and control gels produced μdefects that had a similar depth relative to the surface, irrespective of the collagen coating type (Figure S14). Cells stalled for a significantly longer period on softer (2 kPa) ECMs (Video S5), while stiffer (42 kPa) substrates caused a significant reduction in stall times for both collagen types (Figure 6B). While μdefect is shallower on stiff substrates, we observe that the leading edge briefly collapses into the μdefect on stiff substrates (Video S6). While intermediate ECM stiffness showed lower direction autocorrelation of cell migration for Col-IV μdefects compared to Col-I (Figures 1 and 3), the softer and stiffer ECMs suppress and enhance the directional autocorrelation, respectively, for both collagen types (Figures 6B and 6C, respectively). According to direction autocorrelation (DA) heatmaps, the DA was uniformly reduced for softer ECM (Figure 6Ei and ii) and enhanced for stiffer ECM (Figure 6Eiii and iv) for both collagen types. Similarly, order parameter (Figures S15A and S15C) and migratory speed (Figures S15B and S15D) did not vary between Col-I and Col-IV surfaces for softer or stiffer ECMs. These results demonstrate how collagen-dependent defect sensing is dependent on substrate stiffness.

To further understand why the cells on stiffer substrates were able to overcome the μdefects on Col-IV surfaces, we imaged ArpC2, paxillin, and F-actin in MCF10A cells on stiff PA hydrogels. We found that ArpC2 was highly expressed, mature paxillin puncta (FA protein localization) formed, and F-actin fibers were spread throughout the monolayer on both Col-I (Figure 6F) and Col-IV (Figure 6G) stiff ECMs. Since FA and actin-myosin structures are important for cellular force generation, we also measured cellular tractions by measuring the displacement of fluorescent beads embedded in soft gels (this measurement is not possible in stiffer gels as the beads become immobile under any cellular force). Spatial heatmaps of traction magnitudes reveal that the cells did not produce long-distance force transmission when stalled at the μdefect site on soft PA gels (Figures 6H and 6I). Because of previous paxillin data, we know that when cells stall, the formation of distinct paxillin puncta is lower (Figure 5B), which would translate to the lower tractions. Here, both collagen types produced similar levels of tractions (Figure 6J). These results indicate that collagen type-dependent differences arise in intermediate ECM stiffness, while the impact of softer or stiffer ECM on cell migration predominates the effects of collagen type.

Osmotically enhanced membrane tension softens cells and reduces migratory stalling

In addition to the known cellular mechanotransduction of ECM cues via adhesions and the cytoskeleton, the plasma membrane of the cell also serves as a mechanical modulator. When a wound occurs, internal regions of the body can be exposed to extreme changes in osmolarity, wherein hypotonic environments can improve wound healing by increasing immune cell surveillance and migration,47 increasing wound closure rate,48 and spurring lamellipodial protrusions.49 Osmotic changes can lead to changes in plasma membrane tension, in turn impacting actin network growth and protrusion lengths.

Since wound healing in vivo involves changes in solute concentrations, we next wondered whether changes in osmolarity would alter cell membrane mechanics and the cells’ ability to sense ECM μdefects. To this effect, we added 3 mM methyl-β-cyclodextrin to MCF10A media to create a hypertonic environment and diluted the MCF10A media by 50% with filter-sterilized ultrapure water to create a hypotonic environment, as done previously.28 To assess membrane tension modulation, a commercially available Flipper-TR probe was added to the supplemented osmotic condition media50 and measured with fluorescence lifetime intensity microscopy (FLIM).51 Indeed, membrane tension change was uniform from the leading edge and the surrounding follower cells in both hypertonic (Figure 7A) and hypotonic (Figure 7B) conditions. Our FLIM measurements revealed that hypertonic media reduced membrane tension, while hypotonic media increased membrane tension (Figure 7C). However, there was no difference in membrane tension between collagen types (Figure 7D) or spatially near the μdefect (Figure S16A) and within the monolayers (Figures S16B and S16C) during isotonic (control) conditions.

After confirming membrane tension modulation, we wanted to understand whether changes in the membrane tension can impact collagen-dependent stall time. To do this, we measured the stall time, similar to control experiments, and found that cells in hypotonic media (high membrane tension) reduced stall time on Col-IV μdefects (Figure 7D), similar to control conditions on Col-I (Figure 7D). Cells in hypertonic media (low membrane tension) increased stall time only on Col-IV μdefects, not on Col-I. Consistent with these stalling differences, direction autocorrelation decreased in cells on Col-IV in the hypertonic conditions (Figure S17A), while direction autocorrelation remained constant in the hypotonic medium when cells crossed the μdefect (Figure S17D). There was no significant difference in migration speed and order parameter between hypotonic and hypertonic conditions (Figures S17B–S17F). Since Col-IV both increased cell stiffness (Figure 4H) and induced higher migration stalling (Figure 1) in isotonic conditions, we wondered whether similar trends hold true in the case of changing osmolarity. Furthermore, to date, it remains unknown how epithelial cell stiffness responds to the changes in osmolarity and the associated modulation in membrane tension. Through AFM measurements, we found a multi-fold increase in cell stiffness in hypertonic compared to hypotonic media on Col-IV gels (Figure 7E). However, such a significant change in stiffness did not occur in cells on Col-I gels (Figure 7E). These results align with the notion that the migratory stalling occurs in extracellular conditions that increase cell stiffness, such as the presence of Col-IV alone in control isotonic medium or in hypertonic conditions. Similarly, the presence of Col-I keeps cells relatively softer in all osmotic changes tested here, allowing for minimal migratory stalling in all other conditions.

One reason for the membrane tension-dependent defect sensing on Col-IV may be actin restructuring in cells in hypotonic conditions. Membrane tension can modulate protrusions by expanding and contracting the void space behind the plasma membrane. When cells are in a hypotonic environment, they swell due to the influx of water, increasing the void space, and thereby increasing membrane tension (Figure 7F). Conversely, hypertonic conditions induce the opposite effect: the cell membrane experiences increased ruffling providing less room to expand the actin network, thus reducing micro- and macroscale protrusions (Figure 7F). To test this hypothesis, we imaged fixed cells after a 30-min osmotic treatment to observe actin activity on Col-IV-coated PA gels. We found that cells in a hypotonic media increased ArpC2 activity and actin expression at the leading edge (Figure 7G). On the other hand, cells in hypertonic media had lower actin expression at the leading edge and lower ArpC2 expression (Figure 7H). These results demonstrate how membrane tension modulates cellular mechanics (i.e., cell stiffness) and actin network building (ArpC2 expression), aiding migratory wound healing events akin to those seen in vivo.48

DISCUSSION

Collective cell migration in wound healing is a highly complex and carefully orchestrated biological process, where keratinocytes and epithelial cells must coordinate and negotiate with the external environment to close the wound bed. The ECM material properties can impact wound healing and worsen prognosis. During the wound healing process, fibroblasts come into the wound bed to deposit and contract the new ECM, thus increasing the stiffness of the wound bed over time.52 However, this stiffness variation is not universally consistent; for instance, the wound center after hair follicle removal is softer than the tissue margin.14 There are several open questions regarding how several prominent extracellular cues, such as the collagen type, ECM stiffness, and osmolarity, all affect collective cell migration and in turn influence the ability of epithelia to negotiate μdefects and gaps in the ECM. Indeed, we found that the increase in wound healing stiffness can decrease collagen-dependent defect sensing and allow cells to migrate over μdefects in the stiff, Col-IV-rich ECM. Our work demonstrates the increasing need to understand the microscale effects of the ECM environment during wound healing, tumor invasion, and regeneration.

Epithelia rely on seamless and coordinated collective migration to reach wound sites or move across tissue interfaces. We discovered that human epithelial cells are sensitive to superficial μdefects on soft and intermediate Col-IV ECMs, causing multicellular collapse of the actin structure, shorter filopodia, higher cell stiffness, and lower ArpC2 expression. Previous work on gap closures has focused only on fibronectin as the primary ECM protein, leaving the roles of collagens unclear, and studied gaps greater than 10 μm in diameter.24,28,53,54 By generating ECM defects that were parallel to the leading edge (Video S9), we found that cells stalled at a micron-size defect in the surface of soft Col-IV matrices (Video S2), but easily migrated over when the protein type was changed to Col-I (Video S4). When we made the defects half the length (Video S10) or twice the length (Video S11), the leading edge still collapsed into the μdefect. However, this behavior is primarily due to the voided area of collagen, because when the void is filled with collagen, cells migrated over the μdefect on both Col-IV (Video S7) and Col-I (Video S8). When defect thickness was increased to the length scale similar to previous work, we found cells both on Col-IV (Video S12) and Col-I (Video S13) stalled at the thick defect.24,28,53 This demonstrates how μdefects can lead to a significant difference in cellular response in the presence of different collagen types. Future work is needed to understand the limit of microscale gaps that cells can sense, as well as additional ECM protein types.

When migrating epithelia encounter the μdefects on soft ECM with Col-IV, the leading-edge cells rapidly reorganize their actin architecture to grow parallel with the μdefect. This microscale actin reorganization at the front propagated across cells and caused macroscale disruption in directed collective cell migration. We sought to understand how cells perform this cytoskeletal reorganization. Since FAs are required to form and stabilize to anchor the actin cytoskeletal network, we imaged and analyzed FA morphology. Indeed, paxillin puncta formed parallel along the μdefect in leading cells stalled at a μdefect (Figure 5B), and this aligned formation of paxillin assists in the formation of parallel actin fibers. Previous work has shown that FAs form based on the alignment of the underlying matrix fibers, which has been shown to influence Rac1 localization,55 leading to the reorganization of actin fibers, similar to the cells stalled on Col-IV. The differential influence of FA formation may also be attributed to differences in integrin expression, explaining the differential collagen response.43 For the BM, the α1β1 integrin is a primary BM collagen-binding integrin, although both α1β1 and α2β1 bind to fibrillar collagens.33 Cells on Col-I expressed higher levels of integrin α2 compared to those on Col-IV, which may explain why Col-I promoted seamless cell migration across the μdefect. More work is needed to better understand how different integrin types engaged in binding to different collagen types can impact cytoskeletal response and protrusion dynamics in collectively migrating cells.

Based on our measurements and SEM images showing differences in filopodial length between the two collagen types (Figures S10A–S10D), we posited a physical mechanism of defect sensing wherein longer filopodia on Col-I can reach across the non-adherent region of the μdefect. By contrast, shorter filopodia on Col-IV get stuck behind and initiate a cytoskeletal rearrangement and collective migratory stalling, as discussed above. Accordingly, these defect-sensing differences may fundamentally be due to the cells’ ability to generate longer protrusions, whether by changing the collagen type from Col-IV to Col-I or stiffer ECM. To further build on this idea, we considered osmolarity as another extracellular cue that is relevant for wound healing and how it can mechanically alter protrusions via modulation of membrane tension. By increasing the membrane tension via hypotonic treatment, the cell swells and allows more room for the actin network to grow. Actin-modulating proteins, such as the formin-binding protein 1 (FBP17), accumulate at the leading edge when plasma membrane tension increases.56 Additionally, FBP17, part of the TOCA family, mediates WASP/N-WASP signaling, in turn upregulating Arp2/3 actin branching.57 Indeed, hypotonic media increased stall time along with enhanced ArpC2. Conversely, hypertonic conditions reduced ArpC2-based actin fibers and further exacerbated migratory stalling on Col-IV μdefects. These results demonstrate how an increase in membrane tension can activate protrusions through actin network building. Although the effects of osmotic conditions on membrane tension are widely recognized, it was unknown how these relate to cell stiffness along with cells’ protrusive and migratory activity. Our work demonstrates that while hypotonic media enhances actin-based protrusions and migration, it reduces overall cell stiffness. Conversely, hypertonic conditions that promote cell shrinkage stiffen the cells. While these correlations lend new insights into how osmotically tuned membrane tension relates to cell stiffness and migration, it remains unclear how cell stiffness seems to relate inversely to membrane tension, which remains an important area of future work. Overall, in addition to the novel discovery of epithelial sensing of μdefects, this work showcases the importance of investigating how multiple extracellular cues—protein types, stiffness, and osmotic conditions—alter subcellular mechanics and multicellular collective migration. Since these extracellular cues have convoluted effects on mechanosensing and cell migration, a better understanding of their independent influences on cellular responses will fundamentally advance our knowledge of the basic biological processes in wound healing, development, and tumor invasion.

Limitations of the study

Although the BM is composed of a complex mixture of proteins and linker molecules, our reductionist system only used Col-IV for direct comparisons with Col-I. To better understand how different ECM proteins in the BM might perform micron-scale cellular mechanosensing, further work should start by including laminin, perlecan, and nidogen. It is not well known how these ECM proteins on their own affect cytoskeletal signaling in epithelial cells during collective migration and wound healing. In this study, we found lower expression of integrin α2 in cells seeded on Col-IV. It remains to be studied how different integrin types involved in binding to different collagen types affect downstream signaling for actin assembly and disassembly in epithelial cells. Although our F-actin measurements and cytoskeletal disruptions establish a regulatory function of the actin network and filopodial dynamics in defect sensing, future work could directly target formins, Rho-family GTPases, and other regulators or actin bundling, crosslinking, and branching to assess their individual roles. Last, we showed that higher membrane tension leads to lower cell stiffness. However, it is unclear whether this reflects a higher intracellular water concentration, with lower membrane tension caused by the lower water content, or whether the AFM probe is instead sensing the intracellular organelles. Future work will need to differentiate between these possibilities using cortical stiffness and live actin measurements, which will enhance our understanding of how higher membrane tension due to hypotonic conditions impacts the cytoskeleton of epithelial cells and of how various ECM and integrin types together influence the membrane tension response under hyper- and hypotonic conditions.

METHOD DETAILS

Polyacrylamide gel synthesis and collagen coatings

Polyacrylamide precursor solutions were mixed by adjusting 40% acrylamide, 2% bis-acrylamide, 150mM 2-aminoethyl methacrylate, and ultrapure water. 2-aminoethyl methacrylate is required for collagen type IV crosslinking because it allows for increased functionalization on the PA gel surface.30 The 2-aminoethyl methacrylate is necessary because the cells were unable to maintain adherence to the Col-IV-coated surface (Video S1).30 The ratio of acrylamide/bis-acrylamide was combined to form precursor solutions for varying stiffnesses as follows: soft: 3%A/0.05%B (~2kPa), intermediate: 4%A/0.2%B (~10kPa), stiff: 8%A/0.6%B (~42kPa). Gels were polymerized on glass-bottom well plates for live time-lapse and fixed imaging experiments, and glass-bottom Petri dishes for Atomic Force Microscope (AFM) and live actin experiments. Precursor gel solutions were degassed by vacuum or nitrogen bubbling for at least 1 h before fabrication. Glass coverslips were soaked in Sigmacote for 2h and allowed to air-dry to make them hydrophobic. Glass-bottom plates were plasma treated for 10min under vacuum, then allowed to sit in Bind Silane solution (97% ethanol, 3%, 0.5% 3-(Trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate) for 10 min. Once dried, glass coverslips are rinsed with DI water, and glass bottom plates are sprayed with 70% ethanol. Both are gently wiped to remove excess liquid and dust. To make the PA gels, 5% ammonium persulfate (APS) and 0.5% tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) were added to 1mL PA precursor solution. Use a 1000uL pipette to gently mix the solution, being sure not to add air bubbles to the mixture. Once mixed, 25uL PA gel solution was added to the middle of the gel and covered with a 15mm coverslip. Allow the gel to polymerize for at least 20min before adding PBS. Coverslips are removed using a bent needle to pry them off. Polymerized gels are stored in PBS at 4°C until used.

Gels were coated with two distinct types of collagens: Collagen type I and Collagen type IV. PBS is aspirated and gels are allowed to air dry in the hood during collagen solution preparation. PA gels were functionalized using 0.5mg/mL sulfosuccinimidyl 6-(4′-azido-2′-nitrophenylamino) hexanoate (Sulfo-SANPAH) prepared in 50mM HEPES buffer. Sulfo-SANPAH-coated gels were exposed to 365nm UV for 10 min to adhere sulfo-SANPAH to the surface for collagen functionalization. Gels were rinsed twice with PBS before adding either 0.05mg/mL Collagen type I or 0.05mg/mL Collagen type IV. For split collagen conditions, a combined solution of 0.05mg/mL of Collagen type I and 0.05mg/mL of Collagen type IV was added to gels. Collagen-coated gels were set at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2h to allow for polymerization.

Laser ablation micro-defects to PA gels

Superficial μdefects on collagen-coated gels were done using an Andor Micro point Laser Ablation system attached to the Zeiss Cell Observer system (25× water-immersion objective; Carl Zeiss Microscopy) and controlled with Andor i8 software (Andor-Oxford Instruments). The laser dye was of 435nm wavelength (MP-27-435-DYE; Andor-Oxford Instruments) and was checked once a month. The laser pulsed at 15Hz for 5 cycles to ensure the gel was fully ablated. μm-defects were made using the free-hand tool drawn perpendicular to the leading edge, approximately 250 μm in front of the leading cell edge, and in the same Z-plane cells were in focus. Cells were allowed to migrate into the μdefects for a specific amount of time as described under the specific experimental sections below.

Live time-lapse microscopy

Live cell imaging was performed using a Zeiss Cell Observer microscope equipped with a 10× objective and an incubation chamber. The incubation system ensured that 5% CO2, 37°C, and 95% humidity were maintained during the duration of live cell experiments unless otherwise mentioned.

Migration Analysis

24h after cell seeding, the experimental well-plate is added to the microscope for live time-lapse imaging. These experiments only used the brightfield channel, and images were acquired at 10min intervals for 24–36h using a 10× objective. After imaging, image stacks were post-processed by splitting the parent czi files into separate files based on position files. We cut image stacks so only the 20 frames prior to the leading edge coming in contact with the μdefect and 40 frames after were analyzed, totaling 60 frames. We selected ROIs about 250px by 500px. Image stacks were then run through custom ImageJ macros to convert to an HDF5 file type to be run in the program ilastik. We used ilastik to make binary masks of the migrating cells to exclude the μdefect from PIV analysis. We ran ilastik by selecting frames 1, 20, 40, and 60 to act as the training dataset before applying it to the entire image stack. This was done per biological replicate per condition to increase the accuracy of the masks. Masks were outputted by using the “Simple Segmentation” output in ilastik as an HDF5 file type. We then converted the HDF5 masks to TIFF stacks for PIV analysis using a custom ImageJ macro.

Image stacks after post-processing were analyzed using the MATLAB toolbox, PIVLab.60 Image stacks were uploaded with the corresponding masks for regions without cells. Exported mat files were then post-processed in a custom MATLAB script to calculate speed (Equation 1), directional autocorrelation (Equation 2), and order parameter (Equation 3). Results were plotted in GraphPad’s Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA).

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy was performed to visualize collagen coating on PA gels, μdefects on a structural level, and cellular protrusions at the leading edge. As described above, cell monolayers were seeded on 10kPa PA gels with either Col-I or Col-IV coatings. After 24h, μdefects were made, and cells were allowed to migrate for 4h before fixing. For fixing, cells were removed from the incubator and washed with 0.15M cacodylate buffer with 2mM calcium chloride, warmed to 37°C. Cells were gently agitated on a shaker for 15sec to remove all culture media. After removing the washing buffer, samples were fixed using a solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% PFA in 0.15M cacodylate buffer and 2mM calcium chloride with a pH of 7.4. Cells were again gently agitated on a shaker for 15sec, before placing in an incubator for 15min. Cells were then removed and allowed to sit on a shaker at 4°C overnight. Samples were brought to the Washington University Center for Cellular Imaging (WUCCI) for further sample preparation before imaging on a high-resolution scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Merlin FE-SEM).

Fixed immunofluorescence imaging and image analysis

Similar to live time-lapse microscopy experiments, cells were seeded on collagen-coated PA gels in glass-bottom well plates. Well plates had to be used to ensure the height of the gel would stay constant during laser ablation. After making the μdefects as mentioned above, plates were returned to the incubator for 6h before fixation. During fixing, cells were rinsed with warm sterile PBS once to remove media and then left in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10min at room temperature. Gels were rinsed twice with sterile PBS. Plates were stored in sterile PBS at 4°C until used for staining.

Plates were stained based on a previously used protocol.61 Cells were rinsed three times with staining buffer solution (0.5% bovine serum albumin; BSA) and left to sit for 5min during the last wash step. After staining buffer removal, 0.1% Triton X-100 was added to permeabilize the membrane and left on the plate for 10min. Samples were then washed three times with blocking solution (1% BSA, 10% goat serum, and 0.3M glycine). After the last wash, 1mL of blocking solution was added to each well and allowed to sit for 1h at room temperature. After the blocking step, primary antibodies were diluted in staining buffer and added to the plate. The plate was left out at room temperature for 1h before moving to 4°C storage overnight. After the primary antibodies, the plate was rinsed twice with staining buffer before adding secondary antibodies diluted in staining buffer. Secondary antibodies were left in the well for 1h at room temperature. After secondary antibody treatment, the wells were rinsed twice with non-sterile PBS before adding Phalloidin diluted in PBS for 45min at room temperature. The plate was rinsed once with non-sterile PBS before adding Hoechst (diluted in PBS and filter-sterilized using a 0.2 μm syringe filter). The plate was rinsed with PBS and stored with non-sterile PBS at 4°C. The plate was always kept away from light after the fixation step. All samples had F-actin and Hoechst added to label the cell body and nucleus, respectively. Plates were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 880 laser-scanning confocal microscope with an Airyscanning module at the WUCCI. We used a 40× water immersion objective with 1.5× Zoom for all imaging. Laser power and intensities were kept constant for each protein type to ensure no bias across conditions. All channels except the nucleus channel (405nm) were calibrated for the Airyscan system.

Several different analysis methods were applied, depending on the protein of interest and the structure of interest. All images were turned so the μdefect was to the right of the leading edge unless otherwise stated:

Actin fiber orientation and order

F-actin images were analyzed by projecting the maximum intensity along the z axis. We used the ImageJ plug-in OrientationJ to measure the degree fibers aligned about the μdefect within the ROI.62 The actin order parameter was measured using a MATLAB script adopted from the Alignment by Fourier Transform.32,63 At least three regions of 450px × 170px (front and rear, separately) were selected for both methods.

Filopodia length

To analyze the filopodia length of fixed actin images, z stack images of actin were projected based on max intensity. ROIs of the leading edge were only selected and ran through the ImageJ plugin FiloQuant.37 Edge detection and filopodia detection settings stay consistent across all conditions. The output data was then plotted for all conditions and compared based on collagen-coating type.

Intensity measurements

To analyze protein expression for F-actin ArpC2, CIP4, α-actinin, paxillin, and integrin α2, we used the Integrated Density measurement in ImageJ. Before measurement, the stack for the protein of interest was projected along the along the z axis based on max intensity. We selected ROIs uniform in the area and the front and rear of the ROI. We measured the integrated density of each ROI and compared them to front vs. rear and Col-I vs. Col-IV within the same protein group.

Adhesion measurements

Image stacks were transformed in ImageJ, so the μdefect was at the top of the image with the monolayer below and projected along the z stack based on max intensity. All images were background subtracted by 150px intensity with a sliding parabola. To measure the area, aspect ratio, and angularity, the max image was duplicated and masked for punctuate based on the area range of 0.5–10 μm2. Area is defined as the area of the adhesion body in μm2, aspect ratio is defined as the major axis over the minor axis of the adhesion body, and angularity is defined as the degree the major axis is angled regarding the μdefect (Figure 5A).

NM2A and actin colocalization measurements

NM2A colocalization was measured using the ImageJ Plug-in JaCoP.43 Raw z-stacks were projected along the max intensity along the z axis for both the NM2A channel and the F-actin channel. Using JaCoP, NM2A was assigned to ImageA, and F-actin was assigned to ImageB. Thresholding was applied so that stress fibers were analyzed in the colocalization algorithm. The measurements used were Pearson’s coefficient with Costes’ test (PCC) to answer whether there was a functional coupling between ImageA and ImageB based on intensity variations.

Live actin imaging and image analysis

Live actin images were done using the commercially available SiR-actin kit. Experiments were done in a Petri dish with an 18mm coverslip on the bottom. Hydrogels of 10kPa stiffness were fabricated as mentioned above. Gels were coated with either Col-I or Col-IV, and 25,000 MCF10A cells were seeded in the center of the collagen-coated gel. The monolayer was allowed to grow overnight at 37°C and 5% CO2. After that, the plate was loaded onto the Zeiss Axios Observer with the Andor laser module, and cuts were made approximately 250 μm from the leading edge. Cells were then returned to the incubator for another 4h. SiR-actin probe was diluted into a 1mM stock solution using 50 μL anhydrous DMSO. For each experiment, the stock solution was diluted to 1 μM SiR-actin probe and 10 μM verapamil – a broad efflux pump inhibitor – into warm MCF10A media. 2mL of solution was added to the plate and returned to the incubator for another 2h. 1h before the experiment, the Zeiss LSM 880 laser-scanning confocal microscope with a heated incubation chamber (37°C and 5% CO2) was turned on to allow for sufficient time for the system to warm up. The probe solution was in the dish throughout the entirety of the experiment.

For the experiment, Cy5 laser (555nm excitation wavelength) and a 40× oil objective were used with z-stacks obtained every 1 μm, and time intervals set to every 5min. To ensure there was no biasing between samples, laser power, and laser intensity was kept constant, and four to five regions were chosen for each biological replicate. Experiments ran for 6–10h, depending on how long it took cells to migrate into the μdefect. This protocol is based on previously published work.64

Once taken, image stacks were analyzed by reducing image stacks to 10 frames before coming in contact with the μdefect, and at least 60 frames after (totaling 70–80 frames) to get at least 300min of live actin migration. ROIs were chosen at the leading edge and 70–100 μm for front and rear ROIs, respectively. ROIs were 140px × 183px and kept constant throughout the replicates and conditions. Prior to analysis, masks were overlayed to ensure no stray pixel values that were not SiR-actin are measured during PIVLab runs. Grey-scale images were then uploaded to PIVLab and analyzed using a 64px, 32px, and 16px interrogation window size. Velocity limits were selected based on where a majority of the vectors resided. The Velocity Magnitude and *.mat files were exported and analyzed using a custom MATLAB script similar to the Migration Analysis data pipeline. For the SiR-actin analysis, we used temporal data from PIVLabs to measure the direction autocorrelation of actin fibers, here forth referred to as actin alignment, and the velocity of the actin fibers, here forth referred to as actin movement. Stress fibers were measured based on visible stress fibers in time frame 1, similar to Figure S9A. The same stress fiber was followed and manually measured using the ImageJ straight-line segment tool.

Pharmacological perturbations of actin and contractility

To understand the effects of the actin network, cellular contractility, and contractility activation, inhibitors were used during live imaging to separately quantify their effects. All the following were added 2hr prior to imaging: 0.15mM CK666 (Arp2/3 complex inhibitor), 50 μM ML141 (rac1/Cdc42 inhibitor), and 10 μM Blebbistatin (Myosin ATPase inhibitor). To activate contractility, 1nM of Calyculin A was added 2h before imaging, but removed 45min later and replaced with fresh media due to cytotoxic effects.

Osmotic treatment

To understand the role of membrane tension, we changed the osmolarity of the media based on previously published protocols.28 To increase membrane tension, hypotonic media were made by mixing filter-sterilized ultra-pure water (UPW) and MCF10A media 50%/50% by volume. To decrease membrane tension, hypertonic media were made by adding 3mM methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MbCD) to 10mL MCF10A media. The hypotonic and hypertonic media were added 2h before time-lapse imaging, and 30min before FLIM and fixed imaging.

Fluorescence lifetime intensity measurements and analysis

Fluorescence lifetime intensity microscopy (FLIM) measurements were done on a Leica Sp8 DIVE Multiphoton microscope with a FALCON module and Leica CFX Flex upright fixed stage microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). A 488nm pulsed laser was used for excitation, and photons were collected through a 600–650nm emission bandpass filter. A stage heater was set to keep a constant 37°C. The system was allowed to warm up for 30min before imaging. While warming up, samples were prepared for FLIM imaging. Samples were prepared as described above, and defects were made at least 4h before imaging. The Flipper-TR probe was diluted in Lebovitz media without serum and the addition of MCF10A media supplements to make a 2μM solution. Samples were first washed with PBS before adding the 2μM Flipper probe solution. Samples were allowed to sit for 10–15min to allow the probe to permeate but before membrane turnover. After the sample prep, samples are added to the stage, and a 40× immersion water objective is lowered into the sample. Images are collected in 512 × 512px scan area and rescanned six times to ensure at least 500,000 photons were collected (500 photons per bin). Data was analyzed and filtered for lifetime decay using LAS X Software with the FALCON module for FLIM-FRET analysis (Leica Microsystems).

Atomic force microscopy

To quantify Young’s Modulus of PA gels and cell stiffnesses, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed similarly to previous works.61,65 Measurements were acquired with a Bruker Bioscope Resolve Bio-atomic force microscope (Bruker, Billerica, USA) and a Zeiss Axios Observer. AFM probes were custom-made probes with silicone-nitride cantilever and a 4.5μm (cell stiffness) or 1μm polystyrene bead (gel stiffness) attached as a tip (Novascan Technologies, Inc. Boone, IA) with a reported stiffness of 0.01N/m or 0.06N/m, respectively. Tips were calibrated in room temperature test solution (specified below) by testing deflection sensitivity on a clean glass surface in fluid and then thermal tuning for the cantilever’s spring constant. After, the probe was allowed to equilibrate for 10min to ensure there was no signal drift due to fluid effects. Cell-free substrates were tested in PBS and a 100 μm × 100 μm scan area for force map measurements with a resolution of 1μm per indentation. The measurements were conducted in at least four different areas of the sample. AFM on the cells was conducted similarly; however, the scan area was done 50 μm × 50 μm scan area with a resolution of 1μm per indentation. Force maps were arranged so the defect was in the middle. Data was filtered based on the real height of the probe and removed below −1500nm. A modified Hertz model was implemented in the Nanoscope Analysis Software (Bruker, Billerica, USA) to analyze and export force curves. A custom Python script was used to filter the modulus based on the normalized across samples based on the real height of the probe.

Traction microscopy

The PA gels were fabricated using the same protocol as described above, with the addition of 4 μL 0.2 μm GFP fluorescent beads to every 1mL of PA gel precursor solution. Gels were flipped during the polymerization process to ensure adequate concentration of beads near the top surface of the gel. Collagen-coating and cell seeding were done using the same protocol as mentioned above. Images were taken of z-stacks covering at least 30 μm at 2.5 μm intervals. For live-dead traction measurements, 0.5% trypsin was added after the live cell imaging. Images were immediately reimaged for relaxed bead images. Bead displacements were measured using the PIV (in MATLAB) and tractions were calculated and plotted using a custom MATLAB script.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All data represent at least three biological replicates from separate experiments, each with three to six technical replicates. All bar graphs are presented by mean ± standard error (SE). Violin plots are presented by mean with 10th and 90th percentiles. Line plots are presented by mean as the line and shaded regions as SE. Statistical significance was determined using One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s t-tests for multiple comparisons, unless only two conditions were tested against each other, then a Student’s t test was applied for statistical analysis. All statistical analysis was completed in GraphPad’s Prism.
