Rod-cone dystrophy with myriad systemic manifestations
Authors: Goudappa Patil, Premika Pandarasamy, N. V. S. Krishna Reddy, Pratyusha Ganne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rod-cone dystrophy (RCD) is an inherited retinal disorder primarily affecting rod photoreceptors, followed by cone degeneration. We present the case of a 26-year-old male with RCD and multiple systemic abnormalities (atrial septal defect, hypogonadism, liver disease with gastric varices, hypertriglyceredemia, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and delayed epiphyseal closure) in the same individual. Ophthalmic evaluation revealed retinal pigment epithelial atrophy, bone spicule pigmentation, a macular hole in the right eye, and an epiretinal membrane in the left. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous ABCA4 c.3113C>T (p.Ala1038Val) mutation, previously associated with retinal dystrophies. We postulate that this case represents a unique blended phenotype, i.e., retinal disease due to ABCA4 and multiple systemic issues due to an unidentified syndrome.

Introduction

Rod-cone dystrophy (RCD) is a heterogeneous group of inherited retinal disorders affecting primarily rod photoreceptors with secondary cone degeneration. RCD is genetically and phenotypically heterogeneous. At the genetic level, different genes can produce the same phenotype, and the same genetic mutation can have different phenotypes. In addition, different variants in the same gene can result in varying phenotypes. To date, over 89 genes have been associated with RCD, reflecting its complex genotype–phenotype correlations.[1] ABCA4 gene mutations are implicated in a spectrum of inherited retinal diseases such as Stargardt’s disease, fundus flavimaculatus, recessive RCD, and age-related macular degeneration.[2] However, till date, their association in syndromic RCD is not found.

We describe a unique case of RCD with multiple systemic associations (atrial septal defect, hypogonadism, liver disease with gastric varices, hypertriglyceredemia, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and delayed epiphyseal closure) in the same individual and a mutation in the ABCA4 gene.

Case Report

A 26-year-old man born out of a consanguineous marriage presented with decreased vision in both eyes since childhood. His past medical history included a diagnosis of congenital heart disease (atrial septal disease). There were no similar complaints reported in his parents. The patient had a normal birth and developmental history. He had two siblings who passed away early in life.

The best-corrected visual acuity was counting fingers at 2 meters in both eyes. He had a refractive error of −5D in the right eye (RE) and −6D in the left eye (LE). The anterior segment examination was normal in both eyes. Fundus examination showed retinal pigment epithelial atrophy throughout the fundus with bone spicule pigmentation and arteriolar attenuation in both eyes, suggestive of RCD [Figure 1a and b]. In addition, RE showed a full-thickness macular hole [Figure 1c], and LE showed an epiretinal membrane (CMT = 243 μm) [Figure 1d].

Systemic examination revealed a short stature (152 cm), acanthosis of skin over the neck area, and absence of axillary hair. Bilateral testes were not palpable on physical examination, but the external genitalia appeared to be normal.

Laboratory investigations showed normal hemoglobin (12.6 g/dl), plasma glucose (81mg/dl), increased total bilirubin (2.6 mg/dl) and alkaline phosphatase levels (146U/L), normal renal function, borderline elevation of total cholesterol levels (217 mg/dl), and hypertriglyceridemia (362 mg/dl).

Hormonal analysis showed borderline elevation of serum insulin (25.5 mIU/ml), decreased testosterone (<2.5 ng/dl), luteinizing hormone (0.3 mIU/ml), follicle stimulating hormone (0.3 mIU/ml), insulin-like growth factor-1 (<20 ng/ml), serum cortisol (33.1 nmol/L), T4 (2.4 microgram/dl), Vitamin D3 (10.9 ng/ml), and raised thyroid-stimulating hormone (16.7 mIU/L). However, serum calcium and phosphate levels were normal. The echocardiography of the heart showed a closed atrial septal defect. X-ray of the limbs showed nonfusion of the distal epiphysis of the ulna and radium, and nonfusion of the femur head, greater and lesser trochanter [Figure 1e and f].

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis revealed chronic liver disease with gastric varices and mild splenomegaly. Ultrasonography of the scrotum showed bilateral small testes (size: right: 1.7 cm × 0.7 cm × 1.2 cm, left 1.8 cm × 0.8 cm × 1.0 cm).

Whole exome sequencing identified a mutation in the Exon 21 of the ABCA4 gene (c.3113C>T) (p.Ala1038Val) in a heterozygous state as a pathological variant. Mutations in Exon 2 of the GLB1 gene (c.235G>A) (p.Ala79Thr) (homozygous) and in Exon 3 of the PRPF6 gene (c.308C>G) (p.Ala103Gly) (heterozygous) were found as variants of unknown significance.

Discussion

The current case is interesting because RCD is associated with multiple systemic conditions occurring in the same individual, a combination that has not been previously reported.

Rod cone dystrophy, hypogonadism, hepatic dysfunction, insulin resistance, hypertriglyceridemia, hypothyroidism, and congenital heart disease have been reported to occur in Bardet–Biedl syndrome and Alstrom syndrome.[34] Delayed epiphyseal closure can occur secondary to hypopituitarism.[5] Bardet–Biedl syndrome-1 (BBS1) is caused by a mutation in the BBS1 gene on chromosome 11q13.[6] Alstrom syndrome is caused by mutations in ALMS1, located on chromosome 2p13.[7] However, the only mutation detected in our case was a mutation in the Exon 21 of the ABCA4 gene (c.3113C>T) (p.Ala1038Val) in heterozygous state. ABCA4, a member of the ATP-binding cassette transporter family, subfamily A, is located on chromosome 1p22.1. It comprises 50 exons and encodes a protein made up of 2273 amino acids.[8] ABCA4 plays a critical role in transporting N-retinylidene-phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylethanolamine across photoreceptor disc membranes, from the lumen to the cytoplasmic side. When this function is disrupted, toxic retinoid compounds build up, leading to dysfunction in both retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (RPE) and photoreceptor cells.[9] More than 2000 variants have been identified, with missense mutations being the most common.[10] Till date, although rod cone dystrophy has been reported with a mutation in this gene, no systemic effects of this mutation have been reported.

Mutations in the GLB1 gene are typically associated with two major conditions: GM1 gangliosidosis and mucopolysaccharidosis type IVB (Morquio B disease).[11] The role of PRPF6 in rod-cone dystrophy typically involves isolated retinal degeneration without systemic manifestations.[12] However, in our case, both GLB1 and PRPF6 mutations were reported as mutations of unknown significance.

Hence, we postulate that this case represents a blended phenotype, i.e., retinal disease due to ABCA4 and systemic issues due to an unidentified syndrome.

Recent studies using OCT have shown that macular abnormalities are present in 7.4% of patients with retinitis pigmentosa.[13] While macular holes are not a common feature of ABCA4-related diseases, severe retinal thinning and degeneration in the macula may indirectly contribute to macular hole formation. Macular hole formation in ABCA4-related diseases is likely due to progressive macular degeneration rather than a direct effect of the gene itself.

This case highlights a unique presentation of RCD associated with a heterozygous ABCA4 c.3113C>T (p.Ala1038Val) mutation. While ABCA4 mutations are primarily linked to retinal dystrophies, the presence of a macular hole and systemic findings such as short stature, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and chronic liver disease adds to the phenotypic variability of ABCA4-related conditions. The diverse manifestations in this case could be due to a blended phenotype, i.e., retinal disease due to ABCA4 and systemic issues due to an unidentified syndrome. Further research is warranted to explore the functional implications of this mutation and its potential contribution to systemic abnormalities.
