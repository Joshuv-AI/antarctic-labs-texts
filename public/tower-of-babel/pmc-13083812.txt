Gut microbiome dysbiosis and inflammatory markers in severe COVID-19: links to atherosclerosis and potential therapeutic insights
Authors: Nayebali Ahmadi, Mohammad Javad Hossein Tehrani, Ashkan Alamdary, Rasul Moukhah, Rajab Mardani, Maryam Shahali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:COVID-19 is implicated in the exacerbation of atherosclerosis, potentially leading to its clinical manifestations. The aim of this study was to evaluate serum biomarkers, including albumin (ALB), C-reactive protein (CRP), ferritin (FERR), CD16, CD18, NLRP3, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), in patients with COVID-19. In addition, assessment of microbiome profiles may provide insights into disease severity and gastrointestinal health. Method:In this case-control study, serum samples were collected from 90 individuals: 30 patients with COVID-19 admitted to the intensive care unit, 30 hospitalized patients with COVID-19, and 30 healthy individuals. Concentrations of ALB, CRP, FERR, NLRP3, CD16, CD18, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used to assess microbial diversity in stool samples. Result:No significant age difference was noted between the patient (55.40 ± 10.34 years) and the control groups (58.34 ± 11.71 years). Substantial increases in CRP, CD-18, CD-16, IL-6, Alb, and TNF-α (P < 0.001) were observed in severe cases compared to non-severe cases. Microbiome analysis indicated a 40% decrease in Roseburia and a 35% reduction in Faecalibacterium in ICU patients. Conclusion:Evaluating these biomarkers provides critical insights into atherosclerosis susceptibility in COVID-19 patients and reveals meaningful relationships among COVID-19, atherosclerosis, and gastrointestinal health.Key Words: COVID-19, Inflammasomes, Gut Microbiome, Atherosclerosis

Methods

Sample collection and section

A total of 90 subjects (48 males, 42 females) were enrolled from Ayatollah Kashani Hospital (Tehran, Iran): 30 ICU patients with COVID-19, 30 inpatients with COVID-19, and 30 controls. All participants had throat swabs collected for SARS-CoV-2 testing, and PCR was performed using the SANSURE Novel Coronavirus Nucleic Acid Diagnostic Kit. To assess the accuracy of the study, the ex-ante power analysis included the following exclusion criteria: age <18 years, immunodeficiency/immunosuppressive therapy, recent use of antibiotics/probiotics, chronic gastrointestinal disease, pregnancy, inability to provide samples, and collection of comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, obesity, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease).

Determination of CRP concentration

CRP levels were quantified using a CRP kit (Pars Azmun, Iran). The measurement was based on determining turbidity resulting from the formation of a complex between CRP in the patient's sample and a sensitized antibody against human CRP. The turbidity detected by immunoturbidimetry was directly proportional to the amount of CRP in the sample and was reported in mg/dl.

Measurement of ALB concentration

ALB level was quantified using an ALB kit (MAN, Iran). The measurement was based on serum albumin, measured colorimetrically using Green Bromocresol at pH 4.2, and reported as g/dL.

Measurement of FERR concentration

Serum Ferritin level was measured using Dia. Pro ELISA diagnostic kit (Dia. Pro Diagnostic Bioprobes, Milan, Italy) based on the quantitative immunoenzymometry method. The assay was based on specific monoclonal antibodies and the sandwich method. An anti-ferritin monoclonal antibody (Dia. Pro Diagnostic Bioprobes, Milan, ItalyMilan, Italy) was used to conjugate with the HRP enzyme to cover the space between the solid phase and the anti-ferritin antibody. Upon addition of the sample serum, the ferritin molecule reacted with antibodies, forming sandwiches between the bound antibodies on the solid phase and the conjugated antibody. The intensity of the complex was measured at 450 nm, and the results were reported as ng/ml.

Measurement of serum NLRP3 level

The level of NLRP3 in serum samples was determined using the Abcam Sandwich ELISA Immunoassay kit (Abcam plc, Inc., UK) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, serum samples were added to wells in microplates that were pre-coated with an anti-NLRP3-specific antibody (Abcam, UK). After incubating the microplates at room temperature for 2 hours, a conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, UK) was added to the samples to bind the protein of interest. Finally, the optical density was measured at 450 nm, and NLRP3 levels were expressed in ng/mL.

Measurement of CD16 and CD18 level

The levels of CD16 and CD18 in serum samples were determined using the Abcam Sandwich ELISA Immunoassay kit (Abcam plc, Inc., UK) according to the manufacturer's instructions. This assay employs an antibody specific for Human CD16 and CD18 coated on a 96-well plate. Standards and samples are pipetted into the wells, and Human CD16 and CD18 present in the samples bind to the immobilized antibody. The wells are washed, and biotinylated anti-Human CD16 and CD18 antibody is added. After washing away unbound biotinylated antibodies, HRP-conjugated streptavidin is pipetted to the wells. The wells are washed again, and a TMB substrate solution is added; color develops in proportion to the amount of Human CD16 and CD18 bound. The Stop Solution changes the color from blue to yellow, and the intensity is measured at 450 nm.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Stool DNA was extracted with the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen) with an initial bead-beating step. qPCR (SYBR Green) quantified Roseburia (Forward: 5’-TGATGCTGTTGAAAGGCAAC-3’; Reverse: 5’-CCTTACGGGTTTCCGTCAAT-3’) and Faecalibacterium (Forward: 5’-CCTACGGCAGCAGTAGGAA-3’; Reverse: 5’-GGTATGAAAGTTTCTGCGTGA-3’) against universal 16S rRNA (Forward: 5’-CACCACACCTTCTACAATGAG-3’; Reverse: 5’-TGGGTCAGAAGGATTCCTATG-3’). Each 20 µL reaction contained 2× Master Mix, 0.5 µM primers, and 2 µL diluted DNA (1:10). Thermocycling: 95°C for 10 min; 40 cycles of 95°C/15 s and 60°C/30 s; melt curve 60–95°C. Controls included no-template controls (NTC) and positive controls with known Roseburia faecalis/ Faecalibacterium prausnitzii DNA (10⁴ copies/µL); no reverse transcription was used. Absolute quantification used standard curves from plasmids (10²–10⁸ copies/µL); Ct values were converted to copy numbers. Normalization was performed as (i) copies per ng stool DNA and (ii) relative abundance (target copies / total bacterial 16S copies). LOD was determined empirically (Roseburia ≈ 10², Faecalibacterium ≈ 10², 16S ≈ 10¹ copies/reaction).

Biomarker measurement

The serum levels of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (BioLegend, San Diego, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The assays' sensitivity was verified, and all samples were run in duplicate to ensure accurate results.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Subsequently, a one-way ANOVA followed by a post hoc Tukey test was conducted to compare group means. The correlation of variables was assessed using the Spearman correlation test, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. The operating characteristic (ROC) curve and AUC were used to analyze the optimal cut-off for predicting positive RT-PCR cases. In this study, an AUC of 0.9 to 1 was defined as excellent accuracy, 0.8 to 0.9 as very good, 0.7 to 0.8 as good, 0.6 to 0.7 as sufficient, 0.5 to 0.6 as nasty, and < 0.5 as poor (useless test).

Discussion

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of medium-sized to large arteries, initiated and maintained by leukocyte infiltration into the subendothelial space. COVID-19 is a respiratory disease resulting from infection with the SARS-CoV-2 virus. COVID-19 can adversely affect the endothelium and cardiomyocytes, triggering blood clotting and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby intensifying the development of atherosclerosis (12, 13). Approximately 25% of individuals with COVID-19 experience cardiovascular complications, especially among older individuals with pre-existing atherosclerosis and its associated clinical manifestations (14). In this study, we uncovered novel evidence that COVID-19 can exacerbate atherosclerosis. Atherosclerosis and COVID-19 may affect each other bidirectionally. The presence of cardiovascular disease can be influenced by an individual's susceptibility to viral infections. Numerous studies consistently demonstrate a substantial correlation between the severity of COVID-19 and the concentrations of C-reactive protein (CRP), Albumin, and Ferritin.

Elevated levels of CRP have been associated with severe COVID-19 and poor prognosis (15). CRP is an acute-phase protein produced by the liver in response to inflammation in the body (16). Elevated levels of CRP have been observed in patients with COVID-19, especially those with severe disease (16, 18). In this study, we observed a significant increase in CRP levels among hospitalized and ICU patients with COVID-19 compared with Control individuals (Figure 1). A recent study found that CRP levels can be used as a diagnostic marker and also reflect the severity and prognosis of COVID-19. However, it is essential to note that there are numerous causes of elevated CRP, including acute and chronic responses, which can be infectious or non-infectious in etiology (16). Therefore, CRP alone cannot be used to diagnose COVID-19, but it can provide critical information for guiding clinical diagnosis and treatment of COVID-19-related uncontrolled inflammation (16, 18).

Albumin is a protein produced by the liver and is essential for maintaining blood oncotic pressure (19). Numerous studies have demonstrated a correlation between diminished albumin levels and unfavorable outcomes in individuals with COVID-19, establishing it as a reliable predictor of both disease severity and mortality (19, 21). A study reported that 20% of non-surviving COVID-19 patients had elevated albumin levels (19). Albumin levels have also been found to be an independent risk factor for severe COVID-19 infection and death at 30 days (20). Elevated albumin levels are not commonly observed in COVID-19 patients, and they are not a predictor of disease severity or mortality (19, 21). In the current study, there is a noteworthy increase in albumin levels among hospitalized and ICU patients with COVID-19 compared with control individuals (Figure 2).

Ferritin is a protein that stores and releases iron in response to the body's requirements. Increased ferritin levels have been observed in patients with COVID-19, especially in those with severe disease, in studies by Kurian SJ et al. and Id Z et al. Studies have shown that high ferritin levels are associated with a higher risk of complications and death in COVID-19 patients (17, 22). In this study, we observed a substantial increase in ferritin levels among hospitalized and ICU patients with COVID-19 compared with Control individuals (Figure 3). Ferritin levels can also serve as a diagnostic marker and reflect the severity and prognosis of COVID-19. A study found that individuals with severe or very severe COVID-19 had elevated serum ferritin levels, with the very severe group exhibiting significantly higher levels than the severe group (17). In another study, it was found that patients who succumbed to COVID-19 exhibited elevated ferritin levels upon hospital admission and consistently throughout their hospitalization. Hence, the study concluded that COVID-19 severity is closely associated with serum ferritin levels (17, 23). Furthermore, the ferritin-to-albumin ratio (FAR) has been proposed as a novel prognostic indicator for COVID-19, with higher levels associated with increased mortality risk (24, 25). In the current study, the ferritin-to-albumin ratio shows a significant increase among ICU patients with COVID-19 compared to the levels observed in Control individuals (Figure 4). This is consistent with the findings of Varım et al., who identified the neutrophil count to albumin ratio as a predictor of mortality in COVID-19 patients (26).

Transcriptional changes in CD16 monocytes may contribute to the pathogenesis of COVID-19, with indications suggesting a potential role. In contrast, the significant involvement of the NLRP3 inflammasome in severe COVID-19 pathogenesis leads to systemic hyperinflammation and dysregulated cytokine release. Activating the NLRP3 inflammasome by SARS-CoV-2 infection intensifies cytokine production, further contributing to the cytokine storm observed in severe cases. Additionally, this activation is linked to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which has been found to contribute to atherosclerosis (27, 30). In the current study, a significant increase in NLRP3 levels was observed among both hospitalized and ICU patients with COVID-19 compared to Control individuals. Cytokine release is dependent on the isotype, with rat IgG2b and human IgG1 inducing the highest levels, a response inhibited by antibodies to CD16, the low-affinity Fc receptor for IgG (FcgammaR) (31). Peripheral blood CD16 monocytes are essential in the host response to viral infections, and COVID-19 is characterized by an altered immune and inflammatory response (27). Additionally, CD16 and CD18, two types of integrins, are involved in the recruitment of inflammatory cells in cardiovascular disease. CD18, also known as β2 integrin, is expressed on the surface of leukocytes. At the same time, CD16 is a low-affinity Fcγ receptor expressed on the surface of natural killer cells, monocytes, and macrophages (32, 33). In the present study, we observed a significant increase in CD16 and CD18 levels in both hospitalized and ICU patients with COVID-19 compared with control individuals (Figure 6A, B). Furthermore, the study revealed a significant increase in the CD18:CD16 ratio among ICU patients with COVID-19 compared to control individuals (Figure 6C). The accumulation of pro-inflammatory leukocytes drives the progression of atherosclerosis and its clinical complications, and directly modulates tissue-healing in the infarcted heart after myocardial infarction (34). CD18/CD16 is a pair of integrin receptors that play a crucial role in the recruitment of inflammatory cells in cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis (35). The role of CD16 and CD18 in atherosclerosis is not yet fully understood (36). However, studies have shown that CD16 monocytes are associated with subclinical atherosclerosis. Additionally, CD18 has been shown to play a role in leukocyte localization during atherosclerosis (37). The relationship between atherosclerosis and COVID-19 is complex and multifaceted. Both conditions involve the renin-angiotensin system, with ACE2 playing a key role in SARS-CoV-2 infection and atherosclerosis (38). The conclusion that COVID-19 may exacerbate atherosclerosis by increasing CD16 and CD18 levels, as well as the higher sensitivity and specificity of serum CRP concentrations, CD18, and CD16 levels, strengthens this issue, potentially leading to adverse outcomes, and is supported by standard inflammatory mechanisms observed in both conditions. Compared to other variables, this suggests the potential for common treatment strategies. Further research and clinical validation are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms and clinical implications of these observed associations in the context of COVID-19. In this study, we aimed to investigate the gut microbiome's composition and its association with inflammatory markers in patients with severe COVID-19. Our results demonstrated significant dysbiosis characterized by reduced levels of beneficial bacteria, particularly Roseburia and Faecalibacterium, in ICU patients compared to those in the general ward and healthy controls. These findings align with previous research suggesting that a healthy gut microbiome is crucial for maintaining immune homeostasis and may play a protective role during infectious diseases.

The substantial decrease in the abundance of Roseburia and Faecalibacterium in ICU patients could contribute to heightened systemic inflammation, as indicated by the elevated serum levels of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α). Our correlation analysis further supports this notion, revealing a positive relationship between reductions in these beneficial taxa and increased cytokine levels. This suggests that dysbiosis may exacerbate inflammatory responses in critically ill patients, potentially worsening clinical outcomes.

The significant differences observed in microbiome composition across groups underscore the importance of monitoring gut health in the context of COVID-19. Restoration of beneficial microbiota through dietary interventions or probiotic supplementation could represent a potential therapeutic strategy to mitigate inflammation and improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated excellent predictive validity for the studied biomarkers, reinforcing their potential utility in clinical settings for early identification of patients at risk of severe disease progression. Overall, our findings highlight the crucial role of the gut microbiome in modulating inflammatory responses during COVID-19 and suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting the microbiome could be beneficial in managing severe cases of the disease. Also, these findings can help provide a better understanding of COVID-19 and the factors that cause it, and offer researchers improved diagnostic methods for diseases associated with it.

The findings presented underscore discernible differences in serum parameters among COVID-19 patients, particularly those in the ICU, compared with their control counterparts. Noteworthy variations were observed in key markers, including CRP, ALB, ferritin, NLRP3, CD16, and CD18, revealing distinctive patterns in both ICU and hospitalized patients. Elevated levels of NLRP3, CD16, and CD18 were notably elevated in both ICU and hospitalized patients, implicating their potential involvement in the inflammatory response, the renin-angiotensin system, and endothelial dysfunction. These findings suggest a potential role for CD16 and CD18 in the development of atherosclerosis in COVID-19 patients, adding a layer of complexity to our understanding of the disease's pathophysiology. It is also suggested that new therapeutic approaches can be developed by better understanding the factors involved in COVID-19, including atherosclerosis and its effects after infection, as well as the microbiome structure of infected individuals.
