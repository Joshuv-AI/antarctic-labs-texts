CoFish: co-designing citizen science between fishers and scientists to monitor the phosphorus distribution across two lake Geneva basins
Authors: Tania Jenkins, Laura Fayet, Alexandre Fayet, Daniel Chollet, Yves Depraz, Michaël Dumaz, Neal Ricci, Lydia Lucchetta, Orlane Anneville, Philippe Arpagaus, Maria Luengo Almada, Roxane Fillion, Matteo Gios, Yasmine Moftizadeh, Mridul K Thomas, Bruno J Strasser, Elisa Radosta, Bastiaan W Ibelings
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEutrophication, followed by re-oligotrophication during lake restoration, in many perialpine lakes has caused changes to the biodiversity and functioning of freshwater ecosystems. In Lake Geneva, total phosphorus (TP) concentration has reduced since the 1970-80s and the yearly average is now close to the upper value of the target range of 10–15 µg/L. For over 60 years the lake has been monitored at SHL2, the central, deepest point in the Eastern basin, complemented by data from GE3 in the Western basin. Selection of these reference points was based on a lake-wide TP analysis in the 1950s. Lake Geneva is a popular fishing destination for anglers and over 100 commercial fishers, who have expressed concerns that further TP reductions could damage the sustainability of their livelihoods. They have called for a re-evaluation of the historical sampling points to determine whether SHL2 and GE3 can still be considered representative for lake nutrient concentrations.ResultsWe present the scientific and societal impacts of CoFish, a co-designed research project between scientists and fishers of Lake Geneva. To reassess the spatial variability of TP, we collected integrated water samples across the lake, using stoppered hosepipes as a collection instrument. In this article, we present four key messages: i) there was spatial variation in phosphorus levels, and in most cases the two long-term monitoring stations fall within an acceptable range of variability; ii) the concentrations of phosphorus are generally low, at levels that could impact plankton development; iii) citizen science complemented long-term monitoring for a more spatially extensive dataset; iv) the co-design process resulted in community empowerment and a willingness to further collaborate.ConclusionsThe management implications of this work are that using a single reference sampling point to represent the lake basin is practical but not straightforward. In the discussion we advocate for a re-evaluation of the TP targets, given radical changes in the lake’s physical structure and food web. We further highlight the important role of engaging fishers in citizen science, which in CoFish resulted in bridging existing gaps between lake management, science and fisheries, providing a broader basis for lake conservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02472-x.Keywords: Phosphorus, Fisheries, Citizen science, Co-design, Lake, Spatial variation

Background

Perialpine lakes are ecologically important habitats for a range of aquatic communities [1]. They provide crucially important services for humans ranging from the provisioning of drinking water to fisheries and recreational activities. Optimal lake management that ensures the continuation of these services in times of environmental change, requires stakeholder engagement and balancing of priorities.

Lake eutrophication in the 20th century caused changes in the functioning and biodiversity of perialpine lakes, with the development of anoxia in deeper parts. Problems due to low oxygen have returned in recent years, this time linked to climate change, which prevents full lake overturn in winter, creating a deep isolated layer, cut-off from the atmosphere [2] and the surface layers where oxygen is produced by photosynthesis. Warming has been shown to create more favourable conditions for blooms of toxic cyanobacteria: generally, “blooms like it hot” see [3] for Lake Geneva, however, also see [4, 5] for a contrasting opinion. In peri-alpine lakes, the dominant cyanobacterium Planktothrix rubescens seems to benefit from warm autumns and mild winters rather than hot summers [4]. Moreover, perialpine lakes are impacted by invasive species like the quagga mussel, Dreissena rostriformis bugensis [6], which threatens to change the trophic structure and ecological functioning [7], by a process known as benthification, a shift from dominance of pelagic processes and an open water food web to benthic zone dominated processes. Quagga mussels are strong competitors for zooplankton in the lake. Their impact can be so large that this single benthic species now controls the P-cycle of even the largest lakes [8].

Setting optimal nutrient levels requires balancing the needs for safe drinking water and recreation, i.e. prevention of toxic algal blooms, with those for sustainable fisheries. To a certain extent, controlling eutrophication benefits both. For example, Bninska [9] showed how in Poland, lakes with good water quality had greater fisheries yields compared to those with poor water quality. The reduction of phosphorus levels has been a key strategy to restore water quality of eutrophic lakes, controlling blooms and enhancing biodiversity [10, 11], while also helping to mitigate negative effects of nutrient enrichment on coregonids and fisheries. However, for a set of peri-alpine lakes a reduction in total fish catches – dominated by coregonids and perch - have been reported below 20 µg/L [12], with minimal catches below 10 µg/L. A study by Alexander and Seehausen [12] gives a different perspective, with maximal coregonids biomass at TP levels below 10 µg/L. However, coregonids of small size seem to dominate in lakes of low TP, whereas for fisheries larger individuals are crucial [13]. At lower levels of TP, balancing the different interests of lake users becomes more of a challenge. For example, in Lake Geneva, the focus of this study, the present long-term TP goal of 10–15 µg/L, lowered from 20 – 30 µg/L in 2011, is based upon the consideration of various lake services, including drinking water, recreation and fisheries. Yet, user disagreements, rooted in different opinions, shifting baselines about the natural state of the environment [14] and what constitutes optimal TP for fisheries [15] - and other lake services remain. Sinclair et al. [16] concur that an optimum – intermediate - nutrient level which can support both good water quality and high fishery yields may not exist, in particular if fisheries target species that differ in their sensitivity to lake productivity, like for perch and coregonids, the main species for Lake Geneva.

An issue particularly relevant to large lakes, is whether all parts of a lake have the same nutrient level, yield the same productivity, pose similar risks for cyanobacterial blooms or have comparable fish catches [17]. However, data on the spatial variation of dissolved phosphorus or TP in the water across a lake are surprisingly scarce. This is possibly due to the substantial logistical and economic constraints of sampling a large lake over its full surface on a single day. Sometimes spatial variation in TP is visualized by looking at Chla patterns in a lake [18] or derived from satellite images using algorithms that link in situ TP to reflectance data from “visible” lake parameters [19] or by using water-quality 3D models [20]. It is worth noting that none of these constitute direct nutrient measurements, like done at the routine sampling points.

Given the known considerable spatial variability in large lakes, several publications provide advice on where best to locate a routine sampling point through use of satellite images or modeling [21–23]. For Lake Geneva, the focus of this article, Soulignac et al. [20] addressed this question through 3D modeling. The study confirmed that, despite strong spatial gradients in ecological quality (expressed as nutrients and Chla), the most representative point for the pelagic zone of Lake Geneva as a whole is situated in the central part of the Western most lake basin, Grand Lac, not far from the reference sampling point SHL2 (see Fig. 1). Yet spatially explicit data on nutrient concentrations like TP that could have been used for direct validation of the model output, were not available. Data obtained using a citizen science approach could be one solution for wide-scale lake sampling capturing some of the spatial variation in in situ data. Such data, combined with satellite images and model output could yield a clearer image of nutrient variability in a large lake.

Citizen science, broadly defined as the public’s participation in scientific research and knowledge production [25], has been advocated as a way to gather larger amounts of environmental and ecological data whilst involving societal actors in the research process [26]. Despite the promise of the approach to promote community participation in conservation efforts, several ethical issues have been voiced (reviewed in [27]) relating to the potential of masking exploitation and unremunerated labour under the guise of “democratisation” of science [28]. This has led to a call for a more meaningful engagement with citizens in all parts of the research cycle [27]. Indeed, the co-production of knowledge, where citizens are involved in key parts of the research cycle, such as the formulation of the research questions and communication of results, has been recognized as a tool to help address some of the key sustainability issues of our time [29]. Place-based community projects, centred around a locally rooted issue, and often co-designed to align with the community’s needs, have been highlighted as playing an important role to maximise the impact of the research, resulting in better environmental management and community action [30]. Despite this potential, in a recent review, Gerlak et al. [31] highlight the need for better reporting of societal outcomes of co-produced research.

In the case of water-based research, stakeholder engagement and co-design approaches have a long tradition [32] and have resulted in the long-term monitoring of freshwater systems [30] and collaborative water management of river catchments for example [33]. Participatory management, with anglers in particular, is reported to have led to long-lasting learning outcomes [34] as well as better environmental monitoring, conservation and advocacy [35]. Collaboration with commercial fishers has further improved fisheries population models (e.g. crab fisheries of South Devon, UK), resulting in improved relations and trust between fishers and scientists [36]. In Switzerland and France, the transdisciplinary Fischnetz network (1998–2004) was a very productive research partnership between administration, the chemical industry, fisheries associations and academic researchers, that resulted in 77 research projects and had a policy level impact that improved the state of Swiss watercourses [37]. Co-design of research and participatory fisheries management therefore can have both a scientific and meaningful societal impact [38].

In this article, we propose that citizen science and in particular co-designed citizen science, can provide a useful tool for monitoring the water quality of large lakes, as well as for the empowerment of fishers as citizen scientists. We illustrate this with CoFish, a co-designed research project between fishers and scientists on Lake Geneva. Though citizen science and participatory research are often cited to lead to a greater societal impact, the outcomes - other than the scientific output of the citizen science research itself - are not often measured, reviewed in [39]. This is problematic, especially given the institutionalisation of citizen science in some countries, e.g. UK, Austria, Germany that rely on evaluation and impact assessment for the continuity of particular funding schemes [40] as well as the importance of developing a more reflective approach to inform future programs. Several impact assessment frameworks, e.g [39–41] break down the impact of a project into five dimensions: science and technology, societal, environmental, political and economic. However, societal impacts are rarely reported alongside the scientific results.

The CoFish project, that ran from 2021 – 2024, funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation, was initiated to bridge academic with the local knowledge of commercial and recreational fishers and to study the learning and societal outcomes. In doing so, the CoFish project also further brokered relations between local scientists, policy-makers, lake management and fishers. The project’s two main goals were therefore (i) to co-design an original research project with the fishers of Lake Geneva, and (ii) to evaluate the learning outcomes, both scientific and societal, for fishers and scientists alike.

Methods

Lake Geneva: situating the co-designed CoFish research project

Lake Geneva is the focal study site for our research. We chose it as it faces several issues (outlined in the Introduction) and there are motivated fishing and scientific communities willing to collaborate. The lake borders Switzerland and France and is the largest and one of the deepest lakes in Western Europe. It was formed at the end of the last Ice Age as the Rhone glacier started to melt. It is characterised by two distinct basins: the western, Petit Lac, which is shallower (average/max depth of 41 m/76 m) and smaller (78 km) and the eastern, Grand Lac, which is deeper (mean/max depth of 172 m/310 m) and more than 6 times in size (502 km2). The lake provides several services: it is a source of drinking water for 900,000 people, it provides recreational opportunities such as swimming, sailing, angling, hiking and biking, attracting several millions of tourists a year to the region. There is evidence of fishing activity in the lake since the Neolithic [42]. In 2022, there were 117 commercial fishers and 5841 permits for recreational fishers. Yearly catches of the commercial fisheries amount from close to 1000 tons/year of whitefish as recent as 2013/2014 to less than 200 tons/year in the most recent years. For the other main target species, perch, catches decreased from nearly 1250 tons/year in the 1970s to ca. 1000 tons/year in the 1990s to some 250–500 tons/year this century, though this figure could partially reflect a shift in changing fishing efforts, and may not accurately reflect the size of the fish populations. During the1950s − 1970s there was a rise in nutrient levels (phosphorus and nitrogen) causing eutrophication and phytoplankton blooms. This impacted whitefish and arctic char catches, yet favoured perch. In 1963, the International Commission for the Protection of the Waters of Lake Geneva, an independent Franco-Swiss intergovernmental body, was formed in order to coordinate the water policies in the Lake Geneva watershed and monitor the water quality. The CIPEL commissions meet several times per year and decisions are based on scientific evidence provided by the Scientific Committee to the Operational Committee, with final approval of management decisions once per year in a Plenary Session. Hence the decision making process is based upon consensus between the two countries, thereby accounting for the respective legislative frameworks in France (EU) and Switzerland. CIPEL coordinates bi-weekly to monthly sampling at the single sampling point, SHL2 (46.45270°N,6.58872°E, depth 309 m, Fig. 1). This point was chosen because it overlies the deepest part of the lake, and therefore samples could be collected to cover the entire depth of the water column. In addition, being central in the Central Lake Basin, Grand Lac, it was sufficiently far from the lake shore to not be overly affected by pollution coming from the land [24]. Additionally, Canton Geneva monitors the lake at GE3 (46.29721°N,6.21994°E, depth 72 m,) in the Petit Lac. Over several decades, the water quality has improved in terms of phosphorus concentration, and is now approaching the upper end of the long-term objective of an annual mean of 10–15 µg/L. The main organisation of recreational fishers, FIPAL (see Table 1) became a partner in the project during the application stage. Following this, presidents of the subsections of the main fishing clubs or unions were contacted for consultation and feedback. A kick-off workshop took place on October 15th, 2021 where actors from both science and practice (recreational, commercial fishers, lake managers, NGOs) were invited to brainstorm about the future of the lake`s fish populations and the sustainability of the fisheries. We followed a 7-step Participatory Action Research process [43] to co-design the research through a series of workshops and interviews (see Supplementary Material 1), inspired by methods in the td-net toolbox [44]. Workshops were conducted at different stages of the project cycle and covered: joint problem framing, formulation of the research question, co-design of the methodology, co-evaluation of the project and co-dissemination. By the third workshop, fishers shared a growing concern that the lake may become too oligotrophic and therefore less able to support fish populations and lake fisheries. Fishers wondered whether the two reference points, SHL2 and GE3 (Fig. 1) monitored by CIPEL and Canton Geneva respectively were indeed still representative, decades after they were first selected (a period during which the lake underwent important changes in its environmental drivers, functioning and usage). They also questioned whether the 10-15 μg/L long term TP objective may already have been attained, at least in parts of the lake, for which there is no information given that monitoring is restricted to two locations only. The co-design process resulted in the following two coupled research questions:

To what extent do we observe spatial variation in total phosphorus (TP) and the biologically available form of phosphorus, orthophosphate (P-PO4) in the critical concentration range of 10-20 µg/L - for both phytoplankton biomass and fish stocks - in the productive layer of Lake Geneva?

Sampling

SHL2, as the single reference point for the long term CIPEL monitoring program, was selected as being the best location based on a lake-wide sampling program executed in the 1950s. The 1950s sampling points therefore provided a basis to build the present citizen science monitoring program (Fig. 1). We divided the lake into 9–10 zones and each fisher team, consisting of 1–4 people, were given 2–5 points to cover, close to the location of their usual fishing grounds. The fishers used their own fishing boats and guidance from digital navigation systems to navigate to the exact coordinates (Fig. 1, Supplementary Material 2). Where required, members of the CoFish team and sometimes lake police wardens joined to help individual fishers.

Operation instructions for a self-made water sampling device made of a stoppered 18 m long garden hose and a rope double the length. The weight was 5 kg and waterproof tape was used to join the parts

For sample collection, we constructed and used an 18 m long integrated water sampler made out of a garden hose, fitted with a cork stopper at one end and a 5 kg weight at the other (see Fig. 2), following a design used in the European Multi Lake Survey [45]. We chose 18 m as we aimed to get an integrated sample of the productive part of the Lake Geneva water column- defined by us as the sum of epi- and metalimnion - and this was estimated to be 18 m after consulting the platform http://www.meteolakes.ch (since changed to https://www.alplakes.eawag.ch/) prior to campaign 1. While not the central objective of the study, we also measured lake transparency and trained fishers to use self-made Secchi disks. Average Secchi depths were calculated as the mean of the depth at which the Secchi disk was no longer visible and the depth that it starts to be visible again, while bringing it back up to the surface.

The material was first tested by members of the core team and training videos and protocols were made available in order to train the fishers in correct usage. Hands-on training took place when the sampling kits were distributed among the participants. A workshop before the sampling allowed for final troubleshooting and adjustments.

Prior to sampling, polypropylene collection bottles were washed with 1 M hydrochloric acid overnight, rinsed with demineralised water and left to air-dry [45] before being sealed and distributed to fishers. On the sampling day, the integrated sampler and all materials (e.g. acid-washed bottles, buckets) were provided by the University of Geneva and were rinsed with lake water once at each site prior to each sample collection. Fishers then sampled the water, poured it into the rinsed bucket, and used to fill two pre-labelled bottles, which were stored on ice in the dark. Lake transparency was measured as Secchi depth. Samples were collected from the respective fishers by members of the CoFish team and returned to the laboratory at the University of Geneva laboratory within 24 hours for processing. Bottles for Eawag (see below) were frozen directly.

Lab analyses

Methods were chosen to correspond to those used by the CIPEL for their long-term monitoring of the lake’s single reference point according to a reaction of acidic molybdate in the presence of antimony (EPA 365.3). All 36 sampling points were sampled in all four campaigns except CRG10 that was not sampled at the first campaign due to windy conditions. Orthophosphate measurements were conducted within 48 hours of sample collection.

In the Geneva lab, samples were analysed for TP and P-PO4 using a Seal Analytical AQ2, following protocols EPA 119-A-Rev. 7 and EPA 155-A, which is also based on a reaction of acidic molybdate in the presence of antimony. The limits of quantification were 3 μg/L for both with a range of application 10–1000 μg/L for TP and 3–300 μg/L for P-PO4. A second bottle was sent to Eawag, in a frozen state, where the measurements for TP were repeated, initially to serve as an inter-laboratory comparison. The method also involved a spectrophotometric determination of TP and P-PO4 after the reaction to phosphorus molybdenum blue complex, using the Spectrophotometer Agilent Cary 60. The Eawag method was more sensitive for TP (range of application for TP 3–100 μg/L and LOQ = 3 μg/L). Therefore, the P-PO4 measurements reported are based on the Geneva lab, averaged across three replicates and the TP measures are based on the Eawag lab.

In order to check whether our methods and labs produced similar results to those of the CIPEL, 10 samples from points in the French part of the lake were sent to the Observatoire des Lacs Alpins (OLA- INRAE-CARRTEL), the laboratory that routinely runs the monitoring for CIPEL (Table 1, see Supplementary Material 3).

Analysis of spatial variability

We visualised the distribution of measurements across the four sampling campaigns and compared where the standard points lie in relation to the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the distribution for that part of the lake basin. We therefore defined representativity as measurements that fall within ±1SD of the distribution.

Assessment of societal impacts

For a parallel study on the learning outcomes of the fishers (Jenkins et al. in prep), semi-structured interviews were conducted with ten participants, five of which participated in both the co-design and data collection, and five that participated in data collection only. Of these, five interviewees were commercial fishers and five were recreational. The interviews were adapted from [47–49], see Supplementary Material 4, to measure dimensions of engagement and learning. Participants were subject to ethical procedures of the University of Geneva (see Ethics declaration below), and informed consent to participate was obtained, with the right to withdraw their data at any time. The identities of interviewees were anonymised. Interviews were transcribed with the aid of the online software Happyscribe (Happy Scribe Ltd.). We used the Action Framework [41] to categorise the individual parts of the transcripts into the five societal sub-dimensions that they identify: (i) community building and empowerment; (ii) social inclusion; (iii) researcher growth; (iv) knowledge skills and competences; (v) changes in ways of thinking, attitude and values, (vi) behavioural change.

Results

Profile and engagement of participants

We brought together 13 commercial (11 Swiss/2 French),19 recreational (15 Swiss/4 French) fishers, and 22 scientists from research institutions around the lake to participate in one or more of the co-design workshops (Table 3). Results of our preliminary interviews indicated that at the start of the project half of the fishers had contributed to scientific research in the past. Half of the returning fishers expressed that they felt disappointed as there had been a lack of feedback on the scientific results that were obtained. Commercial fishers sometimes distrusted science as they feared that scientific results may result in further fishing restrictions. In addition, they felt disempowered and lacking a voice in political discussions that have an impact on their fishing practices. Furthermore, they consider themselves to have detailed local knowledge and a potential to act as sentinels of the ecosystem.

Both commercial and recreational fishers contributed to the co-production workshops to define the research question, methodology and in the data collection. There were a few commercial and recreational fishers that were highly engaged in the co-production workshops (23% and 31% of their total groups sizes, respectively). Scientists were involved the co-design workshops and other stakeholders (e.g. lake managers) were involved in consulting roles at different time points.

During the four sampling campaigns 9–10 teams went out to sample and the effort was split amongst 19 fishers. To complement the team effort, 1 fishing guard, 1 member of the lake police, 1 scuba diver and five scientists assisted, as needed. All six commercial fishers that were involved in sample collection were highly engaged as they participated in all 4 sampling campaigns. More recreational fishers participated in the project than commercial fishers, however only about two thirds were highly engaged and participated in all sampling campaigns. The commercial fishers were highly engaged in data collection, though they were less active in the co-production workshops. Similarly, amongst recreational fishers, engagement in data collection was twice as high as in workshop participation (see Table 3). Of the initially engaged fishers, 3 professionals and 2 recreational took active roles in project dissemination, communicating findings at their local meetings, on social media (e.g. videos of lab analyses with up to 2000 views), and in one case at a conference about water. Furthermore, eight fishers, four commercial and four recreational are co-authors on this article. It is worth noting that 3/5 interviewees participants who initially joined for data collection, later joined subsequent co-production meetings, indicating that their engagement shifted during the process.

TP and orthophosphate variation across the sampling points

For TP there was high agreement between the Eawag and OLA measurements in 8/10 cases (see SI3). At the first sampling campaign, TP at CRG5 was 513 μg/L, which was > 20 times higher than the other values, which ranged from below the detection limit ( < 3 μg/L) to almost 24 μg/L. The reason for this high value is not known, possibly due to its proximity to a wastewater treatment plants in Coppet and Abérieaux in Switzerland, or due to contamination in storage of the water sample. Therefore, this point was excluded as an outlier from further analysis. There was substantial variation in lake phosphorus concentration across the lake and between the different sampling periods (Figs. 3 and 4). The range of values for orthophosphate was less variable than TP with most values being between 3 - 8 μg/L with two values around 13 μg/L (Figs. 5 and 6).

Discussion

We co-designed a research project with commercial and recreational fishers from Lake Geneva. A main concern expressed during the co-production workshops was that the lake is becoming too oligotrophic to sustain large fish populations and therefore fisheries. Fishers challenged the use of just two monitoring sampling points, SHL2 and GE3, to inform lake management decisions and they questioned whether the two reference points were still representative of the two lake basins at the time of study. In order to answer their question, we had to avoid confounding temporal variation and therefore collect the data in a single day. This would have been impossible by a single team alone. A citizen science approach was used to allow for consistent timing in sample collection across many sites in a large lake. As a result of our collaboration, we were able to – for the first time since the 1950s – create empirical maps of TP and P-PO4 distribution in Lake Geneva. This gave us three key scientific results:

i)There is spatio-temporal variability in TP (3.4–34.9 μg/L observed across campaigns) and P-PO4 (3–14 μg/L) and 66% of the measurements for Petit Lac and 80% for Grand Lac fell within 1 SD of the distribution;

99% of the time (except for two measurements in Campaign 1 and 4) concentrations of P-PO4 were below 10 µg/L indicating that phosphorus availability may be limiting for many species [51]. Furthermore, for some locations, the concentrations reached levels as low as 3 µg/L which is reported to be the threshold below which phosphorus can cause severe reduction in algal growth [52]. We discuss these findings of the sampling campaign alongside the equally important societal outcomes of the project of greater empowerment of the fishers.

Our empirical results show variability in TP, and to a lesser extent orthophosphate across Lake Geneva. The current work complements other empirical studies that have found spatial variability in nutrient levels in lakes (e.g. [23, 53]), including variability between near-shore and offshore locations, presumably due to the presence of tributaries, or run-off from point sites [54] and/or waste water treatment plants (e.g. SHL6 in our study). A study based on remote sensing data of Lake Chaohu in China by Gao et al. [19] showed spatial variability in TP, indirectly derived from reflectance data. They further show how the representativeness of a reference station improved when TP data are used solely for the specific lake basin where it is located, rather than for the lake as a whole. This would seem to be true for Lake Geneva too, GE3 being the reference station for Petit Lac and SHL2 for Grand Lac, rather than either one for Lake Geneva as a whole. CIPEL data further support that the two lake basins behave differently and have a distinct history of eutrophication and re-oligotrophication; therefore, SHL2 and GE3 both remain key locations to evaluate temporal changes in the lake basins.

We ask the question to which extent SHL2 and GE3 are representative for their respective lake basins as a whole. For our purposes, we defined representativity in statistical terms as being ±1SD of the distribution. The results of campaign 1 indicate that GE3 and SHL2 were not representative for Petit and Grand Lac, as TP levels were much higher than the basin average. We hypothesise that the reason behind the high concentration at SHL2 during this first campaign may in part be due to position of the thermocline of the lake on this day. Typically, in deep stratified lakes, phosphorus concentrations increase in the hypolimnion, and Lake Geneva data from recent years show several-fold higher concentrations of both P-PO4 and TP in the hypo- compared to epilimnion [55]. We confirmed the position of the thermocline in Grand Lac by looking at results of a 3D model of Lake Geneva, see [20] for this day which revealed that the position of the thermocline in Grand Lac was dome-shaped. In the centre of the lake where SHL2 is positioned, the position of the thermocline was in fact shallower than closer to the shore. We suggest that this may have resulted in us sampling closer to the hypolimnion at the reference point with our 18 m long hose than at other locations; this may explain the higher TP level recorded. In other large thermally stratified lakes, the thermocline also tends to develop a dome shape, showing shallower thermocline depths in the centre than in nearshore waters, e.g. described for Lake Erie by Beletsky et al. [55]. In the other three campaigns, the TP and P-PO4 values for SHL2 and GE3 were much closer to the basin-wide averages, in these cases suggesting that these reference sampling points can correctly inform us about the lake as a whole.

Given that no spatial explicit in situ monitoring data are available for Lake Geneva, we look towards lake-wide modelling studies to help us better interpret our spatial data. Obviously, in situ data and modelling approaches have their own constraints, but there might be lessons in comparing the two different approaches, without aiming to integrate CoFish data, presented here, with a model. Soulignac et al. [20] studied the representativeness of SHL2 and GE3 for the ecological status of Lake Geneva (expressed as, Chl-a and Secchi disk). They took the median of the ecological status of all grid cells of the Delft 3D model they ran. An individual grid cell – like SHL2 – was then deemed representative if the ecological status was identical to the theoretical value for the lake as a whole. Clearly, when using our measured data, we have a much coarser spatial distribution than the fine grid cells of a computer model. When looking at this uneven distribution of sampling points across the lake (Fig. 1), a sampling point like SHL2 in the middle of Grand Lac represents a much larger volume of Lake Geneva than a littoral point like VD4, since closer to the shore the sampling points are closer together, while widely spaced apart in the middle of the lake. Furthermore, as one would expect, and with evidence from our littoral zone data, given their proximity to the inflow of P from the land, TP or PO4 levels would be higher for near-shore sampling points than for open water. To obtain mean for TP that truly represents each lake basin, it would be necessary to weigh the volumes of water represented by each sampling point, comparable to what is done to get a weighted average with lake depth (see formula in Introduction). Data from this study could however be very useful to validate and calibrate future modelling efforts; e.g models that use of Soulignac et al. [20] as a basis.

Given that the key research question in CoFish is clearly inspired by the fishers’ apprehension that fish production is limited by low P-availability, we discuss several aspects of this. Almost all of the TP measurements in our data are below 20 µg/L, which according to Müller et al. [50] should limit phytoplankton biomass in Lake Geneva. However, the effects of declining phosphorus levels impact the food web even before phytoplankton biomass goes down, in part since phytoplankton compensates by reducing cellular P-levels [56]. In Lake Geneva, this has led to a sharp rise in the ratio of carbon to phosphorus, C:P [53]. Food of high C:P is considered to be of poor quality for zooplankton, which could be one reason why zooplankton is in decline. It is worth noting that our CoFish samples come from the euphotic zone and not the entire water column and so may not be directly comparable to the CIPEL annual TP averaged over the full water column, presently calculated to be at 15.6 µg/L, very close to the upper limit of the long-term target of 10–15 µg/L. Since the lake has not fully mixed in 13 years, The CoFish data further show that limiting concentrations for phytoplankton growth are already reached early in spring in the productive – upper – part of the water column of the lake. Over the past years, Lake Geneva has provided good quality drinking water and abundant fish biomass. Such an “optimal” situation was largely due to a decrease in TP concentrations after a period of eutrophication in the 1960-70s. When P-levels are brought down to restore lakes from eutrophication, the fish community composition changes, so that salmonids and piscivorous species like pike increased, see for example [13]. However, at the time of writing of Gerdeaux et al. [13], it was expected that the overall fish production would decrease when phosphorus concentrations start to drop below 20 µg/L.

Historically, the motivation to control P in deep lakes suffering from eutrophication were to avoid anoxia in deeper layers, reduce the risk of blooms of nuisance/harmful algae, cyanobacteria in particular, and to support lake biodiversity. Hypoxia is once again a problem in Lake Geneva, like in the eutrophic years, and the legal limit of 4 mg O2 per L is no longer met in the deepest parts of the lake. However, reducing TP further will not solve hypoxia, since phosphorus deposition is not its leading cause [57]. In deep lakes, oxygen concentrations are strongly driven by water mixing processes, and in particular a lack of full winter overturn and increased duration of the period of thermal stratification will bring down oxygen in the deep part [58, 59]. Blooms of cyanobacteria clearly increase with nutrient levels, and risks of nuisance or harmful levels of cyanobacteria at the long-term TP level of 10–15 µg/L would indeed be much reduced [60]. However, blooms, defined here as a period in which levels of phytoplankton are elevated above the long-term average causing discoloration of the lake, are part of the normal functioning of lakes, and will continue to occur, even in oligotrophic systems [5]. It may therefore not be realistic, nor necessary, to avoid blooms altogether.

Optimal P levels to conserve biodiversity is a tricky, multi-faceted issue, that is also subject to shifting baseline syndrome based on, for example, species’ population sizes of that shift within our lifetimes [14]. Based upon long term data from 11 peri-alpine lakes, the optimum TP-level for whitefish seems to be between 20 – 30 µg/L [13]. When re-oligotrophication continues and TP drops to values in the range of 10–20 µg/L, coregonid catches may decrease significantly, albeit still higher than during the most eutrophic years. On the other hand, perch catches peaked in the eutrophic years, likely due to reduced competition for zooplankton. At levels below 20–30 µg/L TP, however, catches of perch became much reduced. Overall, Gerdeaux et al. [13] state that, where total fish biomass decreases below 10 µg/L TP, biomass increases at TP levels between 10 – 15 µg/L, while further increases above 20 µg/L do not further enhance fish biomass.

Conversely, what is the risk of blooms of nuisance or harmful algae at TP levels between 15 – 20 µg/L? If we focus on the main toxic species in peri-alpine lakes, the metalimnetic red cyanobacterium Planktothrix rubescens, it is hard to find critical TP levels below which this cyanobacterium disappears. Clearly factors other than TP play a role, in particular climate change impacts. For example, Suarez et al. [61] studied Lake Hallwil and showed that Planktothrix was absent during the eutrophic period. With re-oligotrophication Planktothrix returned and peaked around TP levels of 50 µg/L. Further reduction of TP did bring Planktothrix down, but the species is still present, albeit now at a position well below the metalimnion. In Lake Constance, Planktothrix has been recorded even at low TP levels had below 10 µg/L, though typically at low abundance [62]. In Lake Bourget (France), however, controlling phosphorus levels (TP reduction from 33 to 14 µg/L) was a key factor in the disappearance of Planktothrix blooms from the lake [63]. Knapp et al. [64] discuss how, under the impact of climate warming, seasonal patterns in the physical structure of the water column of Lake Zurich (Switzerland) are changing and how this can have both positive (shallower lake winter overturn) and negative (instability in the thermocline during spring) effects on Planktothrix, which remains a dominant species. At least at present, for Lake Zurich, TP is not the dominant factor that controls changes in P. rubescens abundance; changing lake physics plays a much more central role. Whereas the risks of blooms under a warming climate remain real, augmented by extreme events in precipitation or temperature, as witnessed by the Uroglena bloom in Lake Geneva in 2021 [65], further re-oligotrophication may very well result in ongoing declines of biological communities, like zooplankton and fish, and lead to a potentially impoverished lake ecosystem. As stated earlier, there is increasingly strong variation in the concentration of TP over depth. A lack of winter overturn is locking-away the elevated P-levels in the deeper parts of the lake, which no longer become available for primary production through deep winter mixing. Salmaso et al. [58] speak of oligotrophication of the euphotic layer. Therefore, it becomes all the more important to consider hydrodynamic processes, which are changing under the impact of climate-warming, for the identification of nutrient levels that balance diverse lake usages.

The nature and impact of the participatory engagement process approach

The work presented would only have been possible due to the commitment and data collection efforts of the fishers. Overall, 32 fishers participated in the co-production workshops, whereas 20 contributed to data collection. However, all those involved in data collection were more engaged, i.e, repeatedly participated. This is consistent with results from the systematic review of co-production processes by Gerlak et al. [35] that indicated that in the co-production literature 95% of participants were still mostly involved in data collection. We suggest that this may be because data collection is concrete and in line with their fishing activity. The lower engagement of scientists by the end of the project reflected the adoption of a specific research line that was less relevant to some. Others stayed on as part of the process despite this; half of the scientists that attended the kick-off meeting also attended the closing meeting, showing a degree of retainment.

The main societal impacts of the participatory approach were the formation of an engaged community of lake stewards. Fishers had felt disempowered and sometimes disappointed by previous experiences in scientific projects. They expressed a great sense of allegiance to the group and in interviews expressed empowerment and the feeling they could achieve action towards an “ecological transition”. They felt more confident in participating and even more empowered to use the results in policy relevant processes, therefore also leaning towards a more policy level impact, often included as part of the societal impact in other frameworks, e.g. [39]. One participant, in particular, who had not previously contributed to scientific research has since contributed to several other projects, and some have since become partners on future projects. There were multiple learning outcomes that are further explored elsewhere (Jenkins et al. in prep). Through the CoFish approach, the project results were more broadly communicated and made accessible, than would have been the case through a routine academic report or publication. For example, all participants communicated some project results within their communities informally (e.g. social circles), at general assemblies, on social media, and standard news media (three news stories), indicating the multiplier effect of the project. The active dissemination of project results by fishers, themselves, indicates ownership and pride in the quality of the data meaning that the results of the study were communicated effectively outside the strictly academic circles. The closing meeting also was attended by authorities responsible for the water sampling, CIPEL and Swiss cantons, offering the opportunity to further bridge the gap between science and policy.

As with any approach, there were several shortcomings to our interviews. The questions were designed to focus on engagement and learning outcomes rather than all dimensions of societal impact. It is therefore possible that there are other impacts that we did not measure and that remain unreported. Several collaborations are planned with fishers in the future: one project is already under way. However, the lack of funding to sustain citizen science efforts is a perennial issue.

Several lessons were learned regarding the co-design process, and are in line with recommendations by other scholars (e.g. [35, 38, 65]). Briefly, these involve the time it takes to successfully run a co-design process, the need for good and neutral communication, facilitation, the importance of fostering trust and redressing power imbalances. Furthermore, there are benefits of collaborating with ambassadors anchored in the community but as a project mediator it is useful to be a neutral outsider. Important discussions for project planners are whether participants are financially rewarded for their work. These fit into larger debates guiding citizen science research and the benefits and pitfalls of financial rewards [65].

Conclusions

Our data broadly support Soulignac et al.’s model-based conclusion [20] that the use of the reference sampling points is an acceptable compromise, in particular for the large open water spaces of the lake. However, monitoring of the littoral zones, whose renaturation is one the key action points for CIPEL may require additional efforts that could be complemented by a citizen science approach.

Generally, among the authors of this paper, who represent academic scientists and fishers, there is an agreement that with the present TP levels, further reduction may risk impoverishment of zooplankton and a number of commercially important fish species. It is further encouraging that the risk of blooms in Lake Geneva does not pose a large public health risk [66]. In addition, with the ongoing invasion of quagga mussels in the lake, the P-cycling and availability of P for the pelagic food web is under increasing pressure, see [67], requiring in itself re-evaluation of the role of phosphorus in the pelagic food web. Setting optimal TP levels therefore remains challenging.

Finally, we report empowerment of the community of fishers by the co-design process. Perhaps most importantly however is the longer-term effect of a community of scientifically engaged fishers, contributing to the conservation of the lake. We further advocate for citizen science projects to measure and report societal outcomes of projects alongside the scientific results.
