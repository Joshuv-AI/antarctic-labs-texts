A novel strategy for reconstruction of AORI type III proximal tibial defects
Authors: Xingzhen Li, Qing Han, Xue Zhao, Jinbo Zhang, Qian Wan, Zhennan Gan, Xiaonan Wang, Xin Zhao, Hao Chen, Aobo Zhang, Jincheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsFor cases of Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) type III proximal tibial defects with intact medial collateral ligaments (MCLs), knee reconstruction is conventionally performed using semi-constrained prostheses with stemmed tibial components in conjunction with cones or sleeves. This study aimed to reconstruct AORI type III tibial defects using a 3D-printed extension-stem augment, followed by surface-type knee reconstruction to achieve long-term stability and promising clinical outcomes.MethodsA patient-specific porous extension-stem augment was designed based on the defect morphology. The microporous structure allowed direct contact for osseointegration, the proximal portion supported tibial baseplate, while the distal stem achieved intramedullary fixation. Finite element analysis (FEA) assessed the biomechanical stability of the augment. A retrospective analysis was conducted between July 2021 and September 2023 on patients who underwent reconstruction of AORI type III proximal tibial defects with extension-stem augment and surface-type total knee prosthesis.ResultsFEA demonstrated favourable stress and strain distribution and micromotion at the prosthesis-bone interface, although localized high-strain regions were observed under higher-demand loading conditions. Four patients (0 male, 4 female) were included, with a minimum follow-up of 20.7 months (20.7 to 30) and a median age at surgery of 63.0 years (IQR 60.3 to 66.3). The mean Knee Society Score (KSS) improved significantly to 85 (82 to 87), and the mean Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score decreased by 18 (16 to 20). One patient developed periprosthetic joint infection on the femoral side at 20.7 months postoperatively and underwent revision surgery. Intraoperatively, the augment was securely integrated and could not be extracted. Radiological evaluation demonstrated satisfactory alignment and joint stability, with no evidence of prosthesis loosening or migration at the latest follow-up.ConclusionThe extension-stem augment achieved anatomical reconstruction of AORI type III proximal tibial defects and provided stable support for the surface-type prosthesis, ensuring reliable knee stability after reconstruction. Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the standard treatment for end-stage knee osteoarthritis, with an estimated 3.6 million new procedures performed worldwide in 2023.1 However, limited implant longevity and postoperative complications have led to a rapidly increasing demand for revision TKA (rTKA). Recent projections indicate that the annual volume of rTKA among Medicare beneficiaries in the USA is expected to increase substantially, reaching approximately 227,515 procedures by 2040,2 and more than 30% of revision cases involve proximal tibial bone defects, which adversely affect the stability of the tibial component.3 The Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) classification is commonly used for defect evaluation and preoperative planning in clinical practice.4 AORI type III proximal tibial defects involve extensive metaphyseal bone loss, posing major challenges for anatomical reconstruction.5 The strategies for bone defect reconstruction and knee restoration should be determined according to the severity of the bone defect and the functional integrity of the surrounding soft tissues.

For AORI type III proximal tibial defects, the current clinical reconstruction strategy typically involves modular metal augments such as metaphyseal cones or sleeves to fill metaphyseal bone defects,4 in an attempt to achieve long-term stability. Luo et al6 previously employed a 3D-printed patient-specific metaphyseal block for anatomical reconstruction of proximal tibial bone defects following tumour resection. However, restoring knee stability and function remains challenging. For cases with irreparable medial collateral ligament (MCL) insufficiency, a constrained prosthesis such as a rotating-hinge knee implant is required for joint restoration.7 Under these circumstances, knee stability and function are maintained by the hinge mechanism, and the prostheses are secured within the tibial canal using a cemented intramedullary stem. For patients with partial insufficiency of the MCLs, a semi-constrained prosthesis such as a condylar constrained knee (CCK) implant is commonly used for joint restoration.8,9 Knee stability is achieved by a high-post insert that limits flexion-extension within the femoral condylar track, while fixation between the prosthesis and the bone is obtained through a stemmed tibial baseplate inserted into the medullary canal.

All of these reconstruction strategies use stemmed tibial baseplates combined in series with modular metal augments and constrained or semi-constrained knee prostheses for direct joint restoration. The augments rely on mechanical fixation through the stem extension to achieve initial stability. Moreover, the high level of prosthetic constraint increases stress both between the implant components and at the prosthesis-bone interface, further amplifying the load on the stem and thereby raising the risk of structural failure.10-13 Kelly et al14 designed modular customized proximal tibial implants for paediatric patients after malignant bone tumour resection, while Li et al15 developed a 3D-printed metaphyseal cone combined with a stemmed tibial baseplate and a CCK implant to reconstruct severe proximal tibial defects. Nevertheless, the modular configuration of these implants results in potential mechanical instability at the junctions, which continues to pose a long-term challenge for structural durability.16 In Luo et al’s6 protocol, the metaphyseal block combined with a stemmed tibial baseplate was designed to anatomically restore the entire metaphysis. Although this configuration achieves anatomical reconstruction of the bone defect, the inherent constraint of the CCK design and the modular junctions still exhibit the same limitations.

To address the stress concentration caused by constrained or semi-constrained prostheses, our team proposed a novel concept of reconstructing the tibial defect first, followed by knee restoration using a surface-type prosthesis. However, the key challenge lies in achieving anatomical reconstruction of the proximal tibial defect. The metaphyseal reconstruction implant should obtain sufficient mechanical stability through adequate contact with the remaining host bone, thereby providing stable support for the tibial baseplate. Based on this, our team developed a 3D-printed extension-stem augment for proximal tibial reconstruction. The augment was designed according to the morphology of the bone defect to achieve anatomical reconstruction of the proximal tibia, while its porous structure allows direct contact with the surrounding host bone to promote osseointegration. The proximal portion of the extension-stem augment supports the tibial baseplate of the surface-type knee prosthesis, while the distal integrated stem is designed for intramedullary fixation. This study employed finite element analysis (FEA) to evaluate the stress-strain distribution and micromotion at the prosthesis–bone interface following reconstruction with the extension-stem augment, to investigate its initial stability. A subsequent clinical study was conducted to assess whether this reconstruction system could meet the biomechanical demands of daily activities, providing a new strategy for the individualized treatment of AORI type III proximal tibial defects.

Methods

FEA

A 30-year-old healthy male volunteer was recruited for tibial CT scanning. The acquired images were exported in DICOM format and imported into Mimics (v. 21.0, Materialise, Belgium) for 3D reconstruction. The model was then exported in STL format to Rhinoceros software (v. 8.0, Robert McNeel & Associates, USA) for geometrical refinement and defect construction. An inclusive AORI type III bone defect was created at the metaphyseal region (Figure 1a). Heterogeneous bone material properties were extracted and assigned in Mimics based on CT-derived density-elastic modulus relationships. The tibia was defined as a linear elastic, isotropic, and non-homogeneous material, with a Poisson’s ratio (ν) of 0.3. The apparent density (ρ) and elastic modulus (E) were determined using the following relationships:17

Reconstruction of the heterogeneous tibial model, implant system, and simulated implantation. a) Material properties and volumetric distribution of the heterogeneous tibial model, with ten material groups (Mat 1 to 10) assigned according to CT-derived density-elastic modulus relationships. b) Implant system consisting of the surface-type total knee prosthesis and the 3D-printed extension-stem augment, connected using bone cement. c) Simulated implantation of the tibial construct.

The implant system consisted of a surface-type knee prosthesis and a patient-specific 3D-printed extension-stem augment (Figure 1b). The augment was designed according to the morphology of the tibial defect and the anatomy of the contralateral tibia. The metaphyseal body of the augment was shaped to match the tibial defect and constructed with a microporous architecture of 75% porosity. Proximally, the augment was cemented to the tibial baseplate of the surface-type prosthesis, while the distal stem was inserted into the tibial canal and secured with bone cement. All components of the tibial implant system were defined as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic materials.17 The porous portion was simplified as a homogeneous solid, and its material properties were determined using the equivalent elastic modulus method (Table I).18

All models were meshed in HyperMesh (v. 2021, Altair Engineering, USA) using C3D4 tetrahedral elements with an element size of 0.8 mm, and subsequently imported into Abaqus 2021 (v. 2021, Dassault Systèmes, France) for boundary condition assignment and implicit dynamic FEA. The contact behaviour at the prosthesis-bone interface between the augment and the tibial defect was defined using a Coulomb friction model with a coefficient of friction of 0.6.19 All interfaces involving cement were modified using tied contact.20 Time-dependent knee joint contact forces and moments were obtained from the OrthoLoad database for five daily activities: walking (WA), ascending stairs (AS), descending stairs (DS), single-leg standing (SL), and squatting (SQ). For each activity, the complete loading cycle was applied as one dynamic activity-based loading condition.21-23 The loading cycle included both knee contact forces and corresponding moments obtained from the OrthoLoad database. Axial (Fz), anterior-posterior (Fy), and medial-lateral (Fx) knee contact forces, together with internal-external (Mz), varus-valgus (My), and flexion-extension (Mx) moments, were uniformly applied to the tibial insert through a coupling constraint, with a medial-to-lateral load distribution ratio of 3:2.24 The distal end of the tibia was fully constrained in six degrees of freedom to prevent any translation or rotation (Figure 2).

The evaluation parameters of the FEA included stress, strain, and micromotion. First, we analyzed the von Mises stress distribution at the prosthesis-bone interface and at specific cross-sections of the tibia (20 mm, 40 mm, 60 mm, and 80 mm) to evaluate the mechanical environment following implantation of the extension-stem augment. After defining the axial plane of the tibia with the centre of the medullary canal as the origin, horizontal and vertical axes were established to divide the prosthesis-bone interface into four quadrants: posteromedial (PM), posterolateral (PL), anteromedial (AM), and anterolateral (AL). The peak and mean stresses within each quadrant were then calculated. In addition, regions with stress values below 0.1 MPa were defined as the potential metaphyseal bone resorption zone,25 and their proportion were calculated to assess the stress-shielding effect of the extension-stem augment. Then, the strain was used to assess the potential failure of trabecular bone at the prosthesis-bone interface. Regions exceeding the threshold range of -7,300 to 6,500 µɛ were identified,26 and the proportion was quantified. Finally, micromotion at the prosthesis-bone interface was analyzed to evaluate the stability of the extension-stem augment. Micromotion was defined as the relative displacement between the closest opposing nodes at the interface, and the proportion of interface areas exceeding 40 µm was calculated to assess the initial stability of the extension-stem augment.27

Retrospective case study

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Hospital of Jilin University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.28 The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients who underwent surface-type TKA between January 2016 and December 2022; 2) radiological and intraoperative confirmation of inclusive AORI type III proximal tibial defects before rTKA; 3) reconstruction of the tibial defect using an extension-stem augment followed by surface-type knee reconstruction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: absence of MCL or severe compromise of the periarticular soft tissues, and lack of follow-up, or follow-up duration less than ten months. Follow-up was ended when a subsequent revision occurred, or the patient died. Ultimately, four patients met the eligibility criteria. Among them, one patient presented with tibial component loosening due to trauma, and three patients exhibited loosening of both femoral and tibial components secondary to periprosthetic joint infection (PJI).

Preoperative assessment included thorough physical examination and imaging studies to evaluate the integrity of the MCL and periarticular soft tissues. All rTKAs were performed between July 2021 and September 2023 by the same senior arthroplasty surgeon (JW). Revision surgery was performed through a medial parapatellar approach. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia were dissected sequentially, followed by removal of scar tissue, residual cement, and devitalized soft-tissue. The medullary canal was then thoroughly irrigated with povidone-iodine solution and normal saline. Trial components of the extension-stem augment were inserted to assess the fit between the implant and the metaphyseal defect. After placement of the trial surface-type tibial component, the joint line, patellar tracking, range of motion (ROM), flexion-extension gaps, and varus-valgus stability were evaluated. Cement was used to secure the distal stem of the augment within the tibial canal. Press-fit fixation was applied proximally to ensure adequate contact between the augment and the host bone, excess cement was removed before attaching the tibial baseplate of the surface-type prosthesis. The femoral-side implantation followed the same procedure. With the knee maintained in full extension until the cement had completely polymerized, the joint was irrigated, haemostasis was achieved, a drain was placed, and the wound was closed in layers. Postoperative management included standard protocols for analgesia, oedema control, haemostasis, and thromboprophylaxis. The drainage tube was removed once the output was less than 50 ml over 24 hours. On the first postoperative day, bedside exercises for knee function were initiated. After drain removal, patients began progressive rehabilitation under the supervision of a physiotherapist, including range of motion (ROM) training, resistance exercises, and weightbearing balance training.

Operating time, total blood loss (TBL), ROM, and postoperative complications were recorded. Knee stability was assessed using varus-valgus stress testing and stress radiographs, while prosthesis positioning and integrity were evaluated on anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs as well as CT scans. Overall functional outcomes were assessed clinically and further evaluated using the Knee Society Score (KSS) and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC).29,30

Results

FEA

Across all five loading conditions (WA, AS, DS, SL, and SQ), the highest stresses were consistently observed in the posterior regions of the prosthesis-bone interface (PM and PL). The peak stresses in the PM region were 4.42, 6.76, 5.12, 6.99, and 6.87 MPa, whereas the corresponding values in the PL region were 4.42, 7.51, 7.96, 11.02, and 8.38 MPa (Figure 3). Mean stresses peaked in the PM and AM regions. The mean stresses in the PM region were 0.24, 0.33, 0.48, 0.73, and 0.37 MPa, while those in the AM region were 0.24, 0.33, 0.35, 0.50, and 0.33 MPa (Supplementary Figure S1). The stress distribution contours at the prosthesis-bone interface are shown in Figure 4. The proportion of the proximal tibial area with stress values below 0.1 MPa was 30.9%, 27.4%, 36.6%, 29.9%, and 23.2% across the five loading conditions. Figure 5 illustrates the stress contours at four selected cross-sections located 20, 40, 60, and 80 mm distal to the tibial baseplate. Under all loading conditions, stresses approached their peak values at the 60 mm and 80 mm levels (distal tibia), measuring 2.89, 3.56, 4.89, 8.99, and 10.71 MPa, respectively, well below the tensile and compressive yield strengths of cortical bone (110 MPa and 150 MPa).31 The strain distribution at the prosthesis-bone interface is shown in Figure 6. The maximum strain values under the five loading conditions were 37,523.3 µε, 58,449.4 µε, 66,688.4 µε, 214,724 µε, and 78,363.1 µε, respectively. Regions exceeding the strain threshold (−7,300 µε to 6,500 µε), defined as potential trabecular bone failure zones, accounted for 5.49%, 7.41%, 11.79%, 22.51%, and 18.11% of the interface area. Figure 7 illustrates the micromotion distribution at the prosthesis-bone interface. Areas of greater micromotion were located predominantly at the anterior and lateral regions, where the bone stock is relatively thin. The proportion of the interface exhibiting micromotion greater than 40 µm was 0%, 0%, 0.56%, 30.63%, and 12.31% across the five loading conditions.

Retrospective case study

All four patients in the follow-up cohort were female, with a median age of 63.0 years (IQR 60.3 to 66.3) at the time of surgery and a mean BMI of 25.3 kg/m² (22.7 to 26.7) (Table II). Varus-valgus stress testing was negative in all cases, confirming intact MCL and periarticular soft-tissue function. Intraoperatively, the extension-stem augment demonstrated excellent conformity to the metaphyseal defect without the need for additional host-bone removal, and prosthesis-bone interface matching was satisfactory. The mean operating time was 130 minutes (90 to 160), and the mean total blood loss (TBL) was 412.5 ml (300 to 500), with no procedure-related complications observed. The follow-up duration was over 20.7 months (ranging from 20.7 to 30), and the cumulative survival rate of the extension-stem augment was 100%. One patient who received only the tibial-side augment underwent a subsequent revision surgery for femoral-side PJI at 20.7 months postoperatively. Intraoperatively, the tibial baseplate was firmly connected to the augment and difficult to remove, while the augment remained securely integrated with the tibia and could not be extracted, and was therefore retained. The mean preoperative knee ROM was 79° (55° to 104°), improving to 115° (100° to 130°) postoperatively. The KSS increased from a preoperative mean of 45 (42 to 47) to 85 (82 to 87), while the WOMAC decreased from 48 (45 to 50) to 18 (17 to 20) (Table III). Stress radiographs confirmed stable varus-valgus alignment (Supplementary Figure S2), and follow-up radiographs and CT images demonstrated no evidence of prosthesis migration or loosening (Figure 8 and Figure 9).

Discussion

AORI type III proximal tibial defects are characterized by extensive metaphyseal bone loss and remain a major reconstructive challenge in rTKA.32 For cases with preserved MCL function, we proposed a strategy that first reconstructs the proximal tibial defect using a 3D-printed extension-stem augment, followed by surface-type knee reconstruction. The extension-stem augment was custom-designed according to the defect morphology, minimizing host-bone resection during prosthesis fitting, enabling anatomical restoration of the defect,33 and providing robust support for the tibial baseplate. Recent studies have also supported the value of 3D planning and patient-specific reconstruction in complex periarticular surgery. Assink et al34 reported that 3D surgical planning with patient-specific drilling guides improved implant positioning accuracy in tibial plateau fracture surgery. Li et al35 further demonstrated that customized implant geometry could influence stress distribution and micromotion in complex acetabular reconstruction. These findings support the rationale for a patient-specific structural design in complex bone defect reconstruction. The surface-type prosthesis, applied thereafter, facilitates maximal soft-tissue balance with minimal mechanical constraint, thereby avoiding the stress concentration associated with highly constrained implants and restoring knee function and stability.36 This study aimed to evaluate a feasible option for the reconstruction of AORI type III proximal tibial defects.

Referring to previous finite element studies, including the work by Ren et al37 we used stress, strain, and micromotion as key indicators to comprehensively evaluate the biomechanical performance of the extension-stem augment in proximal tibial reconstruction. Across five loading conditions, the mean stress was consistently located in the medial metaphyseal regions (PM and AM) (Supplementary Figure S1). This pattern indicates that central stem alignment within the medullary canal and anatomical restoration of the defect allowed axial load transmission along the native tibial alignment, thereby re-establishing the physiological medial load-bearing pattern of the tibia. Peak stresses were primarily concentrated in the PM and PL regions, with local nodes exceeding the mean ultimate strength of trabecular bone (5.3 MPa (SD 2.9))38 under SL and SQ loading conditions (Figure 3). The strain analysis at the prosthesis-bone interface revealed high-strain response across the five loading conditions, with SL showing the most pronounced response, as 22.51% of the interface area was outside the trabecular strain range (−7,300 to 6,500 µε), indicating a greater risk of localized trabecular bone overload and consequent loss of local bone support during single-leg standing (Figure 6). Further FEA quantified the stress-shielding effect in the proximal tibia. The proportion of the potential bone resorption zone was approximately 30% of the metaphyseal volume (Figure 4), which is comparable to the degree of bone resorption reported for surface-type knee prosthesis.39 Given the possibility of stress concentration at the distal stem tip, we analyzed the stress distribution across four cross-sections from the proximal to the distal end of the stem. Across the five loading conditions, the peak stresses at these levels were 2.89, 3.56, 4.89, 8.99, and 10.71 MPa, respectively (Figure 5), all of which were well below the yield strength of cortical bone. Micromotion at the prosthesis-bone interface was assessed to evaluate the immediate postoperative stability of the extension-stem augment. Under the WA and AS conditions, micromotion across the entire interface remained below the 40 µm threshold, while under the DS condition, only 0.56% of the interface exceeded this value. These findings indicate that the augment could withstand the mechanical loads generated during moderate-intensity activities such as walking and stair climbing while maintaining interface stability (Figure 7). Overall, the FEA demonstrated favourable mechanical performance of the extension-stem augment, with trends consistent with those observed in clinical follow-up. At the recent follow-up, all implants remained well aligned with no evidence of migration or radiolucent lines, and the porous regions demonstrated signs of osseointegration. In the patient who underwent a subsequent revision for femoral-side PJI, the tibial extension-stem augment was firmly integrated with the host bone and could not be removed, further confirming the robustness and long-term stability of the prosthesis-bone interface. These findings reinforce the favourable biomechanical characteristics of the extension-stem augment in achieving anatomical reconstruction of the proximal tibial defect.

With anatomical reconstruction of the bone defect, knee reconstruction in patients with AORI type III proximal tibial defects was accomplished using a surface-type prosthesis. For AORI type III tibial defects, most current studies favour the use of constrained or semi-constrained prostheses for knee reconstruction.4,40 Although increasing the level of constraint could provide short-term joint stability, it also results in stress concentration at the implant components and prosthesis-bone interfaces. Previous reports have documented cases of modular junction failure and prosthesis-bone interface loosening associated with stress concentration,11,16 thereby compromising long-term implant survival. In contrast, surface-type knee prostheses restore the native roll-glide kinematics of the knee and offer distinct biomechanical advantages, effectively avoiding the stress concentration issues. However, lacking supplemental varus-valgus or rotational stability, they rely on preserved ligamentous and soft-tissue integrity.41 Therefore, in rTKA cases with preserved soft-tissue integrity, the use of a surface-type prosthesis is expected to restore physiological knee kinematics while reducing mechanical load concentration, thereby improving long-term outcomes. All patients included in this study underwent a comprehensive preoperative clinical and radiological evaluation. Soft-tissue integrity was confirmed using varus-valgus stress testing and stress radiographs, and intraoperative assessment further verified flexion-extension function and varus-valgus stability during rTKA. At the recent follow-up, all four patients demonstrated satisfactory knee ROM and stability. Varus-valgus stress testing and anterior-posterior drawer tests were negative (Supplementary Figure S2), and no flexion-extension lag was observed. The mean WOMAC score at the latest follow-up was 18 (16 to 20), and the mean KSS was 85 (82 to 87), demonstrating superior short-term outcomes compared with those reported for highly constrained reconstruction strategies.42-44 These findings support the feasibility of using a surface-type prosthesis for knee reconstruction after anatomical restoration of the AORI type III tibial defect with preserved ligamentous and soft-tissue function (Figure 8 and Figure 9).

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, which restricts the robustness of the clinical findings. Second, the FEA model focused solely on the tibial extension-stem augment and did not incorporate surrounding musculature, ligamentous structures, or the femoral component, which may influence the accuracy of the simulated loading environment. Third, cement extrusion during implantation remains a technical concern. Although minor extrusion did not compromise the stability of the extension-stem augment in this cohort, achieving optimal prosthesis–bone contact and long-term osseointegration remains a key objective. These limitations will be addressed in future investigations.

To achieve anatomical reconstruction of the tibial defect and the knee joint, we developed a patient-specific extension-stem augment. The present study demonstrated that the implant provided satisfactory biomechanical performance and early clinical outcomes in the reconstruction of inclusive AORI type III proximal tibial defects with intact MCLs. This study may serve as a graded reconstructive option in rTKA, and offers a novel strategy for managing severe proximal tibial bone loss.
