The frequency of ponticulus posticus in orthodontic patient and its relationship with skeletal malocclusion, dental anomalies and hyoid bone position
Authors: Azer Seda Özen, Esra Çifçi Özkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence of ponticulus posticus in relation to sex, skeletal malocclusion, dental agenesis, impacted teeth and hyoid bone position.MethodsLateral cephalometric and panoramic radiographs of 477 Turkish orthodontic patients (sample size = 477, 290 females, 187 males; mean age 17.3 ± 5.88 years) were analyzed. Participants were categorized into three groups based on the presence of ponticulus posticus: complete, incomplete and absence. Relationships between ponticulus posticus and sex, skeletal malocclusion, dental agenesis, impacted teeth and hyoid bone position were assessed. Skeletal malocclusion was determined using sagittal (ANB angle) and vertical (GoGn-SN angle) parameters. The position of the hyoid bone was quantified on lateral cephalometric radiographs using six linear (H-SN, H-FH, H-PP, MPH, H-C3, H-RGn) and four angular measurements (NSH, MPH, Lah-PP, SHGn). The study was conducted between November 2023 and September 2024. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS v23 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA). Ethical approval was obtained from the University Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024-BİAEK/09–50).ResultsPonticulus posticus was absent in 394 individuals (82.6%), complete in 51 individuals (10.7%), and incomplete in 32 individuals (6.7%). Significant differences were found in H-SN, H-FH, H-PP (p < 0.05) and MPH angular measurements (p < 0.05) between groups. No significant differences were observed in ANB, GoGn-SN, MPH linear, H-C3 linear, H-RGn linear, NSH angular, SHGn angular, Lah-PP angular values, dental agenesis, sex or impacted teeth (p > 0.05). Subgroup analysis by sagittal skeletal pattern showed differences in H-SN, H-FH, H-PP, and H-RGn (p < 0.05). Logistic regression revealed a significant association between GoGn-SN and ponticulus posticus presence (p = 0.045).ConclusionsPonticulus posticus was observed in 17.4% of the individuals. A significant association was found between incomplete ponticulus posticus and a lower vertical position of the hyoid bone. No significant relationships were observed between ponticulus posticus and age, sagittal skeletal pattern or other craniofacial variables. However, the GoGn-SN angle showed a significant association with the presence of ponticulus posticus, suggesting that cranio-cervical posture may contribute to its development and clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07533-7.Keywords: Ponticulus posticus, Skeletal malocclusion, Impacted teeth, Hyoid bone, Dental agenesis

Introduction

Lateral cephalometric radiography (LCR) analysis assists in diagnosing pathologies in the cervical spine region [1]. One of the significant anatomical findings in the cervical spine is the ponticulus posticus (PP), first reported by Macalister [2, 3]. PP, also known as the Kimmerle anomaly, arcuate foramen, sagittal foramen or canal vertebralis, represents an anatomical variant of the first cervical vertebra [4]. It is defined as a complete or incomplete ossified structure located between the posterior arch of the atlas and the posterior margin of the superior articular facet [5]. Although its etiology remains unclear, it is considered to have a genetic or congenital origin or to result from the ossification of primitive ligaments [6]. Reported prevalence rates in the literature range from 1.1% to 37.8% [7, 8]. Although often asymptomatic [9], PP can be associated with clinical manifestations such as neck, head, shoulder, and arm pain, migraine attacks without aura, vertigo, hearing loss, Wallenberg syndrome, Barre‒Lieou syndrome, loss of consciousness and nausea [10–13].

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of PP and its potential associations with sex, skeletal malocclusion, dental agenesis, impacted teeth and hyoid bone position. Understanding the clinical importance of PP is important, as its presence has been linked to symptoms including neck pain, headaches, vertigo, and neurological complications. Moreover, potential relationships between PP and dental or skeletal anomalies may have implications for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive evaluation in affected individuals.

The null hypothesis stated that there is no statistically significant relationship between the presence of PP and variables such as sex, skeletal growth pattern, dental anomalies, impacted teeth and hyoid bone position. The corresponding alternative hypothesis posited that there is a statistically significant association between PP and at least one of these variables.

Materials and methods

Sample size calculation

To determine the sample size, similar studies were reviewed and the calculation yielding the highest number was considered based on the main hypotheses and statistical methods to be applied [14, 15]. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power v3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany) program with a 95% confidence level [16]. The analysis was conducted within the χ² tests family (goodness-of-fit tests: contingency tables) using an a priori approach. The effect size was set at w = 0.1435, calculated from the frequency values presented in Reference [14], Table 2, specifically from the distribution of sex and PP type variables, which informed the estimation of Cohen’s w for the contingency table. The significance level (α) was defined as 0.05 (two-sided), the desired statistical power (1-β) as 0.80, and the degrees of freedom (df) as 2, with an allocation ratio of 1. Based on these parameters, the required total sample size was determined to be N = 468, with a noncentrality parameter (λ) of 9.6498432, a critical χ² value of 5.9914645 and an actual power of 0.8006603. This calculation confirmed that the study had sufficient power to detect statistically significant differences among the groups. Ultimately, a total of 477 radiographs from patients were included in the study.

Ethical approval and study sample

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Non-Interventional Clinical Research of Biruni University (Decision No: 2024-BİAEK/09–50). The study was conducted between November 2023 and September 2024. A total of 500 lateral cephalometric radiographs (LCRs) and 500 panoramic radiographs (PRs) were identified by screening the archives of the Department of Oral, Dental, and Jaw Radiology at the Faculty of Dentistry, Biruni University. All radiographs included in this study were obtained as part of the routine pretreatment diagnostic records for orthodontic evaluation.

After applying the inclusion criteria, 23 LCRs and 23 PRs were excluded, resulting in a final sample 477 LCRs and 477 PRs. PRs were evaluated by a single trained examiner to identify dental agenesis and impacted teeth. In cases of ambiguity, the images were reviewed in consultation with a senior orthodontist and the final decision was made by consensus. The presence of PP was determined by direct visualization on lateral cephalometric radiographs. Each radiograph was assessed twice by the same orthodontist to ensure reliability. All radiographs were taken under standardized conditions, in the natural head position, by the same clinician using the same machine (Dentsply Sirona, Orthophos XG5, Hessen, Germany). Each patient received an identification number, and the presence of PP was recorded as complete or incomplete when identified (Fig. 1).

Patient characteristics and measurements

The average age of the patients was 17.3 ± 5.88 years. The ANB angle was used to characterize sagittal skeletal discrepancies, whereas the GoGn–SN angle was used to quantify the vertical skeletal growth pattern. The hyoid bone position was evaluated with six linear measurements (H-SN, H-FH, H-PP, H-MP, H-C3, H-RGn) and four angular measurements (NSH, MP-H, Lah-PP, SHGn). The sagittal skeletal pattern of each patient was determined according to the ANB angle from the cephalometric analysis developed by Steiner (1953): ANB = 0–4° for Class I, ANB > 4° for Class II and ANB < 0° for Class III [17]. The vertical skeletal pattern was assessed using the GoGn-SN angle from the same analysis: GoGn-SN = 32° for normodivergent, GoGn-SN < 32° for hypodivergent, GoGn-SN > 32° for hyperdivergent growth patterns.

The angular (Fig. 2) and linear (Fig. 3) measurements were determined based on the anatomical landmarks in Table 1 and were drawn manually.

Cephalometric landmarks and angles, cephalometric angular and linear measurements

Dental agenesis and impacted teeth were evaluated using PRs. Third molars were excluded from the assessment. Agenesis was defined as the congenital absence of any permanent tooth other than third molars. The presence of impacted teeth was determined using a combination of panoramic radiographs, intraoral photographs, patient age and medical history. A tooth was considered impacted if it failed to erupt into the dental arch within the expected developmental period and demonstrated radiographic signs of impaction, such as abnormal angulation, lack of eruption path, or complete bony enclosure. All evaluation were performed by a single trained examiner to ensure consistency.

To assess the reliability of the cephalometric measurements, a second set of measurements was obtained by the same examiner on 50 LCRs, which were selected from the total sample of 477 radiographs using a simple random sampling method, two weeks following the initial assessments. Intra-observer reliability was analyzed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). The SPSS syntax used to calculate ICC values is available [see Additional file 1]. The ICC values for both linear and angular parameters were above 0.95, indicating an excellent level of consistency and reproducibility (Table 2). These findings confirmed that the initial measurements were methodologically robust and suitable for inclusion in further statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of distribution was tested with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Associations between categorical variables were examined using Fisher’s Exact Test with Monte Carlo correction. For comparisons among three or more independent groups with normally distributed quantitative variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Multiple comparisons were examined with the Bonferroni test. For comparisons of three or more independent groups with non-normally distributed quantitative variables, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used, and multiple comparisons were examined with the Dunn test.

The SPSS syntax used for these comparison analyses is available [see Additional file 2]. Interaction analysis among independent variables was also conducted within the same model [see Additional file 3]. The effect of independent variables on the presence of the dependent variable (PP) was analyzed using binary logistic regression. The logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS syntax provided in this study [see Additional file 4].Agreement between repeated measurements was evaluated using the ICC. Descriptive statistics for quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (median), while categorical variables were presented as (n) and percentage (%). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A power analysis was conducted to confirm that the sample size was sufficient to detect statistically significant differences among skeletal class, impacted tooth and missing tooth groups. If these groups were randomly distributed without true underlying differences, the probability of detecting statistically significant results would be low. This supports the validity and reliability of the study design and findings. The complete anonymized dataset used in this study is available [see Additional file 5]. Participants were categorized in the dataset as follows: 1 = Incomplete PP, 2 = Complete PP and 3 = Absence of PP.

Results

Among the 477 LCRs, 394 (82.6%) showed no PP, 51 (10.7%) exhibited complete PP, and 32 (6.7%) exhibited incomplete PP. The overall prevalence of PP was 17.4%, with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI: 14.2% − 21.0%).

Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation was used to analyze the distributions of clinical characteristics among the study groups and their associations. No statistically significant relationships were found between the study groups, clinical characteristics or sex (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

The detection of the PP, which often remains asymptomatic, is crucial — especially in procedures involving a posterior surgical approach, such as those performed in the craniovertebral junction —due to the potential risk of vertebral artery injury [5]. Orthodontists play an important role in identifying this anatomical variation, which can be easily detected on LCRs and in informing patients about potential complications.

A meta-analysis in the literature has reported a wide range of PP prevalence, from 1.1% to 37.8% [7]. In a study conducted on the Middle Anatolian population using the LCR, the prevalence of PP was 18.8% (9.6% complete and 9.2% incomplete), with a mean age of 20.98 ± 6.95 years (range: 10–39 years) [19]. In the present study, the prevalence of PP was found to be 17.4% (10.7% complete and 6.7% incomplete).

According to geographic subgroup analyses, the highest prevalence of complete PP has been observed in North America (11.3%), followed by Europe (11.2%), Africa (8.9%), South America (8.7%), and Asia (7.5%) [20]. Notable differences also exist among countries within the Asia: the prevalence of complete PP is lower in China (4.4%) and South Korea (5.8%) than in India (7.6%) [20]. These inter-populational differences are generally attributed to racial, genetic and geographic factors [1].

The relationship between sagittal skeletal malocclusion and the presence of PP has been investigated in several studies. Lo Giudice et al. reported a higher prevalence of PP in skeletal Class II patients, although the difference was not statistically significant, based on an analysis of individuals aged 1–60 years using lateral cephalometric radiographs [21]. Di Venere et al. reported that PP was most frequently observed in skeletal Class I patients; however, no statistically significant difference was detected in a study based on LCRs of individuals aged 4–18 years [14]. Consistent with these findings, the present study also revealed the highest PP prevalence in skeletal Class I patients, followed by Class III and Class II, although the differences among groups were not statistically significant.

A study evaluating the relationship between cervical vertebral anomalies and sella turcica bridging through LCRs in a sample of 270 individuals (mean age: 13.53 ± 2.93 years; range: 8–22.6 years) reported no significant association between PP and vertical skeletal growth patterns [22]. Consistent with these findings, the present study also revealed no statistically significant relationship between PP and vertical skeletal growth patterns in initial analyses. However, in the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis, the GoGn-SN angle was found to be significantly associated with the presence of PP (p = 0.045). This finding suggests that variations in the vertical inclination of the cranio-cervical complex or mandibular plane may contribute to the occurrence of PP. Overall, these results indicate that although no direct association was observed between PP and vertical skeletal growth patterns in the general analyses, the GoGn-SN angle—representing the vertical orientation of the mandible—may play a determining role in the manifestation of PP.

Previous studies by Leonardi et al. and Kaya et al. have reported statistically significant relationships between impacted canines and PP [23, 24]. The shared embryological origin of neural crest cells, which contribute to both cervical skeletal development and tooth formation, may explain this association [23, 25]. However, based on the findings of the present study, no statistically significant relationship was found between impacted teeth and PP.

In another study examining the relationship between dental agenesis — another dental anomaly — and PP through LCRs, Putrino et al. identified a statistically significant association in a sample comprising both Caucasian and African populations (age range: 7–21 years; mean age: 13 years) [15]. Conversely, Kaya et al. reported no statistically significant relationship between PP and either maxillary lateral incisor agenesis or mandibular second premolar agenesis in a Turkish population [24].Similarly, neither Di Venere et al., who conducted a study on an Italian population using LCRs in individuals aged 4–18 years, nor the present analysis involving a Turkish population, reported a statistically significant relationship between dental agenesis and PP [14]. The discrepancies among these studies may be attributed to differences in ethnic background, lack of chronological age matching between the study and control groups, wide age ranges, unequal sex distribution across groups, and smaller sample sizes in certain subgroups.

Çakmak et al., who analyzed lateral cervical spine radiographs and cadaveric specimens in a sample aged 26–79 years (mean age: 49 ± 14.43), and Karau et al., who examined dry atlases from individuals aged 25–75 years, both reported a higher prevalence of PP in females than in males [9, 26]. It has been suggested that accelerated ossification associated with estrogen may contribute to the increased prevalence of PP in women [26]. Conversely, Geist et al., using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), reported a significant higher prevalence of PP in males than in females in a sample with a mean age of 12.2 years [27]. Consistent with the findings of Ruprecht et al., who used LCRs, and Paraskevas et al., who conducted a cadaveric study on individuals aged 5–90 years, the present study found no statistically significant relationship between sex and the presence of PP [28, 29].

The position of the hyoid bone may be influenced by orthodontic anomalies, craniofacial structural deviations, oral breathing patterns, and swallowing dysfunctions. Although this position can be affected by sex, the anteroposterior relationship of the jaw and vertical growth patterns, it does not significantly vary according to craniofacial growth patterns [30, 31]. Furthermore, the position of the hyoid bone is diagnostically important for assessing the prognosis of orthodontic treatment and the risk of posttreatment relapse [32].

Unlike previous studies, the relationship between the PP and hyoid bone position was examined both angularly and linearly [15, 23–29]. In the linear analysis of hyoid bone position, statistically significant differences were observed in the vertical position measurements (H-SN, H-FH, and H-PP) between individuals with and without PP. These results indicate that the hyoid bone is positioned more inferiorly relative to the craniofacial reference planes in individuals presenting with PP. This finding suggests a vertical displacement of the hyoid complex, which may reflect postural adaptations within the cranio-cervical region. In contrast, no significant difference was found in the linear measurement of MPH, which may be influenced by mandibular position, jaw movement and dental malocclusion — factors that make this parameter more susceptible to functional variation. Conversly, the angular measurement of MPH demonstrated a statistically significant difference, indicating that the presence of PP affects not only the translational but also the angular orientation of the hyoid bone relative to the mandible. This finding may be related to postural adjustments in the suprahyoid and infrahyoid muscular systems that modify the angular alignment of the hyoid complex.

When sagittal skeletal patterns were considered, significant differences were also identified in the H-SN, H-FH, H-PP, and H-RGn measurements, suggesting that the relationship between PP and hyoid bone position may vary depending on the underlying sagittal morphology. The association of PP with differences in both linear and angular parameters of hyoid position indicates that this anatomical variation may influence the functional and postural balance of the cranio-cervical complex.

These findings have noteworthy clinical implications. The positional alteration of the hyoid bone linked to PP can narrow the upper airway, modify tongue posture, and compromise mandibular stability, thereby placing orthodontic treatment outcomes and their long-term stability at risk. Evidence suggests that an anteriorly positioned hyoid bone — a pattern frequently observed in Class III malocclusions — is associated with functional adaptions that may influence posttreatment stability [33, 34]. Accordingly, identifying PP should prompt clinicians to evaluate hyoid position carefully during diagnosis and to integrate this assessment into both treatment and retention protocols to anticipate and mitigate potential relapse.

The present study has certain limitations. First, the sample consisted exclusively of orthodontic patients from a single university clinic, which may introduce potential selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, the inclusion of a broad and heterogeneous group of orthodontic patients enhances the clinical relevance of the results. In addition, the assessment of PP was based on two-dimensional LCRs. Although this method is inherently limited by the superimposition of anatomical structures compared to three-dimensional imaging, it remains the most commonly used diagnostic tool in orthodontics and forms part of routine patient records. For this reason, the findings are directly applicable to daily clinical practice. While three-dimensional imaging could provide greater diagnostic accuracy, its use in large-scale studies is often constrained by ethical, economic, and radiation-related concerns. In this context, the use of two-dimensional radiographs strengthens both the practicality and ethical validity of the present research. Importantly, the findings highlight that PP should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained cervicogenic symptoms or in cases where detailed evaluation of the cervical region is required for orthodontic or surgical planning. In such circumstances, CBCT may be justified to confirm the diagnosis and minimize surgical risks.

Conclusion

The presence of PP was evaluated in relation to various craniofacial structures and clinical parameters. No statistically significant association was found between PP and sex, age, sagittal skeletal growth patterns, dental agenesis, or impacted teeth. However, linear analyses in the vertical plane (H-SN, H-FH, and H-PP) revealed statistically significant differences, indicating that individuals with PP had a more inferiorly positioned hyoid bone relative to craniofacial reference planes. No significant difference was observed in the linear measurement of MPH, whereas a significant difference in the angular MPH measurement indicated an association between PP and the angular orientation of the hyoid complex relative to the mandible.

Subgroup analyses based on sagittal skeletal patterns demonstrated significant differences in H-SN, H-FH, H-PP, and H-RGn measurements, suggesting that the relationship between PP and hyoid bone position may vary depending on craniofacial morphology. In addition, logistic regression analysis revealed a significant association between the GoGn-SN angle and the presence of PP, indicating a possible link between PP and cranio-cervical postural parameters.

Recommendations

Future research should incorporate three-dimensional imaging techniques such as CBCT to allow a more precise evaluation of PP and its anatomical relationships. Prospective studies with larger and more homogeneous samples are recommended to improve understanding of the impact of PP on various craniofacial patterns. Integrating functional evaluations—such as evaluations of airway volume, tongue posture, and head–neck alignment—would provide deeper insight into the biomechanical and clinical implications of PP. Furthermore, longitudinal studies exploring the potential effects of PP on treatment stability and postural adaptation could enhance understanding of its role within orthodontic and craniofacial contexts.
