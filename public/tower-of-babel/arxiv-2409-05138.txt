Abstract
. Given a real Banach space X X , we show that the Nehari manifold method can be applied to functionals which are C 1 C^{1} in X ∖ { 0 } X\setminus\{0\} . In particular we deal with functionals that can be unbounded near 0 0 , and prove the existence of a ground state and infinitely many critical points for such functionals. These results are then applied to three classes of problems: the prescribed energy problem for a family of functionals depending on a parameter, problems involving the affine p p -Laplacian operator, and degenerate Kirchhoff type problems.

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a uniformly convex (real) Banach space and
\Phi
a
C^{1}
functional (in the sense of Fréchet) over
X
. It is well-known that the Nehari manifold method is one of the main tools in nonlinear analysis when looking for critical points of
\Phi
. In particular this method allows us to obtain ground states (or least energy critical points) as well as infinitely many critical points under appropriate conditions on
\Phi
. We refer to
[
15
]
for a description of these results (among many others) and its applications to boundary value problems. We aim at extending these results to functionals that are
C^{1}
in
X\setminus\{0\}
. As a matter of fact, searching for nonzero critical points of
\Phi
makes sense even when this functional is not defined at
0
, and dealing with the Nehari set associated to
\Phi
seems to be a good choice in view of its definition:

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}(\Phi):=\{u\in X\setminus\{0\}:\Phi^{\prime}(u)u=0\}

Our motivation for dealing with
\Phi\in C^{1}(X\setminus\{0\})
is not merely abstract, as this kind of functional arises in (at least) three classes of problems, namely: the
prescribed energy problem
for a family of functionals depending on a parameter, problems involving the
affine

p
-Laplacian operator and degenerate Kirchhoff type problems. Let us describe them in the sequel.

1.1.
The prescribed energy problem

Let
I_{1},I_{2}\in C^{1}(X)
and
\Phi_{\lambda}:=I_{1}-\lambda I_{2}
with
\lambda
a real parameter. We consider the problem

(1.1)

\Phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(u)=0,\quad\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=c,

where
c
is an arbitrary real number.
By a (nontrivial) solution of (
1.1
) we mean a couple
(\lambda,u)\in\mathbb{R}\times X\setminus\{0\}
satisfying (
1.1
), in which case
u
is a critical point of
\Phi_{\lambda}
at the level
c
. In this sense, a solution of (
1.1
) yields a critical point of
\Phi_{\lambda}
with prescribed energy. Our main goal here is to analyse the solvability of (
1.1
) in terms of
I_{1},I_{2}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We are mainly motivated by the study of boundary value problems whose solutions are critical points of a given functional (the so-called
energy functional
).

The problem (
1.1
) has been recently studied in
[
5
,
11
,
12
]
by means of the
nonlinear generalized Rayleigh quotient
method (the NGRQ method, for short), which is based on the introduction of the functional
\lambda_{c}
given through the following relation:

(1.2)

\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda=\lambda_{c}(u):=\frac{I_{1}(u)-c}{I_{2}(u)}.

Here we assume that
I_{2}(u)\neq 0
for every
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
, so that
\lambda_{c}\in C^{1}(X\setminus\{0\})
.
Furthermore, a simple computation shows that

(1.3)

\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u)=\frac{\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u)}^{\prime}(u)}{I_{2}(u)},\quad\forall u\in X\setminus\{0\}.

Therefore (
1.1
) can be reformulated as follows:

\Phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(u)=0,\quad\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda=\lambda_{c}(u),\quad\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u)=0,

i.e. critical points (along with its associated critical values) of
\lambda_{c}
yield (all) couples
(\lambda,u)
solving (
1.1
).

So far the issue of finding critical points (and critical values) of
\lambda_{c}
has been tackled by means of Pohozaev’s
fibering method
, which leads to the NGRQ functional, cf.
[
5
,
11
]
. In contrast with
[
11
]
, we shall consider now the case where
I_{1}
may contain nonhomogeneous terms. Our main model for this situation is the functional

(1.4)

\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\quad u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

which is associated to the boundary value problem

(1.5)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda u+f(u)&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Here
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\geq 1
) is a bounded domain,
F(s):=\int_{0}^{s}f(t)dt
, and
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
has subcritical Sobolev growth. In this case the functional
\lambda_{c}
is given by

\lambda_{c}(u)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-2\int_{\Omega}F(u)-2c}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}},

for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
.

We shall prove the existence of a ground state and infinitely many pairs of critical points of
\lambda_{c}
and relate it to the problem (
1.1
). These results shall be established under rather general conditions on
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, providing us with applications to a large variety of elliptic problems.

1.2.
Problems involving the affine
p
-Laplacian

The
affine

p
-Laplacian operator is given by

\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{p}u=-{\rm div}\left(H_{u}^{p-1}(\nabla u)\nabla H_{u}(\nabla u)\right)

for every
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
. Here

H_{u}^{p}(\zeta)=\gamma_{N,p}^{-\frac{N}{p}}\;{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}^{N+p}(u)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\|\nabla_{\xi}u\|_{p}^{-(N+p)}|\langle\xi,\zeta\rangle|^{p}\,d\sigma(\xi)\ \ {\rm for}\ \zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,,

\gamma_{N,p}=\left(2\omega_{N+p-2}\right)^{-1}\left(N\omega_{N}\omega_{p-1}\right)\left(N\omega_{N}\right)^{p/N}
,
\nabla_{\xi}u(x)=\nabla u(x)\cdot\xi
,
\omega_{k}
is the volume of the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and

(1.6)

{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)=\gamma_{N,p}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\|\nabla_{\xi}u\|_{p}^{-N}\,d\sigma(\xi)\right)^{-\frac{1}{N}}

is the so-called
affine

p
-energy. In addition, we set

\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{p}0=0
.

We say that
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
is a nontrivial weak solution of

(1.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlllr}\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{p}u&=&f(u)&{\rm in}&\Omega,\\
u&=&0&{\rm on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

if

\langle\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\Omega}H_{u}^{p-1}(\nabla u)\nabla H_{u}(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi

for every
\varphi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
. It follows that nontrivial weak solutions of (
1.7
) correspond to
critical points of the functional

\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)=\frac{1}{p}{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}(u)-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\quad u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\},

where
F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)ds
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfies some growth conditions. By Theorem 1 of
[
7
]
,
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
is continuous in
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
and
C^{1}
in
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
. Furthermore the geometry and compactness properties of
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
are affected by the fact that
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}
is not convex and may remain bounded along unbounded sequences, so
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}
is not coercive (which strongly differs from the classical
p
-Laplacian setting). We refer the reader to
[
7
]
for a discussion on these issues, as well as further properties of the
affine

p
-Laplacian operator.

The existence of at least one nontrivial solution for (
1.7
) has been studied recently. For a powerlike
f
we refer to Corollary 3.8 in
[
13
]
, Theorem 5.1 in
[
14
]
, and Theorems 1.1-1.5 in
[
6
]
, where direct minimization arguments are used. In
[
7
]
the problem (
1.7
) was treated for a
p
-superlinear
f
satisfying the classical Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition (see Theorems 3 therein). To this end the authors adapt the mountain pass and minimization theorems for a functional
\Phi
which is continuous on
X
and
C^{1}
in
X\setminus\{0\}
. More recently the concave-convex case of the problem (
1.7
) was treated in
[
8
]
using the tools obtained from
[
7
]
with an appropriate perturbation argument.

We shall observe that dealing with the Nehari manifold associated to
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
yields a more direct approach (in comparison to
[
7
,
8
]
) with regard to the Palais-Smale condition. Indeed, unlike
[
7
,
8
]
we do not need to perturb the functional
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
to obtain such property. In addition, Theorem
5.1
below seems to be the first result on the existence of infinitely many solutions for (
1.7
).

1.3.
Degenerate Kirchhoff type problems

Our third model of functional is given by

\displaystyle\Phi(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{p}}-\int_{\Omega}F(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta}\|u\|^{\theta}-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\quad u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\},

where
\theta\leq 1<p
and
\theta\neq 0
(in particular
\theta
can be negative). Such functional is related to the degenerate Kirchhoff type problem

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlllr}-\|u\|^{\theta-p}\Delta_{p}u&=&f(u)&{\rm in}&\Omega,\\
u&=&0&{\rm on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)ds
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
is subcritical.
When
p=2
the problem above reduces to the so-called Kirchhoff problem

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlllr}-M(\|u\|^{2})\Delta u&=&f(u)&{\rm in}&\Omega,\\
u&=&0&{\rm on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

with
M(s^{2})=s^{\theta-2}
. Since
M
is not bounded away from zero for
\theta\leq 1
the problem is said to be degenerate. This class of Kirchhoff type problems has been mostly studied for a nondegenerate
M
. In the degenerate case we refer the reader to
[
1
]
, where (
1.9
) is considered with
f(u)
replaced by
\lambda f(x,u)
and
M\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
such that
M(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Assuming
f(x,u)
to be asymptotically linear in
u
the authors use bifurcation arguments to prove the existence of a positive solution for
0<\lambda<\Lambda_{1}:=M(0)\lambda_{1}
, see Theorem 3.1 therein. In
[
10
]
a positive solution is obtained for (
1.9
) with
f(u)=\lambda u^{r-1}
,
1<r<2^{*}
, and
M
such that
s^{2-r}M(s)\to\infty
as
s\to 0
and
s^{2-r}M(s)\to 0
as
s\to\infty
, see Example 3.1 therein.

Besides applying to (
1.8
) in a degenerate case with respect to the left-hand side, our results hold for a wide class of right-hand sides, which allows in particular
f
to vanish at infinity when
\theta<1
, see Example 1 in Section 6 below. More precisely, the more negative is
\theta-1
, the faster
f
can vanish at infinity.

This article is organized as follows: in Section 2 we state and prove our main abstract result, Theorem
2.1
, and apply it to (
1.1
), which yields Theorem
2.3
and Corollary
2.5
. Section 3 contains the proof of Theorem
2.3
, which is then applied to some boundary value problems in Section 4. We also consider some problems that do not fit in the conditions of Theorem
2.3
in Subsection 4.1. Finally, in Sections 5 and 6 we apply Theorem
2.1
to problems (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), respectively.

Main abstract results

As mentioned above, we shall rely here on the Nehari manifold approach in the spirit of
[
15
]
, which we recall now.
Let
X
be an infinite-dimensional uniformly convex Banach space, equipped with
\|\cdot\|\in C^{1}(X\setminus\{0\})
.
We shall verify that the abstract setting of
[
15
]
applies to
\Phi
although this functional is not defined at
u=0
. To this end, we require the following condition:

(H1)

For every
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
the map
t\mapsto\Phi(tu)
, defined for
t>0
, has exactly one critical point
t(u)>0
, which is either a global maximum point or a global minimum point. Moreover, the map
u\mapsto t(u)
is bounded away from zero in
\mathcal{S}
, and bounded from above in any compact subset of
\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the unit sphere in
X
.

Let us recall that in
[
15
]
it is assumed that
\Phi\in C^{1}(X)
with
\Phi(0)=0
, and
t(u)
is a global maximum point of
t\mapsto\Phi(tu)
for every
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
, cf. Figure
1
. This assumption implies that
\Phi
is positive (and therefore bounded from below) on
\mathcal{N}
. We shall deal with cases where
\Phi
is not positive on
\mathcal{N}
, which may occur not only when
t(u)
is a maximum point, but also when it is a minimum point.

From (H1) it follows that

\mathcal{N}=\{t(u)u\in X\setminus\{0\}:u\in\mathcal{S}\}.

In addition, the uniqueness of
t(u)
and the behavior of the map
u\mapsto t(u)
on
\mathcal{S}
(which is already assumed in condition
(A3)
of
[
15
]
) entails that
\mathcal{N}
is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{S}
, and there is a one-to-one correspondence between critical points of
\Phi
and critical points of the map
u\mapsto\Phi(t(u)u)
,
u\in\mathcal{S}
. As a matter of fact, let
\delta>0
be such that
t(u)\geq\delta
for any
u\in\mathcal{S}
. Then the behavior of
t\mapsto\Phi(tu)
for
t\in(0,\delta)
and
u\in\mathcal{S}
does not affect the above one-to-one correspondence as this map has no critical points in
(0,\delta)
for any
u\in\mathcal{S}
. So in particular we may have
\Phi(tu)\to\infty
or
-\infty
as
t\to 0^{+}
, cf. Figure

.

The previous features imply that under (H1) the functional
\Phi
has a ground state critical point whenever it achieves its infimum on
\mathcal{N}
.
The following standard condition shall then provide us with infinitely many pairs of critical points of
\Phi
:

(H2)

\Phi
is even, bounded from below on
\mathcal{N}
, and satisfies the Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.
any sequence
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}
such that
(\Phi(u_{n}))
is bounded and
\Phi^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
has a convergent subsequence (in
X\setminus\{0\}
).

Indeed, the latter condition enables the use of the Ljusternik-Schnirelman principle.
Let

\lambda_{n}:=\displaystyle\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{u\in F}\Phi(t(u)u),

where
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\{F\subset\mathcal{S}:F\mbox{ is compact, symmetric, and }\gamma(F)\geq n\}
, and
\gamma(F)
is the Krasnoselskii genus of
F
. As stated in
[
15
, Theorem 2]
, every
\lambda_{n}
is a critical value of
\Phi
, and if the nondecreasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})
is stationary, i.e.
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k+j}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\lambda_{k}
has infinitely many associated pairs of critical points.

Finally, the following condition shall provide us with infinitely many critical values of
\Phi
:

(H3)

\Phi(t(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
for any
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
such that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
.

From the definition of
\lambda_{n}
it is clear that

\lambda_{1}=\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{S}}\Phi(t(u)u)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}\Phi(u).

The previous discussion is summarized in the following result, which is proved by checking that the arguments of
[

]
still apply to our situation:

Theorem 2.1
.

Under (H1) the following assertions hold:

(1)

If
\lambda_{1}
is achieved then it is the ground state level of
\Phi
, i.e. the least critical level of
\Phi
.

(2)

If (H2) holds then

(\lambda_{n})
is a nondecreasing sequence of critical values of
\Phi
. In particular, this functional has infinitely many pairs of critical points.

(3)

If (H2) and (H3) hold, then
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, so that
\Phi
has infinitely many critical values.

Proof.

It suffices to see that
[
15
, Corollary 10]
still holds if
\Phi\in C^{1}(X\setminus\{0\})
satisfies (H1), as this result does not take into account the value
\Phi(0)
(note that the proofs of
[
15
, Propositions 8 and 9]
hold in a similar way if
t(u)
is a minimum point of
t\mapsto\Phi(tu)
). In particular, if
\lambda_{1}
is achieved then it is the ground state level of
\Phi
. Moreover, Theorem 2 in
[
15
]
yields that
(\lambda_{n})
is a sequence of critical values of
\Phi
, which has infinitely many pairs of critical points. Finally, assertion (3) follows from Lemma A.1 in
[
12
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

Instead of assuming the ‘global’ Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}
, one may assume that
the Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}
holds at the levels
\lambda_{k}
. So
\lambda_{k}
is a critical value of
\Phi
if (H2) is replaced by:

(H2’)

\Phi
is even, bounded from below on
\mathcal{N}
, and satisfies the Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}
at the level
\lambda_{k}
, i.e.
any sequence
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}
such that
\Phi(u_{n})\to\lambda_{k}
and
\Phi^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
has a convergent subsequence (in
X\setminus\{0\}
).

This condition is particularly useful when one looks for prescribed energy solutions of the Brézis-Nirenberg problem, which is considered in Subsection 4.1 below.

2.1.
Applications to the prescribed energy problem

Let us consider the functional
\lambda_{c}
given by (
1.2
) under the assumptions of Subsection
1.1
.
We assume that
I_{2}
is
\alpha
-homogeneous for some
\alpha>1
, i.e.
I_{2}(tu)=t^{\alpha}I_{2}(u)
for any
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
and
t>0
. Note that in (
1.4
) we have
I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}
, which is a
2
-homogeneous functional.

From (
1.3
) we see that
u\in\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
if, and only if,
\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u)}^{\prime}(u)u=0
, i.e.
I_{1}^{\prime}(u)u-\lambda_{c}(u)I_{2}^{\prime}(u)u=0
.
Since
I_{2}
is
\alpha
-homogeneous we have
I_{2}^{\prime}(u)u=\alpha I_{2}(u)
, and consequently

\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})=\{u\in X\setminus\{0\}:H(u)+\alpha c=0\},

where

H(u):=I_{1}^{\prime}(u)u-\alpha I_{1}(u),\quad u\in X.

We see that
H\in C^{1}(X)
and
H(0)=0
if
I_{1}(0)=0
, which shall be assumed in the sequel.
The behavior of the map
t\mapsto H(tu)
is then crucial to understand
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
. In this article we shall focus on the case where the map
t\mapsto\lambda_{c}(tu)
has a unique critical point for any
u\neq 0
, which occurs if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(F1)

For any
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
there exists
s(u)\geq 0
such that the map
t\mapsto H(tu)
is increasing in
(0,s(u))
and decreasing in
(s(u),\infty)
. Moreover
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}H(tu)=-\infty
uniformly on weakly compact subsets of
X\setminus\{0\}
.

(F2)

For any
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
there exists
s(u)\geq 0
such that the map
t\mapsto H(tu)
is decreasing in
(0,s(u))
and increasing in
(s(u),\infty)
. Moreover
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}H(tu)=\infty
uniformly on weakly compact subsets of
X\setminus\{0\}
.

Note that these conditions include the case
s(u)=0
(i.e. the map
t\mapsto H(tu)
is monotone), which occurs in several problems.
Our main results on (
1.1
) will be stated under the condition that
H
is weakly semicontinuous, which is the case, for instance, in (
1.5
) with
f
continuous and having subcritical Sobolev growth, as
H(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(2F(u)-f(u)u\right)
for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, so that
H
is weakly continuous. Note also that in this case we have
H(u)=\int_{\Omega}h(u)
with
h(s)=2F(s)-f(s)s
, which appears in the litterature related to superlinear type problems without the Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition. We refer to
[
4
,
9
]
for more on this subject.

The following additional condition shall be imposed to have (H2) satisfied:

(F3)

I_{1}=J-K
, where
J,K\in C^{1}(X)
are such that
K^{\prime}
is completely continuous, and there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
,
\beta\geq\alpha
, and
\eta>1
such that
J(u)\geq C_{1}\|u\|^{\beta}
and
\left(J^{\prime}(u)-J^{\prime}(v)\right)(u-v)\geq C_{2}(\|u\|^{\eta-1}-\|v\|^{\eta-1})(\|u\|-\|v\|)
for any
u,v\in X
. Moreover, if
(F2)
holds then
\displaystyle\limsup_{\|u\|\to\infty}\frac{K(u)}{\|u\|^{\alpha}}\leq 0
.

Note that in (
1.5
) we have
I_{1}=J-K
with
J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}
and
K(u)=\int_{\Omega}F(u)
, for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

We are now in position to state our main result on (

):

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
I_{1},I_{2}\in C^{1}(X)
be such that
I_{1}(0)=I_{2}(0)=0
,
I_{2}(u)>0
for
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
,
I_{2}
is
\alpha
-homogeneous for some
\alpha>1
, and
I_{2}^{\prime}
is completely continuous. Assume in addition
(F1)
(respect.
(F2)
) if
c>0
(respect.
c<0
),
(F3)
, and
H
is weakly lower (respect. upper) semicontinuous. Then:

(1)

\lambda_{c}
has a ground state level, given by
\lambda_{1,c}:=\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u)u)=\min_{u\in\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})}\lambda_{c}(u)
.

(2)

If, in addition,
I_{1},I_{2}
are even then
\lambda_{c}
has a nondecreasing unbounded sequence of critical values, given by

\lambda_{n,c}:=\displaystyle\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{u\in F}\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u)u).

In particular
\lambda_{c}
has infinitely many pairs of critical points.

Remark 2.4
.

Instead of assuming in
(F3)
that
\left(J^{\prime}(u)-J^{\prime}(v)\right)(u-v)\geq C_{2}(\|u\|^{\eta-1}-\|v\|^{\eta-1})(\|u\|-\|v\|)
for any
u,v\in X
, one may assume that
J
is weakly lower semicontinuous and
H
is weakly continuous to show that the ground state level of
\lambda_{c}
is achieved. Indeed, in this case
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
is weakly sequentially closed and
\lambda_{c}
is weakly lower semicontinuous in
X\setminus\{0\}
, and since
0
does not belong to the weak sequential closure of
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
, we deduce that
\lambda_{c}
achieves it infimum over
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
.

Theorem
2.3
takes the following form when stated in terms of the functional
\Phi_{\lambda}
:

Corollary 2.5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, the following assertions hold:

(1)

\Phi_{\lambda}
has a critical point
u_{1,c}
at the level
c
for
\lambda=\lambda_{1,c}
, and has no such critical point for
\lambda<\lambda_{1,c}
.

(2)

If, in addition,
I_{1},I_{2}
are even, then for every

the functional
\Phi_{\lambda}
has a pair of critical points
\pm u_{n,c}
at the level

for
\lambda=\lambda_{n,c}
.
Moreover
\lambda_{n,c}\nearrow\infty
as

, so there exist infinitely many couples
(\lambda,\pm u)
such that
\Phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\pm u)=0
and
\Phi_{\lambda}(\pm u)=c
.

Remark 2.6
.

When
X
is a function space (which is the case in our applications) and

are even, one can choose

.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

Let us show now that Theorem
2.3
follows from Theorem
2.1
. To this end we shall prove in the next lemmas that
\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H1), (H2), and (H3).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
c\neq 0
. Assume that
I_{2}
is
\alpha
-homogeneous and
I_{1}
satisfies
(F1)
(respect.
(F2)
) if
c>0
(respect.
c<0
). Then:

(1)

For every
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
the map
t\mapsto\lambda_{c}(tu)
has exactly one critical point
t_{c}(u)>0
, which is a global maximum point (respect. minimum point).

(2)

If
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
and
t_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
then
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
. In particular,
t_{c}
is bounded from above in any compact subset of
\mathcal{S}
.

(3)

\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H1).

Proof.

(1)

Let
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
. By homogeneity we have
\lambda_{c}(tu):=\frac{I_{1}(tu)-c}{t^{\alpha}I_{2}(u)}
, so that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\lambda_{c}(tu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{\alpha}I_{2}(u)I_{1}^{\prime}(tu)u-\alpha t^{\alpha-1}I_{2}(u)(I_{1}(tu)-c)}{t^{2\alpha}I_{2}(u)^{2}}=\frac{I_{1}^{\prime}(tu)tu-\alpha(I_{1}(tu)-c)}{t^{\alpha+1}I_{2}(u)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{H(tu)+\alpha c}{t^{\alpha+1}I_{2}(u)},

i.e. the map
t\mapsto\lambda(tu)
has a critical point if, and only if,

H(tu)=-\alpha c.

Since
H(0)=0\gtrless-\alpha c
, we see that under
(F1)
(respect.
(F2)
) the map
t\mapsto\lambda(tu)
has a unique critical point
t_{c}(u)>0
, which is a global maximum (respect. minimum) point if
c>0
(respect.
c<0
).

(2)

Indeed, if
t_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
and
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\neq 0
in
X
, then by
(F1)
(respect.
(F2)
) we have
|H(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})|\to\infty
, which contradicts
H(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})=-\alpha c
.

(3)

It remains to show that
t_{c}
is away from zero on
\mathcal{S}
, which follows from the fact that
H
is continuous and
H(0)=0
.

∎

3.1.
On the Palais-Smale condition for
\lambda_{c}

Let us first show that (
1.3
) provides an easy way to characterize the Palais-Smale condition for
\lambda_{c}
in terms of the functional
\Phi_{\lambda}
. Recall that
(u_{n})\subset X\setminus\{0\}
is a Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
at the level
d
if
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to d
and
\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
in
X^{*}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(u_{n})\subset X\setminus\{0\}
be a Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
at the level
d
, i.e.
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to d
and
\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
in
X^{*}
.
If
I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n})
and
I_{2}(u_{n})
are bounded then
(u_{n})
is a Palais-Smale sequence for
\Phi_{d}
at the level
c
, i.e.
\Phi_{d}(u_{n})\to c
and
\Phi_{d}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
in
X^{*}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\lambda_{c}
we have that
\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}(u_{n})=c
, whereas
(
1.3
) yields that
\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}^{\prime}(u_{n})=I_{2}(u_{n})\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u_{n})
. Thus

\Phi_{d}(u_{n})=\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}(u_{n})+(\lambda_{c}(u_{n})-d)I_{2}(u_{n})=c+(\lambda_{c}(u_{n})-d)I_{2}(u_{n})

and

\Phi_{d}^{\prime}(u_{n})=\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}^{\prime}(u_{n})+(\lambda_{c}(u_{n})-d)I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n})=I_{2}(u_{n})\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u_{n})+(\lambda_{c}(u_{n})-d)I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n}).

The boundedness of
I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n})
and
I_{2}(u_{n})
yields the desired conclusion.
∎

The boundedness condition on
I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n})
and
I_{2}(u_{n})
assumed in Lemma
3.2
is clearly satisfied if
(u_{n})
is bounded and
I_{2}^{\prime}
is completely continuous, which provides the following result:

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume that
I_{2}^{\prime}
is completely continuous. Then
any bounded Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
at the level
d
is a Palais-Smale sequence for
\Phi_{d}
at the level
c
. In particular, if any Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
at the level
d
is bounded, and
\Phi_{d}
satisfies the Palais-Smale condition at the level
c
, then
\lambda_{c}
satisfies the Palais-Smale condition at the level
d
.

The proof of Lemma
3.2
shows that this result can be extended as follows:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(u_{n})\subset X\setminus\{0\}
and
(c_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
be such that
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{c_{n}}(u_{n})\to d
,
\lambda_{c_{n}}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
in
X^{*}
, and
c_{n}\to c
. If in addition
I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n})
and
I_{2}(u_{n})
are bounded then
(u_{n})
is a Palais-Smale sequence for
\Phi_{d}
at the level
c
.

Let us show now that
\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H2) and (H3):

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
c\neq 0
. Assume that
I_{2}
is
\alpha
-homogeneous and weakly continuous,
I_{1}
satisfies
(F1)
(respect.
(F2)
), and
H
is weakly lower (respect. upper) semicontinuous if
c>0
(respect.
c<0
). Assume in addition that
(F3)
holds.

(1)

If
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
and
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
then
u\neq 0
, i.e.
0
does not belong to the weak sequential closure of
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
.

(2)

\lambda_{c}
is coercive and bounded from below on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
.

(3)

Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
. Then

u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0\mbox{ in }X\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad t_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty.

In particular,
\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H3).

(4)

\lambda_{c}
satisfies the Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
c>0
.
If
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
and
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
then the weak lower semicontinuity of
H
implies that
H(u)\leq\liminf H(u_{n})=-\alpha c<0
, i.e.
u\neq 0
. A similar argument applies if
c<0
.

(2)

Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
be such that
\|u_{n}\|\to\infty
. Then
v_{n}=\frac{u_{n}}{\|u_{n}\|}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
. Indeed, otherwise we have (up to a subsequence)
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v\not\equiv 0
in
X
, and consequently by
(F1)
or
(F2)
we have
\alpha|c|=|H(u_{n})|=|H(\|u_{n}\|v_{n})|\to\infty
, which yields a contradiction. Therefore
v_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
.

If
(F1)
holds and
c>0
then, since
t=1
is now a global maximum point of
t\mapsto\lambda_{c}(tu_{n})
, we have

\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\geq\lambda_{c}(tv_{n})\geq\frac{C_{1}t^{\beta-\alpha}-\frac{K(tv_{n})+c}{t^{\alpha}}}{I_{2}(v_{n})},\quad\forall t>0.

Chosing
t>0
such that
C_{1}t^{\beta-\alpha}-ct^{-\alpha}>0
and using the fact that
K(tv_{n})\to 0
and
I_{2}(v_{n})\to 0
, we find that
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
.
If now
(F2)
holds and
c<0
then, by
(F3)
, we have
\liminf\frac{I_{1}(u_{n})}{\|u_{n}\|^{\alpha}}>0
, so that

\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\geq\frac{I_{1}(u_{n})}{I_{2}(u_{n})}=\frac{I_{1}(u_{n})}{\|u_{n}\|^{\alpha}I_{2}(v_{n})}\to\infty.

Thus
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
, so
\lambda_{c}
is coercive on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
. Let us show that it is also bounded from below therein.
Assume by contradiction that
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
and
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to-\infty
. We know that
(u_{n})
is bounded, so that up to a subsequence
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
X
. Since
(F3)
implies that
(I_{1}(u_{n}))
is bounded from below, we must have
I_{2}(u_{n})\to 0
, i.e.
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
, which contradicts the previous item. Therefore
\lambda_{c}
is bounded from below on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
.

(3)

If
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
with
(t_{c}(u_{n}))
bounded, then
t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
, which contradicts (1). Thus
t_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
, and arguing as in the previous item we see that
\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
.
Now, if
\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
then, by (1) we can not have
t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
. Since
(I_{1}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n}))
is bounded from below if
(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})
is bounded, we infer that
(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})
is unbounded, i.e.
t_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
. Lemma
3.1
yields that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
.

(4)

First of all observe that since
I_{2}
is weakly continuous, it is bounded on bounded sets of
X
. Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
be a Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
. By (2) we know that
(u_{n})
is bounded, so that up to a subsequence
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\neq 0
in
X
. From (
1.3
) we know that
\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}^{\prime}(u_{n})=I_{2}(u_{n})\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(u_{n})
, and since
(I_{2}(u_{n}))
is bounded we deduce that
\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
. Thus, from the boundedness of
(\lambda_{c}(u_{n}))
and the complete continuity of
I_{2}^{\prime}
and
K^{\prime}
, we obtain that
J^{\prime}(u_{n})(u_{n}-u)\to 0
, and consequently

\left(J^{\prime}(u_{n})-J^{\prime}(u)\right)(u_{n}-u)\to 0
. Using the inequality
\left(J^{\prime}(u)-J^{\prime}(v)\right)(u-v)\geq C_{2}(\|u\|^{\eta-1}-\|v\|^{\eta-1})(\|u\|-\|v\|)
we find that
\|u_{n}\|\to\|u\|
, and the uniform convexity of
X
provides the desired conclusion.

∎

Remark 3.6
.

Under
(F3)
assume in addition that the map
u\mapsto J^{\prime}(u)u-\alpha J(u)
is weakly continuous. Since
K^{\prime}
is completely continuous, it follows that
K
and
u\mapsto K^{\prime}(u)u
are weakly continuous, and so is
H
. Note that if
J
is
\alpha
-homogeneous then
J^{\prime}(u)u-\alpha J(u)=0
for every
u
, so in this case
H
is weakly continuous under
(F3)
.

4.
Applications of Theorem
2.3
to some boundary value problems

Next we apply Theorem
2.3
(more precisely Corollary
2.5
) to the prescribed energy problem associated to some boundary value problems. First we deal with some cases where

H
is weakly semicontinuous.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
c>0
(respect.
c<0
) and
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy

(f1)

|f(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{r-1})
for some
C>0
and
1<r<2^{*}
, and every
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(f2)

s\mapsto G(s):=sf(s)-2F(s)
is increasing (respect. decreasing) in
(0,\infty)
, and decreasing (respect. increasing) in
(-\infty,0)
, and
\displaystyle\lim_{|s|\to\infty}G(s)=\infty
(respect.
-\infty
).

Then:

(1)

The problem (
1.5
) has a nontrivial solution
u_{1,c}
having energy
c
for
\lambda=\lambda_{1,c}
, and has no such solution for
\lambda<\lambda_{1,c}
.

(2)

If in addition
f
is odd, then we can choose
u_{1,c}
to be nonnegative, and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the problem (
1.5
) has a pair of nontrivial solutions
\pm u_{n,c}
having energy
c
for
\lambda=\lambda_{n,c}
. Moreover
\lambda_{n,c}\nearrow\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
I_{1},I_{2}
be given by
I_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}F(u)
and
I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}
for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Then
I_{2}
is
2
-homogeneous and
I_{2}^{\prime}
clearly is completely continuous. Moreover
I_{1}
satisfies
(F3)
with
J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}
and
K(u)=\int_{\Omega}F(u)
. Note in particular that
H(u)=-\int_{\Omega}G(u)
satisfies
(F1)
(respect.
(F2)
) if
c>0
(respect.
c<0
), and it is weakly continuous by Remark
3.6
.
∎

The condition
(f2)
above can be replaced by the following one, which is standard when applying the Nehari manifold method to (
1.5
), cf.
[
15
, Theorem 16]
:

(f2’)

s\mapsto\frac{f(s)}{|s|}
is increasing (respect. decreasing) in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\displaystyle\lim_{|s|\to\infty}\frac{f(s)}{s}=\infty
(respect.
-\infty
).

Indeed, this condition is slightly stronger than
(f2)
, see e.g.
[
4
,
9
]
.
We deduce then the following result:

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
c>0
(respect.
c<0
) and
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy (f1) and (f2’). Then the conclusions of Corollary
4.1
remain valid.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that
(f2^{\prime})
implies that
\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0^{\pm}}\frac{f(s)}{s}<\infty
(respect.
>-\infty
). So at zero
f
may be superlinear (
\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{f(s)}{s}=0
), asymptotically linear (
\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{f(s)}{s}=l\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
), or satisfy
\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{f(s)}{s}=-\infty
(respect.
\infty
).

Remark 4.4
.

Corollary
4.1
has a natural extension to the
p
-Laplacian problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u=\lambda|u|^{p-2}u+f(u)&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

if we assume that
f
satisfies (f1) and (f2) with
2
replaced by
p
, and

\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}-\frac{\lambda}{p}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p}-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\quad u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega).

We may also deduce the analogue of Corollary
4.2
, assuming now (f2’) with

\frac{f(s)}{|s|}
replaced by
\frac{f(s)}{|s|^{p-1}}
and
\frac{f(s)}{s}
by
\frac{f(s)}{|s|^{p-2}s}
.

Let us consider now the problem

(4.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda|u|^{q-2}u+f(u)&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

with
1<q<2
and
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
being subcritical and superlinear, in which case the problem has a concave-convex nature.

We have now
I_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}F(u)
and
I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{q}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{q}
for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, so that

(4.2)

\lambda_{c}(u)=\frac{\frac{q}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-q\int_{\Omega}F(u)-qc}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{q}},

for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
.
Thus
I_{2}
is now
q
-homogeneous, and

H(u)=\frac{2-q}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\left(qF(u)-f(u)u\right).

Thus
H
is weakly lower semicontinuous.

We shall assume that

(f3)

t\mapsto\frac{(q-1)f(t)}{t}-f^{\prime}(t)
is decreasing in
(0,\infty)
and increasing in
(-\infty,0)
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\left[\frac{(q-1)f(t)}{t}-f^{\prime}(t)\right]=-\infty.

This condition is clearly satisfied by
f(t)=|t|^{p-2}t
, as well as
f(t)=|t|^{p-2}t\ln(|t|+1)
and
f(t)=\frac{|t|^{p-1}t}{|t|+1}
with
p\in(2,2^{*})
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
c>0
,
1<q<2
, and
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{\prime}(0)=f(0)=0
. Assume in addition (f1) and (f3). Then the conclusions of Corollary
4.1
remain valid for (
4.1
).

Proof.

Let us start showing that
(F1)
is satisfied. We have

H(tu)=\frac{2-q}{q}t^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\left(qF(tu)-f(tu)tu\right),

so

\frac{d}{dt}H(tu)=t\left[\frac{(2-q)2}{q}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{(q-1)f(tu)tu-f^{\prime}(tu)(tu)^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\right].

Note also that

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{(q-1)f(tu)tu-f^{\prime}(tu)(tu)^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)=\int_{u\neq 0}\left((q-1)\frac{f(tu)}{tu}-f^{\prime}(tu)\right)u^{2}.

Moreover, from
f^{\prime}(0)=f(0)=0
we have
\frac{f(s)}{s}\to 0
as
s\to 0
.
By (f3) we infer that the map
t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{(q-1)f(tu)tu-f^{\prime}(tu)(tu)^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)
is decreasing and goes to
0
(respect
-\infty
) as
t\to 0
(respect.
t\to\infty
). Finally, since (f3) yields that
\frac{qF(s)-f(s)s}{s^{2}}\to-\infty
as
|s|\to\infty
, we deduce that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{qF(tu)-f(tu)tu}{t^{2}}\to-\infty\quad\mbox{as}\quad t\to\infty

uniformly on weaky compact subsets of
X\setminus\{0\}
, i.e.
(F_{1})
is satisfied. Note also that
(F3)
holds with
J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}
and
K(u)=\int_{\Omega}F(u)
.
∎

4.1.
Some examples with

Instead of
(F3)

(F3’)

,

, and any

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let

and assume that

is
\alpha
-homogeneous and weakly continuous, and
I_{1}
satisfies
(F1)
and
(F3^{\prime})
.

(1)

If
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})

or

in

, then
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
. In particular,

\lambda_{c}
is coercive and bounded from below on

.

(2)

Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
. Then
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
if and only if
\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
.
In particular
\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H3).

Proof.

(1)

Indeed, repeating the proof of Lemma
3.5
we see that if
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
and
\|u_{n}\|\to\infty
then
v_{n}=\frac{u_{n}}{\|u_{n}\|}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.1
and
(F3^{\prime})
, we have

\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\geq\lambda_{c}(tv_{n})\geq\frac{C_{1}t^{\beta}-c}{t^{\alpha}I_{2}(v_{n})},\quad\forall t>0.

Chosing
t>0
such that
C_{1}t^{\beta}-c>0
and letting
n\to\infty
we obtain
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
. If now
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
then
u_{n}\not\to 0
in
X
, and thus

\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\geq\lambda_{c}(tu_{n})\geq\frac{C_{1}t^{\beta}\|u_{n}\|^{\beta}-c}{t^{\alpha}I_{2}(u_{n})}\geq\frac{C_{2}t^{\beta}-c}{t^{\alpha}I_{2}(u_{n})},\quad\forall t>0,

which yields again
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
.

(2)

The latter argument in the previous item shows that if
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
and
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
then
\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
. Finally, if
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\neq 0
in
X
then
(t_{c}(u_{n}))
is bounded (by Lemma
3.1
) and away from zero, so we can assume that
t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n}\rightharpoonup t_{0}u\neq 0
in
X
. Since
I_{1}
is bounded on bounded sets and
I_{2}
is weakly continuous, we infer that

\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})=\frac{I_{1}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})-c}{I_{2}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})}

remains bounded.

∎

Let us consider two problems where
c>0
and
H
is not weakly lower semicontinuous, so that Theorem
2.3
does not apply.

A generalized
(p,q)
-Laplacian problem

Our first application deals with the functional

\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{p}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u|^{p})-\frac{\lambda}{r}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r},\quad u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega),

where
1<r<p^{*}
. Here
A(t):=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}a(s)ds
for
t\geq 0
, and
a:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a
C^{1}
function satisfying the following conditions:

(A1)

k_{0}\left(1+t^{\frac{q-p}{p}}\right)\leq a(t)\leq k_{1}\left(1+t^{\frac{q-p}{p}}\right)
for every
t>0
, and some constants
k_{0},k_{1}>0
and
1<q\leq p
such that
k_{1}<\frac{r}{p}k_{0}
.

(A2)

t\mapsto a(t^{p})t^{p}-\frac{r}{p}A(t^{p})
is decreasing in
(0,\infty)
.

(A3)

t\mapsto A(t^{p})
is convex in
(0,\infty)
.

This choice of
\Phi_{\lambda}
corresponds to the quasilinear problem

(4.3)

-div\left(a(|\nabla u|^{p})|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)=\lambda|u|^{r-2}u,\quad u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega),

which is a generalized
(p,q)
-Laplacian problem, as it becomes

-\Delta_{p}u-\Delta_{q}u=\lambda|u|^{r-2}u,\quad u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega),

if we choose
a(t)=1+t^{\frac{q-p}{p}}
. In this case (A1) and (A2) hold with
k_{0}=k_{1}=1
, and
r>p
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Under the previous conditions, let
c>0
.
Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the problem (
4.3
) has a pair of nontrivial solutions
\pm u_{n,c}
having energy
c
for
\lambda=\lambda_{n,c}
. Moreover, for
\lambda=\lambda_{1,c}
the solution
u_{1,c}
can be chosen nonnegative. Finally, (
4.3
) has no solution having energy
c
for
\lambda<\lambda_{1,c}
.

Proof.

We take now
I_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u|^{p})
and
I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}
for
u\in X=W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, so that
\alpha=r
. Then
H(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(a(|\nabla u|^{p})|\nabla u|^{p}-\frac{r}{p}A(|\nabla u|^{p})\right)
, and by (A2)
the map
t\mapsto H(tu)
is decreasing in
(0,\infty)
. Moreover
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}H(tu)=-\infty
uniformly on weakly compact subsets of
X\setminus\{0\}
. Indeed,
note that (A1) yields that

a(s^{p})s^{p}-\frac{r}{p}A(s^{p})\leq k_{1}(1+s^{q-p})s^{p}-\frac{r}{p}k_{0}\left(s^{p}+\frac{p}{q}s^{q}\right)=\left(k_{1}-\frac{r}{p}k_{0}\right)s^{p}+\left(k_{1}-\frac{r}{q}k_{0}\right)s^{q}.

Thus

H(tu)\leq\left(k_{1}-\frac{r}{p}k_{0}\right)t^{p}\|u\|^{p}+\left(k_{1}-\frac{r}{q}k_{0}\right)t^{q}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{q}.

Since
k_{1}-\frac{r}{p}k_{0}<0
and
q\leq p
, we obtain the desired conclusion. Lemma
3.1
yields that
\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H1).
Note also that
I_{1}
is weakly lower semicontinuous by
(A3)
, and by
(A1)
it is bounded on bounded sets, and satisfies

I_{1}(u)\geq k_{0}\|u\|^{p}
for any
u\in X
. Thus (F3’) is satisfied, and by Lemma
4.6
we infer that
\lambda_{c}
is bounded from below on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
and satisfies (H3).

Finally, if
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
is a Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
, then arguing as in Lemma
3.5
(4) we find that
(u_{n})
is bounded and
\Phi_{\lambda_{c}(u_{n})}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
. By Lemma
4.6
we know that
u_{n}\not\rightharpoonup 0
. The complete continuity of
I_{2}
yields that
I_{1}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
. The strict convexity of
t\mapsto A(t^{p})
implies that
I_{1}^{\prime}
belongs to the class
(S_{+})
cf.
[
3
, Lemma 2.7]
, and consequently
(u_{n})
has a convergent subsequence in
X\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore
\lambda_{c}
satisfies the Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
, and Theorem
2.1
yields the conclusion.
∎

The Brezis-Nirenberg problem

We consider now the problem (
1.5
) with
f(s)=|s|^{2^{*}-1}s
, so that
F(s)=\frac{1}{2^{*}}|s|^{2^{*}}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\lambda_{c}
be given by (
4.2
), i.e.

\lambda_{c}(u)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2^{*}}-2c}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}},

for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

H(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(2F(u)-f(u)u\right)=\frac{2-2^{*}}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2^{*}}=-\frac{2}{N}\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}},

so that

(4.4)

\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})=\{u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega):\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}}=Nc\}.

Thus for any
u\in X\setminus\{0\}
the map
t\mapsto H(tu)
is decreasing in
(0,\infty)
. Moreover, since
H
is weakly upper semicontinuous, we see that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}H(tu)=-\infty
uniformly on weakly compact subsets of
X\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore
(F1)
holds, and Lemma
3.1
implies that
\lambda_{c}
satisfies (H1).

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
c>0
. Then:

(1)

\lambda_{c}
is coercive on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
.

(2)

Assume in addition
c<\frac{S^{\frac{N}{2}}}{N}
, where
S
is the best constant of the embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\subset L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
. If
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
with
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
, then
\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\to\infty
. In particular
\lambda_{c}
is bounded from below on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
. Moreover, for any
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
we have
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
if and only if
\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
.

Proof.

(1)

From (
4.4
) we have

\lambda_{c}(u)=\frac{\|u\|^{2}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}}-2c}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\|u\|^{2}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}Nc-2c}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c}).

Since
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
we deduce that
\lambda_{c}
is coercive therein.

(2)

Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
with
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}(u_{n})\geq\lambda_{c}(tu_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|tu_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}\|tu_{n}\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}}-2c}{\|tu_{n}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\|u_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}Nct^{2^{*}-2}-2ct^{-2}}{\|u_{n}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{S^{-1}(Nc)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}Nct^{2^{*}-2}-2ct^{-2}}{\|u_{n}\|_{2}^{2}}=t^{-2}\frac{j(t)}{\|u_{n}\|_{2}^{2}},

where
j(t):=S^{-1}(Nc)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}t^{2}-\frac{2}{2^{*}}Nct^{2^{*}}-2c
. Since
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
it suffices to have
\displaystyle\max_{t>0}j(t)>0
to obtain the desired conclusion. A simple computation shows that

\max_{t>0}j(t)=\frac{2^{*}-2}{2^{*}}S^{\frac{2^{*}}{2^{*}-2}}-2c,

so that
\displaystyle\max_{t>0}j(t)>0
if and only if
c<\frac{S^{\frac{N}{2}}}{N}
.

Let now
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
. The latter argument shows that
\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})\to\infty
if
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
X
. Finally, if
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\neq 0
then Lemma
3.1
yields that
(t_{c}(u_{n}))
is bounded. Moreover, we know that
(t_{c}(u_{n}))
is away from zero, so

\lambda_{c}(t_{c}(u_{n})u_{n})=\frac{1-\frac{2}{2^{*}}Nct_{c}(u_{n})^{2^{*}-2}-2ct_{c}(u_{n})^{-2}}{\|u_{n}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq C,

for some
C>0
.

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
c<\frac{S^{\frac{N}{2}}}{N}
. Then
\lambda_{c}
satisfies the Palais-Smale condition on
\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
at any level
d\in\mathbb{R}
. As a consequence, the conclusions of Corollary
4.1
remain valid.

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{N}(\lambda_{c})
be a Palais-Smale sequence for
\lambda_{c}
. Since
(u_{n})
is bounded in
L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
we see that
I_{2}(u_{n})
and
I_{2}^{\prime}(u_{n})
are bounded if
I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence by Lemma
3.2
we deduce that
(u_{n})
is a Palais-Smale sequence for
\Phi_{d}
at the level
c
. Since
\Phi_{d}
satisfies the Palais-Smale condition for
c<\frac{S^{\frac{N}{2}}}{N}
(cf.
[
2
]
) we infer that
(u_{n})
has a convergent subsequence in
X\setminus\{0\}
. Thus we can apply Theorem
2.1
to get the desired conclusion.

∎

5.
A problem involving the affine
p
-Laplacian

Let us now deal with the problem (
1.7
). We shall apply Theorem
2.1
to the functional

\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)=\frac{1}{p}{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}(u)-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\quad u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\},

where
{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}
is given by (
1.6
).
We set

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}:\,\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(u)u=0\}

and

\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega):\,{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)=1\}.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

|f(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{r-1})
for some
C>0
and
1<r<p^{*}
, and every
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

f(s)=o(|s|^{p-1})
as
s\to 0
.

(3)

\frac{f(s)}{|s|^{p-1}}
is strictly increasing in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

(4)

\frac{F(s)}{|s|^{p}}\to\infty
as
|s|\to\infty
.

Then the functional
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
has a ground state level, which is achieved by a ground state solution
u_{0}
of (
1.7
). If, in addition,
f
is odd, then we can choose
u_{0}\geq 0
and
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
has infinitely many pairs of critical points, which are solutions of (
1.7
).

The proof of Theorem
5.1
follows from the next lemmas:

Lemma 5.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
the following assertions hold:

(1)

For any
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
the map
t\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(tu)
has exactly one critical point
t(u)
, which is a global maximum point. Moreover, the map
u\mapsto t(u)
is
(-1)
-homogeneous, bounded from below by a positive constant in
\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
, and bounded from above in any compact set
W\subset\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

(2)

The affine
p
-energy
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}
is bounded from below by a positive constant in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
. In particular
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
is away from zero in
L^{q}(\Omega)
for any
1<q<p^{*}
.

Proof.

(1)

The first assertion follows from the
p
-homogeinety of
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}(u)
and the conditions on
f
. Let us show that
t(su)=s^{-1}t(u)
for any
u\neq 0
and
s>0
. Indeed, writing
\phi_{u}(t)=\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(tu)
we see that
\phi_{su}^{\prime}(t)=s\phi_{u}^{\prime}(st)
, which yields that
st(su)=t(u)
, as desired. Finally, if
u\in\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
then

t(u)u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
provides us with

t(u)^{p-1}=\int_{\Omega}f(t(u)u)u,

which combined with the behavior of
f
at zero and infinity yields the final assertions.

(2)

By the previous item we know that

t(u)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}t\left(\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}\right).

Thus, for
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
there holds

1=t(u)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}t\left(\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}\right)
, i.e.
{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)=t\left(\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}\right)
and since
\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}\in\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
the previous item yields the first assertion. The second assertion follows from the previous one and the equality

{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}(u)=\int_{\Omega}f(u)u

for
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
the functional
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
satisfies (H1) and (H2).

Proof.

Let us first deal with (H1). From the
p
-homogeinety of
{\mathcal{E}}^{p}_{p,\Omega}(u)
and the conditions on
f
it is clear that for any
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
the map
t\mapsto\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(tu)
has exactly one critical point
t(u)
, which is a global maximum point. It remains to show that the map
u\mapsto t(u)
is bounded from below by a positive constant in
\mathcal{S}
, and bounded from above in any compact set
W_{1}\subset\mathcal{S}
. Indeed, by Lemma
5.2
there exists
\delta>0
such that
t(u)\geq\delta
for all
u\in\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Since
{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)\leq 1
for any
u\in\mathcal{S}
we find that

(5.1)

t(u)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}t\left(\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}\right)\geq t\left(\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}\right)\geq\delta

for any
u\in\mathcal{S}
. Now, let
W_{1}\subset\mathcal{S}
be a compact set. We set
W_{2}:=\left\{\frac{u}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)}:u\in W_{1}\right\}
, so that
W_{2}\subset\mathcal{S}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
is a compact set as well, and by Lemma
5.2
we know that
u\mapsto t(u)
is bounded from above in
W_{2}
. Since
W_{1}\subset\mathcal{S}
is a compact set, there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u)\geq\delta_{1}
in
W_{1}
. Thus (
5.1
) shows that there exists
\delta_{2}>0
such that
t(u)\leq\delta_{2}
for all
u\in W_{1}
.

Next we prove (H2). It is clear that
\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}
is even and positive on
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Let
(u_{k})
be a sequence in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
(\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{k}))
is bounded and
\|\Phi^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{k})\|_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0
.
We claim that
({\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u_{k}))
is bounded. Otherwise
{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u_{k})\rightarrow\infty
, and we set
v_{k}:=\frac{u_{k}}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u_{k})}
. Thus, up to a subsequence, we have
v_{k}\rightarrow v
strongly in
L^{q}(\Omega)
, for
1\leq q<p^{*}
. Suppose
v=0
. Since
u_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
we have
u_{k}=t(v_{k})v_{k}
. Then for each
t>0
, we obtain

d\geq\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{k})=\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(t(v_{k})v_{k})\geq\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(tv_{k})\geq\frac{t^{p}}{p}-\int_{\Omega}F(tv_{k})\rightarrow\frac{t^{p}}{p}.

This yields a contradiction upon choosing
t>(dp)^{1/p}
. So
v\neq 0
and hence

0\leq\frac{\Phi_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{k})}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}^{p}(u_{k})}\leq\frac{1}{p}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{F({\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u_{k})v_{k})}{{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}^{p}(u_{k})}\rightarrow-\infty

as
k\rightarrow\infty
by assumption (4), and we reach a contradiction again. It follows that
({\mathcal{E}}_{p,\Omega}(u_{k}))
is
bounded, so that by the affine Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem (see Theorem 6.5.3 in
[
16
]
) we can assume that
u_{k}\rightarrow u
in
L^{q}(\Omega)
, for
1\leq q<p^{*}
.
By Lemma
5.2
(2) we know that
u\neq 0
. Hence, by Corollary 2.1 of
[
6
]
,
(u_{k})
is bounded in
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, so that
u_{k}\rightharpoonup u\neq 0
in
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence. Moreover,
\|\Phi^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{k})\|_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0
clearly implies

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\,\Phi^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{k})(u_{k}-u)=0,

which by standard arguments yields

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\,\langle\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{p}u_{k},u_{k}-u\rangle=0.

Finally, by Theorem 2 of
[
7
]
,
u_{k}\to u
in
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, and the proof is complete.

(2)

Lemma 6.3

\Phi
