Cauchy-type identities through collocation matrices
P. Díaz, E. Mainar

Abstract
We present a broader framework for the Cauchy identity derived from the determinant expansion of collocation matrices. This approach yields an infinite family of identities, where the original Cauchy identity stands as a particular case. To illustrate the versatility and depth of this approach, we provide a range of compelling examples, showcasing the connections and applications of these novel identities.



Keywords:

Cauchy identity, Collocation matrices, Schur functions

1
Introduction

The celebrated Cauchy identity is expressed as:

\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(x)s_{\lambda}(y),

where
s_{\lambda}(x)
and
s_{\lambda}(y)
denote Schur functions indexed by the partition
\lambda
, and
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)
are sets of variables.
This identity reveals deep connections between seemingly disparate entities, such as determinants, symmetric polynomials, and partition functions. Rigorous algebraic proofs
of the identity can be found in classical references such as
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
.

Among its many interpretations, one of the most illuminating stems from representation theory.
The Cauchy identity can be understood as a result on the characters of the General Linear group (see, for instance,
[
3
]
, chapter 38), which makes it a fundamental piece in algebraic combinatorics.

The Cauchy identity has inspired numerous generalizations. Extensions exist for characters of Orthogonal and Symplectic groups
[
5
]
, double-Schur polynomials
[
6
]
, Hall-Littlewood polynomials
[
4
]
, and

q
-deformations
[
7
]
, among others. Each of these adaptations reveals new layers of insight into the interplay between algebraic structures.

In this paper, we propose a unified framework that encompasses the Cauchy identity and its variations.
By analyzing the determinant expansions of a family of collocation matrices, we derive an infinite class of identities, with the classical Cauchy identity emerging as a special case.

2
Collocation matrices and Cauchy-type identities

Theorem 1
.

Let
g\in C^{\infty}
, and call
R
the radius of convergence of the McLaurin series of
g
. Consider the
n
-tuplas
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
A=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
, such that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
and
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Let
G=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
, where
g_{k}(x):=g(a_{k}x)
, defined on
x\in(-R/a_{k},R/a_{k})
. Let us call
M_{G}(X)
the collocation matrix of the system
G
on the
n
-tupla
X
,

M_{G}(X):=\big(g(a_{j}x_{i})\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

For
x_{k}\in(-R/a_{max},R/a_{max})
, where
a_{max}=\max\{|a_{1}|,\dots,|a_{n}|\}
, we have

\sum_{\lambda}\frac{G_{\lambda}}{C_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det M_{G}(X)}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|},

(1)

where, for any partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
,
s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is the corresponding Schur polynomial, and

G_{\lambda}:=\prod_{l=1}^{n}g^{(\lambda_{l}+n-l)}(0),\qquad C_{\lambda}:=\prod_{l=1}^{n}(\lambda_{l}+n-l)!.

Proof.

For
x_{k}\in(-R/a_{max},R/a_{max})
we can identify
g(x)
with its McLaurin series. Thus,

\displaystyle\det M_{G}(X)=\det\big(g(a_{j}x_{i})\big)=\det\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!}(a_{j}x_{i})^{k}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{{}^{(1)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\det\Big(\frac{g^{(k_{j})}(0)}{k_{j}!}(a_{j}x_{i})^{k_{j}}\Big)=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(k_{l})}(0)}{k_{l}!}a_{l}^{k_{l}}\bigg]\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{{}^{(2)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}>\cdots>k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(k_{\sigma(l)})}(0)}{k_{\sigma(l)}!}a_{l}^{k_{\sigma(l)}}\bigg]\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{\sigma(j)}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{{}^{(3)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}>\cdots>k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(k_{l})}(0)}{k_{l}!}a_{l}^{k_{\sigma(l)}}\bigg]\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big)\text{sgn}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{{}^{(4)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}>\cdots>k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(k_{l})}(0)}{k_{l}!}\bigg]\det\Big(a_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big)\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{{}^{(5)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\\
l(\lambda)\leq n\end{subarray}}\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(\lambda_{l}+n-l)}(0)}{(\lambda_{l}+n-l)!}\bigg]\det\Big(a_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+n-j}\Big)\det\Big(x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+n-j}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{{}^{(6)}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|\sum_{\lambda}\frac{G_{\lambda}}{C_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),

where, on the indicated equalities, we have used the following facts.

(1)

We used the letters

k_{1},\dots,k_{n}

to represent the non-negative integers specifying the degree of the terms in the McLaurin expansion for each of the
n
columns. The sum on the variables
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
of the determinants follows from the application of usual properties of determinants.

(2)

Considering
\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big)
, it becomes evident that any term in the sum where
k_{l}=k_{m}
for
m,l=1,\dots,n
with
l\neq m
makes no contribution. Therefore, the summation over
n
-tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})
reduces to a summation over tuples with distinct entries. Consequently, we can decompose the sum into two parts: the sum over ordered sequences
k_{1}>\cdots>k_{n}
and the sum over all permutations of the elements of each sequence, expressed as
(k_{\sigma(1)},k_{\sigma(2)},\dots,k_{\sigma(n)})
, where
\sigma
belongs to
S_{n}
, the permutation group of
n
elements.

(3)

Be aware that, for
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we have

\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(k_{\sigma(l)})}(0)}{k_{\sigma(l)}!}=\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{g^{(k_{l})}(0)}{k_{l}!}\quad\text{ and }\quad\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{\sigma(j)}}\Big)=\det\Big(x_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big)\text{sgn}(\sigma),

where
sgn(\sigma)
denotes the signature of the permutation
\sigma
, taking the value
+1
if
\sigma
is even and
-1
if
\sigma
is odd.

(4)

Note that

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{l=1}^{n}a_{l}^{k_{\sigma(l)}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)=\det\Big(a_{i}^{k_{j}}\Big).

(5)

After performing the substitution

k_{l}=\lambda_{l}+n-l,\quad l=1,\dots,n,

each sequence
k_{1}>\cdots>k_{n}
is transformed into a partition with at most
n
parts,
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
. It is straightforward to observe that summing over all sequences
k_{1}>\cdots>k_{n}
is equivalent to summing over all partitions
\lambda
with
l(\lambda)\leq n
.

(6)

We have applied Jacobi’s bialternant formula:

s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det\Big(x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+n-j}\Big)}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}|}.

Let us observe that the restriction
l(\lambda)\leq n
is unnecessary in the summation, since Schur functions are zero whenever
l(\lambda)>n
.

Remarks.
.

As a first consistency check, observe that any permutation of the variables
(a_{\sigma(1)},\dots,a_{\sigma(n)})
or
(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})
leaves both sides of (
1
) invariant. Moreover, note that the evident symmetry under the exchange
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\leftrightarrow(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
on the LHS of (
1
) is mirrored on the RHS of (
1
). This is because, under such exchange, the collocation matrix
M_{G}(X)
is transposed, leaving its determinant unchanged.

Finally, observe that a function
g
uniquely determines both the left-hand side of (
1
), through the coefficients
G_{\lambda}
, and the right-hand side, via the collocation matrix
M_{G}(X)
. Consequently, each analytic function
g
gives rise to a distinct Cauchy-type identity. This result highlights the versatility of Theorem
1
, which will be further demonstrated in the following illustrative examples.

Example 1
.

For a single variable
x_{1}
, and
a_{1}=1
, equation (
1
) is just the McLaurin expansion of
g(x)
. Notice that for this case, the partitions with one part,
l(\lambda)=1
, are the only ones which contribute to the sum. Now, for
|\lambda|=m
, we have

C_{\lambda}=m!,\quad G_{\lambda}=g^{(m)}(0),\quad\det M_{G}(X)=g(x_{1}),

and the McLaurin series of
g
is recovered.

Example 2
.

For
g(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}
,
we have
G_{\lambda}=C_{\lambda}
, and

M_{G}(X)=\left(\frac{1}{1-a_{j}x_{i}}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

Applying (
1
), we recover the Cauchy identity

\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det\left(\frac{1}{1-a_{j}x_{i}}\right)}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}=\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-a_{j}x_{i}},

(2)

where (
2
) follows from an equivalent variant of the classic Cauchy determinant identity

\det\bigg(\frac{1}{x_{i}-y_{j}}\bigg)=\frac{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}}|}{\prod_{i,j}(x_{i}-y_{j})}.

Example 3
.

If
g(x)
is a polynomial of degree
m\geq n
, that is,

g(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x+\cdots+b_{m}x^{m},

then

M_{G}(X)=(P_{j}(x_{i}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

with

P_{j}(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}a_{j}x+\cdots+b_{m}a_{j}^{m}x^{m}.

We obtain a bounded sum since

G_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}C_{\lambda}B_{\lambda},&\text{ if }&\lambda_{1}\leq m-n+1,\\
0,&\text{ if }&\lambda_{1}>m+1-n,\end{array}\right.

with
B_{\lambda}=\prod_{l=1}^{n}b_{\lambda_{l}+n-l}.

Thus,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\\
\lambda_{1}\leq m-n+1\end{subarray}}B_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det(P_{j}(x_{i}))}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}.

Note that if
m<n
, we have
G_{\lambda}=0
, but also
\det(P_{j}(x_{i}))=0
, since the polynomials
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
are not linearly independent. Therefore, the identity holds trivially.

In the particular case where
b_{1}=\dots=b_{m}=1
, for which
B_{\lambda}=1
, we obtain bounded sums that correspond to finite truncations of the Cauchy identity (
2
).

As another particular polynomial case, let us consider the sequence of non-negative integers
\mu_{1}>\dots>\mu_{n}
, and

g(x)=x^{\mu_{1}}+x^{\mu_{2}}+\cdots+x^{\mu_{n}}.

In this case we have

G_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{\lambda},&\text{ if }\,\,\,\lambda=(\mu_{1}-n+1,\,\mu_{2}-n+2,\dots,\,\mu_{n}),\\
0,&\text{ otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

(3)

So, we obtain

s_{(\mu_{1}-n+1,\dots,\,\mu_{n})}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{(\mu_{1}-n+1,\dots,\,\mu_{n})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det(P_{j}(x_{i}))}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}.

(4)

The identity (
4
) can be directly checked by applying the Jacobi’s bialternant formula and basic properties of determinants. Namely,
\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)
, and
\det(A^{t})=\det(A)
.

Let us call
P
the set of integer partitions. We define

\displaystyle P_{n,\,even}:=\{\lambda\in P\mid\lambda_{l}+n-l\text{ is even for }l=1,\dots,n\},

\displaystyle P_{n,\,odd}:=\{\lambda\in P\mid\lambda_{l}+n-l\text{ is odd for }l=1,\dots,n\}.

These subsets of partitions are relevant since for even (odd) functions
g(x)
only the partitions belonging to
P_{n,\,even}
(
P_{n,\,odd}
) will contribute to the sum in (
1
).

Example 4
.

For

g(x)=\frac{1}{1-x^{2}},

we have

M_{G}(X)=\bigg(\frac{1}{1-a^{2}_{j}x^{2}_{i}}\bigg)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

and

G_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{\lambda},&\text{if }\lambda\in P_{n,\,even},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Applying (
1
), we obtain

\sum_{\lambda\in P_{n,\,even}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{i<j}(a_{i}+a_{j})(x_{i}+x_{j})\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-a^{2}_{i}x^{2}_{j}},

(5)

where the last equality follows from the Cauchy determinant identity, and the fact that

|V_{x^{2}_{1},\dots,x^{2}_{n}}|=|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}|\prod_{i<j}(x_{i}+x_{j}).

(6)

Example 5
.

For

g(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}
,
we have

M_{G}(X)=\bigg(\frac{1}{1+a^{2}_{j}x^{2}_{i}}\bigg)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

and

G_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-1)^{\frac{2|\lambda|-n(n-1)}{4}}C_{\lambda},&\text{ if }\lambda\in P_{n,\,even},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Applying (
1
), and taking into account (
6
), we obtain

\sum_{\lambda\in P_{n,\,even}}(-1)^{\frac{2|\lambda|-n(n-1)}{4}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\prod_{i<j}(a_{i}+a_{j})(x_{i}+x_{j})}{\prod_{i,j}(1+a^{2}_{i}x^{2}_{j})}.

(7)

Note that (
7
) can be obtained directly from (
5
) by the change
a_{k}\longrightarrow ia_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,n
.

Example 6
.

For

g(x)=e^{x}
,
we have

M_{G}(X)=(e^{a_{j}x_{i}})_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

and
G_{\lambda}=1
for all partitions. So, we can write

\sum_{\lambda}\frac{1}{C_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det(e^{a_{j}x_{i}})}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}.

Furthermore, if we consider

g(x)=\sinh(x)
,
we have

M_{G}(X)=(\sinh(a_{j}x_{i}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

with

G_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\text{ if }\lambda\in P_{n,\,odd},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then, we have:

\sum_{\lambda\in P_{n,\,odd}}\frac{1}{C_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det(\sinh(a_{j}x_{i}))}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}.

Example 7
.

For

g(x)=\sin(x)
,
we have

M_{G}(X)=(\sin(a_{j}x_{i}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

with

G_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(-1)^{\frac{2|\lambda|+n(n+1)}{4}},&\text{ if }\lambda\in P_{n,\,odd},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and the following Cauchy-type identity is derived

\sum_{\lambda\in P_{n,\,odd}}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{2|\lambda|+n(n+1)}{4}}}{C_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det(\sin(a_{j}x_{i}))}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}.

Example 8
.

For

g(x)=\ln(1-x)
,
we have

M_{G}(X)=\big(\ln(1-a_{j}x_{i})\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\\

with
G_{\lambda}=C_{\lambda}/P_{\lambda}
and
P_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{l(\lambda)}\lambda_{i}
. The following identity is obtained:

\sum_{\lambda}\frac{1}{P_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det(\ln(1-a_{j}x_{i}))}{|V_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}||V_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}|}

.
