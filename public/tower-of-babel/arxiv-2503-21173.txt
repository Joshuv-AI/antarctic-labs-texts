Introduction

Let
\displaystyle(R,\mathcal{M})
be a local ring over a field
\displaystyle k
where
\displaystyle k=R/\mathcal{M}
and
\displaystyle J
an ideal in
\displaystyle R
such that
\displaystyle A=R/J
is an Artinian Gorenstein (AG)
\displaystyle k
-algebra. One of the most important numerical invariants associated with an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra is its Hilbert function. Recall that the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle A
is by definition the Hilbert function of its
tangent cone

\displaystyle G({\mathfrak{m}})=\oplus_{n\geq 0}{\mathfrak{m}}^{n}/{\mathfrak{m}}^{n+1}
where
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathcal{M}/J.

Classifying the Hilbert function of AG
\displaystyle k
-algebras is a widely open problem in commutative algebra.
They have been characterized in certain cases, see
[
3
,
14
,
20
,
21
,
24
]
.
The purpose of this article is to understand the structure of the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle I,
namely, the Hilbert function of its
associated graded ring

\displaystyle G(I):=\oplus_{n\geq 0}I^{n}/I^{n+1}
for any ideal
\displaystyle I
in
\displaystyle A.
The associated graded ring
\displaystyle G(I)
plays an important role in algebraic geometry, in particular in resolution of singularities. There has been a huge research in the literature on studying the properties of
\displaystyle G(I)
in terms of the properties of
\displaystyle I
and a local ring
\displaystyle A
of any dimension (not just AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra), see
[
5
,
10
,
13
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
30
]
.

For a standard graded
\displaystyle k
-algebra
\displaystyle S=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}S_{i}
the Hilbert function
\displaystyle\HF:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
defined as
\displaystyle\HF(S)_{i}:=\ell_{S_{0}}(S_{i}).

It is well known that the Hilbert function of a
graded
AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra is symmetric.
This is no longer true with the Hilbert function of the local AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra.
However, A. Iarrobino in
[
16
]
developed a beautiful theory that shows the Hilbert function of an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra admits a symmetric decomposition, namely,

\HF(G({\mathfrak{m}}))_{i}=\sum_{a=0}^{j-2}H(a)_{i},

where
\displaystyle H(a)
is symmetric about
\displaystyle(j-a)/2.
Here
\displaystyle j
is the
socle degree
of
\displaystyle A,
that is the maximum integer such that
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}^{j}\neq 0.
This became a very powerful tool in classifying the Hilbert functions of local AG
\displaystyle k
-algebras (see for example
[
24
]
). Recently, there has been intense research on symmetric decomposition, for instance in
[
19
]
the authors have studied the possible vectors
\displaystyle H(a)
in a symmetric decomposition of the Hilbert function of AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra, in
[
18
]
the authors have studied its interaction with Jordan type.
Very recently, M. Wojtala has extended the symmetric decomposition theory to self-dual modules in
[
31
]
.

In Section 3, we introduce a symmetric decomposition of the Hilbert function of an arbitrary ideal in an AG local
\displaystyle k
-algebra. We prove that:

Theorem 1
.

(Theorem
3.3
)

Let
\displaystyle(A,{\mathfrak{m}})
be an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra, and
\displaystyle I
an ideal in
\displaystyle A
such that
\displaystyle I^{u}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle I^{u+1}=0
. Then the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle I

\HF(G(I))_{i}=\sum_{a=0}^{u}H(a)_{i},

where
\displaystyle H(a)
is symmetric about
\displaystyle(u-a)/2.

As a consequence, we recover a result by W. Heinzer, M.-K. Kim, and B. Ulrich in
[
12

which asserts that
\displaystyle G(I)
is Gorenstein if and only if the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle G(I)
is symmetric (Theorem

).

Our hope is that Theorem

will be useful in classifying the Hilbert function of an ideal in an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra. One of the difficulties in understanding the structure of the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle G(I)
is that, in this case
\displaystyle G(I)_{0}=A/I,
which is not a field unless
\displaystyle I={\mathfrak{m}}.

\displaystyle R_{0}

\displaystyle G(I)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle G(I)

\displaystyle I

One of the important properties of AG

-algebras is that one can associate a
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle b

2.1.
Properties of bilinear maps

Let
\displaystyle k
be a field and
\displaystyle V,W
be finite-dimensional
\displaystyle k
-vector spaces. Recall that a map
\displaystyle\varphi:V\times W\to k
is said to be bilinear if it satisfies the following properties: for any
\displaystyle x,y\in V,z,w\in W
and
\displaystyle\alpha\in k
,

(i)

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha(x+y),z)=\alpha\,\varphi(x,z)+\alpha\,\varphi(y,z)
;

(ii)

\displaystyle\varphi(x,\alpha(z+w))=\alpha\,\varphi(x,z)+\alpha\,\varphi(x,w)
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi:V\times W\to k
be a bilinear map. For a subset
\displaystyle S
of
\displaystyle V,
we define a subset
\displaystyle S^{\perp}
of
\displaystyle W
as

S^{\perp}:=\{w\in W\mid\varphi(v,w)=0~\textrm{for every}~v\in S\}.

Similarly, if
\displaystyle T
is a subset of
\displaystyle W,
then a subset
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)
of
\displaystyle V
is

\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T):=\{v\in V\mid\varphi(v,t)=0~\textrm{for all}~t\in T\}.

Note that both
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)
and
\displaystyle S^{\perp}
are subspaces of
\displaystyle V
and
\displaystyle W,
respectively. For subsets
\displaystyle S,O
of
\displaystyle V
recall that

S+O:=\{x+y:x\in S\mbox{ and }y\in O\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi:V\times W\to k
be a bilinear map and
\displaystyle S,O\subseteq V,
and
\displaystyle T,U\subseteq W
are subsets.
Then

(i)

If
\displaystyle T\subseteq U
, then
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(U)\subseteq\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)
.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle S\subseteq O
, then
\displaystyle O^{\perp}\subseteq S^{\perp}
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T+U)=\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)\cap\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(U).

(iv)

\displaystyle(S+O)^{\perp}=S^{\perp}\cap O^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Easy to check.
∎

Throughout this paper, for any
\displaystyle k
-vector space
\displaystyle V
, we set
\displaystyle V^{\widecheck{}}:=\mbox{\rm Hom}(V,k).
For a subset
\displaystyle S
of
\displaystyle V,
let

(0\colon_{V^{\widecheck{}}}S):=\{f\in{V^{\widecheck{}}}:f(t)=0\mbox{ for all }t\in S\}.

Note that
\displaystyle(0\colon_{V^{\widecheck{}}}S)
is a subspace of
\displaystyle{V^{\widecheck{}}}.

We denote the
\displaystyle k
-vector space dimension of
\displaystyle V
by
\displaystyle\dim V.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle V,W
be finite dimensional vector spaces of the same dimension, and
\displaystyle S\subseteq V,T\subseteq W
are subspaces. Let
\displaystyle\varphi:V\times W\to k
be a bilinear map.

(i)

If the
\displaystyle k
-linear map
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}:V\to\mbox{\rm Hom}(W,k)
induced by
\displaystyle\varphi
is injective, then

(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T)\cong\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T).

Moreover,
\displaystyle\dim\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)=\dim W-\dim T.

(ii)

If the
\displaystyle k
-linear map
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}:W\to\mbox{\rm Hom}(V,k)
induced by
\displaystyle\varphi
is injective, then

(0\colon_{V^{\widecheck{}}}S)\cong S^{\perp}.

Moreover,
\displaystyle\dim S^{\perp}=\dim V-\dim S.

(iii)

If
\displaystyle\varphi
is non-degenerate, then

\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp})=S,\,(\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}T)^{\perp}=T.

(iv)

Assume
\displaystyle\varphi
is non-degenerate. Let
\displaystyle S,O
be subspaces of
\displaystyle V
and
\displaystyle T,U
be subspaces of
\displaystyle W
. Then

(S\cap O)^{\perp}=S^{\perp}+O^{\perp},\,\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T\cap U)=\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)+\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(U).

Proof.

(i) Since
\displaystyle\dim V=\dim W
,
\displaystyle\dim V=\dim W^{\widecheck{}}.
Therefore, if

\varphi^{\prime}:V\to\mbox{\rm Hom}(W,k)=W^{\widecheck{}}

induced by
\displaystyle\varphi
is injective, then
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}
is surjective as well. Hence
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}
is an isomorphism.

Let
\displaystyle\psi
be the restriction map of
\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}
to
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)
, that is
\displaystyle\psi:\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)\to W^{\widecheck{}}
is defined as
\displaystyle\psi(v)=\phi^{\prime}(v).
Note that for
\displaystyle v\in\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)
,

\phi^{\prime}(v)(t)=\phi(v,t)=0\mbox{ for all }t\in T.

Hence
\displaystyle\psi(v)=\phi^{\prime}(v)\in(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T).
Therefore
\displaystyle\im(\psi)\subseteq(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T)
.
Clearly, as
\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}
is injective,
\displaystyle\psi
is injective. Also, for any
\displaystyle f\in(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T),
surjectivity of
\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}
implies that there exists
\displaystyle v\in V
such that
\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(v)=f.
Then
\displaystyle v\in\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)
because

\phi(v,t)=\phi^{\prime}(v)(t)=f(t)=0\mbox{ for all }t\in T.

Thus
\displaystyle\psi(v)=\phi^{\prime}(v)=f.
Hence
\displaystyle\im(\psi)=(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T)
. Thus we get
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)\cong(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T).

In order to compute the dimension of
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T),
first observe that
\displaystyle(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T)\simeq(W/T)^{\widecheck{}}
. Hence

\dim\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)=\dim(0\colon_{W^{\widecheck{}}}T)=\dim(W/T)^{\widecheck{}}=\dim W/T=\dim W-\dim T.

(ii) Proof is similar to (i).

(iii)
We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dim\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dim W-\dim S^{\perp}\hskip 62.59596pt\mbox{ (from (i))}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dim W-(\dim V-\dim S)\hskip 25.6073pt\mbox{(from (ii))}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dim S\hskip 113.81102pt\mbox{(since $\displaystyle\dim W=\dim V$)}.

Since
\displaystyle S\subseteq\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp})
, we obtain that
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp})=S.

Similarly,
\displaystyle(\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}T)^{\perp}=T
is proved.

(iv) We have

\begin{split}\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp}+O^{\perp})&=\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp})\cap\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(O^{\perp})\quad(\text{by Proposition }\ref{Prop:PerpAnn}\,(iii))\\
&=S\cap O\quad\hskip 82.51282pt(\text{by part (iii)}).\end{split}

Therefore we obtain

\begin{split}(S\cap O)^{\perp}&=(\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(S^{\perp}+O^{\perp}))^{\perp}\\
&=S^{\perp}+O^{\perp}\quad(\text{by part (iii)}).\end{split}

Similarly,
\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T\cap U)=\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(T)+\mbox{\rm Ann}_{V}(U)
is proved.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle A=R/J
be an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra of socle degree
\displaystyle s
. Then there exists a
\displaystyle k
-linear map
\displaystyle\varphi:A\to k
which is non-zero on the socle of
\displaystyle A
. The
\displaystyle k
-bilinear map induced by this linear map
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:A\times A\to k
, namely
\displaystyle\langle a,b\rangle=\varphi(ab)
, is non-degenerate.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
corresponding to
\displaystyle A=R/J
, there exists a
\displaystyle k
-linear map
\displaystyle\psi\colon R\to k
such that
\displaystyle\psi\mid_{\mathcal{M}^{s+1}}=0,\psi\mid_{\mathcal{M}^{s}}\neq 0
. Define
\displaystyle\varphi:A\to k
as
\displaystyle\varphi(a+J)=\psi(a)
.
By Proposition
2.3
, we have
\displaystyle\psi(Rx)=0
for all
\displaystyle x\in J.
Hence, in particular
\displaystyle\psi(x)=0
for all
\displaystyle x\in J.
Thus
\displaystyle\varphi
is well defined. Moreover, since
\displaystyle\text{Soc }A=\mathcal{M}^{s}+J/J
,
\displaystyle\varphi(\mathcal{M}^{s}+J/J)=\psi(\mathcal{M}^{s})\neq 0
. Hence
\displaystyle\varphi
is non-zero on the socle of
\displaystyle A.

Clearly, the induced map
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:A\times A\to k
given by
\displaystyle\langle a,b\rangle=\varphi(ab)
is
\displaystyle k
-bilinear.

We prove that
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is non-degenerate.
Suppose
\displaystyle a+J\in A=R/J
is such that
\displaystyle\langle a+J,b+J\rangle=0,
for all
\displaystyle b\in R.
Then, for all
\displaystyle b\in R,

\langle a+J,b+J\rangle=\varphi(ab+J)=\psi(ab)=0.

Hence by the description of
\displaystyle J
in Proposition
2.3
, we get that
\displaystyle a\in J
. Hence
\displaystyle a+J=0.

Similarly, if
\displaystyle b+J\in A
is such that
\displaystyle\langle a+J,b+J\rangle=0
for all
\displaystyle a+J\in A,
then
\displaystyle b+J=0
in
\displaystyle A
.
Therefore our claim is proved.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle A=R/J
be an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra, and
\displaystyle Q\subseteq S\subseteq A
be ideals in
\displaystyle A
. Then
\displaystyle{(S/Q)}^{\widecheck{}}\cong Q^{\perp}/S^{\perp}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi:A\to k
be a
\displaystyle k
-linear map corresponding to
\displaystyle A
obtained from Lemma
2.4
.
We have a natural
\displaystyle k
-linear map
\displaystyle\psi:Q^{\perp}\to{(S/Q)}^{\widecheck{}}

given by

\psi(a)=\psi_{a}:S/Q\to k

where
\displaystyle\psi_{a}(s+Q)=\varphi(sa)
for
\displaystyle s\in S.
Since

\displaystyle\psi_{a}
is well defined.
Note that

\ker\psi=\{a\in Q^{\perp}\mid\psi(a)=\psi_{a}=0\}=\{a\in Q^{\perp}\mid\varphi(sa)=0\text{ for all }s\in S\}=S^{\perp}\cap Q^{\perp}=S^{\perp}.

2.3.
\displaystyle b
-admissible sequences

In this section, we recall a result by C. Blancafort and S. Nollet which characterizes the Hilbert functions of standard graded algebras over an Artinian local ring that need not be a field.
We remark that possible Hilbert functions of standard graded algebras over a field are classically known due to Macaulay
[
22
]
.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
2
]
, Theorem 2.3)
.

Let
\displaystyle R_{0}
be an Artinian ring,
\displaystyle R=R_{0}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{b}]
, and
\displaystyle H:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a function. Define the functions
\displaystyle q
and
\displaystyle r
by the Euclidean division as
\displaystyle H(n)=\binom{n+b-1}{b-1}q(n)+r(n)
where
\displaystyle 0\leq r(n)<\binom{n+b-1}{b-1}.
Then
\displaystyle H
is the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle R/I
for a homogeneous ideal
\displaystyle I\subset R
if and only if

(i)

\displaystyle H(0)\leq\ell(R_{0})
;

(ii)

\displaystyle H(n+1)\leq\binom{n+b}{b-1}q(n)+{r(n)_{n}}_{+}^{+}
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 0.

Here for the
\displaystyle n^{th}
Macaulay representation
\displaystyle{r(n)_{n}}=\binom{k(n)}{n}+\cdots+\binom{k(1)}{1}
of
\displaystyle r(n),

{r(n)_{n}}_{+}^{+}:=\binom{k(n)+1}{n+1}+\cdots+\binom{k(1)+1}{1+1}.

Note that the integer
\displaystyle b
is the embedding dimension of the ring
\displaystyle R
. A numerical function
\displaystyle H
satisfying the equivalent condition above is called a
\displaystyle b
-admissible sequence
.

3.
Symmetric decomposition of the Hilbert function of
\displaystyle G(I)

Let
\displaystyle(R,\mathcal{M})
be a local ring over a field
\displaystyle k
with
\displaystyle k=R/\mathcal{M}
and
\displaystyle J
be an ideal in
\displaystyle R
such that
\displaystyle A=R/J
is an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra. Let
\displaystyle I
be an ideal in
\displaystyle A
. We define the
end degree of
\displaystyle I
as the least integer
\displaystyle u
such that
\displaystyle I^{u}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle I^{u+1}=0
.
Then we define the following decreasing sequence of ideals of
\displaystyle G(I)
as follows:

G(I)=C(0)\supseteq C(1)\supseteq\cdots\supseteq C(u+1)=0

where
\displaystyle C(a)
is a graded ideal of
\displaystyle G(I)
with
\displaystyle i^{th}
component given by

(3.1)

C(a)_{i}=\dfrac{(0:I^{u+1-a-i})\cap I^{i}}{(0:I^{u+1-a-i})\cap I^{i+1}}\cong\dfrac{(0:I^{u+1-a-i})\cap I^{i}+I^{i+1}}{I^{i+1}}.

Define
\displaystyle Q(a):=C(a)/C(a+1)
.
We note that

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle H

Let
\displaystyle I
be an ideal in an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra
\displaystyle A=k[\![x,y]\!]/J
such that
\displaystyle G(I)
has the Hilbert function
\displaystyle H
with
\displaystyle h_{0}=2.
Then
\displaystyle\ell(A/I)=2.
The following lemma shows that in this case we can assume that
\displaystyle I=(x,y^{2})
, up to a change of coordinates.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle A=k[\![x,y]\!]/J
be an AG
\displaystyle k
-algebra with an embedding dimension two. If
\displaystyle I
is an ideal in
\displaystyle A
such that
\displaystyle\ell(A/I)=2,
then
\displaystyle I=(x,y^{2})
up to a change of coordinates.

Proof.

Suppose
\displaystyle\ell(A/I)=2
. Let
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}=(x,y)A.

Then we have a composition series
\displaystyle I\subset{\mathfrak{m}}\subset A
with
\displaystyle m/I\cong A/{\mathfrak{m}}.

Hence we obtain
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}\subseteq I.
Since
\displaystyle\ell(A/I)=2
whereas
\displaystyle\ell(A/{\mathfrak{m}}^{2})=3,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}\subsetneq I.
We choose an element
\displaystyle v\in I\setminus{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}.
Let
\displaystyle\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}}{I}=\langle u\rangle
as a
\displaystyle k
-vector space.

Claim
:
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}=(u,v).

By Nakayama’s lemma it is enough to show that
\displaystyle\bar{u},\bar{v}
are
\displaystyle k
-linearly independent in
\displaystyle{{\mathfrak{m}}}/{{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}}
where
\displaystyle\bar{u},\bar{v}
denote the image of
\displaystyle u,v
in
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}/{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}.

First note that
\displaystyle u,v\in{\mathfrak{m}}\setminus{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}.

Suppose,
\displaystyle u,v
are linearly dependent in
\displaystyle{{\mathfrak{m}}}/{{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}}.
Then we may assume that
\displaystyle\bar{u}=\bar{c}\bar{v}
for some
\displaystyle c\in A.
Hence
\displaystyle u-cv\in{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}\subsetneq I.
Since
\displaystyle v\in I,
we obtain that
\displaystyle u\in I
. This is a contradiction because
\displaystyle\bar{u}
is a nonzero element in
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}/I
. Hence
\displaystyle u,v
are linearly independent in
\displaystyle{{\mathfrak{m}}}/{{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}}.

Therefore
\displaystyle\ell(A/(v,u^{2}))=2.

Since we have
\displaystyle(v,u^{2})\subset I
and
\displaystyle\ell(A/(v,u^{2}))=\ell(A/I)=2,
we get
\displaystyle I=(v,u^{2})
Therefore by a change of coordinates, we can assume that
\displaystyle I=(x,y^{2}).

∎
