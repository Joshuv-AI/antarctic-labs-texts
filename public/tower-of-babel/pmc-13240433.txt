A Novel Use of Anifrolumab for a Novel Disease
Authors: Zachary Holtz, Adam Schulz, Alexa Meara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the potential role of type‐I interferon (IFN‐I) signaling in autoimmune cytopenias outside the context of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and to evaluate the clinical response to off‐label anifrolumab in two patients with fatigue and immune‐mediated cytopenias who did not meet established criteria for SLE.MethodsWe describe two older White male patients with fatigue and autoimmune cytopenias who did not meet established SLE criteria and were refractory to standard immunosuppressants. Both received off‐label anifrolumab.ResultsBoth patients experienced rapid and sustained improvement in fatigue and resolution of cytopenias following treatment, with continued benefit and no significant adverse effects.ConclusionThese cases suggest IFN‐I signaling may underlie autoimmune cytopenias outside classic SLE. The positive response to anifrolumab raises the possibility of an atypical or distinct IFN‐mediated autoimmune condition and supports broader investigation of IFN blockade in hematologic autoimmunity.

INTRODUCTION

Saphnelo (anifrolumab‐fnia) was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2021 for the treatment of moderate‐to‐severe systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). It functions by antagonizing the type‐1 interferon (IFN‐1) receptor, thereby dampening the IFN‐1 pathway, which is known to be overactive in SLE and triggers immune activation and inflammation. 1 Although anifrolumab has been established as an effective therapy for SLE, its role in other autoimmune‐mediated conditions remains unclear. The cases presented in this series raise questions about the relevance of the IFN‐1 pathway in autoimmune‐mediated cytopenias and whether these cases represent an undifferentiated lupus spectrum disorder or a mixed overlap autoimmune syndrome not currently described but likely falling within the spectrum of interferonopathies.

The existing literature suggests that 10% to 30% of patients with myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) have preexisting autoimmune diseases, highlighting a potential link between immune dysregulation and hematopoietic dysfunction. 2 Overactive immune responses can trigger inflammatory pathways that disrupt normal blood cell production. This relationship has been exemplified by VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X‐linked, autoinflammatory, somatic) syndrome, a recently identified disorder caused by a somatic mutation in the UBA1 gene on the X chromosome. This mutation impairs the function of the major E1 enzyme responsible for ubiquitin‐mediated protein degradation, leading to an accumulation of misfolded proteins in hematopoietic progenitor cells. The resulting inflammatory response contributes to cytopenias and other systemic manifestations. 3 The clinical phenotype of patients in this series, including autoimmune cytopenias with positive autoantibodies, steroid refractoriness, and dramatic response to anifrolumab, suggests an IFN‐driven immune marrow failure syndrome distinct from both classic SLE and conventional autoimmune MDS. Although antinuclear antibody (ANA) positivity could suggest lupus, these patients did not fit established SLE classification criteria, further supporting the likelihood of a poorly defined immune overlap condition with hematologic predominance.

Case 1

A 66‐year‐old White man with a longstanding history of idiopathic autoimmune conditions, including idiopathic pancreatitis, resolved immune thrombocytopenia, and chronic dyspnea with white‐out lung episode, was initially evaluated for suspected T cell large granular lymphocyte leukemia due to persistent fatigue and worsening lymphopenia and neutropenia. This patient did not meet SLE classification by the EULAR/ACR criteria (score 6; constitutional [2], hematologic [4]) or the SLICC criteria (3 of 17 criteria: leukopenia or lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, ANA). Previous treatments with cyclosporine and glucocorticoids had been ineffective. Workup revealed positive ANA and RNP antibodies, raising suspicion for an atypical SLE‐like syndrome versus IgG4‐related disease or mixed connective tissue disease.

The patient was started on hydroxychloroquine. However, following admission for neutropenic fever and a repeat bone marrow evaluation, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) was initiated alongside glucocorticoid therapy. Given ongoing cytopenias, anifrolumab was added to address possible autoimmune etiology. The patient noted initial symptomatic improvement within two weeks of the first infusion. Over six months of treatment, he demonstrated stable white blood cell counts, reduced bruising, and platelet counts consistently above 50 × 103/μL, suggesting a partial response (Table 2).

Case 2

A 72‐year‐old White man with unexplained fatigue and recurrent cytopenias was evaluated for a possible autoimmune etiology, including SLE or autoimmune MDS. This patient did not meet SLE classification by the EULAR/ACR criteria (score 9; hematologic [3], anti‐dsDNA [6]) or the SLICC criteria (3 of 17 criteria: leukopenia or lymphopenia, ANA, anti‐dsDNA). Previous treatment with cyclosporine had provided symptomatic relief, but concerns regarding renal toxicity led to a transition to tacrolimus, which was ineffective. Laboratory evaluation revealed positive ANA and dsDNA antibodies with normal complement levels. He had no significant joint pain or photosensitivity but reported intermittent nasal ulcerations. A previous computed tomography scan showed stable pulmonary nodules and granulomas.

Tacrolimus was discontinued, and MMF was initiated with a planned dose escalation. Despite mild initial improvement, the patient remained symptomatic. Hydroxychloroquine was reinitiated, and anifrolumab therapy was initiated. The patient reported noticeable improvement within three weeks of the first infusion, suggesting a possible role for anifrolumab in managing fatigue and autoimmune cytopenias. Over four months of treatment, white blood cell count, red blood cell count, and hemoglobin levels improved from pre‐anifrolumab baselines, further supporting a therapeutic benefit (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The cases presented highlight a potentially novel application of an IFN‐targeted therapy, an unexpected yet highly effective treatment response, and the possibility of a disease entity distinct from classic SLE.

Anifrolumab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks the IFN‐I receptor and is FDA‐approved for the treatment of moderate‐to‐severe SLE. It has been shown to be particularly effective in patients with high IFN gene expression, a common feature in lupus pathogenesis. 6 However, the patients presented do not fit the typical demographic or clinical profile of SLE, which is most prevalent among young, Black, and American Indian/Alaska Native women. 7 Instead, they are older White men presenting primarily with fatigue and autoimmune‐mediated pancytopenia, without the hallmark signs of SLE such as inflammatory arthritis, rashes, or significant serositis. Both patients had fatigue, but no rash, photosensitivity, inflammatory arthritis, or the clinical or laboratory criteria that would fit for SLE diagnosis. 4 Their serologic findings suggest an autoimmune process within the connective tissue disease spectrum, but their presentation is dominated by hematologic manifestations, consistent with an autoimmune MDS‐like process. Both patients achieved sustained disease control with anifrolumab after failing other immunosuppressive therapies, which suggests that IFN dysregulation may play a key role in their disease process, even in the absence of classic SLE features.

We believe these cases represent a possible age‐related, male‐predominant, mixed overlap autoimmune syndrome characterized by bone marrow autoimmunity and IFN pathway activation. This entity may fall under a broader category of IFN‐driven immune dysregulation similar to the discovery of VEXAS syndrome, a recently defined late‐onset autoinflammatory disease caused by UBA1 mutations, which was initially misdiagnosed as refractory autoimmune and hematologic conditions. 3 Although confirmatory IFN activity testing was not conducted, such assays are not commercially available, and the marked therapeutic response to anifrolumab provides indirect evidence of IFN pathway involvement.

Although neither patient has undergone genetic testing, the striking response to an antagonist of the IFN blockade suggests that their disease might be fundamentally different from classic SLE and may represent a new IFN‐driven autoimmune disorder with a genetic basis. Future studies should explore whether these patients possess unique genetic mutations affecting IFN signaling, akin to the pathogenesis of VEXAS syndrome. 3

These cases challenge existing definitions of SLE and or a new disease. These cases highlight the need for broader investigation into IFN‐targeted therapies in autoimmune conditions that may have an IFN pathway causing the disease, because these two patients had cytopenias and bone marrow failure syndromes, which have historically been difficult to treat with conventional immunosuppressants. 8 , 9

Further investigation is needed to determine whether these cases represent an atypical presentation of SLE or a distinct IFN‐driven autoimmune disorder. Genetic screening, including whole exome or targeted sequencing, could help identify mutations affecting IFN regulation, which may support the classification of this condition as a separate disease entity. Additionally, biomarker analysis to assess IFN‐I signatures in these patients could provide mechanistic insight into their disease process and further validate the role of IFN blockade as a therapeutic strategy. Given the striking response to anifrolumab, expanded clinical trials are warranted to evaluate its efficacy in older male patients with autoimmune cytopenias, MDS, and other hematologic manifestations of immune dysregulation. Broadening the clinical application of IFN‐targeted therapies could help redefine treatment paradigms for patients who do not fit the traditional SLE framework.

The cases presented suggest the possibility of an emerging autoimmune syndrome distinct from classic SLE, characterized by an IFN‐driven pathogenesis and predominantly hematologic manifestations. The remarkable response to anifrolumab raises questions regarding the role of IFN dysregulation in autoimmune cytopenias and bone marrow failure syndromes, particularly in older male patients who do not fit the typical demographic profile of SLE. These findings underscore the need for further investigation into the genetic underpinnings and molecular mechanisms driving this condition, potentially identifying novel therapeutic targets and rethinking diagnostic criteria for autoimmune diseases. Future studies should include genetic screening and biomarker analysis to elucidate the specific pathophysiology of these cases and validate the potential broader application of IFN‐targeted therapies in autoimmune disorders.
