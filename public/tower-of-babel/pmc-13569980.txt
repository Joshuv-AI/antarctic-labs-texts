Patient Safety Competency Among Operating Room Nurses: Associations With Safety Attitudes, Systems Thinking and Burnout in a Multicentre Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Daoming Chen, Chenxi Li, Yubin Su, Fang Wu, Xiaoru Fang, Hongwei Chu, Shuang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo assess self‐reported patient safety competency among operating room nurses in tertiary hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China, and to examine its associations with safety attitudes, systems thinking and burnout dimensions.DesignA multicentre cross‐sectional study.MethodsOperating room nurses were recruited from six tertiary general hospitals in Jiangsu Province, eastern China. Data were collected between January and May 2025 using standardised self‐report questionnaires measuring patient safety competency, safety attitudes, systems thinking and burnout. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, group comparisons, Pearson correlation analyses and linear mixed‐effects modelling, with hospital included as a random intercept. Conventional multiple linear regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis.ResultsOf the 512 operating room nurses invited, 443 were included in the final analysis. The mean total patient safety competency score was 156.40 (SD 25.70). In the primary mixed‐effects model, nurses with no more than 5 years of operating room experience had lower patient safety competency scores than those with more than 20 years of experience (B = −10.185, 95% CI −15.974 to −4.395; p < 0.001). Permanent employment (B = 5.469, 95% CI 0.696–10.242; p = 0.025), participation in patient safety training (B = 8.320, 95% CI 4.033–12.606; p < 0.001), and higher safety attitudes (B = 0.683, 95% CI 0.565–0.801; p < 0.001) were associated with higher scores. Sensitivity analyses retaining clinically and theoretically relevant covariates and accounting for hospital fixed effects supported these associations. Findings for marital status, systems thinking, personal accomplishment and the burnout dimensions varied across model specifications.ConclusionsSelf‐reported patient safety competency among operating room nurses was associated with occupational characteristics, safety attitudes, systems thinking, and personal accomplishment. These associations may inform future research and programme development, but prospective studies are needed before predictive or causal conclusions can be drawn.Implications for Nursing PracticeThe findings identify nurses with limited operating room experience as a priority group for competency development and suggest attention to equitable access to patient safety education and supportive safety practices across employment groups. Prospective studies using observed safety behaviours and patient outcomes are needed to evaluate the practical relevance of these findings.Patient and Public ContributionNo patients or members of the public were involved because the study focused on nurses' professional competency and collected data only from operating room nurses. Patients and the public were not involved in the study design, conduct, analysis, interpretation or reporting.

Background

Surgical care is delivered in a complex sociotechnical environment in which patient safety depends on the coordinated work of people, procedures, equipment, information and organisational routines (Aouicha et al. 2026). In the operating room, invasive procedures, high‐risk medications, sophisticated devices, strict aseptic requirements and rapid changes in patient condition converge within a short period of time. Safety failures may therefore arise not only from individual errors but also from latent system conditions, such as incomplete communication, equipment problems, unclear responsibility or workflow disruptions (Göras et al. 2020). Operating room nurses are continuously involved in patient verification, instrument and equipment preparation, maintenance of asepsis, specimen handling, intraoperative monitoring and communication across the surgical team (Lingard et al. 2004; Jung et al. 2019). Their ability to recognise risks, coordinate care and respond appropriately is central to perioperative patient safety (Sirevåg et al. 2023).

Patient safety competency refers to the knowledge, skills and attitudes required to prevent, identify and manage risks to patients during healthcare delivery (King and Anderson 2012). For operating room nurses, safety competency extends beyond technical performance and includes communication, coordination, situational judgement and professional accountability (Gillespie et al. 2018; von Vogelsang et al. 2020). Although PSC has been examined in several nursing populations, relatively few studies have focused specifically on operating room nurses (Shin and Jang 2023). This gap is important because competency requirements in the operating room are shaped by high procedural complexity, multidisciplinary teamwork and strict time constraints. A clearer understanding of patient safety competency among operating room nurses is needed to inform targeted training and management strategies. Patient safety competency is commonly operationalised as a multidimensional construct that includes patient‐centred care, safe clinical practice, continuous quality improvement, evidence‐based practice, safety culture and risk management (Wang et al. 2018). For operating room nurses, these domains are enacted through highly specific perioperative tasks, including surgical counts, aseptic practice, equipment checks, team communication and handover. Therefore, assessing both the overall level and the dimensional pattern of patient safety competency may help identify specific weaknesses that would be obscured by a total score alone.

Several modifiable psychosocial factors may be relevant to patient safety competency in operating room nurses. Systems thinking enables nurses to recognise how people, information, equipment and organisational processes interact, and previous research has linked stronger systems thinking with higher patient safety competency (Kakemam et al. 2022). Safety attitudes reflect how nurses perceive teamwork, communication and responsibility for safety, and have been associated with patient safety competency in clinical nursing populations (Fu et al. 2022). Burnout has been associated with less favourable patient safety, quality‐of‐care and patient‐satisfaction outcomes, although its relationship with self‐reported PSC remains less clear (Li et al. 2024). These factors are important because they connect individual competency with the organisational conditions under which safe practice is enacted. Existing studies have generally examined systems thinking, safety attitudes or burnout separately and have largely involved emergency, critical care or general clinical nurses (Fu et al. 2022; Kakemam et al. 2022). Their relative associations with PSC have not been examined together among operating room nurses in Chinese tertiary hospitals.

Professional characteristics may also be relevant to patient safety competency. Professional title and clinical ladder level reflect different aspects of nurses' professional development, including formal technical qualification, accumulated clinical experience, and progressive clinical responsibility. Previous studies of perioperative nurses have suggested that perceived competence and educational needs may vary according to clinical experience, specialist preparation, and clinical ladder level (Gillespie et al. 2018; von Vogelsang et al. 2020). Administrative position represents a distinct organisational role; head nurses generally undertake additional responsibilities for staff coordination, supervision, and implementation of safety‐related procedures. However, neither formal professional rank nor administrative position necessarily corresponds directly to individual clinical competency. These variables were therefore examined as contextual occupational characteristics rather than assumed determinants of patient safety competency.

This study aimed to assess the level and dimensional pattern of self‐reported patient safety competency among operating room nurses in tertiary hospitals in China and to identify demographic, occupational and psychosocial factors associated with patient safety competency. The specific research questions were: (1) What is the overall level and dimensional pattern of patient safety competency among operating room nurses? (2) How are systems thinking, safety attitudes and burnout associated with patient safety competency after considering relevant demographic and occupational characteristics?

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A multicentre cross‐sectional survey was conducted from January to May 2025 among operating room nurses in six tertiary Grade A general hospitals in Jiangsu Province, eastern China. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross‐sectional studies (von Elm et al. 2007). No intervention was delivered.

Participants, Sampling and Sample Size

Six tertiary Grade A general hospitals in Jiangsu Province were selected through convenience sampling. The selection was guided by institutional accessibility, willingness to participate, and the feasibility of implementing standardised multicentre data‐collection procedures within the predefined study period. All six hospitals had established operating room nursing teams. Convenience sampling was considered a pragmatic approach in the absence of a province‐wide sampling frame for operating room nurses.

Within each hospital, eligible operating room nurses were recruited through convenience sampling during the predefined data‐collection period. Nurses who received the study invitation were eligible to participate if they: (1) held a valid registered nurse qualification; (2) had at least 1 year of clinical experience in the OR; and (3) voluntarily provided informed consent. Nurses were excluded if they were: (1) interns, nurses undergoing rotation, or nurses temporarily assigned to the OR; (2) on leave during the data‐collection period.

To promote consistency in recruitment across the six hospitals, uniform eligibility criteria, participant information, survey instructions, and data‐collection procedures were used throughout the study. At each hospital, a local coordinator circulated the study invitation to eligible operating room nurses through internal communication channels. Participation was voluntary, and individual responses were not available to hospital managers, nursing supervisors, or local coordinators. Although these measures helped standardise recruitment across sites, they could not eliminate the potential for selection bias associated with the non‐probability selection of hospitals and participants.

The nursing departments assisted with distributing the study invitation but were not involved in individual participation decisions. Study information and electronic survey links were provided to the heads of the nursing departments, who appointed local coordinators to forward the invitation through internal hospital communication channels. A reminder was sent 2 weeks after the initial invitation. Participation was voluntary, and individual responses were not accessible to hospital managers, nursing supervisors, or local coordinators.

A total of 512 operating room nurses were invited, and 443 were included in the final analysis (86.5%). The survey link was circulated through internal hospital communication channels. It was not possible to determine how many nurses opened the link, declined to participate, stopped before submission or submitted responses that were later excluded. The figure of 86.5% therefore represents the proportion of invited nurses included in the final analysis, rather than a questionnaire response, completion or validity rate.

The sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power version 3.1.9.2, based on a multiple linear regression approximation. Assuming a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15), a two‐sided α of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.95, and seven prespecified predictor domains, the minimum required sample size was estimated as 153 participants. This value represented the minimum sample required to ensure adequate statistical power rather than a predetermined recruitment target or stopping threshold. Recruitment therefore continued throughout the predefined data‐collection period. The final sample exceeded the minimum requirement, improving the precision of the parameter estimates and allowing for potential non‐response and exclusion of questionnaires that did not meet the data‐quality criteria.

Variables and Measures

The primary outcome was the total patient safety competency score. Candidate psychosocial variables included safety attitudes, systems thinking, emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment. Demographic and occupational characteristics were also examined as potential covariates. Demographic and occupational covariates included age, sex, educational level, marital status, years of operating room experience, professional title, nursing level, employment type, position, and participation in patient safety training. Higher scores indicated higher levels of the corresponding construct. For personal accomplishment, a higher score indicated a stronger sense of professional accomplishment and a lower level of burnout.

Patient Safety Competency Scale

Patient safety competency was assessed using the Patient Safety Competency Evaluation Scale developed by Wang et al. (2018) based on the Canadian Patient Safety Competency Framework. The scale consists of 35 items across six dimensions: patient‐centred care, clinical practice, continuous quality improvement, evidence‐based nursing practice, patient safety culture, and safety risk management. Each item is rated on a five‐point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Total scores range from 35 to 175, with higher scores indicating higher self‐reported patient safety competency. The original scale‐development study among Chinese nurses demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach's α coefficients ranging from 0.76 to 0.91 (Wang et al. 2018). In the present study, the overall Cronbach's α was 0.993, indicating excellent reliability.

Systems Thinking Scale

Systems thinking was assessed using the validated Chinese version of the 20‐item Systems Thinking Scale (STS), originally developed by Dolansky et al. (2020) and translated and culturally adapted by Zhang et al. (2022) for use among Chinese nursing personnel. Each item is rated from 0 = never to 4 = most of the time, yielding a total score of 0–80, with higher scores indicating stronger systems thinking. The Chinese version demonstrated satisfactory content and construct validity, with an S‐CVI of 0.964 and three factors explaining 71.01% of the total variance. Its Cronbach's α and test–retest reliability were 0.963 and 0.932, respectively. In the present study, Cronbach's α was 0.978.

Burnout

This study employed the Chinese version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory‐Human Services Survey (MBI‐HSS), originally developed by Maslach and Jackson (2007) and translated and validated in Chinese by Feng et al. (2004). The instrument contains 22 items across three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment. Each item is rated on a seven‐point frequency scale from 0 = never to 6 = every day. Higher emotional exhaustion and depersonalization scores, together with lower personal accomplishment scores, indicate greater burnout (Ye et al. 2008). Because the three dimensions represent distinct components of burnout (Maslach et al. 2001), and personal accomplishment is interpreted in the opposite direction, the dimensions were analysed separately, and no overall burnout score was calculated. In the present study, Cronbach's α coefficients were 0.858 for emotional exhaustion, 0.726 for depersonalization, and 0.701 for personal accomplishment, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Safety Attitudes

Safety attitudes were measured using the Chinese version of the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire translated and validated by Guo and Zhou (2010). The questionnaire contains 31 items across six dimensions: teamwork climate, safety climate, perceptions of management, job satisfaction, working conditions and stress recognition. Items are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Negatively worded items were reverse‐scored before analysis. Total scores range from 31 to 155, with higher scores indicating more positive safety attitudes. The Chinese validation study reported Cronbach's α coefficients ranging from 0.549 to 0.880 across dimensions (Guo and Zhou 2010). In the present study, Cronbach's α was 0.963.

Demographic and Occupational Questionnaire

A demographic and occupational questionnaire was developed based on a literature review and expert consultation. It collected information on age, sex, educational level, marital status, years of operating room experience, professional title, nursing level, employment type, position, and participation in patient safety training. Participation in patient safety training was assessed by asking whether participants had attended patient safety‐related training within the previous 12 months.

Professional title, nursing level, and position represented different occupational classifications. Professional title referred to the nationally recognised nursing technical rank, including nurse, senior nurse, charge nurse, associate chief nurse, and chief nurse. Nursing level referred to the institution‐based clinical ladder classification from N1 to N4, reflecting progressive levels of clinical competence, responsibility, and professional development. Position referred to the participant's organisational role and distinguished staff nurses from head nurses. In the participating hospitals, head nurses generally retained some clinical responsibilities in the operating room and were not exclusively engaged in administrative work.

Years of operating room experience were collected in five prespecified categories: ≤ 5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–20, and > 20 years. These categories were selected to represent successive 5‐year stages of operating room practice while maintaining adequate and interpretable group sizes for statistical comparison.

Data Collection and Quality Control

Data were collected from January to May 2025 across six tertiary Grade A hospitals in Jiangsu Province. Each hospital appointed a local coordinator. All coordinators received the same instructions on participant eligibility, electronic informed consent, questionnaire distribution and data‐quality procedures.

Before data collection, the electronic questionnaire was pilot tested with 10 operating room nurses. The pilot test assessed item clarity, technical functionality and completion time. No items required addition, deletion or substantive revision. The median completion time was 15 min. The pilot test was designed to assess feasibility rather than psychometric properties; therefore, internal consistency was not evaluated. Pilot responses were not included in the final analysis.

The questionnaire was administered through Wenjuanxing. Survey links were distributed through WeChat groups or other internal hospital communication channels. The first page provided information on the study purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and estimated completion time. Participants could access the questionnaire only after providing electronic informed consent.

The electronic questionnaire did not collect names, employee numbers or other direct personal identifiers. Technical information available through the survey platform, including IP addresses and submission times, was used solely to screen for potential duplicate submissions and was not treated as a research variable. Questionnaire responses were handled confidentially and analysed in de‐identified form.

All questionnaire items were mandatory. Questionnaires were excluded if the respondent did not meet the eligibility criteria, a potential duplicate submission was identified, or the responses contained clear logical inconsistencies. Two researchers independently reviewed the exported dataset, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics and are presented as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate.

An exploratory two‐stage approach was used to identify factors associated with patient safety competency. First, demographic and occupational group differences were examined using independent‐samples t‐tests or one‐way analysis of variance, as appropriate. Pearson correlation analysis was used for the psychosocial variables. Variables associated with the total patient safety competency score at p < 0.05 in these preliminary analyses were included in the primary multivariable model. These variables were marital status, years of operating room experience, employment type, participation in patient safety training, safety attitudes, systems thinking and personal accomplishment.

Participants were nested within six hospitals. The primary analysis therefore used a linear mixed‐effects model with hospital included as a random intercept. The total patient safety competency score was the dependent variable, and categorical predictors were dummy‐coded. Fixed‐effect estimates are reported as unstandardised regression coefficients (B), standard errors, t‐values, 95% confidence intervals and p‐values. The hospital‐level intraclass correlation coefficient was estimated from an intercept‐only model.

A fully adjusted linear mixed‐effects model was fitted as a sensitivity analysis to assess the influence of preliminary p‐value screening. Demographic and occupational covariates considered clinically or theoretically relevant were retained regardless of their associations in the preliminary analyses. The model included age, sex, educational level, marital status, years of operating room experience, clinical ladder level, position, employment type, participation in patient safety training, safety attitudes, systems thinking, emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and personal accomplishment. Hospital remained a random intercept.

A second sensitivity analysis was conducted to account for the small number of participating hospitals. This analysis used a hospital fixed‐effects linear model with the same participant‐level covariates. Five hospital indicator variables were entered, with Hospital A as the reference category. Residual assessment indicated heteroscedasticity; HC3 robust standard errors were therefore used for statistical inference. All hospital indicators were retained, irrespective of their individual or joint statistical significance.

Professional title and clinical ladder level were not entered simultaneously in the fully adjusted models. These variables reflected overlapping aspects of professional seniority, and their simultaneous inclusion resulted in substantial multicollinearity. Clinical ladder level was retained as it more directly represented participants' current clinical responsibilities and stage of professional development. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Model diagnostics included residual assessment and Cook's distance. Regression diagnostic plots for the conventional multiple linear regression sensitivity analysis are provided in Figures S1–S3. A robust joint Wald test was also used to assess the hospital indicators in the fixed‐effects model. Full results from the sensitivity analyses and model diagnostics are presented in Tables S1, S3–S6. The exploratory dimension‐level correlation analyses are presented in Table S2.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University on 27 August 2024 (Approval No. XYFY2024‐KL414‐01). Administrative permission to distribute the survey was obtained from the nursing administration or relevant department at each participating hospital. All participants received information about the study purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality and data use. Electronic informed consent was obtained before participants accessed the questionnaire.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of the 512 operating room nurses invited, 443 were included in the final analysis. The final sample comprised 120 nurses from Hospital A, 84 from Hospital B, 64 from Hospital C, 51 from Hospital D, 65 from Hospital E, and 59 from Hospital F. All six participating institutions were tertiary Grade A general hospitals located in Jiangsu Province, China.

Most participants were female (380/443, 85.8%), aged 30–39 years (237/443, 53.5%), held a bachelor's degree (398/443, 89.8%), and were married (277/443, 62.5%). 145 participants were unmarried (32.7%), and 21 were divorced (4.7%). The largest subgroups had 11–15 years of operating room experience (127/443, 28.7%), held the professional title of charge nurse (208/443, 47.0%), were classified as N2‐level nurses (173/443, 39.1%), and were employed under long‐term contracts (200/443, 45.1%). Most were staff nurses (410/443, 92.6%), and 359 participants (81.0%) had attended patient safety‐related training within the previous 12 months. Total PSC scores differed significantly by marital status, years of operating room experience, employment type, and participation in patient safety‐related training (all p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed by age, sex, educational level, professional title, nursing level, or position (Table 1).

Patient Safety Competency and Related Variables

The mean total PSC score was 156.40 ± 25.70, corresponding to an item mean of 4.47 ± 0.73. Safety culture had the highest item mean score (4.53 ± 0.74), followed by Clinical Practice (4.51 ± 0.75). Evidence‐based practice had the lowest score (4.37 ± 0.81). The mean total safety attitudes score was 137.68 ± 18.88, and the mean systems thinking score was 68.74 ± 12.88. For burnout, the mean scores were 20.83 ± 13.78 for emotional exhaustion, 6.84 ± 7.60 for depersonalization, and 35.97 ± 10.24 for personal accomplishment (Table 2).

Bivariate Correlations of Patient Safety Competency With Safety Attitudes, Burnout Dimensions, and Systems Thinking

Pearson correlation analyses showed that the total PSC score was positively correlated with the total safety attitudes score (r = 0.700, p < 0.001), total systems thinking score (r = 0.571, p < 0.001), and personal accomplishment (r = 0.366, p < 0.001). Neither emotional exhaustion (r = −0.049, p = 0.304) nor depersonalization (r = −0.092, p = 0.052) was significantly correlated with the total PSC score (Table 3). In the exploratory dimension‐level analyses, safety attitudes (r = 0.589–0.691), systems thinking (r = 0.462–0.594), and personal accomplishment (r = 0.313–0.367) were positively correlated with all six PSC dimensions (all p < 0.001). Depersonalization showed weak negative correlations with patient‐centred care and safety culture, whereas emotional exhaustion was not significantly correlated with any PSC dimension (Table S2).

Factors Associated With Patient Safety Competency

Based on the prespecified criterion of p < 0.05 in the preliminary analyses, marital status, years of operating room experience, employment type, participation in patient safety training, safety attitudes, systems thinking, and personal accomplishment were entered into the multivariable model. Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization were not entered because their bivariate correlations with the total PSC score were not statistically significant.

Participants were nested within six hospitals. The hospital‐level intraclass correlation coefficient estimated from the intercept‐only model was approximately zero. Nevertheless, hospital was retained as a random intercept to account for the multicentre structure of the data.

In the adjusted linear mixed‐effects model (Table 4), divorced nurses had lower PSC scores than married nurses (B = −9.147, 95% CI −16.931 to −1.363; p = 0.021), whereas unmarried nurses did not differ significantly from married nurses (B = −1.625, 95% CI −5.306 to 2.055; p = 0.386). Given the small size of the divorced subgroup (n = 21) and the relatively wide confidence interval, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously. Nurses with no more than 5 years of operating room experience had lower PSC scores than those with more than 20 years of experience (B = −10.185, 95% CI −15.974 to −4.395; p < 0.001). Permanent staff had higher PSC scores than nurses in temporary employment (B = 5.469, 95% CI 0.696–10.242; p = 0.025). Participation in patient safety training was associated with an 8.320‐point higher PSC score (95% CI 4.033–12.606; p < 0.001).

Abbreviations: B, unstandardized regression coefficient; CI, confidence interval; SE, standard error.

Higher safety attitudes (B = 0.683, 95% CI 0.565–0.801; p < 0.001), stronger systems thinking (B = 0.270, 95% CI 0.098–0.443; p = 0.002), and greater personal accomplishment (B = 0.217, 95% CI 0.045–0.388; p = 0.013) were associated with higher PSC scores after adjustment for the other variables included in the model. Unmarried status, 6–20 years of operating room experience, and long‐term contract employment were not significantly associated with PSC. In the conventional multiple linear regression sensitivity analysis, the direction and statistical significance of the associations were consistent with those observed in the primary mixed‐effects model (Table S1).

In the fully adjusted random‐intercept model, the associations with early operating room experience, permanent employment, patient safety training, safety attitudes, systems thinking, and personal accomplishment remained significant (Table S3). Divorced status was no longer significant, while emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation showed associations in opposite directions. Similar results were obtained from the hospital fixed‐effects model with HC3 robust standard errors, although systems thinking and personal accomplishment were no longer significant (Table S4). The hospital indicators were not jointly significant (robust Wald F = 1.220, p = 0.299). Multicollinearity assessment and model fit and diagnostic results are reported in Tables S5 and S6, respectively.

Discussion

This multicentre study found generally favourable self‐reported patient safety competency among operating room nurses. Early operating room experience, permanent employment, patient safety training and safety attitudes showed the most consistent associations across the primary and sensitivity analyses. Systems thinking and personal accomplishment were positively associated with PSC in the primary and fully adjusted random‐intercept models, but these associations were attenuated in the hospital fixed‐effects model. Findings for marital status, emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation also varied across models and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the results suggest that self‐reported PSC reflects both professional experience and nurses' perceptions of safety‐related practice, although some associations were sensitive to model specification.

The generally favourable PSC observed in this study is consistent with findings among operating room nurses in other settings (Shin and Jang 2023). Although direct comparison of score levels is limited by differences in instruments and healthcare contexts, both studies suggest that operating room nurses tend to report positive perceptions of their safety‐related competence. This may reflect the standardised and risk‐sensitive nature of perioperative work, in which safety checks, aseptic practice and team communication are embedded in routine care. Nevertheless, favourable self‐assessment should not be equated with objectively demonstrated competence. Self‐reported PSC may also reflect confidence, professional identity, or socially desirable responding, particularly in environments where safety is strongly emphasised. Previous studies have linked higher self‐reported PSC with a more favourable safety culture and fewer reported adverse events, although these findings were also based mainly on nurses' perceptions (Hafezi et al. 2022). Total and dimension‐level PSC scores were concentrated toward the upper end of the scale. This pattern may indicate high‐end clustering or a possible ceiling effect, reducing score variability and the ability to distinguish among nurses with relatively high self‐reported competency.

Scores across the six PSC dimensions were closely clustered. Evidence‐based practice had the lowest mean score, although the differences between dimensions were small and do not indicate a clear competency deficit. Rather, evidence‐based practice may represent an area in which further development is possible. Although conducted in a surgical department rather than specifically in operating rooms, qualitative research has shown that nurses often rely on established procedures, locally available guidance, and experienced colleagues, while direct searching and critical appraisal of research evidence are less integrated into everyday practice (Halberg et al. 2021). A similar pattern may occur in operating rooms, where rapid workflow and dependence on standardised procedures favour protocol‐based practice. Supporting evidence use may therefore require more than encouraging individual nurses to search the literature. Updated evidence could be incorporated into accessible protocols, audit standards, case discussions, and quality‐improvement activities.

Lower PSC scores were most evident among nurses in the earlier stages of operating room practice. This pattern suggests that nurses in the early stages of operating room practice may warrant particular educational attention. Studies among operating room nurses have likewise shown that competency and educational needs vary according to clinical experience and clinical ladder level (Shin and Kim 2021). In the unadjusted analysis, participation in patient safety training was associated with a relatively large difference in PSC scores. In the primary model, nurses with no more than 5 years of operating room experience scored approximately 10 points lower than those with more than 20 years of experience. The difference was larger in the fully adjusted sensitivity models, which also identified lower scores among nurses with 6–10 years of experience. Patient safety training was associated with an approximately 8‐point higher PSC score across the adjusted models, consistent with the relatively large unadjusted group difference. These differences should not be interpreted as causal effects. The association with work experience may reflect differences in clinical responsibilities, exposure to complex procedures, mentoring and professional development opportunities. The association with training may reflect self‐selection and unmeasured variation in its content, duration and quality. A simulation‐based programme among operating room nurses reported improvements in safety‐related outcomes, supporting further evaluation of scenario‐based education in this population (Park et al. 2023). Permanent employment was also consistently associated with higher PSC scores. This finding may reflect differences in organisational integration, educational opportunities, seniority or personnel policies rather than a direct effect of employment status on competency (Shang et al. 2014).

Divorced nurses had lower PSC scores than married nurses in the primary model, but the association was attenuated and was no longer statistically significant in either fully adjusted sensitivity model. The divorced subgroup included only 21 participants and showed substantial variability in PSC scores, which limited the precision of the estimates. Although the direction of the association was similar across models, the finding should be regarded as exploratory and requires confirmation in a larger and more balanced sample.

Safety attitudes showed the most consistent psychosocial association with PSC across all three models, in keeping with evidence among Chinese emergency nurses and review findings linking nurses' safety attitudes with safety‐related behaviours and patient outcomes (Alanazi et al. 2022). In operating rooms, recognising a hazard does not necessarily lead to corrective action. Nurses must also believe that raising concerns is part of their role and that the team will respond appropriately. The PSC scale includes a safety culture dimension, while the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire assesses related constructs such as safety climate, teamwork climate, and perceptions of management. This conceptual overlap, combined with concurrent self‐report measurement, may have inflated the observed association. It should therefore not be assumed that an improvement in safety attitudes would necessarily produce an equivalent improvement in PSC.

Systems thinking was positively associated with PSC in the primary and fully adjusted random‐intercept models. The estimate remained positive but was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the hospital fixed‐effects model. The association should therefore be interpreted as suggestive rather than fully robust. This pattern is broadly consistent with findings from critical care and other clinical nursing populations (Kakemam et al. 2024; Atalla et al. 2025). A further study among registered nurses found that systems thinking and safety culture were associated with both safety competence and safety performance, although its cross‐sectional design precluded causal interpretation (Chang and Manojlovich 2023). Previous studies have likewise reported positive associations between systems thinking and safety‐related competence. This perspective is particularly relevant to operating room practice because perioperative safety failures rarely result from a single isolated action. Instead, they often emerge from several small weaknesses occurring together. The present study did not examine whether perceived systems thinking translated into incident reporting, process redesign, or observable safety behaviour. Its findings therefore support an association rather than demonstrating that systems thinking improves practice. Future educational approaches could test whether case review, process mapping, and multidisciplinary analysis of near misses strengthen both systems thinking and observable safety performance.

The burnout dimensions showed different and model‐dependent relationships with PSC, supporting their separate interpretation rather than the use of a single overall burnout score (Mahsoon and Dolansky 2021). Emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation were not significantly correlated with total PSC in the bivariate analyses, but both became significant after adjustment for the broader set of covariates. Depersonalisation was negatively associated with PSC, whereas emotional exhaustion showed an unexpected positive coefficient. The latter may reflect a suppression effect or correlations among the burnout dimensions and other psychosocial variables and should not be interpreted as evidence that emotional exhaustion improves safety competency. Personal accomplishment remained positively associated with PSC in the fully adjusted random‐intercept model, but the estimate was attenuated in the hospital fixed‐effects model. Its conceptual proximity to self‐reported competency and the use of concurrent self‐report measures may partly explain the observed association. Overall, the findings for burnout were sensitive to model specification and require confirmation in studies using objective safety outcomes.

Implications for Nursing Practice

The most consistent findings indicate that nurses in the earlier stages of operating room practice warrant particular attention in competency development. Nursing managers and educators could review whether orientation, mentoring, supervised practice and continuing patient safety education adequately address the needs of these nurses (Shin and Kim 2021; Park et al. 2023). The associations with employment type and patient safety training also suggest a need to examine whether nurses working under different employment arrangements have equitable access to education, professional development and organisational support. These findings do not demonstrate that employment status or training caused differences in competency, and the content, intensity and effectiveness of training were not assessed.

The consistent association between safety attitudes and self‐reported patient safety competency further highlights the relevance of work environments that support communication, speaking up and learning from incidents. Evidence‐based practice had the lowest dimension score, although the small differences between dimensions do not indicate a clear competency deficit. Incorporating current evidence into protocols, case review, audit and quality‐improvement activities may therefore be considered for further development (Halberg et al. 2021). The present study assessed self‐reported competency rather than objectively demonstrated safety performance. Prospective studies should therefore evaluate these practice priorities using competency assessments, observed safety behaviours, incident‐related indicators and patient outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot exclude reverse relationships. Second, the concurrent use of self‐report measures may have introduced response fatigue, social desirability bias, shared self‐appraisal, and common method bias. PSC scores were concentrated toward the upper end of the scale, suggesting possible high‐end clustering or a ceiling effect. Conceptual overlap between PSC and safety attitudes, and between self‐reported competency and personal accomplishment, may also have strengthened some associations. Third, although the instruments had been validated in Chinese nursing populations, psychometric assessment in this sample was limited to internal consistency; factor structure, construct validity, measurement invariance, and test–retest reliability were not examined. Fourth, the hospitals and participants were recruited through convenience sampling. Although recruitment procedures were standardised across sites, selection bias cannot be excluded. The six participating institutions were tertiary Grade A general hospitals in Jiangsu Province, providing multicentre evidence from this hospital setting. However, the sample may not fully represent operating room nurses working in other types of healthcare institutions within the province. The findings should also be generalised beyond Jiangsu Province with caution. Fifth, the divorced subgroup was small and showed substantial variability in PSC scores. This reduced the precision and stability of the marital‐status estimate. The finding should therefore be regarded as exploratory. The inclusion of only six hospitals also limited the precision of the between‐hospital variance estimate. Several estimates varied across the primary, fully adjusted random‐intercept and hospital fixed‐effects models. This was particularly evident for divorced status, systems thinking, personal accomplishment and the burnout dimensions. Such variation may reflect overlapping covariates, suppression effects and limited precision for some subgroups. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory. Although the estimated hospital‐level intraclass correlation coefficient was small, only six hospitals were included, limiting the precision of the between‐hospital variance estimate. The near‐zero ICC and the non‐significant joint test of the hospital indicators should not be interpreted as evidence that organisational context is unimportant. Finally, residual confounding by unmeasured factors, including workload, staffing, mentoring, training intensity, management practices, organisational safety climate, and access to continuing education, cannot be excluded. The proportion of time that head nurses devoted to clinical and administrative duties was not recorded. The influence of managerial responsibilities could therefore not be examined separately.

Conclusion

Self‐reported patient safety competency was associated with several occupational and psychosocial characteristics. Early operating room experience, permanent employment, patient safety training and safety attitudes showed the most consistent associations across the primary and sensitivity analyses. Findings for divorced status, systems thinking, personal accomplishment, emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation were more sensitive to model specification and require cautious interpretation. Given the cross‐sectional design, self‐reported measures, convenience sampling and potential residual confounding, these findings represent exploratory associations rather than causal or predictive relationships. Longitudinal studies using objective safety behaviours and patient outcomes are needed to clarify their direction and practical significance.
