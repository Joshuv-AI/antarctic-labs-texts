Recursively Extended Permutation Codes under Chebyshev Distance
Tomoya Hirobe and Kenta Kasai

Abstract
We study recursively extended permutation (REP) codes under the Chebyshev distance. An REP code is built by repeatedly inserting an allowed symbol in the first coordinate and relabeling the remaining symbols. The central question is how large such a code can be for a prescribed length and minimum distance. A condition imposed separately at every extension step is sufficient to preserve distance, but it is not necessary because later extensions can increase distances. To obtain an upper bound despite this difficulty, we count the extension steps that preserve code size but are needed to remove the remaining distance shortfall. Tracking pairwise-disjoint intervals associated with codeword pairs gives a lower bound on the number of these steps. For n > d ≥ 1 n>d\geq 1 , this argument proves that the maximum size of a length- n n REP code with minimum distance at least d d is ∏ j = 0 n − 1 ( ⌊ j / d ⌋ + 1 ) \prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(\lfloor j/d\rfloor+1) . This value equals the size of the corresponding direct product group permutation code. The recursive representation also yields a coordinate-order sequential encoder with complexity O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) . When the allowed insertion symbols at each step differ pairwise by at least d d , it further yields a bounded-distance decoder with complexity O ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n\log^{2}n) .

Second, Kløve et al. introduced a code extension operation

A direct answer cannot be obtained by imposing the distance requirement separately at each step. Requiring the allowed symbols at every step to differ pairwise by at least

We carry out this accounting in two stages. For an intermediate code

, we define

as the minimum number of future singleton-head extensions needed to reach minimum distance

. For a head set

as the total shortfall of its consecutive spacings from

. By following pairwise-disjoint maximum intervals of carefully chosen codeword pairs, we prove that extending by

singleton-head steps. Combining this lower bound with an upper bound on the number of heads that can be placed with a given total spacing shortfall yields

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\text{ REP}\\
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(n)})\geq d\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{C}^{(n)}|=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{j}{d}\right\rfloor+1\right),\qquad n>d\geq 1.

Equally spaced head sets attain the bound. The product is also the cardinality of the corresponding DPGP code, although the two codes need not have the same pairwise-distance structure.

The recursive description also supports efficient algorithms. Kløve et al. gave encoding and decoding algorithms for the case of two allowed heads per step
[
10
, Sec. III.D]

[

The argument proceeds from the comparison value to the obstruction and then to its resolution. Section II fixes the notation and derives the DPGP cardinality that will reappear in the main theorem. Section III analyzes one extension step: first its effect on size and distance, and then the interval structure needed to follow distance growth. Section IV converts those local facts into a lower bound on the number of forced size-preserving steps, proves the maximum-size theorem, and gives an attaining construction. Section V uses the same recursive representation for encoding and decoding. Section VI summarizes the result and its scope.

II
Notation and Preliminaries

The maximum-size theorem compares an REP code with a DPGP code having the same length and target distance. We first establish a common notation for permutation codes and then derive the DPGP cardinality in the product form that will appear again as the REP upper bound.

For a positive integer
n
, let
[n]:=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
[n]
, that is, the set of bijections
f:[n]\to[n]
. More generally, for a finite set
A
, let
\mathcal{S}_{A}
denote the symmetric group on
A
. We represent
f\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
by the array
\underline{f}=[f(0),\ldots,f(n-1)]
. Arrays are denoted by underlined symbols; for example,
\underline{x}=[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
, where
x_{j}
is the component at coordinate
j
. We also set
\mathcal{S}_{0}:=\{\underline{\varepsilon}\}
, where
\underline{\varepsilon}
is the empty permutation.

A nonempty subset
C\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a
permutation code
of length
n
, and its elements are
codewords
. For
\underline{c},\underline{c}^{\prime}\in C
, their Chebyshev distance is

d_{\infty}(\underline{c},\underline{c}^{\prime})=\max_{j\in[n]}|c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}|.

Thus two permutations are close when their symbols differ only slightly at every coordinate; their distance is the largest coordinatewise difference.
The minimum distance of
C
is

d_{\infty}(C):=\min_{\underline{c},\underline{c}^{\prime}\in C:\underline{c}\neq\underline{c}^{\prime}}d_{\infty}(\underline{c},\underline{c}^{\prime}).

For a singleton code, we set
d_{\infty}(C)=\infty
. An
(n,M,d)
code is a length-
n
code of size
M
and minimum distance at least
d
.

II-A

Direct Product Group Permutation Codes

The DPGP code supplies the numerical benchmark for the REP extremal problem. Its symbols can move only within residue classes modulo
d
, which guarantees minimum distance at least
d
and makes its cardinality explicit.

We review the construction independently introduced by Kløve et al.
[
10
, Sec. III.A]
and Tamo and Schwartz
[
7
, Construction 1]
. Fix integers
n,d\geq 1
. For
i\in[d]
, let

A_{i}=(d\mathbb{Z}+i)\cap[n]=\{j\in[n]\mid j\equiv i\ (\bmod\ d)\}.

The corresponding DPGP code is

G:=\{\underline{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:\pi_{i}\equiv i\pmod{d}\text{ for every }i\in[n]\}
.
Equivalently, it is the direct product of the symmetric groups on the residue classes:

G=\mathcal{S}_{A_{0}}\times\mathcal{S}_{A_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{A_{d-1}}.

If
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in G
are distinct, then at some coordinate
j
the nonzero difference
\pi_{j}-\sigma_{j}
is divisible by
d
. Hence
d_{\mathrm{min}}(G)\geq d
. When
n>d
, swapping
0
and
d
in the identity permutation gives a codeword at distance
d
from the identity, so
d_{\mathrm{min}}(G)=d
.

Example 1
.

For
n=6
and
d=2
, the residue classes are
A_{0}=\{0,2,4\}
and
A_{1}=\{1,3,5\}
, so
G=\mathcal{S}_{A_{0}}\times\mathcal{S}_{A_{1}}
. A codeword is obtained by independently permuting each class and interleaving the results in their fixed coordinate positions. For example,
[024]\in\mathcal{S}_{A_{0}}
and
[135]\in\mathcal{S}_{A_{1}}
produce
[012345]\in G
, whereas
[420]\in\mathcal{S}_{A_{0}}
and
[531]\in\mathcal{S}_{A_{1}}
produce
[452301]\in G
. Hence

|G|=|\mathcal{S}_{A_{0}}|\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{A_{1}}|=3!\cdot 3!=36
.

The size of
A_{i}
is
\lfloor n/d\rfloor
when
i\geq n\bmod d
and
\lceil n/d\rceil
otherwise. Consequently,

|G|=\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{d}\right\rceil!\right)^{n\bmod\ d}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{d}\right\rfloor!\right)^{d-(n\bmod\ d)}.

When
d
divides
n
, this reduces to
|G|=((n/d)!)^{d}
.

This derivation follows
[
7
, Construction 1]
; see also
[
10
, Sec. III.A]
.

The same cardinality has the product representation

\displaystyle|G|=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{j}{d}\right\rfloor+1\right).

(1)

This form records one factor for each length increase. It is therefore the form that can be compared directly with a recursively constructed code.
Indeed, write
n=qd+r
with
0\leq r<d
. Grouping the first
qd
factors into
q
blocks of length
d
gives

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{j}{d}\right\rfloor+1\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{q-1}(p+1)^{d}\,(q+1)^{r}

\displaystyle=(q!)^{d}(q+1)^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{d}\right\rceil!\right)^{r}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{d}\right\rfloor!\right)^{d-r}.

III
One-Step Code Extension

An REP code is a sequence of local extension choices, so its global size and distance can be understood only after one extension step is analyzed exactly. Three questions matter: how many codewords the step creates, which distances it preserves, and when it can increase a distance that is still below the target. The first two questions give immediate construction rules; the third leads to the interval analysis used in the upper bound.

III-A

Extension Operation and Head Sets

The extension operation introduced by Kløve et al.
[
10
, Sec. III.C]
inserts a new first symbol while preserving the relative order of all previous symbols. The allowed first symbols determine both the number of new codewords and part of their separation.

Let
\underline{\pi}=[\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{n-1}]\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, where
n\geq 1
. Let
s\in[n+1]
be a symbol to be inserted in the first coordinate; following
[
10
]
, we call
s
a
head
. The
extension
of
\underline{\pi}
by
s
is the permutation

\displaystyle\underline{\pi}^{s}:=\bigl[s,\pi_{0}^{s},\pi_{1}^{s},\dots,\pi_{n-1}^{s}\bigr],

(2)

where
x^{s}:=\phi_{s}(x):=x+\mathbf{1}[x\geq s]
and
\mathbf{1}[P]
is the indicator of proposition
P
. The weak inequality is necessary: the strict inequality printed in
[
10
, Sec. III.C]
does not always produce a permutation.
In words, the new symbol
s
is placed first, and every old symbol at least
s
is increased by one to leave a vacant value for it.

For a code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
, let
S\subset[n+1]
be the set of allowed heads, which we call the
head set
. Define the extended code

\mathcal{C}^{S}:=\{\underline{\pi}^{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}\mid s\in S,\underline{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}\}.

We also write
\phi(\underline{\pi};s):=\underline{\pi}^{s}
and
\phi(\mathcal{C};S):=\mathcal{C}^{S}
.
All head sets are assumed to be nonempty.

For the empty permutation, define

\underline{\varepsilon}^{0}=[0].

For a head set
S\subset[n+1]
, define

d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)\overset{\text{\small def}}{=}\min\bigl\{|s-s^{\prime}|:s,s^{\prime}\in S,s\neq s^{\prime}\bigr\}.

For
|S|=1
, set
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)=\infty
.
The quantity
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)
is the smallest separation between two allowed first symbols. It will give an immediate lower bound on the distance between codewords created from different heads.

Example 2
.

For
\underline{\pi}=[0123]
and
s=2
, the extension is
\underline{\pi}^{s}=[0123]^{2}=[20134]
.
For
\mathcal{C}=\{[0123]\}
and
S=\{0,2,4\}
, we have
\mathcal{C}^{S}=\{[01234],\allowbreak[20134],[40123]\}.

III-B

Order, Size, and Elementary Distance Properties

The extension relabeling is order preserving. This elementary fact keeps interval relations intact and will later allow several distance constraints to be followed simultaneously.

Lemma 1
.

For
\pi,\sigma\in[n]
and
s\in[n+1]
,

i)

\pi<\sigma
implies
\pi^{s}<\sigma^{s}
.

ii)

\pi\leq\sigma
implies
\pi^{s}\leq\sigma^{s}
.

Proof:

For part
i)
, the three possible cases are

\pi^{s}=\pi+1<\sigma+1=\sigma^{s}
if
s\leq\pi<\sigma
,

\pi^{s}=\pi<\sigma=\sigma^{s}
if
\pi<\sigma<s
, and

\pi^{s}=\pi<\sigma+1=\sigma^{s}
if
\pi<s\leq\sigma
.
Part
ii)
follows from part
i)
and the equality
\pi^{s}=\sigma^{s}
when
\pi=\sigma
.

\Box

Before considering a code, we record the maximum possible number of pairwise
d
-separated heads. This is the largest multiplication factor available to one extension when the target separation is imposed at that step.

Theorem 1
.

For any integer
d\geq 1
and any
S\subset[n+1]
with
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)\geq d
,

d(|S|-1)\leq n
and hence
|S|\leq\lfloor n/d\rfloor+1
.
Conversely, the set
S=\{0,d,2d,\ldots,\lfloor n/d\rfloor d\}\subset[n+1]
has
|S|=\lfloor n/d\rfloor+1
and
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)\geq d
; its minimum spacing equals
d
when
|S|\geq 2
.

Proof:

Write
S=\{s_{1}<\cdots<s_{|S|}\}
. The sets
\{s_{1}\}
and
(s_{i},s_{i+1}]
,
1\leq i<|S|
, are pairwise disjoint and their union is contained in
[n+1]
. Therefore,

1+d(|S|-1)\leq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{|S|-1}|s_{i}-s_{i+1}|\leq n+1.

The stated construction attains the cardinality bound and has adjacent spacing
d
whenever it contains at least two elements.

\Box

Example 3
.

For
n=5
,
S=\{0,3,5\}
, we have
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)=2
,
|S|=3
, and
\lfloor n/d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)\rfloor+1=\lfloor 5/2\rfloor+1=3
.
For
n=6
,
S=\{0,3,6\}
, we have
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)=3
,
|S|=3
, and
\lfloor n/d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)\rfloor+1=\lfloor 6/3\rfloor+1=3
.

If the underlying permutation is fixed and only the head changes, the first coordinate determines the entire Chebyshev distance.

Lemma 2
.

For a permutation
\underline{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
s,t\in[n+1]
,

\displaystyle d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})=|s-t|.

(3)

Proof:

If
s=t
, both sides are zero. If
s\neq t
, then
|s-t|\geq 1
and
|\pi_{j}^{s}-\pi_{j}^{t}|\leq 1
for every
j\in[n]
. The head coordinates differ by
|s-t|
, and hence

d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})=\max\{|s-t|,|\pi_{j}^{s}-\pi_{j}^{t}|:j\in[n]\}=|s-t|
.

\Box

To describe when relabeling increases a symbol difference, we use the integer values lying strictly above the smaller endpoint and at most the larger endpoint.

Definition 1

(Interval)
.

For nonnegative integers
x
and
y
, define the
interval between
x
and
y
by

{\mathcal{I}}(x,y):=(\min\{x,y\},\max\{x,y\}]
when
x\neq y
, and
{\mathcal{I}}(x,x):=\emptyset
.

An extension increases a symbol difference by one exactly when its head lies between the two symbols. This identity is the local distance rule used throughout the later arguments.

Lemma 3
.

For
n>0
,
s\in[n+1]
, and
\pi,\sigma\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\left|\pi^{s}-\sigma^{s}\right|=\left|\pi-\sigma\right|+\mathbf{1}[s\in\mathcal{I}(\pi,\sigma)].

(4)

Proof:

\displaystyle|\pi^{s}-\sigma^{s}|

\displaystyle=\left|\phi_{s}\left(\pi\right)-\phi_{s}\left(\sigma\right)\right|

(5)

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\pi-\sigma\right)+\left(\mathbf{1}[\pi\geq s]-\mathbf{1}[\sigma\geq s]\right)\right|

(6)

\displaystyle=|\pi-\sigma|+\mathbf{1}[\sigma<s\leq\pi\mbox{ or }\pi<s\leq\sigma]

(7)

\displaystyle=|\pi-\sigma|+\mathbf{1}[s\in\mathcal{I}(\pi,\sigma)].

(8)

\Box

Applying the same head to two codewords therefore never decreases their distance and can increase it by at most one.

Lemma 4
.

For a length-
n
code
\mathcal{C}
, distinct
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
, and
s\in[n+1]
,

d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})\leq d_{\infty}\bigl(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s}\bigr)\leq d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})+1.

Proof:

The head coordinates of
\underline{\pi}^{s}
and
\underline{\sigma}^{s}
are equal. For each remaining coordinate, Lemma
3
gives

|\pi_{j}-\sigma_{j}|\leq|\pi_{j}^{s}-\sigma_{j}^{s}|\leq|\pi_{j}-\sigma_{j}|+1
.
Taking the maximum over
j\in[n]
proves the claim.

\Box

Applying different heads gives another source of separation: the new first coordinate alone separates the extended codewords by the distance between the heads.

Lemma 5

(Distance lower bound from head symbols)
.

For a code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
, arbitrary
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
, and
s,t\in[n+1]
,

d_{\infty}\bigl(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{t}\bigr)\geq|s-t|
.
Equality holds when
\underline{\pi}=\underline{\sigma}
.

Proof:

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\left(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[n+1]}\left|(\underline{\pi}^{s})_{j}-(\underline{\sigma}^{t})_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|(\underline{\pi}^{s})_{0}-(\underline{\sigma}^{t})_{0}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|s-t\right|.

Equality for
\underline{\pi}=\underline{\sigma}
follows from Lemma
2
.

\Box

Together, the preceding bounds show that neither the original codeword nor the chosen head is lost in the extension.

Lemma 6
.

The map
\phi:\mathcal{S}_{n}\times[n+1]\to\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
is injective.

Proof:

Suppose
(\underline{\pi},s)\neq(\underline{\sigma},t)
. If
s\neq t
, Lemma
5
gives
\underline{\pi}^{s}\neq\underline{\sigma}^{t}
. If
s=t
and
\underline{\pi}\neq\underline{\sigma}
, choose
i\in[n]
with
\pi_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}
. Lemma
3
gives
|\phi_{s}(\pi_{i})-\phi_{s}(\sigma_{i})|\geq|\pi_{i}-\sigma_{i}|>0
, so again
\underline{\pi}^{s}\neq\underline{\sigma}^{t}
.

\Box

Consequently, every original codeword produces exactly one distinct extended codeword for each allowed head.

Theorem 2
.

For a code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
and a head set
S\subset[n+1]
,

|\mathcal{C}^{S}|=|\mathcal{C}||S|
.

Proof:

The claim follows because the map

.

III-C

Lower Bounds on Minimum Distance under Extension

The two sources of separation identified above combine into a simple construction rule. Codewords obtained from the same head remain at least as far apart as the original codewords, while codewords obtained from different heads are separated by the head spacing.

Theorem 3
.

For any code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
and any head set
S\subset[n+1]
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\mathcal{C}^{S}\right)\geq\min\left(d_{\mathrm{min}}(S),d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})\right).

(9)

Proof:

Suppose first that
S=\{s\}
, so
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)=\infty
. Every distinct pair in
\mathcal{C}^{S}
has the form
(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s})
for distinct
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Lemma
4
gives
d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s})\geq d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})\geq d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})
. Hence
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{S})\geq d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})=\min\{d_{\mathrm{min}}(S),d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})\}
.

Now suppose
|S|\geq 2
and consider distinct codewords
\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{S}
.

•

If
s\neq t
, Lemma
5
gives
d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{t})\geq|s-t|\geq d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)
.

•

If
s=t
, then
\underline{\pi}\neq\underline{\sigma}
, and Lemma
4
gives
d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s})\geq d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})\geq d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})
.

The desired lower bound follows in both cases.

\Box

Equivalently, once both the original code and the head set have minimum distance at least
d
, one extension preserves that target distance. Repeating this statement gives the standard sufficient condition for constructing REP codes.

Theorem 4

(
[
10
, Theorem 4]
)
.

For an integer
d\geq 1
, a code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and a head set
S\subset[n+1]
, if
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)\geq d
and
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})\geq d
, then
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{S})\geq d
.

Proof:

The assumptions give

\min\{d_{\mathrm{min}}(S),d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})\}\geq d.

The result follows from Theorem
3
.

\Box

III-D

Upper Bounds on Minimum Distance under Extension

The preceding lower bound explains how to guarantee distance, but an extremal proof also needs to know how much one extension can improve it. Two complementary bounds limit the new minimum distance: one comes from the closest pair of heads, and the other from the old minimum distance plus one.

Theorem 5

(Upper bound on
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{S})
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
be a code and
S\subset[n+1]
a head set. Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{S})\leq d_{\mathrm{min}}(S).

(10)

Proof:

For
|S|=1
, the bound is immediate because
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)=\infty
. Suppose
|S|\geq 2
.
Select
s,t\in S
such that
|s-t|=d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)
.
For any
\underline{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}
, Lemma
2
gives
d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})=|s-t|=d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)
.
Thus
\mathcal{C}^{S}
contains a pair at distance
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)
.

\Box

Even if the heads are far apart, a pair of old codewords extended by the same head can gain at most one unit of distance.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
be a code and
S\subset[n+1]
a head set. Then
d_{\infty}(\mathcal{C}^{S})\leq d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})+1
.

Proof:

If
|\mathcal{C}|=1
, then
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})=\infty
and the claim is immediate. Suppose
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 2
.
Select distinct
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})=d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})
.
For any

, Lemma

shows that
\underline{\pi}^{s}
and
\underline{\sigma}^{s}
are distinct, while Lemma

gives

.
This pair gives the claimed upper bound.

\Box

By Theorem

, an extension is
size-preserving
when
|S|=1
and
size-increasing
when

. It is

when

.

Example 4
.

Let

\mathcal{C}^{S}=\{[10234],[14023]\}

Thus, the extension is size-preserving and distance-increasing.

For
\mathcal{C}=\{[0123],[1032]\}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{S}

\displaystyle=\{[10234],[30124],[12043],[31042]\},

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{min}}(S)

\displaystyle=2,\quad d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C})=1,\quad d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{S})=2.

III-E

Codeword Pairs, Interval Sets, and Maximum Intervals

The upper bounds reveal the central difficulty: a singleton-head extension can repair a distance shortfall without increasing code size. To count how many such repairs are necessary, we need to know which head values can increase the distance of a given codeword pair. Intervals provide exactly this information and, unlike the distance value alone, can be followed through later extensions.

The length of
I={\mathcal{I}}(x,y)
is
|I|:=|x-y|
. For
x,y\in[n]
and
s\in[n+1]
, define its extension by

I^{s}:={\mathcal{I}}(x^{s},y^{s})
.

An interval gains one element precisely when the inserted head lies inside it.

Lemma 7
.

For an interval
I={\mathcal{I}}(x,y)
with
x,y\in[n]
and a head
s\in[n+1]
,

|I^{s}|=|I|+\mathbf{1}[s\in I]
.

Proof:

By Lemma
3
,

|I^{s}|=|{\mathcal{I}}(x^{s},y^{s})|=|x^{s}-y^{s}|=|x-y|+\mathbf{1}[s\in{\mathcal{I}}(x,y)]=|I|+\mathbf{1}[s\in I]
.

\Box

Example 5
.

Let
I=(1,4]
, so
|I|=3
. For
s=2\in I
, we have

I^{s}=(\phi_{2}(1),\phi_{2}(4)]=(1,5]=\{2,3,4,5\},\quad|I^{s}|=4=|I|+1
.

Distinct distance shortfalls can be tracked separately because disjoint intervals remain disjoint under the combinations of extensions used later.

Lemma 8
.

Let
I={\mathcal{I}}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
J={\mathcal{I}}(x_{2},y_{2})
be nonempty intervals with endpoints in
[n]
, and let
s,t\in[n+1]
. If
I
and
J
are disjoint, then:

i)

For any head
s
, the intervals
I^{s}
and
J^{s}
are disjoint.

ii)

If
s\in I
and
t\in J
, then
I^{t}
and
J^{s}
are disjoint.

iii)

If
s\in I
, then
I^{t}
and
J^{s}
are disjoint.

Proof:

For part
i)
, assume without loss of generality that
I=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{1},\sigma_{1})
and
J=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{2},\sigma_{2})
with
\pi_{1}<\sigma_{1}\leq\pi_{2}<\sigma_{2}
.
Lemma
1
gives
\pi_{1}^{s}<\sigma_{1}^{s}\leq\pi_{2}^{s}<\sigma_{2}^{s}
, so
I^{s}
and
J^{s}
are disjoint.

For part
ii)
, assume without loss of generality that
\pi_{1}<s\leq\sigma_{1}\leq\pi_{2}<t\leq\sigma_{2}
.
Then
I^{t}=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{1}^{t},\sigma_{1}^{t})=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{1},\sigma_{1})
and
J^{s}=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{2}^{s},\sigma_{2}^{s})=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{2}+1,\sigma_{2}+1)
.
These intervals are disjoint.

For part
iii)
, first suppose
I
lies to the left of
J
, so
\pi_{1}<s\leq\sigma_{1}\leq\pi_{2}<\sigma_{2}
. If
I^{t}
and
J^{s}
intersected, then
\pi_{2}^{s}<\sigma_{1}^{t}
. However,
\pi_{2}^{s}=\pi_{2}+1
and
\sigma_{1}^{t}\leq\sigma_{1}+1
, which would imply
\pi_{2}<\sigma_{1}
, contradicting
\sigma_{1}\leq\pi_{2}
. If
J
lies to the left of
I
, write
J=(\pi_{2},\sigma_{2}]
and
I=(\pi_{1},\sigma_{1}]
with
\sigma_{2}\leq\pi_{1}<s
. Then
J^{s}=J
, whereas the lower endpoint of
I^{t}
is
\pi_{1}^{t}\geq\pi_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}
, so the intervals are again disjoint.

\Box

Example 6
.

Let
I=(1,3]
,
J=(4,5]
, and
s=2\in I
.

i)

I^{2}=(1,4]=\{2,3,4\}
and
J^{2}=(5,6]=\{6\}
are disjoint.

ii)

For
t=5\in J
,
I^{t}=(1,3]
and
J^{s}=(5,6]
are disjoint.

iii)

For
t=0
,
I^{t}=(2,4]
and
J^{s}=(5,6]
are disjoint.

Containment is also preserved, so an interval that controls all coordinate differences before extension continues to control them afterward.

Lemma 9
.

If nonempty intervals
I
and
J
satisfy
I\subset J
, then
I^{s}\subset J^{s}
.

Proof:

Without loss of generality, write
I=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{1},\sigma_{1})
and
J=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{2},\sigma_{2})
with
\pi_{2}\leq\pi_{1}<\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}
.
From Lemma
1
,
\pi_{2}^{s}\leq\pi_{1}^{s}<\sigma_{1}^{s}\leq\sigma_{2}^{s}
, so
I^{s}\subset J^{s}
.

\Box
For distinct permutations
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, define the following objects.

1.

The
interval set
between
\underline{\pi}
and
\underline{\sigma}
is

\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}):=\{\mathcal{I}(\pi_{j},\sigma_{j}):\pi_{j}\neq\sigma_{j},\ j\in[n]\}
.

2.

An interval in
\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})
that contains every other interval in this set is the
maximum interval
of the pair. If it exists, it is unique.

The interval set records the value gap at every coordinate where the permutations differ. A maximum interval, when it exists, contains all those gaps; its length is therefore the Chebyshev distance of the pair.
By definition,

\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s})=\{\mathcal{I}(\pi_{j}^{s},\sigma_{j}^{s}):\pi_{j}\neq\sigma_{j},\ j\in[n]\}.

and

d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})=\max_{J\in\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})}|J|.

For a pair
\mathsf{P}:=(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}
and
s\in[n+1]
, write
\mathsf{P}^{s}:=(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s})
.
Maximum intervals identify both the current distance and the exact heads that increase it, as stated below.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose
\mathsf{P}:=(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{2}
has maximum interval
I
. Then:

i)

The permutation pair
\mathsf{P}^{s}
has maximum interval
I^{s}
.

ii)

d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})=|I|
.

iii)

d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\sigma}^{s})=d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})+\mathbf{1}[s\in I]
.

iv)

|I^{s}|=|I|+\mathbf{1}[s\in I]{.}

Proof:

For part
i)
, every
J\in\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})
satisfies
J\subset I
. Lemma
9
therefore gives
J^{s}\subset I^{s}
, so
I^{s}
is the maximum interval of
\mathsf{P}^{s}
. Part
ii)
follows from the definition of Chebyshev distance. Lemma
7
gives part
iv)
. If
s\in I
, the length of
I
increases by one; if
s\notin I
, then
s
lies in no interval contained in
I
, so no interval length increases. Together with part
i)
, this proves part
iii)
.

\Box

Example 7
.

Let
n=5
,
\underline{\pi}=[01234]
, and
\underline{\sigma}=[01432]
. The maximum difference is
|\pi_{4}-\sigma_{4}|=|4-2|=2
, so the maximum interval is
I=(2,4]
. For
s=3\in I
,

\underline{\pi}^{3}=[301245],\quad\underline{\sigma}^{3}=[301542]
,
and

. Hence

d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi}^{3},\underline{\sigma}^{3})=3=d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})+1
and
|I^{3}|=3=|I|+1
.

Finally, two codewords created from the same original permutation by different heads have a maximum interval determined solely by those heads. This supplies the initial disjoint intervals used in the counting argument of Section IV.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\underline{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
s,t\in[n+1]
with
s<t
. Then:

i)

\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})=\{{\mathcal{I}}(s,t),{\mathcal{I}}(s,s+1),\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}(t-1,t)\}
.

ii)

The two extended permutations differ in exactly
|s-t|+1
coordinates.

iii)

The codeword pair
(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})
has maximum interval
{\mathcal{I}}(s,t)
.

Proof:

Reordering the coordinates does not change the interval set or the number of differing coordinates, so assume without loss of generality that
\underline{\pi}
is the identity permutation
\underline{\iota}=[0,1,\ldots,n-1]
. By (
2
),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \underline{\iota}^{s}={}&[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s},0,1,\ldots,s-1,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s+1},\\
&\phantom{[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s+2,\ldots,t},t+1,\ldots,n],\\
\underline{\iota}^{t}={}&[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t},0,1,\ldots,s-1,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s},\\
&\phantom{[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s+1,\ldots,t-1},t+1,\ldots,n].\end{aligned}

(11)

The displayed arrays give

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})=\{{\mathcal{I}}(s,t),{\mathcal{I}}(s,s+1),\ldots,{\mathcal{I}}(t-1,t)\}.

The arrays differ in the head coordinate and in the
t-s
coordinates occupied by
s,\ldots,t-1
, hence in exactly
|s-t|+1
coordinates. Different coordinates can determine the same interval; for example, this occurs when
t=s+1
. Therefore, the interval set can contain fewer than
|s-t|+1
elements. Finally,
{\mathcal{I}}(s,t)
contains every other displayed interval and is therefore the maximum interval.

\Box

Example 8
.

Let
n=5
,
\underline{\pi}=[01234]
,
s=1
, and
t=3
. Then

\underline{\pi}^{s}=[102345],\qquad\underline{\pi}^{t}=[301245].

Their interval set is
\mathcal{I}(\underline{\pi}^{s},\underline{\pi}^{t})=\{(1,3],(1,2],(2,3]\}
. The permutations differ in

coordinates, and their maximum interval is
(s,t]=(1,3]
.

IV
REP Codes and Their Maximum Size

Repeated extension makes the code size a product of local head-set sizes. The difficulty is that minimum distance is global: a step that creates close codewords may be repaired later by extensions that do not enlarge the code. We first define the REP family and exhibit this delayed repair, then measure its unavoidable cost and convert that cost into the exact maximum size.

For each
j=0,\ldots,n-1
, let
S^{(j)}
be a nonempty subset of
[j+1]
. Starting from
\mathcal{C}^{(0)}:=\{\underline{\varepsilon}\}
, define, for
j=1,\ldots,n
,

\mathcal{C}^{(j)}=\phi(\mathcal{C}^{(j-1)};S^{(j-1)})
.
The resulting
recursively extended permutation
(REP) code is denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}=\langle S^{(0)},\ldots,S^{(n-1)}\rangle
. Theorem
2
gives

|\mathcal{C}^{(n)}|=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}|S^{(j)}|
.
Thus a message chooses one head from each set, and every head sequence produces one codeword. The cardinality is large when many stages offer several choices.

Example 9
.

For integers
n,d,q
with
q\geq 2
and
(q-1)d<n
,
[
10
, Sec. III.D]
constructs an
(n,q^{n-(q-1)d},d)
REP code as follows. Set
S^{(j)}=\{0\}
for
0\leq j<(q-1)d
, and set

S^{(j)}=\{\lfloor j/(q-1)\rfloor x:x=0,\ldots,q-2\}\cup\{j\}
for
(q-1)d\leq j<n
. The latter sets contain
q
elements with minimum spacing at least
d
. Therefore,

|\mathcal{C}^{(n)}|=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}|S^{(j)}|=q^{n-(q-1)d}
, and repeated application of Theorem
4
gives
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(n)})\geq d
.

Example 10
.

Let
n=7
,
d=2
, and
q=3
. Then
(q-1)d=4<n
, and the head sets are

\displaystyle S^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\{0\},\quad S^{(1)}=\{0\},\quad S^{(2)}=\{0\},\quad S^{(3)}=\{0\},

\displaystyle S^{(4)}

\displaystyle=\{0,2,4\},\quad S^{(5)}=\{0,2,5\},\quad S^{(6)}=\{0,3,6\}.

The resulting REP code has size

|\mathcal{C}^{(7)}|=1\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 3\cdot 3\cdot 3=27=3^{7-(3-1)\cdot 2}.

Each
S^{(j)}
for
j\geq 4
has minimum spacing at least
2
, so Theorem
4
gives
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(7)})\geq 2
.

Theorem
4
ensures
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(n)})\geq d
if
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S^{(j)})\geq d
for every
j
. The converse fails: an intermediate head set may have minimum distance below
d
even when the final REP code has minimum distance at least
d
. This distinction is why a stage-by-stage spacing bound cannot prove the desired upper bound.
For instance, consider
S^{(0)}=\{0\}
,
S^{(1)}=\{0,1\}
, and
S^{(2)}=\{1\}
, where
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S^{(1)})=1
. Then

\mathcal{C}^{(0)}=\{\underline{\varepsilon}\}
,
\mathcal{C}^{(1)}=\{[0]\}
,

\mathcal{C}^{(2)}=\{[01],[10]\}
, and

\mathcal{C}^{(3)}=\{[102],[120]\}
. Hence,
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(3)})=2
.

IV-A

Number of Size-Preserving Extensions Needed to Increase Minimum Distance

A length-
n
code with minimum distance at least
d
is called an
[n,d]
code. A singleton head set preserves code size, whereas a larger head set increases it. We therefore measure the minimum number of future singleton-head extensions required to turn an intermediate code into an
[n,d]
code. This number records the part of the remaining construction that cannot contribute to cardinality.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer and let
\mathcal{C}_{0}
be a code in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
. For
j\geq 0
, choose a nonempty head set
S_{j}\subset[N+j+1]
and set
\mathcal{C}_{j+1}:=\mathcal{C}_{j}^{S_{j}}
. Define
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)
as the minimum number of extension steps with singleton head sets among all finite extension sequences that produce a code of minimum distance at least
d
.
For a fixed number
k
of extension steps, let
c_{1}^{(k)}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)
be the minimum number of size-preserving steps among all length-
k
extension sequences whose final code has minimum distance at least
d
.
Formally,

\displaystyle c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)\overset{\text{\small def}}{=}\min_{k\geq 0}c_{1}^{(k)}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)

(12)

\displaystyle c_{1}^{(k)}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)\overset{\text{\small def}}{=}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0},\ldots,S_{k-1}\\
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\geq d\end{subarray}}\#\{l\in[k]:|S_{l}|=1\}

(13)

Thus
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)
is not the number of all future steps; it counts only the steps at which the encoder has no choice of head and hence gains no new codewords.
If no length-
k
sequence satisfies the distance requirement, define
c_{1}^{(k)}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d):=\infty
.
For
k=0
, the empty extension sequence has cost zero when
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}_{0})\geq d
; otherwise it is infeasible. In particular,

c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)=0
whenever
\mathcal{C}_{0}
already has minimum distance at least
d
.
The value
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)
is finite. For each intermediate code
\mathcal{C}_{j}
, define the total amount by which its pairwise distances fall below
d
as

\Delta_{j}:=\sum_{\{\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\}\in\binom{\mathcal{C}_{j}}{2}}\max\{d-d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}),0\}.

Suppose that
\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
have distance below
d
. Choose a coordinate
i
such that
|\pi_{i}-\sigma_{i}|=d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\sigma})
, choose
s\in\mathcal{I}(\pi_{i},\sigma_{i})
, and extend the code using the singleton head set
\{s\}
. Lemma
3
increases the selected pair’s distance by one and does not decrease the distance of any other pair. By Lemma
6
, this extension gives a one-to-one correspondence between the codeword pairs before and after extension. Hence the selected pair’s contribution to
\Delta_{j}
decreases by one, while no other contribution increases, so
\Delta_{j+1}\leq\Delta_{j}-1
. Because
\Delta_{0}
is a finite nonnegative integer, after at most
\Delta_{0}
such extensions every pair has distance at least
d
. Thus a finite extension sequence satisfying the required minimum distance always exists.

For an intermediate code lying on a completed length-
n
REP construction, the remaining
n-k
stages themselves give an immediate upper bound on this required number.

Lemma 12

(Upper bound on
c_{1}
)
.

Let
n>d\geq 1
, and let
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
be an REP code with
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(n)})\geq d
. For every
1\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle c_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{(k)};d)\leq n-k{.}

(14)

Proof:

The fixed sequence of head sets
S^{(k)},\ldots,S^{(n-1)}
extends
\mathcal{C}^{(k)}
to
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
, whose minimum distance is at least
d
. It is therefore a feasible sequence in the definition of
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{(k)};d)
. This sequence has
n-k
steps and hence contains at most
n-k
singleton head sets, which proves (
14
).

\Box

Example 11
.

Let
n=6
,
d=2
, and consider the REP code constructed from

\displaystyle S^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\{0\},

\displaystyle S^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\{0\},

\displaystyle S^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\{0,2\},

\displaystyle S^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\{0,2\},

\displaystyle S^{(4)}

\displaystyle=\{0,2,4\},

\displaystyle S^{(5)}

\displaystyle=\{0,2,4\}.

This yields a code
\mathcal{C}^{(6)}
with
d_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathcal{C}^{(6)})\geq 2
.
For
k=4
, the displayed extension from
\mathcal{C}^{(4)}
to
\mathcal{C}^{(6)}
contains no singleton head sets. Hence

c_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{(4)};2)=0\leq 6-4=2
,
as required by Lemma

.

The effect of a size-increasing step depends on how closely its heads are placed. For a head set
S\subset[n+1]
, let
\delta_{d}(S)
denote the total amount by which its consecutive spacings fall below
d
. Set
\delta_{d}(S)=0
when
|S|=1
. When
S=\{s_{1}<\cdots<s_{q}\}
with
q\geq 2
, define

\displaystyle\delta_{d}(S)\overset{\text{\small def}}{=}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j<q\\
s_{j+1}-s_{j}<d\end{subarray}}(d-(s_{j+1}-s_{j})).

(15)

Each term is the number of additional distance units needed to raise one short consecutive spacing to
d
;
\delta_{d}(S)
sums these requirements over all consecutive heads.
For every code
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
, Theorem
8
gives
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{S};d)\geq\delta_{d}(S)
.

The spacing shortfall also limits the size of the head set. Allowing total shortfall at most
c
increases the usual packing bound by at most
c/d
. Theorem
1
is recovered after replacing
n
below by
n+1
and setting
c=0
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
n,d\geq 1
be integers. If
S\subset[n]
and
c\geq\delta_{d}(S)
, then

\displaystyle|S|\leq\frac{n-1+c}{d}+1.

(16)

Proof:

Write
S=\{s_{1}<\cdots<s_{|S|}\}
and let

J:=\{1,\ldots,|S|-1\}
,

\underline{J}:=\{j\in J:s_{j+1}-s_{j}<d\}
, and

\overline{J}:=J\setminus\underline{J}
. Then

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq 1+\sum_{j\in J}(s_{j+1}-s_{j})

(17)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j\in\underline{J}}(s_{j+1}-s_{j})+\sum_{j\in\overline{J}}(s_{j+1}-s_{j})

(18)

\displaystyle\geq 1+|\underline{J}|d-c+|\overline{J}|d

(19)

\displaystyle=1-c+(|S|-1)d.

(20)

The first inequality follows from
s_{|S|}-s_{1}\leq n-1
. For the second, use

c\geq\sum_{j\in\underline{J}}(d-(s_{j+1}-s_{j}))
and

s_{j+1}-s_{j}\geq d
for

.
Rearranging proves (

).

\Box

Example 12
.

Let

We next relate the required number of singleton steps before and after one extension. One new extension can reduce that requirement by at most one, and a reduction by exactly one is possible only when the extension itself uses a singleton head set.

Theorem 7
.

For a code
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
, a head set
S_{0}\subset[n+1]
, and
d\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{0}^{S_{0}}
. Then

i)

c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)\leq c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{1};d)+1
;

ii)

c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)=c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{1};d)+1
implies
|S_{0}|=1
.

Proof:

Take an extension sequence from
\mathcal{C}_{1}
attaining
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{1};d)
and prepend the extension by
S_{0}
. The resulting sequence is feasible for
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and contains at most one additional singleton head set. This proves part
i)
.

For part
ii)
, suppose
|S_{0}|\neq 1
. Prepending
S_{0}
to the same sequence adds no singleton head set, so
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)\leq c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{1};d)
. This contradicts
c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{0};d)=c_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{1};d)+1
.

\Box

Example 13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{[01],[10]\}\subset\mathcal{S}_{2}
,

, and

. Then

IV-B

Maximum-Size REP Codes

The preceding results quantify both sides of the tradeoff. A large head set multiplies the code size, but its spacing shortfall forces later singleton steps whose multiplication factor is one. We now combine these facts over the entire extension sequence, derive an upper bound, and attain it with equally spaced heads. All maxima in this subsection are taken over REP codes, and
n>d\geq 1
.

The proof uses the following elementary statement about the sequence of remaining singleton-step requirements. It bounds how many stages can contribute a nontrivial multiplication factor and how large those factors can be.

Lemma 15
.

Let
c^{(0)},\ldots,c^{(n)}
be nonnegative integers satisfying

c^{(k)}\leq c^{(k+1)}+1
for
0\leq k<n
and

c^{(k)}\leq n-k
for
1\leq k\leq n
. Define

If
K
is nonempty and

, then, for

\displaystyle k_{i}+c^{(k_{i}+1)}\leq n-1-(m-i).

(27)

Proof:

We first show that, for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
,

\displaystyle k_{i}+c^{(k_{i}+1)}<k_{i+1}+c^{(k_{i+1}+1)}.

(28)

Since
k_{i+1}\in K
, we have
c^{(k_{i+1})}\leq c^{(k_{i+1}+1)}
. There are two cases.

1.

If

, then

, and hence

k_{i}+c^{(k_{i}+1)}=k_{i+1}-1+c^{(k_{i+1})}\leq k_{i+1}+c^{(k_{i+1}+1)}-1.

If

, then

do not belong to

. The first hypothesis and integrality therefore give

Telescoping yields

which implies

k_{i}+c^{(k_{i}+1)}=k_{i+1}-1+c^{(k_{i+1})}\leq k_{i+1}+c^{(k_{i+1}+1)}-1.

Thus (
28
) holds.

The second hypothesis gives

\displaystyle k_{m}+c^{(k_{m}+1)}\leq n-1.

(29)

Applying (
28
) repeatedly, starting from (
29
), proves (
27
).

\Box

V
Encoding and Decoding Algorithms

The extremal argument treats an REP code as a sequence of head choices. The same representation is also a message description. We first translate the defining recursion directly into an encoder, then remove its repeated relabeling by interpreting the reversed head sequence as ranks among unused symbols. The final permutation can instead be produced from left to right without constructing every intermediate permutation. The head used at the last extension is the first output symbol; each earlier head gives the rank of a later output symbol among those still unused.

The second compatibility lemma says that relabeling all unused symbols gives exactly the unused symbols after extension.

Lemma 17
.

For

V-C

Bounded-Distance Decoding for REP Codes

Encoding identifies, at each coordinate, one candidate value for every allowed head. If the heads at that stage differ pairwise by at least
d
, the corresponding candidates are also separated by at least
d
. The decoder can therefore recover the head sequence from left to right by choosing the candidate nearest to each received value.

Consider an REP code

\mathcal{C}^{(n)}=\langle S^{(0)},\ldots,S^{(n-1)}\rangle

whose head sets satisfy

|s-t|\geq d\quad\text{for distinct }s,t\in S^{(j)}\text{ and every }j.

Let
\underline{s}
,
\underline{\pi}
,
\underline{\rho}
, and
\hat{\underline{s}}
denote the message, transmitted codeword, received array, and estimated message, respectively, where
\underline{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and write
\overline{i}:=n-1-i
. At step
i
, let
\widehat{P}_{i}:=\{\hat{\pi}_{0}^{(n)},\ldots,\hat{\pi}_{i-1}^{(n)}\}
be the set of previously reconstructed symbols, with
\widehat{P}_{0}:=\emptyset
. For
A\subset[n]
with
|A|=i
and
s\in[n-i]
, define

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(s;A)\overset{\text{\small def}}{=}\operatorname{select}_{s}\bigl([n]\setminus A\bigr).

(35)

Here
\psi_{i}(s;A)
is the candidate symbol of rank
s
after the
i
symbols in
A
have already been removed.
At step
i
, Algorithm
3
chooses, among the candidates corresponding to the elements of
S^{(\overline{i})}
, one that is closest to
\rho_{i}
. Ties outside the guaranteed decoding radius may be resolved by any fixed rule.

Algorithm 3

Sequential Decoder for
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}

0:

Received array
(\rho^{(n)}_{0},\ldots,\rho^{(n)}_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

0:

Estimated message
(\hat{s}^{(0)}\in S^{(0)},\ldots,\hat{s}^{(n-1)}\in S^{(n-1)})

1:

for

i=0
to
n-1

do

2:

\displaystyle\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{s\in S^{(\overline{i})}}|\rho_{i}^{(n)}-\psi_{i}(s;\widehat{P}_{i})|

3:

\hat{\pi}^{(n)}_{i}:=\psi_{i}(\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})

4:

end

for

5:

return

({\hat{s}^{(0)},\ldots,\hat{s}^{(n-1)}})

The decoding guarantee is the usual half-minimum-distance statement in coordinatewise form: an error smaller than
d/2
at every coordinate cannot move the received value closer to a different candidate.

Theorem 12
.

For an integer
d\geq 1
and an REP code with
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S^{(j)})\geq d
for every
j
, Algorithm
3
returns
\hat{\underline{s}}=\underline{s}
whenever
|\pi_{i}-\rho_{i}|<d/2
for every
i\in[n]
.

Proof:

We use induction on
i
. Assume that all preceding message symbols have been recovered correctly; for
i=0
this assumption is vacuous. The reconstructed prefix is then correct, so Algorithm
2
gives

\pi_{i}=\psi_{i}(s^{(\overline{i})};P_{i})=\psi_{i}(s^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})
,
where
P_{i}:=\{\pi_{0}^{(n)},\ldots,\pi_{i-1}^{(n)}\}=\widehat{P}_{i}
.

Suppose, for contradiction, that the decoder chooses
\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})}\neq s^{(\overline{i})}
. Since both values belong to
S^{(\overline{i})}
and
d_{\mathrm{min}}(S^{(\overline{i})})\geq d
, their difference is at least
d
. The unused symbols form a strictly increasing integer sequence, so order statistics whose ranks differ by at least
d
also differ in value by at least
d
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|\psi_{i}(\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})-\psi_{i}(s^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})|\geq d.

(36)

On the other hand, the nearest-candidate rule and the channel assumption imply

\displaystyle|\psi_{i}(\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})-\psi_{i}(s^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})|

\displaystyle\quad\leq|\psi_{i}(\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})};\widehat{P}_{i})-\rho_{i}|+|\rho_{i}-\pi_{i}|

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2|\rho_{i}-\pi_{i}|<d,

contradicting (
36
). Thus
\hat{s}^{(\overline{i})}=s^{(\overline{i})}
, completing the induction.

\Box

The coordinatewise hypothesis of Theorem
12
is equivalent to
d_{\infty}(\underline{\pi},\underline{\rho})<d/2
. Thus Algorithm
3
is a bounded-distance decoder for these REP codes.

Assume that each fixed head set is stored in increasing order with random access and that an order-statistics tree stores the unused symbols. At each step, the tree evaluates
\psi_{i}
in
O(\log n)
time
[
17
]
. Because
\psi_{i}
is monotone in
s
, binary search over
S^{(\overline{i})}
finds a nearest candidate using
O(\log|S^{(\overline{i})}|)
evaluations. The reconstructed symbol is then deleted from the tree in
O(\log n)
time. Since
|S^{(\overline{i})}|\leq n
, the overall per-codeword complexity is
O(n\log^{2}n)
.

VI
Conclusions and Future Work

The apparent simplicity of REP-code size growth hides a delayed tradeoff: a large head set can increase cardinality immediately while forcing later singleton-head extensions to restore distance. By measuring the remaining singleton-step requirement and relating it to the spacing shortfall of each head set, we converted this tradeoff into a global cardinality bound. For
n>d\geq 1
, the bound is attained by equally spaced heads and equals the cardinality of the corresponding DPGP construction. The result determines the exact maximum within the REP family. The length-
4
, distance-
2
example shows that equality of cardinalities does not imply preservation of all pairwise Chebyshev distances.

The recursive representation also yields a sequential encoder that outputs permutation symbols in coordinate order. Treating the reversed head sequence as Lehmer-code digits subject to
s^{(j)}\in S^{(j)}
gives an
O(n\log n)
implementation. If every two distinct elements of each head set differ by at least
d
, the sequential decoder corrects every received array at Chebyshev distance less than
d/2
and runs in
O(n\log^{2}n)
time.

Further directions include systematic REP encoders and soft-decision, list, and error-erasure decoders. Extending the method to other permutation metrics will require new distance analyses because the present extension lemmas are specific to the Chebyshev metric.
