On cycles in monotone grid classes of permutations
David Bevan, Robert Brignall, Nik Ruškuc

Abstract
We undertake a detailed investigation into the structure of permutations in monotone grid classes whose row-column graphs do not contain components with more than one cycle. Central to this investigation is a new decomposition, called the M M -sum, which generalises the well-known notions of direct sum and skew sum, and enables a deeper understanding of the structure of permutations in these grid classes. Permutations which are indecomposable with respect to the M M -sum play a crucial role in the structure of a grid class and of its subclasses, and this leads us to identify coils, a certain kind of permutation which corresponds to repeatedly traversing a chosen cycle in a particular manner. Harnessing this analysis, we give a precise characterisation for when a subclass of such a grid class is labelled well quasi-ordered, and we extend this to characterise (unlabelled) well quasi-ordering in certain cases. We prove that a large general family of these grid classes are finitely based, but we also exhibit other examples that are not, thereby disproving a conjecture from 2006 due to Huczynska and Vatter.

Introduction

Throughout the study of various types of combinatorial structures, and indeed general relational structures on finite sets,
decomposition strategies play a prominent role.
Very loosely speaking, a decomposition consists of partitioning the structure in such a way that the individual parts are well understood and ‘simpler’ than the whole, and so that the relationship between parts is also controlled.

One concrete example concerns the so-called modular decompositions. They date back to the 1950s and a talk of Fraïssé, of which only the abstract
[
23
]
survives, and
their use in graph theory can be traced back to Gallai’s work
[
24
,
25
]
.
Since then modular decompositions have become a standard tool, especially in topics related to algorithms and complexity; see
[
15
, Section 12.1]
for an introduction, and
[
10
,
19
,
22
,
27
]
for some recent results.

The defining feature of modular decompositions is that the relationship between points belonging to different blocks is completely determined.
Moving away from such a strict requirement,
in extremal graph (and hypergraph) theory, various decompositions are at the heart of many significant developments: besides the classical results of Turán, there is the celebrated Regularity Lemma of Szemeredi
[
38
]
, the Container method (originally due to Kleitman and Winston
[
33
]
), and notions of
\chi
-bounded families of graphs (originating with Gyárfás
[
26
]
, as a generalisation of perfect graphs).

In the study of hereditary properties of graphs, Balogh, Bollobás and Morris
[
9
]
showed that any graph class whose speed falls below the Bell numbers can be partitioned into a union of cliques and independent sets, so that the connections between each part are either very dense, or very sparse. This was subsequently generalised by Atminas
[
8
]
, and this result is in fact the graph-theoretic equivalent of the characterisation of monotone griddability in permutations, due to Huczynska and Vatter
[
29
]
.

For hereditary classes whose speed is smaller still, progressively finer structural decompositions are available, one notable example being the ‘letter graphs’ of Petkovšek
[
36
]
. These are the graph-theoretic analogue of geometrically griddable permutations
[
5
]
, as established by Alecu, Ferguson, Kanté, Lozin, Vatter and Zamaraev
[
7
]
.
There are therefore parallels at several levels between hereditary graph classes and permutation classes.

The purpose of this paper is to advance the framework of structural decomposition in the area of permutation classes, by undertaking a detailed analysis of the so-called monotone griddable classes. Central to our analysis is a new decomposition, called the
M
-sum (introduced in Section
3
), which significantly expands our understanding of this important family of permutation classes. A list of the consequences of our decomposition is provided later in this introduction.

The structural study of permutation classes has become a major area of endeavour in the last 30 years, motivated by questions in enumerative combinatorics, by parallels with the study of graph classes, and as a topic in its own right. Indeed, the phase transitions in the number and complexity of permutation classes whose growth rates are ‘small’, as established by Kaiser and Klazar
[
32
]
and Vatter
[
39
,
41
]
, are exemplars of the interplay between enumeration and structure. Vatter
[
41
]
also gives a good account of the parallels with the aforementioned study of speeds of hereditary graph classes, which started with Scheinerman and Zito
[
37
]
.

Within this structural study, given a suitable description of some permutation class
\mathcal{C}
, one may ask a number of pertinent questions, such as:

•

Does
\mathcal{C}
have some kind of concise definition? For example, is it finitely based?

•

Can the structure of the permutations in
\mathcal{C}
be
succinctly represented?

•

Does
\mathcal{C}
have some notable order-theoretic properties? For example, is it well quasi-ordered or even labelled well quasi-ordered?

•

Does
\mathcal{C}
have good enumerative properties, such as a tractable generating function, or at least an asymptotic growth that can be
explicitly given or algorithmically computed?

Over recent years it has transpired that a key role in many developments in each of the above strands is played by the
grid classes
.
Roughly speaking, a grid class comprises permutations which, when plotted in the plane, can be subdivided into cells in a grid-like manner so that the entries in each cell belong to a simpler permutation class, specified by a matrix entry.
This notion originates from the “profile classes” studied by Murphy and Vatter
[
34
]
, and the name ‘grid classes’ was introduced in
[
29
]
.

In full generality, where there are no restrictions on the types of classes that the matrix may contain, we have little understanding about the behaviour of the resulting
general
grid classes, beyond knowing their growth rates (see Albert and Vatter
[
4
]
), and some results relating to well quasi-order and labelled well-quasi-order, see Brignall
[
16
,
17
]
.

At the other end of the spectrum are the
geometric grid classes
, in which not only must the entries in each cell of a subdivided permutation form a
monotone
sequence,
but it is additionally required that the permutations in the class can be plotted in such a way that their points lie on the cell diagonals.
A major study, due to Albert, Atkinson, Bouvel, Ruškuc and Vatter
[
5
]
, showed (among other results) that every geometric grid class is finitely based, well quasi-ordered and enumerated by a rational generating function. Brignall and Vatter
[
20
]
subsequently established that geometric grid classes are in fact labelled well quasi-ordered.

The monotone grid classes that form the basis of this article form an intermediate family of grid classes. As they are the only classes we consider in the sequel, we will henceforth refer to them simply as ‘grid classes’.
A
gridding matrix
is a matrix
M
whose entries are all in
\{0,\pm 1\}
, and we let
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
denote the
grid class
of
M
, which comprises all permutations which can be divided into a rectangular grid of cells (of the same dimensions as
M
), such that each cell
ij
of the permutation contains no entries if
M_{ij}=0
, contains entries forming a monotone increasing sequence if
M_{ij}=1
, and contains a monotone decreasing sequence if
M_{ij}=-1
.

For a gridding matrix
M
with
m
columns and
n
rows, the
row-column graph
of
M
, denoted
G_{M}
, is the bipartite graph with vertices

\{1,\dots,m\}\,\cup\,\{1^{\prime},\dots,n^{\prime}\}

such that
ij^{\prime}\in E(G_{M})
whenever the corresponding entry
M_{ij}
of
M
is non-zero.

1

1

A related concept, known as the

, has also been used in the literature (see, for example, Vatter

),

The graphs

. For example, the growth rate of
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
is equal to the square of the spectral radius of

[

).

With results like these in mind, the structure of the components in the graphs

provides us with a taxonomy for the classes

, and we borrow terminology from graphs to name them.

•

If

contains no cycles (that is, every connected component of

is a tree), then we say that

is
acyclic
.

•

If

has exactly one cycle, then

is

,
2
2

2

.
while if

is isomorphic to a cycle then
M
is
cyclic
.

•

When every component of
G_{M}
is either unicyclic or a tree, we say that

is a
pseudoforest
.

•

If
G_{M}
contains a component with more than one cycle, then

is

.

Note that every cycle in

necessarily has even length at least four, since

is a bipartite graph.

We also use the above terms to describe the grid class
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
. While acyclic classes are already well understood (both by Murphy and Vatter
[
34
]
, and by the fact that all such classes are geometric grid classes so the results of
[
5
]
apply), additional tools are required to understand more complex row-column graphs.

Our attention in this paper focuses on the structure of permutations in cyclic, unicyclic and pseudoforest grid classes. By analysing the permutations in
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
that are indecomposable with respect to the
M
-sum (Definition
3.1
), we identify an unavoidable family of permutations called coils (Definition
3.9
), formed by repeatedly traversing a chosen cycle of

in a particular manner.
Harnessing the analysis in Section
3
, our main results are as follows.

Theorem
4.1

A subclass of a pseudoforest grid class is labelled well quasi-ordered if and only if it contains only bounded length coils.

Theorem
4.13

It is possible to decide whether a finitely based subclass of a pseudoforest grid class is labelled well quasi-ordered.

Theorem
5.1

Every unicyclic grid class is finitely based.

Proposition
5.11

There exist pseudoforest grid classes that are not finitely based.

Theorem
6.5

A subclass of a cyclic grid class is well quasi-ordered if and only if it contains only finitely many end-inflated coils.

Proposition
5.11
disproves a 2006 conjecture due to Huczynska and Vatter
[
29
, Conjecture 2.3]
, which claimed that all monotone grid classes must be finitely based. On the other hand, Theorem
5.1
establishes that the conjecture does hold in a smaller family of grid classes.

Section

Permutations and permutation classes

The uninitiated reader may wish to refer to Bevan
[
14
]
or Vatter
[
40
]
for a broader introduction to the area of permutation classes than that given here.
A permutation
\pi=\pi(1)\cdots\pi(n)
of length
|\pi|=n
is an ordering of the numbers
1,\dots,n
, though we frequently refer to a permutation
\pi
by its point set
\{(i,\pi(i))\,:\,i=1,\dots,n\}
. Indeed, this point set leads us to the crucial graphical perspective upon which the notion of grid classes depends: the
plot
of
\pi
is the plot of its point set in the plane.

Given two permutations
\pi
and
\sigma
of lengths
n
and
k
, respectively, we say that
\sigma
is
contained
in
\pi
if there exists a subsequence
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
such that
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
, and the sequence
\pi(i_{1})\cdots\pi(i_{k})
forms a set of points in the same relative order as those in
\sigma
(that is,
\pi(i_{1})\cdots\pi(i_{k})
is
order isomorphic
to
\sigma
). A witness of this containment forms a
copy
, or an
embedding
, of
\sigma
in
\pi
. Containment forms a partial order on the set of all permutations, and when
\sigma
is contained in
\pi
we can write
\sigma\leq\pi
; if, on the other hand, there is no copy of
\sigma
in
\pi
, then we write
\sigma\not\leq\pi
, and say that
\pi

avoids

\sigma
.

A
permutation class
is a set of permutations that is closed under containment. That is, if
\pi
belongs to a permutation class
\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma\leq\pi
, then
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
. The
basis
of a class
\mathcal{C}
is the set of minimal forbidden permutations not in
\mathcal{C}
; such a set is unique, but need not be finite.
For a set
B
we write
\mathsf{Av}(B)
for the permutation class consisting of all permutations that avoid all elements of
B
.
If
B
is the basis of
\mathcal{C}
, then of course
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{Av}(B)
, and if
B
is finite then we say that
\mathcal{C}
is
finitely based
.

The bases of grid classes

Prior to this work, relatively little general progress had been made on Huczynska and Vatter’s conjecture, other than the results mentioned earlier for geometric grid classes in
[
5
]
.
In his PhD thesis
[
43
, Theorem 4.7.5]
Waton showed that one
2\times 2
grid class is finitely based, and subsequently Albert and Brignall
[
2
]
extended Waton’s argument to cover all
2\times 2
grid classes.

Well quasi-ordering in grid classes

A permutation class is
well quasi-ordered
if it contains no infinite antichains with respect to the containment ordering.
We defer the core definitions regarding this property and its labelled version until Section
4
, but give here some context for our work.
In general, the property of being well quasi-ordered is seen as an indicator of ‘tameness’, as described by Cherlin
[
21
]
. For example, every well quasi-ordered permutation class contains at most countably many distinct subclasses, whereas those that are not well quasi-ordered must have uncountably many subclasses (and, therefore, also uncountably many subclasses whose enumeration sequences are intractably difficult). As such, the presence (or otherwise) of infinite antichains dictates much of what we know regarding the structure and growth rates of the so-called “small” permutation classes, see Vatter
[
39
,
41
]
.

As mentioned earlier, Murphy and Vatter
[
34
]
established that a grid class is well quasi-ordered if and only if its graph is acyclic. It may seem something of a surprise, therefore, to note how much of this paper is dedicated to the study of well quasi-ordering in grid classes. Our interest here is in the
subclasses
of pseudoforest grid classes, and there are two principal reasons for this:

•

Our result that unicyclic grid classes are finitely based fundamentally relies on the characterisation of labelled well quasi-order given in Theorem
4.1
; in fact, our counterexamples to the 2006 conjecture are also unlikely to have been discovered without the development of
M
-sums and coils.

•

Our work here is intended to initiate a programme of study similar to that undertaken for subclasses of the 321-avoiding permutations in Albert, Brignall, Ruškuc and Vatter
[
3
]
.
Indeed, it is shown in
[
3
]
that every finitely based or well quasi-ordered subclass of
\mathsf{Av}(321)
is enumerated by a rational generating function, even though
\mathsf{Av}(321)
itself is not.
3
3

3

The 321-avoiding permutations are famously one of the classes of combinatorial structures enumerated by the Catalan numbers, which means it has an algebraic, but not rational, generating function.

The comparison with
\mathsf{Av}(321)
is not a mere coincidence: although
\mathsf{Av}(321)
is not a monotone grid class in our sense, one could view it as the class of the “infinite staircase” matrix

\scriptsize\begin{pmatrix}&&&&&&\iddots\\
&&&&1&1&\\
&&&1&1&&\\
&&1&1&&&\\
&1&1&&&&\\
\iddots&&&&&&\end{pmatrix}.

The resulting “staircase decomposition” of
\mathsf{Av}(321)
, which is one of the main tools in
[
3
]
, very strongly resembles the decomposition we obtain here from coils.

We stop short of attempting to derive enumerative consequences of our work for subclasses of pseudoforest grid classes, but refer the curious reader to our concluding remarks on the topic.

Given an
m\times n
gridding matrix
M
, an
M
-gridding
of a permutation
\pi
of length
\ell
is a division of the points in the plot of
\pi
into a grid of
m\times n
rectangles, called
cells
, such that the cell
(i,j)
contains no points if
M_{ij}=0
, and otherwise the points in this cell form an increasing sequence if
M_{ij}=1
, or a decreasing sequence if
M_{ij}=-1
.
The latter two options also include the possibility of the cell containing no points.
More formally, such a gridding can be viewed as a pair

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{H})
where
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m-1}\}
and
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{n-1}\}

m-1

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\>=\>v_{0}\><\>v_{1}\><\>\cdots\><\>v_{m-1}\><\>v_{m}\>=\>\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}\text{, ~~and}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\>=\>h_{0}\><\>h_{1}\><\>\cdots\><\>h_{n-1}\><\>h_{n}\>=\>\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}.\phantom{\text{ ~~and}}

The permutation
\pi
together with the gridding is referred to as a

, and we write

\pi^{\#}=(\pi,\mathcal{V},\mathcal{H})
.
The rectangle
\{(x,y)\>:\>v_{i-1}<x<v_{i},\ h_{j-1}<y<h_{j}\}
will be referred to as the

.
Sometimes we will identify a cell with the set of all points of
\pi^{\#}
that belong to
C_{ij}
.

-griddings; see Figure

to refer to a second gridding of the permutation

.

The collection of all permutations that possess an

of

, which we denote by
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
.
If
\mathcal{C}
is a permutation class such that every

possesses an

i.e. if
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathsf{Grid}(M)
, then we say that
\mathcal{C}
is

-

.

We denote the collection of all
M
-gridded permutations by
\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(M)
.
Note that the elements of
\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(M)
are
not
permutations, and if
\sigma^{\#}

\pi^{\#}
are two

\sigma\leq\pi
, then it need not be the case that
\sigma
can be embedded in
\pi
in such a way as to respect the two given griddings. However, there is the notion of

: we say that
\sigma^{\#}\leq\pi^{\#}
if there exists a subsequence
1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{|\sigma|}\leq|\pi|
of

that is order isomorphic to

, such that the entries

(j,\sigma(j))
of

of

Equipped with gridded containment, we observe that for any

-griddable class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathsf{Grid}(M)
, the set of gridded permutations

is a downset: that is, if

and

, then

2.2
Partial multiplication matrices and griddings

Let
M
be an
m\times n
gridding matrix, and recall that the vertices of
G_{M}
are

\{1,\dots,m\}\,\cup\,\{1^{\prime},\dots,n^{\prime}\}
.
For any cycle
C
in
G_{M}
, the
sign
of the cycle is the product

\prod_{ij^{\prime}\in E(C)}M_{ij}
,
where
E(C)
is the set of edges of
C
.
Thus, each cycle in

Lemma 3.8
.

Let

be a partial multiplication matrix, and let

be a non-singleton indivisible

\pi^{\#}
that correspond to the edges of some cycle of

Proof.

Consider the permutation
\alpha^{\#}
, formed by taking the last point from every non-empty cell of
\pi^{\#}
. We first show that

must contain a non-singleton indivisible.

Suppose to the contrary that
\alpha^{\#}
can be decomposed as an

with each gridded

being a gridding of the permutation 1. Now let

\alpha_{i}^{\#}

a_{i}\rightarrow a_{j}
only if

Since

is strongly connected, there exists

such that
a_{k}\rightarrow v
.
As
a_{k}
is the last entry in its cell,

cannot lie in that same cell.
Let

(for some

b_{1}\rightarrow b_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow b_{\ell}\rightarrow b_{1}

∎

By construction, each non-empty cell in

Theorem 3.14
.

Let

Proof.

By Observation
3.6
, the points of the

-indivisible permutation

belong to a single component

. If

\pi^{\#}
is not a singleton. By Observation
3.7

\pi^{\#}
contains at least one point from each cell around some cycle, which means that the component

denote the length of this cycle, and let

denote the number of edges in

.

We now proceed inductively on the number

has a refined gridding using an acyclic matrix that has at most

, we see that

The base case, in which

, follows by Lemma

, and the refined gridding of

Now suppose that

that has no other non-empty cells in either its column or its row. (Such a cell must correspond to an edge in

The gridded subpermutation of

In this section, we work towards the following characterisation for subclasses of pseudoforest grid classes. Recall that an

Theorem 4.1

Let

4.1
The well quasi-ordering toolkit

In this introductory subsection, we give only the minimal terminology required for our purposes, and refer the reader to Brignall and Vatter

Given a quasi-ordered set
5
5

5

Recall that a

(X,\leq)

A quasi-order

In the context of collections of combinatorial structures with some notion of embedding, the requirement that there are no infinite strictly descending chains is trivially satisfied, since such quasi-orders are always well-founded.

One widely-used tool in the study of wqo is Higman’s Lemma

Theorem 4.3

(Higman’s Lemma

is wqo under the generalised subword ordering.

Let

Lemma 4.7

(\sigma^{\#},\ell_{\sigma^{\#}})

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that

into
(\pi^{\#},\ell_{\pi^{\#}})
.
Then the image
\phi((\sigma^{\#},\ell_{\sigma^{\#}}))
in

must include

and

and

By Lemma

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a permutation class. If there exists a cyclic partial multiplication matrix

contains arbitrarily long

is not labelled well quasi-ordered.

Proof.

Suppose that

Bases of pseudoforest grid classes

We now turn our attention to the bases of grid classes.
If

.

Let

Given a permutation (or gridded permutation), we say that two points
u
and

are
horizontally separated
if there exists a third point
w
such that
w
lies between
u
and
v
by value, but not by position. Similarly, if there exists a point
x

u
and

and

or
\{u,v,x\}
can form a monotone pattern, and thus if
u
and
v
belong to the same cell of a gridded permutation, then neither
w
nor
x
can belong to that cell.

The following observation follows readily from the axioms defining coils,
C1
–
C4
.

Observation 5.4
.

If
u,v
are nonconsecutive points in some cell of a gridded
M

u
and
v
are separated both horizontally and vertically.

Next, we establish a technical proposition that provides a bound, for any gridding of a coil, on the number of points in any cell, in terms of the number of points in other cells in the same row or column.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
M
be a partial multiplication matrix with at least one cycle, and let
\pi^{\natural}
be a gridded
M
-coil defined on a cycle in
M
of length
\ell
.

Let
\pi^{\#}
be any other
M
-gridding of
\pi

c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
the non-empty cells of
\pi^{\#}
that belong to some single row or column of
M
. If cell
c_{i}
contains
k_{i}
points for
1\leq i\leq r
, and
S_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}k_{j}
, then

k_{i}\leq 2\ell(S_{i}+1).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose that
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
are the non-empty cells belonging to some row of
\pi^{\#}
. For a contradiction, suppose that the cell
c_{i}
contains at least
2\ell(S_{i}+1)+1
points. By the pigeonhole principle, this implies that
c_{i}
must contain at least

2(S_{i}+1)+1

points that belong to a single cell in the gridded
M
-coil
\pi^{\natural}
.

Within this collection of points we can identify at least
S_{i}+1
nonoverlapping nonconsecutive pairs of points, as illustrated in Figure
11
. By Observation
5.4
each of these pairs must be separated in
\pi^{\natural}
by some other entry in the same row, giving a collection of at least
S_{i}+1
distinct separating points in
\pi^{\natural}
. In the gridding
\pi^{\#}
, all these separating points must belong to the cells
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
, except none can lie in

. However, there are only

points available in the other cells of this row.

Now let
M
be a unicyclic partial multiplication matrix, and define
\pi^{\natural}
and
\pi^{\#}
as in Proposition
5.5
. Consider a cell
c
of
\pi^{\#}
that corresponds to an edge incident with a leaf of
G_{M}
. Such a cell is necessarily the only non-empty cell in its row or column, and by applying Proposition
5.5
to this row or column, we conclude that cell
c
contains at most
2\ell
(misplaced) points.

Similarly, if
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
are the non-empty cells of a row of
\pi^{\#}
with the property that
c_{1},\dots,c_{r-1}
correspond to edges incident with leaves of
G_{M}
, then the remaining non-empty cell
c_{r}
in that row can contain at most

2\ell\big(2\ell(r-1)+1\big)=4\ell^{2}(r-1)+2\ell

misplaced points.

We now iterate this process: as soon as all but one of the non-empty cells in a row or column have a bound on the number of misplaced points they contain, we can apply Proposition
5.5
to establish a bound for the misplaced points in the final non-empty cell (which by definition is not part of the cycle). This process will eventually bound the number of points in each non-empty cell of
\pi^{\#}
that is not associated with an edge from the single cycle of
M
. While these bounds are surely a significant overestimate of the actual number of points that the cells can contain, this quantity depends only on properties of the matrix
M
, rather than of the coil
\pi
. Considering the sum of the bounds on all such cells, we conclude the following.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
M
be a unicyclic partial multiplication matrix.
There is a bound
B
, depending only on
M
, such that the following holds:
if
\pi^{\#}
is any
M
-gridding of an
M
-coil
\pi
, then
\pi^{\#}
has at most
B
misplaced points.

As well as there being a bound on the number of misplaced points, there are also restrictions on which points can be misplaced, as a consequence of the following simple observation concerning points sandwiched between two others in a cell.

Observation 5.7
.

Given any gridding matrix
M
, let
\pi^{\natural}
and
\pi^{\#}
be two
M
-griddings of an arbitrary
\pi\in\mathsf{Grid}(M)
.
Suppose
u,v,w
is a monotone triple of points from a single cell
c
of
\pi^{\natural}
.
If
u
and
w
also share a common cell
c^{\prime}
in
\pi^{\#}
, then
v
lies in
c^{\prime}
too.

In the case that
M
is unicyclic and
\pi
is a coil, we have the following specialisation, given that
\pi
has a standard gridding on the cycle.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
M
be a unicyclic partial multiplication matrix whose cycle has length
\ell
, and
let
\pi^{\#}
be any gridded
M
-coil with points
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
.
For any suitable
i,j,k
, if
v_{i-j\ell}
and
v_{i+k\ell}
share a common cell in
\pi^{\#}
, then
v_{i}
also lies in that cell in
\pi^{\#}
.

5.2
Proof of Theorem
5.1

The proof of Theorem
5.1
will be completed by considering the longest possible coil that can be contained in a basis element
\beta
of some
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
, where
M
is a unicyclic partial multiplication matrix.
We will establish a bound on the length of such coils that depends only on
M
and not on
\beta
. We can then conclude that each basis element can be formed by adding a single point to a permutation that belongs to an lwqo subclass of the given grid class. In turn, this is sufficient to establish that the basis elements themselves belong to an lwqo class, and consequently the basis (which forms an unlabelled antichain) must be finite. The bulk of the remaining work is in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
M
be a unicyclic partial multiplication matrix, and let
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{Grid}(M)
.
There is a bound, which depends only on
M
, on the length of the longest coil contained in any basis element
\beta
of
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let the length of the cycle in
M
be
\ell
, and let
L=(\ell+1)\ell^{2}+1
.
Recall that, by Lemma
5.2
, any coil of length at least
L
has only one gridding on the cycle of
M
.
Also, let
B
denote the bound from Lemma
5.6
on the total number of misplaced points in any
M
-gridding of a coil.
We claim that any coil contained in
\beta
has length less than
K=(4B+2)L+2\ell+1
.

To derive a contradiction, suppose that
\beta
contains an
M
-coil
\xi
with at least
K
points.
Note that
\beta
has at least one point not on
\xi
because
\xi
is
M
-griddable; indeed
\xi
can be gridded on the cycle.
Note also that, by Lemma
5.2
,
\xi
only has one gridding,
\xi^{\#}
say, on the cycle.

Fix an embedding
v_{1},\dots,v_{K}
of
\xi
in
\beta
.
Let
m=(2B+1)L+2\ell+1
and let
p
be the point
v_{m}
.
Let
\sigma
be the subpermutation of
\beta
formed of the points of
\xi
before

, and
\tau
the subpermutation formed of the points of
\xi
after

.
Note that both
\sigma
and
\tau
are
M
-coils of length at least
(2B+1)L
, this bound being tight for
\tau
.

Consider
\beta_{p}=\beta-p
, and note that since
\beta
is a basis element of
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
, we have
\beta_{p}\in\mathsf{Grid}(M)
.
Fix any
M
-gridding
\beta_{p}^{\#}
of
\beta_{p}
, and let
\sigma^{\#}
and
\tau^{\#}
denote the inherited
M
-griddings of the subpermutations
\sigma
and
\tau
of
\beta_{p}
.

Let us now consider in detail the structure of
\sigma^{\#}
; exactly the same analysis will apply to
\tau^{\#}
.
By Lemma
5.6
, at most
B
points of
\sigma
are misplaced in
\sigma^{\#}
,
the remaining points of
\sigma
being gridded on the cycle of
\mathcal{C}
.
These points are divided (by the removal of the misplaced points) into at most
B+1
contiguous portions.

If one of these portions of
\sigma
has length
L
or greater, then by Lemma
5.2
, there is only one way in which it can be gridded on the cycle.
Suppose there were two such portions,
\varphi=v_{a},\ldots,v_{b}
and
\psi=v_{c},\ldots,v_{d}
(where

), each of length at least
L
.
Then each would have a unique gridding,
\varphi^{\#}
and
\psi^{\#}
say, on the cycle.
Moreover, since their

-sum
\psi^{\#}\boxplus\varphi^{\#}
is contained in
\xi^{\#}
(the unique gridding of
\xi
on the cycle), we know that this is how
\varphi
and
\psi
would be gridded.
Thus, by Corollary
5.8
, each point

,

, would also be gridded on the cycle, and so would not be misplaced, since for each such

there would be a point of
\varphi^{\#}
and a point of
\psi^{\#}
sandwiching

in a common cell of the cycle.
Hence only one portion of
\sigma
can have length

or greater.

As a consequence, the only points of
\sigma
that can be misplaced in any

-gridding are its first
BL
points and its last
BL
points.
In particular, there is a contiguous portion of
\sigma
of length at least

all of whose points are gridded on the cycle in any

-gridding.
Similarly, there is a contiguous portion of
\tau
of length at least
L
that contains no misplaced points in any
M
-gridding.
Call these portions
\alpha
and
\omega
, respectively.
Because of their length and the fact that they are gridded on the cycle, by Lemma
5.2
, their

-griddings,
\alpha^{\#}
and
\omega^{\#}
, are unique.
Moreover, since their
M
-sum
\omega^{\#}\boxplus\alpha^{\#}
is contained in
\xi^{\#}
, we know that this is how
\alpha
and
\omega
are gridded.
Furthermore, by Corollary
5.8
, no point of
\xi-p
that succeeds

and precedes

is misplaced.
Thus we may assume that
\alpha
is a suffix of
\sigma
and that

is a prefix of

Let

denote the cell that contains the points

(belonging to

) and

(belonging to

), and let

that lie in cell

consists of entries from

and

form a monotone increasing sequence, oriented from bottom left to top right.

We now consider the action of re-inserting

, and below and to the left of

, and below and to the left of

Now let

Consider
\beta_{r}=\beta-r
.
We have

We require one final ingredient in order to prove Theorem
5.1
. Given a permutation class
\mathcal{C}
, the

of

is the class

\mathcal{C}^{+1}=\{\pi:\pi-p\in\mathcal{C}\text{ for some point }p\text{ of }\pi\}.

Lemma 5.10

(See Brignall and Vatter
[
20
, Theorem 4.5]
)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an lwqo permutation class, then the one-point extension class
\mathcal{C}^{+1}
is also lwqo.

We are finally in a position to complete the proof that
\mathsf{Grid}(M)
is finitely based when

is unicyclic.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

By Proposition
2.4
, we can assume that
M
is a unicyclic partial multiplication matrix.

Let
B
be the basis of
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{Grid}(M)
, and recall that
B
must be an antichain. By Lemma
5.9
, there is an absolute bound
K
on the length of the longest coil contained in any basis element of
B
. The same statement holds for any one-point deletion of an element in
B
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the subclass of
\mathcal{C}
comprising all permutations whose coils have length less than
K
. By Theorem
4.1
,
\mathcal{D}
is labelled well quasi-ordered.

Take
\beta\in B
. Any one-point deletion
\beta^{-}
must, by definition, belong to
\mathcal{C}
, but since
\beta^{-}
contains only coils of length less than

, we must in fact have
\beta^{-}\in\mathcal{D}
. Thus,

. Hence

, and
\mathcal{D}^{+1}
is lwqo by Lemma
5.10
, which means that
B
must be finite.
∎

there exists a coil decomposition

and associated coil

such that at least one of the following holds.

|B_{1}|=|B_{2}|=\cdots=|B_{\ell}|=1

2.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
M
be a cyclic partial multiplication matrix, and let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathsf{Grid}(M)
such that
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A}
is finite. Then there exists a constant
K
such that any
M
-indivisible element
\pi^{\#}
of
\mathcal{C}^{\#}
has a coil decomposition

B_{1},\dots,B_{m}

with the property that whenever
|B_{i}|>1
and
|B_{j}|>1
, then
|j-i|\leq K
.

Proof.

Consider any
M
-indivisible
\pi^{\#}\in\mathcal{C}^{\#}
and take a coil decomposition
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
with associated coil
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
which satisfies one (or more) of the conditions in Proposition
6.2
. Now take
i,j
(and without loss suppose
i<j
) as in the statement of the lemma, and consider points
x,y
, distinct from
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, such that
x\in B_{i}
and
y\in B_{j}
.

In the digraph
D_{\pi^{\#}}
, we know that
v_{i}\rightarrow x
and
v_{j}\rightarrow y
. Furthermore, by construction we know that
v_{j-1}\rightarrow y
, while there are two possibilities for the relationship between
x
and the coil point
v_{i+1}
in the cell
i+1
: either
x\rightarrow v_{i+1}
, or
v_{i+1}\rightarrow x
. We will show in each case how to construct an element of
\mathcal{A}
.

Case 1:

x\rightarrow v_{i+1}
. We claim that the set of points

S=\{x,v_{i},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j},y\}

forms a gridded subpermutation of
\pi^{\#}
that is order isomorphic to an element of
\mathcal{A}
. To see this, note that
v_{i},\dots,v_{j}
is a coil, so it suffices to show that
\{v_{i},x\}
and
\{v_{j},y\}
form intervals within the subpermutation defined by the set
S
. Since
v_{i}
and
x
belong to
B_{i}
, they belong to the same cell in
\pi^{\#}
. Consequently the only points in
S
that can separate
\{v_{i},x\}
must belong to
B_{i-1}\cup B_{i}\cup B_{i+1}
. However,
S\cap(B_{i-1}\cup B_{i}\cup B_{i+1})=\{v_{i},x,v_{i+1}\}
, yet
x\rightarrow v_{i+1}
and
v_{i}\rightarrow v_{i+1}
, which establishes that nothing in
S
separates
\{v_{i},x\}
. A similar argument applies to the pair
\{v_{j},y\}
, and so the points in
S
form an element of
\mathcal{A}
of length
j-i+3
.

Case 2:

v_{i+1}\rightarrow x
. There are two subcases, depending on whether
i\leq\ell
or
i>\ell
.

If
i>\ell
, then we claim that the set of points

S=\{x,v_{i-\ell},v_{i-\ell+1},\dots,v_{j},y\}

forms a gridded subpermutation of
\pi^{\#}
of length
j-i+\ell+3
that is order isomorphic to an element of
\mathcal{A}
. The argument is similar to the previous case: note that
x\rightarrow v_{i-\ell}
and
v_{i-\ell}\rightarrow v_{i-\ell+1}
together imply that
x\rightarrow v_{i-\ell+1}
.

If
i\leq\ell
then the coil decomposition of
\pi^{\#}
cannot satisfy condition (a) of Proposition
6.2
, so it must satisfy (b) and/or (c). If the decomposition satisfies (b), then instead of considering
x\in B_{i}
, we take
x^{\prime}\in B_{\ell+1}
with the property that
x^{\prime}\neq v_{\ell+1}
. The point
x^{\prime}
lies in a cell of index greater than
\ell
so is covered by one of the two earlier arguments, and we obtain an element of
\mathcal{A}
of length at least
j-i-\ell+2
.

On the other hand, if the decomposition satisfies neither (a) nor (b), then it must satisfy (c), which means that there exists some index
i^{\prime}\leq\ell
and a point
x^{\prime}\in B_{i^{\prime}}\setminus\{v_{i^{\prime}}\}
such that
x^{\prime}\rightarrow v_{i^{\prime}+1}
. If we consider the point
x^{\prime}
instead of
x
, then this case is again covered by one of the three earlier arguments, and we obtain an element of
\mathcal{A}
of length at least
j-i-\ell+2
.

This completes the case analysis. In every case we have constructed an end-inflated coil in
\mathcal{A}
, and the shortest this permutation can be is
j-i-\ell+2
. If
K^{\prime}
denotes the length of the longest element in
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A}
, then we have
j-i-\ell+2\leq K^{\prime}
, and so the result follows with
K=K^{\prime}-2+\ell
.
∎

We are now nearly ready to state and prove our characterisation of wqo for subclasses of cyclic classes. As noted earlier, the proof follows roughly the same argument as that for Theorem
4.1
, with the added complications that arise by the need to handle leading and trailing coils. Thus, before we state and prove our characterisation, we construct our decomposition in one final technical lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
M
be a cyclic partial multiplication matrix, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathsf{Grid}(M)
such that
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A}
is finite, and let
K
be as in Lemma
6.3
. For any
M
-indivisible element
\pi^{\#}\in\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(M)
, there exists an acyclic matrix
N
with at most
K+2
non-zero entries, such that
\pi^{\#}
can be reversibly encoded as a triple
(\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})\in\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Consider any
M
-indivisible gridded permutation
\pi^{\#}\in\mathcal{C}^{\#}
. If
\pi^{\#}
occupies a single cell, then it has length 1. In this case, the lemma is satisfied by constructing
N
to have the same dimensions as
M
, but with a single non-zero entry that corresponds to the cell containing the one point of
\pi^{\#}
. We set
\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural}=\pi^{\#}
, and
a_{\pi}=b_{\pi}=0
.

Thus we may assume from now on that
\pi^{\#}
occupies all
\ell
cells of the cycle. Take a coil decomposition
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
of
\pi^{\#}
that satisfies Lemma
6.3
, and note that
m\geq\ell
.

If there are boxes with more that one point, let
i
(resp.
j
) be the smallest (resp. the largest) index of such a box. Otherwise set
i=j=2
. Thus, the only nonsingleton boxes are among

B_{i},B_{i+1},\dots,B_{j},

and by Lemma
6.3
we have
|j-i|\leq K
.

Now let
\sigma
be the subpermutation of
\pi^{\#}
formed from the points in
B_{i-1}\cup B_{i}\cup\cdots\cup B_{j+1}
.
Note that
i>1
since
|B_{1}|=1
. If
B_{j+1}
does not exist (which occurs only if
j=m
), then we ‘pad’ our coil decomposition by creating an empty final box
B_{j+1}
, placed in the same cell as
B_{j-\ell+1}
, and preceding
B_{j-\ell+1}
according to that cell’s orientation.

We grid
\sigma
according to the coil decomposition: let
N
denote the acyclic partial multiplication matrix whose non-zero entries correspond to the boxes
B_{i-1},\dots,B_{j+1}
(essentially following the same process as in the proof of Lemma
3.13
). By construction,
\sigma\in\mathsf{Grid}(N)
, and let
\sigma^{\natural}
denote the
N
-gridding inherited from the coil decomposition of
\pi^{\#}
. (Note, we use
\natural
to emphasise that this is an
N
-gridding.)

Next, we specify the two positive integers
a_{\pi}
and
b_{\pi}
. We set
a_{\pi}=i-1
to record the number of coil points that precede the first non-singleton block
B_{i}
of
\pi^{\#}
. Similarly, we set
b_{\pi}=m-j
to record the number of coil points that follow the last non-singleton block
B_{j}
. Note that
a_{\pi}\geq 1
, and that
b_{\pi}=0
if and only if
j=m
(indicating that there is no final coil).

To complete the proof, we need to show that the triple
(\sigma^{\natural},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})\in\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is a reversible encoding of
\pi^{\#}
: that is, we must demonstrate how to recover
\pi^{\#}
from this triple.
This is clear for the encodings of permutations of length
1
, so we need only consider those of permutations of length
\geq\ell
.
We begin by constructing the boxes
B_{i-1},\dots,B_{j+1}
of
\pi^{\#}
, which contain precisely the points in the corresponding cells of
\sigma^{\natural}
. The boxes
B_{i-1}
and
B_{j+1}
are easily identified.

We now re-insert the coil points at the beginning and the end of
\pi^{\#}
, which we do iteratively.
Given the placement of a singleton cell
B_{k}=\{v_{k}\}
, there is a unique way in which to insert the preceding singleton cell
B_{k-1}=\{v_{k-1}\}
: we have
v_{k-1}\rightarrow v_{k}
, while for any
k^{\prime}>k
such that
B_{k^{\prime}}
is in the same row or column of
\pi^{\#}
, we have
x\rightarrow v_{k-1}
for all
x\in B_{k^{\prime}}
: that is,
B_{k-1}
comes after all entries in its row or column except for
v_{k}
. On the other hand, the position of
v_{k-1}
relative to any other entries in
\pi^{\#}
is determined by
M
.
Thus, we may place
v_{k-1}
relative to all later points in a unique way.
A similar process allows us to reinsert entries to the other end of the coil, and the numbers of times we iterate these steps is governed by the integers
a_{\pi}
and
b_{\pi}
, respectively.
Note that the case where
b_{\pi}=0
corresponds precisely to the case that
B_{j+1}
is empty.
Finally, we forget the partition of the points of
\pi^{\#}
into the boxes
B_{i}
in favour of the underlying
M
-gridding, observing that
B_{i}
,
B_{i+\ell}
,
B_{i+2\ell}
, …end up in one cell,
B_{i+1}
,
B_{i+1+\ell}
, …in another, and so on. Thus, we have recovered
\pi^{\#}
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
M
be a cycle partial multiplication matrix, and let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathsf{Grid}(M)
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is wqo if and only if
\mathcal{C}
contains only finitely many end-inflated
M
-coils.

Proof.

One direction is immediate from Proposition
6.1
: If
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A}
is not finite, then
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A}_{(s_{1},s_{2},f)}
is infinite for some triple of cells
(s_{1},s_{2},f)
, thus
\mathcal{C}
contains an infinite antichain.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{A}
is finite, and let
K
be the constant from Lemma
6.3
. By Lemma
6.4
, for each
M
-indivisible
\pi^{\#}\in\mathcal{C}^{\#}
, there exists an acyclic matrix
N
with at most
K+2
non-zero entries such that
\pi^{\#}
can be encoded as a triple
(\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})\in\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
. Note that
\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)
is labelled well quasi-ordered by Proposition
4.10
, and thus
\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is well quasi-ordered by Proposition
4.4
, when
\mathbb{N}
is endowed with the usual (total) ordering on natural numbers.

In general, two
M
-indivisible permutations in
\mathcal{C}^{\#}
do not need to be encoded using the same acyclic matrix. However, every such matrix contains at most
K+2
non-zero entries, and so there can be only finitely many distinct matrices arising from the encodings provided by Lemma
6.4
.

For any such acyclic gridding matrix
N
, define

\mathcal{C}_{N}=\{\pi^{\#}\in\mathcal{C}^{\#}:\pi^{\#}\text{ is $M$-indivisible and }\sigma_{\pi}^{\#}\in\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\}.

We need to show that any such set
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is well quasi-ordered. Since
\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is well quasi-ordered, this will follow by Lemma
4.5
(ii) if we can show that the mapping

\phi:\mathcal{C}_{N}\to\mathsf{Grid}^{\#}(N)\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N},

which sends each
\pi^{\#}
to its encoding
(\sigma^{\natural}_{\pi},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})
,
is order-reflecting.

To this end consider
\pi^{\#}
and
\tau^{\#}
in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
whose encodings
(\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})
and
(\sigma_{\tau}^{\natural},a_{\tau},b_{\tau})
have the property that

(\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})\leq(\sigma_{\tau}^{\natural},a_{\tau},b_{\tau}).

This means that
\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural}\leq\sigma_{\tau}^{\natural}
(as
N
-gridded permutations),
a_{\pi}\leq a_{\tau}
, and
b_{\pi}\leq b_{\tau}
.

To show that
\pi^{\#}\leq\tau^{\#}
, we begin with an embedding that witnesses
\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural}\leq\sigma_{\tau}^{\natural}
, noting that the points in the singleton cells of
\sigma_{\pi}^{\natural}
at the beginning and end (when they exist) must embed into the corresponding points in the singleton cells of
\sigma_{\tau}^{\natural}
. We now follow the process described above to embed successive points at the beginning and end of the coil decomposition of
\pi^{\#}
into the corresponding points of
\tau^{\#}
. Since
a_{\pi}\leq a_{\tau}
, and
b_{\pi}\leq b_{\tau}
, we are guaranteed to embed all such points of
\pi^{\#}
before we run out of points in
\tau^{\#}
. Thus,
\pi^{\#}\leq\tau^{\#}
, which completes the proof that
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is well quasi-ordered.

Since every
M
-indivisible permutation in
\mathcal{C}^{\#}
is encoded using some matrix
N
, and since there are only finitely many possible matrices
N
, the set of all
M
-indivisible permutations in
\mathcal{C}^{\#}
is contained in a finite union of well quasi-ordered sets
\mathcal{C}_{N}
, and is thus well quasi-ordered.
Lemma
4.8
now gives that
\mathcal{C}^{\#}
is well quasi-ordered.

Finally the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is well quasi-ordered now follows by observing that the mapping

\mathcal{C}^{\#}\to\mathcal{C},\ \pi^{\#}\mapsto\pi,

that removes the gridding (first considered in the proof of Lemma
4.7
) is order-preserving.
∎

7
Concluding remarks

Enumeration

We have not considered the enumeration of pseudoforest grid classes in this study. It is known (by
[
5
]
) that acyclic grid classes (and their subclasses) all possess rational generating functions. In his PhD thesis, Bevan
[
13
, Theorem 4.6]
used a ‘diagonalisation’ argument to show that the
gridded
permutations in a pseudoforest grid class have an algebraic generating function. He further conjectures that all pseudoforest grid classes have algebraic generating functions.

The barrier to making progress on the question of enumeration lies in our ability (or lack of) to handle permutations that possess multiple griddings. Even if Conjecture
5.3
could be proved, this is likely to be insufficient for what would be needed for a direct approach. Alternatively, one might wonder whether it is possible to combine the methods in
[
5
]
(where rationality is established by subtracting ‘bad’ griddings from the set of all griddings, and showing that both sets of griddings are encoded by regular languages) with those in
[
13
]
(where pseudoforest classes are formed from acyclic classes by identifying the points in pairs of cells), but this has so far not met with success.

Bases

While we have roughly indicated a boundary between grid classes that are finitely based and those that are not, there is certainly still scope for this to be tightened further. The construction employed in Proposition
5.11
only works in cases where the gridding matrix
M
possesses two identical cycles that are arranged around each other in a very particular way. Other constructions may well exist, but it is probably also the case that the methods used here to show that unicyclic classes are finitely based can be applied slightly more generally.

Well quasi-ordering for unicyclic classes

The question of well quasi-ordering in unicyclic grid classes, let alone pseudoforest grid classes, is more nuanced than the arguments given in this paper for cyclic classes. While we know that every infinite antichain necessarily must include elements that contain arbitrarily long coils, there are multiple ways for these to be ‘anchored’ in unicyclic classes. An example is given in Figure

. Further results are likely possible, but may descend rapidly into a technical case analysis.

\mathcal{C}

k

, which means that for any

\mathcal{C}
contains a grid class whose graph is a subdivision of a binary tree of depth
k

Clearly, an acyclic grid class has neither property, while pseudoforest grid classes have the long path property but not the deep tree property, see
[

or

In the context of this paper, long paths can be constructed by considering a refined gridding of permutations whose coil decompositions are arbitrarily long, with arbitrarily many interleaved points in each set of the partition. On the other hand, that we can decompose any permutation in a pseudoforest grid class using the

Polycyclic classes, on the other hand, do possess the deep tree property (and thus also the long path property), see
