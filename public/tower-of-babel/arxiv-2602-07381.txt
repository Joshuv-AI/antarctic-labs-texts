When the Model Said ‘No Comment’, We Knew Helpfulness Was Dead, Honesty Was Alive, and Safety Was Terrified
Gautam Siddharth Kashyap, Mark Dras, Usman Naseem

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) need to be in accordance with human values—being helpful , harmless , and honest (HHH)—is important for safe deployment. Existing works use Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) to align LLMs. However, these works face challenges in multi-objective settings, such as SFT leading to interference between conflicting objectives, while MoEs suffer from miscalibrated routing. We term this failure mode Axis Collapse , marked by (1) disjoint feature spaces causing catastrophic forgetting , and (2) unreliable inference from misrouted experts . To resolve this, we propose AlignX , a two-stage framework. Stage 1 uses prompt-injected fine-tuning to extract axis-specific task features, mitigating catastrophic forgetting . Stage 2 deploys a MoCaE module that calibrates expert routing using fractal and natural geometry, improving inference reliability. AlignX achieves significant gains on Alpaca (Helpfulness), BeaverTails (Harmlessness), and TruthfulQA (Honesty), with +171.5% win rate, +110.1% in truthfulness-informativeness, and 4.3% fewer safety violations. It also reduces latency and memory usage by over 35% compared to prior MoEs. Results across four LLMs validate its generalizability. Code and data are available at: https://github.com/gskgautam/AlignX

Introduction

As Large Language Models (LLMs) grow increasingly central to real-world applications
Naseem et al. 2025
;
Haltaufderheide and Ranisch 2024
;
Alhafni et al. 2024
;
Cheong et al. 2024
, the demand for models that are not only powerful but also aligned with human values has become more urgent
Liu et al. 2023
. Ideally, LLMs should be
helpful
(providing useful and relevant responses),
harmless
(avoiding outputs that cause harm or offense), and
honest
(providing truth and acknowledging limitations)—a trifecta of alignment objectives important to user trust and societal safety
Ren et al. 2025b
;
Ren et al. 2025a
;
Maskey et al. 2025b
;
Maskey et al. 2025a
;
Liu et al. 2023
. Achieving these objectives simultaneously, however, presents two fundamental challenges
Millière 2025
. First, alignment axes trained in isolation produce disjoint feature spaces that interfere during inference, leading to
catastrophic forgetting
(where optimizing for one alignment goal causes the model to forget previously learned behaviors). Second, most systems lack per-instance calibration, resulting in
miscalibrated expert routing
(where the model selects the wrong alignment pathway or mixes alignment signals inappropriately) when alignment needs vary across queries. Together, these limitations cause what we term
Axis Collapse
: a systemic failure mode in which
helpfulness
,
harmlessness
, and
honesty
or HHH collide at inference time. While
catastrophic forgetting
and
miscalibrated expert routing
are often treated as distinct problems
Zhai et al. 2023
;
Zhai et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Cai et al. 2024
, we present them as concrete manifestations of
Axis Collapse
(see Figure
1
).

Past research has addressed symptoms of
Axis Collapse
—notably
catastrophic forgetting
and
miscalibrated expert routing
—but in isolation. For
catastrophic forgetting
, full-model fine-tuning (SFT) and RLHF
Tekin et al. 2024
often degrade performance across objectives. Modular task vector approaches
Cheng et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024
avoid retraining but suffer from interference when tasks lie in divergent subspaces; for example,
Cheng et al. 2025
assumes geometric alignment, while aTLAS
Zhang et al. 2024
requires backbone consistency. For
miscalibrated expert routing
, methods like temperature scaling
Guo et al. 2017
and Soft Dawid-Skene
Kuzin et al. 2025
assume static, unified logit spaces. FRACAL
Alexandridis et al. 2025
introduces geometric calibration via fractal dimensions, but only operates on output logits and lacks generality for LLMs.

To resolve these, we propose
AlignX
, a two-stage framework designed to resolve the systemic failure mode of
Axis Collapse
. Stage 1 resolves
catastrophic forgetting
by using task vectors with alignment-specific feature matrices, extracted via prompt-injected fine-tuning of LLMs. These matrices capture behavioral signatures for HHH. Stage 2 resolves
miscalibrated expert routing
by introducing the
Mixture-of-Calibrated-Experts (MoCaE)
module. Rather than focusing on post-hoc scaling, MoCaE performs per-instance calibration within the network via a fractal calibrator, which detects geometric rarity using Stage 1 features, and a natural calibrator, which clusters token-level activations to assess semantic coherence. In summary, our contributions are twofold:

•

We propose AlignX, a two-stage framework via prompt-injected fine-tuning and calibrated expert routing to resolve multi-objective conflicts across HHH.

•

Empirically, AlignX—achieving relative gains of 171.5% in win rate, a 4.3% reduction in unsafe completions, and 110.1% improvement in truthfulness-informativeness. AlignX reduces memory usage and inference latency by over 35% compared to MoE-based alignment works.

Related Works

LLM Alignment:

LLM alignment aims to achieve the core objectives of HHH, as emphasized by InstructGPT
Ouyang et al. 2022
, RLHF
Zhong et al. 2025
, and Constitutional AI
Bai et al. 2022
. While Section
1
outlines key challenges such as
catastrophic forgetting
and
miscalibrated expert routing
, prior work
Liu et al. 2024
;
Ji et al. 2024
has shown these limitations in practice and has struggled to fully resolve multi-objective conflicts. This motivates a central question:
How can we overcome catastrophic forgetting and miscalibrated expert routing to resolve Axis Collapse and achieve reliable multi-objective alignment without retraining or extensive supervision?

Task Vectors:

Task vectors represent weight differences between a pre-trained model and its fine-tuned variant, supporting efficient model merging and transfer. Prior methods
Cheng et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024
rely on homogeneous architectures and assume aligned input subspaces, making them ineffective for semantically distinct tasks (e.g.,
helpfulness
vs.
honesty
) and prone to
catastrophic forgetting
—as previously discussed in Section
1
.

Mixture-of-Experts:

MoE models like Switch Transformer
Fedus et al. 2022
and GShard
Lepikhin et al. 2021
improve scalability by sparsely activating experts but often lack semantic alignment in routing. Modular alignment methods such as H
3
Fusion
Tekin et al. 2024
ensemble aligned LLMs using a two-step MoE with FFN fine-tuning and instruction-based routing. However, H
3
Fusion still suffer from
miscalibrated expert routing
, where expert selection is poorly matched to input semantics, causing inconsistent alignment at inference (see Section
1
). This highlights the need for better instance-aware calibration in MoE routing. As they fall short in jointly optimizing HHH objectives due to axis interference, forgetting, and unreliable routing. They either assume aligned subspaces or require costly supervision. In contrast, AlignX introduces alignment-specific task-feature extraction and calibrated expert routing, enabling modular, semantically aware alignment without retraining or manual rules.

3
Method

Overview of Proposed Method:

AlignX is a two-stage framework for aligning LLMs across multiple objectives (see Figure
2
). Stage 1 extracts task-feature matrices via prompt-injected fine-tuning, while Stage 2 uses the MoCaE module to route queries, calibrate expert outputs, and reinject calibrated embeddings for axis-aware generation.

3.1
Fine-Tuning Module

Let
M_{0}
denote the base LLaMA-2-7B

1

https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-hf
model with parameters
\theta_{0}
. For each alignment property
a\in\{\textit{helpful},\textit{harmless},\textit{honest}\}
, we fine-tune
M_{0}
on an alignment-specific dataset
\mathcal{D}_{a}
augmented with property-focused prompt injection examples
\mathcal{P}_{a}
, as shown in Figure
3
. This yields a fine-tuned model
M_{a}
with parameters
\theta_{a}
tailored to behavior
a
.

Prompt injection templates used during alignment-specific fine-tuning. Each alignment axis is reinforced with a targeted

,

honest

We first compute the task vector
\Delta\theta_{a}=\theta_{a}-\theta_{0}
to capture the parameter-level shift introduced by the alignment objective. This delta reflects how the model structurally adapts in weight space to emphasize behavior
a
. Next, we reload the fine-tuned model
M_{a}
and run it on inputs from
\mathcal{D}_{a}\cup\mathcal{P}_{a}
to extract hidden activations. Let
f_{l}^{(a)}(x)
be the hidden representation at transformer layer
l
for input
x
under
M_{a}
. We compute the alignment-aware feature vector:
F_{a}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{a}\cup\mathcal{P}_{a}}\left[f^{(a)}_{l}(x)\right]
. This captures the average behavioral footprint of the model in semantic space under the influence of alignment axis
a
. To unify structural and behavioral adaptation signals, we construct a task-feature matrix
T_{a}
via a learned fusion mechanism:
T_{a}=\phi(\Delta\theta_{a},F_{a})=W_{1}\Delta\theta_{a}+W_{2}F_{a}
, where
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times|\theta|}
and
W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
are learnable projection matrices that map both components to a joint latent alignment space. The resulting
T_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
serves as a compressed, semantically grounded representation for property
a
. These matrices
\{T_{\text{helpful}},T_{\text{harmless}},T_{\text{honest}}\}
are stored for downstream use in Stage 2, where they guide both expert routing and axis-aware calibration in the MoCaE module.

3.2
MoCaE Module

Given a user query
x_{q}
, we first obtain an intermediate representation
h_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from the shared transformer encoder. This is projected through a learned gating mechanism to compute alignment-specific routing weights:
\alpha=\mathrm{softmax}(W_{r}h_{q}+b_{r})
, where
W_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times d}
and
b_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are learnable parameters. The resulting
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
assigns probabilities to the three alignment axes—
helpful
,
harmless
, and
honest
or (HHH)—soft-selecting the corresponding experts
E_{a}
, each implemented as a dedicated FFN head specialized for property
a
.

To calibrate each expert on a per-instance basis, we use the task-feature matrices
T_{a}
extracted via Stage
3.1
. For the top-ranked expert
E_{a^{*}}
, its associated matrix
T_{a^{*}}
encodes fine-tuned alignment-specific behavior. We project the current query representation into this latent space to derive sparse and dense alignment activations:
R_{a^{*}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rare}}(T_{a^{*}}),\quad F_{a^{*}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{freq}}(T_{a^{*}})
, which are evaluated via the fractal dimension
2
2

2

Fractal dimension calculates the complexity of a pattern by measuring how detail changes with scale; it indicates how completely a fractal appears to fill space as one zooms in–often yielding a non-integer value between traditional topological dimensions.
,
\mathrm{FD}_{a^{*}}=\frac{\log(N)}{\log(1/\epsilon)}
, where
N
is the number of clusters, and
\epsilon
indicates clustering resolution.

Furthermore, we extract token-level activation patterns from the expert,
Z_{q}=f_{a^{*}}(x_{q})
, and perform unsupervised clustering (i.e.
k
-means)
\mathcal{N}_{a^{*}}=\mathrm{Cluster}(Z_{q})
to identify semantic groups. The intra-cluster consistency is measured using a variance-based purity coefficient:
\mathrm{score}(\mathcal{N}_{a^{*}})\propto\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{Sim}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
, where
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is the
k
-th cluster and
\mathrm{Sim}(\cdot)
quantifies internal cohesion. The fractal dimension and semantic cluster scores are combined into a joint calibration score for each expert:
s_{a}=\lambda_{1}\cdot\mathrm{FD}_{a}+\lambda_{2}\cdot\mathrm{score}(\mathcal{N}_{a})
, where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1
. These scores are normalized through softmax:
\hat{s}_{a}=\frac{\exp(s_{a})}{\sum_{j}\exp(s_{j})}
to produce calibrated weights that dynamically blend expert outputs:
h_{\mathrm{final}}=\sum_{a}\hat{s}_{a}\cdot z_{a},\quad\text{with }z_{a}=E_{a}(h_{q})
. These calibrated embedding
h_{\mathrm{final}}
are re-injected into the transformer decoder to guide alignment-aware generation.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

To comprehensively evaluate AlignX, we utilize task-specific metrics aligned with each alignment objective, consistent with prior works
Huang et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2023a
;
Tekin et al. 2024
.

•

Helpfulness
is measured by Win Rate (WR), defined as:
\mathrm{WR}=\frac{\#\text{wins}}{\#\text{samples}}\times 100
, where higher values indicate better performance.

•

Harmlessness
is measured using the Beaver-Dam-7B moderation model
7
7

7

https://huggingface.co/PKU-Alignment/beaver-dam-7b
, which categorizes outputs by harm level. Safety Score (SS) is given by:
\mathrm{SS}=\frac{\#\text{unsafe}}{\#\text{samples}}\times 100
, where lower scores representing greater safety.

•

Honesty
is measured via the GPT-Judge
8
8

8

https://github.com/kingoflolz/mesh-transformer-jax
, classifying responses as Truthful (T) and Informative (I). The combined metric (TI) is computed as:
\mathrm{TI}=\frac{\#\text{truthful}}{\#\text{samples}}\times\frac{\#\text{informative}}{\#\text{samples}}\times 100
, where higher values signify better honesty.

•

Overall alignment performance is synthesized through an
Average
score computed as:
\mathrm{Avg}=\frac{\mathrm{Helpfulness}+\mathrm{Honesty}-\mathrm{Harmlessness}}{3}
. Here,
harmlessness
is subtracted due to being a negative metric, thereby explicitly penalizing safety violations, while
helpfulness
and
honesty
are positive metrics. This formulation ensures a balanced reflection of alignment trade-offs.

All metrics are reported as percentages (%). Upward arrows (
\uparrow
) indicate metrics where higher values are preferred, while downward arrows (
\downarrow
) denote metrics where lower values are desirable.

4.3
Hyperparameters

In AlignX framework, the latent alignment space dimensionality is set to
k=256
, and the routing projection matrix
W_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times d}
uses a hidden size
d=1024
. Calibration weights are fixed at
\lambda_{1}=0.6
and
\lambda_{2}=0.4
. The softmax temperature is set to 1. Clustering granularity
\epsilon
is tuned to 0.05. All alignment models are fine-tuned for 3 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
, batch size 64, and weight decay of 0.01. These hyperparameters remain fixed across all experiments.

7
Conclusion

We present AlignX, a modular two-stage framework that effectively aligns LLMs on
helpfulness
,
harmlessness
, and
honesty
. AlignX performs well on benchmarks and models by tackling expert misrouting and catastrophic forgetfulness. Our results highlight a scalable, generalizable approach to multi-objective alignment—paving the way for safer, more trustworthy open-source LLMs.

Limitations

Despite its promising performance, AlignX has certain limitations. First, the effectiveness of task-feature fusion may diminish under extreme distribution shifts or for tasks that exhibit high semantic ambiguity across alignment axes. Second, although MoCaE enables dynamic expert routing, its reliance on expert-specific calibrators can introduce computational overhead in low-latency deployment settings. Lastly, we do not address instruction diversity or multilingual generalization, which are important directions for future work.

Ethics Statement

This work aims to improve the ethical alignment of LLMs by promoting
helpfulness
,
harmlessness
, and
honesty
responses. While our models reduce unsafe and misleading outputs, they are not infallible. We caution against deploying AlignX in high-stakes environments without human oversight. All datasets used in this study are publicly available and do not contain personally identifiable information. We encourage responsible usage and transparent evaluation practices in the development of future alignment systems.
