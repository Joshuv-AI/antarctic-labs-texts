A Change of Heart for DEPDC5: Expanding the Neurocardiac Hypothesis of SUDEP
Authors: Edward Glasscock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiac Remodeling and Arrhythmia in a Mouse Model of Depdc5 Haploinsufficiency Ramos-Mondragon R, Wang S, Liu Q, Chen C, Greiner AM, Marx AM, Shih M, Parent JM, London B, Isom LL. Epilepsia. 2026 Apr 9. doi: 10.1002/epi.70244. Online ahead of print. PMID: 41954126.Objective: Some ion channel genes linked to developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) are also linked to cardiac arrhythmia, leading to the hypothesis that predisposition to cardiac arrhythmias may contribute to the complex disease presentation of DEE and possibly to the mechanism of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy. However, channelopathies represent only ∼25% of the genetic epilepsies. The remainder arise from variants in non-ion-channel genes, moving the disorder beyond channelopathies. Despite evidence that some non-ion-channel variants are linked to sudden cardiac death, we have little information on whether non-ion-channel DEE variants can also result in cardiac phenotypes. Methods: Here, we investigated the DEE gene, DEPDC5, which is expressed in both brain and heart. We studied the cardiac phenotype of a Depdc5 haploinsufficient mouse model that genetically mimics DEPDC5 patients. Results: Depdc5+/− mice showed increased susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias, systolic dysfunction, and ventricular fibrosis, as well as shortened action potential duration, increased levels of sodium and potassium channel proteins, and increased sodium current and transient outward potassium current densities in acutely isolated ventricular myocytes. Thus, at least in mice, Depdc5 variants impact heart as well as brain excitability. Significance: These data strengthen the hypothesis that cardiac arrhythmias may contribute to the complex presentation of DEE and expand our previous work showing cardiac arrhythmia in multiple mouse and human channelopathy models to include a model of a non-ion-channel variant. Although this work may have implications for DEPDC5 patients, critical differences between mouse and human cardiac physiology complicate the translation of mouse data to human disease. Essential next steps must include investigation of Depdc5 function in higher vertebrate models that more accurately mimic human physiology, as well as longitudinal patient natural history studies that monitor cardiovascular health, to test the hypothesis that DEE variants resulting in DEPDC5 haploinsufficiency may predispose patients to cardiac arrhythmias.

Commentary

One of the leading mechanistic explanations for sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is the neurocardiac (“brain-heart”) gene hypothesis, which proposes that pathogenic variants in single genes expressed in both the brain and heart can simultaneously predispose individuals to epilepsy and cardiac arrhythmias, increasing susceptibility to sudden death. 1 Mechanistically, these genes may disrupt cardiac function either directly through intrinsic cardiac roles or indirectly by altering autonomic regulation. Studies in Kcnq1 and Kcna1 mouse models provided initial experimental proof-of-principle, followed by identification of at least 15 brain-heart genes encoding ion channels or related proteins associated with epilepsy, cardiac arrhythmias, and SUDEP.1,2 Systematic review of SUDEP genetics confirms that sodium and potassium channel variants are among the most frequently identified in postmortem studies. 3 Thus, ion channels, which regulate membrane excitability in both neurons and cardiomyocytes, comprise the largest class of candidate brain-heart genes linked to SUDEP.

In 2015, this ion channel-centric view of SUDEP genetics was challenged by the discovery of pathogenic DEPDC5 variants in two SUDEP cases from the same family, 4 followed by molecular autopsy studies identifying DEPDC5 as one of the highest-ranked candidate risk genes. 5 Previously recognized as a cause of autosomal dominant focal epilepsy and developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) through germline loss-of-function mutations, 6 DEPDC5 stood out among SUDEP-associated genes because, unlike most previously implicated genes, it does not encode an ion channel. Instead, DEPDC5 encodes a ubiquitously expressed component of the GATOR1 complex, a negative regulator of mTOR signaling that controls cellular growth and metabolism. 6 Yet after more than a decade of investigation, neither patients nor animal models with DEPDC5 variants have demonstrated structural or electrical cardiac abnormalities, 7 despite evidence that chronic mTOR hyperactivation promotes pathological cardiac remodeling, including hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis. 8 Consequently, the mechanism by which DEPDC5 increases SUDEP risk has been assumed to occur independently of intrinsic cardiac dysfunction.

A recent study by Ramos-Mondragon et al reveals a previously unappreciated role for Depdc5 in maintaining normal cardiac function. 9 Using a new mouse model of Depdc5 haploinsufficiency, the authors combined in vivo and in vitro electrophysiology with cardiac imaging, histology, and molecular profiling to examine the consequences of reduced Depdc5 expression in the heart. Their findings suggest that Depdc5 haploinsufficiency increases susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias through electrical and structural cardiac remodeling, providing a potential intrinsic cardiac mechanism linking DEPDC5 variants to SUDEP.

First, Ramos-Mondragon and colleagues generated a new Depdc5 haploinsufficient mouse to model the genotype present in most patients with DEPDC5-related epilepsy. Global deletion of one Depdc5 allele produced viable heterozygous (Depdc5+/−) mice, whereas homozygous deletion was embryonic lethal. The Depdc5+/− mice exhibited no overt spontaneous seizures, consistent with other Depdc5 haploinsufficiency models. 6 However, continuous EEG monitoring was not performed, leaving open the possibility of subtle electrographic seizures.

The authors next assessed susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias using intracardiac programmed electrical stimulation. Although baseline electrocardiograms were normal, Depdc5+/− mice exhibited a fourfold higher incidence of inducible ventricular tachycardia (VT) compared with wild-type littermates (8/13 vs 2/13 mice), and VT durations were ∼40% longer. However, the apparent difference in VT susceptibility may be less pronounced than the incidence data suggest because the increase in total VT events, estimated from the plotted VT duration data (∼33 vs 23 events), was more modest than the fourfold difference in the proportion of mice developing VT. Nevertheless, these findings support the conclusion that Depdc5 haploinsufficiency increases susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias.

The increased arrhythmia susceptibility was accompanied by evidence of ventricular dysfunction and remodeling. Echocardiography demonstrated increased left ventricular end-systolic volume together with reduced ejection fraction and fractional shortening, indicating impaired systolic contractile function despite preserved gross cardiac morphology. Histological analyses, however, revealed nearly a twofold increase in ventricular fibrosis that was accompanied by increased expression of collagen- and extracellular matrix-related genes. Because fibrosis impairs contractility and electrical conduction, these changes provide a potential substrate for ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.

Finally, the authors investigated whether these abnormalities involved electrical remodeling at the cellular level. Ventricular cardiomyocytes from Depdc5+/− mice exhibited increased sodium (INa) and transient outward potassium (Ito) current densities without changes in L-type calcium current. These changes were accompanied by reduced action potential amplitude and upstroke velocity, shorter action potential duration, and increased expression of Nav1.5 and Kv4.2, which mediate INa and Ito, respectively. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that loss of a non-ion channel epilepsy gene can alter cardiac ion channel expression and electrophysiological function.

The significance of this study extends beyond DEPDC5. These findings provide the first evidence that Depdc5 is required for normal cardiac function and that haploinsufficiency produces structural and electrical remodeling associated with vulnerability to ventricular arrhythmias. These results support the possibility that SUDEP susceptibility in DEPDC5-related epilepsy may involve intrinsic cardiac mechanisms independent of seizures. More broadly, this work expands the neurocardiac hypothesis beyond ion channelopathies by demonstrating that disruption of an mTOR regulator can remodel cardiac electrophysiology. Whereas previously implicated DEE genes with cardiac involvement have primarily encoded ion channel subunits (eg, SCN1A, SCN8A, SCN1B), DEPDC5 emerges as the first major non-ion channel member of this group. 9

However, comparison of this study's findings with previous work suggests the presence of additional complexity still to be revealed. Most notably, the cardiac phenotype contrasts with a previous mouse model in which Depdc5 was deleted selectively in neurons while remaining haploinsufficient in other tissues. 7 That study found no evidence of cardiac fibrosis, ECG abnormalities, or altered heart rate variability, although inducible arrhythmia susceptibility and cardiac contractility were not examined. One potential explanation is the substantial difference in the ages at which animals were studied (≤3 months vs 8 months in this study), raising the possibility that cardiac remodeling develops progressively with age.

Cardiac dysfunction may represent only one component of DEPCD5-related SUDEP. Conditional deletion of Depdc5 in excitatory forebrain neurons of mice produces impaired ventilatory responses, hypoventilation, and seizure-induced apnea preceding terminal cardiac asystole, implicating respiratory dysfunction as another potential mechanism. 10 Thus, DEPDC5 variants may produce a multifaceted brain-heart-lung syndrome in which abnormalities across multiple organ systems converge to increase vulnerability to seizure-related death.

Although global Depdc5 haploinsufficiency models the genotype present in many DEPDC5 patients, heterozygous mice do not faithfully reproduce the human phenotype, limiting their translational utility. Whereas DEPDC5 haploinsufficient patients exhibit epilepsy, Depdc5+/− mice lack spontaneous seizures and therefore SUDEP. Moreover, human studies have thus far failed to identify cardiac abnormalities in DEPDC5 patients by ECG or echocardiography, and postmortem examination of one SUDEP case did not reveal myocardial fibrosis. 7 Whether these discrepancies reflect species differences, limited clinical sampling, or fundamentally different disease mechanisms remains unclear. Future studies in larger animal models with cardiac electrophysiology more closely resembling humans, together with systematic cardiac phenotyping of larger DEPDC5 patient cohorts, will therefore be essential to determine the clinical relevance of these findings.

Ultimately, this study provides compelling evidence that Depdc5 plays an intrinsic role in maintaining normal cardiac electrical and structural integrity, challenging the assumption that DEPDC5-associated SUDEP occurs primarily through brain-mediated mechanisms. Although additional work is needed to establish whether similar abnormalities occur in patients, these findings reinforce the concept that epilepsy genes outside the ion channel family can exert pleiotropic effects across the brain and heart, further strengthening the neurocardiac framework for SUDEP.
