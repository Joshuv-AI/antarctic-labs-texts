Generalized Neural Network Operators with Symmetrized Activations: Fractional Convergence and the Voronovskaya-Damasclin Theorem
Rômulo Damasclin Chaves dos Santos Tecnological Institute of Aeronautics romulosantos@ita.br

Abstract
This paper explores the asymptotic behavior of univariate neural network operators, with an emphasis on both classical and fractional differentiation over infinite domains. The analysis leverages symmetrized and perturbed hyperbolic tangent activation functions to investigate basic, Kantorovich, and quadrature-type operators. Voronovskaya-type expansions, along with the novel Vonorovskaya-Damasclin theorem, are derived to obtain precise error estimates and establish convergence rates, thereby extending classical results to fractional calculus via Caputo derivatives. The study delves into the intricate interplay between operator parameters and approximation accuracy, providing a comprehensive framework for future research in multidimensional and stochastic settings. This work lays the groundwork for a deeper understanding of neural network operators in complex mathematical. Keywords: Voronovskaya expansions, Symmetrized neural networks, Fractional calculus in machine learning, Neural approximation, Computational neural operators.

Mathematical Foundations of Symmetrized Activation Functions

To establish a robust theoretical framework for symmetrized activation functions, we begin by defining the
perturbed hyperbolic tangent activation function
:

g_{q,\lambda}(x):=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}},\quad\lambda,q>0,\;x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1)

Here,
\lambda
is a scaling parameter that controls the steepness of the function, and
q
is the deformation coefficient, which introduces asymmetry. This function generalizes the standard hyperbolic tangent function, recovered when
q=1
. Notably,
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is odd, satisfying
g_{q,\lambda}(-x)=-g_{q,\lambda}(x)
.

Next, we construct the
density function
:

M_{q,\lambda}(x):=\frac{1}{4}\big(g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\big),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\;q,\lambda>0.

(2)

This density function is carefully designed to ensure both
positivity
and
smoothness
. To confirm positivity, we compute the derivative of
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
:

\frac{d}{dx}g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{2\lambda qe^{2\lambda x}}{(e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x})^{2}}>0,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

Since
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is strictly increasing, it follows that
g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)>g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)
, ensuring
M_{q,\lambda}(x)>0
.

To introduce symmetry, we define the
symmetrized function
:

\Phi(x):=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)}{2}.

(4)

To verify that
\Phi(x)
is an
even function
, consider:

\Phi(-x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(-x)+M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(-x)}{2}.

(5)

Using the fact that
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is odd, it follows that
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
and
M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)
are even, satisfying:

M_{q,\lambda}(-x)=M_{q,\lambda}(x),\quad M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(-x)=M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x).

(6)

Thus:

\Phi(-x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)}{2}=\Phi(x).

(7)

This confirms the symmetry of
\Phi(x)
, making it a well-defined
even function
suitable for applications in approximation theory and neural network analysis.

3
Main Results

3.1
Basic Operators

Theorem 1

(Approximation by Operators)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
(bounded and continuous), and
0<\varepsilon\leq N
. Then:

1.

The following approximation holds:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right),

(8)

where
B_{n}
is a linear operator and
B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})
denotes the operator applied to monomials shifted by
x
.

2.

If
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
, then:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,\quad 0<\varepsilon\leq N.

(9)

Proof.

To prove the theorem, we use Taylor’s theorem and the asymptotic properties of the operators
B_{n}
. Let
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, and expand
f
around
x
as:

f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right),

(10)

where the remainder term
R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)
is given by:

R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\frac{\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\left[f^{(N)}(t)-f^{(N)}(x)\right]\,dt.

(11)

We substitute the expansion (
10
) into the definition of the operator
B_{n}
:

B_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k),

(12)

where
\Phi
is a weight function with appropriate support. Substituting, we get:

B_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(13)

Define the error term:

R:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(14)

To estimate
R
, we consider two cases:

Case 1:
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|<\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}
.

Within this interval,
\Phi(nx-k)
has significant support, and the regularity of
f^{(N)}
implies:

\left|R\right|\leq 2\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{1}{N!n^{\beta N}}.

(15)

Case 2:
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|\geq\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}
.

Outside the principal support,
\Phi(nx-k)
decays exponentially, so:

\left|R\right|\leq\frac{4\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{n^{N}\lambda^{N}}\left(q+\frac{1}{q}\right)e^{2\lambda}e^{-\lambda\left(n^{1-\beta}-1\right)}.

(16)

Combining both cases, we obtain:

|R|=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(17)

Substituting
R
back into (
13
), we prove (
8
).

Finally, when
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,N
, we have:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0,

(18)

as indicated in (
9
), completing the proof.
∎

3.2
Kantorovich Operators

Theorem 2
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and
0<\varepsilon\leq N
. Then:

1.

C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+o\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}\right)

(19)

2.

When
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,N
, we have:

\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}}\left[C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,\quad 0<\varepsilon\leq N

(20)

Proof.

We can write:

C_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\Phi(nx-k)

(21)

Let
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
. We have:

f\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+\displaystyle\int_{x}^{t+\dfrac{k}{n}}\left(f^{(N)}(s)-f^{(N)}(x)\right)\dfrac{\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}-s\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,ds

(22)

Hence:

\begin{array}[]{l}C_{n}(f,x)={\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+\\
\\
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}}\Phi(nx-k)\left(n{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\dfrac{1}{n}}}\left({\displaystyle\int_{x}^{t+\dfrac{k}{n}}}\left(f^{(N)}(s)-f^{(N)}(x)\right)\frac{\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}-s\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,ds\right)\,dt\right)\\
\\
\end{array}

(23)

Define:

R:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(nx-k)\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\int_{x}^{t+\frac{k}{n}}\left(f^{(N)}(s)-f^{(N)}(x)\right)\frac{\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-s\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,ds\right)\,dt\right)

(24)

For
\left|\dfrac{k}{n}-x\right|<\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}
:

|R|\leq 2\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}}{N!}

(25)

For
\left|\dfrac{k}{n}-x\right|\geq\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}
:

|R|\leq\dfrac{2^{N}\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}}{n^{N}N!}\left[\dfrac{T}{e^{2\lambda n^{1-\beta}}}+\dfrac{\left(q+\dfrac{1}{q}\right)}{\lambda^{N}}2e^{2\lambda}N!e^{-\lambda\left(n^{1-\beta}-1\right)}\right]

(26)

Thus:

|R|\leq\dfrac{4\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}

(27)

Hence:

|R|=o\left(\left(\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}\right)

(28)

proving the claim.
∎

Fractional Perturbation Stability

Theorem 3

(Stability Under Fractional Perturbations)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, and
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
be the perturbed hyperbolic tangent activation function defined by:

g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}},\quad\lambda,q>0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(29)

For any small perturbation
|q-1|<\delta
, the operator
C_{n}
satisfies the stability estimate:

\|C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)\|\leq\frac{\delta}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty},

(30)

where
0<\varepsilon\leq N
and
\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f^{(N)}(x)|
.

Proof.

To begin, consider
\Phi_{q,\lambda}(z)
as the density function derived from the perturbed activation function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
. The operator
C_{n}
is defined as:

C_{n}(f,x;q)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\Phi_{q,\lambda}(nx-k).

(31)

Next, we expand
\Phi_{q,\lambda}(z)
around
q=1
using the first-order Taylor expansion:

\Phi_{q,\lambda}(z)=\Phi_{1,\lambda}(z)+\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right)(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2}).

(32)

Thus, the perturbed operator can be written as:

C_{n}(f,x;q)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\left[\Phi_{1,\lambda}(nx-k)+\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right)(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2})\right].

(33)

The difference between
C_{n}(f,x;q)
and
C_{n}(f,x;1)
is given by:

C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\left[\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right)(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2})\right].

(34)

Let us focus on the first-order perturbation term. The remainder term involving
\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2})
contributes at a higher order in
(q-1)
, which is negligible for small
|q-1|
. Therefore, we estimate the perturbation as:

\|C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)\|\leq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right|(q-1).

(35)

Assuming
\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\right|
is bounded, we have:

\|C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)\|\leq\frac{\delta}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty},

(36)

where
\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f^{(N)}(x)|
represents the supremum norm of the
N
-th derivative of
f
.

Thus, we have established the desired stability estimate.
∎

Generalized Voronovskaya Expansions for Fractional Functions

Theorem 4

(Generalized Voronovskaya Expansion)
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
N=\lceil\alpha\rceil
,
\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}
,
f\in AC^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
0<\beta<1
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. Assume that
\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,[x,\infty)}
and
\|D_{x-}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,(-\infty,x]}
are finite. Then:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(37)

When
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\dots,N
:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(38)

Proof.

Using the Caputo fractional Taylor expansion for
f
:

f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)dt.

(39)

Substitute this expansion into the definition of the operator
B_{n}
:

B_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k),

(40)

where
\Phi(x)
is a density kernel function. Substituting
f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)
, we separate the terms into two contributions:

Main Contribution:

The first
N
terms of the Taylor expansion yield:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x),

(41)

which captures the local behavior of
f
in terms of its derivatives up to order
N
.

Error Term:

The remainder term involves the fractional derivative
D_{*x}^{\alpha}
and can be bounded as:

R=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(nx-k)\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)dt.

(42)

Bounding the Remainder:

•

For
|k/n-x|<1/n^{\beta}
: The kernel
\Phi(nx-k)
has significant support, and the fractional regularity of
f
ensures:

|R|\leq\frac{\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}}{n^{\alpha\beta}}.

(43)

•

For
|k/n-x|\geq 1/n^{\beta}
: The exponential decay of
\Phi(nx-k)
ensures that contributions from distant terms are negligible:

|R|\leq\frac{\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}}{n^{\alpha\beta}}.

(44)

Combining both cases, the error term satisfies:

|R|=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(45)

Conclusion:

Substituting the bounds for the main contribution and error term into the expansion for
B_{n}(f,x)
, we conclude:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(46)

Moreover, when
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\dots,N
:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(47)

This completes the proof.
∎

Convergence of Kantorovich Operators with Generalized Density

Theorem 5

(Convergence Under Generalized Density)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\Phi(x)
be a symmetrized density function defined by:

\Phi(x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{1/q,\lambda}(x)}{2},\quad M_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\left(g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right).

(48)

Then the Kantorovich operator
C_{n}
satisfies:

C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x)+o\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}\right).

(49)

Proof.

By definition of
C_{n}
:

C_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)dt\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(50)

To analyze this, expand
f
using Taylor’s theorem around
x
:

f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right),

(51)

where the remainder term
R_{N}
satisfies:

R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{N!}\int_{x}^{t+\frac{k}{n}}f^{(N)}(u)\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-u\right)^{N-1}du.

(52)

Substituting this expansion into
C_{n}(f,x)
, we separate the terms into two parts:

Main Contribution:

The sum of the first
N
terms gives:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x),

(53)

which captures the local behavior of
f
around
x
.

Error Term:

The remainder term involves
R_{N}
and can be bounded as:

|R|\leq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{N}\Phi(nx-k)dt.

(54)

Case 1:
|k/n-x|<1/n^{\beta}
:

Within this interval,
\Phi(nx-k)
has significant support. The regularity of
f^{(N)}
ensures:

|R|\leq 2\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}.

(55)

Case 2:
|k/n-x|\geq 1/n^{\beta}
:

Outside this interval, the exponential decay of
\Phi(nx-k)
ensures:

|R|\leq\frac{4\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}.

(56)

Combining Both Cases:

Summing over all terms, we conclude:

|R|=o\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}\right),

(57)

proving the claim.
∎

Vonorovskaya-Damasclin Theorem

Theorem 6

(Vonorovskaya-Damasclin Theorem)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, where
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and let
\Phi(x)
be a symmetrized density function defined as:

\Phi(x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{1/q,\lambda}(x)}{2},\quad M_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\left(g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right),

(58)

where
g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\dfrac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}}
is the perturbed hyperbolic tangent function,
\lambda,q>0
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,[x,\infty)}
and
\|D_{x-}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,(-\infty,x]}
are finite for
\alpha>0
, and let
N=\lceil\alpha\rceil
. Then the operator
C_{n}
satisfies:

\begin{array}[]{l}C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)={\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x)+\\
\\
\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}\,f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}\,f(x)\right)\dfrac{(t-x)^{\alpha-1}}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\,dt+o\left(\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right),\end{array}

(59)

where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily small. Moreover, when
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\dots,N
:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(60)

Proof.

Let
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
and consider the fractional Taylor expansion of
f
around
x
using the Caputo derivative
D_{*x}^{\alpha}
. For
\dfrac{k}{n}
near
x
, we expand
f
as:

f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)dt.

N

The second term involves the Caputo fractional derivative

Appendix A

This appendix provides a detailed analysis of the perturbed hyperbolic tangent activation function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
and its properties, which are fundamental to the derivations and proofs presented in the main text.

A.1
Definition and Basic Properties

The perturbed hyperbolic tangent activation function is defined as:

g_{q,\lambda}(x):=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}},\quad\lambda,q>0,x\in\mathbb{R}

This function generalizes the standard hyperbolic tangent function, which is recovered when
q=1
. The parameter
\lambda
controls the steepness of the function, while
q
introduces asymmetry.

A.1.1
Odd Function Property

The function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is odd, satisfying:

g_{q,\lambda}(-x)=-g_{q,\lambda}(x)

This property is crucial for the symmetry of the derived density function
\Phi(x)
.

A.1.2
Monotonicity

To confirm the monotonicity of
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
, we compute its derivative:

\frac{d}{dx}g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{2\lambda qe^{2\lambda x}}{(e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x})^{2}}>0,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}

Since the derivative is always positive,
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is strictly increasing.

A.2
Density Function

The density function
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
is defined as:

M_{q,\lambda}(x):=\frac{1}{4}\left(g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},q,\lambda>0

A.2.1
Positivity

To confirm the positivity of
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
, note that since
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is strictly increasing:

g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)>g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)

Thus,
M_{q,\lambda}(x)>0
.

A.2.2
Symmetry

The symmetrized function
\Phi(x)
is defined as:

\Phi(x):=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)}{2}

To verify that
\Phi(x)
is an even function, consider:

\Phi(-x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(-x)+M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(-x)}{2}

Using the fact that
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is odd, it follows that
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
and
M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)
are even:

M_{q,\lambda}(-x)=M_{q,\lambda}(x),\quad M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(-x)=M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)

Thus:

\Phi(-x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{\frac{1}{q},\lambda}(x)}{2}=\Phi(x)

This confirms the symmetry of
\Phi(x)
.

A.3
Asymptotic Behavior

The asymptotic behavior of
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
as
x\to\pm\infty
is given by:

\lim_{x\to\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(x)=1,\quad\lim_{x\to-\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(x)=-1

This behavior is crucial for the analysis of the convergence properties of the neural network operators.

A.4

The derivatives and integrals of
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
and
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
are essential for the proofs of the theorems presented in the main text. Here, we provide some key results:

A.4.1
First Derivative

The first derivative of
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is:

\frac{d}{dx}g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{2\lambda qe^{2\lambda x}}{(e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x})^{2}}

Second Derivative

The second derivative of

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{2\lambda^{2}qe^{2\lambda x}(e^{2\lambda x}-q)}{(e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x})^{3}}

The integral of

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}M_{q,\lambda}(x)\,dx=1\,.
