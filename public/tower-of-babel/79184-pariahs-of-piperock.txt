 PARIAHS OF PIPEROCK

 by W. C. Tuttle
 Author of “Alias Whispering White,” “Local Option in Loco Land,” etc.


There comes a time in the life of every danged man when his conscience
hurts him and he wishes to make restitution. Murderers have handed
themselves over to the law, and burglars have been knowed to send back
the proceeds of their burgling on account of them same twinges of
conscience.

Sometimes it’s only a statement made in the time of anger that hurts a
man’s insides and sends him back to square up.

That was the way with me and “Dirty Shirt” Jones. Me and him left the
city of Piperock in anger of mind and agony of body; left the whole
blamed county of Yaller Rock, for that matter. Piperock ain’t so bad
itself. She’s a lot like the feller said about the heated hereafter—

“Hell ain’t so bad itself—it’s the people who live there.”

Me and Dirty participated in a local option campaign in Piperock, the
same of which affects the whole county. In the interests of parched
humanity we puts our best foot forward, and somebody proceeds to kick it
out from under us. Thereupon we holds up our clenched fists and swore by
the horns on the moon that we’re all through, finished and done with
anything pertaining to Piperock or the county in which she lies.

That is September. We points our four burros away from there until we’re
safe from our friends.

Cometh November. Me and Dirty are humped over a fire, trying to make
mulligan out of an ancient fool hen, when Dirty speaks thusly:

“Wonder what Magpie Simpkins will do for a job if ‘Scenery’ Sims is
elected sheriff? When me and you didn’t vote, Ike, local option becomes
an issue. Be funny if Yaller Rock County goes dry by one vote.”

Thus are seeds sown and bear fruit upon the green bay tree. That evening
we’re humped over the fire again. There ain’t been a word said about it
since noon, but we’ve thought a heap, I reckon.

“I reads something once,” says Dirty reflective-like, “and she goes like
this: ‘Now is the time for all good men to come to the aid of their
party.’”

“We answers them prophetic words in the morning, Dirty,” says I.

“What party, Ike?”

“Well,” says I, knowing whereof I speak, “it won’t be no tea-party—not
in Piperock, Mister Jones.”

Going back was a simple matter. We knowed that somewhere under the north
star lieth Piperock; so all we’ve got to do is to point properly, punch
our burros and wear out our boots. Me and Dirty ain’t got much to hold
converse about, being as we’ve been together long enough to exhaust any
lies we might have thought up, and the truth ain’t of interest—when we
do tell it.

We strikes the road which runs between Eagle and the rest of the world,
and we points toward Eagle. About half an hour later we stops. Dirty is
in the lead, and he walks back to me.

“Somebody lost a letter,” says he, holding out an envelope. “Lost off
the stage, I reckon, and got run over.”

The address proclaims it to be the property of the Honorable Fitzhugh
Peleg Alexander, Deer Lodge, Montana. She ain’t never been opened.

“Honorable!” snorts Dirty, slipping his finger under the flap.

The contents seems of interest to him. He takes a deep breath and hands
it to me. I deciphers it thusly:

 DEAR SIR:

 Our committee has decided to take up your offer to make our big
 temperance speech at Piperock. The evening before election will
 be about right. We will give you the fifty dollars after the
 speech.

 Yours truly and respectfully,

 WICK SMITH, Chairman and Treasurer.

“What do you think of that?” I asks.

“Any old time they imports talent to dry up our homes, Ike, it’s time
for us to hurry back. Hump yourselves, you flea-bitten cousins to a
rabbit!”

We enters the portals of Eagle after dark. Eagle is some hook-billed
town if anybody rises to inquire. We irrigates our necks and has a bit
to eat, and then we opines to outguess the wheel, the same of which
wasn’t hung on a spindle for no such purpose.

We ain’t placed more than a couple of bets when in comes a individual
who roars like a bull-fiddle, slaps a twenty-dollar gold piece on the
layout and howls:

“Let her spin, feller! That’s what is left of my gun.”

He sees the dealer pay a couple of piker bets and rake in his twenty.

He stands there sort of in a trance and watches the wheel spin gold for
the house.

“You got a system?” asks Dirty Shirt, and the big feller stares at him.

“System? On that wheel? That wheel has got any system on earth hog-tied
and staked out!”

“Meaning which?” snaps the gambler, and the crowd splits.

The big feller is leaning with his hands on the back of a chair. When he
straightens up, he brings the chair up.

“Meaning,” says he, grinning, “that the law of averages was fixed by you
before you opened the game.”

He whirls that chair across the layout and crowns the gambler complete.
The gambler goes over backward with the layout, wheel and all on top of
him, the same being assisted by me and Dirty Shirt.

There is silence for a while then Dirty says—

“I feel the need of an eclipse.”

His first shot busts the big lamp in the center of the room, and, before
the flying glass hits the ceiling, he plants another slug into the lamp
over the bar, and we has that eclipse.

As old Prof. Phinney would say, “Things got sort of ki-otic.” A slug of
somebody’s lead dusted the dandruff on my head but missed my hat, for
the simple reason that I had it in my hand. I’m so polite that I always
takes off my hat in the presence of the dead. Nobody is dead yet, but
the indications are favorable.

Seems like everybody is exercising their gun rights; so I points toward
the door, shoots twice straight ahead to guarantee an open trail and
don’t stop hopping until I’m at that hitch-rack, mingling with Dirty
Shirt and the burros.

We don’t stop to apologize to anybody. About three miles out we makes
camp, and, about the time we gets coffee to boiling, who comes angling
in but the feller who threw the chair. He sets down and accepts a bite.

“Cleaned?” asks Dirty, and the feller nods.

“Whiter than snow.”

He’s a hefty sort of a _hombre_, born about forty years ago, and he sure
is endowed with vocal cords. He cusses Eagle back to the sixth
generation and then passes around the bottle.

“Clean?” says he. “Gents, I ain’t got nothing but my honor left. My
horse is in hock; my saddle is gone, and all the satisfaction I got was
to hang a chair on that gambler’s head.”

“What can you do?” asks Dirty.

“Anything short of murder.”

“Can you talk like a orator?”

“Me? Go ahead.”

“Listen,” says Dirty, and then he opens up and tells this jasper all
about Piperock.

Then he shows him that letter.

“You can earn that fifty, and me and Ike will give you twenty more.”

“Is that so?” says I. “For why?”

“As a bonus to the Honorable Alexander for getting so drunk that he
can’t make that speech. _Sabe?_ There will be a disappointed and
disgusted crowd, and such will react in our favor.”

“But that letter says he don’t get that money until after the speech,” I
argues.

“Hmm,” muses Dirty. “Ike, am I good for fifty dollars?”

“Not as a investment in live stock,” says I. “But your credit is.”

“I ain’t got a cent,” says the feller. “How will I get there?”

Then me and Dirty assays ourselves and finds that twenty won’t break us,
and he promises to take the first stage north. He stays with us that
night, plants himself beside the road the next morning, and about noon
the stage passes us. Beside the driver is our alleged temperance talker,
but he don’t recognize us, which shows him to be cautious.

 * * * * *

Four days from that ancient fool hen finds us poking through the
Medicine Hills, which are the boundary-lines between Yaller Rock and
Custer. We swings out on a big bluff where we can look down upon the
town of Piperock. The sight of a feller’s old home town always brings
memories, and me and Dirty lingers there, gazing down upon the town of
our past wrath.

We sees a tiny speck of a human being cross the street and get on a
bronc. They whirls and goes out of town in a tiny cloud of dust, and up
to our ears is wafted the faint snap of a shot.

“Danged good old town!” applauds Dirty. “Best little town on earth, Ike,
and she ain’t changed a bit.”

“Sure looks peaceful,” I agrees. “Let’s slide down and hold a reunion.”

When we hits the bottom of the hills, we meets “Mighty” Jones, who is
riding through the mesquite. He stops and stares at us.

“You fellers back?” he asks.

“Well,” says Dirty, “are you so in the habit of talking to yourself that
you can’t believe your eyes?”

“You’ve got a lot of gall!” declares Mighty. “Coming back to the town
you throwed down last September. Fine pair of citizens! When I die from
thirst, I’ll think of you two, but prior to that I forgets you.”

“I hope we’ll be in your thoughts soon, Mighty,” says Dirty.

“How are things coming, Mighty?” I asks. “You might tell us.”

“Nope. When I can’t speak cheerful, I keeps still. You fellers will find
out things soon enough.”

He lopes on. Me and Dirty looks at each other and then pilgrims on. The
old town looks all right—externally. There’s a bronc dozing at the rack
in front of “Buck’s” place, and Ricky Henderson is taking a _siesta_ in
front of his barber-shop. All looks serene; so we ties up our
rolling-stock and enters the saloon.

Buck is standing behind the bar, but somehow there don’t seem to be no
welcome in his eyes. We faces the mirror and elevates one hoof.

“Little touch of hooch, Buck,” grins Dirty, but Buck shakes his head.

“Nope,” he says and points at a sign on the wall. We peers closer and
sees this:

TO WHOM IT MAY CONCERN:

Resolved, that Ike Harper and Dirty Shirt Jones, them same being part
and parcel of our past history, are hereby declared null and void as far
as we are concerned. Them two has violated the principles of liberty and
justice, failed in their duty to mankind and are hereby declared pariahs
and outcasts in Piperock. Resolution framed by Judge Steele and signed
by the following Republicans in good order:

 Magpie Simpkins
 Buck Masterson
 Mighty Jones
 Doughgod Smith
 Ricky Henderson
 Samuel Holt

“Whyfor cometh all this boycotting, Buck?” asks Dirty.

“You two failed to cast votes which would have put local option into the
discard,” says he, solemn-like. “You’re _hyas cultus_ around here.”

“No drinks?” I asks, and he shakes his head.

“O Death, where is thy stinger?” says Dirty dry-like, and we sets down.

“What did you fellers come back here for?” asks Buck. “You ain’t got no
friends left nor nothing.”

Just then old Judge Steele comes in, wiping his brow, and buys himself a
jolt of jiggle juice, but he don’t even seem to see us.

“How’s law and justice nowadays, Judge?” asks Dirty.

The old pelican stares at us like he’d never seen us before.

“Has somebody brought sheep into our fair land, Buck?” he asks.

“Looks thataway, Judge,” admits Buck, sad-like. “Things is changing
every day. Ain’t like the good old days.”

“Ain’t it so!” says the judge. “When we can’t go to the dogs fast
enough, the dogs comes to us, Buck.”

“Listen!” Dirty slides sidewise so his gun is handy. “You pair of
slop-headed snake-hunters think you’re smart, don’t you? Me and Ike felt
that Piperock needed us, and we came back. We’re here, and we’re here to
stay. Hear me holler?”

“If we don’t, we soon will,” admits Buck.

Me and Dirty walks out of there and slams the door. We’re sore. Dirty is
sorer than I am, ’cause he loses all caution and kicks that Lodestone
burro in the south end, the same of which reacts upon him. He picks
himself up and limps over to the sidewalk.

“Dang it all!” he wails. “Somebody has been talking to them burros.”

“I’m going to talk to Magpie,” says I. “He’ll understand.”

If he did, he didn’t show it. That long, lean, sorrowful-looking
_hombre_ never even took his boots off the table when we came in—and
I’ve been his pardner for years. All he done was to hitch his belt
around so his gun rests in his lap and go on spelling out a reward
notice.

“Ain’t you got no bowels of compassion?” I asks, and I put quarts of
tears into that question.

He marks the place with his finger and peers at me.

“Not a gut!” he snaps and goes on reading.

“How does your chances look, Magpie?” asks Dirty.

“Good a minute ago—rotten now.”

We sets there for a while, and then I asks—

“How’s chances for local option to lose?”

“Same as mine to win—now.” And he goes on spelling words.

“Must figure us to be Joners,” opines Dirty sad-like.

“Joner was swallered by a whale,” says I.

“That’s the —— of it,” says Magpie; “we ain’t got no whales.”

“Want me to tell you what I think?” asks Dirty. “Do you?”

“Nope.” Magpie shakes his head. “Our thoughts has got yours beat sixteen
ways from the jack if you thought steady all your life; so you might as
well save your teeth, tongue and wind.”

“Does them sentiments cover me?” I asks.

“I spoke collectively,” says he. “You don’t need to close the door.”

We went out and sat on the sidewalk for a while. Then we happens to
notice Ricky Henderson.

“By cripes, I’m going to get a shave and hair-cut,” says Dirty.

So we both went over. Ricky yawns and looks at us.

“How’s the old razor working, Ricky?” I asks.

“All right, I reckon,” says he indifferent-like.

“We’ll stand a few slices,” grins Dirty. “Set us out two shaves and a
couple of hair-cuts.”

“Not today,” says Ricky. “Come around Friday.”

“Why Friday?” I asks.

“Closed on account of election.”

“Today ain’t election day.”

“No,” says he, “but Friday is.”

 * * * * *

Me and Dirty lets this soak in for a while, and then we walks across to
Sam Holt’s hotel. We ain’t had no dinner, and we hankers for a lot of
ham and eggs. Old Sam meets us in the dining-room, but he don’t grin
like he used to.

“Howdy, Samuel,” says I. “Can the Chink rustle us some ham and eggs?”

Old Sam shakes his head.

“Nope. Far as you fellers are concerned, that pig ain’t been killed yet,
and the chickens are all setting.”

We walks out of there and perches on a hitch-rack like a pair of hairy
old buzzards which have been cheated out of a feed.

“Ike,” says Dirty, “did you ever hear the story about the feller who
went into a dance-hall? He was full of hooch. Well, the manager took him
by the arm and led him outside, but the feller went back again. The
manager throwed him out the next time, but he went back. This time the
manager kicked him out and kicked him plumb across the street. Somebody
says, ‘Going in again?’

“‘Again?’ says he. ‘Me? Think I’m a danged fool? I know when I ain’t
wanted.’”

“They ain’t kicked us out yet, Dirty,” says I.

“No,” agrees Dirty. “But the manager didn’t kick the feller out the
first time either, Ike.”

Then cometh Mrs. Wick Smith. She waddles, talks hoarse and has a lot of
trouble keeping her waist buttoned in the back. She toddles up and looks
us over.

“Back again, eh?” says she. “Going to help us again? We need help.”

“So do we,” nods Dirty.

“We’re going to give a sociable in the Mint Hall tonight and have a big
time. Political temperance speech and charades—you like charades?”

“I don’t,” says Dirty, “although I’m hungry enough to eat mostly any of
them Mexican dishes. For why is the sociable?”

“Campaign fund. We hired the Honorable Fitzhugh Peleg Alexander to make
our big temperance speech. Sent clear to Deer Lodge for him. Fine
talker. Was sent to the Legislature from Sagebrush County in 1899.
Remember him, don’t you? He tried to pass a bill making sheepherding a
misdemeanor.”

“Anything new in the political field?” asks Dirty.

“Well,” says she hoarse-like, “it has been hot in spots. Scenery Sims
and ‘Hassayampa’ Harris has been stumping the county together.”

“And lived?” I asks. “Nobody kill ’em?”

“Not exactly. Nobody is sure whether it was intentional or not. Down at
Paradise Scenery was making a speech, when Chuck Warner’s six-shooter
exploded and cut Scenery’s handkerchief right off his neck. Chuck said
that Scenery’s oration made him feel so melancholy that he tried to
commit suicide, but the gun went off accidental.

“Curlew applauded Hassayampa by hammering on the seats with their guns,
and, when Hassayampa recovered, he found the remains of a bullet
imbedded in his watch and the top of his ear burnt off. Outside of that
there ain’t been no prominent folks shot at.”

“Ike,” says Dirty as Mrs. Smith waddles away, “this country ain’t lost
yet. There is still a slumbering spirit left, and maybe we can fan her
into flame. We’re outlaws—me and you. We’ve soured on them Republican
stummicks, and they don’t hanker for us no more.

“Them _hombres_ proclaim us to be fourteen carats of Democracy. Ain’t
they outcast us? Ain’t they barred us from the flesh-pots? Ain’t they?”

“I won’t argue against it, Dirty.”

“I’m going to turn mean, Ike. In my eyes comes the look of a killer.”

“Make it four eyes and I’ll wear two, Dirty. But we still stand good
with prohibition, don’t we?”

“How so?” squeaks a voice behind us, and we turns to see Scenery Sims.

Me and Dirty gazes upon that little shrimp in chaparejos.

“How so?” parrots Dirty. “Ain’t we helped you a lot?”

“Helped?” he squeaks. “You can’t pull no wool over my eyes—you two. Them
crooked Republicans got you back here for a purpose. Judge Steele just
the same as admitted it, and, when I accused Magpie of the same thing,
he just grins and tells me that I’m hard to fool. You can’t fool
Lindhardt Cadwallader Sims, y’betcha you can’t! I’m going to put them
local optionists wise. _Sabe?_”

And he goes tod’ling across the street, hunching up his shoulders and
elbows like he was riding a trotting horse.

“Home, ho-o-o-o-ome, sweet, sweet ho-o-o-o-ome,” sings Dirty sad and
low. “Ike, it’s like dodging a sidewinder and stepping on a Gila
monster.”

Then “Polecat” Perkins ties his bronc and comes over to us.

“You saved me a trip out to your place, Polecat,” says Dirty, stepping
around until Polecat is down-wind from him.

“Whyfor, Dirty?”

“Ain’t you heard? Ain’t you heard that if Hassayampa Harris is elected
to the Legislature he’s going to pass a bill making skunks a nuisance?”

“Is, eh?” Polecat masticates at top speed for a while. “Is, eh? Well,
well! Let’s all have a little drink.”

“Not in there,” says Dirty. “Buck is in favor of Hassayampa.”

“His touch ain’t contaminated sealed goods,” says Polecat. “I’ll buy a
bottle, and we’ll drink it out here.”

He comes back with a quart in his hand and a grin on his face. He holds
up the bottle, squints through it at the sun and says:

“We’ll drink to——”

_Crash! Bang!_ We gets plentiful sprinkled with liquor and busted glass,
and from the door of the saloon comes the whang of a gun. Polecat stands
there, hanging on to the neck of that bottle.

“——!” says Polecat.

“Under the circumstances that’s as good a toast as any,” says Dirty.

“I ain’t got no gun with me,” complains Polecat as he forks his bronc,
“but, when I come again to this village, I’m coming heeled and in the
proper frame of mind to resent all such maneuvers.”

He rides away, shaking his head and still hanging on to the neck of that
bottle. Me and Dirty humps up on that rack again and smokes to keep from
getting too dry.

“Beg pardon, but I can’t seem to place you two,” says a voice behind us,
and we crooks our necks to look at the Honorable Fitzhugh Peleg
Alexander.

He’s looking at us over a big pair of specs, one hand inside his vest
like he’d been sleeping in a sheep camp and brought away something.

“You don’t need to worry about placing us,” says Dirty. “We’re here.”

“Who have I the honor of addressing?” he asks, and Dirty snorts:

“Honor, ——! Tell that to the natives and get lynched.”

“Where did you get them clothes?” I asks, looking him over.

“There was an honest wheel in Paradise, and I bought ’em from the
undertaker. Glad I ran across you, ’cause I need money.”

“For what?” asks Dirty.

“Running expenses. I’m staying with ‘Old Testament’ Tilton, and he plays
a danged stiff game of seven-up.”

“Reckon we’re going to stake you?” I asks.

“Have to,” says he. “Otherwise I’ll have to go.”

“Feller like you hadn’t ought to hock his gun,” observes Dirty. “How was
Jesse James when you left the gang?”

“Fifty dollars,” says the feller absent-like, “was the price. Maybe you
can find another man to take my place. Remember I’ve got to have enough
to get awful drunk on to make the play right.”

“Me and Ike ain’t been treated right by our friends since we came,” says
Dirty. “We’re outcasts of society, but I reckon we’re strong enough to
suffer for our principle. They’ll make heroes out of us when they finds
out the truth, Ike.”

“Yes,” says I, “but dead men don’t appreciate applause.”

“Wait, and I’ll see what I can do,” says Dirty.

 * * * * *

He drops off the rack and trots across the street to Wick Smith’s store.
He’s in there about five minutes. Then he comes back and hands Honorable
Alexander some bills. The feller looks at it, counts it over and walks
on.

“Well, that’s all over and settled, Ike,” says Dirty relieved-like. “I
was afraid that Wick might hate us too much to do business with me, but
it was a bargain at fifty dollars.”

“What did you sell, Dirty?” I asks.

“Them four burros.”

“Our——”

I sets there and stares at Dirty Shirt, and then I looks over at the
burros.

Lodestone and Pin Ear belongs to me body and soul, if a burro has any
soul. I’ve had them two canaries ever since Sitting Bull sat down. Why,
Lodestone is the champion kicker of the State, and Pin Ear is his pupil.

I looks down at Dirty Shirt, but my tongue won’t speak. My temperature
goes up to one hundred and thirty-five in the shade, and I drops off and
takes Dirty Shirt Jones to my bosom. Right there in the main street of
my old home town I clasps him unto me.

Man, I sure combed that _hombre_ all the way, and he reciprocates to the
best of his ability. We hugs each other laying down, standing up and
spinning on our heads. Being as I can’t see much, I don’t know how the
battle stands, but all to once I hears Magpie’s voice:

“That’s plenty! Don’t kill him!”

“Good thing you spoke,” says I. “I plumb forgot myself.”

“I wasn’t speaking to you, Ike,” says Magpie, and I sets up.

Dirty is standing there, trying to put on two halves of his vest and
hold on a pant-leg at the same time. Wick Smith is standing there at the
hitch-rack beside the burros, grinning like a fool.

“Haw! Haw! Haw!” he whoops and slaps Lodestone on the rump.

I could kiss that burro for what he done. Wick lands about ten feet
away, gets up, walks into the rack and falls on his face.

“Haw! Haw! Haw!” I whoops. “Me and that mule can whip the town.”

“Whip ——!” yelps Dirty, picking up a rock. “You couldn’t whip nobody!”

He hits Magpie right in the neck with that rock. Magpie sets down on the
ground and begins to whistle something I’ve never heard before. Dirty
looks foolish-like at him and weaves off across the street. I’m right
behind him.

“Dirty,” says I. “I apologize for licking you. That was a good shot.”

“Like thunder!” he wails. “I aimed at you—dang your hide! Now I’ll get
arrested.”

“And put in jail,” says I, “and then your brother outcast will have to
get some dynamite and blow you out.”

“You let up on that dynamite talk!” he wails. “Where’d I be?”

“You wouldn’t have any jail around you, Dirty.”

“You let me be in jail,” he howls. “Dog-gone you, don’t you dynamite no
jails what I’m in. Maybe I won’t be in.”

“Yes, you will,” says I. “I’m going to have you arrested for selling
burros what don’t belong to you. _Sabe?_”

“If you do, I’ll swear that you hired that _hombre_ to speak for
prohibition.”

“That ain’t nothing to jail a feller for,” says I.

“Nope. Under the present administration you wouldn’t live to get to a
good, safe jail, Ike.”

“That’s them,” squeaks a voice behind us, and we turns to see Scenery
and Old Testament Tilton standing there looking at us. “Them are
Republican spies and disturbers, Testament. They publically pulls off a
fight to try and fool us.”

“A wrath cometh that will cause the wicked to flee,” says Old Testament
pious-like. “They will talk in many tongues, and their temples will
fall.”

“I talk two now,” says I, “United States and profane. I ain’t got no
temple, and I licked Dirty Shirt in public ’cause I didn’t have time to
take him out of sight.”

“You licked me?” asks Dirty.

“Well, why argue about it,” says I. “Everybody has a right to their own
opinion.”

“When I’m sheriff, I’m going to clean up!” squeaks Scenery.

“Good!” applauds Dirty. “I’d admire to see how you’d look, feller. If
you get beat, you’ll stay filthy, eh?”

“Far be it from me to ask anybody to go away,” says Old Testament, “but
it would be an act of kindness if you two would leave. Your game is
knowed full well, and you ain’t here for no good.”

“We don’t look like we was here for our health; that’s a cinch,” admits
Dirty. “We don’t ask for no fatted calves, but—ain’t you got no hearts?”

“Hearts!” squeaks Scenery. “Why, dang your spying——”

Scenery got that far when Dirty kicked his feet out from under him, and
he bit his tongue when he hit the dirt. He sets there nursing his tongue
and shaking his head.

“Violence,” says Testament, “don’t figure in our program, but you
bug-headed sheep in wolves’ clothing are——”

Testament is a preacher of the Gospel. I’ve got lots of respect for a
preaching preacher, but, when he steps out and calls me a sheep, I sort
of classes him with human beings. Me and Dirty looks back when we’ve got
to the corner, and Dirty says chiding-like:

“Suppose you should die tonight, Ike, and needed a Christian burial
tomorrow. What do you reckon Old Testament would say over you?”

“He’d have to say something different than he said when he sat down on
the ground just now, Dirty, ’cause them words ain’t in no burial service
I ever heard.”

 * * * * *

From force of habit we wanders over to Buck’s place and sets down. Buck
is building a solitaire layout and don’t give us a second look. Pretty
soon Dirty Shirt says—

“Buck, I know something you ought to hear.”

“Yeah?” says Buck. “You’re talking.”

“Them prohibitionists has handed us a raw deal, Buck, and I don’t figure
me and Ike owes them any loyalty. They feeds the hand what bites ’em.
Tomorrow there’s going to be rough stuff pulled in Curlew. A bunch from
Custer County is going to vote Dry. _Sabe?_”

“Illegal voters?” asks Buck interested-like.

“You heard enough, Buck. If you tell on me, I’ll assassinate you.”

“I’m wise,” nods Buck. “Hassayampa’s doings—dang him!”

Me and Dirty walks out and runs into Hassayampa. Dirty takes him to one
side and whispers in his ear. Hassayampa gets interested a heap.

“Right here?” asks Hassayampa. “The gall of them jaspers! I’ll go over
to Wick’s and get you fellers something to eat. Barred you from the
hotel, eh?”

Mike Pelly rides in from Paradise, and me and Dirty goes over to see
him. He ain’t friendly, but his ears are open to talk.

“You can take it or leave it—suit yourself,” says Dirty, “but between
the hours of 8 A. M. and 8 P. M. there’s going to be crooked work pulled
off right here, Mike. None of my business what you do. _Sabe?_”

Mike nods and goes over to Buck’s. Hassayampa brings us over some canned
food, and we enjoys a big feed.

“Dirty,” says I, “you’re a awful, awful liar.”

“Nobody believes the truth, Ike; so we might as well lie.”

“What will it get for us?” I asks.

“Well, there’s more belligerent votes among the Dries than there is
among the Wets. When a man is looking for trouble, he ain’t got time to
vote, and—you ain’t got brains enough to see six feet ahead.”

“Go ahead and lie if it brings happiness to your penny-ante soul.”

“I’ve been looking for you two,” says Magpie Simpkins, and we turns to
see him and Judge Steele beside us. “I arrests you both for assaulting a
officer of the law.”

Magpie is wearing his head cocked to one side like a startled fool hen.

“I tried to hit Ike with that rock,” says Dirty.

“He’s lying,” says I. “He threw it right at you.”

“Think I didn’t know that?” snaps Magpie. “I just wanted to know which
one of you it was. I’ll take Dirty.”

“Dead—maybe,” nods Dirty. “Not alive, Magpie. That bow-legged Harper
honyock says he’ll dynamite the jail if I’m put in there.”

“Dinnymiter, eh?” crows the judge. “Dinnymiter at heart, Magpie. Put him
in.”

“I’ve only got one cell, Judge. They’ll fight.”

“Let ’em,” grins the old pelican. “We’ll watch.”

“Coming peaceful?” asks Magpie, and we nods.

He herds us across in front of Wick’s store. Out comes Hassayampa,
Scenery, and Wick.

“Get out of the way of the law!” yelps the old judge.

“Wait!” yells Hassayampa. “What is the arrest for, Magpie?”

“’Cause we’re Dry,” yells Dirty. “They wants to kill our votes.”

“Starting their skulduggery already, eh?” grunts Wick. “Trying to
cripple a worthy cause on the eve of its victory, eh?”

“We stands by our compatriots!” squeaks Scenery, and I know that
Hassayampa has told ’em what Dirty told him.

Pete Gonyer has rode up beside us, and I sees Wick signal him something.
Pete rides a few steps away, but I ain’t got time to wonder about him.

“I arrests them two, and they’re going to jail!” declares Magpie. “None
of you drouthers are going to hinder me in the performance of my sworn
duty, believe me.”

I reckon Pete had the right idea. The play must ’a’ been to get Magpie,
but the loop was too big. I felt it rasp across my neck, I collides with
Magpie, and we both left the ground. Then we lands on my back. I gets a
fleeting look at things, and I sees that Pete is still in the saddle,
but that rope has got around the bronc’s hind legs and tail, the same of
which aggravates it a heap.

Magpie seems to have got all my left ear in his teeth and is breathing
hard through his nose. I starts in to address him on the evils of
cannibalism, but I feels a tug at my heart-strings and we’re off again.
This time the bronc seems to have a definite object in view. Pete may be
a local optionist, but that bronc didn’t draw no liquor line, ’cause it
bucked right through Buck’s front door and across the corner of the
pool-table, leaving me and Magpie half-way across the bar, with the
saddle and all the slack rope tied around the stove.

I wiggles loose and falls behind the bar, the same of which deposits
Magpie in front of the bar. I lit on something soft, which appears to be
Buck Masterson, but I don’t worry about him.

I’ve been shocked to such an extent that I hankers for courage. My neck
ain’t no-ways in line with my heels, but I decides to drink, anyway, and
let it cut the curves. I grabs a bottle off the back-bar and deluges my
throat with the contents. I took the first bottle I got hold of, and I
found out right then that it pays to be particular. I never swallered so
hard in all my life. I lifts up that bottle to see what I’ve absorbed.
Then there comes a crash and that bottle seems to dissolve like a bad
dream.

“I comes heeled this time,” states a voice, and there is Polecat in the
doorway with a gun in his hand. “Charge me a dollar a bottle and then
shoot it out of my hand, will you!”

Polecat comes sneaking in and peers at me.

“——!” says he. “Wrong again.”

“Polecat,” says I, “will you look on the floor and see if you can find
the label that was on that bottle?”

He looks around and finally picks up a sticky piece of paper and lays it
on the bar.

“Is that what you drink?” asks Polecat wondering-like.

“That’s what I drank,” I admits.

“My ——!” says he. “You don’t care if the country does go dry, do you?
You can always get kerosene, Ike.”

We hears a noise over by the pool-table. A pair of boots comes up, rests
on top of the table, like the owner of them boots was trying to stand on
his head. Buck has got to his feet and is leaning against me, making
funny faces when he tries to talk. From the looks of Buck he must ’a’
tried to keep that bronc out by main strength. He peers at them two feet
on his table, and then he whispers hoarse-like:

“Can’t you—you read? Keep one foot on the floor!”

The boots slid off the table, clumped when they hit the floor, and over
the edge of the table comes Pete’s face. He blinks at us like an owl.

“You—you ——” says Magpie’s voice, and we turns to see him swaying just
inside the door, trying to cock a gun. “You danged arid jassack!” he
wails.

Pete nods his head and slides just below the table level as the gun
explodes. Magpie made a good shot. He ain’t no Buffalo Bill with a
six-gun, and maybe he couldn’t do it again, but that bullet busted right
into the nail that holds the ball-rack, and that piece of furniture and
sixteen pool balls comes down on top of Pete with a crash.

“_Sic semper tyrannis!_” grunts Magpie.

“My gosh!” grunts Polecat. “What a name for a dog! Magpie thinks he’s
herding sheep.”

We sees Pete get up from behind that table, get up like he didn’t have a
care in the world. He’s got lumps on his head until that member ain’t
no-ways shaped to hold a hat, and his clothes wouldn’t bring a nickel in
a rag house.

He pokes around the corner of the table and weaves straight past us and
out of the door, and you can call me a liar if he ain’t trying to take a
bite out of a red pool ball.

“I—I ain’t had a apple for ages,” says Magpie peevish-like. “Not for
ages and ages.”

“Well,” says Buck, “you might foller Pete; he won’t eat it all.”

Magpie nods and limps out of the door.

“Ike drank your kerosene, Buck,” states Polecat.

“Kerosene?” Buck looks at Polecat and then at the back-bar.

“That bottle what was there?” he asks, and Polecat nods.

“Dang it!” snorts Buck. “I was trying a experiment, and now she’s
spoiled! I wanted to see if I could preserve a little snake in oil the
same as you can in alcohol.”

“Could you?” asks Polecat.

“I don’t know,” says Buck sad-like. “It’s only been in there two weeks.”

 * * * * *

That kind of talk makes me sick. I goes outside and runs into old Judge
Steele.

“You’re still under arrest, Ike,” says he positive-like. “The fact that
Pete interferes with his rope don’t lessen the fact that you’re due to
languish in durance vile. Too bad you snake-hunters——”

Thus ended the discourse. I’m from a peaceable family, and I hate a
rumpus of any kind, but any old time they connects my name with snakes I
forgets my breeding.

I’m mean when I gets mad, and I ain’t responsible for what I do. Maybe
it’s a good thing them local optionists pulled us apart. Wick Smith
chased the judge half-way home, while the rest of ’em led me inside the
store, where quite a crowd is congregated.

“This is going to be a big night,” states Hassayampa, who seems to be
making a speech. “Word has been sent to our committees in Curlew and
Paradise, and they will be on hand to figure out ways and means to
combat the evil which confronts us. The stage is set; on with the dance,
and let joy be unrefined.”

“We can thank Mr. Jones for his timely confession,” says Mrs. Smith.

“He has unmasked the liquor ring. Them snakes in the——”

I got up and limped right out of there. I’m only human. I limped all the
way up to our shack and went in. Magpie is setting there on a box,
oiling his gun, and he nods as I come in.

“Going to kill somebody?” I asks.

“Likely.”

“Local?”

“Foreign.”

“They’re slick.”

“Y’betcha, but we’re slicker, Ike. We’ll show them drouthers they can’t
fool with us. We’re going to picket Curlew and Paradise from 6 A. M. to
6 P. M. tomorrow, and they’ll do us no dirt. _Sabe?_”

“Can I lay me down to sleep, Magpie?” I asks. “I’m all in.”

“I ain’t never killed nobody in their sleep—yet,” says he.

Just then comes a knock at the door, and Scenery Sims enters our abode.
He squints at me and Magpie and squeaks:

“I just came to let you know that your nefarious schemes are known,
Magpie Simpkins. We’ll thwart you, dog-gone you; we will!”

“You’ll say something pretty soon,” says Magpie.

“Your spy system is played out and perished,” says Scenery, waving his
arms. “The machinery of the Republican party is full of monkey-wrenches
and its wheels are rusting. Right will prevail, gents and ladies, and
this land will be fairer than the lilies in the dell.”

“You won’t smell ’em,” says Magpie slow-like. “They’ll be over you, you
squeaky little cousin to a chickadee! I’ll give you ten seconds to get
out the window. Hit the glass, Lindhardt!”

“I came in the door,” says Scenery, “and I’m going——”

“Out of the window,” says Magpie, “six, seven, eight, nine——”

Scenery went. The first bullet went into the floor under his feet, and
he hopped four feet high and went through that window like a circus
rider going through a hoop. As his heels disappear, comes the whang of
Magpie’s second shot, and the world seems to rise up and smite me on
four sides at once.

I hears the gentle rain-drops pattering around me, and I sort of rises
my head to take a look at the storm. Two more rain-drops fall beside me,
and, when I claws the dirt out of my eyes so I can see, I finds that the
last two drops consisted of our cook-stove and Magpie’s chair. I looks
at our shack and wonders aloud at what I see. Things sure have changed a
lot around the old homestead.

Just then I sees a busted chair rise up and turn around several times
before the party under it can get loose. Then he shucks a picture-frame
off his neck and kicks the water-bucket off his right leg. Then he peers
at me and says—

“Ike, have you got any lilies—of—the—dell?”

Magpie’s mustache is all gone, and I wonder if his shirt eloped with his
boot.

“No lilies,” says I. “I’m about half out of flowers today. Would you
mind telling me what kind of ammunition you use in that gun?”

He looks at the ruined home and rubs his dirty face.

“In the corner of the shack was forty pounds of dynamite, Ike, and I—I
think maybe I hit it.”

“The evidence is all in your favor,” says I. “Where is Scenery?”

About thirty feet away from us lies the door of our shack, and as I asks
the question that door seems to rise up a little and fall back. Then it
rises up again, and somebody seems to get up behind it. Then it falls
toward us, and standing there in the light of day is a woful-looking
_hombre_. He looks like the breaking up of a hard Winter.

“I came visiting,” says he weak-like. “I came visiting.”

“Yes, yes,” says I. “Proceed.”

“I—I turned the knob,” says he, like he was trying to remember something
that happened long ago. “I turned the—” Then he stops. “My ——! I must be
strong! I—I yanked that door so hard that it came off the hinges and
lifted me plumb over here!”

“You hadn’t ought to yank a door thataway, Dirty Shirt,” chides Magpie.
“Look what you done to our home.”

Dirty looks at the remnants of the shack and then rubs his eyes.

“I can’t stay,” he whispers. “I—I just called—that’s all.”

And he weaves off toward town.

“Called ——!” grunts Magpie. “He never even seen my last raise, Ike.”

We pilgrims up and looks at our shack. One end and all of the roof is in
the discard. There’s a hole about six feet deep near where the window
used to be, and, as we peer down into it, a foolish face looks up at us
and a voice squeaks:

“Don’t shoot! Dog-gone you, don’t shoot again! I’ve got a headache.”

“Didn’t I give you ten seconds to get out?” asks Magpie. “What are you
doing in my house again?”

“Oh!” Scenery peers around and crawls out. He peers around and nods. “I
remember,” says he. “I lit on my hands and knees, didn’t I? And then the
world raised up behind me, and I fell into the hole that it came out
of.”

He peers all around and digs some gravel out of his ears.

“I see it all now!” he yelps. “You tried to assassinate me! You—What are
you looking for?”

“My gun,” says Magpie, poking into the wreck. “Remember now that I only
counted to nine. I beg your pardon, Mister Sims.”

Scenery stumbled over a log and fell down, but he got right up and went
toward town, talking to himself, and I went right behind him. Maybe I
talked a little, too. A feller can’t get blowed through the side of a
house without having something to talk about.

 * * * * *

Then I meets Lodestone. Right in the middle of the road I meets that
lovable burro. We rubs noses, and then I climbs on his back and rides up
the road, where I runs into Dirty Shirt and Polecat. It is almost dark.
Polecat hands me a bottle, the same of which is investigated by all of
my senses before I imbibes.

“Me and Dirty has been appointed guards of honor, Ike,” says Polecat.
“We’ve been appointed to guard the ice cream.”

“Does she need guards?” I asks.

“Sure,” grins Polecat. “The local optionists have heard that the
Republicans are going to steal it, and I volunteers to guard it with my
life and Dirty’s.”

“Who told you they was going to steal it?” I asks.

“Dirty Shirt.”

“It must be true then,” says I.

Then I rode the burro over to Buck’s rack and went inside the saloon.
Curlew, Paradise and Piperock are well represented in there, and they
talks some about the sociable.

“You fellers won’t see none of it,” says I. “You won’t even be allowed
in the hall.”

“How do you know?” asks Mike Pelly.

“Dirty Shirt told me.”

Then I went out and sat on the sidewalk across the street. Lodestone is
the only friend I’ve got left, and he don’t belong to me. The hall is
lit up, and the local optionists are going in right along. Pretty soon
here comes Dirty Shirt and sets down with me.

“Through being warden to a hunk of frozen milk?” I asks.

“Uh-huh. By golly, if we could only be there to see it, Ike!
You’d—— Look at the fire! Buck’s saloon is on fire, Ike!”

From behind Buck’s place comes flame and sparks, which proclaims it to
be a regular honest-to-grandma house on fire.

Somebody runs out of the saloon, shoots into the air and yells at the
top of his voice:

“Fire! Fire! Fire!”

Somebody else joins in the chorus, and that fire sure got advertised
plenty.

It ain’t often that us folks get to enjoy a regular fire, and the local
optionists pour out of the hall like bees. The street is full of folks
doing everything they can to hinder anybody from putting out the fire.
Me and Dirty lopes to the pump and splashes water plentiful until they
begins to arrive with everything from a barrel to a hat. All to once
somebody comes galloping across the street, yelling:

“Brush-pile! Just a bonfire. Wasn’t within fifty feet of the saloon.”

“Gents—” Old Testament enters our midst. His hard hat has been drove
plumb down around his neck, and the brim sticks out from his chin like
he was trying to shade his neck. “Gents, it pains me to inform you that
the opposition has captured the Mint Hall, barred the door and are
proceeding to appease their insides with our provender.”

“How do you know?” yelps Hassayampa, dropping a pail of water on his
feet.

“I know,” says Testament, “because they dumped me out of the
second-story window.”

“My ——!” gurgles Dirty, taking me by the arm. “Come away, Ike.”

I follers him across the street.

“Whyfor the exclamation, Dirty?” I asks, peering across the street,
where the crowd is milling around the door of the hall.

“Polecat hates Hassayampa,” explains Dirty. “Polecat tells ’em that them
Wets are going to steal the ice cream; so they deputizes us to guard it.
We takes out the cream and the ice, Ike, and dumps it in a safe place,
and then me and Polecat climbs into the rafters of the hall, and——”

Yeo-o-o-o-ow! _Bang! Bang! Crash!_ Yeo-o-o-ow!

We sees the lights go out in the hall. Out of the windows and doors come
hurrying men, and for the first time in history the Democrats and
Republicans merge into one. They not only merge, but they seem to get
very close to each other. Some of ’em comes our way, and I lopes over to
the rack to get on Lodestone. He ain’t fast, but he’s the fastest I’ve
got.

A bullet fans my ear, and I turns to shoot back—with the gun I ain’t
got—when somebody slams into me and we both goes down. I rolls over
about six times before I can stop. Then I gets right up, hops on to that
burro and digs him with my heels.

Lodestone is a noble steed. He feels the need of fast work and dashes
away—to the end of the rope. Some danged fool had tied him to the rack.
Me and that other jassack stands on our heads, and I reckon we both got
up at the same time. That darn old rabbit humps up like he was sore at
the whole world, and then that man I ran into gets up and staggers to
his feet.

“Sus-sorry I can’t make speech,” says he thick-like. “Ha, ha, ha! Got
money in advance, too. Ha, ha, ha! Paid twice. Thanks. Rotten campaign.
Pleased to meet you.”

The Honorable Alexander has been hit so hard that he ain’t neither got
sense nor caution. He haw-haws some more to himself and shakes hands
with Lodestone’s tail.

“Good night, gents,” says he. “Lovely party.”

“Good night and good-by,” says I.

Just then Lodestone lifts his legs sort of jerky-like, and I turns away.
Lodestone must ’a’ been dazed a little, too, ’cause he kicked twice when
once was sufficient.

I lights a match and looks at the feller. He squints cross-eyed at the
match, and I know that he’s had plenty. He’s only got one sleeve of his
coat on. I take it off and puts it on myself. All I’ve got left is one
sleeve of my shirt, and I feel ashamed.

Then I herds Lodestone into the open door of the livery-stable, climbs
up into the loft and goes to sleep on the hay. I don’t know how long I
slept, but I do know that the sun is shining through the cracks in the
west side when I wake up.

I hears voices raised in argument and anger below me, but that don’t
mean nothing in Piperock. Sudden-like I sees the hay begin to rise, and
out of that tangle of timothy comes the head of Dirty Shirt.

He nods to me and digs some foxtail out of his neck. Dirty looks like he
had been hit in the face so many times that nothing short of bunion
plasters would give him relief.

“Where did you get that coat?” he whispers.

I reaches inside the coat and hauls out a pocketbook and some old
letters. I looks at ’em and hands ’em to Dirty Shirt. The epitaphs on
the envelopes proclaim ’em to be for the Honorable Fitzhugh Peleg
Alexander, Deer Lodge, Montana.

“He was him?” asks Dirty foolish-like.

“Is,” says I. “Unless Lodestone hit him twice.”

I opens the pocketbook, and she assays fifty dollars.

“Honorable,” says I, “you’re wrong; you only get paid once.”

“Talking to yourself?” asks Dirty.

“Nope. But the feller I’m talking to can’t hear me.”

 * * * * *

Then we crawls over to the hay-hole and peers down. In the center of the
room is a table and a box, and around the table sets old Sam Holt, Wick
Smith, Ricky Henderson and “Jay Bird” Whittaker. Two Wets and two Dries.
Each one of them election officials has a gun in front of him. Scattered
around the room is representatives from abroad. Hassayampa Harris, “Sad”
Samuels, “Weeping” Wilson, “Ace” Allison and “Half-Pint” Perkins from
Curlew, and Cobalt Williams, Mike Pelly, Bill McFee, Andy Johnson, Art
Miller, “Slim” Hawkins, Zeb Abernathy, Hank Padden and Abe Mudgett, all
from Paradise.

We watches ’em for a while, and pretty soon Sam Holt says—

“The time grows late; I’ll cast my vote.”

He takes a ballot and enters a stall. When he comes back, Wick Smith
gets up, takes a ballot and announces—

“I’m going to kill that vote.”

When he comes back, Ricky Henderson shuffles his feet, picks up a ballot
and says, “Wick, your vote is in the sere and yaller leaf.”

And he votes.

Jay Bird don’t say anything. He just looks at Ricky and votes. No need
for him to say a word. The vote now is fifty-fifty.

Things drag along for a while, and Chuck Warner rides in from Paradise.

“Any trouble down there?” asks Mike Pelly anxious-like.

“Nope.” Chuck shakes his head and sets down.

Then cometh Pete Pelliser from Curlew. His bronc is lathered plentiful,
and the contingent from Curlew crowds around him.

“How’s she coming?” asks Hassayampa.

“Only six votes cast when I left,” says Pete. “Ain’t you fellers going
to vote?”

“Not here,” says Sam Holt. “This ain’t their precinct.”

Then that bunch of belligerents stands up and sees things in the right
light. They’ve mistrusted each other to the extent that they forgets
their duties as citizens—and it’s too late to get back.

“How many votes cast in Paradise, Chuck?” yelps Mike.

“Not over twenty, and they’re about fifty-fifty, Mike.”

“My ——!” groans Mike. “And we was in the majority by two.”

Just then old Sam Holt happens to look up and sees me and Dirty. He lets
out a yelp that could be heard in Helena, and the whole bunch looks up
at us.

“There’s the winning votes!” whoops old Sam. “They turns the tide!”

“Come down,” orders Mike Pelly. “Come down and save the country.”

“For us!” whoops Hassayampa. “Come down, friends.”

“We ain’t going to vote,” says Dirty. “We’re pariahs. You boobs has all
broke your picks with Dirty Shirt Jones, Ike Harper and so forth. We
don’t owe allegiance to nothing nor nobody. _Sabe?_”

Me and Dirty enjoys the spectacle below us. Neither side dares to shoot
us; so we leans as far out as we can and chuckles to ourselves.

I never did find out who threw that rope, but I think it was Chuck.
Anyway, it was somebody who was handy with a rope, ’cause they got us
the first swipe. Then me and Dirty gets yanked off that hay, and a
second later that table ceases to be a table and the ballot-box wouldn’t
hold rocks. Me and Dirty got walked upon in the events which followed.

They say that music hath charms to soothe the savage beasts, but every
time I puckers my mouth to whistle somebody kicked me in the face. After
a while the battle passes outside, and we has a chance to get rid of
that rope.

Sam Holt’s feet are sticking out from under the table, and I sees Wick
rise up with a horse-collar around his neck and the rest of the harness
drapin’ his figure. Me and Dirty drags the table off old Sam, and he
lays there, kicking his feet on the floor like he was internally
disconnected.

“Wick,” says I, “I want them burros back.” I shoved that fifty into his
unresisting hand. “Did you pay that speaker fifty dollars?”

“Uh-huh,” he nods. “He—he didn’t earn it though, and we chased him plumb
over to Powder River to get it back, but he got away.”

“What—do—you—want?” gasps old Sam, setting up.

“Want to vote,” says I. “Can I?”

“Use—your—own—judgment,” says Sam.

I sat down and marked a ballot. When I got through, Dirty marked one.
Then we got up, walked to the back of the stable and herded our four
burros out back, where our plunder was piled.

“Now—is—the—time—for—all—wise—men—to—look—out—for—themselves,” says
Dirty, pronouncing each word between yanks on a cinch.

We prods them burros out between the stable and the barber-shop and sees
a big crowd in the street; so we herds ’em right back and goes around
the other way.

“We don’t care what the excitement is about, Ike,” says Dirty. “Me and
you have had about enough—I know I have.”

“I’d like to have my old gun,” says I. “I lost it when the shack blew
up. Maybe we can find it, Dirty.”

We went up there and hunted around in the dark for an hour—we can’t find
it; so we points them burros down the road and runs into Magpie. He
stops and stares at us.

“Where’s your bronc?” I asks.

“Somebody turned it loose!” he yelps.

“Did Scenery beat you?” asks Dirty.

“How do I know? I’m one vote behind, and Piperock to hear from. Local
option is three behind and Piperock to hear from.”

“Cinch,” grins Dirty. “You get four votes in Piperock, Magpie.”

“Gents, I thank you. I—I reckon we misjudged you two, and I apologize.
Come back to your old home town and enjoy the fullness thereof.”

“Well,” says Dirty, “we might take one shot for old times’ sake.”

We left the burros there and walked back with him. There’s a crowd in
Buck’s place, and we horns right in. Magpie pushes his way to the bar
and faces the crowd.

“Gents,” says he, “let’s celebrate our sweeping victory. We win.”

“You do?” squeaks Scenery Sims. “You do not! I got an even break here,
which places me by one vote, and local option went solid, which puts
this county dry by one vote.”

There is silence for a moment, and then Magpie’s voice hits a high key—

“Did Sam Holt and Ricky Henderson throw us down?”

I slipped out of there, ducked around the corner and ran right into
somebody coming the other way. We went down in a heap and fought out to
the middle of the street. My antagonist grunts—

“Dang your soul, fight fair!”

I let loose of Dirty’s ear, and we staggered to our feet. We limped
around back of the saloon and sat down for a while until we gets our
breath; then we gets up and pilgrims toward our burros. I don’t _sabe_
things at all, but I’m wise that there’s been a hitch somewhere.

We walks up to the burros, and a familiar voice says—

“Dog-gone you, I’ve got you now, you pariah pups!”

Magpie’s figure looms up from among our live stock. I sees the moonlight
shine on the barrel of his gun, and I says my little prayer. He pushes a
burro out of the way so he can have open shooting—but that burro was
Lodestone. We propped Magpie up against a mesquite bush and hung his
sombrero on the top so they’d find him, and then we points our cavalcade
toward the north star again. We pokes along until we comes to the top of
the big butte, from which we can see the lights of Piperock, and then we
stops for a breathing spell.

“Dirty,” says I, “you started to tell me what you and Polecat done to
them ice-cream containers last night. What was it?”

“Hornets. Six big nests, Ike. We—we stung our friends. Our old friends.”

“Old is the right word, Dirty. Anything else to tell me? How comes that
only four votes showed up when we know of six that were cast?”

“I was too loyal to my friends, I reckon, Ike. Remember when we busted
that ballot-box? Well, when I put our votes in, I took out two so as to
make it a cinch. I—I reckon I—— Well, Ike, I figured on taking the Dry
votes, but I reckon I got the wrong two.”

“Where are they?” I asks. “Can’t we take ’em back?”

“I didn’t dare to keep ’em, Ike; so I—I fed ’em to Lodestone.”

I took a quart of perfectly good whisky out of my pocket and held it up.

“Dirty,” says I, “being as we’ve got a long dry spell ahead of us, I
reckon we might as well start now by busting this on a rock.”

“My gosh! Don’t do that, Ike. Save it for snakes.”

That awful word again! I slammed that bottle straight for Dirty’s head,
but I missed him, and it flew among them burros. I hears that bottle
thump; then comes a grunt, a rattle, and broken glass and whisky flew
all over us.

“Ike,” says Dirty awed-like, “when a jackass kicks at it on the fly, she
ain’t neither good for man nor beast. I’m glad I killed your vote.”

“Killed my vote?”

“Uh-huh. Ike, I didn’t figure that either side had any favors coming
from me and you, and I knowed how you’d vote; so I decided they wouldn’t
profit if I could help it, and I—I killed your Wet vote. _Sabe?_”

“You did like ——!” says I. “You added to it.”

And we pointed again toward the north star.
