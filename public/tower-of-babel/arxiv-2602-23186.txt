The transient behavior of superconducting multi-cell accelerating cavities.
Volker Ziemann

Abstract
We employ an equivalent-circuit model of a multi-cell cavity to explore its time-dependent behavior in order to understand differences between the multi-cell model and the commonly-used model of a single-cell resonator. Furthermore, we address tolerances that arise from manufacturing imperfections.



1
Introduction

In most simulations of the transient behavior of multi-cell superconducting
acceleration structures the cavity is represented by a single resonator
that consists of a discrete resistor
R
, inductor
L
, and capacitance

C

[
1
,
2
]
. In reality, however, the individual cells
that constitute the cavity should be treated as individual but coupled
resonators, each having its specific resonance-frequency detuning,

Q
-value, and shunt impedance
R
. Only for the analysis of the supported
eigenmodes in multi-cell cavities, the cells are considered as coupled
resonators
[
3
]
. This analysis is mostly static though some
time-dependent effects are mentioned in
[
4
]
. In this report,
we present a comprehensive analysis of multi-cell cavities and discuss
the impact of cavity-fabrication imperfections on their time-dependent behavior.

Figure
1
illustrates our model. The generator with internal

Z_{0}
provides a current
I_{g}
that is transported to the
cavity by a matched transmission line. The input power is fed to the
first cell with an input coupler that is modeled as a transformer with
winding ratio
n
. Each of the individual cells is represented as an
independent RLC circuit and adjacent cells are coupled via capacitors

\hat{C}
. Additionally, the first and last cell experience the coupling
to the beam pipe, here modeled by the capacitors labeled
C_{a}
and
C_{b}
.
We also indicate the beam current
I_{c}
that passes through all cells and
excites fields inside the cells, though in this note we do not consider
this effect and focus on the behavior of the cavity without beam. In
particular, we address variations among cells and how they affect the
forward current
I^{+}_{L}
and the backward current
I^{-}_{L}
measured by a
directional coupler just upstream of the coupler as well as a field
probe connected to the last cell that measures the cavity voltage
V_{n}
.
Reference planes on either side of the coupler are depicted as dashed
lines. The voltages and currents are related by the transformer ratio

V_{1}=nV_{L}
and
I_{1}=I_{L}/n
and consequently the impedances
Z_{0}=Z_{L}=V_{L}/I_{L}

and
Z_{g}=V_{1}/I_{1}
are related by
Z_{g}=n^{2}Z_{L}
.

Before addressing the fully time-dependent problem, we briefly address the
steady state of the bare multi-cell cavity. This analysis follows the
discussion in
[
3
]
and helps us to determine values for the beam pipe
capacitances
C_{a}
and
C_{b}
as well as the supported eigenfrequencies and
corresponding eigenmodes in the cavity. In contrast to the discussion
in
[
3
,
4
]
, which is based on series-resonator circuits, we use
parallel circuits.

We assume that the system oscillates with frequency

such that we can use

to characterize

we thus find

0=I_{1}-I_{2}+I_{LC}=i\omega C_{a}V_{1}-i\omega\hat{C}(V_{2}-V_{1})+\left(i\omega C+\frac{1}{i\omega L}\right)V_{1}\ ,

(1)

where

is the current that flows from ground to the node labeled
V_{1}
. Diving this
equation by
i\omega C
and introducing the abbreviations
\kappa_{a}=C_{a}/C
and
\hat{\omega}^{2}=1/LC

we arrive at

\left(1+\kappa+\kappa_{a}\right)V_{1}-\kappa V_{2}=\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}V_{1}\ .

(2)

Likewise, the current balance at cell
j
leads us to

0=I_{j}-I_{j-1}+I_{LC}=i\omega\hat{C}(V_{j}-V_{j-1})-i\omega\hat{C}(V_{j+1}-V_{j})+\left(i\omega C+\frac{1}{i\omega L}\right)V_{j}\ .

(3)

Dividing by
i\omega C
and some rearranging then gives us

-\kappa V_{j-1}+(1+2\kappa)V_{j}-\kappa V_{j+1}=\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}V_{j}\ .

(4)

Repeating the analysis for the right-most node labeled
V_{n}
finally results in

0=i\omega\hat{C}(V_{n}-V_{n-1})+\left(i\omega C+\frac{1}{i\omega L}\right)V_{j}-i\omega C_{a}(0-V_{n})

(5)

and, after introducing abbreviations, leads to

-\kappa V_{n-1}+(1+\kappa+\kappa_{a})V_{n}=\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}V_{n}\ .

(6)

,
4
, and
6

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1+\kappa+\kappa_{a}&-\kappa&0&0&0\\

0&-\kappa&1+2\kappa&-\kappa&\\
0&0&-\kappa&1+2\kappa&-\kappa\\
0&0&0&-\kappa&1+\kappa+\kappa_{a}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}V_{1}\\
V_{2}\\
V_{3}\\
V_{4}\\

V_{2}\\
V_{3}\\
V_{4}\\
V_{5}\end{array}\right)

(7)

where we assumed that

. Generalizing to other numbers of cells

We follow
[

]
and require the
\pi
-mode to produce alternating signs of

(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3},\dots)=(1,-1,1,\dots)

1+2\kappa+\kappa_{a}=\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}}{\omega_{\pi}^{2}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad-1-4\kappa=-\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}}{\omega_{\pi}^{2}}\ .

(8)

Solving for
\kappa_{a}
and
\hat{\omega}^{2}/\omega_{\pi}^{2}
leads to

\kappa_{a}=2\kappa\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\omega_{\pi}=\frac{\hat{\omega}}{\sqrt{1+4\kappa}}\ .

(9)

We need to point out that in our model the

\pi
-mode frequency lies below the cell’s resonance frequency
\hat{\omega}
, which
is an artefact of using a parallel-resonator circuit. With a series-resonator
circuit the
\pi
-mode frequency is above
\hat{\omega}
.

Now that we know what
\kappa_{a}
should be, we insert it in Equation

and find the following, now properly matched, eigenvalue equation

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1+3\kappa&-\kappa&0&0&0\\

V_{2}\\
V_{3}\\
V_{4}\\
V_{5}\end{array}\right)=\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}V_{1}\\
V_{2}\\
V_{3}\\
V_{4}\\
V_{5}\end{array}\right)

that we inspect further to determine all eigenfrequencies and eigenmodes.

[
3

It is instructive to explore the sensitivity of the eigenfrequencies as a function

\kappa
in one cell. The right-hand plot in

An error of

The analysis in this section is purely static. In the next sections we will,
however, extend it to account for transient phenomena. In that context we also
introduce specific imperfections to individual cells, which will allow us to
explore their consequences.

Model

Let us now consider the current
I_{j}
that flows through the
RLC
-branch
of cell j in the middle of the cavity. The first and last cells need special

6

0=I_{j}-I_{j+1}+\frac{V_{j}}{R_{j}}+\frac{1}{L_{j}}\int V_{j}dt+C_{j}\frac{dV_{j}}{dt}

and
I_{j+1}

\hat{C}_{j-1}

\hat{C}_{j}

V_{j}

(13)

Inserting in Equation
12

0=-\hat{C}_{j-1}\frac{dV_{j-1}}{dt}-\hat{C}_{j+1}\frac{dV_{j+1}}{dt}+\frac{V_{j}}{R_{j}}+\frac{1}{L_{j}}\int V_{j}dt+\left(C_{j}+\hat{C}_{j-1}+\hat{C}_{j+1}\right)\frac{dV_{j}}{dt}\ .

(14)

Dividing by
C_{j}
and introducing the abbreviations
\kappa^{\prime}_{j}=\hat{C}_{j-1}/C_{j}
and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\frac{dV_{j-1}}{dt}-\kappa_{j}\frac{dV_{j+1}}{dt}+\frac{V_{j}}{R_{j}C_{j}}+\frac{1}{L_{j}C_{j}}\int V_{j}dt+\left(1+\kappa^{\prime}_{j}+\kappa_{j}\right)\frac{dV_{j}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\frac{dV_{j-1}}{dt}-\kappa_{j}\frac{dV_{j+1}}{dt}+\frac{\hat{\omega}_{j}}{Q_{j}}V_{j}+\hat{\omega}_{j}^{2}\int V_{j}dt+\left(1+\kappa^{\prime}_{j}+\kappa_{j}\right)\frac{dV_{j}}{dt}\ ,

\pi
-mode.

For the simulations, we multiply the equation by

transform Equations

4
, and

following matrix-valued equation

D\frac{d\vec{V}}{dt}\Delta t=-(A-iB)\vec{V}+\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta tR_{1}}{Q_{1}}\vec{I}

(31)

where we used the abbreviations

\displaystyle\vec{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{V}_{1},\tilde{V}_{2},\tilde{V}_{3},\tilde{V}_{4},\tilde{V}_{5}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\frac{d\vec{V}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\tilde{V}_{1}}{dt},\frac{d\tilde{V}_{2}}{dt},\frac{d\tilde{V}_{3}}{dt},\frac{d\tilde{V}_{4}}{dt},\frac{d\tilde{V}_{5}}{dt}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\vec{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{I}_{g}/n,0,0,0,0\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}2+7\kappa&-\kappa&0&0&0\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left((1+\beta)\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t}{Q},\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t}{Q},\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t}{Q},\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t}{Q},\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t}{Q}\right)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t\kappa}{\sqrt{1+4\kappa}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&1&0&0&0\\
1&2&1&0&0\\

We further manipulate the system by introducing real and imaginary parts of voltages
and currents

\vec{V}=\vec{V}_{r}+i\vec{V}_{i}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\vec{I}=\vec{I}_{r}+i\vec{I}_{i}\ .

(44)

This transforms Equation
31
into

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}D&0_{5}\\
0_{5}&D\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{d\vec{V}_{r}}{dt}\\
\frac{d\vec{V}_{i}}{dt}\end{array}\right)\Delta t=-\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}A&B\\

\vec{I}_{i}\end{array}\right)\ ,

where

is a
5\times 5
matrix containing only zeros. Here we are now dealing

The steady-state conditions are given by vanishing time derivatives on the left-hand
side and that leads to the steady-state voltages

\tilde{V}_{\infty}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{V}_{r}\\
\vec{V}_{i}\end{array}\right)_{\infty}=\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta tR_{1}}{Q_{1}}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}A&B\\
-B&A\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{I}_{r}\\
\vec{I}_{i}\end{array}\right)\ .

(46)

where we introduce
\tilde{V}=(\vec{V}_{r},\vec{V}_{i})^{\top}
.

We obtain the temporal evolution of the voltages
\tilde{V}
as a function of the
re-scaled time

from Equation

\frac{d\tilde{V}}{ds}=-\tilde{A}\tilde{V}(s)+\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta tR_{1}}{Q_{1}}\tilde{I}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\tilde{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}D^{-1}A&D^{-1}B\\
-D^{-1}B&D^{-1}A\end{array}\right)

(47)

and
\tilde{I}(s)=(D^{-1}I_{r},D^{-1}I_{i})^{\top}
. Note that here
\tilde{V}
and
\tilde{I}

depend on

In our case
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{I}
are constant, so we can formally
integrate Equation
47
with the result

\tilde{V}(s)=\exp\left[-\tilde{A}s\right]\tilde{K}+\tilde{V}_{\infty}\ ,

(48)

where
\tilde{K}
is a vector with the integration constants for the voltages.
Matching the initial conditions
\tilde{V}(s=0)=\tilde{V}_{0}
leads us to

\tilde{K}=\tilde{V}_{0}-\tilde{V}_{\infty}
and that gives us the formal solution

\tilde{V}(s)=\exp\left[-\tilde{A}s\right]\left(\tilde{V}_{0}-\tilde{V}_{\infty}\right)+\tilde{V}_{\infty}\ ,

(49)

where the different
\tilde{V}
are ten-component vectors with the five
real parts as upper entries and the five imaginary parts just below. The
matrix-exponent
\exp\left[-\tilde{A}s\right]
is a
10\times 10
matrix
that can be calculated from the eigenvalue decomposition of
\tilde{A}
.

Let us assume that we write
\tilde{A}
expressed through its eigenvalues
and eigenvectors, assembled in
W

\tilde{A}=W\Lambda W^{-1}\qquad\mathrm{with}\qquad\Lambda=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{10}\right)\ .

(50)

Now let us imagine
\exp\left[-\tilde{A}s\right]
being expressed as a power series
of
\tilde{A}s
. Then it is easy to see that calculating the powers of
\tilde{A}s

always brings
W
and
W^{-1}
next to each other, so they cancel, leaving only
the first and last
W
and
W^{-1}
with powers of
(\Lambda s)
in-between.
And they combine to the form the matrix exponential

\exp\left[-\tilde{A}s\right]=W\left(e^{-\lambda_{1}s},\dots,e^{-\lambda_{10}s}\right)W^{-1}\ ,

(51)

which provides us with a recipe to calculate an explicit representation of
the matrix exponential and to numerically evaluate Equation
49
.
All we need is the eigenvalue decomposition of
\tilde{A}
and that is easily
done with Matlab’s built-in function
eig()
. This way of calculating the
voltages is very efficient; we only need to call
eig()
once and then, for
each time

, first evaluate Equation
51
and then Equation
49
.

duration
\Delta t

\beta=5000

From the discussion immediately following Equation
27
one might expect
that the filling time
\tau
of the cavity is given by
1/\tau=\hat{\omega}_{1}/2Q^{\prime}_{L}
.
In reality, however, it is determined by the real part of the eigenvalue that
corresponds to the
\pi
-mode excitation of the cavity. In order to explore this
further we display the ten eigenvalues in Figure
10
. The upper plot
shows the real parts of the eigenvalues and the bottom plot shows the corresponding
imaginary parts, which is much larger than the corresponding real parts. We also
observe that the eigenvalues come in complex-conjugate pairs; adjacent real parts
have the same magnitude and the corresponding imaginary parts have opposite sign.
Here a large imaginary part indicates that the mode is mostly oscillating. They
are responsible for the oscillatory behavior of the cavity voltages at the start
of the pulse, highlighted in Figure
5
. The real parts of eigenvalues 1
and 2 are particularly small, so they cause oscillations of the corresponding mode
to linger on for a relatively long time. At the same time, their imaginary part is
large, such that they are oscillating substantially. This explains the excitation
of the imaginary part, the Q-phase, in Figure
6
. The reason that the
first eight eigenvalues are excited at all comes from the sudden rise of the pulse
from the generator, which contains many frequencies. And some of them are inside
the bandwidth of the first four modes that are described by the first eight eigenvalues.
On the other hand, the pair of eigenvalues numbered 9 and 10 show the smallest
imaginary part (red) while still having a moderate real part (blue). These two
modes therefore mostly absorb the energy delivered by the generator.

The upper row in Figure
10
shows the odd-numbered eigenvectors. The
real parts are shown in blue and the imaginary parts in red. That they come in groups
of five is a consequence of ordering the voltages when transforming the complex-valued

to its real-valued counterpart Equation

. We
observe that the modal pattern follows the modes already seen in Figure

Only here the real (blue) and imaginary (red) parts mimic each other. The
bottom row shows the complex-conjugate even-numbered eigenvectors. They show the
same real part (blue) as the corresponding plot above, but the sign of the imaginary
part (red) is reversed.

\pi
-mode
with adjacent cells having opposite polarity. Notably, we already found that the

therefore, rather than oscillating, this mode absorbs most of the energy from the

of the

\pi
-mode. Comparing its numerical value with

filling a single cell. After all, we have to provide power to fill five cells
rather than one, and that takes five times longer. Using a complementary point
of view: the five cells behave like five bandpass filters in series and therefore
the overall bandwidth is reduced five-fold.

Increased losses in the first cell need a little extra attention, because both

7
Detuned cells

In this section we assume that the resonance frequency
\hat{\omega}_{j}
of cell
j

deviates from the design value
\hat{\omega}
by
\Delta\omega_{j}=\hat{\omega}_{j}-\hat{\omega}
. Moreover, for the

\pi
-mode we have
\hat{\omega}^{2}/\omega^{2}_{\pi}=1+4\kappa
which gives us

\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}_{j}}{\omega_{\pi}^{2}}=\frac{\hat{\omega}^{2}+2\hat{\omega}\Delta\omega_{j}+\Delta\omega_{j}^{2}}{\omega_{\pi}^{2}}\approx 1+4\kappa+2\frac{\Delta\omega_{j}}{\hat{\omega}}\approx 1+4\kappa+\delta_{j}

(74)

with the abbreviation
\delta_{j}=2\Delta\omega_{j}/\hat{\omega}
to describe the relative
detuning of cell
j
.

Assuming all other parameters to be equal their design values, the equation for
the first cell becomes

\displaystyle\left(2+7\kappa+\delta_{1}\right)\frac{d\tilde{V}_{1}}{dt}-\kappa\frac{d\tilde{V}_{2}}{dt}

(75)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\frac{\hat{\omega}}{Q^{\prime}_{L}}\tilde{V}_{1}+i\frac{\hat{\omega}}{\sqrt{1+4\kappa}}\left[(\kappa+\delta_{1})\tilde{V}_{1}+\kappa\tilde{V}_{2}\right]+\frac{\hat{\omega}R_{1}}{Q_{1}}\frac{\tilde{I}_{g}}{n}\ .

For cell
j
in the middle of the cavity we obtain

\displaystyle-\kappa\frac{d\tilde{V}_{j-1}}{dt}+\left(2+6\kappa+\delta_{j}\right)\frac{d\tilde{V}_{j}}{dt}-\kappa\frac{d\tilde{V}_{j+1}}{dt}

(76)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\frac{\hat{\omega}}{Q}\tilde{V}_{j}+i\frac{\hat{\omega}}{\sqrt{1+4\kappa}}\left[\kappa\tilde{V}_{j-1}+(2\kappa+\delta_{j})\tilde{V}_{j}+\kappa\tilde{V}_{j+1}\right]

and the equation for the last cell is

\displaystyle-\kappa\frac{d\tilde{V}_{n-1}}{dt}+\left(2+7\kappa+\delta_{n}\right)\frac{d\tilde{V}_{j}}{dt}

(77)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\frac{\hat{\omega}}{Q}\tilde{V}_{n}+i\frac{\hat{\omega}}{\sqrt{1+4\kappa}}\left[\kappa\tilde{V}_{n-1}+(\kappa+\delta_{n})\tilde{V}_{n}\right]\ .

In all equations we approximated
\hat{\omega}_{j}/Q_{j}
by
\hat{\omega}/Q
and thus neglected the
very small effect of the detuning on damping. This leaves the matrix
A
, used in
the simulations, unaffected. On the other hand, the change of a cell’s resonance
frequency mainly affects the matrices
D
and
B

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}2+7\kappa+\delta_{1}&-\kappa&0&0&0\\
-\kappa&2+6\kappa+\delta_{2}&-\kappa&0&0\\
0&-\kappa&2+6\kappa+\delta_{3}&-\kappa&0\\
0&0&-\kappa&2+6\kappa+\delta_{4}&-\kappa\\
0&0&0&-\kappa&2+7\kappa+\delta_{5}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\omega}\Delta t}{\sqrt{1+4\kappa}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\kappa+\delta_{1}&\kappa&0&0&0\\
\kappa&2\kappa+\delta_{2}&\kappa&0&0\\
0&\kappa&2\kappa+\delta_{3}&\kappa&0\\
0&0&\kappa&2\kappa+\delta_{4}&\kappa\\
0&0&0&\kappa&\kappa+\delta_{5}\end{array}\right)\

which makes it straightforward to incorporate detuned cells in the simulations.

8
Conclusions

We found that multi-cell cavities behave very similar to single-cell cavities,
as is witnessed by the close agreement of the two curves in Figure
11
.
The reason is that it takes only on the order of microseconds for the
\pi
-mode
to form, which is shown in Figure
5
. The other modes are excited
but they do not absorb power to build up a coherent mode. Instead, they damp
away as shown in the upper four plots in Figure
10
, where the
individual time constants are related to the real part of the eigenvalues shown
in Figure
10
. Basically, apart from short-lived transients of the
other modes, the
\pi
-mode dominates the dynamics of the electro-magnetic
fields in multi-cell cavities and oscillates as an entity, rather than the
weakly-coupled fields in the cells oscillating independently.

That the
\pi
-mode oscillates as an entity has ramifications for the tolerances
to which the cells must be manufactured, because the
\pi
-mode averages over
the contributions of the individual imperfections. We determined the tolerance
with respect to the cell-to-cell coupling
\kappa
and show the result on the
right-hand plot in Figure
12
, which shows a resonance-like curve
whose width determines the sensitivity with respect to
\kappa
. Furthermore,
the right-hand plot in Figure
15
shows the sensitivity
with respect to detuning of individual cells, which is more relaxed than detuning
all cells by the same amount. In Figure
14
we found that a
quench in a single cell can be stabilized if it is “small” in the sense
that the losses are comparable to the losses that escape through the input
coupler, which is given by
\beta
. If the reduction
x=Q/Q_{quench}\gg\beta

of one cell, the quench leads to a collapse of the
\pi
-mode as shown on
the right-hand plots in Figure
14
.

The methods developed for this report can be easily extended to other cavities
and can then be used to estimate manufacturing tolerances for a number of
imperfections, such as evenness of
Q
-values or statistically distributed
couplings
\kappa
or detuning
\delta=2\Delta\omega/\hat{\omega}
.

Of particular interest are the initial high-frequency oscillations shown in
Figure
5
. Measuring them experimentally with a high-speed
data-acquisition system and theoretically determining how they are related
to cavity imperfections using methods developed for measuring
Q_{0}

[
7
]

seems to be a worthwhile enterprise.
