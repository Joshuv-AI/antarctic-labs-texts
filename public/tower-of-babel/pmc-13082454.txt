Experimental Platform for Screening and Validation of BacNav Gene Therapy Candidates
Authors: Tianyu Wu, Hung X. Nguyen, Yiu Yan Siu, Yongwu Li, Nenad Bursac
Published: 2026

Impaired cardiac conduction increases vulnerability to reentrant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. We previously showed that NavSheP D60A, an engineered prokaryotic voltage-gated sodium channel (BacNav), can augment electrical excitability and reduce arrhythmia incidence in cardiomyocyte cultures and infarcted primate hearts.1,2 Unlike large mammalian Nav genes, BacNav genes are small (<1 kb) and readily packageable in adeno-associated viral vectors. While the NavSheP channel is a promising gene therapy candidate, it has slower gating kinetics than cardiac Nav1.5 channels.2 BacNav orthologs with faster kinetics could more effectively improve peak INa and cardiac conduction slowing. More generally, engineered BacNavs with different biophysical properties could be customized for different therapeutic applications.3

To identify novel BacNav orthologs with fast gating kinetics (Figure [A]), we first searched the UniProt bacterial database for sequences homologous to NavSheP,4 with the requisite presence of (1) a C-terminal coiled-coil region, (2) a selectivity filter (eg, TLESW), and (3) conserved arginine residues in the S4 region. The top 50 identified BacNav orthologs were human-codon optimized, synthesized, and subcloned to generate CMV-BacNav-GFP lentiviruses, each of which was used to transduce 293KC cells (a monoclonal human embryonic kidney (HEK293) line stably expressing Kir2.1 and connexin-43).2 Nav1.5-GFP- and NavSheP-GFP-expressing 293KC cells served as controls. Whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings of GFP+ 293KC cells revealed measurable Na+ currents for 29/50 BacNav orthologs (Figure [Bi]). Recorded currents exhibited a wide range of gating kinetics (lowest activation ($τm$) and inactivation (τh) time constants within the recorded membrane voltage (Vm) range, Figure [Bii], top) and Vm dependencies (V1/2, half-activation Vm, Figure [Bii], bottom).

Notably, the V1/2 for the majority of the 29 channels was considerably different from Nav1.5 (−35 mV; Figure [Bii], bottom), with only 7 of the 29 yielding successful action potential (AP) conduction in 293KC monolayers (data not shown). We next selected the 2 BacNav orthologs with the fastest conduction velocity (CV), namely NavRhi from Rhizobiales bacterium and NavXan from Xanthomonadaceae bacterium, to generate monoclonal 293KC lines for comparison with a monoclonal NavSheP+Kir2.1+Cx43 line. In addition, we generated a monoclonal 293KC line expressing a D60A mutant of NavMarP from Marinomonas polaris (to right-shift its hyperpolarized Vm-dependence).4 Optical mapping revealed that both NavXan and NavRhi had faster CVs than NavSheP, but only NavRhi exhibited shorter AP duration and a significantly increased maximum capture rate, consistent with faster kinetics (Figure [Ci]). In addition, the D60A mutation enabled AP conduction in NavMarP 293KC monolayers, demonstrating the ability to tailor BacNav function through site-directed mutagenesis.

Overall, among all 50 candidates, NavRhi exhibited the most favorable electrophysiological properties, with comparable late sodium current to that of Nav1.5 (Figure [Cii]). The significantly higher CV and maximum capture rate of NavRhi than NavSheP prompted us to perform further validations in cardiomyocytes. Previously, we showed that NavSheP expression significantly improved CV in relatively slow-conducting human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived and fibrotic neonatal rat cardiomyocyte monolayers.2 NavRhi similarly improved CV in human induced pluripotent stem cell-cardiomyocyte monolayers (Figure [Di]) without changing AP shape or duration (Figure [Dii]). Considering its significantly faster kinetics, we further tested whether NavRhi could also improve conduction in a setting of higher cardiomyocyte excitability using engineered 3-dimensional neonatal rat cardiac tissues (cardiobundles, Figure [E]). Unlike NavSheP, NavRhi significantly improved cardiobundle CV by 15.4%, to 51.8±1.9 cm/s, without altering AP duration.

Encouraged by these results, we assessed NavRhi effects in Scn5a± mice, (Figure [Fi]), in which global heterozygous knockout of Scn5a leads to an ≈50% reduction in Nav1.5 gene expression (Figure [Fii]). The 13.5±1-month-old mice received retro-orbital injection of myoAAV2A-MHCK7-NavRhi-HA-mScarlet or control myoAAV2A-MHCK7-mScarlet virus5 (1×1013 vg/kg). Wild-type littermates received vehicle solution. Ten weeks post-injection, immunostaining confirmed robust ventricular transgene expression (Figure [Fiii]). Super-resolution imaging demonstrated NavRhi localization in cardiomyocyte t-tubules (Figure [Fiv]), similar to the previously observed NavSheP distribution in mouse and primate hearts.2 Quantitative qPCR revealed an ≈1.6-fold higher NavRhi versus Scn5a gene copy number in transduced Scn5a± ventricles (0.76-fold of the Scn5a+/+ expression, Figure [Fv]). Considering ≈60% transduction efficiency (Figure [Fiii]) and the homotetrameric nature of BacNav channels, the effective NavRhi gene expression per cardiomyocyte was ≈66% of the Scn5a expression in Scn5a± mice. Importantly, optical mapping of the right ventricle revealed a large conduction slowing in Scn5a± mice injected with the control virus, and significant improvement with NavRhi expression to levels approaching wild-type littermates (Figure [Fvi]).

Here, we for the first time exploited rich bacterial diversity to screen, identify, and validate novel BacNav gene therapy candidates. Specifically, we identified a BacNav ortholog, NavRhi, that effectively improved cardiac AP conduction in engineered cardiac tissues in vitro and in a murine Scn5a± model of reduced excitability ex vivo. Customizing the biophysical properties of BacNav channels could enable new precision gain-of-excitability therapies targeting deficiencies in cardiac conduction (eg, Brugada syndrome, heart failure) and other excitable tissue disorders (eg, Dravet syndrome, hypokalemic periodic paralysis).
