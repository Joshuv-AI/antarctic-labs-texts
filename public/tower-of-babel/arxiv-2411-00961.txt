A rigidity theorem for Kolmogorov-type operators
Alessia E. Kogoj, Ermanno Lanconelli

Abstract
. Let D ⊆ R n D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{n}} , n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , be a bounded open set and let x 0 ∈ D x_{0}\in D . Assume that the Newtonian potential of D D is proportional outside D D to the Newtonian potential of a mass concentrated at { x 0 } . \{x_{0}\}. Then D D is a Euclidean ball centered at x 0 x_{0} . This Theorem, proved by Aharonov, Shiffer and Zalcman in 1981, was extended to the caloric setting by Suzuki and Watson in 2001. In this note, we show that Suzuki–Watson Theorem is a particular case of a more general rigidity result related to a class of Kolmogorov-type PDEs.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Rigidity results in harmonic and caloric settings

Let
K
be the fundamental solution with pole at the origin of the Laplacian
\varDelta
in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
,
n\geq 3
.
We denote by
{B(x_{0},r)}
the Euclidean ball of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
centered at
x_{0}
with radius
r>0
. For every
y\notin{B(x_{0},r)}

the function
x\longmapsto K(x-y)
is harmonic in
{B(x_{0},r)}
so that, by Gauss Mean Value Theorem,

(1.1)

\int_{B(x_{0},r)}K(x-y)\ dx=c\,K(x_{0}-y),

where
c
is the volume of
{B(x_{0},r)}
.

In 1981, Aharonov, Shiffer and Zalcman proved that identity (
1.1
) is a rigidity property of the Euclidean ball. More precisely, they proved (see
[
1
]
, see also
[
5
]
) that a bounded open set
D
such that, for a point
x_{0}\in D
and a suitable positive constant
c
,

\ \int_{D}K(x-y)\ dx=c\,K(x_{0}-y)\quad\forall\,y\notin D,

has to be the Euclidean ball
B(x_{0},r)
with Lebesgue measure equal to
c
.

Suzuki and Watson, in 2001, extended the previous theorem to the
heat balls
in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
,
n\geq 1
(see
[
11
]
). To be more precise, we need some notation.

Let us denote by
G
the fundamental solution of the heat operator
\mathcal{H}:=\varDelta-\partial_{t}
in

\mathbb R^{n+1}=\mathbb R^{n}_{x}\times\mathbb R_{t}
. We call
heat ball
with center
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
and
r>0
the following set

\Omega_{r}(z_{0}):=\left\{z\in\mathbb R^{n+1}:G(z_{0}-z)>\frac{1}{r}\right\}.

The caloric functions, i.e., the solutions to the heat equation

\mathcal{H}u=0

can be characterized in terms of the following caloric mean value formula

(1.2)

u(z_{0})=M_{r}(u)(z_{0}):=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}u(\zeta)W(\zeta-z_{0})\ d\zeta,

where
W
is the Pini–Watson kernel

(1.3)

W(\eta)=W(\xi,\tau):=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{\tau}\right)^{2},(\xi,\tau)\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\tau\neq 0.

Indeed, a continuous function
u:O\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},

O
open subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
, is smooth and solves
\mathcal{H}u=0
in
O
if and only if

u(z_{0})=M_{r}(u)(z_{0})

for every
z_{0}\in O
and
r>0
such that
\overline{{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}}\subseteq O
(see
[
12
]
).

As a consequence, for every heat ball
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
and for every
z\notin{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
, one has

(1.4)

\frac{1}{r}\int_{{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}}G(\zeta-z)W(\zeta-z_{0})\ d\zeta=G(z_{0}-z).

Indeed, if
0<\rho<r
and and
z\notin{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
,
z\neq z_{0}
, then

\zeta\longmapsto G(\zeta-z)

is caloric in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{{\Omega_{\rho}(z_{0})}}.
Hence, by the caloric mean value formula (
1.2
),

\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\Omega_{\rho}(z_{0})}G(\zeta-z)W(\zeta-z_{0})\ d\zeta=G(z_{o}-z).

From this identity, as
\rho\nearrow r
, one gets (
1.4
) in the case
z\neq z_{0}.
On the other hand, if
z=z_{0}

identity (
1.4
) is trivial. Suzuki and Watson, extending Aharonov, Shiffer and Zalcman’s Theorem to the caloric setting, proved that (
1.4
) is a rigidity property of the heat balls.

Their result reads as follows: let
z_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
and let
D
be a bounded open set of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
. Assume that for a suitable constant
c>0
,

\int_{D}G(\zeta-z)W(\zeta-z_{0})\ d\zeta=c\,G(z_{o}-z)\quad\forall\,z\notin D.

Then, if

(1.5)

\zeta\longmapsto(\mathbb{1}_{D}-\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{c}(z_{0})})(\zeta)W(z_{0}-\zeta)\in L^{p}\mbox{\ for some\ }p>\frac{n}{2}+1,

then

D=\Omega_{c}(z_{0}).

Here, and in what follows,
\mathbb{1}_{E}
denotes the characteristic function:

\mathbb{1}_{E}(x)=1
if
x\in E
,
\mathbb{1}_{E}(x)=0
otherwise.

We want to stress that condition (
1.5
) replaces the condition
x_{0}\in D
of the harmonic case; its meaning is that
D
and
\Omega_{c}(z_{0})
are indistinguishable in the vicinity of
z_{0}
.
The present authors, together with G. Tralli, in
[
7
]
partially extended this last rigidity theorem to a class of second order hypoelliptic operators containing in particular the prototype of the so called Kolmogorov operators.

In this note, we provide a full extension of Suzuki–Watson’s rigidity Theorem to such a class of partial differential operators. Our technique is inspired by the one used in the paper
[
5
]
where
harmonic characterizations of the Euclidean balls are proved.

1.2.
Our operators

We will deal with Partial Differential Operators of the type:

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:=\mathrm{div}\left(A\nabla\right)+\left\langle Bx,\nabla\right\rangle-\partial_{t},

where
(x,t)\in\mathbb R^{n}\times\mathbb R
,
\nabla
and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
denote the gradient and the inner product in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\dots,n}
and
B=(b_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\dots,n}
are
n\times n
real constant matrices taking the following block form:

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

B=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
B_{1}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&B_{2}&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&B_{r}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
A_{0}
is a
p_{0}\times p_{0}
(
1\leq p_{0}\leq n
) symmetric and positive definite
constant matrix and
B_{j}
is a
p_{j}\times p_{j-1}
(
j=1,2,...,r
) block with rank equal to
p_{j}
. Moreover
p_{0}\geq p_{1}\geq...\geq p_{r}\geq 1
and
p_{0}+p_{1}+...+p_{r}=n
.

We explicitly remark that the operator (
1.6
) becomes the heat operator if
A=\mathbb{I}_{n}
- the identity matrix - and

B=0
. In this case, with the previous notations,
p_{0}=n
and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
disappear.

It is quite well known that, under these block form assumptions on
A
and
B
, the operator
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.6
) is hypoelliptic, i.e., every distributional solution
u
to
\mathcal{L}u=f
is actually smooth whenever
f
is smooth
(see
[
10
]
, see also
[
2
, Chapter 4, Section 4.3.4]
).
It is also well known that
\mathcal{L}
is left translation invariant and homogeneous of degree two on the homogeneous Lie group

\mathbb{K}:=({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\circ,\delta_{\lambda})

whose composition law is the following one

\left(x,t\right)\circ\left(\xi,\tau\right)=\left(\xi+E(\tau)x,t+\tau\right),

with
E(\tau)=\exp\left(-\tau B\right);
moreover the dilation
\delta_{\lambda},\lambda>0
, is the linear map from

{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
to
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
whose Jacobian matrix is given by

D(\lambda):=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda\mathbb{I}_{p_{0}},\lambda^{3}\mathbb{I}_{p_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{2r+1}\mathbb{I}_{p_{r}},\lambda^{2}),

being
\mathbb{I}_{p_{j}}
the
p_{j}\times p_{j}
identity matrix. We remark that

\det D(\lambda)=\lambda^{Q},

with
Q:=p_{0}+p_{1}+\ldots+(2k+1)p_{r}+2.

This natural number is the
homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{K}
. Since
\mathbb{K}
is a homogeneous Lie group in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
, the Lebesgue measure in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
is left and right translation invariant on
\mathbb{K}
.

An explicit fundamental solution for (
1.6
) is given by

(1.7)

\Gamma\left(z,\zeta\right):=\gamma\left(\zeta^{-1}\circ z\right)\text{
for }z,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

where
\zeta^{-1}=\left(\xi,\tau\right)^{-1}=\left(-E\left(-\tau\right)\xi,-\tau\right)
denotes the opposite of
\zeta
with respect to the composition law in
K
and

(1.8)

\gamma(z)=\gamma\left(x,t\right):=\left\{\begin{tabular}[c]{ll}$0$&$\text{for }t\leq 0$\\
$\frac{\left(4\pi\right)^{-N/2}}{\sqrt{\det C\left(t\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\left\langle C^{-1}\left(t\right)x,x\right\rangle\right)$&$\text{for }t>0$\end{tabular}\ \right..

Here
C\left(t\right)
stands for the matrix

C(t):=\int_{0}^{t}E(s)AE^{T}(s)\,ds.

This matrix is strictly positive definite for every
t>0
and strictly negative definite for every
t<0
(see
[
10
]
). In the case of the heat operator,
C(t)
is simply given by
t\mathbb{I}_{n}.

The function
\gamma
, the fundamental solution of
\mathcal{L}
with pole at the origin - the neutral element of
\mathbb{K}
- is
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous of degree
2-Q
, i.e.,

\gamma(\delta_{\lambda}(z))=\lambda^{2-Q}\gamma(z)\quad\forall\,z\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\ \forall\,\lambda>0.

1.3.
Mean Value formula

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the operator (
1.6
). For every
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
and
r>0
we call
\mathcal{L}-
ball with center
z_{0}
and radius
r>0
the following open set

(1.9)

\Omega_{r}(z_{0})=\left\{z\in\mathbb R^{n+1}:\Gamma(z_{0},z)>\frac{1}{r}\right\}.

From (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), one easily verifies that
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
is a non-empty bounded open set; moreover,

\bigcap_{r>0}{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}=\{z_{0}\}.

A continuous function
u:O\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
O\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
open, actually is smooth in
O
and solves

\mathcal{L}u=0\mbox{ in }O

if and only if

(1.10)

u(z_{0})=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}u(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta,

for every
\mathcal{L}
-ball
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
such that
\overline{{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}}\subseteq O
(see
[
6
]
, see also
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
). In (
1.10
) the kernel
W
is defined as follows

(1.11)

W(z)=W(x,t):=\frac{\langle AC^{-1}(t)x,C^{-1}(t)x\rangle}{4}.

W
is a well-defined and strictly positive almost everywhere in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
smooth function. Indeed, since
A\geq 0
,
W\geq 0
in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus({\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times\{0\}).
Moreover, as

C^{-1}(t)>0\quad\forall\,t>0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad C^{-1}(t)<0\quad\forall\,t<0,

one has
W(x,t)=0
if and only if
x\in F_{t}:=C(t)(\ker(A)).

Being
\mathrm{rank}(A)=p_{0}>0,

C(t)(\ker(A))
has dimension
n-p_{0}
, hence strictly less then
n
. It follows that
F_{t}

has
n
-measure equal to zero for every
t\neq 0
and this implies that

F:=\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\ |\ W(x,t)=0\}

has
n+1
-measure equal to zero.

We remark that when
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}
, the kernel (
1.11
) becomes the Pini–Watson kernel in (
1.3
).

From the Mean Value formula (
1.10
), just proceeding as in the caloric case, one gets

(1.12)

\int_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta=r\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\notin{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}.

1.4.
Main Theorem

The aim of this paper is to prove that identity (
1.12
) is a rigidity property of the
\mathcal{L}
-balls; equivalently, we want to extend the Suzuki–Watson Theorem to the
\mathcal{L}
-setting. Here is our main theorem in which
\Gamma
denotes the fundamental solution of the operator
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.6
),
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
is the
\mathcal{L}
-ball in (
1.9
) and
Q
is the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{K}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
and let
D
be a bounded open subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
such that, for a suitable
r>0
,

(1.13)

\int_{D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta=r\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus D.

If, moreover,

(1.14)

(\mathbb{1}_{D}-\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})})W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\cdot)\in L^{p}\mbox{ for some }p>\dfrac{Q}{2},

then
D={\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}.

We explicitly remark that identity (
1.13
) implies the inclusion

D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times]-\infty,t_{0}[,

being
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
the time-component of
z_{0}
, i.e.,
z_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})
for a suitable
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.
Indeed, the right hand side of (
1.13
) is equal to zero for every
z=(x,t)
with
t\geq t_{0}
while
\Gamma(\zeta,z)>0
if
\zeta=(\xi,\tau)\in D
and
\tau>t
.

Due to the invariance of the Lebesgue measure with respect to the right (and left) translation on
\mathbb{K}
, identity (
1.13
) is equivalent to the following one

(1.15)

\int_{z_{0}^{-1}\circ D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(\zeta)\ d\zeta=r\Gamma(0,z)\quad\forall\,z\notin z_{0}^{-1}\circ D.

Then, it is enough to prove Theorem
1.1
in the case
z_{0}=0
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
and let
D
be a bounded open subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
such that, for a suitable
r>0
,

(1.16)

u(z_{0})=\frac{1}{r}\int_{D}u(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta

for every non negative function
u

\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in an open set containing
D\cup\{z_{0}\}.

Then

D={\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}

if condition (
1.14
) is satisfied.

Remark 1.3
.

If we replace condition (
1.14
) with the following stronger ones:

(i)

there exists a neighborhood
V
of
z_{0}
s. t.
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}\cap V=D\cap V
;

(ii)

\overline{D}\setminus\{z_{0}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times]-\infty,t_{0}[,
being
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
the time-component of
z_{0}
, i.e.,
z_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})
for a suitable
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}};

then Theorem
1.1
becomes a particular case of Theorem 1.4 in
[
7
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we recall some notion and several results from Potential Analysis for the operator
\mathcal{L}
and its adjoint
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
. These will be the main ingredients and tools of our proof of Theorem
1.1
. Section 3
will be devoted entirely to the proof of Theorem
1.1
and Section 4 to the proof of its Corollary
1.2
.

2.
Basic notions and results from Potential Analysis for
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}

2.1.
Harmonic and subharmonic functions for
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
be open. A function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-harmonic
-in short notation
u\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(\Omega)
- if
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}u=0
in
\Omega
. We explicitly observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\ast}:=\mathrm{div}\left(A\nabla\right)-\left\langle Bx,\nabla\right\rangle+\partial_{t},

and therefore, as
\mathcal{L}
, it is hypoelliptic.

Analogous meanings as above for

\mathcal{L}
-harmonic functions and for
\mathcal{L}(\Omega).

A bounded open set
V\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
is called
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-regular
if for every function
\varphi\in C(\partial V,\mathbb{R})
there exists a unique
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-harmonic function in
V
, denoted by
H_{\varphi}^{V}
, such that

\lim_{z\rightarrow\zeta}H_{\varphi}^{V}(z)=\varphi(\zeta)\quad\forall\,\zeta\in\partial V.

Analogous meaning for
\mathcal{L}
-regular set.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
be open and let
u:\Omega\longrightarrow[-\infty,+\infty[
be an upper semicontinuous function.
We say that
u
is
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-subharmonic in
\Omega
- in short notation
u\in\underline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}(\Omega)
- if it satisfies
the following conditions:

(i)

u>-\infty
in a dense subset of
\Omega
;

(ii)

for every
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-regular open set
V
with
\overline{V}\subseteq\Omega
and for every
\varphi\in C(\partial V,\mathbb{R})
such that
u|_{\partial V}\leq\varphi
one has
u\leq H_{\varphi}^{V}
in
V
.

We shall denote by
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}(\Omega)
the family of the
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-superharmonic functions, i.e., the family of the functions
v
such that
-v\in\underline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}(\Omega).

2.2.
Maximum principle for
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-subharmonic functions

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
be a bounded open set and let
u\in\underline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}(\Omega)
be such that

\limsup_{z\rightarrow\zeta}u(z)\leq 0\quad\forall\,\zeta\in\partial\Omega.

Then

u\leq 0\mbox{ in }\Omega

(see e.g.
[
3
, Proposition 2.3]
).

2.3.
Propagation of maxima along drift-trajectories

We call
drift-trajectory
of
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
any path of the type

s\longmapsto\gamma(s):=\alpha+se_{j}\quad\mbox{ or }\quad s\longmapsto\gamma(s):=\alpha-se_{j},

where
0\leq s\leq S,\quad\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\quad e_{j}=(0,\ldots,0,\underset{j}{1},0,\ldots,0),\quad 0\leq j\leq p_{0}.

Then, if
u\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}(\Omega),\Omega
open subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
, and
z_{0}\in\Omega
is such that

u(z_{0})=\max_{\Omega}u,

then
u(\gamma(s))=u(z_{0})
for every drift-trajectory
\gamma:[0,S]\longrightarrow\Omega
such that
\gamma(0)=z_{0}

(see e.g.
[
4
]
,
[
8
]
).

2.4.
\Gamma
-potentials

Let
\Gamma
be the fundamental solution of
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
1.7
).
and
\mu
be a non-negative Radon measure with compact support. We let
\Gamma_{\mu}:{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\longrightarrow[0,\infty],

\Gamma_{\mu}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\Gamma(z,\zeta)\ d\mu(\zeta).

Then, see e.g.
[
3

,

\Gamma_{\mu}\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}})

and

\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\Gamma_{\mu}=-\mu\quad\mbox{in the weak sense of the distributions.}

In particular
\Gamma_{\mu}
is
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-harmonic in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\mu
. We want to explicitly remark that
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\mu
is the open set union of the family of the open sets
O
such that
\mu(O)=0
.

2.5.
An inequality for the
\Gamma
-potentials of the
\mathcal{L}
-balls

For fixed
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
and
r>0
, denote by
\mu
the Radon measure in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
such that

(2.1)

d\mu(\zeta)=\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta,

where
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
is the
\mathcal{L}
-ball centered at
z_{0}
with radius
r
and
W
is the kernel (
1.11
). We have already noticed - see (
1.12
) - that

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)=\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\notin{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}.

From Corollary 3.2 in
[
9
]
, we also get the following inequality

(2.2)

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)<\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\in{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}.

This inequality will play a crucial rôle in the proof of the Theorem
1.1
. Here, we stress that, together with (
1.11
), it implies

(2.3)

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)\leq\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}.

2.6.
A convolution continuity result

Since
\gamma
is
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogenous of degree
2-Q
we have

\gamma\in L^{q}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}})\mbox{\quad if }q(Q-2)<Q

or, equivalently, if

0<\frac{1}{p}:=1-\frac{1}{q}<\frac{2}{Q}.

Then, if
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}})
for some
p>\dfrac{Q}{2}
and, moreover, the support of
f
is compact,
the function

z\longmapsto\Gamma(z):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}}\Gamma(\zeta,z)f(\zeta)\ d\zeta

is a well-defined real continuous function in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. The proof of this statement is completely standard if one remarks that, being the Lebesgue measure translation invariant in
\mathbb{K}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\Gamma(\zeta,z)f(\zeta)\ d\zeta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\gamma(z^{-1}\circ\zeta)f(\zeta)\ d\zeta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\gamma(\eta)f(z\circ\eta)\ d\eta.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

As we already noticed, it is not restrictive to assume
z_{0}=0
. Let
\mu
be the compactly supported Radon measure defined in (
2.1
), and let
\nu
be the measure such that

(3.1)

d\nu(\zeta)=\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{1}_{D}(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta.

Since
D
is bounded,
\nu
is also a compactly supported Radon measure. For our aims, it is crucial to remark that

\mathrm{supp}\nu=\overline{D}
, hence

(3.2)

\partial D\subseteq\mathrm{supp}\nu.

It is also crucial for us to remark that

(3.3)

\mu|_{{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}\cap D}=\nu|_{{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}\cap D}.

Let
\Gamma_{\mu}
and
\Gamma_{\nu}
be the
\Gamma
-potentials of
\mu
and
\nu
respectively, i.e.,

(3.4)

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\Gamma(\zeta,z)\ d\mu(\zeta)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta,

and

(3.5)

\Gamma_{\nu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\Gamma(\zeta,z)\ d\nu(\zeta)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta.

From (
1.12
) and assumption (
1.13
), we have

(3.6)

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)=\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\notin{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}

and

(3.7)

\Gamma_{\nu}(z)=\Gamma(z_{0},z)\quad\forall\,z\notin D.

Therefore,

(3.8)

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)=\Gamma_{\nu}(z)\quad\forall\,z\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus({\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}\cup D).

Let us remark that

(\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu})(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\Gamma(z,\zeta)f(\zeta)\ d\zeta,

where

f(\zeta):=\frac{1}{r}(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}-\mathbb{1}_{D})(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta).

From assumption (
1.14
), the function
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}})
for a suitable
p>\frac{Q}{2}
. Furthermore, as
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
and
D
are bounded,
f
has compact support. Then,

(3.9)

\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu}\in C({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\mathbb{R})

(see Subsection
2.6
). Then, since
\Gamma\mu
is finite at any point (see (
2.3
)), we have

\Gamma_{\nu}(z)<\infty\quad\forall\,z\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}.

With all these results at hand, we can prove Theorem
1.1
with a procedure inspired by the one used in
[
5
]
. We split our procedure into several steps in which we simply denote by
\Omega
the
\mathcal{L}
-ball
{\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}.

Step I.
The aim of this step is to prove the inequality

(3.10)

\Gamma_{\mu}\leq\Gamma_{\nu}\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\Omega.

To this end, keeping in mind (
3.8
) and remarking that

{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\Omega\subseteq({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus(\Omega\cup D))\cup D,

it is enough to prove that

(3.11)

\Gamma_{\mu}(z)\leq\Gamma_{\nu}(z)\quad\forall\,z\in D.

Define

(3.12)

\mu_{1}:=\mu|_{\Omega\setminus D}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\nu_{1}:=\nu|_{D\setminus\Omega}.

Then
\mu_{1}
and
\nu_{1}
are compactly supported Radon measures whose
\Gamma
-potentials are finite at any point of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}
.
If
z\in D
we have

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\cap D}\Gamma(z,\zeta)\ d\mu(\zeta)+\int_{\Omega\setminus D}\Gamma(z,\zeta)\ d\mu(\zeta)

\displaystyle-\left(\int_{D\cap\Omega}\Gamma(z,\zeta)\ d\nu(\zeta)+\int_{D\setminus\Omega}\Gamma(z,\zeta)\ d\nu(\zeta)\right),

so that, keeping in mind that

\mu|_{\Omega\cap D}=\nu|_{\Omega\cap D}

(see (
3.3
)), we get

(3.13)

(\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu})(z)=(\Gamma_{\mu_{1}}-\Gamma_{\nu_{1}})(z)\quad\forall\,z\in D.

We let

(3.14)

\hat{u}:=\Gamma_{\mu_{1}}-\Gamma_{\nu_{1}}.

Since
D
is open, one has the following inclusions:

D\cap\mathrm{supp}\ \mu_{1}\subseteq D\cap(\overline{\Omega\setminus D})\subseteq D\cap({\mathbb{R}^{n}}\setminus D)=\emptyset.

As a consequence,

\Gamma_{\mu_{1}}\mbox{ is $\mathcal{L}^{\ast}$-harmonic in $D$}

(see Subsection
2.4
). It follows that

\hat{u}\in\underline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}(D).

We claim that

(3.15)

\limsup_{D\ni z\rightarrow\zeta}\hat{u}(z)\leq 0\quad\forall\,\zeta\in\partial D.

Taking this claim for granted, for a moment, from the Maximum Principle for
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-subharmonic functions (see Subsection
2.2
), we get

\hat{u}\leq 0\mbox{ in }D.

Then, by (
3.13
) and (
3.14
),

\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu}\leq 0\mbox{ in }D.

This proves (
3.11
), hence (
3.10
).

We are left with the proof of claim (
3.15
). Keeping again in mind (
3.14
) and (
3.13
), and using the continuity of
the function
\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu}
- see (
3.9
) - for every
\zeta\in\partial D
we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\limsup_{D\ni z\rightarrow\zeta}\hat{u}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{D\ni z\rightarrow\zeta}(\Gamma_{\mu_{1}}(z)-\Gamma_{\nu_{1}}(z))=\limsup_{D\ni z\rightarrow\zeta}(\Gamma_{\mu}(z)-\Gamma_{\nu}(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow\zeta}(\Gamma_{\mu}(z)-\Gamma_{\nu}(z))=\Gamma_{\mu}(\zeta)-\Gamma_{\nu}(\zeta).

On the other hand, by (
2.3
),
\Gamma_{\mu}(\zeta)\leq\Gamma(z_{0},\zeta)
, while, since
\zeta\in\partial D
so that
\zeta\notin D
, from (
3.7
) it follows
\Gamma_{\nu}(\zeta)=\Gamma(z_{0},\zeta).
Using this information in (
3.16
) we finally get

\displaystyle\limsup_{D\ni z\rightarrow\zeta}\hat{u}(z)\leq 0.

This proves the claim (
3.15
) and completes the proof of (
3.10
).

Step II.

The aim of this step is the proof of the inclusion

(3.17)

D\subseteq\Omega.

Since
\mathrm{supp}\ \mu=\overline{\Omega}
, the potential function
\Gamma_{\mu}
is
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-harmonic in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.
Therefore

(3.18)

v:=\Gamma_{\mu}-\Gamma_{\nu}\in\underline{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}).

We know, by
Step I
, that

(3.19)

v\leq 0\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

On the other hand, by (
3.8
),

(3.20)

v\equiv 0\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus(\Omega\cup D).

Now, let
z=(x,t)
be an arbitrary point of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. From the very definition of
\Omega
(
={\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}
and
z_{0}=0
) the bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\Omega_{t}:=\left\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ |\ (\xi,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\right\}

is empty or convex. As a consequence, for every fixed
j\in\{1,\ldots,p_{0}\},

z+se_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\quad\forall\,s\geq 0

or

z-se_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\quad\forall\,s\geq 0.

To fix ideas, let us suppose that the first case occurs. Then, since
\Omega\cup D
is bounded, there exists
S>0
such that

z^{\ast}:=z+Se_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus(\Omega\cup D)

and

z+se_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\quad\forall\,s\in[0,S].

Then, by (
3.20
) and (
3.19
),

v(z^{\ast})=0=\max_{{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}}v.

The propagation of maxima for
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-subharmonic functions (see Subsection
2.3
) implies that

v(z+se_{j})=v(z^{\ast})=0\quad\forall\,s\in[0,S].

In particular, for
s=0
, we get
v(z)=0.
Since
z
is an arbitrary point of
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega},
we have so proved that

v\equiv 0\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

This means that

\Gamma_{\mu}=\Gamma_{\nu}\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

On the other hand, as we have already observed,
\Gamma_{\mu}
is
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
-harmonic in
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

As a consequence,

\nu=-\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{\nu})=-\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{\mu})=0\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

i.e.,

\nu({\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega})=0,

or, equivalently,

\mathrm{supp}\ \nu\subseteq\overline{\Omega}.

Then
D\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
since
\mathrm{supp}\ \nu=\overline{D}.

As a consequence,
D\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\overline{\Omega}).
On the other hand, keeping in mind that
\gamma
is
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous of degree
2-Q
, it is easy to show that

\mathrm{int}(\overline{\Omega})=\Omega.

Hence, from the last inclusion, we get

D\subseteq\Omega.

Step III.
In this final step we prove that

(3.21)

D=\Omega.

We argue by contradiction and assume
D\neq\Omega.
In this case, since
D\subseteq\Omega

by
Step II
,
there exists
z\in\Omega
such that
z\notin D
. Then, by inequality (
2.2
)

(3.22)

\Gamma(z_{0},z)>\Gamma_{\mu}(z).

On the other hand, since
D\subseteq\Omega
, from (
3.3
) we get

(3.23)

\mu|_{D}=\nu.

Moreover, since
z\notin D
,

(3.24)

\Gamma_{\nu}(z)=\Gamma(z_{o},z).

Putting (
3.22
), (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) together we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(z_{0},z)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(\zeta,z)\ d\mu(\zeta)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)\ d\mu(\zeta)=\int_{D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)\ d\nu(\zeta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\nu}(z)=\Gamma(z_{o},z),

that is
\Gamma(z_{0},z)>\Gamma(z_{0},z).
This contradiction proves (
3.21
) and completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

4.
Proof of Corollary
1.2

We first remark that Corollary’s assumptions imply

(4.1)

D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times]-\infty,t_{0}[,

if
t_{0}
is the time component of
z_{0}
(i.e.
z_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})
for a suitable
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
). Indeed, assume by contradiction that

D\cap({\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times[t_{0},\infty[)\neq\emptyset.

Then, since
D
is bounded, there exists
z^{\ast}=(x^{\ast},t^{\ast})\notin D
such that
,t^{\ast}>t_{0}
and

D^{\ast}:=D\cap({\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times]t^{\ast},\infty[)\neq\emptyset.

Let us now consider the function

u^{\ast}(\zeta):=\Gamma(\zeta,z^{\ast}),\ \zeta\in{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}.

Since
z^{\ast}\notin D,u^{\ast}
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic and nonnegative in
D
. Moreover

u^{\ast}(z_{0})=\Gamma(z_{0},z^{\ast})=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad u^{\ast}>0\mbox{ in }D^{\ast}.

Then, by assumption (
1.16
)

u^{\ast}(z_{0})=\frac{1}{r}\int_{D}u^{\ast}(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta.

This is a contradiction since
u^{\ast}(z_{0})=0
and

\int_{D}u^{\ast}(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta\geq\int_{D^{\ast}}u^{\ast}(\zeta)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta>0.

This contradiction proves the inclusion (
4.1
).

Let us now observe that, for every
z\notin D,z\neq z_{0},
the function

\zeta\longmapsto\Gamma(\zeta,z)

is non-negative and
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in an open set, precisely
{\mathbb{R}^{{n+\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}}\setminus\{z\}
, containing
D\cup\{z_{0}\}.
Then by assumption (
1.16
)

(4.2)

\int_{D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta=r\Gamma(z_{0},z)

for every
z\notin D,\ z\neq z_{0}.
On the other hand, identity (
4.2
) is trivial if
z=z_{0}
since
\Gamma(z_{0},z_{0})=0
and, being
D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times]-\infty,t_{0}[,

\Gamma(\zeta,z_{0})=0
for every
\zeta\in D
so that

\int_{D}\Gamma(\zeta,z)W(z_{0}^{-1}\circ\zeta)\ d\zeta=0.

We have so proved that
D
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
1.1
, keeping in mind that we are assuming condition (
1.14
). Then, by Theorem
1.1
,

D={\Omega_{r}(z_{0})}.
