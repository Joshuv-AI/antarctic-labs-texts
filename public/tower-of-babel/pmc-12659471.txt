Impact of somatic comorbidities on healthcare costs in patients with severe mental disorders: a cross-sectional study in Beijing
Authors: Qingzhi Huang, Yu Xin, Junli Zhu, Yao Dong, Yun Chen, Chen Chen, Wei Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Patients with severe mental disorders (SMD) face a heavy financial burden, and somatic comorbidity is one of the factors that cannot be ignored. The purpose of this study is to understand the current status of somatic comorbidity in patients and to explore the relationship between medical expenses and different somatic disease comorbidities.MethodThe group obtained medical data of 4940 patients in 2019 through a follow-up survey. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the distribution of patient co-morbidities, and CLAD was used to analyze the impact of various factors on patient expenses.ResultsThe most common comorbidity was circulatory disorders (36.6%), followed by respiratory disorders (32.3%), digestive disorders (30.6%) and endocrine, nutritional or metabolic disorders (29.8%). As the types of somatic comorbidity increase, the expenses will also increase. In terms of comorbidity types, a total of 9 ICD-11 chapter group diseases will lead to increased expenses, while 5 chapter group diseases have no statistically significant impact on expenses.ConclusionsThis study found that comorbidities are widespread in patients with SMD, and most of them lead to a significant increase in patients’ medical expenses. Therefore, the treatment of patients with severe mental disorders should take into account the needs of the prevention, management and treatment of the comorbidity of the population, reduce the occurrence and development of patients’ somatic comorbidity, and thus reduce the burden of patients’ medical expenses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07335-9.

Introduction

Severe Mental Disorders (SMD) are defined as mental disorders whose severe symptoms significantly impair patients’ social adaptation and other functions, rendering them incapable of comprehensively understanding their own health status or objective reality, or managing their own affairs [1]. In China, SMD primarily encompasses schizophrenia (SCZ), bipolar disorder (BD), delusional disorder (DD), schizoaffective disorder (SAD), mental disorders caused by epilepsy (MD-E), and intellectual disability with mental disorders (ID-MD).

People with SMD are at increased risk of falling into or remaining in poverty due to decreased productivity, increased health care expenses, stigma, and loss of employment-related income [2, 3]. They not only suffer from the heavy economic burden of mental illness, but they also face the additional expenses of somatic illness. There is growing evidence that SMD is associated with frequent somatic illness in this population [4]. Baughman (2015) found that 91.13% of patients with SMD concurrently suffered from chronic physical conditions such as chronic pain, respiratory problems, and arthritis, while the proportion among non-SMD patients was 74.88% [5]. Furthermore, Dickey (2002) also revealed that individuals with SMD exhibit higher prevalence rates of comorbidities across eight surveyed somatic illness compared to those without [6]. Despite rapid economic development in China, poverty among individuals with SMD has not been effectively alleviated; instead, the relative poverty of this population has intensified [7]. The expense of medical care for somatic diseases aggravates the poverty of this group [8], and the economic distress makes their diseases inadequately treated, which forces patients to be involved in a vicious circle [2]. Therefore, effective prevention or reduction of comorbidity impact is increasingly important for countries like China where health and social care expenditures are under tremendous pressure [9, 10]. Studies have shown that some of the expenses of comorbidities may be avoidable or mitigated by certain policy measures or timely interventions [11, 12].

In this context, examining the impact of various types of comorbidities on patients’ medical expenses and identifying and categorizing patients with high medical costs can offer valuable insights for health care security policies reform and the prevention, treatment, and management of diseases within this population in China and similar developing countries.

In the past, numerous scholars have investigated the correlation between comorbidity and expenses among patients with SMD. Several studies have utilized dichotomous measures [13–17] or simply counted the number of comorbidities [8, 18, 19] to analyze the association between comorbidity and healthcare expenses. These methods overlook the varied impacts of different disease types, despite the significance of understanding the additional burden of distinct comorbidities on patients. Moreover, some studies have only focused on individual diseases or specific groups [20, 21], lacking comprehensive research across a broad spectrum of diseases. Additionally, many previous studies have predominantly examined inpatient data [22–24]. However, treating SMD typically involves long-term use of antipsychotic medications, and managing non-psychiatric chronic illnesses also entails prolonged medication regimens. Therefore, outpatient medication expenses constitute a crucial component, and exclusive focus on inpatient expenses fails to fully capture the actual healthcare costs incurred.

Given China’s distinctive health system, cultural context, and lifestyle patterns, existing international findings may not be directly generalizable, leaving a critical research gap in understanding the interplay between mental and physical health in the Chinese setting. To date, limited evidence has systematically examined how different types of physical comorbidities influence healthcare utilization and costs among patients with severe mental disorders (SMD) in China. Addressing this gap is essential for developing targeted and effective strategies to alleviate patients’ economic burden and optimize integrated care. Therefore, this study leverages comprehensive medical data from community-based patients with SMD in Beijing to (1) map the current landscape of physical comorbidities among this population, and (2) explore how different comorbidity patterns are associated with variations in medical expenditures. Through this, the study aims to provide an evidence base for improving resource allocation and informing health policy tailored to China’s unique healthcare context.

Methods

Data sources

The data in this study comprised demographic characteristics information and patient medical records detailing medical utilization and expenses. This research was conducted by following the visits of the patients with community psychiatrists, and data were obtained through questionnaires and the patients’ medical insurance records. Demographic characteristics information was gathered through questionnaire surveys and the questionnaire can be found in supplementary file 1. Community psychiatrists manage patients in their jurisdiction year-round and possess a thorough understanding of the patients’ basic situations. This can effectively control the quality of the questionnaire. Demographic characteristics information included gender, age, marital status, financial situation, occupational status, and diagnosed SMD registered in the community in 2019. Data on medical utilization and expenses were extracted from patients’ medical insurance records in 2019. Medical insurance records can document a patient’s complete medical history of visits to public hospitals and designated private medical institutions under the social medical insurance plan. Data on patient medical records of medical utilization and expenses in 2019, including date of visit, results of the primary and secondary diagnosis, type of medical service (outpatient or inpatient service), expense of medical services, and expenses composition (out-of-pocket expense, medical insurance payment expense, and drug expense). All survey contents were disclosed to and consented by the patients and their families beforehand.

Definition of some concepts

Demographic characteristics information refers to the statistical characteristics of patients with SMD. It encompassed gender, age, marital status, financial situation, occupational status, medical insurance status and diagnosed SMD.

This study focuses on patients’ total medical expenses. Total medical expenses refer to the total direct medical costs, including out-of-pocket expenses, fiscal burden expenses, and medical insurance payment expenses. The study categorizes these expenses into mental health-related expenses and somatic health-related expenses based on the primary diagnosis.

Sample

The sample was obtained through stratified whole-group sampling, utilizing the administrative division of Beijing as the stratification basis. Beijing’s 16 districts were categorized into two tiers based on economic or geographical factors. Mentougou District and Shijingshan District, characterized by similar internal traits and a small anticipated sample size, were not stratified. Within each tier, one community health service center was chosen through whole-group sampling, and all eligible patients from these centers were included in the study. A final sample of 30 health service centers was selected and 4940 cases were obtained.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria: first, diagnosed by a psychiatrist with a SMD as described above in the community record management according to ICD-11 criteria; second, resume health records in selected community health centers in Beijing before 2019.

Exclusion criteria: first, SMD health file has been withdrawn before 2019; second, has died or moved out of the extracted community health center before 2019; third, there are special circumstances such as missing file information, abnormal status or lost visits.

Comorbidity measurement

Two primary methods exist for assessing comorbidity. The first method involves assessing comorbidity based on the presence or absence, or by tallying the number, of comorbidities. This method is primarily employed to examine the correlation between the presence or quantity of comorbidities and healthcare expenses. The alternative method involves assessing comorbidity by selecting specific diseases or grouping them together, primarily to investigate the various types of comorbidities in relation to expenses. To comprehensively assess the influence of various disease types on expenses, this study classifies the primary diagnosed disease of patients in each medical record according to the chapters specified in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11).

Statistical analysis

The descriptive analysis of qualitative data was expressed as frequencies, rates, or composition ratios; the chi-square test was used for group comparisons of quantitative data, and the Z test and Kruskal-Wallis H test were used for group comparisons of quantitative variables that did not obey a normal distribution.

The expense data in this study are characterized as a non-negative, right-skewed, semi-continuous variable, with a substantial proportion of observations concentrated at zero. Traditional linear regression models, such as Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), assume normally distributed and homoscedastic residuals, which are often violated under such data conditions. Similarly, single-part generalized linear models (e.g., exponential or log-linear models) may not adequately account for the mass point at zero and the skewness of the positive values. To address these limitations, we employed the Censored Least Absolute Deviations (CLAD) model. The CLAD estimator, proposed by Powell (1984) [25], is designed to handle data with censoring at known points—such as zero—and estimates conditional medians rather than means, making it inherently robust to non-normality, skewed distributions, and outliers. Moreover, the CLAD model does not require strong distributional assumptions and remains consistent under heteroskedasticity. Compared with other models that assume a specific functional form of error distribution or rely on two-part modeling strategies, CLAD provides a single-equation framework that naturally accommodates left-censoring while maintaining robustness to extreme values. This makes it particularly suitable for analyzing healthcare expenditure data, where zero-inflation and heavy right tails are common.

Total medical expenses were used as the dependent variable in this study. The independent variables in model 1 included the patient’s basic information (age, gender, diagnostic grouping of the six SMD, marital status, economic status, and occupational status). In model 2, the independent variables consisted of the patient’s basic information along with the number of comorbidities. Model 3 incorporated the patient’s basic information as well as comorbidities grouped by ICD chapter as independent variables.

Model 2 shows the impact of the number of comorbidity on medical expenses after adjusting for demographic characteristics. A notable association emerged between increased comorbidities and higher medical expenses across all comorbidity groups, showing statistical significance (p < 0.001). Model 3 specifically examined diverse somatic comorbidity subgroups while adjusting for demographic factors. Most comorbidity subgroups demonstrated significant statistical contributions to the model. Within the medical comorbidities section, comorbidities categorized under ICD-11 codes Chaps. 2, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 15, and 16 significantly influenced the model (p < 0.05). Given the potential sensitivity of CLAD to outliers, we applied a top 1% Winsorization to the key variable Total Medical Expenses—that is, values above the 99th percentile were set to the 99th percentile—while no adjustments were made to the lower end of the distribution. The regression results after this adjustment remained consistent with the main analysis in terms of statistical significance, indicating that extreme high values had a limited impact on the primary conclusions (see Supplementary File 2 for details).

Discussion

This study researched the relationship between healthcare expenses and comorbidities of patients with SMD in Beijing, providing new data on the healthcare expenses of patients with SMD in China.

In this study, 60.59% of patients with SMD having at least one type of somatic comorbidity, while 17.11% had comorbidities involving more than seven types of diseases. This is consistent with the findings of other related studies. For example, Razzano et al. found that 87% of patients with psychiatric disorders reported at least one comorbidity, 82% reported at least two or more, and 69% reported at least three or more comorbidities [26]. However, one study found that only 38.6% of rural Chinese patients had comorbidities, which could be attributed to the study’s focus on rural patients and the use of self-reported data for collecting comorbidity information [27]. In this study, diseases of the circulatory system (36.6%), diseases of the respiratory system (32.3%), diseases of the digestive system (30.6%), and endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases (29.8%) were more common. Mohamad Isa reported the prevalence of co-morbid somatic disorders in their study as follow: metabolic (28.9%), endocrine (25.6%), haematological (18.3%), gastrointestinal (15%), cardiovascular (12.2%), neurological (9.4%), and oral disorders (8.3%) [28]. However, the structural proportions and prevalence of diseases found in this study differ from other relevant studies, although most studies reported a high proportion of comorbidities. This variation may reflect differences in healthcare systems, culture, socio-economic environments, and lifestyle across different regions. Given these discrepancies, providing updated data on somatic comorbidities in patients with severe mental disorders (SMDs) in Beijing is essential for understanding the current status of this population. Additionally, this finding can help health policymakers gain a better understanding of the health needs of different regions, serving as an important reference for improving medical care and formulating public health policies.

Most somatic comorbidities grouped by ICD-11 chapters had a significant impact on medical expenses. Medical expenses increased as the number of comorbidities grew. This is consistent with the previous research. A US study found that both severe mental disorders (SMD) and comorbid health conditions led to an increase in total healthcare expenses [8]. A total of nine chapters of somatic comorbidities had a significant effect on expenses, such as Neoplasms, Diseases of the circulatory system, Endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases. Caballer-Tarazona et al. examined the influence of comorbidities on inpatient expenses grouped by ICD chapter, which found that the influence of comorbidities on inpatient expenses was not significant across most subgroups [29]. Their study focused on the issue of comorbidity expenses in inpatients, while due to the fact that comorbidities in patients with SMD are predominantly chronic non-communicable diseases, and outpatient treatment and medication expenses also represent a significant portion of the overall medical burden. By incorporating outpatient costs, our study revealed significantly different results regarding total medical expenses.

Previous studies have also demonstrated in various ways that different somatic comorbidities can impact healthcare expenses for patients. Neoplasms, Endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases, Diseases of the blood or blood-forming organs, Diseases of the nervous system, Diseases of the circulatory system and Diseases of the digestive system have been found a significantly relationship with the increase in patient expenses. A US study found that patients with diabetes had much higher all-cause expenses (US$11,317) than those without diabetes in the year following the first bipolar-related claim [30], Another study in the US found that patients with bipolar disorder had a significantly higher visits and expenses among those who suffer from diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, cerebral-vascular disease, ischemic heart disease and arthritis [31]. Our results in Chinese communities replicate the conclusions of previous studies, providing evidence for developing countries. However, few studies have investigated the impact of Diseases of the genitourinary system, Sleep-wake disorders and Diseases of the musculoskeletal system or connective tissue on expenses, we found that these diseases likewise lead to significant increases in patients’ expenses.

There are several possible explanations for the increased expenses. Firstly, the original medical costs of these diseases may be high [32, 33], and behavioral and emotional issues within this population can hinder illness coping mechanisms and self-care practices, potentially increasing various risk factors such as sedentary lifestyles, obesity, and substance abuse. These potential factors, in turn, can further influence patient care and may lead to further increases in healthcare expenses [34, 35]. Simultaneously, the low screening rates and low primary care utilization faced by these diseases may also contribute to their high healthcare expenses [17]. Low screening rates and underutilization of primary care may result in a lack of early prevention and timely interventions for these patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that timely screening and treatment significantly contribute to reducing healthcare expenses for patients, while the absence of such interventions may lead to disease progression and higher expenses [29, 36, 37]. Also, stigma may impede disclosure and cause difficulty in reporting medical problems, all of which may also lead to inaccurate early diagnosis of somatic illnesses, inadequate treatment, and poor management of somatic illnesses, resulting higher expenses [21, 38, 39].

Comorbidities in five chapters, such as certain infectious or parasitic diseases, Diseases of the respiratory system and Diseases of the ear or mastoid process were not significantly associated with patient health expenses. However, previous studies on respiratory and skin diseases have predominantly shown a correlation with expense increases [40, 41]. For instance, a Japanese study revealed that chronic respiratory diseases, skin diseases, and vision abnormalities all contributed to expense increases. This contradicts the findings of the present study [17]. The possible reason for this disparity could be that the diseases examined in the previous study were primarily chronic conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, necessitating continuous medication or treatment. Conversely, the community patients in this study predominantly suffered from upper respiratory tract infections, pharyngitis, dermatitis, eczema, etc., most of which were neither chronic noninfectious diseases nor acute severe illnesses requiring hospitalization and therefore would not impose excessive medical burdens. Zhang et al. found that compared with other combinations, patients with respiratory diseases were more inclined to seek outpatient treatment, and the average medical expense was lower [42].

Referring to a blueprint for protecting somatic health in people with mental illness by The Lancet Psychiatry Commission, this study makes the following recommendations for policy and practice. First, the relevant authorities should conduct regular screening in this population for the diseases mentioned above that have a significant impact on expenses, in order to improve the early detection of diseases and reduce the impact of somatic comorbidities in patients with mental illness. Second, the community needs not only regular follow-up to understand the current health status of patients, but also effective interventions to help improve the health status of patients with characteristics of high risk of health care burden. Third, the treatment and rehabilitation of patients in the Chinese community is usually undertaken by psychiatrists in community or specialized hospitals, often lacking attention to somatic comorbidities. Policy should strengthen the cooperation between medical-related institutions at all levels, fully consider the geographical characteristics, population characteristics, somatic health level and mental illness status of local patients, and establish multidisciplinary teams to provide comprehensive treatment and rehabilitation for patients [14, 43].

The main contributions of this study are as follows: samples were obtained by random sampling of patients in all communities of the city, which can reflect the current situation of patients more comprehensively and accurately, and reduce the influence of selection bias. In addition, compared with developed countries such as the United States, Canada, Japan, and Spain in previous studies [17, 29, 44], this article provides empirical evidence under different research context. China is a country with low and medium economic development levels with a large population. These findings provide a valuable reference for the prevention, management and treatment of somatic comorbidities in patients with SMD by clinical or competent authorities.

This study also has the following limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, we can’t know the developmental trajectories of comorbidity and the causal relationship between healthcare expense and comorbidity. Another limitation is that this article only explored the effect of different types of somatic disorders individually on the expenses of patients with mental illness in Beijing, China, but previous studies have indicated that there may also be interactions between different somatic disorders, and the effect on the interaction between diseases is not discussed in this paper. Finally, the classification by ICD chapters is still crude, with diseases classified into the same category because they belong to the same system, but the severity of the same disease may vary, and the impact on expenses may be different for different diseases in the same subgroup, which makes the results need to be generalized with caution for specific diseases.

Conclusion

This study utilized a city-wide representative sample to investigate the prevalence of somatic comorbidities among community-dwelling patients with severe mental disorders (SMD) and to evaluate the impact of different comorbidity patterns on medical expenditures. The findings revealed a high prevalence of somatic comorbidities in this population, which are closely associated with significantly increased medical costs. These results highlight the urgent need to shift from a fragmented care model toward an integrated approach that simultaneously addresses both mental and physical health. From a policy perspective, healthcare reform efforts should prioritize the establishment of collaborative care mechanisms across mental health and general medical services, enhance early screening for chronic physical illnesses within psychiatric care settings, and promote reimbursement policies that incentivize comprehensive, whole-person care. In clinical practice, mental health professionals should be equipped with the tools and training necessary to identify and manage physical comorbidities, while primary care providers should be more actively involved in the ongoing care of patients with SMD. By strengthening the integration of mental and physical health services, these strategies can help reduce patients’ economic burden and improve health outcomes in this vulnerable population.
