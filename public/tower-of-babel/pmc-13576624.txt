Concurrent strength and conductivity enhancement in a dilute Al–Ce alloy: grain refinement, Fe/Si redistribution, and localized interface bonding
Authors: Ruanlin Li, Wencong Dong, Linhui Zhang, Chunhui Li, Guolin Zhang, Binnian Zhong, Haihua Chen, Jing Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While rare-earth Ce additions can improve both the electrical conductivity and mechanical strength of commercial-purity Al, the origins of this simultaneous enhancement remain difficult to separate. Here, multi-scale characterization (OM, SEM/EDS, XRD, and HRTEM/HAADF) was combined with first-principles calculations to examine the effects of 0.07 wt% Ce in 1085Al. Ce addition increased the mean electrical conductivity from 58.86 ± 2.05 to 63.62 ± 0.78 %IACS. The mean ultimate tensile strength increased from 44.86 ± 2.36 to 61.87 ± 3.82 MPa, while the mean elongation increased from 29.43 ± 2.11% to 35.43 ± 0.35%. The mean grain size decreased from 126.8 ± 10.9 to 78.5 ± 14.2 µm. SEM/EDS showed Fe and Si enrichment in micrometre-scale spheroidal Al–Fe–Si–Ce particles, while local HRTEM fringes and FFT features were consistent with an Al11Ce3-type lamellar constituent within these particles. The resistivity calculated from the mean conductivity decreased from approximately 29.29 to 27.10 nΩ m (Δρ ≈ −2.19 nΩ m). A semi-quantitative Matthiessen-rule analysis associated this change mainly with reduced matrix-solute scattering after Fe/Si redistribution. The local orientation suggested by HRTEM was represented by a (200)Al//(060)Al11Ce3 interface model with nominal in-plane mismatches of 8.27% and 6.11%. PDOS and COHP analyses showed localized Al-p/Ce-d hybridization and greater stabilization of the selected L1 Al–Ce contact than of L2, with ICOHP values of −1.027 and −0.690 eV per bond, respectively. These bond-specific descriptors characterize relative stability but do not quantify interface resistance or an electron-scattering cross-section. The results link Fe/Si redistribution and localized interface bonding to the concurrent changes in conductivity and mechanical properties while retaining the limits of the available measurements.

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of the electrolytic aluminium industry, minimising energy consumption and carbon emissions has become an urgent priority.1–3 The expansion of electrolytic cells and the utilization of highly conductive external components can effectively achieve this objective. The global aluminum industry is currently shifting towards ultra-high amperage electrolytic cells, with capacities reaching 500 kA or even 600 kA.4 In these systems, the busbar is a critical component. Employing high-conductivity aluminum alloys5–7 can significantly minimize the voltage drop, thereby reducing overall energy consumption.

The Al ingots produced by traditional Al electrolysis cells contain 99.7% Al,8 and their conductivity is usually around 55.95 %IACS. This lower conductivity can easily cause higher voltage losses, resulting in greater power consumption. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a new type of highly conductive Al alloy material to meet the production and development needs of enterprises.

According to Matthiessen's rule,9 the electrical conductivity of Al alloy materials is influenced not only by their intrinsic conductivity but also by grain boundaries, dislocations, solute atoms, and precipitation phases. Among these factors, the effects of Fe and Si elements, when solid-solved in Al, on electrical conductivity properties are particularly pronounced.

In terms of regulating chemical composition, the rare earth element cerium (Ce) refines the grain size,10–12 enriches Fe and Si,13–15 and also enhances the electrical conductivity and strength of the Al busbar.14 Li Pengfei16 indicates that when the addition of Ce reaches 0.07%, the electrical conductivity can reach a maximum of 62.06 %IACS. It has previously been proven that Ce has the capacity to enhance the formation of binary or ternary compounds with Al, Fe, and Si,5 and promotes their precipitation. This, in turn, reduces the solid solubility of impurity elements in α-Al and mitigates the electron scattering effect caused by the impurity solid solution.

To clarify possible grain-refinement mechanisms, numerous studies have used first-principles calculations to construct interface models between Al and candidate second phases. Such models can assess crystallographic matching and interfacial bonding, which are relevant to the stability of a potential heterogeneous nucleation substrate.17–19 Studies of Al/AlB2,20,21 Al/Al3Ti,22,23 Al/Al3Zr,18,19 Al/Al3Li,24 and related interfaces have shown that the work of adhesion (Wad) and local electronic structure are useful descriptors of interfacial stability. For Al–Ce conductor alloys, these descriptors characterize bonding and relative stability.

Composition regulation has been used to balance mechanical properties and transport performance in Al alloys.25 Zhang et al.26 investigated Al//Al11Ce3 interfaces and reported several orientation relationships involving (001)Al11Ce3. Here, the local orientation suggested by HRTEM is used to construct a (200)Al//(060)Al11Ce3 interface model. A semi-quantitative Matthiessen-rule discussion is combined with adhesion work, PDOS, differential-charge-density, and COHP analyses to examine alloy-scale scattering trends and local interface bonding.

Experimental

Materials

The experimental materials, including commercial 1085 aluminum alloy (1085Al) and Al–20Ce intermetallic alloy, were purchased from Qinghai Aluminum Co., Ltd, China. The raw ingredients were put in a graphite crucible in the appropriate ratio. The Al alloy was initially melted in an electric resistance furnace at a temperature of 750 °C. Subsequently, Al–20Ce intermetallic alloys were added to the melt and left to set for a duration of 30 min. Performance test

Electrical conductivity was measured at room temperature using an MRS-3 thermoelectric parameter testing system (Wuhan Jiayitong Technology Co., Ltd). Three independent specimens were tested separately for each alloy condition. Test specimen dimensions were 1 mm × 5 mm × 15 mm. The measured values were reported without temperature correction.

Flat dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens were machined with their longitudinal axes parallel to the casting direction. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the specimens had an overall length of 40 mm, a grip-section width of 10 mm, a reduced-section width of 4 mm, a reduced-section length of 14 mm, and a thickness of 2.5 mm. The transition regions had fillet radii of 2 mm and 0.5 mm. Room-temperature tensile tests were conducted in accordance with GB/T 228.1-2021 (ref. 27) using an Instron 5982 electronic universal testing machine. The crosshead separation rate was maintained at 0.2 mm min−1. The estimated strain rate was 2.38 × 10−4 s−1. Three independently machined specimens were tested for each alloy condition.

Schematic geometry and dimensions of the flat dog-bone-shaped tensile specimen used in this study. All dimensions are in millimetres.

Vickers hardness tests were conducted in accordance with GB/T 4340.1-2024 (ref. 28) using a VH1102 digital microhardness tester (Wolpert Wilson, USA). A test force of 50 gf (0.490 N) was applied for a dwell time of 10 s. Three indentations were made for each alloy condition. The EC, UTS, and EL values are reported as the arithmetic mean ± sample standard deviation (SD), with n = 3 independent specimens for each alloy condition. Hardness values are reported as the arithmetic mean ± sample SD, with n = 3 indentations for each alloy condition.

Metallographic structures were examined using an ICX41M inverted metallurgical microscope. Grain sizes were quantified from three non-overlapping optical micrographs for each alloy condition using the equivalent circular diameter. In total, 128 grains in 1085Al and 296 grains in Al–0.07Ce were measured. Grain-size results are reported as the mean ± sample SD of the three field means (n = 3 fields per alloy). Second-phase morphology and elemental composition were analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using a JSM-7900F scanning electron microscope (SEM). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded in the range of 20° ≤ 2θ ≤ 90°. Because of the low Ce addition (0.07 wt%), slow-scan XRD at 2° min−1 was used to detect potential minor phases.

The microstructure and phase composition of the samples were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Initially, the sample was divided into thin sections with a thickness of approximately 1.0 mm. The sections were then successively ground with 500 grit, 800 grit, and 1200 grit emery paper until they reached a thickness of approximately 0.05 mm. A punch cutter was then employed to trim the sample into circular sections with a diameter of 3 mm. Subsequently, the surface was polished using a suitable polishing compound. Following a meticulous polishing process, circular thin sections were subjected to electrolytic double-jet thinning using a Helios 5CX electrolytic double-jet thinning apparatus. The resulting specimens were then subjected to a thorough cleansing process using anhydrous ethanol, followed by meticulous transfer to filter paper via tweezers for the purpose of drying.

The microstructure of the Al–0.07Ce sample was characterized using a Talos F200X high-resolution aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (HRTEM), with partial phase interfaces characterized via high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging.

First principles calculations

The computational approach in this work was based on density functional theory using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) for first-principles calculations. The exchange–correlation functional utilizes the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation within the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA).29 Pseudopotentials for Al and Ce are described using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method.30 All calculations were performed with a plane wave cutoff energy of 500 eV, sufficient to achieve electronic convergence. To obtain an accurate crystal structure, we performed full relaxation of the unit cell volume, unit cell vector, and atomic positions within the lattice. This approach ensured both energy convergence and computational efficiency.

The integration over the Brillouin zone was performed with a 15 × 15 × 1 Gamma-centred31k-point mesh for the (200)Al interface, 15 × 15 × 1 for the (060)Al11Ce3 interface, 4 × 15 × 1 for the (200)Al//(060)Al11Ce3 interface, 15 × 15 × 15 for bulk Al, and 11 × 5 × 4 for Al11Ce3. The energy accuracy for self-consistent calculations is set to 1 × 10−6 eV per atom, with forces acting on each atom less than 0.01 eV Å−1 and internal stresses less than or equal to 0.02 GPa. Following convergence testing, the plane-wave cutoff energy for all calculations in this section is set to 500 eV, employing a 15 Å vacuum layer to eliminate interactions arising from periodic boundary condition calculations in surface and interface models.

Bond-resolved COHP analysis was performed with LOBSTER v5.1.1 using the pbeVaspFit2015 basis set. Al33–Ce68 in L1 and Al18–Ce68 in L2 were selected as representative short Al–Ce contacts spanning the relaxed interfaces. The absolute charge-spilling values were 2.74%/2.68% for L1 and 2.82%/2.91% for L2 (spin channels 1/2), indicating adequate projection quality. The plotted quantity is the spin-summed −COHP; positive and negative values denote bonding and antibonding contributions, respectively. Spin-summed ICOHP values at the Fermi level were obtained directly from ICOHPLIST.lobster. Under the conventional ICOHP sign definition, a more negative value indicates greater bond stabilization.

Regarding mechanical properties, the mean hardness (27.73 ± 0.90 HV) and UTS (61.87 ± 3.82 MPa) of Al–0.07Ce are lower than those of the heavily alloyed counterparts reported in ref. 14 and 35, which can reach UTS values exceeding 100 MPa. However, compared with 1085Al, the present alloy improves the mean mechanical properties while also increasing conductivity.

This compositional optimization is specifically tailored to the functional demands of aluminum electrolytic cells. In this context, components serve primarily as conductive pathways rather than heavily loaded structural elements, meaning the demand for ultra-high mechanical strength is moderate. Consequently, the moderate mechanical properties are justified by the exceptional gain in electrical conductivity, which is the critical factor for minimizing voltage drop and reducing overall Direct Current (DC) power consumption in the industrial electrolytic process.

The fracture surfaces in Fig. 6 were examined in relation to the increase in mean elongation from 29.43 ± 2.11% to 35.43 ± 0.35%. Both alloys show mixed ductile–brittle features, including cleavage planes and dimples. The 1085Al surface contains more smooth cleavage regions and relatively shallow dimples (Fig. 6(a) and (c)). Al–0.07Ce contains more numerous deep, equiaxed dimples, although local cleavage features remain (Fig. 6(b) and (d)). This difference is consistent with greater plastic deformation before fracture in Al–0.07Ce.

Casting defects and dislocations are additional electron-scattering sources in metallic conductors and form part of the competing scattering contributions considered in the Matthiessen-rule framework.9

The grain-refinement and particle-strengthening contributions can be described by the Hall–Petch and Orowan relationships, respectively:142ΔσHP = ky(d2−1/2 − d1−1/2),34Here, ky is the Hall–Petch coefficient; M, G, b, and ν are the Taylor factor, shear modulus, Burgers vector, and Poisson's ratio; and r, Vp, and λp are the particle radius, volume fraction, and edge-to-edge spacing. Thus, the grain-size decrease from 126.8 ± 10.9 to 78.5 ± 14.2 µm is consistent with grain-boundary strengthening, while finer particle spacing would increase the Orowan contribution.

Particle spheroidization can reduce local stress concentration and delay cracking, whereas porosity, inclusions, and other casting defects reduce the effective load-bearing area. Consistently, Liu et al.36 reported that RE additions refine α-Al, increase grain roundness, and promote Al–RE intermetallic compounds, including Al11Ce3; they further showed that the strength–transport balance depends on solute partitioning together with intermetallic phase fraction and morphology. The present strength change is therefore associated with grain refinement, particle–dislocation interactions, second-phase morphology, and casting quality.

Matthiessen's rule (eqn (5)) is used here to organize the competing microstructural contributions to resistivity and, on this basis, to interpret the influence of Ce on the microstructure of the Al alloy.15,37,385ρtotal = ρ0 + ρss + ρgb + ρd + ρpwhere ρ0 = 26.5 nΩ m is the intrinsic resistivity of pure Al at 300 K,9ρss is the solute scattering contribution, ρgb arises from grain boundaries, ρd from dislocations, and ρp from precipitates. Converting the mean electrical conductivity to resistivity (ρ = 17.241/%IACS × 100 nΩ m), the 1085Al base alloy exhibits ρtotal ≈ 29.29 nΩ m, while the Al–0.07Ce alloy shows ρtotal ≈ 27.10 nΩ m, corresponding to a net reduction of approximately Δρ = 2.19 nΩ m.

For a dilute Al solid solution, the solute-scattering contribution can be approximated by6where ci and ki are the matrix concentration and resistivity coefficient of solute i, respectively. Accordingly, the change associated with Fe and Si redistribution may be expressed qualitatively as7Δρss ≈ kFeΔcFe + kSiΔcSi.

Previous studies show that Ce can promote the incorporation of Fe and Si into Ce-containing intermetallic compounds and thereby decrease their concentrations in Al solid solution.15,38 In the present alloy, SEM/EDS shows Fe and Si enrichment in spheroidal Al–Fe–Si–Ce particles. This observation is consistent with redistribution from the matrix into second phases. The qualitative XRD trend does not resolve the dissolved concentrations; therefore, the direction of the solute-scattering change is inferred without assigning a numerical magnitude.

The dislocation contribution is commonly related to dislocation density by ref. 378Δρd = AdΔNdwhere Nd is the dislocation density and Ad is the specific dislocation-resistivity coefficient. Grain refinement, residual dislocations, casting defects, and second-phase interfaces may increase electron scattering, whereas the redistribution of Fe and Si from the matrix into Ce-containing particles reduces matrix-solute scattering. The resulting resistivity reflects the combined effects of these microstructural factors.

The mechanism of second phases

To further substantiate the aforementioned conclusions, the effect of Ce on inhibiting the growth of α-Al can be measured using a limiting factor. Eqn (9) is used to calculate the growth restriction factor Q:399Q = mlc0(k0 − 1)where c0 is the added amount of Ce, ml and k0 denote the liquid phase slope and equilibrium solute distribution factor in the Al–Ce binary system respectively, and Q is the growth-limiting factor. According to the formula, the solute content within the same system is directly proportional to the growth-limiting factor. However, calculations indicate that the growth-inhibiting factor of Ce is essentially negligible.

As shown in Fig. 8(a) and (b), SEM/EDS reveals a representative micrometre-scale spheroidal Al–Fe–Si–Ce particle. The diameter of the particle shown in Fig. 8(a) is approximately 2.9 µm (one imaged particle; n = 1 descriptive observation). This value is not a population mean, and no particle-size standard deviation is inferred. Fe and Si enrichment in the particle is consistent with partitioning from the Al matrix into a Ce-containing second phase.

Local TEM/HRTEM observations in Fig. 8(c–e) show a lamellar constituent within the micrometre-scale particle. Its morphology, lattice fringes, and FFT features are consistent with an Al11Ce3-type structure.14,26 SEM/EDS describes the composition of the complete Al–Fe–Si–Ce particle, whereas the Al11Ce3-type assignment applies only to the locally examined lamella. The measured spacings are approximately 0.204 nm for (200)Al and 0.217 nm for (060)Al11Ce3, consistent with the orientation used in the interface model. Because site-specific compositional data are unavailable for the lamella, the assignment remains qualified as Al11Ce3-type.

As detailed in subsubsection 3.4.4, the periodic model was constructed by matching two independent in-plane directions and distributing the lattice adjustment between the two phases. The resulting common in-plane periodicities provide a consistent interface cell for comparing the L1 and L2 stacking configurations. The layered Al11Ce3-type constituent may contribute to heterogeneous nucleation and grain refinement.

First principles calculations

Bulk properties calculations

To provide a theoretical basis for the interface model, the bulk structures of Al and Al11Ce3 were first optimized. Bulk fcc-Al crystallizes in the Fm3̄m space group with a calculated lattice constant of a = 4.038 Å. Orthorhombic Al11Ce3 crystallizes in the Immm space group, with calculated lattice parameters of a = 4.372 Å, b = 9.983 Å, and c = 12.854 Å. These values are consistent with the available calculated and experimental lattice parameters.26,40–42

The Al/Al11Ce3 interface was constructed using (060)Al11Ce3‖(200)Al, [100]Al11Ce3‖[010]Al, and [001]Al11Ce3‖[001]Al. Because Al11Ce3 is orthorhombic, [100] and [001] are the two independent in-plane directions of the (060) surface. The [010] direction is normal to this surface; consequently, b = 9.983 Å affects the normal layer spacing and slab thickness but does not enter the in-plane mismatch calculation. The corresponding in-plane directions of Al (200) are [010] and [001].

Surface stability of (060)Al11Ce3

In light of the adoption of non-stoichiometric (060)Al11Ce3 surfaces with symmetric terminations, it is imperative that the chemical potential of Al and Ce atoms is taken into account during the calculation of surface energy. Ref. 44 and 45 state that the surface energy of the (060)Al11Ce3 surface is calculated from eqn (12):12where Eslab represents the total energy of the Al11Ce3 surface slab, while NAl and NCe denote the numbers of Al and Ce atoms in the supercell, respectively. µCe and µAl are the chemical potentials of Ce and Al in their respective stable bulk phases.

Furthermore, assuming that the surface or interface is in thermodynamic equilibrium with the bulk reservoir, the chemical potentials of the species in the slab system are constrained by the total energy of the bulk phase:13µAl11Ce3bulk = 6µbulkCe + 22µbulkAl + ΔΔH0f

Eqn (14) indicates that µAl and µCe are interdependent under equilibrium conditions between the slab and the bulk reservoir:14µAl11Ce3bulk = 6µslabCe + 22µslabAl

By varying these potentials within the thermodynamic stability limits (bounded by the formation enthalpy), the interface properties can be evaluated under different chemical environments, such as Al-rich or Ce-rich conditions. According to the formula, the independent variable of the function is ΔµCe = µslabCe − µbulkCe. When ΔµCe = 0, the horizontal coordinate reaches its maximum value, corresponding to the AlCe-terminal limit; conversely, when ΔµCe = ΔH0f, the horizontal coordinate reaches its minimum value, corresponding to the Al-terminal limit. Under Al-rich conditions, the surface energy of the Al-terminated end is significantly greater than that of the AlCe-terminated end. Electronic structure and bonding

To investigate the bonding behaviour at the (200)Al//(060)Al11Ce3 interface, differential charge density was employed to calculate the electronic distribution at the interface. By comparing differential charge analyses using different terminal interface models, the electronic interactions and charge distribution at the interface can be revealed. The calculation of the differential charge density can be calculated using the following eqn (17):17Δρ = ρ(200)Al//(060)Al11Ce3 − ρ(200)Al − ρ(060)Al11Ce3where ρ(200)Al//(060)Al11Ce3 is the total charge density of the interface system, ρ(200)Al and ρ(060)Al11Ce3 represent the charge densities of the isolated (200)Al and (060)Al11Ce3 surfaces within the same model, respectively. Fig. 11 presents the differential charge graphs for two distinct terminal interface models. The figure reveals a comparatively large green area surrounding the Al atoms, indicating that electron dissipation occurs primarily around these atoms, with a lesser portion occurring around the Ce atoms. The charge distributions between the Ce terminal interface model and the Al terminal interface model exhibit substantial differences.

Charge density difference for L1; L2.

The electron redistribution is primarily localized, with the interface effect occurring only on one or two layers at the interface. Notably, significant irregular motion occurs among the atoms at interface L1, with electron loss from the Al atoms appearing at the L1 end interface. Electron loss in this region is more obvious than at the L2 end. Moreover, the L1 interface exhibits charge deposition regions at the interface boundary, with charge movement displaying a certain directionality, exhibiting characteristics of ionic bonding. A strong electrostatic interaction exists between the two interfaces. This is consistent with the adhesive energy calculation results, indicating that the L1 interface is the most stable interface.

Fig. 12 presents the layer-resolved PDOS of the L1 and L2 interface models. Both models retain non-zero states at the Fermi level (EF = 0 eV), consistent with metallic character. Al-p and Ce-d states overlap from approximately −12 to 3 eV, mainly within the first one or two interfacial layers. The interfacial perturbation is more pronounced in L1. These features are consistent with localized p–d hybridization, while the finite states at EF show that metallic states remain in both configurations. Conclusions

In this study, multi-scale microstructural characterization and first-principles calculations were combined to examine the simultaneous changes in electrical conductivity and mechanical properties in dilute Al–Ce alloys. The principal findings are as follows:

(1) The addition of 0.07 wt% Ce increases the mean EC from 58.86 ± 2.05 to 63.62 ± 0.78 %IACS, corresponding to a decrease in the resistivity calculated from the mean EC from approximately 29.29 to 27.10 nΩ m (Δρ ≈ −2.19 nΩ m). The measured resistivity decrease and the observed enrichment of Fe and Si in Ce-containing particles jointly support decreased matrix-solute scattering after Ce addition.

(2) The PDOS retains finite states at the Fermi level and shows localized Al-p/Ce-d hybridization near the interface. The representative L1 Al–Ce contact has a more negative ICOHP than L2 (−1.027 versus −0.690 eV per bond) and a shorter bond distance. These bond-specific results, together with the adhesion work, indicate greater stabilization of L1.

(3) Ce promotes the redistribution of Fe and Si into micrometre-scale spheroidal Al–Fe–Si–Ce particles, thereby decreasing matrix-solute scattering, while local HRTEM/FFT observations identify an Al11Ce3-type lamellar constituent within these particles. After Ce addition, the mean grain size decreases from 126.8 ± 10.9 to 78.5 ± 14.2 µm, the particles become more spheroidal, the mean UTS increases from 44.86 ± 2.36 to 61.87 ± 3.82 MPa, and the mean elongation increases from 29.43 ± 2.11% to 35.43 ± 0.35%.

(4) The present study focuses on the as-cast condition with 0.07 wt% Ce and an idealized Al/Al11Ce3 interface model. Future work will extend the range of Ce contents and processing conditions, while quantifying Fe and Si redistribution and electron transport more directly. Additional interface orientations, long-term thermal stability, and scale-up performance will also be evaluated.
