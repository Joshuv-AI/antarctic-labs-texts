Star Formation under a Cosmic Microscope: Highly magnified z = 11 z=11 galaxy behind the Bullet Cluster Facilities: HST (ACS,WFC3), JWST (NIRCam, NIRSpec)
Maruša Bradač, Jon Judež, Chris Willott, Gregor Rihtaršič, Nicholas S. Martis, Anishya Harshan, Giordano Felicioni, Yoshihisa Asada, Guillaume Desprez, Douglas Clowe, Anthony H. Gonzalez, Christine Jones, Brian C. Lemaux, Vladan Markov, Lamiya Mowla, Gaël Noirot, Annika H. G. Peter, Andrew Robertson, Ghassan T. E. Sarrouh, Marcin Sawicki, Tim Schrabback, Roberta Tripodi

Abstract
We present measurements of stellar population properties of a newly discovered spectroscopically confirmed z = 11.10 − 0.26 + 0.11 z=11.10^{+0.11}_{-0.26} , gravitationally lensed galaxy, using JWST NIRSpec PRISM spectroscopy and NIRCam imaging. The arc is highly magnified by the Bullet Cluster (magnification factor μ = 14.0 − 0.3 + 6.2 \mu=14.0^{+6.2}_{-0.3} ). It contains three star-forming components of which one is barely resolved and two are unresolved, giving intrinsic sizes of ≲ 10 ​ pc \lesssim 10\mbox{pc} . The clumps also contain ∼ 50 % \sim 50\% of the total stellar mass. The galaxy formed the majority of its stars ∼ 150 ​ Myr \sim 150\mbox{Myr} ago (by z ∼ 14 z\sim 14 ). The spectrum shows a pronounced damping wing, typical for galaxies deep in the reionisation era and indicating a neutral IGM at this line of sight. The intrinsic luminosity of the galaxy is 0.086 − 0.030 + 0.008 ​ L ∗ 0.086^{+0.008}_{-0.030}\,L^{*} (with L ∗ L^{*} being the characteristic luminosity for this redshift), making it the lowest luminosity spectroscopically confirmed galaxy at z > 10 z>10 discovered to date.



I
Introduction

The epoch of reionisation, which signified the transformation of the universe from opaque to transparent, is poorly
understood. The cosmic microwave background (CMB) observations now
indicate that reionisation began within a few hundred
Myr of the Big Bang
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. However, how it proceeded and which
are the primary sources of reionisation are still the topics of much
debate. Studying the stellar properties of galaxies at the highest redshifts and a wide range of stellar mass is, therefore, necessary to make advances.

The advent of
JWST
imaging surveys and follow-up spectroscopy has led to a rapid advance in our knowledge of the universe at redshifts
z>10
. The space density of luminous galaxies at this epoch is greater than previous predictions
(
Harikane et al. 2023
)
, requiring revisions to galaxy evolution models such as enhanced early star formation
(
Dekel et al. 2023
)
, increased burstiness
(
Sun et al. 2023
)
, lack of dust
(
Ferrara et al. 2023
)
and potentially a significant contribution from accreting supermassive black holes
(
Maiolino et al. 2024
)
. Extremely luminous galaxies have been spectroscopically identified up to
z=14.4

(
Carniani et al. 2024
;
Naidu et al. 2025
)
. It is important to measure the properties of less luminous galaxies that potentially dominate the ionising photon output of galaxies in the early Universe.

JWST
with its high spatial resolution and excellent sensitivity has enabled
us to map the physics of star formation within some early
galaxies. However, NIRCam’s pixel scale (
0.031\arcsec/\mbox{pix}
at short wavelengths, equivalent to
\sim 100\mbox{pc}
at
z=10
) and resolution do not allow the detailed studies of star formation at sub-
100pc
scales.

Already, the studies with the Hubble Space Telescope have shown that star-forming regions smaller than 100 pc play an important role in galaxies at
z\sim 3

(
Johnson et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. However, such scales are only revealed when gravitational lensing with high magnification is present. At high redshifts
z>5
, this was recently shown by several studies. Extremely magnified
high-z galaxies have been studied in, among others,
Strait et al. 2023
;
Asada et al. 2023
;
Vanzella et al. 2023
;
Vanzella et al. 2024
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
;
Bradač et al. 2024
;
Mowla et al. 2024
;
Adamo et al. 2024
;
Messa et al. 2025
;
Claeyssens et al. 2023
;
Claeyssens et al. 2025
. These galaxies appear to consist of several clumps, with a significant fraction of total stellar mass being in compact star-forming components. These
compact and dense structures are found with a range of stellar masses (
10^{4}-10^{7}M_{\odot}
). They have large stellar surface densities (some with
>10^{5}M_{\odot}\>\mbox{pc}^{-2}
), up to three orders of magnitude larger than what is seen in the Milky Way star clusters. Early results suggest that the majority of star formation is happening in these dense structures; however, the samples are still small.

The Bullet Cluster (
z=0.295
) is a well-known galaxy cluster merger, which was used in the past to study the properties of dark matter (
Bradač et al. 2006
;
Clowe et al. 2006
;
Randall et al. 2008
). Due to its merger geometry, it is also a very efficient gravitational lens
(
Bradač et al. 2009
)
. It was recently observed by JWST (JWST-GO-4598 co-PIs Bradač, Rihtaršič, Sawicki). In this paper, we present the highest redshift highly magnified (
\mu=14.0^{+6.2}_{-0.3}
) galaxy to date. At the spectroscopic redshift of
z=11.10^{+0.11}_{-0.26}
, we are able to study a resolved stellar population at less than
10\mbox{pc}
scale. The galaxy, named BulletArc-z11, is stretched into an arc with a clumpy structure. The high magnification of the galaxy allows us to increase the resolution of our observations, down to a few parsecs.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
we
present the data used in this paper and in
Section
III
we describe the analysis of the photometric and spectroscopic data. In
Section
IV
we present the main science results. We summarise our results in
Section
V
.
Throughout the paper, we assume a

\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
\Omega_{\rm m}=0.3
and Hubble constant

H_{0}=70{\rm\ kms^{-1}\>\mbox{Mpc}^{-1}}
for ease of comparison with previous work.

II
Data

JWST
NIRCam and NIRSpec observations of BulletArc-z11 were taken as part of the GO Program #4598 Silver Bullet for Dark Matter (co-PIs Bradač, Rihtaršič, Sawicki). The field was observed on 20 January 2025 with NIRCam imaging using filters F090W, F115W, F150W, F200W, F277W, F356W, F410M, and F444W with exposure times of
6.4\mbox{ks}
each, reaching S/N between 5 and 10 for a
m_{\rm AB}=29
point source. We used the INTRAMODULEX 6-point dither pattern to fill in the gaps between the short-wave (SW) detectors. We also utilized archival data of
HST/ACS
imaging from HST-GO-10200 (PI Jones), 10863 (PI Gonzalez), and 11099 (PI Bradač). NIRSpec Micro-Shutter Assembly (MSA) observations of targets selected from the NIRCam and ACS imaging were executed on 27 March 2025. The observations consisted of three separate MSA configurations with the low-resolution prism, using the standard 3 shutter nodding procedure with total integration time of
3.5\mbox{ks}
per MSA configuration. BulletArc-z11 was observed in the third MSA configuration.

We use the CANUCS photometric pipeline presented in more detail in
43
to reduce the imaging data.
Bright cluster and foreground galaxies and
intracluster light are removed from the images, following the procedure outlined in
Martis et al. 2024
. BulletArc-z11 was identified as a
z\approx 11
candidate in the NIRCam imaging with the Lyman break lying in the F150W filter. We show cutouts of the arc in Fig.
1
.

Details of the NIRSpec processing are given in
43
and
Heintz et al. 2025
. Initial processing uses the STScI
jwst
stage 1 pipeline with custom snowball and 1/
f
noise correction. The
jwst
stage 2 pipeline is run up to the photometric calibration step followed by the
grizli

(
Brammer 2023a
)
and
msaexp

(
Brammer 2023b
)
packages. The wavelength calibration uses a correction for the known intra-shutter offset along the dispersion direction. The spectral background is removed using the standard nodded background subtraction. One-dimensional spectra are extracted using a wavelength-dependent optimal extraction that accounts for the increase in PSF FWHM with wavelength.

III
Data Analysis

III.1
Spectroscopy

The central bright clump of BulletArc-z11 (#1 in Fig.
1
) was the target position for the NIRSpec MSA observation. The resulting spectrum and the slit placement are shown in Fig.
1
. No emission lines are securely detected with
S/N>3
. However, a clear Lyman break is observed at 1.47
\mu
m, confirming the high-redshift nature of this galaxy. The smooth rollover at the Lyman break is suggestive of strong Lyman-
\alpha
absorption from a neutral IGM, potentially with additional absorption from a proximate dense neutral absorber.

The redshift is determined by fitting a power-law continuum model with the Monte-Carlo Markov-Chain code
Emcee

(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
. The model contains four free parameters, corresponding to the normalization, redshift,
\beta_{\rm UV}
slope, and neutral hydrogen damping wing column,
N_{\rm HI}
. The neutral hydrogen damping wing was modeled as in
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
and a fully neutral intergalactic medium (IGM) was assumed.

The point source contaminant described in the following subsection can be seen in the spectrum as a faint flux excess below the Lyman break. We model the contaminant as a globular cluster (GC) in the Bullet Cluster with a simple stellar population with metallicity 0.1 solar and age at redshift 0 of 12  Gyr (c.f.
Harris et al. 2025
), normalized by the observed spectrum flux between 0.85
\mu
m and 1.4
\mu
m where the flux from BulletArc-z11 should be zero. This ‘GC contaminant’ model is subtracted from the spectrum prior to fitting.

The resulting redshift is
z=11.10^{+0.11}_{-0.26}
(Table
1
), with the significant uncertainty due to the degeneracy with
N_{\rm HI}
. The value of
N_{\rm HI}
is not meaningfully constrained in the absence of an emission line redshift. From the spectral fit, we also find
\beta_{\rm UV}=-2.44^{+0.17}_{-0.18}
. Because of possible uncertainty in the wavelength-dependent slit losses, we adjust the spectrum with the NIRCam photometry (using the custom photometric aperture with
S/N=16
, see next section) following
Felicioni et al. 2025
, to obtain
\beta_{\rm UV}=-2.3\pm 0.2
(Table
1
).

III.2
Photometry

Photometry is derived using the updated
zero-points, and corrected for Milky Way extinction using the value of colour excess
E(B-V)=0.207
from
Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011
. We also assume the extinction law by
Fitzpatrick 1999
using the factor between the extinction coefficient and colour excess
R_{\rm V}=3.1
. BulletArc-z11 is detected in the F150W,
F200W, F277W, F356W, F410M, and F444W NIRCam filters (Fig.
1
). In the F115W filter, there is a compact source detected just 40 milliarcsec north-west of the brightest component of BulletArc-z11, which we also see in the spectrum (Sect
III.1
). As noted, this source has colors and compactness consistent with a globular cluster within the intracluster region of the Bullet cluster. There are many similar sources in this region of the NIRCam imaging. We do not subtract the flux of the contaminant GC, since it is negligible, as evidenced by the spectrum and measured F115W flux of 2 nJy.

For the photometry of the entire arc, we initially follow the CANUCS pipeline and procedure as described in
43
. We build a
\chi_{\rm mean}
-detection image
(
Drlica-Wagner et al. 2018
)
by combining all images before any PSF convolution. Due to high distortion of the arc, standard photometric apertures are not optimal. To measure the total stellar mass and luminosity we use a custom aperture including all pixels above a detection image threshold of
S/N=3
. To measure the most accurate colors we use the custom aperture with the highest average
S/N
across all filters at
\geq 2\mu
m, which corresponds to pixels with
S/N>16
in the detection image. All fluxes are measured on images that have been convolved to the PSF of the F444W image to ensure the same regions contribute at all wavelengths. For the total flux we additionally scale the PSF-convolved fluxes by the ratio of the non-convolved to convolved F200W fluxes to take account of flux scattered out of the custom aperture during the PSF convolution process. Flux uncertainties are determined from the distribution of sky values measured in identically-shaped apertures located in empty regions of the image.

We present fluxes in Table
2
, using label total for the total flux and SN16 for the optimal S/N aperture for the best colours. The source has total F200W AB magnitude of
26.63\pm 0.07
, giving absolute magnitude (corrected for lensing magnification and uncertainties) of
M_{\rm UV}=-18.13^{+0.47}_{-0.09}
. Using the characteristic luminosity
L^{*}
from
Willott et al. 2024
results in a luminosity of BulletArc-z11
0.086^{+0.008}_{-0.030}\,L^{*}
. We find BulletArc-z11 is the lowest luminosity spectroscopically confirmed galaxy at
z>10
discovered to date (Fig.
2
). The photometry shows a marked upturn at
>4.3\,\mu
m, similar to but stronger than that observed in GN-z11
(
Tacchella et al. 2023
)
. Corresponding to rest-frame wavelengths
>3600\,
Å, this could be due to a Balmer break and/or strong higher-order Balmer, [O
ii
] and [Ne
iii
] emission lines. Such emission lines are not ruled out by the non-detections in our shallow prism spectroscopy.

Since BulletArc-z11 is resolved into three distinct clumps and a smooth galaxy component, we also perform spatially resolved photometry. We fit the central coordinates of the four components using three point sources (for the clumps, later we redo the fit with extended components to measure sizes) and a Gaussian profile (for the underlying galaxy distribution G) in the F200W image with
Galfit

(
Peng et al. 2011
)
. These central coordinates were then used as fixed priors to repeat the process in all filters and extract the photometry of the four components. Errors are measured by injecting artificial sources of similar brightness. This photometry is also given in Table
2
.

Because of high lensing distortion, we also measure sizes and positions in the source plane by forward modelling the observed photometry with
Lenstruction

(
Yang et al. 2020
)
. We use a four-component model consisting of a Sersic profile for the underlying galaxy and Gaussian profiles for the three clumps. The source model is then distorted, using gravitational lensing maps derived by
Rihtaršič et al. 2025
(see also Sect.
III.3
) and convolved with the empirical point spread function (PSF), determined from the observations of stars. The resulting image is then compared to the observations to obtain the best possible size measurement parameters. The results are shown in Fig.
3
.

. From the top left, we plot the observed image (in F200W), the source model, the best fit image model (after lensing and PSF convolution have been applied), and normalised residuals. The source was modelled using an underlying galaxy component G and 3 small clumps (#1-3) modelled using Gaussian profiles.

III.3
Gravitational lens model

A strong lensing model was derived using the parametric lens modelling tool
Lenstool

(
Kneib et al. 1996
;
Jullo et al. 2007
;
Jullo & Kneib 2009
)
and is constrained using a catalogue of 141 multiple images from 52 multiply lensed systems (28 distinct galaxies) with spectroscopic redshifts, mostly obtained from our JWST program. Prior models have used limited number of spectroscopic redshifts available for multiply imaged sources (
6
,
Richard et al. 2021
;
Cha et al. 2025
). The lens model will be published in
Rihtaršič et al. 2025
and includes large-scale dark matter halos, group-scale substructure halos surrounding the inner cluster regions, cluster gas, included as a mass map from Chandra X-ray observations, and cluster member halos with parameters, scaling with NIRCam F277W magnitude. BulletArc-z11 is situated in a highly magnified region,
40^{\prime\prime}
from the subcluster BCG in a region with high gas density. It is furthermore highly influenced by a magnitude 18.97 cluster member
\sim 4^{\prime\prime}
away. When modeled with scaling relations, the cluster member mass is likely overestimated, as it predicts additional BulletArc-z11 counter-images nearby for which we find no evidence in NIRCam imaging. Depending on its true mass, it can produce arbitrarily high magnifications of BulletArc-z11, depending on the position of the critical curve. In the absence of any multiple images which would constrain its mass individually, we derived magnifications with the cluster member excluded, and note that our results conservatively represent lower limits on magnification, and hence upper limits on derived sizes and fluxes. The BulletArc-z11 magnification, derived from this model, is
\mu=14.0^{+6.2}_{-0.3}
, with the lowest limit of
\mu>10.4
, derived from the Bayesian samples of the lens model. We also note that magnification values across the arc do not vary significantly (less than
\sim 10\%
, which is within the error bars).

IV
Results

We performed the SED fitting to the photometry (spectrum S/N is too low for a more detailed analysis) using the
DENSE BASIS

method
(
Iyer & Gawiser 2017
;
Iyer et al. 2019
)
to determine
nonparametric star formation histories (SFHs), masses, and ages for our
sources in BulletArc-z11. We adopt the
Calzetti attenuation law
(
Calzetti 2001
)
, flat dust prior and a Chabrier IMF
(
Chabrier 2003
)
. We fix the redshift to the spectroscopic redshift, allowing it to vary within the uncertainties given in Table
1
(we also generate the atlas using these values). All other parameters are left free. The primary advantage of using
DENSE BASIS
is that it allows us
to account for flexible star formation histories.

BulletArc-z11 has intrinsic (corrected for magnification) total stellar mass of
1.6^{+0.6}_{-0.9}\times 10^{8}M_{\odot}
and star formation rate (SFR) of
0.29^{+0.42}_{-0.28}M_{\odot}\mbox{yr}^{-1}
(Fig.
4
). The galaxy formed 50% of its total mass by
t_{50}=140^{+200}_{-90}\mbox{Myr}
. At face value, this is relatively old, given that the age of the universe is just 400 Myrs old at this redshift, meaning that BulletArc-z11 might have formed 50% of its stellar mass by redshift
z\sim 14
. This is similar mass to the
z=14.4
galaxy with a stellar mass of
1.2^{+1.3}_{-0.4}\times 10^{8}M_{\odot}

(
Naidu et al. 2025
)
. However, also note that the uncertainties are large. The measurements are also prone to systematics, given that we only have access to rest-frame UV colours of this object.

The SED fitting results for the individual components are also listed in Table
1
and shown in Fig.
4
. Note that the galaxy component as determined by
Galfit
includes the total light (beyond the aperture of the detected source). Hence, the stellar mass of all components is larger than what we measure as the total stellar mass. The clumps themselves contain
\sim 50\%
of the measured stellar mass of the system. All components show similar star formation histories (Fig.
5
).

(
t_{50}\sim 150{\rm Myr}
). Note, however, that the results indicate a large range of possible SFHs (as indicated by the errors given as the shaded regions).

Using the SW F200W band image (with highest S/N and smallest PSF), we also measure sizes of each of the components. We model the source in the source plane using
Lenstruction
with a combination of an underlying galaxy component with a round Sersic profile and a fixed Sersic index of 1. Reported size is the half-light radius. The other three components are barely resolved (clump 2) or unresolved (clumps 1 and 3) and are considered to be a Gaussian (which produces a better fit than Sersic profiles). Reported sizes are the
\sigma
of the Gaussian profile. All the sizes are listed in Table
1
.

The clumps have very small sizes that are similar to those reported in other high-z gravitationally lensed sources, e.g.,
Mowla et al. 2024
;
Adamo et al. 2024
. The stellar surface mass density of clumps 1 and 3 is
>10^{5}M_{\odot}/\mbox{pc}^{2}
, which is three orders of magnitude higher than typical young star clusters in the local universe. Clump 2 also has a high stellar surface mass density of
\sim 2\times 10^{4}M_{\odot}/\mbox{pc}^{2}
. Given stellar mass and proximity to the main galaxy’s centre of clump 1 (Table
1
), clump 1 could also be an AGN, although we do not see any other signatures.

V
Conclusions

We report a new discovery of a
z=11.10^{+0.11}_{-0.26}
, highly magnified gravitationally lensed galaxy behind the Bullet cluster. The NIRSpec spectrum of BulletArc-z11 shows a distinct damping wing, indicating the presence of a neutral IGM, typical for a galaxy deep in the reionization era. This is the lowest luminosity (
0.086^{+0.008}_{-0.030}\,L^{*}
)
z>10
galaxy spectroscopically confirmed to date. Its high magnification (
\mu=14.0^{+6.2}_{-0.3}
) not only allows for its detection, but it also gives an excellent opportunity to study star formation at ten parsec scales at high redshift.

The galaxy consists of the main component and three barely/unresolved clumps. The clumps show a very high stellar surface mass density (
>10^{5}M_{\odot}/\mbox{pc}^{2}
for clumps 1 and 3 and
\sim 2\times 10^{4}M_{\odot}/\mbox{pc}^{2}
for clump 2). This is similar to other studies of highly magnified objects, where we see star formation broken down into smaller clumps when gravitational lensing allows us to observe at high enough resolutions.

The central brightest clumps could either indicate the presence of an AGN or a very compact star cluster. We would require deeper/higher resolution NIRSpec spectroscopy to establish the presence of possible broad and high ionisation lines to test this hypothesis.
