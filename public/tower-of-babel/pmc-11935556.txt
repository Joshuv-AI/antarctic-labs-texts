Long‐Term Statin Use Is Associated With Reduced Rates of Adverse Events in Patients With Newly Diagnosed Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Ting‐Chun Huang, Li‐Hao Yap, Chao‐Yu Chen, Hui‐Wen Lin, Sheng‐Hsiang Lin, Yi‐Heng Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effectiveness of statin use in preventing adverse cardiovascular events in individuals with atrial fibrillation (AF) has remained uncertain. This study aimed to assess whether statin use could lead to better outcomes among individuals with AF.Methods and ResultsWe enrolled 397 787 patients with AF from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2020. Patients with AF were divided into 2 groups (statin user and statin nonuser), and the risks of composite outcomes (including ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and transient ischemic attack), all‐cause death, and major adverse cardiovascular events (which encompassed cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure hospitalization) were analyzed. We analyzed 288 958 patients with newly diagnosed AF (mean age, 73 years; 44% women; mean CHA2DS2‐VASc score, 3.5). Compared with patients without statin use, statin users had lower risks of composite end points (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 0.91 [95% CI, 0.87–0.94]; P<0.01). In regard to all‐cause death, statin users exhibited a 67% risk reduction compared with statin nonusers (adjusted HR, 0.33 [95% CI, 0.32–0.33]; P<0.01). Statin use was also associated with reduced incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (adjusted HR, 0.64 [95% CI, 0.63–0.66]; P<0.01). In the subgroups stratified by CHA2DS2‐VASc score, statin therapy was particularly effective for patients with CHA2DS2‐VASc scores 0 to 3 for composite end points but consistently reduced all‐cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events across all score categories.ConclusionsAmong patients with newly diagnosed AF, statin use was associated with lower risk of ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack, all‐cause mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent clinically significant cardiac arrhythmia, contributing to a greater risk of cardiovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke. 1 Clinical guidelines emphasize the importance of anticoagulation therapy using warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants for patients with an elevated stroke risk, as evaluated using the CHA2DS2‐VASc score. 2 However, anticoagulation therapy does not eliminate the risk of stroke entirely. Enhanced prevention strategies for AF and its associated complications continue to be a global public health priority.

Dyslipidemia is a well‐known risk factor for cardiovascular disease. 3 , 4 Lowering low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) through the use of statins significantly reduces the risk of adverse cardiovascular events in the general population. 5 , 6 However, the association between dyslipidemia and AF is not well‐defined. Given that cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity are established risk factors for AF, 7 it appears plausible that dyslipidemia should also be a risk factor. However, previous studies have shown inconsistent results, and some research even suggested that elevated levels of LDL‐C are linked to a reduced likelihood of AF. 8 The clinical significance and pathophysiological mechanisms of this paradoxical inverse association between lipid levels and AF risk remain unclear. 9

Despite the current inconsistent results of statin use in the prevention of AF occurrence or recurrence, 10 the data about the usage of statins specifically for stroke risk reduction in patients with AF are limited. 10 Another concern associated with statin use is the potential to increase the risk of hemorrhagic strokes by aggressively lowering lipid levels. 11 This emphasizes the necessity to investigate whether statin use can lead to improved outcomes in patients with AF.

In this nationwide cohort study, we sought to demonstrate the effect of statin therapy on the clinical outcomes, including the rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and mortality in patients with AF.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Database

We conducted a retrospective cohort study by using the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), provided by the Taiwan Health and Welfare Data Science Center. The National Health Insurance scheme is an obligatory, all‐encompassing health insurance initiative that provides extensive health care coverage to all Taiwan residents. NHIRD encompasses health care information from >23 million beneficiaries, encompassing >99% of Taiwan's total population. Within this cohort data set, the patients' initial identification numbers have undergone encryption to safeguard their privacy. However, the encryption process was applied consistently, enabling the connection of claims associated with the same patient within the National Health Insurance database and facilitating continuous tracking.

Study Population and Design

From January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2020, a total of 397 787 patients with newly diagnosed AF were enrolled. Patients <18 years of age and those who had past history of AF, hyperthyroidism, rheumatic mitral stenosis, prosthetic heart valve, missing data, or death before index date were excluded. The remaining 293 942 patients were divided into 2 groups based on whether a statin was used or not (statin user versus statin nonuser). Statin users were defined as those who received atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, or other statins for >27 consecutive days per month in the 6 months before the diagnosis of AF. Statin users with <27‐day statin refills per month were excluded. The remainder of 288 958 patients were analyzed and followed up until December 31, 2021. The diagnosis of AF was established using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM) code 427.31 and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes I48.0, I48.1, I48.2, and I48.9. To ensure the precision of diagnosis, individuals were categorized as patients with AF only when this was a discharge diagnosis or confirmed for ≥2 times in the outpatient department in 1 year. The diagnostic accuracy of AF using this definition in the NHIRD has been validated previously. 12 The flowchart of categorization of the study subjects is presented in Figure 1.

Definition of Clinical Score and End Points

The patients' baseline characteristics (including age and sex) were recorded on the index date of enrollment. To ensure the accurate diagnosis of the comorbidity, we defined patients with the comorbidity only when it was a discharge diagnosis or confirmed for ≥2 times in the outpatient department 1 year before the index date. Patients' medications used within 3 months after the index date were reviewed. Patients were also screened for any AF‐related intervention (ie, surgical or percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion and/or pulmonary vein isolation), done within 1 year after the index date. The CHA2DS2‐VASc score (congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 years, diabetes, prior stroke or transient ischemia attack [TIA], vascular disease, age 65–74 years, female sex) was calculated for each patient by assigning 1 point each for being between 65 and 74 years of age and having a history of hypertension, diabetes, recent cardiac failure, vascular disease (myocardial infarction or peripheral artery disease), and female sex, and 2 points each for having a history of stroke/TIA, or being ≥75 years of age. 13

The primary end point of this study was a composite of ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and TIA. The secondary end points included all‐cause death and MACEs. MACEs were defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and heart failure hospitalization in this study. Cardiovascular death was defined as death due to an event of acute myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death, heart failure, stroke, cardiovascular procedures, or other cardiovascular causes. 14 Notably, the study follow‐up ended once the primary end point was reached, so patients who experienced the primary event were no longer considered at risk for any further event. If patients were lost to follow‐up or died, the follow‐up was also terminated.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means±SDs, whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Because randomization was not possible in this retrospective cohort study, we conducted greedy matching among patients with newly diagnosed AF for the comparison between statin users versus statin nonusers, with a caliper of 0.25 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score. Propensity scores for the likelihood of receiving a statin compared with no statin therapy were calculated using multivariable logistic regression, taking into account age, sex, CHA2DS2‐VASc score, and comorbidities (including diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, prior ischemic stroke/TIA, prior hemorrhagic stroke, chronic kidney disease, end‐stage renal disease, and prior heart failure). We matched patients in the statin user group to those in the statin nonuser group with a 1:1 ratio on the basis of the closest propensity score. Distributions of the clinical characteristics between the study groups after propensity score matching were also evaluated using the absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD). ASMD was calculated as the difference in the means or proportions of a variable divided by the pooled estimate of the SD of that variable. An ASMD <0.1 indicates a negligible difference between the 2 comparison groups. For survival analysis, study populations were stratified into 3 groups based on CHA2DS2‐VASc scores (ranging from 0 to 3, 4 to 6, and 7 to 9). Kaplan‐Meier curves were generated to illustrate the cumulative incidence and the time to the primary end point (composite of ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and TIA) and secondary end points (all‐cause death and MACEs) in both the statin user and nonuser groups. The method of stratified log‐rank test for multiple comparison was used to compare the survival distributions of groups with different spectrums of CHA2DS2‐VASc scores. Using univariate Cox proportional hazards model, the crude (unadjusted) hazard ratio (HR) was provided to assess the risk of end points. In the multivariable Cox model, adjustment for potential confounding covariates with ASMD >0.1 was conducted. Before the Cox proportional hazards model was used, proportional hazards assumption for covariates was also tested, and the stratified Cox regression model was used to account for nonproportional hazards. A robust variance estimator was used to account for the clustering within matched sets. In Cox proportional hazards models, we also performed a time‐dependent analysis by using statin exposure time as a segmented time‐dependent covariate to avoid bias introduced by different drug treatment time. In addition to time‐dependent analysis, the Fine‐Gray model was performed in the presence of death as competing events. 15 Subgroup analysis was also conducted to assess whether the effects of statins on primary and secondary end points differ across different subgroups. The level of significance was established as a 2‐sided P value <0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 for Windows (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

This study was approved by the institutional review board at National Cheng Kung University Hospital, Tainan, Taiwan. The institutional review board agreed that the informed consent of subjects was not required, because this study was conducted using the Taiwan NHIRD, and the original identification numbers of the patients had been encrypted. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) cohort checklist when writing our report. 16

RESULTS

Demographic Characteristics of the AF Cohort

In a cohort consisting of 288 958 patients diagnosed with AF, the average CHA2DS2‐VASc score stood at 3.52, with an SD of ±1.86. Among this group, 161 412 patients (55.86%) were men, and their mean age was 73.11 years, with an SD of ±13.35. Moreover, 67 798 patients (23.46%) diligently followed a regimen of regular statin therapy, whereas 221 160 patients (76.54%) did not include statins in their treatment. Using 1:1 propensity score matching, a total of 134 812 patients were matched, with 67 406 patients from the statin user group and 67 406 from statin nonuser group, as displayed in Figure 1. The mean age, sex distribution, and mean CHA2DS2‐VASc score among statin users and statin nonusers were comparable, as indicated in Table 1. A mere 332 patients (0.11%) within the AF cohort underwent left atrial occluder implantation, and 7575 patients (2.62%) received pulmonary vein isolation procedures. Class III antiarrhythmics were the most commonly prescribed antiarrhythmic medications, accounting for 40.34% of usage, with 11.85% of patients opting for class I antiarrhythmics. In terms of other medications, approximately 52.16% of patients were on angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), whereas 56.14% were using β‐blockers. Antiplatelet agents were taken by 143 519 patients (49.67%), and anticoagulants were used by 127 347 patients, with 39 989 (13.84%) on warfarin and 87 358 (30.23%) on non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants. Within this AF cohort, 191 258 patients (66.19%) had hypertension, whereas 82 645 (28.6%) had diabetes. Furthermore, 29.34% of patients had a history of coronary artery disease, and 24.05% had a history of heart failure. The number of patients with a prior history of ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic stroke was 46 155 (15.97%) and 6061 (2.1%), respectively.

Risks of Adverse Events of Statin Users and Statin Nonusers

During the 3.54‐year follow‐up period, there were 5047 events observed among patients who consistently used statins, whereas 5010 events occurred in those who did not adhere to regular statin use, as presented in Table 2. The initial analysis showed that statin users exhibited a lower risk of composite end points (crude HR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.89–0.96]; P<0.01), as presented in Table 3. Upon adjusting for potential confounding covariates with ASMD >0.1, such as ACEI/ARB, non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant, and DPP4 (dipeptidyl peptidase 4) inhibitor use, statin users consistently exhibited a lower risk of composite end points (adjusted HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.87–0.94]; P<0.01). Remarkably, individuals who used statins exhibited a reduced risk not only of ischemic stroke (adjusted HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.87–0.95]; P<0.01) but also of hemorrhagic stroke (adjusted HR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.75–0.90]; P<0.01) compared with those who did not use statins. In terms of all‐cause mortality, statin users demonstrated a 23% reduction in risk compared with statin nonusers (adjusted HR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.76–0.79]; P<0.01). Additionally, a lower incidence of MACEs, including cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and heart failure admission, was observed in statin users (adjusted HR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.87–0.90]; P<0.01).

When taking into consideration the timing of statin usage during the follow‐up period and adjusting for covariates (as presented in Table 3), the time‐dependent analysis consistently demonstrated significant reductions in risk. Specifically, there was a 29% reduction in the risk of composite end points, a noteworthy 67% reduction in all‐cause mortality, and a 36% reduction in the incidence of MACEs. Furthermore, recognizing that all‐cause mortality can be a competing factor with composite end points and cardiovascular outcomes, we computed subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) after accounting for all‐cause mortality (as shown in Table 3). Among survivors within the AF cohort, those who used statins exhibited a decreased risk of experiencing composite end points (adjusted sHR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.72–0.78]; P<0.01) and MACEs (adjusted sHR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.65–0.68]; P<0.01).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this study represents the largest observational cohort of patients newly diagnosed with AF to investigate the preventive effects of statin use on cardiovascular events. Our primary findings can be summarized as follows: (1) Regular statin usage was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of stroke or TIA. (2) Statin users consistently exhibited lower risks of all‐cause mortality and MACEs when compared with those who did not use statins, even after adjusting for key variables. Additionally, a time‐dependent analysis was conducted to account for the timing of statin use, which consistently yielded results indicating a significant reduction in risk.

Dyslipidemia is a modifiable cardiovascular risk factor that is often underappreciated in patients with AF. 17 Lowering LDL‐C levels through the use of statins significantly decreases the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, such as stroke, in the general population. 18 , 19 , 20 Despite this, only a limited number of studies have investigated the correlation between statin use and cardiovascular event rates particularly in individuals with AF. A similar previous study conducted on a prospective cohort (START registry) clearly demonstrated that statin use can help reduce the residual mortality risk in patients with AF. 21 However, our study did show several novelties compared with the previous study. In the Survey on Anticoagulated Patients Register (START), the study population was relatively small (5769 patients) and older (mean age of 80.2 years), with the study's end point focused on all‐cause mortality. In comparison, our study included a much larger population (288 958 patients) with younger patients (mean age of 73 years). In addition to all‐cause mortality, we also focused on the risk of strokes and MACEs, which are important prognostic factors in patients with AF. Furthermore, we did subgroup analysis and demonstrated that statin treatment consistently reduced the risk of composite end points across nearly all subgroups. Another retrospective multicenter study comprised 2309 patients in Poland demonstrated that statin therapy before ischemic stroke was associated with a reduced risk of mortality and improved functional outcome in patients with AF‐related stroke. 22 The North Dublin Population Stroke Study, which included patients with AF, demonstrated that poststroke statin therapy was associated with a reduction in mortality. 23 Eun et al conducted a meta‐analysis, which showed that statins had possible benefits of reducing all‐cause death and improving functional outcomes in patient with AF‐related stroke irrespective of the time of initiation of statin administration. 24 In a cohort study comprising 13 440 patients with heart failure, a substantial portion of whom also had AF, statin therapy contributed to reduced mortality and hospitalization in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. 25 A systematic review performed by Pastori et al, using the data of 100 287 patients with AF, revealed that statin therapy was associated with reductions in all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality by 41% and 25%, respectively. However, this analysis did not demonstrate the association of statin use and lower rates of ischemic stroke or other MACEs. 26 A recent study by Shweikialrefaee et al conducted on a cohort in Ontario revealed that the use of statins was linked to a 17% reduction in the risk of stroke in the entire cohort. Even after adjusting for lipid data, a 13% decrease in the risk of stroke was observed. 27 It is important to highlight that our study consistently demonstrated the beneficial effects of statins even after adjusting for key variables and comorbidities, raising the possibility that statins may offer specific advantages for patients with newly diagnosed AF. In our perspective, the mechanisms by which statins lower the risks of stroke, mortality, and MACEs in this specific population are linked to their effects that extend beyond merely lowering LDL‐C levels. Upstream pathways involved in AF include inflammation, oxidative stress, and other mechanisms that increase susceptibility to or progression of AF. 28 , 29 , 30 Interestingly, statins have been shown to produce pleiotropic effects, such as anti‐inflammation, antioxidant, and antithrombotic properties. 31 We believe that these pleiotropic effects may play a significant role in reducing cardiovascular risk in this particular population with AF. Remarkably, our research revealed that initiating statin therapy early in patients newly diagnosed with AF not only showed promise in reducing the chances of ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and TIA, but also exhibited a substantial correlation with decreased rates of all‐cause mortality and MACEs.

Our study highlighted the protective effects of statin therapy against combined end points of strokes and TIA, which were statistically significant among patients with CHA2DS2‐VASc scores ranging from 0 to 3. However, this protective effect diminished in patients with scores of 4 to 6 and 7 to 9 on the CHA2DS2‐VASc scale. This observation reflects the fact that individuals with higher CHA2DS2‐VASc scores typically have a greater burden of comorbidities, leading to an elevated risk of cardiovascular complications even when using statins. These findings emphasize the importance of implementing multiple vascular risk factor modifications in patients with AF.

The EAST‐AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention) trial 32 and the Korea nationwide cohort study 33 both indicated that, regardless of cardiovascular risks, early rhythm control resulted in a reduced risk of cardiovascular death, stroke, hospitalization due to worsening heart failure, or acute coronary syndrome. In our subgroup analysis, our study consistently demonstrated the protective effects of statins, even in patients concurrently using oral antiarrhythmic medications or those undergoing pulmonary vein isolation procedures. Furthermore, our analysis showed that statin use remained protective even when used alongside oral anticoagulants. These findings align with the results from the J‐RHYTHM registry, which revealed that the addition of statins to warfarin therapy was associated with lower all‐cause mortality compared with warfarin use alone. 34

Only 52% of patients received an ACEI/ARB as upstream medical therapy in our study. Although several meta‐analyses reported lower incidences of new AF with the use of an ACEI/ARB, 35 , 36 larger randomized controlled trials failed to show the benefit of renin‐angiotensin system inhibition in reducing recurrent AF and subsequent cardiovascular events. 37 , 38 With limited or inconsistent evidences, there is currently no guideline or recommendation provided on the use of an ACEI/ARB as upstream therapy for AF. Therefore, relatively lower usage of an ACEI/ARB was observed in our cohort. However, a recently published study (the START registry) demonstrated the protective effect of using an ACEI/ARB to reduce the mortality rate in older patients with AF. 39 It is noteworthy that statin users were prescribed an ACEI/ARB more frequently than statin nonusers after propensity matching in our cohort. Thus, adjustment for ACEI/ARB use was done in a multivariable Cox model to address this potential bias. Importantly, the protective effect of statins persisted after adjustment in our study.

A possible issue with statin use in patients with AF is that previous research has indicated an elevated risk of hemorrhagic stroke with high‐dose statin use, particularly in those with a history of stroke. 18 , 40 However, in a study involving patients with AF who experienced a stroke, higher statin adherence was linked to a 39% reduction in the risk of recurrent stroke among patients treated with warfarin. 41 In our study, patients who used statins demonstrated a decreased risk not only of ischemic stroke but also of hemorrhagic stroke compared with those who did not use statins.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that the protective effects of statins vanished in patients with a prior history of stroke, particularly hemorrhagic stroke. Examining the overall stroke risk within our cohort, the occurrence of ischemic stroke was significantly higher compared with the occurrence of hemorrhagic stroke. This is anticipated, because stroke attributable to atrial fibrillation and cardioembolism is a major risk factor for the hemorrhagic transformation of an ischemic stroke. 42 Nevertheless, statin therapy continued to reduce the risk of all‐cause mortality and MACEs in this patient subset. This implies that statins provide a net clinical benefit, even in the presence of a potentially increased risk of strokes in patients with prior strokes. Therefore, it is imperative to conduct a thorough assessment of the potential benefits and risks before considering a statin prescription for individuals within this specific subgroup.

Limitations

First, this research is a retrospective cohort study and nonrandomized in nature. However, as far as we are aware, this is the largest cohort to investigate statin use among patients with AF, and the strength of our study was the use of a nationwide database, which enrolled a large sample of patients. Second, selection bias cannot be ruled out, where statins might be withheld in frail patients who could not tolerate a statin, and the benefit attributed to statins may be a result of the healthy user effect, meaning patients treated with statins were heathier. 43 To adjust for potential bias of the healthy user effect, we performed propensity score matching for patients' characteristics and comorbidities as variables in the analysis. Third, there was only ≈43% of the total cohort treated with an oral anticoagulant. The underuse of oral anticoagulants remains a global concern, which is particularly prevalent in Asia. 44 , 45 In addition, more patients in the statin‐user group received non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants, which have superior efficacy and safety as compared with warfarin in the management of AF. 46 , 47 Our results showed a prescription rate of oral anticoagulation comparable with previous read‐world data. To overcome these limitations, adjustment for non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant use was performed in a multivariable Cox model. Our study exhibited the beneficial effects of statins even after adjustment. Fourth, we did not have available data of smoking status and lipid levels in patients with AF due to limitations in the NHIRD data set; thus, we could not examine the association between smoking, degree of LDL‐C level lowering, and stroke rates. Another issue is that the protective effect of statins diminished in patients with a prior history of stroke, particularly hemorrhagic stroke. It should be noted that this assertion is drawn from subgroup analysis, which has a statistical limitation of underpower and multiplicity, which may lead to a spurious association.

CONCLUSIONS

Regular statin use reduces the risk of stroke, mortality, and MACEs in patients with newly diagnosed AF. Additional randomized trials are necessary for further confirmation.
