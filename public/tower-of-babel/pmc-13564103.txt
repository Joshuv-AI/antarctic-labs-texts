Response Shift and Item Interpretation: Two Caveats for Using Minimal Symptom Expression as a Treatment Goal in Generalised Myasthenia Gravis
Authors: Hai‐Feng Li, Qi Li, Yu‐Zhou Guan, Huan Yang
Published: 2026

We welcome the proposal of Minimal Symptom Expression (MSE, MG‐ADL 0‐1) as a patient‐centric treatment goal in generalised myasthenia gravis (gMG) [1]. However, its validity as a surrogate for minimal disease activity requires closer scrutiny, particularly regarding how disease duration and patient comprehension affect self‐reports.

A key mechanism is response shift, the recalibration of internal standards due to long‐term adaptation [2]. Patients with longstanding MG may perceive mild ptosis as ‘normal’, reporting lower MG‐ADL scores than those with recent onset despite comparable objective weakness measured by the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis Score (QMG). This can systematically underestimate residual impairment when using patient‐reported measures alone.

The MGTX study found a weaker correlation between QMG and MG‐ADL in patients with minimal manifestation status [3]. We further explored this issue in a prospective cohort of 134 patients who had achieved minimal manifestation status or better. The correlation between QMG and MG‐ADL was moderate (Spearman's ρ = 0.450, p = 0.03). When we dichotomized MG‐ADL according to the MSE definition (0–1 vs. ≥ 2) and dichotomized QMG by its sample median, the kappa coefficient for agreement between the two binary classifications was only 0.296 (p < 0.01), indicating only fair agreement beyond chance. Notably, disease duration > 12 months was independently associated with discordance between the two binary classifications (OR = 3.93; 95% CI: 1.09–14.19, p = 0.04). Longer disease duration drives patient–clinician disagreement, most likely through response shift.

Beyond response shift, a more fundamental issue exists. The MG‐ADL assumes that all respondents interpret its wording uniformly. Even though acceptable concordance between patient‐ and physician‐rated MG‐ADL has been reported [4], this does not guarantee uniform understanding among patients. Terms such as ‘intermittent’, ‘extra effort’, and ‘mild’ lack objective anchors; different patients may apply them based on their own experience and coping strategies. Moreover, the available detailed item descriptions provided in the instructions are often not read by patients in routine practice or even in trial settings. Instead, patients tend to answer based on their intuitive understanding of the wording, introducing additional variability in responses. Brief instruction or interview‐based assessment may reduce this bias, but qualitative evidence on how patients actually arrive at their answers, whether they read the descriptions, how they interpret key terms, and which internal shortcuts they rely on remains lacking [5].

We therefore suggest: (1) Qualitative studies should explore how patients interpret MG‐ADL items to establish content validity and identify sources of misinterpretation. (2) Disease duration should be incorporated as a contextual factor when validating MSE as a treatment goal, given its independent association with discordance. (3) Achieving MSE should prompt brief clinician‐administered assessment to verify that low symptom scores reflect true resolution of impairment rather than adaptive recalibration.

Our critique does not diminish the value of MSE but calls for its refinement in both clinical trials and practice.
