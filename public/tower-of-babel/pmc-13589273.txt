Single- vs Multiple-Dose Antibiotic Prophylaxis for Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Armita Armina Abedi, Marie Anneberg, Alma Becic Pedersen, Claus Varnum, Robin Christensen, Søren Overgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs a single preoperative dose of prophylactic antibiotics noninferior to multiple doses in preventing periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) after total hip arthroplasty (THA)?FindingsIn this comparative effectiveness research study including 76 126 patients who underwent primary THA for osteoarthritis, PJI within 90 days occurred in 0.98% of patients receiving a single dose and 0.94% receiving multiple doses, meeting the prespecified noninferiority margin.MeaningThese findings suggest that single-dose antibiotic prophylaxis has effectiveness comparable to multiple-dose regimens for preventing PJI after THA and may support efforts to reduce antibiotic overuse.

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) after primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a rare but serious complication, associated with repeated surgeries, impaired function, increased mortality, and substantial health care costs.1,2,3 Although perioperative systemic antibiotic prophylaxis is standard practice to reduce the risk of PJI, the optimal number of doses in primary THA remains a subject of ongoing debate.4 Despite standard use, clinical practice varies considerably, with some centers administering only a single preoperative dose and others providing additional postoperative doses within the first 24 hours. This variation is mirrored in international guidelines, which offer divergent recommendations, reflecting the limited and inconsistent evidence base.5,6,7,8,9,10 Although randomized clinical trials and meta-analyses suggest no benefit beyond a single preoperative dose, most studies were decades old, underpowered, or based on heterogeneous populations and PJI definitions, limiting their relevance to contemporary patients with comorbidities, such as obesity and diabetes.11,12,13,14

Large registry studies have provided additional insights, but their value is constrained by incomplete infection data, lack of microbiology data, and potential misclassification of diagnostic codes.15,16 Residual confounding from patient characteristics and antibiotic selection further complicates interpretation. High-quality contemporary evidence is therefore lacking.

Ensuring patient safety through effective infection prevention must be balanced against antibiotic stewardship because antibiotic overuse increases resistance, adverse events, and public health risks.17,18,19,20 By linking data from the Danish Hip Arthroplasty Register and the Danish Microbiology Database, we applied a validated algorithm for PJI capture that markedly improves diagnostic accuracy compared with registry data alone.21,22 Leveraging this unique nationwide data infrastructure, we emulated a target trial to address the evidence gap regarding the number of prophylactic antibiotic doses. The primary aim was to assess whether a single preoperative dose of prophylactic antibiotic was noninferior to multiple doses in reducing the risk of PJI within 90 days after primary THA for osteoarthritis.

Methods

Study Design

In this comparative effectiveness research study, we emulated a nationwide target trial using Danish registry data, following a prespecified protocol.23 The target trial specification and its emulation are detailed in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. Reporting followed TARGET Statement recommendations where applicable.24 This study also followed the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) reporting guideline. This study was conducted in accordance with Danish legislation, registered in accordance with Danish data protection regulations, and did not require approval from an institutional review board or research ethics committee or informed consent from participants because it used deidentified registry-based data.

Data Sources

All data sources have been previously validated, with high completeness and accuracy for arthroplasty, prescription, and hospitalization data. We linked nationwide Danish health registries using the unique personal identification number assigned to all residents at birth or on immigration.25,26 Data on arthroplasty procedures and antibiotic prophylaxis were obtained from the Danish Hip Arthroplasty Register21,27,28; comorbidities and hospital admissions from the Danish National Patient Registry25; microbiology data from the Hospital Acquired Infections Database, an automated system for the surveillance of hospital acquired infection29,30; community antibiotic use from the Danish National Prescription Registry31; socioeconomic variables from Statistics Denmark32,33; and vital status from the Civil Registration System.26

Study Population

We included all patients who underwent primary THA for osteoarthritis in Denmark between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2020. Patients with fracture-related indications, primary tumors, or metastases were excluded. Additional exclusions were age younger than 18 years, death or emigration before surgery, and missing data on antibiotic prophylaxis or key surgical variables. If both hips were operated on during the study period, only the first procedure was included. We classified antibiotic prophylaxis at surgery as either a single preoperative dose or a multiple-dose regimen, defined as 1 or more doses administered within 24 hours postoperatively.

Clinical and Sociodemographic Variables

The propensity score model included age, sex, calendar year, educational level, wealth, living situation (cohabiting or living alone), comorbidity status, primary diagnosis (primary osteoarthritis or secondary reasons for osteoarthritis, including acetabular dysplasia, atraumatic avascular necrosis of the femoral head, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, other forms of arthritis, congenital hip dislocation, Perthes disease, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, or traumatic hip dislocation), previous hip surgery, fixation type, cement type, and duration of surgery. For the 2017 to 2020 cohort, we also included body mass index (BMI; calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) and morbid obesity (full model specification is shown in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Baseline covariates included socioeconomic variables (educational level, wealth, and living situation) and comorbidity burden, derived from national registries; detailed variable definitions are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was PJI within 90 days of primary THA, in line with National Healthcare Safety Network recommendations.34 Revision was defined as any new surgical procedure involving debridement with partial or complete removal or exchange of prosthetic components. PJI was defined as 2 or more intraoperative deep-tissue cultures with phenotypically indistinguishable bacteria from at least 3 deep-tissue samples35 or surgeon-reported deep infection as the indication for revision in the Danish Hip Arthroplasty Register.21 This definition is based on a modified version of the European Bone and Joint Infection Society criteria35 and an international consensus of the most widely accepted definition of PJI.6 The definition aligns with an algorithm developed to capture cases of PJI using national databases and has been previously validated.22

Secondary outcomes captured within 90 days after primary THA included PJI likely, which was defined as 1 intraoperatively obtained positive culture result (from aspiration fluid or tissue biopsy) from a first-time revision, identified through Hospital Acquired Infections Database data. This definition aligns with a modified European Bone and Joint Infection Society criteria35 and a prior study,36 in which first-time revisions with a single positive culture were associated with an increased risk of a second revision for PJI. Other secondary outcomes included any revision for any cause, identified from the Danish Hip Arthroplasty Register; hospital-treated infections (not PJI or PJI likely), defined as first-time hospital admissions with an infection diagnosis after discharge, identified in The Danish National Patient Registry; community-based antibiotic use, defined as at least 1 redeemed antibiotic prescription identified in The Danish National Prescription Registry; all-cause mortality, identified through the Civil Registration System; and revision for PJI within 365 days, as defined for the primary outcome.

Follow-Up

Follow-up began on the date of surgery (time zero) and extended for a fixed 90-day period for all outcomes and extended to 365 days for PJI. Patients who died or emigrated within 90 days without prior infection remained in the risk set as non-PJI cases.

Assumptions for Causal Contrasts

The causal contrast corresponded to the observational analog of the intention-to-treat effect, defined as assignment to single- vs multiple-dose prophylaxis at the date of surgery (time zero), regardless of adherence. Estimation assumed conditional exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding) given measured baseline covariates, positivity (each patient had a nonzero probability of receiving either regimen), and consistency (the recorded regimen reflected the intended treatment). We approximated exchangeability by including all covariates identified a priori from a directed acyclic graph as potential confounders (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1) and evaluated positivity by assessing overlap in weighted propensity score distributions. We further assumed a stable baseline infection risk across treatment groups for the noninferiority estimation.

Statistical Analysis

Propensity Score Estimation and Weighting

We used stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to balance baseline confounders between treatment groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, including year of surgery (continuous, per calendar year), age (continuous, in years), sex (male or female), comorbidity status based on the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (<0, 0, 1-4, ≥5), educational level (low, medium, high, or unknown), wealth tertile (to account for annual variation, wealth was based on the 5-year average family income for patients aged younger than 65 years and family liquid assets for those aged 65 years and older; wealth was categorized into age-specific tertiles as low [lowest tertile], medium [middle tertile], or high [highest tertile], or unknown), primary diagnosis (primary or secondary osteoarthritis, or unknown), primary diagnosis (primary or secondary osteoarthritis, or unknown), previous hip surgery for secondary hip osteoarthritis (eg, osteotomies for hip dysplasia; yes or no), fixation type (cemented, uncemented, or hybrid), cement type (antibiotic-loaded or plain), and duration of surgery (continuous, in minutes).

Hospitals without within-hospital variation in prophylactic regimen (ie, no single vs multiple dosing) and procedures with durations less than 25 or greater than 180 minutes were excluded. We calculated stabilized weights using the marginal probability of treatment assignment and truncated at 5 to reduce the influence of extreme values. Covariate balance and overlap after weighting were assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) and diagnostic plots (eFigures 1 and 3 in Supplement 1). We did not include hospital variable in the propensity score model; clustering by hospital was instead accounted for using cluster-robust SEs in outcome analyses (eFigures 1 and 3 in Supplement 1). Data were analyzed from July 1, 2025, to October 15, 2025.

Outcome Analysis

We estimated the odds ratio (OR) and absolute risk difference (ARD) using stabilized IPTW with hospital-clustered robust SE. Noninferiority was prespecified as an upper 95% CI for the OR less than 1.40. We calculated ORs with 95% CIs using logistic regression and estimated absolute risk differences using average marginal effects. The noninferiority margin was defined a priori based on clinical consensus from the Pro-Hip-Quality Trial.16 Because OR margins depend on baseline risk, we also report ARDs from marginal models for clinical interpretability. All statistical tests were 2 sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Systematic missingness in socioeconomic registers was handled by coding missing values as unknown; educational level was missing for 6.6% and 4.9%, and wealth for 0.2% in each group. For other covariates (eg, cement type, fixation type, comorbidity index), missing values were excluded from the analysis due to their low frequency or lack of interpretability as distinct categories (Figure 1).

Given the very low 90-day mortality, deaths were not treated as a competing risk in the primary analysis. To evaluate the influence of incomplete follow-up, we performed sensitivity analyses in which patients who died within 90 days without a recorded PJI were assigned outcomes under alternative assumptions (worst-case and best-case scenarios). Patients who emigrated within 90 days were excluded from this analysis due to data restrictions (<5 events).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

Results were examined using 1:5 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching with a caliper of 0.2 SDs of the propensity score logit (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). Matched analyses used logistic regression with cluster-robust SEs accounting for matched sets and hospitals. Sensitivity analyses also assessed the implications of incomplete follow-up and alternative analytical approaches. Patients who died within 90 days of surgery without a recorded PJI were assigned outcomes under best- and worst-case assumptions.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted for sex, age, diabetes, BMI, comorbidity burden, socioeconomic factors, antibiotic type, and fixation type. Subgroups with sparse events (<5 events in some groups) were excluded from regression analyses. No multiplicity correction was applied, as subgroup analyses were not powered as formal hypothesis tests and should be interpreted as hypothesis generating. BMI analyses were restricted to the 2017 to 2020 subcohort due to data availability. All analyses were conducted in R, version 4.5.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). The Elixhauser Comorbidity Index was computed using the comorbidity package.

Results

Patient Selection and Baseline Characteristics

From 93 368 primary THA procedures recorded in the Danish Hip Arthroplasty Register, 79 477 patients with osteoarthritis and complete information on surgery side and antibiotic practice were eligible for inclusion (Figure 1). After excluding 1268 patients who received prophylaxis beyond 24 hours and 2083 patients with missing covariate data or implausible surgical characteristics, the final cohort consisted of 76 126 patients (mean (SD) age, 68.8 years (11.0); 42 559 women (55.9%). Of these, 6409 received a single-dose regimen, and 69 717 received a multiple-dose regimen. Stabilized IPTW was then applied to this cohort, yielding a weighted pseudopopulation (single dose: 6660 weighted; multiple dose: 69 705 weighted) with balanced baseline covariates, corresponding to an effective sample size of approximately 92% of the original cohort.

Confounders were well balanced after applying standardized IPTW, with SMDs less than 0.10 for all variables included in the propensity score model except for year of surgery (SMD, 0.128) (Table 1). Additional baseline characteristics not included in the propensity score model, such as antibiotic type and diabetes status, are presented for descriptive purposes in eTable 2 in Supplement 1.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

PJI within 90 days occurred in 63 patients (0.98%) in the single-dose group and 656 patients (0.94%) in the multiple-dose group. Weighted analyses yielded an OR of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.73-1.29) meeting the prespecified noninferiority criterion. Results were consistent for PJI within 365 days and across secondary outcomes. Based on weighted standardized IPTW, the OR for PJI within 90 days was 0.97 (95% CI, 0.73-1.29), with an ARD of −0.03% (95% CI, −0.28% to 0.23%) (Table 2). To aid interpretation across settings with different baseline PJI risks, eTable 3 in Supplement 1 translates the noninferiority margin (OR 1.40) into ARDs per 1000 procedures.

Secondary Outcomes

No statistically significant differences were observed between the single- and multiple-dose groups across secondary outcomes. ORs were 1.53 (95% CI, 0.69-3.39) for PJI likely, 1.07 (95% CI, 0.85-1.44) for any revision, 0.92 (95% CI, 0.48-1.90) for hospital-treated infections, 0.68 (95% CI, 0.45-1.02) for community-based antibiotic use, and 1.00 (95% CI, 0.49-2.05) for all-cause mortality. At 365 days, revision for PJI showed similar results (OR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.74-1.30]; ARD, −0.02% [95% CI, −0.34% to 0.30%]).

Sensitivity Analyses

Results for PJI at 90 and 365 days were consistent across unadjusted, weighted, and propensity score–matched analyses (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Associations observed in crude analyses were attenuated after adjustment (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.81 to 1.36]; ARD, 0.04% [95% CI, −0.20% to 0.28%]). Under extreme assumptions regarding incomplete follow-up, estimates varied substantially (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Ninety-day mortality was low (<0.4%) in both groups. In addition, doubly robust sensitivity analyses adjusted for calendar time using natural splines and additional covariates in the weighted outcome model yielded consistent results (eTable 6 in Supplement 1), confirming robustness to residual year imbalance. A sensitivity analysis that included hospital as a fixed effect in the weighted outcome model yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (eTable 6 in Supplement 1).

In analyses restricted to 2017 to 2020, when BMI data were available, results for PJI at both 90 days (OR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.45-1.80]) and 365 days (OR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.45-1.86]) remained consistent with the primary analysis, although estimates were less precise (eTable7 in Supplement 1). Divergent associations for secondary outcomes were observed, including higher odds of PJI likely (OR, 3.22 [95% CI, 1.44-7.21]) and revision (OR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.04-1.94]), but lower community antibiotic use (OR, 0.41 [95% CI, 0.24-0.75]) and mortality (OR, 0.30 [95% CI, 0.14-0.64]) in the single-dose group.

Subgroup Analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested lower odds of PJI with single-dose prophylaxis among males and among patients receiving noncephalosporin antibiotics (Figure 2). These findings were based on few events and wide CIs and should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

In this comparative effectiveness research study with a target trial emulation design, a single preoperative antibiotic dose was noninferior to multiple-dose prophylaxis for preventing PJI after THA. We observed no clinically meaningful differences between regimens for the primary outcome or for secondary outcomes, including revision surgery, other infections, antibiotic use, mortality, or PJI within 365 days. These findings indicate that extended postoperative prophylaxis offers no additional benefit over a single-dose strategy in primary THA. Sensitivity analyses highlight that the noninferiority conclusion depends on assumptions about incomplete follow-up and may not hold under extreme asymmetric scenarios. Results in the BMI-restricted subcohort were less precise and should be interpreted cautiously. Divergent findings for PJI likely, revision, and mortality are most plausibly attributable to chance given sparse event counts, while consistently lower community antibiotic use across both cohorts may reflect residual confounding by indication.

Our findings align with those of prior meta-analyses of randomized trials, cohort studies, and registry analyses, which show no additional benefit of multiple postoperative doses compared with a single preoperative dose.10,11,12,13,14 In contrast to much of the existing literature, which has been constrained by small cohorts and residual confounding, our study applied causal inference methods to improve comparability between treatment groups.10,11,12,13,14 Studies have also highlighted patient-level risk factors, such as BMI and diabetes, as risk factors for infection. In our study, comorbidity burden was adjusted for in the main analysis, and a BMI-inclusive sensitivity analysis confirmed consistency for the primary outcome. In Denmark, prophylaxis is typically based on second-generation cephalosporins (eg, cefuroxime) or penicillinase-resistant penicillins, both of which have shorter half-lives than cefazolin, the first-generation cephalosporin associated with stronger protective benefits in the subgroup analysis of a large Norwegian registry study.15 Together, these findings provide routine clinical practice evidence to support the use of a single preoperative dose as adequate prophylaxis in primary THA.

Strengths and Limitations

Study strengths include the target trial design with prespecified estimand, nationwide high-quality registry data, a validated PJI algorithm combining microbiology and surgeon-reported data, and consistent findings across sensitivity analyses. There are also several limitations. Although the use of causal inference methods improves comparability across treatment groups, confounding by indication cannot be fully ruled out. The choice of prophylactic regimen may have been affected by departmental guidelines and individual surgeon judgment, based on perceived patient risk and procedure-related factors. Although measured risk factors, such as BMI and diabetes, were similar between groups, unmeasured confounders, including frailty or other markers of physiological vulnerability, may still have played a role in treatment assignment. As emphasized in methodological literature, target trial emulation can reduce, but does not eliminate, bias arising from confounding by indication and unmeasured confounding, both of which may remain in observational analyses. Exposure classification was based on the assigned regimen at surgery, and heterogeneity within the multiple-dose category, as well as postoperative deviations, may have attenuated contrasts between groups. If a substantial proportion of the multiple-dose group received only 1 postoperative dose, the pharmacological contrast between regimens would be reduced, potentially biasing estimates toward the null and obscuring a true benefit of multiple-dose prophylaxis. The magnitude of this bias cannot be quantified from available data. Furthermore, information on the exact timing and route of antibiotic administration was unavailable.

Interpretation of noninferiority requires caution. Noninferiority was prespecified as an upper 95% CI bound for the OR less than 1.40, consistent with the simulation framework applied in the Pro-Hip-Quality trial,16 and was met in the primary analysis. However, when expressed as ARDs, the upper confidence bound corresponded to 2.8 per 1000 THA procedures; the absolute margin varies with baseline risk as shown in eTable 3 in Supplement 1. Given variation in baseline PJI risk across settings,23 these findings support noninferiority on the OR scale, while acknowledging that modest absolute risk increases remain compatible with the data.

Conclusions

In this nationwide comparative effectiveness research study with a target trial emulation design, a single preoperative antibiotic dose was noninferior to multiple doses for preventing PJI after THA. These results support efforts to minimize perioperative antibiotic exposure, although confirmation of the present study’s findings in randomized trials is warranted.
