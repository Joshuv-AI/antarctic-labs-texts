Marching to a new beat: Choosing the right pacing strategy in transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis
Authors: Nicholas Wettersten, Marcus A. Urey, Gordon Ho
Published: 2026

With the emergence of novel diagnostic tools and disease-modifying therapies, the prevalence of transthyretin (transthyretin amyloidosis [ATTR]) cardiac amyloidosis continues to rise.1 Concurrently, awareness has grown for coexisting cardiac and noncardiac conditions that require tailored management in this population. Among these, cardiac rhythm disturbances are particularly common and clinically challenging.2 Deposition of transthyretin within the myocardium predisposes patients to conduction disease, atrial arrhythmias, and ventricular arrhythmias, whereas the use of conventional therapies, such as β-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and digoxin, may be limited by intolerance and potential toxicities.2,3 In addition, higher pacing thresholds further complicate device-based management.4 Despite these complexities, optimal strategies for arrhythmia management, including cardiac pacing, remain poorly defined.

In this issue of Heart Rhythm O2, Dobner et al5 provide important insights into pacing strategies from a single-center cohort of 67 patients with ATTR cardiac amyloidosis. The authors highlighted a shift from single- and dual-chamber (VVI/DDD) pacing before ATTR diagnosis to cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and conduction system pacing (CSP) after diagnosis. They also demonstrated the feasibility of CSP in this population, despite potential technical challenges, and suggested that CSP may represent an attractive physiologic pacing strategy in the setting of progressive conduction disease. Although these observations are compelling, several aspects of the study warrant further consideration to appropriately contextualize the findings.

First, the rationale underlying the observed shift in pacing modality deserves closer examination. The authors attributed this transition to concerns regarding high right ventricular (RV) pacing burden and its potential contribution to heart failure progression. Indeed, most patients in the cohort ultimately exhibited >90% RV pacing. Current guidelines support physiologic pacing in patients with reduced ejection fraction who are expected to require substantial RV pacing, with weaker support in those with preserved ejection fraction.6 However, it is unclear what specific criteria were being used at the time of device implantation to select CRT/CSP vs VVI/DDD pacing in this cohort. Notably, despite the high RV pacing burden, the authors did not report a significant decline in ejection fraction, increased heart failure events, or worse clinical outcomes in patients receiving VVI/DDD pacing. These findings raise important questions regarding the clinical drivers of device selection and whether the observed shift toward CRT/CSP meaningfully altered outcomes.

Second, the role of CSP in ATTR cardiac amyloidosis remains incompletely defined. Although Dobner et al demonstrated the feasibility and short-term stability of CSP, these findings must be interpreted in the context of comparatively shorter follow-up in the CSP group. Progressive amyloid infiltration may affect the intrinsic conduction system and pacing thresholds. Although such deterioration was not observed for either CSP or CRT, the available follow-up period may be insufficient to capture these changes. Furthermore, it remains unknown whether progressive conduction disease may lead to loss of left bundle branch capture over time, limiting the efficacy of CSP.7 Finally, CSP was associated with a paradoxical increase in N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels, in contrast to stable levels with VVI/DDD pacing and reductions observed with CRT. The clinical significance of this biomarker signal remains uncertain, particularly given baseline differences between groups and the observational study design.

Finally, and most importantly, this study underscores a central unresolved question: Can the choice of pacing modality meaningfully influence clinical outcomes in ATTR cardiac amyloidosis? Although no significant differences in mortality or heart failure hospitalization were observed, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the lack of adjustment for confounding variables and differences in follow-up duration. In this population, multiple competing factors likely influence outcomes. On one hand, physiologic pacing strategies may be advantageous in a population characterized by high RV pacing burden and frequent use of atrioventricular node ablation for atrial fibrillation. On the other, patients with ATTR cardiac amyloidosis are typically older and frequently have substantial comorbidities, which may limit the ability to detect long-term benefits or harms attributable to pacing modality. Moreover, patient-centered outcomes such as quality of life remain unexplored. The evolving landscape of disease-modifying therapies, including transthyretin stabilizers, silencers, and emerging depleter therapies, further complicates interpretation, as improved survival may increase both exposure to and potential impact of pacing strategies over time. These clinically meaningful questions remain largely unanswered.

Taken together, the findings of Dobner et al5, along with substantial gaps in evidence, highlight the need for prospective randomized trials. Although caution is warranted in overinterpreting the potential benefits of CSP, its physiologic rationale remains compelling. A randomized trial comparing VVI/DDD pacing, CSP, and CRT in patients with ATTR cardiac amyloidosis and preserved ejection fraction receiving contemporary disease-modifying therapy would provide important guidance for clinical practice. Similarly, in patients with reduced ejection fraction, comparative evaluation of CSP and CRT may be informative, although the benefits of CRT in this population appear established on the basis of findings from this study and others.8 Ultimately, this study underscores a significant clinical dilemma: pacing needs are substantial in ATTR cardiac amyloidosis, but the optimal pacing strategy remains undefined.
