Analysis of a class of Recursive Distributional Equations Including the Resistance of the Series-parallel graph
Peter S. Morfe

Abstract
. This paper analyzes a class of recursive distributional equations (RDE’s) proposed by Gurel-Gurevich [ 17 ] and involving a bias parameter p p , which includes the logarithm of the resistance of the series-parallel graph. A discrete-time evolution equation resembling a nonlinear, fractional Fisher-KPP equation is derived to describe the CDF’s of solutions. When the bias parameter p = 1 2 p=\frac{1}{2} , this equation is shown to have a PDE scaling limit, from which distributional limit theorems for the RDE are derived. Applied to the series-parallel graph, the results imply that N − 1 / 3 log R ( N ) N^{-1/3}\log R^{(N)} has a nondegenerate limit when p = 1 2 p=\frac{1}{2} , as conjectured by Addario-Berry, Cairns, Devroye, Kerriou, and Mitchell [ 1 ] .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Resistance of the Series-Parallel Graph

This paper seeks to address an old problem, namely, the asymptotic analysis of the resistance of the series-parallel graph. This is the random hierarchical lattice introduced by Hambly and Jordan
[
18
]
and constructed as follows: Start with a graph
G^{(0)}
consisting of one edge connecting two nodes,
A
and
B
. At step
n
of the construction, obtain
G^{(n)}
from
G^{(n-1)}
by replacing every edge by two edges, either in series or in parallel, the result being decided by independent coin flips. Interpreting
G^{(n)}
as a resistor network where each edge has resistance one, one can study the effective resistance
R^{(n)}
between
A
and
B
.

The asymptotic analysis of
R^{(n)}
as
n\to\infty
was initiated in
[
18
]
. Since
G^{(n)}
can be realized by combining two independent copies
G^{(n-1)}_{1}
and
G^{(n-1)}_{2}
of
G^{(n-1)}
, say, in series with probability
p
and in parallel with probability
1-p
, the law of the resistance
R^{(n)}
is a solution of the recursive distributional equation (RDE)

\displaystyle R^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}R^{(n-1)}_{1}+R^{(n-1)}_{2},&\text{with probability}\,\,p,\\
\left(\frac{1}{R_{1}^{(n-1)}}+\frac{1}{R_{2}^{(n-1)}}\right)^{-1},&\text{with probability}\,\,1-p.\end{array}\right.

Using this fact, a number of results were proved in
[
18
]
: When
p>\frac{1}{2}
, the logarithm
\log R^{(n)}
grows linearly in
n
. More precisely, there is a constant
\lambda(p)>0
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{(2p-1)\log 2\leq\frac{1}{N}\log R^{(N)}\leq\lambda(p)\right\}\to 1\quad\text{as}\,\,N\to\infty.

Due to symmetry, this implies that
\log R^{(n)}\to-\infty
linearly when
p<\frac{1}{2}
.

At the critical point
p=\frac{1}{2}
, Hambly and Jordan instead proved that
\log R^{(n)}
exhibits sublinear growth. The main results of this paper (see Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
below) show that, in fact,
\log R^{(N)}
grows like
N^{\frac{1}{3}}
. Specifially,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(72\zeta(3))^{\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{1}{3}}}\log R^{(N)}+\frac{1}{2}\overset{d}{\to}\text{Beta}(2,2)\quad\text{as}\,\,N\to\infty,

where
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function. This confirms a conjecture of Addario-Berry, Cairns, Devroye, Kerriou, and Mitchell
[
1
]
.

The starting point for this work is an observation made by Gurel-Gurevich during a presentation at the open problem session at the Workshop for Disordered Media at the Rényi Institute in Budapest in January 2025
[
17
]
. He noted that the transformation
X^{(n)}=\log R^{(n)}
leads to an RDE of the form

(2)

\displaystyle X^{(n)}=\Phi(X^{(n-1)}_{1},X^{(n-1)}_{2}),

where
\Phi
is the random function defined by

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\max\{x,y\}+f(|y-x|),&\text{with probability}\,\,p,\\
\min\{x,y\}-f(|y-x|),&\text{with probability}\,\,1-p,\end{array}\right.

and
f(u)=\log(1+e^{-u})
. This led him to pose the following problem:

Problem
.

Consider the RDE (
2
) with forcing
\Phi
given by (
1.1
) under general assumptions on the nonincreasing function
f:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
. What can be said about the long-time behavior of the law of solutions?

This paper endeavors to address this problem in some generality when
p=\frac{1}{2}
, focusing on the distributional limit of
n^{-1/3}X^{(n)}
. The general framework developed here applies not only to the resistance of the critical series-parallel graph, but also to some other RDE’s considered recently in the literature. For instance, as is shown in Section
4
below, the class of RDE’s determined by (
2
) and (
1.1
) includes the symmetric hipster random walk from
[
1
]
and two-player symmetric cooperative motion, one of the RDE’s analyzed very recently by Addario-Berry, Beckman, and Lin in
[
3
]
.

While the analysis of the asymptotic behavior in the case
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
is not touched on here, the framework developed in this paper suggests that it is related to the singular limit of certain fractional, quasilinear variants of the Fisher-KPP equation. This is made precise in Section
1.3.4
below.

1.2.
General Setting

Consider the following general setting, which generalizes the forcing
\Phi
in (
2
). Given a Bernoulli random variable
\Theta
and random nonincreasing functions
f_{+},f_{-}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
, define
\Phi
by

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\max\{x,y\}+f_{+}(|x-y|),\quad\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
\min\{x,y\}-f_{-}(|x-y|),\quad\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

For simplicity, the pair
(f_{+},f_{-})
will be assumed to be independent of
\Theta
henceforth.

The object of interest in this paper is the RDE determining the law of random variables
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
through the recursion

(7)

\displaystyle X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}\Phi(X^{(n)}_{1},X^{(n)}_{2}),

where
X^{(n)}_{1},X^{(n)}_{2}
are i.i.d. copies of
X^{(n-1)}
, which are independent of
\Phi
. Throughout the paper, equality is only required to hold in law; questions of couplings or constructions of
\{X^{(n)}\}
will not be treated.

The form (
1.2
) of
\Phi
is not just a repackaging of (
1.1
). In the asymmetric setting where
f_{+}\neq f_{-}
, this class of RDE’s includes the one associated with Pemantle’s min-plus binary tree, the asymptotics of which were analyzed by Auffinger and Cable
[
5
]
, as well as certain versions of the asymmetric hipster random walk from
[
1
]
and two-player totally asymmetric,
q
-lazy cooperative motion, one of the RDE’s analyzed by Addario-Berry, Beckman, and Lin in
[
2
]
. In all of these examples, as well as the symmetric models from
[
1
,
3
]
already mentioned above, the asymptotic analysis carried out here generalizes the limit theorems previously obtained within a unified setting.

Henceforth define the bias parameter
p
by

p=\mathbb{P}\{\Theta=1\}.

The joint law of the functions
(f_{+},f_{-})
will be denoted by
\mathbf{P}
in what follows.
\mathbf{P}
is assumed to be a Borel probability measure on the space
BC([0,\infty))\times BC([0,\infty))
of pairs of bounded continuous functions in
[0,\infty)
with the uniform norm topology. In most of the examples of interest here, however,
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
are deterministic; see the discussion in Section
4
.

1.3.
Evolution of the CDF

The main results of this paper are obtained by treating the evolution of the law of
X^{(n)}
as if it were governed by a parabolic partial differential equation (PDE). Following
[
2
,
3
]
, this is done at the level of cumulative distribution functions (CDF’s) rather than probability measures.

In particular, in what follows, define the operator
T
by taking a CDF
F
, letting
X_{1},X_{2}
be i.i.d. random variables distributed according to
F
, and defining
TF
to be the CDF of the random variable
Y
determined by

(8)

\displaystyle Y=\Phi(X_{1},X_{2}),

where
\Phi
is sampled independently of
(X_{1},X_{2})
. With this notation, it follows that the random variables
\{X^{(n)}\}
solve the RDE (
7
) if and only if the corresponding CDF’s
\{F_{n}\}
solve the recursive equation

F_{n+1}=TF_{n}.

In the first step of this work, a discrete-time evolution equation is derived to describe the evolution of the CDF of
X^{(n)}
. Namely, there is a nonlinear operator
\mathscr{L}
defined on the space of CDF’s such that the recursive equation
F_{n+1}=TF_{n}
can be rewritten in the following suggestive form

(9)

\displaystyle F_{n+1}-F_{n}=\mathscr{L}F_{n}+(1-2p)F_{n}(1-F_{n}).

As will be argued below,
\mathscr{L}
has the character of an advection-diffusion operator, albeit a nonlinear one, while the “reaction term”
F(1-F)
is familiar from the Fisher-KPP equation. The equation thus resembles a reaction-diffusion equation.

As far as convergence in distribution is concerned, deriving a limit for
n^{-1/\alpha}X^{(n)}
for some exponent
\alpha
is equivalent to proving a scaling limit for the equation (
9
). In the main result of this paper, which treats the critical point
p=\frac{1}{2}
, this is done by adapting the classical approach to numerical approximations of parabolic PDE’s developed by Barles and Souganidis
[
6
]
.

To have a sense of why it is reasonable to expect a PDE scaling limit, consider what happens in (
1.2
) when
f_{+}=f_{-}=f_{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
f_{\mathbb{Z}}(u)=(1-u)_{+}
. In this case, if the initial datum
X^{(0)}
is integer-valued, then that remains true of
X^{(n)}
for all
n
, and thus the CDF
F_{n}
can be regarded as a function on
\mathbb{Z}
. In fact, these
\mathbb{Z}
-valued solutions of the RDE coincide with two-player symmetric cooperative motion, one of the RDE’s introduced in
[
3
]
. As shown in that work, the evolution equation governing the CDF now takes a very concrete form, namely,

\displaystyle F_{n+1}-F_{n}=|\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}F_{n}|\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}}F_{n}\quad\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{Z},

where
\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}}
are certain discrete derivative operators. This observation was then used in conjunction with the method of
[
6
]
and a regularization effect of the equation to show that, after a suitable rescaling, the CDF converges to the solution of the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F=|\partial_{x}F|\partial_{x}^{2}F,\quad F(x,0)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x).

After differentiation, this PDE becomes the porous medium equation satisfied by the PDF
\rho=\partial_{x}F
with Dirac initial data, the solution of which is known to be a self-similarly growing parabolic cap (the so-called Barenblatt solution). In probabilistic terms, this implies the convergence of
N^{-1/3}X^{(N)}
to a shifted and dilated Beta(2,2) random variable.

In this work, this general strategy — derivation of a discrete-time evolution equation and application of a convergence result in the spirit of
[
6
]
— is shown to still apply in the setting of the RDE’s defined above, although its execution is complicated somewhat by the fact that the operator
\mathscr{L}
is generally nonlocal.

1.3.1.
Assumptions

Some assumptions on the law
\mathbf{P}
of the forcing
(f_{+},f_{-})
are in order. First, define the class of functions
\mathcal{S}
so that
f\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if

(10)

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\quad\text{is continuous and nonincreasing, and}

(11)

\displaystyle u\mapsto u+f(u)\,\,\text{is a nondecreasing function in}\,\,[0,+\infty).

Note that the function
f(u)=\log(1+e^{-u})
relevant to the series-parallel graph belongs to
\mathcal{S}
since
-1\leq f^{\prime}\leq 0
.

Throughout the paper,
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
are assumed to lie in
\mathcal{S}
. For
\rho\in\{+,-\}
, let
g_{\rho}:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
be the function

(12)

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(s)=\sup\left\{u\geq 0\,\mid\,u+f_{\rho}(u)\leq s\right\}\quad\text{if}\,\,s\geq f_{\rho}(0),\quad g_{\rho}(s)=0,\quad\text{otherwise.}

Notice that the restriction of
g_{\rho}
to the set
[f(0),+\infty)
is the right-continuous right-inverse of
u\mapsto u+f_{\rho}(u)
.

By definition, if
f_{\rho}(+\infty)=0
, then
g_{\rho}(s)-s\to 0
as
s\to+\infty
. The next assumption asks that
g_{\rho}(s)-s
vanishes in a quantitative manner:

(13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+s)\mathbf{E}[|g_{+}(s)-s|+|g_{-}(s)-s|]\,ds<\infty.

1.3.2.
Main Result

Under the assumption (
13
), it is possible to define effective coefficients
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\geq 0
according to the formulae

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=-2p\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[|g_{+}(s)-s|]\,du+2(1-p)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[|g_{-}(s)-s|]\,ds,\quad

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[(3s-g_{+}(s))(s-g_{+}(s))]\,ds+(1-p)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{E}[(3s-g_{-}(s))(s-g_{-}(s))]\,ds.

The nonnegativity of
a
follows from the fact that
g_{+}(s),g_{-}(s)\leq s
for any
s\geq 0
.

Define the exponent
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha=2\quad\text{if}\,\,\sigma\neq 0,\quad\alpha=3,\quad\text{otherwise.}

The next theorem characterizes the scaling limit of the CDF in the critical case
p=\frac{1}{2}
. The relevant background from PDE theory, particularly the theory of viscosity solutions, is reviewed in Appendix
A
below.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
p=\frac{1}{2}
,
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=1
, and (
13
) holds. If
\{F_{n}\}
is the sequence of CDF’s associated to some solution
\{X^{(n)}\}
of (
7
), then, independent of the initial CDF
F_{0}
, if
F_{\epsilon}
is the rescaled CDF defined by

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(x,t)=F_{[\epsilon^{-\alpha}t]}(\epsilon^{-1}x),

then
F_{\epsilon}\to F
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
, where
F
is determined as follows:

(i)

If
\sigma\neq 0
, then
F
is the unique bounded discontinuous viscosity solution of the initial value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\partial_{t}F-\sigma|\partial_{x}F|^{2}=0&\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
F(x,0)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x).\end{array}\right.

(ii)

If
\sigma=0
, then
F
is the unique bounded discontinuous viscosity solution of the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\partial_{t}F-a|\partial_{x}F|\partial_{x}^{2}F=0&\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
F(x,0)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x).\end{array}\right.

These PDE’s become more familiar when passing to PDF’s: The spatial derivative
\rho=\partial_{x}F
is a solution of Burger’s equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\sigma\partial_{x}(\rho^{2})
in case (i) and a solution of the porous medium equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\frac{1}{2}a\partial_{x}^{2}(\rho^{2})
in case (ii).

As observed in
[
3
,
2
]
, the solutions of the PDE’s above are explicitly known and closely related to the
\text{Beta}(2,1)
and
\text{Beta}(2,2)
distributions. The next corollary makes this precise.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
p=\frac{1}{2}
,
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=1
, and (
13
) holds, and let
\{X^{(n)}\}
be a solution of the RDE (
7
) with an arbitrary initial distribution.

(i)

If
\sigma\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle-\frac{\text{sgn}(\sigma)}{2\sqrt{|\sigma|}\sqrt{N}}X^{(N)}\overset{d}{\to}\text{Beta}(2,1).

(ii)

If
\sigma=0
and
a>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(36a)^{\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{1}{3}}}X^{(N)}+\frac{1}{2}\overset{d}{\to}\text{Beta}(2,2)

(iii)

If
\sigma=a=0
, then
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+}=f_{-}=0\}=1
and
X^{(N)}\overset{d}{=}X^{(0)}
for each
N
.

It is not hard to show, through manipulations of the definitions above, that
\sigma>0
holds, for instance, if

f_{-}>f_{+}

\mathbf{P}
-almost surely, while
\sigma=0
if
f_{+}=f_{-}
. In the case of the resistance
R^{(n)}
of the critical series-parallel graph,
\sigma=0
and
a=2\zeta(3)
. For more on this and further discussion of examples, see Section
4
.

In addition to characterizing the asymptotics of the resistance of the critical series-parallel graph, the proof of Theorem
1
also leads to a characterization of the limiting behavior of Pemantle’s min-plus binary tree (or first passage percolation on the critical series-parallel graph). This is discussed in Section
4.2
below.

1.3.3.
Idea of Proof

The PDE scaling limit of the CDF is obtained using an extension of the approach of
[
6
]
, which is broadly applicable to elliptic and parabolic PDE’s studied through the lens of Crandall and Lions’ theory of viscosity solutions. There are two key ingredients in that approach, namely,
monotonicity
and
consistency
.

In the present context, monotonicity means the following: If
\{F_{n}\}
and
\{G_{n}\}
are two sequences of CDF’s arising from solutions of the RDE and if the inequality
F_{0}\leq G_{0}
holds pointwise, then
F_{n}\leq G_{n}
holds for any
n
. For the purposes of this paper, this is the bare minimum structural condition necessary to treat the evolution (
9
) as a parabolic equation.

Consistency, on the other hand, refers to the behavior of
T
under rescaling. Specifically, Proposition
8
below shows that if
\Phi
is a smooth CDF, rescaled according to
\Phi_{\delta}(x)=\Phi(\delta x)
, then

(18)

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}\Phi_{\delta})(\delta^{-1}x)=\delta^{2}\sigma|\partial_{x}\Phi(x)|^{2}+\delta^{3}a|\partial_{x}\Phi(x)|\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)+\cdots.

Naïvely inserting this expansion in the equation (
9
) and recalling the definition of the rescaled CDF
F_{\epsilon}
, one is led to guess that the PDE’s (
(i)
) and (
(ii)
) ought to appear in the limit. The approach of
[
6
]
provides a framework for making such a formal argument rigorous.

1.3.4.
Variants of the Fisher-KPP Equation when
p\neq\frac{1}{2}

The expansion (
18
) leads to interesting new questions away from the critical point
p=\frac{1}{2}
. Passing to the ballistically rescaled CDF

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(x,t)=F_{[\epsilon^{-1}t]}(\epsilon^{-1}x),

one finds that formally

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}a|\partial_{x}F_{\epsilon}|\partial_{x}^{2}F_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\sigma|\partial_{x}F_{\epsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}(1-2p)F_{\epsilon}(1-F_{\epsilon})+\cdots.

After discarding the error terms, this is the PDE obtained after applying a ballistic rescaling to the following quasilinear, fractional variant of the Fisher-KPP equation

(19)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G=a|\partial_{x}G|\partial_{x}^{2}G+\sigma|\partial_{x}G|^{2}+(1-2p)G(1-G).

The asymptotics of this equation, particularly the propagation of “front-like” solutions (such as CDF’s), does not seem to have been treated thus far in the literature. Both the cases where
a=0
or
\sigma=0
appear to be relevant to the problem at hand; see Section
4.3
for justification of this.

1.4.
Related Literature

There is considerable interest in RDE’s in the probability literature. The reader is referred to the survey article of Aldous and Bandyopadhyay
[
4
]
, which discusses applications in the study of branching processes, percolation, and mean-field combinatorial optimization problems.

There has been a recent burst of activity concerning RDE’s in which the relevant asymptotic behavior is not convergence to a fixed point as in the examples in
[
4
]
, but instead a distributional scaling limit in the same spirit as the central limit theorem.

Auffinger and Cable
[
5
]
analyzed Pemantle’s Min-Plus Binary Tree (or, equivalently, first passage percolation on the critical series-parallel graph, see
[
18
, Section 3]
), proving that solutions of the associated RDE converge to a
\text{Beta}(2,1)
distribution after a diffusive rescaling provided the initial distribution is concentrated at one. This is improved to the case of an arbitrary initial distribution in Corollary
2
below.

Addario-Berry and coauthors recently introduced two classes of RDE’s, called hipster random walks and cooperative motion, in
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. In
[
1
]
,
\text{Beta}(2,1)
and
\text{Beta}(2,2)
limit theorems for some hipster random walks were proved by treating the evolution of the PMF as a numerical approximation of the solution of a divergence-form PDE. As discussed therein, some of the motivation to study these RDE’s came from interest in the resistance of the series-parallel graph and Pemantle’s Min-Plus Binary Tree.

Cooperative motion was analyzed in
[
2
,
3
]
, this time through the analysis of the CDF and using techniques developed for PDE’s in nondivergence form, specifically the theory of viscosity solutions. In the present paper, the idea to focus attention on the CDF came from these last two works.

The approach used here is similar in spirit to that in
[
2
,
3
]
. A few differences are worth highlighting. Many of the RDE’s treated in
[
2
,
3
]
are not monotone, hence the method of
[
6
]
does not apply out of the box. Instead, those works showed that, after a finite time, the law of
X^{(n)}
is pulled into a region in which the RDE
is
monotone; at the level of the CDF, the key observation is that the CDF obtains a uniform Lipschitz bound in a (universal) finite time, which mimics the properties of the PDE’s that emerge in the scaling limit. After this finite waiting period, monotonicity can be used as in
[
6
]
(or, in the case of
[
2
]
, following the earlier work
[
12
]
).

By contrast, here the RDE’s of interest are all monotone. There is a technical issue, namely, that the result of
[
6
]
does not apply directly as the evolution (
9
) only makes sense as an equation posed in the space of CDF’s, whereas
[
6
]
works with evolution equations posed in the space of bounded functions. In
[
3
]
, this is arguably less of an issue as the equations all involve finite differences (although the relevant details are not discussed there). Here
\mathscr{L}
behaves like a nonlocal operator with a kernel with infinite range so at first blush one would like to know that
[
6
]
can be carried out with globally nondecreasing test functions, which is not obvious. It is, in fact, possible to adapt the method to monotone semigroups in the space of CDF’s. In the hope that it may be useful in other contexts, the result is formulated in an abstract way in Appendix
B
below.

When
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
, the evolution equation obtained here suggests a strategy for tackling the asymptotics of the RDE. This is of interest both in the analysis of the resistance and the distance on the series-parallel graph. The latter was recently considered by Chen, Derrida, Duquesne, and Shi in
[
11
]
. They proved that when
p>\frac{1}{2}
, the logarithm of the expected value of the distance grows at a linear rate with slope
\alpha(p)
, and they proved that
\alpha(1/2+\epsilon)\approx\sqrt{\zeta(2)\epsilon}
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

As mentioned above, convergence of the rescaled CDF’s to the solution of a PDE is proved here by adapting the classical approach of Barles and Souganidis from
[
6
]
. At a high level, as soon as the monotonicity and scaling behavior (“consistency”) of
\mathscr{L}
is understood, convergence follows. This is not the only setting where some extra tailoring of the method is needed to furnish a proof. For instance, in
[
9
]
, the same authors adapted their approach to the setting of geometric flows and interface motions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Extended-Real-Valued Random Variables

Note that the definition (
7
) still makes sense if the input random variables are permitted to take the values
+\infty
and
-\infty
. It will be convenient to extend the definition to include such extended-real-valued random variables. To that end, for an extended-real-valued random variable
X
, define the CDF
F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
via the rule

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{X=-\infty\}=\lim_{y\to-\infty}F(y),\quad\mathbb{P}\{X\leq x\}=F(x),\quad\mathbb{P}\{X=+\infty\}=1-\lim_{y\to+\infty}F(y).

Notice that the space
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
of all such CDF’s is simply

\displaystyle CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})=\{F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\,\,\text{increasing, right-continuous}\}.

Using standard extended-real-number arithmetic, from now on, the formula (
8
) defining
T
will be understood to hold in the domain
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
.

2.2.
Symmetry

In order to reduce redundancy in the proofs that follow, it is worth observing that if
Y=\Phi(X_{1},X_{2})
, where
\Phi
is given by (
1.2
), then

\displaystyle-Y=\Psi(-X_{1},-X_{2}),

provided
\Psi
is defined by

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\max\{x,y\}+f_{-}(|x-y|),&\text{if}\,\,1-\Theta=1,\\
\min\{x,y\}-f_{+}(|x-y|),&\text{if}\,\,1-\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

Thus, in proofs where the events
\{\Theta=1\}
and
\{\Theta=0\}
would in principle need to be treated independently, by symmetry, there is no loss of generality in assuming
\Theta=1
.

The symmetry property above has a counterpart at the level of the CDF. If
\{F_{n}\}
is the sequence of CDF’s associated with a solution
\{X^{(n)}\}
of the RDE
X^{(n)}=\Phi(X^{(n-1)}_{1},X^{(n-1)}_{2})
, then the functions
\{G_{n}\}
defined by

\displaystyle G_{n}(x)=1-\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}F_{n}(-x-\delta)

are the CDF’s associated with the solution
\{Y^{(n)}\}
of the RDE
Y^{(n)}=\Psi(Y^{(n-1)}_{1},Y^{(n-1)}_{2})
obtained by setting
Y^{(n)}=-X^{(n)}
.

2.3.
Monotonicity

In this section, the monotonicity of
T
is proved. Here
monotone
means that

\displaystyle\text{if}\,\,F_{1}\leq F_{2}\,\,\text{pointwise in}\,\,\mathbb{R},\quad\text{then}\,\,TF_{1}\leq TF_{2}\,\,\text{pointwise in}\,\,\mathbb{R}.

To this end, it is convenient to exploit the quadratic structure inherent in the definition. Specifically, it will be useful to consider a certain natural operator

S:CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})\times CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})\to CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})

with the property that
S(F,F)=TF
for any CDF
F
.

S
is defined analogously to
T
: Given two CDF’s
F,G\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
, let
X_{F}
and
X_{G}
be independent extended-real-valued random variables distributed according to
F
and
G
, respectively, and sample the data
(\Theta,f_{+},f_{-})
independently of
(X_{F},X_{G})
. Let
S(F,G)
denote the CDF of the random variable
Y
defined as follows:

\displaystyle Y=\Phi(X_{F},X_{G})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\max\{X_{F},X_{G}\}+f_{+}(|X_{F}-X_{G}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
\min\{X_{F},X_{G}\}-f_{-}(|X_{F}-X_{G}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

Clearly
S(F,F)=TF
as desired. Further,
S(F,G)=S(G,F)
by symmetry.

The next result asserts that
S
is monotone provided
f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=1
. Given
F_{+},F_{-},G\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
, if
F_{+}\leq F_{-}
holds pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
, then
S(F_{+},G)\leq S(F_{-},G)
also holds. Therefore,
T
is monotone.

Proof.

Since
S(F,F)=TF
and
S
is symmetric, the desired monotonicity of
T
readily follows from that of
S
.

It only remains to prove the monotonicity of
S
. Fix
F_{+},F_{-},G\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
such that
F_{+}\leq F_{-}
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
. Recall that, by Strassen’s Theorem, this is equivalent to the existence of a Borel probability measure
\Pi
on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\times\overline{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\Pi\{(x,y)\,\mid\,x\leq y\}=1
and the first and second marginals of
\Pi
are distributed according to
F_{-}
and
F_{+}
, respectively.

Let
(X_{1}^{-},X_{1}^{+},X_{2})
be a random vector such that
(X_{1}^{-},X_{1}^{+})
is distributed according to
\Pi
, independent of
X_{2}
, and
X_{2}
is distributed according to
G
. Note that
X_{1}^{-}\leq X_{2}^{+}
almost surely. Define
Y^{+}=\Phi(X_{1}^{+},X_{2})
and
Y^{-}=\Phi(X_{1}^{-},X_{2})
, where
\Phi
is independent of
(X_{1}^{-},X_{1}^{+},X_{2})
. By definition,
S(F_{\rho},G)
is the CDF of
Y^{\rho}
for
\rho\in\{+,-\}
. Thus, to conclude, it only remains to show that
Y^{-}\leq Y^{+}
holds almost surely.

This can be checked via a case analysis. The details for the event
\{\Theta=1\}
are provided below; the case when
\{\Theta=0\}
follows by symmetry as in Section
2.2
. Assume henceforth that
\Theta=1
.

If
X_{2}\leq X_{1}^{-}\leq X_{1}^{+}
, then the assumption that the map
u\mapsto u+f(u)
is nondecreasing (see (
11
)) implies

\displaystyle Y^{-}=X^{-}_{1}+f_{+}(X^{-}_{1}-X_{2})\leq X_{1}^{+}+f_{+}(X^{+}_{1}-X_{2})=Y^{+}.

On the other hand, if
X^{-}_{1}\leq X_{2}\leq X^{+}_{1}
, then invoking first (
11
) and then the fact that
f_{+}
is nonincreasing (see (
10
)), one finds

\displaystyle Y^{-}=X_{2}+f_{+}(X_{2}-X^{-}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq X_{2}+f_{+}(0)

\displaystyle=X_{2}+f_{+}(X_{2}-X_{2})\leq X^{+}_{1}+f_{+}(X^{+}_{1}-X_{2})=Y^{+}.

Finally, if
X^{-}_{1}\leq X^{+}_{1}\leq X_{2}
, then, again, the fact that
f_{+}
is nonincreasing implies

\displaystyle Y^{-}=X_{2}+f_{+}(X_{2}-X^{-}_{1})\leq X_{2}+f_{+}(X_{2}-X^{+}_{1})=Y^{+}.

∎

2.4.
Continuity

For the sake of developing intuition, it may be useful to note that
T
maps the space of continuous CDF’s to itself.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=1
. If
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
is continuous, then
TF
is also continuous.

Proof.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2})
be i.i.d. random variables distributed according to
F
and let
Y=\Phi(X_{1},X_{2})
, where
\Phi
is sampled independently of
(X_{1},X_{2})
. Since
TF
is the CDF of
Y
, to see that it is continuous, it suffices to prove that
\mathbb{P}\{Y=x_{0}\}=0
for each
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Consider the event
\Theta=1
: Since the law of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is invariant under permutation of the coordinates,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Y=x_{0}\,\mid\,\Theta=1\}

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{X_{2}-x_{1}+f(X_{2}-x_{1})=x_{0}-x_{1},\,\,X_{2}>x_{1}\}\mu(dx_{1}),

where
\mu
is the law of
X_{1}
. Since
u\mapsto u+f(u)
is nondecreasing by (
12
), there are two possibilities: Either the set
\{u\geq 0\,\mid\,u+f(u)=x_{0}-x_{1}\}
is an interval, or it is a point. If it is a point, then the corresponding probability in the integral above vanishes since
X_{2}
is a continuous random variable. On the other hand, there are at most countably many disjoint intervals on which
u+f(u)
is constant, hence countably many values of
x_{0}-x_{1}
. Since
\mu
has no atoms, these values of
x_{0}-x_{1}
do not contribute to the integral either. Therefore, the probability above is zero.

A similar analysis shows that
\mathbb{P}\{Y=x_{0}\,\mid\,\Theta=0\}=0
. ∎

3.
Evolution of the CDF

This section concerns the evolution equation (
9
) satisfied by CDF’s. To this end, it will be useful to decompose the random variable
Y
in the definition of
T
(see (
8
)) in a specific way. Given
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
, let
X_{1},X_{2}
be i.i.d. extended-real-valued random variables distributed according to
F
, and sample the data
(\Theta,f_{+},f_{-})
independently of
(X_{1},X_{2})
. Define the extended-real-valued random variable
Z
by

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\},&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\},&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

so that the random variable
Y
of (
8
) can be written in the form

(24)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=Z+\Theta f_{+}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|)+(1-\Theta)f_{-}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|).

The next result describes the difference
TF-F
in terms of
(Y,Z,\Theta)
. Specifically, let
\mathscr{L}
be the operator defined on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
by

(25)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}F(x)=-p\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x<Y\mid\Theta=1\}+(1-p)\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x<Z\mid\Theta=0\}.

Proposition 3
.

For any
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
.

TF-F=\mathscr{L}F+(1-2p)F(1-F).

Further, if
p=\frac{1}{2}
, then
TF(\pm\infty)=F(\pm\infty)
for any
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
.

Using the explicit formulas (
24
) and (
3
), one obtains very concrete expressions for the action of
\mathscr{L}
on smooth CDF’s, as stated in the next result.

Proposition 4
.

Assume that
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=\mathbf{P}\{f_{+}(+\infty)=f_{-}(+\infty)=0\}=1
. If
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
is smooth, then

(26)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}F(x)

\displaystyle=2p\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}+g_{+}(x-x_{1}))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-p)\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}-g_{-}(x_{1}-x))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1}.

Proposition
4
has two immediate consequences that will be used in the sequel. First, it makes it relatively easy to see that
T
commutes with the addition of constants whenever such addition makes sense.

Proposition 5
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4
, if
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
, then
T(F+c)=TF+c
for any constant
c\in[-F(-\infty),1-F(+\infty)]
.

The next consequence of Proposition
4
, which follows from Proposition
5
and is stated precisely in Appendix
B.3
, concerns the contractive property of
T
, namely,

\|TF-TG\|_{\sup}\leq\|F-G\|_{\sup}.

This is classical for operators with the same properties as
T
defined instead on the larger space
B(X)
of bounded functions on some set
X
, which is precisely the setting considered in
[
6
]
. The fact that
T
is defined only on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
makes the proof slightly less obvious, but contractivity holds nonetheless.

The remainder of this section is organized as follows: In Section
3.1
, Proposition
3
is proved. The formula for
\mathscr{L}
on smooth CDF’s is then proved in Section
3.2
, along with Proposition
5
.

3.1.
Proof of Proposition
3

Fix a CDF
F
and define
(X_{1},X_{2},Z,Y,\Theta,f_{+},f_{-})
as in the discussion preceding the statement of the proposition. Start by decomposing the difference
TF-F
as follows

\displaystyle TF(x)-F(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x\}-F(x)

(27)

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x\}-\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x\}\Big)+\Big(\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x\}-F(x)\Big).

Observe that, since
f_{+},f_{-}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle Y-Z\geq 0\quad\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\quad\text{and}\quad Y-Z\leq 0\quad\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.

Thus, since
p=\mathbb{P}\{\Theta=1\}
by definition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x\}-\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x\}

\displaystyle=-p\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x<Y\mid\Theta=1\}+(1-p)\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x<Z\mid\Theta=0\}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}F(x).

It remains to treat the remaining summand,
\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x\}-F(x)
. Notice that

\displaystyle\{Z\leq x\}=\{\Theta=1,X_{1}\leq x\,\,\text{and}\,\,X_{2}\leq x\}\cup\{\Theta=0,X_{1}\leq x\,\,\text{or}\,\,X_{2}\leq x\}.

Thus, since
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are i.i.d. with CDF
F
, one obtains, exactly as in
[
18
, Lemma 1.1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x\}=pF(x)^{2}+(1-p)(1-(1-F(x))^{2}).

Upon subtracting
F(x)
and simplifying, this becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x\}-F(x)=(1-2p)F(x)(1-F(x)).

Remark 1
.

In
[
18
, Lemma 1.1]
, it is observed that the fixed points of the map
q\mapsto pq^{2}+(1-p)(1-(1-q)^{2})
are fundamental in the study of the asymptotics of the series-parallel graph. Yet, as in the derivation above, a fixed point of this map is nothing but a zero of the polynomial
(1-2p)q(1-q)
, the “reaction term” appearing in the equation.

3.1.1.
Stationarity at infinity if
p=\frac{1}{2}

If
p=\frac{1}{2}
, then

\displaystyle TF(\pm\infty)-F(\pm\infty)=\mathscr{L}F(\pm\infty)

and inspection of the formula (
25
) shows that
\mathscr{L}F(\pm\infty)=0
independently of
F
. ∎

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
4

Suppose that
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
is smooth. To obtain the formula (
26
) for
\mathscr{L}F
in this case, it suffices to establish that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x<Y\mid\Theta=1\}=-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}+g_{+}(x-x_{1}))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x<Z\mid\Theta=0\}=2\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}-g_{-}(x_{1}-x))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1}.

Since the law of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is invariant under permutation of the coordinates, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x<Y,X_{1}\leq X_{2}\mid\Theta=1\}=-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}+g_{+}(x-x_{1}))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x<Z,X_{2}\leq X_{1}\mid\Theta=0\}=\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}-g_{-}(x_{1}-x))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1}.

In particular, in the analysis that follows, only the case where
Z=X_{2}
needs to be treated. Further, there is no loss of generality assuming that
x\in\mathbb{R}
as the integral vanishes for
x\in\{-\infty,+\infty\}
because
F(x_{1}+g_{+}(+\infty-x_{1}))=F(+\infty)
.

3.2.1.
Case:
\Theta=1

Condition first on the event
\{\Theta=1\}
. Before going further, it is worth noting that the assumption that
f_{+}(+\infty)=0
implies that
X_{1}>-\infty
almost surely on the event
\{Z\leq x<Y,\,\,X_{1}\leq X_{2},\,\,\Theta=1\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. This is immediate from the fact that
Y=X_{2}=Z
on
\{X_{1}=-\infty,\,\,\Theta=1,\,\,f_{+}(+\infty)=0\}
.

Subtracting
X_{1}
from both sides of the equation defining
Y
while assuming that
X_{2}\geq X_{1}
leads to

\displaystyle Y-X_{1}=(X_{2}-X_{1})+f_{+}(X_{2}-X_{1}).

At the same time, notice that
g_{+}
is defined precisely so that, for any
u>0
and
s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle g_{+}(s)<u\quad\text{if and only if}\quad s<u+f_{+}(u).

Thus,

\displaystyle\{Z\leq x<Y,\,\,X_{1}<X_{2},\,\,\Theta=1\}

\displaystyle=\{-\infty<X_{1}<X_{2}\leq x,\,\,g_{+}(x-X_{1})<X_{2}-X_{1}\}

\displaystyle=\{-\infty<X_{1}<x,\,\,X_{1}+g_{+}(x-X_{1})<X_{2}\leq x\}.

Therefore, since
f_{+}
is independent of
(X_{1},X_{2})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{Z\leq x<Y,X_{1}\leq X_{2}\mid\Theta=1\}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbf{E}[F(x_{1}+g_{+}(x-x_{1}))-F(x)]F^{\prime}(x_{1})\,dx_{1}.

3.2.2.
Case:
\Theta=0

The analysis of
\mathbb{P}\{Y\leq x<Z,X_{2}\leq X_{1}\mid\Theta=0\}
follows from arguments very similar to those used when
\Theta=1
. In fact, the desired formula can be quickly derived from the fact that the transformation
(X_{1},X_{2},Z,Y)\mapsto(-X_{1},-X_{2},-Z,-Y)
preserves the structure of the problem (see Section
2.2
). ∎

3.3.
Proof of Proposition
5

Suppose that
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies
c_{-}\leq F\leq c_{+}
pointwise for some
c_{-},c_{+}\in[0,1]
. Fix
c\in[-c_{-},1-c_{+}]
. The goal at this stage is to show that
T(F+c)=TF+c
.

Let
X
be an extended-real-valued random variable with CDF
F
. Consider the CDF
F_{n}
of the random variable
X+n^{-1}Y
, where
Y
is a mean-zero, variance-one Gaussian, independent of
X
. Then
F_{n}
is a smooth function and a standard coupling argument shows that both
F_{n}\to F
and
TF_{n}\to TF
pointwise in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, except for points of discontinuity, as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, since the same reasoning applies to
F+c
, it suffices to consider the case when
F
is smooth. Yet direct inspection of formula (
26
) shows that
\mathscr{L}(F+c)=\mathscr{L}F
for smooth
F
.

4.
Examples

This section describes a few relevant examples, namely, those that have appeared earlier in the literature, and describes some of the useful, concrete forms of
\mathscr{L}
that can be derived.

4.1.
Resistance of the series-parallel graph

As mentioned already in the introduction, if
X^{(n)}=\log R^{(n)}
is the logarithm of the resistance of the series-parallel graph, then
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a solution of the RDE (
7
) provided
f_{+}=f_{-}=f

\mathbf{P}
-almost surely, where
f
is given by the formula

\displaystyle f(u)=\log(1+e^{-u}).

In this case, the right-continuous, right-inverse
g
of the function
u\mapsto u+f(u)
can be computed to be

\displaystyle g(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\log(e^{s}-1),&\text{if}\,\,s\geq\log 2,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

It is immediate to check that
f\in\mathcal{S}
and
s-g(s)=f(g(s))
for
s\geq\log 2
so assumption (
13
) of Theorem
1
is satisfied.

In this case, the constant
a
is given by
a=2\zeta(3)
. See Appendix
C
for the proof.

4.2.
Distance on the series-parallel graph

Let
D^{(n)}
be the distance between the two terminal nodes in the series-parallel graph at stage
n
. Due to the recursive structure inherent in the graph,
D^{(n)}
is a solution of the RDE

\displaystyle D^{(n)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}D^{(n-1)}_{1}+D^{(n-1)}_{2},&\text{with probability}\,\,p,\\
\min\{D^{(n-1)}_{1},D^{(n-1)}_{2}\},&\text{with probability}\,\,1-p.\end{array}\right.

This RDE also arises in Pemantle’s min-plus binary tree, and its asymptotics were analyzed in
[
5
]
. Analogous to the case of the resistance, the logarithm
X^{(n)}=\log D^{(n)}
gives a solution of the RDE (
7
) with
\Phi
given as in (
1.2
) provided
f_{+}(u)=\log(1+e^{-u})
and
f_{-}=0
.

A complete analysis of the asymptotics of this RDE in the subcritical case (
p<\frac{1}{2}
) is given in
[
18
]
. There is a random variable
K
such that if
\lambda
denotes the essential infimum of
D^{(0)}
, then
D^{(n)}\to\lambda K
as
n\to\infty
. This implies that
K
is the unique (up to multiplication by a constant) fixed point of the RDE in this case.

In the critical case (
p=\frac{1}{2}
), the main result of
[
5
]
obtains a
\text{Beta}(2,1)
limit for
N^{-1/2}\log D^{(N)}
provided
D^{(0)}=1
almost surely. The next corollary of Theorem
1
describes the asymptotics for an arbitrary initial distribution. Notice the contrast with the subcritical case: In the subcritical case, if
D^{(0)}
can be arbitrarily small with positive probability, then
D^{(n)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, whereas here, conditionally on the event
\{D^{(n)}>0\}
, mass at zero only slows the rate of growth.

Corollary 2
.

Assume that
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mathbb{P}\{0\leq D^{(0)}<\infty\}=1
and let
q=\mathbb{P}\{D^{(0)}>0\}
. For any
n
,
\mathbb{P}\{D^{(n)}=0\}=q
. Further,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\{-\frac{\text{sgn}(\sigma)}{2\sqrt{|\sigma|}\sqrt{N}}\log D^{(N)}\leq x\,\mid\,D^{(N)}>0\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}0,&\text{if}\,\,x<0,\\
\frac{x^{2}}{q},&\text{if}\,\,0\leq x\leq\sqrt{q},\\
1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Here the constant
\sigma=-\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}
.

Proof.

Let
X^{(n)}=\log D^{(n)}
so that
\{D^{(n)}>0\}=\{X^{(n)}>-\infty\}
. By Proposition
3
,
\mathbb{P}\{X^{(n)}>-\infty\}
is constant in
n
.

Let
F_{n}
be the CDF of
X^{(n)}
and let
G_{n}
be the CDF conditional on
\{X^{(n)}>-\infty\}
, that is,

\displaystyle G_{n}(x)=\mathbb{P}\{X^{(n)}\leq x\,\mid\,X^{(n)}>-\infty\}.

Notice that
G_{n}=q^{-1}(F_{n}-(1-q))
. At the same time, the strengthened version of Theorem
1
from Section
5
(Theorem
2
) applies: If
F_{\epsilon}(x,t)=F_{[\epsilon^{-2}t]}(\epsilon^{-1}x)
, then the theorem implies that

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}\to F\quad\text{locally uniformly in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),

where
F
is the unique upper semicontinuous viscosity solution of the PDE
\partial_{t}F-\sigma|\partial_{x}F|^{2}=0
with initial datum
F(x,0)=(1-q)+q\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)
. After writing
G_{\epsilon}(x,t)=G_{[\epsilon^{-2}t]}(\epsilon^{-1}x)
, it follows from this that
G_{\epsilon}\to G
, where
G
is the unique discontinuous viscosity solution of
\partial_{t}G-q\sigma|\partial_{x}G|^{2}=0
with
G(x,0)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}
. The proof of Corollary
1
in Section
5
gives an explicit formula for this function, from which the claimed asymptotics of
\log D^{(N)}
follow.

The claimed value of
\sigma
is computed in Appendix
C
and is consistent with
[
5
]
. ∎

It is worth emphasizing that
D^{(n)}
is integer-valued, hence its logarithm takes values in the set
\log\mathbb{N}
for all
n
. For the present choice of
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
, it is not hard to show that this remains true for any solution that starts initially in
\log\mathbb{N}
. This is worth mentioning because the equation for the CDF of such a solution takes a particularly simple form, namely, if the backward-difference operator
\nabla^{-}_{\log\mathbb{N}}
is defined for functions on
\log\mathbb{N}
by
\nabla^{-}_{\log\mathbb{N}}F(x)=F(x)-F(x_{-})
, where
x_{-}
denotes the largest element of
\log\mathbb{N}
smaller than
x
, then

\displaystyle F_{n+1}-F_{n}=-p(\nabla^{-}_{\log\mathbb{N}}F)^{2}+(1-2p)F_{n}(1-F_{n})\quad\text{in}\,\,\log\mathbb{N}.

Notice that this equation looks like a discrete-time, discrete-in-space version of (
19
) with
a=0
.

4.3.
\mathbb{Z}
-valued Solutions and Finite-Difference Equations

This section discusses the subclass of RDE’s described by (
7
) and (
1.2
) in the case when
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
take values in the set
\{0,f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, where
f_{\mathbb{Z}}(u)=(1-u)_{+}
. For instance, if the law of the data is chosen so that
f_{+}=f_{-}=f_{\mathbb{Z}}
surely, then
X^{(n)}
takes a jump only if
X^{(n-1)}_{1}
and
X^{(n-1)}_{2}
are at most distance one apart, and the size of the jump decays linearly with the distance.

What is nice about this particular case of the RDE is a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued RDE is embedded within it: If the initial data
X^{(0)}
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}
, then
X^{(n)}
remains integer-valued for all
n
since
f_{\mathbb{Z}}
maps integers to integers. For this
\mathbb{Z}
-valued RDE, the one-step evolution is described by

\displaystyle Y\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}+\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}-\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

The RDE associated with this equation provides a very natural distribution-dependent random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
, and it has the nice feature that the equations describing the CDF are nothing other than finite-difference discretizations of the quasilinear Fisher-KPP equations mentioned in the introduction.

For the rest of this subsection, assume that the joint law
\mathbf{P}
of
(f_{+},f_{-})
is concentrated on the set
\{0,f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}\times\{0,f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. Notice that

\displaystyle f_{\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{S}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)=s\quad\text{for each}\,\,s\geq 1,

where
g_{\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the left-inverse of the function
u\mapsto u+f_{\mathbb{Z}}(u)
. For such a law
\mathbf{P}
, all the assumptions of Theorem
1
are satisfied. In particular, as long as
(f_{+},f_{-})
is not identically zero, either
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}X^{(n)}
or
n^{-\frac{1}{3}}X^{(n)}
converges to a nondegenerate limit.

Let
q_{+}=\mathbf{P}\{f_{+}=f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
and
q_{-}=\mathbf{P}\{f_{-}=f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
in the discussion that follows.

For
\mathbb{Z}
-valued solutions of the RDE, there is no loss restricting the CDF to the domain
\mathbb{Z}
. By invoking the formula (
25
), one obtains, in this case,

(30)

\displaystyle F_{n+1}-F_{n}

\displaystyle=-pq_{+}(\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-}F_{n})^{2}+(1-p)q_{-}(\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}F_{n})^{2}+(1-2p)F_{n}(1-F_{n}),

where
\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
,
\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-}
are the forward- and backward-difference operators

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}F(x)=F(x+1)-F(x),\quad\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-}F(x)=F(x)-F(x-1).

Using the identity
\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}-\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-}=\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}}
for
\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}}F(x)=(F(x+1)+F(x-1))-F(x)
and simplifying the difference of the two squares, this becomes

(31)

\displaystyle F_{n+1}-F_{n}

\displaystyle=a\nabla^{\text{sym}}_{\mathbb{Z}}F_{n}\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}}F_{n}+\sigma(\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}F_{n})^{2}+(1-2p)F_{n}(1-F_{n}).

where
\nabla^{\text{sym}}_{\mathbb{Z}}=2^{-1}(\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}+\nabla_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-})
,
\sigma=(1-p)q_{-}-pq_{+}
, and
a=2pq_{+}
.

Equation (
31
) looks like a discrete analogue of the quasilinear Fisher-KPP equation (
19
) derived above. Notice that here
a
or
\sigma
may vanish, irrespective of whether or not
p=\frac{1}{2}
. Since replacing
\mathbb{Z}
by
\mathbb{R}
and finite-differences by derivatives does not seem likely to fundamentally change the large-scale asymptotic behavior, equation (
31
) motivates the general study of (
19
) for arbitrary
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\geq 0
.

It is worth emphasizing that there is no contradiction between the formulas (
31
) and (
26
) since the former involves the CDF of a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable while the latter describes smooth CDF’s.

4.4.
Hipster Random Walks and Cooperative Motion

Inspection of (
31
) also reveals a connection with the symmetric hipster random walk and two-player cooperative motion, examples considered in in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
, respectively.

To understand the connection, consider again the random variable
Z
defined by
Z=\Theta\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}+(1-\Theta)\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
. Recall that
Z
and
X_{1}
have the same distribution if
p=\frac{1}{2}
. At the same time, since the two coincide when
X_{1}=X_{2}
, a stronger statement is true:
Z
and
X_{1}
have the same conditional laws on the event
\{X_{1}\neq X_{2}\}
. The same reasoning applies if
X_{1}
is replaced by the more symmetric
\Theta X_{1}+(1-\Theta)X_{2}
. From this, it follows that the law of the output
Y
is unchanged if the equation is instead changed to

\displaystyle Y\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}X_{1}+\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
X_{2}-\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0,\end{array}\right.

or

\displaystyle Y\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}X_{1}+\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
X_{1}-\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

These are precisely the one-step updates used to define the symmetric hipster random walk and two-player symmetric cooperative motion, respectively. Thus, although it is not immediately obvious from inspection of (
4.4
) or (
4.4
), those two RDE’s belong to the class considered here.

In fact, by this reasoning, it is also possible to draw a connection with the other RDE considered in
[
1
]
, called the totally asymmetric hipster random walk. This is the RDE in
\mathbb{Z}
with the one-step update determined by two independent Bernoulli random variables
\Theta,\Xi
via the rule

\displaystyle Y\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}X_{1}+\Xi\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=1,\\
X_{2}+\Xi\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}-X_{1}|),&\text{if}\,\,\Theta=0.\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbb{P}\{\Theta=1\}=\frac{1}{2}
. This is a class of RDE’s parametrized by
q:=\mathbb{P}\{\Xi=1\}
. By the same reasoning employed above, as long as
q\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the totally asymmetric hipster random walk belongs to the subclass of RDE’s considered in the previous subsection. This is made precise in the next result.

Proposition 6
.

Assume that
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\{0,f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}\}=1
, and define
(q_{+},q_{-})
by
q_{\rho}=\mathbf{P}\{f_{\rho}=f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. If
\{X^{(n)}\}
is the solution of the RDE (
7
) with forcing
\Phi
given by (
1.2
) for some integer-valued initial condition
X^{(0)}
, then, for each
n
,
X^{(n)}
has the same law as (i) the symmetric hipster random walk with the same initial datum if
q_{+}=q_{-}=1
and (ii) the totally asymmetric hipster random walk with the same initial datum if
q_{-}=0
provided the parameter
q
is set to
q=\frac{1}{2}q_{+}
.

Note that the equation
q=\frac{1}{2}q_{+}
introduces the constraint
q\leq\frac{1}{2}
, so that the previous result only covers the asymmetric hipster random walk for half of the possible values of
q
. In fact, it is possible to show (by manipulation of the evolution equation satisfied by the CDF) that the asymmetric hipster random walk is a monotone RDE only if
q\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, it cannot be described using (
7
) outside this regime.

Similar arguments apply to the two-player version of the RDE’s analyzed in
[
2
]
. Specifically, two-player totally asymmetric,
q
-lazy cooperative motion is the RDE for
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variables given by

\displaystyle X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}X_{1}^{(n-1)}+\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}^{(n-1)}-X_{1}^{(n-1)}|),&\text{with probability}\,\,rq,\\
X_{1}^{(n-1)}-\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(|X_{2}^{(n-1)}-X_{1}^{(n-1)}|),&\text{with probability}\,\,(1-r)q,\\
X_{1}^{(n-1)},&\text{with probability}\,\,1-q.\end{array}\right.

The RDE is parameterized by the pair
(r,q)
. The next result describes the connection with the RDE’s considered here. As for the asymmetric hipster random walk, only a subset of the parameter values are covered.

Proposition 7
.

Assume that
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\{0,f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}\}=1
, and define
(q_{+},q_{-})
by
q_{\rho}=\mathbb{P}\{f_{\rho}=f_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. If
\{X^{(n)}\}
is the solution of the RDE (
7
) with forcing
\Phi
given by (
1.2
) for some integer-valued initial condition
X^{(0)}
, then, for each
n
,
X^{(n)}
has the same law as (i) two-player symmetric cooperative motion with that initial condition if
q_{+}=q_{-}=1
or (ii) two-player asymmetric,
q
-lazy cooperative motion with that initial condition if
q_{+}\neq q_{-}
, provided the parameters
(r,q)
are set to
q=\frac{1}{2}(q_{+}+q_{-})
and
r=\frac{q_{+}}{q_{-}+q_{+}}
.

Notice that when
q_{+}\neq q_{-}
, the range of the map
(q_{+},q_{-})\mapsto(r,q)
is

(36)

\displaystyle\{(r,q)\,\mid\,q\in[0,1],\,\,|r-\frac{1}{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(q^{-1}-1)\}.

In general, this is a proper subset of the unit square. However, for any fixed
q\leq\frac{1}{2}
, it covers all possible values of
r\in[0,1]
.

As in the case of hipster random walks, the restriction in (
36
) is fundamental. Two-player totally asymmetric,
q
-lazy cooperative motion is monotone only for
(r,q)
in the set given above; this can readily be proved via differentiation of the evolution equation satisfied by the CDF (see
[
2
, Equation (1.6)]
).

In view of the proposition above, the
\text{Beta}(2,2)
limit theorems for the symmetric RDE’s considered in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
follow as a special case of this paper’s main theorem (Theorem
1
) and Corollary
1
. The
\text{Beta}(2,1)
limit theorems for asymmetric RDE’s obtained in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
also follow, albeit only in the parameter regimes identified above. Interestingly, in the asymmetric case, those works obtain
\text{Beta}(2,1)
limits, even in the nonmonotone regime.

5.
Scaling Limit

This section proves the main result, Theorem
1
. In fact, a slightly more general version is presented here, which allows for extended-real-valued solutions and diffuse initial conditions. In order to state the theorem efficiently, as in the introduction, let
\alpha=2
if
\sigma\neq 0
and
\alpha=3
, otherwise, and define the nonlinearity
\mathcal{F}
appearing in the limiting PDE analogously:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(p,X)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\sigma|p|^{2},&\text{if}\,\,\sigma\neq 0,\\
a|p|X,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The theorem that follows involves the notion of viscosity solutions of parabolic PDE’s. The relevant definitions and existence and uniqueness results are reviewed in Appendix
A
below.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=1
and (
13
) holds and fix an arbitrary
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
.

For each
\epsilon>0
, let
\{X_{\epsilon}^{(n)}\}
be an extended-real-valued solution of the RDE (
7
) and let
\{F^{\epsilon}_{n}\}
be the associated sequence of CDF’s. Let
F_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
be the rescaled CDF given by

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(x,t)=F^{\epsilon}_{[\epsilon^{-\alpha}t]}(\epsilon^{-1}x).

If convergence holds as
\epsilon\to 0
at the initial time in the sense that

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(x,0)\to F_{\text{in}}(x)\quad\text{for each point of continuity}\,\,x\,\,\text{of}\,\,F_{\text{in}},

then

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}\to F\quad\text{locally uniformly in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),

where
F
is the unique bounded discontinuous viscosity solution of the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)=0&\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
F(x,0)=F_{\text{in}}(x).\end{array}\right.

The theorem will be proved by following the approach of
[
6
]
, suitably extended to monotone semigroups in the space
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. Notice that if
T_{\epsilon}
and
\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}
are the operators on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
defined by

(39)

\displaystyle(T_{\epsilon}G)(x)=(TG_{\epsilon})(\epsilon^{-1}x),\quad\text{where}\,\,G_{\epsilon}(x)=G(\epsilon^{-1}x),\quad\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}=T_{\epsilon}-\text{Id},

then the rescaled CDF
F_{\epsilon}
above satisfies the equation

(40)

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(x,t+\epsilon^{\alpha})-F_{\epsilon}(x,t)=\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}F_{\epsilon}(x,t).

This motivates the consideration of one-parameter families of operators
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
defined on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
.

5.1.
Abstract Framework

Let
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
be a family of operators on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
with the following properties:

(i)

Monotonicity:
If
F\leq G
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
, then
T_{\epsilon}F\leq T_{\epsilon}G
also holds.

(ii)

Commutation with addition of constants:
If
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies
c_{-}\leq F\leq c_{+}
pointwise for some constants
c_{-},c_{+}\in[0,1]
, then
T_{\epsilon}(F+c)=T_{\epsilon}F+c
for any
c\in[-c_{-},1-c_{+}]
.

(iii)

Stationarity at infinity:
For any
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
,
T_{\epsilon}F(+\infty)=F(+\infty)
and
T_{\epsilon}F(-\infty)=F(-\infty)
.

Motivated by (
40
), let
\{\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}\}
be the operators on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}=T_{\epsilon}-\text{Id}.

In addition to (i) and (ii), assume that there is an exponent
\alpha>0
and a continuous function
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is increasing in the second variable, for which the following property holds:

(iv)

Consistency:
If
\Phi\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
is smooth with bounded, uniformly continuous second derivatives, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-\alpha}\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}\Phi(x)=\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}\Phi(x),\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)),

where convergence holds uniformly with respect to
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Note that (iii) implies
Tc=c
for any constant (function)
c\in[0,1]
, and, thus, by (iv),

(41)

\mathcal{F}(0,0)=0.

Under these assumptions, the following convergence result holds for rescaled solutions
\{F^{\epsilon}_{n}\}
of the recursion
F^{\epsilon}_{n+1}=T_{\epsilon}F^{\epsilon}_{n}
.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
is a family of operators on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfying assumptions (i)-(iv) above. For each
\epsilon
, let
\{F^{\epsilon}_{n}\}
be a sequence in
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
generated by the recursion
F_{n+1}^{\epsilon}=T_{\epsilon}F_{n}^{\epsilon}
. If
F_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is the rescaled CDF defined by

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(x,t)=F^{\epsilon}_{[\epsilon^{-\alpha}t]}(x),

and if there is a continuous
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
such that
F_{\epsilon}(x,0)\to F_{\text{in}}(x)
pointwise as
\epsilon\to 0
, then

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}\to F\quad\text{locally uniformly in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),

where
F
is the unique bounded continuous viscosity solution of the equation

(42)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\partial_{t}F=\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)&\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
F(x,0)=F_{\text{in}}(x).\end{array}\right.

Notice that the one-parameter family
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
of interest in this paper (see (
39
)) satisfies assumptions (i)-(iii) according to Propositions
1
and
5
. It only remains to verify condition (iv). That is the subject of the next proposition.

Proposition 8
.

Assume that
\mathbf{P}\{f_{+},f_{-}\in\mathcal{S}\}=1
and (
13
) holds, and consider the operator
\mathscr{L}
given by (
25
). If
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is a nondecreasing function with bounded, uniformly continuous second derivative, rescaled according to the rule
\Phi_{\delta}(x)=\Phi(\delta x)
, and if
R_{\Phi}
is defined by the identity

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}\Phi_{\delta})(\delta^{-1}x)=\delta^{2}\sigma|\partial_{x}\Phi(x)|^{2}+\delta^{3}a|\partial_{x}\Phi(x)|\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)+\delta^{3}R_{\Phi}(\delta;x),

then

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup\left\{|R_{\Phi}(\delta;x)|\,\mid\,x\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=0.

The proof of the proposition is relegated to the end of this section.

There is one last technicality to address, namely, whereas Theorem
3
asks for the continuity of the initial datum
F_{\text{in}}
, the main theorem of this section allows for discontinuities. This gap is bridged using the fact that the equations of interest here are regularizing. This is made precise in the next condition:

(v)

Asymptotically regularizing:
If
\underline{F}
and
\overline{F}
denote the minimal and maximal viscosity sub- and supersolutions of the initial-value problem (
42
) for a given
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
(see Appendix
A.1
), then

\displaystyle\underline{F}=\overline{F}\quad\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty).

The next corollary asserts that if the family
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
satisfies assumption (v) in addition to (i)-(iv), then Theorem
3
remains true even if
F_{\text{in}}
has discontinuities.

Corollary 3
.

Assume that
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
is a family of operators on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfying assumptions (i)-(v) above. Then the statement of Theorem
3
remains true for an arbitrary
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
provided
F
is taken to be the unique discontinuous viscosity solution of the initial-value problem (
42
).

See Appendix
B
for the proof.

In Theorem
2
, the nonlinearity
\mathcal{F}
takes the form (
5
), which satisfies assumption (v) by Propositions
10
and
11
below. Therefore, the proof of the theorem is complete.

Remark 2
.

Theorem
3
is stated for semigroups defined in
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
, the space of nondecreasing,
right-continuous
functions taking values in
[0,1]
. There is nothing special about right-continuity, however. In fact, let
F_{-}
and
F^{+}
denote the left- and right-increasing versions of a nondecreasing function
F
. If operators
\{T_{\epsilon,-}\}
are defined on the space
CDF_{\text{left}}(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
of nondecreasing, left-continuous functions taking values in
[0,1]
by the formula
T_{\epsilon,-}F=(T_{\epsilon}(F^{+}))_{-}
, then
\{T_{\epsilon,-}\}
satisfies assumptions (i)-(iv) if and only if
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
does. Further, it is not hard to see that a PDE scaling limit of
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
holds as in Theorem

if and only if the same conclusion applies to
\{T_{\epsilon,-}\}
.

5.2.
Proof of Corollary
1

It only remains to reinterpret Theorem
2
in terms of the
\text{Beta}(2,1)
and
\text{Beta}(2,2)
distributions.

Let
\{X^{(n)}\}
be the solution of the RDE (
7
) started from an arbitrary initial datum. According to the theorem,

(43)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\{N^{-1/\alpha}X^{(N)}\leq x\}=F(x,1),

where
F
is the unique bounded discontinuous viscosity solution of (
42
) with initial datum
F_{\text{in}}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,+\infty)}
.

If
\sigma<0
, then
\mathcal{F}(p,X)=\sigma|p|^{2}
and

\displaystyle F(x,t)=\frac{x^{2}}{4|\sigma|t}\quad\text{for each}\,\,x\in[0,2\sqrt{|\sigma|}t^{1/2}],

as can be proved directly or by using the Hopf-Lax formula (see Proposition
10
below). If
\sigma>0
, then one can instead study the asymptotics of
-X^{(n)}
reasoning as in Section
2.2
, which has the effect of flipping the sign of
\sigma
. In any event, (
43
) implies

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\{-\frac{\text{sgn}(\sigma)}{2\sqrt{|\sigma|}N^{1/2}}X^{(N)}\leq x\}=F(2\sqrt{|\sigma|}x,1)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}0,&\text{if}\,\,x<0,\\
x^{2},&\text{if}\,\,0\leq x\leq 1,\\
1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The right-hand side is precisely the CDF of the
\text{Beta}(2,1)
distribution. Thus, case (i) is complete.

If
\sigma=0
and
a>0
, then
\mathcal{F}(p,X)=a|p|X
and Proposition
11
asserts that
F(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho(y,t)\,dy
, where
\rho
is the unique continuous distributional solution of the porous medium equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\frac{1}{2}a\partial_{x}^{2}(\rho^{2})
with initial datum
\rho(0)=\delta_{0}
. This solution, called the Barenblatt (or ZKB) solution, is given by

\displaystyle\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{(6at)^{\frac{1}{3}}}\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}-\frac{x^{2}}{(6at)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{[-(9at/2)^{\frac{1}{3}},(9at/2)^{\frac{1}{3}}]}(x).

By (
43
), for any bounded continuous function
h
, if
Y^{(N)}=(36a)^{-\frac{1}{3}}N^{-1/3}X^{(N)}+\frac{1}{2}
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[h(Y^{(N)})]

\displaystyle=\int_{-(9a/2)^{\frac{1}{3}}}^{(9a/2)^{\frac{1}{3}}}h((36a)^{-\frac{1}{3}}x+2^{-1})\rho(x,1)\,dx=6\int_{0}^{1}h(y)y(1-y)\,dy,

and the integrand
6y(1-y)\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(y)
is nothing but the PDF of the
\text{Beta}(2,2)
distribution. This proves case (ii).

Finally, if
\sigma=a=0
, then the formula in Section
1.3.2
implies that
(3s-g_{\rho}(s))(s-g_{\rho}(s))=0
for all
s\geq 0
and
\rho\in\{+,-\}
almost surely. Since
g_{+}(s),g_{-}(s)\leq s
hold automatically by definition, this implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{f_{+}=f_{-}=0\}=\mathbf{P}\{g_{+}(s)=g_{-}(s)=s\,\,\text{for all}\,\,s\geq 0\}=1,

and thus
X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}X^{(0)}
for all
n
. This is case (iii).

5.3.
Proof of Proposition
8

Let
\omega_{\Phi}
denote the modulus of continuity of the second derivative
\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi
:

\displaystyle\omega_{\Phi}(\epsilon)=\sup\left\{|\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)-\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(y)|\,\mid\,x,y\in\mathbb{R},\,\,|x-y|\leq\epsilon\right\}.

In view of the representation of
\mathscr{L}
from Proposition
4
and symmetry (see Section
2.2
), it suffices to prove that

(44)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\delta^{-1}x}\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{\delta}(y_{1}+g_{+}(\delta^{-1}x-y_{1}))-\Phi_{\delta}(\delta^{-1}x)]\partial_{x}\Phi_{\delta}(y_{1})\,dy_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=-\delta^{2}(\partial_{x}\Phi(x))^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[|g_{+}(u)-u|]\,du

\displaystyle\qquad+\delta^{3}\Phi^{\prime}(x)\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x)\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[(g_{+}(u)-u)^{2}]\,du-\int_{0}^{\infty}u\mathbf{E}[g_{+}(u)-u]\,du\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{0}\delta^{3}\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[\omega_{\Phi}(\delta|g(u)-u|)\{g_{+}(u)-u\}^{2}]\,du\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\int_{0}^{\infty}u\omega_{\Phi}(\delta u)\mathbf{E}[|g(u)-u|]\,du+\delta\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{a}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[\{g_{+}(u)-u\}^{2}]u\,du\right),

where
\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\Phi\|_{\sup}+\|\partial_{x}\Phi\|_{\sup}+\|\partial^{2}_{x}\Phi\|_{\sup}
.
To this end, first, notice that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\delta^{-1}x}\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{\delta}(y_{1}+g_{+}(\delta^{-1}x-y_{1}))-\Phi_{\delta}(\delta^{-1}x)]\partial_{x}\Phi_{\delta}(y_{1})\,dy_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(x_{1}+\delta g_{+}(\delta^{-1}(x-x_{1})))-\Phi(x)]\partial_{x}\Phi(x_{1})\,dx_{1}.

Next, it is worth noting that, by definition, the difference
s\mapsto g_{+}(s)-s
is bounded above and below as follows

\displaystyle-\sup\left\{|f(u)|\,\mid\,u\geq 0\right\}\leq g_{+}(s)-s\leq 0.

Therefore, the assumption (
13
) implies that the integrals above are all finite.

Let
L
and
Q
be the Taylor polynomials

\displaystyle L(x_{1})=\partial_{x}\Phi(x)+\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)(x_{1}-x),\quad Q(x_{1})=\partial_{x}\Phi(x)(x_{1}-x)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)(x_{1}-x)^{2}.

For notational ease, define
x_{1,g}
according to the formula

\displaystyle x_{1,g}=x_{1}+\delta g(\delta^{-1}[x-x_{1}]).

The term of interest then becomes

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x}\{\Phi(x_{1,g})-\Phi(x)\}\partial_{x}\Phi(x_{1})\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}L(x_{1})Q(x_{1,g})\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-\infty}^{x}L(x_{1})\{\Phi(x_{1,g})-\Phi(x)-Q(x_{1,g})\}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-\infty}^{x}\{\partial_{x}\Phi(x_{1})-L(x_{1})\}\{\Phi(x_{1,g})-\Phi(x)\}\,dx_{1}.

Expanding the main term and using the change-of-variables
x_{1}=x+\delta u
leads to

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x}L(x_{1})\mathbf{E}[Q(x_{1,g})]\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}(\partial_{x}\Phi(x))^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[g_{+}(u)-u]\,du

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{3}\partial_{x}\Phi(x)\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbf{E}[(g_{+}(u)-u)^{2}]\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad-\delta^{3}\partial_{x}\Phi(x)\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x)\int_{-\infty}^{x}u\mathbf{E}[g_{+}(u)-u]\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{4}(\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x))^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{x}u\mathbf{E}[(g(u)-u)^{2}]\,dx_{1}.

Notice that the order
\delta^{2}
term has the desired form: In general,
g_{+}(s)\leq s
always holds so
g_{+}(s)-s=-|g_{+}(s)-s|
.

It only remains to estimate the error terms:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-\infty}^{x}L(x_{1})\{\Phi(x_{1,g})-\Phi(x)-Q(x_{1,g})\}\,dx_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\omega_{\Phi}(|x_{1,g}-x|)\left(1+|x_{1}-x|\right)\{x_{1,g}-x\}^{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\delta^{3}\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega_{\Phi}(\delta|g(u)-u|)(1+\delta|u|)\{g(u)-u\}^{2}\,du,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-\infty}^{x}\{\partial_{x}\Phi(x_{1})-L(x_{1})\}\{\Phi(x_{1,g})-\Phi(x)\}\,dx_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\omega_{\Phi}(|x_{1}-x|)|x_{1}-x||x_{1,g}-x|\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\delta^{3}\|\Phi\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega_{\Phi}(\delta u)u|g(u)-u|\,du.

∎

Appendix A
Viscosity Solutions

This section reviews relevant definitions and results from the theory of viscosity solutions. General references for this material are
[
13
,
7
]
. The main interest here is the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)=0&\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
F(x,0)=F_{\text{in}}(x).\end{array}\right.

Since the problem at hand naturally leads to consideration of possibly discontinuous initial data, some care will be needed in defining precisely what is meant by a solution, subsolution, or supersolution.

The next definition recalls what it means for a function to satisfy a partial differential equation in the viscosity sense.

Definition 1
.

An upper semicontinuous function
F:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to satisfy the differential inequality
\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial_{x}^{2}F)\leq 0
in the viscosity sense if it has the following property: If
\varphi
is a smooth function defined in some open set
U
and if there is a point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in U
at which
\varphi
touches
F
from above (that is,
F(x,t)\leq\varphi(x,t)
for all
(x,t)\in U
with equality when
(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})
), then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}),\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}))\leq 0.

A lower semi-continuous function
G:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to satisfy the differential inequality
\partial_{t}G-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}G,\partial_{x}^{2}G)\geq 0
in the viscosity sense if it has the following property: If
\varphi
is a smooth function defined in some open set
U
and if there is a point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in U
at which
\varphi
touches
F
from below (that is,
F(x,t)\geq\varphi(x,t)
for all
(x,t)\in U
with equality when
(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})
), then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}),\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}))\geq 0.

A continuous function
F:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to satisfy the differential equation
\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)=0
in the viscosity sense if it satisfies both
\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial_{x}^{2}F)\leq 0
and
\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)\geq 0
in the viscosity sense.

The next definition specifies what is meant by a solution, subsolution, or supersolution of the initial-value problem (
A
).

Definition 2
.

An upper semicontinuous function
F:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
said to be a
viscosity subsolution
of (
A
) if it satisfies
\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial_{x}^{2}F)\leq 0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and

(47)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{F(y,0)-F_{\text{in}}(x)\,\mid\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\text{such that}\,\,|y-x|\leq\delta\right\}\leq 0.

A lower semicontinuous function
G:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
viscosity supersolution
of (
A
) if it satisfies
\partial_{t}G-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}G,\partial^{2}_{x}G)\geq 0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\inf\left\{G(y,0)-F_{\text{in}}(x)\,\mid\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\text{such that}\,\,|y-x|\leq\delta\right\}\geq 0.

A continuous function
F:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
(continuous) viscosity solution
of (
A
) if it is both a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity supersolution.

Since the main theorem of this work, Theorem
1
, involves problems with a discontinuous initial datum, a different definition of solution is needed. In order to state it, it is necessary to recall the notions of
upper
and
lower semicontinuous envelopes.
Specifically, for a function
F:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, the upper and lower semicontinuous envelopes
F^{*}
and
F_{*}
are the functions defined in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
via the formulae

\displaystyle F^{*}(x,t)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{F(y,s)\,\mid\,y,s\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\,\,\text{such that}\,\,|x-y|+|t-s|\leq\delta\right\},

\displaystyle F_{*}(x,t)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\inf\left\{F(y,s)\,\mid\,y,s\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\,\,\text{such that}\,\,|x-y|+|t-s|\leq\delta\right\}.

Notice that
F^{*}
and
F_{*}
are always upper and lower semicontinuous, respectively.

Definition 3
.

A function
F:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
discontinuous viscosity solution of (
A
)
if
\partial_{t}F^{*}-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F^{*},\partial_{x}^{2}F^{*})\leq 0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
;
\partial_{t}F_{*}-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F_{*},\partial_{x}^{2}F_{*})\geq 0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
; and
F^{*}(\cdot,0)=(F_{\text{in}})^{*}
and
F_{*}(\cdot,0)=(F_{\text{in}})_{*}
in
\mathbb{R}
.

A.1.
Comparison Principle

This subsection begins by recalling the comparison principle for bounded sub- and supersolutions of the initial-value problem (
A
), which is standard. An existence and uniqueness result is then stated in terms of the maximal subsolution and minimal supersolution.

Theorem 4

(Comparison Principle)
.

Assume that the nonlinearity
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is jointly continuous and nondecreasing in the second argument. If
F,G:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
are respectively a bounded upper and a bounded lower semicontinuous function such that
\partial_{t}F-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial_{x}^{2}F)\leq 0
and
\partial_{t}G-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}G,\partial^{2}_{x}G)\geq 0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{F(x,0)-G(y,0)\,\mid\,x,y\in\mathbb{R},\,\,|x-y|\leq\delta\right\}\leq 0,

then
F\leq G
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
.

In particular, if
F
and
G
are respectively bounded viscosity sub- and supersolutions of (
A
) for some initial datum
F_{\text{in}}
, then
F\leq G
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
.

The reader is referred to
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
, for instance, for the proof. The comparison principle implies that there is at most one continuous viscosity solutions of (
A
). Existence of the solution is also known in the present setting. However, there is no need to state an existence result here as it actually follows from the main convergence results (see the proof of Theorem
3
).

Let
\overline{F}
and
\underline{F}
be the maximal sub- and minimal supersolution of (
A
):

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{F(x,t)\,\mid\,F\,\,\text{viscosity subsolution of}\,\,\eqref{E: initial value problem appendix}\right\},

\displaystyle\underline{F}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{G(x,t)\,\mid\,G\,\,\text{viscosity supersolution of}\,\,\eqref{E: initial value problem appendix}\right\}.

Notice that the comparison principle implies that
\overline{F}\leq\underline{F}
and any viscosity solution (continuous or discontinuous) lies in between the two. This immediately implies a uniqueness criterion.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\overline{F}
and
\underline{F}
be the maximal subsolution and minimal supersolution of (
A
), respectively. If
\underline{F}=\overline{F}
in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
, then any discontinuous viscosity solution
F
of (
A
) must also satisfy
F=\overline{F}
in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
. In particular, in this case, modulo the freedom to choose the value at discontinuities of
F_{\text{in}}
at
t=0
, there is a unique discontinuous viscosity solution of (
A
).

Proof.

As in the discussion preceding the statement, if
F
is any discontinuous viscosity solution, then
\overline{F}\leq F\leq\underline{F}
in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
in general. Therefore, in the present setting,
F=\overline{F}=\underline{F}
for
t>0
, implying that there is at most one discontinuous viscosity solution. The fact that at least one exists is similarly immediate: The equality
\overline{F}=\underline{F}
in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
actually implies that both
\overline{F}
and
\underline{F}
are discontinuous viscosity solutions. ∎

A.2.
Uniqueness for Nondecreasing Data

In this section, the PDE’s of interest here are shown to satisfy the uniqueness criterion of Proposition
9
. For the Hamilton-Jacobi equations arising in the
\text{Beta}(2,1)
case, the fact that almost-everywhere-continuous data leads to a unique solution at positive times is known from
[
10
]
. In the case of the second-order equation in the
\text{Beta}(2,2)
case, the uniqueness of solutions follows from the characterization of nonnegative solutions of the porous medium equation. Short, simplified proofs are presented here for the reader’s convenience.

First, consider the case when
\mathcal{F}
is a first-order operator of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(p,X)=-H(p)

for some convex or concave function
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
exhibiting superlinear growth:

(48)

\displaystyle\lim_{|p|\to\infty}|p|^{-1}H(p)=+\infty.

At this stage, it is worth noting that there is no loss of generality in assuming that
H
is convex. Indeed, in general, if
\partial_{t}F+H(\partial_{x}F)=0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
, then the function
G(x,t)=1-F(-x,t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G-H(-\partial_{x}G)=0\quad\text{in the viscosity sense in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)

and
H
is convex if and only if the function
p\mapsto-H(-p)
is concave.

For superlinear convex/concave
H
, the Hopf-Lax formula furnishes the unique discontinuous viscosity solution of (
A
).

Proposition 10
.

Let
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. If
\mathcal{F}(p,X)=-H(p)
for a convex
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying (i) and (ii) above, and if
\overline{F}
and
\underline{F}
are the maximal subsolution and minimal supersolution, respectively, of (
A
), then, for any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
,

(49)

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x,t)=\underline{F}(x,t)=\inf\left\{F_{\text{in}}(y)+tL\left(\frac{x-y}{t}\right)\,\mid\,y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

where
L
is the Legendre-Fenchel transform of
H
.

Some regularity of
F_{\text{in}}
is necessary to ensure that
\overline{F}
and
\underline{F}
coincide at positive times. For instance,
F_{\text{in}}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
provides a counterexample. An optimal version of the result can be found in
[
10
]
.

Proof.

When
F_{\text{in}}
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous, the fact that the right-hand side of (
49
) determines the (continuous) viscosity solution is classical (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Section 10.3.4]
). Otherwise, let
\{F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}\}\subseteq CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
be a sequence of uniformly Lipschitz continuous functions such that

\displaystyle F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}\leq F^{(N+1)}_{\text{in}}<F_{\text{in}}\quad\text{pointwise for each}\,\,N\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}(x)=F_{\text{in}}(x)\quad\text{for each point of continuity}\,\,x\,\,\text{of}\,\,F_{\text{in}}.

If
\{F_{N}\}
are the corresponding solutions of (
A
), then the comparison principle implies that
F_{N}\leq F_{N+1}\leq\overline{F}
for each
N
. By the stability of viscosity solutions, the limit
F=\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}
satisfies
\partial_{t}F-H(\partial_{x}F)=0
in the viscosity sense in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
.

At the same time, recall that the superlinearity of
H
implies that
L
is also superlinear. Therefore, for any
(x,t)
, it is possible to write
F_{N}(x,t)=F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}(y_{N})+tL((x-y_{N})/t)
for some bounded sequence
\{y_{N}\}
. If
y_{N}\to y
as
N\to\infty
(say, along a subsequence), then

F_{\text{in}}(y-\delta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}(y-\delta)\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}(y_{N})\quad\text{for almost every}\,\,\delta>0,

from which it follows that

\displaystyle\inf\left\{F_{\text{in}}(y)+tL\left(\frac{x-y}{t}\right)\,\mid\,y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}(x,t)=F(x,t)\leq\overline{F}(x,t).

\underline{F}
can be treated similarly: Let
\{F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}\}\subseteq CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be uniformly Lipschitz functions such that
F_{\text{in}}<F^{(N+1)}_{\text{in}}\leq F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}
for each
N
and
F_{\text{in}}(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}(x)
at points of continuity
x
of
F_{\text{in}}
. This time, if
F_{N}
is the corresponding solution of (
A
), then, for any point of continuity
y
of
F_{\text{in}}
and any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\underline{F}(x,t)\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}(x,t)\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{\text{in}}^{(N)}(y)+tL(\frac{x-y}{t})=F_{\text{in}}(y)+tL(\frac{x-y}{t}).

Since
F
is right-continuous and points of continuity are dense, this proves
\underline{F}
is bounded above by the right-hand side of (
49
).
∎

Next, consider the case of the nonlinearity

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(p,X)=a|p|^{m-1}X

Notice that if
F
were a smooth solution of
\partial_{t}F-a|\partial_{x}F|^{m-1}\partial_{x}^{2}F=0
, then the derivative
\rho=\partial_{x}F
would solve the porous medium equation (PME)
\partial_{t}\rho=\frac{a}{m}\partial_{x}^{2}(\rho^{m})
. To prove the uniqueness of solutions with possibly discontinuous, nondecreasing data, this reasoning will be inverted.

Proposition 11
.

Let
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. If
\mathcal{F}
has the form (
50
) for some
m>1
, then the maximal subsolution
\overline{F}
and minimal supersolution
\underline{F}
of (
A
) are given for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
by the formula

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x,t)=\underline{F}(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho(y,t)\,dy,

where
\rho\geq 0
is the unique continuous distributional solution of the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\partial_{t}\rho-\frac{a}{m}\partial_{x}^{2}(\rho^{m})=0&\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
\rho(0)=\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}.\end{array}\right.

When
F_{\text{in}}
is uniformly Lipschitz, this result is a special case of
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Unless otherwise noted, basic properties of the PME used in the proof that follows can be found in the monograph
[
21
]
.

Proof.

First, assume that
F_{\text{in}}
is smooth and
\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}
is bounded and nowhere vanishing. In this case, classical results on the PME imply that there is a unique smooth solution
\rho
of (
11
), which is positive and satisfies
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(y,t)\,dy=F_{\text{in}}(+\infty)-F_{\text{in}}(-\infty)
for all
t>0
. Since
\rho
is smooth, the function
F(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho(y,t)\,dy
is also, and, thus, it satisfies
\partial_{t}F-a|\partial_{x}F|^{m-1}\partial_{x}^{2}F=\partial_{t}F-\frac{a}{m}\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}F)^{m}=0
classically. As is well-known, being a classical solution, it is also a viscosity solution.

If
F_{\text{in}}
is merely absolutely continuous with
\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, nonetheless
\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}
can be approximated in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
by smooth, nowhere-vanishing functions. The
L^{1}
contraction property of the PME then implies that the corresponding solutions converge in
L^{\infty}([0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
, which implies uniform convergence of the antiderivatives. Thus, since the viscosity solution property is stable under uniform convergence, even if
\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}
is merely in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, the unique viscosity solution of (
A
) in this case is still given as the antiderivative of
\rho
.

Finally, it remains to consider the case when
\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}
is only a finite positive measure (so that
F_{\text{in}}
may have discontinuities). Let
\{F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}\}\subseteq CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
be smooth functions such that
F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}\leq F^{(N+1)}_{\text{in}}<F_{\text{in}}
for each
N
,
F^{(N)}_{\text{in}}\to F_{\text{in}}
at points of continuity as
N\to\infty
. If
\rho_{N}
is the corresponding solution of the PME (
11
) and
F_{N}
, its antiderivative, then
F_{N}\leq\overline{F}
for each
N
since
F_{N}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
A
).

At the same time, some subsequence of
\{\rho_{N}\}
converges locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
as
N\to\infty
to a continuous, nonnegative distributional solution
\rho
of (
11
) with initial trace equal to the measure
\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}}
; this follows, for instance, from the fact that, in this one-dimensional setting, the pressure
\{\rho_{N}^{m-1}\}
is both uniformly bounded and uniformly Lipschitz in
\mathbb{R}\times[\tau,+\infty)
for any
\tau>0
, independently of
N
(see
[
21
, Chapter 14]
). A classical result of Dahlberg and Kenig
[
14
]
implies that
\rho
is unique, hence the entire sequence
\{\rho_{N}\}
converges to
\rho
. At the level of antiderivatives, this implies
F_{N}\to F
as
N\to\infty
, where
F
is given by (
11
) and is a discontinuous viscosity solution of (
A
). By comparison,
\overline{F}\leq F
, hence actually
F=\overline{F}
.

Approximating
F_{\text{in}}
from above, one similarly finds
F=\underline{F}
. ∎

A.3.
Viscosity Solutions in
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})

Recall that throughout this work, the functions of interest belong to the space
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
of CDF’s of extended-real-valued random variables. (Recall that this is nothing other than the space of nondecreasing, right-continuous functions taking values in
[0,1]
.) This section covers some specific simplifications of viscosity solutions theory that are applicable as long as the sub- or supersolutions in question are nondecreasing in the spatial variable. Specifically, it shows that, in this case, there is no loss of generality assuming that the test functions involved are themselves nondecreasing in space and defined globally in space.

The first lemma concerns the case when a subsolution is touched above by a test function that is increasing close to the touching point.

Lemma 1
.

Fix
T,A>0
and let
F:\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)\to[0,A)
be an upper semicontinuous function that is nondecreasing in the spatial variable. Fix
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and let
\varphi
be a smooth function defined in some neighborhood of
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
such that
\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})<A
and
\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})>0
. If
\varphi
touches
F
from above at
(x_{0},t_{0})
, then there is an
r>0
, a nondecreasing function
\psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
with
\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{1}(x_{0}-r)
for each

and
\psi(x)=A
for each

, and a

function
\psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\psi_{2}(t_{0})=0
such that if
\psi
is the function

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)=\psi_{1}(x)+\psi_{2}(t),

then

\displaystyle F(x,t)\leq\psi(x,t)\quad\text{for each}\,\,(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]

and equality holds if and only if
(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})
.

Proof.

By Taylor expansion, there is an
r>0
and degree two polynomials

and

such that

and

\displaystyle F(x,t)\leq\varphi(x,t)\leq R(x)+Q(t-t_{0})\quad\text{for each}\,\,(x,t)\in[x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r]\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]

Adding a term of the form
(x-x_{0})^{2}+(t-t_{0})^{2}
to
R+Q
, there is no loss of generality in assuming in addition that
\varphi(x,t)=R(x)+Q(t-t_{0})
if and only if
(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})
. Since
0<\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})=\partial_{x}R(x_{0})
, after possibly making
r
smaller, it can similarly be assumed that
\partial_{x}R(x)>0
for each

Given

and
\rho(x)=1
if

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)=(1-\rho(x))R(x)+A\rho(x)\mathbf{1}_{[x_{0},+\infty)}(x)+R(x_{0}-\frac{a}{2})\rho(x)\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,x_{0}]}(x).

Since

is small enough. Fix such a small

henceforth and let

for each

, the function
\psi(x,t)=\psi_{1}(x)+\psi_{2}(t)

The next lemma concerns the case when a subsolution is touched above by a test function that is flat close to the touching point.

Lemma 2
.

Fix
T,A>0
and let

be an upper semicontinuous function that is nondecreasing in the spatial variable. Assume that
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and
\varphi
is a smooth function defined in some neighborhood of
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
such that
\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})<A
and
\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})=\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})=0
. If
\varphi
touches
F
from above at
(x_{0},t_{0})
, then there is an
r>0
, a nondecreasing
C^{2}
function
\psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
with
\psi(x)=\psi(x_{0})
for each

and
\psi(x)=A
for each

, and a

function
\psi_{2}:[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]\to\mathbb{R}
with
\psi_{2}(t_{0})=0
such that if
\psi
is the function

then

\displaystyle F(x,t)\leq\psi(x,t)\quad\text{for each}\,\,(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(x_{0},t_{0})\in\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]\,\mid\,F(x,t)=\psi(x,t)\}\subseteq(-\infty,x_{0}]\times\{t_{0}\}.

Proof.

For concreteness, choose

such that
F(x,t)\leq\varphi(x,t)
for each
(x,t)\in[x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r]\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]
with equality when
(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})
. Expanding
\varphi
in a fourth-order Taylor expansion around
(x_{0},t_{0})
, one finds

\displaystyle\varphi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})+Q(t-t_{0})+\frac{1}{3!}\partial^{3}_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})(x-x_{0})^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4!}\partial^{4}_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})(x-x_{0})^{4}+o(|x-x_{0}|^{4}+|t-t_{0}|^{4}),

where
Q
is some polynomial of degree four satisfying

. Since

is nondecreasing in the
x

F(x_{0},t_{0})=\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})
; and
F(x,t)\leq\varphi(x,t)
for

close enough to
(x_{0},t_{0})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\partial^{3}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})\geq 0.

Let
\alpha=\max\{1+\partial_{x}^{4}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}),0\}
. Making

smaller if necessary, there is no loss of generality in assuming that, for each
(x,t)\in[x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r]\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]

\displaystyle\varphi(x,t)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})+Q(t-t_{0})+\frac{(t-t_{0})^{4}}{4!}+\frac{1}{3!}\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})(x-x_{0})^{3}+\frac{1}{4!}\alpha(x-x_{0})^{4}

\displaystyle=:P(x,t).

with equality if and only if
(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})
.

has the desired form, but it is not nondecreasing in

Define the nondecreasing function

by

\displaystyle\eta(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})+\frac{1}{3!}\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})(x-x_{0})^{3}+\frac{1}{4!}\alpha(x-x_{0})^{4},&\text{if}\,\,x_{0}\leq x\\
\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}),&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Notice that
\eta
is

. Given
a\leq r
, let
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a smooth, nondecreasing function such that
\rho(x)=0
if

and

henceforth and let

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)=h(x)+Q(t-t_{0})+\frac{(t-t_{0})^{4}}{4!}.

Since

in

,

is nondecreasing in the

\mathbb{R}\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r]
with equality only if

and

and

Appendix B
Scaling Limits of Monotone Semigroups in
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})

This appendix proves Theorem
3
, the extension of the convergence framework of
[
6
]
to approximations of PDE’s posed in
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. As described already briefly in the introduction, technical issues arise stemming from the fact that
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
is not a vector space, and the smooth test functions used in the definition of viscosity solution are not necessarily nondecreasing. These issues are not fatal and, indeed, similar ones have been overcome in other settings, such as geometric flows (see
[
9
]
).

In the proof of the theorem, there is one technicality that needs to be overcome. When one checks that, for instance, the limit
F
satisfies
\partial_{t}F\leq\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)
, it is desirable to invoke the asymptotics of
\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}\varphi
for an arbitrary smooth test function
\varphi
that touches
F
from above. If
\partial_{x}\varphi>0
at the contact point, then Lemma
1
above shows that
\varphi
can be assumed to be nondecreasing in
\mathbb{R}
and then
\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}\varphi
is well-defined. Otherwise, if
\partial_{x}\varphi=0
at the contact point, of course,
\varphi
generally will not be monotone at all, and some work is required to proceed.

At the same time,
smooth
nondecreasing functions have a particular property that is suggestive here: If
\Phi
is a smooth nondecreasing function in
\mathbb{R}
, and if
\partial_{x}\Phi(x_{0})=0
at some point
x_{0}
, then
\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi(x_{0})=0
. This motivates the following lemma (which applies even if the subsolution is not monotone in the spatial variable).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function that is nondecreasing in the second argument. An upper semicontinuous function
F:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\partial_{t}F\leq\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F,\partial^{2}_{x}F)
in the viscosity sense if and only if it has the following property: If
\varphi
is a smooth test function that touches
F
from above at some point
(x_{0},t_{0})
and

\displaystyle\text{either (i)}\quad\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})\neq 0,\quad\text{or (ii)}\quad\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})=\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})=0,

then

\partial_{t}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})\leq\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0}),\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},t_{0})).

Proof.

The fact that this weaker definition of viscosity subsolution is equivalent to the usual one can be proved exactly as in
[
8
, Proposition 2.2]
(see also
[
20
, Proof of Theorem 1.2]
). ∎

Remark 3
.

A corresponding version of Lemma
3
holds for supersolutions, which can be deduced from the transformation
G(x,t)=1-F(-x,t)
.

Now Lemma
3
can be leveraged in conjunction with Lemma
2
above to resolve the issue when
\partial_{x}\varphi=0
at the contact point: There is no loss of generality assuming that
\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi=0
also holds there, and then the lemma shows that
\varphi
can be taken to be globally nondecreasing in the spatial variable.

B.1.
Proof of Theorem
3

Since
F_{\epsilon}
is discontinuous in time, it will clarify the picture somewhat to observe that the statement that
F_{\epsilon}\to F
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
is equivalent to the following: For each
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
,

(53)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{|F^{\epsilon}_{n}(y)-F(x,t)|\,\mid\,|y-x|+|\epsilon^{\alpha}n-t|\leq\delta\right\}=0.

This is the precise statement that will be proved below.

Since the operators
(T_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
are monotone and nondecreasing continuous functions can be approximated locally uniformly from above and below by smooth functions, it suffices to consider the case when
F^{\epsilon}_{0}=F_{\text{in}}
for each
\epsilon>0
and
F_{\text{in}}
has a bounded, uniformly continuous second derivative. This will be assumed for the remainder of the proof.

Throughout what follows, define the constants
c_{-},c_{+}\in[0,1]
by

(54)

\displaystyle c_{-}=F_{\text{in}}(-\infty)=F_{\epsilon}(+\infty,t),\quad c_{+}=F_{\text{in}}(+\infty)=F_{\epsilon}(-\infty,t),

where property (iii) implies that the identities above hold independently of
t>0
.

Step 1: Lipschitz Estimate in Time.
Since
F_{\text{in}}
is currently assumed to have a bounded, uniformly continuous second derivative, by the consistency condition (iv),

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-\alpha}\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}F_{0}^{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-\alpha}\mathscr{L}_{\epsilon}F_{\text{in}}=\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F_{\text{in}},\partial^{2}_{x}F_{\text{in}})+R_{\epsilon}

where

uniformly with respect to

as
\epsilon\to 0
. Thus, since
\mathcal{F}
is bounded on compact sets by continuity, there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that, for sufficiently small
\epsilon

\displaystyle\|T_{\epsilon}F_{0}^{\epsilon}-F_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{\text{sup}}\leq C_{0}\epsilon^{\alpha}\quad\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}.

In Proposition
12
below, the assumptions (i)-(iii) are shown to imply the following contractivity property of

\displaystyle\|T_{\epsilon}F-T_{\epsilon}G\|_{\text{sup}}\leq\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}.

Thus, the estimate of
T_{\epsilon}F_{0}^{\epsilon}-F_{0}^{\epsilon}
may be iterated to find

\displaystyle\|F^{\epsilon}_{n+1}-F_{n}^{\epsilon}\|_{\text{sup}}=\|T_{\epsilon}F^{\epsilon}_{n}-F_{n}^{\epsilon}\|_{\text{sup}}\leq C_{0}\epsilon^{\alpha}\quad\text{for each}\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Note, in particular, that
|F^{\epsilon}_{n}(x)-F_{\text{in}}(x)|\leq C_{0}\epsilon^{\alpha}n
uniformly in
(x,n)
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Step 3: Convergence.
The proof uses the classical half-relaxed limit method. Let
F_{\star}
and

be the half-relaxed limits of

defined by

\displaystyle F_{\star}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\inf\left\{F_{n}^{\epsilon}(y)\,\mid\,|x-y|+|\epsilon^{\alpha}n-t|+\epsilon\leq\delta\right\},

\displaystyle F^{\star}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{F_{n}^{\epsilon}(y)\,\mid\,|x-y|+|\epsilon^{\alpha}n-t|+\epsilon\leq\delta\right\},

To prove the local convergence statement (
53
), it suffices to show that

(see
[
13

or
[
7

). To do this, since

is immediate, it is only necessary to show that

. This will follow from the comparison principle, Theorem
4
, as soon as it is established that

and
F_{\star}
define respectively a viscosity sub- and a viscosity supersolution of the initial value problem (

Step 4:
F^{\star}
is a Subsolution.
In view of what was proved in Step 1, it is clear that

in the sense of (

In order to avoid boundary issues that can arise if

is a subsolution, it suffices to prove that, for any

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G^{\star}\leq\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}G^{\star},\partial_{x}^{2}G^{\star}).

For the rest of this step, fix

\displaystyle G^{\epsilon}_{n+1}\leq T_{\epsilon}G^{\epsilon}_{n}\quad\text{pointwise for each}\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Further, by definition of

\displaystyle G^{\star}(x,t)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{G^{\epsilon}_{n}(y)\,\mid\,|x-y|+|\epsilon^{\alpha}n-t|+\epsilon\leq\delta\right\}.

Thus, in passing from

Suppose that

According to Lemma

\displaystyle G^{\star}(x,t)\leq\varphi(x,t)\quad\text{for each}\,\,(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[t_{0}-r,t_{0}+r],

\displaystyle(x_{0},t_{0})\in\{(x,t)\,\mid\,G^{\star}(x,t)=\varphi(x,t)\}\subseteq(-\infty,x_{0}]\times\{t_{0}\}.

Step 5:

on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
via the formula

\displaystyle G_{\text{rev}}(x)=1-G_{-}(-x).

Let
\{\tilde{T}_{\epsilon}\}
be the operators on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
obtained from
\{T_{\epsilon}\}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{\epsilon}F=(T_{\epsilon}(F_{\text{rev}}))_{\text{rev}}.

It is straightforward to check that the family
\{\tilde{T}_{\epsilon}\}
satisfies assumptions (i)-(iv), the only difference being
\mathcal{F}
has to be replaced by the function
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(p,X)=-\mathcal{F}(p,-X)
.

Define
\{\tilde{F}^{\epsilon}_{n}\}
by the rule
\tilde{F}^{\epsilon}_{n}=(F^{\epsilon}_{n})_{\text{rev}}
. Notice that the recursion
F^{\epsilon}_{n+1}=T_{\epsilon}F^{\epsilon}_{n}
implies that
\tilde{F}^{\epsilon}_{n+1}=\tilde{T}_{\epsilon}\tilde{F}^{\epsilon}_{n}
. Therefore, if
\tilde{F}^{\star}
is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\star}(x,t)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{\tilde{F}^{\epsilon}_{n}(y)\,\mid\,|y-x|+|\epsilon^{\alpha}n-t|+\epsilon\leq\delta\right\},

then
\tilde{F}^{\star}
is a subsolution of the initial value problem (
A
) with
\mathcal{F}
replaced by
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
and
F_{\text{in}}
by
(F_{\text{in}})_{\text{rev}}
. Further, by definition,
\tilde{F}^{\star}(x,t)=1-F_{\star}(-x,t)
. From these last two observations, one readily concludes that
F_{\star}
is a viscosity supersolution of (
A
).∎

B.2.
Proof of Corollary
3

In the proof that follows, it it convenient to work with the half-relaxed limits
\liminf_{*}F_{\epsilon}
and
\limsup_{*}F_{\epsilon}
of the rescaled CDF
F_{\epsilon}
defined by

\displaystyle(\liminf\nolimits_{*}F_{\epsilon})(x,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\inf\left\{F_{\epsilon}(y,s)\,\mid\,|y-x|+|s-t|+\epsilon\leq\delta\right\},

\displaystyle(\limsup\nolimits_{*}F_{\epsilon})(x,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\left\{F_{\epsilon}(y,s)\,\mid\,|y-x|+|s-t|+\epsilon\leq\delta\right\}.

Proof of Corollary
3
.

Fix
F_{\text{in}}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. Suppose that
F_{\epsilon}(x,0)\to F_{\text{in}}(x)
at each point of continuity
x
of
F_{\text{in}}
. Let
\overline{F}
and
\underline{F}
be the maximal subsolution and the minimal supersolution of the problem (
A
). In view of Proposition
9
and well-known properties of half-relaxed limits (see
[
13
, Remark 6.4]
or
[
7
, Lemma 6.2]
), to see that
F_{\epsilon}
converges locally uniformly to the discontinuous viscosity solution, it suffices to establish that

\displaystyle\overline{F}\leq\liminf\nolimits_{*}F_{\epsilon}\leq\limsup\nolimits^{*}F_{\epsilon}\leq\underline{F}\quad\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty).

The details will be provided only for the lower bound as the upper bound follows similarly.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define
F_{\epsilon,N}
like
F_{\epsilon}
, albeit instead with the initial datum

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon,N}(x,0)=2^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{\epsilon}(x-y,0)\rho(2^{N}y)\,dy,

where
\rho:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a smooth function supported in
[1,2]
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(y)\,dy=1
. From the fact that
\rho
is supported in
[1,2]
and
F_{\epsilon}
is nondecreasing in
x
, it follows that
F_{\epsilon,N}(x,0)\leq F_{\epsilon,N+1}(x,0)\leq F_{\epsilon}(x,0)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Due to the monotonicity assumption (i), this implies that
F_{\epsilon,N}\leq F_{\epsilon,N+1}\leq F_{\epsilon}
in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
for each
\epsilon>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. By Theorem
3
,
F_{\epsilon,N}\to F_{N}
as
\epsilon\to 0
, where
F_{N}
is the solution of (
A
) with initial datum
F_{\text{in}}^{(N)}
given by
F_{\text{in}}^{(N)}(x)=2^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{\text{in}}(x-y)\rho(2^{N}y)\,dy
. Note that
F_{N}\leq\liminf\nolimits_{*}F_{\epsilon}
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
for any fixed
N
.

Standard arguments (see
[
7
, Theorem 6.2]
) show that the function
F_{\infty}
defined by

\displaystyle F_{\infty}(x,t)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\inf\left\{F_{N}(y,s)\,\mid\,|y-x|+|t-s|+N^{-1}\leq\delta\right\}

satisfies
\partial_{t}F_{\infty}-\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}F_{\infty},\partial_{x}^{2}F_{\infty})\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and
F_{\infty}
equals the left-continuous version of
F_{\text{in}}
at
t=0
. Therefore, by the comparison principle,
\overline{F}\leq F_{\infty}
in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
, and this implies
\overline{F}\leq F_{\infty}\leq\liminf\nolimits_{*}F_{\epsilon}
as desired. ∎

B.3.
Contractivity

This subsection establishes that operators such as the family
(T_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
are contractive on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
with respect to the supremum norm.

Proposition 12
.

Suppose that
T
is an operator on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfying the following two properties:

(i)

Monotonicity:
If
F\leq G
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
, then
TF\leq TG
also holds.

(ii)

Commutation with constants:
If
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies
c_{-}\leq F\leq c_{+}
for some constants
c_{-},c_{+}\in[0,1]
, then
T(F+c)=TF
for any
c\in[-c_{-},1-c_{+}]
.

(iii)

Stationarity at infinity:
For any
F\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
,
TF(+\infty)=F(+\infty)
and
TF(-\infty)=F(-\infty)
.

Then
T
is contractive, that is,

\displaystyle\|TF-TG\|_{\sup}\leq\|F-G\|_{\sup}\quad\text{for any}\,\,F,G\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}}).

The proof is based on the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
T
be an operator on
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
satisfying assumptions (i)-(iii) of Proposition
12
. If
F_{1},F_{2}\in CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
are chosen so that

\displaystyle F_{2}\leq TF_{1}\quad\text{pointwise in}\,\,\mathbb{R},

then, for any
\delta\in[0,1]
and
c\in[0,1-\delta]
, the functions
G_{1},G_{2}
defined by

G_{i}=\max\{F_{i}-\delta,c\}

satisfy
G_{2}\leq TG_{1}
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Notice that
G_{1}+\delta=\max\{F_{1},c+\delta\}
, which is a function in
CDF(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
that is pointwise larger than
F_{1}
. Therefore, by monotonicity,

\displaystyle T(G_{1}+\delta)\geq TF_{1}\geq F_{2}.

On the other hand, since property (iii) implies
T(G_{1}+\delta)(-\infty)=G_{1}(-\infty)+\delta\geq c+\delta
, one deduces that
T(G_{1}+\delta)\geq c+\delta
pointwise. Therefore,
T(G_{1}+\delta)\geq\max\{F_{2},c+\delta\}=G_{2}+\delta
follows. By property (ii), this implies
TG_{1}\geq G_{2}
.
∎

Here is the proof of contractivity:

Proof of Proposition
12
.

Let
F,G\in\text{CDF}(\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. Note that

\displaystyle G-\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}\leq F\leq G+\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}

and the goal is to show that the same inequality holds if
(F,G)
is replaced by
(TF,TG)
. By symmetry, it suffices to prove that
TF\leq TG+\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}
.

Since the desired inequality is trivial if
\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}\geq 1
, assume that
\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}<1
in what follows.

Let
G_{1}=\max\{F-\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}},0\}
and
G_{2}=\max\{TF-\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}},0\}
. By Lemma
4
,

\displaystyle G_{2}\leq TG_{1}.

Yet
G_{1}\leq G
holds so
G_{2}\leq TG_{1}\leq TG
by (i). This implies
TF-\|F-G\|_{\text{sup}}\leq TG
.∎

Appendix C
Effective Coefficients in the Series-Parallel Graph

This appendix computes the constants
\sigma_{D}
and
a_{R}
for the logarithms of the distance
D^{(n)}
and the resistance
R^{(n)}
of the critical series-parallel graph. In either case, the relevant forcing
f
takes the form

\displaystyle f(u)=\log(1+e^{-u}).

The left-inverse
g
is given by

\displaystyle g(s)=\log(e^{s}-1)\quad\text{if}\,\,s\geq\log 2,\quad g(s)=0,\quad\text{otherwise.}

Since
g(s)+f(g(s))=s
for
s\geq\log 2
, the formulas from Section
1.3.2
become

\displaystyle\sigma_{D}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\log 2}s\,ds-\int_{\log 2}^{\infty}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-s}}\right)\,ds,

\displaystyle a_{R}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\log 2}s^{2}\,ds+\int_{\log 2}^{\infty}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-s}}\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{0}^{\log 2}s^{2}\,ds+2\int_{\log 2}^{\infty}s\log\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-s}}\right)\,ds

The integrals above can be computed using polylogarithms, a family of functions related to the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
. The dilogarithm and trilogarithm
Li_{2}
and
Li_{3}
are defined for
t\in[0,1]
by the formulas

Li_{2}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{x}\log(1-x)\,dt,\quad Li_{3}(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{x}Li_{2}(x)\,dx.

These functions have the power series representation
Li_{k}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-k}t^{n}
, hence, in particular,
Li_{k}(1)=\zeta(k)
. The computations that follow seem to be related to Euler’s formula for
Li_{2}(\frac{1}{2})
and Landen’s formula for
Li_{3}(\frac{1}{2})
(see
[
19
, Section 1.1]
).

To compute the integrals, use the change-of-variables
x=e^{-s}
,
ds=\frac{1}{x}dx
. In the case of
\sigma_{D}
, this leads to

\displaystyle\sigma_{D}

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\log(x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{2})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\log(x)\frac{1}{1-x}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{2})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{2})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}Li_{2}(1)=-\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2).

In the case of
a_{R}
, this change-of-variables yields

\displaystyle a_{R}

\displaystyle=3\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\log^{2}(x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx+2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(x)\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx

After integrating-by-parts and changing variables in the second integral, one has

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(x)\log(1-x)\frac{1}{1-x}\,dx+\log^{3}(\frac{1}{2})

\displaystyle=2\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\log(x)\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx+\log^{3}(\frac{1}{2}).

This leads to

\displaystyle a_{R}

\displaystyle=-\log^{3}(\frac{1}{2})+2\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\log(x)\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx+\log^{3}(\frac{1}{2})+2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(x)\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}\log(x)\log(1-x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx.

Finally, integrating-by-parts again and invoking the definitions of
Li_{2}
and
Li_{3}
yields

\displaystyle a_{R}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}Li_{2}(x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx-Li_{2}(1)\log(1)=2\int_{0}^{1}Li_{2}(x)\frac{1}{x}\,dx=2Li_{3}(1)=2\zeta(3).
