Investigation of area-dependent high-power failure of film bulk acoustic resonators
Authors: Yiran Wei, Danyu Mu, Wei Wang, Feng Gao, Weipeng Xuan, Hao Jin, Jikui Luo, Shurong Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFilm bulk acoustic resonators (FBARs) are widely used in radio frequency (RF) filters for wireless communication because of their high operating frequency and high quality factor. With the increase of high-power applications, ensuring device robustness has become a critical challenge. This study presents an investigation into the high-power failure behaviors and mechanisms of FBARs, specifically examining the role of active area, film thickness, and geometry. Experimental results demonstrate that small-area FBARs exhibit distinct failure characteristics compared to large-area devices. Small-area devices are governed by progressive spallation at electrode edges, which is induced by high-temperature oxidation and stress concentration, whereas large-area FBARs are prone to sudden structural fracture or short-circuiting caused by excessive thermal stress. Crucially, the study reveals a thickness-dependent transition in large-area devices, where short-circuiting and structural fracture correspond to distinct stress-severity regimes. Furthermore, dynamic evaluations demonstrate that these FBARs preserve strict electrical linearity right up to the point of catastrophic collapse. Based on these phenomenological findings, a thermo-mechanical coupling mechanism is proposed that goes beyond the conventional thermal-only model. Finally, we propose new design guidelines to enhance the power handling capability of FBAR devices. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic devices

Introduction

Film bulk acoustic resonators (FBARs) are micro/nanoelectromechanical devices featuring high operation frequencies and high quality (Q) factors, and compatibility with semiconductor fabrication processes1–3. They have been widely utilized in advanced sensors2,4,5, phononic crystal devices6,7, oscillators, frequency selectors8, energy transducers9, and filters, among which RF filters are particularly important for mobile communications10,11.

In recent years, the rapid development of next-generation wireless and telecommunication systems, including satellite communications requiring long-distance transmission, 5 G base stations, and Carrier Aggregation (CA), has promoted the transmission power levels to tens or even hundreds of Watts2,12–15. For these applications, FBAR devices are required to operate reliably under conditions of higher power density and stronger radio frequency (RF) excitation. However, the power handling capability of current commercially available FBAR devices often falls short of these demands. For instance, the maximum power handling of Broadcom’s FBAR filter is limited to approximately 27 dBm16, while the comparable devices from Qualcomm range between 25 dBm and 28 dBm17,18. Even though high-performance products from Qorvo achieve higher levels up to 37 dBm19,20, they still fail to meet the stringent requirements for high-power applications. This limitation constitutes a formidable technical barrier that is yet to be overcome. Consequently, it has become a challenge to ensure the sustained operation of FBARs under high-power RF excitation without inducing device failure.

Existing studies indicate that the failure of FBAR devices under high-power operation is predominantly driven by thermal effects. When a resonator is subjected to high-power RF excitation, significant power dissipation leads to a rise in device temperature, leading to failure of the device21. The heat and acoustic motion could also increase the roughness of the electrode surface, causing electrode degradation and ultimately resulting in device failure22,23. In addition, the nonuniform distribution of current within the active area of resonators would generate localized hot-spots24,25, compromising thermal performance and power-handling capability of the devices. These findings suggest that the process consistency and uniformity of the thin-film layers have profound impacts on power tolerance of the devices. Based on the understanding of thermally induced failure, engineering solutions such as series/parallel equivalent structures have been proposed22,26,27, which can restrict temperature rise to a certain extent by dispersing current density, thereby enhancing the device’ s power handling capability to a level exceeding 40 dBm.

Despite these advancements, research on high-power reliability has predominantly focused on complete filter networks. In practical operation, the series and parallel resonators within a filter act as a complex network where impedance varies dynamically across the passband. Consequently, the actual power load distributed to each resonator in the filter differs significantly depending on the excitation frequency and active area, making it difficult to pinpoint the precise causes of failure within the system. This complexity often makes the discussions on FBAR failure mechanisms relatively superficial and one-sided. Therefore, a clear understanding of the power-induced failure in standalone resonators is essential and necessary. The challenge is to elucidate the distinct failure modes and intrinsic mechanisms of individual resonators under high-power conditions, especially how the active area affects device reliability. This fundamental understanding could facilitate the accurate evaluation of the power handling capacity of complex systems composed of multiple FBARs. To date, however, investigations into the high-power failure mechanisms of individual resonators within a network remain notably absent from the existing literature.

In this study, we conduct power and frequency sweep tests on FBAR devices with varying structural parameters to statistically characterize the failure frequencies and power handling capacities. By combining in-situ high-speed microscopic imaging, micro-physical characterization, and thermo-mechanical coupling simulations, we elucidate the underlying failure mechanisms of FBARs with different effective areas. Specifically, this work clarifies the fundamental transition from a purely thermally dominated mechanism to a synergistic thermo-mechanical driving force, providing theoretical and experimental foundations for the reliability assessment and power optimized design of high-power FBAR filters.

Results and discussion

This section investigates the failure characteristics of FBARs with varying structural parameters under high-power RF excitation. Based on the experimental observations, the corresponding failure behaviors and characteristic trends are summarized, and the underlying failure mechanisms are analyzed.

Devices under test design

To ensure the generality of the results and conclusions, all devices are fabricated using a mature 8-inch industrial process. Two types of resonators are fabricated with irregular ellipse and regular pentagon shapes, as shown in Fig. 1a, which are commonly used in most commercial products. These devices have resonant frequencies at around 1.6 GHz and 2.0 GHz (named as Type A and Type B, respectively), enabling a comprehensive investigation. Previous studies have shown that both geometries provide excellent spurious suppression28,29, effectively smoothing impedance curves and minimizing the influence of parasitic modes, which is critical for accurate analysis of high-power failure behavior. The active area of the devices ranges from 1000 μm2 to 30000 μm2, encompassing the mainstream size spectrum commonly employed in contemporary filter applications.

All the resonators have the five-layer stack structure, comprising an aluminum nitride (AlN) seed layer (30 nm thickness), a molybdenum (Mo) bottom electrode (682 nm /273 nm), an AlN piezoelectric layer (586 nm /972 nm), a Mo top electrode (601 nm /371 nm), and an AlN passivation layer (408 nm /319 nm) for the Type A and Type B devices, respectively. The seed layer is used to improve the crystallographic quality of the bottom electrode, while the passivation layer protects the top electrode from oxidation and facilitates the frequency trimming process. The focused ion beam (FIB) cross-section image (Fig. 1b) shows a dense film structure, with no voids and other defects observed across the active area for both types of resonators. These process and structural features collectively ensure that variations and defects inherent to fabrication are effectively excluded, providing a solid foundation for the reliability of the conclusions drawn in this study.

High-power failure behaviors

As shown in Fig. 2a, devices with active areas ranging from 5000 to 30,000 μm2 exhibit highly consistent impedance responses. Their corresponding S-parameter characteristics are presented in Fig. 2b. With increasing area, the S-parameter traces remain largely self-similar within each frequency band, while the frequency of the S11 minimum (fS11min) shifts toward higher values due to the area-induced impedance mismatch.

High-power frequency-sweep measurements indicate that the failure frequency (fbroken) does not correlate strongly with fs. Instead, the failure points display a clear area-dependent partitioning behavior for both Type A and Type B devices, as shown in Fig. 2c. For devices with areas between 1000 μm2 and 10,000 μm2, failures predominantly occur at the lower end of the operating band (slightly below fs), whereas in those with areas ≥ 15000 μm2, failures occur between fs and fp. We calculate the frequency differences (Δf) relative to three characteristic points (fs,fp, and fS11min). For the devices with areas between 1000 μm2 and 10,000 μm2, the frequency differences cluster as shown in Fig. 2d; whereas for those areas ≥15,000 μm2, the clustering pattern shifts as depicted in Fig. 2e. The failure frequencies fall within a narrow window of approximately 15–40 MHz below the original fS11min. Dynamically, this interval coincides with the S11 valley under realistic operating conditions, where the resonance curve shifts leftward due to the temperature coefficient of frequency (TCF ≈ –25 to –30 ppm/°C)32,33. Since the S11 minimum corresponds to minimal reflection and thus maximal absorbed power under fixed input power, failure emerges once the absorbed power exceeds the energy-handling capability of the device.

Beyond the failure frequency, the power threshold at failure exhibits a distinct trend governed jointly by the incident (Pin) and absorbed power (Pabs), representing the actual power delivered to the input port and the difference between incident and reflected power (Pref), respectively. The incident power reflects the external drive level, whereas the absorbed power directly represents the internal energy load. As shown in Fig. 2f, the failure input power expressed in dBm increases nonlinearly with area and approaches saturation, closely matching the fitting of Eq.(3). We further convert the input powers to linear units (mW) using Eq.(4). The resulting trend (Fig. 2g) has a very different behavior: for areas <15,000 μm2, the failure input power scales approximately linearly with area, corresponding to an average incident power density of ~270 W/mm2. For areas >15,000 μm2, the growth rate compresses and falls below the linear extrapolation, indicating that enlarging the area alone does not indefinitely enhance power capacity. This behavior is consistent across Types A and B and is largely independent of device geometry.

Because only part of the incident power is absorbed during operation, the absorbed power ultimately determines failure of the FBARs. As shown in Fig. 2h, the maximum absorbed power exhibits a step-like variation with area. For Type B devices with areas <10,000 μm2, the absorbed power clusters tightly within 400–600 mW, suggesting a safe absorbed power limit of <400 mW. For areas of 15,000 –25000 μm2, the distribution rises to the second plateau at 600 to 800 mW; at 30,000 μm2, the absorbed power surpasses 1 W, forming the third plateau. Type A devices show the same qualitative structure but with consistently higher absorbed power levels (by ~150 to 200 mW), attributable to their larger film thickness at lower operating frequencies. These results highlight that both higher-frequency operation (thinner films) and device miniaturization (smaller area) intrinsically reduce the absorbed power threshold, underscoring the increasing challenges of power reliability in next generation FBARs. Taken together, the area-dependent stepwise behavior of absorbed power, combined with the two-regime trend in input power thresholds, establishes a framework that links device area with power-handling capability.

Three main failure characteristics

Based on the high-power failure testing results of FBAR devices across different frequency bands, we observe three distinct failure characteristics in resonators with varying areas and frequencies:

Oxidative spallation at electrode edges

For Type A and Type B resonators with an active area of ≤3000 μm2, high-power testing results in distinct electrode edge spallation characteristics. As shown in Fig. 3a–c, the spallation regions are concentrated around the free boundaries of the device’s active area, producing debris with a radially scattered distribution. High-magnification scanning electron microscope (SEM) imaging shows that this debris consists of typical submicron-scale grains (Fig. 3d). Figure 3e, f shows Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping. The distribution of N and Al on the device surface is highly consistent, corresponding to the undamaged AlN piezoelectric layer and passivation layer. However, in the spallation region at the edges, the N and Al elemental signals are negligible, replaced by high-intensity signals of Mo and O. Considering that a dense oxygen distribution should not exist on the surface of an intact device, the high overlap between Mo and O clearly verifies that the spalled material is primarily composed of molybdenum oxide. It is the accumulation of this oxide layer covering the underlying AlN that results in the distinct complementary characteristics observed in the Al/N and Mo/O distribution maps. (Note: Si, serving as the substrate, is distributed beneath the entire thin film, and Au is deposited on the GSG pads; neither participated in the compositional anomalies of the failure region.)

At the damage interface, the destruction is strictly confined to the device periphery. Notably, the underlying piezoelectric layer and substrate structure are unaffected, while spallation occurs primarily in the top electrode and passivation layers, which are directly exposed to air. This layer specificity further implies that ambient oxygen plays a critical role in the failure. Furthermore, real-time monitoring during high-power testing indicates that this edge spallation and outward dispersion are not instantaneous but exhibit a time-dependent progression on the order of seconds (please refer to Supplementary Videos 1–3). Figure 3h, i compares the evolution of the impedance spectra and S-parameters at 30 s intervals during high-power stressing, reflecting the gradual degradation of the resonator’s Q-value until total loss of performance.

Short-circuit failure and electrode breakdown

When the resonator active area increases to ≥4000 μm2, the failure modes of Type A and Type B devices change significantly, no longer exhibiting the extensive spallation observed in smaller devices. Notably, for devices with the same film thickness, the failure morphologies remain highly consistent across the active area range of 4000 μm2 to 30,000 μm2, with no hybrid failure modes observed. The failure of Type A resonators demonstrates a high degree of spatially localized failure, typically manifesting as a single discrete damage spot located near the edge far from the GSG port. This stands in sharp contrast to the continuous circumferential spallation observed in small-area devices: the former appears as a point-like localized defect, while the latter presents as linear or planar distribution features (Fig. 4a). Under SEM observation, the surface of the damage spot exhibits a distinct crack-like morphology (Fig. 4b). Typically, physical cracks imply an open circuit; however, a comparison of the impedance curves and Smith charts before and after failure indicated distinct electrical behavior: this single damage spot induces a short circuit in the device (Fig. 4c, d). The surface crack morphology alone could not explain this discrepancy between the observed disconnection and electrical conduction, prompting vertical cross-sectional analysis to investigate the internal structure.

FIB cross-sectional views of the short-circuit crack region: e magnified FIB view of the left breakdown region; f full-width FIB cross-section across the failure site; g magnified view of the right breakdown region

FIB cross-sectional imaging (Fig. 4f) clearly shows the internal structural evolution perpendicular to the surface crack. The film structure in the damage center has collapsed, showing significant interlayer mixing. The region surrounding the crack exhibits a slight upward buckling deformation, suggesting the involvement of mechanical factors in the failure process. As shown in Fig. 4e, g, the Mo electrode undergoes significant material migration and reshaping, presenting clustered metal distribution. These metal clusters penetrate the piezoelectric dielectric layer, directly bridging the top and bottom electrodes, thereby physically establishing a through-thickness conductive path. This explains the phenomenon where the devices exhibit complete short-circuit characteristics despite only showing micro-cracks on the surface.

Open-circuit failure and structural fracture

For Type B resonators with further increased areas (>4000 μm2), the failure region is no longer confined to the device edges but manifests as a catastrophic transverse fracture crossing the entire active area as shown in Fig. 5a. Beneath the void formed by the fracture, the bare silicon substrate is directly observable, indicating that the entire film stack of the FBAR, including the top/bottom electrodes and the piezoelectric layer, suffers complete tearing (Fig. 5b). This total physical separation of the multilayer structure is in agreement with the open-circuit characteristics observed in electrical testing (Fig. 5c, d).

FIB cross-sectional characterization of the rupture site: e magnified FIB view of the left fracture terminal; f panoramic FIB cross-section across the failure site; g magnified view of the right fracture terminal

FIB cross-sectional analysis demonstrates distinct upward buckling deformation at the fracture edges (Fig. 5f). This deformation feature is highly similar to the microscopic arching observed at the short-circuit point of Type A resonators, but with a larger amplitude and greater destructiveness. This commonality suggests that regardless of whether the final outcome is a short circuit or an open circuit, stress-driven mechanical deformation plays an important role in the failure mechanism analysis for all large-area resonators (both types). However, unlike the short-circuit case, the film structure near the open-circuit failure region maintains relative layer distinctness (Fig. 5e, g). Although the Mo layer undergoes slight deformation, the interfaces between layers exhibit no severe penetration. Therefore, the final open-circuit characteristic originates from the physical tearing of the thin film structure. Consistent failure morphologies for both the short-circuit and open-circuit modes are observed across different device geometries (irregular elliptical and regular pentagonal), as further documented in Fig. S5 of the Supplementary Material.

Failure mechanisms analysis

Based on the aforementioned failure characteristics, we observe two distinct failure morphologies that are strongly dependent on the resonator’s effective area: oxidative spallation at electrode edges and structural buckling/fracture. These suggest that the conventional perspective, which attributes failure solely to a single thermal mechanism, is insufficient to account for such complex failure behaviors. Consequently, this section investigates the interplay between thermal and stress fields, with a specific focus on elucidating how this thermo-mechanical coupling mechanism drives the three distinct failure modes described above. As previously noted, the oxidative spallation at the edges of small-area devices evolves over a timescale of seconds, characterizing a progressive degradation process. In contrast, the short-circuit and open-circuit failures associated with large-area devices are transient processes. Their extremely rapid occurrence makes them difficult to capture using conventional observation techniques. To resolve this significant temporal disparity, we employ a high-speed camera for in-situ observation, successfully capturing the dynamic evolution of the three failure modes (in Figs. 6 and 7). Subsequently, based on these distinct timescales, we present a mechanistic analysis of the progressive performance degradation in small-area devices and the transient catastrophic failure in large-area devices, respectively.

Failure for small-area resonators: high-temperature oxidation and thermal stress concentration

Combining the failure characterization results with the time-domain phenomena captured by the high-speed camera (Fig. 6a), the degradation of small-area devices manifests as a progressive evolution process consistent with the continuous nature of high-temperature oxidation. First, regarding deformation characteristics, 3D morphological characterization using laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) demonstrates that among the three failure modes, the vertical height difference caused by edge spallation is minimal (approximately a few tenths of a micrometer). This variation is primarily attributed to the accumulation of granular debris scattered around the periphery of the top electrode, while the active area of the device exhibits no significant buckling deformation (Fig. 6b). In contrast, the short-circuit and open-circuit failures observed in large-area devices are accompanied by significant upward buckling, with height differences ranging from several micrometers to over ten micrometers (Fig. 7e, f). This “area-dependent deformation” can be explained by Euler’s buckling theory for thin plates34. For a slender structure constrained at two ends, the critical load Pcr required to initiate buckling instability is expressed as: Pcr=π2EI/(KL)2 where K is the effective length factor, E is the temperature-dependent Young’s modulus, and I is the area moment of inertia, which scales with the cube of the film thickness h (I∝h3). In this study, since the film thickness remains constant for a given device type, I is treated as a fixed parameter. Regarding the temperature effect, although high-power operation elevates the device temperature, the resulting degradation in material stiffness is quantitatively minor. Specifically, as temperature rises from 27 to 827 °C, the Young’s modulus of Mo decreases from ~335 to ~300 GPa (≈10%)35. Similarly, for AlN, the primary elastic constants (c11 and c33) exhibit only slight reductions (changing from ~420 to ~400 GPa, and ~390 to ~370 GPa, respectively) when temperature is changed from 27 to 1000 °C, representing a variation of less than 6%36. In contrast, across the full experimental range (from 1000 to 30,000 μm2), the effective length term (L2) in the denominator of the Euler formula increases by a factor of 30. Consequently, for small-area FBARs, this geometric reduction increases the critical buckling load (Pcr∝E/L2) drastically, overshadowing the negligible temperature effects.

Another question that immediately follows is: what is the origin of the metal oxides accumulated at the edges? Fig. 6c illustrates the temperature distribution of the operating resonator obtained via infrared thermal imaging. (Note: Data from the 2000 μm2 sample under low power was selected for clarity, though the distribution pattern remains consistent across all active areas). The thermal map demonstrates that, in addition to the center of the active area, the temperature at the free edge distal to the GSG pads is higher than that on the side closer to the GSG pads. This phenomenon is attributed to the efficient heat dissipation facilitated by the metal interconnects on the GSG-anchored side, whereas the free edge suffers from relatively poor thermal conduction. The lateral temperature profile in Fig. 6d intuitively quantifies this temperature difference between the edge and the center resulting from thermal conduction asymmetry. Further temperature-area dependency testing (Fig. 6e) indicates that the heat dissipation capability of the resonators improves as the effective area increases. However, when the active area is reduced to 3000 μm2 or less, experimental results demonstrate that the maximum device temperature exceeds 600 °C. This temperature range corresponds to the threshold for the rapid oxidation of metallic Mo37,38. Since the device surface is covered by an AlN passivation layer, only the side edges of the top electrode are directly exposed to air. This provides an explanation for why the oxidation phenomenon is exclusively localized to the lateral edges of the active area. On this basis, we establish a 2D finite element model (FEM) based on the actual device dimensions. A mechanical perfectly matched layer (PML) is introduced at the lateral boundaries to absorb outgoing elastic waves and avoid artificial reflections from the truncated model boundaries, thereby approximating the extended on-wafer film environment surrounding the resonator. Rather than independently solving the heat-transfer process, the FEM analysis incorporates the measured lateral temperature gradient data as the prescribed thermal input to the model (Fig. 6f, g), and stress simulations are then performed considering the effects of thermal expansion. The simulation results (Fig. 6h) show that, at the power and frequency points corresponding to the observed failure, a significant stress peak emerges at the outer edge of the active area. This is due to the location being at the interface between the free and fixed regions, compounded by severe thermal expansion compression.

In summary, the failure mechanism for small-area devices can be described as follows: high-frequency repetitive cyclic stress continuously acts upon the brittle Mo/molybdenum oxide interface, leading to interfacial delamination and material spallation. It should be emphasized that the top thin AlN passivation layer is spalled together with the underlying Mo oxide. Driven by the localized stress action near the FBAR edge, the spalled material is dispersed radially, forming the observed accumulated oxide debris. As the edge material continues to spall, the oxidation reaction continuously propagates toward the central region. This “oxidation-spallation-oxidation” cycle progressively destroys the acoustic boundary conditions of the FBAR, resulting in severe energy leakage. Macroscopically, this manifests as a continuous degradation of the Q-value until total device failure.

Failure for large-area resonators: thermal stress exceeding material strength

As the effective area of the resonator increases, the enhanced heat dissipation capability results in a marked reduction in the average temperature of the device under high-power steady-state operation (Fig. 6e). Consequently, the threshold conditions for high-temperature metallic oxidation are not met. The progressive edge spallation observed in small-area devices is absent, replaced by instantaneous structural fracture. This constitutes the reason for the transition in failure characteristics dependent on device area. As captured by the high-speed photography in Fig. 7c, d, on a millisecond timescale, the short-circuit failure of Type A resonators manifests merely as discrete spot damage at the edge distal to the GSG pads. In contrast, the film rupture of Type B resonators initiates at the geometric center, followed by rapid crack propagation that tears the edges, ultimately creating a catastrophic, through-thickness fracture zone. The spatial locations of these instantaneous failures coincide with the local temperature peaks identified in the thermal distribution (Fig. 7a, b).

To explain this process, we perform thermal-stress coupling simulations using the aforementioned model (using the 30,000 μm2 sample as a representative case). Upon introducing the measured lateral temperature, under the combined driving forces of the piezoelectric effect and thermal expansion, the stress distribution of the Type B resonator exhibits a “three-peak” profile: stress is lower at the edges, whereas the peak stress in the central piezoelectric layer exceeds 2.0 GPa, and the electrode stress reaches approximately 1.25 GPa, as shown in Fig. 7g. These values significantly surpass the strength limits of AlN and Mo (literature indicates the fracture strength of Mo is typically ≈600 MPa39, while that of AlN ranges from 1.42 to 1.54 GPa40). Since the central region corresponds to the thermal maximum and is bounded by free boundaries at the top and bottom, it lacks mechanical constraint. As deformation accumulates with rising temperature, the excessive in-plane tensile stress eventually tears the thin film, resulting in instantaneous rupture of the stack. This mechanism also elucidates the origin of the substantial vertical height difference observed in the confocal microscopy results (Fig. 7e).

Conversely, when the simulation parameters (film thickness and boundary conditions) are adjusted to Type A specifications, the stress distribution shifts significantly (Fig. 7h): the stress maximum relocates from the center to the anchored edge distal to the GSG pads, aligning with the observed micro-crack location. Stress levels at this location similarly reach the fracture strength threshold of AlN. Because the edge is mechanically anchored and slightly cooler than the center, deformation is strictly confined, resulting in a much smaller macroscopic deformation compared to Type B devices (Fig. 7f). In Type A devices, the localized thermo-mechanical stress concentration at the anchored edge initiates micro-cracking within the AlN layer. As evaluated in Supplementary Note 2, the local electric field is insufficient to trigger intrinsic dielectric breakdown of the AlN layer but significantly exceeds the breakdown threshold of the air filling these cracks. This triggers an air-assisted electrical breakdown, creating a highly conductive path through the fractures. Consequently, the metal electrodes bridge across this breakdown channel, ultimately leading to the catastrophic short-circuit failure. A systematic area-dependent mechanical stress sweep comparing the center and edge stress levels of Type A and Type B devices is provided in Supplementary Note 1 and Fig. S7.

Furthermore, it is essential to clarify the physical connection between the two distinct area thresholds observed in this study: the failure mode transition at ≈4000 μm2 and the incident power linearity compression at ≈15,000 μm2. Although the dominant driving force of failure discussed in this work is mechanical stress, temperature also serves as the critical enabling condition. Consequently, both thresholds are intrinsically governed by the thermal response of the device. The 4000 μm2 boundary represents a critical absolute temperature threshold; as the active area exceeds this value, improved heat dissipation keeps the peak temperature below the level that causes severe Mo oxidation, thereby suppressing slow spallation. In contrast, the 15,000 μm2 boundary corresponds to the saturation of the thermal dissipation trend. As evidenced by the temperature plateau in Fig. 6e, increasing the area beyond this yields diminishing returns in cooling efficiency, which causes the observed compression in power handling capacity (Fig. 2g). Thus, while stress executes the damage, the transition in failure mode is influenced by the specific reaction temperature, and the nonlinearity in power capacity is dictated by the limitations of the heat dissipation trend.

Finally, the thickness-dependent difference between the short-circuit mode in thicker Type A stacks and the open-circuit tearing in thinner Type B stacks can be understood as a stress-severity-dependent difference in the dominant failure pathway. The marginal overstress in Type A favors localized micro-cracking followed by electrically assisted shorting, whereas the severe overstress in Type B leads to mechanically dominated through-thickness rupture. Therefore, the shift in the dominant failure pathway should be viewed as a gradual transition regime rather than a sharply defined critical point. Looking forward to future 5 G/6 G applications, as piezoelectric films become even thinner and operating frequencies rise, additional mechanisms beyond the thermo-mechanical route may emerge, including defect-assisted dielectric breakdown, field-driven degradation, and non-thermal nonlinearities. Consequently, the conclusions of this work are limited to the thickness/frequency window studied here, where thermo-mechanical damage remains the dominant failure driver. A detailed quantitative evaluation of the electric field distributions is provided in Supplementary Note 2 and Figs. S8 and S9.

Signal integrity and linearity prior to failure

While the catastrophic thermo-mechanical failure mechanism sets the ultimate boundary for device survivability, evaluating the signal integrity and electrical linearity approaching this limit is equally critical for practical RF front-end applications. Treating the FBAR device as a nonlinear system with a second-order term, its input-output response can be expressed as Eq.(5). By applying a single-tone input signal x(t)=Acos(ωt), transformations Eqs.(6)–(7) reveal that the input power Pin is proportional to A2, while its second harmonic (2ω) power (PH2) is proportional to 14a22A4.

Consequently, as long as the system remains in an intrinsically stable state (i.e., with constant coefficients), PH2 is strictly proportional to Pin2. Converting these relationships into logarithmic units (dBm) according to Eqs.(8)–(10), the ratio ΔPH2(dBm)/ΔPin(dBm) theoretically yields a constant 2:1 slope.

Based on this theoretical model, we monitor the dynamic wideband H2 response across various device areas up to the failure point (detailed in Supplementary Material Fig. S6). The results indicate that in both the off-resonance static capacitance region and at the peak H2 response frequency, the H2 trajectories versus Pin rigorously follow the 2:1 slope right up to the point of instantaneous physical rupture. This explicitly demonstrates that prior to instantaneous short-circuiting or catastrophic burst failure, the tested medium-to-large-area devices exhibit no abnormal nonlinear distortion deviating from the theoretical model, maintaining a highly stable and predictable nonlinear state. From a system-level and industrial application perspective, this implies that medium-to-large-area devices, which are primarily responsible for high-power handling in RF front-ends, can preserve fundamental signal integrity even when driven exceptionally close to their physical survivability limits.

Design guidelines for high-power FBARs

Our investigations reveal that the fundamental catalyst behind the transition in failure mechanisms does not arise from the specific film thickness, but from the intricate interplay between area-dependent thermal dissipation and lateral stress concentration. Having consistently verified this underlying physical mechanism for thick-film and thin-film resonators, we propose the following design guidelines. By addressing the root causes of failure, these strategies are independent of specific operating frequencies and piezoelectric thicknesses, providing practical guidance within the thickness/frequency window studied here.

Abandoning fixed power density metrics. Our findings demonstrate that the power limits (both incident and absorbed) do not scale linearly with the active area for FBARs with different thicknesses (as evidenced in Fig. 2). Therefore, in engineering practice, relying on a static power density value (e.g., 5 W/mm226) is insufficient for estimating the power-handling capacity of individual FBARs or cascading filter networks. Instead, an area-dependent nonlinear threshold model, incorporating the identified linear and compressed regions, should be adopted to allocate power margins accurately and safely.

If the layout permits, the active area of individual resonators should be scaled above 4000 μm2 to bypass this high-temperature oxidation regime and simultaneously increase the overall power limit. If extreme miniaturization strictly constrains the device footprint, targeted isolation/passivation of the exposed electrode sidewalls may be considered to suppress ambient-air-assisted Mo oxidation; alternatively, oxidation-resistant and high-temperature-stable electrode materials may be explored to mitigate oxide formation at the electrode edges, although both strategies require further process-level validation in FBAR devices.

Advanced thermal management and stress relief for large-area devices. Once the area exceeds 4000 μm2, the marginal benefits of heat dissipation diminish rapidly. Devices in this regime predominantly fail due to extreme thermo-mechanical stress (e.g., catastrophic central rupture or instantaneous peripheral breakdown). Simply expanding the area beyond 15,000 μm2 is highly inefficient for power scaling. Thus, the design paradigm for large-area devices must shift from increasing dissipation area to mitigating thermo-mechanical stress. From a thermal perspective, we recommend integrating structural cooling solutions, such as thermal vias or heat-conducting pillars41 routed to the substrate, synergistically coupled with continuous active cooling like substrate microfluidics42,43. Furthermore, the heat-extraction potential of electrocaloric materials (e.g., PBZ44, PZT45, or high-polar-entropy perovskite oxides46) exploiting the positive electrocaloric effect (PCE) is highly promising for sub-micron chip thermal management; arranging stacked electrocaloric layers around the FBAR active region holds the potential to actively pump heat away. Beyond these thermal management strategies, alleviating localized strain is equally critical. Thus, from a mechanical perspective, implementing stress-relief corrugations at the anchored edges is strongly advised to buffer the extreme transient stresses that trigger mechanical tearing or electrical breakdown.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the area-dependent statistical regularities of high-power failure and the underlying thermo-mechanical coupling mechanisms in FBARs. The results indicate that the power handling capability exhibits a distinct “linear-compression” dual-zone characteristic with increasing active area, while the absorbed power threshold displays a step-like distribution. Mechanistic analysis identifies a transition in the failure regime from thermal dominance to thermo-mechanical coupling: small-area devices are governed by progressive spallation induced by high-temperature oxidation and stress concentration at the edges, whereas large-area devices suffer from transient structural fracture or short-circuiting due to thermally induced stresses exceeding material strength limits. Crucially, a thickness-dependent transition is revealed in large-area devices, where the ultimate failure mode, namely short-circuiting versus open-circuit tearing, reflects a stress-severity-dependent shift in the dominant failure pathway from electrically assisted shorting to mechanically dominated rupture. Furthermore, dynamic evaluations demonstrate that these devices preserve excellent signal integrity and strict electrical linearity right up to the point of catastrophic structural collapse.

Furthermore, the results suggest that the conventional design benchmark based solely on a fixed power density (e.g., 5 W/mm2) may be insufficient for characterizing failure risks across all device scales. Consequently, area-dependent design strategies are recommended: for small-area devices, increasing the active area enhances power durability; however, for large-area devices, the improvement in power handling capability through simple geometric area expansion yields diminishing returns, indicating that future designs should prioritize stress alleviation and advanced thermal management.

Testing system building

Prior to connecting the device for testing, the test setup shown in Fig. 8a is used to verify the signal amplitude at the input port across the target frequency band and power range. The spectrum analyzer (SA) reading at this stage is recorded as PSA1, and the actual power delivered to the input port Pin is calculated using Eq.(11), where S21_element represents the insertion loss contributed by each element in the forward path, including the attenuator (ATT), the coupled port of the directional coupler, and the total cables in the reflection path. Because the feeding network remains identical before and after inserting the DUT, the obtained Pin directly corresponds to the incident power applied to the device.

As illustrated in Fig. 8b, the reflected signal generated during high-power excitation returns through the same port of the DUT, where it is extracted via the bidirectional coupler and then routed through the ATT into the SA. The corresponding SA reading is denoted as PSA2, and the reflected power Pref is calculated using Eq.(12). It is important to note that both Pin and Pref are expressed in dBm; therefore, the actual absorbed power Pabs must be computed by converting these logarithmic values into linear units before subtraction, as shown in Eq.(13). Based on Eqs.(11)–(13), the collected experimental data can be post-processed to quantify the power-dependent failure behavior of the FBAR devices. The key components in the measurement setup of Fig. 8a and b are summarized as follows. The host computer controls the signal generator (SG) via USB to generate signals with designated frequency and amplitude (automated via a custom Python-based script for RF power sweeping and data acquisition, see Supplementary Note 3), which are subsequently amplified to high-power levels by the power amplifier (PA). An isolator (ISO) is inserted to prevent high-power reflections from feeding back into the PA output and damaging the equipment. A bidirectional coupler (COUPLE) is used to extract the reflected signal, and all unused ports are terminated with 50Ω loads to ensure proper matching. The ATT protects the SA from high-power signals, enabling safe and accurate measurement. The photograph of the actual system setup is shown in Fig. 8c. All test components and interconnecting cables are impedance-matched to 50Ω and are rated for average power levels exceeding 20 W, ensuring reliable operation for high-power failure characteristics.

Testing procedures

For acoustic resonators, when the excitation frequency reaches the series resonant frequency fs under a fixed port voltage amplitude, both the mechanical and electrical fields inside the device exhibit their most pronounced responses47. As a result, the resonant frequency is usually regarded as the key operating point for characterizing device behavior. However, in most practical applications of FBARs, discussions of power-handling performance are more commonly expressed in terms of energy or power rather than voltage. Accordingly, using dBm or mW for excitation levels during failure characteristics and analysis aligns better with practical engineering standards. To identify the frequency at which the device is most vulnerable to failure, we perform power-incremental swept-frequency tests. At a relatively low and fixed input power, the device is swept from the static capacitance region to the effective resonant band. Frequency sampling in the static capacitance region is relatively sparse (20 MHz spacing), whereas sampling near the resonant band is denser (5 MHz spacing) to screen the failure-prone frequency window under high-power excitation and ensure adequate coverage of the primary operating range. Each frequency point is energized for 30 s. After each full sweep, the device morphology and performance metrics are examined. If no visible damage or substantial degradation is observed, the input power is increased and the frequency sweep is repeated until failure is observed. The frequency at which the first detectable damage appeared is defined as the most failure-prone frequency of the device. Incident and reflected power levels are also recorded to support comprehensive failure analysis.

A potential drawback of this method is that continuous power input may introduce temporal accumulation effects, which could affect the accuracy of the identified failure frequency. To address this concern, we vary the dwell time at each frequency point and compare failure signatures obtained under 15 s, 30 s, and 60 s conditions. The results show no significant differences in either failure frequency or failure threshold across these dwell times, thereby validating the reliability of the testing methodology. It should be noted, however, that long-duration energization tests on the order of weeks or months are not performed due to experimental constraints. Therefore, the methodology and conclusions of this study mainly reflect the short-term failure behaviors of FBAR devices under extreme power conditions.

Testing and characterization equipment

To ensure the reproducibility of the experiments, the exact manufacturers and model numbers of the core equipment used in this study for both high-power RF testing and physical characterization are summarized as follows. For the RF testing system, the excitation signals were provided by a signal generator (SSG5083A, SIGLENT), amplified by a power amplifier (3871AT, Ceyear), and routed through an isolator (TG4040AS-1, RFTYT) and a bidirectional coupler (CP30218A-10, Amtery). The measured signals were appropriately attenuated by an attenuator (XQY-SMAJKS-06-20W-20dB, XQY) before being recorded by a spectrum analyzer (SHA852A, SIGLENT). Additionally, the S-parameter measurements were conducted using a vector network analyzer (E5080B, KEYSIGHT). For the physical and structural characterization, morphological observation and elemental analysis were performed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope equipped with EDS (JSM-IT800, JEOL). Cross-sectional cutting for internal failure analysis was conducted using a FIB (NX5000, HITACHI). The 3D surface topography was evaluated with a confocal microscope (VK-9710, Keyence). Furthermore, the transient structural failure process was captured in-situ by a high-speed camera (MV-CH050-10UM, HIKROBOT), and the steady-state thermal distribution was monitored via an infrared thermal camera (InfraScope II, QFI).
