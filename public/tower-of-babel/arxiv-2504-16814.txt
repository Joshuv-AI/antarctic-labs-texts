Association-Based Track-Before-Detect with Object Contribution Probabilities
Thomas Kropfreiter, Jason L. Williams, Florian Meyer

Abstract
Multiobject tracking provides situational awareness that enables new applications for modern convenience, applied ocean sciences, public safety, and homeland security. In many multiobject tracking applications, including radar and sonar tracking, after coherent prefiltering of the received signal, measurement data is typically structured in cells, where each cell represent, e.g., a different range and bearing value. While conventional detect-then-track (DTT) multiobject tracking approaches convert the cell-structured data within a detection phase into so-called point measurements in order to reduce the amount of data, track-before-detect (TBD) methods process the cell-structured data directly, avoiding a potential information loss. However, many TBD tracking methods are computationally intensive and achieve a reduced tracking accuracy when objects interact, i.e., when they come into close proximity. We here counteract these difficulties by introducing the concept of probabilistic object-to-cell contributions. As many conventional DTT methods, our approach uses a probabilistic association of objects with data cells, and a new object contribution model with corresponding object contribution probabilities to further associate cell contributions to objects that occupy the same data cell. Furthermore, to keep the computational complexity and filter runtimes low, we here use an efficient Poisson multi-Bernoulli filtering approach in combination with the application of belief propagation for fast probabilistic data association. We demonstrate numerically that our method achieves significantly increased tracking performance compared to state-of-the-art TBD tracking approaches, where performance differences are particularly pronounced when multiple objects interact.

I-B

Contribution

In this paper, we introduce a PMB filter for TBD that relies on a new measurement model based on the notion of random object contributions and corresponding object contribution probabilities.
More precisely, we assume that each object contributes to at most a single data cell, which is in contrast to other TBD methods that are based on superpositional object contrition models
[
28
,
29
]
.
Within the filter derivation, we apply certain approximations, including efficient BP-based marginalization operations, leading to our PMB filter that exhibits a linear complexity scaling.
We verify the performance of our proposed method numerically and compare it to state-of-the-art TBD methods
[
22
,
28
,
29
]
.
An important result of our work is that our proposed method using association-based cell contributions achieves very good performance even when the sensor data was generated according to a superimposed data cell model.
This implies that the approximations made by methods with superpositional models have a stronger impact on the overall tracking performance than the moderate model mismatch of our proposed method.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as

follows:

•

We propose a novel TBD measurement model based on random object-to-cell contributions with corresponding object contribution probabilities.

•

We derive a new PMB filter for TBD that uses BP for efficient probabilistic object-to-cell associations.

•

We demonstrate performance improvements of our TBD PMB filter compared to state-of-the-art TBD methods.

This paper differs from
[
40
]
in that we extend the TBD measurement model by a probabilistic object contribution model with corresponding object contribution probabilities.
This leads to a new TBD PMB filter that achieves improved tracking accuracy
in scenarios with multiple interacting objects, i.e., when multiple objects come in close proximity and contribute to the same resolution cell

.

RFS Fundamentals

An RFS
\mathsfbr{X}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\{\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}^{({\sf n})}\}
is a random variable where both the elements
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}^{(i)}
and the number of elements
{\sf n}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt|\mathsfbr{X}|
, i.e., the cardinality of
\mathsfbr{X}

Thus, an RFS consists of a random number of random vectors.
The statistics of an RFS
\mathsfbr{X}
can be described by its multiobject pdf
f({\cal{X}})

[
7
]
.
For any realization
{\cal{X}}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\big\{\bm{x}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{x}^{(n)}\big\}
, the multiobject pdf
f({\cal{X}})
is given by

f({\cal{X}})\hskip 0.85358pt=\hskip 0.85358ptn!\hskip 0.85358pt\rho(n)\hskip 0.85358ptf_{n}(\bm{x}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{x}^{(n)})\vskip 2.84526pt

(1)

where
\rho(n)\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt\mathrm{Pr}\{|\mathsfbr{X}|\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358ptn\}
,
n\hskip-0.85358pt\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the pmf of the cardinality
{\sf n}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt|\mathsfbr{X}|
, and
f_{n}(\bm{x}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{x}^{(n)})
is the joint pdf of the random vectors
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}^{(n)}
that is permutation invariant with respect to its arguments
\bm{x}^{(i)}
.
While conventional Riemann integration cannot be applied to functions with set arguments, one can define a set integral
of a real-valued set function

according to

7
]

\displaystyle\int g({\cal{X}})\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\delta{\cal{X}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.53581pt\triangleq\sum_{n\hskip 0.85358pt=\hskip 0.85358pt0}^{\infty}\hskip 0.85358pt\frac{1}{n!}\hskip 0.85358pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{nn_{x}}}\!g(\{\bm{x}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{x}^{(n)}\})\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\text{d}\bm{x}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\cdots\text{d}\bm{x}^{(n)}.

(2)

Note that for each cardinality value

there is exactly one summation term. The multiobject pdf

integrates to one with respect to the set integral (

\mathsfbr{X}

(3)

A

\mathsfbr{X}
is the union of a fixed number

\mathsfbr{X}
is a weighted sum of MB RFSs.

For any realization

The cardinality of the
Poisson RFS

\mathsfbr{X}
is Poisson distributed with mean

(5)

\mathsfbr{X}

Object States and State Transition Model

We model the multiobject state at time
k
by an RFS
\mathsfbr{X}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=\{\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}^{(n_{k})}\}

The elements, i.e., the single-object state vectors

and the object’s intensity

The multiobject state transition model describes the statistics of the state

at time

[

,

Here, at time

(8)

{\cal{X}}_{k}
, will be described in the following

.

IV-A

Association-Based Data Cell Model

In what follows, we present the association-based data cell model used by our proposed multiobject tracking method.
The key assumptions of our model are as follows

.

1.

An object
\bm{x}_{k}
can only contribute to data cell
m
if it is located in it

.

2.

If there is only a single object
\bm{x}_{k}
located in data cell
m
, it must contribute to it

.

3.

If there are multiple objects
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
,
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}
, located in data cell
m
, only one of them contributes to it

.

Assumption 3) is in contrast to superpositional data cell models
[
28
,
29
]
, but, as demonstrated in Section
VIII
, can lead to a very good tracking performance even if the true data model in force is superpositional.
Assumptions 2) and 3) lead to the derivation of object contribution probabilities in Section
IV-C
.

To describe unknown object-to-cell associations, we introduce the random association vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq[\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\ldots\hskip 0.85358pt\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(n_{k})}]^{\text{T}}
with entries
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
.
Here,
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358ptm\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
indicates that object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
is located in data cell
m
.
We collect all possible association vectors
\bm{a}_{k}
in the set
\mathcal{A}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}^{n_{k}}
.
For

future reference, we also introduce the sets

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{a}_{k}}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt\big\{a^{(i)}_{k}\big\}_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}}

and
{\cal{I}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}^{(m)}\triangleq\big\{i\in{\cal{I}}_{k}\hskip 0.85358pt|\hskip 0.85358pta^{(i)}_{k}=m\big\}
as well as
n_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt|{\cal{I}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}^{(m)}|
.

To further describe which object contributes to a data cell occupied by multiple objects, we introduce the random binary vector
\bm{\stheta}_{k}
.
Here,
\bm{\stheta}_{k}
is defined as
\bm{\stheta}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq[\stheta_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\ldots\hskip 0.85358pt\stheta_{k}^{(n_{k})}]^{\text{T}}
, where each entry
\stheta_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\{0,1\}
indicates whether object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
contributes to a data cell
(
\stheta_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt1
)
or not
(
\stheta_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt0
)
.
For a given
\bm{a}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathcal{A}_{k}
, we introduce the set
\Theta_{\bm{a}_{k}}
, which consists of all vectors
\bm{\theta}_{k}
that have exactly one entry
\theta_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt=1
for all
i\in{\cal{I}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}^{(m)}
.

For example, lets consider the case where
M=4
,
n_{k}=3
, and
\bm{a}_{k}=[2\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt3\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1]^{\text{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
. Here,
\bm{a}_{k}
indicates that each of the three objects with indexes
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\{1,2,3\}
is in a different data cell, i.e.,
a^{(i)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\neq\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pta^{(j)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
,
\forall i,j\in{\cal{I}}_{k}
with
i\neq j
.
Accordingly, the set
\Theta_{\bm{a}_{k}}
consists of only one binary vector, which is given by
\bm{\theta}_{k}=[1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1]^{\text{T}}
.
On the other hand, let now

\bm{a}_{k}=[2\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt3\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt2]^{\text{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
. Now
\bm{a}_{k}
indicates that the objects with indexes
i\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt1
and
i\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt3
are in the same data cell
m=2
.
In this case, the set
\Theta_{\bm{a}_{k}}
consists of two binary vectors, which are given by
\bm{\theta}_{k}=[1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt0]^{\text{T}}
and
\bm{\theta}_{k}=[0\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1]^{\text{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
. Here,
\bm{\theta}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt[1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt0]^{\text{T}}
indicates that object
i\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt1
contributes to data cell
m=2
, while
\bm{\theta}_{k}=[0\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt1]^{\text{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
indicates that object
i\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt3
contributes to data cell
m\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt2
.

In summary, the object-to-cell association vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}_{k}
in combination with the object contribution vector
\bm{\stheta}_{k}
allows us to define all possible object-to-cell contributions.
In particular,
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
defines in which data cell object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
is located and
\stheta_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\{0,1\}
defines whether it contributes to that cell or not.
If object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
contributes to data cell
m
,
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
is distributed according to
f_{1}\big(z^{(m)}_{k}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)
.
If no object is located in data cell
m
,
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
is purely determined by noise and is distributed according to
f_{0}\big(z^{(m)}_{k}\big)

.

IV-B

The Joint Likelihood Function

Based on
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}_{k}
and
\bm{\stheta}_{k}
, the joint likelihood function
f(\bm{z}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
can now be computed by using the total probability theorem according to

\displaystyle f(\bm{z}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-0.85358pt\sum_{\bm{a}_{k}\in\hskip 0.85358pt\mathcal{A}_{k}}\hskip 0.85358pt\sum_{\bm{\theta}_{k}\in\hskip 0.85358pt\Theta_{\bm{a}_{k}}}f(\bm{z}_{k}|\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 2.84526ptp(\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt.

In what follows, we present expressions for the conditional distributions
f(\bm{z}_{k}|\bm{a}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k},{\cal{X}}_{k})
and
p(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
based on the data cell model proposed in the previous subsection.

By further assuming that, conditioned on
\bm{a}_{k}
,
\bm{\theta}_{k}
, and
{\cal{X}}_{k}
, all cell measurements
z_{k}^{(m)}
,
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
are statistically independent among each other, the conditional pdf
f(\bm{z}_{k}|\bm{a}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k},{\cal{X}}_{k})
can be found

as

\displaystyle f(\bm{z}_{k}|\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt=\Big(\prod_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}}\hskip-2.84526ptf_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(a_{k}^{(i)})}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)\Big)\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\prod_{m\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt\mathcal{M}\backslash\mathcal{M}_{\bm{a}_{k}}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358ptf_{0}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big).

(10)

Here, the set
{\cal{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}
consists of all object indexes
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}
with
\theta_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt=1
.

Next, we can factorize the prior object-to-cell association pmf
p(\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
according to

p(\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})=\hskip 0.85358ptp(\bm{\theta}_{k}|\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358ptp(\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt.

(11)

Here, the association pmf
p(\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
is defined as

p(\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})=\prod_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}}\delta^{(a_{k}^{(i)})}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})\vskip-1.42262pt

(12)

with the indicator function
\delta^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})
given by

\delta^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})=\begin{cases}1,&\bm{x}_{k}\hskip 2.84526pt\text{is in data cell}\hskip 2.84526ptm\\[0.0pt]
0,&\bm{x}_{k}\hskip 2.84526pt\text{is not in data cell}\hskip 2.84526ptm\hskip 0.85358pt.\end{cases}\vskip 1.42262pt

(13)

Note that the definition of
p(\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
in (
12
) and (
13
) enforces Assumption 1 in Section
IV-A
.
Finally, we introduce the
object contribution probabilities

\displaystyle p(\bm{\theta}_{k}|\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-0.85358pt\prod_{m\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{M}}_{a_{k}}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358ptp^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt.

(14)

Here, the vector
\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=\big[\theta_{k}^{(m,1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\ldots\hskip 0.85358pt\theta_{k}^{(m,n_{k}^{(m)})}\big]^{\text{T}}
comprises all entries
\theta_{k}^{(i)}
of
\bm{\theta}_{k}
with
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}^{(m)}
in an arbitrary but fixed order.
Inserting (
12
) and (
14
) into (
11
)

yields

\displaystyle\hskip-8.53581ptp(\bm{a}_{k},\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\hskip 0.85358pt\Big(\prod_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}}\delta^{(a_{k}^{(i)})}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})\Big)\prod_{m\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{M}}_{a_{k}}}\hskip-0.85358ptp^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big).

(15)

IV-C

Object Contribution Probabilities

The object contribution probabilities
p^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
are obtained based on concepts from classical detection theory
[
42
]
.
More precisely, we first propose object contribution probabilities for the case of
thresholded data cells
described by
p_{\eta}^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
, which are based on the detection threshold
\eta
. As we will derive later in this section, an expression for
p^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
can then be obtained by letting
\eta
approach zero. For future reference, we introduce the set of indexes
{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}=\big\{1,\dots,n_{k}^{(m)}\big\}
.

Let us first consider the case where a single object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
is located in data cell
m
.
Following classical detection theory, the probability that object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
is detected, i.e.,
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\geqslant\eta
, is given

by

\displaystyle p_{\eta}^{(i,m)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\hskip 0.85358pt\int_{\eta}^{\infty}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358ptf_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt\text{d}z_{k}^{(m)}

(16)

where
f_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)
is the conditional pdf of
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
given
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
(cf. Section
IV-A
). Thus, for the case where a single object is in the cell
m
,
p_{\eta}^{(i,m)}
is the probability that the object is detected and
1-p_{\eta}^{(m,i)}
is the probability that the object is not detected.

We now define our model for the object contribution probabilities based on the probabilities of detection discussed above and Assumption 2) and Assumption 3) from Section
IV-A
. In particular, for
i\in{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}
, we define

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054ptp^{(m)}_{\eta}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(m,i)}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt=\frac{p_{\eta}^{(i,m)}\prod_{i^{\prime}\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}\setminus\{i\}}\big(1-p_{\eta}^{(i^{\prime},m)}\big)}{C^{(m)}_{\eta}}

(17)

where the vector
\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(m,i)}
is defined as the
i
’s entry of
\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(m)}
being one, i.e.,
\theta_{k}^{(m,i)}\hskip-0.85358pt=1
, and all the other entries being zero, i.e.,
\theta_{k}^{(m,i^{\prime})}\hskip-0.85358pt=0
for
i^{\prime}\in{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\setminus\hskip-0.85358pt\{i\}
.
The normalization factor in (
17
) can be obtained by summing over all possible numerators in (
17
), i.e.,

\displaystyle C^{(m)}_{\eta}\hskip 0.85358pt=\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}}\hskip 0.0ptp_{\eta}^{(i,m)}\hskip-2.84526pt\prod_{i^{\prime}\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}\setminus\{i\}}\hskip-2.84526pt\big(1-p_{\eta}^{(i^{\prime}\hskip-0.85358pt,m)}\big).

(18)

Note that for the case with a single object, i.e.,
{\cal{I}}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\{i\}
,
p^{(m)}_{\eta}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(m,i)}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)=1
.

After having defined
p_{\eta}^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
, we finally obtain the object contribution pmf
p^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
by letting the threshold
\eta
approach zero, i.e.,

p^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)=\lim_{\eta\hskip 0.85358pt\rightarrow\hskip 0.85358pt0}\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358ptp_{\eta}^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}\big).

(19)

Now that we have formulated our general modeling approach for
p^{(m)}\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}|\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
, let us next elaborate it in terms of the concrete example of Swerling 1 objects, which are often assumed in radar applications
[
21
,
43
]

.

IV-D

Example: Swerling 1 Objects

In many radar applications, a standard model for
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
is given by
[
23
]

\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}=\left|\sum_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}}\srho(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})\hskip 0.85358pt\sqrt{d^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})}+\mathsfbr{w}_{k}^{(m)}\right|\hskip 0.85358pt.\vskip-2.84526pt

(20)

Here, object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
,
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}
contributes to cell intensity
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
via the deterministic factor
\sqrt{d^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})}
and the random factor
\srho(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})
, where
d^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})
is usually referred to as point spread function (PSF).
In addition,
\mathsfbr{w}_{k}^{(m)}
is an object-independent additive complex noise component, which is assumed to be iid across time
k
and cell index
m
and is described by the pdf
f(w_{k}^{(m)})
.
In this general setting, cell intensity
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
can have contributions from all objects
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
,
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}
, and object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
can contribute to all data cells
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}
.
However, our modeling approach assumes that there is at most one object contribution to a single data cell
m
, which simplifies (
20
) to

\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip 0.85358pt=\hskip 0.85358pt\Big|\srho(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})\hskip 0.85358pt\sqrt{d^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})}+\mathsfbr{w}_{k}^{(m)}\Big|\hskip 0.85358pt.\vskip 0.0pt

(21)

Note that, for
n_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt0
, expression (
21
) further simplifies to
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}=\big|\mathsfbr{w}_{k}^{(m)}\big|
.

In the Swerling 1 case, the fluctuation of object returns
\srho^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})
and the background noise
\mathsfbr{w}_{k}^{(m)}
are both modeled as independent and circularly symmetric zero-mean complex Gaussian with variance one and
\sigma^{2}_{0}
, respectively.
It can then be shown
[
43
]
that under this assumption
f_{0}\big(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)
and
f_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)

are Rayleigh distributed and given by

\displaystyle f_{0}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big)

\displaystyle={\cal{R}}\big(z^{(m)}_{k};\sigma_{0}\big)

\displaystyle f_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)

\displaystyle={\cal{R}}\big(z^{(m)}_{k};\sigma_{m}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})\big)

respectively, with
\sigma_{m}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})\triangleq\sqrt{d(\bm{x}^{(i)}_{k})+\sigma^{2}_{0}}
.
Next, we insert
f_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)
into (
16
), which yields

p_{\eta}^{(i,m)}=\hskip 0.85358pt\mathrm{e}^{\hskip-5.69054pt-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\eta^{2}}{\sigma_{m}^{2}\left(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\right)}}\hskip 0.85358pt.\vskip 1.42262pt

(22)

In order to illustrate the application of (
19
), let us consider the case with
n^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt2
objects being in data cell
m
, i.e.,

\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}
with
i\hskip-0.85358pt\in\{1,2\}
. We now compute
p^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\big(\theta_{k}^{(m,1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\theta_{k}^{(m,2)}|\{\bm{x}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt,\bm{x}_{k}^{(2)}\}\big)
for
\theta_{k}^{(m,1)}\hskip-0.85358pt=1
and
\theta_{k}^{(m,2)}\hskip-0.85358pt=0
according to (
19
).
However, this first requires the specification of
p^{(m)}_{\eta}\big(1,\hskip-0.85358pt0\hskip 0.85358pt|\hskip 0.85358pt\{\bm{x}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt,\bm{x}_{k}^{(2)}\}\big)
according to (
17
), which can be found as

p^{(m)}_{\eta}\big(1\hskip 0.85358pt,\hskip-0.85358pt0|\{\bm{x}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt,\bm{x}_{k}^{(2)}\}\big)=\frac{p_{\eta}^{(1,m)}\hskip 0.85358ptp_{\eta}^{(2,m)}}{C^{(m)}_{\eta}}\vskip-2.84526pt

(23)

with

C^{(m)}_{\eta}=\hskip 0.85358ptp_{\eta}^{(1,m)}\big(1-p_{\eta}^{(2,m)}\big)+\big(1-p_{\eta}^{(1,m)}\big)p_{\eta}^{(2,m)}\hskip 0.85358pt.

By inserting (
22
) into (
23
) and applying the limit
\eta\hskip-0.85358pt\to\hskip-0.85358pt0
according to (
19
), we see that we face a “zero-by-zero” division.
However, this indeterminate limit can still be computed by applying the rule of de L’Hospital
[
44
]
. In particular, as we show in Appendix
A
, we get

p^{(m)}\big(1\hskip 0.85358pt,\hskip-0.85358pt0|\{\bm{x}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt,\bm{x}_{k}^{(2)}\}\big)=\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(1)})}{\sigma_{m}^{2}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(1)})+\sigma_{m}^{2}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(2)})}\hskip 0.85358pt.\vskip 0.85358pt

An analogous expression can be obtained for
\theta_{k}^{(m,1)}\hskip-0.85358pt=0
,
\theta_{k}^{(m,2)}\hskip-0.85358pt=1
.

The described procedure can be generalized to the case where there is an arbitrary number of objects in data cell
m
.
As further shown in Appendix
A
, for this general Swerling 1 case, we get

p^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\big(\bm{\theta}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=\bm{\theta}_{k}^{(m,i)}|{\cal{X}}_{k}\big)=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{m}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n^{(m)}_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{m}(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i^{\prime})})}.\vskip 4.2679pt

(24)

Interestingly, in this concrete example where multiple Swerling 1 objects share the same cell, object association probabilities are nonzero and highly intuitive.

V
Prediction Step

To derive our proposed algorithm, we perform sequential Bayesian estimation, which requires the specification of a multiobject state model, a state transition model (cf. Section
III
), and a measurement model (cf. Section
IV
).
The multiobject state
\mathsfbr{X}_{k}
at time
k
, given all collected measurements
\bm{z}_{1:k}\triangleq(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{k})
up to time
k
, is described by the posterior pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k})
.
Here, we model the multiobject state
\mathsfbr{X}_{k-1}
at time
k\hskip-0.85358pt-\hskip-0.85358pt1
by a PMB RFS.
Thus, the posterior pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k-1}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})
at time
k\hskip-0.85358pt-\hskip-0.85358pt1
is given as (cf. Eq. (
6
))

\displaystyle f({\cal{X}}_{k-1}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})\hskip 0.85358pt

VII-B

Recycling of Bernoulli Components

The MB pdf
f^{\text{MB}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
in (
63
) consists of
J_{k}=J_{k-1}\hskip-0.85358pt+\hskip-0.85358ptM
Bernoulli components, i.e., the number of Bernoulli components increases by
M
after each time step
k
.
However, many Bernoulli components typically have a very low existence probability and are thus unlikely to represent an existing object.
In order to reduce computational complexity and memory requirements, we are interested in maintaining a rather small number of Bernoulli components.
Hence, we employ the concept of recycling
[
34
]
. Here, all Bernoulli components
j\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\text{R}}\hskip-0.85358pt\subseteq\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{J}}_{k}
with an existence probability
r_{k}^{(j)}
below a predefined threshold
\gamma_{\text{R}}
are approximated by a Poisson RFS by means of moment matching
[
34
]
, which results in a single Poisson component for each recycled Bernoulli component.
The overall approximated posterior PHD is given by

\tilde{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{k})=\lambda(\bm{x}_{k})+\sum_{j\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\text{R}}}r_{k}^{(j)}\hskip 0.85358ptf^{(j)}(\bm{x}_{k})\vskip-4.2679pt

(64)

where
\lambda(\bm{x}_{k})
is the posterior PHD given by (
41
) and
r_{k}^{(j)}
and
f^{(j)}(\bm{x}_{k})
are the Bernoulli parameters given by (
59
) and (
) or (
61
) and (
62
).
An interpretation of this approximation in terms of the Kullback-Leibler divergence was studied in
[
34
]
.

Summarizing, after applying the exact update step and the approximations described in the previous and this section, the approximate posterior pdf is again of PMB form. In fact, the MB part is represented by the existence probabilities
r_{k}^{(j)}
and spatial pdfs
f^{(j)}(\bm{x}_{k})
,
j\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{J}}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\text{R}}
in (
59
)–(
62

\tilde{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{k})
in (
64

Before we will assess the performance of our proposed algorithm in terms of some simulation experiments, we present a fast approximate computation of the marginal association probabilities
p\big(\bar{a}_{k}^{(j)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\hskip-0.85358pt\theta_{k}^{(j)}\big)
defined in Section
VII-A

IX
Conclusion

We proposed a track-before-detect (TBD) multiobject tracking method based on a new measurement model relying on probabilistic object-to-cell contributions.
Based on the new model, we derived a Poisson multi-Bernoulli (PMB) filter for TBD that uses scalable, belief propagation (BP) based probabilistic data association to achieve moderate runtimes despite the potentially large number of data cells used as measurements.
Our numerical results demonstrated that the proposed PMB filter for TBD can achieve very high tracking accuracy and outperforms existing TBD methods in scenarios with multiple interacting objects, i.e., in scenarios where multiple objects come close to each other and potentially contribute to the same resolution cells.
A possible direction of future research is the extension to scenarios where objects contribute to multiple data cells by adopting BP for mulitple-measurement to object associations
[
47
]
or an extension to neural-enhanced processing
[
48
]

.
