Power properties of the two-sample test based on the nearest neighbors graph
Rahul Raphael Kanekar

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of testing the equality of two multivariate distributions. One class of tests used for this purpose utilizes geometric graphs constructed using inter-point distances. So far, the asymptotic theory of these tests applies only to graphs which fall under the stabilizing graphs framework of Penrose and Yukich [37] . We study the case of the K K -nearest neighbors graph where K = k N K=k_{N} increases with sample size, which does not fall under the stabilizing graphs framework. Our main result gives detection thresholds for this test in parametrized families when k N = o ⁡ ( N 1 / 4 ) k_{N}=o(N^{1/4}) , thus extending the family of graphs where the theoretical behavior is known. We propose a 2-sided version of the test that removes an exponent gap that plagues the 1-sided test. Our result also shows that increasing the number of nearest neighbors boosts the power of the test. This provides theoretical justification for using denser graphs in testing the equality of two distributions.



1
Introduction

Given data from two sources, a natural question to ask is whether the two sources can be considered different in some sense. This is a basic question of comparison that arises in a variety of scientific settings. From the viewpoint of a statistician, this is the two sample problem and can be stated as follows.

Let
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N_{1}}\}
and
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{2}}\}
be i.i.d samples from the distributions
F
and
G
, respectively. The two sample problem is to test the hypotheses

H_{0}:F=G\quad\text{ v/s }\quad H_{1}:F\neq G.

In this paper, we focus on two sample tests that are nonparametric—they do not assume that
F,G
belong to some parametrized family of distributions—and distribution free—under the null
F=G,
the test is valid for any distribution
F
. While parametric approaches such as
t
-tests, likelihood ratio tests, score tests require the model to be well specified in order to be powerful (see
[
30
]
), nonparametric tests are often more reliable under weaker assumptions. For univariate distributions
F,G
, a host of nonparametric tests such as the two sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
[
48
]
, Mann-Whitney test
[
33
]
and the Wald–Wolfowitz runs test
[
52
]
. Univariate tests are often rank-based; they rely on the fact that univariate data can be ranked (see
[
29
]
for rank-based methods). Since there is no natural extension of ranks to multivariate data, it is difficult to generalize these tests to multivariate data.

This paper focuses on a class of multivariate two sample tests known as
graph-based two sample tests
. The idea behind graph-based two sample tests is to utilize inter-point distances to construct a graph that can summarize the data, in place of ranks.
Weiss [53]
explored this approach first, but the resulting test was not distribution-free. Following this,
Friedman and Rafsky [16]
introduced a two sample test using the Euclidean Minimal Spanning Tree (MST) constructed from the pooled data. In addition to being nonparametric and distribution free, this test extends the Wald–Wolfowitz runs test to higher dimensions, bolstering the idea of using graphs instead of ranks in higher dimensions. By changing the underlying graph, numerous alternate tests have been proposed.
Schilling [46]
and
Henze [22]
studied the test based on the
K
-nearest neighbors graph. Later,
Rosenbaum [43]
provided a test based on the minimal bipartite matching (the Cross-Match test) which is distribution free in finite samples.
Biswas et al. [8]
proposed a test based on the Hamiltonian Cycle, while
Ruth [45]
considers the regular minimum-weight spanning subgraph.

Key factors in the appeal of graph-based two sample tests are that they are versatile and easy to implement. Like many nonparametric tests, they can be implemented as a permutation test. When the sample size makes permutation tests infeasible, one can instead use the asymptotic normality under the null
[
16
,
22
,
21
]
to obtain a distribution free test. As shown in
Bhattacharya [6]
, this holds for a general family of graphs. Going beyond Euclidean data,
Chen and Friedman [12]
proposed a graph-based test for high-dimensional and object data which has good power in practice. Finally, graph-based two sample tests have found applications in a number of contexts where the problem at hand is one of comparing two populations
[
14
,
11
,
13
,
23
,
34
,
54
,
55
]
.

Related work

Apart from graph-based tests, a large class of two sample tests target some metric or measure of dissimilarity between distributions. In particular, tests based on the energy distance
[

4

Having said this, we refer the reader to more recent examples including
Chatterjee and Ramdas [10]

Other approaches include that of
Huang and Sen [24]

Ghosal and Sen [17]
defines a notion of multivariate ranks and uses it to generalize many univariate, distribution-free tests to higher dimensions. In addition to these, other solutions draw on a host of techniques including projection-averaging

]
, ball-divergence measure

[

,

and neural networks
[

]
.

1.2
Graph-based two sample tests

We now define a general graph-based test statistic, provide some examples and show how the statistic is used in testing the null. To this end, we define some terminology.

A graph functional
\mathscr{G}
is a function that for any finite
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
defines a graph
\mathscr{G}(S)
with vertex set
S.
The edge set of the graph
\mathscr{G}(S)
is denoted by
E(\mathscr{G}(S))
. For simplicity, we will assume that
\mathscr{G}(S)
has no self loops or multi-edges. The graph
\mathscr{G}(S)
can be directed or undirected.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathscr{X}_{N_{1}}:=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N_{1}}\}
and
\mathscr{Y}_{N_{2}}:=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{2}}\}
be i.i.d samples of size
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
from densities
f,g
, respectively. Let
\mathscr{G}
be a graph functional. The two sample test statistic based on
\mathscr{G}
is given by

T(\mathscr{G}(\mathscr{X}_{N_{1}}\cup\mathscr{Y}_{N_{2}}))=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{(X_{i},Y_{j})\in E(\mathscr{G}(\mathscr{X}_{N_{1}}\cup\mathscr{Y}_{N_{2}}))\}.

The statistic
T(\mathscr{G})
counts the number of edges in the graph that go across samples. To ease notation, we will often denote the statistic by
T(\mathscr{G})
, when the samples are clear.

:

N(0,I_{2})
(in red) and 10 samples of
N(0.2\cdot\mathbf{1},I_{2})
(in blue). On the right is MST formed out of 10 samples each of
N(0,I_{2})
(in red) and
N(2\cdot\mathbf{1},I_{2})
(in blue). The edges going across samples are colored black. Edges within samples are colored gold.

Example 1.1
.

(Friedman–Rafsky Test) The Friedman–Rafsky test results from taking
\mathscr{G}
to be the Euclidean minimal spanning tree. Given a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},
a spanning tree
\mathcal{T}
of
S
is a connected, undirected graph with vertex set
S
and no cycles. The length of a spanning tree is the sum of the lengths of all edges in the tree. A tree
\mathcal{T}
is called the Euclidean MST of
S
if its length is at most the length of any other spanning tree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of
S.
Thus, the Euclidean MST
\mathcal{T}
is a graph functional and yields a two sample test. Figure
1
gives examples of the Euclidean MST.

This test, introduced in
Friedman and Rafsky [16]
, was one of the first graph-based tests proposed. One fact of particular interest is that when
d=1,
the Friedman–Rafsky test gives exactly the Wald–Wolfowitz runs test. This is because, in dimension one the Euclidean MST is simply the line graph connecting adjacent points in the ranked data. While it was originally presented as a permutation test, the test statistic is also asymptotically normal (see
[
6
,
16
,
22
]
) which provides an alternate way of implementing the test.

Example 1.2
.

(
K
-NN test) Given a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
K\in\mathbb{N},
the
K
-nearest neighbors graph
\mathscr{G}_{K}(S)
on
S
is obtained by inserting the directed edge
(a,b)
in
E(\mathscr{G}_{K}(S))
for
a,b\in S
, if and only if
b
is one of the
K
closest points to
a
in Euclidean distance among the points in
S
. In this case,
\mathscr{G}_{K}
is a directed graph functional since each edge is from a point to one of its
K
-nearest neighbors. Figure
2
gives examples of the
K
-NN graph.

Figure 2
:
On the left is the directed
3-
NN graph formed from 10 samples of
N(0,I_{2})
(colored red) and 10 samples of
N(0.2,I_{2})
(colored blue). On the right is
3-
NN graph formed out of 10 samples each of
N(0,I_{2})
(red) and
N(2,I_{2})
(blue). The edges going from sample
1
to sample
2
are colored black. Edges within samples are colored gold.

The test based on the
K
-nearest neighbors graph was introduced in
Henze [22]
and will be our main object of interest.

Figures
1
and
2
highlight an interesting feature of graph-based two sample tests. Both figures contain data where the null (red points) in each case is
N(0,I_{2})
and the alternates (blue points) are
N(0.2\cdot\mathbf{1},I_{2})
and
N(2\cdot\mathbf{1},I_{2})
, respectively. In both figures, there are much fewer cross-sample edges in the second case i.e. where the alternate is
N(2,I_{2}).
This highlights an interesting principle namely, the more ”different” two distributions are, the fewer cross-sample edges there are. Accordingly, the two sample test is often implemented as a 1-sided test where the null is rejected for

T(\mathscr{G}(\mathscr{X}_{N_{1}}\cup\mathscr{Y}_{N_{2}}))\leq\tau_{\alpha},

where
\tau_{\alpha}
is a threshold to be determined that will give a level-
\alpha
test. We will elaborate on this further in Section
2
when we look at the consistency of the test based on the
K
-nearest neighbors graph. We will also present a 2-sided version of the test and a key contribution of our work is a rigorous comparison of the power properties of the two tests.

1.3
Poissonization

In order to make the asymptotic behavior easier to analyze, we will be considering the Poissonized setting. This set up allows us to use the spatial independence of the Poisson process. Using the well known de-Poissonization techniques described in
Penrose [39]
, the results for the Poissonized setting can be used to derive analogous results for the un-Poissonized setting. See
Remark

1.1
for more on de-Poissonization.

Let
f,g
be densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define
\phi_{N}(x):=\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(x)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(x)
where
N_{1}+N_{2}=N.
Let
\mathcal{Z}_{N}:=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{L_{N}}\}
be the points sampled in the Poisson process with intensity function
N\phi_{N}=N_{1}f+N_{2}g.
Here, the number of points
L_{N}
is a Poisson random variable with parameter
N.
For each point
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N},
we assign the value
1
or
2
to the label
c_{z}
with

c_{z}=\begin{cases}1\text{ with probability }\frac{N_{1}f(x)}{N_{1}f(x)+N_{2}g(x)},\\
\\
2\text{ with probability }\frac{N_{2}g(x)}{N_{1}f(x)+N_{2}g(x)}.\end{cases}

(1.1)

The labels are assigned to all the points in
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
independently. We will assume that
N_{1},N_{2}
are such that

\frac{N_{1}}{N}-p=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\penalty\ \frac{N_{2}}{N}-q=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

where
q:=1-p
. For the Poissonized setting we define the test statistic as

T(\mathscr{G}_{K}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\psi(c_{x},c_{y})\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\},

(1.2)

where
\psi(c_{x},c_{y})=\mathbf{1}\{c_{x}=1,c_{y}=2\}.
In Section
3.2
we will show that if
\{k_{N}\}_{N}
is a sequence of natural numbers such that
k_{N}=o(N^{1/4})
, then under
H_{0}

\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))))\to N(0,\sigma^{2}_{0}),

for some
\sigma^{2}_{0}>0.
Hence, the test that rejects when

\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))))\leq\sigma_{0}z_{\alpha},

(1.3)

where
z_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-quantile of the standard normal, is an asymptotically level-
\alpha
test. Traditionally, this is the way the two sample graph-based test is implemented; as a 1-sided test. However, we will also be considering the 2-sided test i.e. the test that rejects when

\left|\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))))\right|\geq\sigma_{0}z_{1-\alpha/2},

(1.4)

where
z_{1-\alpha/2}
is the
1-\alpha/2
quantile of the standard normal.

Remark 1.1

(Regarding de-Poissonization)
.

The Poissonized setting assumes that the sample size is
\textnormal{Poisson}(N)
, while the binomial setting takes the sample size to be some fixed
N
. De-Poissonization is a method by which asymptotic results in the Poissonized setting can be transferred to the fixed size binomial setting. In the context of geometric probability
Penrose and Yukich [36]
,
Penrose and Yukich [38]
define what are called stabilizing graph functionals, and provide results on de-Poissonization for this class (see, for example
[
36
, Lemma 4.2]
and
[
38
, Theorem 2.3]
). For
k_{N}
increasing with sample size, the
k_{N}
-NN graph is not stabilizing, and hence the results do not apply directly. A more general recipe for de-Poissonization is outlined in
Penrose [39, Section 2.5]
. We believe that this can be used to de-Poissonize in the setting we consider. However, we do not have a proof at this time.

1.4
Summary of results

The present work investigates the power properties of two sample tests based on the
K
-nearest neighbors (KNN) graph as defined in
Example

1.2
. Our primary focus is on the local asymptotic power of this test in parametric families, when the number of neighbors
K
is allowed to grow with the sample size
N
. Since the parameter
K
directly controls the edge density of the underlying graph, its influence on the power is an interesting theoretical question. From an empirical standpoint, the improvement in power obtained by increasing the edge density in graph-based tests has been noted before in the literature.
Friedman and Rafsky [16]
, and
Chen and Friedman [12]
consider a denser version of the MST, known as the
K
-MST. In both works, the authors noted an improvement in power with a higher value of
K
. In particular, the authors of
Chen and Friedman [12]
suggested that the power improves because an increased graph density captures greater
similarity information
between samples. More in line with our work, simulations in
Bhattacharya [5]
showed that allowing
K
to grow with
N
leads to improved power for the
K
-NN test.

In our work, we study the local asymptotic power of the
K
-NN test when
K=k_{N}=o(N^{\frac{1}{4}}).
This fills an important gap in the literature by linking the growth of
K
to the power of the two sample test. Our contributions are two-fold:

•

Previous works on the local asymptotic power of graph-based statistics (see
[
6
,
24
]
) have typically relied on the stabilizing graphs framework
[
36
,
37
]
. While the
K
-NN graph is stabilizing when
K
is fixed, it is not stabilizing when
K
is allowed to grow with the sample size. To go beyond the fixed
K
case, we rely on a careful, hands-on analysis of the test statistic which allows us to precisely quantify the local dependence structure induced by the
K
-NN graph. A high-level description of these techniques is given in
Section

1.4.1
.

•

Using the above approach, we establish exact detection thresholds of the 1- and 2-sided tests defined in (
1.3
) and (
1.4
). The detection thresholds depend intimately on the growth of
K
with respect to
N
and on the dimension
d
of the data. We make this dependence explicit and highlight the consequences on the power of the 1- and 2-sided tests. This is described in
Section

1.4.2
with the detailed results stated in
Section

4
.

1.4.1
Technical contributions

When deriving results for the case of growing
K,
the main technical hurdle is that the underlying graph functional is no longer stabilizing. Recall that a graph functional
\mathcal{G}
associates a graph
\mathcal{G}(S)
given any finite set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Given any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
we denote by
E(x,\mathcal{G}(S))
the set of edges incident on
x
in the graph
\mathcal{G}(S\cup{x}).
A graph functional
\mathcal{G}
is said to be stabilizing if for any
S
and
x,
there exists an
R<\infty
such that for any finite
\mathcal{A}\subset B(x,R)^{c},

E(x,\mathcal{G}(S))=E(x,\mathcal{G}((S\cap B(x,R))\cup\mathcal{A})).

Intuitively,
\mathcal{G}
is said to be stabilizing if for any point
x
in the graph, adding points far away enough does not affect the edges incident on
x
. Since edges are local, it is enough to focus on a small neighborhood of a typical point which in turn converges to a homogeneous Poisson process of intensity one after suitable spatial rescaling. Further, stabilizing graph functionals often have natural analogs on infinite sets. For instance, the fixed
K
-nearest neighbor graph can be defined on any locally finite point set, and the minimal spanning tree admits a version on infinite vertices (see
Aldous and Steele [1]
). As a result, the large sample limit of statistics calculated on a stabilizing graph functional can often be identified as expectations of the corresponding infinite volume graph functional evaluated on a homogeneous Poisson process. An example of this is
Penrose and Yukich [37, Theorem 2.8]
We refer the reader to
Penrose and Yukich [36]
,
Penrose and Yukich [37]
,
Penrose and Yukich [38]
for full details on stabilization.

In the case of
K
growing, the value of
K
depends on the size of the vertex set, and adding enough points to the vertex set eventually changes the neighbors of
x
. As a result, the graph functional is not stabilizing for growing
K
. At a more fundamental level, since the neighborhood of each vertex depends on the size of the vertex set there is no natural extension of this graph functional to infinite point sets. However, the local structure of the graph is still intact since each point
x
only connects to the
K
points closest to it. This allows us to restrict our attention to a ball of shrinking radius around
x
for all the calculations involved in the proofs. When
K
is fixed, the distance between nearest neighbors is of the order
N^{-\frac{1}{d}}
regardless of the value of
K
. However, when
K=k_{N}
grows with
N,
the distance is of order
(k_{N}N^{-1})^{\frac{1}{d}}.
By incorporating
k_{N}
into the spatial scaling, it is still possible to approximate the neighborhood of each point by a homogeneous Poisson process. However, the limiting values have to be calculated explicitly since they cannot be expressed in terms of known graph functionals. The technical tools we use are provided in the supplementary material.

We have three main technical results.

1.

In
Section

2
we derive the weak limit of the statistic (
1.2
). In particular, we show that appropriately normalized, this converges in probability to the Henze-Penrose (HP) divergence. This same property was shown in
Bhattacharya [6]
for fixed
K
. This is the first of our results that show the continuity between the asymptotics of the statistic for fixed and growing
K
.

2.

We derive the limiting distribution of the Poissonized test statistic under general alternatives and establish a central limit theorem for the appropriately standardized statistic (Theorem
3.2
). We show that the asymptotic variance is of order
Nk_{N}^{2}
and that asymptotic normality holds for
k_{N}=o(N^{1/4})
. The proof uses Stein’s method for dependency graphs applied to a truncated version of the statistic, in the spirit of techniques developed for stabilizing graph functionals in
Penrose and Yukich [38]
. Since the stabilizing graph framework is not directly applicable in our setting, the argument relies on more hands-on bounds that quantify the local dependence structure of the nearest neighbor graph. These bounds allow us to establish convergence of the standardized statistic to normality in Wasserstein distance and suggest that stabilizing-graph methods may extend to a broader class of graph functionals.

3.

We also provide a CLT for a conditional version of the statistic which relates to the implementation of the two sample test as a permutation test. Under the null, after conditioning on the locations of the points, the labels are exchangeable. Thus, instead of centering by the marginal expectation, we can center by the conditional expectation after conditioning on the locations of the points. The resulting statistic can be used to implement a conditional test. This statistic too is asymptotically normal after scaling by
k_{N}N^{\frac{1}{2}}
(Theorem
3.1
). Using Stein’s method for dependency graphs, one can show that this holds for any sequence
k_{N}\to\infty
such that
k_{N}=o(N).

As an aside, we should note that the result for the asymptotic normality of the unconditional statistic holds for a smaller range of
k_{N}
than that for the conditional statistic. We elaborate on this further in the discussion following
Theorem

3.2
.

1.4.2
Power under local alternatives

Local asymptotic power is most naturally formulated by considering alternatives that approach the null as the sample size increases. In parametric families, where distributions can be represented by low-dimensional parameters, this corresponds to allowing the parameter under the alternative to converge to the null parameter at a certain rate with respect to the sample size.

More rigorously, let
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a family of distributions parametrized by elements of
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}.
Fix
\theta_{1}\in\Theta
to be the null parameter and let
\{\theta_{N}\}_{N}
be a sequence in
\Theta
representing the local alternatives. The quantity of interest is the
detection threshold
—the maximal rate at which
\theta_{N}
may approach
\theta_{1}
while the test continues to exhibit nontrivial asymptotic power. More precisely, the detection threshold of a two sample test is the sequence
\{\epsilon_{N}\}_{N}
such that
\|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}\|\gg\epsilon_{N}
implies that the limiting power is
1
and
\|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}\|\ll\epsilon_{N}
implies that the limiting power of the test is its level,
\alpha.

Figure 3
:
A comparison of the detection thresholds exponents of the 1- and 2-sided tests for a given
k_{N}=N^{\gamma}
. A lower value of the exponent represents a better detection threshold. The orange line represents the parametric rate and the blue line represents the case of fixed alternatives. In both cases, change in detection threshold occurs at the critical dimension
d=d_{c}(\gamma)
.

The graph-based two sample tests studied in the literature formulate the test as a 1-sided test. A key contribution of our work is to study the 2-sided version in (
4.3
) and carry out a comparison of the two, in the setting where
K=k_{N}=o(N^{\frac{1}{4}})
. To simplify matters, we will assume that
k_{N}=N^{\gamma}
for some
0<\gamma<1/4.

•

We can define a critical dimension
d_{c}
given by

d_{c}(\gamma)=\lfloor 8(1-\gamma)\rfloor.

(1.5)

When
d\leq d_{c}(\gamma)
, the detection thresholds of the 1- and 2-sided tests are the same and equal
N^{-\frac{1}{4}}.
This means, if
\|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}\|\ll N^{-\frac{1}{4}},
the limiting power of both tests is
\alpha
and if
\|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}\|\gg N^{-\frac{1}{4}},
then the limiting power is 1. When
\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}=hN^{-\frac{1}{4}}
for some
h\in\mathbb{R}^{p},
we give an expression for the limiting power in terms of
h
and the parametric family
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
.

•

When
d>d_{c}(\gamma),
the situation is more delicate and the behavior of the two tests is drastically different. The limiting power of the 1-sided test can be described as follows.

–

If
\|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}\|\ll N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}},
then the limiting power is
\alpha
.

–

If
\|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}\|\gg N^{-\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}},
then the limiting power is 1.

–

If
\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}=h\epsilon_{N}
for some unit vector
h
and
N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}}\ll\epsilon_{N}\ll N^{-\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}},
then the limiting power is 0 or 1 depending on
h.

For
d>d_{c}(\gamma),
there is an
exponent gap
in the detection thresholds. Let
h
be the unit vector in the direction of
\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}
. Depending on
h,
the detection threshold is either
N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}}
or
N^{-\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}}
. In the former case, the detection threshold represents a blessing of dimensionality since it improves with the dimension. On the other hand, the second case displays a curse of dimensionality since the detection threshold worsens with dimensions. The first frame of
Figure

3
makes this clearer. The black and green curves represent the two rates. Depending on the direction
h,
the detection threshold lies on either one of the curves. We also see the exponent gap widening with dimension.

•

The 2-sided test on the other hand demonstrates much more stable behavior with respect to the direction when
d>d_{c}(\gamma).
The detection threshold is
N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}}
. The 2-sided test has limiting power
\alpha
or 1 depending on whether
\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}
converges to 0 faster or slower than
N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}}
, regardless of the direction in which
\theta_{N}
approaches
\theta_{1}
. In particular, it does not demonstrate the exponent gap that plagues the 1-sided test. This is shown in the second frame of
Figure

3
. The detection threshold exponent for the 2-sided test is given by a single curve as opposed to two curves for the 1-sided test. We also see the detection threshold improving with dimension after the critical point
d_{c}(\gamma)
, and it approaches the parametric rate of
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

1.5
Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we derive the weak limit of the test statistic and use it to show that the 1- and 2-sided tests are consistent. In Section
3
, we derive the limiting distribution of the test statistic under general alternatives. Section
4
gives detailed results on the detection thresholds of the 1- and 2-sided tests as well as the limiting power at these thresholds. Section
5
is dedicated to simulations on synthetic data used to validate the theoretical results, and a real data experiment. This section also contains a runtime analysis of the test.

2
Consistency

In this section we show that the 1-sided and 2-sided tests are both consistent. For this, we require the notion of the
Henze-Penrose dissimilarity measure
between two densities.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f,g
be two densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Let
p\in(0,1)
and
q:=1-p.
Then, the Henze-Penrose (HP) dissimilarity measure between
f,g
is defined as

\delta(f,g,p)=pq\int\frac{f(x)g(x)}{pf(x)+qg(x)}\penalty\ dx.

This belongs to the larger class of
f-
dissimilarities as defined in
Györfi and Nemetz [20]
. The following proposition shows that the HP dissimilarity is the limiting value of the statistic
T(\mathcal{Z}_{N}).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f,g
be two densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Let
\{k_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of natural numbers such that
k_{N}=o(N)
. Then,

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\xrightarrow{p}\delta(f,g,p).

Under the null
f=g,

\delta(f,f,p)=pq.
Furthermore, from
Györfi and Nemetz [20, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1]
we have that
\delta(f,g,p)\leq pq
with equality holding if and only if
f,g
are equal everywhere except on a set of measure
0.
These facts combined with the asymptotic normality of
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
under the null (
Theorem

3.2
in the next section) show that the 1- and 2-sided tests are consistent. The convergence in
Proposition

2.1
hinges on the fact that for
k_{N}\to\infty,
the distance between a point and its
k_{N}
nearest neighbors converges to 0 at the rate
(k_{N}N^{-1})^{\frac{1}{d}}
. As an estimator of the HP dissimilarity, the statistic
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
is akin to nearest neighbor estimators of densities. Our proof also suggests that it should be possible to extend this convergence for other norms in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The HP dissimilarity has been repeatedly obtained as the limiting value of graph-based statistics. Proposition 2.1 of
Bhattacharya [6]
shows that when
K
is fixed,

\frac{1}{N}T(\mathscr{G}_{K}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\xrightarrow{p}K\cdot\delta(f,g,p),

In fact, the same result proves the above convergence to the HP dissimilarity holds for any stabilizing graph functional which also satisfies some additional degree conditions. Earlier,
Henze and Penrose [21]
proved the same result for the MST. In
Arias-Castro and Pelletier [2]
the authors showed that the HP dissimilarity is also the weak limit for the statistic based on the minimum bipartite matching (MBM) graph, as defined in
Rosenbaum [43]
. It is unknown whether the MBM is stabilizing, which shows that stabilization is not a prerequisite.

These results accompanied by
Proposition

2.1
suggest a general extension to
dense
versions of a given base graph. Note that the
K
-NN graph can be constructed by iteratively constructing the
1
-NN graph. Initially, all edges are available to us, and we construct the usual
1
-NN graph. On the second iteration, we construct the
1
-NN graph restricted to those edges not occurring in the first iteration. We can repeat this process
K
times, each time restricting the set of available edges to those that have not been selected in any previous iteration. Taking the union of these
K
graphs gives the
K
-NN graph. This procedure can be used to produce a dense version of any given base graph. One can now ask a more general question—does a result similar to
Proposition

2.1
hold if we consider the number of copies
K
to be fixed or increasing with
N
, for a different base graph? By tailoring the approach for each specific base graph, it should be possible to show the HP dissimilarity as the limiting object in other cases. To handle a large family of graphs in one fell swoop, one can attempt to extend the definition of stabilization itself to incorporate the notion of varying densities.

The proof of Proposition
2.1
is given in
Appendix

B
.

3
Distribution under general alternatives

Recall the Poissonized set up from Section
1.3
. Let
f,g
be densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Define

\displaystyle\phi_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(x)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(x),

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=pf(x)+qg(x).

\mathcal{Z}_{N}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{L_{N}}\}
denotes the set of points sampled from a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity function
N\phi_{N}(x)=N_{1}f(x)+N_{2}g(x)
where
N_{1}+N_{2}=N.
Since
f,g
are densities, we have that
L_{N}\sim\textnormal{Poisson}(N).
To each point
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N},
we assign the label
1
or
2
with probabilities proportional to
N_{1}f(z),N_{2}g(z).

The normalized test statistic is

\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))=\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{1}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})))).

In this section, we will derive the asymptotic distribution of
\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))
as
N\to\infty
when
k_{N}=o(N^{1/4}).
To start, we prove the asymptotic normality of
\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
after conditioning on an appropriate sigma algebra.

3.1
CLT for a conditional statistic

To motivate the conditional CLT, it is useful to consider formulation of graph-based two sample tests as permutation tests. In the un-Poissonized setting, the data consists of
X_{1},..,X_{N_{1}}\overset{\textnormal{i.i.d}}{\sim}F
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{2}}\overset{\textnormal{i.i.d}}{\sim}G
for two distribution
F,G
. The test statistic is then defined as

T(\mathscr{G}_{K})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{(X_{i},Y_{j})\in\mathscr{G}_{K}\},

where
\mathscr{G}_{K}
is the directed
K
-NN graph assembled from
\{X_{1},..,X_{N_{1}},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{2}}\}.
Under the null
F=G,
an alternate way to sample the data is to first consider
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
points,
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}\overset{\textnormal{i.i.d}}{\sim}F.
The labels of the points are
\{c_{1},\dots,c_{N}\}
such that
c_{i}=1
for
i\leq N_{1}
and
c_{i}=2
otherwise. The test statistic is now defined as

T(\mathscr{G}_{K})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{(Z_{i},Z_{j})\in\mathscr{G}_{K},c_{i}=1,c_{j}=2\}.

Sampling
B
i.i.d uniform permutations
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
, we can define

T^{(b)}(\mathscr{G}_{K})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{(Z_{i},Z_{j})\in\mathscr{G}_{K},c_{\tau_{b}(i)}=1,c_{\tau_{b}(j)}=2\}.

Under the null,
T(\mathscr{G}_{K}),T^{(1)}(\mathscr{G}_{K}),\dots,T^{(B)}(\mathscr{G}_{K})
are exchangeable due to the independence of locations and labels, which yields the permutation test. Thus, under the null the permutation test is obtained by conditioning on the locations of the points and resampling the labels.

Analogously, in the Poissonized setting we instead condition on
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\sigma(\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{L_{N}}\})
, the sigma algebra containing the information on the locations of the points. The randomness now comes only from the labels, and we consider the conditional statistic

\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}k_{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{1}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})|\mathcal{F}_{N})).

We now show the asymptotic normality of this statistic under general alternatives.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f,g
be densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Let
k_{N}=o(N).
Define
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\sigma(\mathcal{Z}_{N},L_{N}),
the sigma algebra generated by the points in the Poisson process. Then we have that

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))}{\sqrt{\textnormal{Var}(\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{N})}}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).

(3.1)

Furthermore,

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\textnormal{Var}(\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))|\mathcal{F}_{N})\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{\textnormal{cond}},

(3.2)

where

\sigma^{2}_{\textnormal{cond}}=pq\int\frac{f(x)g(x)(pf(x)-qg(x))^{2}}{\phi^{3}(x)}\penalty\ dx.

Under the null, the conditional variance in (
3.1
) is easily obtained from the adjacency structure of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{k_{N}}.
Indeed, after conditioning on
\mathcal{Z}_{N},
the statistic
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
is a sum of dependent Bernoulli random variables, with the dependence structure encoded by
\mathcal{G}_{k_{N}}.
We elaborate on the exact conditional variance calculation in
Section

5.1
. The above result gives a pivotal statistic that can be used to carry out the test when the sample size makes the permutation test computationally prohibitive. The proof used Stein’s method for dependency graphs and is given in
Appendix

B
.

3.2
CLT for the test statistic

We now show the asymptotic normality in the unconditional case.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f,g
be densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
k_{N}\to\infty
with
k_{N}=o(N).
Then,

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})))\to\sigma^{2}

where

\sigma^{2}=pq\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\frac{f(x)g(x)(pf(x)-qg(x))^{2}}{\phi(x)^{3}}\penalty\ dx+p^{2}q^{2}\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\frac{f(x)^{2}g(x)^{2}}{\phi(x)^{3}}\penalty\ dx.

(3.3)

Furthermore, when
k_{N}=o(N^{1/4}),

\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^{2})

Theorem

3.2
relies on the fact that when
k_{N}=o(N),
the
k_{N}
-nearest neighbors of a point all lie in a ball of shrinking radius around it. In particular, the probability of two points lying in well-separated regions being nearest neighbors is negligible. To use this in the proof, we divide the region into a grid of boxes shrinking at an appropriate rate. By the argument sketched above, we can then restrict ourselves to considering nearest neighbors within neighboring boxes. This allows us to prove asymptotic normality using Stein’s method for a truncated version of
\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))
. By using Slutsky’s theorem, we recover the asymptotic normality result for the original statistic. Theorem
3.2
is proved in
Appendix

B
.

The asymptotic normality in
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

3.2
is proved using Stein’s method for dependency graphs. However, the range of
k_{N}
considered is different for the two—respectively,
k_{N}=o(N)
and
k_{N}=o(N^{\frac{1}{4}})
. We now give a heuristic of why this happens. The Stein’s method approach requires bounding the Wasserstein distance to the standard normal. The Wasserstein bound involves powers of
k_{N}
in both cases. These are unavoidable, since the dependence increases with
k_{N}
, which makes the normal approximation worse with increasing growth of
k_{N}
. In
Theorem

3.1
, after conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{N},
the randomness comes only from the labels of the points. In this case, the test statistic is a sum of Bernoulli random variables indexed by the edges of the graph. Only the random variables corresponding to the edges that share an end point can be correlated. Hence, the dependency graph in Stein’s method is closely related to the
k_{N}
-NN graph obtained from the points in
\mathcal{Z}_{N}.
This additional structure allows us to quantify the local dependence very well.

In contrast, the marginal CLT in
Theorem

3.2
has much greater dependence between points. This arises because, without any conditioning, potentially any two points could be nearest neighbors of each other. The truncation argument we use does restrict this dependence to nearby points, but the Wasserstein bound still contains additional powers of
k_{N}
compared to the conditional case. As a result, we only obtain asymptotic normality for
k_{N}=o(N^{\frac{1}{4}})
. It is likely that there is a different argument which holds for the full range of
k_{N}=o(N)
. Having pointed this out, we should note that
Theorem

3.1
gives a valid way to test the null for
k_{N}=o(N)
. Thus, the restriction on the growth of
k_{N}
does not pose a practical threat to the test. We elaborate on this further in
Section

5
.

4
Local power of the two sample test

We now come to the local power of the
K
-NN test in a parametric family. We first state the assumptions we make on the family of distributions we consider. The assumptions we make are the same as the ones made by
Bhattacharya [6]
in proving the results for fixed
K.

For a function
g(z_{1},z_{2}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R},
we denote the gradient and Hessian with respect to
z_{1}
for a fixed
z_{2}
by
\nabla_{z_{1}}g(z_{1},z_{2})
and
\textnormal{H}_{z_{1}}g(z_{1},z_{2})
similarly denote by
\nabla_{z_{2}}g(z_{1},z_{2})
and
\textnormal{H}_{z_{2}}g(z_{1},z_{2})
the gradient and the Hessian of
g
with respect to
z_{2}
for a fixed
z_{1}.
With the notation defined, we now state our assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a family of distributions parametrized by the elements of a convex set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.
We will assume the following properties for
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta}:

1.

For all
\theta\in\Theta,
the density
p(\cdot|\theta)
has a common support
S
such that
S
is compact, convex with non-empty interior and
p(\cdot|\theta)
are uniformly bounded above and below on
S.

2.

The support
S
satisfies
S=\overline{\textnormal{int}(S)}
and
\partial S
has Lebesgue measure zero.

3.

For all
\theta\in\Theta
the functions
p(\cdot|\theta)
and
\nabla_{\theta}p(\cdot|\theta)
are three times continuously differentiable over
S
.

4.

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{h^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{1}}p(X|\theta)}{p(X|\theta)}\right]^{2}
is finite and positive for all
\theta\in\Theta
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\penalty\ h\neq 0.

5.

The function
p(x|\cdot)
is three times continuously differentiable in
\Theta
for all
x\in S.

We assume that all densities have the same compact support to simplify the proofs. However, we fully expect that one can circumvent it assuming that the tails of the distributions decay fast enough. Indeed, our simulations involve the spherical normal family which does not satisfy the compact support assumption. However, the behavior predicted by our results is observed, indicating that our results do hold in the more general setting as well. The assumptions on the differentiability and smoothness are required to analyze the difference in the null and alternate means under local alternative cases. Under looser assumptions on the differentiability, our methods could give upper and lower bounds on the detection thresholds, but it will be difficult to obtain exact power expressions.

To find the local power of the
K
-NN test, we need the asymptotic distribution of the statistic when the two densities
f,g
are given by
f=p(\cdot|\theta_{1}),g=p(\cdot|\theta_{N})
where
\theta_{1}\in\Theta
is fixed and
\theta_{N}\to\theta_{1}
. The proof of
Theorem

3.2
, which gives the asymptotic distribution under general alternatives, can be adapted to give the required result. This is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f=p(\cdot|\theta_{1}),g=p(\cdot|\theta_{N})
such that
\theta_{N}\to\theta_{1}
as
N\to\infty.
Let
k_{N}=o(N^{1/4}).
Then

\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^{2}_{0})

where

\sigma^{2}_{0}=pq((p-q)^{2}+pq).

(4.1)

The null variance in (
4.1
) is obtained by considering the general unconditional variance in (
3.3
) for
f=g.
Under the null, the expected value of the statistic is
Nk_{N}\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N^{2}}.
Therefore, the 1-sided test rejects the null hypothesis when

\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}\sigma_{0}}\left(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-Nk_{N}\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N^{2}}\right)<z_{\alpha}

(4.2)

and the 2-sided test rejects when

\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}\sigma_{0}}\left|T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-Nk_{N}\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N^{2}}\right|>z_{1-\alpha/2}

(4.3)

where
z_{\alpha},z_{1-\alpha/2}
are the
\alpha
and
1-\alpha/2
quantiles, respectively, of the standard normal.

To state the theorems, we need some notation. For
\theta_{1}\in\Theta
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{m},
we define

\displaystyle a(\theta_{1},h)

\displaystyle:=\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{h^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{1}}p(X|\theta_{1})}{p(X|\theta_{1})}\right]^{2}

(4.4)

\displaystyle b(\theta_{1},h)

\displaystyle:=\frac{p^{2}q}{2(d+2)V_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}\sigma_{0}}\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle S$}}$}}\nolimits h^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\left(\frac{\text{tr}(\text{H}_{x}p(x|\theta_{1}))}{p(x|\theta_{1})}\right)p^{\frac{d-2}{d}}(x|\theta_{1})\penalty\ dx,

(4.5)

where
\sigma^{2}_{0}
is the null variance in (
4.1
),
r=2pq
,
V_{d}
denotes the volume of the
d
dimensional unit ball and
\text{H}_{x}
(as stated previously) denotes the Hessian with respect to
x
at
x.

In our results, we assume
k_{N}=N^{\gamma}
for some
0<\gamma<1/4
. Recall that the critical dimension
d_{c}(\gamma)
is defined as

d_{c}(\gamma)=\lfloor 8(1-\gamma)\rfloor.

(4.6)

The limiting power of both tests is different depending on whether
d\leq d_{c}(\gamma)
or

below criticality
and

However, the definition of the critical dimension can be tedious. To better communicate the results, we stick to the case of

4.1
Local power below criticality

We now state the detection thresholds for

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a parametrized family satisfying
Assumption

4.1
. Let

4.3
Proof sketch

We briefly describe the proof strategy of
Theorems

4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
. To test the null, we use the statistic

\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}\left(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})))\right)

Using the CLT under local alternatives given in
Lemma

4.1
the power analysis comes down to estimating the difference in null and alternate means,

\Delta_{N}:=\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}(\mu_{N}(\theta_{1},\theta_{N})-\mu_{N}(\theta_{1},\theta_{1})),

where
\mu_{N}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
denotes the expected value of
T(\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))
when
f=p(\cdot|\theta_{1}),g=p(\cdot|\theta_{2}).

Suppose we take
\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}=h\delta_{N}
for some
h\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.
Expanding the function
\mu_{N}(\theta_{1},\cdot)
around
\theta_{1}
and analyzing the gradient and Hessian terms, we get that

\Delta_{N}\approx-a(h,\theta_{1})N^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{N}^{2}+b(h,\theta_{1})N^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\delta_{N},

(4.9)

where
a(h,\theta_{1}),b(h,\theta_{1})
are as defined in (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), respectively. With the above expansion, we can now derive the limiting power for the 1- and 2-sided tests

We define the following, more general versions of the critical dimension.

\displaystyle d_{c}^{\textnormal{lower}}(k_{N}):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lfloor 8(1-\log_{N}k_{N})\rfloor,

(4.10)

\displaystyle d_{c}^{\textnormal{upper}}(k_{N}):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lfloor 8(1-\log_{N}k_{N})\rfloor.

(4.11)

Note that for
k_{N}=N^{\gamma},
the critical dimensions above are the same and equal
d_{c}(\gamma)
defined in (
4.6
).

The 1-sided test has limiting power 0,
\alpha
and 1 when
\Delta_{N}\to-\infty,0,\infty
, respectively. The 2-sided test has limiting power
\alpha
and
1
when
|\Delta_{N}|\to 0,\infty
, respectively. Using (
4.9
), we can derive the limiting power. When,
d\leq d_{c}^{\textnormal{lower}}(k_{N}),
we have

•

If
\delta_{N}\ll N^{-\frac{1}{4}},
then
\Delta_{N}\to 0
and the limiting power of both tests is
\alpha.

•

If
\delta_{N}\gg N^{-\frac{1}{4}},
then
\Delta_{N}\to-\infty
and both tests have power
1.

This is exactly the result of
Theorem

4.1
, with a more general version of the critical dimension. Similarly, we can derive a general version of
Theorem

4.2
by considering
d\geq d_{c}^{\textnormal{upper}}(k_{N}).
In this case, we have more general definitions of the lower and upper threshold. Specifically, we see:

•

The lower threshold is given by
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{N}{k_{N}}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}.
When
\delta_{N}
decays faster than this rate,
\Delta_{N}\to 0
and both tests have power
\alpha.
When
k_{N}=N^{\gamma},
the lower threshold is equal to the lower threshold defined in
Section

4.2
.

•

The upper threshold is given by
\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}.
When
\delta_{N}
decays slower than this rate, the first term in (
4.9
) dominates and
\Delta_{N}\to-\infty.
For
k_{N}=N^{\gamma},
the upper threshold is exactly the upper threshold defined in
Section

4.2
.

•

The exponent gap is given by the rates
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{N}{k_{N}}\right)
and
\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}.
In this gap, it is always the second term of (
4.9
) that dominates, and
|\Delta_{N}|\to\infty
. Hence, the 2-sided test always has power
1
. The sign of
b(h,\theta_{1})
on the other hand determines the power of the 1-sided test. Depending on
b(h,\theta_{1})
being positive or negative,
\Delta_{N}\to\infty
or
-\infty
, respectively, and the 1-sided test has power 0 or 1 accordingly.

Similarly, at the exact thresholds
\delta_{N}=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{N}{k_{N}}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}
and
\delta_{N}=\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}
, the limiting value of
\Delta_{N}
can be found out from (
4.9
), and we get the general version of
Theorem

4.3
. The restrictions of
b(h,\theta_{1})\neq 0
and
a(h,\theta_{1})-b(h,\theta_{1})\neq 0
in
Theorems

4.2
and
4.3
come from the fact that we only consider the first two terms in the expansion (
4.9
). The cases of
b(h,\theta_{1})=0
and
a(h,\theta_{1})-b(h,\theta_{1})
are not covered by the above expansion. However, by considering further terms, it should be possible to cover other cases as well.

(
4.9
) provides intuition for the exponent gap of the 1-sided test. Recall that the test statistic counts the number of cross-group edges in the
K
-NN graph formed by pooling the two groups
\mathcal{X}_{N}:={X_{1},\dots,X_{N_{1}}}\sim P_{\theta_{1}}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{N}:={Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{2}}}\sim P_{\theta_{N}}
. The number of points in
\mathcal{Y}_{N}
with nearest neighbors in
\mathcal{X}_{N}
depends on both cross-group distances
|X_{i}-Y_{j}|
and within-group distances
|Y_{i}-Y_{j}|
. As
|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}|
increases, cross-group distances typically increase, corresponding to the first term of (
4.9
), where
-a(h,\theta_{1})
is always negative. In contrast, within-group distances may increase or decrease depending on whether
P_{\theta_{N}}
is an overall expansion or shrinking of
P_{\theta_{1}}
. This effect is captured by the second term, where
b(h,\theta_{1})
quantifies the induced distributional expansion/shrinkage under a deformation of
P_{\theta_{1}}
in the direction
h
. Thus, the difference in means reflects the competing effects of parameter separation and distributional expansion/shrinkage.

If
\theta_{N}\neq\theta_{1}
are fixed, the shrinkage term in (
4.9
) vanishes asymptotically, leaving parameter separation as the dominant effect, consistent with
Proposition

2.1
. However, when
\theta_{N}\to\theta_{1}
, the shrinkage term dominates over a certain range of
|\theta_{N}-\theta_{1}|
, precisely the exponent gap of the 1-sided test. If
b(h,\theta_{1})<0
, corresponding to overall shrinkage, within-group distances decrease relative to cross-group distances, reducing the number of cross-group edges and yielding high power for the 1-sided test. Conversely, if
b(h,\theta_{1})>0
, corresponding to expansion, within-group distances increase relative to cross-group distances, producing more cross-group edges than under the null, and the 1-sided test has limiting power 0.

In

An alternative way is to use the conditional CLT given in

3.1

the

-NN graph formed from the sample at hand. By the exchangeability of the labels, the covariance structure of the

•

U_{e}\sim\textnormal{Ber}\left(\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N(N-1)}\right)
for all

.

•

\textnormal{Cov}(U_{e},U_{f})=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}(N_{2}-1)}{N(N-1)(N-2)}-\frac{N_{1}^{2}N_{2}^{2}}{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}}
if
e=(i,j),f=(i,k)
for
i,j,k
distinct.

•

\textnormal{Cov}(U_{e},U_{f})=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}(N_{1}-1)}{N(N-1)(N-2)}-\frac{N_{1}^{2}N_{2}^{2}}{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}}
if
e=(i,j),f=(k,j)
for
i,j,k
distinct.

•

\textnormal{Cov}(U_{e},U_{f})=-\frac{N_{1}^{2}N_{2}^{2}}{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}}
if
e=(i,j),f=(j,k)
for
i,j,k
distinct.

•

\textnormal{Cov}(U_{e},U_{f})=-\frac{N_{1}^{2}N_{2}^{2}}{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}}
if
e=(i,j),f=(j,i)
for
i\neq j.

•

\textnormal{Cov}(U_{e},U_{f})=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}(N_{1}-1)(N_{2}-1)}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}-\frac{N_{1}^{2}N_{2}^{2}}{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}}
for
e=(i,j),f=(k,l)
for
i,j,k,l
distinct.

k_{N}=N^{\gamma}
is given by
N^{-\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{d}}.
For
d=25

On the other hand, the power of the 2-sided test is almost unchanged, and we also see an improvement in the power with increasing

Further simulations exploring alternate settings are given in

.

Normal equicorrelation family

We will now consider the family of distributions over
R^{d}
given by
p(\cdot\mid\rho)
given by
N(0,\Sigma_{\rho})
where
\Sigma_{\rho}
is the

\rho
. This is the well known normal equicorrelation family.

Since
\Sigma_{\rho}=(1-\rho)I_{d}+\rho\mathbf{1}_{d}\mathbf{1}_{d}^{T},
there exists an orthogonal matrix
U
such that

U\Sigma_{\rho}U^{T}=I_{d}+\rho\cdot\textnormal{diag}(d-1,-1,\dots,-1).

In addition,
U
does not depend on
\rho
. Further, we use the Euclidean distance to calculate distances between points, which is unchanged under orthogonal transformations. Hence, the normal equicorrelation family is a subset of the larger family of diagonal perturbations of the normal covariance matrix we considered in the previous section.

We take the null to be
\rho_{0}=0
which corresponds to the standard normal, and the alternate to be
\rho_{N}=hn^{b}
for some negative exponent
b
and some nonzero

. In terms of the normal scale family of the previous section, this corresponds to
\theta_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{d}
,
\theta_{N}=\theta_{1}+\rho hN^{b}
, where
h=(d-1)e_{1}-\sum_{i=2}^{d}e_{i}.
The number of variance parameters
m
is now equal to the ambient dimension
d
.

Following the calculation in (
5.1
), it is enough to determine the sign of
\sum h_{i}
to determine the sign of
b(h,\theta_{1})
and hence find the detection thresholds. In this case
\sum h_{i}=0.
Therefore,
b(h,\theta_{1})=0
and the results of
Theorem

4.2
do not apply. However, even in this case the 1- and 2-sided tests can behave quite differently depending on the sign of
\rho
.

There is again a stark difference between the two tests, the 1-sided test being quite poor and having power close to 0 even for

we still see that the 1-sided test can have extremely low power. The 2-sided test, on the other hand, performs well, as does the energy distance test. Furthermore, its power improves with increasing

In the case of

we chose not to include a plot since all tests consistently displayed low power in our simulations. This is expected since the equicorrelation family imposes the restriction

Appendix A
Initial technical results

This appendix is dedicated to some initial technical results that we will frequently use in our calculations. We begin with a standard bound on the lower tails of Poisson random variables which follows from the Chernoff bound for Poisson random variables.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X
be a Poisson random variable with mean
\mu.
Then, for
0\leq t\leq 1,

\mathbb{P}(X\leq(1-t)\mu)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}\mu}{2}\right).

For all
t>0,

\mathbb{P}(X\geq(1+t)\mu)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}\mu}{2}\right).

A corollary of the above lemma is the following concentration inequality for
\Gamma(M,1)
random variables for
M\in\mathbb{N}.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
X\sim\Gamma(M,1)
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}.
Then

\mathbb{P}(X\geq M+u)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(u+1)^{2}}{2(M+u)}\right).

Proof.

Since
M\in\mathbb{N},
we know that the CDF
F_{X}
of
X
is

F_{X}(v)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\frac{v^{k}}{k!}e^{-v}=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Poisson}(v)\geq M).

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq M+u)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Poisson}(M+u)\geq M)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Poisson}(M+u)\leq M-1)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(u+1)^{2}}{2(M+u)}\right)\quad\ldots\quad\textnormal{from \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::poisson_bound}.}

∎

The core set-up involves a Poisson process with intensity function
N_{1}f+N_{2}g
for some densities
f,g.
We will often be interested in the probability of two points being nearest neighbors. Specifically, we want to know how close the typical nearest neighbor of a point is to it. For this, we will need to integrate the intensity function over balls of small radii. The following lemma will prove useful.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
S
be an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
f
a real valued, three times continuously differentiable function defined on
S

denote the Hessian of

at

Let

Let

\displaystyle\int_{B(x,r)}f(z)\penalty\ dz

\displaystyle=f(x)V_{d}r^{d}+\frac{V_{d}\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}f(x))}{2(d+2)}r^{d+2}+\delta_{1}(x,r),,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}f(z)\penalty\ dz

\displaystyle=f(x)dV_{d}r^{d-1}+\frac{V_{d}\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}f(x))}{2}r^{d+1}+\delta_{2}(x,r),

(A.2)

where

and
|\delta_{2}(x,r)|\leq C_{2}(x)r^{d+2}
for all

as above, for some non-negative functions

of

The functions
C_{1},C_{2}
can be taken as constants if

has uniformly bounded third partial derivatives on

Proof.

WLOG we assume that

Expanding

f(y)=f(0)+\nabla f(0)^{T}(y-x)+\frac{1}{2}y^{T}\textnormal{H}f(x)y+O(\|y\|^{3}).

The constant in the big-O term depends only on
x.
If

has bounded third derivatives, then the constant depends only on
f.

We prove (
A.2
) first. We change to spherical coordinates and parametrize
\partial B(x,r)
by the variables
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d-1}
with
\psi_{d-1}\in[0,2\pi)

\psi_{i}\in[0,\pi)
for

The change of variables is given by

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=r\sin(\psi_{1})...\sin(\psi_{i-1})\cos(\psi_{i})\text{ for }i\neq d,

\displaystyle y_{d}

\displaystyle=r\sin(\psi_{1})...\sin(\psi_{d}).

Let

denote the Jacobian for the change of coordinates. It can be shown that the determinant of the Jacobian

at

|J(\Psi)|=r^{d-1}\sin^{d-2}(\psi_{1})\sin^{d-3}(\psi_{2})...\sin(\psi_{1}).

We now find the integral over
\partial B(0,r)
by integrating every term in the Taylor expansion individually.

\int_{\partial B(x,r)}f(0)\penalty\ dy=f(0)\int J(\Psi)\penalty\ d\Psi=f(0)dV_{d}r^{d-1}.

To calculate the integral of the gradient term, we can notice that due to the parametrization given above,

\int_{\partial B(0,r)}y_{i}\penalty\ dy=0.

Hence, the gradient term integrates to
0.
To find the Hessian term, we first notice that for

\int_{\partial B(0,r)}y_{i}y_{j}\penalty\ dy=0.

Further more,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(0,r)}y_{i}^{2}\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\partial B(0,r)}\|y\|_{2}^{2}\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}r^{2}\int|J(\Psi)|\penalty\ d\Psi=V_{d}r^{d+1},

where the first equality is due to symmetry. Hence, the integral of the Hessian term is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B(0,r)}y^{T}\textnormal{H}f(0)y\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B(0,r)}\sum h_{ii}y_{i}^{2}\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{d}\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}f(0))}{2}r^{d+1}.

Finally, the big-O term is bounded by
C_{x}r^{3}.
Hence, by using the same ideas as above we get that the integral of the remainder term can be bounded by
O(r^{d+2}).
This proves (
A.2

\int_{B(0,r)}f(z)\penalty\ dz=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\int_{\partial B(0,u)}f(z)\penalty\ dz\right)\penalty\ du,

(

) follows by integrating the individual expressions from 0 to

We now present a result that is sometimes called the Palm theory of Poisson processes. It allows us to write the expectation of certain functionals of Poisson processes in a cleaner manner.

(A.4)

A.5
). Finding the limiting value of the second moment is similar so we only provide a brief sketch. Note that

d^{\downarrow}_{N}(0)^{2}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{G}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{(x,0)\in\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{0})\}+\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{G}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{(y,0),(z,0)\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{z}))\}.

Using
Lemma

A.6
and the discussion following it, we see that there exists a constant
C_{d}
that depends only on
d
such that

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{(x,0)\in\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{0})\}\leq C_{d}k_{N}.

Hence, it is enough to find

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{G}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{(x,0),(y,0)\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{0}))\}\right).

By the Palm Theory identity (
A.4
),

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{y,z\in\mathcal{G}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{(y,0),(z,0)\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{z}))\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{k_{N}^{2}}\int h(x)h(y)\mathbb{P}((x,0),(y,0)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{x,y,0})))\penalty\ dx\penalty\ dy.

Using the same arguments as before, we can restrict the integral to
x,y\in B(0,r_{N}(K)).
For
x,y
in this region, the probability in the above integral can be written as

\mathbb{P}((x,0),(y,0)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{N}^{x,y,0})))=\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}+Z_{3},Z_{2}+Z_{3}\leq k_{N}-1)

where
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
are independent with

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle\sim\textnormal{Poisson}\left(N\cdot h(B(x,\|x\|)\setminus B(y,\|y\|))\right),

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle\sim\textnormal{Poisson}\left(N\cdot h(B(y,\|y\|)\setminus B(x,\|x\|))\right),

\displaystyle Z_{3}

\displaystyle\sim\textnormal{Poisson}\left(N\cdot h(B(x,\|x\|)\cap B(y,\|y\|))\right)

In particular, we see that

\displaystyle Z_{1}+Z_{3}

\displaystyle\sim\textnormal{Poisson}\left(N\cdot h(B(x,\|x\|))\right),

\displaystyle Z_{2}+Z_{3}

\displaystyle\sim\textnormal{Poisson}\left(N\cdot h(B(y,\|y\|))\right)

Using the same arguments as before, we get that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}+Z_{3}\leq k_{N}-1)\geq 1-k_{N}^{-2}
for
\|x\|\leq\left(\frac{k_{N}(1-\epsilon)}{N\cdot h(0)V_{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
and
\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}+Z_{3}\leq k_{N}-1)\leq k_{N}^{-2}
for
\|x\|\geq\left(\frac{k_{N}(1+\epsilon)}{N\cdot h(0)V_{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
and we have similar bounds on
\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}+Z_{3}\leq k_{N}-1)
in terms of
\|y\|.
The rest of the proof is almost identical to the proof of the limiting first moment.

∎

Appendix B
Consistency and asymptotic distributions

The main results of this appendix prove the consistency of the 2 sample test and that the test statistic has an asymptotically normal distribution under general alternatives. Specifically, we will prove
Proposition

2.1
, and
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
in this section. For this, we recall some of the notation defined previously.

We work in the Poissonized setting where we sample the set of points
\mathcal{Z}_{N}:=\{Z_{1},...,Z_{L_{N}}\}
from a Poisson process
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
with intensity function
N\phi_{N}(x)
where
\phi_{N}(x):=\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(x)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(x)
and
N_{1}+N_{2}=N.
We assume that
\frac{N_{1}}{N}-p=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\frac{N_{2}}{N}-q=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})
and denote
\phi(x):=pf(x)+qg(x)
. The number of points in the process
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
is denoted by
L_{N}
which means
L_{N}\sim\textnormal{Poisson}(N).
For each point
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N},
we assign the label
c_{z}
to
z
with

c_{z}=\begin{cases}1\text{ with probability }\frac{N_{1}f(x)}{N_{1}f(x)+N_{2}g(x)},\\
\\
2\text{ with probability }\frac{N_{2}g(x)}{N_{1}f_{n}(x)+N_{2}g(x)}.\end{cases}

(B.1)

The labels are assigned to all points in
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
independent of all others. For a given
K,
the test statistic is defined as

T(\mathscr{G}_{K}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\psi(c_{x},c_{y})\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{K}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\},

(B.2)

where
\psi(c_{x},c_{y})=\mathbf{1}\{c_{x}=1,c_{y}=2\}.
We will be considering the case where
K=k_{N}\to\infty.
Hence, the statistic in our case will be denoted by
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})).

For any function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(h,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}h(z,w)\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}\},

(B.3)

When
h
is clear from context, we will simply denote this by
\kappa_{N}(z)
. Note that
\kappa_{N}
is bounded by
1
since a point can have at most
k_{N}
out-neighbors in the
k_{N}
-NN graph.

Given a function
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},
we define
\tau^{\uparrow}_{N}(\omega,z),\tau^{\downarrow}(\omega,z)
as

\displaystyle\tau_{N}^{\uparrow}(\omega,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(z,w_{1}),(z,w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\},,

(B.4)

\displaystyle\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(\omega,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(z,w_{1}),(z,w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\},

(B.5)

\displaystyle\tau_{N}^{+}(\omega,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(z,w_{1}),(w_{2},z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}.

(B.6)

Each of these sums refer to one of the ‘stars’ that can be formed at a point
z.
The first sum is over the outgoing stars, the second is over the incoming stars and the third is over the stars that have one incoming edge and one outgoing edge. We will see later that these terms come up in calculating the conditional variance of the statistic.

B.1
Technical results

We begin our results with some technical lemmas which will enable us to find the limiting value of certain objects easily.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a uniformly continuous function. For any given
z,

\kappa_{N}(h,z)\overset{L^{1}}{\to}h(z,z).

(B.7)

Consequently,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(h,z))\to h(z,z).

(B.8)

Furthermore, as
N\to\infty

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\kappa_{N}(h,z)\overset{L^{2}}{\rightarrow}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz.

(B.9)

Proof.

For
K>0,
define
r_{N}(K)=\left(K\dfrac{\max(k_{N},(\log N)^{2})}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}.

We will prove (
B.7
) first. We start by writing

\displaystyle\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}),w\in B(z,r_{N}(K))\}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}),w\not\in B(z,r_{N}(K))\}

\displaystyle=:E_{1}+E_{2}.

The above is true for any fixed
K.
Consider
E_{1}
first.

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}),w\in B(z,r_{N}(K))\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{w\in B(z,r_{N}(K))\}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{k_{N}}\int_{B(z,(r_{N}(K))}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\phi_{N}(w)\penalty\ dw\quad\ldots\quad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::palm theory identity}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N}{k_{N}}\left(C\cdot r_{N}(K)\right)\cdot\text{vol}\left(B(z,r_{N}(K))\right)\quad\ldots\quad\textnormal{by uniform continuity of $h$}

\displaystyle\to 0.

Now we come to
E_{2}
. We use the fact that
0\leq h\leq 1.
Then,

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}),w\not\in B(z,r_{N}(K))\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}),w\not\in B(z,r_{N}(K))\}

\displaystyle\leq N\cdot\mathbb{P}(B(z,(r_{N}(K))\text{ contains less than $k_{N}$ points})

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\ldots\quad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::nearest_neighbor_distance_bound}, for all large enough $K$.}

Since
E_{1},E_{2}\to 0,
we have

\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|h(z,w)-h(z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\to 0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(h,z)\overset{L^{1}}{\to}h(z,z),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(h,z))\to h(z,z).

We now come to the proof of (
B.9
) i.e. the
L^{2}
convergence. Using the Palm Theory identity in
Lemma

A.5
, and by (
B.8
) and the DCT we see that

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\kappa_{N}(z)\right)=\int\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(Z))\phi_{N}(z)\penalty\ dz\to\int h(z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz.

Hence, to prove
L^{2}
convergence, we only need to show

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\kappa_{N}(h,z)\right)^{2}\to\left(\int h(z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz\right)^{2}.

Using the Palm Theory identity, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\kappa_{N}(h,z)\right)^{2}

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int\phi_{N}(z)\mathbb{E}\kappa^{2}_{N}(z)\penalty\ dz+\int\phi_{N}(z_{1})\phi_{N}(z_{2})\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2}))\penalty\ dz.

(B.11)

Since
h\in[0,1],
we have that
\kappa_{N}(h,z)\leq 1
. Hence, the first term tends to
0
almost surely. We want to prove that for any
z_{1},z_{2},

\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2}))\to h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2}).

By the DCT, this will show that

\displaystyle\int\phi_{N}(z_{1})\phi_{N}(z_{2})\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2}))\penalty\ dz

\displaystyle\to\int\phi(z_{1})\phi(z_{2})h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2})\penalty\ dz_{1}\penalty\ dz_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz\right)^{2},

which in turn will show
L^{2}
convergence.

Define
A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2})
to be the region

A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2}):=B(z_{1},(r_{N}(K))\times B(z_{2},(r_{N}(K))

Then, we can split up the product
\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2})
as

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2}}h(z_{1},w_{1})h(z_{2},w_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2}}h(z_{1},w_{1})h(z_{2},w_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2}),(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2}}h(z_{1},w_{1})h(z_{2},w_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2})^{c},(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

We will call the two terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively. We will first prove that
\mathbb{E}(T_{2})\to 0
if we choose a large enough
K.

Since
h\in[0,1]
we have that

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{\uparrow}(z_{1})d^{\uparrow}_{K}(z_{2})}{(k_{N})^{2}}+\frac{d^{\uparrow}(z_{2})d^{\uparrow}_{K}(z_{1})}{(k_{N})^{2}}

where
d^{\uparrow}(z)
denote the total number of out-neighbors of
z
and
d^{\uparrow}_{K}(z)
denotes the number of out-neighbors of
z
that lie outside the ball of radius
\left(\frac{r_{N}(K)}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
around
z
. Note that
d^{\uparrow}(z)\leq k_{N}
since
\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}
is the
k_{N}
-NN graph. Also, by
Lemma

A.4
, for any large enough
K,

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{d^{\uparrow}_{K}(z)}{k_{N}}\right)\to 0.

Hence,
\mathbb{E}(T_{2})\to 0
for a large enough
K.
Now we need to find the limiting value of
\mathbb{E}(T_{1}).
We will show that

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2})}\Delta_{h}\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2}),(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\right)\to 0.

where
\Delta_{h}:=|h(z_{1},w_{1})h(z_{2},w_{2})-h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2})|
. Note that quantity inside the expectation can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2})}\Delta_{h}\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2}),(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(k_{N})^{2}}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2}}|h(z_{1},w_{1})h(z_{2},w_{2})-h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2})|\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2})\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{2}}{(k_{N})^{2}}\int_{A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2})}|h(z_{1},w_{1})h(z_{2},w_{2})-h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2})|\penalty\ dw_{1}\penalty\ dw_{2}\quad\ldots\quad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::palm theory identity}}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{N^{2}}{k_{N}^{2}}r_{N}(K)(\text{vol}(B(0,r_{N}(K)))^{2}\quad\ldots\quad\text{by uniform continuity of $h$}

\displaystyle\to 0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2}))

\displaystyle=\lim\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2}}h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2})\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2});(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\right).

By the same argument as used to prove that

we get

\lim\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{2}}\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in A_{K}(z_{1},z_{2});(z_{1},w_{1}),(z_{2},w_{2})\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\right)=1

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim\mathbb{E}(\kappa_{N}(z_{1})\kappa_{N}(z_{2}))

\displaystyle=h(z_{1},z_{1})h(z_{2},z_{2}).

This completes the proof.

∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a uniformly continuous function. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{\uparrow}(\omega,z)

\displaystyle\overset{L^{2}}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{2}\int\omega(z,z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz,

\displaystyle\vskip 5.69054pt\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(\omega,z)

\displaystyle\overset{L^{2}}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{2}\int\omega(z,z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz,

\displaystyle\vskip 5.69054pt\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{+}(\omega,z)

\displaystyle\overset{L^{2}}{\rightarrow}\frac{1}{2}\int\omega(z,z,z)\phi(z)\penalty\ dz.

Proof.

We will only prove the second statement. The other two can be shown in similar ways. The proof is very similar to that of
Lemma

B.1
where we first show

convergence of

for a fixed

and then analyze the second moment of the sum to get

convergence. We first show
L^{1}
convergence. This entails showing that for every

\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\tau_{N}(\omega,z)-\frac{1}{2}\omega(z,z,z)\right|\right)\to 0.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\omega(z,z,z)}{2k_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},z),(w_{2},z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(z,z,z)}{2k_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}(d^{\downarrow}_{N}(z)(d^{\downarrow}_{N}(z)-1)),

where

denotes the in-degree of

Using the limits established in

Hence, to show
L^{1}
convergence, it suffices to show

\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})-\omega(z,z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(z,w_{1}),(z,w_{2})\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}\to 0.

For
K>0,
define
r_{N}(K)=\left(K\cdot\frac{\max((\log N)^{2},k_{N})}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}.
Define

B^{2}(z)=B(z,r_{N}(K))\times B(z,r_{N}(K)).

We start by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})-\omega(z,z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},z),(w_{2},z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})-\omega(z,z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in B^{2}(z),(w_{1},z),(w_{2},z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}|\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})-\omega(z,z,z)|\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in B^{2}(z)^{c},(w_{1},z),(w_{2},z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}.

Call the last two terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively. We will show that both tend to

By
Lemma

A.5

T_{1}=\frac{N^{2}}{k_{N}^{2}}\int_{B^{2}(z)}|\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})-\omega(z,z,z)|\penalty\ \phi_{N}(w_{1})\phi_{N}(w_{2})\penalty\ dw_{1}\penalty\ dw_{2}

This can be bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{N^{2}}{k_{N}^{2}}\int_{B^{2}(z)}|\omega(z,w_{1},w_{2})-\omega(z,z,z)|\penalty\ \phi_{N}(w_{1})\phi_{N}(w_{2})\penalty\ dw_{1}\penalty\ dw_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot r_{N}(K)\cdot\frac{N^{2}}{k_{N}^{2}}\cdot(\text{vol}(B(z,r_{N}(K))))^{2}\quad\ldots\quad\text{by uniform continuity of $\omega$}

\displaystyle\to 0.

Now we have to show that
T_{2}
tends to
0.

Let
d^{\downarrow}(z),d^{\downarrow}(K,z)
denote the number of in-neighbors of
z
and the number of in-neighbors of
z
that lie outside the ball of radius

around

respectively.

Since
\omega
is bounded above by

we can bound

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{k_{N}^{2}}\sum_{w_{1}\neq w_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in B^{2}(z)^{c},(w_{1},z),(w_{2},z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{2L_{N}}{k_{N}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{w\in B(z),(w,z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{2}}{k_{N}^{2}}\int_{B(z)^{c}}\mathbb{P}(\text{ $B(w,r_{N}(K))$ contains less than $k_{N}$ points.})\phi(w)\penalty\ dw.

Since the densities are bounded below, by

the point-wise probability decays faster than

This shows that

and hence, for any given

\displaystyle\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(z)\overset{L^{1}}{\to}\frac{1}{2}\omega(z,z,z),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(z))\to\frac{1}{2}\omega(z,z,z).

Having established the limits in expectation, the proof of

B.1
. We provide a brief sketch here.

We show that for

being two distinct points in
\mathcal{Z}_{N},

\mathbb{E}\left(\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(\omega,z_{1})\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(\omega,z_{2})\right)\approx\mathbb{E}\left(\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(\omega,z_{1})\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(\omega,z_{2})\right)\approx\frac{\omega(z_{1},z_{1},z_{1})\omega(z_{2},z_{2},z_{2})}{4}.

This is done once again, as in the proof of
Lemma

B.1
, by looking at the nearest neighbors of
z_{1},z_{2}
that are close and bounding the probability of having a nearest neighbor that is far away.

Additionally, the discussion following
Lemma

A.6
shows that the in-degree in the
k_{N}
-NN graph is bounded by
C_{d}k_{N}.
Hence,
\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}
is bounded when
\omega
is bounded. This combined with the point wise convergence and the DCT gives
L^{2}
convergence.

∎

B.2
Consistency and limiting variance

Using the convergence theorems of the previous section, we can now show that
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))
converges in probability to
\delta(f,g)
where

\delta(f,g)=pq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f(x)g(x)}{pf(x)+qg(x)}\penalty\ dx.

Since this is a divergence between probability distributions, we will have that the test is consistent.

To analyze the variance of
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
, we condition on
\mathcal{F}_{N},
the sigma algebra generated by
\mathcal{Z}_{N}.
We will then deal with the variance of the conditional expectation and the conditional variance separately. This is the aim of
Section

B.4
and
Section

B.6
.

After conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{N},
which is the sigma algebra containing information on the location of all points of
\mathcal{Z}_{N},
the only randomness is in the labels
c_{z}
for
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}
. Since the labels are assigned with probability proportional to the density, the conditional expectation can be written down comfortably. We define
h_{N}(x,y)
as

h_{N}(x,y)=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N^{2}}\frac{f(x)g(x)}{(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(x)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(x))(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(y)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(y))}.

(B.12)

Then the conditional expectation can be written as

\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}h_{N}(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}.

We are now ready to prove Proposition
Proposition

2.1
.

B.3
Proof of
Proposition

2.1

Note that
h_{N}\in[0,1]
where
h_{N}
is defined in (
B.12
). We also know that
f,g
are uniformly continuous and that
\frac{N_{1}}{N}-p=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\frac{N_{2}}{N}-q=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Hence, we get that

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})=\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}+o(1),

where

h(x,y)=pq\frac{f(x)g(y)}{(pf(x)+qg(y))(pf(y)+qg(y))}.

(B.13)

With
\kappa_{N}(h,z)
as defined in (
B.3
), we get that

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\kappa_{N}(h,z)+o(1).

By
Lemma

B.1
, we know the
L^{2}
limit, of
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\kappa_{N}(h,z).
From the definition of
h
in (
B.13
), we get

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})\overset{p}{\to}pq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x,x)\phi(x)\penalty\ dx=\delta(f,g).

(B.14)

With this, we only need to show that

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})-\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N}))\overset{p}{\to}0,

in order to show consistency. This follows from (
B.16
) in the next section which shows

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})\overset{L^{2}}{\to}\sigma^{2},

for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. This proves
Proposition

2.1
.

B.4
Limit of the conditional variance

For
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N},
let

V_{x,y}:=\psi(c_{x},c_{y})\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}.

(B.15)

Note that conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{N},

V_{x,y}\sim\textnormal{Ber}(h_{N}(x,y)).
Hence, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{N}
the statistic
T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
is the sum of the Bernoullis
\{V_{x,y}\}_{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}.

V_{x,y}
and
V_{w,z}
are conditionally independent if and only if the two edges
(x,y),(w,z)
do not share an endpoint.

Using this, we see that the conditional variance can be written as

\displaystyle\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Var}(V_{x,y}|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle+\sum_{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\quad\sum_{z\neq y:(x,z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x,y},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle+\sum_{(y,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\quad\sum_{z\neq y:(z,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{y,x},V_{z,x}|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle+2\sum_{(y,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\quad\sum_{z:(x,z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{y,x},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle+\sum_{(x,y),(y,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x,y},V_{y,x}|\mathcal{F}_{N}).

We will first deal with the sum of the variances. Note that for any
(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}),

\textnormal{Var}(V_{x,y}|\mathcal{F}_{N})=h_{N}(x,y)(1-h_{N}(x,y))=h(x,y)(1-h(x,y))+o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

Hence, using the same ideas as in
Lemma

B.1
, we get

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}}\sum_{(x,y)}\textnormal{Var}(V_{x,y}|\mathcal{F}_{N})\overset{L^{2}}{\to}\int h(x,x)(1-h(x,x))\phi(x)\penalty\ dx.

This gives us that

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(x,y)}\textnormal{Var}(V_{x,y}|\mathcal{F}_{N})\overset{L^{2}}{\to}0.

We now come to each of the sums of the covariances. Define the functions
\omega_{N}^{\uparrow},\omega_{N}^{\downarrow},\omega^{\uparrow}
and
\omega^{\downarrow}
as

\displaystyle\omega_{N}^{\uparrow}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}^{2}}{N^{3}}\frac{f(x)g(y)g(z)}{(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(x)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(x))(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(y)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(y))(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(z)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(z))}

\displaystyle\omega^{\uparrow}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{pq^{2}f(x)g(y)g(z)}{(pf(x)+qg(x))(pf(y)+qg(y))(pf(z)+qg(z))}

\displaystyle\omega_{N}^{\downarrow}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{1}^{2}N_{2}}{N^{3}}\frac{g(x)f(y)f(z)}{(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(x)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(x))(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(y)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(y))(\frac{N_{1}}{N}f(z)+\frac{N_{2}}{N}g(z))}

\displaystyle\omega^{\downarrow}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}qg(x)f(y)f(z)}{(pf(x)+qg(x))(pf(y)+qg(y))(pf(z)+qg(z))}.

For pairs of edges of the form
(x,y),(x,z)
with
y\neq z
, we have

\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x,y},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})=\omega_{N}^{\uparrow}(x,y,z)-h_{N}(x,y)h_{N}(x,z).

By uniform continuity of the densities, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\sum_{z\neq y:(x,z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x,y},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\left(\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{\uparrow}(\omega^{\uparrow},z)-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{\uparrow}(h^{\uparrow},z)\right)+o(1),

where
h^{\uparrow}(x,y,z)=h(x,y)h(x,z).
By
Lemma

B.2
, we get

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(x,y),(x,z)}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x,y},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})\overset{L^{2}}{\to}\int(\omega^{\uparrow}(x,x,x)-h^{2}(x,x))\phi(x)\penalty\ dx.

Similarly, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x)\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\quad\sum_{z\neq y,(z,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{z,x},V_{y,x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x),(z,x),y\neq z}(\omega^{\downarrow}(x,y,z)-h(y,x)h(z,x))+o(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k_{N}^{2}}\left(\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(z,\omega^{\downarrow})-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{\downarrow}(h^{\downarrow},z)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{L^{2}}{\to}\int(\omega^{\downarrow}(x,x,x)-h^{2}(x,x))\phi(x)\penalty\ dx\quad\ldots\quad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::convergence_of_cross_terms_in_conditional_variance}},

where
h^{\downarrow}(x,y,z)=h(y,x)h(z,x).
Hence,

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x)\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\quad\sum_{z\neq y,(z,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{z,x},V_{y,x})\overset{L^{2}}{\to}\int(\omega^{\downarrow}(x,x,x)-h^{2}(x,x))\penalty\ \phi(x)\penalty\ dx.

The third sum of covariances can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{2}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x),(x,z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{y,x},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x),(x,z)\in\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}h_{N}(y,x)h_{N}(x,z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x),(x,z)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}h(y,x)h(x,z)+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\tau_{N}^{+}(h^{+},z)+o(1)

\displaystyle\overset{L^{2}}{\to}-2\int h^{2}(x,x)\phi(x)\penalty\ dx\quad\ldots\quad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::convergence_of_cross_terms_in_conditional_variance}},

where
h^{+}(x,y,z)=h(y,x)h(x,z).
Hence,

\frac{2}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(y,x),(x,z)}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{y,x},V_{x,z}|\mathcal{F}_{N})\overset{L^{2}}{\to}-2\int h^{2}(x,x)\phi(x)\penalty\ dx

Finally, coming to the fourth sum we see that

\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\sum_{(x,y),(y,x)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})}\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x,y},V_{y,x}|\mathcal{F}_{N})\leq\frac{|\mathcal{Z}_{N}|k_{N}}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\overset{L^{2}}{\to}0.

Put together, we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})&\overset{L^{2}}{\to}\int\left(\omega^{\uparrow}(x,x,x)+\omega^{\downarrow}(x,x,x)-4h^{2}(x,x)\right)\phi(x)\penalty\ dx\\
&=pq\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\frac{f(x)g(x)(pf(x)-qg(x))^{2}}{\phi(x)^{3}}\penalty\ dx=:\sigma^{2}_{\textnormal{cond}}.\end{split}

(B.16)

This gives us the
L^{2}
limit of the conditional variance.

B.5
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

Recall that the conditional statistic
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}k_{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{1}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})|\mathcal{F}_{N})).

From (
B.16
) we get the limiting variance of
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}.
Hence, to complete the proof of
Theorem

3.1
we only need to show asymptotic normality of
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}.

Recall the definition
V_{x,y}
from (
B.15
). As in the previous section, we can take

T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}V_{x,y}.

As noted before,
V_{x,y}
is a Bernoulli random variable conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{N}.
Let
G(\mathcal{Z}_{N})
denote the dependency graph of
\{V_{x,y}\}_{x,y}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{N}.

V_{x,y}
and
V_{w,z}
are conditionally independent if and only if the edges
(x,y)
and
(w,z)
do not share an endpoint in
\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}.
Hence, using
Lemma

A.6
and the discussion following it, we see that

1+\textnormal{deg}(G(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\leq Mk_{N}

for some deterministic constant
M>0
where
\textnormal{deg}(G(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))
denotes the maximum among the degrees of the vertices of
G(\mathcal{Z}_{N}).
We are now in a position to use the following theorem on Stein’s method based on dependency graphs.

Theorem B.1
.

(
Ross [44, Theorem 3.6]
) Let
G
be a graph and let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in V}
be a collection of random variables indexed by the vertices of a graph
G.
Suppose
\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=0,

\sigma^{2}:=\textnormal{Var}\left(\sum X_{i}\right)
,
W:=\frac{\sum X_{i}}{\sigma}
and
D:=1+\max(\textnormal{deg}(G))
. If
Z\sim N(0,1)
then

\textnormal{Wass}(W,Z)\leq\frac{6}{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{D^{3}\sum\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{4}}+\frac{D^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}\sum\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{3},

where
\textnormal{Wass}(W,Z)
denotes the Wasserstein distance.

Let

W_{N}:=\frac{T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})-\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})}{\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})}

and
W_{N}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}
denote the distribution of
\mathcal{F}_{N}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{N}.
Using
Theorem

B.1
and the upper bound established on
1+\textnormal{deg}(G(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))
we get

\displaystyle\textnormal{Wass}(W_{N}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}},Z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6M^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{k_{N}^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{L_{N}k_{N}}}{\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})}+\frac{k_{N}^{2}L_{N}k_{N}}{\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}))^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6M^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\sqrt{L_{N}}}{N}\left(\frac{\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\right)^{-1}+\frac{L_{N}}{N^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left(\frac{\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}))^{\frac{3}{2}}}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}

where
L_{N}=|\mathcal{Z}_{N}|.
Since
L_{N}\sim\textnormal{Poisson}(N),
we get
\frac{L_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}=o(1)
. Furthermore, (
B.16
) gives

\frac{\textnormal{Var}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}))^{\frac{3}{2}}}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\overset{p}{\to}\sigma^{2}_{\textnormal{cond}}.

Hence, we can marginalize over
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
to get asymptotic normality of
W_{N}
and hence of
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{cond}}.
This completes the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

B.6
Limiting variance of the conditional expectation

The conditional expectation is given by

\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}h_{N}(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}.

By uniform continuity of
f,g
and since
\frac{N_{1}}{N}-p=o(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),
this can be written as

\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N})=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}+o_{p}(N^{\frac{1}{2}}k_{N})

We will now find it’s asymptotic variance. In order to do this, we require some notation.

For
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N},
let
J_{x,y}:=h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\}.
Let
w(x):=\frac{pf(x)}{pf(x)+qg(y)}
and let
v(y)=\frac{qg(y)}{pf(y)+qg(y)}.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a finite set and
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a Borel set, let

\xi_{\mathcal{H}}^{x}(A):=w(x)\sum_{y\in A\cap\mathcal{H}^{x}}\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{H}^{x}))\}

where
\mathcal{H}^{x}
denotes the set
\mathcal{H}
with the point
x
added to it.
\xi_{\mathcal{H}}^{x}
defines a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Let
\mu_{N}
denote the sum of these measures across the Poisson process. That is,

\mu_{N}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}.

We can integrate the function
v
with respect to the measures
\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}
and
\mu_{N}
to get the quantities of interest to us. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}))\},

\displaystyle\langle v,\mu_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))\}.

Note that
\langle v,\mu_{N}\rangle
gives us exactly the conditional expectation. Writing the conditional expectation in this form allows us to use
Penrose [40]
, Lemma 4.2 which gives that

\textnormal{Var}(\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N}))=Na_{N}+Nb_{N},

where

\displaystyle a_{N}

\displaystyle:=\int\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\rangle^{2})\phi_{N}(x)\penalty\ dx,

\displaystyle b_{N}

\displaystyle:=\int(\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle)\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle))\phi_{N}(x)\phi_{N}(x_{N}(z))\penalty\ dx\penalty\ dz,

where
x_{N}(z)=x+N^{-\frac{1}{d}}z.

From the
L^{2}
convergence in
Lemma

B.1
and the DCT, we get

\frac{a_{N}}{k_{N}^{2}}\to\int h(x,x)^{2}\phi(x)\penalty\ dx.

We will now show that

\frac{b_{N}}{k_{N}^{2}}\to 0.

This will give the scale of the limiting variance of the conditional expectation. To show the second limit, notice that for any
x,z

\displaystyle\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}h(x_{N}(z),y)\mathbf{1}\{(x_{N}(z),y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}^{x}_{N}))\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x_{N}(z),y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}^{x}_{N}))\}+o(k_{N}N^{-\frac{1}{d}}).

After writing it in this form, we can use arguments similar to
Lemma

B.1
, to get that

\frac{\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle}{k_{N}}\overset{L^{1}}{\to}h(x,x).

Similarly, we also have

\frac{\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle}{k_{N}}\overset{L^{1}}{\to}h(x,x).

Hence, we have that the expectations converge which in turn gives us that

\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle)\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle))\to h(x,x)^{2}.

We now come to the term
\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle).
We wish to show that

\frac{\mathbb{E}(\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle)}{k_{N}^{2}}\to h(x,x)^{2}.

Note that both inner products inside the expectation are bounded above by
k_{N}.
This is because they are both sums of at most
k_{N}
many summands each of which lies in
[0,1].
Specifically, we have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}h(x,y)\mathbf{1}\{(x,y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}))\}\leq 1,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}h(x_{N}(z),y)\mathbf{1}\{(x_{N}(z),y)\in E(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}))\}\leq 1.

As a result,

0\leq\frac{\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle}{k_{N}},\frac{\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle}{k_{N}}\leq 1.

Using the individual
L^{1}
convergence and the boundedness, we get that

\frac{\langle v,\xi^{x}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x_{N}(z)}}\rangle\langle v,\xi^{x_{N}(z)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}^{x}}\rangle}{k_{N}^{2}}\overset{L^{1}}{\to}h(x,x)^{2}.

This shows convergence to the same quantity point wise. Once again, using the boundedness and the DCT, we have that
\frac{b_{N}}{k_{N}^{2}}\to 0.
Altogether, this gives us

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\textnormal{Var}(\mathbb{E}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|\mathcal{F}_{N}))&=\frac{a_{N}}{k_{N}^{2}}+\frac{b_{N}}{k_{N}^{2}}\\
&\to\int h(x,x)^{2}\phi(x)\penalty\ dx\\
&=p^{2}q^{2}\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\frac{f(x)^{2}g(x)^{2}}{\phi(x)^{3}}\penalty\ dx.\end{split}

(B.17)

B.7
Proof of
Theorem

3.2

Recall that the statistic
\mathcal{R}
was defined as

\mathcal{R}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))=\frac{1}{k_{N}\sqrt{N}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}))-\mathbb{E}_{H_{1}}(T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})))).

From (
B.16
) and (
B.17
) we get the limiting variance of
\mathcal{R}.
To prove
Theorem

3.2
we need to show asymptotic normality. We will show asymptotic normality of a slightly truncated statistic
T^{\prime}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})
which we will now define.

Define
r_{N}(K)=\left(K\frac{\max((\log N)^{2},k_{N})}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
. For a given
K,
let

is the number of edges in the graph

the label of the head is
2,

or one of it’s neighboring boxes. Define

We now bound
\|T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})-T^{\prime}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})\|_{2}.
Note that

\displaystyle|T(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})-T^{\prime}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}})|

\displaystyle\leq k_{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\mathbf{1}\{|\mathcal{Z}_{N}\cap B(x,r_{N}(K))|\leq k_{N}-1\}.

A.4
, and the above bound, we get by choosing a large enough

and in particular,

Note that for each

\mathbb{E}|X(i,N,K)-\mathbb{E}(X(i,N,K))|^{3}&\leq C(K)\cdot k_{N}^{3}(\max((\log N)^{2},k_{N}))^{3},\end{split}

where

is some constant that depends only on
K.

B.1
. If
G
denotes the dependency graph of

then, the max degree is bounded since the edge counts in two boxes that are not neighboring or do not share a common neighbor are independent. Hence,

for some constant
C
that depends on the dimension

Finally, we also see that

for some constant
C(K)
depending only on
K.
Hence, the bound coming from

B.1
gives

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{6C(K)\sqrt{C_{d}^{3}Nk_{N}^{3}(\max((\log N)^{2},k_{N})^{4}}}{\sqrt{\pi}Nk_{N}^{2}}\left(\frac{\textnormal{Var}(T^{\prime}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}))}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{d}^{2}C(K)Nk_{N}^{2}(\max((\log N)^{2},k_{N}))^{3}}{N^{\frac{3}{2}}k_{N}^{3}}\left(\frac{\textnormal{Var}(T^{\prime}(\mathscr{G}_{k_{N}}))}{Nk_{N}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}.

C.2
Technical results

In order to prove the results on the limiting values of the gradients and hessians, we will need some technical results.

Lemma C.4
.

For any
K\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{2}{d}+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)}=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{\Gamma(K+\frac{2}{d}+1)}{\Gamma(K)}.

Proof.

We can prove this by induction. For
K=1,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{2}{d}+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{\Gamma(2+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(1)}.

The identity holds for
K=1.
Suppose it holds for some
K\in\mathbb{N}.
To show that it holds for
K+1
we consider the summation for
K+1.
By the induction hypothesis and the recurrence of the Gamma function,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{2}{d}+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{2}{d}+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)}+\frac{\Gamma(K+1+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(K+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d}{d+2}+\frac{1}{K}\right)\frac{\Gamma(K+1+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(K)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{K+1+\frac{2}{d}}{K}\frac{\Gamma(K+1+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(K)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{\Gamma(K+2+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(K+1)}.

This shows the identity for any
K\in\mathbb{N}.

Lemma C.5
.

Let
x
be in the support of
p_{\theta_{1}}.
Define
g_{N}(u)=\lambda_{N}\left(x,\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right).
Then, there exists a non-negative sequence
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
such that

(1-\epsilon_{N})p_{\theta_{1}}(x)V_{d}\leq g_{N}^{\prime}(u)\leq(1+\epsilon_{N})p_{\theta_{1}}(x)V_{d},

for all
0\leq u\leq Nr_{N}(K)^{d}.
Consequently, for all
u,v>0
such that
u+v\leq Nr_{N}(K)^{d},

v(1-\epsilon_{N})p_{\theta_{1}}(x)V_{d}\leq g_{N}(u+v)-g_{N}(u)\leq v(1+\epsilon_{N})p_{\theta_{1}}(x)V_{d}.

The sequence
\epsilon_{N}
does not depend on
x.

Proof.

We will first prove the bounds on
g_{N}^{\prime}.
The other bound follows from using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for
g_{N}.

WLOG we assume
x=0.
Using the chain rule and the definition of
g_{N},
we have

\displaystyle g_{N}^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\frac{u^{\frac{1}{d}-1}}{N^{\frac{1}{d}}}\lambda_{N}^{\prime}\left(\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\frac{u^{\frac{1}{d}-1}}{N^{\frac{1}{d}}}N\int_{\partial B\left(0,\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)}p_{\theta_{1}}(z)\penalty\ dz

\displaystyle=p_{\theta_{1}}(0)V_{d}+\frac{V_{d}\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(0))}{2}\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}+O\left(\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{3}{d}}\right)\quad\ldots\quad\textnormal{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma::ball_and_surface_integrals}.}

The proof of the bounds on
g_{N}^{\prime}
is completed by noticing that
\frac{u}{N}\leq r_{N}(K)^{d}\to 0
and due to the fact
p_{\theta_{1}}
is bounded below with uniformly bounded second and third derivatives.

∎

The next lemma gives an expansion of the inverse of
g_{N}
as defined above.

Lemma C.6
.

Let
x\in\textnormal{int}(S)
For
u\leq Nr_{N}(K)^{d},
let
g_{N}(u):=\lambda_{N}\left(x,\frac{u^{\frac{1}{d}}}{N^{\frac{1}{d}}}\right).
Then for
v\leq g_{N}((Nr_{N}(K))^{d})
the following hold.

\displaystyle g_{N}^{-1}(v)=\frac{v}{p_{\theta_{1}}(x)V_{d}}-\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(x))}{2(d+2)V_{d}^{1+\frac{2}{d}}p_{\theta_{1}}(x)^{2+\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+v\delta_{N}^{(1)}(v)

(C.9)

\displaystyle\frac{g_{N}^{-1}(v)^{\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}=\frac{1}{p_{\theta_{1}}(0)^{\frac{2}{d}}V_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+\delta_{N}^{(2)}(v)

(C.10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g_{N}^{\prime}(g_{N}^{-1}(v))}=\frac{1}{p_{\theta_{1}}(0)V_{d}}\left(1-\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(0))}{2dp_{\theta_{1}}(0)^{1+\frac{2}{d}}V_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+\delta_{N}^{(3)}(v)\right)

(C.11)

where
\delta_{N}^{(i)}(v)\leq Cr_{N}(K)^{3}
for for
i=1,2,3,
and some constant
C
that depends on
p_{\theta_{1}}
but does not depend on
x.

Proof.

WLOG, we assume
x=0.
We prove (
C.9
) first. The other two results follow as easy consequences. From
Lemma

A.3
, we know

\displaystyle g_{N}(u)

\displaystyle=N\lambda_{N}\left(\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\theta_{1}}(0)V_{d}u+\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(0))V_{d}}{2(d+2)}\frac{u^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+O\left(\frac{u^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right)

\displaystyle=:\alpha u+\beta\frac{u^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+O\left(\frac{u^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right).

Denote

\tilde{v}:=\frac{v}{\alpha}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{2+\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}.

Then

\displaystyle g_{N}(\tilde{v})

\displaystyle=v-\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\left(\frac{v}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\right)^{1+\frac{2}{d}}+O\left(\frac{\tilde{v}^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right).

For
v\leq g_{N}(Nr_{N}(K)^{d}),
we know that
\left(\frac{v}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\leq r_{N}(K)^{2}\to 0.
Hence, the big-O term above can be replaced with
O\left(\frac{v^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right).
Furthermore, we also get

\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha^{2+\frac{2}{d}}}\left(\frac{v}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}=1+O\left(\left(\frac{v}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\right).

Putting these together, we get that

g_{N}(\tilde{v})=v+O\left(\frac{v^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right).

Let
C>0
be a constant such that for all
v,

|g_{N}(\tilde{v})-v|\leq C\frac{v^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}.

Finally, by
Lemma

C.5
, we get that for large all
N
, the inequalities

g_{N}\left(\tilde{v}-2C\frac{v^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right)<v<g_{N}\left(\tilde{v}+2C\frac{v^{1+\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right).

Since
g_{N}
is monotonically increasing, this shows that

g_{N}^{-1}(v)=\tilde{v}+v\cdot O\left(\left(\frac{v}{N}\right)^{\frac{3}{d}}\right).

Noticing that
\frac{v}{N}\leq r_{N}(K)^{d}
completes the proof of (
C.9
). To prove (
C.10
), we can simply use the fact that for
y\approx 0,

(1+y)^{\frac{2}{d}}=1+O(y)

combined with (
C.9
).

From
Lemma

A.3
and (
C.10
), we get

\displaystyle g_{N}^{\prime}(g_{N}^{-1}(v))

\displaystyle=V_{d}p_{\theta_{1}}(0)+\frac{V_{d}\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(0))}{2d}\left(\frac{g_{N}^{-1}(v)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}+O\left(\left(\frac{g_{N}^{-1}(v)}{N}\right)^{\frac{3}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=V_{d}p_{\theta_{1}}(0)\left(1+\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(0))}{2dp_{\theta_{1}}(0)^{1+\frac{2}{d}}V_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+O\left(\frac{v^{\frac{3}{d}}}{N^{\frac{3}{d}}}\right)\right)

Using the above expression along with that the fact that for
y\approx 0

\frac{1}{(1+y)}=1-y+O(y^{2})

we get (
C.11
). The completes the proof.

∎

To find the limiting values of the gradient and hessian of
\mu_{N}({\theta_{1}},{\theta_{1}}),
we will differentiate under the integral sign to write the derivatives as double integrals. Using the DCT, one can show that it is enough to find the point-wise limit of the single integrals. The next three lemmas enable us to find these point-wise limits.

Lemma C.7
.

Let

defined on
S
be three times differentiable and bounded on
S

we have

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x)}{p_{\theta_{1}}(x)}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\left(\frac{\textnormal{tr}(H_{x}f(x))}{2dV_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}(p_{\theta_{1}}(x))^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{k_{N}^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\sum_{k=0}^{k_{N}-1}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{2}{d}+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{d}}\left(\frac{f(x)\textnormal{tr}(H_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(x))}{2dV_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}p_{\theta_{1}}(x)^{2+\frac{2}{d}}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{k_{N}^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\sum_{k=0}^{k_{N}-1}\frac{\Gamma(k+1+\frac{2}{d})}{\Gamma(k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle+O\left(r_{N}(K)^{3}\right)

Proof.

WLOG we assume that
x=0.
From

A.4
, and the definition of
\rho_{K_{N}}^{{\theta_{1}},{\theta_{1}}}(0,y),

Recall the definition of

) and (
C.8

We now make the change of variables

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}{k_{N}d}\int_{0}^{(Nr_{N}(K))^{d}}u^{\frac{1}{d}-1}\left(\int_{\partial B(0,\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}})}f(z)\penalty\ dz\right)\rho_{N}\left(\left(\frac{u}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)\penalty\ du.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{N}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{f(0)}{p_{\theta_{1}}(0)}-\frac{f(0)\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{\theta_{1}}(0))}{2dV_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}p_{\theta_{1}}(0)^{2+\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}+\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}f(0))}{2dV_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}p_{\theta_{1}}(0)^{1+\frac{2}{d}}}\frac{v^{\frac{2}{d}}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{k_{N}-1}\frac{v^{k}}{k!}e^{-v}\penalty\ dv+O(r_{N}(K)^{3})

In particular, this shows that

\displaystyle|g_{N}(g_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1})+\tilde{\Delta}_{2})-(X+Y_{1}+Y_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885ptg_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1})$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle g_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1})+\tilde{\Delta}_{2}$}}$}}\nolimits g_{N}^{\prime}(z)\penalty\ dz-Y_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885ptg_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1})$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle g_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1})+\tilde{\Delta}_{2}$}}$}}\nolimits g_{N}^{\prime}(z)\penalty\ dz-\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885ptg_{N}^{-1}(X)$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle g_{N}^{-1}(X)+\tilde{\Delta}_{2}$}}$}}\nolimits g_{N}^{\prime}(z)\penalty\ dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{(Y_{1}+Y_{2})}{N\tilde{p}^{d}}\tilde{\Delta}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{(Y_{1}+Y_{2})Y_{2}}{N\tilde{p}^{d}},

where the last step follows from the fact that
\tilde{\Delta}_{2}\leq CY_{2}
for some global constant
C.
Note that since
g_{N}
is increasing and
g_{N}^{\prime}
is positive and bounded below, the above bound shows that

|g_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1})+\tilde{\Delta}_{2}-g_{N}^{-1}(X+Y_{1}+Y_{2})|\leq C^{\prime}\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{(Y_{1}+Y_{2})Y_{2}}{N\tilde{p}^{d}}

for some other global constant
C^{\prime}.
In particular this shows that

|\Delta_{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{2}|\leq C^{\prime}\left(\frac{k_{N}}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{(Y_{1}+Y_{2})Y_{2}}{N\tilde{p}^{d}}.

(C.33)

Using (
C.33
) and (
C.32
), we get the required bound on
|l-\tilde{l}|
and hence the bound needed on
|\tilde{\delta}_{2}-\delta_{3}|
in (
C.27
). As noted before, this completes the proof of showing
\mathcal{I}_{N}
is bounded.

Calculations for normal scale perturbations

In this section, we complete the full calculation of

for the normal scale perturbations in

Recall the untruncated density is

p(x\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}(\theta_{1}...\theta_{m})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2\theta_{i}}+\sum_{j>m}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}\right),

while the truncated density is

p_{L}(x\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{Z_{L}(\theta)}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2\theta_{i}}+\sum_{j>m}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}\right)\mathbf{1}\{x\in[-L,L]^{d}\}.

for
x\in[-L,L]^{d}
, where
Z_{L}(\theta)
is some normalizing constant. We want to find the sign of the integral

\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle[-L,L]^{d}$}}$}}\nolimits h^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\left(\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{L}(x|\theta_{1}))}{p_{L}(x|\theta_{1})}\right)p_{L}^{\frac{d-2}{d}}(x|\theta_{1})\penalty\ dx,

To calculate the trace of the Hessian, we first note that

\nabla_{x}p_{L}(x\mid\theta)=-p_{L}(x\mid\theta)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{i}}{\theta_{i}}e_{i}+\sum_{i>m}x_{i}e_{i}\right).

Hence,

\nabla^{2}_{x_{i}}p_{L}(x\mid\theta)=\begin{cases}-p_{L}(x\mid\theta)\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{i}}-\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{\theta_{i}^{2}}\right)\text{ if $i\leq m$,}\\
-p_{L}(x\mid\theta)\left(1-x_{i}^{2}\right)\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This gives

\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p(x_{L}\mid\theta))}{p_{L}(x\mid\theta)}\implies h^{T}\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{\textnormal{tr}(\textnormal{H}_{x}p_{L}(x\mid\theta))}{p_{L}(x\mid\theta)}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(1-2x_{i}^{2}).

As a result we have for
\theta_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{m},

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle S$}}$}}\nolimits h^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\left(\frac{\text{tr}(\text{H}_{x}p_{L}(x|\theta_{1}))}{p(x|\theta_{1})}\right)p_{L}^{\frac{d-2}{d}}(x|\theta_{1})\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle[-L,L]^{d}$}}$}}\nolimits\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(1-2x_{i}^{2})\right)p_{L}^{\frac{d-2}{d}}(x|\theta_{1})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(1-2\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{2})),

where
X_{i}\sim N\left(0,\frac{d}{d-2}\right)\mid_{[-L,L]}
. Thus, the sign of
b(h,\theta_{1})
is determined by the sign of
\sum h_{i}
for
\theta_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{m}.

In calculating the gradients and Hessians, we never used the boundedness of
L
. The truncation only entered at the final step when actually evaluating the integral. Hence, in the untruncated case we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits h^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\left(\frac{\text{tr}(\text{H}_{x}p_{L}(x|\theta_{1}))}{p(x|\theta_{1})}\right)p_{L}^{\frac{d-2}{d}}(x|\theta_{1})\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(1-2x_{i}^{2})\right)p_{L}^{\frac{d-2}{d}}(x|\theta_{1})

D.5
Performance of test based on MST

In this section, we consider the power of another graph based test in the same settings as before in order to compare with the
k
-NN test. A popular graph-based test is the one based on the minimal spanning tree (MST),
Friedman and Rafsky [16]
. Just as increasing the number of neighbors provides denser versions of the nearest neighbors graphs, one can also obtain denser versions of the MST, known as
k
-MST’s for
k\in\mathbb{N}
which can be defined inductively.

The 1-MST is the usual MST. Given a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},
a spanning tree
\mathcal{T}
of
S
is a connected, undirected graph with vertex set
S
and no cycles. The length of a spanning tree is the sum of the lengths of all edges in the tree. A tree
\mathcal{T}
is called the MST of
S
if its length is at most the length of any other spanning tree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of
S.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}(S)
denote the MST of
S
. To construct the 2-MST, we consider all spanning trees of
S
which use no edges from the 1-MST. The spanning tree
\mathcal{T}_{2}(S)
with minimum length among all such trees is known as the 2-MST. Similarly, the
k
-MST
\mathcal{T}_{k}(S)
is the minimum length spanning tree among all spanning trees of
S
which use no edge belonging to
\mathcal{T}_{1}(S)\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{T}_{k-1}(S)
.

Using a
k
-MST as the underlying graph gives the test statistic to be the number of edges going across groups. As before, one can use either the 1-sided version which rejects when the statistic is too small, or the 2-sided version which rejects when the observed value of the statistic deviates significantly from the bulk of the distribution. To calibrate and implement the test, we use the permutation test based on resampling the labels. This is the same procedure as described for the
k
-NN test in
Section

5.1
with the only change being that the underlying graph is now the
k
-MST.
