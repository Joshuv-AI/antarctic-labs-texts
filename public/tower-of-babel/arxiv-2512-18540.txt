Scaling up Stability: Reinforcement Learning for Distributed Control of Networked Systems in the Space of Stabilizing Policies
John Cao, Luca Furieri

Abstract
We study distributed control of networked systems through reinforcement learning, where neural policies must be simultaneously scalable, expressive and stabilizing. We introduce a policy parameterization that embeds Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) into a Youla-like magnitude-direction parameterization, yielding distributed stochastic controllers that guarantee network-level closed-loop stability by design. The magnitude is implemented as a stable operator consisting of a GNN acting on disturbance feedback, while the direction is a GNN acting on local observations. We prove robustness of the closed loop to perturbations in both the graph topology and model parameters, and show how to integrate our parameterization with Proximal Policy Optimization. Experiments on a multi-agent navigation task show that policies trained on small networks transfer directly to larger ones and unseen network topologies, achieve higher returns and lower variance than a state-of-the-art MARL baseline while preserving stability.



keywords

Learning methods for optimal control; Reinforcement learning; Distributed robust controller synthesis; Stability of nonlinear systems; Graph neural networks

1
Introduction

1.1
Background

The control of large-scale dynamical systems is a fundamental topic in control theory, with applications ranging from UAV coordination
(
Qie et al. 2019
)
, search and rescue
(
Kumar et al. 2004
)
to warehouse robotics
(
Kattepur et al. 2018
)
.

Learning-based controllers, such as those obtained via Reinforcement Learning (RL), often outperform traditional controllers on complex systems
(
Kaufmann et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2020
)
. In distributed settings, they are typically implemented under the
centralized training with decentralized execution
(CTDE) paradigm, often using actor-critic methods
(
Konda and Tsitsiklis 1999
)
with a centralized critic and decentralized actor. Despite strong empirical results in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), CTDE methods face major challenges: lack of formal safety and stability guarantees, high training variance, and poor generalization when the number of agents change between training and deployment. Safety and stability are usually encouraged heuristically via reward shaping or by layering safety certificates and fall-back controllers on top of trained policies. Existing work largely emphasizes safety filtering, with Control Barrier Functions (CBF)
(
Ames et al. 2017
)
and Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) Reachability
(
Bansal et al. 2017
)
as core tools, while methods that ensure both safety and stability while retaining expressivity remain underexplored despite their importance in safety-critical applications.

Recent approaches often enforce stability using simple nominal controllers.
Zhang et al. 2025
consider a multi-agent navigation problem where LQR- and PID-based controllers guide agents to target states with a learned safety filter added on top. However, convergence is not guaranteed, and the approach can suffer from deadlocks when scaled to large systems that simple controllers cannot resolve. Combined with safety filtering, basic nominal policies can also induce jittery, bang-bang inputs
(
Borquez et al. 2024
)
. These limitations highlight the need for control policies that are simultaneously
expressive
,
scalable
and
stable
, capable of reasoning over complex feedback while retaining closed-loop guarantees.

Designing such policies is a challenging task, as distributed optimal control is hard even for linear quadratic (LQ) cases, where optimization problems become impractical to solve due to the exponential growth of the search space as systems scale up. For nonlinear networked systems, deriving distributed stabilizing controllers becomes substantially more difficult, which motivates the need for neural policies to capture system complexity. However, direct neural parameterizations generally lack stability guarantees and can destabilize the system unless carefully tuned
(
Fazel et al. 2018
)
.

Several works aim to address this problem by incorporating stability into the design and training of neural policies. Lale et al. (2022) learn stabilizing policies using a Lyapunov constraint derived from Krasovskii’s method. An adjacent line of work
(
Roberts et al. 2011
;
Furieri et al. 2022a
;
Furieri et al. 2024
)
generalizes Youla and system-level parameterizations to nonlinear systems, characterizing all stabilizing policies through the lens of disturbance feedback. These methods, however, are either limited by restrictive assumptions on system and controller structures, or rely exclusively on disturbance feedback without exploiting state and graph-structured information, limiting their expressivity and applicability. More recently,
Furieri et al. 2025
extended these policies to include explicit state feedback, enlarging the class of learnable controllers and making them compatible with RL frameworks. However, the method focuses on fully centralized setups, limiting its scalability and ability to transfer to systems of varying sizes.

In parallel, distributed neural control has been studied for structured systems.
Furieri et al. 2022b
learn large-scale stabilizing distributed controllers for port-Hamiltonian networks, while
Saccani et al. 2024
enforce stability during training of neural controllers in networked settings. These works demonstrate that stability-constrained neural architectures can be deployed in distributed control, but tailored assumptions on system structure limit their general applicability, and they do not address scalability in the sense of training on small systems and transferring to much larger ones with modified topologies. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) naturally address some of these challenges by performing layer-wise local aggregations over a graph.
Gama and Sojoudi 2022
use GNNs as deterministic controllers for LQR problems and show that GNN-based controllers are permutation equivariant, enabling training on small networks and generalization to larger ones. However, their stability and robustness guarantees are established only for linear systems and small GNN parameters, which limits expressivity and risks destabilization if parameters exceed certain thresholds during training.

Despite the many advances in distributed control, stability-constrained RL and MARL, a unifying framework that simultaneously offers (1) distributed computation with guaranteed closed-loop stability, (2) robustness to topology changes with transfer from small to large systems, and (3) state-of-the-art performance remains a desideratum.

1.2
Contributions

We make the following contributions:

•

Distributed and scalable GNN-based policies:
We present a Youla-like distributed policy parametrization that guarantees closed-loop stability while being realized by GNNs, enabling training on small networked systems and transferring to larger ones with different topologies during deployment.

•

Integration with PPO:
We provide a recipe for making our parameterization compatible with Proximal Policy Optimization
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
, a popular RL algorithm that has enabled training stochastic policies achieving state-of-the-art performance. This allows our method to enjoy the benefits of PPO and directly plug into existing RL frameworks.

•

Empirical validation of benefits:
On a multi-agent navigation task, we show that our distributed GNN policies trained on a few agents generalize to larger systems with unseen network topologies, improve performance over a state-of-the-art MARL baseline, and exhibit more stable and sample efficient training.

2
Problem Formulation

2.1
Distributed Nonlinear Systems

We consider a network of
N
interconnected nonlinear subsystems. The coupling network among the subsystems is defined as an undirected dynamical coupling graph
\mathcal{G}_{d}=(\mathcal{V}_{d},\mathcal{E}_{d})
, denoted by the
d
subscript, with
\mathcal{V}_{d}=\{1,\dots,N\}
representing the subsystems in the network, and the set of edges
\mathcal{E}_{d}
containing the pairs of subsystems
\{i,j\}
, which can communicate with each other. Each subsystem is of the form

x_{t}^{[i]}=f^{[i]}(x^{[\mathcal{N}_{i,d}]}_{t-1},u^{[i]}_{t-1})+w^{[i]}_{t},\ \ \ \forall t\geq 1,

(1)

where state and input of each subsystem

are denoted by
x^{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
and
u^{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
respectively. Moreover,
w^{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
is an unknown process noise with
w^{[i]}_{0}=x^{[i]}_{0}
.

In operator form, we can express the subsystem (
1
) as:

\mathbf{x}^{[i]}=\mathbf{F}^{[i]}(\mathbf{x}^{[\mathcal{N}_{i,d}]},\mathbf{u}^{[i]})+\mathbf{w}^{[i]},

(2)

where
\mathbf{F}^{[i]}:\ell^{n_{\mathcal{N}_{i,d}}}\times\ell^{m_{i}}\rightarrow\ell^{n_{i}}
is a strictly causal operator defining the dynamics such that
\mathbf{F^{[i]}}(\mathbf{x^{[\mathcal{N}_{i,d}]},u^{[i]}})
=\left(0,f^{[i]}\left(x^{[\mathcal{N}_{i,d}]}_{0},u_{0}^{[i]}\right),\dots,f^{[i]}\left(x^{[\mathcal{N}_{i,d}]}_{t},u^{[i]}_{t}\right),\dots\right)
.

By combining the local system dynamics in (
1
), the dynamics of the global system result in

x_{t}=f(x_{t-1},u_{t-1})+w_{t},\ \ \ \forall t\geq 0,

(3)

where
x_{t}=col_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(x^{[i]}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u_{t}=col_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(u^{[i]}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
w_{t}=col_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(w^{[i]}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Similarly to the subsystems, we can rewrite system (
3
) in operator form as:

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x,u})+\mathbf{w},

(4)

Every pair of disturbance and input sequences maps to a corresponding state trajectory, inducing a causal operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}:(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})\rightarrow\mathbf{x}
mapping inputs and disturbances to trajectories. We make the following Assumption on the system to be controlled:

Assumption 1

We assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
is such that the map
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})\mapsto\mathbf{x}
lies in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
, and that the process noise

is distributed according to an unknown distribution

, and that

belongs to

The Assumption above means that the interconnection is stable or locally controlled to achieve
\mathcal{L}_{p}
stability.
This is typically true in practical applications where systems are either pre-stabilized or can be stabilized using simple local controllers. While these controllers ensure stability, they often result in suboptimal closed-loop performance. Our goal is to enhance performance while maintaining stability.

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\text{ }\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta},w_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(x_{t},u_{t})\right]

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t}\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbf{x}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}),

\displaystyle\hskip 26.17648ptu^{[i]}_{t}\sim\pi^{[i]}_{\theta}\left(\cdot\text{ }|~x^{[\mathcal{N}^{i}_{\mathcal{G}_{c}}]}_{t:0},u^{[\mathcal{N}^{i}_{\mathcal{G}_{c}}]}_{t-1:0}\right),\quad\forall i\in[1,N]\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\hskip 26.17648pt\mathbf{u}\in\ell_{p},~\forall\mathbf{w}\in\ell_{p}\,.

(7)

2.3
The Benefits of GNNs for Distributed Control

A natural way to enforce the distributed controller structure in (
6
) is to unify the policies
\pi_{\theta}^{[i]}
under a single parameterization using a Graph Neural Network (GNN). A GNN
\Phi_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times F_{\text{in}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times F_{\text{out}}}
consists of
L
layers of neighborhood level computations on a given graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, mapping node features
x^{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{F_{\text{in}}},i=1,...,|V|
to embeddings of size
F_{\text{out}}
. A layer
l
performs 2 core operations:

1.

Message Computation:
For each node
v\in\mathcal{V}
with associated hidden embeddings
h_{l}^{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{F_{l}}
,
i=1,...,|\mathcal{V|}
, produce
messages
through a function
g_{\text{msg}}:\mathbb{R}^{F^{l}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{F^{l,m}}
mapping
\{h_{l}^{[i]}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\rightarrow\{m_{l}^{[i]}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
, where
F^{l,m}
denotes the dimension of the messages.

2.

Message Aggregation:
For each neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}
,
aggregate
the computed messages using a function
g_{\text{agg}}:({\mathbb{R}^{F^{l,m}}})^{|\mathcal{N}_{i}|}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{F^{l+1}}
, which combines the messages from each neighborhood to produce the subsequent set of hidden embeddings
\{h_{l+1}^{[i]}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
for layer
l+1
.

To make our setup computationally compatible with this framework, we modify the global system state (
3
) to make it
horizontally
stacked, denoted as
X_{t}=\mathrm{col}_{i\in V_{c}}(x_{t}^{[i]})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}_{c}|\times\max_{i}\{n_{i}\}}
, where
\max_{i}\{n_{i}\}
is the largest subsystem dimension. This structure compacts the node information in the graph into a single matrix, which enables efficient graph-level computations. To encode the structure of
\mathcal{G}_{c}=(\mathcal{V}_{c},\mathcal{E}_{c})
, we denote by
S_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}_{c}|\times|\mathcal{V}_{c}|}
a support matrix consistent with the sparsity of
\mathcal{G}_{c}
(e.g the adjacency matrix). Given this definition, the message computation and aggregation for a given layer are combined in a single step

\displaystyle H^{l}=\sigma\left(G(H^{l-1};S_{c},\theta^{l-1})\right),

(8)

where
\sigma
is a nonlinearity,
H^{l}=\text{col}_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{c}}(h_{l}^{[i]})^{T}
,
\theta^{l}
are the
l
-layer parameters, and
G
is a function that combines the message computation and passing operations. As an example,

\displaystyle G(H^{l};S_{c},\theta^{l})=D^{-1}S_{c}H^{l}W^{l}

(9)

for a Graph Convolutional Network
(
Kipf 2016
)
, where
D=\text{diag}(|\mathcal{N}_{1}|,...,|\mathcal{N}_{|\mathcal{V}|}|)
,
S_{c}
is the adjacency matrix, and
\theta^{l}=W^{l}
denotes the weight matrix. A GNN
\Phi_{\theta}
is a cascade of such layers. Using an
L
-layer GNN, we embed the global state as

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\Phi_{\theta}(X_{t};S_{c},\theta),

\displaystyle H^{0}=X_{t},

\displaystyle Y_{t}=H^{L}=f_{\text{out}}(H^{L-1}),

(10)

where
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times F_{\text{out}}}
are the embedded state vectors, and
f_{\text{out}}
is a function projecting the last hidden layer to the output space.

Besides its simplicity and naturally distributed nature, GNNs carry several desirable properties for distributed control setups. First, GNNs are permutation equivariant, satisfying

\displaystyle P\Phi_{\theta}(X;S,\theta)=\Phi_{\theta}(PX;PSP^{T},\theta).

(11)

In (
11
),
P
is a permutation matrix that reshuffles the ordering of the nodes. In other words, changing the node order will only reorder the output matrix accordingly. This means that GNNs effectively leverage the underlying graph topology by learning to map neighborhoods to outputs, irrespective of how the nodes are numbered. This allows for generalization to unseen graph topologies with varying number of nodes and edges, as the model is agnostic to the identity of the nodes and operates only on their features and neighborhoods. Furthermore, GNNs are also Lipschitz with respect to changes in the graph topology
(
Gama et al. 2020
)
, meaning that a perturbation to the graph structure will induce a change in the GNN output that is bounded as a function of the size of the perturbation. Therefore, small changes in the graph topology won’t alter the output of the GNN too much, making the architecture inherently robust.

In the distributed control setting, these properties directly yield several highly desirable benefits. A GNN policy can naturally generalize across scales, meaning that training on
N
subsystems allows the policy to generalize to larger systems with number of nodes greater than
N
. Moreover, the robustness of GNNs makes the closed-loop behavior robust to time-varying communication topologies, which arise in many distributed control tasks such as in multi-agent coordination problems. However, using GNNs directly as closed-loop policies can induce instability unless the parameters are carefully regularized
(
Gama and Sojoudi 2022
)
. This conservatism presents a trade-off between stability and expressiveness. In the next section, we propose a parameterization that guarantees stability irrespective of the chosen model parameters, enabling stable yet GNN expressive policies.

3
Methodology

A crucial decision to make when solving (
5
) is the parameterization of distributed policies in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
. In this section, we introduce an expressive class of stable policies based on
Furieri et al. 2025
, extending its centralized and deterministic computations to the distributed and stochastic setting. We present a decomposition of the controller into a magnitude and direction term using GNNs, and prove its robustness against changes in both the graph communication topology and the model parameters.

3.1
Separating Magnitude and Direction

To restrict our search over the space of stable policies for the global networked system, we recall the following sufficient and necessary conditions from
Furieri et al. 2025
:

Theorem 1

(
Furieri et al. 2025
)
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}:\ell^{n}\rightarrow\ell^{m}
is a causal operator. Then the closed-loop system induced by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}))=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{w})

(12)

satisfies
\mathbf{w}\rightarrow(\mathbf{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}})\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
. On the other hand, if there exists a causal controller
\mathbf{u}=\boldsymbol{\pi}(\mathbf{x})
satisfying
\mathbf{w}\rightarrow(\mathbf{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}})\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
, then there exists an operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
which induces the same closed-loop behavior.

Theorem
1
states that the task of searching over stable policies is equivalent to searching over operators in
\mathcal{L}_{p}
that map disturbances to control inputs. Thus, (
5
) can be solved by parameterizing
\mathcal{L}_{p}
operators
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
to automatically satisfy the stability constraint. However, deriving
\mathbf{u}
purely from disturbances leaves the entire closed-loop behavior to be characterized by how well you can approximate the space of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
operators, which limits the range of achievable policies since there is no known universal approximator of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
.

A critical limitation of the policy under Theorem
1
is that it is
not
necessarily distributed. Works such as
Saccani et al. 2024
consider a distributed variant of the theorem, but still relies purely on disturbance feedback, unable to react to state features.
Furieri et al. 2025
introduces a state-feedback parameterization that achieves better generalization, however, it is neither distributed nor scalable. In this work, we unite the properties of these approaches with the benefits of GNNs to synthesize a policy that is both stable, scalable and expressive enough to achieve state-of-the-art performance. Our proposed policy embeds GNNs in a stochastic version of MAD policies in
Furieri et al. 2025
, which uses a polar decomposition of the control input into magnitude and direction terms. Specifically, our proposed policy is as follows:

\displaystyle u_{t}=|\mathcal{M}_{t}(w_{t:0})|\cdot D_{t},

\displaystyle D_{t}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|\psi(x_{t:0})),

\displaystyle|D_{t}|\leq 1,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathcal{L}_{p},

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
must be constructed as a stable GNN operator.
The direction term
D_{t}
is sampled from a distribution
\mathcal{P}
conditioned on the output of a function
\psi
of the past states
x_{t:0}
. Specifically, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}(w_{t:0})=\text{LRU}(z_{t:0}),

(14)

where
LRU
is a
Linear Recurrent Unit
defined as

\displaystyle\xi_{t+1}=\Lambda\xi_{t}+\Gamma(\Lambda)Bz_{t},

\displaystyle\text{LRU}(z_{t:0})=\text{NN}(\Re(C\xi_{t})+Dz_{t};\phi)+Fz_{t},

(15)

where NN denotes a neural network,
\Re
is the real operator and
\Lambda,B,C,D,F,\phi
are learnable parameters. We enforce stability by designing
\Lambda
such that its eigenvalues
|\lambda_{i}|<1
, with
\Gamma(\Lambda)
being a diagonal normalization term. Lastly, the inputs to the LRU are computed using a GNN

\displaystyle z_{t}=\Phi(W_{t};S_{c},\theta_{1}).

(16)

For the direction term
D_{t}
, we use a separate GNN to define

\displaystyle\psi(x_{t:0})=\text{RNN}(v_{t:0}),

\displaystyle v_{t}=\Phi(X_{t};S_{c},\theta_{2}),

(17)

where RNN denotes a Recurrent Neural Network acting on the sequence of past GNN outputs
v_{t:0}
.

Corollary 1

Under Assumption
1
, a controller defined according to (
3.1
)-(
3.1
) induces a mapping
\mathbf{w}\rightarrow(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
if
\Phi(\cdot;S_{c},\theta_{1})
has finite
\mathcal{L}_{p}
-gain
\gamma(\Phi)<\infty
.

As shown in Corollary
1
, our parameterization guarantees stability of the closed-loop system, while stochasticity is naturally enabled through parameterizing classes of probability distributions by mapping state observations to density parameters (e.g mean and covariance for Gaussians). This construction decouples the handling of stability and expressivity, allowing the magnitude term to keep the system stable while leveraging the universal approximation properties of GNNs to freely control the direction to produce rich closed-loop behaviors.

Remark 1

While the control input computations are distributed, each node might still receive information from its
n
-hop neighbors. For example, a L-layer GNN performs L local information aggregation operations, meaning the final control uses information from its L-hop neighbors.

3.2
Robustness Against Perturbations

Next, we show the robustness properties of the policy parameterization (

) against perturbations in the graph communication topology and model weights for a specific GNN architecture. We follow
Nayak et al. 2023

)

\displaystyle H^{l}=\sigma(\tilde{A}H^{l-1}W^{l-1}+H^{l-1}B^{l-1}).

\sigma
is nonlinear activation such that

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\text{softmax}\left(\frac{(W_{1}x_{i})^{T}(W_{2}x_{j}+W_{3}e_{i,j})}{\sqrt{F^{l}}}\right)&\text{if }(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{c},\\

The attention scores in (
19
) depend on learnable parameters
W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}
, which uses the node and edge features
x_{i},x_{j},e_{i,j}
to compute a scalar weight for each edge in a given neighborhood, such that
\sum_{j}\tilde{A}_{i,j}=1
. Intuitively, these scores ”attend” to each edge, assigning larger values to important edges. We refer the reader to
Dwivedi and Bresson 2020
for a full characterization of this architecture.

We now show that this choice of GNN architecture paired with the parameterization (
3.1
) is inherently robust against perturbations in the graph topology and model parameters, with the deviations in closed-loop trajectories scaling with the size of the perturbations. For simplicity, we consider the update (
18
) with the attention matrix replaced by a generic support matrix
S
.

Lemma 1

Let
\Phi,\hat{\Phi}
be two
L
-layer GNNs with updates according to

\displaystyle H^{l}=\sigma(SH^{l-1}W^{l-1}+H^{l-1}B^{l-1}),

(20)

with support matrices and parameters
(S,W,B)
and
(\hat{S},\hat{W},\hat{B})
. Denote the differences between these values as
(\Delta S,\Delta W,\Delta B)
, i.e
\hat{S}=S+\Delta S,\hat{B}=B+\Delta B,\hat{W}=W+\Delta W
. Let
||\cdot||
denote the element-wise matrix
p
norm. Then for a given input
X
, the output differences between the GNNs are bounded by

\displaystyle||\Phi(X)-\hat{\Phi}(X)||\leq L_{\sigma}^{L}||X||\left(\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\Delta_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{L-1}\rho_{j}\prod_{v=0}^{i-1}\zeta_{v}\right)

(21)

where
\Delta_{i}=||\Delta S||\cdot||\Delta W^{i}||+||\Delta S||\cdot||W^{i}||+||\Delta B^{i}||+||S||\cdot||\Delta W^{i}||
,
\zeta_{v}=||\hat{S}||\cdot||\hat{W}^{v}||+||\hat{B}^{v}||
and
\rho_{j}=||S||\cdot||W^{j}||+||B^{j}||
.

Lemma
1
leads us to the following result about closed-loop robustness.

Theorem 2

Let
\mathbf{\Phi},\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}
be two GNN operators with updates specified by (
20
), with support matrices and parameters differing by
(\Delta S,\Delta W,\Delta B)
. Assume
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
is a causal operator with finite
\ell_{p}
-gain
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})
. Then for all disturbances
\mathbf{w}\in\ell_{p}
, the deviations in closed-loop trajectories induced by
\mathbf{\Phi}
and
\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}
when used in the policy (
3.1
)-(
3.1
) are upper bounded by

\displaystyle||\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}||\leq

\displaystyle\leq\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})\gamma(\text{LRU})L_{\sigma}^{L}||\mathbf{w}||\left(\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\Delta_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{L-1}\rho_{j}\prod_{v=0}^{i-1}\zeta_{v}+2\prod_{k=0}^{L-1}\zeta_{k}\right)

(22)

Theorem
2
implies that the policy parameterization (
3.1
)-(
3.1
) is robust to changing communication topologies and model parameters (since
\tilde{A}
is a support matrix). This highlights one of the core strengths of our method: finite changes in the graph structure and weights can never cause the closed-loop system to destabilize, even if the policy is untrained. This allows stability to be retained at every stage of the training process, while enabling generalization to unseen topologies without the risk of destabilization. We note that the bound in Theorem
2
is not tight, meaning that it would be non-zero even in the absence of perturbations. This is caused by the stochasticity of
D_{t}
, which induces trajectory mismatches even under identical model parameters. However, the benefits of stochastic policies outweigh the lack of tightness, and the trajectories still eventually coincide if
\mathbf{w}\in\ell_{p}
.

3.3
Practical Implementation with PPO

When training stochastic RL policies, it is often desirable to prevent updates that result in overly large changes in the policy to improve training stability. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
is a policy-gradient method
(
Sutton et al. 1999
)
that encourages this behavior by constraining the update ratio
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(u_{t}|x_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(u_{t}|x_{t})}
between the new and old policies.

PPO has been empirically validated as one of the most effective RL algorithms and has enabled state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of domains. In order to make our method compatible with PPO, we need to compute the log probabilities of the update ratio between the new and old policies. To simplify notation and reduce clutter, we abstract the total control computation as the simplified representation

\displaystyle u_{t}=u_{\text{base},t}+|\mathcal{M}_{t}(w_{t:0})|\cdot g(D_{t}),

\displaystyle D_{t}\sim\mathcal{P}(d|x_{t:0}),

(23)

where
u_{\text{base},t}
is the stabilizing base controller, and
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an invertible function ensuring
|D_{t}|\leq 1
(e.g tanh). Let
z_{t}=\frac{u_{t}-u_{\text{base},t}}{|\mathcal{M}_{t}(w_{t:0})|}
, then the policy describing the distribution of
u_{t}
is

\displaystyle\pi(u_{t}|x_{t:0})=\mathcal{P}(D_{t}|x_{t:0})\left|\frac{dD}{du}\right|=\mathcal{P}(g^{-1}(z_{t})|x_{t:0})\left|\frac{dg^{-1}}{dz_{t}}\frac{dz_{t}}{du_{t}}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(g^{-1}(z_{t})|x_{t:0})\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{t}(w_{t:0})|}\left|\frac{dg^{-1}}{dz_{t}}\right|.

(24)

This leads us to the following computation of the log probabilities:

\displaystyle\log\pi(u_{t}|x_{t:0})=\log\mathcal{P}(g^{-1}(z_{t})|x_{t:0})-\log|\mathcal{M}_{t}(w_{t:0})|+

\displaystyle+\log\left|\frac{dg^{-1}}{dz_{t}}\right|.

(25)

In other words, the computation of the distribution over control inputs requires reconstructing the direction term
D_{t}
which was sampled during the roll-out. The log-probability of the direction sample needs to be complemented by additional terms derived from the change of variables used to construct
u_{t}
. Another takeaway is that one would also need to collect
w_{t}
and base controller inputs
u_{\text{base},t}
during the roll-outs apart from the states and control inputs.

These modifications allow our policy to be trained using PPO, enabling it to achieve stable training and state-of-the-art performance.

4.1
Training Environment and Setup

We benchmark on the Multi-Agent Particle Environment from
(
Lowe et al. 2017
;
Nayak et al. 2023
)
. It consists of
N
agents modeled as 2D integrators with nonlinear collision and speed dynamics. The state of agent
i
is
x_{t}^{[i]}=[v_{x,t}^{[i]},v_{y,t}^{[i]},p_{x,t}^{[i]},p_{y,t}^{[i]}]
, with
v
and
p
denoting the velocity and position of the agent at the
x,y
coordinates and time
t
. At the start of each episode, every agent is randomly assigned an initial position and a target position
p_{\text{goal}}^{[i]}
. The task is to reach the goal while avoiding collisions with other agents and obstacles.

Agents share a global reward
R(x_{t},u_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r(x_{t}^{[i]},u_{t}^{[i]})
, where

r(x_{t}^{[i]},u_{t}^{[i]})=-\|p_{t}^{[i]}-p_{\text{goal}}^{[i]}\|_{2}+r_{\text{coll},t}^{[i]}+r_{\text{goal},t}^{[i]}
, with

p_{t}^{[i]}=[p_{x,t}^{[i]},p_{y,t}^{[i]}]
,
r_{\text{coll},t}^{[i]}=-5
when colliding with an agent or obstacle, and
r_{\text{goal},t}^{[i]}=5
upon entering the vicinity of the goal position.

Each agent
i
senses neighbors within a communication radius
r
, receiving disturbances
w_{t}^{[j]}
and node features
[x_{t}^{[j]},p_{\text{goal}}^{[j]},\text{entity}\_\text{type}(j)]
for all agents
j
within this radius, where
\text{entity}\_\text{type}(j)
is a unique index for each entity type. Thus, the GNN operates on each agent’s local,
dynamically
changing communication graph
\mathcal{G}_{c,t}^{[i]}
, with the global graph
\mathcal{G}_{c,t}=\cup_{i}\mathcal{G}_{c,t}^{[i]}
.

We implement (
3.1
)-(
3.1
) by choosing
\mathcal{P}
to be a Gaussian distribution, and let the GNN
\rightarrow
RNN cascade output
\psi(x_{t:0})=(\mu(x_{t:0}),\Sigma(x_{t:0}))
, the mean and covariance of the distribution. The sample
D_{t}
is wrapped around a tanh to ensure
|D_{t}|<1
. Pre-stabilization of the system is achieved using a base proportional controller
u_{\text{base},t}^{[i]}=K^{[i]}(p^{[i]}_{t}-p_{\text{goal}}^{[i]})
for each agent. We set an upper limit on the value the magnitude can attain, which we found to result in more stable training.

Our method restricts the policy search to exclusively stabilizing policies. The top of figure (

Despite the absence of training, our method drives the system to equilibrium, while the system remains unstable without our guarantees. The bottom figure shows that after training, the InforMARL policy drives

5
Conclusion

We proposed a scalable policy class that is expressive, stable by design and enables generalization across system sizes. This is achieved through combining GNNs with a Youla-like magnitude and direction polar decomposition. The magnitude term is realized as a stable GNN–LRU operator, while the stochastic direction term is implemented via a GNN–RNN module, yielding stabilizing yet expressive controllers that are robust to perturbations in model parameters and communication topology. Experiments on a multi-agent navigation task show built-in stability, scalability and generalization to varying number of agents and new graphs, while improving performance over a state-of-the-art MARL baseline. Future work includes probabilistic analysis of trajectory deviation bounds and extension of the method to output-feedback systems.
