Evaluation of the Association of TNF‐α rs361525 Gene Polymorphism in Bangladeshi Patients With Breast and Cervical Cancer: A Case‐Control Study and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Syed Masudur Rahman Dewan, Amrin Yeasin Proma, Sakif Ahamed Khan, Sara Shahid Meem, Md Tashdid Alam, Md Abdul Barek, Taslima Jamal Urmi, A T M Sazzad Hossain, Mohiuddin Ahmed Bhuiyan, Mohammad Safiqul Islam, Mohammad Shahriar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIncidence and fatality rates are highest for breast cancer (BC) and cervical cancer (CC), making them the most prevalent types of cancer. Prior research has documented certain cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), as being accountable for the growth and advancement of these cancerous conditions. To assess the possible association, we examined the correlation between the TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism (−238 G > A) and the susceptibility to both BC and CC.MethodsA case‐control study was conducted on a total of 155 BC patients, 157 CC patients, and 150 controls. The genotyping process utilized the polymerase chain reaction‐restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR‐RFLP) technique. To ascertain the extent of the risk association of rs361525, we assessed the p value, odds ratio (OR), and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Again, 13 eligible studies were selected for meta‐analysis, and RevMan 5.3 and the MetaGenyo web‐based tool were utilized.ResultsIn terms of TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism, the GA genotype (OR = 0.42, p = 0.0009), dominant model (OR = 0.51, p = 0.007), over‐dominant model (OR = 0.45, p = 0.001), and allelic model (OR = 0.53, p = 0.001) significantly reduced the risk of BC. On the other hand, the examination of the link between TNF‐α rs361525 and CC revealed that no genotype or allele exhibited a statistically significant association (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the meta‐analysis of the rs361525 variant in association with BC did not reveal any statistically significant association among any genetic models across the overall and ethnic populations (p > 0.05).ConclusionIn summary, our research demonstrated that the rs361525 gene polymorphism in the TNF‐α locus may possess a protective effect against BC and CC in the Bangladeshi population. Furthermore, the meta‐analysis results suggested no link between rs361525 polymorphism and BC. We suggest carrying out future studies with a larger sample size on the other ethnic population and cutting‐edge genotyping technologies to obtain comprehensive and precise outcomes.Keywords: Bangladesh, breast cancer gene, cervical cancer, cytokine, gene polymorphism, good health and well‐being, rs361525, tumor necrosis factor alpha

Introduction

Cancer is a medical condition characterized by the irregular and uncontrolled proliferation and dissemination of cells inside the body [1]. Based on the 2020 GLOBOCAN estimates, almost 19 million new instances of cancer were diagnosed globally, resulting in over 10 million deaths. This makes cancer the second‐leading cause of death [2]. Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly identified form of cancer that occurs around the globe. The onset typically occurs within the breast lobules, tubes, or connective tissue. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) recently published a report stating that there were 2.3 million new instances of BC, accounting for 11.7% of all new cancer cases, and a mortality rate of 6.9% across 185 nations [3]. In the case of mortality, BC stands as the fifth most frequent cause of death worldwide, resulting in around 685,000 fatalities [4]. On the other hand, cervical cancer (CC) is ranked as the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of death among women. In 2020, there were a total of 604,000 newly diagnosed cases of CC and 342,000 fatalities caused by this disease [5]. Furthermore, CC was identified as one of the leading three types of cancer occurring in women below the age of 45 in 146 nations. This accounts for 79% of the 185 countries that were evaluated [6]. In Bangladesh, BC resulted in 6846 fatalities and 12,764 new diagnoses, whereas CC, the second highest cause of female mortality, accounted for 5214 deaths and 8068 new cases [7].

Several factors have been found to be associated with an increased risk of developing and progressing BC. These factors include the age of the patient, reproductive and hormonal factors (such as early age at first birth or menarche, having fewer children, less breastfeeding, later age at menopause, use of menopausal hormone therapy and oral contraceptives), personal or family history of BC, genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and lifestyle factors (such as alcohol consumption, excessive body weight, and physical inactivity) [8]. The risk factors for cervical malignancy encompass both behavioral factors (such as sexual activity and lifestyle choices) and specific viral contributors (such as the human papillomavirus). Additional risk factors include the age at the first full‐term pregnancy, dietary habits, family medical history, immunosuppression, immunological deficiency, use of oral contraceptives, number of pregnancies, and the habit of smoking [9].

Tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) is an inflammatory mediator primarily synthesized by activated macrophages and monocytes [10]. TNF‐α triggers a series of other inflammatory cytokines and chemokines and is well recognized as a major contributor to inflammation. TNF‐α, when produced by immune cells locally, has a significant therapeutic role in eliminating tumor blood vessels and triggering the programmed cell death (apoptosis) and death of tumor cells (necrosis). However, in cases where TNF‐α is consistently produced and inflammation continues in the tumor microenvironment, it can function as a tumor promoter [11, 12]. This is achieved by causing deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage, enhancing pro‐angiogenic functions, elevating the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) and endothelial adhesion molecules, and inducing a favorable environment for the growth‐promoting hormone. The TNF‐α gene is found in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) locus on chromosome 6p21 and consists of several single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). The G to A transition at position −238 (rs361525) in the promoter region of TNF‐α is widely recognized for its influence on gene expression [13, 14]. Given the significant role of TNF‐α in carcinogenesis, the common functional polymorphism, rs361525 in TNF‐α, has been extensively investigated in numerous studies; however, the findings have been contradictory across various populations, particularly within the Asian demographic [15, 16, 17]. Therefore, investigations have documented the involvement of such TNF‐α genetic polymorphisms in a range of diseases, notably BC and CC [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

Although previous studies have assessed the association between the TNF‐α gene rs361525 polymorphism and vulnerability with BC and CC, the findings were inconclusive. Furthermore, there has been no research conducted on BC and CC patients in Bangladesh to assess the correlation with the rs361525 polymorphism. Hence, we performed the present case‐control study with meta‐analysis to investigate the association between the common SNP in the TNF‐α (rs361525) gene and the susceptibility with BC and CC.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

Ethical permission for this research was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Asia Pacific, Dhaka, Bangladesh (Ref: UAP/REC/2023/103). The study's aims were conveyed to the participants, and written consent was taken from each individual. This study was performed in accordance with the principle of conducting research on human subjects according to the Helsinki Declaration.

Study Population

For this study, we recruited two groups of patients: one group consisting of 155 patients with BC, and another group consisting of 157 patients with CC. The patients were selected from the National Institute of Cancer Research and Hospital (NICRH) in Mohakhali, Dhaka, Bangladesh. For the control arm, we enrolled 150 healthy individuals who visited the NICRH at the same time or outside the hospital. We ensured that the volunteers were of similar age range and sex to the patients. Consent was obtained from all participants after we provided them with relevant information. It was verified that all the volunteers were legally adults, meaning they were over 18 years old, and they were also genetically unrelated to each other. The demographic parameters of both cases and controls were carefully controlled to minimize heterogeneity. We used a standard questionnaire to gather comprehensive information about the patients, encompassing their sociodemographic characteristics, clinicopathological background, and present health status. The controls' sociodemographic characteristics were also documented. Ensuring the absence of comorbidities such as pulmonary, renal, and hepatic disorders in all patients. The information about the recruited control population did not reveal any evidence of a personal history of cancer or other malignant illnesses. This study was conducted in the Biotechnology Laboratory, located at the Department of Pharmacy, University of Asia Pacific, Dhaka, Bangladesh. For this case‐control study, blood samples from all subjects were collected between October 2023 and March 2024.

Blood Sample Collection and DNA Isolation

In this case‐control study, 5 mL of venous blood samples were collected from each participant using a 5 mL syringe. The blood was taken from either the median vein or cephalic vein in the arm, following the blood‐drawing method recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO). The collected blood was then transferred into a sterile tube that already contained EDTA‐Na2. The extraction of genomic DNA was performed using a commercially available DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China). The DNA extraction process was conducted according to the instructions supplied in the DNA extraction kit. DNA purity and concentration of extracted DNA samples were determined by a micro‐volume spectrophotometer (Optima, Japan) and setting the absorbance ratio at 260 and 280 nm.

Primer Design and Genotyping

The polymerase chain reaction‐restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR‐RFLP) assay was employed to complete the genotyping of the rs361525 SNP. Table 1 summarizes the primer sequences, their corresponding PCR thermal conditions, and digested fragment lengths. The online software Primer Blast was used to design the primer sequences (both forward and reverse). The PCR primers were generated based on the TNF‐α gene reference sequence in GenBank (accession no. NG007462). The PCR (Thermal Cycler, ESCO, USA) was conducted using Emerald Amp GT PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan), with validation achieved using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis after completion. The PCR reaction comprised 5 μL of PCR master mix, 2.5 μL of DNA, 0.2 μL of forward primer, 0.2 μL of reverse primer, and 2.6 μL of ddH₂O. Following PCR, the PCR products were subjected to digestion with the BamHI (Takara Bio, Japan) restriction enzyme at 37°C overnight, including 8 μL of PCR‐amplified product, 1 μL of 10× buffer, 9 μL of nuclease‐free water, and 1 μL of enzyme. The enzyme cleaved the G allele of the TNF‐α gene rs361525, yielding two fragments of 130 bp and 25 bp in the case of the normal homozygote (GG). In the heterozygote (GA), fragments of 25, 130, and 155 bp were identified, but the A allele for the mutant homozygote (AA) was observed exclusively at 155 bp (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). The gel electrophoresis (2.5% agarose) was conducted to verify the existence of allele‐specific DNA bands, observed using ethidium bromide staining, utilizing a 20 bp DNA ladder for precise fragment measurement.

Meta‐Analysis

Data Search Strategy

This study adhered to the PRISMA 2020 standards (Figure 1). Information was obtained from numerous web sources based on pre‐established search parameters [24]. Data were retrieved from various online databases, such as Google Scholar, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE, using predetermined search terms: “TNF‐alfa/tumor necrosis factor‐alfa”, “rs361525”, “single nucleotide polymorphism/SNP/polymorphisms/variants”, “TNF‐alpha rs361525”, “breast cancer”, and “breast cancer and TNF‐α rs361525”. Only English‐language articles that reported the correlation between TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism and the prognosis of BC were selected after a systematic search. The citations of the chosen papers were carefully examined to discover any more pertinent articles that may have been overlooked.

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection.

Eligibility Criteria for Meta‐Analysis

The eligibility requirements for the current meta‐analysis were determined based on precise inclusion and exclusion criteria. The articles were selected based on the following criteria: The TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism has been associated with BC. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) have been calculated for specific genetic association models. These calculations were based on studies using human samples, specifically case‐control‐based articles. On the other hand, the subsequent criteria were employed to ascertain which studies should be excluded: The reviews, comments, or letters to the editors did not include enough genotyping data for calculating the OR, and the research was conducted using animal samples.

Data Extraction

The data were gathered in a prearranged MS Excel spreadsheet following their extraction from the selected articles. All writers utilized this Excel document to impartially extract relevant data. A group discussion was employed to address any irregularities in data collection. When collecting data, we considered several important criteria of the included publications, including the first author's name, the year of publication, the research population, sample size, ethnicity, genetic model, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE), and the OR with 95% CI. The methodology involved examining the selection criteria, comparability, and study results.

Statistical Analysis

The sociodemographic and clinicopathological characteristics were presented as percentages. The genotypes and allele frequencies were assessed to determine the deviation from the HWE using the χ 2 statistic. The association between the TNF‐α gene rs361525 polymorphism and BC and CC was assessed using logistic regression using the OR with 95% CI. A statistically significant risk was determined based on a p value of < 0.05. The statistical calculations were performed using the most recent version [25] of the SPSS software developed by IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA. We employed Review Manager 5.3 (RevMan 5.3, The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and the MetaGenyo web tool to conduct the meta‐analysis and evaluate heterogeneity among the included studies. The association of TNF‐α polymorphism (rs361525) has been examined in seven genetic models. The heterogeneity of the research was evaluated using the I² statistic. If the level of heterogeneity was below 50%, a fixed effects model was employed. If the heterogeneity value surpassed 50%, a random effects model was used. The Egger's test was employed to detect publication bias [25]. The statistical significance was determined to be p < 0.05. Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis by sequentially eliminating each study to evaluate the stability of the overall analysis.

Association Between TNF‐α Gene rs361525 Variant With BC

Table 5 illustrates the genotypic and allelic frequencies, as well as the association of different genetic models of the TNF‐α gene rs361525 polymorphism with BC risk. The analysis of genotypes and allelic frequencies of the TNF‐α gene rs361525 polymorphism in patients indicated that 72.90% exhibited the GG common homozygote genotype, 21.94% presented the GA heterozygote genotype, and 5.16% displayed the AA mutant homozygote genotype. In contrast, 54% of controls carried the GG common homozygote genotype, while the heterozygote and mutant homozygote frequencies were 38.67% and 7.33%, respectively. The genotype distribution across patients was inconsistent with HWE (p value < 0.05) (χ 2 = 5.55, p value = 0.018), with a minor allele frequency of 16.13%. In the controls, the genotype distribution adhered to HWE (p value > 0.05) (χ 2 = 0.019, p value = 0.889), with a minor allele frequency of 26.67%. Analysis revealed that for TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphisms, all genetic inheritance models, apart from additive model 2 and the recessive model, exhibited a significant association between this gene and BC risk in Bangladeshi populations. Moreover, the strong protective effect of this gene in terms of BC risk was observed across all genetic models, including the additive model 1 (GA vs. GG: OR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.25–0.70, p = 0.0009), the dominant model (GA + AA vs. GG: OR = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.31–0.83, p = 0.007), the over‐dominant model (GA vs. GG + AA: OR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.27–0.74, p = 0.001), and the allelic model (A vs. G: OR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.36–0.79, p = 0.001). Though a protective effect was also found in both additive model 2 and the recessive model, this link lacked statistical significance (AA vs. GG: OR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.20–1.35, p = 0.181; AA vs. GG + AA: OR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.27–1.76, p = 0.435).

Association Between TNF‐α Gene rs361525 Variant With CC

Table 6 delineates the genotype and allele frequencies, as well as the correlation of different genetic models of the TNF‐α gene rs361525 polymorphism with CC risk in the Bangladeshi population. Analysis of genotypes and allelic frequencies of the TNF‐α gene rs361525 polymorphism in studies indicated that 60.51% of patients presented the GG common homozygote genotype, 31.21% exhibited the GA heterozygote genotype, and 8.28% showed the AA mutant homozygote genotype. The genotype distributions of CC patients adhered to HWE, with a p value of 0.076 (p value > 0.05) in the cases (χ 2 = 3.15, p value = 0.076), and the frequency of the minor allele was 23.89%. The analysis revealed that while both the stimulant and protective nature of the TNF‐α rs361525 variant in CC risk were observed across various genetic inheritance models, no significant genotypic associations were identified in any model, including the additive model 1 (GA vs. GG: OR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.44–1.17, p = 0.182), additive model 2 (AA vs. GG: OR = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.43–2.37, p = 0.986), dominant model (GA + AA vs. GG: OR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.49–1.21, p = 0.249), recessive model (AA vs. GG + GA: OR = 1.14, 95% CI = 0.49–2.63, p = 0.758), over‐dominant model (GA vs. GG + AA: OR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.45–1.15, p = 0.171), and allelic model (A vs. G: OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.60–1.24, p = 0.428).

Comparative Analysis of Genotypes and Risk Association Between BC and CC

The genotype frequencies of TNF‐α rs361525 and their comparison between BC and CC patients are presented in Figure 2. The prevalence of the GG homozygote (GG = 113) is higher in BC patients compared to CC patients (GG = 95). The distribution of GA heterozygotes and AA mutant homozygotes indicated that the frequencies of GA and AA genotypes are elevated in CC patients (GA = 34 vs. 49 and AA = 8 vs. 13) compared to BC patients. Furthermore, ORs for assessing the risk association of TNF‐α rs361525 between BC and CC patients (Tables 5 and 6) indicated that the ORs were elevated for two genetic association models of CC—additive model 2 and the recessive model—when compared to BC (1.01 vs. 0.52 and 1.14 vs. 0.69, respectively), although these associations were not statistically significant. While other genetic models in BC exhibited a more substantial connection than in CC, they, however, reduced the risk of BC, including the additive model 1, dominant model, overdominant model, and allele model. This analysis suggested a protective function of the TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism in BC and CC risk.

Heterogeneity and Publication Bias

Tables 9 and 10 represent the results of the heterogeneity test. The present investigation found significant heterogeneity in four genetic models: COD1 (I 2 = 69.18%, p < 0.0001), DM (I 2 = 72.95%, p < 0.0001), ODM (I 2 = 65.78%, p = 0.0005), and AM (I 2 = 74.21%, p < 0.0001) in the overall population. In the Asian population, significant heterogeneity was observed in the genetic models of DM (I 2 = 78.13%, p < 0.0001), ODM (I 2 = 73.27%, p = 0.0002), and AM (I 2 = 78.42%, p< 0.0001). Nevertheless, there was no significant heterogeneity observed in any genetic models among the Caucasian population (COD1: I² = 0.00, p= 0.764; COD3: I² = 11.02%, p= 0.3431; DM: I² = 8.91%, p= 0.3486; RM: I² = 49.42%, p= 0.115; ODM: I² = 0.00, p= 0.9526; AM: I² = 51.73%, p= 0.1016), except for COD2 (I² = 55%, p= 0.0833). To demonstrate the presence of publication bias, we employed Egger's test, as indicated in Tables 9 and 10. The current investigation did not find any genetic model that exhibited a significant bias (Figure 5).

Sensitivity analysis by removing each study one by one of the meta‐analysis of TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism on BC risk in different genetic models.

Sensitivity Analysis

In addition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in all seven genetic models (Figure 5). The sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically omitting each study one by one to evaluate the impact of each study's results on the overall OR. However, the ORs did not show any significant changes based on the findings of the current investigation. This evidence indicates that the individual studies did not have a substantial impact on the entire analysis, suggesting that the meta‐analysis outcomes are statistically robust and stable.

Discussion

Globally, BC and CC are the most frequently diagnosed carcinomas in females [4, 33]. Genes associated with cytokines, which play a role in inflammation and metabolism, have been the focus of particular interest in the field of cancer genetics [34]. The relationship between cytokine genes and BC is extensively researched, with particular focus on the correlation of TNF‐α polymorphisms [35]. Several studies have been carried out to investigate the involvement of genetic variations in cytokine genes, such as TNF‐α and TNF‐β, in BC patients [26]. Moreover, TNF‐α can induce the growth of cervical epithelial cells that are immortalized by the human papillomavirus (HPV) and derived from carcinomas in a laboratory setting [36]. Prior research has additionally documented that CC cell lines or tissues have elevated levels of TNF‐α production [37]. Hence, it is plausible that the expression level of TNF‐α could have a role in the development of both BC and CC.

Furthermore, prior research has demonstrated a significant correlation between the rs361525 variation in the TNF‐α gene and other types of cancers in diverse ethnic groups, including BC and CC [13, 38]. Based on prior research findings, we conducted a case‐control study with meta‐analysis to investigate the association between the TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphism and the susceptibility to BC and CC.

All genetic inheritance models had a protective role, and no statistically significant correlation was observed between the rs361525 variant and BC risk, except for additive model 2 and the recessive model, which revealed a significant link with BC (Table 5). The present study aligns with previous findings on the TNF‐α gene rs361525 (G > A) polymorphism and BC, suggesting that there is no substantial correlation in North European and Northeast Chinese Han populations [13, 32]. Again, a prior Chinese study indicated that the GA and AA genotypes substantially reduced BC risk [19]. In contrast to our study, Pooja et al. and Aznag et al. have documented a positive link between TNF‐α rs361525 polymorphisms and BC in the populations of South India and Morocco, respectively [16, 30]. Nevertheless, the absence of a linkage between TNF‐α polymorphisms and BC in this study could be due to the low occurrence of TNF‐α –238 A alleles, which are frequently seen among Indians and other Asian populations [39].

Furthermore, the results of our present study indicate that there is no statistically significant association between the TNF‐α gene rs361525 variant and CC. Even though we found a stimulant and protective role of the rs361525 polymorphism regarding CC risk in patients, no statistically significant link was identified in any genetic inheritance models (Table 6). Furthermore, Zuo et al. and Barbisan et al. also have similarly observed no correlation between the TNF‐α –238 G > A polymorphism and CC in the Southwest region of China and in Argentina populations, respectively [40, 41]. In a previous study, Chinchai et al. also found no link between TNF‐α polymorphisms and CC in Thai women [42]. Conversely, prior studies identified the A allele of rs361525 as a risk factor for squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), suggesting that the A allele plays distinct roles in the various kinds of CC [20, 43]. SCC is the predominant pathological type of CC, comprising roughly 75%–80%, followed by adenocarcinoma (AC), which constitutes around 10%–25% [44]. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the significance of rs361525 in various pathological kinds of CC in the future. Moreover, the rs361525 (−238) locus is situated on a 25‐base pair repressor gene, and its SNP has been found to link with protection against cancer [32].

Also, we have performed a meta‐analysis on the rs361525 polymorphism of the TNF‐α gene, using a sample size of 37,175 BC cases and 39,263 controls. Nevertheless, we did not discover any significant correlation between rs361525 and BC in any of the genetic models among the overall population (Table 9). Our study aligns with previous meta‐analysis findings, which indicated that there is no association between the TNF‐α rs361525 (−238G > A) polymorphism and the risk of BC in the general population or when the analysis was divided based on ethnicity [21]. In the prior meta‐analysis conducted by Liu et al., six case‐control studies on TNF‐α rs361525 (−238A) were analyzed. The meta‐analysis results also showed that the rs361525 (−238A) allele was linked to a reduced risk of CC [37]. Likewise, previous research indicated that the prevalence of the rare TNF‐α rs361525 (−238A) allele was substantially reduced in cancer patients compared to controls within the Asian population [45, 46]. Furthermore, in human T and B cells, the transcription of the TNF‐α gene was significantly reduced by the TNF‐α (−238A) allele, as reported by Kaluza et al. [47].

The present study assessed the ethnic population‐based heterogeneity of the included studies across all genetic models. Significant heterogeneity was identified among the different genetic models, including the co‐dominant model 1, dominant model, over‐dominant model, and allelic model (Table 9). All genetic models, with the exception of the COD2, COD3, and recessive model, exhibited significant variation within the Asian population. Nevertheless, only COD2 among the Caucasian group exhibited signs of heterogeneity. In cases of significant heterogeneity, we choose the random‐effects model. Additionally, we have identified no significant publication bias in any genetic models (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis confirmed the consistency, accuracy, and stability of the outcomes of the present analysis (Figure 5).

It should be noted that the present study has certain limitations. Firstly, the study includes a relatively small number of individuals. In addition, it was not feasible to gather full sociodemographic and clinicopathological information from the subjects, which could potentially affect the association. Furthermore, for this study, we have exclusively chosen the accessible single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the publicly accessible electronic database. Ultimately, the demographic factors were inadequate in accurately depicting the real‐world circumstances of cancer patients and their correlation with the genotype distribution of the specific gene. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct larger studies with a greater sample size and more detailed environmental exposure data in order to verify the prognostic and therapeutic significance of this research. Moreover, due to the insignificant relationship of this polymorphism with CC in the Asian population, we refrained from performing a meta‐analysis for that association.

Conclusion

The present study concludes that the rs361525 polymorphism in the TNF‐α gene is not associated with BC and CC risk in Bangladeshi women. However, we recommend conducting future studies with a larger sample size and advanced genotyping technology to obtain comprehensive and accurate results.
