Investigating the impact of discretization techniques on real-time digital control of DC-DC boost converters: A comprehensive analysis
Authors: Cağfer Yanarateş, Zhongfu Zhou, Aytaç Altan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper provides a thorough comparative analysis of discretization techniques commonly utilized in digital control applications for DC-DC boost converters (DBCs). Given the crucial role of DC-DC converters in various industries such as renewable energy systems, electric vehicles, and portable electronic devices, it is imperative to optimize their performance through efficient digital control. This study aims to improve the understanding of the effects of discretization methods on control system behaviour. It investigates their impact on the performance, stability and accuracy of control algorithms in the context of DBC, using dSPACE real-time interface (RTI) and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation for validation. This approach replicates real-world conditions, allowing performance to be evaluated in a controlled environment. The analysis examines behaviour in the time and frequency domains, providing insight into the strengths and weaknesses of each method. Experimental validation using MATLAB/Simulink/Stateflow on dSPACE RTI 1007 processor, DS2004 high-speed A/D and CP4002 timing and digital I/O boards ensures that the comparative analysis provides practical benefits for DBC digital control applications.

Introduction

In modern engineering, control systems have undergone a significant evolution, particularly in the domain of non-linear systems, exemplified by applications such as switch mode power supplies (SMPS) and DC-DC boost converters (DBCs) [1]. This transformation has given rise to sophisticated tools and methodologies that are critical for enhancing control accuracy [2]. Non-linear systems, characterized by intricate input-output relationships, present both challenges and opportunities for control engineers [3]. In DBCs, inherent non-linearities arise from factors such as varying input voltage, output load conditions, and duty cycle interactions, rendering traditional control paradigms inadequate [4].

Within this dynamic landscape, real-time interfaces (RTIs) have emerged as indispensable conduits, seamlessly bridging the digital and physical domains [5]. RTI systems allow complex nonlinear algorithms to interact with the tangible behaviour of power electronics in both SMPS and DBCs [6,7], empowering engineers to implement sophisticated control strategies with precision and agility.

Complementing RTI, hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) systems have become invaluable for navigating the complexities of non-linear systems within power electronics [8]. HIL systems facilitate the integration of physical components with virtual simulations, creating an ecosystem where real hardware interacts with algorithmic models [9], enabling exhaustive testing and refinement of control strategies even in scenarios that push the boundaries of non-linearity [10].

In this intricate landscape, digital control methodologies shine as beacons of innovation that address the intricate dynamics of nonlinear systems [11,12]. Harnessing computational power, these methodologies process vast amounts of data and implement complex algorithms, adding finesse and adaptability to control strategies [13]. The precision of digital control enables engineers to optimize the efficiency, stability and overall performance of systems operating within the non-linear envelope of SMPS and DBCs [14,15].

Central to the success of digital control in this context are discretization techniques, which skillfully transform the continuous intricacies of non-linear systems into a format that can be effectively managed by computational tools [16]. Techniques such as sampling and quantization convert the analogue behaviour of power electronics into digital counterparts [11,17], allowing non-linear processes to be fully understood, manipulated and optimized in the digital domain [18,19].

It is imperative to address the influence of a right half-plane zero (RHPZ) in the transfer function of boost converters [20]. This RHPZ introduces non-minimum phase characteristics that affect both time and frequency domain analyses. In the time domain, the RHPZ can cause initial output overshoot and oscillatory behaviour [21]. In the frequency domain, it can cause instability, limiting the achievable bandwidth of the control loop and affecting the transient response of the system [22]. Discretization techniques play a crucial role not only in ensuring accurate system modelling in digital control systems but also in enabling the development of sophisticated control strategies such as model predictive control (MPC) and active disturbance rejection control (ADRC) for DBC applications [23,24]. These advanced control methods rely heavily on real-time computation and prediction of system behaviour, which is sensitive to the choice of discretization technique. For example, MPC requires accurate system state updates at discrete time intervals to make optimal control decisions, while ADRC depends on accurate real-time disturbance estimation. In RTI systems, discretization-induced delays or inaccuracies can degrade control performance, so it is important to select appropriate methods such as first-order hold (FOH), zero-order hold (ZOH), bilinear approximation (Tustin's method), impulse response matching (IRM), and matched pole-zero (MPZ). A thorough understanding of these techniques is essential for achieving stability, especially in systems dealing with RHPZ and HIL simulations.

In conclusion, this study embarks on a comprehensive exploration of nonlinear control in power electronics. The aim is to identify the temporal and spectral responses inherent in non-linear dynamic operation, to account for system latencies, and to evaluate the effectiveness of various discretization strategies. The incorporation of empirical data through systematic experimentation enhances the robustness and credibility of the study's findings and increases its analytical depth and scope. This introduction serves as a prelude to a thorough exploration of the evolving frontiers of nonlinear control, where complexity and innovation intersect in the quest for improved power electronic systems.

The main contributions of this work are as follows: This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of the above-mentioned discretization techniques and their impact on the real-time control of a DBC. Key parameters such as discretization sampling time, fundamental sampling time, and operational delay are thoroughly investigated. An operational delay of 0.01s introduced by a rate transition block resulted in steady-state times between 100 ms and 146 ms. Fundamental sampling times ranging from 2.5×10−5 s to 2.5×10−2 s yielded transient response times between 50 ms and 100 ms, with unstable waveforms at higher values. Additionally, a 10 V step change in the reference voltage produced transient responses that varied by 18 %–100 %, with oscillations, overshoot, and waveform degradation of varying magnitude across all methods. These findings provide practical guidelines for optimizing discretization techniques in RTI systems to improve stability and performance.

Components of the DBC and transfer function estimation

The power stage is a pivotal element within a DC-DC asynchronous boost converter, essential for elevating a lower DC input voltage (Vin) to a higher DC output voltage (Vout). This switching topology utilizes a configuration where a diode acts as the upper switch, working in tandem with a lower switch typically implemented through a MOSFET or IGBT. The key components and operational dynamics of the power stage in DC-DC asynchronous boost converters encompass the input voltage, inductor, diode (upper switch), output capacitor, continuous conduction mode (CCM), output voltage, switching operation, and control signal.

Fig. 1(a), (b), and (c) provide a comprehensive illustration of an asynchronous boost converter, offering a block diagram, a differential equation model, and CCM waveforms, respectively. (a) Block diagram (b) differential equation model (c) CCM waveforms of a DBC.

While this study focuses primarily on CCM for a DBC, it is important to note that discontinuous conduction mode (DCM) is also a common operating mode in practice. CCM is often preferred in applications where high efficiency, better output voltage regulation and reduced output ripple are required, making it a more common choice in many industrial applications. The stability and performance characteristics of CCM under varying load conditions and its ability to handle higher output currents provide significant advantages over DCM. However, DCM can be advantageous in lower load scenarios where it can lead to improved efficiency and reduced component stress. By focusing on CCM, this study aims to highlight the specific challenges and optimizations relevant to this mode, while acknowledging that DCM remains an important aspect of DBC operation worthy of further exploration in future work.

Changes in component values, such as inductance (L), diode characteristics, and capacitance (C), have a significant effect on the pole and zero locations as well as the time constants in DC-DC converters. Precise adjustment of the control signals allows these parameters to be deliberately manipulated to achieve the desired transient response and ensure system stability. It is also important to consider the influence of parasitic elements and snubber circuits, as they introduce additional dynamics into the system that must be taken into account for a full understanding of the converter's behaviour.

Analyzing time delays in digital control systems

In dynamic systems, inherent time delays are prevalent and require effective consideration in controller design. These delays can vary widely, from negligible intervals to substantial durations that may impact system performance or stability. They fall into two main categories: distorting delays, which alter the output signal, and non-distorting delays, which introduce temporal shifts without altering the signal's fundamental properties. Distinguishing between these delay types is crucial when crafting tailored control strategies. Fig. 2 illustrates the unity feedback configuration of the asynchronous boost converter, which includes both distorting and non-distorting delays.Fig. 2The unity feedback configuration of the asynchronous DBC with delays.Fig. 2

The unity feedback configuration of the asynchronous DBC with delays.

Time delays significantly impact the digital control of asynchronous DC-DC boost converters, affecting performance and stability. These delays, originating from computation and communication, introduce temporal lags that can disrupt output regulation and transient responses, potentially leading to operational deviations. Addressing delays with advanced compensation strategies like predictive control or adaptive techniques is crucial for maintaining efficiency and stability under varying conditions in converter control algorithms.

Time delay has a significant impact on system performance by introducing phase lag, which affects the stability and transient response of the control system. Specifically, time delay can reduce the phase margin of the control loop, potentially leading to undesirable oscillations and increased settling time. For example, a time delay of 0.01s can reduce the phase margin by approximately 30°, resulting in a transient response characterized by overshoot and longer settling times. Increasing the time delay can also exacerbate the instability, as observed in simulations where delays greater than 0.02s resulted in sustained oscillations with amplitude variations of up to 20 % of the desired output voltage. By examining different time delay scenarios, the specific quantification of these effects through simulation and experimental results is presented in the following sections. Furthermore, the effect of time delay on controller design parameters such as gain and bandwidth is analyzed to provide insight into the necessary adjustments required to maintain system stability. This comprehensive analysis enhances the understanding of the effects of time delay in real-time control applications and provides valuable insights for optimizing system performance.

In digital control systems, controllers operate with discrete-time measurements, which can result in information loss and bandwidth reduction. The selection of the appropriate discretization technique depends on the specific application requirements, as summarized in Table 4. The FOH is ideal for systems where a balance between accuracy and efficiency is required, especially for moderate dynamics. The ZOH is suitable for slower systems where simplicity and consistent performance are essential. For precise control over a range of frequencies, the Tustin method is recommended, but requires tuning. The IRM, despite its complexity, is effective for achieving specific transient characteristics. Finally, the MPZ techniques are best for systems that need to retain original dynamics, with careful pole and zero selection being crucial.

Simulation and experimental results

To improve both step and frequency response characteristics, deliberate adjustments are made to increase the settling time. The aim is to improve system performance in terms of both step and frequency response by deliberately increasing the settling time. This strategic increase in settling time is critical to improving not only the step response but also the frequency response characteristics of the system. By extending the settling time, the system gains the ability to reach and stabilize at the desired state more gradually. This in turn improves the system's response to transient disturbances and dynamic input signals [25]. In order to mitigate the undershoot phenomenon caused by the presence of the RHPZ, it is essential to choose the electrical load impedance wisely. An electrical load with a nominal impedance of 30 Ω has been deliberately chosen. This choice stems from the realisation that reducing the load impedance below this threshold carries the inherent risk of exacerbating the undershoot, possibly at the expense of an increased overshoot.

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that specific impedance values have been analyzed to quantify the trade-off between undershoot and overshoot. For instance, when the load impedance is set at 20 Ω, the system exhibits an undershoot of 1.5854 % and an overshoot of 72.6655 %. Conversely, with a load impedance of 10 Ω, the undershoot increases to 7.6557 %, while the overshoot is reduced to 54.1454 %. These empirical observations emphasize the delicate balance required in choosing the load impedance to achieve the desired response characteristics while minimizing both undershoot and overshoot.

Conclusion

This paper presents a comprehensive comparative analysis of discretization techniques commonly used in digital control applications for DBCs. The significance of DBCs across industries, including renewable energy systems, electric vehicles, and portable electronic devices, emphasizes the crucial role of efficient digital control in optimizing performance and ensuring reliable power conversion. Through a meticulous examination and comparison of discretization methods, such as FOH, ZOH, Tustin, IRM, and MPZ techniques, this study aims to enhance understanding of their impact on control system behavior. The effects of these techniques on control algorithm performance, stability, and accuracy within the context of DBC are thoroughly scrutinized, exploring both time and frequency domain behaviors to provide insights into the strengths and weaknesses of each method. Moreover, the performance of the proposed system under various discretization techniques is experimentally validated. This validation is performed by implementing MATLAB/Simulink/Stateflow on the dSPACE RTI 1007 processor, DS2004 high-speed A/D, and CP4002 timing and digital I/O boards. The results of this experimental validation confirm the practical benefits derived from the comparative analysis of DBC for digital control applications, thereby contributing to the advancement of digital control methods in this field.
