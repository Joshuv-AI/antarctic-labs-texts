Development and psychometric validation of the Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire for Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults
Authors: Abanish Singh, Lisa Jamieson, Gina Guzzo, Pedro Henrique Ribeiro Santiago, Johanna Groundwater, Joanne Hedges, Ria Aiyar, Kostas Kapellas, Sonia Nath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe aim of this study was to develop and validate a culturally adapted Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire (PIDAQ) for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults.MethodThe short-form PIDAQ was culturally adapted and administered to 187 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults. Network psychometric analyses examined dimensionality, item redundancy, network loadings, and model fit. Additionally, internal consistency (McDonald’s Ω), construct validity, and criterion validity were also measured.Results Two statistically redundant but conceptually distinct item pairs (Look/Nice and Proud/Show) were retained on cultural and content-validity grounds. The “Worry” item was removed for a low network loading (0.169), yielding a final seven-item PIDAQ. Model fit was good (χ2(4) = 6.26, p < 0.180; CFI = 1.000; RMSEA = 0.059, 90% CI: 0.000–0.140). Internal consistency was excellent (Ω=0.94), inter-item correlations were moderate to adequate (ρ= 0.520–0.790), and the correlation with the SEWB scale provided directionally consistent support for criterion validity (ρ=0.320). Participants reported mixed feelings about dental aesthetics, often not minding showing their teeth in photographs yet being dissatisfied with how their teeth look.ConclusionsThe PIDAQ demonstrated strong psychometric properties, making it a valid, reliable tool for assessing the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics.KEYWORDS: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, dental aesthetics, malocclusion, psychometrics, quality of life

Introduction

Dental aesthetics play an important role in shaping self-esteem, psychological well-being, and social interactions, with the appearance of teeth influencing perceptions across many aspects of daily life (Venete et al., 2017). Dentofacial aesthetic ideals are deeply influenced by cultural, social, and historical norms, with each community holding distinctive perspectives on what constitutes an attractive smile and harmonious facial features (Islam et al., 2022; L. Jamieson et al., 2023). For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples (hereafter respectfully referred to as Indigenous Australians), dental appearance is deeply tied to cultural identity, dignity, and social and emotional wellbeing. It is not only a clinical outcome but also a factor influencing oral health-related quality of life (Amarasena et al., 2015; Butten et al., 2019). The consequences of visible dental irregularities can be compounded by social stigma and a history of structural disadvantage, increasing vulnerability to negative judgements from others, bullying, racism, and internalised feelings of shame or embarrassment (Islam et al., 2022; L. Jamieson et al., 2023). As the concept of health has broadened to include physical, mental, and psychosocial wellbeing, aesthetic dental care has become a key part of holistic health paradigms (Aiyar et al., 2026; L. M. Jamieson et al., 2011).

Malocclusion, the misalignment of teeth and jaws, ranks as the third most common reason for dental care, after dental caries and periodontal disease, and is a significant contributor to dental aesthetic concerns (Lombardo et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2006). It encompasses a wide range of conditions, including crowding, spacing, overbites, underbites, crossbites, and other deviations from an ideal occlusion (Zhang et al., 2006), with a worldwide prevalence of 39% to 93% (Cenzato et al., 2021; Lombardo et al., 2020). In Australia, data from the National Child Oral Health Study indicate that higher-severity malocclusions are more common among males, Indigenous children, and children from lower-income households. Those with handicapping malocclusions are more likely to receive orthodontic care (46.6%), and the treatment was provided mainly by private orthodontists (Nguyen et al., 2024). While some malocclusions are mild and asymptomatic, in more severe cases it can impair chewing, speech, and oral hygiene, and contribute to tooth wear, temporomandibular joint discomfort, and an elevated risk of dental disease (Zhang et al., 2006; Zou et al., 2018). A range of indices is used to assess malocclusion and treatment need, including the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) (Brook & Shaw, 1989), the Dental Aesthetic Index (DAI) (Bellot-Arcís et al., 2012), and patient-reported measures such as the Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire (PIDAQ) (Klages et al., 2006).

The PIDAQ is an instrument for measuring psychosocial effects and dental appearance, capturing the aesthetic and psychosocial dimensions of malocclusion beyond clinical severity. The subscales consist of dental self-confidence, social impact, psychological impact, and aesthetic concerns, comprising 23 items on a five-point Likert scale (Klages et al., 2006). To reduce respondent burden and improve the feasibility of the application, shortened versions of the PIDAQ have been developed across countries, comprising eight similar items that retain robust psychometric reliability in a condensed format (Alsanabani et al., 2022; Wan Hassan et al., 2022). The PIDAQ has been validated across various cultural contexts, including European (Campos et al., 2021; Göranson et al., 2021), Asian (Faraj et al., 2023), South American (Sardenberg et al., 2010), and Australian populations (Wahab et al., 2022). However, most of these assessment tools have been developed and validated mainly in non-Indigenous populations and may not fully reflect the clinical and psychosocial complexities of dental appearance and its impact on quality of life and remain under-researched for Indigenous Australian communities. Therefore, the aim of this study is to develop and psychometrically validate a culturally adapted shorter version of the PIDAQ for Indigenous Australian adults.

Methods

Study design

The data for the validation study were part of an interventional study (registration number ACTRN12626000046303), with baseline data collected from July 2022 to December 2023 (Nath et al., 2025), followed by a 12-month follow-up from March 2024 to March 2025 (Nath, Groundwater, et al., 2026). The data used in this study are from the 12-month follow-up. Between baseline and follow‑up, participants were referred to private or public clinics; treatment costs were covered by the project, with appointment booking and transport organised on request (Aiyar et al., 2025). The referrals were for general dental care based on clinical need assessed at baseline; the nature and extent of treatment received varied across participants. The goal of the intervention was to reduce oral bacterial load and achieve dental fitness through clinically indicated preventive and restorative care; orthodontic treatments were outside the scope of the intervention. The PIDAQ was administered cross-sectionally at the 12-month follow-up to all consenting participants regardless of their individual treatment history.

Study participants

This research was conducted in the Greater Adelaide Region, Port Augusta, Murray Bridge, and Hawker. Ethical approval for this study was obtained. All participants provided written and verbal informed consent prior to their participation. Recruitment began with convenience sampling through Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHOs), community centres, and Aboriginal organisations, followed by snowball sampling, in which enrolled participants referred family members or friends to the study. Participants were eligible if they identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander, were aged 18 years or older, and were residing in South Australia for the duration of the study. Participants were not required to have received or be receiving orthodontic treatment to be eligible. Individuals were excluded if they were unable to complete the questionnaire due to cognitive impairment, severe illness, or language barriers that could not be addressed with available support. Participants were assessed at home, in local community clinics, ACCHOs or at Aboriginal organisations, or, when preferred, at the clinical research facility or the dental clinic. The project information was shared with participants using the yarning method, with Indigenous Health Workers facilitating discussions to ensure clarity and cultural safety. Yarning is a culturally grounded, conversational method of knowledge exchange facilitated by Indigenous Health Workers, providing a culturally safe space for participants and community members to reflect on and discuss the meaning, clarity, and personal resonance of each item (Aiyar et al., 2025; Nath, Aiyar, et al., 2026).

Positionality statement

This research team acknowledges the importance of positionality in Indigenous oral health research. The team comprised Indigenous and non‑Indigenous researchers, including the last author, who worked in partnership with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community advisors, researchers, and the Indigenous Oral Health Unit Reference Group (IOHURG) (Nath, Aiyar, et al., 2026). Indigenous partners were consulted from the earliest stages of project conception and funding applications through to study design, recruitment strategies, questionnaire adaptation, and interpretation of findings, ensuring that community priorities shaped each phase of the research. Regular yarning sessions, reference group meetings, and feedback loops were used to refine the instrument and analytic approach, and protocols for cultural safety were embedded across data collection, analysis, and dissemination (Pham et al., 2025). The team recognises Indigenous communities as knowledge holders and is grateful for their leadership, guidance, and ongoing partnership throughout the project (Australian Institute of Aboriginal Torres Strait Islander Studies, 2020). The final questionnaire and its implications were discussed with community partners and IOHURG members, and findings were shared back with participating communities in accessible formats, consistent with the CONSIDER statement (Huria et al., 2019).

Development of a culturally adapted Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire

This study used the short eight-item version of the PIDAQ (Alsanabani et al., 2022) for cultural adaptation and psychometric validation. The culturally adapted short version was developed through an iterative, collaborative process, informed by consultations and guided by cultural safety principles (Nath, Groundwater, et al., 2026). The short version of PIDAQ included eight items, each mapped to four domains, with each domain consisting of two questions: Dental Self-Confidence (2 items), Social Impact (2 items), Psychological Impact (2 items), and Aesthetic Concern (2 items) (Table 1). The cultural appropriateness of the original PIDAQ items across the four established domains was evaluated.

An expert panel of Indigenous oral health researchers, IOHURG members, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community advisors was convened through a series of fortnightly structured focus group meetings between January and March 2024. This panel functioned as a formal content validity committee: each item was systematically evaluated for cultural relevance, linguistic appropriateness, and community acceptability, with consensus required before any item was finalised. Items were iteratively revised across multiple rounds until all panel members agreed that the wording was culturally safe and conceptually sound. In lieu of conventional interviewing, which can be culturally inappropriate, a yarning methodology was employed throughout the adaptation and data collection process. A pilot study was not conducted as a pre-validation phase; however, the multi-round expert panel review and yarning-based community consultations were conducted over three months.

All items were rephrased for cultural relevance and to foreground a strengths-based perspective, while preserving the scale’s original conceptual domains. Items in the “Dental Self‑Confidence” domain did not require modification and were retained in their original form. For the “Psychosocial Impact” domain, deficit-focused language such as “I wish my teeth looked better” was replaced with affirmative phrasing (“I like the way my teeth look”), and comparative statements were rephrased in positive terms, converting “Most people have nicer teeth than I do” to “I feel like I have nice teeth compared to others”. The “Social Impact” domain was rephrased to more empowering language, such as “When I talk, smile or laugh, I like to show my teeth”, and to diminish concern about external evaluation by affirming “I am not worried about what others think about my teeth”. Within the “Aesthetic Impact” domain, items were similarly revised to emphasise personal choice and cultural factors, including culturally relevant options like “I don’t mind showing my teeth in photos”. At the same time, it recognises that, according to community lore and cultural norms, some individuals may choose not to display their teeth in photos or public settings.

The decision to reformulate negatively worded items into positive, strength-based statements was deliberate and grounded in both ethical and cultural considerations. Deficit-focused language was identified by Indigenous community advisors and IOHURG members as potentially stigmatising, inconsistent with strength-based Indigenous health frameworks, and likely to compromise cultural safety and participant engagement. All reformulations were collaboratively developed through the expert panel process and confirmed to preserve the conceptual intent of each original item while reframing it in affirmative terms. Critically, no reverse coding was required in the final instrument, as all retained items were positively worded.

Data collection

All participants completed oral epidemiological examinations and self-report questionnaires in REDCap (Harris et al., 2009), independently or with assistance as preferred. The sociodemographic characteristics included age, gender, education (secondary vs high school), income source (job vs Centrelink income), ownership of a Healthcare card (HCC), and self-rated general and oral health. A Centrelink income refers to government payments administered through Services Australia, such as unemployment benefits. An HCC is an Australian government concession card that helps eligible people with low income or specific circumstances access subsidised health care. When concerns arose during the research, such as issues identified in psychosocial assessments, referrals were coordinated with social and emotional wellbeing services, mental health services, and counsellors, following a streamlined protocol to ensure appropriate, culturally sensitive follow-up and support.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2, 2024) using the R packages EGAnet (H. Golino & Christensen, 2022), qgraph (Epskamp et al., 2012), and lavaan (Rosseel, 2012). Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarise study participant characteristics, including self-rated general and oral health immediately after dental treatment. The analysis followed the steps: (1) item redundancy; (2) dimensionality; (3) model fit; (4) criterion validity; and (5) reliability.

In the first stage of analysis, the network model was estimated using a Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM), in which nodes represent the questionnaire items and edges represent the strength of partial correlations between them. Then the item redundancy was identified using weighted topographical overlap (wTO), a Unique Variable Analysis method. Taking the edge weights into account, wTO measures how similar two nodes are based on their shared connections. A cut-off value of 0.25 or higher is considered to determine redundancy. Based on wTO statistics, if the items were redundant, the construct and theoretical relevance of the item were determined to determine whether to combine or remove one of the items. In the next step, Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA) was used, which uses a community detection algorithm and partial correlation coefficients to estimate the edges in the GGM (H. F. Golino & Demetriou, 2017). 1,000 bootstrap samples were used to evaluate the stability of identified dimensions. This was employed in part to assess the replicability of the network structure, given the available sample size, as network psychometric methods generally benefit from larger samples to ensure precise edge-weight estimation. Item stability (proportion of times the items clustered within their EGA-identified dimension) was calculated. Furthermore, network loadings were also calculated. In network psychometric research, a network loading value of 0.20 or higher indicates a substantive network loading (Christensen & Golino, 2021).

The model fit was evaluated using the X2 statistic, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and Comparative Fit Index (CFI). The RMSEA ≤ 0.05 and CFI ≥ 0.95 indicate good approximate model fit (Kline, 2023; Santiago et al., 2021). Furthermore, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to compare the network and factor models in our study. Reliability was assessed using McDonald’s Ω, and a value ≥ 0.70 is considered adequate for research purposes. For criterion validity, the Spearman correlation coefficient between PIDAQ and social and emotional wellbeing (SEWB) was calculated. The SEWB was measured with a standardised tool named “Community Warriors” (Soares et al., 2025), and based on theoretical expectations, we expected that people with higher scores on the PIDAQ would also have higher scores on social and emotional wellbeing. To provide further evidence on structural validity, inter-item correlations were also calculated.

Psychometric analysis

In the redundancy analysis, two redundant pairs were identified (Look and Nice; Proud and Show) based on the wTO statistics (>0.25). After careful evaluation of the conceptual content and cultural significance of each item, it was indicated that both pairs captured meaningfully distinct constructs, warranting retention. Within the Look/Nice pair, the Look item (“I like the way my teeth look”) reflects aesthetic self-perception, while the Nice item (“I feel like I have nice teeth compared to others”) captures social comparison. Within the Proud/Show pair, the Proud item (“I am proud of my teeth”) reflects positive self-affirmation and cultural identity, whereas the Show item (“When I talk, smile or laugh I like to show my teeth”) captures behavioural expression in social settings. Both distinctions were affirmed by Indigenous community advisors and IOHURG members as culturally meaningful and non-interchangeable in the lived experience of this population. Accordingly, consistent with best practices in culturally adapted instrument development, in which content validity and community priorities must be balanced with psychometric criteria, none of the paired items were removed.

In the evaluation of dimensionality using EGA, a single dimension was identified, with only positive partial correlations among the items, indicating that all items were consistent in their evaluation of the PIDAQ (Supplementary Figure S1). Network loadings for the eight items are presented in Supplementary Table S1, indicating that all items demonstrated adequate network loadings except “Worry” (network loading: 0.169), which was below the conventional threshold. Hence, the item “Worry” was removed, resulting in a seven-item PIDAQ tool (Figure 2(A)). Furthermore, we considered construct-irrelevant item attributes of the original items which evaluate topics beyond the construct of interest, such as approval by members of the opposite sex. The original item “Worry” was structured as “I sometimes worry about what members of the opposite sex think about my teeth”. However, given the cultural sensitivity, the adapted item was worded as “I am not worried about what others think about my teeth” (Supplementary Table S2).

Overall model fit for the network structure was adequate, with an RMSEA of 0.059 and a CFI of 1.000 (Table 3). EFA also identified unidimensionality with a mixed-model fit (RMSEA: 0.140; CFI: 0.940) (Table 3). The internal consistency reliability of the seven-item scale was excellent (Ω = 0.94). The Spearman correlation between the PIDAQ total score and the SEWB scale was 0.320, providing modest but directionally consistent support for criterion validity. This magnitude is expected, given the conceptual distance between dental aesthetics, a specific aspect of oral health, and the broad, multi-domain construct of social and emotional wellbeing. The correlations among the PIDAQ items were calculated to provide further evidence of structural validity; they were moderate to strong correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.520–0.790), providing support for structural validity (Supplementary Figure S2). The parallel analysis scree plot (Supplementary Figure S3) further reinforced these findings. The first eigenvalue from the actual data substantially exceeded the corresponding eigenvalues from the resampled and simulated data, whereas eigenvalues for factors 2–7 fell at or below the reference lines, indicating the results support a clear unidimensional structure for the culturally adapted PIDAQ, with no evidence for additional meaningful factors.

Discussion

The present study is the first to adapt the PIDAQ using a strength-based, community-led framework for an Indigenous population and to assess the psychometric properties of the eight-item short-form PIDAQ among Indigenous Australians, using network analysis and EGA for validation. The scale demonstrated excellent psychometric properties in this population, with strong dimensionality, stability, reliability, and criterion validity. These results support its potential usefulness as a brief measure of the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics among Indigenous Australians, where culturally valid oral health tools remain limited.

Dental aesthetics deeply influence self-confidence and social interactions, emphasising the importance of addressing these concerns for both functional and psychological reasons. In this study, approximately one‑third of participants felt uncomfortable showing their teeth when smiling, and almost half were dissatisfied with how their teeth looked, indicating a substantial psychosocial burden associated with dental appearance in this population. When people feel unhappy with their teeth, this may compound existing experiences of stigma and shame, making it harder to speak up, smile, or participate confidently in community, work, and cultural activities (Amarasena et al., 2015; Nath, Groundwater, et al., 2026). Because oral health is closely tied to cultural identity, dignity, and social and emotional wellbeing for Indigenous peoples, unmet aesthetic concerns may reinforce feelings of being judged or devalued (Pithon et al., 2014). Other studies (Grewal et al., 2019; Prado et al., 2016) similarly report that aesthetic improvement following orthodontic care is associated with improved quality of life and enhanced self‑esteem, reinforcing the value of addressing appearance‑related concerns as part of comprehensive care. By comparison, a Serbian university sample reported an even higher prevalence of dissatisfaction with dental appearance (66%), indicating that the meaning and impact of dental aesthetics are shaped by cultural and social norms (Stojilković et al., 2024). Together, these findings highlight that addressing these concerns through culturally safe, community‑led oral health care and improved access to orthodontic and restorative options, therefore, has the potential not only to improve smiles but also to strengthen confidence, social connectedness, and overall wellbeing.

Due to low network loadings, the item “Worry” was removed, resulting in a seven-item PIDAQ tool. To ensure cultural sensitivity, the adapted “Worry” item was worded as “I am not worried about what others think about my teeth”. Others’ perceptions have traditionally been considered an important component of the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics. But the current evidence suggests, self-perception of dental aesthetics is highly subjective and frequently driven by internal psychological factors such as personality, which sometimes outweigh external judgements (Xu et al., 2023). Notably, among all the eight items of the PIDAQ, the “Worry” item was the only one concerning external judgement, and this may be the reason behind its lack of contribution (low network loadings) to the scale compared to the other items. Hence, a seven-item, single-dimensional scale, as determined through EGA, supported by positive partial correlations and strong item stability, indicates that all items cohesively measure a single underlying construct (the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics). This simplifies interpretation, allowing the total PIDAQ score to serve as a clear and direct indicator of participants’ overall psychosocial response to their dental appearance (Reise et al., 2013). This is particularly valuable in community and clinical settings where brief, easy-to-use tools are essential (Haroz et al., 2020). Given that a substantial proportion of participants reported dissatisfaction with their dental appearance, a unidimensional measure that allows calculation of a total score ensures that these perceptions are captured simply, efficiently, and meaningfully. Conventionally, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses are used to determine the psychometric properties of dental aesthetics scales (Alsanabani et al., 2022; Wahab et al., 2022), as in our study. The network perspective can provide new insights, such as conditional associations among items. For example, respondents who said that “When I talk, smile or laugh I like to show my teeth”, also said that “I don’t mind showing my teeth in photos”, after controlling for all other items in the network. EFA also identified a single dimension and a mixed fit (CFI = 0.940). The fit of the network model was best, meaning that it’s more likely that the construct (dental aesthetics) is explained by a network model than a factor model. Cultural differences can change how respondents interpret questions (Cruchinho et al., 2024) and certain scale shortening practices, such as selecting items with only the strongest loadings, can create a short scale focused only on the more dominant dimension(s) of the construct and unintentionally induce unidimensionality (Smith et al., 2000). Creating a short scale reduces respondent burden, balancing out the specific nuances of the construct (Kemper et al., 2019) and adapting the original items can alter the psychometric functioning. Hence, the observed unidimensionality may have been influenced by cultural adaptations, or the use of a short-form version, or the reformulation of several original items.

An important methodological consideration is the potential effect of item polarity reformulation on the conceptual equivalence of the adapted PIDAQ relative to the original instrument. Reformulating every item into a uniformly positive, strength-based polarity removes wording-related method variance introduced by mixed positively and negatively framed items in the original PIDAQ. Replacing negatively worded items with positive, strength-based equivalents was a deliberate cultural adaptation strategy endorsed by community advisors to ensure cultural safety and minimise stigmatising language. However, it is recognised that item polarity can subtly influence response tendencies, and that positively framed items may not be psychometrically interchangeable with their deficit-framed counterparts. The strong internal consistency (Ω = 0.94), unidimensional factor structure, and moderate-to-strong inter-item correlations (ρ = 0.52–0.79) observed in the present study suggest that the reformulated items are internally consistent and measure the intended construct, with no evidence of polarity-driven distortion. Because item polarity, wording, and response framing were all modified, the adapted items should not be assumed to be metrically equivalent to their original counterparts, and formal measurement invariance testing against the original PIDAQ would be required before scores from the two versions were pooled or directly compared. From a cross-cultural measurement perspective, the adaptation was intended to preserve conceptual and content equivalence, namely the underlying construct and the meaning of each item, rather than strict equivalence with the original instrument.

Across the broader PIDAQ validation literature, the present study’s psychometric findings compare favourably. In terms of internal consistency, our study’s McDonald’s Ω = 0.94 for scale exceeds the reliability reported for the short-form PIDAQ validated among Malaysian adults (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) (Wan Hassan et al., 2022) and is consistent with the higher values reported for the full 23-item version (Cronbach’s α = 0.96) (Wahab et al., 2022), suggesting that the cultural adaptation did not compromise scale coherence despite the reduced item count. These findings are broadly consistent with PIDAQ studies in other cultural settings. Similarly, a study evaluating the psychological impact of dental appearance among dental students reported high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94) (Akbeyaz Şivet et al., 2026). In terms of dimensionality, our finding of a single underlying factor via EGA aligns with previous confirmatory factor-analytic findings in German adolescents aged 11–15 years (Klages et al., 2015) and Arabic short-form adaptations (Alsanabani et al., 2022), supporting the cross-cultural replicability of the PIDAQ’s unidimensional structure. The original PIDAQ development study used factor analysis to determine the dimensionality (Klages et al., 2006), whereas the current study used novel network psychometrics. In factor analysis, latent factors underlie the observed correlations, and the associations among items are explained by their shared dependence on these factors. Whereas in network models, each observed variable is a node, and the edges represent conditional dependencies after controlling for all other variables, thereby overcoming latent causes. Our study demonstrated a positive association between PIDAQ scores and SEWB (ρ = 0.320), providing preliminary and directionally consistent evidence of criterion validity. A similar approach was undertaken, in which the correlation between the Child Oral Impact Scale and the PIDAQ was calculated (ρ = 0.935) (Wan Hassan et al., 2022). This correlation, while modest, is consistent with theoretical expectations: dental aesthetics is a specific domain of oral health, which, in turn, is only one of many contributors to the broad construct of SEWB as measured by the Community Warriors scale. This also suggests that additional constructs such as cultural identity, experiences of racism, or broader oral health status likely mediate or moderate the relationship between dental aesthetic perceptions and overall wellbeing in this population.

From a clinical perspective, the adapted PIDAQ is a brief, culturally grounded tool that helps clinicians and community oral health services identify adults experiencing psychosocial impacts related to dental appearance. Because items were collaboratively reworded with Indigenous advisors to emphasise strengths, autonomy, and cultural practices, the measure is likely to be more acceptable and less stigmatising than direct deficit‑focused questions, thereby supporting its integration into person‑centred assessments and shared decision‑making. For research and policy, this instrument provides a means to quantify aesthetic‑related oral health impacts, enabling evaluation of orthodontic and restorative interventions, monitoring of program outcomes, and incorporation of psychosocial measures into epidemiological studies. The use of network psychometric methods also illustrates how contemporary analytic approaches can complement traditional factor analysis for instrument validation in Indigenous populations. It may encourage wider uptake of EGA and redundancy analyses in oral health research (Haroz et al., 2020).

Strengths of the study include the community‑guided adaptation process, attention to cultural safety, and the use of both modern network methods and traditional factor analysis to evaluate dimensionality and reliability. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample was drawn from South Australian communities and may not fully represent the diversity of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples across Australia, limiting generalisability. The complete-case sample was relatively modest for advanced network psychometric methods such as GGM and EGA, for which larger samples are recommended for precise edge-weight estimation. Model fit indices were estimated in the same sample used for model development and may therefore be overestimated. The cross-sectional design precludes assessment of test–retest reliability and sensitivity to change, and the moderate association with social and emotional wellbeing suggests that additional constructs, such as discrimination, cultural identity, or the lived experience of oral health inequity, may also shape the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics in this population. Importantly, test–retest reliability was administered at a single time point; it is not possible to confirm whether scores remain consistent over short intervals in the absence of genuine change in participants’ dental aesthetic perceptions. Future studies should prioritise test–retest reliability assessment using an appropriate test–retest interval (typically two to four weeks) under stable conditions, in samples where no clinical intervention is expected between administrations. Furthermore, a formal pilot testing phase was not conducted prior to the main validation study. Future work should examine longitudinal performance and responsiveness to clinical and community-based interventions and explore how PIDAQ scores relate to clinical indices of malocclusion and other oral health outcomes in larger, more diverse Indigenous samples. Finally, the present study’s findings represent an initial validation, and confirmation in larger, independent populations would provide stronger evidence of the psychometric performance of the adapted instrument.

Conclusion

This study delivers the first culturally adapted, psychometrically robust measure of the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics for Indigenous adults, using contemporary network-based methods to demonstrate its validity and reliability. This instrument now provides clinicians, researchers, and policymakers with a practical tool to systematically assess aesthetic‑related oral health concerns, track the benefits of orthodontic and restorative care, and embed Indigenous‑led perspectives on dental appearance into service planning and evaluation. Used alongside clinical indices and community‑driven programs, it has the potential to inform more responsive, culturally informed oral health interventions and to contribute to improved social and emotional wellbeing for Indigenous communities.
