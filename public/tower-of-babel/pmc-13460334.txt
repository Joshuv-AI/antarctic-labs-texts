Vulvar Squamous Cell Carcinoma: Scientific Progress and Impacts to Pathology Practice
Authors: Lynn Hoang, Somayah Alsolami, Blake Gilks, Jessica N. McAlpine, Amy Jamieson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite vulvar cancer’s rarity, meaningful advances have been made to improve the clinical care of affected patients—particularly with respect to vulvar squamous cell carcinoma (VSCC). These advances are reflected in changes to tumor classification, surgical strategies, early detection of precursors, and targeted therapeutic exploration. Refinements in histopathologic and molecular classification have enhanced diagnostic precision, enabling more accurate prognostication and facilitating personalized therapeutic decision-making, particularly tailored surgical strategies that can be explored through clinical trials—to improve patient outcomes while reducing patient morbidity. Increased awareness of VSCC precursors have also opened avenues for earlier intervention. This review highlights the key changes in the evolution of VSCC care, with particular emphasis on the expanding responsibilities of pathologists in tumor subtyping [by human papillomavirus (HPV) and p53 status], biomarker-based prognostication, margin evaluation, recognition of precursors and alignment of precursor terminology (particularly HPV independent p53 wild-type lesions). By highlighting both progress and limitations, this review aims to inform future strategies that will further optimize patient outcomes and advance the standard of care in VSCC.

Vulvar squamous cell carcinoma (VSCC) constitutes ~5% of all gynecologic malignancies. Approximately 47,336 new cases of VSCC were estimated worldwide in 2022, with 18,579 deaths from the disease.1 The 5-yr overall survival (OS) is ∼60% to 70%.2,3 One-third of patients will suffer multiple disease recurrences, where the 5-yr OS drops substantially to 14%.4 Patients with VSCC face numerous challenges, spanning from surgical complications to psychological health (eg, wound dehiscence, lymphedema, infection, pain, neuropathy, sexual dysfunction, suicidal ideation).5–7

Over the last several decades, clinicians and researchers have attempted to improve the clinical care of VSCC patients, pushing advances in tumor classification, tailored surgical treatment strategies, recognition of early precancerous lesions and therapeutic options. In this review, we discuss the progress made in VSCC care, with an emphasis on changes that impact pathology practice. Ongoing controversies regarding tumor depth of invasion and staging are not discussed here, in anticipation that future endeavors [eg, International Society of Gynecological Pathologists (ISGyP) vulvar cancer working group] may provide more clarity on those issues.

TUMOR CLASSIFICATION BY HPV AND TP53/P53 STATUS AND PROGNOSTIC SIGNIFICANCE

It was recognized over 3 decades ago that VSCC can occur through 2 distinct biologic pathways. One biologic pathway is driven by high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) infection and the other occurs independently of HPV, usually in the setting of lichen sclerosus, “squamous hyperplasia” and chronic vulvar inflammation.8–10 Since this original finding, numerous publications have examined the prognostic importance of HPV status in VSCC, frequently using p16 immunohistochemistry (IHC) as a surrogate marker for hrHPV infection. Most published studies have shown that HPV-associated (HPVA) VSCC portends a better prognosis [OS, disease-specific survival (DSS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS)] than HPV-independent (HPVI) VSCC, demonstrating lower rates of lymph node involvement, lower rates of margin positivity and higher sensitivity to chemoradiation.11–18 A small number of studies have found no significant differences.19–23 This may be attributed to varying surgical approaches across institutions, cohort size, follow-up period, use of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) sequencing for HPV testing (which does not account for viral integration), and/or differences in adjuvant therapy. It is of interest that McAlpine et al.12 found in the 10-yr span before 1995, HPVA and HPVI VSCC had similar clinical outcomes, but in the 10-yr span after 1995, HPVI VSCC had markedly worse outcomes. It was postulated that this difference was attributed to the global surgical practice change shifting away from radical en bloc surgical resections (removing all vulvar tissue with full inguinal lymph node dissection and encompassing all intervening tissues between the groin and the vulva) to more conservative vulvar surgeries (localized radical vulvectomy with separate inguinal incisions).24 The historical en bloc resections were morbid and associated with prolonged hospital stays, but equal survival within HPVI and HPVA VSCC cohorts. When assessing outcomes in the modern era with less morbid surgery, outcomes were actually superior in the HPVA group, suggesting spared morbidity/mortality with this approach, but far inferior for HPVI VSCC patients, supporting the importance of more extensive surgery in the latter subset. This theory was supported in a subsequent cohort study of VSCC treated in Germany, where patients were uniformly treated with radical vulvar field resection surgery,25,26 encompassing much larger anatomic sites and routinely requiring reconstructive surgery. Survival metrics were improved compared with other series (~85% 3-yr recurrence-free and disease-specific survival).27

As the importance of HPV status was clarified, studies also showed that HPV status could not be reliably determined using histologic features alone.12,13,28 In the largest study of VSCC to date (n=1594), Rakislova et al.28 found that if HPV status was assigned using morphology alone, 40% of cases would be misclassified (37% of HPVA tumors had keratinizing features—typical of HPVI tumors, and 5% of HPVI VSCCs exhibited basaloid or warty characteristics—typical of HPVA tumors). Altogether, these studies eventually persuaded the World Health Organization (WHO),29 College of American Pathologists (CAP),30 International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR),31 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO)32 and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)33 to now endorse HPVA and HPVI status as a mandatory reporting item in pathology reports. This can be done through HPV testing, or preferably p16 IHC testing, as the latter has excellent concordance with HPV and even higher sensitivity.34,35

The more nuanced question is whether TP53/p53 status has any additional prognostic value in the context of HPVA and HPVI stratification. As far as we are aware, the possibility of a third group of VSCC [where HPVI is divided into p53 wild-type (HPVI/p53wt) and p53 abnormal (HPVI/p53abn)] was first raised by Chochzick et al.36 In their analysis of a small cohort of 39 VSCC, no prognostic differences were found. Attention to this topic was raised a few years later in 2 major studies reported from the Netherlands, where in 236 VSCC there was a significantly lower local recurrence rate for HPVA tumors (5.3%) compared with HPVI/p53wt tumors (16.3%) and HPVI/p53abn tumors (22.6%) (P=0.044).37 When comparing HPVI/p53wt to HPVI/p53abn directly, no statistically significant difference in local recurrence was observed.37 In a follow-up study, with 413 VSCC from 2 hospitals treated between 2000 and 2015, the same separation in 5-yr OS was observed (HPVA 83%, HPVI/p53wt 64% and HPVI/p53abn 48%; P<0.001). There was no significant difference in recurrence-free survival (RFS) between HPVA VSCC and HPVI/p53wt VSCC, whereas HPVI/p53mut VSCC showed significantly worse RFS than either HPVA or HPVI/p53wt VSCC38.

In a subsequent study of 190 VSCC over a 47-yr period (1975–2022) by Carreras-Dieguez and colleagues, HPVA demonstrated a trend toward better DSS than HPVI. HPVI/p53wt VSCC had the worst RFS among the 3 groups (P<0.05), but interestingly, also a trend toward better DSS than HPVI/p53abn (P > 0.05). The 10-yr recurrence rate of HPVA VSCC was significantly lower than HPVI/p53wt and HPVI/p53abn (18.8%, 60% and 48.9%, respectively, P = 0.002).35 In another study of a Norwegian cohort of 123 VSCC, HPVA tumors had better progression-free survival (PFS) and DSS than HPVI/p53wt and HPVI/p53abn tumors (P<0.001 and <0.021).34 Taken together, publications have shown slightly differing findings when considering three subgroups of VSCC (HPVA, HPVI/p53wt vs. HPVI/p53abn). These data may be confounded by factors such as adjuvant therapy use, radicality of surgical resection and re-excision rates, which are not always recorded or analyzed. As such, the use of p53 IHC to substratify VSCC is not consistently endorsed in international guidelines.31–33,39,40

CHALLENGES IN TUMOR CLASSIFICATION BY HPV AND P53 STATUS

Pathologic interpretation of p16 IHC as a surrogate marker for hrHPV infection is generally not problematic, with strong diffuse/block-type nuclear and cytoplasmic staining, in at least the lower-third of the squamous epithelium in in situ lesions and squamous nests in invasive carcinomas, representing a positive result in squamous lesions of the lower genital tract.41

p53 IHC interpretation, however, is more nuanced due to the fact that squamous cells exhibit a maturation phenomenon (terminal differentiation) as the cells migrate towards the epithelial surface. This phenomenon influences p53 IHC interpretation and, instead of IHC interpretation in a simple binary signal versus no-signal assessment, requires interpretation in a pattern-based scheme. p53 IHC can be classified as wild-type (scattered or basal-sparing, the latter unique to HPVA neoplasms), or mutant (basal or parabasal/diffuse overexpression, absent/null, cytoplasmic).42,43 While Tessier-Cloutier and colleagues reported a concordance between p53 IHC pattern and TP53 mutation status in 95%, with some caveats (i.e. strong wild-type staining being particularly difficult to distinguish from mutation-type staining), the concordance rate reported by Ordi et al was lower at ~78%44. In addition, interrater agreement in p53 IHC interpretation is between 50% and 87% and can vary by level of expertise.45–47

A positive p16 IHC result and abnormal p53 IHC result are generally mutually exclusive, making subclassification of tumors into HPVA, HPVI/p53wt, and HPVI/p53abn straightforward in the majority of cases (Fig. 1). In a study of 236 VSCC and 1293 VSCC, >95% of VSCC could be easily subclassified using p16 and p53 IHC, but several unusual scenarios did exist46,48. Yang and colleagues found 5 tumors were “double positive” (block-type positive for p16 IHC and abnormal for p53 IHC—4 overexpressed, 1 null), instead of being mutually exclusive. Subsequent testing showed they were all negative for hrHPV RNA ISH and harbored a TP53 mutation (missense or splice-site mutations). There was one false negative p16, where p53 IHC showed a helpful basal-sparing pattern, and hrHPV RNA ISH was positive48. In a study by Höhn ānd colleagues, concordance in VSCC subtyping between biopsy and resection was excellent (91%). In a minority of cases, the discrepancies were attributed to difficulties in p53 interpretation (wild-type vs. abnormal/aberrant) and to the aforementioned double-positive cases (3.5%)49.

In a case study by Sisuashvili and colleagues, a VSCC was p53 abnormal but had varying p16 (block-type in the first tumor and nonblock in a recurrence). Low-risk HPV67 was detected in the first tumor and high-risk HPV66 was detected in the recurrence. The authors argued that the finding of HPV by PCR and consistent negative results by HPV RNA ISH suggested the PCR testing was a false positive, and that the finding of mutations typically seen in HPVI tumors (TP53, CCND1, and COL6A1) supported the tumor being classified as HPVI.50 Chochzick and colleagues also described unusual p16/p53 patterns, including a “double positive” HPVI VSCC with block-type p16 and p53 abnormal overexpression. It had a TP53 mutation, absence of PIK3CA mutations (usually seen in HPVA) and negative HPV RNA ISH36. They also describe a HPVA tumor with a pathogenic TP53 mutation that still expressed a p53 IHC basal sparing pattern,36 consistent with the findings by Yang et al.48 It seems so far that a VSCC that is “double positive” for p16 and p53 is usually HPVI, but this observation requires further study because only a handful of such cases have been described to date.

The existing tumor classification scheme for VSCC does not consider the possibility of infection by low-risk HPV. Lozar and colleagues postulated that some types of VSCC may be related to beta genera HPV (β-HPV), more frequently seen in cutaneous SCC. In their study of 26 squamous lesions adjacent to VSCC, they found almost half had the detectable presence of β-HPV (in 8/10 p16 negative lesions and 3/14 p16 positive lesions), and this was enriched in the atypical squamous proliferation (over the HSIL and differentiated vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (dVIN)) groups51. These exploratory findings raise the possibility that these HPV genotypes contribute to the etiology of some HPVI/p53wt SCC in the vulva. Williams and colleagues also reported two cases of VSCC thought to be related to low-risk HPV6, which were p16 nonblock and TP53 wild-type by sequencing. HPV6 was episomal. They state these cases were different from condyloma—even though the surface was verrucopapillary and looked condylomatous, there was superficial stromal invasion52.

SURGICAL MARGIN CLEARANCE: HOW FAR IS ENOUGH AND THE POTENTIAL ROLE FOR PRECISION SURGERY

Historically, the recommended surgical margin clearance for VSCC was 1 to 2 cm clinically, corresponding to 8 mm by pathologic examination (allowing for tissue shrinkage upon processing), to reduce the risk of local recurrence53–55. This was largely based on the study by Heaps et al. 56 in 1990, who found that in 134 patients, those with a surgical tumor-free margin of ≥8 mm had no recurrences, while those <8 mm had a 50% rate of local recurrence.

More recently, the cut-off for optimal VSCC margin clearance has been contested. Several studies support the 8 mm cut-off for margin clearance, and found higher rates of local recurrence as the distance decreased from 8 mm57–60. A meta-analysis of 10 studies found that a tumor-free margin of <8 mm was associated with a 2-fold increased risk of local recurrence (defined as a local recurrence within 2 yr)61. The same authors in their cohort study of 148 patients, however, found no clear difference in the risk of local recurrence when using the 8 mm cut-off, but acknowledged that this finding was complicated by the fact that many patients with margins <8 mm received additional treatment61.

Other studies did not find an association between local recurrence and margin status with a cut-off of 8 mm62–65, and some authors alternatively suggested lower cut-offs of 2, 3, or 5 mm as stronger predictors of recurrence66–68. One of the most impactful studies was a German AGO-CaRE multi-center study examining 289 patients with complete tumor resection and node-negative disease. Vulvar recurrence rates were not significantly impacted by margin distance as either a binary or continuous variable (recurrences were 12.6% in patients with a margin <8 mm and 10.2% in patients with a margin ≥8 mm).69 In a meta-analysis of 22 studies, the 8 mm cut-off was not supported.70

Alternatively, studies have reported the presence of lichen sclerosus (LS) and/or dVIN at the margin or in the field (instead of margin distance) as more significant risk factors for local recurrence.35,63,64,71–73 In light of this growing body of literature, recent guidelines published by FIGO 2025,32 NCCN 2024,33 The European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) 202339 and British Gynaecological Cancer Society (BGCS) 202340 have stepped away from the 8 mm rule (Table 1). Both FIGO and NCCN still encourage a 1 to 2 cm surgical margin clearance, but have dropped the pathologic margin clearance to 2 to 3 mm. ESGO similarly accepts a pathologic minimal margin clearance of 2 to 3 mm, but does not explicitly endorse a surgical margin clearance and acknowledges that the optimal margin distance remains to be decided. The BGCS states that the aim of surgery is to clear the cancer at all microscopic margins, but the consensus threshold for distance is uncertain. The BGCS is also the only body that explicitly acknowledges the importance of clearing the adjacent dVIN and/or LS to reduce local recurrence risk. The BGCS further emphasizes rigorous pathologic examination of margins through careful specimen orientation, that is, “the excised skin specimen should be secured in a way that allows accurate orientation by the pathologist (eg, marker suture and pinned to cork board)”. All guidelines encourage consideration of additional treatment (eg, re-excision, adjuvant radiation therapy) in the setting of close margins (defined as <5 mm by FIGO and <2 mm by BGCS), taking into account anatomy, comorbidities, and other tumor parameters.

A major deficit of the published literature is the lack of consideration of margins in the context of HPV and p53 status. As mentioned above, McAlpine et al.12 reported that outcomes for HPVA and HPVI VSCC were similar between 1985 and 1995 (when all patients underwent more radical vulvar surgeries) compared with 1995–2005, when there was a shift towards more conservative surgeries and HPVI VSCC suffered worse outcomes compared with HPVA (PFS hazard ratios (HR) 4.29; DSS HR 4.52; OS HR 2.73; P=0.026). We posited that the shift towards more conservative surgery and higher rates of local recurrence are related to the phenomenon of “field cancerization” often seen in HPVI VSCC. Thompson and colleagues studied 73 VSCC and found that dVIN was documented in the pathology reports in half of cases. The additional use of p53 IHC identified a p53-abnormal field of cells adjacent to the main VSCC tumor in an additional 29% of cases, not originally reported, highlighting the frequency of under-recognized VIN/squamous dysplasia75. Furthermore, the use of p53 IHC highlighted these p53-abnormal cells extending to margins, changing margin status from negative to positive in approximately one-third of cases,75 similar to the finding reported by Singh et al.76 The local recurrence risk when these p53-abnormal cells (with subtle histologic features of dysplasia) extended to a surgical margin was astonishingly 73% at 2-yr, and equivalent to the recurrence rate with morphologically bona fide dVIN (p53 abnormal cells with overt histologic features of dysplasia) at the margin77. Residual dVIN at margin is important as it leaves patients at very high risk of recurrence, with a high-risk of malignant transformation of dVIN.78–82 The overall estimated rate of dVIN transformation to VSCC is in the range of 50% to 90% over 2 yr,78,80,81,83 and can be higher when the dVIN bears undifferentiated/HSIL-like morphology.79,84

Taken together, there is substantial evidence that surgical margin clearance should not be approached in the same way for HPVA and HPVI VSCC. We hypothesize that HPVA VSCC may be treated with more conservative margins, given the absence of occult fields of dysplasia and lower risk of malignant transformation in HSIL. Conversely, HPVI VSCC may necessitate wider margins and margins that incorporate removal of any residual dVIN or p53-abnormal fields of cells. Ultimately, this line of thinking is what prompted the creation of the STRIVE clinical trial (STRatIfication of Vulvar squamous cell carcinoma by HPV and P53 status to guide Excision; NCT06358469; https://gynecancerinitiative.ca/participate-in-research/strive-trial/), hosted by the Canadian Clinical Trials Group (CCTG)85. In this prospective, international, multicentre, phase II clinical trial, primary surgically staged I-II (FIGO 2021) VSCC are stratified by HPV status (using p16 IHC) and HPVI tumors are confirmed to be p53 abnormal by IHC (HPVI p53wt VSCC are excluded). HPVA VSCC with margins negative for cancer but <8 mm undergo active surveillance (regardless of HSIL status at margins). HPVI VSCC with either (i) margins negative for cancer but <8 mm, (ii) margins positive for morphologic dVIN (also confirmed to be p53 abnormal) or (iii) margins positive for dysplastic cells (do not meet the threshold for morphologic dVIN but are confirmed to be p53 abnormal) are randomized to re-excision or active surveillance. Three-year local recurrence rates will be compared as the primary study outcome. This important clinical trial will answer several important questions, including whether <8 mm margins are acceptable for HPVI and HPVA tumors and the importance of excising the adjacent dVIN/p53 abnormal cells. It also addresses whether dVIN can be adequately excised by surgery without causing undue morbidity for patients.

From the perspective of the practicing pathologist, all this data and inquiry raises several considerations. In current reporting guidelines, VSCC margin distance and the presence of dVIN at a margin are already recommended/core items.30,31 In the STRIVE trial, dVIN is considered synonymous with HPVI p53abn VIN, and therefore dVIN at margin(s) need to be confirmed with p53 IHC (Fig. 2). Pathologists rarely misinterpret p53 abnormal IHC patterns at a margin when there is margin involvement (<3% of instances), likely because they can compare the p53 pattern with that seen in the adjacent VSCC (unpublished observations). The other question of how many margins should be stained for p53, to exclude possible dVIN involvement, is a more difficult question. This would depend on whether the entire surgical site would be re-excised (in which case, any margin positivity is all that is needed) or whether more targeted margin re-excision would be attempted, particularly for large surgical scars. In the former scenario, in preparation for the STRIVE trial, we found that pathologists were able to identify a margin positive for histologic dVIN (and triage for confirmatory p53 IHC testing) in 1 to 2 attempts in ~80% of cases (unpublished observations). In current pathology practice, there is no universal guideline on gross processing and assessment for dVIN (i.e. need for p53 for confirmation); these aspects will vary by individual institution and practitioner. Given that the recurrence risk for morphologically bone fide dVIN (p53abn) and more subtle dVIN (p53abn) is the same,77 we believe any abnormal epithelium at margin (regardless of morphologic threshold) can be designated under the umbrella term HPVI p53abn VIN at margin (also see precursor section).

GROWING RECOGNITION OF HPVI/P53WT PRECURSOR LESIONS

There has been growing recognition and interest in HPVI/p53wt squamous precursor lesions in the vulva (Fig. 3). The terms vulvar acanthosis with altered differentiation (VAAD) and differentiated exophytic vulvar intraepithelial lesion (DE-VIL) were originally described in 2004 and 2017 by investigators at Brigham and Women’s Hospital, as precursor lesions to verrucous carcinoma.86,87 In 2020–2021 The International Society for the Study of Vulvovaginal Disease (ISSVD) proposed the term vulvar aberrant maturation (VAM) to convey a lesion that exhibited aberrant maturation, and wanted to avoid the term neoplasia, as the malignant potential was unclear.88–90 In 2022, in an attempt to harmonize terminology, the term HPV-independent, p53 wild-type verruciform acanthotic vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (HPVi(p53wt) vaVIN) was proposed. This term was proposed to harmonize terminology with the WHO 5th edition umbrella of VIN and highlight the neoplastic nature of these lesions. These vaVIN lesions were defined as having retained but altered maturation that exhibited (i) hypo- or hypergranulosis, (ii) acanthosis that is flat or verruciform, (iii) absence of overt cytologic nuclear atypia, (iv) p16 IHC non-block staining, and (v) p53 IHC wild-type staining.91 These lesions have been found to be associated with synchronous or metachronous VSCC. Approximately 10% to 40% will transform into invasive carcinoma, over a time period of 15 to 65 mo, and ~50% have recurrent/persistent disease.92–94 They harbor pathogenic mutations in PIK3CA, HRAS, CDKN2A, NOTCH1-2, and the TERT promoter.37,86,92,95 After a long journey, the incumbent 6th edition of the WHO is likely to move away from these morphologic descriptors to an overarching umbrella term, HPVI VIN, which can be further subdivided into p53 wild-type and p53 aberrant types.

Currently, the great challenge for pathologists is how to confidently recognize HPVI VIN. vaVIN can be exceptionally challenging to distinguish from benign mimics,96 such as lichen simplex chronicus (LSC), lichen planus, psoriasis, hypertrophic LS, condyloma and pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia. Some immunohistochemical markers have been reported to be helpful in the diagnosis. CK17 and SOX2 have been shown to have diffuse, moderate-strong staining extending from suprabasal to full epithelial expression in vaVIN, compared with patchy staining in non-dysplastic vulvar tissue. Recently, Zhang and colleagues showed 100% sensitivity of GLUT1 staining in all 24/24 vaVIN, where staining was strong, membranous in the basal layer with suprabasal extension to the full thickness epithelium. Staining was weak in the 48 non-neoplastic vulvar lesions, which included condyloma.97 It should be noted that CK17, SOX2, and GLUT1 will also stain strongly/diffusely in dVIN.98,99 These are less common IHC markers that not all pathologists will have at their disposal. If IHC and molecular testing are unavailable, we believe pathologists should have a high index of suspicion for HPVI p53wt VIN, particularly in the setting of a clinically visible lesion or plaque. However, this should also be coupled with a high threshold for morphologic diagnosis, given the overlap with other benign pathologic entities and trigger for surgical excision (as there is currently no compelling evidence to support the use of nonsurgical therapies in these lesions).

We also believe a first-time diagnosis of HPVI VIN should have p53 performed, due to the higher risk of progression and local recurrence for HPVI p53abn VIN.79 This applies even if the lesion shows bona fide dVIN morphologic features [eg, basal nuclear atypia (pleomorphism, nucleomegaly, hyperchromasia), basal cell disarray, premature maturation, abnormal rete ridges]. The interpretation of p53 IHC is similar to that described in VSCC, although with greater interpretative challenges.47,100 Many inflammatory or non-neoplastic lesions of the vulva exhibit “high wild-type” p53 IHC staining that can be very challenging to distinguish from aberrant basal-overexpression—and is the greatest source of discrepancy in p53 interpretation. In such cases, we exercise a high threshold to call the p53 basal-overexpression pattern, and if uncertain, lean toward being conservative and reporting the p53 as equivocal/uninterpretable. In cases where the morphology and p53 are discrepant (eg, morphologically appears like vaVIN but exhibits a p53 aberrant staining pattern), use of the umbrella term HPVI p53abn VIN is appropriate. A free online training module on p53 IHC interpretation in squamous in situ lesions is available on the Canadian Biomarker Quality Assurance (CBQA) website, https://cbqareadout.ca/modules.php.

OTHER PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS IN VSCC

Unlike in other types of gynecologic cancer, targeted therapy options remain limited in VSCC. PD-L1 expression has been explored and has shown high rates of expression in VSCC, whether using the combined positive score (CPS) (~83%) or tumor proportion score (TPS) (~54%), with higher albeit variable rates in HPVI tumors.101 In KEYNOTE-158, 101 advanced stage VSCC had an objective response rate (ORR) of 10.9% with pembrolizumab (regardless of PD-L1 score using CPS ≥1 or <1)102. In CHECKMATE-358, there was an ORR of 20% with nivolumab, but only a handful of VSCC were included (n=5) and all were of HPVA type.103 So far, there does not appear to be strong evidence to support universal or reflex testing of PD-L1 in VSCC.

Antibody drug conjugates (ADC) targets have also been recently investigated in VSCC. ADCs are antibodies conjugated to a chemotherapeutic payload and are selected to target antigens enriched on tumor cells. Upon binding to the tumor cell surface, they release their chemotherapeutic payload and cause cell death to the bound tumor cell and adjacent cells (bystander effect). Although the data so far remains limited, TROP2, Nectin4 and tissue factor have been shown to exhibit intermediate to high expression in VSCC, suggesting that ADCs targeting these proteins may be therapeutic options.74,104–106

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Research activity in vulvar cancer has accelerated in recent years, reflected in changing tumor classification systems, impacts on surgical management, increased attention to precursor lesions (for the purposes of both early detection and mitigating disease recurrence), and interest in leveraging newer targeted therapies. These shifts affect the demands on pathology practice, which still continues to evolve as we learn more about this historically understudied disease.
