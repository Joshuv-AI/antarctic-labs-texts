Meta-learning of human motor adaptation via the dorsal premotor cortex
Authors: Taisei Sugiyama, Shintaro Uehara, Jun Izawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHumans exert cognitive control over cognitive functions. Meta-learning is one such metacognitive ability, in which people learn how to efficiently learn the same or similar tasks through experience. Although a prefrontal network is believed crucial for meta-learning by reinforcement learning in decision making, little is known about a meta-learning network for motor adaptation. Based on a previously suggested hierarchical information motor stream, this study demonstrates that brain stimulation to the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd), but not to the dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC), attenuates meta-learning, revealing a distinct meta-learning network in motor adaptation in which PMd serves a central function. The brain utilizes PMd to achieve meta-learning in motor adaptation, possibly because of its involvement and interplay in cognitive, motor, and reward processes.

People exhibit metacognition, a high-level cognitive ability to monitor and control other cognitive functions (1, 2). Similar metacognitive behavior can be found in different types of tasks, suggesting its general, task-independent nature at the behavioral level. An example is meta-learning, the capacity to learn about how to learn the same/similar tasks more efficiently through experience (3–5). Such metacognitive regulation of learning is crucial for solving problems in practical settings, such as stroke rehabilitation (5). In laboratory settings, studies on decision making and motor adaptation report similar meta-learning behavior, which can be modeled as a hierarchical system in which a higher reinforcement learning system monitors and controls a lower learning system, i.e., decision making or motor adaptation (4, 6–8). For example, in meta-learning of motor adaptation, a meta-learning system controls how much a motor adaptation system learns and retains new behavior according to how adaptation influences reward from the environment (Fig. 1A). Since similar hierarchical processing is also suggested in decision making (6–8), computational structures are likely shared between the two, leading to similar meta-learning behavior that aims for reward maximization in both tasks.

Despite such computationally similar meta-learning between motor adaptation and decision making, the brain must form anatomically distinct meta-learning networks because the lower learning systems have different neural representations and computations. For decision making, some part of the prefrontal cortex (PFC), such as the orbitofrontal cortex or anterior cingulate cortex, is crucial for hierarchical information processing in meta-learning (6–8). Then, for motor adaptation, which part of the brain is crucial?

For motor information streams, hierarchical information processing has been suggested among the dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC), premotor cortex (PM), and primary motor cortex (M1) (9, 10), where more rostral (anterior) regions are thought to be hierarchically higher and responsible for more abstract information processing. This theory is consistent with both anatomical and functional aspects of PM, which has bidirectional projections with the DLPFC and M1 (11, 12) and exhibits a functional cognitive-motor gradient in the rostrocaudal direction (13–15).

If this hierarchical organization mediates meta-learning in motor adaptation, one possibility is that the DLPFC is a crucial site, processing meta-learning signals or sending those signals to the PM and M1 (Fig. 1 B, Left) (16, 17). Since the DLPFC has direct anatomical projections to the PM (11, 12), the DLPFC is likely to serve as an output region of the prefrontal network that passes meta-learning signals from other metacognitive areas, e.g., the orbitofrontal cortex, to the PM. Thus, this possibility accords with the commonly accepted link between the PFC and meta-learning/metacognition in cognitive neuroscience (6–8, 18, 19).

However, literature on motor learning and control questions this possibility and raises an alternative possibility that the PM, in particular the dorsal PM (PMd), is the crucial site (Fig. 1 B, Right). Computationally, meta-learning requires integration of motor adaptation and rewards (4). Since the PMd is involved in both motor adaptation (16, 17, 20–24) and reward processing (25–27), it can access both types of information that are necessary for meta-learning. Therefore, in theory, the PMd can achieve meta-learning without inputs from the DLPFC. In addition, there is empirical evidence from imaging studies and brain stimulation studies that suggests a regulatory role of the PMd in motor adaptation. In these studies, interactive changes between the PMd and the cerebellum are correlated with motor adaptation behavior, and neurostimulation of the PMd can indeed alter learning at a trial-by-trial level (24, 28, 29). Since these regulatory functions of PMd and its interaction with reward and motor circuits (16, 17, 20–27) are consistent with our proposed computational process of meta-learning that regulates motor adaptation by reinforcement learning (4), we hypothesize that the PMd serves as the meta-learning site and regulates motor adaptation via downstream signals to the M1 without requiring downstream signals from the DLPFC.

In contrast, evidence for the DLPFC is more tenuous than that for the PMd, mainly coming from metacognition in nonmotor tasks and putative involvement of the (DL)PFC in metacognitive functions (6–8, 18, 19). However, such explicit cognitive regulation of explicit strategies during motor adaptation, for which DLPFC is likely involved (30), is a possible alternative mechanism underlying meta-learning of motor adaptation (5). Thus, given the previously proposed hierarchy among the DLPFC, PM(d), and M1, the alternative hypothesis is that the DLPFC serves as the meta-learning site that regulates motor adaptation in the PMd and M1.

In this paper, we tested these two hypotheses by applying transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to either the PMd or DLPFC (Fig. 1C) during the previously established meta-learning training task (Fig. 1 D and E) (4). In theory, motor learning is achieved through updating memory in response to a given sensory error, while meta-learning is achieved through monitoring and controlling this motor learning process to maximize rewards and minimize punishments. To manipulate this process in the meta-learning task, sensory error feedback and reward feedback were experimentally dissociated (31) and provided in two separate but consecutive trials: Sensory Error (E) and Reward (R) trials, with the reward feedback computed as a function of memory updates to represent the association between learning and outcome (Learning-Outcome Structure, LOS). Consequently, as participants repeated pairs of ER trials over cycles and blocks of training (Fig. 1 E, Left and Bottom), they up-regulated motor adaptation in the Promote condition (where the score increases as the memory update enlarges) and down-regulated it in the Suppress condition (where the score decreases as the memory update enlarges), successfully maximizing rewards and minimizing punishments through meta-learning in these two contrasting LOSs (Fig. 2 A, Bottom). Thus, long-term modulation of learning rates and retention rates for the two LOSs (Promote and Suppress) demonstrates effects of meta-learning, which are dissociated from motor learning itself, as memory updates in each E trial.

To investigate disruption of function in the PMD and DLPFC during this meta-learning task, we administered TMS prior to movement initiation in the E (visuomotor rotation) trial (Fig. 1D). This design is based on previous reports of the effects of microstimulation on the PMd during motor adaptation, which demonstrated that stimulation before movement onset attenuates the trial-by-trial motor adaptation size (24). Although the authors of this paper assumed that this reduction in adaptation size resulted from attenuation of motor adaptation rates, we considered that a contribution of meta-learning existed behind this process and that attenuation of meta-learning caused the reduction of motor adaptation size. Here, the PMd hypothesis predicts that TMS to the PMd, not the DLPFC, affects meta-learning. Conversely, the DLPFC hypothesis predicts that TMS to the DLPFC, not to the PMd, influences meta-learning. Since these two conditions are identical except the TMS location, each condition serves as the “test” condition in one of the two hypotheses while it serves as the control condition in the other hypothesis. Therefore, comparing the two groups can test the two hypotheses without having a sham condition (32, 33).

Results

General.

Forty-four healthy participants volunteered for the experiment approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Tsukuba, giving informed consent before participation.

They were assigned to participate in up to four sessions with different conditions (described below) in a randomized order. None of them had participated in the previous meta-learning study (4).

The task schedule was identical to the original study (4). Participants performed 24 cycles of meta-learning training over four blocks (six cycles per block, Fig. 1E). These meta-learning training cycles were interspersed with another task called Probe, which is a typical visuomotor rotation task without any score feedback (Learning Phase), followed by visual error-clamp trials (Retention Phase) (Fig. 1 E, Right). With Probe, we assess the learning rate through the visuomotor rotation (Learning Phase) and the retention rate of motor memory through error-clamp trials (Retention Phase). Since the preceding meta-learning training alters both the learning rate and the retention rate of motor memory, accumulated meta-learning effects during the meta-learning training also influence the memory update rate in the Learning Phase and the memory retention profile in Retention Phases (4). If TMS modulates the meta-learning process during training, its effects could also be assessed in these Probe trials. Therefore, Probe allows us to investigate whether TMS influences meta-learning of error sensitivity, the retention rate of motor memory, or both. Note that since Probe did not include TMS or score feedback, it also controlled for artifacts and reward-based changes in behavior (31).

In the meta-learning training task, participants repeated pairs of ER trials (Fig. 1E). In an E trial, online cursor feedback about the hand position was shown to participants, but unbeknownst to them, the cursor was rotated during the movement, inducing a sensory prediction error and thereby motor adaptation, i.e., an update of motor memory. Then, in the next R trial, participants moved the hand to the target without the cursor, and the degree of learning was measured from an “aftereffect,” a corrected hand direction that would counteract the rotational perturbation in the previous E trial. Participants received a negative numerical score representing a monetary loss at the crossing in the R trial, and critically, the score was calculated based on the size of aftereffect, i.e., degree of learning. Note that the cursor feedback was absent in R trials so that the score would not interfere with the reach performance (error between the cursor and the target). In this way, motor adaptation and punishment were presented separately in the two consecutive trials (E and R trials), which enabled us to freely manipulate the association between learning and punishment by different reward functions (LOSs).

As in the original study (4), there were two LOSs with opposite reward functions: Promote and Suppress (Fig. 1 E, Left). In Promote, more motor adaptation (more aftereffect) would lead to less punishment (less negative score) in R trials, thereby promoting learning. On the other hand, in Suppress, less motor adaptation would lead to less punishment in R trials, thereby suppressing learning. Thus, over time, effects of meta-learning training could be probed by contrasting how learning behavior changed differently between Promote and Suppress. The original study demonstrated that people were indeed able to change learning behavior according to a given LOS, reducing punishment by up-regulating learning in Promote and down-regulating learning in Suppress. Here, the focus of the present study was how TMS over the PMd/DLPFC modulated training effects. Therefore, meta-learning effects were probed and compared between TMS locations. Thus, there were two independent factors with two levels each, TMS location (PMd/DLPFC) and LOS (Promote /Suppress), leading to four conditions: PMd-Promote (PMdProm, N = 21), PMd-Suppress (PMdSupp, N = 20), DLPFC-Promote (PFCProm, N = 25), and DLPFC-Suppress (PFCSupp, N = 20). Note that the present study applied the same amount and timing of TMS over relatively close locations (PMd and DLPFC, Fig. 1C), minimizing artifacts of TMS across the four conditions, e.g., location-independent effects, including potential sensory side effects and the placebo effect. Therefore, comparing these conditions can subtract those artifacts from main location effects, thereby controlling for these potential artifacts. As such, active TMS control conditions with different stimulus location sites are required in TMS experiments, and the sham TMS condition has technical limitations in controlling these factors (33); thus, the present study did not include a sham TMS condition.

Meta-Learning Training.

Each participant experienced four blocks of six cycles, each cycle composed of 10 Null trials and five pairs of ER trials (20 trials per cycle, Fig. 1 E, Left). Fig. 2A shows memory profiles of the four conditions during ER trials, averaged in six cycles, in Blocks 1 and 4. Memory profiles were similar between Promote and Suppress in each TMS condition in Block 1. Then, in Block 4, group differences were apparent in the DLPFC but not in the PMd, suggesting that stimulation of the PMd attenuated effects of LOS, i.e., meta-learning effects. For comparison, the original results (4) without TMS (NoTMS) are shown in the Bottom panel of Fig. 2A, although they are not included in the main statistical analysis below. (Statistical comparisons with NoTMS are available in SI Appendix.)

To quantify behavior, the degree of learning over trials was approximated by a linear fit to each learning curve, i.e., memory profiles over ER trials, as shown in Fig. 2A. The slope of a linear fit, slope, approximated the overall rate of learning. Here, the focus was how the slope changed over blocks (from Block 1 to Block 4) and how that differed between conditions. Therefore, the baseline slope (in Block 1) and the rate of change in the slope over blocks, Δslope, were estimated for each condition. Specifically, a linear mixed-effects model was fit to individual learning curves to estimate slope and Δslope at the group level while considering individual biases in the hand direction. Note that to stabilize estimation of the change that would accumulate over blocks (4), all blocks were included in the fitting to estimate Δslope per block instead of only comparing Block 1 and Block 4.

Also, to represent different group comparisons, e.g., Promote vs. Suppress, PMd vs. DLPFC, three coding sets were used (see Materials and Methods for details). First, Δslope was estimated separately for each condition (Fig. 2 B, Top), which apparently captured changes in learning curves (Fig. 2A). Δslope was significantly nonzero positive in PMdProm (μ = 0.05, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.08], P = 0.001) and PFCProm (μ = 0.16, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.19], P < 10−25). On the other hand, Δslope was not significantly nonzero in either PMdSupp (μ = 0.009, 95% CI = [−0.02, 0.04], P = 0.61) or PFCSupp (μ = −0.03, 95% CI = [−0.06, 0.002], P = 0.07), although the latter was close to significantly nonzero negative.

Note that these separate estimations would not control factors that might also influence motor adaptation over time, e.g., fatigue. Thus, to statistically evaluate these group differences in Δslope while controlling those factors, a different coding set in the mixed-effects model was used to directly estimate differences between Promote and Suppress in Δslope for each TMS location (Fig. 2 B, Middle, see Materials and Methods for details). Δslope was significantly higher in Promote than Suppress both in PMd (μ = 0.044, 95% CI = [0.006, 0.081], P = 0.02) and in DLPFC (μ = 0.193, 95% CI = [0.155, 0.231], P < 10−22). While both differences were significant, the difference seemed smaller in PMd than DLPFC, as suggested by the estimated means and the nonoverlapping 95% CI. To confirm this, a difference in meta-learning effects between PMd and DLPFC was directly estimated by yet another coding set for the mixed-effects model (Fig. 2 B, Bottom). As expected, the difference (PMd minus DLPFC) was significantly negative (μ = −0.15, 95% CI = [−0.20, −0.10], P < 10−7), confirming that the meta-learning was attenuated more in PMd than DLPFC. A supplementary analysis (SI Appendix) further supports the results by comparing these meta-learning effects with NoTMS from the original study (4) by demonstrating that meta-learning effects were comparable between NoTMS and DLPFC, but significantly attenuated in PMd compared to NoTMS.

The original study suggested that meta-learning is driven by reinforcement learning by demonstrating improvement in score performance in both Promote and Suppress (4). Here, score performance was examined in four conditions with TMS by separately estimating the linear slope of changes in score performance over blocks (Fig. 2C). Note that since the score was negative, the score was considered improved when it was less negative, i.e., closer to 0. Score performance significantly improved in both PFCProm (μ = 0.19, 95% CI = [0.06, 0.33], P = 0.004) and PFCSupp (μ = 0.22, 95% CI = [0.07, 0.37], P = 0.004), consistent with the original results without TMS. On the other hand, score performance was not significant in either PMdProm (μ = −0.03, 95% CI = [−0.17, 0.11], P = 0.67) or PMdSupp (μ = 0.13, 95% CI = [−0.01, 0.28], P = 0.08). The analysis of score improvement was consistent with the analysis of meta-learning training effects, both of which suggested impaired meta-learning in PMd compared to DLPFC.

Probe.

In motor adaptation, an overall degree of learning can be influenced by two learning properties: error sensitivity (learning rate) and retention rate (forgetting rate) of motor memory (4, 34). Error sensitivity refers to the degree of update per error unit. The retention rate of motor memory refers to the strength of memory against decay over time. Since meta-learning influences both learning properties (4), impaired meta-learning in PMd can be due to altered meta-learning of either error sensitivity, retention rate of motor memory, or both. Again, Probe was designed to separately estimate the two learning properties, allowing us to examine how TMS modulated effects of meta-learning training on the two properties that would accumulate over the blocks.

Probe consisted of two main subphases (Fig. 1 E, Right). After five trials without rotation (Probe trials 1 to 5), in the first learning phase, participants adapted to a step perturbation over 10 trials (Probe trials 6 to 15). Here, since memory was washed out before the learning phase, the slope of the initial memory update would approximate error sensitivity (4). Then, once motor memory accumulated in response to the step perturbation over 10 trials, participants performed the second retention phase, where the error was clamped to zero by fixing the cursor feedback direction to the target for 15 trials (Probe trials 16 to 30). Therefore, error sensitivity would not influence memory updates, and the slope of decay over trials would approximate the retention rate of motor memory (35). Thus, learning behavior was analyzed with the same linear mixed-effects model fitting to individual learning and retention curves to estimate slope and Δslope at the group level while considering individual biases in reach direction, except that we estimated two separate slopes, learning slope and retention slope (Fig. 1 E, Right). See Materials and Methods for details. Again, to stabilize estimation of the change that would accumulate over blocks (4), all blocks were included in the fitting to estimate Δslope per block instead of only comparing Block 1 and Block 4.

The Fig. 2D shows the group average memory profiles during the learning and retention phases of Probe in Blocks 1 and 4. For comparison, the original results without TMS (NoTMS) are shown in the Bottom panel of Fig. 2D, although again, they are not included in the main statistical analyses below. (Statistical comparisons with NoTMS are available in SI Appendix.)

The Fig. 2E shows Δslope from Block 1 to Block 4 during the learning phase, which approximates error sensitivity. As with Fig. 2B, and F shows separate group estimation (Top), meta-learning effects (Promote–Suppress) in each TMS location (Middle), and effects of TMS location on meta-learning effects (PMd minus DLPFC, Bottom). In separate estimation (Fig. 2 E, Top), Δslope was significantly nonzero positive in PFCProm (μ = 0.50, 95% CI = [0.09, 0.92], P = 0.02). On the other hand, Δslope was not significantly nonzero in either PMdProm (μ = 0.32, 95% CI = [−0.13, 0.76], P = 0.16), PMdSupp (μ = 0.41, 95% CI = [−0.04, 0.85], P = 0.08) or PFCSupp (μ = 0.30, 95% CI = [−0.15, 0.75], P = 0.19). Also, unlike meta-learning training, meta-learning effects (Fig. 2 E, Middle) were not significant either in PMd (μ = −0.09, 95% CI = [−0.62, 0.43], P = 0.73) or DLPFC (μ = 0.20, 95% CI = [−0.30, 0.71], P = 0.43). Therefore, effects of TMS location were not significant either (Fig. 2 E, Bottom, μ = −0.29, 95% CI = [−1.03, 0.44], P = 0.43). Thus, no evidence was found for meta-learning effects on error sensitivity in either TMS location or the difference between TMS locations in meta-learning effects.

The Fig. 2F shows Δslope from Block 1 to Block 4 during the retention phase, which approximates the retention rate of motor memory. In separate estimation (Fig. 2 F, Top), Δslope was significantly nonzero positive in PFCProm (μ = 0.03, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.05], P < 10−5) and negative in PMdProm (μ = −0.02, 95% CI = [−0.03, −0.008], P = 0.003) and PMdSupp (μ = −0.03, 95% CI = [−0.04, −0.01], P = 0.0006). On the other hand, Δslope was not significantly nonzero in either or PFCSupp (μ = 0.30, 95% CI = [−0.15, 0.75], P = 0.19). Here, meta-learning effects (Fig. 2F, Middle) were significantly positive in DLPFC (μ = 0.034, 95% CI = [0.017, 0.051], P < 10−4) but not in PMd (μ = 0.003, 95% CI = [−0.014, 0.020], P = 0.70). Therefore, the difference (PMd minus DLPFC) was significantly negative for meta-learning effects for the retention rate of motor memory (μ = −0.031, 95% CI = [−0.055, −0.007], P = 0.01), consistent with the difference in meta-learning training (Fig. 2 B, Bottom).

Taken together, the results suggest that meta-learning of retention rate of motor memory is significantly attenuated by TMS to PMd compared to DLPFC, whereas results are inconclusive (nonsignificant) about effects of TMS locations on meta-learning of error sensitivity. A control analysis (SI Appendix) confirms that potential order effects do not alter these statistical conclusions. In addition, a supplementary analysis (SI Appendix) further supports the results by comparing these meta-learning effects with NoTMS from the original experiment, where meta-learning is attenuated in PMd compared to DLPFC/NoTMS in the retention phase, whereas differences are not confirmed between the three groups in the learning phase.

Discussion

The present study examines a potential implication of PMd in meta-learning in motor adaptation, in contrast with PFC, which is believed crucial for meta-learning in decision making. The present study demonstrates that TMS over PMd indeed attenuates effects of meta-learning training according to the association between motor adaptation and future reward outcomes, i.e., LOS. Examining training effects in the conventional Probe task, the present study reveals that TMS over PMd attenuates meta-learning of the retention rate of motor memory, whereas no effects are evident for meta-learning of error sensitivity. Since meta-learning of retention rate of motor memory is evident when TMS is applied to the DLPFC, disruptive effects cannot be due to mere artifacts of TMS. Thus, this study provides empirical evidence that the PMd, not the DLPFC, is central in meta-learning in motor adaptation, suggesting different meta-learning networks between decision making and motor adaptation.

In motor learning and control, the PMd has been known for two learning processes, reinforcement learning on the one hand and motor adaptation on the other. Being sensitive to rewards (25–27), the PMd is implicated in selection, preparation, and execution of goal-directed actions through associative learning (25, 36). Meanwhile, being sensitive to sensory prediction errors (20), the PMd is also implicated in learning from sensory prediction errors and retaining learned behavior (16, 17, 21–24). While the dual role of the PMd has been accepted, it is puzzling that the same region of the brain contributes to two seemingly different learning processes. The present study links the dual role with a third role of the PMd in cognitive regulation of motor adaptation, suggesting that the PMd utilizes reinforcement learning to regulate motor adaptation.

This reveals the third role of the PMd in forming goal-directed plans for motor adaptation through a reinforcement learning algorithm. A principal computational process of motor adaptation is updating and retaining motor memory to reduce sensory prediction errors, but this process exhibits considerable adaptability to tasks and environments (4, 34, 37). Specifically, the brain can regulate learning parameters (error sensitivity and retention rate of motor memory) to maximize rewards or minimize punishment based on a structure between motor adaptation and reward outcome, i.e., LOS (4). This flexible planning according to LOS parallels reinforcement learning, in which the brain optimizes actions to maximize outcomes based on an action-outcome structure. That is, in meta-learning, the brain optimizes learning parameters to maximize outcomes based on LOS. Our results suggest that TMS application to the PMd is likely to intervene in this goal-directed planning of motor adaptation, attenuating both upregulation in Promote and downregulation in Suppress. Thus, these TMS effects differ from the previously reported mere enhancement (38) or attenuation of motor adaptation (24). The PMd functions beyond monotonic modulation of motor adaptation, participating in optimizing plans to maximize long-term reward outcomes of motor adaptation by integrating reinforcement learning and motor adaptation. Such integration enables the brain to deal with environments where reward maximization (reinforcement learning) and error minimization (motor adaptation) are in conflict, as in the Suppress condition. In such environments, the brain can down-regulate motor adaptation for reward maximization even if that compromises error minimization, and the downregulation is shown to remain in Probe, a typical task where reward maximization is no longer in conflict with error minimization.

However, this role of the PMd in meta-learning may be limited to regulation of the retention rate of motor memory. In Probe, significant attenuation of meta-learning is found in the retention phase, but not in the learning phase, raising the possibility that the PMd is selectively involved in meta-learning in the retention rate of motor memory, but not in error sensitivity. While a conclusion about potential selectivity must be withheld with nonsignificant results, this possibility is plausible, given different neural representations and temporal dynamics between learning from error and retention of motor memory. Indeed, conventional neural representations of motor adaptation suggest that sensory prediction error-driven updates occur in the cerebellum while motor memories are stored in cortical regions (38, 39). Such dissociation is rational given their different time scales, where a memory update occurs within a hundred milliseconds after experiencing sensory prediction error (40) while motor memories must be stored for a much longer time, on a scale of seconds or even minutes (39). Also, growing evidence suggests that the cerebellum processes not only sensory error-driven updates but also reward information (41). Therefore, the cerebellum is another possible site that can achieve meta-learning by integrating reward and motor learning information, and given its crucial involvement in sensory prediction error-based learning, meta-learning of error sensitivity may be processed in the cerebellum, in contrast with meta-learning of retention rate of motor memory in the PMd. Note that regardless of whether the present TMS effects are truly selective, the present results still support the main hypothesis that there is a distinct neural circuit for meta-learning in motor adaptation in which the PMd is central, at least in meta-learning of the retention rate of motor memory.

Theoretically, meta-learning is facilitated through a hierarchical system in which a higher-level reinforcement learning algorithm monitors and controls a lower-level motor adaptation process (Fig. 1A) (4). This meta-learning mechanism is distinct from the previously suggested additive contribution of explicit aiming strategies to implicit motor adaptation (42). However, it remains unclear whether meta-learning driven by reinforcement learning at a higher layer is explicit or implicit. It is possible that the monitoring and control at the higher layer could be an explicit process, potentially mediated by the PFC (6, 8), similar to how the explicit aiming strategy is mediated by the DLPFC (30). Alternatively, it might be an implicit process mediated by higher motor areas (24). It is also plausible that some aspects of the meta-learning process at the higher layer are explicit, while others are implicit. Although this question remains unresolved, we speculate that at least part of the meta-learning process is implicit and mediated by the PMd. This is particularly supported by our finding that TMS applied to the PMd inhibits individuals from regulating (through meta-learning) the retention rate of motor memory.

As components of motor circuits, the PM(d), M1, and the cerebellum have different functions and interactions with other regions to fulfill their own roles in motor learning (28, 43). The present study has identified a unique contribution of the PMd in regulation of motor adaptation through meta-learning by reinforcement learning. This suggests that the brain recruits different neural circuits between meta-learning in motor adaptation and meta-learning in decision making. Perhaps this is because PMd is situated between the DLPFC and M1 (11, 12), thereby functioning as part of the (meta)cognitive, reward, and motor systems, serving as an information hub between them.

Materials and Methods

Participants.

Forty-four right-handed individuals with no history of motor or neurological disorders participated (22 males, aged 18 to 26, mean age 21.5). The number of datasets for each condition is reported below. The Edinburgh Handedness Inventory was used to confirm handedness. For each participation, they were paid 2,760 or 2,880 JPY as compensation for their time, plus additional performance-based compensation up to 1,000 JPY. Note the two amounts of compensation for time were due to an increase in the local minimum wage during data collection.

Task Design.

The task design was mostly identical to the previous study about human meta-learning in motor learning (Fig. 1 D and E) (4), except for addition of TMS and modifications for the addition, as described below.

General.

The task was performed by manipulating a homemade robot manipulandum that only moved in a horizontal plane. Participants sat on a chair and moved a handle with the right hand. Above the horizontal task space, a mirror was placed, occluding the forearm and the hand. A computer screen above the mirror displayed visual stimuli, and participants saw stimuli that were reflected in the mirror. The screen, mirror, and handle were equally spaced in height so that visual stimuli appeared at the same height as the task space. The manipulandum was homemade and operated with Python 3.7.9, LabView 2019, and C++.

Trial flow.

Fig. 1D shows the general flow of a single trial. In each trial, participants moved the handle to bring the cursor (a 5-mm white circle) onto the start position (a 9-mm gray circle) right in front of them. Then, a target (a 5-mm dark blue circle) appeared 10 cm from the start point, and 500 ms later, the target became brighter for 100 ms and then disappeared, served as a go-signal. This modification was made from the original study to ensure that in case TMS induced eye blink, the participants still knew the target position. Participants made a quick, shooting movement, and the target reappeared when the hand reached 8 cm. To avoid use-dependent learning (44), target location was randomly selected from one of seven directions: −15°, −10°, −5°, and 0° (right in front of the participant), 5°, 10°, and 15°.

Trial type and task schedule.

There were four types of trials, as in the original study: Null, Sensory Error (E), Reward (R), and Error-Clamp trials. In Null trials, veridical online cursor feedback about the hand position was provided as participants moved toward the target. In E trials (Fig. 1E), a rotational perturbation was applied to online cursor feedback, creating a discrepancy between the cursor and the actual hand position to induce a sensory prediction error. The degree of rotation was manipulated among E trials, as described below. In addition, the reappeared target was shifted to the cursor direction so that the cursor crossed the target, clamping the task error to zero (TE-clamp). Thus, a sensory prediction error, but not a task error was presented in E trials (37). In R trials (Fig. 1E), participants moved to the target without cursor feedback, as the cursor disappeared at the go-signal. The target did not reappear at crossing, and a numerical score was displayed based on aftereffect, y, the angular distance between the target direction T and the hand direction at crossing h (i.e., y = T – h). y reflected the size of updated motor memory in response to experienced sensory prediction error. A score was calculated according to score functions, which differed between conditions, as described below. In Error-Clamp trials (Fig. 1E), cursor direction was fixed toward the target while preserving a movement distance from the start, clamping both sensory prediction error and task error to 0 regardless of actual hand direction (35).

The task consisted of two main phases: meta-learning training and Probe (Fig. 1E). In meta-learning training, there were six cycles of training, each of which consisted of 10 Null trials (Washout), followed by five pairs of E and R trials. The rotation degree in E trials was +5°. Since score (reward) feedback and TMS were provided (as discussed below) during training, there might be reward-based bias in hand direction (31) and/or artifact of stimulation that influenced observed behavior. Thus, the Probe phase was used for measuring motor adaptation ability in a typical visuomotor rotation task without a potential influence of score or TMS. It consisted of 5 E trials without rotation, followed by the two subphases: the learning phase with 10 E trials with +7° and by the retention phase with 15 Error-Clamp trials.

An experiment session comprised four blocks (Fig. 1 E, Bottom). The first block started with a Probe phase, followed by a meta-learning training phase and another Probe phase. To measure baseline behavior without score (reward) feedback, the score was removed in R trials only in the first block. The remaining blocks consisted of a meta-learning training phase followed by a Probe phase. Each Probe phase was preceded by 30 Null trials and also followed by one-min break.

Instruction.

The same instruction as the original study was used (4), except that an additional instruction about TMS was included. Participants were told, “There may be some manipulation of the cursor and/or the target. Even if you notice any manipulation, your task remains the same: to move your hand to the target as best you can. Also, pay attention to the task while ignoring brain stimulation as much as possible. If you feel any disturbing effects of TMS, including, but not limited to eye blink, facial/arm twitch, nausea, and dizziness, notify us immediately.” They answered a posttask written questionnaire to confirm that they did not intentionally aim other than the target direction and that they did not experience side effects. Participants were also told that 1) one point was worth 1 JPY, 2) additional compensation was initially maximum (1,000 JPY), but decreased as the total negative score increased, and 3) the task goal was to reach the target as accurately as possible to minimize the loss. They were also informed about feedback presented in each type of trial (Null, E, and R), and practiced all of them briefly before the task. On the other hand, the following information was not given to participants: the exact manipulation of the cursor and the target, the order of trial types, the relationship between hand direction and score, and expected effects of TMS.

Stimulation parameters and protocols.

For timing of stimulation, TMS was delivered at the onset of the go-signal, right before experiencing the sensory prediction error (24). Namely, TMS was delivered to the PMd at the time of target presentation in E trials. To prevent TMS from functioning as a cue for rotation/sensory prediction error, TMS was also delivered at every odd Null trial, so that only half of TMS coincided with rotation. Stimulation intensity was set to 90% of the resting motor threshold for first dorsal interosseous (FDI) to avoid muscle twitch in the hand. The resting motor threshold was defined as the minimum intensity required to induce a motor evoked potential of at least 50 μV 50% of the time, while the target muscle was relaxed (45). The location of PMd was defined as 2 cm anterior and 1 cm medial to the “hotspot” of FDI (46), whereas the location of the DLPFC was defined as F3 in the 10 to 20 system (47) (Fig. 1C). Stimulation was delivered to the left hemisphere, i.e., contralateral to the hand used in the motor task.

Experimental groups, score calculation, and study design.

There were two independent variables with two levels each, leading to four conditions in total. One independent variable was TMS location, which was either the PMd or DLPFC. The other independent variable was LOS, which was either Promote or Suppress. As in the original study (4), the two had different score functions that determined the score based on the size of aftereffect (y), which reflected the size of updated motor memory in response to experienced sensory prediction errors (Fig. 1E). The same score functions were used such that score = S⋅y + s0. In Promote, [S, s0] = [1, −5] so that the score was low without aftereffect (learning), but increased as participants showed more aftereffect; therefore, it was intended to promote more learning. On the other hand, in Suppress, [S, s0] = [−1, 0] so that the score was high without aftereffect (learning), but decreased as participants showed more aftereffect; therefore, its effect was to suppress learning. The maximum score was set to 0 so that the sign of the score did not change. One point was worth 1 JPY, and additional monetary compensation was given to participants at the end of an experimental session. Thus, the four conditions were labeled: PMd-Promote (PMdProm), PMd-Suppress (PMdSupp), DLPFC-Promote (PFCProm), and DLPFC-Suppress (PFCSupp).

The study employed a within-subject design to reduce interindividual variability. To minimize order and carryover effects, the order of conditions was randomized across participants, and there were at least 3 d between sessions. Only one condition was used per session, and returning participants were not assigned to the same condition again. For each condition, 25 sessions of data collection were scheduled so that, even with some dropout, the final number of datasets would be about 20 per condition, as in the original work (4). Indeed, not all participants returned to complete all sessions. Also, we terminated sessions for two participants who reported mild sickness during motor thresholding (before the task). Reparticipation was not allowed for them. Thus, in the end, numbers of datasets were as follows: PMdProm (N = 21), PMdSupp (N = 20), PFCProm (N = 25), and PFCSupp (N = 20). Note that the data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (described in detail below), which could handle dropout (missing data) while considering interindividual variability as random effects (48).

Data Analysis.

Estimation of motor adaptation and meta-learning.

The goal of the present analyses was to estimate rates of learning/retention from observed data and to evaluate how they differ between conditions. Here, rates were approximated by slopes from linear fits, as done previously (28). Observed data were individual aftereffects (y), which changed over trials and formed learning/retention curves. Note that previous literature suggested a bias in a two-step approach in which individual parameters were estimated and then statistically tested (49). Therefore, linear mixed-effects model analyses were used to directly estimate slopes at the condition (group) level while considering individual biases (differences) in y. Specifically, in a given condition (cond), block (blk), individual (i), and trial number (k), y was modeled as[1]ycond,blk,i,k=slopecond,blkk+b0+r(i),

where slopes represented rates of learning/retention. b0 was a global intercept (initial memory size), which was modified by the random intercept effect, r(i) that represented a bias for the i-th individual. Meta-learning was manifested as changes in a slope over blocks, Δslope; thus, a main interest was how Δslope differed between groups. Δslope was also approximated by a linear fit as[2]slopecond,blk=slope0+Δslopecond·blk,

where slope0 denoted an initial slope in Block 1. Combining (Eq. 1) and (Eq. 2) yielded[3]ycond,blk,i,k=slope0+Δslopecond·blkk+b0+r(i).

Using (3), Δslope could be estimated for each condition from observed individual data (y).

Estimations were performed separately for meta-learning training and learning and the retention phase of the probe. In meta-learning training, to avoid possible artifacts of TMS, the last Null trial and all R trials were included (corresponding to cycle trials 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20. See Figs. 1E and 2A). In the learning phase of the Probe, as in the original experiment, slope and Δslope were estimated from the initial learning from error in the learning phase (Probe trials 6 to 7, see Figs. 1E and 2D) as an approximation of error sensitivity. Finally, in the retention phase of Probe, all trials Probe trials 16 to 30 (see Figs. 1E and 2D) were included as an approximation of retention rate of motor memory because, in theory, gradual changes in the reach direction would reflect partial retention when error was clamped to 0 (35). While slope0 and Δslope were separately estimated in Probe, the random effect (r) was shared between the learning phase and the retention phase. Thus, an additional variable, phase, was included in Equation: [3] such that[4]ycond,blk,i,k,phase=slope0phase+Δslopecond,phase·blkkphase+b0(phase)+r(i).

Note that (Eq. 4) is identical to (Eq. 3) when there is only one phase (as in meta-learning training). For convenience, 1) blk was aligned to 0; 2) k was aligned to 0 in each task/phase; and 3) the increment of k was rescaled to 1 for meta-learning training data where every other trial was included.

Since conditions (cond) were a categorical variable, they needed to be recoded for mixed-effects model analyses. Here, three sets of coding were used (summarized in SI Appendix, Tables S1–S3). The first set was for separate estimation of Δslope, where Δslope was represented as[5]Δslopecond=β1PMdprom+β2PMdsupp+β3PFCprom+β4PFCsupp.

Here, each regressor was set to 1 for the corresponding condition while it was set to 0 for other conditions. Each coefficient c represented Δslope for that condition. The second set was for estimating meta-learning effects, i.e., Promote – Suppress, in each TMS location, in which Δslope was represented as[6]Δslopecond=β1PMd+β2PMd ·LOS+β3PFC+β4PFC· LOS,

where LOS represented LOSs (Promote = 1, Suppress = 0) and where PMd and PFC represented TMS locations that were set to 1 for corresponding conditions and 0 for other conditions. Main coefficients of interest in this set were β2 and β4, which represented the meta-learning effects in PMd and DLPFC, respectively. Finally, the third set was for contrasting meta-learning effects between PMd and DLPFC, where Δslope was represented as[7]Δslopecond=β1Reference+β2TMS+β3LOS+β4TMS ·LOS.

Reference was set to 1 for all conditions, and it was called “reference” because it represented Δslope for the condition where the other regressors, TMS and LOS, were both set to 0 (in this analysis PFCSupp, as the other regressors are set as following). TMS was set to 1 for PMd and 0 for DLPFC. LOS was again set to 1 for Promote and 0 for Suppress. The main coefficient of interest in this set was β4, which represented the difference in meta-learning effects between PMd and DLPFC. Note that these three sets were essentially performing the same estimations, but they gave estimated values (mean, CI, and P-value) for different factors/contrasts by changing how each coefficient was estimated independently or in relation to other coefficient(s). For example, in the first set, the estimated mean Δslope of PFCProm was 0.16 in the meta-learning training (SI Appendix, Table S4). The same value could be obtained in the second set by summing the estimated mean Δslope of PFCSupp and the difference between PFCProm and PFCSupp, which was −0.03 + 0.19 = 0.16 (SI Appendix, Table S5). On the other hand, only the first set directly computed the CI and P-value for PFCProm, whereas only the second set computed the CI and P-value for the difference between PFCProm and PFCSupp.

Estimation of improvement in score.

For analysis of the improvement in score performance, only the first coding set was used to examine which conditions showed improvement and which did not. Also, Eq. 3 needed to be modified as follows. First, average scores per trial in each block, score, were used as observed data instead of hand direction (y); thus, k was removed. Second, rates (slopes) of improvement were represented by a single variable, a, for each condition, i.e., a(cond). Third, intercepts differed notably between Promote and Suppress (Fig. 2C) because of different score functions; thus, another random intercept effect for LOS, r(LOS), was included. Finally, the random intercept effect of hand direction (r(i)) affected the score differently under Promote and Suppress, so it needed to be estimated not only for each individual (i) but also for each LOS. Taken together, the equation was modified as follows:[8]score∼slopecond∙blk+b0+rLOS+r(i,LOS).

Other notes.

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 with the lme4 (50) and lmerTest (51) packages. Data preprocessing was identical to that in the original study (4). Specifically, to minimize potential online feedback correction of hand movements, y was calculated when the hand reached 5 cm from the start. However, since hand reaches were not perfectly straight, measuring y at the midpoint resulted in a small bias. Therefore, the average bias measured at baseline trials in Block 1 was subtracted from all reach direction data. All tests were performed as two-sided tests, and the threshold for statistical significance was set at 0.05. While key statistical summaries are reported in the results, more detailed summaries are available in SI Appendix, Tables S4–S10.
