Sharp Convergence Rates for Masked Diffusion Models
Yuchen Liang, Zhiheng TanNess ShroffYingbin LiangThe Ohio State University

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models have achieved strong empirical performance in text and other symbolic domains, with masked (absorbing-rate) variants emerging as competitive alternatives to autoregressive models. Among existing samplers, the Euler method remains the standard choice in many applications, and more recently, the First-Hitting Sampler (FHS) has shown considerable promise for masked diffusion models. Despite their practical success, the theoretical understanding of these samplers remains limited. Existing analyses are conducted in Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, which often yields loose parameter dependencies and requires strong assumptions on score estimation. Moreover, these guarantees do not cover recently developed high-performance sampler of FHS. In this work, we first develop a direct total-variation (TV)–based analysis for the Euler method that overcomes these limitations. Our results relax assumptions on score estimation, improve parameter dependencies, and establish convergence guarantees without requiring any surrogate initialization. Also for this setting, we provide the first convergence lower bound for the Euler sampler, establishing tightness with respect to both the data dimension d d and the target accuracy ε \varepsilon . Finally, we analyze the FHS sampler and show that it incurs no sampling error beyond that induced by score estimation, which we show to be tight with a matching lower error bound. Overall, our analysis introduces a direct TV-based error decomposition along the CTMC trajectory and a decoupling-based path-wise analysis for FHS, which may be of independent interest.

Diffusion models have become one of the central paradigms of generative modeling, offering state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of domains, including image synthesis

Recently, for applications in discrete and symbolic domains such as language, graphs, and molecules

Despite these empirical advances, the theoretical understanding of discrete diffusion models, especially MDMs, remains limited.
Existing works using deterministic-step-size samplers have largely relied on convergence analyses in terms of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, most under the uniform-rate matrix
[
5
,
35
,
29
,
30
,
23

, and, more recently, some under the absorbing-rate
[

,

,
7

.
However, there are currently no lower bounds showing whether existing KL-based guarantees for discrete diffusion are tight. In
continuous
diffusion, prior work
[
20
]
often finds that TV analyses yield sharper bounds and more accurate parameter dependence such as the dimension
d
. This motivates a TV-based analysis for
discrete
diffusion to obtain tighter guarantees that potentially admit matching lower bounds. Doing so is nontrivial, since KL-specific tools such as change-of-measure arguments
[
30
]
do not extend to TV, and the discontinuity of the underlying space
[
23
]
introduces additional technical challenges.
Furthermore, these KL-based guarantees often require strong assumptions on the estimated score function. For example, all of
[
5
,
35
,
30
,
23
]
require the assumption of a bounded score estimate, which needs typically be achieved through an additional clipping step during training
[
35
]
.
These limitations give rise to our first central question.

Question 1: Can we establish convergence guarantees with
tighter
parameter dependencies via a direct analysis in TV distance? Moreover, can we derive matching lower bounds that complement the existing upper bounds?

More recently, the First-Hitting Sampler (FHS) has been shown to be highly effective for generation with MDMs
[
36
]
. Unlike classical deterministic-step-size samplers, such as Euler methods, whose number of steps must grow unboundedly to achieve vanishing TV distance, FHS can produce a high-quality sample in exactly
d
steps, where
d
denotes the data dimension. Despite its empirical success, the theoretical error bound of FHS has not been explored. Given its different design philosophy, the analysis of FHS requires new techniques that explicitly exploit its underlying structure. This leads us to the next question below.

Question 2. Given a
d
-step sampling procedure like FHS, how to characterize the sampling error of the algorithm? Moreover, can we also derive a matching lower bound for FHS?

In this work, we address both questions and give affirmative answers to both of them.

1.1
Our Contributions

The central contributions of this work are (i) a new direct TV analysis tailored to the Euler method for masked diffusion models with a matching lower bound, and (ii) a novel analysis for the FHS algorithm, leading to
tight error bound that matches our lower bound.
Our detailed contributions include:

Improved Parameter Dependencies for the Euler Method (with straightforward generalization to
\tau
-leaping type samplers):
For the Euler method for the absorbing-rate (masked) discrete diffusion models,
we strengthen existing theoretical guarantees with an improved
\varepsilon
dependence while retaining the best-known dependencies on
d
and
S
.
Note that the same guarantees can be straightforwardly extended to vanilla
\tau
-leaping samplers using the technique in
[
23
]
.
In particular, we show that, in order to reach a slightly perturbed target distribution, the required number of steps is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(dS/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)
. Compared to prior results, ours yields an improvement by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
. Notably, our analysis removes the need for bounded score assumptions and removes the need for a surrogate initialization typically required in previous works.

Tight Lower Bound for the Euler Method:
We provide, to our knowledge, the first
lower-bound
analysis for absorbing-rate discrete diffusion models using the Euler method in TV distance. Combined with our improved upper-bound, we show that our bound is tight in both the data dimension
d
and the target accuracy
\varepsilon
with constant step-sizes and without early-stopping.
Our result identifies the intrinsic limits of convergence when the Euler method is used.

Error Analysis for FHS and Tight Lower Bound:

We perform a rigorous analysis for the First-Hitting Sampler (FHS)
[
36
]
for masked diffusion models, which generates a sample in exactly
d
steps.
Specifically, we show that FHS incurs no sampling error beyond that arising from score estimation itself. In particular, the resulting error bound is independent of both the data dimension
d
and the vocabulary size
S
.
We further show that this bound is tight, by constructing a worst-case instance for which the FHS output error matches exactly the upper bound of the score estimation error. Our result further highlights the advantages of masked diffusion models, for which efficient finite-step sampling with dimension-free guarantees is possible.
Specifically, we develop a novel error analysis that decomposes the total sampling error into token-wise components. Our key insight is that FHS exploits an
intrinsic decoupling structure
in MDMs: the transition time
\tau
and transition index
i
can be sampled exactly from the true reverse process, regardless of the accuracy of score estimation. Consequently, the overall sampling error depends solely on the estimation error incurred at each predicted token, and is therefore independent of
d
and
S
.

Related Works

See
Appendix

A

Theory on Uniform-Rate Discrete Diffusion Models.
Considerable effort has been devoted to improving convergence guarantees for uniform-rate diffusion models. Unlike continuous diffusion, the resulting guarantees in the discrete setting crucially depend on the choice of sampler. Early analyses focused on uniformization-based methods
[
5
,
30
]
, exact per-step samplers
[
35
]
, and
\tau
-leaping schemes
[
3
,
30
]
. More recently, theoretical guarantees have been established for DMPM
[
29
,
7
]
as well as for more practical Tweedie
\tau
-leaping and Euler method
[
23
]
.
Notably, all of these results are derived in terms of KL divergence, which yields suboptimal guarantees when translated to total variation distance.

Theory on Masked (Absorbing-rate) Discrete Diffusion Models.

[
22
]
was the first to establish convergence guarantees for masked diffusion models under the
\tau
-leaping and the uniformization samplers, showing improved dimensional dependence compared to uniform-rate diffusion.
[
15
]
studied faster convergence rates using the Euler method and a specialized uniformization scheme, the Mask-Aware Truncated Uniformization (MATU), which eliminates the need for a bounded score assumption. In parallel,
[
7
]
derived improved dimension-dependent rates for the DMPM sampler, also without assuming bounded score.

Comparison with concurrent work
[
10
]
.
After we submitted this paper to a conference, a concurrent work
[
10
]
was recently posted on arXiv, which also studies convergence guarantees for absorbing-rate discrete diffusion models albeit under a modified truncated
\tau
-leaping sampler (in addition to uniform-rate models, which is not the focus of our paper). The two papers differ in several important ways. (i) The two works make orthogonal improvements over prior convergence results for masked diffusion models.
[
10
]
derives a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D}/\epsilon)
under the KL divergence, where
\mathcal{D}\leq d\log S
is a distribution-dependent quantity, improving previous bounds in the dependence on
S
and potentially
d
. In contrast, our analysis focuses on Euler discretization under the total variation (TV) metric for general distributions, improving prior results by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\epsilon})
. Moreover, the TV metric allows us to start from the all-mask singleton distribution (as is typical in practice) without resorting to a surrogate initialization. (ii) We establish the first lower bound for masked diffusion models, which matches our upper bound in its dependence on
d
and
\epsilon
, whereas
[
10
]
does not provide a lower bound for masked diffusion models. (iii) We also analyze the recently introduced, highly efficient masked diffusion sampler FHS, and show that it attains provable
\epsilon
-accuracy with the best-known sampling complexity of finite
d
steps for masked diffusion models. This sampler is not considered in
[
10
]
.

2
Preliminaries of Discrete Diffusion Models

As in standard diffusion models, discrete diffusion models comprise a forward noising process and a reverse generation process. In this section, we describe two common frameworks used to model discrete diffusion dynamics.

2.1
CTMC Forward Process

Discrete diffusion models can be modeled as continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) defined over the
discrete
data space
[S]^{d}
. Here
d
is the number of tokens and each token is drawn from a vocabulary of size
S

[
3
]
.
In the forward process, the initial data is denoted by
x_{0}\in[S]^{d}
, with corresponding probability mass function (p.m.f.)
q_{0}
. Let
R_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{S^{d}\times S^{d}}
be the rate matrix of the forward process. For states
x,y\in[S]^{d}
,
R_{t}(x,y)
specifies the instantaneous rate of transition from
x
to
y
at time
t
. The conditional distribution of
y
at
t+\Delta t
given
x
at
t
satisfies

q_{t+\Delta t|t}(y|x)=\mathds{1}\left\{y=x\right\}+R_{t}(x,y)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)

where
\mathds{1}\left\{\cdot\right\}
is the indicator function. For a valid CTMC,
R_{t}(x,y)\geq 0
whenever
x\neq y
, and
\sum_{y}R_{t}(x,y)=0
.

To simplify computation for large
S
and
d
, it is common to assume that tokens evolve
independently
and
homogeneously
across dimensions
[
3
,
25
]
. Under this assumption, the forward conditional distribution factorizes as

q_{t|0}(x_{t}|x_{0})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}q_{t|0}^{i}(x_{t}^{i}|x_{0}^{i}),

(1)

where
q_{t|0}^{i}
is the conditional for the
i
-th token. Equivalently, if we define the corresponding token-level rate matrix by
R_{t}^{tok}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times S}
, then
[
3
]
shows that

R_{t}(x,y)=\begin{cases}R_{t}^{tok}(x^{i},y^{i}),&\text{if }\mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)=1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)
is the Hamming distance between
x
and
y
.
Following
[
3
]
, we set
R_{t}^{tok}=\beta_{t}R_{\text{base}}
with a noise schedule
\beta_{t}>0
. In this paper, we focus on the constant schedule
\beta_{t}\equiv 1
, as in prior works
[
30
,
35
,
23
,
22
]
.

In the literature of CTMC framework,
the
absorbing-rate
(corresponding to masked diffusion model) matrix has attracted special attention
[
2
,
25
]
.
Define one vocabulary word as
\mathrm{[MASK]}\in[S]
.
The rate matrix is given by

R_{\text{base}}:=\mathbf{1}_{S}e_{\mathrm{[MASK]}}^{\intercal}-I_{S},

(3)

where
e_{i}
is a unit vector with only the
i
-th element being 1. Then,
q_{T}\approx\bm{\delta}_{\mathrm{[MASK]}^{d}}
, a singleton at the all-mask state.

and
\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{t}(x,x)=-\sum_{y\neq x}\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{t}(x,y)
.
In practice, to avoid instability near
t=0
, one often employs
early-stopping
by truncating the reverse chain at
t=T-\delta
for some small
\delta>0
.
Also, since the ratio
\frac{q_{t}(y)}{q_{t}(x)}
(a.k.a., the
concrete score function
) is intractable, one typically train a neural-network to estimate it. Let
s_{t}(y,x)
be such an estimator.
One typical loss is the score-entropy (SE) loss
[
25
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{SE}(s;t)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim q_{t}}\sum_{y:y\neq x_{t}}R_{t}(y,x_{t})\cdot\\
\left(s_{t}(y,x)-\frac{q_{t}(y)}{q_{t}(x)}-\frac{q_{t}(y)}{q_{t}(x)}\log\frac{s_{t}(y,x)}{q_{t}(y)/q_{t}(x)}\right).

(5)

Let
q_{t}
be the marginal p.m.f. at time
t\in[0,T-\delta]
in the sampling process. Since
q_{T}
is unavailable, the sampling process starts with
p_{0}
to be the stationary distribution of the forward CTMC. Then, the continuous-path is discretized for practical algorithms. Let
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in[N]}
be the discretization points on which
s_{T-t_{k}}
is accessible, where
t_{0}=0
and
t_{N}=T-\delta
. Let
\eta_{k}:=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
be the step-sizes. Define the estimated reverse rate as

\hat{R}_{t_{k}}(x,y):=R_{T-t_{k}}(y,x)s_{T-t_{k}}(y,x).

(6)

Euler method

[

,
27
]
.

, the next-token on the

.

2.3
D3PM and MDM

Apart from the continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) framework, discrete diffusion models can also be modeled as in Discrete Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (D3PMs).
In the
forward process
, still assuming that each dimension propagates independently as in (
1
), the (token-wise) transition probability is represented as

q^{i}_{t|0}(x_{t}^{i}|x_{0}^{i})=\mathrm{Cat}(\bar{Q}_{t}^{\intercal}e_{x_{0}^{i}}),

(8)

where
\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{p})
represents a categorical distribution with parameter
\mathbf{p}
,
and
\bar{Q}_{t}
is the transition probability matrix.
Specifically, define the
mask
transition matrix as

\bar{Q}_{t}=\alpha_{t}I+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{1}_{S}e_{\mathrm{[MASK]}}^{T}.

(9)

Here
\alpha_{t}
is some pre-defined noise schedule, and in this paper we adopt a typical choice that
\alpha_{t}=e^{-t}
.

Following
[
13
,
2
]
, a so-called “bridge distribution”
q_{s|t,0}
for
s<t
is particularly useful.
Indeed, under the mask transition in (
9
), one can show that
[
33
,
36
]

q(x_{s}^{i}|x_{t}^{i},x_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}(e_{x_{t}^{i}}),&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}\neq\mathrm{[MASK]}\\
\mathrm{Cat}\Big(\frac{(1-\alpha_{s})e_{\mathrm{[MASK]}}+(\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t})(e_{x_{0}^{i}})}{1-\alpha_{t}}\Big),&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}=\mathrm{[MASK]}\end{cases}.

Thus, in the sampling process of MDMs with discretized time grid
\{t_{k}\}_{k}
, a sample
x_{t_{k-1}}
is obtained token-wisely with the sampling probabilities
q(x_{t_{k-1}}^{i}|x_{t_{k}})=\sum_{x_{0}^{i}}q(x_{t_{k-1}}^{i}|x_{t_{k}}^{i},x_{0}^{i})q(x_{0}^{i}|x_{t_{k}})
.
While in practice, in order to sample
x_{t_{k-1}}^{i}
in MDMs, one typical way is to train a predictive model
\mu^{i}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)
to estimate the posterior conditional distribution
q(x_{0}^{i}|x_{t_{k}})
. Then, given the discretized time
t_{k}
’s and the current state
x_{t_{k}}
, the algorithm replace the
e_{x_{0}}^{i}
above with
e_{\hat{x}_{0}}^{i}
sampled from the predictive model
\mu_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t_{k}},t_{k})
at each step.

One typical loss function for training
\mu_{\theta}
is
negative evidence lower bound
(NELBO) of the parameterized model
[
36
]
, given as

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\;\mathbb{E}_{q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})}\left[\sum_{l:\,x_{t}^{l}=\mathrm{[MASK]}}e_{x_{0}^{l}}^{\top}\log\mu_{\theta}^{l}(x_{t},t)\right]dt,

(10)

where
\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}\alpha_{t}/\mathrm{d}t=-e^{-t}
.
Interestingly, there is a close relationship
[
36
]
between the predictive model
\mu_{\theta}
in MDMs and the score estimator
s_{t}(y,x)
in the absorbing-rate CTMC:

s_{t}(\cdot,x)=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\,\mu_{\theta}(x,t)[\cdot].

(11)

Indeed, with
\alpha_{t}=e^{-t}
,
one can show that the forward process of such an MDM is equivalent to that of the time-homogeneous absorbing-rate CTMC. Therefore, such MDM shares the same reverse process with the absorbing-rate CTMC.

This relationship can also be generalized from between estimators into between true values, i.e., concrete score
\frac{q_{t}\!\left(x_{t}^{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{t}^{i},\cdots,x_{t}^{d}\right)}{q_{t}\!\left(x_{t}^{1},\cdots,x_{t}^{i},\cdots,x_{t}^{d}\right)}
and true token-wise reverse conditional distribution
q_{0}\!\left(\hat{x}_{t}^{i}|x_{t}^{\mathrm{UM}}\right)

[
28
]
:

\frac{q_{t}\!\left(x_{t}^{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{t}^{i},\cdots,x_{t}^{d}\right)}{q_{t}\!\left(x_{t}^{1},\cdots,x_{t}^{i},\cdots,x_{t}^{d}\right)}=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\,q_{0}^{i}\!\left(\hat{x}_{t}^{i}|x_{t}^{UM}\right),

(12)

where
x_{t}^{UM}
denotes the collection of unmasked coordinates. Given such a close relationship, we will use “MDMs” and “absorbing-rate diffusion models” interchangeably.

2.4
First Hitting Sampler (FHS)

First Hitting Sampler (FHS), as referenced in
Algorithm

1
, is a sampling algorithm specifically designed for MDMs, which was first proposed in
[
36

Convergence Guarantees for the Euler Method

In this section, we present our improved upper error bounds and the first convergence lower bound for the Euler method.
In particular, differently from most existing theoretical results (e.g.,

3.1
General Decomposition in Total Variation

The following theorem shows the decomposition of the total-variation error incurred by a mismatched reverse process under general reverse rates. It will later be instantiated to obtain an improved parameter dependence.

Theorem 1

Recall that the reverse process
p_{t}

\mathrm{TV}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle q\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern Compared with prior KL-based analyses

3.2
Convergence Upper Bound for the Euler Method

In this subsection, we characterize the convergence rate with explicit parameter dependencies for the Euler method. Our analysis is made only under the following estimation error.

Assumption 1
.

The score estimation error satisfies that
\mathcal{L}_{TV}(s)\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{score}}}
, where

\mathcal{L}_{TV}(s):=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim q_{T-t_{k}}}\bigg[\sum_{y:y\neq x_{k}}R_{T-t_{k}}(y,x_{k})\left|s_{T-t_{k}}(y,x_{k})-\frac{q_{T-t_{k}}(y)}{q_{T-t_{k}}(x_{k})}\right|\bigg].

Here we assume that the score estimation is accurate in
L_{1}
(i.e., the expected sum of absolute differences between the rate matrices), a condition that naturally arises from our direct TV-based analysis. A similar criterion also appears in the TV-based analysis of
[
3
]

While both

and
[
3

capture the estimation error in terms of the sum of absolute rate differences, there is a key difference between the two.
[

all

and

where the score function diverges for all

1
only requires the sum

An alternative objective commonly used in the literature is the score entropy loss
[

[

30
,
23
]

In

Proposition 1
.

Recall the score entropy loss at time

in (
5

If
\hat{R}_{t}(x,y)-\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{t}(x,y)=o(1),\penalty\ \forall t,x,y

\sup_{t\in(\delta,T)}\mathcal{L}_{SE}(s_{t};t)\lesssim\varepsilon_{\text{score}}^{\prime}

Theorem 2

Suppose that

Comparing to prior works, our study features the following improvements.

(i)

(Improving parameter dependence)

2

guarantee for the

discrete diffusion models to achieve

(ii)

While all of

(iii)

3.3
Convergence Lower Bound for the Euler Method

Given the strong performance guarantees of the Euler method, an important question is whether these upper bounds are tight, as this directly implies the fundamental theoretical limits of the algorithm. As follows, we provide a convergence lower bound under the absorbing-rate using the Euler sampler, assuming no estimation error.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that the Euler method with constant step-sizes is used such that
\eta_{k}=\kappa
.
Then,
there exists
q^{*}
where
\gamma(q^{*})>0
,
such that in order to achieve
\mathrm{TV}(q^{*},p_{T})\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, the number of steps must satisfy
N=\tilde{\Omega}(d/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
.

Theorem

3
is the
first
convergence lower bound for the Euler method, and in the discrete diffusion literature at large. It shows that in order to achieve
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-TV error, the number of steps required for the Euler method is at least in the order of
d/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
(ignoring log-factors). If combined with
Corollary

1
, it shows that our upper bound on the number of steps is tight in
d
and
\varepsilon
(up to log-factors).

The full proof of
Theorem

3
is provided in
Appendix

G
.
The key idea is to couple the forward process starting from a singleton
\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
with another forward process that starts from
q_{\gamma}=:q^{*}
. Such a construction
enables an explicit characterization of the score function and, consequently, the Euler updates at each step. Because tokens remain fixed once they are unmasked, we can derive the final element-wise mask probability
p_{M}
, which captures the key dynamics of the process. Comparing
q_{\gamma}
with the resulting distribution then yields a lower bound on the TV distance, from which we obtain the required relationship of
T
and
\kappa
as a function of
\varepsilon
, and hence the step complexity.

In this section, we provide our convergence analysis of FHS (see

Assumption 2

Recall
\mathcal{L}_{SE}

\textstyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{L}_{SE}(s;t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{score}}.

In the studies using the integrated score entropy loss, the time integral is typically truncated at

Therefore, extending the integration range to

\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

Suppose there are no
\mathrm{[MASK]}

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{L}_{SE}(s;t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim q_{0}}[\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x_{0})]-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim q_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}H\left(q_{0}^{l}(\cdot|x_{0}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}^{c}})\right),

where

denotes the entropy,

is defined in (

Proposition

\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim q_{0}}[\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x_{0})]-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim q_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}H\left(q_{0}^{l}(\cdot|x_{0}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}^{c}})\right)\geq 0,

Theorem 4

Suppose there are no
\mathrm{[MASK]}

5
Conclusion

In this work, we presented a novel direct TV analysis for discrete diffusion models that yields tighter parameter dependencies than prior KL-based results.
Our framework improves convergence rates, removes the need for bounded score assumptions and surrogate initialization, and establishes the first tight lower bounds in TV.
Further,
we showed that FHS incurs no sampling error beyond that arising from score estimation itself, without additional dependence on the system parameters. We then showed that this error bound is tight via a lower bound by a worst-case construction.
Looking ahead, an interesting direction is to
investigate high-order variants of FHS for further acceleration.

Appendix A
Full Related Works

Empirical Studies on Discrete Diffusion Models.
Due to the large portion of works, we will only include those early works and those most relevant works recently. Compared to continuous-space diffusion models, discrete-space diffusion models are emerging as strong alternatives for generative tasks involving symbolic data
[
3
,
25
]
(see also surveys:
[
24
,
1
]
). The continuous-time formulation of discrete diffusion was first introduced in
[
3
]
. More recently,
[
25
]
proposed the score-entropy estimation loss and demonstrated empirical success in text generation. They further introduced Tweedie
\tau
-leaping, a new sampler derived from an approximation of Tweedie’s formula. For per-step updates, most empirical implementations adopt categorical sampling, which has shown strong practical performance.

More recently, an increasing number of empirical studies have focused on masked diffusion models, which is often found to deliver superior performance in text and image applications.
[
34
]
simplified the variational training objective into a weighted cross-entropy integral and introduced a state-dependent masking schedule to dynamically adjust rates for better generation quality. Building on this,
[
28
]
reparameterized the concrete score as a time-dependent scalar and a conditional distribution, leading to the Reparameterized Absorbing Discrete Diffusion (RADD) model for efficient training and sampling. Similarly,
[
33
]
exploited the absorbing state structure to derive a tighter ELBO through Rao-Blackwellization and proposed a semi-autoregressive decoding strategy for flexible sequence generation. Building on this line of work,
[
36
]
introduced the First-Hitting Sampler, which exactly samples from the continuous-time diffusion process under the assumption of a perfectly learned score. More recently,
[
26
]
developed a pre-trained model for masked diffusion models.

Theory on Uniform-Rate Discrete Diffusion Models.
In contrast to the extensive theoretical literature on continuous diffusion models, rigorous results for discrete diffusion remain relatively limited. An early contribution is
[
3
]
, which analyzed
\tau
-leaping under the total variation metric but relied on strong estimation assumptions and exhibited unfavorable parameter dependencies. Subsequent works shifted focus to controlling the score–entropy estimation error. In particular,
[
5
]
established convergence guarantees for the uniformization sampler on the
d
-dimensional hypercube, later extended to general discrete spaces
[S]^{d}
in
[
30
]
.
For deterministic step-size methods,
[
35
,
29
]
assumed access to an exact per-step solver, while
[
30
]
analyzed the more practical
\tau
-leaping sampler. Among these works,
[
35
]
required control of the score–entropy loss along the entire continuous sampling trajectory, whereas
[
30
,
29
]
imposed this requirement only on the discrete time grid. More recently,
[
23
]
developed an alternative analysis that avoids the Girsanov change-of-measure technique, yielding improved parameter dependencies and directly motivating the present work.
Notably, all of these analyses
[
5
,
30
,
35
,
29
,
23
]
are conducted in terms of KL divergence, which leads to suboptimal bounds when converted to total variation distance. Beyond convergence analysis,
[
31
]
proposed a theoretically motivated improvement to discrete classifier-free guidance (CFG), and
[
16
]
extended discrete diffusion techniques to continuous data distributions via quantization.

Theory on Masked (Absorbing-rate) Discrete Diffusion Models.

On the theoretical side,
[
22
]
was the first to establish convergence guarantees for masked diffusion models, showing improved dimensional dependence compared to uniform-rate diffusion.
[
15
]
studied faster convergence rates using the Euler method and a specialized uniformization scheme, the Mask-Aware Truncated Uniformization (MATU), which eliminates the need for a bounded score assumption. In parallel,
[
7

derived improved dimension-dependent rates for the DMPM sampler, also without the need of a bounded score. From a different perspective,
[

provided theoretical guarantees for masked language models. Beyond the analysis of unconditional models,
[

,

Appendix G
Proof of
Theorem

3

We first construct a distribution
q^{*}
as follows. Define
\gamma:=\frac{\varepsilon^{1/4}}{d}>0
. We want to define an augmented forward process such that
q^{*}
is the
\gamma
-perturbation of
q_{0}=\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
, denoted by
q_{\gamma}
.
Here we let
\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
denote a delta-distribution such that
a^{i}\neq\mathrm{[MASK]},\penalty\ \forall i\in[d]
.
Note that such an augmentation is key to the proof, where we are coupling the forward process starting from
\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
and that starting from
q_{\gamma}
.

We first explain why such a
q_{\gamma}
satisfies
\gamma(q_{\gamma})>0
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{q_{\gamma}^{i}(\mathrm{[MASK]}|x^{-i})}{\max_{u^{i}\in[S]:u^{i}\neq\mathrm{[MASK]}}q_{\gamma}^{i}(u^{i}|x^{-i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\max_{u^{i}\in[S]:u^{i}\neq\mathrm{[MASK]}}q_{\gamma}(u^{i},x^{-i})/q_{\gamma}(\mathrm{[MASK]},x^{-i})}

\displaystyle\geq\gamma>0,

where the last line follows by the score upper bound in Lemma 1 of
[
22
]
.

With such a special setup, the (augmented) forward CTMC has the following properties. Suppose that
y\neq x
only on the
i
-th component. Then, with
t\in[\gamma,T+\gamma]
(due to augmentation),

\displaystyle q_{t}(x)=(1-e^{-t})^{\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left\{x^{i}=\mathrm{[MASK]}\right\}}(e^{-t})^{\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left\{x^{i}=a^{i}\right\}}0^{\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left\{x^{i}\neq\mathrm{[MASK]},x^{i}\neq a^{i}\right\}}

\displaystyle\frac{q_{t}(y)}{q_{t}(x)}=\frac{e^{-t}}{1-e^{-t}},\quad\text{only if}\penalty\ y^{i}=a^{i},x^{i}=\mathrm{[MASK]}

After placing equal-spaced discretization points on
[\gamma,T+\gamma]
, the Euler method proceeds as in (
7
) with

\hat{R}_{k}^{i}(\mathrm{[MASK]},y^{i})=\frac{e^{-(T+\gamma-t_{k})}}{1-e^{-(T+\gamma-t_{k})}},\quad\text{only if}\penalty\ y^{i}=a^{i},\penalty\ \text{otherwise}\penalty\ 0.

Due to the particular design with the absorbing rate, once this token jumps from
\mathrm{[MASK]}
to
a^{i}
, it will stay there.
Also note that the probability on the right-hand side is independent of
i
. This implies that the resulting
p_{T}
is independent in
i
, with

p_{T}^{i}(\mathrm{[MASK]})=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{e^{T+\gamma-t_{k}}-1}\right)=:p_{M}.

(21)

Now we proceeds to analyze
p_{M}
. We have

\displaystyle\log p_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\log\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{e^{T+\gamma-t_{k}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}-\frac{\kappa}{e^{T+\gamma-t_{k}}-1}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2(e^{T+\gamma-t_{k}}-1)^{2}}+O(\kappa^{3})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\gamma}^{T+\gamma}\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{O}(\gamma^{-1}\kappa),

(22)

where we explain the term
\tilde{O}(\gamma^{-1}\kappa)
as follows.
First, by the residual of the Riemann sum,

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}-\frac{\kappa}{e^{T+\gamma-t_{k}}-1}-\int_{\gamma}^{T+\gamma}\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq\gamma^{-1}\kappa T=\tilde{O}(\gamma^{-1}\kappa)

if
T=\tilde{O}(1)
. Also,

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2(e^{T+\gamma-t_{k}}-1)^{2}}=O\left(\kappa\int_{\gamma}^{T+\gamma}\frac{1}{(e^{t}-1)^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\right)=O(\gamma^{-1}\kappa).

Also note that
\gamma^{-1}\kappa=O(\varepsilon^{1/4})
. The upper bound for the integral is due to the augmented forward process. For small
\gamma
and large
T
, the integral can be evaluated as

\int_{\gamma}^{T+\gamma}\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\mathrm{d}t=\log\gamma^{-1}-e^{-T}+O(1),

and thus

p_{M}\asymp e^{\log\gamma+e^{-T}}(1+\tilde{O}(\gamma^{-1}\kappa))=\gamma e^{e^{-T}}+\tilde{O}(e^{e^{-T}}\kappa).

Now, note that, when
\gamma
vanishes quickly enough such that
\gamma d\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(q_{\gamma},p_{T})

\displaystyle\geq\left|q_{\gamma}(\bm{a})-p_{T}(\bm{a})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|e^{-\gamma d}-(1-p_{M})^{d}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|1-\gamma d-(1-dp_{M})\right|+O(d^{2}\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=d\left|p_{M}-\gamma\right|+O(d^{2}\gamma^{2}).

Here note that
O(d^{2}\gamma^{2})=O(\varepsilon^{1/2})
. Thus, in order that
\mathrm{TV}(q_{\gamma},p_{T})\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, we must have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\gtrsim d\left|p_{M}-\gamma\right|+O(d^{2}\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=d\left|\gamma e^{e^{-T}}+\tilde{O}(e^{e^{-T}}\kappa)-\gamma\right|+O(d^{2}\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=d\left|\gamma e^{-T}+\tilde{O}(e^{e^{-T}}\kappa)\right|+O(d^{2}\gamma^{2}+\gamma de^{-2T})

where the last line follows when
T
is large and because
e^{z}-1=z+O(z^{2})
when
z\to 0
. Thus, when
\gamma=\frac{\varepsilon^{1/4}}{d}
, it is required that

T\gtrsim\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1/4}},\quad\kappa\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{d}.

Then, the number of steps satisfies that

N=\frac{T}{\kappa}\gtrsim\frac{d}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1/4}}=\tilde{\Omega}\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right).

The proof is now complete.

We let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}

2.

Let

To simplify the notation, we use

The following proposition provides useful statistical properties of the FHS and reverse CTMC processes and their connections.

Proposition 3

The FHS and reverse absorbing-rate CTMC processes satisfy the following properties:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\tau_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k})=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\tau_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k})=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\tau_{k-1}|\tau_{k}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(i_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1})=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(i_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1})=\frac{1}{k},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})=\mu_{\theta}^{i_{k-1}}(x_{k},\tau_{k-1})[a_{k-1}],

Proof.

The proof contains three steps: Step 1 shows the basic properties of transition dynamics of reverse CTMC, Step 2 further develops the statistical distributions for reverse CTMC, and Step 3 establishes the connection between the statistics of FHS and reverse CTMC processes.

Step 1. Basic properties of reverse CTMC:
We use

to denote the rate matrix of the reverse absorbing-rate CTMC indexed by forward time

.
This is for the convenience to analyze both processes with the same time indexing.
Specifically, we define the reverse rate matrix

\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)\;:=\;\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{t-\delta}=y\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right)}{\delta},

\mathbb{P}(X_{t-\Delta t}=y|X_{t}=x)=\mathds{1}\left\{y=x\right\}+\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

The diagonal entries are defined by

\widetilde{R}_{s}(x,x)\;=\;-\sum_{y\neq x}\widetilde{R}_{s}(x,y).

When horizon

\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{t}

\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)=\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{T-t}(x,y),\quad\forall y\neq x.

Based on the notations and definitions above, we next have several basic results concerning time-inhomogeneous CTMCs in

Appendix J
Proof of
Theorem

4

As shown by
Proposition

3
, the FHS sampling process and the reverse CTMC decoupling structure: they share the same statistical distributions on the unmasking times
\tau_{k}
and the indices
i_{k}
of tokens that are unmasked, and differ only in the mechanism used to sample the predicted token value
a_{k}
for each
k
. In particular, FHS predicts
a_{k-1}
via
\mu_{\theta}^{i_{k-1}}
as an approximation of
q_{0}^{i_{k-1}}(a|x_{k}^{UM})
used in the ground-truth reverse CTMC. Thus, the estimation error in
\mu_{\theta}
affects only the sampling of the token value
a_{k}
and does not propagate to
\tau_{k}
or
i_{k}
. As a consequence, the subsequent convergence analysis of FHS can be carried out by technically accumulating only the errors arising from sampling the predicted token value
a_{k-1}
for
k={1,2,\ldots,d}
.

In the following, we first derive the KL divergence between the path distributions of the two processes, i.e.,
\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\mathrm{path})||\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\mathrm{path}))
. Then, by applying the data processing inequality, we obtain the error bound on the distance between the ground-truth distribution
q_{0}(\cdot)
and the output distribution of FHS, i.e.,
\mathrm{KL}(q_{0}(\cdot)||\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\cdot))
.

By the Markov property, the joint path distribution can be factorized as

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\mathrm{path})\\
=p_{d}(x_{d},\tau_{d})\prod_{k=d}^{1}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\tau_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k})\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(i_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1})\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1}),

(32)

and

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\mathrm{path})=p_{d}(x_{d},\tau_{d})\prod_{k=d}^{1}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\tau_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k})\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(i_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1})\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1}),

(33)

where

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})=q_{0}^{i_{k-1}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k}^{UM})

and

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})=\mu_{\theta}^{i_{k-1}}(x_{k},\tau_{k-1})[a_{k-1}]

as shown in
Proposition

3
, and
p_{d}(x_{d},\tau_{d})=1
is the common initialization distribution with
x_{d}
being all masked and
\tau_{d}
satisfying
\alpha_{\tau_{d}}=0
.

Dividing (
32
) by (
33
) and applying the properties in
Proposition

3
, we have

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\mathrm{path})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\mathrm{path})}=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}.

Then, we derive the KL divergence between the path distributions as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\mathrm{path})||\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\mathrm{path}))

(34)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{path}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}}\left[\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\mathrm{path})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\mathrm{path})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{path}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{d}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{path}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}\\
(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})\end{subarray}}\bigg[\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a_{k-1}|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})}\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{(x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\cdot|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})||\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\cdot|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1}))\right].

Thus, the KL divergence between two path distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}
has been decomposed into summation of the expected KL divergence of two token-prediction distributions, which is the error arising from predicting
x_{k-1}^{i_{k-1}}
via
\mu_{\theta}
in FHS. We next analyze

\mathbb{E}_{(x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{CTMC}}(\cdot|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1})||\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\cdot|x_{k},\tau_{k-1},i_{k-1}))\right]

for each
k
.

The following lemma shows that
\alpha_{\tau_{k-1}}
follows the Beta distribution.

Lemma 6
.

The random variable
\alpha_{\tau_{k-1}}
sampled by FHS algorithm follows a Beta distribution, i.e., for each
k=1,2,\cdots,d
,

\alpha_{\tau_{k-1}}\sim Beta(d-k+1,k).

The proof of
Lemma

6
is provided in
Section

L.6
.

Further,
[
36
]
shows that NELBO can be equivalently reformulated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x_{0})=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim\tilde{q}_{k|0}(\cdot\mid x_{0})}\left[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l:x_{k}^{l}=\mathrm{[MASK]}}\log\bar{\mu}_{\theta}^{(l)}(x_{k}))[x_{0}^{l}]\right],

Then combining

Appendix K
Proof of
Theorem

5

We construct both a target distribution
q_{0}
and a special form of the estimated predictive model
\mu_{\theta}
, which yield a lower error bound.

To proceed, fix
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in[S]^{d}
where
\bm{b}^{i}\neq\bm{a}^{i}
and
\bm{a}^{i},\bm{b}^{i}\neq\mathrm{[MASK]}
for all
i\in[d]
. Let
q_{0}=\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
. Also, for any
x_{t}
,
t
, and
i
, let
\mu_{\theta}
be as follows:

\mu_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t},t)[y^{i}]=\begin{cases}1-\rho,&\text{ if }y^{i}=\bm{a}^{i},\\
\rho,&\text{ if }y^{i}=\bm{b}^{i}.\end{cases}

where we choose choose
\rho=1-e^{-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{score}}/d}
.

We next show that the above constructed
\mu_{\theta}
satisfies

2
, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{L}_{SE}(s;t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{score}}
. First note that under
q_{0}=\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
, we have
q_{0}^{l}(\cdot|x_{0}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}^{c}})=\bm{\delta}^{l}_{\bm{a}^{l}}
. Then, we can apply
Proposition

2
and compute the integrated
\mathcal{L}_{SE}
as follows.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{L}_{SE}(s;t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim q_{0}}[\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x_{0})]-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim q_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}H\left(q_{0}^{l}(\cdot|x_{0}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}^{c}})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\bm{a})-0

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t|0}(x_{t}|\bm{a})}\left[\sum_{l:x_{t}^{l}=\mathrm{[MASK]}}\log\left(\mu_{\theta}^{l}(x_{t},t)[\bm{a}^{l}]\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t|0}(x_{t}|\bm{a})}\left[\sum_{l:x_{t}^{l}=\mathrm{[MASK]}}\log\left(1-\rho\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\log\left(1-\rho\right)\mathbb{E}_{q_{t|0}(x_{t}|\bm{a})}\left[m(x_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\log\left(1-\rho\right)d(1-\alpha_{t})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-d\log(1-\rho)=\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{score}}

where
m(x_{t})
denotes the number of
\mathrm{[MASK]}
tokens in
x_{t}
, and the last inequality follows from the choice of
\rho
.

Now, under FHS, since each token is predicted exactly once and
\mu_{\theta}
predicts the correct token
\bm{a}^{i}
at each position
i
with the probability
(1-\rho)
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(\bm{a})=(1-\rho)^{d}
.
Then, since
q_{0}=\bm{\delta}_{\bm{a}}
, it follows that

\mathrm{KL}(q_{0}||\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}})=-\log\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{FHS}}(a)\right)=-d\log(1-\rho)=\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{score}}

(39)

where the last inequality follows from the choice of
\rho
.

The proof is now complete. Note that this special case with such a

and

indicates that the upper bound argued in

4
is tight.

In this section, we provide all the proofs for the auxiliary lemmas used in this paper.

L.1
Proof of
Lemma

1

Recall that
(T-t_{k})-(T-t_{k+1})=\kappa\min\left\{1,T-t_{k}\right\}
. Define
k^{*}:=\sup\left\{k:T-t_{k}>1\right\}
. We thus have the following cases.

1.

Case 1:
k<k^{*}
. This implies that
T-t_{k}>T-t_{k+1}>1
, and
t_{k+1}-t_{k}=\kappa
. Thus, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{k^{*}-1}\frac{(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{p}}{\min\left\{1,T-t_{k+1}\right\}^{p}}=\kappa^{p}k^{*}.

2.

Case 2:
k>k^{*}
. This implies that
T-t_{k+1}<T-t_{k}\leq 1
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k^{*}+1}^{N-1}\frac{(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{p}}{\min\left\{1,T-t_{k+1}\right\}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{p}\sum_{k=k^{*}+1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{T-t_{k}}{T-t_{k+1}}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{p}\sum_{k=k^{*}+1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\asymp\kappa^{p}(N-k^{*}-1)

where the first line follows by definition of
t_{k+1}
, and the last line follows due to the following. Note that for Case 2, the step-sizes satisfies that

T-t_{k}\leq T-t_{k+1}+\kappa.

Since
T-t_{k+1}\geq\delta^{-1}
, this implies that
1\leq\frac{T-t_{k}}{T-t_{k+1}}=1+O(\kappa)=1+o(1)
.

3.

Case 3:
k=k^{*}
. This implies that
T-t_{k+1}\in(1-\kappa,1]
,
T-t_{k}\in(1,1+\kappa]
, and
t_{k+1}-t_{k}\leq\kappa
. For this case, we can similarly get
\frac{T-t_{k}}{T-t_{k+1}}=1+o(1)
, and thus

\frac{(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{p}}{\min\left\{1,T-t_{k+1}\right\}^{p}}=\kappa^{p}(1+o(1)).

Summing up all three cases, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{p}}{\min\left\{1,T-t_{k+1}\right\}^{p}}\asymp\kappa^{p}N.

Next, we need to determine the total number of steps. When
k\leq k^{*}
, the number of steps is simply

k^{*}=:N_{1}=\left\lfloor\frac{T-1}{\kappa}\right\rfloor\asymp\frac{T}{\kappa}.

When
k>k^{*}
, in order to reach that
T-t_{N}\asymp\delta
, we need

N_{2}\asymp\log_{1-\kappa}\delta\asymp\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\kappa}.

Therefore, the total number of steps satisfies that

N=N_{1}+N_{2}\asymp\frac{T+\log\delta^{-1}}{\kappa}.

The proof is now complete.

L.5
Proof of
Lemma

5

(1) Instantaneous total rate of leaving
x
at time
t
.
For any
t
and
x\neq y
, the reverse CTMC process
satisfies the local transition given by

\mathbb{P}(Y_{t-dt}=y\mid Y_{t}=x)=\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)\,dt+o(dt).

(41)

Taking summation over all
y\neq x
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(Y_{t-dt}\neq x\mid Y_{t}=x)=\sum_{y\neq x}\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)\,dt+o(dt)=\Lambda(t,x)\,dt+o(dt),

(42)

where

\Lambda(t,x)=\sum_{y\neq x}\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y).

Thus
\Lambda(t,x)
is the instantaneous rate of leaving
state
x
at time
t
.

(2) Unmasking time
\tau
in state
x
.

Fix
t
and
x
, and define the survival function

S(h):=\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h\mid Y_{t}=x),\qquad h\geq 0,

where

\tau:=\sup\{s<t:Y_{s}\neq x\}

is the first unmasking time after
t
for the reverse process indexed by the forward time.

To derive a differential equation for
S(h)
, let
\delta>0
be a small constant, and
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
denote the indicator function. Using the Markov property at time
t-h
, we have

\displaystyle S(h+\delta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t}=x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h-\delta\}}|Y_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau|Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h-\delta\}}|Y_{t-h},Y_{t}=x\right]|Y_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau|Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h-\delta\}}|Y_{t-h}=x,Y_{t}=x\right]|Y_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau|Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}|Y_{t-h}=x,Y_{t}=x\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t-h}=x)|Y_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau|Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}|Y_{t-h}=x,Y_{t}=x\right]|Y_{t}=x\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t-h}=x)

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau|Y_{t-h}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}|Y_{t-h},Y_{t}=x\right]|Y_{t}=x\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t-h}=x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}|Y_{t}=x\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t-h}=x),

Here
(i)
follows because
\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}
is non-zero only when
Y_{t-h}=Y_{t}=x
. Also,
(ii)
follows because
\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}
is a (deterministic) function of
Y_{t-h}
and
Y_{t}
. Also, by Markov property,
\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t-h},Y_{t})=\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta|Y_{t-h})
. Then,
(iii)
follows again because
\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<t-h\}}
is non-zero only when
Y_{t-h}=Y_{t}=x
.
Thus,

\displaystyle S(h+\delta)

\displaystyle=S(h)\,\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta\mid Y_{t-h}=x)

\displaystyle=S(h)\big(1-\Lambda(t-h,x)\,\delta+o(\delta)\big)

(43)

where the second equality follows because given
Y_{t-h}=x
, the condition
\tau<t-h-\delta
exactly defines the process that does not leave
x
in
[t-h-\delta,\,t-h]
, and hence
(
42
) yields

\mathbb{P}(\tau<t-h-\delta\mid Y_{t-h}=x)=1-\Lambda(t-h,x)\,\delta+o(\delta).

Equation (
43
) further yields

\frac{S(h+\delta)-S(h)}{\delta}=-\Lambda(t-h,x)\,S(h)+o(1),

which implies the following ODE as
\delta\to 0
:

S^{\prime}(h)=-\Lambda(t-h,x)\,S(h),\qquad S(0)=1.

Solving the above ODE, we get

S(h)=\exp\!\Big(-\int_{h}^{0}\Lambda(t-u,x)\,du\Big)=\exp\!\Big(-\int_{t-h}^{t}\Lambda(u,x)\,du\Big),

which completes the proof of the second claim.

(3) Distribution of the predicted token.

We first derive:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(Y_{\tau}=y\mid Y_{t}=x,\tau\in(t-\delta,t])=\frac{\mathbb{(}Y_{\tau}=y\mid Y_{t}=x)}{\mathbb{P}(Y_{\tau}\neq x\mid Y_{t}=x)}=\frac{\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)\delta+o(\delta)}{\Lambda(t,x)\delta+o(\delta)}.

Then, letting
\delta\downarrow 0
, we have

\lim\limits_{\delta\downarrow 0}\ \mathbb{P}(Y_{\tau}=y\mid Y_{t}=x,\tau\in(t-\delta,t])=\frac{\widetilde{R}_{t}(x,y)}{\Lambda(t,x)},

which implies the distribution of next state.
The proof is now complete.

Following from the proof of Proposition 4.1 in

1-\alpha_{\tau_{k-1}}=u_{(k)},
