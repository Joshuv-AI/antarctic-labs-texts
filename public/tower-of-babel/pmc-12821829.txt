Assessment of transformer-based AI in clinical oncology
Authors: Lim Weng Seong, Pietro Lio, Nur Aishah Taib, Mogana Darshini Ganggayah, Sarinder Kaur Dhillon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer survival prediction plays a significant role in better understanding the relationship between clinical features and demographic features to improve decision-making among clinicians. This is done with the help of machine learning models, which assist clinicians in making decisions for the improvement of patients. However, there has been an over-saturation of machine learning models in breast cancer survival prediction, of which the only distinction is the difference in data sampling used and better tuning parameters. This study explores the possibilities of a recent deep learning model called Transformers as a breast cancer survival prediction model. The breast cancer dataset used for the study ranges from 2006 until 2015. The dataset was retrieved from the University Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC), Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. The dataset contains 26 variables comprising 24 categorical variables and two numerical variables. A comparison of performance matrices on Transformer models such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach) and ALBERT (A Lite BERT for Self-supervised Learning of Language Representations) to existing machine learning models such as random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbours (KNN), artificial neural network (ANN) and extreme gradient boosting (XGB) was conducted. In terms of overall performance, the Transformer models produced scores that were close to those of other machine learning models. The best accuracy among the Transformer models was the ALBERT model at (81.1%) while the best accuracy among the machine learning models was the LR model at (83.4%). Based on the Transformer models’ close outcomes compared to machine learning models, Transformers can be introduced as an alternative prediction model in breast cancer survival studies. Background

Breast cancer is one of the most common forms of cancer afflicting women globally [1]. In Malaysia, 1 in 23 women are susceptible to the risk of contracting breast cancer throughout their lifetime [2]. Breast cancer survival prediction plays a significant role in providing better understanding of the relationship of clinical features and demographic features to improve on decision making among clinicians. Over the years, the incidence to mortality rate for breast cancer has decreased. However, this is only seen in better developed countries which have better medical advances [3, 4]. In Malaysia, majority of breast cancer cases in Malaysian women are detected at later stages, thus contributing to lower survival rates [5, 6]. Hence then need of prediction tools and models to assist clinicians in making better judgement and decisions.

In the current era of technological advancement, artificial intelligence (AI) is seen as one of the core structures in many fields of knowledge. Artificial intelligence is defined as a machine or system that is able to understand, to reason and to adapt from information in order to achieve goals and tasks with the flexibility of a human being [7]. While AI exists as a broad concept, machine learning (ML) exists as an entity of application in AI. Over the years, machine learning serves as an assisting role for clinicians in making decisions for the improvement of patients. ML is defined as a relationship of both knowledge extraction and self-learning to perform specific downstream tasks [8]. Over the years, ML has become a staple in breast cancer related studies in providing clinicians better understanding and better decision making [9–11]. This however leads to the problem where ML models used for breast cancer survival prediction becomes overly saturated and the only distinction would be different sample population and tuning parameters. Some examples of current machine learning models in breast cancer survival prediction include Random Forest, Decision Tree, Support Vector Machine and etc [10, 12]. In recent years, artificial intelligence has advanced to a point where there are many types and variants of machine learning models. Thus, producing an over saturation of machine learning models in breast cancer survival prediction. Apart from machine learning, deep learning has also seen a rise in application in breast cancer studies due to its model complexity, yielding positive feedback [13]. Among the deep learning models, Transformers has been the new rage throughout the community.

Recent advancements in Transformer-based models have shown impressive results in histopathology classification. For instance, Maistry &Ezugwu [14] demonstrated that Vision Transformers achieved approximately 95.15% accuracy in breast cancer histopathological classification, outperforming CNN baselines [14]. Similarly, Jahan etal. [15] reported a ViT-based patch-level model reaching 96.74% accuracy for cancer detection and 89.78% accuracy for subtype classification, across curated pathology datasets [15]. Ensemble ML models such as XGBoost and SVM have achieved AUC up to 0.92 in EHR-based mortality prediction tasks using structured MIMICIV data—highlighting comparable performance in clinical prediction scenarios [16].

The aim of the study is to assess the application of Transformers in the survival prediction of breast cancer patients in a Malaysian cohort. This study compares 3 forms of Transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa and ALBERT) against traditional machine learning models as used in the study by [10]. An exploratory step on whether having a vocabulary which could recognise medical corpus based off our dataset was also performed. In this study, comparison of Transformers to traditional machine learning models in terms of structural composition and use cases in healthcare, and its functionality in a breast cancer survival study will be explored. The novelty of the study lies in the adoption of Transformers for breast cancer survival study, apart from the machine learning models presented in [10].

Machine learning techniques

ML approaches can be categorised based on the method of learning which are supervised learning, unsupervised learning, semi-supervised learning and reinforcement learning as shown in Fig. 1 [8]. Supervised learning is defined as a task-driven approach where the model is trained upon labelled data. Unsupervised learning is defined as a data-driven approach where the model is trained upon unlabelled data. Semi-supervised learning is defined as a hybridization of supervised learning and unsupervised learning as the model is trained upon both labelled and unlabelled data. Reinforced learning defers slightly from the previous learning techniques where the model is defined as an environment-driven approach where the model is trained by assigning a reward or penalty based on environmental activities.Fig. 1Types of machine learning techniques (extracted from: [8])

Types of machine learning techniques (extracted from: [8])

Supervised learning is one of the most used approaches in ML related studies. There are two types of analysis under supervised learning which are classification and regression. Both types of analysis share a similar workflow with the purpose in predicting a target variable. However, a slight difference is that classification analysis uses categorical data whereas regression analysis uses continuous data [8, 17]). Some examples of classification algorithms are Random Forest and Decision Trees while examples of regression algorithms are Gaussian Regression and Linear Regression. Both methods of analysis are seen widely in breast cancer related studies, where classification studies mainly predict class variables such as survival status of patients or subtypes for breast cancer [11, 18]; whereas regression studies mainly revolve around predicting the survival duration of breast cancer patients [19, 20].

Unsupervised learning is the antagonist to supervised learning where rather than relying on the assistance of supervision, the algorithms will learn and devise a structure based on solely raw data inputs. This is seen in the two analysis methods of unsupervised learning which are clustering and association. The purpose of clustering analysis is to identify and form meaningful subgroups among the data while association analysis is to learn and discover relationships and connections among the data [21, 22]. An example of clustering algorithm is K-means clustering while an example of association algorithm is the Apriori algorithm. Usage of unsupervised learning in breast cancer related studies share close resemblance to studies using supervised learning. A study in 2020, showcased the usage of different unsupervised clustering methods in classifying metabolic signatures in patients with localized breast cancer [23].

Semi-supervised learning is described as an in between of supervised learning and unsupervised learning. This is seen through the usage of small amounts of labelled data and large amounts of unlabelled data for the training of a model [24]. Semi-supervised learning can be divided into classification and clustering. Although both methods are seen in supervised learning and unsupervised learning, there are some slight differences. A notable difference in semi-supervised classification to supervised classification is that the training data is a combination of both labelled and unlabelled data. An example of this is a study on sentiment analysis classification performances by using different sizes of labelled data combined with unlabelled data to train the classifier model [25]. Semi-supervised clustering on the other hand, trains the clustering model on labelled data and unlabelled data to predict the cluster group of the unlabelled data with limited amounts of labelled data. An example of this is presented in the study where semi-supervised clustering was used for triaging breast pathology images and nuclei classification [26].

Reinforced learning in comparison to the other forms of learning, is built upon a stochastic decision-making process known as Markov decision process (MDP) [27, 28]. MDP is made up of several key components such as an agent, environment, state, action, policy and reward. An agent represents the model or entity that is being trained to make the correct decisions. Environment represents a controlled area surrounding the agent as it is being trained. State represents the current situation or problem the agent is facing. Action represents the decision made by the agent to solve the state it is currently facing. Policy represents the thought process of the agent in making the decision. Reward represents the outcome of a decision that underwent a policy by the agent. The interaction of key components in a reinforced learning framework is shown in Fig. 2. Applications of reinforced learning seen in a healthcare setting include precision medicine, dynamic treatment regimes, personalized rehabilitation, medical imaging, diagnostics systems, control systems, interactive chat-bot systems and health management systems [29].Fig. 2Interaction of agent and environment in reinforced learning (extracted from: [28])

Transformers architecture

In 2017, the paper “Attention is All You Need” brought forth major changes in the AI community with the introduction of a self-attention-based model known as Transformers [30]. The proposed Transformer model’s architecture derives from a traditional encoder-decoder structure but with varying properties. As seen in Fig. 3, the Transformers’ model architecture is segregated into two fully connecting segments which comprises of and encoder and decoder. The encoder layer comprises of two sub-layers which are a multi-head attention layer and a feed forward network layer. A multi-head attention layer is a layer comprising of multiple scaled dot-product attentions. The decoder layer shares a similar setup as the encoder layer with the difference being an extra sub-layer which performs multi-head attention over the output from the encoder layer.Fig. 3Model architecture of transformers (adapted from: [30])

Model architecture of transformers (adapted from: [30])

Since then, there has been many different variants of Transformers emerging to accomplish different tasks such as natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision. For the current study, our focus is only on the BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) model, the RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach) model and the ALBERT (A Lite BERT for Self-supervised Learning of Language Representations) model.

The BERT model introduced in 2018, is an encoder only model with the purpose of learning representations of words in a sentence or sequence [31]. The BERTs model architecture is based only on the encoder segment of the original Transformers’ model architecture. This allows the BERT model to be able to read input sequences as a whole and understand the context of the words based on its surroundings. Pre-training in the BERT model consists of two strategies which are Masked Language Modeling (MLM) and Next Sentence Prediction (NSP). MLM is an unsupervised learning technique which utilizes random masking of input tokens in a sequence and trains the model to predict the masked tokens based on available information surrounding it. NSP on the other hand, is also an unsupervised learning approach which predicts whether the given sentence is the next sentence in a sequence or a random sentence in the entire corpus. After pre-training, fine-tuning in BERT is inexpensive and only certain changes to the hyperparameters in order to cater the BERT model for a variety of tasks.

Although the BERT model advanced NLP tasks, it however also came with criticisms. According to an article by [32], the BERT models suffer in downstream performances due to poorly optimized training regime and non-adequate sizing of dataset for the model. The authors introduced the RoBERTa model which shares a similar model architecture with its predecessor but with several modifications. These improvements include a larger training dataset, larger batch size and longer sequences, the usage of dynamic masking and removal of NSP from the model. In contrast to MLM, dynamic masking is a process of selecting masked words in a sentence randomly for every epoch during pre-training for the model. It is also a self-supervised learning technique which predicts the masked words based on the surrounding words in the sentence. Thus, allowing the RoBERTa model to overcome issues such as positional bias, bias representation of patterns and generalization. With the removal of the NSP module from the training regime, it allowed the model to focus more on the MLM task. Thus, cascading into better downstream task performances.

NLP models tend to perform better exponentially over time with increase in number of parameters and training on larger datasets. Resulting in the demand of computational prowess to model requirements increasing substantially. The ALBERT model proposed by [33], introduces a variant of a BERT model which incorporates three forms of parameter reduction techniques, resulting in lower memory consumption and faster training speeds. The first technique is factorized embedding parameterization which a technique for reducing the number of parameters in the model through decomposition of a large vocabulary embedding matrix into two smaller matrices. This allows the size of the hidden layers to grow without increasing the parameter size of the vocabulary embeddings. The second technique is the cross-layer parameter sharing which shares all parameters across the multiple layers. This solves the issue of parameter redundancy across the layers, resulting in a more compact model structure. The third technique is the usage of inter-sentence coherence loss which avoids topic predictions and focuses on coherence between sentences.

Apart from apparent differences, both models have advantages and disadvantages when applying to real-world data scenarios. Traditional ML models are widely preferred due to its simplicity, interpretability, low computational requirements, perform well with small datasets, faster training time, and effectiveness in structured data tasks. Although traditional ML models yield good outcomes, it is disadvantageous when handling real-world data scenarios such as larger datasets, unstructured data formats, and tasks that scale in complexity.

In contrast, Transformers perform better in scenarios like long-range dependencies tasks (machine translation and text summarization), larger datasets, unstructured data tasks, different domain of interest (computer vision, audio processing etc.) and contextual understanding tasks (sentiment analysis and question answering). Although Transformers can be seen to excel tasks that are more complex, they are not as commonly applied due to it being computationally intensive and complex.

Transformers in healthcare

Since its introduction in 2017, Transformers has appeared in various application among the healthcare sector. Several applications of Transformers in healthcare include clinical NLP tasks, usage with structured electronic health records (EHRs), medical imaging tasks, bio-molecular tasks, bio-physical signals and social media presence [34]. In clinical NLP, Transformers attempts to overcome the disadvantage of the general lexicon in understanding words associated with the medical corpus. Two prime examples of Transformers addressing this issue are BioBERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers for Biomedical Text Mining) for biomedical text-mining [35] and ClinicalBERT for predicting clinical outcomes like hospital readmissions [36]. Structured EHRs are vast information repositories for clinicians handling information regarding diagnosis, treatment and demographic information of patients. Several use cases of Transformers with EHRs include ontological structured learning using attention-based approach of Transformers [37], multi-modal fusion of imaging and clinical data for survival prediction [38] and prediction of clinical and disease outcome based on patient visitation EHRs [39]. Medical imaging is also another area of which Transformers is seen widely applied. Influenced by the popularity of the U-net model for image segmentation proposed by Ronneberger et al. [40], Transformers are incorporated with the U-net model architecture to improve and refine medical image segmentation [41, 42]. Transformers also play a role in medical image registration for understanding long-range spatial relationships [43] and medical image capturing which generates a text-based representation from medical images [44]. Other applications of Transformers in medical images include visual question answering [45], medical image synthesis [46], and medical image reconstruction such as refining MRI images [47] and cardiac images [48]. As Transformers are a form of sequence-based models, they are not foreign in bio-molecular tasks relating to DNA sequencing [49] and RNA sequencing [50]. In addition, drug-related studies also employ Transformers for drug target identification, drug virtual screening, optimization of drug compounds, and solutions for drug addiction [51]. For bio-physical signals, Transformers is seen in modelling physical activities such as Electroencephalogram (EEG) [52] and Electrocardiogram (ECG) signals [53]. Many use cases of Transformers in social media revolves around sentiment analysis [54]. Some examples of Transformers in social media include predicting mental health from social media posts [55] and identification of adverse events regarding usage of medical products [56].

Breast cancer dataset

The dataset and methods involved in the study has received ethical approval from the Medical Research Committee of the UMMC, Kuala Lumpur (MREC ID NO: #733.22). As anonymised data were used, the need for informed consent was waived. This study focuses on breast cancer survival prediction and not survival analysis. Therefore, all clinical features were used except for survival months.

As shown in Appendix A, the dataset consists of 27 variables in total, consisting of 25 categorical variables and 2 numerical variables. As the study focuses on survival prediction, the target variable used is “Status” (V26) which describes the status of breast cancer patients.

Data pre-processing

Data pre-processing plays a crucial role by retaining majority of the samples used. Data grouping and data replacement were the preferred methods used in data pre-processing. The total sample size after pre-processing is (n = 5190). Following this, the dataset was then divided into two separate sets, which are training set and testing set with a ratio of 80:20. Data imputation was performed on the training set using the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (mice) package introduced by [64] in R while missing values exclusion was performed on the testing set with the ‘complete.cases’ function in R.

For Transformers, the process requires extra pre-processing before proceeding with model training. Both datasets retrieved after pre-processing from R, has to be converted from columns into a singular string form as Transformer models take inputs in the forms of sequences rather than tabular form. In Python, for the Transformer models to undergo 10-fold cross validation, the train dataset is split into train data and test data for each fold with the ‘StratifiedKFold’ class from the ‘sklearn’ library. The instance of Transformer model selected for downstream tasks, is based on the lowest training loss of each fold.

Results

Data pre-processing

The breast cancer dataset used consisted of initial (n = 5190) samples which ranges from 2006 until 2015. The purpose for selected a specific range was to ensure that the study underwent similar up-to-data medical diagnosis and clinical methods.

After initial data pre-processing, the dataset consisted of a total 26 features of which were 24 categorical variables and 2 numerical variables. The features are shown in Appendix A and Appendix B respectively.

As shown in Fig. 7, the raw dataset displays high frequencies of missingness in features such as ‘Height’ and ‘Weight’. The columns of ‘Height’ and ‘Weight’ were removed as high frequencies of missingness, would lead to poorer generation of imputed data as MICE imputation assigns missing values based on existing information from columns. Another thing to note is the removal of temporal data such as survival months as the study focuses on predicting survival status of patients based on clinical data rather than the survival rate of patients.Fig. 7Heatmap showing the proportion of missingness in the BC dataset

Heatmap showing the proportion of missingness in the BC dataset

The dataset was split in accordance to the 80:20 ratio which were respectively training dataset (n = 4153) and validation dataset (n = 1037). Using data imputation with MICE, the training dataset was retained with the same number of samples, however the validation dataset was reduced to (n = 856) by removing the rows which contain missing values.

Discussion

ML is widely used in breast cancer survival prediction studies [10, 12]. This in turns leads to an oversaturation of ML models in breast cancer studies with its distinction in sampling data used and varying parameters. Therefore, our study explores the application of Transformers as an alternative to traditional ML models in breast cancer survival prediction.

Data pre-processing

Due to high frequencies of missingness in our dataset, we proposed the use of data imputation on the training set to retain the number of samples for the study. The reasoning behind only imputing the training set is to keep the testing set free from any manipulation. Data imputation not only preserves the number of samples, it also helps in improving downstream predictive performances [65]. For the traditional ML models, minimal pre-processing was used after imputation as the dataset has already been prepared. In contrast, Transformers needed the dataset to undergo extra steps to ensure that it was able to perform classification. Although structured data processing for Transformers has been seen in studies [66], we still chose to concatenate all the feature columns into a single string sequence for its simplicity in execution.

Model training

Based on the training performance scores, the Transformer models were closely similar to the traditional ML models in terms of mean accuracies and mean Kappa scores. As seen from the mean Kappa scores, the majority of the models are of moderate agreement as it falls between the (0.41–0.60) range [67]. Although Cohen’s Kappa is regarded poorly, it can still be used in determining a classifier, as the Kappa value is based on how well the model’s prediction align with the actual outcomes [68]. Based on the loss scores for the Transformer models, indifferent to BERT, RoBERTa and ALBERT models, the BERT+ model has a large difference between the mean training loss and mean validation loss. This means that the BERT+ model may have overfitted to an extent of which has contributed to misclassification.

The discrepancy among the Transformer models where the BERT model has an increase of approximately 20% in accuracy as compared to RoBERTa (71.2%) and ALBERT (79.1%) could be explained via the models’ architectural design. BERT has more parameters and no compression tricks, thus able to learn richer representations. This is advantageous in our case, where utilizing imputation on the dataset may yield better representations due to imputation generating clearer patterns. In comparison, a more complex model such as RoBERTa model requires more calibration adjustments to achieve a better outcome. The ALBERT model may be less computational heavy, but in this case, a reduced model size would have limited the overall ability to capture relationships. Thus, leading back to our case of the BERT model outperforming RoBERTa and ALBERT during model training.

Model evaluation

Based on the testing performances, the Transformer models were able to yield closely similar performance outcomes to the traditional ML models. In terms of accuracy, the ML models were able to outperform the Transformer models, with the exception of the KNN model. By observing the precision and recall scores for the Transformer models and ML models, it seems the Transformers model can identify higher frequencies of positive class cases. In contrast, the ML models are better in classifying the cases as positive class. Based on the F1 scores, the ML model with the best overall performance is the LR model at (0.900) and the Transformer model with the best overall performance is the ALBERT model at (0.885). In comparison to the ML models’ performances by [10], our study’s ML models showed similar outcomes for the metrices such as accuracy, precision and recall as shown in Fig. 10.Fig. 10Comparison of ML models’ performances between our study and the study by [10]

Comparison of ML models’ performances between our study and the study by [10]

Although most performance metrics show good results, the AUC score for all Transformer and ML models is low, below the clinically acceptable score of (0.8) [69, 70]. The low AUC score for the study could be influenced by an imbalance in class, where majority of the samples used were of ‘ALIVE’ status in comparison to the ‘DEAD’ status; and the possibility of data imputation introducing more noise to the dataset when imputing on dataset with high frequencies of missingness [71].

Findings and considerations

Although the proposed Transformer models were below the expected performance outcome, several key insights can be derived to further improve Transformers for future breast cancer survival studies. Firstly, the characteristics of dataset used is key in determining a model’s classification performance. A classification model’s performance is directly influenced with different forms of data structure, data context and data features [72, 73]. This is reflected in our study, where the Transformer models were unable to fully utilize its potential due to limitations such as small sample population and the dataset converted from tabular form did not contain more contextual meaning.

In our study, we tried to improve the model performance of the BERT model by including the words extracted from the training data which are not found in the BERT vocabulary. This is also done by other clinical studies with Transformers in order to improve text-mining related tasks in biomedical and clinical fields [35, 36]. One aspect of improvement to our current approach is that instead of learning tokens from the training dataset, we could implement text-mining extraction on radiology reports [74] and pathology reports [75] to build a breast cancer specific corpus.

Although the BERT+ model showed an overall improvement to the BERT model in performance, it also came with criticisms which should be emphasized for future studies. The mean training loss for the BERT+ model was the lowest (0.145), while its mean validation loss was quite high (0.510). A low mean training loss means that the model generalized quickly to the training dataset. However, a large difference between the mean training loss to mean validation loss showed that the model was too generalized, leading to the model overfitting [76]. Several reasons to overfitting could be due to the Transformer models were too complex, a small training dataset was used and the possibility of noise in the training dataset.

Another consideration for improvement is the tuning of hyperparameters for Transformers. As reflected in this study, hyperparameter tuning was not the focus as this is an exploratory study on the application of Transformers in breast cancer survival prediction. However, two considerations can be inferred based on the hyperparameters used in this study for the improvement in future studies. Setting aside resource complications, the number of epochs should be increased when handling Transformers. This is due to the fact that Transformer models are high in complexity and having more epochs will increase the learning opportunities for the models. Although increasing epochs seem feasible aside from the extended training duration, we should take into account that as training time goes on, the model will be less reliable as it has generalized with the training data [77]. This should not be disheartening as it can overcome with early stopping once the model reaches peak performance [78]. Secondly, the learning rate should also be taken into consideration as it is crucial to the training of Transformer models as it influences model convergence speed which openly affects overall performances. A study by Liang et al., found that by using the sensitivity of parameters and local temporal variation of sensitivity to adaptively adjust the learning rate, it leads to better generalization of the models [79].

Limitations

Throughout the study, several limitations have been identified. Firstly, the dataset used contains high frequencies of missingness as seen in Fig. 5. Although the use case of data imputation helped in retaining the number of samples in this study, the pitfalls for data imputation should be taken in consideration if used for future studies [71, 80].

Secondly, the dataset used for the study was too small. For Transformers, having larger quantities of training sample would be more effective for the model to learn and generalize better. A suggestion for this, is to use multiple breast cancer data from other health institutions. As Transformers mainly learn from representational context of words or sentences, researchers could use pathology or radiology reports to build a breast cancer specific corpus. In addition, if using tabular format, sentence fillers could be used to enhance the information regarding the columns in each sequence [81]. An alternative if using tabular representation would be to implement a tabular pre-processing method of tokenization for Transformers [66].

Finally, fine-tuning of hyperparameters for Transformer models was kept to a minimal as the study focuses on introducing Transformers as an alternative to traditional ML models in breast cancer survival prediction. For further breast cancer survival studies with Transformers, researchers are advised to delve deeper into fine-tuning hyperparameters as Transformers are highly sensitive models [81].

Our study demonstrated that Transformers can be considered as an alternative to ML models in breast cancer survival prediction. However, the limitations of the study also showed that there are room for improvement in Transformers for breast cancer survival prediction. Other notable future advancements of Transformers in breast cancer survival include a knowledge hub specifically for breast cancer, text-mining improvements in radiology and pathology reports, and feature extraction and understanding with Transformers.

Conclusion

This study explored Transformers as an alternative to traditional ML models in breast cancer survival prediction in Malaysia. Based on the training and testing performances, the Transformer models were able to achieve closely similar prediction outcomes to traditional ML models. This seen as the Transformer models achieved close performance scores (Accuracy [77.2% − 81.1%], Precision [0.832–0.858], Recall [0.870–0.942], Specificity [0.328 - 0.482], F1 Score [0.856 - 0.885] and AUC [0.630–0.690]) to the traditional ML models (Accuracy [79.9% − 83.4%], Precision [0.940–0.985], Recall [0.815–0.852], Specificity [0.608 - 0.800], F1 Score [0.881 - 0.900] and AUC [0.598–0.684]). Although there are several limitations surrounding the study, the possibilities for improvement are vast for Transformers in breast cancer survival prediction. In summary, with proper improvements and considerations, Transformers can be further utilized not only as an alternative to ML models in breast cancer survival prediction, but also in other tasks, like a knowledge hub specifically for breast cancer, text-mining improvements in radiology and pathology reports, and feature extraction and understanding for breast cancer studies.
