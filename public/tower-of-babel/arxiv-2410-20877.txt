Surfaces proper homotopy equivalent to graphs and their Dehn–Nielsen–Baer maps
Ryan Dickmann, Hannah Hoganson, and Sanghoon Kwak

Abstract
. Motivated by the recent work of Algom-Kfir–Bestvina introducing the mapping class group of an infinite graph via proper homotopy equivalences, we give a necessary and sufficient condition for a surface to be properly homotopy equivalent to a graph. We consider second-countable orientable surfaces that are possibly infinite-type and have noncompact boundary. For surfaces proper homotopy equivalent to graphs, we explore the basic properties of the induced map between the mapping class groups of the surface and the graph. We view this induced map as the basis of a Dehn–Nielsen–Baer analog in the setting of infinite-type surfaces.



1.
Introduction

Let a surface be a connected, orientable, second-countable, 2-manifold with boundary, and let a graph be a connected 1-dimensional CW complex. It is well known that any noncompact surface or any surface with nonempty boundary is homotopy equivalent to a graph. However, the basic question of which surfaces are
proper
homotopy equivalent (PHE) to a graph has not been addressed. Recall a continuous map is said to be proper when the preimage of any compact set is compact.

Intuitively speaking, for a surface to be PHE to a graph it needs to have enough boundary to push off onto a graph in a proper manner. We say a separating simple closed curve
\alpha
in
S
is
boundary isolating
if the quotient space
S/\alpha
is a wedge of surfaces consisting of a surface with boundary and a noncompact surface without boundary. See
Figure

1
for some examples. We will show that having a boundary isolating curve is an obstruction to being PHE to a graph, and in fact we have the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

A surface
S
is proper homotopy equivalent to a graph if and only if
S
has nonempty boundary and no boundary isolating curve.

The forward direction is proved by comparing the 2nd compactly supported cohomology groups of surfaces and graphs. Since top compactly supported cohomology is trivial for manifolds with boundary, we actually introduce a new proper homotopy invariant we call
compactly supported cohomology at infinity
(see
Section

3
). For a noncompact manifold, the top compactly supported cohomology at infinity detects ends that are not accumulated by boundary. For the other direction, we build a proper deformation retraction by cutting the surface
S
into pieces and performing a proper deformation retraction onto a graph in each one.

Our discussion will largely focus on surfaces of infinite type, meaning surfaces with infinitely generated fundamental group, such as the surfaces in
Figure

1
. We remark that every surface with nonempty boundary and no boundary isolating curve has noncompact boundary. By allowing an infinite-type surface
S
to have noncompact boundary, we are considering a larger class of surfaces than much of the recent literature on infinite-type surfaces. For example, not only can there be infinitely many compact boundary components accumulating to an end, but there can be noncompact boundary components homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
with complicated behavior. See
Section

2.3
for more examples and the classification for surfaces with noncompact boundary components.

Mapping class groups.
The mapping class group of a surface
S
with nonempty boundary is

\operatorname{Map}(S):=\operatorname{Homeo}(S,\partial S)/\sim,

where
\operatorname{Homeo}(S,\partial S)
is the group of homeomorphisms of
S
pointwise fixing the boundary of
S
, and
f\sim g
for
f,g\in\operatorname{Homeo}(S,\partial S)
when
f
is isotopic to
g
relative to
\partial S
. Note a homeomorphism fixing the boundary must also preserve orientation. If
\partial S=\varnothing
, then we write
\operatorname{Homeo}^{+}(S)
, the group of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms, in the definition of the mapping class group. When
S
has noncompact boundary components, its mapping class group was first systematically studied by the first author
[
Dic23
]
. Recently, he classified the
pure
mapping class groups with automatic continuity including mapping class groups of surfaces with noncompact boundary
[
Dic24
]
.

Following Algom-Kfir–Bestvina
[
AB25
]
we define the mapping class group of a graph
{\Gamma}
as

\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma}):=\operatorname{PHE}({\Gamma})/\sim,

where
\operatorname{PHE}({\Gamma})
is the group of proper homotopy equivalences of
{\Gamma}
. Recall a
proper homotopy equivalence
or PHE is a homotopy equivalence
f
that is proper with a proper inverse
g
such that
fg
and
gf
are properly homotopic to the identity. For
f,g\in\operatorname{PHE}({\Gamma})
, we let
f\sim g
when
f
is properly homotopic to
g
.

The mapping class group for a graph or surface is a topological group with a natural topology. This topology is a quotient of the respective compact-open topology on either the group of PHEs of the graph or the homeomorphism group of the surface. See
[
AB25
, Section 4]
and
[
Vla19
]
for more details.
When a proper homotopy equivalence exists between a surface and a graph, it naturally determines a homomorphism between their mapping class groups. We show the following basic properties of the induced map.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
S
be an infinite-type surface properly homotopy equivalent to a graph
{\Gamma}
. Then the induced homomorphism

\Theta:\operatorname{Map}(S)\rightarrow\operatorname{Map}(\Gamma)

is continuous and has a closed image. Moreover, the kernel is topologically generated by Dehn twists about the compact boundary components.

We remark that as a corollary to
Theorem

1.2
we have the mapping class group of a Sliced Loch Ness Monster surface
S_{SLN}
(for example the bottom-right surface in
Figure

1
) is not residually finite. This follows from the observation that
S_{SLN}
is PHE to the Loch Ness Monster graph
{\Gamma}_{LN}
, that
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma}_{LN})
is not residually finite by
[
DHK25
, Theorem D]
, and that
\Theta
is injective when
S
has no compact boundary components. This was also shown by the first author
[
Dic24
]
who proved that
\operatorname{Map}(S_{SLN})
is automatically continuous. As a corollary, he shows it has no nontrivial homomorphisms to a countable group and hence is not residually finite.

We also remark
Theorem

1.1
says any infinite-type surface with compact boundary is not properly homotopic to a graph, so
Theorem

1.2
does not apply. This is the class of surfaces much of the recent literature on big mapping class groups has focused on, so we propose the following open-ended question.

Question 1.3
.

When
S
is an infinite-type surface with (possibly empty) compact boundary, does there exist any geometrically meaningful homomorphism from
\operatorname{Map}(S)
to
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
where
{\Gamma}
is a locally finite, infinite graph?

On the other hand, one can ask for a characterization of the image of the map
\Theta
in
Theorem

1.2
.

Question 1.4
.

Is there an algebraic/geometric description of the image of
\Theta
with respect to
{\Gamma}
?

One natural guess for the image is the following.
Fix
\phi:S\to{\Gamma}
a proper homotopy equivalence.
Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of properly immersed oriented curves
S^{1}\to{\Gamma}
and immersed oriented lines
\mathbb{R}\to{\Gamma}
in
{\Gamma}
that are images of the components of
\partial S
via
\phi
. Denote by
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma},\mathcal{B})<\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
the subgroup of mapping classes that fix each line or curve in
\mathcal{B}
and its orientation up to homotopy.

Conjecture 1.5
.

The image of
\Theta
is
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma},\mathcal{B})
.

In
Section

6
, we verify that this conjecture holds if one restricts themselves to a subgroup of the domain and image where elements are the limit of compactly supported elements. We also discuss another possible restriction on the image when one considers elements that are not the limit of compactly supported maps. In particular, elements in the image of
\Theta
have even flux in the sense of Domat–Hoganson–Kwak
[
DHK23
,
DHK25
]
.

Extended mapping class groups.

We note that the image of
\Theta
lies in
\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})
, the pure mapping class group of
{\Gamma}
, the subgroup of elements fixing ends pointwise. This is because
\operatorname{Map}(S)=\operatorname{PMap}(S)
when
S
has no boundary isolating curve as every end of
S
is accumulated by boundary, and elements in
\operatorname{Map}(S)
fix boundary components pointwise. Since
\Theta
is an equivariant homomorphism under the action on the space of ends of
S
and
{\Gamma}
, the
\Theta
-image of
\operatorname{PMap}(S)=\operatorname{Map}(S)
lies in
\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})
.

To achieve a map that hits more elements of the mapping class group
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
, we consider the
(boundary) extended mapping class group

\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)
of a surface
S
defined as:

\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S):=\operatorname{Homeo}(S)/\sim,

where
\operatorname{Homeo}(S)
is the group of homeomorphisms on
S
(not necessarily fixing boundary pointwise or orientation preserving), and for
f,g\in\operatorname{Homeo}(S)
, we let
f\sim g
when
f
is isotopic to
g
(not necessarily rel boundary). See
Example

5.8
for some cases where replacing isotopy with homotopy or proper homotopy can change the isomorphism type of the extending mapping class group.
When
\partial S=\varnothing
, then
\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)
coincides with the (usual) extended mapping class group
\operatorname{Map}^{\pm}(S)
, introduced in, e.g.,
[
FM12
, Section 8]
.

Dehn twists about compact boundary components are isotopic to the identity in
\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)
as we are allowed to ‘undo the twist’ by an isotopy along the boundary. For surfaces with noncompact boundaries, we have some new interesting maps in the extended mapping class group. Consider a surface formed by deleting a set of points from a compact boundary component. We will refer to the union of the resulting boundary components as a
degenerate chain
. For more detail, see
Example

2.5
and the discussion thereafter. Now we have Dehn twists about degenerate chains, and as shown in
Figure

2
, we can even have
fractional Dehn twists
about them, which cyclically permute the ends in the degenerate chain. Disarlo
[
Dis15
]
also studied surfaces with points removed from the boundary (called
ciliated surfaces

(S,\mathbb{P})
where
\mathbb{P}
is a finite set of marked points on the boundary of
S
) and made the observation that the extended mapping class group contains fractional Dehn twists.

In this context, a proper homotopy equivalence
S\to{\Gamma}
still induces a well-defined homomorphism between mapping class groups, and we obtain a similar theorem to
Theorem

1.2
for the extended mapping class group as follows.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
S
be an infinite-type surface properly homotopy equivalent to a graph. Then the induced homomorphism
\widetilde{\Theta}
from the proper homotopy equivalence

\widetilde{\Theta}:\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)\rightarrow\operatorname{Map}(\Gamma)

is continuous and has a closed image. Moreover, it is injective.

One can also ask
Questions

1.3
and
1.4
about the homomorphism
\widetilde{\Theta}
in
Theorem

1.6
. For the set
\mathcal{B}
, as defined in
Conjecture

1.5
, of properly immersed oriented curves and lines in
{\Gamma}
coming from
\partial S
, denote by
\operatorname{Map}^{\star}({\Gamma},\mathcal{B})<\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
the subgroup of mapping classes that fix the set of lines and curves up to homotopy (possibly permuting classes and reversing orientations). Then we have the following conjecture, analogous to
Conjecture

1.5
.

Conjecture 1.7
.

The image of
\widetilde{\Theta}
is
\operatorname{Map}^{\star}({\Gamma},\mathcal{B})
.

Dehn–Nielsen–Baer Theory.
We now discuss the motivation for the investigation of the homomorphisms between mapping class groups of surfaces and mapping class groups of graphs. The initial object of interest is the natural homomorphism
\sigma
from a mapping class group of a surface
S
to
\operatorname{Out}(\pi_{1}(S))
determined by the action of a homeomorphism of
S
on
\pi_{1}(S)
, up to changing base point. For closed surfaces possibly with finitely many points removed, the well-known Dehn–Nielsen–Baer theorem describes the relationship as follows. Let
S_{g,p}
denote the unique surface with
g
genus and
p
punctures.

Theorem 1.8

(Dehn–Nielsen–Baer,
[
FM12
, Section 8]
)
.

Let
S
be a compact closed surface with genus
g\geq 1
. The homomorphism

\sigma:\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)\rightarrow\operatorname{Out}(\pi_{1}(S))

is an isomorphism. If
S=S_{g,p}
with
g\geq 1
or
p\geq 4
, then
\sigma
is injective, but not surjective when
p\neq 0
. Moreover, the image of
\sigma
is the subgroup of outer automorphisms that preserve the set of conjugacy classes in
\pi_{1}(S)
of the
2p
oriented curves bounding a single puncture.

The conditions on
g
and
p
are to remove sporadic cases. For surfaces with boundary, a similar statement holds where we additionally need the conjugacy classes coming from boundary curves to be preserved. Moreover, the kernel of
\sigma
is no longer trivial, and it consists of Dehn twists about compact boundary components. Note when a compact surface has nonempty boundary, it is homotopy
equivalent to a finite graph
{\Gamma}
. Thus, its fundamental group is the free group
F_{n}
of finite rank
n=2g+p-1
, and
Theorem

1.8
gives a homomorphism
\operatorname{Map}(S)
to
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
.

We remark that the existence of the map
\sigma
in
Theorem

1.8
is immediate. However, characterizing the image of
\sigma
(in particular proving surjectivity when
S
is closed) requires nontrivial work. In comparison with our contribution in
Theorems

1.2
and
1.6
, establishing the maps
\Theta
and
\widetilde{\Theta}
themselves already requires a substantial amount of work. These maps are built for the class of surfaces that are PHE to graphs. In some sense, this additional difficulty is inevitable, since we require more information than how each homeomorphism
h
acts algebraically on the
\pi_{1}
, as in the classical Dehn–Nielsen–Baer theorem; we do not discard all the topology of
S
.
Moreover, as described in
Question

1.4
, a complete characterization of the image of the DNB maps remains open.

For a finite graph
{\Gamma}
, it is well known that
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
is naturally isomorphic to
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
via the homomorphism determined by the action of a proper homotopy equivalence on
\pi_{1}({\Gamma})
. Therefore, for compact surfaces with boundary we can view the Dehn–Nielsen–Baer homomorphism
\sigma
as the homomorphism from
\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)
to
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
where it agrees with the map
\widetilde{\Theta}
induced from the PHE from
S
to
{\Gamma}
.

Since
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
is extensively studied in analogy with the mapping class groups of closed surfaces, we initiate the investigation of the relationship between mapping class groups of general noncompact surfaces with the mapping class groups of infinite graphs due to Algom-Kfir–Bestvina
[
AB25
]
. Since homotopy classes of oriented curves in a surface
S
are in one-to-one correspondence with conjugacy classes in
\pi_{1}(S)
, we can view
Theorem

1.6
and
Conjecture

1.7
as extensions to
Theorem

1.8
.

A more classical Dehn–Nielsen–Baer (DNB) map
\operatorname{Map}(S)\to\operatorname{Out}(F_{\infty})
can also be studied, where
S
is an infinite-type surface and
\pi_{1}(S)
is identified with
F_{\infty}
, the free group with a countable generating set. This map agrees with the composition of
\Theta
with an analogous natural map
\operatorname{Map}(\Gamma)\to\operatorname{Out}(F_{\infty})
studied by Algom-Kfir–Bestvina
[
AB25
]
. In particular, they show the map is continuous with natural topologies on both groups, but the image is not a closed subgroup for infinite rank graphs. The map to
\operatorname{Map}(\Gamma)
is potentially more fruitful as, notably, the image is a closed subgroup. An interpretation of this is that the topology on
\operatorname{Out}(F_{\infty})
is in a sense weaker than the topology on
\operatorname{Map}(\Gamma)
. Additionally,
\operatorname{Out}(F_{\infty})
contains no information about the ends of the original space, and there are no immediate geometric objects to study such as lines up to proper homotopy in graphs.

Having the DNB map as an isomorphism has another interpretation that
every homotopy equivalence
S\to S
is homotopic to a homeomorphism.

Das in
[
Das24
]
showed this is not the case when
S
is a noncompact surface without boundary, by providing an uncountable set of pairwise nonhomeomorphic surfaces that are all homotopy equivalent. Furthermore, Das–Gadgil–Nair in
[
DGN25
]
characterized when a homotopy equivalence between noncompact surfaces without boundary is homotopic to a homeomorphism using the Goldman bracket.

As a different kind of homomorphism to
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
, we also remark that recently Udall
[
Uda24
]
constructed a map
\Psi:\operatorname{Map}(M_{\Gamma})\to\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
using a retraction
M_{\Gamma}\to{\Gamma}
, where
M_{\Gamma}
is the double handlebody constructed from the double of the thickening of
{\Gamma}
. Extending
[
Lau74
,
HV04
,
BBP23
]
to infinite graphs
{\Gamma}
, he showed that
\Psi
is surjective and the kernel of the map
\Psi
is generated by the Dehn twists about core spheres (corresponding to the core subgraph of
{\Gamma}
, the smallest connected subgraph of
{\Gamma}
containing all immersed images of
S^{1}
). He further showed that the corresponding short exact sequence splits.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Infinite graphs

A graph is a connected 1-dimensional CW complex. An
infinite
graph is a locally finite graph that is infinite as a CW complex. Namely, it has infinitely many 0-cells (vertices) and 1-cells (edges) with every vertex having finite valence. The
rank

\operatorname{rk}({\Gamma})
of a graph
{\Gamma}
is the rank of its fundamental group, a free group. The rank itself is a complete homotopy invariant for a finite graph. Indeed, two finite graphs are homotopy equivalent if and only if they have the same rank. However, for an infinite graph, we need more information to appropriately determine their
equivalence
.

A graph is an example of a
generalized continuum
, a topological space that is locally
compact, locally connected,
\sigma
-compact, and connected Hausdorff. A
generalized continuum
X
admits a compact exhaustion

\{K_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
X
such that
K_{n}\subset\textrm{Int}(K_{n+1})
. The

(Freudenthal) end space

E(X)
(due to
[
Fre31
]
) of a generalized continuum
X
is the inverse limit

E(X):=\varprojlim_{K\subset X}\pi_{0}(X\setminus K),

where
K
runs over the compact subsets of
X
, and
\pi_{0}(X\setminus K)
is
the set of path components of
X\setminus K
. Each element of
E(X)
is called
a
end
of
X
. See
[
BQ01
, Section I.9]
for more
details. See also
[
Uda24
, Section 2.1]
.

Now consider a locally finite, infinite graph
{\Gamma}
and consider its end space
E({\Gamma})
.
If the sequence of complementary components corresponding to
e\in E({\Gamma})
consists of infinite rank components, then we say
e
is
accumulated by loops
. We denote by
E_{\ell}({\Gamma})
the set of ends accumulated by loops. With the standard inverse limit topology on
E({\Gamma})
, the set
E_{\ell}({\Gamma})
is a closed subset of
E({\Gamma})
.

Recall a continuous map is called
proper
if every compact set has compact preimage.
Ayala–Dominguez–Márquez–Quintero
[
ADMQ90
]
classified infinite graphs up to proper homotopy equivalences using their ranks and end spaces. A homeomorphism between pairs of spaces
(A,B)\to(C,D)
with
A\supset B
and
C\supset D
is a homeomorphism
A\to C
that restricts to a homeomorphism
B\to D
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
ADMQ90
, Theorem 2.7]
)
.

Let
{\Gamma}
and
{\Gamma}^{\prime}
be infinite graphs. If
\operatorname{rk}{\Gamma}=\operatorname{rk}{\Gamma}^{\prime}
and there is a homeomorphism
(E({\Gamma}),E_{\ell}({\Gamma}))\to(E({\Gamma}^{\prime}),E_{\ell}({\Gamma}^{\prime}))
, then the homeomorphism extends to a proper homotopy equivalence
{\Gamma}\to{\Gamma}^{\prime}
. If
{\Gamma}
and
{\Gamma}^{\prime}
are trees, then this extension is unique up to proper homotopy.

This classification motivates using proper homotopy equivalence (PHE) as the notion of symmetry of an infinite graph. Indeed, Algom-Kfir–Bestvina
[
AB25
]
introduced the
mapping class group
of a locally finite infinite graph
{\Gamma}
as:

\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma}):=\{\phi:{\Gamma}\to{\Gamma}\textrm{ PHE}\}/\{\textrm{proper homotopy}\}.

More precisely, they defined the mapping class group of
{\Gamma}
as the group of proper homotopy equivalences that necessarily have a proper homotopy inverse up to proper homotopy. See
[
AB25
, Example 4.1]
for an example of a homotopy equivalence which itself is proper, but whose homotopy inverse is never proper.
Hence, such an element should not be included in the mapping class group.

A proper homotopy equivalence induces a homeomorphism between their end spaces.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
Por95
, Section 2]
, see also
[
Geo08
, Section 13.4]
)
.

A proper map
f:X\to Y
between connected, locally compact, Hausdorff spaces induces a continuous map
Ef:E(X)\to E(Y)
between the end spaces. Moreover, two properly homotopic proper maps
f,g:X\to Y
induce the same continuous map
Ef=Eg
on
E(X)
. In particular, a proper homotopy equivalence
f:X\to Y
induces a homeomorphism
Ef:E(X)\to E(Y)
. Also,
E
is functorial;
E(f\circ g)=E(f)\circ E(g)
, for proper maps
g:X\to Y
and
f:Y\to Z
.

In fact,
Proposition

2.2
extends to the end space pair
(E,E_{\ell})
; a proper homotopy equivalence with proper inverse on an infinite graph induces a homeomorphism on the pair
(E,E_{\ell})
. Also, properly homotopic proper homotopy equivalences induce the same homeomorphism on the pair, so this induces the action of
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
on
(E({\Gamma}),E_{\ell}({\Gamma}))
by homeomorphism. We call the kernel of this action the
pure mapping class group
of
{\Gamma}
, and denote it by
\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})
. In short, it fits into the short exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Homeo}(E({\Gamma}),E_{\ell}({\Gamma}))\longrightarrow 1

We record here the pure mapping class group of a
finite type
graph for
the future reference.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
AB25
, Corollary 3.9]
and
[
DHK25
, Proposition 2.3]
)
.

Let
{\Gamma}
be a graph with finite rank
n
and finite end space with
e
ends.
Then

\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})\cong\begin{cases}\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})&\text{if $e=0$,}\\
F_{n}^{e-1}\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(F_{n})&\text{if $e>0$.}\end{cases}

2.2.
Surfaces with compact boundary

As mentioned in the introduction, a surface is a connected, orientable,
second-countable 2-manifold. We first focus on those with compact boundary. The
end space
E(S)
of a surface
S
with its natural topology is defined
analogously to the end space of a graph by considering complementary components
of compact subsurfaces. An end is said to be
accumulated by genus
when
any complementary component corresponding to the end has infinite genus. The set
of ends accumulated by genus is denoted by
E_{\infty}(S)
. With the standard
inverse limit topology on
E(S)
, the set
E_{\infty}(S)
is a closed subset of

E(S)
. Note
E_{\infty}(S)=\varnothing
exactly when the surface has finite
genus. Due to the work of Kerékjártó
[
Ker23
]
and
Richards
[
Ric63
]
, the homeomorphism type of a surface with compact
boundary is essentially determined by these two sets as well as its genus and the
number of compact boundary components.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
Ker23
,
Ric63
]
)
.

Two surfaces with compact boundary are homeomorphic if and only if the pairs
(E(S),E_{\infty}(S))
are homeomorphic and they have the same genus and the same number of boundary components.

2.3.
Surfaces with noncompact boundary

First, we introduce some examples that highlight the new phenomena in the noncompact boundary case.

Example 2.5

(Disks with boundary points removed)
.

Take a disk and remove an embedded closed subset
I
of the Cantor set from the boundary. In the case that
I
is homeomorphic to a Cantor set, this gives a surface with countably many noncompact boundary components yet uncountably many ends.

Each of these surfaces can be constructed by taking a small closed neighborhood of an infinite, locally finite, tree properly embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Figure 3.
Nonhomeomorphic but proper homotopy equivalent surfaces.

See
Figure

3
for an example of two disks with boundary points removed. Although they have the same end spaces, they are not homeomorphic as the one has a noncompact boundary component whose ends are both accumulating points of other ends, but the other does not have such a noncompact boundary component. Hence, to distinguish them, we need more information.

We refer to the ends from
Example

2.5
as
degenerate ends
. More precisely, a
degenerate end
is an end that has a closed neighborhood
U
which is homeomorphic to a disk with boundary points removed.

Example 2.6

(Sliced Loch Ness Monsters)
.

Take a surface from

Example

2.5
with
n\in\{1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}

noncompact boundary components. Attach infinitely many handles going out to
the single end in the
n=1
case, and in all other cases attach infinitely

3.
Surfaces not properly homotopic to graphs

Now we prove the forward direction of

A curve on a surface

is the image of an immersion

, and we say a curve is simple if it can be realized as the image of an embedding
S^{1}\to S

Definition 3.1
.

Let

•

•

•

Equivalently, the quotient

is a wedge of surfaces consisting of a surface with nonempty boundary and a noncompact surface without boundary.

See

The existence of a boundary isolating curve says that the surface “has a large enough subsurface without boundary.” On the other hand, for a surface without a boundary isolating curve we can say “every end sees boundary.” The following statement makes this precise.

Proposition 3.2
.

S
has a boundary isolating curve if and only if

has nonempty boundary and there exists a closed neighborhood

of some end such that

has one compact component.

Proof.

For the forward direction, take

. For the other direction,

is a boundary isolating curve.

The following two facts are the main technical tools.

Fact 3.3

(Poincaré Duality)
.

Let
M
be a closed oriented (possibly noncompact)
n
-manifold and

k
be any integer such that
0\leq k\leq n
.
Then we have an isomorphism

H^{k}_{c}(M;\mathbb{Z})\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}H_{n-k}(M;\mathbb{Z}).

Fact 3.4

(
[
Ive86
, Proposition III.6.6]
, PHE-invariance of Compactly Supported Cohomology)
.

Let
G
be an abelian group.
Two properly homotopic maps
f,g:X\to Y
between locally compact spaces
X,Y
induce the same map

f^{*},g^{*}:H_{c}^{*}(Y,G)\to H_{c}^{*}(X,G).

In particular, if
h:X\to Y
is a proper homotopy equivalence (with proper homotopy inverse), then

h^{*}:H_{c}^{*}(Y,G)\to H_{c}^{*}(X,G)

is an isomorphism.

These facts quickly show that a surface that is PHE to a graph must have nonempty boundary:

Proposition 3.5
.

If
S
is a surface without boundary, then
S
is not properly homotopy equivalent to a graph.

Proof.

Poincaré duality (
Fact

3.3
) gives an isomorphism
H^{2}_{c}(S;\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{0}(S;\mathbb{Z})
, and
H_{0}(S;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}
because we have assumed our surfaces are connected. On the other hand,
H^{2}_{c}({\Gamma};\mathbb{Z})=0
for any graph
{\Gamma}
because it is one-dimensional. By
Fact

3.4
, we see that
S
cannot be properly homotopy equivalent to a graph.
∎

Note that we needed to use compactly supported cohomology as opposed to standard cohomology since the top cohomology group of a noncompact manifold is trivial. On the other hand, any manifold with boundary has trivial compactly supported top cohomology, so we need a new invariant to detect when an end is accumulated by boundary.

Let
n\geq 0
. Let
X
be a locally compact,
\sigma
-compact, Hausdorff space. For compact sets
K,L\subset X
, an inclusion
K\hookrightarrow L
induces a restriction homomorphism between relative compactly supported cohomology groups:
H^{n}_{c}(X,L)\to H^{n}_{c}(X,K)
. Let
H^{\bullet}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
be the
compactly supported cohomology at infinity
of
X
, defined as the inverse limit:

H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z}):=\varprojlim_{K\subset X\>\text{compact}}H^{n}_{c}(X,K;\mathbb{Z}),

for every
n\geq 0
. As a compact exhaustion
\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of
X
is cofinal with respect to the set of compact subsets of
X
, it can be simply put as:

H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})=\varprojlim_{i}H^{n}_{c}(X,K_{i};\mathbb{Z}).

We denote by
r_{i}^{X}:H^{n}_{c}(X,K_{i};Z)\to H^{n}_{c}(X,K_{i-1};\mathbb{Z})
the restriction homomorphisms induced by the inclusions
K_{i-1}\hookrightarrow K_{i}
from the compact exhaustion of
X
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Compactly supported cohomology at infinity is a proper homotopy invariant in locally compact,
\sigma
-compact, Hausdorff spaces.

Proof.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a proper map between locally compact,
\sigma
-compact, Hausdorff spaces. Take a compact exhaustion
\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of
Y
. As
f^{-1}(K_{i})
is compact for each
i\geq 0
, the collection
\{f^{-1}(K_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a compact exhaustion for
X
. Also,
f
restricts to a proper map
f_{i}:f^{-1}(K_{i})\to K_{i}
for each
i\geq 0
. Hence, it induces a homomorphism
(f_{i})^{*}:H^{n}_{c}(Y,K_{i};\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c}(X,f^{-1}(K_{i});\mathbb{Z})
for each
n\geq 0
and
i\geq 0
.
Then the
(f_{i})^{*}
commute with the restriction homomorphisms
r_{i}^{X}
and
r_{i}^{Y}
induced by the compact exhaustions of
X
and
Y
respectively, it follows by the universal property of inverse limits that
f^{*}:=\varprojlim_{i}(f_{i})^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(Y;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is a homomorphism.
Moreover, once again by the universal property of inverse limits, for proper maps
f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to Z
, it follows that
(gf)^{*}=f^{*}g^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(Z;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
.

Consider the standard projection
p:X\times[0,1]\to X
defined as
p(x,t):=x
for each
x\in X
and
t\in[0,1]
. We claim the induced map

p^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X\times[0,1];\mathbb{Z})

is an isomorphism. Consider a compact exhaustion
\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of
X
. Then
p^{-1}(K_{i})=K_{i}\times[0,1]
for each
i\geq 1
, and
\{K_{i}\times[0,1]\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms a compact exhaustion of
X\times[0,1]
. The restriction
p_{i}:=p|_{K_{i}\times[0,1]}:K_{i}\times[0,1]\to K_{i}
is a proper map, as
p^{-1}(K_{i})=K_{i}\times[0,1]
. Moreover,
p_{i}
is a proper homotopy equivalence with a proper homotopy inverse (the inclusion). Therefore,
p
induces an isomorphism
H^{n}_{c}(X,K_{i};\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c}(X\times[0,1],K_{i}\times[0,1];\mathbb{Z})
for each
i\geq 1
. Since the
r_{i}^{X}
and
r_{i}^{X\times[0,1]}
commute with the
p_{i}^{*}
, we get the desired isomorphism
p^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X\times[0,1];\mathbb{Z})
.

Now suppose
f
and
g
are properly homotopic proper maps. We will show
f^{*}=g^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(Y;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
for
n\geq 0
.
Let
H:X\times I\to Y
be the homotopy between
f
and
g
such that
H(x,0)=f(x)
and
H(x,1)=g(x)
for all
x\in X
. For
t\in[0,1]
, let
\iota_{t}:X\to X\times[0,1]
be the section of
H
such that
\iota_{t}(x):=(x,t)
for
x\in X
.
Then we have
p\iota_{t}=\text{Id}_{X}
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
Also, by the previous paragraph, the induced map
p^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X\times[0,1];\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism.
Since
(p\iota_{t})^{*}=(\text{Id}_{X})^{*}=\text{Id}_{H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X)}
, we have
(\iota_{t})^{*}=(p^{*})^{-1}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X\times[0,1];\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
for every
t\in[0,1]
. In particular,
(\iota_{0})^{*}=(\iota_{1})^{*}
. Since
f=H\iota_{0}
and
g=H\iota_{1}
, it follows that

f^{*}=\iota_{0}^{*}H^{*}=\iota_{1}^{*}H^{*}=g^{*},

as desired.

Finally, suppose
f:X\to Y
is a proper homotopy equivalence and say
g:Y\to X
is its proper homotopy inverse. That is,
f\circ g
is properly homotopic to the identity, and so is
g\circ f
. By the previous paragraph,
(gf)^{*}=f^{*}g^{*}
is the identity map on
H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
and
g^{*}f^{*}
is the identity map on
H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
. This implies that
f^{*}:H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(Y;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}_{c_{\infty}}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism, concluding the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main proposition of this section.

Proposition 3.7
.

A surface has a boundary isolating curve if and only if it has nontrivial 2nd compactly supported cohomology at infinity. Therefore, a surface is PHE to a graph only if it has no boundary isolating curve.

Proof.

The therefore statement follows from
Lemma

3.6
and the observation that a graph has no 2-cells and thus trivial 2nd compactly supported cohomology at infinity.

Suppose

has a boundary isolating curve
\alpha
. Let
\{K_{i}\}
be a compact exhaustion of
S
such that each
K_{i}
contains
\alpha

is boundary isolating, the quotient

is a wedge of surfaces at least one of which is noncompact with empty boundary. Fixing some
i
, we have from excision that
H^{2}_{c}(S,K_{i};\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}_{c}(S/K_{i};\mathbb{Z})
with some nonzero
a\in H^{2}_{c}(S,K_{i})
corresponding to this noncompact surface with empty boundary.
A surface component in the wedge
S/K_{i}
corresponds to a connected component of
S\setminus K_{i}
. Let
S_{i}
be the connected component of
S\setminus K_{i}
corresponding to
a
.

Note for all

, the containment
K_{i}\subseteq K_{j}
gives a reverse containment on connected components of the complements
S\setminus K_{i}
and
S\setminus K_{j}
.
Since
S_{i}
has no boundaries or isolated ends (other than the ones coming from removing
K_{i}
from

), there is some component
S_{j}
of

without boundaries or isolated ends (excluding those arising from deleting
K_{j}
from

S_{j}\subset S_{i}
. This implies
we get an

corresponding to

whose image in

is

is nontrivial.

On the other hand, if a surface does not have a boundary isolating curve then there exists

is a wedge of surfaces with nonempty boundary or

(when

We note that the above proof also applies to
Proposition

3.5
. The 2nd compactly supported cohomology at infinity is also useful for distinguishing the proper homotopy type of some surfaces.

Example 3.8
.

Let
S_{1}
be the genus zero surface with three ends, two of which are accumulated by compact boundary components. Let
S_{2}
be the genus zero surface with three ends, one accumulated by compact boundary components. By

3.7
, these two surfaces are not PHE to a graph. Moreover, we can distinguish their PHE types using

. Since
H^{2}_{c_{\infty}}(S_{1};\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}
and
H^{2}_{c_{\infty}}(S_{2};\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, these surfaces are not PHE to each other.

We note that there are some alternative approaches to the problem that may be useful when considering the proper homotopy classes of all surfaces. One can directly prove a surface with an interior puncture is not PHE to a graph using the fact that any finite tuple of non-nullhomotopic curves that are homotopic intersects. First, parameterize the puncture as
S^{1}\times[0,\infty)
and note the images of the
S^{1}\times\{t\}
are not nullhomotopic as a PHE preserves
\pi_{1}
. Since they are homotopic to each other in
S
, their images are also homotopic in the graph. In particular, the images of
S^{1}\times\{0\}
and
S^{1}\times\{t\}
intersect for all
t>0
, so the inverse image of the image of
S^{1}\times\{0\}
is noncompact, contradicting the properness.

Alternatively, surfaces with any interior planar ends are not PHE to a graph by
the following line of reasoning: 1) proper homotopy equivalent spaces
have homotopy equivalent end space compactifications, 2) a graph and its
end space compactification are homotopy equivalent by the natural inclusion, and 3) the natural inclusion of a surface to its compactification is
not
a homotopy equivalence since loops bounding interior punctures are killed in the compactification.
We wonder if these ideas and compactly supported cohomology at infinity can be used to address the following problem.

Classify surfaces up to proper homotopy equivalences.

4.
Surfaces properly homotopy equivalent to graphs

In this section, we prove the reverse direction of
Theorem

1.1
:

Proposition 4.1
.

A surface with nonempty boundary and no boundary isolating curve is PHE to a graph.

Our strategy is as follows. Given a surface with no boundary isolating curve, we will

(1)

cut the surface into compact pieces via some process,

(2)

deformation retract each piece onto a graph, fixing the cutting curves and arcs, then

(3)

glue the pieces back together along the cutting curves and arcs to get a (proper) deformation retract of the entire surface onto a graph.

Throughout we will use the term
original boundary
to refer to boundary of a subsurface that is also part of the boundary of the whole surface. For step (2), we will require that each compact piece contains original boundary. This original boundary will be allowed to move during the deformation retraction while the boundary components from the cutting must stay fixed.

It is standard knowledge that a compact surface with nonempty boundary deformation retracts onto a graph, however, we want to emphasize that this deformation retraction can be done in a way that fixes any proper closed subset, so we give the following statement whose proof we leave to the reader.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S
be a compact surface with nonempty boundary, and let
I\subsetneq\partial S
be a closed subset. Then there exists a proper deformation retraction of
S
to a graph which fixes
I
.

Note that the proper containment
I\subsetneq\partial S
is required in
Lemma

4.2
. If there were a deformation retraction of
S
to a graph which fixes all of
\partial S
, then by collapsing the boundary components of
S
to points we get a deformation retraction of a closed surface to a graph. This is impossible by
Proposition

3.5
.

Now we present a proof of the reverse direction of
Theorem

1.1
where the first step of our strategy is entirely non-constructive relying only on the condition that “every end sees boundary”.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.

Suppose
S
has nonempty boundary and no boundary isolating curves. Let
\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a compact exhaustion of
S
. By enlarging
K_{i}
’s, we may assume the complementary components of
K_{i}
are infinite-type surfaces. We want to show that we can find a new exhaustion
\{K^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
so that each component of
K^{\prime}_{i+1}\setminus K^{\prime}_{i}
contains original boundary. Then by
Lemma

4.2
we can find a proper homotopy equivalence of the closure of each component to a graph that fixes its boundary except for some piece of original boundary. We are then done by gluing together all proper homotopy equivalences into a single proper homotopy equivalence on
S
.

To find this new exhaustion, we begin by setting
K^{\prime}_{1}
to be the first
K_{i}
that contains original boundary. Note
S\setminus K^{\prime}_{1}
has finitely many components
U_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,U_{n_{1}}^{(1)}
, which are all infinite-type by our choice of
K_{i}
, and each
U_{j}^{(1)}
contains original boundary, otherwise there is a boundary isolating curve. Now we can set
K^{\prime}_{2}
to be the first
K_{i}
such that
K_{i}\cap U_{j}^{(1)}
contains original boundary for all
j=1,\ldots,n_{1}
. Letting the complementary components of
S\setminus K_{2}^{\prime}
be
U_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,U_{n_{2}}^{(2)}
, choose
K_{3}^{\prime}
to be the first
K_{i}
such that
K_{i}\cap U_{j}^{(2)}
contains original boundary for
j=1,\ldots,n_{2}
. Doing this process inductively, we obtain the desired exhaustion
\{K_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

See

However, other planar ends of the surface may be mapped to ends accumulated by loops. The two possible cases are ends accumulated by compact boundary components and ends accumulated by boundary chains (see

The cut-and-paste construction of the deformation retraction in the proof above provides the following corollary which we use in the following sections.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let

be a surface with boundary, but without boundary isolating curve, and let

be a proper deformation retraction to a locally finite, infinite graph properly homotopy equivalent obtained from

for

The map

Moreover, the map

Proof.

To show the moreover statement, recall

Example 5.3

.

The main tools for the remainder of the section are based on the often used Alexander methods for surfaces (see

Lemma 5.4

(
[

, Corollary 3.6]
)
.

Let

be proper. Then

is properly homotopic to the identity if and only if it preserves the homotopy class of every oriented closed curve and the proper homotopy class of every oriented proper line in
\Gamma
that in each direction converges to an end in

We will also need a version for surfaces that relies on an Alexander system on a surface with noncompact boundary components.

Lemma 5.5

(See also
[
Dic23
, Theorem 6.8]
)
.

Let
S
be any surface with nonempty boundary. If
[f]\in\operatorname{Map}(S)
fixes every oriented closed curve up to homotopy, it is in the subgroup topologically generated by Dehn twists about the compact boundary components and Dehn twists about the degenerate boundary chains.

Proof.

Note the Alexander method for surfaces without boundary from
[
HHMV18
]
shows that
[f]
, when considered as a homeomorphism on the interior, is the identity element for the mapping class group of the interior, but we need more to show the lemma. However, if we also fix all arcs up to homotopy (relative to the boundary), then the general Alexander method from
[

]
shows

is the identity. We will use this to show there is some representative

that is the identity on a subsurface, then achieve the lemma by examining the mapping class group of the complementary surfaces.

To simplify, fill in the degenerate ends of

to turn each degenerate chain into a compact boundary component and call the resulting surface

. Note that
\operatorname{Map}(S)
and

with an element of
\operatorname{Map}(S^{\deg})

Since

fixes every curve of

must also fix every arc

up to homotopy possibly moving the endpoints. A homotopy that moves the endpoints may untwist an arc if it lies on a compact boundary component.
Let

be the union of open regular neighborhoods of the (compact) boundary components of

so that

is homeomorphic to

. Note we can homotope

so that it preserves components of

and restricts to a homeomorphism on

fixing the boundary pointwise. Since we can homotope

to

possibly moving the endpoints, we can compose this homotopy with an additional homotopy in

to ensure that

and
\alpha
be homotopic as arcs in

by a homotopy fixing the boundary. Now since
f
fixes every curve and arc of

in

is homotopic to the identity. Therefore, after homotopy,

is supported in

are annuli, so it follows that

can be written as an infinite product of twists in these annuli in

We are now ready to prove the first part of
Theorem

.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
S
be a surface PHE to a graph
{\Gamma}
. Then the kernel of
\Theta:\operatorname{Map}(S)\rightarrow\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})
is topologically generated by Dehn twists about the compact boundary components.

Proof.

Suppose
[f]
is in the kernel of
\Theta
. By
Lemma

5.4
,
\Theta([f])
fixes the homotopy class of every oriented closed curve in
{\Gamma}
. In order to apply
Lemma

5.5
, we want to show
f
fixes the homotopy class of every oriented closed curve of
S
.
Let
\phi:S\to{\Gamma}
be a proper homotopy equivalence and
\psi:{\Gamma}\to S
be a proper homotopy inverse used to define
\Theta
. Let
\alpha\subset S
be an oriented closed curve in
S
. Then as
\Theta([f])=[\phi f\psi]
, and
[f]\in\ker\Theta
, we have that

(\phi f\psi)(\phi(\alpha))\simeq\phi(\alpha).

However we know
\psi\phi\simeq\text{Id}_{S}
, so
(\phi f)(\alpha)\simeq\phi(\alpha)
. Finally, by postcomposing
\psi
on the equation we get
f(\alpha)\simeq\alpha
. Hence,
f
preserves every oriented closed curve on
S
up to homotopy, and thus by
Lemma

5.5
,
f
is a possibly infinite product of Dehn twists about compact boundary components and degenerate chains.

It remains to show the subgroup topologically generated by Dehn twists about compact boundary components lies in the kernel, but not the subgroup topologically generated by Dehn twists about degenerate chains. Each Dehn twist about a compact boundary component is in the kernel since its support can be homotoped to be disjoint from any curve or line in the surface, and so it fixes each curve and line up to homotopy in the graph. Such a homotopy can be done simultaneously even for infinitely many collection of Dehn twists about compact boundary components. Hence, any infinite product of these twists must be in the kernel by
Lemma

5.4
. Note whenever the curve
\gamma
about the degenerate chain is nontrivial, any nontrivial power of the Dehn twist about
\gamma
is mapped by
\Theta
to a PHE that does
not
fix every nontrivial line going to and from a degenerate end in the chain. Note after homotopy, such a nontrivial line still intersects
\gamma
, and powers of

increase the (minimal) intersection number with
\gamma
. Thus,
Lemma

5.4
implies the Dehn twist about a degenerate chain is not in the kernel.
∎

We now want to understand the interplay between topologies for mapping class groups. We equip
\operatorname{Map}(S)
with the standard topology given by a neighborhood basis of the identity consisting of clopen subgroups
U_{K}
of elements with representatives that pointwise fix a given compact subsurface

. See
[
Vla19
]
for more details. The cosets of these subgroups then give a basis for all of
\operatorname{Map}(S)
.

Following
[
AB25
]
, we equip
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
with a similar topology where the neighborhood basis of the identity is given by the clopen subgroups
U_{\Delta}
of elements with representatives

that satisfy the following properties:

(1)

f
fixes a finite subgraph
\Delta
pointwise,

(2)

f
preserves the complementary components of
\Delta
,

(3)

f
has a proper homotopy inverse
g
satisfying the first two properties, and

(4)

fg
and
gf
are homotopic to the identity via proper homotopies that are stationary on

and preserve the complementary components.

The mapping class groups
\operatorname{Map}(S)
and
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
are known to be Polish groups with these topologies (See
[

, Corollary 5]
,
[
AB25
, Proposition 4.11]
.) Recall the kernel of

. Since the kernel is closed, the quotient of
\operatorname{Map}(S)
by the kernel is a Polish group with the quotient topology, call it
\operatorname{Map}^{\star}(S)
. Let
\operatorname{Map}_{S}({\Gamma})
denote the image of
\Theta
equipped with the subspace topology from
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
. Now
\Theta
descends to an abstract group isomorphism
\Theta^{\star}
from
\operatorname{Map}^{\star}(S)
to
\operatorname{Map}_{S}({\Gamma})
.

Proposition 5.7
.

The map
\Theta
is continuous. Furthermore,
\Theta^{\star}
is a topological isomorphism and
\operatorname{Map}_{S}({\Gamma})
is closed in
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.2
, we can argue for a particularly chosen PHE
\phi:S\to\Gamma
. According to
Corollary

4.4
, we can assume
\phi
is a deformation retraction such that there is an exhaustion

of
{\Gamma}
by finite graphs and an exhaustion

of

such that the restriction

is a proper deformation retraction. We can also assume the PHE inverse

is inclusion.

We claim that the image of
U_{S_{i}}
via
\Theta
is contained in
U_{\Gamma_{i}}\cap\operatorname{Map}_{S}({\Gamma})
. Note (1) holds by our choice for the PHE. The additional properties hold for a homeomorphism on a surface and thus for an element in the image. Since
\bigcap_{i}U_{S_{i}}
and
\bigcap_{i}U_{\Gamma_{i}}
are each the identity, we have that a sequence converging to the identity in
\operatorname{Map}(S)
is sent by

to a sequence converging to the identity in
\operatorname{Map}(\Gamma)
, and since these are metrizable topological groups, continuity of

follows. The continuity of
\Theta^{\star}
follows immediately.

Now we analyze the preimage of

via
\Theta
. Any element in the preimage admits a representative homeomorphism

that setwise preserves

. Otherwise, there exists a curve

such that

is not contained in

up to homotopy. Consequently, the curve
\phi(\alpha)\subset{\Gamma}_{i}
is mapped by

to a curve that is not contained in

up to homotopy; indeed,

is homotopic to

, which is not contained in

up to homotopy, as

is not contained in

up to homotopy. Then, by the argument in the proof of

, we have

is homotopic to a product of Dehn twists about the (compact) boundary components of

.

It follows the preimage of

via

is contained in

, the union of countably many translates of

by products of Dehn twists about (compact) components of

. Note some of these Dehn twists are about boundary components of

are different than the original compact boundary components of

is the group topologically generated by Dehn twists about only the original boundary components. It follows, a sequence converging to the identity in

is mapped by

We now have that

is a topological isomorphism, so the image

is a Polish group. A subgroup of a Polish group is Polish iff it is closed, so
\operatorname{Map}_{S}({\Gamma})
is closed in
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
.∎

5.2.
The extended mapping class group

For surfaces with interesting boundary, it is natural to expand our discussion to the
extended
mapping class group,
\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)
. We define
\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)
as simply the homeomorphism group of
S
modulo isotopy. So, the homeomorphisms are allowed to act nontrivially on boundary components and the equivalence relation allows isotopies which are nontrivial on the boundary. One effect of this change is that Dehn twists about compact boundary components become trivial mapping classes because they are isotopic to the identity. However, when a surface with nontrivial
\pi_{1}
contains punctured boundary components (a boundary chain of degenerate ends) the Dehn twist about the boundary chain is nontrivial. When there are finitely many punctures, there are also elements with the same support as the Dehn twist whose finite powers are Dehn twists, called
fractional Dehn twists
. See
Figure

2
for an example of a
\frac{1}{3}
-Dehn twist. When there are infinitely many punctures, the types of twists and shifts supported in a neighborhood of the boundary chain depend on the topology of the points removed from the boundary, but there is always a full Dehn twist.

For surfaces of low-complexity, their extended mapping class groups recover a familiar group as in the following example.

Example 5.8

(Dihedral group as extended mapping class group)
.

Let
S
be a disk with a finite number
n
of boundary points removed. The mapping class group is trivial, but the extended mapping class group is
D_{n}
, the dihedral group of order
2n
, since
S
can be viewed as a regular polygon with
n
(open) vertices.

Removing an
infinite
set from the boundary can have different effects on the extended mapping class group depending on the topology of the infinite set. For example, removing a set homeomorphic to
\omega+1\cong\{0\}\cup\{\frac{1}{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
yields a surface with trivial extended mapping class group (note there is a unique boundary component going off to the limit point, and once this is fixed all other boundary components are as well). On the other hand, if we remove two disjoint sets each homeomorphic to
\omega+1
then there are two possible surfaces depending on the direction in which the points accumulate to the two limit points. See
Figure

3
.
One of these surfaces has
D_{\infty}
, the infinite dihedral group, as its extended mapping class group, and the other has
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
as its extended mapping class group. However, each of these two surfaces is PHE to the same graph (and thus to each other) demonstrating that different surfaces can produce different maps to the same
\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma})
.

There is a natural map

\widetilde{\Theta}:\operatorname{Map}^{\dagger}(S)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Map}({\Gamma}),\qquad[f]\mapsto[\phi f\psi].

Following the same proof as
Proposition

5.1
we have the following proposition

Proposition 5.9
.

The map

Examples 5.10

We first discuss the surfaces for which

fails to be injective.

(1)

Consider the disk. The extended mapping class of the disk is

(2)

Consider the half-strip,
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times[0,1]

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(3)

Consider the strip,
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]

Suppose two distinct compact boundary components of

Since we are considering surfaces with boundary, note the universal cover is a region of the hyperbolic plane bounded by geodesics. If two of these geodesics are proper homotopic within the universal cover (or a geodesic is proper homotopic to its reverse), then there cannot be any other boundary geodesics separating their endpoints. Suppose

Recall that for surfaces PHE to a graph, the pure mapping class group agrees with the mapping class group. By Patel–Vlamis
[
PV18
]
and the extension to the surfaces with noncompact boundary by Dickmann
[
Dic23
, Theorem 6.13]
, the pure mapping class group of surfaces fits into the following split short exact sequence.

1\longrightarrow\overline{\operatorname{Map}_{c}(S)}\longrightarrow\operatorname{PMap}(S)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}\longrightarrow 1

where
n
is the number of ends of the surface accumulated by genus, using the convention that
\infty-1=\infty
. Furthermore, any element of
\operatorname{PMap}(S)
can be written as a product of an element in
\overline{\operatorname{Map}_{c}(S)}
with a product of
handle shift
maps. A handle shift map has a representative supported in a strip with genus, a strip
\mathbb{R}\times[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
modified by attaching genus at each integer point (see top of
Figure

10
). This representative is formed by shifting the strip by 1 in the
\mathbb{R}
coordinate and then homotoping this shift map so that the boundary is fixed pointwise.

The pure mapping class group for infinite graphs was studied by Domat–Hoganson–Kwak
[
DHK25
]
who showed the existence of the following split exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\overline{\operatorname{Map}_{c}({\Gamma})}\longrightarrow\operatorname{PMap}({\Gamma})\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}\longrightarrow 1

where
n
is the number of ends of the graph accumulated by loops.

For surfaces and for graphs, the homomorphisms from the pure mapping class group to
\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
are referred to as the
flux
homomorphisms. The even flux subgroup will refer to the subgroup with even entries in each coordinate.
