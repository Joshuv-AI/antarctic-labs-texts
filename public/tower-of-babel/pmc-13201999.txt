Clinic-based burden of glaucoma among Sahrawi refugees in the Tindouf camps
Authors: Ouafa Mhamdi-Lehebib, Carmen Mendez-Hernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the clinic-based frequency of glaucoma among Sahrawi refugees attending humanitarian ophthalmology missions in the Tindouf camps (Algeria), to identify demographic and clinical factors associated with glaucoma and to contextualise the clinic-based frequency of other ophthalmic diseases.Methods and analysisWe conducted a clinic-based cross-sectional study among Sahrawi refugees during Médicos del Mundo ophthalmology missions. All participants underwent a standardised ophthalmological examination including targeted history, visual acuity assessment, slit-lamp biomicroscopy and intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement. Glaucoma diagnosis relied on structural optic nerve head assessment and elevated IOP, with independent confirmation by a second ophthalmologist. Age-standardised and sex-standardised clinic-based frequencies were calculated using camp population demographics. Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with glaucoma.ResultsA total of 900 symptomatic care-seeking individuals were examined (56% men; median age 70 years). Overall clinic-based glaucoma frequency was 13.3% (95% CI 11.1% to 15.5%), representing the main disease of interest. After standardisation, the estimated clinic-based frequency was 6.7% (95% CI 3.8% to 9.7%). Older age was independently associated with glaucoma (adjusted OR per year 1.02; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.03; p=0.003). Inverse associations with cataract and keratopathy, likely reflected selection patterns and diagnostic overshadowing. Cataract was the most common coexisting diagnosis (59.8%), followed by keratopathy (9.1%) and retinopathy (3.4%). Among participants, 37.8% (n=340) had severe bilateral visual impairment, and 40.9% (n=368) had monocular vision; 17.5% (n=60/340) of those with bilateral impairment were blind from glaucoma.ConclusionsGlaucoma represents a substantial clinic-based burden among symptomatic Sahrawi refugees attending humanitarian missions, with a high proportion of irreversible visual loss. Other ophthalmic conditions were common but functioned as coexisting causes of visual impairment in this care-seeking sample. These findings highlight the need for pragmatic, context-appropriate strategies for opportunistic glaucoma detection and continuity of care in refugee settings, acknowledging limitations including the non-probabilistic, mission-based sampling and absence of visual-field testing.Keywords: Cataract, Glaucoma, Public health, Vision, Epidemiology, Intraocular pressure, Optic Nerve

Introduction

Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness in many regions worldwide. In Europe, the estimated prevalence of glaucoma is 2.99% making it one of the most common causes of blindness and visual impairment worldwide.1,3 The disease is often asymptomatic in its early stages, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and population-based screening programmes.4 Socioeconomic disparities further exacerbate disease progression, as low-income populations and minority groups face reduced access to early screening and treatment.5

The humanitarian crisis affecting the Sahrawi refugee population in the Tindouf camps, Algeria, is one of the longest standing in the world. Health planning and care are hampered by the lack of updated population data. According to data from the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees and official sources in the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR), the estimated population in the Sahrawi camps is approximately 173 000 people.6

Refugee populations, such as the Sahrawi community in Tindouf, face heightened health vulnerabilities. These are due to climate-related displacement and systematic exclusion from national health systems.7 Therefore, ophthalmic diseases may disproportionately affect this population. Currently, no data are available on the clinic-based frequency of glaucoma or other ocular diseases among Sahrawi refugees.

This study aims to estimate the clinic-based frequency of glaucoma among Sahrawi refugees evaluated during ophthalmology missions in October 2024 and May 2025, and to identify the demographic and clinical factors associated with a glaucoma diagnosis in this care-seeking population. A secondary contextual aim is to describe the most common coexisting ophthalmic diseases observed among patients who sought eye care in the Tindouf refugee camps.

Materials and methods

To address the objectives of this study, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of Sahrawi refugees residing in the Tindouf camps, Algeria, who voluntarily attended the Médicos del Mundo (MdM) ophthalmology missions held between 30 September and 13 October 2024, and 6 May and 16 May 2025, due to visual symptoms or ophthalmological problems.

This was a non-probability, clinic/mission-based sample of symptomatic or care-seeking individuals. Therefore, the observed proportions should be interpreted as clinic-based frequencies among attendees rather than population prevalence. Age and sex standardisation was performed to approximate potential population burden under camp demographic weights but cannot eliminate selection effects.

MdM is a member of the Doctors of the World International Network (https://www.medicosdelmundo.org), a non-governmental organisation dedicated to ensuring the human right to healthcare for refugee populations.

Selection of participants and sample size

The inclusion criterion was permanent residence in the Tindouf refugee camps and evaluation by MdM’s ophthalmology missions in October 2024 or May 2025 for ophthalmic symptoms or conditions. The final sample comprised 900 patients.

Exclusion criteria were limited to non-residents or those lacking a complete ophthalmological assessment.

Main and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was the clinic-based frequency of glaucoma in the study group. Secondary outcomes, included for contextual clinical interpretation, comprised the clinic-based frequency and distribution of other common ophthalmic diseases (cataract, keratopathy, retinopathy), as well as the coexistence of these conditions.

Data collection and variables

All participants underwent a standardised ophthalmological examination, including a symptom-focused medical history (eg, vision loss, pain, discomfort, secretions), assessment of visual acuity (VA) at a distance of 3 m using a Snellen VA chart, slit lamp examination of the anterior and posterior segments with a+90 D Volk lens, and contact tonometry using a rebound tonometer (iCare, Tiolat Oy, Helsinki, Finland) to assess intraocular pressure (IOP). Patients with decreased VA attributable to cataracts underwent keratometry and axial length measurement using the Visionix VX 90 autorefractometer (Visionix, Luneau Technology Group, France), and the OcuScan RxP ultrasonic biometer (Alcon Laboratories, Texas, USA).

The following parameters were recorded in a database: VA, IOP, appearance of the optic nerve (glaucomatous or not), presence of pseudoexfoliation and the diagnosis as determined by the ophthalmologist. Additionally, age, gender, and Wilaya of origin were documented for all participants.

Glaucoma diagnostic criteria

Glaucoma diagnosis was based on structural optic nerve damage consistent with glaucoma and increased IOP. Functional damage assessed by visual field testing was not considered, following criteria used in epidemiological studies and clinical guidelines for resource-limited settings where perimetry is not feasible.8,10 Diagnosis was established by the combination of increased optic cup excavation and elevated IOP. Pathological optic nerve excavation was defined as cup-to-disc ratio greater than 0.7 or an interocular asymmetry of 0.2 or more, in the absence of other causes of optic nerve head damage or non-glaucomatous optic neuropathy. High IOP was defined as >21 mm Hg, consistent with thresholds used in epidemiological studies and international guidelines.11

Given the humanitarian and resource-limited context of the missions, the diagnostic criteria for glaucoma followed a pragmatic, epidemiologically validated approach. Diagnosis relied on structural optic nerve head assessment and IOP measurement, in line with international guidelines for settings where perimetry and advanced imaging are not feasible. To enhance diagnostic consistency and reduce inter-examiner variability, all suspected cases were independently reviewed and confirmed by a second ophthalmologist with specific training in glaucoma. This dual assessment aimed to minimise misclassification, although early-stage disease may still have been under-detected due to the absence of functional testing and structural imaging.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (V.28.0; IBM). Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as mean and SD if normally distributed, or median and IQR if not. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

For group comparisons, parametric tests (t-test, analysis of variance) were used for continuous variables with normal distribution and non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis) were used otherwise. χ² tests were applied for categorical variables (eg, gender, Wilaya and coexisting diagnoses). The clinic-based frequency of glaucoma and other ocular diseases was calculated for the total sample and stratified by age group, gender and Wilaya.

Bivariate analyses were performed to assess the association between each independent variable and the presence of glaucoma, using crude ORs (CORs) with 95% CIs and p values. Variables with clinical or statistical relevance were then included in a multivariate logistic regression model to estimate adjusted AOR, controlling for potential confounders. The independent factors included were age group, gender, keratopathy, retinopathy, cataract and Wilaya of residence.

For the secondary outcomes, descriptive and comparative analyses were conducted as above.

As this was a cross-sectional study in which only data from patients who attended for evaluation were collected, there were no missing data. All analyses were performed on complete cases.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Population adjustment of clinic-based frequency

The age-and-gender-adjusted clinic-based frequency of glaucoma among attendees was estimated using the formula: Padjusted=i∑(pi×wi), where pi is the observed clinic-based frequency of glaucoma in each age and gender group, and wi is the relative weight of that group in the total population of the camps. The age and gender groups used were: 0–4 years (girls/boys), 5–17 years (boys and girls), 18–49 years (men and women) and ≥50 years (men and women). Adjusted clinic-based frequencies were estimated by direct standardisation across age–gender strata using camp population weights; 95% CIs were calculated using the stratified variance method.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis, writing or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Findings represent clinic-based frequencies among symptomatic attendees. Data from a total of 900 patients were analysed, of whom 56% (n=508) were men and 44% (n=392) were women. The median age was 70 years (IQR: 16; range: 2–104). Most participants were from Laayone (28.7%; n=258), followed by Smara (27.6%; n=248), Awserd (18.6%; n=168), Boujdour (12.8%; n=115) and Dakhla (10.4%; n=94). In 17 patients (1.9%), the Wilaya of origin could not be identified.

Main outcome: clinic-based frequency of glaucoma

The overall clinic-based frequency of glaucoma was 13.3% (n=120; 95% CI 11.1 to 15.5%). No statistically significant differences in glaucoma clinic-based frequency were observed between men (12.8%; 95% CI 9.9% to 15.7%) and women (14.0%; 95% CI 10.6% to 17.5%) (p=0.589). No significant differences were found between Wilayas (p=0.060). The majority of glaucoma cases were concentrated among patients aged over 18 years. After adjusting for the population structure of the Sahrawi camps, the age-adjusted and gender-adjusted clinic-based frequency estimate of glaucoma was 6.7% (95% CI 3.8% to 9.7%; see table 1).

Among coexisting ophthalmic conditions, cataract was the most common (59.8%, n=538), followed by keratopathy (9.1%, n=82) and retinopathy (3.4%, n=31). The retinopathy group included patients with retinal diseases affecting vision (scarring or other macular damage, retinal diseases consistent with vascular problems such as thrombosis or diabetic retinopathy, and retinal detachment). Among the ophthalmic conditions identified, glaucoma accounted for a disproportionate share of irreversible visual loss. The coexistence of two or more ocular diseases was observed in 8.1% of patients, with the most common combinations being cataract and glaucoma (3.4%) and cataract and keratopathy (3.2%) (table 3).

Among study participants, 37.8%, n=340 out of 900 patients had severe bilateral visual impairment (VA≤10% in both eyes), while 40.9%, n=368 out of 900 patients had only one functional eye, defined as having a best VA of less than 10% in one eye and greater than 20% in the other. Within the group with low vision in both eyes, the leading causes were cataract (71.1%, n=242 out of 340 patients), glaucoma (17.5%, n=60 out of 340 patients), keratopathy (13.3%, n=45 out of 340 patients) and retinopathy (7.1%, n=24 out of 340 patients). For those with only one functioning eye, cataract accounted for 63.3% of cases, n=233 out of 368 patients, glaucoma for 16.3%, n=60 out of 368 patients, keratopathy for 11.4%, n=42 out of 368 patients and retinopathy for 3%, n=11 out of 368 patients. It is particularly relevant to highlight that 17.5% of patients with bilateral low vision are affected by irreversible blindness in both eyes due to glaucoma. No statistical differences were observed in the proportion of patients with low binocular (p=0.076) or monocular (p=0.236) VA between the main ophthalmic diseases analysed (glaucoma, cataract, keratopathy and retinopathy). In our sample, 31%, 37 out of 120 glaucoma patients had bilateral low vision.

Analysis by age group showed that the clinic-based frequency of cataract, glaucoma, monocularity and low binocular vision increased with age (p<0.001 for all comparisons except for low binocular vision, where p=0.005).

Online supplemental figure 1 shows the clinic-based frequency of cataract, glaucoma, keratopathy and retinopathy by gender, while online supplemental figure 2 shows the clinic-based frequency of these diseases by Wilaya. No significant differences were found in the clinic-based frequencies of cataract, glaucoma or retinopathy between men and women, (except for keratopathy which was more frequent in men, p=0.023, and low bilateral VA, more frequent in women, p=0.038). In all Wilayas, cataract was the most common eye disease, followed by glaucoma. Cataract was most frequent in Dakhla (p=0.026), but the distribution of other diseases was similar across Wilayas.

Population adjustment results

The clinic-based frequency of glaucoma in our sample was 13.3%. After adjusting the results according to the Saharawi camp population structure (considering age and gender distribution: 6% children aged 0–4, 13% boys and 13% girls aged 5–17, 22% men and 23% women aged 18–49, and 8% men and 9% women aged 50 or over), the age-adjusted and gender-adjusted clinic-based frequency estimate of glaucoma was 6.7%. This value was obtained by multiplying the observed clinic-based frequency of glaucoma in each age and gender group by the relative weight of that group in the total population and adding the contributions of all population strata. The age-adjusted and gender-adjusted clinic-based frequency estimates of the other most common diseases among participants in this study were as follows: keratopathy: 5.7% (95% CI 2.2% to 9.2%), retinopathy: 12.2% (95% CI 8.9% to 15.5%) and cataract: 17.9% (95% CI 14.7% to 21.2%).

Discussion

This study provides robust evidence that glaucoma represents a major clinic-based burden among symptomatic Sahrawi refugees attending humanitarian ophthalmology missions in the Tindouf camps.

The age-adjusted and sex-adjusted clinic-based frequency of glaucoma based on the demographic structure of the camps was higher than figures reported in international systematic reviews and meta-analyses in low-resource settings and in our region.12 12,18 A substantial proportion of glaucoma patients presented with bilateral low vision, and 17.5% of individuals with bilateral low vision were blind in both eyes due to irreversible glaucomatous damage. In this clinic-based sample, glaucoma therefore represents a major cause of permanent visual disability.

Older age was significantly associated with glaucoma after adjustment for coexisting ocular pathology, and many cases were already at an advanced stage. The inverse association observed between glaucoma and either cataract or keratopathy likely reflects diagnostic overshadowing, whereby media opacity or cataract-related visual loss reduces the detectability of glaucomatous structural changes during examination.

The high proportion of advanced glaucoma likely reflects structural barriers to healthcare access, diagnostic limitations, and the absence of systematic early-detection strategies in refugee settings. In resource-limited environments, glaucoma is frequently underdiagnosed even when optic-nerve damage is severe. Although our data cannot support causal conclusions, these patterns are consistent with other populations with restricted access to general and ophthalmic care.25 12 15 16 19,22

In addition to glaucoma, coexisting conditions such as cataract, retinopathy and keratopathy highlight the cumulative effects of desert environmental exposure, ageing and systemic comorbidities, as reported in other displaced populations.14 17 20 23 Cataract, as a coexisting condition, was the leading cause of reversible visual disability, and cataract surgery remains highly cost-effective. Keratopathy appeared less frequent, consistent with reports from populations exposed to similar environmental stressors.24,27 As a secondary contextual finding, the age-adjusted and sex-adjusted clinic-based frequency of cataract (17.9%) was consistent with studies from comparable clinical settings.28,31 In contrast to cataract, glaucoma leads to irreversible blindness, underscoring its disproportionate contribution to long-term visual disability when diagnosis and treatment are delayed.

From a public-health perspective in humanitarian settings, our results suggest that glaucoma may represent an under-addressed structural burden in refugee populations, where service models commonly prioritise surgically reversible conditions. The lack of systematic screening and limitations in treatment continuity favour the silent accumulation of advanced glaucoma.

These findings have practical operational implications. In mission-based and intermittent service delivery models, opportunistic glaucoma detection—including integration within high-volume cataract care pathways—is a feasible and efficient strategy that takes advantage of the large number of patients seeking care for cataract-related visual loss. The follow-up limitations inherent to displaced populations require simplified diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, including treatment initiation during the same clinical visit and culturally adapted instructions supported by community health workers to minimise loss to follow-up. Ensuring continuity of glaucoma medication through prepositioned supplies and coordination with local pharmacies or NGOs is essential when long-term follow-up cannot be guaranteed.

Implementing this model does not require highly specialised infrastructure but does require a minimum set of conditions: trained local personnel able to screen for glaucoma, portable ophthalmic equipment (handheld tonometry, slit-lamp examination and low-cost optic-nerve documentation such as non-mydriatic fundus photography), reliable medication and instrument supply chains, and clear referral pathways for complex cases. Beyond reporting clinic-based disease frequencies, our findings outline an adaptable operational framework for integrating glaucoma management into humanitarian eye-care programmes.

Equity and safeguarding measures were prioritised throughout the mission, including free care, culturally adapted consent, support from local health personnel who acted as interpreters, confidentiality within the constraints of the camp setting, and established referral circuits for complex cases. Vulnerable groups—older adults, people with disabilities and minors—received additional support to ensure safe access to care. Participants diagnosed with advanced or severe disease received immediate counselling and a clear explanation of their condition and management options.

This study has limitations. Given the humanitarian context in which the clinical missions were conducted, all data used in this study were generated during routine patient care rather than for research purposes. As a result, ethical approval was necessarily obtained retrospectively, after the clinical team recognised the disproportionate burden of glaucoma-related visual loss among attendees. Importantly, all information was fully anonymised prior to analysis, in line with data-protection requirements for studies involving vulnerable refugee populations. While these procedures ensured strong safeguards for participant confidentiality, they also limited the possibility of longitudinal follow-up or linkage to individual patient trajectories. Selection bias due to voluntary attendance of symptomatic individuals may overestimate clinic-based frequencies. Glaucoma diagnosis relied on optic-nerve appearance and IOP without visual-field testing, which increases the risk of misclassification, particularly in the presence of media opacity. Although requiring agreement between two ophthalmologists reduced diagnostic variability, both overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis may have occurred, and this should be considered when comparing with studies using more comprehensive diagnostic protocols.8 9 The cross-sectional design and non-random sample limit causal inference, and findings should be interpreted as clinic-based frequencies rather than population prevalence. Although demographic adjustment improves interpretability, these estimates cannot be extrapolated to the broader Sahrawi refugee population.

Although the use of a mission-based convenience sample limits the extrapolation of these findings to the general Sahrawi population, the reported clinic-based frequencies offer a high-fidelity snapshot of the actual ophthalmic demand. These results are indispensable for international health organisations to optimise the allocation of diagnostic and surgical resources in the Tindouf camps.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a significant burden of glaucoma with irreversible visual loss, alongside other coexisting causes of visual disability among symptomatic individuals attending humanitarian eye-care missions in the Tindouf refugee camps. Beyond quantifying clinic-based frequencies, our findings highlight the need to integrate feasible strategies for glaucoma detection and treatment continuity within humanitarian ophthalmology programmes to prevent irreversible blindness in this vulnerable population.
