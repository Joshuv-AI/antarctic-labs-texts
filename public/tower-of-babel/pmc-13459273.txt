Training satisfaction, competency gaps and well-being in gastroenterology residency in Spain: results of a national SEPD survey
Authors: Marisa Iborra, María Dolores Martín-Arranz, Judith Gómez-Camarero, Rafael Gómez-Rodríguez, Julio Iglesias-Garcia, Aitor Orive-Calzada, Luis Téllez, Federico Argüelles-Arias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the training experience, competency needs and professional wellbeing of Gastroenterology trainees in Spain, with a particular focus on factors associated with burnout.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using an anonymous online survey distributed to residents (R1–R4) and young specialists (≤ 5 years after completing the training program). The survey collected information on training satisfaction, perceived competency gaps, working conditions, and burnout. Responses were collected using 5-point Likert scales. Descriptive analyses, subgroup comparisons and logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with burnout.ResultsA total of 241 responses were obtained (60.6% women; mean age 30.2 ± 3.6 years). Nearly all participants trained in university hospitals (97.5%) and most in tertiary centres (82.6%). Overall satisfaction with the training program was 3.99 ± 0.71/5, with practical training rated more favourably (4.25 ± 0.71) than theoretical training (3.30 ± 0.98). The main areas identified as needing reinforcement were clinical nutrition (26.0%), digestive ultrasonography (25.2%) and endoscopy (23.3%). Burnout was reported by 63.5% of respondents. Lack of adequate rest was strongly associated with higher burnout risk, while satisfaction with working conditions showed a protective effect, an association that remained significant in multivariate analysis. Additionally, 89.5% believed that an additional year would improve their preparation, and 97.1% considered that a five-year program would be sufficient to acquire the necessary competencies.ConclusionsWhile practical training is highly valued, gaps persist in theoretical education and key technical areas. Burnout is common and closely linked to insufficient rest and dissatisfaction with working conditions. The strong support for extending residency to five years suggests that this measure may not only enhance training quality but also promote wellbeing and a more balanced acquisition of competencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04980-6.Keywords: Gastroenterology Training, Burnout, Competency-based medical education, medical residency

Introduction

Specialised training in gastroenterology provides the foundation upon which future specialists build their clinical, technical and cross-disciplinary competencies. In recent decades, the field has undergone a substantial transformation. The growing complexity of digestive diseases and advances in therapeutic endoscopy, interventional hepatology, clinical nutrition and inflammatory bowel disease management have markedly expanded the breadth and depth of skills required during training. At the same time, there is a growing expectation that trainees will develop cross-cutting competencies such as clinical communication, complex case management, teamwork and leadership, which are essential in contemporary, patient-centred care models [1, 2].

This evolution has occurred alongside a broader shift towards competency-based medical education (CBME), which emphasises outcomes, structured supervision and continuous assessment rather than time-based training alone [1, 3]. However, several studies have highlighted persistent gaps between training requirements and real-world clinical practice, particularly in procedural exposure, structured curricula and feedback systems [4–6].

This challenge is not unique to Spain. Multiple countries have documented significant gaps between the expectations of trainees and those of their supervisors, as well as objective shortcomings in key components of gastroenterology training programmes. In the United Kingdom, national evaluations of gastroenterology training have highlighted the need to reinforce transversal competencies include communication skills, leadership, teamwork and complex case management and revise the structure of the training pathway [4]. Likewise, a recent German survey identified marked differences between trainees and trainers regarding the perceived quality of teaching and the need for more flexible and individualized approaches [5]. In Canada, gastroenterology residents have reported limited exposure to advanced techniques such as therapeutic endoscopy, clinical nutrition, and disorders of gut-brain interaction [6]. In many countries, advanced competencies such as therapeutic endoscopy or transplant hepatology are typically acquired through additional fellowship training beyond standard residency programmes [7]. In the United States, professional societies have highlighted the need to redesign gastroenterology training frameworks to incorporate emerging technologies and competency-based, continuous assessment models [8]. More recently, the development of advanced fellowship programmes in highly specialised areas, such as foregut disorders, further reflects the growing complexity of the field and the need for training beyond standard gastroenterology curricula [7].

In Spain, the four-year Gastroenterology residency program (MIR or Medical Internal Resident training programme) appears to show considerable variability between training centres. These differences can result in unequal exposure to complex clinical cases, variable access to advanced procedures and disparate clinical workload [9]. In this context, it is essential to obtain first-hand information from those currently undergoing training, as robust national data on training experience, competency gaps and trainee wellbeing remain limited.

To this end, the Spanish Society of Gastroenterology (Sociedad Española de Patología Digestiva; SEPD) launched a national survey for trainees and early career specialists. The survey aimed to assess overall satisfaction with the MIR programme and satisfaction in specific areas, identify areas of perceived training deficits, and explore the influence of the working environment on professional well-being. This study presents the findings of the survey and examines differences across training levels, as well as considering the potential value of extending the programme to five years. The study ultimately aims to determine whether the current training model effectively addresses the practical requirements of the specialty and whether an extended programme could facilitate a more balanced, consistent and competency-based curriculum.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional, observational study using an online structured survey distributed to gastroenterology residents (R1–R4) and young specialists (≤ 5 years since completing residency training) in Spain. The study was undertaken as an initiative of the Spanish Society of Gastroenterology (SEPD).

Eligible participants included all residents and young specialists working in Spanish hospitals accredited for gastroenterology training. Participants were recruited from hospitals accredited for gastroenterology training across Spain, including both tertiary referral centres and secondary-level hospitals. Eligibility criteria included being a current gastroenterology resident (R1–R4) or a young specialist within five years of completing residency. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Electronic informed consent was obtained prior to survey initiation. Only complete responses submitted between 10 October 2024 and 15 January 2025 were included in the analysis.

Survey instrument

A questionnaire was developed specifically for this study based on previous literature and expert input. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material 1. It comprised 92 items across eight sections, including 5-point Likert scales, closed-ended questions and free-text fields. For Likert-scale items, response options ranged from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), with anchors adapted to the context of each question (e.g., from ‘never’ to ‘always’ or from ‘very dissatisfied’ to ‘very satisfied’). Burnout was assessed using a single-item measure, which has been previously validated as a pragmatic and reliable screening tool in survey-based research settings, particularly when response burden and feasibility are important considerations [10]. The questionnaire required approximately 10–12 min to complete. Content validity was ensured through iterative expert review by members of the SEPD Young Committee. The final version was piloted with a small group of residents to ensure clarity and feasibility. The survey was implemented using Google Forms® and disseminated through professional mailing lists, social media channels and SEPD communication platforms. The survey platform was configured to limit responses to one submission per user.

Participants accessed the survey through a secure link. No incentives were provided and no IP addresses or identifying data were collected.

Variables

The primary outcome was self-reported burnout, assessed using a single dichotomous item (yes/no). This pragmatic approach was adopted to facilitate participation in a nationwide survey. While this measure is not based on a validated burnout instrument and does not allow assessment of severity or specific burnout dimensions, it provides an initial approximation of perceived burnout suitable for exploratory analyses. Independent variables included sociodemographic, training-related and organisational factors that could plausibly influence wellbeing. These consisted of gender, training level (R1–R4 or young specialist), hospital type (tertiary vs. other centres) and hospital size. Training-related predictors included overall satisfaction with the residency program, perceived adequacy of theoretical and practical training, and domain-specific deficits identified by participants. Organisational variables included working conditions, perceived quality of supervisory support, and compliance with rest and duty-hour regulations. All variables were obtained from structured questionnaire items using Likert-scale or categorical response formats.

Missing data were minimal (< 5% for all variables). As core items were mandatory, missingness was confined mainly to optional questions and free-text responses. A complete-case analysis approach was used, restricting analyses to respondents with available data for each variable set. No imputation was performed due to the low proportion of missing data and its unlikely impact on inferential validity. The number of observations included in each analysis is provided in the corresponding tables.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SD). Group comparisons were conducted using the χ² test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify factors independently associated with burnout, adjusting for relevant covariates (training level, job satisfaction and compliance with rest regulations). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using Stata® version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. No identifiable personal data were collected, and all responses were processed anonymously. Data handling complied with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and the Spanish Organic Law 3/2018 on Data Protection and Digital Rights (LOPDGDD). As the study involved an anonymous, non-interventional survey on educational perceptions, formal ethics committee approval was not required under national regulations. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 241 valid responses were collected. The mean age of participants was 30.2 ± 3.6 years, and 60.6% were women. Most respondents (82.6%) had completed or were completing their training in tertiary-level hospitals, and 97.5% in university-affiliated centres. Regarding training stage, 48.1% were residents (R1–R4) and 51.9% were young specialists within five years of completing their residency. Resident respondents were relatively evenly distributed across training stages, without marked overrepresentation of junior residents. Based on national data, approximately 220 gastroenterology residency positions are offered annually in Spain, corresponding to an estimated total of around 850–900 residents across all training years. The number of young specialists within five years of completing residency is more difficult to determine but can be estimated at approximately 1,000 individuals. Although an exact response rate cannot be calculated due to the open distribution of the survey, resident respondents represented approximately 15% of the estimated national population of gastroenterology residents in Spain. The survey nevertheless achieved broad geographic participation and included a substantial number of young specialists.

Wellbeing and burnout

Overall, 63.5% of participants reported experiencing burnout during residency.

Only 12.6% of respondents reported having access to emotional wellbeing resources at their institution, while 57.3% indicated that no such resources were available. With respect to compliance with rest periods, 4.8% reported that rest was “never” respected, 11.5% “rarely,” 56.7% “often,” and 26.9% “always.”

In the univariate analysis, non-compliance with rest requirements was strongly associated with burnout. Compared with those reporting that rest periods were “always” respected, burnout risk was significantly higher among participants reporting “rarely” (OR 5.09; 95% CI 1.92–13.49; p = 0.001) or “never” (OR 23.22; 95% CI 2.59–208.62; p = 0.005).

In the multivariable analysis, satisfaction with working conditions remained an independent protective factor against burnout (OR 0.46; p = 0.011), while compliance with rest periods was not statistically significant (OR 2.19; p = 0.051). By training stage, young specialists exhibited significantly lower odds of burnout than R1–R2 residents, including specialists with 3–4 years of post-residency experience (OR 0.22; p = 0.012) and those with 4–5 years of experience (OR 0.21; p = 0.028). Training satisfaction

Overall satisfaction with the gastroenterology residency program was 3.99 ± 0.71 out of 5. Practical training received substantially higher ratings (4.25 ± 0.71) than theoretical training (3.30 ± 0.98) (p < 0.001). Regarding supervisory support, 62.2% of participants reported being satisfied or very satisfied with their tutors, with no statistically significant differences between residents and young specialists (p = 0.089). When comparing satisfaction with training level, no statistically significant differences were found in overall satisfaction with the residency program (p = 0.433) or in the perceived quality of practical training (p = 0.138).

Satisfaction with supervisory support differed according to hospital size (p = 0.013), with lower satisfaction reported in medium-sized hospitals (200–500 beds) compared with larger centres (501–1000 beds).

Training needs

Participants identified several priority areas requiring further strengthening during residency, with percentages representing the proportion of respondents who selected each area as requiring additional training. The most frequently reported areas were clinical nutrition (26%), digestive ultrasonography (25%), digestive oncology (23.7%), basic endoscopy (23.3%), advanced endoscopy (23.3%), and hepatology (22%) (Fig. 2).

In addition, 54.8% reported insufficient exposure to key rotations, particularly in therapeutic endoscopy and transplant hepatology.

Perceptions regarding the duration of the residency program

A total of 77.0% of respondents reported that four years are insufficient to acquire the necessary competencies. Similarly, 89.5% believed that an additional year would improve their preparation, and 97.1% considered that a five-year programme would be sufficient.

Discussion

Burnout represents the most critical outcome of this study, as it directly reflects the impact of training conditions on trainee wellbeing and professional sustainability [11–13]. Nearly two-thirds of respondents (63.5%) reported having experienced burnout during residency, a prevalence higher than that reported among general surgery residents in the United States [14] and comparable to that observed in Canadian gastroenterology training programmes [6]. These findings are consistent with previous literature indicating that burnout affects a substantial proportion of medical trainees, frequently exceeding 50% in some settings [11, 13].

The strong association between burnout, job dissatisfaction and insufficient compliance with mandated rest periods underscores the influence of organisational factors on trainees’ emotional and psychological wellbeing. The lower risk of burnout among young specialists compared with R1–R2 residents further suggests that the early years of training concentrate the greatest pressures, likely due to heavier workloads, limited autonomy and the challenge of adapting to the hospital environment. This pattern has been consistently described in international studies and supports the need for targeted institutional support during the initial stages of residency [13].

The findings of this national survey show that gastroenterology trainees consistently report high satisfaction with practical training, whereas theoretical instruction remains the weakest component of the residency programme. This gap was observed across all training levels, suggesting that it reflects a structural issue rather than deficiencies specific to certain years of training. This pattern mirrors reports from other countries, where trainees similarly emphasise the need for more robust formal teaching and protected time for structured educational activities [4–6]. Altogether, these results reinforce the importance of moving towards a more standardised and coherent national training strategy.

Although overall satisfaction did not differ significantly between residents and young specialists, we observed significant heterogeneity in satisfaction with supervision according to hospital size. Trainees in medium-sized hospitals (200–500 beds) reported lower levels of satisfaction, suggesting potential differences in mentoring structures and teaching capacity across centres. These findings underscore the need to standardise mentorship models and align them with international recommendations emphasising structured supervision and continuous feedback as core components of competency-based training [6, 8].

Regarding training needs, respondents identified clinical nutrition, digestive ultrasonography and both basic and advanced endoscopy as priority areas. The identification of basic endoscopy as an area requiring reinforcement may reflect variability in exposure, supervision and procedural volume across centres, rather than a deficiency in the core curriculum itself. These findings are consistent with previous studies from the United States, Canada and Europe, which highlight substantial variability in access to advanced endoscopy and the need to incorporate competency-based medical education models, including structured assessments such as OSCEs, mini-CEX and accreditable logbooks [15]. In this context, our results reinforce the importance of establishing minimum procedural volumes and strengthening specific rotations to ensure more homogeneous training across centres.

Another consistent finding was the strong support for extending gastroenterology training to five years, with 97.1% of respondents considering that a five-year programme would be sufficient to acquire the necessary competencies. This view was shared across all training levels and aligns with changes implemented in other medical specialties in Spain, such as Cardiology, Internal Medicine and Medical Oncology. It is also consistent with international recommendations advocating for more structured and competency-based training programmes capable of integrating advanced procedural skills, research training and transversal competencies [16, 17].

Despite the high levels of satisfaction reported, particularly regarding practical training, a substantial proportion of respondents considered the current duration of residency insufficient. This apparent paradox may reflect the increasing complexity of the specialty and the expansion of required competencies beyond what can realistically be achieved within a four-year programme. While core clinical and procedural skills may be adequately acquired, trainees may perceive limited opportunities to develop advanced competencies or consolidate their expertise. In this context, extending training to five years could help address these needs by allowing greater exposure to advanced areas such as therapeutic endoscopy, clinical nutrition, clinical leadership and other key domains.

Although the survey did not directly assess the motivations underlying support for extending training duration, this finding is likely multifactorial. Beyond the desire for broader competency acquisition, trainees and young specialists may perceive increasing pressure to develop advanced or differentiating skills in an increasingly specialised professional environment. Competencies in areas such as therapeutic endoscopy, hepatology, clinical nutrition or research may be viewed not only as relevant for highly specialised career pathways, but also as important for professional competitiveness, employability and preparedness for independent practice. These considerations may partially explain the strong support for extending training duration observed in our cohort.

Our findings point to a clear need to shift towards a more homogeneous, competency-based training model. This would entail defining a common core curriculum across subspecialty areas, developing a national mentoring model with standardised criteria and implementing institutional programmes that safeguard trainee wellbeing by ensuring compliance with rest regulations, promoting balanced workloads and providing access to psychological support. Extending the programme to five years may offer a coherent structure in which to integrate these reforms and strengthen the overall training pathway.

This study has several strengths, including its national scope, relatively large sample size, stratified subgroup analyses and the incorporation of burnout as a key outcome variable. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the voluntary nature of participation may have introduced selection bias, as trainees with particularly positive or negative experiences may have been more motivated to respond. Second, although missing data were minimal, the number of responses varied across items, particularly in optional sections. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships. Finally, although the survey achieved broad national participation, the response rate among residents was modest and may limit generalisability if non-respondents differed systematically from participants.

In conclusion, this national survey shows that while practical training in Gastroenterology is highly valued, important gaps persist in theoretical education and technical skill development. Trainee wellbeing was closely linked to compliance with rest regulations and to the quality of the working environment, underscoring the influence of organisational factors on training outcomes. The overwhelming support for extending residency to five years underscores a widely perceived need to revise the current training model and move towards a more balanced, coherent and competency-based programme, offering an evidence-based foundation for national discussions on the future restructuring of gastroenterology training in Spain.
