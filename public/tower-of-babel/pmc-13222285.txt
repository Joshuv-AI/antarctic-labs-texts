Real-World Evidence for Sevelamer vs Calcium-Containing Phosphate Binders in Long-Term Effectiveness and Safety Among Patients with Non-Dialysis CKD: The REVEAL Study
Authors: Ping Li, Chaofan Li, Shuang Liang, Xuefeng Sun, Xinru Guo, Xieguanxuan Xu, Lichen Hao, Xiangmei Chen, Zhe Feng, Guangyan Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionElevated serum phosphate is a major risk factor for cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Both calcium- and non-calcium-containing phosphate binders (CPBs and NCPBs) are effective at lowering serum phosphate in patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD (ND-CKD). The critical knowledge gap is whether CPBs or NCPBs provide meaningful long-term benefits for patients with ND-CKD, including slowing progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and reducing cardiovascular events.MethodsThis retrospective study was based on the Optum Clinformatics® administrative claims data from between 1 August 2000 and 30 November 2021. Adults with ND-CKD and hyperphosphataemia or serum phosphorus ≥ 1.78 mmol/L (≥ 5.5 mg/dL) who had received either sevelamer (a NCPB) or a CPB for ≥ 90 days underwent propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline characteristics. The primary endpoint was initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT) over 3 years. Secondary composite endpoints were the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE; myocardial infarction, stroke and all-cause death) and MACE plus (MACE, unstable angina pectoris and heart failure).ResultsOf 9047 patients who underwent PSM (sevelamer, n = 6644; CPBs, n = 2403), data from 4798 were analysed (2399 in each group). Over 3 years, RRT initiation had significantly lower incidence with sevelamer than CPBs (65.0% vs 70.3%; hazard ratio [HR] 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.79–0.91, P < 0.0001). MACE (44.9% vs 50.7%; HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.99, P = 0.0249) and MACE plus (51.9% vs 59.1%; HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.95, P = 0.0009) also had significantly lower incidence with sevelamer than CPBs. RRT initiation had significantly lower incidence with sevelamer versus CPB in subgroup analyses by age, sex, race, disease stage and medical history.ConclusionIn this real-world long-term study, sevelamer was associated with beneficial outcomes over CPBs for hyperphosphataemia treatment in patients with ND-CKD. Further definitive studies are needed to confirm this finding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03572-w.

Introduction

Phosphorus imbalance is a common disorder among patients with progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD). In particular, high serum phosphorus or hyperphosphataemia is a significant risk factor for morbidity and mortality in both patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD (ND-CKD) and dialysis-dependent CKD [1, 2]. Moreover, high serum phosphorus is a strong risk factor of elevated morbidity of cardiovascular disease, with or without CKD [3, 4]. Phosphate binders can be broadly classified as calcium-containing phosphate binders (CPBs) and non-CPBs (NCPBs). CPBs include calcium acetate and calcium carbonate, while NCPBs include sevelamer, sucroferric oxyhydroxide and lanthanum. Phosphate binders are recommended to prevent hyperphosphataemia in patients with CKD, but their effects on disease progression, mortality and cardiovascular outcomes are unknown [5].

While a significant portion of research on phosphate binders has been conducted in patients with dialysis-dependent CKD, the benefits of NCPBs, such as sevelamer, have been observed in patients with ND-CKD [6]. Previous studies have shown that hyperphosphataemia may be a risk factor for the development of CKD in patients with normal kidney function [7]. The presence of hyperphosphataemia in patients with CKD can further exacerbate CKD progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in individuals with compromised kidney function [4]. We hypothesis that treating hyperphosphataemia with sevelamer may have pleiotropic effects, such as decelerating the progression from ND-CKD to ESRD, in addition to regulating phosphorus levels [8, 9]. As such, we conducted a retrospective analysis of data from patients with ND-CKD receiving sevelamer or CPBs to determine the potential impact of these therapies on renal and non-renal outcomes.

Methods

Study Design, Data Source and Population

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted between 1 May 2020 and 30 November 2021 using claims data from the Optum Clinformatics® administrative claims database (https://business.optum.com/en/data-analytics/life-sciences/real-world-data/claims-data.html; Fig. 1a). This database contains the de-identified records of 87 million commercially insured patients in the USA, providing a comprehensive and single-payer perspective of patient demographics, diagnoses, procedures received during inpatient stays and outpatient visits, and prescription information. In this study, the claims database was linked to the Social Security Administration Death Master File so information on death could also be obtained.Fig. 1Study a design and b flow. The study population consisted of patients aged ≥ 18 years with ND-CKD who were diagnosed with hyperphosphataemia or had a serum phosphorus ≥ 1.78 mmol/L (≥ 5.5 mg/dL) and receiving continuous treatment with either sevelamer or a CPB for ≥ 90 days between 1 August 2000 and 30 November 2021 (the identification period). The index date anchored the start of follow-up and was defined as the date of the first prescription of sevelamer or CPBs during the identification period. All eligible patients were required to have ≥ 90 days of treatment prior to the index date (which defined the start of the baseline period) and had ≥ 1 follow-up visit during the study. Patients who had undergone renal replacement therapy (RRT) before the index date, had used sevelamer and CPBs simultaneously for ≥ 1 day or had no records of the CKD stage at baseline were excluded from the study.

A full list of all the Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9/ICD-10 codes used for diagnoses of patients included in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Ethical Approval

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice, Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice and the applicable local legislation. The protocol for this study was reviewed by the First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB); however, as all data used in this study were de-identified, the IRB deemed this study exempt from requiring approval or further review. Furthermore, the requirement for patients’ informed consent was waived by the IRB because all personal identifiers were removed to ensure patient confidentiality. Permission was obtained from the database provider (Optum) to access and use the de-identified administrative claims data for this study.

Assessments and Outcomes

Data collected included patient characteristics (Supplementary Table S2), treatment exposure and renal and non-renal outcomes.

The use of sevelamer or CPBs was identified using the generic drug names. Patients prescribed sevelamer at any dosage were considered exposed to sevelamer. For calcium acetate, exposure was limited to patients prescribed a minimum dosage of 4002 mg/day for controlling serum phosphate. There were no approved indications for phosphate lowering with calcium carbonate in the USA, and exposure to calcium carbonate was defined as prescription of calcium carbonate and a hyperphosphataemia diagnosis or serum phosphorus ≥ 1.78 mmol/L (≥ 5.5 mg/dL) in the baseline period.

The primary outcome of the study was the incidence of RRT initiation, defined as the composite of haemodialysis (HD), peritoneal dialysis and kidney transplantation. Secondary outcomes were the incidence of (1) major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as the composite of myocardial infarction, stroke and all-cause death; (2) MACE plus, defined as a composite of MACE, unstable angina pectoris and heart failure; and (3) all-cause death. All outcomes were assessed over a 3-year follow-up period.

Subgroup analyses for the primary and secondary outcomes were conducted for a broad range of confounders for CKD progression, including age (≥ 65 and < 65 years), sex, race, CKD stage (3, 4 and 5) and medical history of diabetes mellitus (DM) or MACE plus before index date. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to evaluate study outcomes estimated in a 5-year follow-up period using the PPE approach.

Statistical Analysis

All patients who met the eligibility criteria were included in this study and categorised into two exposure groups: sevelamer or CPB. A multivariable logistic regression model was performed to calculate the propensity score (PS) and the probability of receiving sevelamer or a CPB for each patient using the baseline characteristics. To achieve and check the balance between two groups, a 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching method without replacement was used with a 0.01 calliper of the PS. Absolute standardised differences (ASDs) were calculated before and after PS matching (PSM) for baseline characteristics. Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted and log rank tests were conducted. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression models to compare the effects of sevelamer and CPB on the outcomes of interest and across subgroups. Average treatment effects were estimated with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on PSM, using the per-protocol effect (PPE) of a point-intervention approach. Right censoring was applied among the occurrence of outcome events, the date of last contact, the date of death, the end of follow-up visit, the date of treatment discontinuation for 60 days, the date of treatment switch or the end of the study period (30 November 2021), whichever came first. Patients with stabilised weights in the < 2.5 and > 97.5 percentiles were excluded to reduce the impact of extreme values before the analysis. A two-side P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3.

Patients receiving sevelamer also had a significantly lower incidence of MACE (44.9% vs 50.7%, log-rank P = 0.0258) and MACE plus (51.9% vs 59.1%, log-rank P = 0.0014) within 3 years compared with those receiving CPB therapy (Table 2). There was a significantly lower risk of MACE (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.99, P = 0.0249) and MACE plus (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.95, P = 0.0009) with sevelamer versus a CPB (Fig. 3b, c; Table 3).

The incidence of all-cause death was similar between the two treatment groups over the 3-year follow-up (25.7% vs 28.4%, log-rank P = 0.4770; Table 2), with no significant difference in risk of all-cause death (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87–1.08, P = 0.5944; Fig. 3d and Table 3). However, throughout the entire study period (mean [SD] follow-up 1032.1 [985.9] days), there was a significantly lower proportion of deaths in the sevelamer group versus the CPB group (48.7% vs 54.2%; P = 0.0445; Table 2).

IPTW Estimates

When IPTW estimates were used as an alternative to complement the PSM approach to balance baseline characteristics, patients receiving sevelamer still exhibited a significantly lower incidence of RRT initiation than those receiving a CPB within the 3-year follow-up period (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.82–0.94, P = 0.0001; Table 3).

Sensitivity Analyses

Within 5 years of follow-up, the incidence of RRT initiation remained significantly lower in the sevelamer group than in the CPB group (68.0% vs 72.5%; Table 2). The Cox regression-based risk was significantly lower with sevelamer versus a CPB (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.81–0.92, P < 0.0001; Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this PSM cohort study indicate that sevelamer may be associated with a significantly lower incidence of RRT initiation, MACE and MACE plus when compared with a CPB in patients with ND-CKD and hyperphosphataemia within 3 years of follow-up. Further, these results suggest that patients receiving sevelamer may have a lower incidence of mortality than those receiving a CPB over the entire study period.

The 2017 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines suggested lowering elevated phosphate levels toward the normal range with 2C-grade evidence [10]. In these guidelines, it was advised to limit the CPB dosage [10]. In addition, limiting total calcium intake to 800–1000 mg/day in adults with stage 3 or 4 CKD and without taking active vitamin D analogues was proposed by the Kidney Disease Outcome Quality Initiative in 2020 [11]. As discussed earlier, higher phosphate levels are associated with CKD progression [12, 13], cardiovascular events [12] and mortality [14] in patients with ND-CKD. Furthermore, cohort studies have shown that the prescription of phosphate binders in patients with dialysis-dependent CKD may be associated with increased survival [15–17].

Importantly, this PSM cohort study provides confirmation of the long-term benefits of sevelamer over CPB with regard to hard endpoints (i.e. RRT initiation, cardiovascular events and mortality). Despite the fact that there are more patients with ND-CKD than dialysis-dependent CKD, relatively few studies have focused on the long-term effects of phosphate binders in patients with ND-CKD. Of 13 previous studies that explored the impact of phosphate binders on renal endpoints [3, 18–29], only one (INDEPENDENT) explored the effect of phosphate binders on the incidence of dialysis and mortality in patients with ND-CKD [21]. Other outcomes were glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in 10 studies and serum creatinine in one study. These studies report contradictory findings, presumably because of their relatively short follow-up periods (ranging from 6 weeks to 36 months) and small population sizes (between 22 and 212 patients). Most studies focused on patients with stage 3 or 4 CKD. In contrast, the current study evaluated long-term outcomes in a large PSM cohort of almost 5000 patients.

In the INDEPENDENT study in 212 individuals with stage 3 or 4 CKD, patients receiving sevelamer showed significantly lower rates of all-cause death and the composite endpoints (a composite of all-cause death and dialysis initiation) compared with those receiving calcium carbonate after 36 months of follow-up [21]. However, there was no significant between-group difference for RRT initiation [21]. To the best of our knowledge, after evaluating the literature, our study is the second to explore the impact of phosphate binders on RRT initiation in patients with ND-CKD. Compared with the INDEPENDENT study, our study included more patients (2399 for each arm after PSM) and a wider range of CKD stages (including patients with stage 1–5 ND-CKD).

Very few studies have explored the effects of different phosphate binders on cardiovascular endpoints in patients with ND-CKD. In 2022, a meta-analysis by Lioufas and colleagues reported improvements in serum phosphate and urinary phosphate excretion with NCPBs, but unclear effects on cardiovascular outcomes across seven randomised controlled trials (RCTs; with 35–278 patients and follow-up times of 12–96 weeks) [30]. Again, conflicting findings on the cardiovascular impact of phosphate binders have been reported in the literature [3, 18, 25, 29, 31]. To our knowledge, no study has yet examined how phosphate binders affect cardiovascular events as the primary or secondary outcome in patients with ND-CKD. Our retrospective, real-world study of almost 5000 PSM patients with ND-CKD and hyperphosphataemia provides new evidence of the effectiveness of different phosphate binders on MACE. It should be emphasised that there is no evidence that reducing serum phosphate improves mortality or cardiovascular outcomes in any stage of CKD.

As a result of the limitations of our study, it was not possible to identify aetiological information for patients with ND-CKD. Instead, subgroup analyses were conducted according to comorbidities, including DM, hypertension and cardiovascular disease. The analyses indicated that sevelamer may be superior to CPBs in terms of renal endpoints for patients with relatively better clinical characteristics, such as younger age (< 65 years), early stages of CKD and no history of MACE. Sevelamer was also associated with a significantly lower risk of RRT initiation compared with CPBs in patients with prior DM, although there was no significant difference between treatment groups in those with no prior DM. This difference between patients with or without prior DM may be because patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) have a significantly faster rate of annual decline in estimated GFR (eGFR) than those with non-diabetic CKD [32]. Therefore, patients with DKD who have faster progressing CKD may better reflect the differences between sevelamer and CPB than those with non-diabetic CKD. The pleiotropic effects of sevelamer, including reduction in atherogenic lipoproteins and clearance of some uremic toxins [33], may further explain the observed differences between patients with or without prior DM. The results were similar with regard to cardiovascular endpoints. These patterns support the hypothesis that treating hyperphosphataemia early could improve clinical outcomes in patients with ND-CKD. When the outcomes were estimated by IPTW methods or the follow-up time was extended to 5 years or the entire study period, the trends in renal and non-renal endpoints remained similar. When sevelamer was continued for a longer treatment period, the probability of all-cause death appeared to be significantly reduced. These analyses suggest the potential ability of sevelamer to improve clinical outcomes. Furthermore, it could explain why CPBs performed worse over time than sevelamer, as previously documented for NCPBs. For instance, Spoendlin and colleagues reported that patients aged > 65 years with dialysis-dependent CKD on HD who received NCPBs did not have a significantly decreased rate of cardiovascular events compared with those treated with CPBs [34]. No previous studies have explored whether the effects of phosphate binders on patients with ND-CKD varied by race, although the Dialysis Outcomes Practice and Patterns Study (DOPPS) indicated that patients with dialysis-dependent CKD in the USA showed higher rates of death and cardiovascular events compared with those in Japan [35, 36]. Our study, which was based on US data, indicates that patients receiving sevelamer may have significantly lower rates of RRT initiation than those receiving CPB across all races (i.e. African American, Asian, white, Hispanic and other/unknown), with Asian patients experiencing the greatest reduction in RRT initiation.

As a result of the limitations of the database used, our study was unable to offer further explanation of the underlying mechanism for the benefits of sevelamer over CPBs. On the basis of prior research, the following potential mechanisms were identified: (a) sevelamer has been shown to be an effective treatment for high serum phosphate levels [37–39], which have been linked to the progression of CKD [12, 40], cardiovascular events [12, 41] and cardiovascular-associated death [42]; (b) sevelamer has been proven to significantly reduce low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels compared with CPB [37, 43], which is a major risk factor for cardiovascular events, CKD progression and death [44, 45], potentially owing to the high binding capacity of sevelamer with bile acids and the independent association between serum phosphorus and cholesterol metabolism [46–48]; (c) other possible mediating effects, such as reduced levels of inflammatory and oxidizing factors, decreased protein-bound uremic toxin and a lower risk of hypercalcemia and its complications (e.g. vascular calcification) [22, 49–51]. Future research should investigate these potential benefits of sevelamer.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has a number of strengths. Firstly, it had a large sample size (9047 patients in total and 4798 after PSM), which included patients with ND-CKD of different races, ages and CKD stage. Secondly, the mean follow-up time for all patients was 1032.1 days, which allowed for a sufficient number of clinical events (RRT initiation, MACE and death) to be available for a between-group comparisons. Thirdly, to lessen the complexity of the research, the use of calcium acetate and calcium carbonate was restricted to the reduction of serum phosphate levels rather than calcium supplementation, a broader indication than hyperphosphataemia. Finally, the use of dispensing data for medications provided a reasonable indicator of actual use of the medications.

The study also has several limitations. Firstly, the classification of patients, medication measures and events of interest were derived from reimbursement data rather than based on the findings of well-planned studies. Moreover, it was not possible to confirm that the medication listed as having been dispensed was actually taken by the patients; thus, miscoding or inaccurate coding was unavoidable. In addition, there was no information in the database regarding transplant eligibility or receipt, laboratory testing (e.g. serum phosphorus or inorganic serum parathyroid hormone), bone mineral-related outcomes, or diet or other lifestyle choices, so unmeasured confounding factors or missing data were possible. Furthermore, other confounders could not be accounted for, such as differing practices across healthcare centres in terms of prescribing (e.g. some centres prescribing CBPs may have a higher overall incidence of complications or clinical endpoints) and the parameters used to define the need to start RRT. It should also be noted that patients with early-stage CKD (stage 1–2) were not included in the subgroup analysis of the risk of RRT, MACE, MACE plus and mortality by CKD stage because of the insufficient number of patients in this subgroup. There was also a lack of PSM for baseline eGFR as a continuous indicator and baseline eGFR slope as an indicator of CKD progression, which hampered the ability to infer the impact of phosphate binder exposure on RRT initiation. Finally, healthcare received outside of claims and payment for services made in cash by patients were not included in the administrative claims data.

Conclusion

This real-world study showed that sevelamer was associated with a better prognosis than CPB for renal and cardiovascular events in patients with ND-CKD and hyperphosphataemia and provided benefits for long-term survival. This suggests that sevelamer may be a preferred option for the treatment of hyperphosphataemia in patients with ND-CKD.
