Robust chirality-induced spin selectivity in topologically chiral molecular knots
Authors: Xi Sun, Kai-Yuan Zhang, Shu-Zheng Zhou, Hua-Hua Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Molecular knots with topological chirality demonstrate superior spin selectivity compared to conventional chiral materials, exhibiting ultra-high spin polarization of nearly 90%, conductivity increased by two orders of magnitude and high-temperature stability (up to 350°C). However, the physical origin of this phenomenon remains elusive. We establish a fundamental theoretical framework for the chirality-induced spin selectivity in topologically chiral molecular knots and identify its essential conditions. Our calculations show that a single trefoil knot molecule exhibits spin polarization exceeding 60% along with significant conductivity, robustness unaffected by lattice reduction or structural strain. Importantly, when the topological knot degenerates into a trivial structure, accompanied by the transition from topological chirality to structural chirality, the spin polarization sharply declines, demonstrating a strong correlation between the ultrahigh spin polarization and the knot topology. This work elucidates the mechanism of spin selectivity in knot molecules and provides new design principles for future spintronic devices.

Introduction

The interplay between electron spin and structural chirality leads to a spin-dependent transport phenomenon known as chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS)1–6. This effect was first discovered in photoelectron spectroscopy measurements on DNA molecules7 and has since been reported in various chiral systems such as helicenes8, 9, chiral crystals10,11, donor-acceptor molecules12, and supramolecular assemblies13–16. A key signature of CISS is the generation of high spin polarization (up to 80%) in charge transport even without any external magnetic field17,18, and the spin-polarized state that can persist even at room temperature19. This capability for manipulating spin states and generating spin currents in non-magnetic materials at high temperatures makes CISS promising for spintronic device design20–22, as evidenced by recent demonstrations of room-temperature spin-emitting LEDs23,24 and high-efficiency chiral perovskite photovoltaics25–29. Beyond spin-dependent electronics, CISS also influences enantiospecific chemistry30, catalytic activity31–34, and biological functions35,36. Although multiple theoretical models have been proposed to understand this effect37–46, they still exhibit systematic contradictions with or explanatory limitations when confronted by experimental observations2, and a universal theory capable of fully explaining the CISS effect in all chiral materials remains elusive47.

The traditional CISS effect is typically related to structural chirality, such as helical chains that break the inversion symmetry to produce left- or right-handed chirality (Fig. 1a). However, recent experiments point to a different kind of system where strong CISS arises without such conventional stereogenic units, namely, in molecular trefoil knots19 (Fig. 1b). These molecules derive their chirality not from a helical shape but from the topological configuration of their entanglement, which is determined by the sequence and orientation of the strand crossings (Fig. 1c, d). This form of topological chirality constitutes a distinct level of spatial organization, one that appears to confer exceptional stability on the resulting spin selectivity. Experimentally, molecules that display this knot-based chirality achieve spin polarization as high as 90%, accompanied by an increase in conductivity of roughly two orders of magnitude compared to traditional chiral molecules, and retain performance up to temperatures near 350∘C19, exhibiting various advantages over traditional CISS1–6. These experimental observations establish topologically chiral knots as a distinctive platform for achieving enhanced and robust CISS. Although pioneering experimental work has presented a theoretical model to explain the highly spin-polarized current in these systems, the model itself remains grounded in a simplified picture, that of a uniform helical chain with structural chirality19. This representation, although valuable, does not fully capture the spatial variations and specific geometrical arrangement inherent in a knot topology. As a result, it offers limited insight into the distinctive physical processes that govern CISS in these entangled structures. In this work, we construct a series of spin-dependent transport devices based on trefoil knot molecules to establish a theoretical framework for the CISS effect in topologically complex systems. A central challenge is to clarify the origin of spin polarization in such structures. Although established mechanisms, including intramolecular spin-orbit coupling (SOC)37,38,48,49, chiral-induced orbital polarization43, spin-interface interactions50–52, and non-Hermitian skin effects under open boundaries46, have been invoked to explain CISS in other chiral molecules, they do not directly account for the role of global molecular topology. Recent studies highlight that SOC in chiral molecules is atomic in nature but also a component related to molecular geometry and chirality53–55. In trefoil knots19,56, the three-dimensionally curved and closed transport pathways further enrich the SOC mechanism. Here, SOC emerges from the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac equation in a curved space, intrinsically linked to the geometry of the twisted electron transport paths. This form of SOC, termed geometric SOC57,58, is different from conventional Rashba or atomic SOC in its global geometric origin and strong spatial variation along the knotted contour. Moreover, its magnitude can significantly exceed that of conventional SOC57, providing a physical basis for ultra-high spin polarization (up to 90%), observed experimentally in trefoil-knot molecules19.

Results

Theoretical model with geometric SOC

From a theoretical modeling perspective, the trefoil knot molecule can be simplified as a closed helical configuration with continuously varying curvature, forming its characteristic topological knot structure (Fig. 1d). The three-dimensional twisted pathways in this configuration increase the spatial degrees of freedom available for electron transport, giving rise to complex SOC features. Crucially, conventional SOC models for CISS, often derived from the Dirac equation in a flat spacetime electromagnetic field37,38,48, are fundamentally inapplicable to these continuously deforming trefoil structures. Here, we demonstrate that the trefoil-knot arrangement of monomers generates geometric SOC. Specifically, within the helical structure of trefoil knot molecules, quantum interference fundamentally occurs between direct (i-j) and indirect (i-k-j) paths for electron transmission, introducing an extra spin-dependent Berry phase during electron motion (see “Method”). This phenomenon constitutes the microscopic origin of geometric SOC, whose form is determined by molecular chirality. Like a conventional SOC, this geometric SOC can induce spin flipping during electron transport. To quantitatively characterize this mechanism, we construct a closed lattice-knot model in which the molecule is discretized into N lattice sites spaced at equal arc length (positions of sites 1 and N are shown in Fig. 2a; see also Supplementary Movie 1). Fundamental conditions for CISS in knot molecules

Using the Hamiltonian model described above, we identify the key conditions for spin-polarized electron transport through trefoil knot molecules. We begin by analyzing a two-terminal setup (γ = 2) in which a trefoil knot molecule (N = 60 lattice sites) bridges two non-magnetic metal electrodes (Fig. 3a, top). Theoretical calculations reveal that when the electrodes are coupled to the lattice sites i = 1 and j = 31, dividing the molecule into two perfectly symmetric halves, the system exhibits high conductivity but no spin polarization in the transmitted charge current (Fig. 3a, bottom). This behavior comes from the presence of two symmetric tunneling channels. The resulting phase cancellation suppresses quantum interference, and consequently spin polarization, a mechanism previously reported in closed helical molecular rings59–61, although the origins of SOC differ. We further test an open trefoil knot configuration, where the knot is cut at one point, and electrodes are placed at the resulting terminals (Fig. 3a, right). Numerical simulations show that although conductivity remains high (Supplementary Fig. 2), spin polarization remains negligible. When the two electrodes are attached to lattice sites that break the local left-right symmetry of the trefoil knot, the Berry phases induced by spatial curvature no longer cancel. This leads to the emergence of spin polarization, as illustrated in Fig. 3b. Specifically, in the asymmetric two-terminal configuration (1,15)60, the system maintains robust conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 2) and exhibits spin polarization of conducting electrons up to 20%. In particular, the mirror-symmetric configuration (1,47)60 shows spin polarization of the same magnitude but opposite sign (Fig. 3b, lower panel). This inversion of the spin direction upon spatial mirroring reflects the intrinsic C3 symmetry of the trefoil knot and strongly confirms that the geometric SOC term correctly captures the relationship between molecular geometry and spin-dependent transport. These results establish that asymmetric multichannel transport is necessary for spin selectivity in trefoil knots, with geometric SOC providing a key mechanism for spin polarization in these topologically chiral knot molecules.

Given the distinctive geometry of the trefoil knot molecule and the experimental setup typically used for current measurements (Fig. 1b), this molecular system presents three symmetrically positioned lower lattice sites that can be coupled to a metal substrate, while any one of the three upper sites may connect to an STM probe. To simulate this arrangement, we construct a three-terminal device (γ = 3) based on a trefoil knot with N = 60 lattice sites: two of its lower sites are connected to the metal substrate, and one upper site (i = 7) is coupled to the STM probe. We examine three different coupling configurations for the lower electrode pairs, corresponding to the structures shown in Fig. 3d (j = 15, 36), Fig. 3e (j = 36, 56), and Fig. 3f (j = 15, 56). Across these three configurations, the spatial symmetry between the left and right transport channels formed by the lower electrodes relative to the top electrode progressively decreases. The calculation results show a corresponding sequential enhancement in spin polarization, from approximately 20 to 75%, providing direct evidence that reduced symmetry in transport channels enhances spin selectivity.

Robustness of high-spin polarization in knot molecules

To further elucidate the origin of high spin polarization in trefoil knot molecules, we model a four-terminal transport device (γ = 4) that mirrors experimental setups for charge current measurement19. In this configuration, the three lowest lattice sites are connected to a metal substrate, while the uppermost site is coupled to an STM probe (Fig. 4a). We first consider a trefoil knot with N = 20 lattice sites, coupled at positions i = 3, j = 6, 14, 20. The calculated conductances G↑ and G↓ reveal a spin polarization of up to 60% (Fig. 4b, top). Reversing the molecular chirality from left- to right-handed also reverses the spin polarization direction, confirming that the effect is intrinsically tied to the topological chirality of the knot. Moreover, the four-terminal setup exhibits high electrical conductivity, especially near the Fermi level, where finite conductance indicates metallic transport behavior (Supplementary Fig. 3). This contrasts with conventional chiral materials such as DNA7 and helicenes8, which typically show low conductance due to significant band gaps. The observed metallic conduction is consistent with experimental reports of nearly two orders of magnitude conductivity enhancement relative to traditional chiral systems. Additional simulations for a knot with N = 60 lattice sites (at constant molecular length) show that the maximum spin polarization remains around 60% (Fig. 4b, bottom). In particular, this value is slightly lower than the ~75% polarization obtained in the analogous three-terminal model with the same number of lattice sites. In particular, in the four-terminal device based on the trefoil knot, spin polarization remains stable at approximately 60% as the total lattice density increases from N = 20 to 100, while the overall molecular length remains constant (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Movie 1). These results indicate that increasing lattice resolution does not significantly alter the spin polarization. Rather, the polarization is governed primarily by the intrinsic geometric configuration of the molecule and the coupling scheme, specifically the number and placement of electrodes, rather than by the total number of discrete lattice sites. This behavior stands in sharp contrast to conventional CISS effects observed in helical molecular chains, where spin polarization often varies considerably, typically increasing with either the number of lattice points per unit length or the total chain length. Together, these findings suggest that the robust spin selectivity in trefoil knots stems from the molecule’s intrinsic topological character: the stable knotted geometry enforces a form of topological invariance, which in turn ensures consistent spin polarization across different lattice discretizations.

To assess the stability of high spin polarization observed in trefoil knot molecules, we examine a four-terminal device (γ = 4) based on a N = 60 trefoil knot with initial parameters l1=l2 = 1.0 and h = 1.0 (central structures in Fig. 5). Two different strain protocols are applied to the 3D knot structure: (i) lateral strain, in which the height is fixed (h = 1.0) while one side is asymmetrically compressed (l1 increases from 1.0 to 2.0, l2 remains 1.0; Fig. 5a, left) and (ii) vertical strain, in which the lateral dimensions are maintained while the height is stretched (h increases from 1.0 to 2.0; Fig. 5a, right). The numerical results show that lateral deformation does not cause significant changes in conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 4) or spin polarization, which remains close to ~ 60%. Similarly, under vertical strain, spin polarization remains largely unchanged compared to the unstrained knot (Fig. 5a, right curves). The preservation of high spin selectivity under both deformation modes underscores the structural robustness conferred by the knot topology. These findings offer a theoretical basis for experimental reports of high-temperature spin polarization stability in trefoil knots: lattice vibrations, which induce dynamic structural distortions, do not disrupt the spin-selectivity transport pathways protected by the underlying topological configuration. Topological phase transition of CISS in knot molecules

To clarify the role of knot topology in stabilizing high spin polarization, we systematically vary the structural parameter R in a trefoil knot with N = 50 lattice sites. Changing R progressively suppresses the knot geometry and ultimately removes its topological character, allowing us to track the corresponding evolution of spin polarization. As R decreases from 2.0 to 1.1, the central triangular region contracts while the knot topology remains intact (Structure 1, Fig. 5b; Supplementary Movie 2). In this regime, spin polarization stays stable near 60%. At the critical value R = 1.0, the central region collapses to a crossing point (Structure 2, Fig. 5b) and the spin polarization drops markedly, although it remains finite. When R is further reduced to 0.9, the topological knot is eliminated: the helical ring opens into a trivial conformation that can be continuously deformed into a simple annulus, while maintaining a fan-like structural chirality (Structures 3–4, Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movie 2). This structural change corresponds to a topological transition in which topological chirality gives way to structural chirality. Upon completing this transition, spin polarization falls to approximately 20%, and conductivity is substantially reduced (Supplementary Fig. 5). Sequential suppression of spin polarization as the knot topology is removed provides clear evidence that the previously observed high polarization (>60%) is protected by the nontrivial topology of the trefoil knot.

As the parameter R gradually decreases, the trefoil knot deforms into a fan-shaped geometry. At R = 0, the structure opens completely into a vertically oriented circular ring (Structures 5 and 6, top view, Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movie 2). Although the knot topology is lost in this limit, the persistence of asymmetric transport paths with non-zero curvature maintains a finite degree of spin polarization in the transmitted electrons. In contrast, reducing the height parameter h flattens the molecular structure (Structures 5-6, side view, Fig. 5b). When h reaches zero, the system degenerates into a circular planar ring with uniform curvature, and the spin polarization decays asymptotically to zero. This marked difference in behavior reveals that spin selectivity in structurally chiral systems is highly sensitive to geometric deformation, a property that fundamentally distinguishes it from the robust spin polarization observed in topologically chiral knots. Importantly, the geometric SOC introduced in this work offers a unified physical framework that accounts for both conventional CISS in helical chains and robust CISS in trefoil knots.

Discussion

This study presents a theoretical framework to explain the microscopic origin of ultra-high spin polarization in trefoil knot molecules. The underlying mechanism involves two key elements: asymmetric multiple transport channels and geometric SOC. The latter stems from the accumulation of a quantum geometric phase in a curved configuration space, which distinguishes it from conventional SOC typically used to explain spin selectivity in chiral chains. We develop multi-terminal device models based on a tight-binding Hamiltonian that incorporates both electronic hopping and the geometric SOC term. Theoretical results show that when electrode coupling breaks spatial symmetry while maintaining the connectivity of the molecular knot, substantial spin polarization emerges. Simulations performed at the single-particle level, without additional electron correlation effects, reproduce spin polarization exceeding 60% with a direction governed solely by the topological chirality of the knot, alongside consistently high electrical conductivity. These theoretical predictions align with and help explain recent experimental reports19, establishing a coherent microscopic picture of spin selectivity in topologically chiral materials.

Theoretical results further show that high spin polarization (>60%) and high conductivity remain stable under changes in lattice site density when the overall molecular length is fixed. These properties also persist under considerable structural deformation, with lateral and vertical strains of up to 200% causing no significant degradation. In contrast, suppressing the topological knot structure, leading to its degeneration into a trivial knot along with a transition from topological to structural chirality, results in a pronounced decrease in spin polarization. This clear dependence on topological integrity confirms that the observed spin selectivity in trefoil knots is protected by the knot topology, endowing it with notable robustness. This inherent stability provides a natural explanation for experimental observations of sustained high spin polarization at elevated temperatures.

Our theoretical model predicts a maximum spin polarization of approximately 60% in a single trefoil knot molecule, which is significantly lower than the 90% polarization reported in recent experiments19. This difference can be attributed to several important factors that are not included in our idealized model. First, in the simulated single-molecule device, only one upper lattice site is connected to the STM tip, whereas experimentally, the tip may interact with multiple sites or even several molecules simultaneously, enhancing the overall spin-selective signal. More significantly, experimental samples typically consist of molecular thin films with thicknesses ranging from 2 to 20 nm, in which spin-polarized electrons traverse multiple molecular layers. Such a multilayer arrangement can substantially amplify spin filtering through sequential polarization steps. Additional physical mechanisms that are not captured in our current single-particle treatment, such as electron-electron interactions that may enhance spin correlations41 and electron-phonon coupling that could influence spin relaxation dynamics42, are also likely to contribute to higher polarization in real materials. Incorporating these structural and many-body effects into future models is expected to bring theoretical predictions closer to the 90% polarization observed experimentally, thus reconciling the current discrepancy while affirming the physical consistency of the proposed geometric SOC mechanism.

The theoretical framework presented here offers a consistent explanation for variations in spin polarization across systems with different types of chirality, including the transition from topological to structural chirality. Although developed for trefoil knots, the model can be naturally extended to other topologically chiral molecules, such as the cinquefoil knot (51)62,63, suggesting that molecular knots may form a versatile platform for exploring spin-dependent transport. More broadly, the approach introduced in this work, centered on geometric SOC and multi-terminal transport, provides a conceptual connection between different classes of chiral materials. By identifying common physical elements underlying spin selectivity in both conventional helical and topologically knotted systems, this study contributes to the ongoing effort toward a unified understanding of CISS phenomena.
