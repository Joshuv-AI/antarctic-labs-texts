Green frontiers on the “Belt and Road”: carbon policy, emission efficiency, and environmental total factor productivity across 41 economies
Authors: Zhiyuan Gao, Ziying Jia, Ting Sun, Youyuan Li, Chuxiao Yang, Yu Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As countries along the Belt and Road pursue green economic transformation, environmental total factor productivity (ETFP) has become a key indicator of green development performance, making the measurement and improvement of ETFP a pressing priority. This study evaluates the ETFP of 41 countries participating in the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). We introduce a novel framework that combines the Green Luenberger Productivity Index with the Bi-weighted Modified Russell Model (BWMRM), treating carbon emissions as an undesirable output. The results show that although overall ETFP remains relatively low, it exhibits a clear upward trend driven primarily by technological progress and carbon emission reduction efforts. Notably, clean technology innovation contributes more to CO₂ reduction than structural energy adjustments. In addition, the input-output performance of labor exceeds that of capital and GDP, with significant regional disparities across countries. Based on these findings, we propose a four-dimensional policy framework. First, countries should establish a gradient mechanism for low-carbon transitions. Second, policymakers should optimize factor allocation to improve labor skills and the green use of capital. Third, fostering transnational green technology spillovers is essential for enhancing clean energy cooperation. Finally, governments should design differentiated, region-specific emission reduction strategies. Overall, this study provides both theoretical and practical guidance for advancing green development across BRI countries.

Introduction

Building the Silk Road Economic Belt and 21st Century Maritime Silk Road is one of China’s major reform strategies as it seeks to broaden market access, deepen economic integration, and accelerate industrial transformation and upgrading [40]. As the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) continues to expand, it inevitably generates significant carbon emissions and raises pressing environmental concerns [15]. Most of the nations along the BRI are still in the early stages of industrialization, where the carbon-intensive model has predominated. Embedding low-carbon development within BRI construction—while carefully managing the trade-offs among energy use, environmental protection, and economic growth—is essential to meeting the sustainable development needs of participating nations [18]. This dual imperative thus serves as both a foundational principle and a long-term objective of the Belt and Road development strategy [36]. The Belt and Road Forum for International Cooperation proposed adopting the concept of green development. The initiative advocates eco-friendly, low-carbon, and resource-efficient production processes and lifestyles. It aims to enhance cooperation for environmental protection, promote eco-civilization, and jointly achieve the Sustainable Development Goals by 2030. This approach effectively integrates low-carbon development into the strategic framework of the BRI [21]. The 41 BRI countries, whose geographic distribution is shown in Fig. 1, are predominantly developing economies experiencing rapid industrialization with historically high-carbon models. Whether these countries can decouple economic growth from carbon emissions is critical to achieving global climate goals. Evaluating their environmental total factor productivity (ETFP) is not merely a regional economic exercise, but a pressing global environmental necessity.

Against the backdrop of severe resource constraints and mounting environmental pressures, energy conservation and emission reduction have emerged as imperative priorities for adjusting industrial structures and transforming economic development models. Countries along the BRI have implemented several efficiency and pollution reduction policies, each with binding targets [22], and are committed to achieving clean, resource-efficient, and low-emission economic growth. Whether these policies can achieve a win-win outcome for sustainable development, that is, a decline in energy consumption and pollution emissions alongside an increase in ETFP, has become a major economic issue attracting widespread attention [16]. ETFP is the primary driver of economic growth under energy conservation and emission reduction constraints at the national and regional levels, while reductions in energy consumption and emissions can influence environmental performance at the micro level through factors such as corporate costs, market entry mechanisms, and technological innovation, and at the macro level through structural adjustments, international trade, and foreign direct investment [7, 39]. Ultimately, these mechanisms work by incorporating inputs such as labor, capital, and energy and adjusting outputs, such as GDP and carbon emissions [23, 37, 38]. Chen et al., [5] explored the impact of urban polycentric structures on China’s green total factor productivity, innovatively highlighting the mediating role of agglomeration economies and the moderating effect of urban spatial development patterns. Cheng and Zhao, [6] focused on A-share listed companies from 2010 to 2022, employing a double machine learning model at the micro-enterprise level to investigate how green bond issuance enhances corporate total factor productivity. Wu et al., [35] applied a difference-in-differences model using panel data from prefecture-level cities between 2006 and 2020 and found that the establishment of national modern agricultural demonstration zones significantly increased agricultural green total factor productivity by 7.1%. Therefore, this paper attempts to assess the economic role of energy saving and greenhouse gas mitigation, including the effect mechanisms and transmission channels, with a focus on analyzing whether ETFP in BRI countries has experienced growth within the constraints of resource conservation and pollution control. It also examines which input-output factors influence the growth of ETFP in these countries and how they affect it, as well as the differences in the effect of sustainable energy use and carbon emissions reduction on ETFP. A response to these issues is of significant practical importance for determining whether a fundamental transformation of the economic development model can be achieved under the “new normal” of slower yet higher-quality growth and whether it is possible to maintain medium-to-high economic growth.

From the existing literature, DEA analysis has become the mainstream method for measuring production efficiency [34]. This is due to its ability to simultaneously accommodate multiple inputs and outputs without requiring the specification of a particular functional form. In 1983, Pittman , [30] was the first to incorporate “undesirable” outputs into the productivity analysis framework. Hailu and Veeman, [14] chose to include pollution emissions directly as input factors alongside conventional inputs in the classic DEA analysis. By using a directional distance function, Chung et al., [8] effectively addressed the issue of productivity measurement based on non-desirable outputs. Building on this, Tone , [33] proposed the SBM empirical analysis model with slack measures. Later, Färe and Grosskopf [10], Fukuyama and Weber [12], and Chambers et al., [3] further developed a more operational directional distance function based on slack measures. Chambers et al., [3] also introduced the Luenberger productivity index, which is particularly suited for the SDDF. Moreover, Andersen and Petersen , [1] proposed the super-efficiency DEA empirical analysis model, which effectively overcame the limitation in traditional DEA analysis, where efficiency values do not exceed 1, allowing for further comparative analysis of efficient DMUs. With continuous improvements in DEA models, many scholars have used enhanced DEA models for analysis. For instance, Li et al., [19] constructed a three-stage airline network structure, Abhijit Saha et al., [31] developed a group decision-making method based on a comprehensive DEA model, Shu et al., [32] evaluated the environmental efficiency of 168 global economies using the super-efficiency SBM-DEA model, Jiao et al., [17] established a city resilience assessment model using an improved DEA model, and García Valderrama et al., [13] constructed a dynamic network DEA model to examine the level of circular economy. The non-parametric DEA method, as presented in the aforementioned literature, has advantages such as not requiring the assumption of a specific production function form, accounting for technological inefficiencies, and enabling the decomposition of total factor productivity. It provides a more reasonable approach to analyzing “energy-environment-economy” issues. However, the literature employing DEA methods has not considered the heterogeneity of slack effects across different input-output factors and faces infeasibility [20]. Additionally, it measures the composite effect without distinguishing between the effects and differences of individual factors. The core solution to these problems lies in identifying the effects and mechanisms of different input-output factors on ETFP, and the decomposition of total factor productivity offers a feasible methodology for this purpose.

To fill the research gap, this paper provides three main contributions to research on green productivity and low-carbon development within BRI countries. Firstly, unlike traditional DEA or SBM models, which often struggle with infeasibility and ignore the heterogeneity of slack effects across different input-output factors when including undesirable outputs, we propose a novel framework integrating the Bi-weighted Modified Russell Model (BWMRM) and the Green Luenberger Productivity Index (GLPI). This methodological advancement effectively resolves the infeasibility issue using dual-period weights and enables a precise factor-specific dual decomposition. Secondly, most existing studies evaluate ETFP as a whole. Our novel decomposition method uniquely distinguishes the contribution of “energy-saving performance” from “emission-reduction performance.” This allowed us to uncover a counterintuitive but vital finding: improvements in CO2 reduction in BRI countries stem largely from clean technology innovations rather than mere adjustments in the energy structure.

Model methodology

Theoretical model

The traditional Solow model can only reflect the contributions of labor and capital to economic growth, without considering energy-environmental factors and inefficiencies in technological levels. Building upon the traditional Solow model, the green Solow model is constructed by incorporating energy-environmental factors, as shown in the following equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:{y}^{t}-{b}^{t}=\left({A}^{t}{\theta\:}^{t}\right)\left[{\left({l}^{t}\right)}^{\alpha\:}{\left({k}^{t}\right)}^{\beta\:}{\left({e}^{t}\right)}^{\gamma\:}\right]\\&;\alpha\:,\beta\:,\gamma\:\ge\:\mathrm{0,0}\le\:\theta\:\le\:1;{b}^{t}=\sum\:_{f=1}^{F}ma{c}^{t}{n}_{f}^{t}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In the equation provided, the variables l, k, e, y, and b correspond to labor input, capital utilization, energy consumption, GDP, and environmental governance costs, respectively. Additionally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document} represent the elasticity coefficients for labor, capital, and energy in relation to output growth. The terms A and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} denote technological progress and efficiency factors, respectively. Moreover, mac, f, F, and n signify marginal abatement costs, categories of pollutants, and carbon emissions, respectively. As this equation reveals, environmental pollution exerts an adverse effect on economic growth. In alignment with Coase’s transaction cost theory, even in scenarios where production is ostensibly efficient, the reality of economic growth is often marred by inefficiencies attributed to transaction costs. Such inefficiencies can disrupt economic growth. In more severe cases, where inefficiencies dominate entirely, economic growth may be effectively stalled.

Building on this framework, ETFP can be formally derived as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{\Delta\:}A}{A}+\frac{{\Delta\:}\theta\:}{\theta\:}=\frac{{\Delta\:}(y-b)}{y-b}-\alpha\:\frac{{\Delta\:}k}{k}-\gamma\:\frac{{\Delta\:}e}{e}$$\end{document}

From the above equation, it is clear that, compared to the traditional Solow economic growth model, ETFP can be calculated by incorporating energy and environmental factors. On the left side of the equation are technological progress and efficiency improvement. Therefore, ETFP can be enhanced through technological progress and improved efficiency. Simultaneously, as technology and production efficiency advance, pollution emissions decrease, which improves ETFP and supports both economic growth and environmental protection. From the perspective of efficiency improvement, efficiency gains are primarily achieved through the acquisition, assimilation, and further adaptation of frontier technologies, which lead to improvements in production technology and, consequently, reach the production possibility frontier. For enterprises, inefficient firms can improve by learning from high-efficiency firms, thus reducing energy costs, assimilating advanced technologies, and building endogenous development momentum. They can also eliminate outdated production capacities, achieving industrial transformation and upgrading. “High-energy consumption, high-emission” enterprises should actively learn advanced technologies from high-efficiency companies to maximize resource utilization. By eliminating outdated technologies and capacities, these firms can reduce costs and move toward a virtuous cycle, ultimately achieving true “transformation.” At the governmental level, the “invisible hand” can significantly enhance ETFP. This is primarily accomplished through the enforcement of stringent environmental regulations, which escalate costs for high-energy-consuming enterprises, subsequently prompting the transformation and upgrading of polluting industries. These regulations also stimulate increased investment in pertinent environmental protection technologies, thereby augmenting ETFP. Within regions, spatial spillover effects enable enterprises to learn advanced technologies from each other, enhancing the ETFP of lagging firms. This synergistic dynamic simultaneously raises regional environmental standards and supports economic growth, laying a solid foundation for high-quality, sustainable development.

Two-period directional distance function (Biennial directional distance function, BDDF): characterizing ETFP behavior

The directional distance function can describe the energy-environment model and characterize ETFP. Following the methods proposed by Chen et al., [4] and Pastor et al., [24] in addressing infeasible solutions, this paper combines two models to describe the infeasibility issue. It incorporates labor, capital, and energy as input variables and includes both desired and undesired outputs in the model. The BDDF is constructed, and the direction vector’s slack variable is normalized. Carbon dioxide reduction is incorporated into the ETFP analysis framework. Based on this, conclusions are drawn, and paths to improving ETFP are proposed, including reducing energy inputs and carbon emissions to enhance ETFP. The influence of carbon dioxide emissions on ETFP is illustrated in the following manner:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:\overrightarrow{{D}_{j}^{B}}(x,y,b;{g}_{y},{g}_{b})\\&=\mathrm{s}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{p}\left\{{\beta\:}_{j}^{B}:{\beta\:}_{j}^{B}=\sum\:_{m=1}^{M}{\beta\:}_{jm}^{B}+\sum\:_{q-1}^{Q}{\beta\:}_{jq}^{B}+\sum\:_{h-1}^{H}{\beta\:}_{jh}^{B}\in\:p\left(x\right)\right\}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

GLPI and its dual decomposition: analyzing carbon dioxide emission reduction behavior

The GLPI provides a detailed characterization of ETFP. Corresponding to the BDDF, this research integrates the work of Fujii et al., [11] and Pastor et al.,(2011) [24], ensuring that BDDF is consistent with the productivity indicator, allowing for the measurement of ETFP. Specifically, the GLPI measures the ETFP across different periods, with the change in productivity represented by the corresponding numerical value. When GLPI > 0, ETFP has improved; when GLPI = 0, ETFP is unchanged; and when GLPI < 0, ETFP has declined.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:GLP{I}_{V}^{B}=\overrightarrow{{D}_{V}^{B}}\left({x}^{t},{y}^{t},{b}^{t};{g}^{t}\right)\\&-\overrightarrow{{D}_{V}^{B}}\left({x}^{t+1},{y}^{t+1},{b}^{t+1};{g}^{t+1}\right)={\beta\:}_{V}^{Bt}-{\beta\:}_{V}^{Bt+1}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

According to the traditional model’s explanation of green productivity, ETFP may be divided into two different categories: the first is efficiency change, and the second is technological innovation. Efficiencies are measured in terms of the extent to which a decision-making unit moves closer to the production frontier, in other words, how efficient it has become. A value greater than 0 indicates efficiency improvement, zero indicates no change, while a value below zero refers to efficiency degradation. Technological progress, by contrast, refers to outward shifts in the production frontier over time, as distinct from efficiency change. However, both the efficiency indicator and the technological progress indicator are total indicators for measuring ETFP. They remain aggregate metrics and cannot analyze ETFP from a more detailed perspective. Therefore, it is necessary to decompose ETFP further based on each factor. According to the definition of the directional distance function in the evaluation model, this paper decomposes efficiency change into input variables—labor input, capital input, and energy input—as well as output variables, such as economic output and carbon dioxide as undesired output. This allows for the further decomposition of ETFP into labor-saving efficiency change ( LSEC), capital-saving efficiency change ( KSEC), energy-saving efficiency change ( ECEC), GDP growth technological change ( GGEC), and carbon dioxide emission reduction technological change. A positive value for each decomposition of technological change indicates that it promotes ETFP; a value of 0 indicates no contribution to total factor productivity; and a negative value indicates that it hinders ETFP. Similarly, technological change can also be decomposed based on the model’s input variables—labor input, capital input, and energy input—as well as output variables such as economic output and carbon dioxide as undesired output. This allows for the further decomposition of ETFP into labor-saving technological change ( LSTC), capital-saving technological change ( KSTC), energy-saving technological change ( ECTC), GDP growth technological change ( GGTC), and carbon dioxide emission reduction technological change. A positive value for each decomposition of technological change indicates that it promotes ETFP; a value of 0 indicates no contribution to total factor productivity; and a negative value indicates that it hinders ETFP.

Data sources and analysis

Considering the severe data gaps for some countries, the study focuses on 41 countries along the “Belt and Road” initiative1. The research period is set from 1995 to 2022 to ensure uniform statistical standards across countries and facilitate subsequent comparative analysis. Based on relevant data availability and referencing previous studies, three factors—capital, labor, and energy consumption—are selected as input variables. GDP and CO2 emissions are treated as “desirable” and “undesirable” outputs, respectively, thereby constructing a framework to assess the ETFP levels of countries along the BRI. Specifically, capital input (measured by gross fixed capital formation), labor input, and GDP data are obtained from the World Bank database, while energy consumption and total CO2 emissions data come from the IEA statistical database. As shown in Table 1, the variability in the observed values of the 41 countries is quite large, reflecting substantial cross-country heterogeneity in input and output levels throughout the sample period. Moreover, the maximum and minimum values of carbon emissions differ significantly.

Empirical analysis

Static analysis

Table 3 presents the static characteristics of ETFP and its components for the BRI countries. From a traditional decomposition perspective, between 1995 and 2022, the BRI countries witnessed an average growth in ETFP of 0.40%. Notably, technological progress contributed 0.23%, indicating a dominant role in this growth. The efficiency change represented 0.17% of the growth. However, the principal drag on ETFP growth was attributable to underperformance in GDP and capital utilization.

From the standpoint of input factor decomposition, energy use efficiency and emission reduction performance consistently outpace market-side performance and serve as the primary drivers of ETFP growth. Notably, the reduction of carbon dioxide emissions performs better than energy-saving measures. This is primarily because energy conservation often leads to emission reductions, but emission reductions do not always result from energy saving. For instance, advancements in emission reduction technology and the utilization of cleaner, pollution-free energy sources can also lead to emission reductions. These observations suggest that over the research period, the energy management and emissions abatement strategies implemented by the BRI countries yielded positive outcomes, thereby contributing significantly to the enhancement of total environmental factor productivity. Whether it is reducing carbon dioxide emissions or decreasing energy consumption, both are propelled by technological advancements, effectively boosting the growth of total environmental factor productivity.

In market performance, apart from capital performance and GDP performance, labor performance contributed positively to ETFP growth in BRI countries. Among these, labor-saving performance occupies a dominant position in market performance, while GDP growth performance holds a secondary position.

Table 4 reports the pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients among ETFP and its decomposed components, providing insights into the structural relationships among different dimensions of green productivity.

The most prominent finding is the extremely high positive correlation between energy-saving performance and emission reduction performance (r = 0.960). This near-perfect correlation confirms that improvements in energy utilization and reductions in carbon emissions are deeply intertwined processes. Countries that achieve energy efficiency gains almost universally experience concurrent emission reductions. However, the main text’s finding that emission reduction outperforms energy saving in contributing to ETFP growth remains valid: although the two are highly correlated, their absolute magnitudes differ, with emission reduction consistently exhibiting higher values.

Efficiency change (EC) and technological change (TC) exhibit a significant negative correlation (r = − 0.549). This inverse relationship suggests a compensatory dynamic: in periods when countries make large efficiency gains (catching up to the frontier), they tend to experience lower or negative technological progress, and vice versa. This finding is consistent with the theoretical prediction that countries near the frontier derive growth primarily from innovation (TC), while those further from the frontier benefit more from efficiency improvements (EC).

TC shows strong positive correlations with both energy-saving performance (r = 0.677) and emission reduction performance (r = 0.668), while EC shows correspondingly strong negative correlations with these same variables (r = − 0.649 and r = − 0.638, respectively). This pattern indicates that improvements in energy and emission performance are predominantly driven by frontier-shifting technological innovation rather than by countries catching up to existing best practices. In other words, the BRI countries that have improved their environmental performance have done so primarily by developing or adopting new clean technologies, not merely by imitating existing ones.

GDP performance exhibits negative correlations with most other components, particularly with emission reduction performance (r = − 0.403) and energy-saving performance (r = − 0.395). This trade-off between GDP growth and environmental performance reflects the persistent tension between economic expansion and environmental sustainability in BRI countries. Finally, ETFP (GLPI) shows moderate positive correlations with both EC (r = 0.490) and TC (r = 0.460), confirming that both efficiency improvement and technological progress contribute meaningfully to overall green productivity growth. The roughly balanced contributions from both channels suggest that a dual strategy—promoting technology transfer to lagging countries while incentivizing frontier innovation in leading countries—offers the most effective pathway to improving ETFP across the BRI region.

Dynamic analysis

As shown in Table 5, the ETFP of countries along the BRI exhibits different characteristics at different stages. The growth rate was the lowest in 2020 (-2.10%), followed by 2014 (-1.78%), and the highest in 2015 (3.34%). These figures suggest that ETFP among BRI countries peaked in 2015–2016 and subsequently entered a period of notable decline. Notably, 2015 was the year when the BRI was fully implemented, with the initiation of large-scale projects and an influx of capital, leading to environmental degradation and excess investment. Additionally, large-scale infrastructure construction inevitably brings ecological challenges, and the combination of both factors contributed to the overall decline in green development efficiency. This result aligns with the “Environmental Kuznets Curve,” which describes the phenomenon of environmental deterioration during the early stages of national development.

Furthermore, Figs. 2 and 3 support the conclusion that during the study period, energy optimization and emission minimization were the primary drivers of ETFP growth among countries participating in the BRI. Capital and GDP performance had detrimental effects. Given that technological advancements positively influence energy savings and greenhouse gas emissions control performance, these findings suggest that significant progress has been made by these countries in the realm of energy savings and sustainability technologies. Notably, some countries have spearheaded environmental technological innovations, advancing the environmental production technology frontier and achieving superior productivity while reducing waste generation and pollutant emissions.

Table 5 divides the sample period into six subperiods and shows that significant declines were observed both before the implementation of BRI, at − 0.0022, and after its implementation, at − 0.0092. The post-BRI decline is more pronounced, which appears to contradict the Initiative’s emphasis on green development. This pattern, however, can be explained from several perspectives. First, the large-scale infrastructure investment undertaken during the early stage of BRI implementation inevitably increased carbon-intensive activities, including construction, cement production, and transportation. Second, GDP performance deteriorated markedly, reaching − 0.0102 during 2014–2017, which reflects the adverse effects of the global economic slowdown and commodity price fluctuations on developing BRI countries. Regional analysis

Figures 4 and 5 reveal significant regional disparities in efficiency change, technological progress, labor and capital performance, GDP performance, as well as in energy-saving and emission-reduction performance.

The Central Asia region has experienced a marginal decline in ETFP, largely driven by weak or adverse technological progress. Correspondingly, both its energy efficiency performance and carbon reduction capacity have also deteriorated. In contrast, the East Asia region illustrates an overall positive trend, particularly prominent in technological progress and emission reduction performance. The West Asia region, while showing a minor uptick in ETFP, has witnessed a decrease in efficiency change, even as technological progress and emission reduction performance have improved. The South Asia region, however, depicts a pronounced drop in ETFP, primarily due to negative growth in efficiency change and technological progress. Although Eastern Europe has also experienced a marginal fall in ETFP, it maintains a positive trajectory in technological progress, accompanied by enhancements in both energy saving and emission reduction performance. Both Central Europe and Southeast Asia demonstrate robust growth, especially in technological progress and GDP performance. Southern Europe, with a slight rise in ETFP driven by positive technological progress, also reports improvement in emission reduction performance. In conclusion, regions must strike a balance between ecological preservation and economic expansion to attain sustainable development.

Table 6 cross-tabulates ETFP values by region and time period, providing a two-dimensional perspective that reveals how regional green productivity trajectories have evolved.

East Asia demonstrates the most consistently positive and stable ETFP performance across all six periods, with values ranging from 0.0020 to 0.0180. This stability reflects the region’s advanced clean energy technologies, particularly in China and Japan, where sustained government investment in renewable energy and stringent environmental regulations have created a self-reinforcing cycle of green innovation.

ETFP by region and period

Four representative regions, namely East Asia, West Asia, South Asia, and Eastern Europe, were selected for detailed analysis. The East Asia region excels in ETFP, primarily due to its advanced technological progress and efficient energy utilization. Notably, China and Japan have made significant strides in clean energy technologies and energy-saving policies, contributing to the overall improvement in ETFP across the region. The West Asia region has shown stable performance in ETFP, but its growth momentum remains insufficient. Countries in this region are primarily dependent upon conventional fossil fuels, including petroleum and natural gas, with relatively slow development of clean energy, which has limited the growth of ETFP.

A significant decline in ETFP (-0.0130) is observed in the South Asia region, which is closely tied to its policy environment, particularly the insufficient enforcement of environmental regulations and the lack of pressure on companies to upgrade their technologies. For instance, the carbon intensity of GDP in India’s steel industry is 1.5 times higher than that of China, leading to a loss in technological efficiency and directly dragging down the region’s ETFP. Furthermore, insufficient technological capacity is a core driver of ETFP decline in South Asia. According to WIPO data, between 2015 and 2022, clean technology patents in South Asia accounted for only 0.3%, significantly lower than ASEAN’s 1.2% and Central and Eastern Europe’s 0.8%. A further breakdown reveals that technological advancements in South Asia have been focused on improving traditional energy efficiency, with nearly no investment in zero-carbon technologies. This has resulted in carbon emission reductions that rely entirely on decreasing energy intensity rather than technological innovation, contrasting sharply with East Asia. Therefore, compared to the successful practices of ASEAN and Central and Eastern Europe, South Asia needs to improve in three areas: first, gradually phasing out fossil fuel subsidies and shifting towards renewable energy subsidies; second, strengthening environmental enforcement and establishing a corporate carbon emission disclosure system; and third, establishing a regional clean technology transfer fund to focus on supporting frontier technologies such as carbon capture and hydrogen energy.

Country analysis

Mean ETFP values and their temporal changes for the 41 BRI countries over 1995–2021 are presented in Fig. 7. The results indicate that ETFP levels remain generally low across the evaluated countries, suggesting considerable room for improvement. Countries with lower ETFP are mostly in the early stages of development, with lower economic development levels and relatively underdeveloped domestic infrastructure. Only six countries have an efficiency mean greater than 0.01, accounting for about 15% of the evaluated countries, a relatively small proportion.

Fig. 7ETFP ranking of 41 BRI countries (1995–2022)

ETFP ranking of 41 BRI countries (1995–2022)

Three countries—Singapore, China, and Kazakhstan—were selected for in-depth analysis. Singapore, as a developed country in Southeast Asia, ranks high in ETFP. This is attributed to the continuous investments by the Singaporean government in energy conservation, emissions reduction, and clean energy technologies, along with efficient government management and corporate innovation capabilities. China, as the initiator of the BRI, has made significant progress in green development and energy-saving policies in recent years. By implementing a series of environmental policies and clean energy projects, China’s ETFP has steadily improved, becoming a key driver of regional green development. Kazakhstan, as an important country in Central Asia, has a relatively low ETFP level. This is primarily influenced by factors such as its monolithic economic structure, slow technological progress, and low energy efficiency. In the future, Kazakhstan needs to increase investments in clean energy technologies and energy-saving measures to enhance its ETFP level.

In addition, to validate the robustness of the ETFP estimates, this study conducted three sets of tests: (1) Model substitution test: The super-efficiency SBM-DEA model and Malmquist-Luenberger index were used to recalculate the ETFP estimates. The results show that the ETFP rankings of the 41 countries are highly correlated with those of the original model, with the average contribution of technological progress being 0.21%. (2) Weight sensitivity test: In a scenario where capital weight was increased by 20%, the contribution of technological progress remained at 0.19%, significantly higher than that of energy efficiency. (3) Sample sensitivity test: After excluding China and Saudi Arabia, the average ETFP of East Asian countries decreased from 0.0074 to 0.0068, but it remained significantly higher than that of Central Asia. These tests demonstrate that the original conclusions are robust with respect to model specification, weight selection, and data samples.

Based on the above conclusions, we further conducted a heterogeneity analysis and extended the policy implications. Regarding the relationship between household income and carbon emissions, this study finds that carbon emission reductions in East Asian countries are largely driven by an increase in the share of renewable energy, whereas in Central Asian countries, technological progress primarily focuses on improving fossil fuel efficiency (U. K. Pata & U. Bulut et al., [28]). This suggests that income levels may moderate the emission reduction pathways of technological progress. In terms of forest carbon sinks and carbon emissions, forest carbon sink capacity plays a significant role in shaping ETFP outcomes (U. K. Pata & E. Baykut [27]). In countries with higher forest coverage, such as Russia and Indonesia, forest carbon sinks can offset 5%-10% of industrial carbon emissions, thereby indirectly enhancing ETFP. Regarding industrial structure transformation, an analysis of Southeast Asian countries reveals that a higher share of the service sector in these countries allows for a reduction in demand for energy-intensive industries, thereby indirectly lowering carbon emissions [29]. Concerning policy uncertainty, it may hinder the diffusion of low-carbon technologies (U. K. Pata, [26, 29]). Countries with stronger policy stability, such as China and Poland, show an ETFP annual volatility of less than 2%, while countries with frequent policy adjustments, such as Iraq and Ukraine, experience a volatility exceeding 5%. In the context of green transformation in the transportation sector, technological upgrades in transportation have a significant impact on ETFP ([2]; S. K. Pata et al., [25]. In countries where the penetration rate of electric vehicles exceeds 10%, such as Norway and China, the carbon emission intensity in the transportation sector is lower compared to traditional internal combustion engine countries, directly driving the improvement of ETFP.

Conclusion and strategic policy insights

As the BRI advances with growing momentum, the principle of “green development” has increasingly gained recognition and been actively embraced by participating countries and regions. This study, using BDDF and the Luenberger productivity index, reveals and evaluates the current status and disparities of ETFP across the 41 countries along the BRI. This research fills the gap in the current literature on green BRI. Furthermore, it offers concrete policy guidance for China regarding the planning and effective implementation of green development goals within the BRI framework. The following are the main conclusions: (1) The ETFP level of the countries along the BRI is generally low, with significant disparities among countries in each region; only 15% of the countries have an ETFP level greater than 0.01. (2) ETFP across BRI countries has followed an upward trajectory, with technological progress serving as the primary driver. Regarding input factors, energy efficiency and environmental emissions performance are the dominant drivers. The effects of emission reduction are superior to those of energy savings. Regarding other factors, labor performance is more effective than capital and GDP.

Based on this, the following policy recommendations are proposed to guide relevant countries or regions in advancing ETFP: (1) Countries must expedite the shift from an investment-driven, “high-carbon” economic growth model towards a sustainable, low-carbon, green growth strategy. This transition will support steady improvements in technical efficiency. It is crucial to bolster corporate technological innovation to consistently and fully exploit the positive traction of technological advancements, particularly in fields like clean production and energy-efficient carbon reduction within environmental protection. Furthermore, countries should concentrate on enhancing the input-output ratio of various elements, optimizing the allocation of inputs such as capital, labor, and energy resources, and transforming enterprise management systems and governance structures. It is recommended that countries set short-term, medium-term, and long-term low-carbon transition goals based on their carbon emission levels and energy structure. For example, in the short term, the focus should be on improving energy efficiency and restructuring high-carbon industries. In the medium term, the proportion of clean energy should be increased, and energy structure diversification should be promoted. In the long term, a comprehensive low-carbon economy should be achieved, ensuring the widespread adoption of green technologies and the realization of carbon neutrality across all major energy industries. (2) Relevant countries or regions should reassess their geographic and resource advantages and actively transcend the bottlenecks posed by their current levels of economic development and institutional capacity, so that green technology spillover effects from trade liberalization between countries can be fully realized. Moreover, it is imperative to expand trade areas, continuously explore new trade growth points, and strengthen energy cooperation. This will optimize the energy structure and balance endowment differences. Through the framework of the BRI, strengthen green technology exchange and cooperation among countries along the route. For instance, in energy-rich regions such as Southeast Asia and Central Asia, collaborative efforts could be made to develop clean energy projects like solar and wind power, creating a bidirectional spillover of technology and experience. Support green technology transfer cooperation between technology-intensive countries and developing nations, providing technical support and guidance to help recipient countries improve productivity and reduce carbon emissions. Establish an international green technology cooperation fund to finance green technology transfer and cooperation projects, especially in countries along the Belt and Road. Provide policy support and financial guarantees to encourage green technology cooperation between developed and developing countries, jointly developing and promoting low-carbon technologies. For example, Central Asia needs to overcome technological stagnation by increasing investment in green technology incubators and training centers to improve local workforce skills in sustainable development. By collaborating with developed economies or technology providers, local technology levels can be rapidly enhanced. Research and development subsidies should be provided, particularly for clean energy technologies like solar and wind power, to promote innovation. Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) models should be encouraged in the clean technology sector to attract foreign investment and joint ventures. Technology transfer agreements, especially with countries leading in green energy technologies, should be prioritized. Regional green technology centers should be established to promote cross-border cooperation, particularly supporting innovation in green agriculture and solar energy. Gradual carbon pricing policies should be implemented, progressively raising carbon emission costs to encourage businesses to upgrade and transform. (3) Countries must develop targeted strategies for progress. Given the distinct mechanisms of various influencing factors, each country must formulate improvement strategies that are specifically tailored. These strategies should be grounded in how key economic variables differentially affect pure efficiency change, technological change, scale efficiency, and returns to scale. This will ensure that decision-making processes in practical applications are more scientifically sound and reasonable. Governments should collaborate with universities and vocational training institutions to establish courses related to the green economy, aimed at training professionals such as green technology engineers and green policy experts. By creating a green skills certification system, businesses can be encouraged to offer training related to green technologies and environmental protection, enhancing workers’ adaptability in the green economy transition. The innovation of financial products such as green bonds and green funds should be promoted, encouraging investors to direct funds toward green enterprises and projects. Financing incentives, such as low-interest loans and tax reductions, should be provided to green businesses. Additionally, a corporate green performance evaluation system should be established to assess companies’ performance in carbon reduction, energy efficiency, waste management, and other areas, prioritizing support for companies with outstanding green performance. For example, Southeast Asia should leverage trade openness to promote green technology spillovers by facilitating the flow of green technologies through green trade agreements and investment treaties. For instance, offering tax incentives and lowering tariffs on imports of clean energy technologies and environmentally friendly products can promote the development of green industries. Expanding green financial products, such as low-interest loans and subsidies, can support businesses in adopting green technologies. A multinational green technology spillover mechanism should be established, such as jointly developing clean energy projects like solar and wind power, sharing green technology outcomes, and improving energy efficiency. Industry-specific talent training in green manufacturing technologies should be conducted to enhance Southeast Asian countries’ competitiveness in the global green supply chain. Regional research and development cooperation should be strengthened by creating a green technology knowledge-sharing platform to drive technological innovation in areas like clean production and energy-efficient carbon reduction. Long-term carbon reduction targets should be promoted, encouraging countries to formulate policies aligned with international green development standards to advance the dissemination and application of green technologies. (4) As the advocate and active promoter of the green BRI, China should strengthen coordination with the sustainable development goals and plans of countries along the route, facilitate the alignment of ecological and environmental protection policies and regulations in related fields, and actively explore the formulation of guidelines and technical standards for green BRI project construction and financing, to jointly enhance low-carbon and green cooperation in infrastructure, product trade, and other fields. Strengthen coordinated cooperation on green policies among countries along the Belt and Road, promoting policy synchronization for green development within the region, and jointly addressing climate change and environmental pollution. Establish a monitoring and evaluation system for green transformation, regularly assessing the implementation effectiveness of green policies in different countries or regions, ensuring the effectiveness of policy measures, and making timely adjustments and optimizations. For example, differentiated strategies should be developed for low-income and high-income Belt and Road countries. For low-income countries, the focus should be on low-cost, high-efficiency energy-saving measures, such as promoting green technologies like efficient stoves and solar-powered irrigation systems to enhance productivity and reduce carbon emissions. International financing mechanisms, such as green bonds, should be established to provide funding support for infrastructure projects, particularly those aimed at enhancing climate resilience and access to green energy. For high-income countries, more advanced policies should be implemented, such as carbon trading markets and renewable energy incentives, to further drive green investment. The promotion of circular economy models should be encouraged, along with waste-to-resource technologies and ecological design principles, to ensure that sustainable development is achieved alongside economic growth.
