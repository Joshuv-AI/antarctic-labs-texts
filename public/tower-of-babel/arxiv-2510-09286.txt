Confluence of the Node-Domination and Edge-Domination Hypergraph Rewrite Rules
Antoine Amarilli 1, Mikaël Monet 1, Rémi De Pretto 1,2 1 : Univ. Lille, Inria, CNRS, Centrale Lille, UMR 9189 CRIStAL 2 : École supérieure de chimie, physique, électronique de Lyon

Abstract
In this note, we study two rewrite rules on hypergraphs, called edge-domination and node-domination , and show that they are confluent. These rules are rather natural and commonly used before computing the minimum hitting sets of a hypergraph. Intuitively, edge-domination allows us to remove hyperedges that are supersets of another hyperedge, and node-domination allows us to remove nodes whose incident hyperedges are a subset of that of another node. We show that these rules are confluent up to isomorphism, i.e., if we apply any sequences of edge-domination and node-domination rules, then the resulting hypergraphs can be made isomorphic via more rule applications. This in particular implies the existence of a unique minimal hypergraph , up to isomorphism.

Introduction

This note studies the confluence of two natural rewrite rules on hypergraphs,
called the
edge-domination
and
node-domination
rules. Informally,
given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, the edge-domination rule consists in removing a
hyperedge
e^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
if there is another hyperedge
e
which is contained
in
e^{\prime}
, and the node-domination rule consists in removing a node
v
of
\mathcal{H}
if there is another node
v^{\prime}
which is incident to a superset of the
hyperedges to which
v
is incident. We give a formal definition of these rules
later in this note.

These two rules have been commonly used for decades, especially when studying
the minimum hitting sets of hypergraphs. Recall that a
hitting set
of a
hypergraph (also called
hypergraph transversal
) is a subset of its nodes
such that every hyperedge contains a node of the hitting set, and a

minimum hitting set
1
1

1

This should not be confused with a

minimal hitting set
, which refers to minimality under set inclusion, and
which can be tractably computed via a greedy algorithm.
is one of minimal cardinality. It is well-known that
computing minimum hitting sets is NP-hard, because this
includes as a special case the
vertex cover
problem (when the input
hypergraph is a graph, i.e., every hyperedge has cardinality
2
). The point of
the edge-domination and node-domination rules is that we can apply them to
eliminate some hyperedges and nodes of the input hypergraph in a way that does
not affect the size of a minimum hitting set. Intuitively this is because
dominated hyperedges will always be covered by the hyperedges that they
contain; and (strictly) dominated nodes will never be part of a vertex cover.
See
[
1
, Claim 4.8]
for a formal proof. Thus, the
edge-domination and node-domination rules can be used as an easy
polynomial-time preprocessing on hypergraphs before computing minimum hitting
sets.

In this note, we do not discuss the computation of minimum hitting sets, but
study these rules in their own right. Our goal is to formally show that the
rules are
confluent up to isomorphism
. In other words, consider a hypergraph

\mathcal{H}
. Apply
node-domination and edge-domination rules to
\mathcal{H}
in some fashion to obtain
a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and apply the rules to
\mathcal{H}
in a different fashion to
obtain a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
Can we transform
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
into isomorphic hypergraphs by applying
rules in some fashion on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, or is it possible that we
can obtain genuinely different hypergraphs depending on which rules we apply?
Indeed, it is
not difficult to see that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
may be different – e.g., when
we have the choice between removing one of two “twin” nodes that are incident to
precisely the same set of hyperedges. However, as we will see, we can
necessarily converge to isomorphic hypergraphs with further rule applications.

In other words, the point of this note is to show that, for any hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, if we obtain
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
by applying different sequences of rules from
\mathcal{H}
,
then we can still converge to isomorphic hypergraphs.
This fact is rather intuitive, but to our knowledge the question had not been
addressed by previous works. We show:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two hypergraphs
obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by iterative application of edge-domination and
node-domination rules. Then, there are two hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\prime}
that are isomorphic and that can be respectively obtained from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
by
application of these rules.

In particular, this directly implies that minimal hypergraphs are unique up to
isomorphism, in the following sense:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an hypergraph, and let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two
hypergraphs obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by iterative application of edge-domination
and node-domination rules such that it is not possible to apply any rule to

\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Then
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are isomorphic.

The rest of this note is devoted to a proof of
Theorem

1.1
. It is established using Newman’s lemma
[
6
,
5
]
, i.e., by showing local confluence. Specifically, it suffices to
establish the following local confluence claim: for any hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, if
we can obtain
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
from
\mathcal{H}
in one step (by two
different ways to perform a single rule application), then we can
obtain some hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{3}
(up to isomorphism) from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
in a (possibly empty) sequence of steps. The local confluence of the rules
is itself shown by a case distinction, depending on which of the two rules were
applied to obtain
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and to obtain
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

Preliminaries

Hypergraphs.

A
hypergraph
is intuitively defined like a graph but with edges that can be incident to more
than two nodes.
We formalise a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
as a 3-tuple
(V,E,I)

where
V
is a finite set of
nodes
,

E
is a finite set of
hyperedges
(or simply
edges
), and

I\subseteq V\times E
is a binary relation called the
incidence relation
:
for
v\in V
and
e\in E
, we have
v\mathrel{I}e

(i.e.,
(v,e)\in I
) if
e
is
incident
to
v
.
For
v\in V
a node, we define
E(v)
as the set of edges incident to
v
,
i.e.,
E(v)\coloneq\{e\in E\colon v\mathrel{I}e\}
. Symmetrically, for
e\in E
an edge, we define
V(e)

as the set of nodes that
e
is incident to, i.e.,
V(e)\coloneq\{v\in V\colon v\mathrel{I}e\}
.

Note that our definition of hypergraphs is a generalisation of the classic
definition where the hyperedges of
E
is a set of subsets of
V
. Indeed,
unlike that definition, our definition makes it possible to have two distinct hyperedges
containing the same set of nodes.
For this reason, the hypergraphs that we define here can also be seen as
a notion of
multi-hypergraphs
, i.e., hypergraphs defined according to the
classic definition but with
E
being
a
multiset

of subsets of
V
. However, in the present note, we use the definition with an
incidence relation given in the paragraph above, because it makes the role of
hyperedges and nodes symmetric.
Moreover, the relation
I
can be viewed as a matrix
M
, called the

incidence matrix
, where rows and columns represent nodes and hyperedges, respectively.
For
v
a node and
e
a hyperedge, the cell
M[v,e]
of the matrix
M

contains
1
if
v\mathrel{I}e
and
0
otherwise.

Rewrite rules on hypergraphs.

A
rewrite rule
on hypergraphs is a process that, given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
as input, transforms this
hypergraph into another hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. Here we present three rewrite rules,
the third rule being the union of the first two.

Definition 2.1

(Edge-domination)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E,I)
be a hypergraph. If there are two edges
e,e^{\prime}\in E
such that
e\neq e^{\prime}
and

e^{\prime}
is incident to a superset of the nodes to which
e
is incident,
then
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},I^{\prime})
is obtained by removing
e^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{H}
.
Formally, if
e\neq e^{\prime}
and
V(e)\subseteq V(e^{\prime})
then
we set
V^{\prime}\coloneq V
,
E^{\prime}\coloneq E\setminus\{e^{\prime}\}
and
I^{\prime}\coloneq\{(v,f)\in I\colon f\neq e^{\prime}\}
.
We denote this by
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{edge}}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.

Notice that this definition is consistent with the usual one where hyperedges
are sets of nodes: the rule then says that we can remove a hyperedge if it is a
superset of another hyperedge. Note that the inclusion is not necessarily
strict, because in our definition of hypergraphs we can have multiple edges
containing the same set of nodes: in this case we can choose any of the edges
and remove it using the rule.

Definition 2.2

(Node-domination)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E,I)
be a hypergraph. If there are two nodes
v,v^{\prime}\in V
such that
v\neq v^{\prime}
and

v^{\prime}
is incident to a superset of the edges to which
v
is incident,
then
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
is obtained by removing
v
from
\mathcal{H}
.
Formally, if
E(v)\subseteq E(v^{\prime})
then we set

V^{\prime}\coloneq V\setminus\{v\}
,
E^{\prime}\coloneq E
and
I^{\prime}\coloneq\{(u,e)\in I\colon u\neq v\}
.
We denote this by
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.

For
\mathcal{H}
a hypergraph, if
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by some
application of a rewrite rule above,
then we write
\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
(i.e.
{\to}={\to_{\text{edge}}}\cup{\to_{\text{node}}}
).
If
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is obtained by applying some (possibly empty) sequence of rewrite rules,
we call
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
a
reduced hypergraph
of
\mathcal{H}
and denote it
\mathcal{H}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.
In other words,
\to^{*}
is the reflexive and transitive closure of
\to
.

Since each rewrite rule eliminates either a
node or a hyperedge, and since we work with finite hypergraphs only, it is clear that any sequence of rule
applications must terminate:

Claim 2.3
.

The rewrite rule
\to

terminates
, i.e., for every hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, any sequence of
the form
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\ldots
must be finite.

This motivates the definition of a
minimal
hypergraph:

Definition 2.4

(Minimal hypergraph)
.

For
\mathcal{H}
a hypergraph, if we cannot apply any rewrite rules
to
\mathcal{H}
(formally if there is no hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
such that

\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
), then we say that
\mathcal{H}
is
minimal
.
We call
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
a
minimal hypergraph of
\mathcal{H}
if
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

is a reduced hypergraph of
\mathcal{H}
which is minimal.

By
2.3
, one gets that
every hypergraph has at least one minimal hypergraph. The question is then to
understand how different choices of applying the
rules can lead to different minimal hypergraphs. Note
that this can happen already in simple cases:

Example 2.5

Let

Hypergraph isomorphism.

We now formally define the notion of
hypergraph isomorphism
:

Definition 2.7

(Hypergraph isomorphism)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E,I)
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},I^{\prime})
be two hypergraphs. We
say that
\mathcal{H}
is
isomorphic
to
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
if there exist two
bijective functions
f\colon V^{\prime}\to V
and
g\colon E^{\prime}\to E
which satisfy the following:
for every
v\in V^{\prime}
and
e\in E^{\prime}
, we have
v\mathrel{I^{\prime}}e
if and only if we have

f(v)\mathrel{I}g(e)
.

It is clear that hypergraph isomorphism is an equivalence relation; we
denote it by
\equiv
. For a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, we denote its equivalence class
under hypergraph isomorphism by
[\mathcal{H}]_{\equiv}
.

Problem statement.

In this work, we study the rewrite rules of
edge-domination and node-domination on hypergraphs.
Our goal is to show that these rules are confluent up to isomorphism. In other
words, our goal is to show
Theorem

1.1
, which we
reformulate here:

Theorem 2.8

(Confluence under isomorphism)
.

Let
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
be three hypergraphs such that
\mathcal{H}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Then there exist two hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{3}

and
\mathcal{H}_{4}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{1}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}_{3}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}_{4}
and

\mathcal{H}_{3}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{4}
.

Note that, as in the introduction, this directly implies the following corollary:

Corollary 2.9

(Unicity of minimal hypergraphs up to isomorphism)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be any hypergraph, and let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two minimal hypergraphs of
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are isomorphic.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2.8
, there exist two hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{3}

and
\mathcal{H}_{4}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{1}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}_{3}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}\to^{*}\mathcal{H}_{4}
and

\mathcal{H}_{3}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{4}
. As
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are minimal, we must have
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{3}

and
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{H}_{4}
, hence
\mathcal{H}_{1}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{2}
indeed.

Definition 3.1

Let

Let

For two hypergraph classes

we have that
e_{1}^{\prime}\neq e_{2}^{\prime}
,
that
V(e_{1})\subseteq V(e_{1}^{\prime})
, and that
V(e_{2})\subseteq V(e_{2}^{\prime})
,
and we have that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is obtained from
\mathcal{H}

by removing
e_{1}^{\prime}
(
V_{1}=V
,
E_{1}=E\setminus\{e_{1}^{\prime}\}
and

I_{1}=\{(v,e)\in I\colon e\neq e_{1}^{\prime}\}
) and
likewise
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by removing
e_{2}^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{3}=(V_{3},E_{3},I_{3})
be the hypergraph obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by removing

e_{1}^{\prime}
and
e_{2}^{\prime}
, i.e., we set
V_{3}\coloneq V
,
E_{3}\coloneq E\setminus\{e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\}

and
I_{3}\coloneq\{(v,e)\in I\colon e\neq e_{1}^{\prime}\text{~and~}e\neq e_{2}^{\prime}\}
.
We have several easy sub-cases.

•

If
e_{1}^{\prime}\neq e_{2}
and
e_{2}^{\prime}\neq e_{1}
then, as we already have that

e_{1}^{\prime}\neq e_{2}^{\prime}
,
we can simply remove
e_{2}^{\prime}

in
\mathcal{H}_{1}

(because
e_{2}
is still there and we still have
V(e_{2})\subseteq V(e^{\prime}_{2})
) to yield
\mathcal{H}_{3}
.
For the same reason,
we can obtain
\mathcal{H}_{3}
from
\mathcal{H}_{2}
by removing
e^{\prime}_{1}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{H}_{1}\to_{\text{edge}}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{3}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}\to_{\text{edge}}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{3}
, so local confluence is verified.

•

If
e_{1}\neq e_{2}^{\prime}
and
e_{2}=e_{1}^{\prime}
, then we have
V(e_{1})\subseteq V(e_{1}^{\prime})\subseteq V(e_{2}^{\prime})

and once again, we can remove both independently using
e_{1}
as a witness, which satisfies local confluence.

•

Symmetrically, if
e_{1}=e_{2}^{\prime}
and
e_{2}\neq e_{1}^{\prime}
, we have the same result.

•

Otherwise,
e_{1}=e_{2}^{\prime}
and
e_{2}=e_{1}^{\prime}
, and we have
V(e_{1})\subseteq V(e_{1}^{\prime})=V(e_{2})
and
V(e_{2})\subseteq V(e^{\prime}_{2})=V(e_{1})
, so that
V(e_{1})=V(e_{2})
. We already have that
e_{1}^{\prime}\neq e_{2}^{\prime}
, so that
e_{1}\neq e_{2}
.
Here, once we have removed one of the two edges,
it may not be possible to remove the second one, because the witness
justifying the applicability of the second edge-removal rule might not exist
anymore. Nevertheless, in fact we immediately have that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, via the bijections

f\colon V_{2}\to V_{1}=\mathit{id}
and
g\colon E_{2}\to E_{1}
defined by

g_{|E\setminus\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}=\mathit{id}
and
g(e_{2})=e_{1}
. As
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are isomorphic, local confluence for equivalence classes holds.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{edge}}\mathcal{H}_{2}
:

Let
v_{1},v_{1}^{\prime}\in V
and
e_{2},e_{2}^{\prime}\in E
such that
v_{1}\neq v_{1}^{\prime}
,
E(v_{1})\subseteq E(v_{1}^{\prime})
,

e_{2}\neq e_{2}^{\prime}
and
V(e_{2})\subseteq V(e_{2}^{\prime})
.
We have that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is obtained by removing
v_{1}
(
V_{1}=V\setminus\{v_{1}\}
,
E_{1}=E

and
I_{1}=\{(v,e)\in I\colon v\neq v_{1}\}
)
and that
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is obtained by removing
e_{2}^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{3}=(V_{3},E_{3},I_{3})
be the hypergraph obtained from
\mathcal{H}
in which
we removed
v_{1}
and
e_{2}^{\prime}
: formally,

V_{3}\coloneq V\setminus\{v_{1}\}
,
E_{3}\coloneq E\setminus\{e_{2}^{\prime}\}
, and

I_{3}\coloneq\{(v,e)\in I\colon e\neq e_{2}^{\prime}\text{~and~}v\neq v_{1}\}
.
We need to show that
\mathcal{H}_{1}\to_{\text{edge}}\mathcal{H}_{3}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}_{3}
.

In
\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
in all cases if we removed
e_{2}^{\prime}
from
E(v_{1})
then we also erased it from
E(v_{1}^{\prime})
.
More formally, since
E(v_{1})\subseteq E(v_{1}^{\prime})
, and since we have
E_{2}(v_{1})=E(v_{1})\setminus\{e_{2}^{\prime}\}
and
the same for
E_{2}(v_{1}^{\prime})
, then we have
E_{2}(v_{1})\subseteq E_{2}(v_{1}^{\prime})
.
This means we can apply the node-domination rule to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
on
v_{1}
and

v_{1}^{\prime}
and obtain
\mathcal{H}_{3}
, hence
\mathcal{H}_{2}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}_{3}
.

Likewise, in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, the situation is analogous up to exchanging the roles
of nodes and edges: if we removed
v_{1}^{\prime}
from
E(e_{2})
then we also erased it
from
E(e_{2}^{\prime})
.
Hence
\mathcal{H}_{1}\to_{\text{edge}}\mathcal{H}_{3}
.

(iii)

If
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{edge}}\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}_{2}
:

As we can exchange
the role of
[\mathcal{H}_{1}]_{\equiv}
and
[\mathcal{H}_{2}]_{\equiv}
without loss of generality, this case
is symmetric to

Item

(ii)
.

(iv)

Else
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}\to_{\text{node}}\mathcal{H}_{2}
:

This case is analogous to
Item

(i)
but exchanging
the roles of nodes and of edges, considering the node-domination rule
instead of the edge-domination rule, and reversing all inclusion
relationships on sets of incident objects.

In all cases, the reasoning is independent of the representatives. We conclude that

[\mathcal{H}_{1}]_{\equiv}\to^{*}[\mathcal{H}_{3}]_{\equiv}
and
[\mathcal{H}_{2}]_{\equiv}\to^{*}[\mathcal{H}_{3}]_{\equiv}
.
∎

,
