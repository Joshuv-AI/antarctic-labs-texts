A Modified Suspension-Balance Model for Deformable Particle Suspensions: Application to Blood Flows with Cell-Free Layer
Hugo A Castillo-Sánchez, Weston Ortiz, Richard Martin, Rukiye Tuna, Rekha R Rao, Z Leonardo Liu

Abstract
We propose a modified suspension balance model (SBM) for the flow of red blood cells (RBCs) and other deformable particle suspensions in confined geometries. Specifically, the method includes the hydrodynamic lift force generated by deformable particles interacting with walls leading to a cell-free layer. The lift force is added to the SBM to drive RBCs migrating away from the wall. Using the modified SBM (MSBM), we simulate blood flows through microvascular channels and tubes. The method is able to capture the transient development of the cell-free layer (CFL) and the corresponding hematocrit and velocity profiles with the development of the CFL. The CFL thickness and hemorheological hallmarks in microcirculation, such as the Fåhræus Effect and the Fåhræus-Linqvist Effect, are captured and in good agreement with existing experimental and direct numerical results of blood flows. This work establishes a novel continuum computational framework that can efficiently capture the microstructural heterogeneity and non-Newtonian flow behavior of concentrated deformable particle suspensions under confinement. Keywords: Red blood cells; suspension balance model; lift force



1
Introduction

Suspension flows of non-Brownian or non-colloidal particles have incited much interest in the past decades.
Suspensions arise in many applications, including blood flow, food processing, pharmaceuticals, and detergents
[
51
,
15
,
23
]
. In non-colloidal suspension flows, the radii of the suspended particles are typically
larger than one micron and the effect of thermal fluctuation is negligible. Even without colloidal effects, these flows can be difficult to model for a number of reasons including inherent nonlinearity, multi-body interactions, particle polydispersity, fluid-particle interactions, and non-Newtonian suspending fluids
[
40
,
60
,
14
]
.

In concentrated particle suspensions, one unique rheological aspect lies in the presence of particle stress in addition to the fluid stress generated by the disturbance and interactions of suspended particles, as elegantly conceptualized and deduced by Batchelor
[
6
]
. The presence of particle stress can lead to particle cross-stream migration, where particles in suspension irreversibly migrate across the streamlines to preferred locations determined by inertia or gradients in viscosity, concentration, and shear rate
[
26
,
27
]
. For example, for particle suspension flow through a channel, there is a cross-channel migration of particles from the high-shear regions near the walls to low-shear-rate regions near the centerline. The particles also move from high viscosity and high concentration regions to low viscosity and low concentration ones.

Blood is a biological non-colloidal suspension of deformable cells, and the interactions between the suspended red blood cells can give rise to shear-thinning behavior as well as other interesting rheological phenomena, including thixotropy, viscoelasticity and non-zero yield stress
[
12
,
61
,
34
,
18
]
. It is well known that blood flow in vessels, especially in microcirculation, exhibits a cell-free layer (CFL), where RBCs migrate away from the wall and deplete the RBC concentration (hematocrit) near the wall
[
58
]
. The formation of CFL is physiologically essential to maintain a relatively low apparent viscosity
[
46
]
and to promote platelet and leukocite margination needed for healthy hemostasis
[
1
,
25
,
24
]
. Previous studies
[
1
,
13
,
47
,
32
,
28
,
4
,
55
]
have confirmed the formation of CFL in vitro and in silico. This particle migration phenomenon differs from the shear-induced migration generally encountered in rigid particle suspensions in Poiseuille-type flows
[
11
]
, as it is specific to deformable particles and no CFL would be observed in the rigid particle scenarios. Mechanistically, the RBC migration against the wall is driven by a deformability-induced hydrodynamic lift force induced hydrodynamically from the particle-wall interactions
[
2
]
. During this process, the cell deformability and the RBC tank-treading dynamics play important roles in generating and controlling the lift force and consequently the CFL formation
[
53
]
.

To properly model hemodynamics and hemorheology in microcirculation, the lift force-induced migration of RBCs needs to be considered to capture the CFL properly. Cellular-scale direct computational models have been able to directly capture the RBC migration, forming physiologically consistent CFL phenomenon
[
32
,
17
,
57
]
. However, direct computational models can be computationally prohibitive for vascular network or organ-scale simulations. Continuum scale models provide an alternative that can be feasibly extended to at-scale applications
[
49
,
47
,
48
,
20
]
. Qin and Shaqfeh
[
48
]
presented a continuum model to simulate the time evolution of RBCs and platelet concentration distributions in pressure-driven flow. Their model considers deformability-induced lift velocity and shear-induced diffusion as governing mechanisms for the cross-flow transport of RBCs.
Karmakar
et. al.

[
20
]
presented a homogenized two-phase computational framework for blood flow simulations to predict RBC velocity, spatial distribution, and cell-free layer thickness, where a wall-induced lift force expression for RBCs as a function of hematocrit is introduced.

Rooted in the rigorous particle stress framework established by Batchelor
[
6
]
, the suspension balance model (SBM) has been successfully developed and applied to model a variety of flows for rigid particle suspension, including channel flows
[
36
,
39
]
, pipe flows
[
36
]
, and contraction flows
[
36
,
37
]
. Morris and Boulay
[
38
]
modeled curvilinear flows, including flows in a wide-gap Couette, between parallel circular plates, and in a cone-and-plate device. The SBM first proposed by Nott and Brady
[
41
]
postulates that particle migration can be modeled by accounting for the particles stresses induced by the particle-particle hydrodynamic interactions at high concentration. In this work, we propose a modified suspension balance model (MSBM) that incorporates an additional lift forcing term to to the conventional SBM method to account for the wall-RBC hydrodynamics interactions near the wall.

The paper is organized as follows. A detailed derivation of the model is presented in section
2.2
, where we discuss the lift force term that will be incorporated into the conventional SBM. In section
2.3
, we introduce the numerical solvers we use to simulate blood flows using the MBSM. In section
3.1
, we present blood flow results in channel flows, where the effect of different model parameters on hematocrit, velocity distribution and CFL thickness are presented. The temporal evolution of the hematocrit and velocity profiles is shown in section
3.1.2
, where we also directly compare our continuum results with those predicted by discrete simulations using our multiscale solver. The effect of the bulk hematocrit on the flow, along with a direct comparison between our numerical results and experimental data reported in the literature, is presented in section
3.1.4
. In section
3.2
, we study tubular blood flows, where we compare the predictions from the MBSM with results from cellular blood flow simulations and we show that the MSBM is able to capture the Fåhræus effect. Concluding remarks are given in section
4
.

2
Methodology

2.1
Conventional Suspension Balance Model

The suspension balance model (SBM) is derived by considering the conservation of mass
and momentum both for the suspension bulk and for the particle phases separately
[
41
]
.
The mass and momentum balance equations for the suspension bulk in terms of averaged quantities can be obtained as

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{u}\rangle=0

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\langle\rho\bm{u}\rangle}{\partial t}+\langle\bm{u}\rangle\cdot\nabla\langle\rho\bm{u}\rangle=\langle\bm{b}\rangle+\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle\mbox{ ,}

(2)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes a suspension volume average,
\bm{u}
is the suspension velocity,

\rho
is the suspension density, and
\bm{b}
is the body force. The suspension stress,

\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle
, comprises the contributions from both the fluid and particle phases

\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle=-\langle p\rangle_{f}\bm{I}+2\mu_{f}\langle\bm{e}\rangle+\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}\mbox{ ,}

(3)

where
\langle p\rangle_{f}
is the averaged pressure in the fluid phase,

\mu_{f}
is the dynamic viscosity of the suspending fluid, and

\langle\bm{e}\rangle=\left(\nabla\langle\bm{u}\rangle+\nabla\langle\bm{u}\rangle^{T}\right)/2
is the
bulk rate of strain tensor. The stress due to the presence of the particle phase,
\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}
,
will be discussed below. Note that
\langle\cdot\rangle_{f}
is a fluid-phase average and

\langle\cdot\rangle_{p}
is a particle-phase average.

Since we consider suspensions with neutrally buoyant effects and vanishing inertial effects,
the governing equations (
1
) and (
2
) can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{u}\rangle=0\mbox{ ,}

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle=0\mbox{ .}

(5)

The mass and momentum balance equations for the particle phase in terms of the volume fraction can be obtained as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}\phi\right)=0\mbox{ ,}

(6)

\displaystyle\rho_{p}\left[\phi\frac{\partial\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}}{\partial t}+\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}\cdot\nabla\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}\right]=\langle\bm{b}\rangle_{p}+\langle\bm{F}^{H}\rangle_{p}\mbox{ ,}

(7)

where
\phi
is the particle volume concentration,
\rho_{p}
is the particle density,

\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}
is the particle-phase average velocity, and
\langle\bm{b}\rangle_{p}
is the particle-phase body force that is set to zero in the current study. The term
\langle\bm{F}^{H}\rangle_{p}
is the total hydrodynamic force that captures the inter-phase momentum transfer.

Similarly, the particle-phase inertial and body force effects can be assumed to be vanishing, which yields a simplified particle-phase momentum balance equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}\phi\right)=0\mbox{ ,}

(8)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{F}^{H}\rangle_{p}=0\mbox{ .}

(9)

Equation (
8
) can be written as

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\langle\bm{u}\rangle\cdot\nabla\phi=-\nabla\cdot\bm{j}_{\perp}\mbox{ ,}

(10)

where
\bm{j}_{\perp}=\phi(\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}-\langle\bm{u}\rangle)
is
the particle migration flux relative to the bulk motion
[
36
]
.
To close this system, a constitutive relationship for the inter-phase hydrodynamic force is needed, which is the key idea of the SBM method. Nott
et. al.

[
42
]
decompose
\langle\bm{F}^{H}\rangle_{p}
into two
components, including a sedimentation hindrance drag,
\langle\bm{F}^{d}\rangle_{p}
, and a divergence of a particle-phase stress,

\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}
, that is

\langle\bm{F}^{H}\rangle_{p}\approx\langle\bm{F}^{d}\rangle_{p}+\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}\mbox{ ,}

(11)

where
a
is the particle radius.
The term
\langle\bm{F}^{d}\rangle_{p}
can be linearly related to the difference between
\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}

and
\langle\bm{u}\rangle
by setting

\langle\bm{F}^{d}\rangle_{p}=-\frac{9\mu_{f}}{2a^{2}}\phi\left[f(\phi)\right]^{-1}\left[\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}-\langle\bm{u}\rangle\right]\mbox{ .}

(12)

Here,
f(\phi)
is the hindered settling function in the form of Miller and Morris
[
36
]
given by

f\left(\phi\right)=\left(1-\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)\left(1-\phi\right)^{\alpha-1}\mbox{ ,}

(13)

where the parameter
\alpha
has values in the range
2\leq\alpha\leq 4
.
Substituting Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
) into Eq. (
9
) yields

-\frac{9\mu_{f}}{2a^{2}}\phi\left[f(\phi)\right]^{-1}\left[\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}-\langle\bm{u}\rangle\right]+\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}=0\mbox{ ,}

(14)

from which
\bm{j}_{\perp}
can be evaluated as:

\bm{j}_{\perp}=\frac{2a^{2}}{9\mu_{f}}f(\phi)\left[\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}\right]\mbox{ .}

(15)

The particle-phase stress can be expressed in terms of a concentration-dependent viscosity
function, a flow-aligned tensor
\bm{Q}
, and the average rate of strain
[
38
]
, as follows:

\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}=-\mu_{f}\mu_{n}(\phi)\bm{Q}\big(\dot{\gamma}+\dot{\gamma}_{NL}\big)+2\mu_{f}\left(\mu_{r}(\phi)-1\right)\langle\bm{e}\rangle\mbox{ ,}

(16)

where
\mu_{n}(\phi)
is a normal viscosity given by Morris and Boulay
[
38
]
,

\mu_{n}(\phi)=K_{n}\left(\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)^{-2}\mbox{ ,}

(17)

with
K_{n}=0.75
. The flow-aligned tensor
\bm{Q}
captures the anisotropy of the normal stresses
[
36
]
with the form:

\bm{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&0&\lambda_{3}\end{bmatrix}\mbox{.}

(18)

The directions of
\bm{Q}
correspond to the principal directions of viscometric shear flow, where
1
,
2
and
3
denote flow, gradient and vorticity directions, respectively. The diagonal elements of
\bm{Q}
take the following values:
\lambda_{1}=1
,
\lambda_{2}\approx 0.8
and
\lambda_{3}\approx 0.5
, which provide reasonably good agreement with concentrated suspension rheology
[
65
]
and with observed migration behavior in viscometric flows
[
43
]
. The relative viscosity
\mu_{r}(\phi)
is modeled by Krieger’s correlation

\mu_{r}\left(\phi\right)=\left(1-\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)^{-2}\mbox{ ,}

(19)

where
\phi_{m}
is the maximum packing concentration. The shear rate
\dot{\gamma}

is given by

\dot{\gamma}=\sqrt{2\langle\bm{e}\rangle:\langle\bm{e}\rangle}\mbox{ .}

(20)

As stated by Dbouk
et al.

[
11
]
, there might exist some regions where the flow experiences a zero shear rate (for instance, the centre-line of a channel), and in these scenarios, the model will predict concentration profiles with a cusp representing a singularity at
\phi=\phi_{m}
. This singularity, which is also observed in equivalent models (Qi
et al.

[
47
]
), is located in a very narrow zone whose size is of the order of magnitude of the particle size
a
. The simplest way to reduce this singularity in these zero shear-rate regions is to add a non-local shear rate
\dot{\gamma}_{NL}
to the local shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
(see
[
35
,
47
]
), as shown in equation (
16
), where
\dot{\gamma}_{NL}
can be defined as:

\dot{\gamma}_{NL}=a_{s}\,\frac{U_{0}}{H}.

(21)

Here,
U_{0}
is a characteristic velocity (i.e. the center-line velocity in a channel),
H
is a characteristic length (i.e. channel height) and
a_{s}
is a parameter equal to
0
,
\epsilon
or
\epsilon^{2}
, where
\epsilon
is a dimensionless length that take the form
\epsilon=a/H
in the present work.

The SBM consists of solving
Eqs. (
4
), (
5
), and (
10
), with
supporting Eqs. (
3
) and (
15
)–(
19
). It is also noteworthy that Clausen
[
9
]
points out that in order to form consistent SBM
formulation only the shear-induced normal stress term,
\langle\bm{\Sigma}^{NS}\rangle_{p}
,
should be used in equation (
11
) in place of the complete
particle stress term,
\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}
. This is subject to further discussion.

2.2
Modified Suspension Balance model including Lift Force

We now modify the suspension balance model for red blood cells (or more generally, suspensions of deformable capsules, bubbles, or vesicles). For blood flow, RBCs form a cell-free layer near the wall, and this is caused by a wall effect (confinement) where a lift force arises pushing the particles normal to the
wall. This is caused by the particle deformability. To account for this RBC deformability-induced lift force,
in addition to the Stokesian-type drag force, equation (
11
) needs to be augmented as

\langle\bm{F}^{H}_{A}\rangle_{p}\approx\langle\bm{F}^{d}\rangle_{p}+\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}+\phi\bm{L}_{\perp}\mbox{ ,}

(22)

where the volume averaged lift forcing term
\bm{L}_{\perp}
can be approximated based on the direct measurement of the lift force exerted on deformable vesicles/capsules. Abkarian
et al.

[
2
]
studied the behavior of vesicles in a shear flow close to a wall, and their experimental observations allowed them to determine a viscous lift force
F_{l}
of the form
F_{l}=\mu_{f}\,\dot{\gamma}\,(a^{3}/h)f(1-\nu)
, where
\mu_{f}
is the external fluid viscosity,
a
is the radius of the vesicle,
h
is its distance from the substrate (
h=R-r
), and
f(1-\nu)
is a monotonically decreasing function of the reduced volume
\nu
.

Other experimental and theoretical works have also studied the phenomenon of non-inertial lateral migration of vesicles
[
10
]
and red blood cells
[
47
,
48
]
in bounded Poiseuille flow, and they have postulated a lateral migration lift velocity,
u_{lift}
, that takes the following dimensionless form
u_{lift}=\xi\dot{\gamma}(r)/h^{\beta}
. Using a cylindrical coordinate system, where
z
is the flow direction, and
r
is the gradient direction, a fluid is moving with velocity
u_{z}
in a 2D channel with
L\times R
dimensions creating a lift velocity in the r-direction normal to the wall (
L
being the length and
R
the radius of the channel). For this case, this law illustrates that the migration of particles away from the wall depends on the local shear rate
\dot{\gamma}(r)
, on the particle-wall distance
y
, and on two fitting parameters
\xi
and
\beta
. This equation implies that as particles move away from the wall, the migration velocity will decrease to zero as it approaches the centerline (located at
0
). The migration is amplified if both parameters
\xi
and
\beta
increase their values.

Combining the lift models developed by Abkarian
[
2
]
and Coupier
[
10
]
, we have derived a lift force term
\bm{L}_{\perp}
that is volume averaged in order to be incorporated into the suspension balance model and takes the following form:

\bm{L}_{\perp}=\frac{3\,\mu_{f}\,\dot{\gamma}}{4\,\pi\,(h+h_{0})^{\beta}}\,f(1-\nu)\,\bm{i}_{\perp}\mbox{ ,}

(23)

where
h
is the normal distance from the wall,
h_{0}
is a geometric factor that accounts for the finite-size effects of RBCs and can also numerically avoid division by zero,
\beta
is a power-law coefficient,
f(1-\nu)
is a function of the reduced volume
\nu
, and
\bm{i}_{\perp}
is a unit vector normal pointing away from the wall. For RBCs, a value of
f(1-\nu)=1.2

can be used when the values of
\beta
are close to the unit
[
2
]
. It is also worth noting that the function
f(1-\nu)
is dimensionless if
\beta=1
, but when
\beta\neq 1
,
f(1-\nu)
will have the corresponding units that will result in consistent units for the lift force
\bm{L}_{\perp}
.

Therefore, we have obtained the modified particle-phase governing equations using the modified SBM formulation (
22
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\langle\bm{u}\rangle\cdot\nabla\phi=-\nabla\cdot\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}\mbox{ ,}

(24)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{F}^{d}\rangle_{p}+\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}+\phi\bm{L}_{\perp}=0\mbox{ ,}

(25)

where
\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}
is the modified particle migration flux for RBCs.
Substituting Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
25
) results in

-\frac{9\mu_{f}}{2a^{2}}\phi\left[f(\phi)\right]^{-1}\left[\langle\bm{u}\rangle_{p}-\langle\bm{u}\rangle\right]+\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}+\phi\bm{L}_{\perp}=0\mbox{ .}

(26)

The modified particle migration flux can then be evaluated as

\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}=\frac{2a^{2}}{9\mu_{f}}f(\phi)\left[\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}+\phi\bm{L}_{\perp}\right]\mbox{ .}

(27)

where
\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}
and
\bm{L}_{\perp}

are determined by Eqns. (
16
) and
(
23
), respectively. In summary, the system of equations that define our constitutive model are shown below:

\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{u}\rangle=0\mbox{ ,}\qquad\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle=0\mbox{,}

(28)

\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle=-\langle p\rangle_{f}\bm{I}+2\mu_{f}\langle\bm{e}\rangle+\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}\mbox{ ,}

(29)

\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}=-\mu_{f}\mu_{n}(\phi)\bm{Q}\big(\dot{\gamma}+\dot{\gamma}_{NL}\big)+2\mu_{f}\left(\mu_{r}(\phi)-1\right)\langle\bm{e}\rangle\mbox{ ,}

(30)

\bm{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0.8&0\\
0&0&0.5\end{bmatrix}

(31)

\mu_{n}(\phi)=K_{n}\left(\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)^{-2}\qquad\mu_{r}\left(\phi\right)=\left(1-\frac{\phi}{\phi_{m}}\right)^{-2}\mbox{ .}

(32)

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\langle\bm{u}\rangle\cdot\nabla\phi=-\nabla\cdot\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}\mbox{ ,}\qquad\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}=\frac{2a^{2}}{9\mu_{f}}f(\phi)\left[\nabla\cdot\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle_{p}+\phi\bm{L}_{\perp}\right]\mbox{ .}

(33)

\bm{L}_{\perp}=\frac{3\,\mu_{f}\,\dot{\gamma}}{4\,\pi\,(h+h_{0})^{\beta}}\,f(1-\nu)\,\bm{i}_{\perp}\mbox{ ,}

2.4
Multiscale blood flow solver

To verify our MSBM capability for modelling microcirculatory blood flows, we employ a direct numerical simulation solver of blood flows as described in our previous studies
[
32
,
29
,
28
,
30
]
. The fluid phase is modeled using the three-dimensional lattice Boltzmann (LB) method
[
3
]
. The RBC is modelled as a coarse-grained deformable capsule that adopts a biconcave discoidal shape under stress-free equilibrium, as outlined by Fedosov
et al.

[
13
]
and Pivkin and Karniadakis
[
44
]
. The membrane nonlinear mechanics capture the RBC membrane viscoelasticity with physiological RBC shear elasticity, bending stiffness, and membrane viscosity
[
30
]
. The RBC has a maximum diameter of
8\,\mu
m, and a membrane shear modulus (G) of
0.0063
dyne cm
-1
. The internal viscosity of RBC is set to five times that of plasma (
1.2
cP). For channel flow, RBCs are immersed within a three-dimensional computational domain. Periodic boundary conditions (unbounded) in the
x
and
z
directions and no-slip boundary conditions in the
y
direction are implemented. For tubular flows, periodic boundary condition in
x
direction and no-slip boundary conditon on the tubular shell are used. Pressure gradients are utilized to drive the flow.

Here we describe the planar channel-flow geometry that is used to simulate the pressure-driven flow of blood in a channel of rectangular cross-section (see figure

x

y
is the gradient direction and

To initialize the simulation, at
t=0
, the field velocity is zero (
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
), the stress tensors are the zero tensor (i.e.
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
), the pressure is zero and the volume fraction in the whole channel is equal to the bulk volume fraction (or the hematocrit)
\phi_{b}

At the inlet, we set a velocity profile

that will adopt a parabolic shape of the form
u_{x}(y)=U_{0}\,(H-y)\,y/(H/2)^{2}
; for the volume fraction, we impose the value of the hematocrit (

), and zero gradient boundary conditions for both the pressure and the flux vector
\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}
. At the walls (

\bm{j}_{\perp}^{\,\prime}

Finally, at the outlet, we set fully developed boundary conditions (i.e. zero value for pressure and zero-gradient boundary conditions for the velocity field and for the volume fraction).

Co=\Delta t\,U_{0}/\Delta x\ll 1
, where
\Delta x
is the value of the cell size) is always satisfied.

To satisfy the zero-inertia conditions seen in blood flows, a particle Reynolds number
Re_{p}
and a global Reynolds number for channel flow were defined following Dbouk
et al.

[
11
]
, which are
Re_{p}=4/3\,\rho\,a^{3}\,U_{0}/\big[\eta\,(H/2)^{2}\big]
and
Re=\rho\,U_{0}\,H/\eta
, where
\eta=\mu_{f}\,\mu_{r}(\phi)
. As these Reynolds numbers are typically much smaller than one for microcirculatory flows, we reasonably neglect the inertia.

The aspect ratio
L/H
of the channel has to be carefully chosen to ensure that the flow profiles from our simulations are fully developed. Nott and Brady
[
41
]
derived an equation that guarantees this condition provided that:

\Big(\frac{L}{H}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{12g(\phi)}\Big(\frac{H}{a}\Big)^{2}\qquad g(\phi)=\frac{1}{3}\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\exp{(8.8\phi)}\Big).

(35)

The velocity and other flow quantities will depend on the coordinate
y\in[0,H]
(
H
being the channel-height) and since we will be integrating the governing equations from
y=0
to
y=H
and along the
x
-direction, we consider the effect of the two-walls on the blood flow. Thus, the lift term becomes:

\bm{L}_{\perp}=\frac{3\,\mu_{f}\,\dot{\gamma}}{4\,\pi\,(y+h_{0})^{\beta}}\,f(1-\nu)\,(0,1,0)+\frac{3\,\mu_{f}\,\dot{\gamma}}{4\,\pi\,(H-y+h_{0})^{\beta}}\,f(1-\nu)\,(0,-1,0)\mbox{ .}

It is also important to point out that the average hematocrit
\hat{\phi}
(computed as
\hat{\phi}=\int_{0}^{H}\big[\phi(y)\big]\,dy/H
) is smaller compared to the bulk hematocrit value
\phi_{b}
imposed at the inlet, and this is more evident when comparing the hematocrit profiles when

\hat{\phi}
. Miller
et al.

[
36
]
explained this as the need to maintain a constant flux of particles at any axial station. However, it is noteworthy that this phenomenon is well known as the Fåhræus Effect in the blood flow and hematology community
[
45
]
, which describes that the discharged hematocrit (bulk hematocrit) is greater than the tube hematocrit (average hematocrit) due to the faster-moving red blood cells compared to the slower-moving plasma while one withdrawing blood from the vessel (more discussed in §
3.2.1
). In fact, from mass conservation for the RBC phase, a velocity weighted “average” hematocrit (or equivalently the discharge hematocrit)
\hat{\phi_{b}}
can be computed as
\hat{\phi_{b}}=\int_{0}^{H}\big[\phi\,U_{x}(y)\big]\,dy/\int_{0}^{H}U_{x}(y)\,dy
. It is confirmed in our simulation that
\hat{\phi_{b}}\approx\phi_{b}
always holds with a 3% variance at any axial location and any time point of the simulation, despite
\hat{\phi}
varying in response to the evolution of velocity profiles.

As the flow evolves, we start to observe the particle migration towards the centre, where a peak starts to appear around

3.2.1
Fåhræus Effect

To test the physiological relevance of our model, we further tested our prediction against well-known hemorheological principles such as the
Fåhræus effect
, which describes the discharge hematocrit exceeding the (average) tube hematocrit due to RBC-laden region discharges faster than the average outflow plasma.
Quantitatively, Pries
et al.

[
45

developed an empirical correlation to quantify the ratio of the tube hematocrit

\mu

\frac{H_{t}}{H_{d}}=H_{d}+(1-H_{d})\big[1+1.7\exp(-0.415\,D)-0.6\exp(-0.011\,D)\big].

(37)

3.2.2
Fåhræus-Linqvist Effect

The Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect describes how the apparent viscosity of blood decreases when it flows through smaller-diameter vessels, particularly those between about 10 µm and 300 µm in diameter (the scale of arterioles and capillaries)
[
56
]
. To test the physiological relevance of our model, we also study the blood viscosity dependence on the tube diameter. We carried out simulations for different radii
R=D/2
using the same MSBM parameters described above. Once steady state is reached, we obtain the pressure gradient
\Delta p/L
and the volumetric flow
Q=\int_{0}^{R}\big[2\pi r\,U_{x}(r)\big]\,dr
to compute the blood apparent viscosity
\eta_{b}
using the Hagen-Poiseuille equation:

\eta_{b}=\frac{\Delta p\,\pi\,R^{4}}{8\,L\,Q}\qquad\eta_{r}=\frac{\eta_{b}}{\eta_{f}},

(38)

Once
\eta_{b}
is obtained, we can compute the apparent relative viscosity of blood
\eta_{r}
, which is defined as the ratio between
\eta_{b}
and the plasma viscosity
\eta_{0}
.
We compare our predicted apparent viscosity with various sources of experimental results
[
56
,
5
,
54
]
shown in figure
13
. It can be shown that our continuum model results overall agree well with the experimental observations due to the well capture of the cell free layer. The slightly weaker dependence of the apparent viscosity on the change of diameter may be due to the lack of shear-thinning behavior in the viscosity model.

4
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have demonstrated that the conventional suspension balance model can be modified to model deformable particle suspension physics under confinement through the incorporation of a lift force to account for the force resulting from the particle deformability pushing the particle away from the wall. The modified SBM was applied to simulate physiological blood flows in straight channels and tubes. Our model can also reproduce the plug-flow velocity profiles characteristic of non-Newtonian blood flows. Moreover, the hematocrit distribution in these geometries predicted using our new model predicts the cell-free layer thickness quantitatively matching those reported in experiments.

We also studied the effect of the bulk hematocrit
\phi_{b}
and the channel height on the CFL thickness
\delta_{CFL}
; for the hematocrit, an increase in
\phi_{b}
will lead to a decrease of the
\delta_{CFL}
. For the channel height, higher values of
\delta_{CFL}
will be observed for taller channels. These results are in excellent agreement with experimental
[
55
]
and theoretical
[
59
]
data reported in literature. Both the channel and tube flow simulations are also consistent with predictions made by similar models (Qin and Shaqfeh theory
[
48
]
to describe the time evolution of RBCs concentration distributions in pressure-driven flow through straight channels) and other methodologies, such as the discrete red blood cell suspension simulations
[
31
]
. We have also shown that the exponent
\beta
controls the cell-free boundary layer thickness. We have shown that a value of
\beta=1.2
is an optimal value to reproduce the experimental behavior observed in blood flows in microvessels, although values of
\beta
within the range between
1.1
and
1.2
can also be used, especially when dealing with much more smaller microvessels heights/diameters. In addition, although the MSBM model can capture both the Fåhræus and the Fåhræus-Linqvist effects in tubular flows, the next step to improve the prediction capabilities of the modified SBM is to incorporate shear-rate time dependent viscosities to have a better match with experimental results.

Overall, this work shows that continuum models can be properly designed to capture particle-level details for blood flow and particle suspension flows to potentially enable efficient, at-scale biomedical and industrial applications.
