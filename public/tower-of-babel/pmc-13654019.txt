Managing growth and puberty concerns in individuals with Duchenne muscular dystrophy: Updated guidance and future directions
Authors: Meilan M Rutter, Janet L Crane, Funmbi Babalola, Robert W Benjamin, Carol Lam, Laura McAdam, Stefan Nicolau, Anne Marie Sbrocchi, Mena Scavina, Julia Sorbara, Alexandra Ahmet, Susan Apkon, Chiara Arpaia, Tommaso Aversa, Shipra Bansal, Luca Bello, Diana X Bharucha-Goebel, Sabrina Corbetta, Natascia Di Iorgi, Ryan Farrell, Alberto Ferlin, Danilo Fintini, Melissa Fiscaletti, Rana Halloun, Maria-Elena Lautatzis, Hugh J McMillan, Nadia Merchant, Patrick Moeschen, Nat Nasomyont, Maria Fernanda Ochoa Molina, Chiara Panicucci, Nora E Renthal, Luisa De Sanctis, Jaclyn Tamaroff, Cuixia Tian, Aravindhan Veerapandiyan, Leanne M Ward, David R Weber, Sze Choong Wong, Ilaria Zito

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term glucocorticoid therapy is standard of care for individuals with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) but often results in impaired growth and delayed or incomplete puberty, impacting their physical and psychosocial health. Although DMD endocrine care has evolved, leading to inclusion in the 2018 Care Considerations, these issues remain prevalent and concerning, and gaps in care persist. The Growth and Puberty Working Group of the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium, an international multidisciplinary group including endocrinology and neuromuscular specialists with DMD expertise, conducted a literature review and surveyed clinicians to inform updated clinical guidance on growth and puberty. This report is based on evidence where available, plus expert consensus, and thereby provides revised guidance on the investigation and management of growth and pubertal delay, as well as hypogonadism beyond the pubertal years, among individuals with DMD. It also addresses new topics subsequent to the 2018 Care Considerations, including: 1) the dissociative glucocorticoid vamorolone and its impact on growth, and 2) gonadal function in older adolescents and young adults. With the current lack of long-term data on growth and puberty outcomes, prevailing themes underscore the importance of person- and family-centered care with informed shared decision-making aligned with individualized goals plus interdisciplinary team communication. This report also highlights specific areas in these domains in need of research. Through ongoing collaborations within the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium, growth and puberty care continues to evolve and will inform and advance research to improve the health and quality of life of individuals with DMD.Keywords: bone, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, endocrine, fertility, growth, hypogonadism, puberty, steroid, testosterone, vamorolone

Introduction

The “Optimizing Endocrine and Bone Health Management in DMD” (OPTIMIZE DMD) Consortium Growth and Puberty Working Group

Endocrine care of individuals with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) has advanced significantly in the past two decades, from inaugural international workshops around 2010,1,2 to the publication of Care Considerations in 2018 that included endocrine management as a new topic.3,4 Despite this, endocrine issues remain prevalent in individuals with DMD and are a significant concern for many. 5 Gaps in care persist, necessitating critical evaluation and updating of the 2018 Care Considerations. The international “Optimizing Endocrine and Bone Health Management in DMD” (OPTIMIZE DMD) Consortium was therefore formed in 2023 to address these unmet needs through collaborative efforts. 6

Within the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium, the Growth and Puberty Working Group, comprising endocrinology, neuromuscular and pediatric specialists with expertise in DMD and a Parent Project Muscular Dystrophy patient and family advocate, searched the literature for updated evidence to revise guidance for best practice, and summarized issues that lacked evidence and/or consensus as areas for research and future directions. To further improve guidance in areas lacking evidence, the Working Group also surveyed the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium (n=28) and allied Italian clinicians (n=9) to identify areas of consensus and variance around clinical practices for growth and puberty management in individuals with DMD. 7

This report provides an overview and discusses guidance from the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium to advance and standardize clinical care related to growth and puberty in children, adolescents and adults with DMD. Secondarily, areas of need for evidence and future research in these domains were identified.

Impact of glucocorticoids on growth and puberty in individuals with Duchenne muscular dystrophy

Long-term glucocorticoid (GC) therapy remains standard of care for the treatment of individuals with DMD for its known benefits on muscle strength, ambulation, cardiorespiratory function, and scoliosis.8–11 However, the benefits to the muscle come with detrimental effects on the endocrine system, such as impaired growth, delayed or incomplete puberty, excessive weight gain, osteoporosis, and adrenal suppression.1,3

Several retrospective studies have reported that children with DMD not on GC tend to be shorter than their peers.12–17 These findings were corroborated by baseline measurements in a prospective trial of nearly 200 GC-naïve children with DMD between the ages of 4 and 8 years, which showed mean height to be 0.9 (range -3.1 to 2.2) standard deviations (SD) below the mean for age 18 and mean height on the 26th (range 0.1 to 98.5th) percentile. 19 This relative shorter stature is speculated to result from reduced biomechanical loading on the skeleton associated with muscle weakness, as well as potential genetic factors, such as possible association with short stature genes on the X chromosome or specific DMD genotypes.13,14,16,20 Most individuals with DMD not on GC, however, do not have intrinsic hormonal deficiencies that would impair growth. 13

On the other hand, GC regimens used in the management of individuals with DMD frequently lead to growth failure and short stature.8,18,20,21 Growth failure is defined as a linear growth velocity of more than one SD below the mean for age and sex, and may result in short stature, defined as an absolute height of more than 2 SD below the mean. A single-center study of 97 individuals with DMD who had received daily GC for at least 3 years revealed that 72% had short stature, compared with 9% before GC therapy. 22 Growth impairment is greater with daily GC regimens compared to intermittent or no GC therapy,18,20,23,24 and more so with daily deflazacort compared with daily prednisone.8,18 Individuals treated with daily deflazacort have been shown to lose 0.25 SD of height every year relative to the general pediatric population. 20 In a 36-month randomized trial of 196 previously GC-naïve children, daily deflazacort resulted in 8.1 cm of height reduction compared with intermittent prednisone and 2.3 cm of height reduction compared with daily prednisone. 8 The principal mechanism of GC-induced growth failure is the direct local action of GC on growth plate chondrocytes, including apoptosis, impaired proliferation, and disruption of local growth factor signaling.25–28 Glucocorticoids also inhibit growth through central mechanisms involving the hypothalamic-pituitary axis by decreasing growth hormone (GH) and insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) production and action.29–31

Similar to growth in GC-naïve individuals with DMD, the majority do not have inherent hormone deficiencies of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH), glycerol kinase deficiency and adrenal hypoplasia congenita have been reported in a few individuals with DMD, due to contiguous Xp21 deletions encompassing NROB1/DAX-1, but this is extremely rare.32,33 Long-term GC therapy in individuals with DMD, however, frequently causes HH, leading to delayed or absent puberty in younger adolescents, and incomplete or arrested puberty in older adolescents and young adults. Delayed puberty in males is defined as lack of puberty (testicular volumes of <4mL) by age 14 years. 34 Delayed puberty is a common complication of GC treatment in those with DMD; a recent study reported that 82% of 28 boys with DMD treated with daily GC had delayed puberty. 35 Glucocorticoid-related delayed puberty, in turn, is an additional factor that affects linear growth in those with DMD, resulting in a late and attenuated growth spurt around the time of puberty. 36 Long-term GC administration has also been linked to HH in multiple studies of patients with other medical conditions, with a 4-fold increased risk compared to GC-naïve patients. 37 The mechanism for GC-related hypogonadism is not fully elucidated but is thought to be due to GC receptor-mediated effects at all levels of the HPG axis. 38 At the level of the hypothalamus, GC-related effects are thought to be due to inhibition of gonadotropic releasing hormone (GnRH) synthesis and dampened GnRH pulsatility. At the level of the pituitary gland, there is thought to be decreased GnRH receptor expression. 39 Response to provocative stimulation by GnRH analogues is often insufficient in those on daily GC. 35 At the level of the testes, there is reduced stimulation from luteinizing hormone (LH) and decreased expression and function of steroidogenic enzymes amongst other possible effects including impact on the number of LH receptors and Leydig cell apoptosis. Taken together, long term GC exposure appears to impact the HPG axis at all levels, 38 leading to HH in individuals with DMD. Both the GC dose and regimen (daily vs intermittent) may affect the development and degree of HH in individuals with DMD, with pubertal suppression being more likely in those on daily GC regimens. 35

Aside from classic GC, as reviewed above, vamorolone is a novel dissociative GC that became available for treating individuals with DMD from 2023 in several countries.40–42 Through selective modulation of GC receptor activity, 43 it confers benefit for muscle function similar to prednisone, but spares some adverse effects, such as growth failure in the short term.44–46 Its effects on the HPG axis and pubertal timing remain unknown.

Importance of growth and puberty for the physical and psychosocial health of individuals with DMD

Glucocorticoid-related growth impairment and pubertal suppression can impact the physical and psychosocial wellbeing of individuals with DMD. With advances in medical therapies and multidisciplinary care, individuals with DMD are now living into adulthood, making psychosocial concerns related to physical appearance increasingly prominent.

Short stature causes psychological distress in many individuals with DMD, leading some families/caregivers to seek endocrine intervention.47,48 Although short stature itself may not directly impact physical health, lack of growth may indirectly inhibit the potential for recovery or reshaping of vertebrae following compression fractures resulting from GC-induced osteoporosis, a process that requires concurrent linear growth.49,50 Height is also an important factor to consider when evaluating other aspects of DMD health, such as puberty, body composition, bone mineral density (BMD), and cardiopulmonary function.

In healthy individuals undergoing puberty, testosterone promotes the development and maintenance of lean muscle mass as well as bone mass accrual; around half of adult bone mass is accrued during puberty. 51 Furthermore, puberty is a key developmental process during which adolescents cultivate personal and sexual identities, become increasingly independent, strengthen peer relationships, and develop more mature coping and problem-solving skills. 52 Testosterone also drives physical changes, such as the pubertal growth spurt, facial hair development and voice deepening. Acquiring an outward appearance that is congruent with age-matched peers may be particularly important to adolescents with DMD who have significant growth impairment and Cushingoid features related to long-term GC, which can make them look and feel younger. 48 Delayed puberty can thus compound the psychological burden to youth already facing significant physical differences compared with typically developing peers. Aside from physical immaturity, hypogonadism may have detrimental effects on health, including increased risk of fragility fractures and diminished bone mass accrual. 35 Hypogonadism may also cause fatigue, decreased erythropoiesis leading to anemia, muscle loss, and increased fat mass.37,53,54

Addressing concerns around growth, puberty and ongoing hypogonadism should be holistic, considering not only the interactions between growth and puberty, but also how these are integrated into other endocrine (such as bone) and interdisciplinary aspects of health and psychological well-being for those with DMD.

Summary of 2018 previous Care Considerations for growth and puberty concerns

Growth referrals, investigation, and management

The 2018 Care Considerations recommended assessment of height every 6 months until completion of puberty and attainment of adult height. 3 Standing height should be obtained in ambulatory individuals and a surrogate measure obtained in non-ambulatory individuals, the latter preferably starting before loss of ambulation to ensure consistency of measurements. For patients who met criteria for impaired growth, referral to endocrinology was recommended “to identify and diagnose hormone deficiencies […and…] to provide endocrine hormone replacement therapy when indicated”. 3

The 2018 Care Considerations also recommended performing a bone age x-ray and standard laboratory investigations in the initial evaluation of all patients with growth failure. Growth hormone stimulation testing was recommended “on an individual basis only in patients in whom a compelling clinical case for the use of growth hormone exists”. 4

The 2018 Care Considerations iterated that “the treatment of DMD-related short stature is controversial”, given lack of randomized controlled trials to evaluate efficacy and safety of recombinant human GH (rhGH) in DMD, and recommended that the decision to treat be made on a “case-by-case basis with biochemical evidence for GH deficiency, and after discussion of the benefits and known plus unknown risks”.3,4 Anticipatory counseling and shared decision-making around the range of potential options to address growth including GC regimen adjustment were not addressed, and previous guidance predated the advent of the dissociative GC, vamorolone. 43

Delayed puberty referrals, investigation, and management

The 2018 Care Considerations 3 recommended physical assessment of puberty by Tanner staging every 6 months from age 9 years, with prompt referral to an endocrinologist if there was an absence of pubertal development (based on testicular size) by age 14 years.

The 2018 Care Considerations also recommended measuring serum gonadotropin (LH and follicle stimulating hormone [FSH]) and testosterone concentrations to confirm delayed puberty, and considering a bone age x-ray, in individuals referred for this concern.

Testosterone replacement therapy was recommended for treating confirmed hypogonadism in patients aged 14 years and older and could be considered from 12 years of age in those on GC who showed no signs of puberty. Treatment should mimic natural pubertal progression, beginning at a low dose and gradually increasing to adult levels over several years through intramuscular (IM) or topical formulations. 55 During therapy, recommended monitoring included clinical signs of puberty and biochemistry such as testosterone, lipids, hemoglobin, hematocrit and glucose concentrations. It was also underscored that if a patient’s functional status or cardiac function were felt to be negatively impacted by testosterone, that a dose reduction or discontinuation of treatment should be considered.

Hypogonadism referrals, investigation and management

The 2018 Care Considerations addressed the initial treatment of delayed puberty in younger adolescents but made no recommendations regarding the clinical assessment or management of ongoing hypogonadism in older adolescents and young adults.3,4 Herein we offer new guidance for the care of individuals with arrested puberty or ongoing hypogonadism who may have had onset of initial spontaneous puberty that did not progress and/or been previously treated with testosterone to induce puberty.

Monitoring and referrals for growth concerns

Growth should be measured every 6 to 12 months, at the time of clinic visits . This extends the time interval from every 6 months as originally recommended in 2018 to allow flexibility and align with when individuals are seen in clinic, be that 6- or 12-monthly. Standing height should be measured for all individuals for as long as possible using a calibrated stadiometer and standard procedures. 47 Height should be measured, and thus growth velocity determined, until completion of puberty and/or cessation of growth.

A surrogate height measurement by a consistent method should be obtained in all individuals . As the muscle phenotype progresses, measuring standing height will become inaccurate and not feasible. Achilles tendon contractures can lead to over-estimation of standing height, while lumbar lordosis and scoliosis can lead to underestimation, highlighting the importance of surrogate height measurement, even in ambulant individuals. Obtaining surrogate measures of height in ambulant individuals also allows for a consistent method when transitioning to a wheelchair. A reliable, consistent assessment of height is important when evaluating growth velocity and following longitudinal trends, as well as interpreting height-derived indices for clinical care and research, including body mass index (weight [kg]/height [m2]), lean mass index by dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) (lean mass [kg]/height [m2]), 36 body surface area such as for adrenal stress dose calculations, 56 BMD, 57 cardiac imaging,58,59 and pulmonary function tests. 60

Surrogate measures used clinically include ulna length,61,62 arm span, 63 tibial length, knee height, 64 and segmental recumbent length. 65 Each poses certain challenges and considerations, with none having been validated in the DMD population to date, and there is insufficient evidence to recommend which measure should be used. However, while practices vary across the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium, ulna length, which has been validated in healthy children, 61 is currently the most commonly utilized surrogate method, used by 59% of OPTIMIZE-DMD clinicians, 7 and is widely used in clinical DMD trials targeting muscle function. 66 This requires specialized equipment and some training (Supplemental Figure) but is relatively inexpensive, easy, and reproducible. 61 While ulna length provides a consistent method to estimate height longitudinally, there are caveats to consider for individuals with DMD. For example, the Gauld prediction equation likely over-estimates height in children with DMD who have GC-induced short stature and delayed bone age because these equations are derived from healthy growing children and include chronological age as an independent variable. 61 Alternatively, the Rasouli equation uses a simple linear formula that does not require age or gender, but was developed in an intensive care setting and compared against height measured by a paper tape stretched alongside the recumbent patient. 62 It is unclear whether the Rasouli formula is valid in GC-treated children with DMD, who may have skeletal disproportion with more marked reduction of distal long bones. 67 Importantly, a consistent method should be used, with appreciation of potential inherent systematic biases.

Other surrogate measures, such as arm span and segmental recumbent length, may be affected by limb contractures or lumbar lordosis, and are subject to inaccuracy and poor reproducibility in individuals with DMD.61,63 Tibial length and knee height measures have not been validated in GC-treated children with DMD, and height predictions may be affected by skeletal disproportion with relatively shorter leg lengths, possibly resulting from reduced muscular and weight-bearing stimuli and/or GC effect on cortical and trabecular bone. 67 Whole-body imaging by DXA to evaluate bone lengths has been used in a research setting in children with DMD, but the accuracy can be affected by the type of DXA machine67–70 and measurements can be cumbersome and subject to parallax errors due to lumbar lordosis, lower limb contractures, or improper patient positioning, 67 limiting its use clinically.

Endocrine referrals for individuals with impaired growth should be patient-centered, driven by patient and/or parental concerns . There is still a paucity of data on health outcomes related to height or medications to augment height in individuals with DMD. Amongst OPTIMIZE-DMD Consortium clinicians surveyed, the predominant reasons prompting referral were patient and parent concerns, more so than physician concern or routine referral of all patients meeting growth criteria. 7 Regardless of referrals, we recommend endocrine and/or neuromuscular providers proactively engage in anticipatory discussions with patients and families from an early stage, with communication and collaboration across disciplines.

Investigation of growth concerns

It is reasonable to adopt a “due diligence” approach to the routine initial evaluation of individuals with growth failure, if deemed clinically indicated by the endocrinologist. This approach is consistent with guidance from 2018.3,4 A standard workup for growth failure involves screening for malabsorptive causes such as celiac disease, and other chronic conditions including GH deficiency and abnormalities of thyroid function (Figure 1). If the endocrinologist is considering GH deficiency and/or contemplating rhGH therapy, GH provocation testing could be considered on a case-by-case basis.

A baseline bone age x-ray assessment at the initial endocrine evaluation for growth concerns and/or onset of growth deceleration may provide a reference for longitudinal follow-up and can be repeated thereafter as clinically indicated. Skeletal maturation is typically delayed with long-term GC exposure and bone age interpretation can be challenging due to the discordance between carpal and phalangeal maturation, with more pronounced delay in carpal maturation due to the predilection of GC action for trabecular bone. 71 The degree of delay in skeletal maturation may offer additional context when interpreting BMD in individuals with both short stature and pubertal delay, in whom height-standardization alone may be insufficient. 71 However, given the caveats and ongoing impact of GC therapy on growth potential, we suggest against the use of bone age-based height prediction methods in individuals with DMD, even when a bone age is available.

Management of growth concerns

Proactive anticipatory education can help support informed shared decision-making around potential management of GC-related growth impairment. The 2018 Care Considerations recommended continued monitoring of growth as the standard practice for management of growth failure.3,4 Our survey indicated that around half of the multidisciplinary specialists in this field routinely discuss options in addition to continued monitoring to address impaired growth with their patients with DMD, 7 citing concerns expressed by patients and caregivers regarding short stature, suggesting there is a need for shared decision-making discussions to be incorporated into proactive team care. For those who do engage in discussions around addressing growth concerns, potential options include continued monitoring (standard practice) or intervention (on a case-by-case basis). The intervention options to optimize growth uniformly lack long-term controlled studies on safety and efficacy, including neuromuscular outcomes, in individuals with DMD. These options include adjusting the GC regimen, dose, or type, or consideration of rhGH therapy. Shared decision-making discussions should include pros, cons, knowns and unknowns of potential management strategies (Table 2). The individual’s goals, collaboration with the multidisciplinary team, and psychological counseling 72 should all be considered on a case-by-case basis prior to any intervention to address growth concerns.

The optimal GC type and regimen should be determined for each individual. Daily classic GC (prednisone/prednisolone and deflazacort) regimens have been shown to improve motor and pulmonary function but cause more growth deceleration (with daily deflazacort impacting growth more than daily prednisone) as compared with intermittent prednisone/prednisolone (alternating 10 days on and 10 days off).8,20,21,24,73 The intermittent 10 days on and 10 days off prednisone/prednisolone regimen is, however, inferior to daily regimens for motor outcomes.8,24 A 12-month randomized study of 64 ambulant GC-naïve boys treated with twice-weekly prednisone 10 mg/kg/week (divided over two days) versus daily prednisone 0.75 mg/kg/day showed improved linear growth, with equivalent efficacies for the primary motor outcomes of quantitative muscle testing arm and leg scores; longer-term studies are needed to determine efficacy with respect to loss of ambulation and motor and cardiorespiratory function. 74 The dose of daily GC also impacts linear growth, 21 underscoring a need for judicious dosing, weighing efficacy against side effects. Finding the optimal dose may be challenging as no minimum effective dose has been established for classic GC regimens in DMD. Often, daily regimens are chosen, considering that the strongest evidence regarding efficacy on motor outcomes has been collected for these regimens. Prioritizing efficacy above growth impairment, pubertal delay, and other adverse effects is an understandable strategy in a severely disabling disease such as DMD. However, GC treatment should always be tailored on patients’ and families’ needs and expectations, and shared decision-making is paramount. If adjustment of the GC regimen is to be considered, it is typically led by the neuromuscular specialist in collaboration with endocrinology, and the individual and family.

Vamorolone preserves growth velocity in previously GC-naïve children with DMD, based on short-term data. Vamorolone is a novel dissociative GC that was first approved in 2023 by the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency in the European Union, and is indicated for individuals with DMD aged 2 years and above.40–42 It has since been approved in other countries such as the United Kingdom, Canada, Hong Kong, and China and is increasingly being used in clinical practice. By selectively modulating GC receptor activity, 43 it retains the anti-inflammatory benefits of classic GC with the goal of maintaining GC beneficial properties on muscle function, while potentially sparing some adverse effects, including growth failure in the short term. In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of previously GC-naïve pediatric patients with DMD aged 4 to 7 years, vamorolone 6 mg/kg/day for 24 weeks had similar efficacy to prednisone (0.75 mg/kg/day) on motor outcomes, yet caused no linear growth suppression. 44 Similarly, a Phase 2A open-label study of GC-naïve children aged 4 to 7 years with DMD showed that children treated with vamorolone 2 to 6 mg/kg/day for 30 months had increased growth with stable height percentiles compared with historical controls treated with daily or intermittent classic GC who had growth deceleration. 45 While these studies involved GC-naïve patients, the aforementioned randomized trial 44 was followed by a 24-week crossover extension with patients allocated to vamorolone (2 and 6 mg/kg). 46 Normal growth trajectories were observed in children who continued vamorolone and significantly increased growth trajectories were seen in the prednisone-to-vamorolone crossover group. 46 Vamolorone-treated individuals did not show the decline in serum bone turnover markers seen with classic GC therapy, but had similar rates of weight gain and adrenal suppression.44,46,75 Current data are limited regarding the effect of vamorolone on growth in older (7- to <18-year-old) children, but appear to show a similar effect to that seen in younger children, in both those who are GC-naïve and who have received previous GC therapy. 76 Shared decision-making discussions, if starting or transitioning to vamorolone, need to ensure that patient goals are aligned with expectations regarding treatment, as well as highlighting the current absence of knowledge regarding the long-term growth-ameliorating effects, pubertal progression, motor function (including age at loss of ambulation) and cardiac effects (Table 2).

Shared decision-making between individuals, caregivers, endocrinologists, and neuromuscular physicians can help clarify the potential benefits and risks of growth hormone therapy. To date, there remain limited studies, specifically a lack of controlled studies, reporting outcomes in individuals with DMD treated with rhGH. In non-DMD patient populations, rhGH may increase muscle mass and strength, reduce fat mass, and have anabolic effects on bone.77–80 A small number of short-term studies have analyzed clinical outcomes of individuals with DMD who have received medications to augment growth. In a 12-month retrospective study of 39 individuals with DMD and GC-induced growth failure, adding rhGH therapy increased growth, with normalization of growth velocity relative to age and stabilization of pre-treatment decline of height SD scores, with no observed detrimental effects on neuromuscular and cardiopulmonary function. 47 One individual developed transient benign intracranial hypertension and two had insulin resistance, including one with impaired glucose tolerance, on treatment. 47 Other isolated reports have corroborated effects of rhGH on growth without observed detriment on motor function.81,82 Similarly, a 6-month randomized controlled trial of recombinant human insulin-like growth factor-1 (rhIGF-1) therapy (IGF-I being the primary mediator of GH for growth) in GC-treated individuals with DMD showed height gain, while motor function remained stable and lean mass increased, in treated individuals relative to controls. 83 A longitudinal retrospective review of 27 individuals with muscular dystrophy, followed for a median of 28 months, suggested that rhGH may decrease vertebral compression fracture risk and was associated with a greater increase in vertebral body height, 84 indicating the possible potential to facilitate growth-mediated vertebral body remodeling after compression fractures. In summary, in the short term, rhGH therapy in individuals with DMD normalizes growth velocity, stabilizes height SD scores (without catch-up to pre-GC height SD), may confer benefit for bone health, and appears safe.

On the contrary, long-term safety and efficacy of rhGH in individuals with DMD remain unknown. In the absence of information about adult height and given the challenges of using bone age in concert with current height to predict adult height in GC-treated patients, it is difficult to adjudicate the extent to which the improved growth velocity observed in these short-term studies translates into adult height.

Our survey showed that over half of the endocrinologists reported having prescribed rhGH on a case-by-case basis in this context, with variations in practice depending on the country. 7 This suggests that concerns regarding height are significant for some individuals and caregivers, in keeping with a qualitative study citing short stature as the most common concern amongst its adolescent participants, 48 highlighting the importance of shared decision-making discussion of all potential management options on a case-by-case basis. 85 Local drug approval indications and funding may also influence considerations around treatment. Given the lack of high-quality evidence to support a clear recommendation for the routine use of rhGH in individuals with GC-treated DMD, decisions regarding its use should therefore involve detailed discussion of potential benefits, risks, knowns and unknowns 85 with the interdisciplinary care team, especially endocrinology and neuromuscular specialists.

The postulated association between height and motor function in GC-treated individuals with DMD is inconclusive. There is ongoing speculation that short stature may be beneficial for those with DMD, 86 arising from historical reports predating the advent of GC treatment.87–90 These concerns are confounded by the natural history of the disease. Some recent studies have suggested an association between height and motor performance, but are subject to limitations that complicate drawing conclusions about the independent effect of growth on motor outcomes.18,91,92 Notably, longitudinal observations of children with DMD showed that changes in 6-minute walk distance were not correlated with height gain but depended on age-related disease progression.93,94 Glucocorticoid exposure itself (with the possible confounding factor of consequent short stature) is an undisputed prognostic indicator for slowing disease progression in DMD. 95 Overall, there are multiple challenges to studying the independent impact of height on motor function in GC-treated individuals with DMD and it is therefore unclear whether short stature confers a mechanical advantage or simply represents a biomarker of GC response and effect in those with DMD. These discussions continue to be pertinent not only to the considerations involving rhGH, but also the GC regimen, including vamorolone therapy and its desired long-term sparing of growth impairment.

2026 Updated guidance for delayed puberty referrals, investigation, and management

Updated guidance for the management of delayed puberty is summarized in Figure 2 and Table 1.

Monitoring for delayed puberty and management approaches. Monitoring of puberty should begin at 10 years of age with discussions about puberty and anticipatory guidance in those with developmental readiness and interest in the discussion. Physical examination of puberty should begin by 12 years of age. Delayed puberty as defined by lack of testicular enlargement by 14 years of age guides the management approach. Individuals on daily glucocorticoids are at high risk for delayed puberty and should be referred to an endocrinologist by 12 years of age for evaluation and discussions around management. If delayed puberty is confirmed by physical examination and laboratory testing, treatment should be considered using low-dose testosterone with clinical monitoring every 6 months. A shared decision-making approach should be undertaken when initiating and continuing or discontinuing testosterone, taking developmental readiness into account. Endogenous pubertal progression should be monitored clinically or via laboratory evaluation to assess for completion of puberty or ongoing hypogonadism.

Monitoring and referrals for delayed puberty

There should be anticipatory introduction of puberty discussions, including what to expect in individuals with DMD. Considering the high rates of pubertal delay in those treated with daily GC, 35 a proactive approach should be taken. Age- and developmentally appropriate discussions around puberty, the clinical implications of untreated pubertal delay and the potential need for treatment should begin by age 10 years with the caregiver and individual. As different institutions may have different models for DMD care (for instance, some patients may be seen in separate subspeciality clinics whereas others may have integrated interdisciplinary clinics where patients see both the neuromuscular and endocrinology teams together), this anticipatory guidance may occur with the neuromuscular team prior to referral to endocrinology or as a joint discussion. Regardless, having such conversations early on will ensure that, when pubertal examination and treatment are necessary, patients and caregivers may be more receptive to this topic.

Individuals on daily GC should be evaluated by an endocrinologist for assessment and management of delayed puberty by age 12 years. Physical assessment of puberty by Tanner staging with examination of testicular volumes should occur every 6 to 12 months and begin no later than age 12 years. Most (91%) OPTIMIZE-DMD Consortium clinicians reported that pubertal assessments occur after the patient has been referred to endocrinology, with only a few (9%) completing physical assessment of puberty prior to involvement of an endocrinologist. 7 Therefore, we advise timely referral to an endocrinologist (if not already under the care of one) to ensure that puberty is appropriately monitored and treated. In those on daily GC, we recommend referral to endocrinology for pubertal evaluation by age 12 years, given the high incidence of delayed puberty.

For individuals who are GC-naïve or on intermittent GC regimens, referral to endocrinology for pubertal evaluation should occur if puberty is not present by age 14 years, or earlier if there are questions or concerns about puberty. It is currently unknown if vamorolone suppresses puberty; individuals on this therapy should therefore be monitored by an endocrinologist and evaluated for signs of puberty by age 12 years.

Investigation of delayed puberty

Biochemical evaluation of puberty should be undertaken by age 14 years and/or prior to initiating testosterone supplementation. Serum testosterone concentration should be measured prior to testosterone treatment to confirm the diagnosis of delayed puberty (i.e. testicular volumes <4 mL or in those who decline a pubertal examination). We recommend beginning to monitor serum testosterone by age 14 years. Serum testosterone should ideally be measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), in the early morning when levels are at their peak. While LH and FSH are usually suppressed due to GC-mediated suppression of the HPG axis and resultant HH, these markers could be included in the initial evaluation of delayed puberty to definitively rule out an alternative etiology, such as hypergonadotropic hypogonadism due to primary gonadal insufficiency.

Management of delayed puberty

Management of delayed puberty should begin with age-appropriate education about puberty from around age 10 years. Management of delayed puberty should not wait to begin when initiating treatment but should involve early anticipatory conversations with individuals and their families/caregivers that are age- and developmentally appropriate. These conversations can be initiated by either neuromuscular clinicians or endocrinologists. Given the higher frequency of neurodiversity in the DMD community compared to the general population,96–98 early and phased introduction of topics such as puberty is particularly important to give them time to prepare. Topics that should be covered include a general discussion around typical progression of puberty in healthy adolescents, the potential and reason for delayed puberty in individuals with DMD, how puberty is monitored, and how delayed puberty is treated. Psychological counseling could be considered, if indicated. 72

Patient-centered shared decision-making discussions regarding pubertal induction should start around age 12 years in individuals who remain prepubertal. The decision to induce puberty should align with the goals of care for each individual and their caregivers and should be made following informed discussions that not only include the potential benefits and other effects of testosterone therapy, but also the uncertainties that remain 85 (Table 3). The individual’s gender identity and understanding (and comfort with the prospect) of the effects of pubertal hormones should also be considered. 99

Beneficial effects of testosterone therapy include the development of secondary sexual characteristics (increased penile size, which may make toileting easier, and physical maturity in appearance and voice), psychosocial development, bone mineral accrual and possible protection against vertebral fractures, 84 increased linear growth100,101 (although it is important to note that this may be significantly counteracted and blunted by long-term GC use and that the impact on adult height is unknown 36 ), increased red blood cell production,54,102 and a potential effect on body composition and muscle mass 100 (but largely unknown in those with DMD).

There is growing evidence to suggest beneficial effects of testosterone on bone health in the DMD population, and early exploratory data around muscle function. In a longitudinal retrospective review of 27 individuals aged 14 to 16.6 years with severe Becker and DMD on GC, of whom 12 (57%) received monthly IM testosterone injections to induce puberty, combination therapy of testosterone and zoledronic acid resulted in a prolonged time to sustaining a new vertebral fracture compared to zoledronic acid alone. 84 Although the sample size was small, the study focused on detailed analysis of vertebral fracture severity involving more than 400 observations of vertebral height - the spine being a key skeletal site affected by GC-induced osteoporosis - and provides support for a bone-protective effect of testosterone in this patient population. 84

Another retrospective observational study examined the primary outcome of areal BMD by DXA in 16 individuals aged 14 to 17.7 (median 14.5) years with DMD and delayed puberty on GC, who received oral testosterone alone, or (in 10 who achieved mid-puberty) oral testosterone followed by IM testosterone undecanoate given every 3 months. 103 Fifteen of the 16 patients had received zoledronic acid for 6 to 42 months prior to testosterone, and 14 continued it during testosterone treatment. The patients were observed to have an increase in absolute areal BMD of the lumbar spine relative to baseline, but no change in Z-scores after standardizing for age and sex, or height. Vertebral morphometry was stable in 12 of 14 and worsened in two. Fat and lean mass indices and SD scores were unchanged. Motor function by Egen Klassifikation scores 104 improved in 7 of 8 non-ambulatory individuals, while 4 of the 7 ambulatory individuals became non-ambulatory during the treatment period and 2 of the 3 individuals who remained ambulatory had a decline in their North Star Ambulatory Assessment score. Cardiac function by echocardiography was stable. While this study suggested a beneficial effect of testosterone on bone mass accrual, most (13 of 16) of the patients were or became non-ambulatory, limiting accuracy of height measures and therefore interpretation of the height-dependent bone and body composition results. Furthermore, areal BMD values that were not standardized for age and height could have increased due to height gain from testosterone treatment. 103

Finally, in an observational study of 15 patients with DMD and delayed puberty who were treated with escalating testosterone doses via monthly IM injections over two years, the patients were observed to have stable lumbar spine BMD and reported high treatment satisfaction scores. 100 The cohort was pre-pubertal at the start of the study, aged 12-17 years, and using long-term high-dose GC. All patients were in late puberty after two years. The study also demonstrated an increase in contractile cross-sectional area of arm and leg muscle (i.e. the area that is not fat-replaced) when evaluated by MRI during the two-year incremental regimen of testosterone. The T2 relaxation times for all muscle groups except the upper arm flexors also decreased significantly at two years post baseline, raising the question of a possible anti-inflammatory effect of testosterone on dystrophic muscle. 100

Limited data on other anabolic steroids in individuals with DMD (albeit not used for pubertal induction) have not shown convincing evidence of a benefit on muscle. Oxandrolone is a non-aromatizing androgenic steroid that has been studied in the DMD population but is no longer commercially available from standard pharmacies. In a 6-month randomized double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial of oxandrolone, GC-naive boys with DMD aged 5 to 10 years did not show a change in the primary outcome of manual muscle strength or timed functional tests although there was an improvement in quantitative muscle test secondary outcomes. 105

In summary, the extant data in individuals with DMD, albeit limited, suggest a potential benefit of testosterone therapy for bone health in this population.84,103 While one study suggested a possible positive effect of testosterone on DMD muscle, 100 the overall evidence is lacking and remains inconclusive. Individuals and their caregivers should be made aware that the effects of testosterone therapy on muscle strength and cardiac function in those with DMD remain unclear, and further study on these important outcomes is needed.

Finally, it is also important to note that, should goals of care change over time or individuals develop unwanted or negative effects of testosterone, the treatment trajectory should be revisited. Potential challenges may include increased libido and sexual desire. Another example may be gender dysphoria, for which consultation and collaboration with pediatric specialists in gender care may be required, given the complexity of navigating pubertal induction in pre-pubertal gender diverse individuals. 99

Puberty should be induced by age 14 years in those who are developmentally ready and can be considered from age 12 years in individuals receiving daily GC. Given the high risk of hypogonadism associated with daily GC, as well as the question of potential short- and long-term benefit to earlier testosterone exposure, 106 inducing puberty at an earlier, more physiologically appropriate age than previously recommended merits consideration. Osteoporosis severity may also influence decisions around treatment initiation. Surveying practices for pubertal induction amongst the OPTIMIZE-DMD and allied Italian endocrinologists, 32% typically initiate therapy at age 12 years, 14% at age 13 years, 50% at age 14 years, and 5% at age 15 years or older. 7 Ultimately, treatment decisions will be influenced by local practices and should respect individuals’ goals of care.

Testosterone therapy is the treatment of choice for delayed puberty. Treatment of delayed puberty is usually with exogeneous testosterone administration. Some clinicians may use gonadotropins or human chorionic gonadotropin for induction of puberty in individuals with HH107,108 as this might improve longer term fertility outcomes. However, the latter involve intensive and burdensome regimens, are much more expensive, and could be difficult to justify in the setting of delayed puberty that might be transient. Regarding testosterone regimens, there is treatment heterogeneity and the most effective route or regimen has not been comprehensively studied in the DMD population.109,110 Route of testosterone administration should be chosen depending on clinician, patient and caregiver preference. Options include IM, subcutaneous (SC) or transdermal testosterone preparations. Our survey indicated that IM monthly testosterone was the most frequently used route by about two-thirds of endocrinologists, 7 in keeping with another international survey of DMD pediatricians of whom 96% prescribed this regimen. 111 Topical testosterone may be preferred by some 101 ; a theoretical consideration for pubertal induction with this preparation may be the potential risk of attaining higher serum concentrations than desired at initial low doses. Administration also requires protective measures for the caregiver such as gloves; additional precautions may be needed to avoid unwanted skin-to-skin transfer, especially in individuals who are fully care-dependent. The SC route has been used to induce puberty with published dosing recommendations. 108 While newer oral testosterone formulations without significant liver toxicity are now available in some countries, they remain inferior to other formulations in terms of stability of testosterone levels and some are associated with increased risk of hypertension. 112 Some recent guidelines include oral testosterone as a possible option for male pubertal induction although absorption can be variable, dosing may need to be more than once daily, and outcomes are not well studied.108,110,112 We therefore currently recommend IM, SC, or transdermal routes as the optimal testosterone formulations for pubertal induction.

Testosterone dosing should start low and be gradually increased to adult doses over a period of two to four years to mimic natural pubertal progression, in accordance with published protocols.55,108,110 Treatment duration should be individualized based on response to treatment, whether there is activation of endogenous puberty, and the goals of care of patients and caretakers.

Individuals receiving testosterone therapy should be monitored every 6 months for endogenous puberty. Unlike treatment for constitutional delay of growth and puberty, in which spontaneous puberty often occurs after a short course of three to six months of testosterone, it is likely that those on daily GC will need treatment to complete puberty, as ongoing supraphysiologic GC can cause persistent HPG axis suppression, which can be complete or partial. Some adolescents with DMD, however, may eventually have endogenous pubertal activation. 101 Those receiving testosterone therapy should therefore be evaluated every 6 months for activation of endogenous puberty by assessment of testicular volumes and/or biochemical measurement of serum testosterone concentrations (preferably early morning), with consideration of ultrasensitive LH measurement. Increased testicular volumes (4 mL or greater) are not seen due to testosterone therapy and indicate endogenous activation of puberty. If serum testosterone is measured to confirm endogenous puberty, it should be undertaken after withholding the dose of injectable testosterone therapy for at least one month - or for a suitable duration, depending on the specific formulation used - to ensure the results reflect endogenous hormone production rather than exogenous treatment. (A suggested approach could be to ask the family to withhold the monthly injection the month prior to returning to clinic.) An ultrasensitive LH measurement while receiving low-dose treatment may also indicate endogenous pubertal activation but is contingent upon local assay method and sensitivity.

During the early phase of pubertal induction, if endogenous puberty develops, one could consider withholding testosterone treatment and reassessing endogenous pubertal progression in 3 to 6 months. Should it then be evident that spontaneous pubertal progression is occurring, the individual could continue to be monitored closely for ongoing progression. If endogenous puberty is not present or progressing, testosterone treatment should be continued or resumed, and the dose increased.

By about mid-pubertal induction (e.g. injectable testosterone doses around 150 mg monthly), if there are no signs of endogenous puberty or its progression, or clinical/biochemical assessment suggests hypogonadism, then treatment with exogeneous testosterone should be advanced until adult doses are reached, with guidance from commonly used protocols,55,108,110 before considering stopping to reassess endogenous function. As doses are advanced, transition to more frequent dosing may be undertaken.55,108,110

Individuals receiving testosterone therapy should be closely monitored for potential physical and psychosocial effects of testosterone as outlined earlier, and re-evaluated for their satisfaction with and goals for treatment, at least 6-monthly.

2026 Updated recommendations for hypogonadism referrals, investigation, and management in older adolescents and young adults

Updated guidance for the management of hypogonadism in older adolescents and young adults is summarized in Figure 3 and Table 1.

Monitoring for hypogonadism and management approaches. Individuals should be continually assessed for hypogonadism (even following completion of puberty), with clinical examination and/or laboratory evaluation. Shared decision-making regarding treatment of hypogonadism should incorporate the individual’s goals of care. Treatment of hypogonadism should follow established guidelines with ongoing discussion regarding endogenous hormone production and the individual’s goals of continuation or discontinuation of treatment.

Monitoring and referrals for hypogonadism

Ongoing monitoring and discussion of hypogonadism in older adolescents and young adults post-spontaneous puberty or testosterone therapy should occur. Despite treatment to induce puberty by early-to mid-adolescence, which may be followed by endogenous pubertal progression and/or achievement of physical maturation, older adolescents and young adults with DMD continue to be at high risk for GC-induced hypogonadism. 113 As these individuals become older, clinicians are now faced with uncertainty regarding whether to continue or pause testosterone therapy, especially when there are signs of endogenous puberty following pubertal induction. Individuals with DMD are likely to already be receiving care from an endocrinologist at this stage/age, but some may be transitioning from pediatric to adult care; in addition, neuromuscular specialists need to be aware of this concern and consider an endocrinology referral, if indicated.

Clinicians should offer the opportunity for private conversations with the individual to inquire about their concerns and symptoms of testosterone deficiency, whilst being sensitive to the individual’s desire (or otherwise) to discuss these topics. Symptoms of testosterone deficiency include low energy, erectile dysfunction, and decreased libido, sexual interest and satisfaction; however, these can be difficult to assess in the context of DMD, notwithstanding the challenges of engaging in intimate relationships when living with this condition.114,115

Clinicians should also inquire about medications other than GC that can cause or exacerbate secondary hypogonadism. For example, antidepressants (especially selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) and antipsychotics can contribute by causing hyperprolactinemia or direct central suppression of the HPG axis,53,116 and collaboration with the prescribing mental health provider may be indicated. Alcohol, marijuana, obstructive sleep apnea, and obesity can also lower serum testosterone concentrations.53,116

The relevance of hypogonadism to bone and other aspects of health, and potential options for management, as described below, should also be discussed.

Investigation of hypogonadism

Annual clinical examination of testicular size is encouraged until it is confirmed that the patient has completed endogenous puberty. Serum testosterone concentration should be measured every 6 to 12 months in older adolescents and young adults to assess endogenous hormone production. Increased testicular volumes indicate endogenous gonadotropin activation; in particular, FSH stimulates Sertoli cell proliferation and spermatogenesis, contributing to testicular volume increase during puberty. 117 However, if previous testicular volumes have already suggested progression to later-stage puberty, regular ongoing examinations (which can be uncomfortable and difficult for patients in wheelchairs) may not be necessary or helpful for clinical decision-making, and measurement of serum testosterone concentrations should suffice.

To evaluate hypogonadism, serum testosterone concentrations need to be measured off treatment. Because obesity and GC can lower sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) and thus total testosterone concentrations, free testosterone should be assessed if total testosterone concentrations are near the lower limit of the normal range. 53 Free testosterone concentrations should be determined either by equilibrium dialysis followed by LC-MS/MS or by calculations using total testosterone, SHBG and albumin concentrations, but not by analog-based immunoassays, which are inaccurate. 53 Serum testosterone concentrations exhibit diurnal variation with peak values in the morning and may be suppressed by glucose or food intake, and should ideally be measured in the early morning when fasting. 53 Regardless, interpretation of testosterone concentrations should consider the timing of the test and the individual’s age and expected stage of puberty.

If hypogonadism is present, measuring serum LH and FSH concentrations can help distinguish between primary and secondary hypogonadism. 53 Anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) and inhibin B are markers of Sertoli cell function and could be potentially helpful in predicting testicular function and fertility potential.118–120 However, there is a lack of consensus regarding the clinical utility of these markers in individuals with DMD; in our survey, only 4 of 25 (16%) endocrinologists reported measuring these indices in older adolescents and young adults with DMD and hypogonadism, 7 indicating this as an area for future study.

Bone health investigations should be integrated into the overall evaluation of adolescents and young adults with hypogonadism. As osteoporosis is prevalent and often severe in adolescents and adults with DMD, the investigation of hypogonadism should encompass assessment of the severity and progression of osteoporosis in addition to serum testosterone concentrations, which may inform decisions around treatment of hypogonadism. Endocrinologists who were surveyed cited bone health as a key element of decision-making in the management of hypogonadism. 7 Detailed evaluation of bone health is addressed in the companion manuscript of this Special Issue.

Management of hypogonadism

Testosterone supplementation should be considered in older adolescents and young adults with DMD who have persistently low serum testosterone concentrations, following person-centered shared decision-making discussions. The choices faced by older adolescents and young adults with hypogonadism include continued monitoring without intervention, or continuation or initiation of treatment, and decisions should be made on a case-by-case basis.

Individuals should be informed and educated about the potential pros and cons of testosterone supplementation versus monitoring alone (Table 3). In addition to virilization (including often-desired effects at this age of facial hair and phallus enlargement), considerations include bone and metabolic health, 53 sexual function and drive, erythrocytosis, 102 and possible energy, motivational, and psychological impacts. 121 The effect of testosterone on dystrophic muscle remains unclear.100,105 Potential downsides to normalizing serum testosterone concentrations may be frequency of nocturnal emissions, which may pose management challenges and affect privacy for those with severe physical disability needing assistance with daily care activities. 114 Enhanced libido may come with the practical challenges of accessing healthy sexual relationships. Decisions should be individualized and respect individuals’ goals of care, which can change over time.

There is variability in practice across endocrinologists regarding thresholds to intervene with testosterone supplementation in older individuals with DMD.7,111,113 The decision to treat may not be straightforward as hypogonadism may be partial. This is underscored in a prospective observational follow-up study of 15 GC-treated males with DMD who had previously received testosterone over two years to induce puberty, and in whom there was evidence of HPG activation with continued progression of puberty and improved markers of testicular function in more than half of the cohort when they were followed up three years after their last testosterone injection. 113 However, mean testicular volumes and serum testosterone concentrations were well below age-matched norms at six months and three years after completion of treatment, and some had been advised to restart testosterone supplementation during follow-up. 113 There was considerable variability in testosterone values that did not appear to be mutation-specific or related to ambulation status, suggesting that there may be additional factors affecting the HPG axis, including inter-individual sensitivity to GC.

The treatment threshold for optimizing bone health is also unclear. In healthy male adults, testosterone supplementation improves bone accrual and muscle strength.53,122–124 Bone accrual is likely induced by stimulation of osteoblast function and bone-specific growth factors, 125 with much of the effect of testosterone on bone accrual mediated through conversion to estradiol.126,127 Testosterone administration appears to have the greatest impact on BMD in those with lower testosterone concentrations and does not have a significant impact when administered to those with values in the normal range. 128 It has been suggested that 200 ng/dL (6.9 nmol/L) should be considered as a target threshold for healthy male adults, as skeletal health may be compromised when serum testosterone concentrations fall below 200-250 ng/dL (6.9-8.7 nmol/L).126,129,130 The Endocrine Society has recommended considering testosterone therapy for hypogonadal men with testosterone concentrations below 200 ng/dL (6.9 nmol/L) who are at risk for fracture, with or without an agent with proven antifracture efficacy, depending on the level of risk. 126 However, testosterone is not an approved treatment for osteoporosis or for reducing fracture risk 53 and should be considered adjunctive to other therapies in individuals with DMD and osteoporosis.

Regimens for testosterone therapy in older adolescents and adults with DMD should be consistent with established treatment guidelines for hypogonadism. There are limited data regarding the optimal treatment regimen for males with HH. Testosterone replacement is the standard therapy.53,108 There are multiple testosterone formulations available, from injectable esters with varying half-lives, to transdermal, nasal and oral preparations. 53 The availability of these in each healthcare system and patient preference may influence choice. 131

Our survey of endocrinologists in the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium highlighted variability in practices around testosterone regimens in older adolescents and young adults with DMD. 7 In contrast to younger adolescents undergoing pubertal induction who were usually treated with monthly IM depot testosterone injections, there was increased use of weekly or two-weekly SC testosterone injections and daily topical gel formulations (prescribed by 57 and 43% of endocrinologists, respectively), in addition to more frequently administered IM (one-, two- or four-weekly) injections (prescribed by 65%). There was variation across countries, SC injections being widely used in the USA and Canada, testosterone gel in the USA and Italy, and injectable long-acting testosterone undecanoate in the UK.

Accordingly, to achieve adult serum concentrations in this population, methods such as weekly or two-weekly injections or topical formulations may cause less fluctuation of serum concentrations as compared with monthly injections, with SC administration being potentially less burdensome than the IM route. Testosterone formulations may be country-dependent and local formularies should be consulted.

Individual goals and serum testosterone concentrations should be assessed every 6 to 12 months to guide ongoing decisions around treatment. The timing of testosterone measurement depends on the clinical question being asked:

• To assess endogenous production in an individual on testosterone therapy, a suggested approach would be to measure testosterone concentrations after withholding therapy.

• To assess endogenous production in an individual on testosterone therapy, a suggested approach would be to measure testosterone concentrations after withholding therapy.

• To assess adequacy of treatment dosing, testosterone concentrations should be measured at an optimal time for the regimen used (for example, midway between injections for weekly or two-weekly injectable regimens, or a trough level for longer-acting injectable regimens).

• To assess adequacy of treatment dosing, testosterone concentrations should be measured at an optimal time for the regimen used (for example, midway between injections for weekly or two-weekly injectable regimens, or a trough level for longer-acting injectable regimens).

There should be ongoing evaluation of the benefits and other effects of treatment (Table 3) at every visit, with individualized decisions to continue monitoring or continue or stop therapy contingent upon the degree of endogenous testosterone production, severity of osteoporosis, and/or patients’ goals of care. For individuals who choose not to undergo supplementation, similar monitoring and discussions should ensue. For those in whom it is indicated, testosterone supplementation should be considered as a long-term therapy for arrested puberty/hypogonadism. If long-term testosterone supplementation is initiated, then hematocrit and serum testosterone concentrations should typically be measured every 6 to 12 months.53,132 Prostate-related adverse effects are unlikely to be relevant in the young adult population with DMD; guidelines should nonetheless be followed and recommend screening after 40 years of age. 53

The impact of both long-term GC and testosterone therapies on fertility potential is unclear and is discussed in the companion manuscript on sexual health and fertility of this Special Issue. For those who want to pursue fertility, referral to a fertility service could be considered (contingent upon local resources) to discuss undertaking sperm analysis and cryopreservation, following a period off testosterone treatment and before resuming long-term therapy. Gonadotropin treatment may stimulate testicular growth and spermatogenesis in individuals with HH107,108; the need for this is unclear in young adults with DMD in whom hypogonadism may be partial.

Future directions and research questions.

Abbreviations: AMH, Anti-Mullerian hormone; DMD, Duchenne muscular dystrophy; DXA, dual X-ray absorptiometry; GC, glucocorticoid; rhGH, recombinant human growth hormone.

Growth measures and management

Research is needed to determine how to accurately measure height in non-ambulatory individuals, as multiple assessments in individuals with DMD are indexed to height, such as cardiac parameters and pulmonary function tests. Whole-body imaging by DXA can be used to measure bone lengths in children, including with DMD, and warrants further study.67,68 Accuracy of measuring bone lengths or height, however, can be affected by the type of DXA machine (e.g. Lunar GE DXA versus Hologic) and fan x-ray beam used,69,70 as well as the presence of lumbar lordosis, hip or knee contractures, and patient positioning issues. 67 In addition, given skeletal disproportion and immaturity in individuals with DMD,67,71 DMD-specific prediction equations to estimate height from bone lengths are needed.

It will be important to develop an enhanced understanding around patient and caregiver perspectives on short stature, as well as interventions to augment height. Data on long-term growth (including adult height) and multidisciplinary health outcomes resulting from therapeutic interventions, including GC regimen (classic GC adjustment or transition to vamorolone) or rhGH, are lacking and are needed to establish the safety and efficacy of growth-promoting interventions.

Assessment of gonadal function using Sertoli cell markers

Research is needed to determine if there is utility in measuring Sertoli cell markers in adolescents and young adults with DMD. Inhibin B and anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) are both secreted by testicular Sertoli cells and may have a role in predicting the potential for endogenous puberty 118 and future fertility. 119 These markers are often low in individuals with HH, while inhibin B may be helpful in distinguishing between HH and constitutional delay of growth and puberty in healthy adolescents with delayed puberty.133,134 In older adolescents and young adults with DMD, who have secondary (often partial) HH due to GC, a research question would be whether Sertoli cell markers could provide information on testicular function and fertility potential and guide clinical decisions surrounding the need for testosterone supplementation and sperm cryopreservation.

Induction of puberty in younger adolescents and outcomes

There are many questions regarding the treatment of delayed puberty in adolescents with DMD. Current testosterone replacement therapy regimens in adolescent males (without DMD) are based on consensus and expert opinion, but evidence-based guidelines in this age group are lacking. 110 There is sparse literature on the tolerability and efficacy of different testosterone regimens in the DMD population, in whom there are unique challenges associated with mobility and accessibility. Short- and long-term muscular, cardiopulmonary, bone health and psychological impacts of testosterone therapy to induce puberty need to be further elucidated in the DMD population and may impact decision-making regarding treatment, including optimal timing of pubertal induction and duration of therapy. Lastly, it is not known whether the dissociative steroid vamorolone affects puberty, and more data are needed.

Management of hypogonadism in older individuals and outcomes

In older adolescents and young adults with DMD, questions remain regarding the duration of testosterone treatment and the need to reinitiate therapy when there are signs of endogenous puberty following pubertal induction. In the setting of (partial) hypogonadism, questions remain about optimal dosing (e.g. lower doses to supplement versus higher doses that suppress HPG function). The impact of testosterone on, and optimal treatment thresholds for, skeletal health and muscle mass in young men with DMD are unknown, particularly if on high-dose GC. While preliminary data in a small number of individuals with DMD provide insight into potential physical and psychological benefits of testosterone in this population,48,84,100,113,135 multidisciplinary outcomes data, including muscle strength, body composition, and bone, metabolic, sexual, psychological, and cognitive health, are needed to understand the long-term effects of testosterone therapy in older individuals with DMD and GC-induced hypogonadism.

In young adults with DMD, there are unanswered questions around whether long-term testosterone (and/or GC) therapy impacts fertility potential and whether gonadotropin therapy has a role. While the question of whether prior testosterone therapy negatively impacts the potential for future spermatogenesis is often debated, meta-analyses of collective studies do not suggest a negative effect from prior treatment.107,136 There is extensive experience with testosterone therapy over gonadotropin therapy, and testosterone is cheaper and involves a less burdensome regimen. On the other hand, pubertal replacement therapy with gonadotropin analogs has several advantages over testosterone administration, including direct stimulation of Leydig and Sertoli cells, increased testicular size and improved spermatogenesis compared to testosterone treatment.107,108,137 A question to consider might be whether to escalate testosterone dosing starting at 12 to 14 years of age, monitor for signs of endogenous puberty off therapy, and then follow with gonadotropin administration if there is no response.

Shared decision-making and educational resources

Finally, in all these areas, there is a need for knowledge dissemination for individuals, their families, and clinical providers, including educational resources and shared decision-making tools around management options (including what is known and what is not known about the various strategies).

Conclusions

Management of impaired growth and hypogonadism leading to delayed and incomplete puberty in individuals with DMD is complex and nuanced in the setting of their multisystem health issues and goals of clinical care. Endocrine care needs to be holistic and involve communication with, and monitoring by, the interdisciplinary team, regardless of the physical clinic model. Referrals and treatment decisions need to be person- and family-centered and individualized, with clear discussions about the state of the evidence informing the various decisions. Education around these issues should be proactive 5 and involve consistent messaging from endocrinologists and other specialists, underscoring a need for guidance for clinicians and individual and family resources that can be used across disciplines. There needs to be informed and shared decision-making over time to guide management, which should be aligned with individuals’ goals and developmental readiness. 138 Emerging disease-modifying therapies may also have important implications for growth and pubertal development and overall health outcomes in individuals with DMD, further highlighting the need for continued evaluation of endocrine management strategies amidst a changing landscape. With ongoing collaborations within the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium, we aspire to elevate and improve endocrine care, which should continue to evolve and be informed by ongoing advances in clinical practice and research, to ultimately optimize the physical and psychological health of individuals with DMD.
