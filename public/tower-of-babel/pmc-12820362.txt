Synthesis of 2′-fucosyllactose using multi-enzyme cascade with cofactor regeneration
Authors: Chenqi Cao, Yi Bai, Mengxin Zhang, Shahin Shah Khan, Yu Ji, Luo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract2′-Fucosyllactose (2′-FL) is a major human milk oligosaccharide with nutritional and medicinal benefits, widely used in infant formula and nutraceuticals. Now most studies focus on the synthesis of 2′-FL in vivo starting from glucose and glycerol. Here, we designed the first in vitro multi-enzyme cascade system for the de novo synthesis of 2′-FL using mannose and lactose as substrates. Central to this pathway is an engineered polyphosphate-dependent glucomannokinase (PPGMK), which enables efficient phosphorylation of mannose to G6P and thereby establishes mannose as an effective entry point for the de novo synthetic route. To enhance the production of 2′-FL, the cascade system was optimized, and apart from α-1,2-fucosyltransferase, GDP-D-mannose-4,6-dehydratase (gmd) was determined as a crucial rate-limiting enzyme for the de novo synthesis of 2′-FL. Additionally, an efficient GTP regeneration system was achieved, which converted GDP to GTP using polyphosphate kinase 2, resulting in a 2.94-fold improvement in 2′-FL production. Finally, under optimized conditions incorporating the cofactor regeneration system, 13.18 mM 2′-FL was synthesized through a one-pot enzymatic reaction using 20 mM mannose and lactose as substrates. The engineered system demonstrated exceptional cofactor efficiency, requiring only 2 mM each of the essential cofactor NADPH and GTP representing a 6.6-fold reduction in cofactor consumption. Overall, this study establishes a novel de novo enzymatic route for 2′-FL and provides new insights for both pathway construction and enzyme engineering in oligosaccharide production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32265-7.Keywords: Human milk oligosaccharides; 2′-Fucosyllactose; α-1,2-fucosyltransferase; Multi-enzyme cascade; GTP regenerationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery

Introduction

Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) are among the most abundant component of human milk, comprising over 200 distinct structural types1. Among these, 2′-FL stands out as one of the most prevalent, offering significant nutritional benefits, particularly for infants. These include supporting gut microbiota regulation, promoting brain development, and protecting against pathogenic microbial infections2. To date, 2′-FL has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in infant formula, food products, and nutraceuticals3. The broad application prospects of 2′-FL have garnered considerable attention from researchers.

2′-FL can be produced through breast milk extraction4, chemical synthesis5, microbial fermentation5–7, and enzymatic synthesis8,9. In the 1980s, research on the separation of 2′-FL from human milk was reported, but it faced challenges such as limited raw materials and inefficient extraction4. Although the chemical synthesis of 2′-FL is technically feasible and kilogram-scale production has been reported using multi-step protection strategies10, the process involves complex protection and deprotection of functional groups. Such complexity and labor-intensive procedures make chemical synthesis unsuitable for large-scale applications in the food industry5. At present, most of the research of 2′-FL synthesis focuses on the construction of the metabolic pathway, using Escherichia coli (E. coli), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae), Corynebacterium glutamicum (C. glutamicum), and Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) as the cell factory platform11–15. To improve the production of 2′-FL, the key genes were optimized through plasmid and/or chromosome integration, and downregulated or knocked out from the genome16–20. Synthesized 2′-FL in engineered S. cerevisiae using xylose as a co-substrate with a yield of 25.5 g/L14. Earlier studies engineered E. coli BL21 (DE3) to produce 2′-FL in fed-batch cultivation, achieving titers of approximately 79.23 g/L through multiple combinatorial strategies21. More recently, further metabolic and process optimizations have increased the production titer to 121.4 g/L22. Although 2′-FL is now produced at industrial levels through microbial fermentation, several studies have noted challenges that remain at the strain and process levels, including plasmid instability in certain engineered E. coli hosts, accumulation of metabolic by-products that reduce yield, and the relatively high cost and complexity of downstream purification of fucosylated oligosaccharides5. These factors continue to drive efforts toward improving robustness and reducing production costs.

Compared with metabolic engineering, enzymatic synthesis has the advantages of high efficiency, no bypass interference, no by-products and easy purification of products23–25. The α-1,2-fucosyltransferase (FucT) catalyzes the transfer of a fucose residue from GDP-L-fucose onto the galactose moiety of lactose, resulting in the formation of 2′-FL26. Lactose is added externally, and GDP-L-fucose is accumulated via either the de novo or salvage pathway18,27. The de novo biosynthesis of GDP-L-fucose relies on the microorganism’s endogenous metabolic mechanism26,28. Initially, fructose-6-phosphate is converted to mannose-1-phosphate by Mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (manA) and phosphomannomutase (manB). Next, Mannose-1-phosphate guanylyltransferase (manC) catalyzes the transfer of a nucleotide from GTP to mannose-1-phosphate, forming GDP-mannose. Finally, GDP-mannose is converted to GDP-L-fucose through the sequential action of GDP-D-mannose-4,6-dehydratase (gmd) and NADPH-dependent GDP-L-fucose synthase (wcaG)18,26,29. In the salvage pathway of GDP-L-fucose, a bifunctional fucose kinase/fucose-1-phosphate guanylyltransferase (fkp) from Bacteroides fragilis (B. fragilis) converts L-fucose to GDP-L-fucose, and the process requires the participation of ATP and GTP9,11,27.

Both the de novo and salvage pathways for the synthesis of 2′-FL necessitate the involvement of GTP, posing an economic challenge for industrial production11,30. It is imperative to develop a GTP cycle system that minimizes costs while enhancing the catalytic efficiency of the entire system. Polyphosphate kinase 2 (PPK2) catalyzes the phosphorylation of nucleoside monophosphate and nucleoside diphosphate using inorganic polyphosphate as a donor31–34. PPK2 derived from different sources exhibit varying preferences. For example, PPK2 from C. glutamicum can regenerate ATP using hexametaphosphate as a donor35. In contrast, the activity of PPK2 SMc02148 from Sinorhizobium meliloti for converting GDP is approximately 3-fold lower than its activity for converting ADP34.

In this study, an in vitro multi-enzyme cascade system was developed for the synthesis of 2′-FL (Fig. 1). By incorporating polyphosphate-dependent glucomannokinase (PPGMK) into the de novo pathway, 2′-FL was successfully synthesized using mannose and lactose as substrates. The cascade system was systematically optimized, and efficient PPK2 enzymes were identified and integrated to enable effective GTP regeneration. As a result, 2′-FL was efficiently produced through this in vitro enzymatic cascade system with cofactor regeneration. This work offers a novel and efficient strategy for the synthesis of 2′-FL.

Results and discussion

An in vitro multi-enzyme cascade for the synthesis of 2′-FL from mannose

An in vitro multi-enzyme cascade system was designed for synthesizing 2′-FL using mannose as the starting substrate (Fig. 1). This pathway consists of the following enzyme cascade reactions: Firstly, mannose is converted to mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) by polyphosphate-dependent glucomannokinase (PPGMK); Then, through the de novo pathway (involving enzymes manB, manC, gmd, and wcaG), mannose-6-phosphate is transformed into GDP-L-fucose; Finally, GDP-L-fucose and lactose are used by FucT to generate 2′-FL. Additionally, we established a GTP and NADPH regeneration system to facilitate the cost-effective production of 2′-FL.

The synthesis of M6P can be achieved by polyphosphate-dependent glucomannokinase (PPGMK), using sodium hexametaphosphate (SHMP) as substrate36,37. Through directed evolution, the variant PPGMK (H92K/E119R) exhibited exceptional catalytic properties, with an increase in activity and thermal stability by 2-fold and 5.4-fold respectively, and a conversion rate of nearly 100%37. Herein the variant PPGMK (H92K/E119R) from Arthrobacter species was introduced into the de novo pathway to produce M6P. Based on previous reports, the enzymes involved in the de novo pathway (manB, manC, gmd, and wcaG) were sourced from various hosts (Table 1) and utilized to catalyze the cascade reaction8,21. Additionally, the classic FucT from Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) was employed in the reaction for the synthesis of 2′-FL8,38,39.

Initially, His-tagged FucT was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3), and attempts were made to purify the enzyme. Although the target protein could be detected, the purification efficiency was exceedingly low, rendering it unsuitable for use in the cascade reaction. Similar purification challenges have also been reported in recent studies40. Therefore, FucT was employed in the form of an unpurified crude enzyme. However, crude lysates from standard expression strains contain endogenous lacZ, which hydrolyzes lactose and thereby interferes with the reaction. To avoid this issue, the C43ΔlacZ strain (a C43 derivative lacking lacZ) preserved in our laboratory was used for FucT expression22.

Furthermore, FucT was expressed in the E. coli C43ΔlacZ strain. Other enzymes, including PPGMK, manB, manC, gmd, and wcaG were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) strains, respectively. All enzymes were successfully expressed (Figure S1). A 0.5 mL one-pot reaction mixture was incubated at 30℃ for 12 h. First, the capacity of PPGMK to synthesize Man6P within 12 h was evaluated. HPLC analysis revealed that the conversion rate of mannose reached nearly 100% after 12 h of reaction catalyzed by PPGMK (Figure S2). Subsequently, 2′-FL was synthesized via a cascade reaction using mannose as the substrate. HPLC analysis revealed that, compared to the standard, the reaction samples exhibited a prominent 2′-FL peak at a retention time of 7.2 min (Figure S3). Therefore, it is confirmed that this pathway enables the synthesis of 2′-FL with mannose and lactose as substrates.

Notably, it is conceivable that glucose could also serve as a substrate for 2′-FL synthesis. This is because PPGMK has the capability to convert glucose into glucose-6-phosphate, which can then be isomerized to fructose-6-phosphate by glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI), as demonstrated in previous studies18,37. In the de novo synthesis pathway of 2′-FL, manA is typically employed to convert fructose-6-phosphate into M6P21,26. Therefore, by incorporating GPI and manA into the in vitro multi-enzyme cascade system, it is theoretically feasible to synthesize 2′-FL from glucose.

Optimization of cascade system

Effect of metal ions and pH on the synthesis of 2′-FL

Since the cascade reaction involves various enzymes (PPGMK, manC, and FucT) that require metal ions, the type and concentration of metal ions were optimized to further enhance the production of 2′-FL.37,38 The effect of metal ion types was evaluated with 10 mM various ions (Mg²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ni²⁺, Cu²⁺). As shown in Fig. 2a, Mg2+ exhibited a significant positive impact on 2′-FL production, with the yield increased 6.19-fold (1.9 mM) compared to the sample without addition of metal ions. Mn2+ slightly increased 2′-FL production by 0.42-fold. In contrast, the other metal ions (Zn2+, Ca2+, Ni2+, and Cu2+) had inhibitory effects on the 2′-FL production. Subsequently, the effects of Mg²⁺ concentration was evaluated across the range of 0–30 mM, and the optimal 2′-FL production was 3.7 mM with 25 mM Mg²⁺ (Fig. 2b). To further investigate the impact of pH on multi-enzyme systems, the pH was optimized in the range of 5.0–8.0 and reached the highest 2′-FL production at pH 7.5 (Fig. 2c). The yield of 2′-FL remained relatively stable within the range of pH 6.5 to 8.0 (relative yield: 90–100%), while a significant decrease (about 44%) was observed at pH 5.0.

(b) The 0.5 mL cascade reaction was performed at 30℃ for 12 h with 0–30 mM Mg²⁺ in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0). Optimization of enzymes concentration involved in the system

Actually, there are rate-limiting enzymes in the 2′-FL synthesis pathway, in particular α-1,2-fucosyltransferase (the key step of the catalytic metabolic reaction), need to be improved8,26,38,39. Despite efforts by some researchers to modify FucT through directed evolution, the α-1,2-fucosyltransferase continues to pose significant challenges for 2′-FL biosynthesis, characterized by its low activity, poor soluble expression8,38. To explore whether there are other rate-limiting steps in the de novo synthesis pathway, we sequentially optimized and evaluated the effects of different concentrations of various enzymes involved in the pathway on 2′-FL yield (Fig. 3).

Optimization of enzyme concentration for 2′-FL production in cascade reactions. (a) Effect of manB concentration: 2–24 µM manB, 10 µM other enzymes (manC, gmd, wcaG), and 10 µM FucT. (b) Effect of manC concentration: 12 µM manB, 2–24 µM manC, 10 µM gmd, 10 µM wcaG, and 10 µM FucT. (c) Effect of gmd concentration: 12 µM manB, 8 µM manC, 2–24 µM gmd, 10 µM wcaG, and 10 µM FucT. (d) Effect of wcaG concentration: 12 µM manB, 8 µM manC, 20 µM gmd, 2–24 µM wcaG, and 10 µM FucT. (e) Effect of FucT concentration: 12 µM manB, 8 µM manC, 20 µM gmd, 20 µM wcaG, and 1–10 µM FucT. The values are the average of three parallel reactions.

PPGMK effectively catalyzed the synthesis of M6P, resulting in almost complete conversion of mannose within 12 h. Given its high catalytic efficiency at the tested concentration, further optimization of its enzyme concentration was deemed unnecessary. Figure 3a showed that the highest yield of 2′-FL was 3.88 mM with 12 µM manB. Figure 3b showed that the highest yield of 2′-FL was 3.85 mM with 8 µM manC. Notably, gmd concentration had a significant impact on 2′-FL production (Fig. 3c), with an optimal yield of 2′-FL was 6.25 mM with 20 µM gmd. The optimization of wcaG concentration revealed that 2′-FL yield was maintained at similar levels to the control (Fig. 3d), with an optimal yield of 2′-FL was 6.75 mM with 20 µM wcaG. More importantly, FucT, as the key rate-limiting enzyme in 2′-FL production, demonstrated a pronounced rate-limiting effect (Fig. 3e).

Figure 3c and e show that as the concentrations of gmd and FucT decrease, the production of 2′-FL also decreases significantly. Specifically, when the concentration of gmd is reduced from 20 mM to 2 mM, the yield of 2′-FL is reduced to about 1/10. Similarly, a decrease in the concentration of FucT from 10 µM to 1 µM results in a notable reduction of 90% in 2′-FL production. Sturla et al. investigated the expression, purification and properties of gmd from E. coli, it was found that GDP-L-fucose, the crucial precursor in the 2′-FL synthesis pathway, can selectively inhibit gmd41. The crystal structure of gmd was determined with a resolution of 2.3 Å by Somoza42. They have confirmed that GDP-L-fucose is a competitive inhibitor which binds to the same site as GDP-mannose. Furthermore, we found that the purified gmd lacked sufficient stability. In summary, the analysis suggested that gmd had a significant impact on the production of 2′-FL, and is a crucial rate-limiting enzyme in the de novo synthesis of 2′-FL.

The conditions that were optimized for 2′-FL production were as follows: 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5), 25 mM MgCl2, 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 10 mM GTP, 10 mM NADPH, 20 mM SHMP, 10 µM PPGMK, 12 µM manB, 8 µM manC, 20 µM gmd, 20 µM wcaG, and 10 µM FucT at 30℃ for 12 h. Under above optimum conditions, 6.75 mM 2′-FL was obtained from 20 mM mannose.

GTP regeneration system development

GTP and NADPH are essential cofactor in the 2′-FL cascade production11,30. However, their relatively high cost makes direct addition preventive for achieving a cost-effective process. Therefore, an efficient cofactor regeneration system is necessary. In this study, the regeneration of NADPH was achieved by the commercially available isocitrate dehydrogenase (Icd) or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH), and here we focus on developing GTP regeneration system by PPK2. Four potential PPK2 (PPK-Ft, PPK-Pa, PPK-Rs, and PPK-Mt) were selected for GTP regeneration and their effects on 2′-FL cascade production were evaluated32,33,43–45. Three PPK2 (PPK-Ft, PPK-Pa, and PPK-Rs) were soluble expression in BL21 (DE3), except of PPK-Mt, which was in the form of inclusion body (Figure S4). Hence, the purified three PPK2 (PPK-Ft, PPK-Pa, and PPK-Rs) were used to regenerate GTP was evaluated (Fig. 4a).

Effect of cofactor regeneration on 2′-FL cascade production. (a) GTP regeneration: 2 mM GTP, 10 mM NADPH, and 5 µM of either PPK-Ft, PPK-Pa, or PPK-Rs. (b) NADPH regeneration: 10 mM GTP, 2 mM NADPH, 1U/mL G6PDH or 1U/mL Icd. (c) GTP and NADPH regeneration: 2 mM GTP, 2 mM NADPH, 5 µM PPK-Ft, 1U/mL Icd. The values are the average of three parallel reactions.

Firstly, only the GTP regeneration system was introduced, with SHMP used as the substrate for regeneration. Since SHMP also serves as the substrate for PPGMK, this approach eliminates the need for any additional substrates, thereby streamlining the process and reducing costs. Figure 4a showed that PPK-Ft and PPK-Pa exhibited similar GTP regeneration capabilities in the cascade system, with the yields of 2′-FL increasing by 2.94-fold and 2.91-fold respectively compared to the control group (1.52 mM). However, PPK-Rs only resulted in 0.48-fold increase in 2′-FL yield. Subsequently, the effect of commercially available NADPH regeneration enzymes (G6PDH and Icd) was measured (Fig. 4b), which required the additional addition of glucose-6-phosphate and sodium isocitrate as substrates, respectively. The results showed that Icd was more effective than G6PDH for NADPH regeneration in the cascade system. Specifically, the yields of 2′-FL were enhanced by 2.66-fold with Icd and 0.68-fold with G6PDH, compared to the control group (1.72 mM). Therefore, PPK-Ft and Icd were chosen for GTP and NADPH regeneration. By introducing the GTP and NADPH regeneration system (Fig. 4c), the reaction cost was further reduced, resulting in an approximately 5-fold increase in 2′-FL yield compared to the control group (None, 1.02 mM).

Additionally, the 2′-FL yield from the 10 µM FucT group (Fig. 3e) was compared with that from the PPK-Ft + Icd cofactor regeneration system (Fig. 4c). Identical reaction conditions were shared between the two groups except for the cofactor regeneration module: in the 10 µM FucT group (Fig. 3e), cofactors were directly supplemented at 10 mM GTP and 10 mM NADPH; in contrast, cofactor regeneration was achieved in the PPK-Ft + Icd system (Fig. 4c) using 2 mM GTP, 2 mM NADPH, 5 µM PPK-Ft, 1 U/mL Icd, and 20 mM sodium isocitrate. Under these respective conditions, the measured 2′-FL yields were 5.90 mM (10 µM FucT group, Fig. 3e) and 6.12 mM (PPK-Ft + Icd group, Fig. 4c). Thus, the yield from the cofactor regeneration system was essentially comparable to and slightly higher than that from direct cofactor supplementation, and the effectiveness of the employed cofactor regeneration module was further validated.

One-pot synthesis of 2′-FL by multi-enzyme cascade in vitro

Finally, the synthesis of 2′-FL was accomplished in an in vitro enzymatic cascade system with cofactor regeneration through a one-pot reaction. The synthesis of 2′-FL was carried out using a mixture containing 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 2 mM GTP, and 2 mM NADPH. The yields of 2′-FL were measured at different time points (Fig. 5). At 48 h, the highest yield of 2′-FL was 13.18 mM, with a conversion rate of 65.9%.

One-pot synthesis of 2′-FL with 20 mM mannose and lactose as substrates. The values are the average of three parallel reactions.

Research on the enzymatic synthesis of 2′-FL is scarce and predominantly focuses on the salvage pathway. Engels leveraged the salvage pathway to successfully synthesize 4.3 mM 2′-FL from 10 mM L-fucose, using the fkp (from B. fragilis) and wbgL (α-1,2-fucosyltransferase from E. coli O126)46. Li et al. conducted directed evolution of the FucT gene from H. pylori, resulting in the optimal mutant V6, which produced 3.38 g/L of 2′-FL from L-fucose (10 g/L) and lactose (10 g/L)8. While research on the production of 2′-FL through the salvage pathway has already been reported, this pathway necessitates L-fucose, which is generally deemed unsuitable for industrial-scale production of 2′-FL47. FucT from H. pylori catalyzed the conversion of GDP-L-fucose (35 mg) into 2′-FL with a yield of 65%48. It is also worth noting that a related in vitro de novo cascade for 2′-FL production has previously been described in the patent literature49, whereas, to our knowledge, no comparable report has been published in the academic literature.

Conclusion

This is the first academic study to report the de novo synthesis of 2′-FL through an in vitro one-pot multi-enzyme cascade using mannose and lactose as substrates. The core of this pathway lies in the high catalytic efficiency of the engineered PPGMK, which selectively phosphorylates mannose to G6P, thereby establishing mannose as a stable and efficient entry point for the de novo 2′-FL synthetic route. Upon optimizing the reaction system, it was discovered that GDP-mannose dehydrogenase (gmd) had a profound impact on the synthesis of 2′-FL, potentially serving as the pivotal rate-limiting enzyme in the de novo synthesis of 2′-FL. Meanwhile, efficient PPK2 were screened for achieving low-cost GTP regeneration, thereby ensuring a continuous and ample supply for the production of 2′-FL. Under the optimal reaction conditions, after a 48-h reaction period, 13.18 mM of 2′-FL was produced from 20 mM of mannose. The enzymatic synthesis of 2′-FL in vitro presents the benefits of being eco-friendly, highly efficient, safe, cost-effective, and facilitating easy purification of the resulting product. Hence, it has a potential for further development. This study will provide new guidance and reference for the 2′-FL synthesis pathway in vivo and in vitro.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

2′-FL was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) and Isocitrate Dehydrogenase (Icd) were purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology (Shanghai, China). Other chemicals and reagents were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China).

Strains and plasmids

E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain was purchased from Weidi (Shanghai, China), and E. coli C43Δ lacZ was stocked in our lab. The origin of all enzymes used in the multi-enzyme cascade is summarized in Table 1, including the source organism and details regarding any truncations or expression tags applied to each sequence, and were codon-optimized and inserted into the vector by Genscript (Nanjing, China).

Culture conditions and protein purification

Recombinant plasmid FucT-pETDuet-1 was transferred into the E. coli C43ΔlacZ strain, and the rest of the recombinant plasmids were transferred into E. coli BL21 (DE3) strains, respectively. The fresh cells were placed into a shaking flask that contained LB medium (containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin or 100 µg/mL ampicillin) and cultured on a shaker at 37℃ and 220 rpm. When the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, whereupon IPTG was added with a final concentration of 0.2 mM. PPGMK, manB, manC, gmd, and wcaG were induced at 25 °C and 200 rpm for 14 h. FucT was induced at 22 °C and 200 rpm for 16 h. PPKs were induced at 20 °C and 180 rpm for 16 h.

The cells were collected by centrifugation, and then resuspended in 1/10 volume of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5). The cells were broken by sonication, and cell debris were removed by centrifugation. The supernatant was purified by the gravity Ni-NTA column. Wash buffer: pH 7.8-8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 30 mM imidazole; Elution buffer contained 300 mM imidazole, with the other conditions being the same as the wash buffer. The purified protein was desalted and concentrated for immediate use or stored at -80 °C with 20% glycerol.

Multi-enzyme cascade synthesis and identification of 2′-FL

One-pot cascade reaction for synthesis of 2′-FL in a 0.5 mL mixture, including 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM MgCl2, 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 10 mM GTP, 10 mM NADPH, 20 mM sodium hexametaphosphate (SHMP), five enzymes (PPGMK, manB, manC, gmd, wcaG) were 10 µM, and 10 µM FucT. The reaction was conducted at 30 ℃ for 12 h.

10 µL of each sample were analyzed by HPLC-RID (Shimadzu, Japan) using Aminex HPX-87 H Column (300 × 7.8 mm) for a duration of 30 min. The mobile phase was 5 mM H2SO4, with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 50 ℃. HPLC analysis was conducted on lactose and 2′-FL standards across various concentration levels, and the yields were determined by calculating the peak areas of the samples at corresponding retention times. The concentrations of all purified enzymes were determined using the coomassie brilliant blue assay, and the concentration of the FucT crude extract was quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA, v1.54d).

Optimization of cascade system conditions

Metal ions and pH

The effects of metal ions (types and concentrations) and pH on 2′-FL production were investigated. The same reaction system was performed, including 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 10 mM GTP, 10 mM NADPH, 20 mM sodium hexametaphosphate (SHMP), five enzymes (PPGMK, manB, manC, gmd, wcaG) were 10 µM, and 10 µM FucT. The reaction was conducted at 30 ℃ for 12 h. For the types of metal ions, various ions (Mg²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ni²⁺, and Cu²⁺) at a concentration of 10 mM were tested. For the concentrations of metal ions, Mg²⁺ was used at concentrations ranging from 0 to 30 mM. For pH, various buffers including 50 mM HAc-NaAc (pH 5.0 and 6.0), 50 mM PBS (pH 6.5 and 7.0), and 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5, and 8.0) were used. The reaction was conducted at 30 ℃ for 12 h. The concentration of 2′-FL was measured by HPLC-RID.

Enzymes concentration

In order to attain the optimal catalytic efficacy, the concentrations of various enzymes were optimized. A one-pot cascade reaction for the synthesis of 2′-FL was carried out in a 0.5 mL mixture containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 25 mM MgCl2, 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 10 mM GTP, 10 mM NADPH, 20 mM SHMP, 10 µM PPGMK, 10 µM manB, 10 µM manC, 10 µM gmd, 10 µM wcaG, and 10 µM FucT. The concentration of each enzyme was individually varied within the following ranges: manB, manC, gmd, and wcaG were tested at 2–24 µM, while FucT was tested at 1–10 µM. The reaction was performed at 30 °C for 12 h and then terminated. The concentration of 2′-FL was determined by HPLC-RID.

Cofactor regeneration of 2′-FL synthesis

One-pot cascade reaction containing regeneration system was 0.5 mL, including 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 25 mM MgCl2, 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 20 mM SHMP, 10 µM PPGMK, 12 µM manB, 8 µM manC, 20 µM gmd, 20 µM wcaG, and 10 µM FucT. For GTP regeneration, 2 mM GTP, 10 mM NADPH, and 5 µM of either PPK-Ft, PPK-Pa, or PPK-Rs were used. For NADPH regeneration, 10 mM GTP, 2 mM NADPH, 1U/mL G6PDH with 20 mM glucose-6-phosphate or 1U/mL Icd with 20 mM sodium isocitrate were used. For double regeneration system, 2 mM GTP, 2 mM NADPH, 5 µM PPK-Ft, 1U/mL Icd, and 20 mM sodium isocitrate were used. The reaction was carried out at 30℃ for 12 h. The concentration of 2′-FL was measured by HPLC-RID.

One-pot synthesis of 2′-FL by multi-enzyme cascade in vitro

The final one-pot cascade reaction system was 5 mL, comprising 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 25 mM MgCl2, 20 mM mannose, 20 mM lactose, 20 mM SHMP, 2 mM GTP, 2 mM NADPH, 10 µM PPGMK, 12 µM manB, 8 µM manC, 20 µM gmd, 20 µM wcaG, 10 µM FucT, 5 µM PPK-Ft, 1U/mL Icd, and 20 mM sodium isocitrate. The reaction was carried out at 30℃, and 200 µL of samples at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h were removed, and measured by HPLC-RID.
