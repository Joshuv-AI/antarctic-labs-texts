Confronting financial and political headwinds against sexual and reproductive health, rights, and justice
Authors: Chelsea Clinton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth and political leaders must seize the opportunity to build lasting systems for governance, financing, and delivery of sexual and reproductive health, rights, and justice

Recent cuts to foreign aid1 are hitting sexual and reproductive health particularly hard. Right wing political movements are pressing governments and multilateral institutions to restrict reproductive rights, exacerbating the general retrenchment in bilateral and multilateral health funding.2 Institutions that finance sexual and reproductive health commodities, set standards, and defend related rights are losing money and political support at the same time.3

The BMJ Collection on protecting global sexual and reproductive health and rights is therefore welcome and timely (www.bmj.com/collections/SRHR). It examines how these disruptions are rolling back decades of progress and identifies new opportunities to mobilise support for sexual and reproductive rights, health, and justice (SRHRJ).

Meaningful advances have been made in SRHRJ over recent decades, many of which were enabled by global coordination, including through formal governance mechanisms, donor funding, and sustained civil society action. From 2000 to 2023, global maternal mortality fell 40%.4 Millions more women around the world have access to contraception than they did a decade ago, especially in low and middle income countries,5 partly because of coordinated global financing and market shaping work that simultaneously slashed prices and expanded access to care.6

Multiple challenges

But now, as articles in the collection note,7 8 9 10 11 12 strategies that have been effective are facing ferocious financial and political headwinds despite no change in the evidence of what works for women and families. Even the institutions built to steward progress related to women broadly, and reproductive healthcare specifically, are under strain. UN80, a reform initiative of the United Nations, includes a proposed merger of UN Women and the UN Population Fund (UNFPA), two vital institutions for protecting women’s rights and health. Such a move could weaken the distinct mandates and institutional protections built around gender equality and SRHRJ.7 12

Official development assistance is estimated to have contracted by 29-46% from 2024 to 2026,13 with reproductive health among the hardest hit, while the World Health Organization, UNFPA, and other multilateral institutions face their own financial pressures.14 The US has driven much of the shock, but other major donors also are pulling back.15 US reductions alone could lead to 40-55 million additional unintended pregnancies and 12-16 million unsafe abortions across 51 countries.10 16

In the collection, McGovern and colleagues analyse the ways this funding retreat coincides with political change. Anti-rights movements are gaining influence in national governments and multilateral forums to restrict access to contraception, abortion, and LGBTQ+ health services—often borrowing the language of rights and autonomy even as they work to restrict access to those very things.8 Gher and colleagues warn that funding cuts particularly threaten feminist civil society, which in many countries provides a critical accountability layer in monitoring gaps in care access and escalating rights violations.11

At the 79th World Health Assembly in May 2026, eight countries pledged more than $175m to expand access within their countries to reproductive and maternal health supplies, including contraceptives.17 But, domestic financing cannot simply replace aid, at least not now or in the near term. Many governments will need to spend more on reproductive health, but the lowest income and most indebted countries cannot replace donor finance on the timetable the cuts impose.

We know what works in terms of advancing SRHRJ. At this moment of upheaval and uncertainty, the immediate call is to keep services running as external funding falls. At the same time, leaders across the public, private, and non-profit sectors, including in health, have a unique opportunity—a shared responsibility—to build governance structures and financing, procurement, and delivery systems that can withstand the next donor exit.

Role of philanthropy

Philanthropy has a role, though not as a replacement for public financing. Catalytic private investments have the agility and risk tolerance to shape a new era of sustainable health financing in ways that traditional donor channels cannot. This is particularly true when it comes to safe abortion access.18 Donor withdrawal weakens the groups that keep providing services that women need and deserve. Local organisations need flexible funding to support continuation of care, document denials of care, challenge restrictive laws, broadly support providers, and defend lawful abortion access.

Philanthropy can also work further upstream, investing in and partnering with the government systems through which care, including sexual and reproductive health, is financed and delivered. In sub-Saharan African countries such as Malawi and Zambia, the Clinton Health Access Initiative, a global non-profit foundation that I help lead, has supported governments in developing mechanisms to track and map health resources, reduce fragmented funding, and guide external investment towards national priorities, including more strategic responses to financing gaps, as well as improved forecasting, procurement, and supply chain capacity.19 When funding for family planning or maternal health declines, this capability enables governments to identify emerging gaps, safeguard access to contraception and essential maternal care, and allocate limited domestic resources to areas of greatest need.

Neither domestic funding nor philanthropy is sufficient alone. Governments under fiscal constraints cannot fully replace disappearing aid, and philanthropy cannot fund national commodity and service delivery needs indefinitely. What both can do is work together with a multilateral system focused on the functions countries cannot provide alone, from pooled purchasing (which can enable lower prices and more favourable contract terms through greater collective bargaining power) to accountability, and leave governments with more control over financing, commodity procurement, and service delivery before the next donor cuts arrive. The new BMJ Collection charts both the scale of what is at risk and the concrete paths—fiscal, institutional, and community led—through which progress can be protected and advanced.
