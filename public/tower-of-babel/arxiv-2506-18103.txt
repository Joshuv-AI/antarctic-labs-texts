A study of a family of self-referential sequences: asymptotics, exact forms and combinatorial connections
Benoit Cloitre

Abstract
We introduce and analyze a three-parameter family of self-referential integer sequences S ⁡ ( x , y , z ) S(x,y,z) : starting from a ⁡ ( 1 ) = x a(1)=x , each term advances by y y when the index k k has already appeared as a value and by z z otherwise. This simple rule generates a surprising zoo of behaviors, many of which are catalogued—albeit in a rather unstructured fashion—in the OEIS. Whenever y > z > 0 y>z>0 , we prove that the density a ⁡ ( k ) / k a(k)/k converges to the positive root of r 2 − z ​ r − ( y − z ) = 0 r^{2}-zr-(y-z)=0 . Two subfamilies, S ⁡ ( x , Z + 1 , Z ) S(x,Z+1,Z) and S ⁡ ( x , Z , Z + 1 ) S(x,Z,Z+1) , yield explicit non-homogeneous Beatty sequences, providing explicit formulas for numerous OEIS entries. For y = 0 y=0 and z ≥ 2 z\geq 2 , the sequences eventually become periodic and satisfy linear recurrences. Critical cases with a zero discriminant unveil geometric patterns on triangular, square, and hexagonal lattices. Finally, via tree-like representations we uncover a tight link with meta-Fibonacci recurrences and highlight unexpected phenomena when y < 0 y<0 . These results position S ⁡ ( x , y , z ) S(x,y,z) as a unifying framework connecting additive combinatorics, number theory, and discrete dynamics.



1
Introduction and definitions

Self-referential integer sequences, where the rule for generating the next term depends on the sequence’s history, often yield intricate global structures from simple local rules. Their appeal lies in understanding the emergence of complex, predictable behavior from recursive definitions. Such sequences frequently appear in combinatorial number theory and have connections to fractal geometry, dynamical systems, and combinatorics.

In our 2003 paper, "Numerical analogues of Aronson’s sequence"
[
3
]
, we announced our intention to study the following family of sequences.

Definition 1.1:
The
S(x,y,z)
family

Let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}
. The sequence
(a(k))_{k\geq 1}
is defined by:

(i)

a(1)=x
.

(ii)

For
k>1
, let
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}=\{a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\}
. Then,

a(k)=\begin{cases}a(k-1)+y&\text{if }k\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}\quad(\text{a \emph{hit}}),\\
a(k-1)+z&\text{if }k\notin\mathcal{A}_{k-1}\quad(\text{a \emph{miss}}).\end{cases}

At the time, a lack of a cohesive framework and general results compelled us to postpone a detailed study. Over twenty years later, this paper fulfills that original goal. We believe this work demonstrates the rich structure of these sequences, offering significant points of interest for number theorists and combinatorists alike, particularly in the areas of combinatorics on words and enumerative problems.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we establish the general asymptotic linearity of the sequences for the case
y>z>0
. Section
3
is dedicated to two important subfamilies that generate non-homogeneous Beatty sequences, providing explicit formulas for many OEIS entries. In Section
4
, we analyze the case where
y=0
, which leads to ultimately periodic sequences satisfying linear recurrences. Section
5
explores cases where the characteristic discriminant vanishes, revealing deep connections to combinatorial objects like triangular, square, and hexagonal numbers. Finally, in Section
6
, we establish a formal bridge between our sequences and the theory of meta-Fibonacci sequences, uncovering a shared combinatorial foundation.

2
Asymptotic linearity for
y>z>0

We first establish that when the hit increment
y
exceeds the miss
increment
z
, the sequence exhibits a stable asymptotic slope. Let

r_{0}
be the positive root of the characteristic equation
r^{2}-zr-(y-z)=0
:

r_{0}=\frac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}+4(y-z)}}{2}.

This root satisfies the equilibrium equation
r_{0}=z+\frac{y-z}{r_{0}}
.

Theorem 2.1:
Main asymptotic result

For a sequence
S(x,y,z)
with
y>z>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}=r_{0}.

Proof

Step 1: Exact relation.

By definition:

a(n)-a(n-1)=z+(y-z)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{n\in\mathcal{A}_{n-1}\}}.

Summing for
k=2
to
n
:

a(n)-a(1)=z(n-1)+(y-z)H_{n},

where
H_{n}=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{k\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}\}}
is the number of hits up to step
n
. Thus:

\boxed{a(n)=x+z(n-1)+(y-z)H_{n}.}

Step 2: Link between
H_{n}
and the counting function.

Let
N(t)=\max\{k\mid a(k)\leq t\}
be the counting function of the sequence.

•

Key property:

k\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}
if and only if
k
is in the image of the sequence, i.e.,
\exists j<k
such that
a(j)=k
.

•

Thus
H_{n}=|\{k\in\{2,\dots,n\}\mid k\in\operatorname{im}(a)\}|
.

•

Since
N(n)=\max\{k\mid a(k)\leq n\}
, we have:

H_{n}=N(n)-\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in\operatorname{im}(a)\}}+O(1)=N(n)+O(1),

since
\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in\operatorname{im}(a)\}}
is constant (0 or 1). We deduce:

\frac{H_{n}}{n}=\frac{N(n)}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Step 3: Asymptotic analysis via
\limsup
and
\liminf
.

Let:

\overline{r}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n},\quad\underline{r}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}.

From the equation in Step 1:

\frac{a(n)}{n}=\frac{x}{n}+z\frac{n-1}{n}+(y-z)\frac{H_{n}}{n}.

Taking upper and lower limits, and using
\frac{H_{n}}{n}=\frac{N(n)}{n}+O(1/n)
:

\boxed{\overline{r}=z+(y-z)\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{N(n)}{n},\quad\underline{r}=z+(y-z)\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{N(n)}{n}.}

Step 4: Duality between sequence and counting function.

Lemma 2.2:
Density-counting duality relation

Let
(a(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a strictly increasing sequence of real numbers such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}a(n)=+\infty
.
Let
N:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{N}
be the counting function defined by
N(t)=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid a(k)\leq t\}
.
Define the limit superior and limit inferior of the sequence’s rate of growth as:

\overline{r}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{r}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}.

Then the following duality relations hold:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}=\frac{1}{\underline{r}}\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}=\frac{1}{\overline{r}}.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

The proof is based on the fundamental inequality derived from the definition of the counting function
N(t)
.

Step 1: The fundamental inequality.

By definition,
a(N(t))
is the largest term in the sequence that is less than or equal to
t
. This directly implies:

a(N(t))\leq t<a(N(t)+1).

This inequality holds for all
t\geq a(1)
.

Step 2: Bounding the ratio
N(t)/t
.

We manipulate the fundamental inequality to establish a tight bound on the ratio
N(t)/t
.
From the left side,
a(N(t))\leq t
, we invert to get
\frac{1}{t}\leq\frac{1}{a(N(t))}
, which implies:

\frac{N(t)}{t}\leq\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t))}.

From the right side,
t<a(N(t)+1)
, we similarly get
\frac{1}{a(N(t)+1)}<\frac{1}{t}
, which implies:

\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t)+1)}<\frac{N(t)}{t}.

Combining these gives the sandwich inequality:

\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t)+1)}<\frac{N(t)}{t}\leq\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t))}.\quad(*)

Step 3: Asymptotic analysis.

We now analyze the limits of the bounding terms in
(*)
as
t\to\infty
. Let
v_{k}=k/a(k)
. We are comparing the asymptotic behavior of
v_{N(t)}
with that of the sequence
(v_{k})
. The function
N(t)
is a non-decreasing step function from
[a(1),\infty)
onto
\mathbb{N}
. Because it is non-decreasing and surjective onto the integers, the set of accumulation points of the function
v_{N(t)}
as
t\to\infty
is identical to the set of accumulation points of the sequence
(v_{k})_{k\to\infty}
. Therefore, their respective limsup and liminf are equal.

Let’s apply this to our bounds. For the right-hand side of
(*)
:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t))}=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{k}{a(k)}=\frac{1}{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{a(k)}{k}}=\frac{1}{\underline{r}}.

For the left-hand side of
(*)
, we rewrite it as
\frac{N(t)}{N(t)+1}\cdot\frac{N(t)+1}{a(N(t)+1)}
. Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{N(t)+1}=1
, we have:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t)+1)}=1\cdot\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)+1}{a(N(t)+1)}=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{k}{a(k)}=\frac{1}{\underline{r}}.

Step 4: Conclusion by the squeeze theorem.

Taking the
\limsup
across the inequality
(*)
yields:

\frac{1}{\underline{r}}\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}\leq\frac{1}{\underline{r}}.

This proves the first relation:
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}=\frac{1}{\underline{r}}
.

The argument for the second relation is identical. Taking the
\liminf
across
(*)
:

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t)+1)}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{a(N(t))}.

Both the left and right bounds evaluate to
\frac{1}{\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{a(k)}{k}}=\frac{1}{\overline{r}}
. Thus:

\frac{1}{\overline{r}}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}\leq\frac{1}{\overline{r}}.

This proves the second relation:
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t)}{t}=\frac{1}{\overline{r}}
.

\blacksquare

Step 5: System of equations and uniqueness of the limit.

Injecting Lemma
2
into the equations from Step 3:

\overline{r}=z+\frac{y-z}{\underline{r}},\quad\underline{r}=z+\frac{y-z}{\overline{r}}.

Multiplying the first equation by
\underline{r}
, the second by
\overline{r}
:

\overline{r}\underline{r}=z\underline{r}+(y-z),\quad\underline{r}\overline{r}=z\overline{r}+(y-z).

By equality of the right-hand sides:

z\underline{r}+(y-z)=z\overline{r}+(y-z)\implies z\underline{r}=z\overline{r}\implies\underline{r}=\overline{r}\quad(\text{since }z>0).

The limit
r=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}
therefore exists.

Step 6: Solving the characteristic equation.

The equation
r=z+\frac{y-z}{r}
becomes
r^{2}-zr-(y-z)=0
. Since
r>0
(as
a(n)\geq n\min(y,z)+O(1)
), we have:

\boxed{r=\frac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}+4(y-z)}}{2}=r_{0}.}

\blacksquare

3
Beatty sequences

In this section, we show that sequences from the families
S(x,Z+1,Z)
(family A)
and
S(x,Z,Z+1)
(family B) correspond to non-homogeneous Beatty sequences. To distinguish these specific integer values from the formal parameters of the general definition, we let
Z\geq 2
be a fixed integer and consider cases where the increments
(y,z)
are consecutive.

These two specific families are particularly remarkable as they account for a significant number of entries in the OEIS, highlighting their fundamental importance in the landscape of integer sequences.

3.1
The family A:
S(x,Z+1,Z)

Proposition 3.1:
Closed-form representations for
S(x,Z+1,Z)

The irrational slope governing this family is

r_{A}=\frac{Z+\sqrt{Z^{2}+4}}{2}.

For different starting values
x
, the sequences are given by the
following Beatty sequences:

•

For
x=0
:

Z=2\implies a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor(1+\sqrt{2})\,n-\tfrac{3+4\sqrt{2}}{2+\sqrt{2}}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 2.

Z\geq 3\implies a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor r_{A}\,n-\tfrac{(Z+1)\,r_{A}-1}{r_{A}+1}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 1.

•

For
x=1,Z\geq 2
:

a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor r_{A}\,n-\tfrac{Z\,r_{A}-1}{r_{A}+1}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 1.

•

For
x\geq 2,Z\geq 2
:

a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor r_{A}\,n-\tfrac{(Z-x)\,r_{A}-1}{r_{A}+1}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 1.

For the specific case
x=Z+1
, this simplifies to the quasi-homogeneous
Beatty sequence

a(n)=\lfloor n\,r_{A}+1\rfloor=\lceil n\,r_{A}\rceil.

The proof of the general closed-form representations follows the exact same logic as the proof of the special case presented below.

Theorem 3.2:
Special case for
x=Z+1

Let
Z\geq 2
be an integer. Define
r_{A}=\frac{Z+\sqrt{Z^{2}+4}}{2}
and the
sequence

a(n)\;=\;\bigl\lceil n\,r_{A}\bigr\rceil.

Then
a(n)
satisfies the self-referential definition of the sequence

S(Z+1,\,Z+1,\,Z)
.

Proof

We proceed in three steps.

Step 1: Properties of
r_{A}
.

r_{A}
is the positive root of
r^{2}-Zr-1=0
, hence:

r_{A}^{2}=Zr_{A}+1.

We verify that
Z<r_{A}<Z+1
(since
r_{A}>Z
and
r_{A}^{2}=Zr_{A}+1<(Z+1)r_{A}
). Thus:

\lfloor r_{A}\rfloor=Z,\quad a(1)=\lceil r_{A}\rceil=Z+1.

Let
\alpha=r_{A}-Z\in(0,1)
. The equation
r_{A}^{2}-Zr_{A}=1
gives:

\alpha=\frac{1}{r_{A}}.

Step 2: Functional equations.

For all
n\geq 1
, write
a(n)=nr_{A}+\varepsilon_{n}
with
\varepsilon_{n}\in(0,1)
. For any integer
k
:

a(a(n)+k)=\left\lceil(a(n)+k)r_{A}\right\rceil=\left\lceil nr_{A}^{2}+kr_{A}+\varepsilon_{n}r_{A}\right\rceil.

Substituting
r_{A}^{2}=Zr_{A}+1
and
nr_{A}=a(n)-\varepsilon_{n}
:

a(a(n)+k)=Za(n)+n+\left\lceil kr_{A}+\varepsilon_{n}\alpha\right\rceil,

where
\alpha=r_{A}-Z
. Let
\delta_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}\alpha\in(0,\alpha)
, so:

\boxed{a(a(n)+k)=Za(n)+n+\left\lceil kr_{A}+\delta_{n}\right\rceil.}\quad(\star)

Step 3: Verification of the self-referential condition.

•

Increment property:
Since
Z<r_{A}<Z+1
, for all
k\geq 2
:

d_{k}=a(k)-a(k-1)\in\{Z,Z+1\}.

•

Case "miss" (
k\notin\{a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\}
):

There exists a unique
n\geq 1
such that
a(n)<k<a(n+1)
. Write
k=a(n)+m
with
1\leq m<a(n+1)-a(n)
.

From
(\star)
, we have:

a(k)=a(a(n)+m)=Za(n)+n+\left\lceil mr_{A}+\delta_{n}\right\rceil,

a(k-1)=a(a(n)+m-1)=Za(n)+n+\left\lceil(m-1)r_{A}+\delta_{n}\right\rceil.

Thus:

d_{k}=\left\lceil mr_{A}+\delta_{n}\right\rceil-\left\lceil(m-1)r_{A}+\delta_{n}\right\rceil.

Let
y=(m-1)r_{A}+\delta_{n}
. Then:

d_{k}=\lceil y+r_{A}\rceil-\lceil y\rceil.

Since
k<a(n+1)
, we have
m<r_{A}-\varepsilon_{n}
(from
a(n)+m<(n+1)r_{A}
). This implies
m+\varepsilon_{n}<r_{A}
.

The fractional part of
y
is
\{y\}=\alpha(m-1+\varepsilon_{n})
, and:

\{y\}+\alpha=\alpha(m+\varepsilon_{n})<\alpha r_{A}=1,

hence:

\lceil y+r_{A}\rceil-\lceil y\rceil=\lfloor r_{A}\rfloor=Z.

Thus,
d_{k}=Z
.

•

Case "hit" (
k\in\{a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\}
):

Since
d_{k}\in\{Z,Z+1\}
and
d_{k}=Z
for misses, we necessarily have
d_{k}=Z+1
.

Conclusion:

We have
a(1)=Z+1
, and for
k\geq 2
:

a(k)-a(k-1)=\begin{cases}Z+1&\text{if }k\in\{a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\},\\
Z&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which exactly corresponds to the definition of
S(Z+1,Z+1,Z)
.

\blacksquare

3.2
The family B:
S(x,Z,Z+1)

Proposition 3.3:
Closed-form representations for
S(x,Z,Z+1)

The irrational slope for this family is

r_{B}=\frac{Z+1+\sqrt{Z^{2}+2Z-3}}{2}.

The sequences are given by:

•

For
x=0,Z\geq 2
:

a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor r_{B}\,n-\tfrac{(Z-1)\,r_{B}+1}{r_{B}-1}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 1.

•

For
x=1,Z\geq 2
:

a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor r_{B}\,n-\tfrac{(Z-2)\,r_{B}+1}{r_{B}-1}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 2.

•

For
x\geq 2,Z\geq 2
:

a(n)=\Bigl\lfloor r_{B}\,n-\tfrac{(Z-x)\,r_{B}+1}{r_{B}-1}\Bigr\rfloor,\;n\geq 1.

For the specific case
x=Z+1
, this simplifies to the quasi-homogeneous
Beatty sequence

a(n)=\lfloor n\,r_{B}+1\rfloor=\lceil n\,r_{B}\rceil.

Theorem 3.4:
Special case for
x=Z+1

Let
Z\geq 2
be an integer. Define
r_{B}=\frac{Z+1+\sqrt{Z^{2}+2Z-3}}{2}
and the sequence

a(n)\;=\;\bigl\lceil n\,r_{B}\bigr\rceil.

Then
a(n)
satisfies the self-referential definition of the sequence
S(Z+1,\,Z,\,Z+1)
.

Proof

The proof follows the same structure as Theorem
3.1
.

Step 1: Properties of
r_{B}
.

r_{B}
is the positive root of
r^{2}-(Z+1)r+1=0
, hence:

r_{B}^{2}=(Z+1)r_{B}-1.

We verify that
Z<r_{B}<Z+1
. Thus:

\lfloor r_{B}\rfloor=Z,\quad a(1)=\lceil r_{B}\rceil=Z+1.

From the equation, we deduce:

r_{B}-(Z+1)=-\frac{1}{r_{B}}.

Step 2: Functional equations.

For all
n\geq 1
, write
a(n)=nr_{B}+\varepsilon_{n}
with
\varepsilon_{n}\in(0,1)
. For any integer
k
:

a(a(n)+k)=\left\lceil(a(n)+k)r_{B}\right\rceil=\left\lceil nr_{B}^{2}+kr_{B}+\varepsilon_{n}r_{B}\right\rceil.

Substituting
r_{B}^{2}=(Z+1)r_{B}-1
and
nr_{B}=a(n)-\varepsilon_{n}
:

a(a(n)+k)=(Z+1)a(n)-n+\left\lceil kr_{B}-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{r_{B}}\right\rceil.

Let
\delta_{n}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{r_{B}}\in(-\frac{1}{r_{B}},0)
, so:

\boxed{a(a(n)+k)=(Z+1)a(n)-n+\left\lceil kr_{B}+\delta_{n}\right\rceil.}\quad(\star)

Key cases:

•

If
0\leq k\leq Z-1
:
Since
0<\frac{k+\varepsilon_{n}}{r_{B}}<1
, we have
-\frac{k+\varepsilon_{n}}{r_{B}}\in(-1,0)
, so:

a(a(n)+k)=(Z+1)a(n)-n+(Z+1)k.\quad(\dagger)

•

If
-Z\leq k\leq-1
(i.e.,
k=-m
with
1\leq m\leq Z
):
Since
0<\frac{m-\varepsilon_{n}}{r_{B}}<1
, we have:

a(a(n)+k)=(Z+1)a(n)-n+(Z+1)k+1.\quad(\dagger\dagger)

Step 3: Verification of the self-referential condition.

•

Case "miss" (
k\notin\{a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\}
):

There exists a unique
n\geq 1
such that
a(n)<k<a(n+1)
. Write
k=a(n)+m
with
m\geq 1
.
Since
m\geq 1
, we apply
(\dagger)
:

d_{k}=a(k)-a(k-1)=[(Z+1)a(n)-n+(Z+1)m]-[(Z+1)a(n)-n+(Z+1)(m-1)]=Z+1.

•

Case "hit" (
k\in\{a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\}
):

By complementarity and since
d_{k}\in\{Z,Z+1\}
, we necessarily have
d_{k}=Z
.

Conclusion:

The increments follow exactly the definition of
S(Z+1,Z,Z+1)
.

\blacksquare

The Case
y=0
,
z\geq 2
: Linear Recurrences

When the hit increment is zero, the sequences exhibit ultimately periodic behavior and satisfy linear recurrences.

Theorem 4.1:
Linear recurrence for
y=0

Let
(a(k))
be
S(x,0,z)
with
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and integer
z\geq 2
. Then
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{a(k)}{k}=z-1
, and for sufficiently large
k
,
a(k)
satisfies

a(k)-a(k-1)-a(k-z)+a(k-z-1)=0.

Proof

We establish a sequence of properties that build upon each other to prove both claims.

Property 1 (Modular invariance):
All terms satisfy
a(j)\equiv x\pmod{z}
.

Proof:
By induction. Base case:
a(1)=x\equiv x\pmod{z}
.
Inductive step: Assume
a(1),\dots,a(k-1)\equiv x\pmod{z}
.
If
k\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}
, then
a(k)=a(k-1)+0\equiv x\pmod{z}
.
If
k\notin\mathcal{A}_{k-1}
, then
a(k)=a(k-1)+z\equiv x\pmod{z}
.

Property 2 (Membership characterization):
For sufficiently large
k
,

k\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}
if and only if
k\equiv x\pmod{z}
.

Proof:
From property 1,
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}\subseteq\{x+jz:j\geq 0\}
.
Since
a(n)\to\infty
and increments are non-negative, for large enough
k
,
all values
x+jz
with
x+jz<k
appear in
\mathcal{A}_{k-1}
.
Thus for large
k
:
k\in\mathcal{A}_{k-1}\iff k\equiv x\pmod{z}
.

Property 3 (Periodic increments):
For large
k
, the increment sequence becomes periodic with period
z
:

\Delta a(k)=a(k)-a(k-1)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }k\equiv x\pmod{z}\\
z&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof:
Direct consequence of property 2 and the definition of
S(x,0,z)
.

Property 4 (Linear recurrence):
For large
k
,
a(k)
satisfies

a(k)-a(k-1)-a(k-z)+a(k-z-1)=0
.

Proof:
From property 3,
\Delta a(k)
has period
z
, so
\Delta a(k)=\Delta a(k-z)
.
Therefore:

\displaystyle a(k)-a(k-1)

\displaystyle=\Delta a(k)=\Delta a(k-z)=a(k-z)-a(k-z-1)

Rearranging gives the claimed recurrence.

Property 5 (Asymptotic slope):

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{a(k)}{k}=z-1
.

Proof:
In each period of length
z
, exactly one increment is 0 (when
k\equiv x\pmod{z}
)
and
z-1
increments are
z
. Thus the average increment per step is:

\frac{1\cdot 0+(z-1)\cdot z}{z}=\frac{z(z-1)}{z}=z-1.

Since the increments become exactly periodic, this average becomes the asymptotic slope.

\blacksquare

5
Vanishing discriminant and combinatorial connections

When the discriminant of the characteristic equation vanishes (i.e., when
z^{2}+4(y-z)=0
), the sequences exhibit particularly interesting combinatorial structures. For integer parameters, this occurs when
z=2K
and
y=2K-K^{2}
. We explore several cases that reveal connections to combinatorics.

5.1
The sequence
S(3,1,2)
(OEIS
A080036
)

This sequence corresponds to the Ramsey core numbers
rc(2,n)
and is a notable case of a vanishing discriminant (
z^{2}+4(y-z)=2^{2}+4(1-2)=0
).

Theorem 5.1:
Ramsey core numbers

The sequence
S(3,1,2)
is given by the closed form

a(n)=n+\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{8n-7}+3}{2}\right\rfloor.

A key property of this sequence is that its set of values,
\operatorname{im}(a)
, is precisely the set of positive integers that are not of the form
s_{j}=\frac{j(j+1)}{2}+1
for
j\geq 1
(i.e., triangular numbers plus one).

Proof

Let’s define the function
f(n)=n+\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{8n-7}+3}{2}\right\rfloor
and show it satisfies the recurrence for
a(n)=S(3,1,2)
. The proof proceeds by induction, showing that
a(n)=f(n)
for all
n\geq 1
.

Step 1: Base case.

For
n=1
, the definition gives
a(1)=3
. The formula gives
f(1)=1+\lfloor(1+3)/2\rfloor=1+2=3
. The base case holds.

Step 2: Analysis of the increment of
f(n)
.

Let’s analyze the increment
\Delta f(n)=f(n)-f(n-1)
.

\Delta f(n)=\left(n+\lfloor\dots\rfloor\right)-\left((n-1)+\lfloor\dots\rfloor\right)=1+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{8n-7}+3}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{8(n-1)-7}+3}{2}\right\rfloor\right).

The difference between the floor terms is either 0 or 1. It is exactly 1 if and only if an integer lies in the interval
(\frac{\sqrt{8n-15}+3}{2},\frac{\sqrt{8n-7}+3}{2}]
. This happens if and only if
n
is of the form
s_{k}=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+1
for some integer
k\geq 2
.
Therefore, the increment of
f(n)
follows a simple rule:

f(n)-f(n-1)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }n=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+1\text{ for some }k\geq 2\quad\text{(e.g., }n=2,4,7,11,\dots\text{)},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Step 3: Inductive equivalence.

We need to show that the increment rule for
a(n)
is identical to that of
f(n)
.
The increment for
a(n)
is:

a(n)-a(n-1)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }n\notin\{a(1),\dots,a(n-1)\}\quad\text{(a "miss")},\\
1&\text{if }n\in\{a(1),\dots,a(n-1)\}\quad\text{(a "hit")}.\end{cases}

Our inductive hypothesis is that
a(k)=f(k)
for all
k<n
. To complete the proof, we must show that the condition for an increment of 2 is the same for both sequences. That is:

n\notin\{f(1),\dots,f(n-1)\}\iff n=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+1\text{ for some }k\geq 2.

This statement is equivalent to showing that the set of values
not
taken by the function
f
(its avoided integers) is precisely the set of numbers
\{s_{k}=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+1\mid k\geq 2\}
. This is a known property of the function
f(n)
.

Since
a(n-1)=f(n-1)
by hypothesis, and we have just shown that
a(n)-a(n-1)
and
f(n)-f(n-1)
are equal for all
n
, it follows that
a(n)=f(n)
for all
n\geq 1
.

\blacksquare

for

, which correspond to the leftmost points in each row of a triangular grid, as illustrated in Figure

5.2
The sequence
S(4,1,2)
(OEIS
A217334
)

This sequence, also corresponding to a vanishing discriminant case, has a powerful combinatorial interpretation related to covering a grid with squares.

Theorem 5.2:
Thumbtack sequence

The sequence
S(4,1,2)
has the closed form

a(n)=n+\lfloor\sqrt{4n-3}\rfloor+2.

Combinatorially, it represents the minimum number of vertices required to define
n
unit squares placed in an outward spiral on the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
grid, starting from the origin.

Proof

Let
g(n)=n+\lfloor\sqrt{4n-3}\rfloor+2
. We prove by induction that
g(n)
is identical to
a(n)
generated by the
S(4,1,2)
rule.

Step 1: Base case.

For
n=1
, the rule gives
a(1)=4
. The formula gives
g(1)=1+\lfloor\sqrt{1}\rfloor+2=4
. The base case holds.

Step 2: Analysis of the increment of
g(n)
.

The increment
\Delta g(n)=g(n)-g(n-1)
is:

\Delta g(n)=1+\left(\lfloor\sqrt{4n-3}\rfloor-\lfloor\sqrt{4(n-1)-3}\rfloor\right).

The difference of the floor terms is 1 (making the total increment 2) if and only if the term
\sqrt{4n-3}
crosses an integer value. This happens precisely when
4n-3
is slightly larger than a perfect square
k^{2}
. The values of
n
where this jump occurs are of the form
n=\lfloor k^{2}/4\rfloor+1
. These points correspond to the "misses". For all other values of
n
, the increment is 1.

Step 3: Inductive equivalence.

We must show that the increment of
a(n)=S(4,1,2)
matches that of
g(n)
at every step. The increment of
a(n)
is 2 if
n
is a "miss" (
n\notin\{a(1),\dots,a(n-1)\}
) and 1 if
n
is a "hit".
Assuming
a(k)=g(k)
for
k<n
, we need to show that
n
is a miss for the sequence
a(n)
if and only if
n
is a "jump point" for the function
g(n)
.
This equivalence can be established by showing that the set of integers not in the image of
g
corresponds to the set of jump points.

Geometric connection:

A "hit" (
n\in\operatorname{im}(g)
) means that adding the
n
-th square requires only one new vertex (increment 1), as it can be placed adjacent to two existing squares. A "miss" means the
n
-th square starts a new "layer" or corner of the spiral, requiring two new vertices (increment 2). This happens exactly at the jump points of the floor function
\lfloor\sqrt{4n-3}\rfloor
. This step-by-step construction is visualized in Figure
2
.
Since the initial values and all subsequent increments match, we conclude that
a(n)=g(n)
for all
n\geq 1
.

\blacksquare

.

5.3
The sequence
S(5,1,2)
(OEIS
A080353
)

This case reveals a surprisingly deep and elegant connection to hexagonal lattices. While its closed form is complex, it precisely captures the recursive "hit/miss" dynamic.

Theorem 5.3:
Hexagonal lattice sequence

The sequence
S(5,1,2)
is given by the closed form:

a(n)=n+3m+1+\left\lfloor\frac{n-1-\frac{3m(m-1)}{2}}{m}\right\rfloor,

where the parameter
m
, corresponding to the layer of a hexagonal spiral, is given by:

m=\left\lceil\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+\frac{8n}{3}}}{2}\right\rceil.

Proof outline and geometric interpretation

A full algebraic proof showing that this closed form satisfies the
S(5,1,2)
recurrence is technical and omitted for brevity. The logic relies on demonstrating that the increment of the closed form,
\Delta a(n)=a(n)-a(n-1)
, is equal to 2 if and only if the index
n
is a "miss" for the recursive sequence.

This set of "misses" has a rich geometric structure tied to the hexagonal lattice. The misses can be decomposed into two classes, as visualized in Figure
3
:

•

Primary misses
: Indices of the form
H_{k}=\frac{3k(k-1)}{2}+1
for
k\geq 1
(e.g.,
1,4,10,19,\dots
). These correspond to vertices that initiate new hexagonal layers or shells in the spiral growth.

•

Secondary misses
: Additional indices (e.g.,
2,3,6,8,\dots
). These correspond to other "corner" or "edge" vertices required to complete a layer before the next primary miss is reached.

The closed form perfectly models this complex distribution of miss events, confirming the deep connection between the simple
S(5,1,2)
recurrence and the geometry of hexagonal lattices.

6
Connections to deeper combinatorics and algorithm analysis

Beyond the connections to classical number theory (Beatty sequences) and geometric lattices, the
S(x,y,z)
family reveals deep ties to other areas of modern combinatorics, notably the study of tree structures, the combinatorics of words, and the analysis of algorithms.

6.1
Connection to trees and Meta-Fibonacci sequences

The connection between our
S(x,y,z)
sequences and meta-Fibonacci sequences can be made precise through the combinatorial work of Ruskey and Deugau
[
7
]
. Their results establish a formal bridge between a subfamily of our sequences and a well-studied class of meta-Fibonacci recurrences, revealing a shared combinatorial foundation related to k-ary trees.

The cornerstone of their work is the sequence
a_{s,k}(n)
, defined by the meta-Fibonacci recurrence relation:

a_{s,k}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{s,k}(n-i-(s-1)-a_{s,k}(n-i)).

For the case
s=0
, which is central to our connection,
a_{0,k}(n)
counts the number of leaves in a forest of complete k-ary trees with a total of
n
nodes.

They then consider the self-referential sequence, which they denote
b_{k}(n)
, defined as:

b_{k}(n)=\begin{cases}k+1,&\text{if }n=1;\\
b_{k}(n-1)+1,&\text{if }n\in\{b_{k}(1),\ldots,b_{k}(n-1)\};\\
b_{k}(n-1)+k+1,&\text{if }n\notin\{b_{k}(1),\ldots,b_{k}(n-1)\}.\end{cases}

This definition corresponds exactly to our family of sequences
S(k+1,1,k+1)
.

Ruskey et Deugau 6.1

For all
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
, the sequence
b_{k}(n)=S(k+1,1,k+1)
is given by the explicit, non-recursive formula:

b_{k}(n)=n+k\cdot a_{0,k}(n).

This identity is powerful, as it reveals that the "hit/miss" rule for this subfamily is governed by the number of leaves in growing k-ary trees. For example, for
k=2
, the sequence
S(3,1,3)
is given by
A045412
. This sequence, in turn, has a further, surprising connection to computer science. As noted by C. J. Quines, it precisely describes the indices of all terms greater than 1 in the sequence
A182105
, which counts the number of elements being merged at each step of a bottom-up merge sort algorithm. Remarkably, the relevance of this sequence extends to modern AI research, as it appears in recent work on strategy scheduling for automated theorem provers
[
1
]
.

6.2
Connection to Combinatorics on Words

Other choices of parameters yield sequences that are best understood through the lens of combinatorics on words, where the sequence gives the positions of certain letters in an infinite word generated by a morphism.

A prime example is the sequence
S(1,2,4)
, which is identical to
A080580
. As proven on its OEIS page, this sequence gives the positions of the letter ’0’ in the infinite word generated as the fixed point of the morphism:

\sigma:\begin{cases}0\to 01\\
1\to 1101\end{cases}

Starting with ’0’ and repeatedly applying
\sigma
gives:

0\to 01\to 011101\to 0111011101110101\to\dots

The positions of the ’0’s in this infinite word are
1,5,9,13,15,\dots
, which exactly match the sequence
S(1,2,4)
. This shows that the simple hit/miss recurrence can act as a compact and efficient generator for otherwise complex morphic sequences.
