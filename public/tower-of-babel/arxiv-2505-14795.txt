Controllability and Inverse Problems for Hyperbolic and Dispersive Equations with Dynamic Boundary Conditions
S. E. Chorfi, L. Maniar, R. Morales

Abstract
. This review examines classical and recent results on controllability and inverse problems for hyperbolic and dispersive equations with dynamic boundary conditions. We aim to illustrate the applicability of Carleman estimates to establish exact controllability of such equations and derive Lipschitz stability estimates for inverse problems of source terms and coefficients with general dynamic boundary conditions. We highlight the challenges associated with dynamic boundary conditions compared to classical static ones. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of open problems and future research directions.

1.
Introduction and motivation

Hyperbolic and dispersive equations with dynamic boundary conditions model various complex physical phenomena, such as wave propagation in macroscopic or microscopic media, where the boundary behavior changes over time. This complexity can also reflect scenarios like vibrating membranes, oscillations, or fluid flows, where the boundaries interact dynamically with the medium. On the other hand, the controllability and inverse problems of these equations with dynamic boundary conditions involve understanding how we can design controls or reconstruct unknown parameters from partial measurements.

In this paper, we examine hyperbolic and dispersive equations subject to dynamic boundary conditions, focusing on aspects such as exact controllability and inverse problems. These topics are well understood in the context of static boundary conditions like Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin, mixed, and periodic conditions, etc. We mainly focus on wave and Schrödinger equations as prototype models. We refer to
[
51
]
for the null controllability of parabolic equations with dynamic conditions. The reader can also refer to the recent review
[
18
]
for controllability and inverse problems for parabolic equations with dynamic conditions.

1.1.
Literature on hyperbolic equations

The exact controllability problems of wave equations with static boundary conditions are fairly well-studied, see for instance
[
45
]
,
[
64
]
and the references therein. Namely, the controllability problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{tt}-\Delta y=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y=\mathbbm{1}_{\Gamma_{\star}}v&\text{ in }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y(\cdot,0)=y_{0},\;y_{t}(\cdot,0)=y_{1}&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
T>0
is a fixed time,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
,
y
is the displacement state,
y_{0},y_{1}
are the initial data,
v\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))
is a control acting only on a portion
\Gamma_{\star}\subset\partial\Omega
suitably chosen, and
\mathbbm{1}_{\Gamma_{\star}}
is the indicator function of
\Gamma_{\star}
. The problem of exact controllability of (
1.1
) at time
T
concerns whether there exists a control
v\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))
such that we have at time
T
:

y(\cdot,T)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{t}(\cdot,T)=0.

(1.2)

Similarly, one can also deal with the case of a distributed control acting on a subset
\omega\subset\Omega
(see
[
44
]
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{tt}-\Delta y=\mathbbm{1}_{\omega}v&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y=0&\text{ in }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y(\cdot,0)=y_{0},\;y_{t}(\cdot,0)=y_{1}&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.3)

Generally speaking, the controllability of such models depends on the so-called Geometric Control Condition (GCC in short) and a time condition; see, e.g.,
[
2
]
. The GCC asserts that every ray in geometric optics encounters the boundary at a point where diffraction does not occur before time
T
. See Figure
1
for a special case of GCC known as
\Gamma
-set:

\Gamma_{\star}:=\{x\in\partial\Omega\,:\,(x-x_{0})\cdot\nu(x)>0\},

(1.4)

where

is the outward unit normal field to
\partial\Omega
. Intuitively, if you stand at

and shine a light,
\Gamma_{\star}
is the shadowed part of
\partial\Omega
. If there exists

such that

is given by (

) and

, then it can be proved by the multiplier method that (
1.1
) is exactly controllable at time

(see
[
33
]
). We refer to
[
56
]
for an alternative approach using rotated
multipliers and providing an extension of the set

.

1.2.
Literature on dispersive equations

There is a vast literature concerning the exact controllability of the Schrödinger-type equations with static boundary conditions. To fix some ideas, let us consider the following (controlled) problem in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a boundary
\partial\Omega
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iy_{t}-\Delta y=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y=\mathbbm{1}_{\Gamma_{\star}}v&\text{ in }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y(\cdot,0)=y_{0}&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.5)

Here,
i
is the imaginary unit,
y:\Omega\times(0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the state describing the amplitude of a quantum particle,
y_{0}
is the initial condition, and the control
v\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))
is active only on
\Gamma_{\star}\subset\partial\Omega
. We can also consider the case of a distributed control acting on a subset
\omega\subset\Omega
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iy_{t}-\Delta y=\mathbbm{1}_{\omega}v&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y=0&\text{ in }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
y(\cdot,0)=y_{0}&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.6)

We first mention the paper
[
41
]
where the author proved that the GCC for the exact controllability of the wave equation implies the exact controllability for the Schrödinger equation in any time
T>0
. In
[
48
,
49
]
, the exact controllability was proved using multiplier techniques. Consequently, these results have been used in
[
38
]
and
[
50
]
to study the problem of boundary feedback stabilization.

It is well-known that, when GCC is not satisfied at any time
T
, one can still achieve controllability results for the Schrödinger equation. This can be obtained using auxiliary controllability results for the plate equation with hinged boundary conditions, see for instance
[
27
]
and
[
9
]
. Roughly speaking, the strategy is to use the fact that the plate operator can be written as a composition of two conjugate Schrödinger operators and transfer the exact controllability results from one system to another as in
[
41
]
. Furthermore, the paper
[
57
]
estimates the cost of approximate controllability for the Schrödinger equation when GCC is not satisfied, and provides how the control size depends on the control time.

The exact controllability of the systems (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) is equivalent to suitable observability inequalities for the following adjoint system (see, e.g.,
[
58
]
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iz_{t}-\Delta z=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
z=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
z(\cdot,T)=z_{0}&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.7)

More precisely, the exact controllability of (
1.5
) is equivalent to the existence of a constant
C_{\text{obs}}>0
such that the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\|z_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{\text{obs}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma_{\star}}\left|\partial_{\nu}z\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall\,z_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(1.8)

where
z
is the associated solution of (
1.7
). In the same manner, regarding the controllability of (
1.6
), we shall prove that there is
C_{\text{obs}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|z_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{\text{obs}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\omega}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall\,z_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(1.9)

In
[
4
]
, the authors used Carleman inequalities (in the context of inverse problems) for a Schrödinger equation with real potentials. After that, in
[
39
]
and
[
40
]
, some Carleman inequalities and observability estimates in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\Omega)
, respectively, are obtained for non-conservative Schrödinger systems with mixed boundary conditions. We also mention
[
47
]
and
[
28
]
where some inverse problems for the magnetic Schrödinger equation are considered.

But, as we mentioned before, the exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation can be obtained in domains in which the GCC condition is not satisfied. See the example of a ‘stadium’ in Figures
2
and
3
where the observation region is taken in the interior and on a part of the boundary, respectively. In both cases, a trapped ray (represented by an arrow) avoids the observation region.

Figure 2.
Domain with an interior observation region which does not satisfy GCC.

Figure 3.
Domain with a boundary observation region which does not satisfy GCC.

In
[
53
]
, in the context of inverse problems, the authors obtained Carleman inequalities for the Schrödinger equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions, by using suitable degenerate weights. Then, with these estimates at hand, the authors solved the problem of retrieving a time-independent potential for the Schrödinger equation from a single boundary or internal measurement. In a similar way, in
[
46
]
, the exact controllability of a system of Schrödinger equations in cascade form is studied. Using the degenerate weights proposed in
[
53
]
, they achieve a Carleman estimate for the associated adjoint system. Moreover, the recent paper
[
14
]
establishes uniqueness results for a singular Schrödinger equation with an inverse square potential to study approximate controllability and inverse source problems.

Regarding the case of the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions, we mention
[
12
]
, where the authors studied the well-posedness and stability issues for some nonlinear Schrödinger equations with Wentzell boundary conditions. A complex Ginzburg-Landau equation with dynamic boundary conditions has been studied in
[
19
]
. Moreover, in
[
54
]
, the exact controllability of the linearized case has been analyzed under suitable geometric assumptions. In
[
15
]
, an inverse problem of retrieving the source terms of a Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions from final time measurements is considered. Recently, in
[
11
]
, the authors studied an inverse coefficient problem for such Schrödinger-type models. Then, using a suitable Carleman estimate and the Bukhgeim-Klibanov method, a Lipschitz stability estimate is obtained from observations on a part of the boundary.

Geometric setting

2.2.
Functional setting

We denote the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
by
\mathrm{d}x
and the surface measure on
\Gamma
by
\mathrm{d}S
. Additionally, we consider the functional space:
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(\Omega,\mathrm{d}x)\times L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1},\mathrm{d}S\right)
. It is a real Hilbert space equipped with the inner product given by

\displaystyle\langle(u,u_{\Gamma}),(v,v_{\Gamma})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=\int_{\Omega}uv\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma^{1}}u_{\Gamma}v_{\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}S,

which will be used for the wave equation. For the Schrödinger equation, the function spaces will refer to complex-valued functions, and the space
\mathcal{H}
will be endowed with the Hermitian product

\displaystyle\left((u,u_{\Gamma}),(v,v_{\Gamma})\right)_{\mathcal{H}}:=\int_{\Omega}u\overline{v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma^{1}}u_{\Gamma}\overline{v_{\Gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}S.

We also consider

\displaystyle H_{0,\Gamma^{0}}^{k}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{y\in H^{k}(\Omega):y=0\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left(y,y_{\Gamma}\right)\in H_{0,\Gamma^{0}}^{k}(\Omega)\times H^{k}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right):y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}\right\},\qquad k=1,2.

We also set
\mathcal{E}^{1}=\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
for its topological dual with respect to the pivot space
\mathcal{H}
.

We point out that
\mathcal{E}^{1}
is a Hilbert space in
\mathbb{C}
equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\left((y,y_{\Gamma}),(z,z_{\Gamma})\right)_{\mathcal{E}^{1}}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla y\cdot\nabla\overline{z}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma^{1}}\nabla_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\overline{z_{\Gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}S

for all
(y,y_{\Gamma}),(z,z_{\Gamma})\in\mathcal{E}^{1}
.

3.1.
Boundary controllability

In this section, we provide a concise discussion on the exact boundary controllability of the following system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{tt}y-d\Delta y+q_{\Omega}(x,t)y=0,&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{tt}y_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}y+q_{\Gamma}(x,t)y_{\Gamma}=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}},&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y=\mathds{1}_{\Gamma_{\star}}v,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(y(\cdot,0),y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(y_{0},y_{0,\Gamma}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\\
(\partial_{t}y(\cdot,0),\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(y_{1},y_{1,\Gamma}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\end{cases}

(3.1)

with a single control
v\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))
, where
q_{\Omega}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
,
q_{\Gamma}\in L^{\infty}\left(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)\right)
, and
\Gamma_{\star}
is the control region defined in (
2.3
). The function
y_{\mid_{\Gamma}}
is the trace of
y
on
\Gamma
,
\Delta_{\Gamma}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, and
\partial_{\nu}y
is the normal derivative with the outer unit normal vector field
\nu
.
Moreover,
\sqrt{d}
and
\sqrt{\delta}
represent the wave velocities in the bulk and on the boundary, respectively (
d,\delta>0
being constant).

When the initial data are less regular, the solution of (
3.1
) should be interpreted in the transposition sense.

Simultaneously with the system (
3.1
), we consider its adjoint backward system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{tt}z-d\Delta z+q_{\Omega}(x,t)z=0,&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{tt}z_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}z_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}z+q_{\Gamma}(x,t)z_{\Gamma}=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
z_{\Gamma}=z_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}},&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
z=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(z(\cdot,T),z_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(z_{0},z_{0,\Gamma}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\\
(\partial_{t}z(\cdot,T),\partial_{t}z_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(z_{1},z_{1,\Gamma}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Next, we define the minimal time

T_{*}=\dfrac{2}{\min\left(\sqrt{\rho d},\sqrt{\frac{d(\delta-d)}{8\delta}}\right)}\left(\underset{x\in\overline{\Omega}}{\max}\,\psi_{0}(x)-\underset{x\in\overline{\Omega}}{\min}\,\psi_{0}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.3)

Note that the time
T_{*}
that will be needed for observability depends on the Minkowski gauge function
\mu
, the parameter
\rho
in Lemma
1
-
\mathrm{(iv)}
, the constant
d
, and the difference
\delta-d
.

By a standard duality argument, the exact controllability of system (
3.1
) is equivalent to satisfying the observability inequality for the solution of the adjoint system (
3.2
), as stated in the following result.

Theorem 1
.

We assume that
T>T_{*}
and
\delta>d
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
(z_{0},z_{0,\Gamma},z_{1},z_{1,\Gamma})\in\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{H}
, the solution of (

) satisfies the observability inequality

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}&\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|z_{1}(x)\right|^{2}+d|\nabla z_{0}(x)|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}\left(\left|z_{1,\Gamma}(x)\right|^{2}+\delta\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}z_{0,\Gamma}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}S\\
\leq&C\int_{\Gamma_{\star}\times(0,T)}|\partial_{\nu}z|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(3.4)

Next, we set the following notations:

\displaystyle\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(-T,T)\quad

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\Gamma^{k}_{T}=\Gamma^{k}\times(-T,T)\quad\text{ for }k=0,1,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\star}:=\left\{x\in\Gamma\colon\;\partial_{\nu}\psi_{0}(x)\geq 0\right\}\subseteq\Gamma^{0}.

(3.5)

Now, we introduce the Carleman weights for the wave system (
3.1

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)=\psi_{0}(x)-\beta t^{2}+C_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(x,t)=e^{\lambda\psi(x,t)},\quad(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\times[-T,T],

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{1}}e^{2s\varphi}\left(s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}|y_{\Gamma}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\mathcal{I}_{\pm T}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\pm T}

\displaystyle=Cs\lambda\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi(x,T)(|\partial_{t}y(x,T)|^{2}+|\nabla y(x,T)|^{2})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi^{3}(x,T)|y(x,T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt+Cs\lambda\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi(x,-T)\left(|\partial_{t}y(x,-T)|^{2}+|\nabla y(x,-T)|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi^{3}(x,-T)|y(x,-T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+Cs\lambda\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi(x,T)|\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(x,T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi^{3}(x,-T)|y_{\Gamma}(x,-T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}S.

Remark 1

Theorem

Remark 2

In the case when

The main result of this section reads as follows.

3.2.
Inverse problems

In this section, we review recent results on inverse source problems of wave systems with dynamic boundary conditions. Consider the following wave system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{tt}y-d\Delta y+q_{\Omega}(x)y=f(x,t),&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{tt}y_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}y+q_{\Gamma}(x)y_{\Gamma}=g(x,t),&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}},&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(y(\cdot,0),y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(0,0),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\\
(\partial_{t}y(\cdot,0),\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(0,0),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.\end{cases}

(3.8)

Inverse Source Problem (ISP):
Can one determine, in a stable manner, the unknown source terms
(f,g)
from the knowledge of a single measurement
\partial_{\nu}y_{|\Gamma_{\star}\times(0,T)}
, where
(y,y_{\Gamma})
is the corresponding solution of (
3.8

Indeed, uniqueness fails in general, and one needs to restrict the set of unknown source terms. Thus, we introduce the set of admissible source terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(C_{0}):=\left\{(f,g)\in H^{1}\left(0,T;\mathcal{H}\right):\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-5.69046pt|\partial_{t}f(x,t)|\leq C_{0}|f(x,0)|,&\hskip-11.38092pt\text{ a.e. }(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)\\
\hskip-5.69046pt|\partial_{t}g(x,t)|\leq C_{0}|g(x,0)|,&\hskip-11.38092pt\text{ a.e. }(x,t)\in\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)\end{array}\right\},

where
C_{0}>0
is a fixed constant. The main result is the global Lipschitz stability for the inverse source problem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
T>\frac{T_{*}}{2}
and
C_{0}>0
. We assume that

\displaystyle\delta>d.

(3.11)

Then there exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,T,C_{0},\|q_{\Omega}\|_{\infty},\|q_{\Gamma}\|_{\infty})
such that for any source term
(f,g)\in\mathcal{S}(C_{0})
, we have

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T))}\leq C\|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{\star}\times(0,T))}

(3.12)

for any sufficiently smooth solution
(y,y_{\Gamma})
of the system (
3.8
).

The proof of Theorem
4
relies on the so-called Bukhgeim-Klibanov method
[
8
]
. This is done thanks to the Carleman estimate (Theorem
2
) and an extension of a recent argument avoiding cut-off functions
[
34
]
.

One can also consider

Coefficient Inverse Problem (CIP):
Determine
(q,q_{\Gamma})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times L^{\infty}(\Gamma^{1})
from the knowledge of
\partial_{\nu}y
on
\Gamma_{\star}\times(0,T)
,
(\Gamma_{\star}\subseteq\Gamma^{0})
, where
(y,y_{\Gamma})
is the solution of (
3.8
) associated to
(q,q_{\Gamma})
.

We can prove Lipschitz stability for the CIP, but we omit the proof as it is similar to the arguments of Section
4.2
.

4.
Dispersive equations with dynamic boundary conditions

In this section, we review the main results concerning the controllability of the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions. The details can be found in
[
54
]
.

4.1.
Boundary controllability

Consider the following non-conservative Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+d\Delta u-\vec{p}_{1}\cdot\nabla u+p_{0}u=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
i\partial_{t}u_{\Gamma}+\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}u-\vec{p}_{\Gamma,1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}+p_{\Gamma,0}u_{\Gamma}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
u_{\Gamma}=u_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)\\
u=\mathbbm{1}_{\Gamma_{\star}}h&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(u(\cdot,0),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0})&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.\end{cases}

(4.1)

Here, the pair
(u,u_{\Gamma})
is the state,
h\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))
is the control acting on a portion of the boundary
\Gamma_{\star}\subseteq\Gamma^{0}
. Moreover,
d>0
and
\delta>0
represent the quantum diffusion in the bulk and on the boundary, respectively.

We mention that the boundary control
h
acts only on a subset of
\Gamma^{0}\subset\partial\Omega
. This implies that the first equation satisfied in the bulk is controlled by
h
, while the side condition
u_{\Gamma}=u_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}
is satisfied on
\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)
.

The exact controllability of the system (
4.1
) is understood in the following way:

Definition 5
.

We say that the system (
4.1
) is exactly controllable in a functional space
\mathcal{X}
if for every time
T>0
and all states
(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0}),(u_{1},u_{\Gamma,1})\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a boundary control
h\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))
such that the associated state
(u,u_{\Gamma})
of (
4.1
) fulfills

\displaystyle(u(\cdot,T),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(u_{1},u_{\Gamma,1})\qquad\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.

Now, define the function
\psi_{0}
as in (
2.2
). For
\lambda>0
we consider the weight functions

\displaystyle\theta(x,t):=\dfrac{e^{\lambda\psi_{0}(x)}}{t(T-t)},\quad\alpha(x,t):=\dfrac{K-e^{\lambda\psi_{0}(x)}}{t(T-t)},\qquad(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T),

(4.2)

where
K>\|e^{\lambda\psi_{0}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
is a constant.

Let us define the following differential operators:

\displaystyle L(w)

\displaystyle:=i\partial_{t}w+d\Delta w+\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\nabla w+q_{0}w,

\displaystyle L_{\Gamma}(w,w_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle:=i\partial_{t}w_{\Gamma}+\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}w_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}w+\vec{q}_{\Gamma,1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}w_{\Gamma}+q_{\Gamma,0}w_{\Gamma}.

The proof of exact controllability draws on the following Carleman estimate.

Theorem 6
.

Consider the drift coefficients

(\vec{q}_{1},\vec{q}_{\Gamma,1})\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))]^{n}\times[L^{\infty}(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T))]^{n}

and the potentials

(q_{0},q_{\Gamma,0})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))\times L^{\infty}(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)).

In addition, suppose that

\displaystyle\delta>d.

(4.3)

Then, there exist positive constants
C_{1},\lambda_{1}
and
s_{1}
such that the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}e^{-2s\alpha}(s^{3}\lambda^{4}\theta^{3}|w|^{2}+s\lambda\theta|\nabla w|^{2}+s\lambda^{2}\theta|\nabla\psi_{0}\cdot\nabla w|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{-2s\alpha}(s^{3}\lambda^{3}\theta^{3}|w_{\Gamma}|^{2}+s\lambda\theta(|\partial_{\nu}w|^{2}+|\nabla_{\Gamma}w_{\Gamma}|^{2}))\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&C_{1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}e^{-2s\alpha}|L(w)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+C_{1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{-2s\alpha}|L(w,w_{\Gamma})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+C_{1}s\lambda\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma_{\star}}e^{-2s\alpha}\theta|\partial_{\nu}w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(4.4)

for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{1}
,
s\geq s_{1}
, and
(w,w_{\Gamma})\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{E}^{1})
such that

\displaystyle L(w)\in L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T)),\quad L(w,w_{\Gamma})\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)),\quad\partial_{\nu}w\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T)).

Now suppose that

\displaystyle\vec{p}_{1}:=d\nabla\pi-i\vec{r},\qquad\vec{p}_{\Gamma,1}:=\delta\nabla_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}-i\vec{r}_{\Gamma},

(4.5)

where

\displaystyle\pi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})),

\displaystyle\pi_{\Gamma}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(\Gamma^{1};\mathbb{R}))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\Gamma^{1};\mathbb{R})),

with
\pi_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}=\pi_{\Gamma}
and

\displaystyle\vec{r}\in[L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}))]^{n},\qquad\vec{r}_{\Gamma}\in[L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,\infty}(\Gamma^{1};\mathbb{R}))]^{n},

with
\vec{r}_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}=\vec{r}_{\Gamma}
and
\vec{r}\cdot\nu\leq 0
on
\Gamma\times(0,T)
. We also assume that

\displaystyle(p_{0},p_{\Gamma,0})\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\Omega))\times L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\Gamma^{1})).

(4.6)

Theorem 7
.

Let
(\vec{p}_{1},\vec{p}_{\Gamma,1})
and
(p_{0},p_{\Gamma,0})
satisfy (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), respectively. We assume (
4.3
) (i.e.,
\delta>d
). Then, for all states
(u_{0},u_{\Gamma,0}),(u_{1},u_{\Gamma,1})\in\mathcal{E}^{-1}
, there exists a control
h\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))
such that the associated solution
(u,u_{\Gamma})
(in the sense of transposition) of (
4.1
) satisfies

\displaystyle(u(\cdot,T),u_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(u_{1},u_{\Gamma,1})\qquad\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.

(4.7)

By duality, the exact controllability of (
4.1
) is equivalent to proving a suitable observability inequality for the adjoint system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}z+d\Delta z-\vec{p}_{1}\cdot\nabla z+pz=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
i\partial_{t}z_{\Gamma}+\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}z_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}z_{\Gamma}-\vec{p}_{\Gamma,1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}z_{\Gamma}+p_{\Gamma}z_{\Gamma}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
z_{\Gamma}=z_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
z=0&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(z(\cdot,T),z_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(z_{T},z_{\Gamma,T})&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\end{cases}

(4.8)

where
(p,p_{\Gamma})
are given by

\displaystyle p:=-d\Delta\pi+i\text{div}(\vec{r})+\overline{p}_{0},\quad p_{\Gamma}:=-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}\pi_{\Gamma}+i\text{div}_{\Gamma}(\vec{r}_{\Gamma})-i\vec{r}\cdot\nu+\overline{p}_{\Gamma,0}.

(4.9)

More precisely, the exact controllability of (

) is equivalent to proving the existence of a constant

such that for all

, the following observability inequality holds

\displaystyle\|(z_{T},z_{\Gamma,T})\|_{\mathcal{E}^{1}}^{2}\leq C_{\text{obs}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma_{\star}}|\partial_{\nu}z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}t,

(4.10)

where

is the associated solution of (
4.8
). Note that the observability inequality (
4.10
) is a direct consequence of the Carleman estimate (
4.4
).

4.2.
Inverse problems

First, we set the differential operators

\displaystyle L(y)

\displaystyle:=i\partial_{t}y+d\Delta y+\vec{q}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla y,

\displaystyle L_{\Gamma}(y,y_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle:=i\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}+\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}y+\vec{q}_{\Gamma,1}(x)\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}.

We consider the following Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}L(y)+p(x)y=g&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
L_{\Gamma}(y,y_{\Gamma})+p_{\Gamma}(x)y_{\Gamma}=g_{\Gamma}&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y=0&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(y(\cdot,0),y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(y_{0},y_{\Gamma,0})&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\end{cases}

(4.11)

where
(g,g_{\Gamma})
is a source term,
p,p_{\Gamma}
are unknown real potentials, and
(y_{0},y_{\Gamma,0})
is the pair of initial data.

Coefficient Inverse Problem (CIP):
Can we determine the unknown potentials

(p,p_{\Gamma})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}(\Gamma^{1};\mathbb{R})

from a single measurement of the flux
\partial_{\nu}y
on
\Gamma_{\star}\times(0,T)
,
(\Gamma_{\star}\subseteq\Gamma^{0})
, where
(y,y_{\Gamma})
is the solution of (
4.11
) associated with
(p,p_{\Gamma})
?

To emphasize the dependence of the solution on
(p,p_{\Gamma})
in (
4.11
), sometimes we shall write

\displaystyle y=y[p,p_{\Gamma}]\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{\Gamma}=y_{\Gamma}[p,p_{\Gamma}].

For
m>0
and
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we introduce the set

\displaystyle L_{\leq m}^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R}):=\{p\in L^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{R})\,:\,\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}\leq m\},

and define the set of admissible potentials as

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\leq m}^{\infty}:=L_{\leq m}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\times L_{\leq m}^{\infty}(\Gamma^{1};\mathbb{R}).

We also set
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}:=L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})\times L^{\infty}(\Gamma^{1};\mathbb{C})
.

The next result establishes the Lipschitz stability for the coefficient inverse problem
(CIP)
:

Theorem 8
.

For a given
m>0
we consider
(q,q_{\Gamma})\in\mathbb{L}_{\leq m}^{\infty}
. Assume that
\delta
and
d
fulfill the condition (
4.3
) (i.e.,
\delta>d
). In addition, let us assume that

\displaystyle(y[q,q_{\Gamma}],y_{\Gamma}[q,q_{\Gamma}])\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{\infty})

and the initial data
(y_{0},y_{\Gamma,0})
are real-valued functions satisfying

\displaystyle|y_{0}|\geq r_{0}\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\quad\text{ and }\quad|y_{\Gamma,0}|\geq r_{0}\text{ a.e. on }\Gamma^{1}

for some constant
r_{0}>0
. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\Omega,T,y_{0},y_{\Gamma,0})>0
such that if

\displaystyle(\partial_{\nu}y[q,q_{\Gamma}]-\partial_{\nu}y[p,p_{\Gamma}])\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma_{\star})),

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{C}\|(q,q_{\Gamma})-(p,p_{\Gamma})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|\partial_{\nu}y[q,q_{\Gamma}]-\partial_{\nu}y[p,p_{\Gamma}]\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma_{\star}))}\leq C\|(q,q_{\Gamma})-(p,p_{\Gamma})\|_{\mathcal{H}}

(4.12)

for all

Theorem
8
is also proved by adapting the Bukhgeim-Klibanov method
[
8
]
. One of the main ingredients of this strategy is a suitable Carleman estimate for the Schrödinger operator with dynamic boundary conditions.

Consider the function
\psi_{0}
defined in (
2.2
). We keep the same notation for the following modified weight functions

\displaystyle\theta(x,t):=\dfrac{e^{\lambda\psi_{0}(x)}}{(T+t)(T-t)},\quad\alpha(x,t):=\dfrac{K-e^{\lambda\psi_{0}(x)}}{(T+t)(T-t)}\quad\forall\,(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\times(-T,T),

where

We introduce the differential operators

\displaystyle\tilde{L}(w):=i\partial_{t}w+d\Delta w-\vec{\rho}_{1}\cdot\nabla w+\rho_{0}w\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle\tilde{L}\left(w,w_{\Gamma}\right):=i\partial_{t}w_{\Gamma}+\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}w_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}w-\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma,1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}w_{\Gamma}+\rho_{\Gamma,0}w_{\Gamma}\quad\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}_{T}.

.

Consider
(\vec{\rho}_{1},\vec{\rho}_{\Gamma,1})\in L^{\infty}(-T,T;[\mathbb{L}^{\infty}]^{n})

. Suppose that

satisfy the condition (

,

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&s^{3/2}\lambda^{3/2}\left(\int_{\Omega}e^{-2s\alpha(\cdot,0)}|w(\cdot,0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{-2s\alpha(\cdot,0)}|w_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\right)\\

(4.13)

for all

, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}:=\big\{(w,w_{\Gamma})\in L^{2}(-T,T;\mathcal{E}^{1})\,:\,

\displaystyle(\tilde{L}(w),\tilde{L}_{\Gamma}(w,w_{\Gamma}))\in L^{2}(-T,T;\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\text{ and }\partial_{\nu}w\in L^{2}(-T,T;L^{2}(\Gamma_{\star}))\big\}.

5.
Conclusion and open problems

This review summarizes the recent results concerning controllability and inverse problems for hyperbolic and dispersive PDEs with dynamic boundary conditions. The results have been obtained by proving new Carleman estimates for the corresponding operators with dynamic boundary conditions. These estimates have a particular interest since they can be used to deduce new controllability or stability results for systems of PDEs with dynamic boundary conditions.

However, several interesting questions are still open and deserve further investigation in the future. In the following, we describe some of them.

5.1.
The case
\delta\leq d

As we have seen in both the wave and Schrödinger equations, the Carleman estimates (
2
) and (
4.4
) have been obtained under the assumption
\delta>d
on the wave speeds in
\Omega
and on
\Gamma^{1}
. In both cases, this assumption is imposed to control the global term

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma^{1}_{T}}|\nabla_{\Gamma}z_{\Gamma}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}t

and put it on the left-hand side of the Carleman estimate. Thus, at this point, one might think that this assumption is technical for both equations. However, it was shown in
[
5
, Theorem 2.4]
that, in the context of the wave equation with dynamic boundary conditions posed in an annulus, the system is not controllable at any time
T>0
when
\delta<d
. To the best of the author’s knowledge, the exact controllability of both equations remains open when
\delta=d
(identical wave speeds in
\Omega
and
\Gamma
). As for the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions, the exact controllability in the case
\delta<d
is still open in contrast to the wave equation. Finally, we emphasize that the stability of inverse problems for both equations also remains open when
\delta\leq d
.

5.2.
Geometric assumptions

The results presented in Sections
3
and
4
are valid under the assumption that the domain
\Omega
satisfies the geometric configuration described in Section
2.1
. This assumption is a particular case of the Geometric Control Condition (GCC) and is connected to the derivation of Carleman inequalities for the wave or Schrödinger operators with dynamic boundary conditions. In particular, it allows us to apply the surface divergence theorem to control global terms involving tangential derivatives. Whether this geometric assumption can be relaxed while still achieving controllability or stability results remains an open question.

For the wave equation with dynamic boundary conditions, we have obtained the formula (
3.3
) of a minimal time
T_{*}
needed for exact controllability and stability. However, we do not know if this time is sharp and how it relates to the GCC time.

Another interesting question regarding the Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions is the identification of geometric conditions under which controllability results can be obtained in domains that do not satisfy the GCC. In particular, it would be worthwhile to establish Carleman inequalities for these models by employing suitable degenerate weights, as done in
[
53
]
.

5.3.
Controllability results for nonlinear models

The results reviewed in Sections
3
and
4
pertain to linear models. However, as shown in the case of the heat or Ginzburg–Landau equations with dynamic boundary conditions (see, for instance,
[
10
]
), one can expect similar controllability results for nonlinear models, at least for semilinear wave and Schrödinger equations with dynamic boundary conditions.
