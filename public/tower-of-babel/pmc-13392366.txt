A tunable Cas12a platform for single-cell perturbation screening and CRISPRi
Authors: Valentina Snetkova, Carolina Galan, Romain Lopez, Antonio R. Rios, Takamasa Kudo, Kristel Dorighi, Søren Warming, Benjamin J. Haley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-cell perturbation (Perturb-seq) screens have primarily relied on Cas9 for inducing loss-of-function phenotypes, whereas Cas12a, despite its unique effectiveness for multiplex guide expression, remains underexplored. This may be due to Cas12a’s guide RNA array (pre-crRNA) self-processing activity and the subsequent challenges associated with pre-crRNA sequence recovery during single-cell RNA sequencing library preparation. To overcome the self-processing constraint, we optimized pre-crRNA expression vectors and established a degron-based, enhanced Cas12a system for gene knock-out. As demonstrated across cell types, target genes, and with a minimized guide RNA library, this platform allows for accurate detection of pre-crRNAs and gene editing-induced effects on the transcriptome in single cells. Additionally, we show that HyperLbCas12a outperforms other existing variants for multiplexed gene suppression. While the rapid reversibility of this repressor highlights specific kinetic constraints for degron-based single-cell recording, the system provides a potent, modular tool for contexts requiring tunable, transient silencing. Together, this suite of technologies greatly expands the possibilities for future Perturb-seq efforts and broader application of Cas12a for genetic disruption at scale.

Introduction

CRISPR-based functional genomics has enabled systematic, large-scale interrogation of gene function in diverse biological systems1–3. The associated technology platforms have been instrumental for identifying regulators of proliferation, drug resistance, cell fate, and immunity, among many other phenotypes4–6. While traditional pooled CRISPR screens rely on simplified phenotypic outputs such as survival or reporter activity, the integration of high-throughput perturbation with single-cell RNA sequencing, collectively termed Perturb-seq, has created a powerful approach for researchers to link multiple and specific gene modifications to transcriptomic profiles and information-rich phenotypes at cellular resolution7,8.

Despite continued progress in Perturb-seq methodologies, most implementations rely on Cas9, which introduces practical constraints for combinatorial (multi-sgRNA) perturbations and compact library design9–12. These limitations are magnified in single-cell screens, where the number of cells profiled per sequencing run restricts total library size, making efficient use of each perturbation essential. Cas9-based multiplexing generally takes advantage of distinct Pol III promoters (e.g., U6, H1) for each sgRNA, resulting in vectors that may be large, repetitive, and labor-intensive to assemble at scale13. Repetitive Pol III cassettes can promote lentiviral recombination, leading to mis-pairing or “swapping” of linked elements and reducing the fidelity of perturbation assignment in pooled screens14,15. Moreover, Pol III promoters vary in strength and activity across cell types and differentiation states, which could introduce guide-to-guide imbalance and reduce the efficiency or uniformity of multi-target perturbations16–18.

In contrast, Cas12a (Cpf1) endogenously processes a single transcript encoding multiple pre-crRNAs into individual, mature guide RNAs, enabling expression of multi-guide arrays from a single promoter19,20. In effect, this streamlines cloning, minimizes repetitive sequences that influence vector recombination, reduces DNA synthesis costs, and enables delivery of multiple guides targeting the same gene, or distinct genes, from comparatively fewer constructs21,22. The result is a more scalable and cost-effective platform for both individual and combinatorial perturbations, ideally suited for constructing information-rich libraries compatible with high-resolution single-cell functional genomics.

To date, Cas12a has yet to be applied for Perturb-seq, likely due to technical barriers related to linking (pre-)crRNAs to single-cell transcriptomes. In standard functional genomic applications, Cas12a and, in particular, its pre-crRNA array are delivered using lentiviral vectors that integrate into the genome of target cells22,23. While the guide RNAs are transcribed from a Pol III promoter to ensure functional activity, a separate Pol II–derived mRNA is also transcribed from the lentiviral backbone. This transcript includes both the selectable marker and upstream elements, including the Pol III promoter and the pre-crRNA array. We hypothesize that Cas12a cleaves the pre-crRNA sequence within this Pol II transcript as part of its normal guide processing activity, destabilizing the RNA and preventing its capture during single-cell RNA sequencing. To overcome this issue, we developed a chemically controlled “enhanced” Acidaminococcus Cas12a (enAsCas12a) variant fused to an FKBP12F36V degron, allowing acute degradation of the nuclease prior to transcriptome profiling24. This preserves the integrity of Pol II–derived transcripts containing the guide array, enabling accurate perturbation assignment. We further optimized the guide cassette design and implemented complementary recovery strategies to improve guide detection efficiency. In a proof-of-concept Perturb-seq screen targeting chromatin regulators, this approach enabled efficient gene disruption and robust assignment of perturbation-specific transcriptional signatures across thousands of single cells.

In parallel, we sought to expand Cas12a-based loss-of-function screening through the development of improved transcriptional repressors. To this end, we engineered a chemically-degradable “Hyper” Lachnospiraceae Cas12a (HyperLbCas12a) fused to the ZIM3/KRAB repressor domain. The resulting HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3 variant achieved near-complete repression of up to three target genes per cell, outperforming previously described Cas12a CRISPRi systems across targets and cell types. As expected for a KRAB-based transcriptional repressor25, silencing was rapidly reversed following degron-mediated depletion of the editor. This rapid rebound places practical limits on the use of degron-based Cas12a CRISPRi for Perturb-seq, where stable perturbation during the transcriptional capture window is essential. Nevertheless, HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3 represents a potent and modular CRISPRi system, enabling multiplexed, tunable repression and providing a strong foundation for future Cas12a-compatible, persistence-enhanced CRISPRi designs.

Together, our next-generation Cas12a platform expands the scalability, efficiency, and flexibility of Perturb-seq screening, while opening new avenues for programmable gene silencing in complex cellular contexts.

Results

Optimization of pre-crRNA expression and capture for Perturb-seq

To assess the potential for Cas12a in single-cell applications, we first generated a population of NCI-H358 (H358, bronchioalveolar carcinoma) cells to stably express a 4XNLS-enAsCas12a transgene. We then explored adaptations of the direct-capture12 and CROP-seq26 strategies for lentiviral delivery and subsequent single-cell detection of pre-crRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 1a). For Cas9-based direct-capture, one of two different capture sequences (CS 1 or 2) is incorporated within a stem loop of the single-guide (sg)RNA scaffold or is appended to the sgRNA 3’ terminus12. Due to differences between the Cas9-associated sgRNAs and the relatively shorter, reversed orientation of Cas12a-associated crRNAs, we tested three different pre-crRNA configurations with varying CS positions: at the 3’ end of the spacer (position 1), between the direct repeat (DR) and the spacer (position 2), and in the hairpin of the DR (position 3) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The effectiveness of each approach was tested through expression of a representative pre-crRNA array designed to simultaneously disrupt either the CD63 and CD47 or CD81 and CD9 genes (one crRNA per target, two targets per vector), respectively, followed by quantification of cell surface levels for each gene product (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). The pre-crRNA engineered with a CS in position 1 showed comparable activity to an unmodified pre-crRNA, while CSs in the two other positions interfered with Cas12a targeting (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Modest, context-dependent differences in targeting activity were observed with constructs incorporating CS1 vs. CS2, suggesting capture sequence or experiment-specific effects could impact function.

Having established the optimal CS position, we compared the efficacy of pre-crRNAs expressed from the direct-capture format vs. otherwise unmodified pre-crRNAs within a CROP-seq vector. In the CROP-seq context, the pre-crRNA cassette is embedded within the lentiviral 3’ long terminal repeat (LTR), enabling duplication to the 5'LTR during integration (Supplementary Fig 2a). This enables the guides to be functionally expressed from an RNA Pol III (U6) promoter formed from a duplication event during lentiviral integration. Separately, the pre-crRNA will be incorporated into an RNA Pol II (EF1A) promoter-derived mRNA necessary for antibiotic (puromycin) selection marker expression; this mRNA is later amplified, and the pre-crRNAs are identified following oligo(d)T priming and 3’ scRNA-seq12,26. In the direct capture context, the pre-crRNA is not positioned within the LTR-derived Pol II transcript in a manner that supports recovery by conventional 3' or 5' scRNA-seq workflows27. For relative assessment, we designed pre-crRNAs to be expressed from either the direct-capture Perturb-seq or CROP-seq formats for simultaneous, multiplexed disruption of either two (CD47/CD63 or CD81/CD9) or four genes (CD47/CD63/CD81/CD9) per cell (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Comparable perturbation of multiple targets resulted from each of the pre-crRNA vector contexts (Supplementary Fig. 2c). There were no overt differences in activity levels when individual crRNAs were reordered within the array. As anticipated, efficacy was impacted by the spacer sequence for a given target gene (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Note 1).

Cas12a impairs guide detection in pooled scRNA screens

Epigenetic regulators play central roles in shaping gene expression programs and guiding cell fate decisions. Unlike many lineage-defining transcription factors that produce strong, binary shifts in cell/transcriptomic states, perturbation of epigenetic modifiers might be expected to yield more subtle, broadly acting changes in gene expression28. To evaluate whether our approach could sensitively detect such transcriptional shifts, we selected a representative set of epigenetic factors for pooled enAsCas12a CROP-seq or direct-capture scRNA-seq profiling (Fig. 1a)29. Accordingly, we selected a subset of these factors for evaluation with pooled enAsCas12a CROP-seq or direct-capture libraries (Fig. 1a). For both formats, the vector library included identical crRNAs targeting genes encoding 45 total factors that were reported to be highly expressed in H358 cells30 and play diverse roles such as chromatin remodeling (e.g., ACTL6A, CHD3, SMARCA4), histone modification (e.g., EHMT1, EHMT2, KAT6A, KDM1A), and DNA methylation (e.g., DNMT1, MECP2) (Fig. 1b). A single vector was generated per gene, with each gene targeted by a pair of crRNAs. Beginning with the CROP-seq format library, we appended a 3’ 19 bp barcode separated by a direct repeat and a barcode segment-specific primer site to each pre-crRNA array to assist with both pre-crRNA capture and accurate single-cell assignment of each crRNA pair (Fig. 1c, d). Sequencing amplicon preparation was optimized for either the full-length or barcode-only regions of the pre-crRNA cassettes (Fig. 1d). To map the full-length pre-crRNA sequences, we developed a computational method based on the creation of a custom pre-crRNA transcriptome with the Cell Ranger algorithm32, whereas mapping and assignment of cells to each barcode were performed using a standard scRNA-seq analysis pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Next, we compared recovery of pre-crRNAs from cells transduced with the CROP-seq library in the presence or absence of enAsCas12a. Library transduction conditions were optimized for integration of ~1 vector per cellular genome, and this was followed by four days in culture after 3–4 days of antibiotic selection to facilitate pre-crRNA expression and target gene disruption. The barcode sequencing strategy enabled relatively higher fraction assignment of pre-crRNAs to individual cells when compared to the full-length pre-crRNA approach (Fig. 1e, f). However, in cell populations expressing enAsCas12a, we detected substantially fewer pre-crRNAs and reduced consistency in the assignment of pre-crRNAs within each cell, regardless of the method for sequencing library preparation (Fig. 1e–g). To account for potential vector-specific influences on pre-crRNA recovery, we performed a similar series of screens and sequencing studies with an otherwise equivalent direct-capture library. This format showed inadequate pre-crRNA recovery and was deprioritized for further usage (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b Supplementary Note 2). We hypothesized that Cas12a’s self-processing activity was leading to degradation of pre-crRNA-containing transcripts and, as a result, sub-optimal assignment of perturbation sequences within individual cells.

Efficient recovery of guide detection after degron-mediated removal of Cas12a

Elimination of enAsCas12a from library-transduced cells after it has triggered gene knockout could provide an opportunity for pre-crRNA accumulation and improved recovery in single cells. As a simple pharmacological means to accomplish this, we leveraged the degradation tag (dTag) system33 to enable rapid and complete degradation of Cas12a. Within a standard lentiviral vector, an enAsCas12a transgene was engineered to be expressed with a C-terminal FKBP12F36V fusion domain (enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V). This enables selective ubiquitination and, subsequently, degradation of the protein upon addition of a dTag small molecule (e.g., dTagV1). After validating drug-induced degradation of the enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V protein in stably-expressing H358 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5), we tested the impact of this fusion on gene targeting efficiency in the presence or absence of dTagV1. As before, lentivirus-delivered pre-crRNAs were used to deplete CD81 and CD9 or CD47 and CD63, this time in cells expressing either enAsCas12a or enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V (Fig. 2a). In the absence of dTagV1, enAsCas12a and enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V showed similar levels of targeting efficiency (Fig. 2b). Continuous culture of enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V with dTagV1 (before and after pre-crRNA delivery) prevented knock-out for three out of four genes; only CD9 levels were reduced in cells treated with dTagV1. After establishing that enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V maintained high gene editing capacity, we repeated the epigenetic factor screen with dTagV1-mediated depletion of enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V for 24 or 48 h before collecting samples for single-cell sequencing (Fig. 2c). Analysis of pre-crRNA counts showed continued improvement in guide recovery across both time points tested with dTagV1 treatment (Fig. 2d, e). For the 24- and 48-h treatments, we recovered and assigned pre-crRNAs to individual cells at frequencies comparable to library-infected populations lacking enAsCas12a (up to ~80%) (Figs. 1f, 2f). Consistent with a generalized negative influence from enAsCas12a expression on pre-crRNA recovery, similar patterns were observed when quantifying full-length pre-crRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c) and when assigning guides using either full-length or barcode-only capture (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Increased pre-crRNA recovery was observed following a 48-h dTagV1 pulse in an independent K562 (chronic myeloid leukemia) line expressing CROP-seq-formatted pre-crRNAs and enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Although enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V protein degradation occurred rapidly (~6 h of dTagV1 treatment), robust recovery of the pre-crRNA transcript required a longer interval (24–48 h). This likely reflects a kinetic lag during which previously cleaved pre-crRNA molecules must be degraded and replaced by newly synthesized, full-length transcripts suitable for polyA-capture. Together, these results demonstrate that transient reduction of enAsCas12a levels immediately prior to Perturb-seq sample collection substantially improves guide recovery and assignment.

A heatmap summarizing gene expression patterns for all perturbations can be found in Supplementary Fig. 8. All datasets were normalized to five non-targeting controls to account for dTagV1-associated transcriptional effects. Transcriptomic changes following disruption of KDM1A (LSD1), EP300, CREBBP, and NAT10 exemplify both the specificity and efficacy of phenotypes generated using the dual crRNA library (Fig. 2g, h).

KDM1A disruption triggered upregulation of an innate-immune and antigen-presentation program, including type-I interferon (IFN)–stimulated genes, STAT1, and multiple HLA-I family members (i.e., HLA-B, C, and E, Fig. 2g). This expression signature aligns with studies showing that KDM1A loss or inhibition derepresses pattern recognition receptors34 and IFN-stimulated gene networks35 and/or elevates HLA-I levels in separate cancer models36. Indeed, the most upregulated gene in this context was VIM, which is known to be induced upon type-I IFN expression37. Consistent with previous reports showing that loss of EP30038 or CREBBP39 can destabilize epithelial adhesion programs and promote EMT-like transitions in epithelial cancers and SCLC, disruption of either factor in H358 cells produced a broadly overlapping transcriptional shift marked by reduced expression of epithelial-identity genes (CDH1, EPCAM (TACSTD2), AGR2, GRHL2, ELF3) and induction of TGF-β–responsive and cytoskeletal programs (VIM, TGFBI, CNN3, RHOC) (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Unbiased clustering found that these two perturbations group together, further emphasizing the similarity of their functions in this cellular context. By contrast, NAT10 knockout yielded a distinct ribosome-biogenesis phenotype, characterized by broad downregulation of ribosomal subunits and strong enrichment for translation-related pathways, consistent with its central role in rRNA modification and ribosomal maturation40 (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Fig. 8b). Successful discernment of these phenotypes underscores the high sensitivity of our Cas12a-degron platform for identifying post-perturbation transcriptional changes at the single-cell level.

Potent, rapidly-reversible Cas12a-based CRISPR interference

To extend the capabilities of our Cas12a-degron system beyond gene knockout, we sought to establish a CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) strategy designed for transcriptional silencing without genomic disruption. Accordingly, we engineered HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3, a Lachnospiraceae Cas12a variant fused to the KRAB-ZIM3 repressor domain. This effector achieved robust, multiplexed repression of endogenous targets, including CD81, CD47, and CD9, following delivery of a single pre-crRNA expression vector (Fig. 3a–c). HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3 consistently outperformed existing Acidaminococcus Cas12a-based repressors in direct benchmark assays and across multiple cell types (Fig. 3c)41,42. However, unlike Cas12a-mediated knockout, where edits are permanent, CRISPRi generally requires continuous effector occupancy to sustain repression25,43,44. Following dTagV1-induced degradation of HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3, we observed rapid target re-expression, with silencing beginning to reverse within 24 h and largely recovering by 48 h (Fig. 3d–f, Supplementary Note 3). This reversibility presents a practical limitation for Perturb-seq; clearing Cas12a immediately prior to scRNA-seq facilitates pre-crRNA detection, but during this window, dissolution of the repressive state permits target-gene reactivation and corresponding changes in observed gene-expression patterns.

To address this constraint, we evaluated fusions of Cas12a to epigenetic effectors, such as DNA methyltransferases, to generate CRISPRoff-like constructs45. Despite their potential for stable repression in other contexts, constitutive expression of these effectors was not tolerated by the cells used in our study. Further optimization of CRISPRoff-like variants or their expression (e.g., conditional, short-term induction), or the use of emerging systems, such as CHARM46, may ultimately provide a path forward for durable Cas12a-mediated repression compatible with degron-based transcriptomic screens. While not compatible with the timing requirements for Perturb-seq, HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3 enables strong, reversible, and multiplexed gene silencing, further expanding the CRISPR-based gene editing toolbox.

Discussion

By combining a chemically-degradable enAsCas12a variant with an optimized, multiplexed pre-crRNA expression cassette, we established a versatile platform for single-cell CRISPR screening that fully leveraged the unique properties of this gene editing nuclease. A key barrier for adaptation of Cas12a for Perturb-seq had been the recovery of pre-crRNA-containing transcripts, which are required to link perturbations to single-cell expression states. We overcame this limitation by acutely degrading Cas12a prior to RNA-seq library preparation, preventing Cas12a-mediated cleavage of pre-crRNAs and enabling robust guide detection by scRNA-seq.

To support a range of experimental designs, we developed two complementary pre-crRNA detection modalities: full-length sequencing and barcode-based guide identification. In a proof-of-concept loss-of-function screen targeting epigenetic regulators, the enAsCas12a-degron system enabled precise and efficient gene knockout at single-cell resolution, validating its suitability for high-throughput functional genomics. Because epigenetic factors typically modulate chromatin and gene expression in a graded manner, the transcriptional responses observed in our screen were modest in amplitude but readily-observed and consistent with published phenotypes.

Our implementation using the FKBP12F36V degron/dTAG system provided one effective approach for controlling Cas12a stability. Future alternatives for rapid Cas12a inactivation prior to sequencing could include targeted degradation of untagged Cas12a47 or disruption of Cas12a RNP assembly. As one precedent for the latter, heparin has been shown to interfere with Cas12a–crRNA binding48,49, although more specific molecules better suited for cellular use remain to be developed. These strategies could be especially useful in contexts where dTAG delivery or kinetics present limitations.

In parallel, we developed HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3, a compact and potent transcriptional repressor that expanded the capabilities of Cas12a beyond genome editing. This variant supported strong and multiplexed gene silencing from a single guide array, making it well-suited for multi-gene targeting or compact repression screens. Despite its potent target silencing effects, the transient nature of CRISPRi, powered here by the KRAB-ZIM3 domain, limited the HyperLbCas12a-degron-based approach’s compatibility with Perturb-seq. However, this represents a challenge of timing and stability, not necessarily of incompatibility. Future adaptations that incorporate more persistent repression mechanisms (i.e., low-toxicity, high-efficacy epigenetic silencers) or refined temporal control may bridge this gap. Regardless, we suggest that there could be screening applications where temporary repression of a primary transcriptional regulator is enough to elicit sustained secondary transcriptional changes. Upon degradation of the CRISPRi machinery, despite re-expression of the primary target gene, one would be able to obtain scRNA-seq readouts of the secondary changes with accurate primary target guide assignment.

Recent advancements have begun to address the technical hurdles of Cas12a-based single-cell genomics through diverse strategies. Platforms such as CHyMErA-seq and scCHyMErA-Seq have successfully utilized hybrid Cas9-Cas12a systems and modified direct repeat (DR) sequences to enable single-cell transcriptomic readouts50,51. While effective for paired sgRNA-crRNA expression, CHyMErA-based approaches impose significant constraints on scalability, due to a requirement for simultaneous expression of two distinct nucleases. Furthermore, this particular guide RNA expression format makes use of chimeric guide structures that preclude the simple chaining of multiple native Cas12a guides within a uniform, compact, autonomous array. In contrast, taking advantage of degron-tagged Cas12a addresses guide capture and scalability concerns for DNA nuclease-driven applications.

Furthermore, while recent high-throughput CRISPRi screens using dHyperCas12a have demonstrated the utility of this Cas variant for transcriptional silencing, current implementations use an earlier generation KOX1-derived KRAB repressor52. We demonstrate that HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3 achieves near-complete silencing across multiple targets and cell lines, effectively closing the performance gap between Cas12a and established Cas9-based CRISPRi tools. In addition, linking this system to a degron provides an added layer of temporal control (enabling rapid reversibility of silencing within 24–48 h) and distinguishes our platform as a unique tool for studying the effects of target silencing in the context of dynamic biological processes.

Together, the enAsCas12a-degron system and HyperLbCas12a-ZIM3 expand the toolkit for CRISPR-based functional genomics, enabling more compact, controllable, and scalable perturbation strategies. Importantly, this platform addresses key technical bottlenecks that previously prevented Cas12a from being applied to single-cell transcriptomics. By enabling efficient capture of pre-crRNA arrays, the enAsCas12a-degron system makes possible experimental designs that remain difficult with Cas9; namely, high-order combinatorial screens (three or more perturbations per cell) without the use of multiple Pol III promoters or recombination-prone constructs, while making it theoretically possible to execute orthogonal experiments wherein Cas12a-mediated knockout is combined with Cas9-based gene activation (e.g., CRISPRa)53. The ability to run such multiplexed or orthogonal perturbation schemes at single-cell resolution will be valuable for both basic discovery and applied efforts in disease modeling, target validation, synthetic biology, and large-scale screening campaigns.

Methods

Cell culture

H358 (ATCC CRL-5807™), K562 (ATCC CCL-243™) and DLD-1 cells (ATCC CCL-221™) were sourced from Genentech’s internal cell bank, where the cell lines were maintained under mycoplasma-free conditions. All cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 growth medium + 10% heat inactivated FBS (Sigma, F4135) + 2mM L-Glutamine and 100 U/mL Penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122).

Vector sequences

Transgene sequences for enAsCas12a, enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V, dead enAsCas12a, multiAsCas12a, HyperLb Cas12a and pre-crRNA sequences are detailed in the Supplementary Data 1. Knock-out crRNA sequences for CD47 and CD63 were sourced from the literature22. Knock-out crRNA sequences for CD81, CD9, epigenetic factors, and non-targeting controls (NTCs) and CRISPRi crRNAs (targeting CD81, CD47 and CD9) were designed using the crisprVerse Bioconductor ecosystem54 using the GRCh38 genome build. Sequences not found in the human genome or targeting olfactory receptors were used as non-targeting controls (NTCs) to account for non-cutting and cutting but non-targeting activity. Different direct repeats were used in the As pre-crRNA containing multiple crRNAs to limit possible recombination22. The same direct repeat was used for Lb pre-crRNA. pre-crRNA sequences were produced as DNA oligonucleotides and cloned into a puromycin-selectable lentiviral vector pLKO_SHC201 (Sigma) by Genscript. For libraries, final plasmids were pooled together in equimolar ratios, concentrated (Millipore, UFC5010), and column-purified (Qiagen, 28004).

Lentiviral production and cell transduction

Plasmids expressing desired cargo (enAsCas12a or pre-crRNA) and lentiviral packaging constructs (VSVg and Delta8.9) were transiently co-transfected into HEK293T cells with Lipofectamine 2000. After 48 h, supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.45 µM PES filter and concentrated with Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara, 631232) according to the manufacturer’s manual.

Transduction was performed in 8 µg/ml polybrene (Sigma Cat#TR-1003r) with direct addition of filtered and concentrated viral supernatant. Transduced cells were selected with 15 µg/ml blasticidin (for enAsCas12a constructs) or 1 µg/ml puromycin (for pre-crRNA constructs and libraries). MOI of lentiviral infection was assessed with CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, G7572).

Establishment of CRISPRi cell lines

Stable cell lines were generated by electroporation of piggyBac transposon (PB) and transposase (PBO, Transposagen/Hera BioLabs) plasmids with the SF Cell Line Kit (Lonza, V4XC-2032) using a 3:1 PB:PBO ratio (1 µg total DNA) into 1 x 106 cells in a 20 µl reaction. The FF-120 code was used for K562 cells and CM-130 for DLD-1. Expression of Cas12a constructs was confirmed with mScarlet fluorescence and selected with 10 µg/ml blasticidin treatment.

Flow cytometry

For surface protein analysis, staining was performed using the following antibodies: anti-human CD47 (FITC, BioLegend Cat No. 323106, Clone CC2C6), anti-human CD63 (PE-Cy7, BioLegend Cat No. 353010, Clone H5C6), anti-human CD81 (APC, Thermo Fisher Cat No. 17-0819-42, Clone 1D6-CD81), and anti-human CD9 (PE, BioLegend Cat No. 312106, Clone HI9a). Each antibody was used at a 1:25 dilution. The staining was conducted for ~60 min on ice in the MACS buffer. Then, cells were washed twice with the MACS buffer and fixed with 1% PFA for 5 min at RT to inactivate any remaining lentiviral particles, followed by a final wash with the MACS buffer. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD FACSymphony instrument, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10.10, plotted in BioRender and statistical analysis performed with R 4.0.0.

RT-qPCR

RNA was extracted from cells with Zymogen Quick-RNA 96 Kit (Cat. # R1052) and converted to cDNA with Applied Biosystems High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat. # 4374966) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA was diluted with water to allow for 5 µl of cDNA per qPCR reaction. qPCR was performed using TaqMan™ Fast Advanced Master Mix for qPCR (Thermo Fisher Cat. #4444963) according to the manufacturer’s instructions in a 384-well plate format with technical triplicates by combining 5 µl of cDNA with 5 µl of the master mix and 0.5 µl of TaqMan Probes (20x) under the FAST cycling protocol on the Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR Machine. The following TaqMan assays (Thermo Fisher TaqMan™ Gene Expression Assay (FAM), Cat. #4331182) were used to detect the genes of interest: GAPDH (Hs02786624_g1), CD81 (Hs01002167_m1), CD47 (Hs00179953_m1), and CD9 (Hs01124022_m1). The data was analyzed with Excel, plotted in PRISM 11 and statistical analysis performed with R 4.0.0.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Plasmid libraries consisting of 50 dual pre-crRNAs each (45 with two crRNA on one pre-crRNA targeting the same epigenetic factor and 5 NTCs) were transduced into H358-enAsCas12a or H358-enAsCas12a-FKP12F36V cells at a targeted MOI of 0.3 and a minimum coverage of 1000 cells per pre-crRNA. 1 μg/ml puromycin selection was initiated 48 h post-transduction. Some cells were split before puromycin selection to confirm MOI with Cell-Titer-Glo (Promega, G7570). The confirmed MOI was 0.27 for the CROP-seq library and 0.38 for the direct-capture Perturb-seq library in the H358-enAsCas12a cells, and 0.29 for the CROP-seq library in H358-enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V cells.

Cell processing and library preparation

In the case of H358-enAsCas12a cells, after the introduction of the targeting library and initiation of puromycin selection, cells were cultured for a week, then dissociated, washed in 1xPBS, counted to ensure high viability (>90%) and resuspended in 1% BSA in 1xPBS to process through the 10x Chromium Controller according to the manufacturer’s protocol (CG000316 or CG000418) with a recovery aim of 10,000–20,000 cells per lane. In the case of H358-enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V, prior to 10x processing, cells were cultured in 1 µM dTAGV-1 (Tocris Bioscience, 6914) for 24 h or 48 h. dTagV1 was continuously supplied until the reverse transcription step. Gene expression libraries were prepared according to the 10x protocol. For hashtag oligo (HTO) libraries, cells were labeled using Total-seq A antibodies (BioLegend, Cat. 394601-394610).

Pre-crRNA library preparation

The CROP-seq and polyadenylated sequences from direct-capture CROP-seq constructs were amplified from cDNA with forward primer 5’- GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTtggaaaggacgaggtacc (for full-length pre-crRNA) OR 5’-GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTTCCCAGAGCCACCGTTACAC (for barcodes) and reverse primer 5’- CTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT with the following conditions: 250 ng cDNA, 1 µM final primer concentration in a 50 µl reaction with NEBNext® Q5 Hot Start HiFi PCR Master Mix (NEB, M0543L) cycling at 98 °C – 3’, (98 °C – 30”, 66 °C – 10”, 72 °C – 30”) x 11, 72 °C – 1’. 10 µl from the finished PCR were used to add multiplexing indexes with 10 µl of pre-mixed primers from Dual Index TT Set A (10x Genomics, PN-1000215) in a 50 µl reaction with NEBNext® Q5 Hot Start HiFi PCR Master Mix (NEB, M0543L) cycling at 98 °C – 3’, (98 °C – 30”, 68 °C – 10”, 72 °C – 30”) x9, 72 °C – 1’. The long direct-capture CROP-seq transcripts bound to beads through capture sequences were amplified from cDNA with forward primer 5’- GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTtggaaaggacgaggtacc (for full-length pre-crRNA) and reverse primer 5’- GCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG with the same conditions as the polyadenylated transcripts amplification, except for the indexing primers, which were taken from Dual Index NT Set A (10x Genomics, PN-1000242). Libraries for direct-capture Perturb-seq and direct-capture CROP-seq constructs were created according to 10x genomics user manuals (CG000316 or CG000418).

Sequencing

Gene expression and pre-crRNA libraries were multiplexed and sequenced on NextSeq 2000. The intended read coverage for gene expression libraries was 20,000–30,000 reads per cell, approximately 10% of that for the HTO libraries and a quarter of that for the pre-crRNA libraries. The gene expression libraries, HTO libraries and pre-crRNA barcode libraries were sequenced with the P3 kit at R1: 28 bp, R2: 90 bp, and 10 bp each for i7 and i5. The full-length pre-crRNA libraries were sequenced with P1 or P2 kits at varying lengths of R2 to cover the full length of the pre-crRNA.

Statistics and reproducibility

Each figure legend reports the sample size for the corresponding data, with additional information in “Methods”. Sample size was guided by established practices for cell-based and molecular assays and experimental replication, without application of a formal statistical method. Flow cytometry experiments were performed with a minimum of two biological replicates. qPCR data were generated from two biological replicates, each performed in technical triplicates. Representative images and blots were obtained from independent experiments, with the number of independent experiments indicated in the figure legends. For the scRNAseq experiment, data were obtained from 41 out of the 45 genetic perturbations, and no data were excluded from the analysis. Randomization was not used in the study design. Experimental groups were defined by the constructs tested, and assays were performed under standardized conditions. Blinding was not possible and was not applied during study design, data collection, and analysis. Experiments were conducted in defined cell lines and treatment groups with objectively measured molecular readouts (e.g., flow cytometry, qPCR, scRNA-seq, Western blot).

Data analysis

Read alignment, demultiplexing and data pre-processing

Data analysis workflow is described in Supplementary Fig. 4. Briefly, scRNA-seq reads were mapped to the reference human genome GRCh38-2020-A or a custom reference containing full-length pre-crRNA sequences using Cell Ranger v7.1.0. To call 19 bp barcodes from the reads, we used kallisto-bustools55. HTO barcodes were processed using Cell Ranger v7.1.0. Demultiplexing of HTO and pre-crRNA feature barcoding data and assigning identity to cells was performed with a custom script as described in Xie et al., 201956. This analysis was performed for both full-length pre-crRNA sequences and barcodes alone. Downstream analysis was conducted using the assigned gene expression and pre-crRNA or barcode matrix. Cells with either more than 10% mitochondrial reads or less than 3000 UMIs were removed. Cells assigned to a perturbation with less than 15 total assigned cells were removed. Genes with less than 500 UMIs across all cells were removed, leaving a total of 12,522 genes. Single-cell expression matrix and feature barcodes were processed in an anndata object format in Scanpy. Expression counts per cell were normalized to a total of 10,000 counts per cell, and normalized values were log transformed (natural log), after adding a pseudo-count of 1. Separately, Cumulus was used to create barcode and HTO matrices for data from K562 cells.

Statistical analysis of transcriptome-wide changes in epigenetic factor screen data

A linear model (ordinary least-squares regression) was fit for each of the 12,522 (response) genes, where the gene expression values were the response variable and the perturbation (using the NTC as a reference), as well as the total number of UMIs, were used as covariates. We tested for statistical significance of the coefficient linking the perturbation identifier to the response gene expression. The false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure across all the tested genes.

Western blot

enAsCas12a and enAsCas12a-FKBP12F36V-expressing NCI-H358 (H358) cells were treated with 1 µM dTAGV-1 (Tocris Bioscience, 6914) for various time points (48 h, 24 h, 6 h, 2 h, 30 min, and 10 min) or with no dTAGV-1 compound as a control (DMSO alone). Following treatment, cells were collected and lysed using RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer (Thermo Fisher, 89900) supplemented with cOmplete, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor (Roche, 11873580001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein samples were prepared with 4x Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, 16107472) containing 2-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma, 63689) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were then separated on Novex 4–12% Tris-glycine gels (Invitrogen, XP04122BOX) using Novex™ Tris-Glycine SDS Running Buffer (10X) (Thermo Fisher, LC2675) with Chameleon® Duo Pre-stained Protein Ladder (Licor, 928-60000) as the marker. Gels were transferred to PVDF membranes (Invitrolon™ PVDF/Filter Paper Sandwiches, 0.45 μm, 8.3 x 7.3 cm, LC2005) using Tris-Glycine Transfer Buffer (25X) (Thermo Fisher, LC3675). The membranes were then blocked with Intercept® (PBS) Blocking Buffer (Licor, 927-70001) for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C: Cas12a antibody [3D3-F7] (Abcam, ab273438) at a 1:1000 dilution and β-Actin Mouse Monoclonal Antibody for normalization (Licor, 926-42212) at a 1:2000 dilution. Following overnight primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed with PBSt and incubated with IRDye® 680RD Donkey anti-Mouse IgG Secondary Antibody (Licor, 926-68072) at a 1:5000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were again washed in PBS, and protein bands were visualized using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System.
