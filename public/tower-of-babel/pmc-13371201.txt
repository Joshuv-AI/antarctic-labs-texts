Factors associated with level of knowledge on childhood cancer early warning signs among intern doctors in Tanzania
Authors: Irene Msirikale, Henry Jofrey Mwalongo, Jackson Bwimba, Juliana Mwakasitu, Maureen Rishya, Regina Kabona, Lulu Chirande, Phares Mujinja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, about 400,000 children are diagnosed with cancer each year, with nearly 90% living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to diagnosis and treatment remains limited. While high-income countries report cure rates above 80%, survival in LMICs is often below 30%. In Tanzania, delayed diagnosis contributes to poor outcomes, largely due to low awareness of childhood cancer warning signs among healthcare workers. Existing literature has focused mainly on medical students and community health workers, leaving a gap in understanding intern doctors’ knowledge, despite their frontline role in detecting and referring suspected cases. This study aimed to assess the factors influencing intern doctors’ knowledge on childhood cancer early warning signs in national and regional referral hospitals in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.MethodAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 176 intern doctors from Muhimbili National Hospital and three regional referral hospitals in Dar es Salaam in 2025. Data were collected using a structured, pre-tested, self-administered questionnaire that assessed demographics, clinical exposure, perceptions, and knowledge of childhood cancer early warning signs. Descriptive statistics summarized responses, while chi-square and ordinal regression analyses identified predictors of knowledge. Ethical approval was obtained from the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS-REC).ResultsOnly 23% of intern doctors demonstrated high knowledge of childhood cancer early warning signs, while 67% had moderate knowledge and 10% low knowledge. Awareness was greatest for retinoblastoma (91.0%) and least for neuroblastoma (29.5%). Clinical exposure during undergraduate internship and working in hospitals with pediatric oncology services showed positive trends with higher knowledge. A gradual increase in knowledge was observed with the increasing number of workshops attended. Interns who answered “Yes” to pediatric cancers being less curable than adult cancers had higher knowledge compared to those who answered “No” (aOR = 1.35; 95% CI: 0.70–2.60).ConclusionKnowledge of childhood cancer early warning signs among intern doctors remains suboptimal, highlighting the need for improved oncology training during medical education. Strengthening clinical exposure, integrating structured pediatric oncology modules, and addressing misconceptions through targeted training that not only transfers knowledge but also reinforces evidence-based optimism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09485-0.

Background

Every year, approximately 400,000 children are diagnosed with cancer worldwide. Alarmingly, about 90% of these children live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where access to timely and affordable treatment is limited [1]. This unequal distribution contributes to a disproportionately high burden of childhood cancer mortality in LMICs. Evidence from high-income countries demonstrates cure rates exceeding 80%, whereas survival outcomes remain considerably lower in LMIC settings, reflecting disparities in early diagnosis, treatment availability, and supportive care capacity ([2–7]). In Tanzania, the true burden of childhood cancer is uncertain due to the absence of a national cancer registry, though estimates suggest an incidence of about 134 cases per million children [1].

Childhood cancers often present with warning signs such as persistent fever, severe headaches, bone pain, and weight loss, which trained healthcare providers can recognize [8] However, limited awareness of these signs frequently delays referral and diagnosis, leading to poor outcomes [9]. In sub-Saharan Africa, up to 50–80% of children are diagnosed at advanced stages due to delayed presentation and diagnosis [10]. These delays are driven by socioeconomic barriers, cultural beliefs, misconceptions about curability, inadequate health-seeking behavior, and poor access to specialized care [11–13]. Insufficient knowledge among healthcare providers further compounds these challenges, contributing significantly to late diagnosis [10].

To address these challenges, several interventions have been implemented globally and locally, ranging from healthcare provider training workshops [14], public awareness campaigns [15], and integration of childhood cancer education into vaccination programs through twinning initiatives [16]. In Tanzania, organizations such as Tumaini la Maisha (TLM) and Global HOPE-MUHAS, in collaboration with the Ministry of Health, have played a pivotal role in improving awareness through hospital-based programs and community outreach initiatives using diverse media platforms.

Despite these efforts, much of the existing research has focused on assessing knowledge among healthcare providers and community health workers [17, 18], as well as the knowledge and attitudes of final-year medical students [9, 19, 20]. However, little is known about how factors such as clinical exposure, other forms of exposure, and personal perceptions influence the knowledge on early warning signs among intern doctors, who often serve as the first point of contact in the healthcare system upon completing their mandatory internship year. Therefore, this study aims to determine factors that influence Intern Doctors’ Knowledge on childhood cancer early warning signs in the National and Regional Referral hospitals in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.

Methods

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among intern doctors undergoing their one-year mandatory internship at four major hospitals in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, in a year 2025. using a quantitative approach.

Inclusion criteria

Intern doctors undergoing their one-year mandatory internship at Amana, Mwananyamala, Temeke Regional Referral Hospitals, and Muhimbili National Hospital in Dar es Salaam at the time of data collection.

Exclusion criteria

Interns who were on leave/ unavailable during data collection, and interns who did not provide informed consent to participate.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size for this study was determined using a formula for a finite population. Based on a prevalence of 70% (0.7) obtained from a previous study of respondents able to correctly identify the majority of signs and symptoms of childhood cancer [21]. A 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and a margin of error of 5% (d = 0.05), the calculated sample size for the finite population of 242 intern doctors was 176 after adjusting for anticipated non-response.

For sampling, a probability proportionate to size (PPS) approach was used to allocate participants across the four hospitals based on the number of interns at each site. Within each hospital, simple random sampling was employed to select the required number of interns.

Study instrument and data collection

Permission to collect data was obtained from the administrations of the three regional referral hospitals and Muhimbili National Hospital. Data was collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The research team developed the questionnaire following a thorough review of the existing literature on pediatric cancer knowledge and early warning signs. Content Validity was assessed and reviewed by supervisors and two pediatric hemato-oncology specialists, who assessed the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the questions. A pilot study was conducted among a small group of intern doctors outside the study sites to test the questionnaire’s clarity, appropriateness, and ability to measure the intended variables. Based on feedback, necessary revisions were made to improve question wording and structure. “The questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.”

The questionnaire consisted of five sections: the first section captured individual characteristics, the second focused on other exposures influencing knowledge, the third section addressed clinical exposure, encounters with pediatric cancer cases, the fourth section assessed perceptions, and the fifth section evaluated knowledge on childhood cancer early warning signs. Participants were given time to read and sign informed consent forms before participation. Printed questionnaires were then distributed with clear instructions provided at the beginning of the form. Research assistants supported the process by distributing the questionnaires, collecting the completed forms and checking them for completeness. Once completed, the questionnaires were securely stored for later analysis.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was done using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) 23.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics of intern doctors and their knowledge scores on early warning signs of childhood cancer. Associations between socio-demographic variables, clinical exposure, and perceptions with knowledge levels were examined using chi-square tests for bivariate analysis. Finally, ordinal regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of knowledge levels, incorporating demographic factors, clinical exposure, perceptions, and external exposure. Knowledge level were categorized into low, moderate, and high based on Bloom’s Taxonomy cut-offs. 13 points in total, which were categorized into 0–5, 6–9, and 10–13, respectively.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents

A total of 176 intern doctors participated in the study. More than half (54.5%) of respondents were aged between 26 and 30 years. More than half (56.3%) of the respondents were male. About 93.7% were unmarried. The majority had graduated from local medical schools (89.8%). Nearly all participants (97.7%) entered after completing high school (Form Six). Showed in Table 1.

Relationship between selected factors and knowledge levels on childhood cancer early warning signs among intern doctors

Clinical exposure to pediatric cancer cases during both internship and undergraduate training showed consistent associations with knowledge levels. Compared to interns who had encountered more than five cases during internship, those with no exposure (aOR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.19–1.52) and those with 1–2 cases (aOR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.17–2.24) had lower odds of being in a higher knowledge category, while those with 3–5 cases had slightly higher odds (aOR = 1.23; 95% CI: 0.34–4.38). A similar pattern was observed for undergraduate exposure, where interns with no exposure had lower odds (aOR = 0.34), those with 1–2 cases had slightly lower odds (aOR = 0.83), and those with 3–5 cases had higher odds (aOR = 1.27), compared to those with more than five cases.

Regarding institutional context, interns working in hospitals that provide pediatric cancer care had higher odds of being in a higher knowledge category compared to those in hospitals without such services (aOR = 1.38; 95% CI: 0.61–3.11).

Workshop attendance showed little difference in knowledge when considered as a binary variable (aOR = 1.03; 95% CI: 0.15–8.45). However, when examined by frequency, higher numbers of workshops attended were associated with progressively higher odds of being in a higher knowledge category, including 1–2 workshops (aOR = 1.48), 3–4 workshops (aOR = 1.81), and five or more workshops (aOR = 5.79), compared to those who had not attended any workshops.

For perception, interns who responded “Yes” to the statement that pediatric cancers are less curable than adult cancers had higher odds of being in a higher knowledge category compared to those who responded “No” (aOR = 1.35; 95% CI: 0.70–2.60). As seen in Table 3.

Discussion

This study examined the knowledge of childhood cancer early warning signs among intern doctors and explored the influence of clinical exposure, other forms of exposure, and perceptions. Evidence on this topic among medical interns in Tanzania is limited. The findings reveal important knowledge gaps and highlight areas for improvement in medical training. This discussion interprets these findings in relation to existing literature and the Tanzanian health system context.

Intern doctors’ knowledge on childhood cancer early warning signs

Our study assessed knowledge of childhood cancer early warning signs among intern doctors in four major hospitals in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Most interns demonstrated moderate knowledge, with only a small proportion showing high knowledge levels, which were lower compared to previous studies. In South Africa, Geel et al. reported that most final-year medical students were able to recognize a significant proportion of early warning signs of childhood cancer [19]. Similarly, a study from South India found that medical students were able to identify key symptoms effectively [9]. In Saudi Arabia, a study found that more than half of medical students correctly answered basic pediatric oncology questions, demonstrating higher knowledge levels than those observed in the current study [20]. This disparity likely reflects differences in medical curricula, availability of pediatric oncology rotations, and exposure to specialized cancer care facilities. In Tanzania, pediatric oncology is typically concentrated in tertiary centers, limiting intern exposure and practical experience during both undergraduate and internship training. However, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution, as the categorization and measurement of knowledge varied across the studies.

Intern doctors demonstrated better recognition of commonly encountered cancers such as retinoblastoma, leukemia, and Wilms tumor, while awareness of less common and more complex cancers remained limited. This suggests that clinical exposure significantly influences knowledge acquisition. Similar findings have been reported in South Africa, where fewer than half of final-year medical students correctly identified warning signs of central nervous system tumors, including neurological deficits and increasing head circumference [19]. Similarly, evidence from Indonesia shows that neurological tumors are associated with the longest physician-related and overall diagnostic delays, whereas hematological malignancy conditions more familiar to clinicians, tend to have shorter delays [11]. In our context, these knowledge gaps maybe influenced by both the structure of the health system and the training environment during undergraduate and internship periods. Childhood cancer services are concentrated in a few referral hospitals, which limits exposure during internship training. Similarly, during undergraduate training, opportunities for direct clinical experience with pediatric cancer cases are often limited, as students rotate through general wards where such cases are infrequent. In addition, limited access to diagnostic services at lower-level facilities and complex referral pathways delay confirmation of diagnosis and reduce opportunities for supervised learning.

Factors associated with the knowledge of childhood cancer, early warning signs

Clinical exposure appears to play an important role in shaping knowledge among intern doctors. Interns who had any exposure to pediatric cancer cases demonstrated higher levels of knowledge, with better performance observed among those who had seen three to five or more cases. This pattern is consistent with findings from Turkey, where participation in pediatric rotations and adequate clinical training were associated with improved knowledge and competence, emphasizing the importance of hands-on clinical experience in skill development [22]. In Tanzania, however, repeated exposure to pediatric cancer cases is limited because most specialized services are limited to a small number of referral hospitals. This reduces opportunities for interns to consistently encounter and manage such cases during both undergraduate training and internship. As a result, the impact of clinical exposure on knowledge, although positive, appears modest in this setting.

The hospital setting also appeared to shape knowledge. Training in hospitals that provide pediatric cancer care also trends toward higher knowledge than those in hospitals without such services. This pattern agrees with the notion that institutional case mix and on-site specialist services offer richer learning opportunities, as implied by Martins et al., who find that professional category and clinical training strongly influence early cancer detection among health professionals [23]. Our weaker effect size compared with Martins et al. likely reflects contextual differences. Their sample includes experienced physicians and nurses with varied roles, while our interns are early-career clinicians with shorter, more uniform clinical exposure. Additionally, in the Tanzanian context, the expected benefit of training in hospitals that manage childhood cancers may be reduced by several practical constraints. Teaching quality is not always uniform across training sites, and structured bedside teaching may be limited due to high patient loads and staff shortages. Supervision by specialists can also be inconsistent, with consultants often managing large clinical workloads that limit time for teaching. In addition, the number of pediatric cancer cases seen by interns may be small or irregular, even in referral hospitals. As a result, opportunities for repeated, guided clinical learning are limited, which reduces the overall impact of exposure on knowledge acquisition.

Workshop exposure did not increase the odds of a high level of knowledge, as interns who attended workshops on pediatric oncology had comparable odds to those who did not (aOR = 1.03). However, when considering the number of workshops attended, the highest category (≥ 5 workshops) shows substantially elevated odds (aOR = 5.79). This pattern suggests that the effect of workshops may be cumulative rather than immediate, and that a single or sporadic training event is unlikely to produce measurable gains in knowledge. This finding diverges from studies that report significant improvements in provider knowledge following short-term training interventions, where structured training was associated with better detection capacity among primary healthcare workers [17]. The discrepancy may be explained by differences in training design and implementation. In the present context, workshops are often intermittent, heterogeneous in content, and not systematically integrated into internship curricula. As a result, their impact may be diluted by variability in quality, lack of reinforcement, and absence of competency-based evaluation. From a health systems perspective, this suggests that workshops, as currently implemented, function more as supplementary exposure rather than core learning mechanisms. Without standardization, repetition, and alignment with clinical practice, their contribution to sustained knowledge acquisition remains limited. The observed upward trend with increasing workshop attendance nevertheless indicates that repeated engagement may have additive value, particularly if delivered within a structured framework that incorporates case-based learning, mentorship, and follow-up assessment.

Interns who believed that childhood cancers are less curable than adult cancers showed slightly higher odds of being in a higher knowledge category (aOR = 1.35). This suggests that perceptions alone may not be a strong determinant of knowledge, particularly when other factors such as clinical exposure and other forms of exposure opportunities are limited. This finding contrasts with regional studies, such as Njuguna et al. in Kenya, where more optimistic perceptions about the curability of childhood cancers were common among healthcare providers and were often linked to improved engagement in care [24]. In contrast, the pattern observed in this study may reflect the realities of clinical practice in resource-limited settings, where late presentation, limited diagnostic capacity, and treatment constraints can influence how providers perceive outcomes. Interns exposed to such environments may develop more cautious views, which do not necessarily translate into lower knowledge of early warning signs. This indicates that knowledge of pediatric cancer warning signs can exist independently of personal beliefs about treatment outcomes. This demonstrates the need to strengthen both knowledge and attitudes in training programs, ensuring that interns can recognize early warning signs while maintaining balanced and evidence-based views on treatment outcomes.

These findings are particularly important in the Tanzanian and similar contexts, where childhood cancer outcomes are strongly affected by delays in recognition and referral. Evidence from East Africa shows that such delays are largely driven by health system inefficiencies rather than patient-related factors [25]. In this setting, intern doctors play a critical role as first-contact clinicians in busy emergency and general wards. Limited exposure during undergraduate training, together with variable clinical learning opportunities during internship, may contribute to delayed suspicion and referral of childhood cancer cases.

Conclusion

This study found that intern doctors have suboptimal knowledge of childhood cancer detection, with most demonstrating moderate rather than high levels of knowledge and notable gaps in recognizing less common and more complex cancers. While clinical exposure, training setting, and number of workshops attended showed some influence on knowledge, these effects were limited, suggesting that current training approaches may not provide sufficient or consistent learning opportunities. Given the central role of intern doctors as first-contact clinicians, these gaps have important implications for early detection and timely referral of childhood cancers. Strengthening pediatric oncology education through structured and continuous training beginning at the undergraduate level and reinforced during internship is essential. Approaches such as improved supervised clinical exposure, case-based learning, and integration of pediatric oncology into existing curricula may enhance knowledge and diagnostic confidence. Addressing these gaps is critical to support earlier recognition and improve outcomes for children with cancer in Tanzania and similar resource-limited settings.

Study strengths and limitations

This study provides recent and context-specific evidence on intern doctors’ knowledge on childhood cancer early warning signs detection in Tanzania. It includes participants from both national and regional referral hospitals and captures variation in training backgrounds. It is also among the few studies to examine multiple factors, including clinical exposure, other forms of exposure, and perceptions, among early-career doctors.

The study was conducted in only four major hospitals in Dar es Salaam, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to interns in other regions of Tanzania or similar low-resource settings.

Recommendations

Based on these findings, efforts should focus on strengthening both knowledge and practical skills. This includes integrating pediatric oncology into undergraduate and internship curricula, improving structured bedside teaching, and providing more consistent training such as workshops and continuing medical education. Simulation-based approaches may further support clinical decision-making and early recognition. These strategies could improve identification of less common cancers, promote timely referral, and reduce diagnostic delays.

Future studies should include intern doctors from a wider range of hospitals across different regions of Tanzania and similar settings to improve generalizability and capture potential regional differences in training, exposure, and knowledge. Future research should include objective evaluations such as Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), simulations, or clinical case discussions to assess practical competence in detecting and managing childhood cancer.
