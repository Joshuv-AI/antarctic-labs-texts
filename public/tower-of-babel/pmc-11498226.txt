Engineering supramolecular peptide nanofibers for in vivo platelet-hitchhiking beyond ligand-receptor recognition
Authors: Yan Feng, Chenyang Liu, Weiping Cui, Liuqing Yang, Di Wu, Hua Zhang, Xueqing Wang, Yuqian Sun, Bing He, Wenbing Dai, Qiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ex vivo or in vivo cell-hitchhiking has emerged as a potential means for efficient drug delivery and various disease therapies. However, many challenges remain, such as the complicated engineering process and dependence on ligand-receptor interaction. Here, we present a simple in vivo platelet-hitchhiking strategy based on self-assembling peptides without ligand modification. The engineered peptide nanofibers can hitchhike ultrafast (<5 s) and efficiently on both resting and activated platelets in a receptor-independent and species-independent manner. Mechanistic studies showed that unique secondary structure of nanofibers, which lead to surface exposure of hydrophobic and hydrogen bond–forming groups, might primarily contribute to the selective and efficient platelet-hitchhiking behavior. After intravenous injection, these peptide nanofibers hitchhiked in situ on circulating platelets and achieved almost 20-fold lung accumulation. Our study provides not only a different paradigm of in vivo platelet-hitchhiking beyond ligand-receptor recognition but also a potential strategy for lung-targeted drug delivery and pulmonary disease therapy.

INTRODUCTION

As self-components of living organisms, cells have shown great promise in the biomedical field, especially in drug delivery, due to their intrinsic tropism for target tissues or pathological sites, efficient therapeutic loading capability, immune escape ability, and excellent biocompatibility (1–3). Cell-hitchhiking for drug delivery and disease treatment is mainly classified into two types. The major type refers to loading therapeutic cargo or nanoparticles into cells isolated from whole blood in vitro and infusing them back into the body. The other type is when deliberately designed nanoparticles hitchhike on circulatory cells in situ after administration (4). Moreover, advances in theoretical foundations for cell-hitchhiking and cell-nanomaterial interactions have promoted the development of various cell-based delivery strategies (5–7). Depending on the cell type for hitchhiking, cell-based drug delivery systems have been successfully used for the treatment of a wide range of diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, inflammation, and bacterial infection (3, 5, 8). Over the past few years, erythrocytes (9, 10), macrophages (11, 12), T cells (13–15), and neutrophils (16–19) have gained increasing attention based on the huge demands for pathological tissue targeting, immunotherapy, and prolonging the circulation time of nanocarriers.

Platelets are fragments derived from megakaryocytes and are most widely known for their blood clotting function and involvement in intrinsic immunity and tumor metastasis (20, 21). Recently, platelets have gained increasing interest as hitchhiking vehicles because of their clear roles in damaged vascular endothelium homing and excellent targeting ability for circulating tumor cells (22, 23). Similar to other cell-hitchhiking strategies, the overwhelming majority of reported platelet-hitchhiking systems are prepared by a series of processes, including isolating the platelets, loading drugs or drug-loaded carriers into the platelets, and purifying the complex system for reinfusion (2, 24, 25). Besides being time-consuming and associated with complications, the ex vivo engineering process inevitably interferes with the structural integrity of platelets and makes it difficult to maintain the biological functions. The reported strategies for in vivo platelet-hitchhiking essentially include active-targeting delivery strategies, which rely on the binding between the ligands modified on the nanocarriers and the receptor that is specially or highly expressed on the surface of activated platelets (26, 27). The ligand-receptor interaction is susceptible to the presentation of ligands on the surface of nanocarriers, the receptor expression level of platelets, and the affinity environment, all of which may compromise the hitchhiking efficiency or cause loss of control of quality consistency. However, ligand-modified nanoparticles can also induce unwanted biological responses or side effects. Moreover, because molecular recognition-based strategies can only hitchhike on activated platelets, they not only have limited clinical application scenarios, but the outcome is also dependent on the pathological environment (28–30) and is vulnerable to individual heterogeneity (21). Therefore, it is necessary to explore a simple, general, and efficient in vivo platelet-hitchhiking strategy to address the drawbacks of existing systems.

Over the past decade, peptide self-assembly has emerged as one of the most promising supramolecular strategies in the fields of cellular scaffolding, drug delivery, disease diagnostics, and regenerative medicine (31–36). Many natural or rationally designed peptides can spontaneously generate various supramolecular assemblies such as nanofibers, nanoparticles, and nanoscaled layers by π-π stacking, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and other noncovalent driving forces (37). In particular, supramolecular peptide nanofibers have been increasingly considered as an appropriate tool for studying interactions between nanomaterials and cells because of their easy access, hydrogel formation ability, programmable bioactivity, high density of ligand display, good biocompatibility, and various polymerization pathways (38, 39).

In this work, during the preliminary screening and validation of ligand-modified peptides (LMPs) for platelet-hitchhiking, it was found that some unique peptide self-assemblies without ligand modification could efficiently, rapidly, and selectively hitchhike on activated and resting platelets (Fig. 1). Therefore, receptor-independent platelet-hitchhiking behavior was fully validated in this study. Furthermore, the characteristics and mechanisms of platelet-hitchhiking with supramolecular assemblies were systematically investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), the molecular design of self-assembling peptides, and artificial cell membrane binding. We found that supramolecular long nanofibers with specific secondary structures could selectively hitchhike on platelets in a fibrous flocking manner. Moreover, after intravenous injection, peptide nanofibers with platelet-hitchhiking capacity in vitro were found to preferentially accumulate in lung tissue. These findings not only serve as a paradigm for an in vivo platelet-hitchhiking strategy but also propose a potential lung-targeted drug delivery strategy.

RESULTS

Discovery of platelet-hitchhiking peptides

Inspired by the work on cell-hitchhiking strategies based on the molecular recognition strategy (26, 40), we initially sought to design LMP nanofibers for platelet-hitchhiking. P-selectin, a glycoprotein translocated to the plasma membrane of activated platelets, is considered an established marker for platelet activation. Therefore, the DAEWVDVS peptide, a ligand for P-selectin, was used to modify self-assembled peptides to recognize overexpressed P-selectin on activated platelets and hitchhike on platelets in this study (27, 41, 42). The designed LMPs (Fig. 2A) were composed of the N-terminal fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc), assembled module, ligand sequence, and C-terminal hydrophilic amino acids. Specifically, the Fmoc group provides a hydrophobic domain and promotes the π-π interaction with bis-phenylalanine (FF). Variable numbers of valine (V) modulate the assembly of the peptides, and glutamic acid (E) and ligand peptide contribute to the hydrophilicity of the LMPs. The peptides in this study were synthesized using the Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis method, purified by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and identified by LC–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (fig. S1). As shown in figs. S2 and S3, all of these peptides could self-assemble in the aqueous phase and form cylindrical nanofibers with lengths >1 μm. The nanofibers were then simply co-incubated with activated platelets expressing P-selectin (Fig. 2B) for 5 min. The platelet-hitchhiking efficiency of the nanofibers, herein defined as the quantity of peptides engage in cellular hitchhiking, was then determined by measuring the peptide content in the supernatant after centrifuging the platelet-nanofiber complex. As shown in Fig. 2C, LMP2 and LMP3 nanofibers, rather than LMP1 nanofibers, could efficiently hitchhike on activated platelets, with a hitchhiking efficiency of nearly 50%. As shown in Fig. 2 (D and E) and fig. S4, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and scanning TEM (STEM) measurements revealed that large amounts of fibrous LMP2 and LMP3 adhered to the surface of activated platelets in a crowded manner. As seen in the enlarged images, the hitchhiked nanofibers flocked to partial areas of the platelet membrane, crosslinked together, and formed a fibrous network, which was substantially different from the patterns of existing cell-hitchhiking systems (3, 43).

To establish whether the platelet-hitchhiking of LMPs was mediated by a ligand-receptor recognition between the DAEWVDVS peptide and P-selectin, we synthesized self-assembled peptides without ligand modification [non-LMPs (NLMPs)] on the basis of the sequence of LMPs. Notably, one more glutamate (E) was added to the C-terminal to increase hydrophilicity and enable self-assembly under physiological conditions (Fig. 2F). All of these peptides showed good self-assembly capacity (fig. S7) and could also form stable nanofibers with a diameter of approximately 10 nm and lengths in the submicrometer to micrometer range (Fig. 2G). Notably, NLMP3 tended to form shorter nanofibers than NLMP1 and NLMP2. Using the same methods, we measured and calculated the hitchhiking efficiency for various types of peptide nanofibers, including the random peptide Nap-FFE. Unexpectedly, NLMP2 not only attached to the activated platelets but also exhibited a much higher hitchhiking efficiency than LMP2 and LMP3 (Fig. 2H). Because the hitchhiking behavior of NLMP2 might not depend on their binding to specific receptors on activated platelets, we sought to determine whether NLMP2 nanofibers could hitchhike on resting platelets. As shown in Fig. 2I, the average hitchhiking efficiency of NLMP2, NLMP1, NLMP3, and Nap-FFE with resting platelets was 87.5, 3.7, 7.79, and 1.54%, respectively. The results showed that NLMP2 did have hitchhiking ability on resting platelets, whereas other peptides, which did not hitchhike on activated platelets, could not hitchhike on resting platelets either. Moreover, there was no notable difference in the hitchhiking efficiency of NLMP2 nanofibers between resting and activated platelets (fig. S8). In addition, we also investigated the ratio of platelets that engage in hitchhiking of peptides. As shown in fig. S9, nearly all of the platelets were hitchhiked by NLMP2 assemblies after incubation for 5 min.

To confirm whether the NLMP2 monomer had platelet-hitchhiking capability, we examined the hitchhiking efficiency of different concentrations of NLMP2 with the same amount (3 × 108 cells/ml) of platelets. The results showed a precipitous drop in the efficiency of cell hitchhiking when the concentration of NLMP2 was below the critical aggregation concentration (CAC), indicating that the NLMP2 monomer had little ability to bind to platelets (Fig. 2L). In other words, efficient platelet-hitchhiking behavior was mediated by supramolecular peptide aggregates.

Next, the morphological characteristics of complexes formed by platelets with NLMP2 assemblies were assessed. The results of STEM (fig. S10) and confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) images (fig. S11) were similar to those of LMP2 or LMP3 hitchhiking on activated platelets. NLMP2 formed a denser and cross-linked fibrous network around the platelet, whereas the other two peptides (NLMP1 and NLMP3) were virtually absent. We also compared the morphology and structure of complexes formed by NLMP2 hitchhiking on activated or resting platelets, which showed almost identical results (Fig. 2, J and K). Ligand modification was not necessary for self-assembled peptides to hitchhike on platelets, and NLMP2 nanofibers hitchhiked on both resting and activated platelets in the form of fibrous networks.

Platelet-hitchhiking characteristics of supramolecular peptide nanofibers

As peptide assemblies efficiently hitchhiked on platelets, we next investigated the characteristics of hitchhiking. It is well known that cell targeting mediated by ligand-receptor recognition is generally species dependent (9), which motivated us to examine whether this cell-hitchhiking can be freed from species restriction. NLMP2 nanofibers could hitchhike on resting platelets derived from different species, including humans, with the same efficiency (Fig. 3A). In addition, according to the results of the temporal dynamics study (Fig. 3B), the platelet-hitchhiking occurred quickly. In particular, approximately 60% of NLMP2 hitchhiked on resting platelets during the first 5 s and then went through slower growth and achieved equilibrium at 5 min. To investigate cell-hitchhiking selectivity, a thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assay was used to image the NLMP2 nanofibers. Notably, when platelets and red blood cells were present simultaneously, peptide nanofibers preferentially formed radiating aggregations around the platelets (Fig. 3C). Evidently, NLMP2 nanofibers had high selectivity between platelets and red blood cells, which account for more than 99% of all blood cells. Similarly, an increased quantity of NLMP2 nanofibers directly adhered to platelets when simultaneously exposed to both platelets and leukocytes (Fig. 3, D and E). Furthermore, the function of platelets was examined after binding with NLMP2 nanofibers. Flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 3G) and SEM images (fig. S12) showed notable activation of resting platelets by NLMP2 nanofiber binding. In addition, NLMP2 nanofibers enhanced platelet secretion of cytokines. Compared to CaCl2, a typical agonist of platelet activation, NLMP2 nanofibers notably increased platelet secretion of epidermal growth factor (EGF), platelet-derived growth factor–AB (PDGF-AB), and vascular EGF (VEGF) (fig. S13). Moreover, the relative high turbidity of NLMP2 nanofibers group reflected that platelet-hitchhiking systems inhibited adenosine diphosphate (ADP)–induced platelet aggregation (Fig. 3F and fig. S14).

We then focused our study on the distribution landscape of nanofibers on the platelet membrane. The NLMP2 nanofibers tended to have both dense and sparse distribution areas on the platelet membrane (fig. S15). Given the possible retention of the collagen-binding capacity of hitchhiked platelets, we next examined the hydrogel formation capacity of platelet-hitchhiked peptides on the type IV collagen-coated surface. As shown in Fig. 3I, platelet-NLMP2 complexes could rapidly form a hydrogel layer, whereas NLMP2 nanofibers alone or platelet-NLMP1 complexes could not. The gelation concentration of NLMP2 is approximately 0.25 mg/ml in this context, which is much lower than that of NLMP2 alone (approximately 7.0 mg/ml) (Fig. 3J).

Platelet-hitchhiking mechanism of supramolecular peptide nanofibers

In addition to receptor-mediated binding between cells and nanomaterials, many other structural and surface properties of nanomaterials could also lead to their interaction (39, 44). Next, structural analysis of peptide assemblies was performed to explore the potential mechanism of platelet hitchhiking. Generally, the structural properties of peptide assemblies are governed by the peptide monomers; therefore, we designed a series of derivatives based on NLMP2 to investigate the possible structure- or sequence-involved hitchhiking mechanism. As shown in Fig. 4A, a variety of derivative peptides (DPs) were designed by changing the N-terminal modification or replacing valine (V) in NLMP2 in consideration of valine’s important role in platelet-hitchhiking (figs. S16 and S17). The TEM images (fig. S18) illustrated that all derivatives could self-assemble into cylindrical nanofibers with diameters similar to those of NLMP2 assemblies, except DP4 and DP5, which formed irregular assemblies and flat nanoribbons, respectively. The variability in platelet-hitchhiking capacity among multiple derivatives was also observed (Fig. 4B). Fortunately, nanofibers self-assembled by DP3, the derivative obtained by replacing the valine of NLMP2 with tyrosine (Y), had a platelet-hitchhiking efficiency close to that of NLMP2 nanofibers (78.6% versus 79.7%).

According to the reported studies, the secondary structure of peptides may have an impact on the interaction between peptide nanofibers and cells (45). As a result, we compared the variances in circular dichroism (CD) spectra among various peptide assemblies. We noticed that the secondary structure of NLMP2 nanofibers was much less ordered than those of NLMP1 and NLMP3, both of which barely hitchhiked on platelets. As shown in fig. S19, the CD spectra of NLMP1 revealed a positive peak at 200 to 205 nm and a strong negative peak at 216 to 218 nm, a typical spectrum of β sheet secondary structure. The CD spectra of NLMP3 nanofibers had a clear positive peak near 206 nm, which showed that the secondary structure was dominated by β turn. In contrast, NLMP2 nanofibers did not exhibit typical α helix, β sheet, or β turn CD spectra, and the highest proportion of the secondary structure was random coils (48.7% random coils, 38.9% β sheet, and 12.3% β turn). It is noteworthy that the CD spectra of NLMP2 and DP3 nanofibers were almost consistent with each other (Fig. 4C). Specifically, the secondary structure composition of DP3 was composed of 52% random coils, 33.8% of β sheet, and 14.3% of β turn. It was noted that the CD spectra of other peptide assemblies obviously differed from those of NLMP2 and DP3 nanofibers, which could account for their poor platelet-hitchhiking efficiency. At this point, we cannot help but think that if the secondary structure of NLMP2 is changed, its platelet-hitchhiking capability may be compromised. Therefore, NLMP2 nanofibers with different secondary structures were successfully prepared through reasonable control of the assembly pathway. As shown in fig. S20 and Fig. 4D, the as-prepared NLMP2 nanofibers exhibited comparable morphology but distinct secondary structures compared to the original nanofibers. Expectedly, as depicted in Fig. 4D, the alteration of the secondary structure resulted in a notable decrease in the platelet-hitchhiking efficiency of NLMP2 nanofibers (40.7% versus 76.0%). In other words, the unique secondary structure of peptide assemblies might be a dominating factor for efficient hitchhiking on platelets.

Although the secondary structure was considered a key governing force in platelet hitchhiking, it is an implicit property of supramolecular assemblies. A given specific secondary structure would endow self-assembled nanofibers with distinct explicit properties, especially surface features, for regulating the cell-nanofiber interaction. To investigate which property is associated with platelet-hitchhiking abilities, we comprehensively characterized the microscopic surface structure of NLMP2 and DP3 nanofibers by AFM. DP1 and DP2 nanofibers were chosen as controls because of their resemblance to NLMP2 structures but minimal binding with platelets. NLMP2 and DP3 nanofibers presented a substantial quantity of rough bulges on their surfaces (figs. S21 and S22). Conversely, the self-assembled structures of DP1 and DP2 (fig. S22) showed dissimilar attributes, specifically a smooth plane on the surface of fibers. It is speculated that the rougher surface texture of peptide assemblies achieves harmony with the rugose surface of platelets, which would be beneficial to their platelet-hitchhiking ability. In addition, hydrophobic interaction is one of the most important nonspecific interactions at the nanobio interface (6, 44). Next, 8-aniline-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS), a fluorescent probe frequently used to characterize protein hydrophobicity, was used to calculate the degree of hydrophobicity on the nanofiber surface. The experimental results (fig. S23) showed that the S0 values of NLMP2 and DP3 were 3364.3 and 5539.4, respectively, both of which are notably higher than those of DP2 (981.53) and DP1 (1458.3). The S0 value, representing the overall hydrophobic property of the peptide fiber surface, increased with the enhancement of fiber hydrophobicity (46). These results indicated that peptide assemblies with platelet-hitchhiking ability had a more hydrophobic surface, which may be the result of hydrophobic group exposure on the surface induced by the specific secondary structure of peptides. To confirm whether the hydrophobic surface of nanofibers was directly involved in platelet-hitchhiking processes, one or two glutamates (E) were added at the C-terminal of NLMP2 and DP3 to decrease the hydrophobicity of peptide assemblies (table S1 and figs. S24 and S25). The TEM images (fig. S26) showed that all of these derivatives could form regular cylindrical nanofibers. The data depicted in Figs. 4, E and F revealed a notable decrease in hitchhiking efficiency as the hydrophilicity of the peptide molecules increased. In addition, the findings in fig. S27 indicate that the extent of nanofibers surrounding the platelets decreased gradually with an increase in the hydrophilicity of the peptide, highlighting the crucial role of the hydrophobic surface in the platelet-hitchhiking process.

The effect of the structural integrity of peptide nanofibers on hitchhiking efficiency was also examined via freezing thawing. As shown in figs. S28 and S29 (A and C), the results of TEM and CD spectra illustrated that the macroscopic and secondary structures of the NLMP2/DP3 assemblies were disrupted. As shown in fig. S29 (B and D), a notable decrease in the hitchhiking efficiency of both NLMP2 and DP3 was observed. These results again revealed that the presentation and maintenance of distinct random coil-dominated secondary structures were important for the platelet-hitchhiking ability of these peptide assemblies.

Although NLMP2 does not contain peptide sequence of a known ligand, we still evaluated whether this hitchhiking behavior of NLMP2 nanofibers was platelet membrane receptor dependent. Tirofiban, monoclonal antibody anti-GPVI (JAQ1) (47), and prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) (48) were used to inhibit glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa), glycoprotein VI (GPVI), and purinergic receptor (P2Y1), respectively. As depicted in Fig. 4G, the platelet-hitchhiking efficiency of NLMP2 nanofibers retained its original level after receptor blocking. Combining the receptor-blocking results and the sequence of NLMP2, we conclude that the platelet-hitchhiking behavior of NLMP2 nanofibers was not dependent on the molecular recognition between the receptor and ligand. In view of the receptor-independence nature and fibrous structure macroscopically flocking to the regional area of platelet membrane after binding of NLMP2 nanofibers, we termed this binding behavior as receptor-independent fibrous flocking hitchhiking.

Compared to the erythrocyte membrane, the platelet membrane contained more highly unsaturated phospholipids and less cholesterol (49). To recapitulate the dissimilarities in cellular membranes between red blood cells and platelets, dioleoylphosphatidylcholine (DOPC) vesicles containing 10% cholesterol and hydrogenated soy phosphatidylcholine (HSPC) vesicles containing 30% cholesterol were synthesized (fig. S30). The confocal results depicted in fig. S31 illustrate that NLMP2 nanofibers had more colocalization with DOPC vesicles than with HSPC vesicles. In addition, it is noteworthy that NLMP2 nanofibers exhibited exclusive enrichment around DOPC vesicles in the presence of both types of vesicles (Fig. 4H), a phenomenon analogous to the selectivity of NLMP2 between platelets and erythrocytes. Furthermore, the findings depicted in fig. S32 indicate that the temporal dynamic binding behavior of NLMP2 nanofibers with DOPC vesicles closely approximated to that with platelets. Meanwhile, we also found that the peptides, barely hitchhiking on platelets, also hardly bound with DOPC vesicles (Fig. 4I and fig. S33). To further clarify the mechanism of this platelet-selective hitchhiking phenomenon, all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to study the interaction between NLMP2 assemblies and phospholipid membrane. As shown in figs. S34 and S35, NLMP2 assemblies had a tendency to generate more robust interaction with less rigid DOPC membrane. Moreover, in comparison to phospholipid membranes primarily composed of 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, those primarily composed of DOPC exhibited enhanced van der Waals and Coulomb forces with NLMP2 assemblies. By analyzing the binding interface between NLMP2 assemblies and phospholipid membrane, it was found that NLMP2 nanofibers could form hydrogen bonds with the phosphate group of DOPC via the hydroxyl of glutamic acid and also with the terminal hydroxyl of cholesterol via the amide group at its C terminus. On the basis of the above results, we proposed that the hydrogen bonding between the less rigid biomembrane and NLMP2 assemblies was responsible for this selective cell binding and cellular-hitchhiking behavior.

Overall, these results provide important insights into the possible hitchhiking mechanism of peptide nanofibers. When the self-assembled peptide in our work adopts a distinct secondary structure, it contributes to the formation of a less ordered nanofibrous structure with a specific nanotopographical, hydrophobic surface and hydrogen bond–forming groups exposure. Consequently, these nanofibers may selectively bind to platelets with similar hydrophobic, less rigid, and rugose plasma membrane in a receptor-independent manner (Fig. 5).

Hitchhiking on platelets in vivo and lung targeting of supramolecular peptide nanofibers

Next, we examined the in vivo fate of peptide assemblies and whether NLMP2 nanofibers could in situ hitchhike on platelets circulating in the blood. Figure 6B shows that Cy5.5-labeled NLMP2 nanofibers efficiently accumulated in the lung after intravenous injection. Moreover, the number of lung-accumulated nanofibers was demonstrated to be highly related to their in vitro platelet-hitchhiking ability. NLMP2 nanofibers exhibited more than 20-fold greater fluorescence intensity in the lungs than the NLMP3 nanofibers (Fig. 6C and fig. S37). Correspondingly, the distribution of NLMP2 in the liver and spleen was reduced. In addition, lung targeting transpired expeditiously within 5 min following intravenous administration (Fig. 6E), plausibly attributed to the ultrafast and extremely efficient platelet-hitchhiking behavior of NLMP2. Furthermore, the hypothesis that NLMP2 could hitchhike on platelets in vivo was indirectly validated via immunofluorescence. The results shown in fig. S38 indicate that NLMP2 in the pulmonary tract could colocalize with platelets within a certain range, suggesting that platelet hitchhiking may generate pulmonary tropism. To confirm the morphology of NLMP2 in the lung, ultrathin sections of the lung were prepared 5 min after intravenous injection of NLMP2. The results shown in Fig. 6D demonstrated that NLMP2 nanofibers could form complexes with platelets, which is very likely attributed to in situ platelet hitchhiking in the blood. In addition, a notable decrease in platelet count (Fig. 6F) was observed compared to that in the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group after the injection of NLMP2, while there were no obvious changes in the number of erythrocytes (Fig. 6G) and leukocytes (fig. S39). We speculated that platelets rather than erythrocytes and leukocytes were bound by NLMP2 and transported to the lungs. Platelet-mediated lung targeting was also demonstrated by the fact that the pulmonary tropism of NLMP2 was markedly lost when the platelets were depleted via the R300 antibody before the administration of NLMP2 (Fig. 6, H and I) (50). As platelets hitchhiked by NLMP2, the forward scatter values changed notably, indicating that the nanofiber-platelet complexes had a higher volume than platelets themselves (fig. S40). Overall, these results suggest that NLMP2 could form complexes by hitchhiking on platelets in vivo and distribute to the lung through mechanical interception due to enlarged volume of platelet-nanofiber complexes, which was similar to the typical passive lung-targeting manner (9, 51).

Last, we performed animal pharmacokinetics (PK) and preliminary in vitro and in vivo biosafety evaluation of NLMP2. From the plasma concentration time curve (Fig. 6, J and K), it was easy to see that NLMP2 exhibited a much more rapid blood clearance than NLMP1 after intravenous administration with a same dose. The main PK parameters in table S3 also confirmed it. It was speculated that after intravenous injection of NLMP2 nanofibers, following injection, a substantial quantity of them binds with platelets very quickly and efficiently and then accumulated in the lung tissue in a platelet-hitchhiking manner, thus resulting in a fast clearance NLMP2 from systemic circulation. The results of in vitro safety assessment showed that NLMP2 assemblies did not appear negative impact on activity of leukocyte and would not cause hemolysis of erythrocyte (fig. S41). With regards to the in vivo safety, according to the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining results (Fig. 6N), no notable organ damage (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) was observed 48 hours after NLMP2 injection. Routine blood tests at 48 hours after injection also showed no notable differences in the counts of red blood cells, leukocytes, and platelets between the NLMP2 and PBS groups (fig. S42). Moreover, the blood biochemistry test (fig. S43) profile of the kidney and liver showed that NLMP2 did not affect organ function. Besides, there was no notable elevation observed in the level of the inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) (Fig. 6, L and M) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the bloodstream and lung of mice after injection of NLMP2 (fig. S44). In addition, the bleeding time of tail transection mice (fig. S45), which is an indicator of hemostatic ability, was not affected after administration of NLMP2 nanofibers via the tail vein. In summary, the aforementioned studies provided evidence that NLMP2 nanofibers had favorable biocompatibility in vivo.

DISCUSSION

In vivo cell hitchhiking has emerged as an attractive alternative for cell-based drug delivery and disease treatment. In this work, we developed an in vivo platelet-hitchhiking strategy based on self-assembled peptides. Unlike traditional design patterns based on ligand-receptor interaction for in vivo activated platelet-hitchhiking strategies, the supramolecular peptide nanofibers, represented by NLMP2 nanofibers, could selectively, very quickly (within seconds), and efficiently hitch a ride on both resting and activated platelets in a manner that nanofibers could in situ flock to the platelet membrane. The in vivo platelet-hitchhiking strategies of our work exhibit several unique advantages over receptor-mediated active targeted hitchhiking strategies. First, this strategy exhibits sufficiently high platelet-hitchhiking efficiency, that is, more than 90% of the engineered peptide nanofibers can bind with platelets. Second, and more importantly, as the platelet-hitchhiking efficiency of our strategy is not independent of the ligand-receptor interaction, the performance of our strategy is more controllable and will not be affected by heterogeneity of receptor expression and disease progression. However, the performance of receptor-mediated active targeted delivery systems would be inevitably affected by the factors mentioned above. Moreover, the engineered nanofibers can hitchhike on resting platelets, which can broaden the future application in disease treatment without platelet activation. Last, the hitchhiking efficiency of NLMP2 nanofibers with platelets was consistent across different species, including humans, which reduces the concerns of redesign and species variation in possible future clinical translation.

Obtaining a complete understanding of cell-nanomaterial interactions remains challenging. In addition, our work has limitations in terms of elucidating the platelet-hitchhiking mechanism of our engineered nanofibers. Our preliminary results suggested that the unique secondary structure of self-assembled peptides, which drive the formation of the unique surface of nanofibers, may play a decisive role in the platelet-hitchhiking behavior of supramolecular nanofibers studied in this work. For example, the platelet-hitchhiking efficiency of nanofibers from the same peptide was greatly reduced when the secondary structure was changed, while the similar long nanofiber structure remained. Although a better proof-of-concept method would be to change the original secondary structure of self-assembled peptides without platelet-hitchhiking ability and give them the platelet-binding ability, we cannot achieve the desired secondary structure change at this stage. Although the result of molecular dynamics simulation indicated that the hydrogen bond formation may play an important role in efficient and selective binding between the NLMP2 nanofibers and less rigid membrane, there are evident difference between the simulation condition and the actual condition of nanofiber-platelet binding. In addition, our work mainly focused on the discovery of platelet-hitchhiking supramolecular nanofibers, repeatedly verifying the hitchhiking ability, and studying their in vitro and in vivo behaviors. Therefore, the drug loading and disease treatment studies will be addressed in our future work.

In summary, we developed an in vivo platelet-hitchhiking strategy based on peptide self-assembly. In vivo hitchhiking avoids the perturbation of platelets involved in the ex vivo strategies that require platelet extraction and engineering procedures. The well-designed peptide nanofibers can efficiently hitchhike on both resting and activated platelets in a receptor- and species-independent fashion. The hitchhiking paradigm of nanofibers and the mechanism study may be used for reference in engineered systems for hitchhiking on other cells. We further found that the above-mentioned nanofibers exhibited extensive accumulation in the lung after intravenous injection, which presents more opportunities for lung-targeted drug delivery and illness treatment.

Materials

EPR4330) was purchased from Abcam (UK); phycoerythrin (PE) anti-mouse CD62P antibody (catalog no.148305) and Pacific Blue anti-mouse CD45(catalog no. 103125) were purchased from BioLegend (USA); collagen type IV powder derived from human placenta was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA); goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L/AF488 antibody (catalog no. bs-0295G-AF488) was purchased from Bioss (China); anti-GPIba R300 was purchased from Emfret Analytics (Germany); 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (DiI) and 1,1-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine perchlorate (DiD) were purchased from Bioss (China); DOPC was purchased from TCI (Japan); HSPC was purchased from AVT (China); 1, 2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine–poly(ethylene glycol), molecular weight 2000 was purchased from Nippon Oil Corporation (Japan); ADP was purchased from Steellex (China); mouse EGF, VEGF, and PDGF-AB ELISA kits were purchased from Yojanbio (China); and Hochest 33258 was purchased from Solarbio (China); mouse TNF-α and IL-6 ELISA kits were purchased from BioLegend (USA); FIXABLE VIABILITY DYE EF506 was purchased from Invitrogen (USA).

BALB/c mice (6 to 8 weeks, 18 to 20 g, male) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Peking University Health Science Center (Beijing, China) and kept under specific pathogen–free conditions. All animal experiments were performed at the Center for Experimental Animals, Peking University. All of the animal experiments were executed according to the guidelines of the National Institutes of Animal Care and Use Committee and the protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (ethical approval number: BCJB0093).

Peptide synthesis

The peptides were synthesized on rink amide-MBHA resin using the solid-phase synthesis method. Before coupling amino acids, 0.25 mmol resin was swollen with dichloromethane (DCM) for 30 min and transferred into a shaking vessel. Then, N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) that contained 20% piperidine (v/v) was added to remove the protective group of resin and was shaken for 30 min. To conjugate amino acid, fmoc-amino acid, HBTU, and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA) (resin/amino acid/HBTU/DIEA = 0.25 mmol:1 mmol:2.5 mmol) were dissolved in DMF, added to the resin, and reacted for 2 hours. After the reaction was completed, the resin was washed three times with DMF and DCM. Before conjugating the next amino acid, DMF solution containing piperidine was added into the vessel to remove the protective group and washed with DMF three times. To cleave the peptide from the resin, 10 ml of cleavage solution [92.5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), 2.5% H2O, and 5% triisopropylsilane] was added and shaken for 3 hours. After removing TFA under air, the crude peptides were precipitated with icy diethyl ether. Last, the crude peptide was purified by preparative HPLC (Waters, USA) and identified with LC-MS (AB SCIEX 4000, USA).

Platelet isolation

Fresh blood was collected from healthy male BALB/c mice, Sprague-Dawley rats, rabbits, and donors. The protocol of human blood experiments was approved by the ethics committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (ethical approval number: 2021PHB362-001). Informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsink. Sodium citrate–anticoagulated blood was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 4 min at room temperature to remove red and white blood cells. Then, the obtained platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was diluted with an equal volume of acid-citrate-dextrose (ACD; 39 mM citric acid, 75 mM sodium citrate, and 135 mM dextrose) buffer and centrifuged at 1250g for 8 min. The pellet of platelets was gently rinsed with ACD buffer and centrifuged again. Last, the platelet precipitate was gently resuspended by PBS containing EDTA. To activate platelets, ADP (final concentration: 3 × 10−5 M) was added into the platelet suspension and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C.

Flow cytometry for detecting platelet activation

For flow cytometry, the number of platelets was adjusted to 1 × 107 cells/ml in PBS. To test whether the platelets were activated by the peptide nanofiber hitchhiker, self-assembled peptide solution (80 μM, final concentration) was added to the platelet suspension and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. The platelets were fixed using 1% paraformaldehyde before staining. Then, 2.5 μl of anti-CD62P antibody (PE labeled) was added into 300 μl of platelet suspension and incubated at room temperature for 20 min before detecting the samples immediately using a flow cytometer (FACS Calibur, BD).

Platelet-hitchhiking efficiency calculation

The peptide to be tested was diluted to 1 mg/ml with PBS and kept at room temperature overnight. Next, 350 μl of the peptide solution was carefully mixed with 150 μl of platelets (3 × 108 cells/ml) in PBS. Then, the mixture was incubated for 5 min at room temperature and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 25°C before determining the peptide concentration in the supernatant by HPLC (Shimazu, Japan). For the control group, an equal volume of PBS was added instead of platelets. The hitchhiking efficiency of the peptide was calculated as followsPlatelet−hitchhiking efficiency%=(1−Cx∕C0)*100where Cx refers to the peptide concentration of the supernatant after incubation with platelets and C0 refers to the peptide concentration of the supernatant of the control group.

Characterization of peptide assemblies

The morphology of peptide assemblies was observed via TEM (JEM1400PLUS, JEOL Ltd. Japan) and AFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker AXS). For TEM measurement, samples were prepared using the negative stain method with 1% uranyl acetate. For AFM measurement, samples were dropped on the clean mica surface and air-dried. The secondary structure of peptide assemblies was recorded by a CD spectrometer (J1500, JASCO, Japan) with a 0.2-nm step size.

Morphology assessment of the peptide-platelet complex

For SEM measurement, samples were prepared as reported previously (52). To observe the overall morphology of platelets and nanofibers simultaneously, a SEM image of the complex was captured by a SEM (JSM-7900F, JEOL Ltd. Japan). The STEM mode was used to observe the peptide nanofibers around the platelet clearly.

CAC assay

The CAC of peptide was determined using a ThT assay. A peptide solution with a wide concentration range was mixed with an equal volume of ThT stock solution (0.32 mg/ml). After overnight incubation, the fluorescence intensity of the peptide solution was recorded by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Shimadzu RF6000, Japan) with excitation/emission: 450/482 nm.

Fluorescence images of platelets with peptide assemblies

Before mixing with platelets, peptide powder was dissolved in PBS and diluted to 1 mg/ml. The peptide solution was incubated with an equal volume of ThT solution (3.2 mg/ml) overnight at room temperature. Then, 350 μl of peptide solution was gently mixed with 150 μl of resting platelet suspension (3 × 108 cells/ml), with platelets stained with ThT solution (1.6 mg/ml) set as the control. The mixture was kept for 5 min at room temperature. The distribution of peptide assemblies around platelets was observed by CLSM (ZEISS LSM880, Germany).

Assessment of the cell selectivity of NLMP2 nanofibers

To investigate the cellular selectivity of the hitchhiking behavior of peptide assemblies, an equal number (1 × 108 cells/ml) of platelets and erythrocytes were mixed in PBS before incubating with ThT-stained peptide assemblies. Then, the peptide solution was gently added into the cell suspension. After incubating for 5 min at room temperature, the distribution of peptide assemblies between platelets and erythrocytes was detected by CLSM immediately.

The cellular selectivity assay of peptide nanofibers for platelets and leukocytes was conducted using flow cytometry. The isolated cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently labeled with fluorescent markers. The two cell types were mixed before adding Cy5.5-labeled co-assembled peptide nanofibers and incubating for 30 min and immediately assayed.

Platelet aggregation assay

Platelet aggregation was performed by the turbidimetric method. Platelet-rich plasma and platelet-poor plasma, which was produced by centrifuging PRP at 1250g for 8 min, were set as controls. Next, 200 μl of peptides (0.5 mg/ml) or PBS was gently added to 100 μl of platelet suspension. Subsequently, the mixture was transferred into a cuvette before adding ADP. The platelet aggregation level was recorded by a blood coagulation analyzer (Steellex SC20, China) after oscillation for 2000 s.

Hydrogel formation induced by type IV collagen

Type IV collagen could be solubilized to a concentration of 1 mg/ml in 0.5 M acetic acid. Next, 150 μl of collagen suspension was uniformly added to each well of the 24-well assay plate and incubated overnight at 4°C. Thereafter, the collagen-coated 24-well plates were washed three times with PBS buffer. To test the gel-forming capacity of the platelet peptide complexes on the collagen surface, different concentrations of peptide solutions were mixed with platelet suspension (3 × 108 cells/ml). Last, 150 μl of the sample to be tested was gently added to the collagen-coated wells and incubated for 5 min at 37°C.

Hydrophobicity assay of peptide assemblies

The hydrophobic property of the nanofiber was characterized by ANS fluorescence assays. Before detection, 10 μl of ANS stock solution (1 mM) was transferred to a peptide PBS solution with a concentration ranging from 0.0625 to 1 mM and kept at room temperature overnight. The emission spectra signal from 480 to 700 nm of peptide-ANS solution was recorded by a spectrofluorophotometer (Shimadzu RF6000, Japan) with excitation at 380 nm. The S0 value, which represented the hydrophobic ability of the nanofiber surface, was the initial slope of the peptide concentration versus the largest fluorescence intensity of the spectra.

Disruption of intact peptide assemblies

We next developed a quick frozen method to break the intact secondary structure of the peptide nanofiber and avoid introducing impurities. Briefly, the peptide solution was transferred into liquid nitrogen directly for 30 min and thawed at room temperature. The broken nanofiber was used within 2 hours.

Preparation of an artificial cell membrane

To prepare the DOPC artificial cell membrane, DOPC, cholesterol, and DSPE–PEG-2000 were dissolved in 1 ml of chloroform at the mass ratio of 90:10:1, and the total mass of lipid material was 3 mg. For fluorescently labeled formulations, DiI was loaded into the membrane of the artificial cellular vesicles. After removing chloroform by rotary evaporation and forming a homogeneous lipid film at the bottom of the vial, it was resuspended with 1 ml of PBS. Last, the lipid solution was sonicated using a bath sonicator at a power of 40 W for 20 min. The morphology of DOPC vesicles was characterized by TEM. HSPC vesicles were composed of HSPC, cholesterol, and DSPE–PEG-2000 with a mass ratio of 70:30:1, prepared in a similar manner to DOPC vesicles, while DiD was used to label the HSPC vesicles.

Preparation of Cy5.5-labeled peptide nanofiber

The self-assembled peptide (Fmoc-FFEEK-NH2), Cy5.5–N-hydroxysuccinimide, HBTU, and DIEA were mixed in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with the molar ratio of 1:1:1:3. The reaction proceeded overnight, and DMSO was removed by the dialysis method. The obtained Cy5.5-labeled peptide was purified by preparative HPLC (Waters, USA) and identified with LC-MS. Fmoc-FFEEK(Cy5.5)-NH2 and peptides were dissolved in 10 μl of DMSO with a molar ratio of 1:300. To obtain co-aassembled Cy5.5-labeled nanofibers, the DMSO solution was diluted to 1 ml by PBS and incubated overnight.

Biodistribution of peptide assemblies

To detect the biodistribution of different self-assembly peptides in vivo, 150 μl of Cy5.5-labeled nanofibers (1 mg/ml) was injected via the tail vein. The in vivo fluorescence signals of major organs—such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney—were recorded by IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) at different time points.

In vivo platelet-hitchhiking assay of peptides

Healthy BALB/c mice were injected intravenously with Cy5.5-labeled NLMP2 or NLMP3 nanofibers at an equal peptide dose of 7.5 mg/kg. At 30 min after injection, the mice were euthanized, and their lungs were harvested to be prepared into frozen sections with a thickness of 10 μm. Then, the frozen sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min and washed twice with PBS. The fixed slices were incubated with blocking buffer for 1 hour and washed with PBS. Next, the slices were incubated with CD41 antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with goat anti-rat IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 488 for 1 hour. Last, the nucleus was stained by Hochest (33258) and washed three times with PBS. Last, we performed an immunofluorescence assay of the lung sections using CLSM (ZEISS LSM880, Germany).

Preparation of ultrathin sections

First, healthy male mice were injected with 100 μl of NLMP2 (1 mg/ml) peptide via the tail vein. After 5 min, the mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol by intraperitoneal injection. When the mice were unconscious, the skin of the chest was cut open to expose the subcutaneous tissue. After cutting open the chest cavity, the pericardium was separated with forceps to expose the heart. Then, the right auricle was cut open with scissors before lifting the apex and inserting the prepared saline syringe into the left ventricle. First, saline was instilled to drain the blood; when the fluid coming out of the right heart ear was clarified, the saline was immediately switched to 2.5% glutaraldehyde instillation. Last, the lung tissue was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4°C overnight. To prepare ultrathin sections, the harvested lung tissue was washed three times with PBS and fixed with 1% OsO4. The dehydration step was conducted as follows: 70% acetone for 10 min, 80% acetone for 10 min, 90% acetone for 10 min, and 100% acetone for 10 min. Then, the sample was embedded with epoxy resin, cut into ultrathin sections, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before observing by TEM.

In vivo biosafety evaluation

Before routine blood tests and H&E staining, healthy male BALB/c mice were randomly divided into two groups. Forty-eight hours after administration of NLMP2 nanofibers or PBS via tail vein (150 μl; NLMP2 concentration: 1.0 mg/ml), 20 μl of blood was collected from the orbits of the anesthetized mice and added to the diluent solution for immediate routine blood testing (n = 3) using a HITACHI 7020 Automatic Biochemical Analyzer (HITACHI, Japan). To recognize the morphological changes of tissues, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected and subjected to the H&E staining protocol. For the blood chemistry test, serum from the mice of the NLMP2 and saline groups was collected and analyzed for indicators including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, creatinine, and urea nitrogen at 48 hours after administration of NLMP2 nanofibers. The effect of NLMP2 on hemostasis in vivo was studied by the mouse tail transection bleeding model as reported previously (53). Briefly, at different time points after administration of 150 μl of NLMP2 (1.0 mg/ml) or PBS, a 0.4-cm transverse cut was made from the tail end of the anesthetized mice, and the bleeding time was measured.

Hemolysis rate assay

To examine whether self-assembled peptides promote hemolysis of red blood cells, the peptides were dissolved in phosphate buffer and formulated into solutions of varying concentrations. Then, 50 μl of peptide solution was mixed with 500 μl of erythrocyte suspension and 450 μl of 0.9% NaCl solution, followed by incubation for 90 min at 37°C. Last, the mixed suspension was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance of acquired supernatant was measured at 540 nm. Sterile distilled water was used as the positive control, and 0.9% NaCl solution was used as the negative control. Hemolysis rate was calculated as reported method (54).

Leucocyte viability assay

The peptide to be tested was solubilized in phosphate buffer, followed by the addition of 100 μl of the peptide solution to 300 μl of leukocyte suspension, and subsequently incubated for 5 min. The sample group was incubated on ice for 20 min after the addition of Fixable Viability Dye EF506, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The pellets were then washed with PBS and incubated with Pacific Blue anti-mouse CD45 antibody for 20 min. Next, the samples were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with PBS, and analyzed using flow cytometry.

PK study

Randomized groups of mice were administered via tail vein with self-assembled peptide suspension at a single dose of 10 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected from retrobulbar venous plexus at specific time point (before administration, 2 min, 10 min, 30 min, 1 hour, 2 hours, 4 hours, 6 hours, and 24 hours after administration). Last, Protein precipitation was conducted by the addition of methanol to the samples, followed by analysis using LC-MS.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the means ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using two-tailed Student’s t test for two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).
