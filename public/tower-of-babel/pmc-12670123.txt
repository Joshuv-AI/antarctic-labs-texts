Indirect Annuloplasty for Functional Mitral Regurgitation: CINCH or CLIP?
Authors: Mi Chen, Lian Krivoshei, Maurizio Taramasso
Published: 2025

Transcatheter mitral edge-to-edge repair (TEER) has recently achieved a Class IA recommendation for patients with functional mitral regurgitation (MR) in the latest guideline, supported by robust evidence from two major randomized controlled trials—the Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation (COAPT) and the Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure: 2nd Trial in Patients with Clinically Significant Functional Mitral Regurgitation (RESHAPE-HF2) trials.1, 2, 3 These trials established TEER as a cornerstone therapy for appropriately selected “COAPT-like” patients, characterized by proportionate MR and preserved leaflet coaptation.

Fichtlscherer et al.4 reported the interim outcomes of the CINCH registry, a prospective, multicenter study evaluating transcatheter indirect annuloplasty with the Carillon Mitral Contour System (Cardiac Dimensions). Across 22 centers in Germany, 228 patients with secondary mitral regurgitation were enrolled and followed for a median of 2 years. Notably, the registry included both heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)—a population largely excluded from TEER studies.

The results are remarkable: the proportion of patients in New York Heart Association functional class (NYHA) class III or IV fell from 81 to 29% at 1 year, and the rate of moderate-to-severe MR (grade 3+/4+) decreased from 84 to 9%. Over 5 years, ≥92% patients maintained stable or improved NYHA class and ≥97% showed sustained MR improvement. Serious device- or procedure-related adverse events occurred in only 1.8% of patients. These findings reinforce the concept that indirect annuloplasty is not only safe but also durable and clinically effective in a broad heart failure population.

A Broader, Older, and More Diverse Population

The CINCH cohort differs significantly from prior TEER trials in both demographics and disease severity. The mean age was 78 years (75 in HFrEF, 80 in HFpEF), notably older than COAPT (71.7) and RESHAPE-HF2 (70). The sex distribution was more balanced (49% male overall; 62% in HFrEF vs. 35% in HFpEF), contrasting with the male predominance in earlier TEER populations (Figure 1). Symptomatically, patients were more advanced in heart failure yet presented with somewhat less severe MR. NYHA III–IV symptoms accounted for 82% of CINCH participants (83% in HFrEF, 77% in HFpEF) versus 57% in COAPT and 76% in RESHAPE-HF2. In contrast, grade 4+ MR was seen in only 27% of the CINCH cohort (26% in HFrEF, 23% in HFpEF), lower than the 51 and 44% reported in COAPT and RESHAPE-HF2, respectively. This indicates that CINCH explored a different physiological spectrum—older, more symptomatic, but with less extreme MR severity—potentially influencing both procedural outcomes and long-term event rates.

Efficacy, Safety, and Remaining Uncertainties

CINCH outcomes compared favorably—even impressively—with contemporary interventional data. The mortality rates were comparable or lower than those seen with TEER, though heart failure hospitalizations appeared slightly higher. Whether this difference stems from baseline comorbidity or represents a specific limitation of indirect annuloplasty remains uncertain.

The registry’s design inevitably introduces interpretative constraints. Inclusion criteria were institution-based and intentionally liberal, without a predefined MR-severity threshold, enhancing generalizability but limiting external comparability. Echocardiographic data on baseline and postprocedure annular or left ventricular dimensions were not systematically reported—important mechanistic endpoints for annuloplasty efficacy. Moreover, procedural variables such as anticoagulation strategy and management of coronary sinus–related complications were insufficiently detailed, leaving gaps in procedural standardization and reproducibility.

Positioning Indirect Annuloplasty in the Therapeutic Algorithm

Despite these limitations, CINCH represents a successful real-world validation of indirect annuloplasty as a low-risk intervention capable of producing sustained MR reduction and symptomatic benefit. The inclusion of HFpEF populations is particularly relevant, as this group often falls outside the anatomic window suitable for TEER or transcatheter mitral replacement.

Another unanswered question is patient overlap with other interventions. It remains unclear whether any patients had prior failed TEER or surgical repair. Understanding how indirect annuloplasty performs as a bridge, complementary, or salvage therapy could reshape procedural sequencing. Conceptually, indirect annuloplasty may also serve as a foundation for staged therapy, restoring annular geometry and facilitating subsequent leaflet repair. The possibility of hybrid or combined approaches—annuloplasty followed by TEER in selected cases—warrants dedicated investigation to identify which phenotypes derive maximal synergistic benefit.
