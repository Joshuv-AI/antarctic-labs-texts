Efficacy and Safety of Acupuncture-Related Therapies for Central Post-Stroke Pain: An Umbrella Review
Authors: Linjia Wang, Jiangyi Dou, Yue Yang, Di Qin, Benhua Yu, Xiaoqing Li, Jian Ying, Lili Zhu, Nan Jiang, Yuan Zhou, Ling Zhao, Xi Zhou, Chunyan Gou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCentral post-stroke pain (CPSP) severely impairs survivors’ quality of life, and pharmacological therapies are often limited by resistance and safety. Acupuncture-related therapies (AT) shows potential, but evidence is fragmented. This umbrella review synthesizes existing systematic reviews/meta-analyses (SRs/MAs) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of AT for CPSP.Patients and MethodsThis umbrella review included SRs/MAs of randomized controlled trials comparing AT with pharmacotherapy, placebo, or conventional therapy for CPSP. AT methods included traditional acupuncture methods such as manual acupuncture (MA) and electroacupuncture, as well as non‑pharmacological physical interventions such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS). The outcomes included pain, limb function, psychological status, quality of life, and adverse events (AEs). Methodological quality, reporting quality, risk of bias, and evidence certainty of the SRs/MAs were assessed using the AMSTAR 2, PRISMA-A, ROBIS, and GRADE tools.ResultsA total of 9 SRs/MAs were included in this review. The reporting quality was relatively complete overall (median PRISMA-A score: 23.5/27). The methodological quality and certainty of evidence were predominantly low or critically low, with publication bias and risk of bias as the major downgrading factors. In terms of efficacy, AT significantly reduced pain scores versus control therapies (e.g. Visual analogue scale/ Numeric rating scale, mean difference [MD]=−1.11, 95% confidence intervals[CI] [−1.41,-0.81], p<0.00001), improved limb function (e.g. Modified Rankin Scale, MD=−0.62, 95% CI [−0.92,-0.32], p<0.00001), and enhanced quality of life (e.g. MOS 36-Item short-form health survey score, MD=6.77, 95% CI [2.50,11.04], p=0.002). Psychological outcomes did not show consistent improvement (e.g. Hamilton anxiety scale, MD=−0.96, 95% CI [−2.94,1.02], p=0.34). AEs were mostly mild and transient, although rTMS was associated with the highest incidence (61.08%).ConclusionAT can alleviate pain and improve limb function and quality of life in CPSP, though its effect on psychological symptoms remains unclear. The treatment is generally safe; however, rTMS carries a high risk of AEs. These findings require confirmation through rigorous, high-quality studies.Keywords: central pain, stroke complications, complementary and alternative medicine, overview

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death from non-communicable diseases worldwide, claiming approximately 7 million lives annually, and the management of its long-term sequelae continues to pose a major clinical challenge.1 Post-stroke pain is a common and severe sequela that includes peripheral post-stroke pain (eg., hemiplegic shoulder pain and spasticity-related pain) and central post-stroke pain (CPSP),2 with the latter being more prevalent.3 Epidemiological studies have reported that CPSP affects approximately 11% of all survivors of stroke, with a prevalence exceeding 50% in subgroups, such as those with thalamic or medullary strokes.4 Clinically, CPSP is primarily characterized by constant or intermittent neuropathic pain, frequently accompanied by dysesthesia such as impaired temperature and/or pressure sensation.5 These symptoms not only hinder rehabilitation progress but also substantially impair activities of daily living, sleep quality, and social participation. Furthermore, the chronicity and complexity of CPSP frequently contribute to psychological comorbidities, including depression and anxiety, thereby compounding the overall burden on survivors of stroke.6,7

Currently, CPSP management relies primarily on pharmacological and surgical interventions. Pharmacological options typically include antidepressants, antiepileptic drugs, and opioids, whereas surgical management primarily involves invasive motor cortex stimulation and deep brain stimulation.8 Nevertheless, due to the frequent resistance of CPSP to available pharmacological therapies and the high occurrence of adverse events (AEs), no standardised pharmacological treatment algorithm has been established.5 Furthermore, the surgical approaches mentioned carry risks of complications such as infections, depression, and memory impairment.9,10 This underscores the urgent need for safer and more effective treatment alternatives.

Acupuncture-related therapies (AT) is a complementary and alternative medicine that has been successfully applied to various neuropathic and chronic pain conditions, demonstrating not only analgesic benefits, but also positive effects on mood and functional recovery.11–13 Currently, AT has garnered increasing attention as a potential therapeutic strategy for CPSP, representing an important approach for managing post-stroke sequelae and complications.14,15 Modern neurobiological research suggests that its analgesic effects may be mediated through modulation of central structures such as the thalamus,16 as well as via endogenous opioids17 and other neurotransmitters.18 The thalamus serves as a critical relay station in the spinal-thalamo-cortical pathway, and dysfunction within this pathway is recognised as a central mechanism underlying CPSP pathophysiology.19 Given that the thalamus is a key target for AT modulation, this provides a strong mechanistic rationale for exploring AT as a potential treatment for CPSP. Further, several clinical studies have specifically explored AT for CPSP, reporting superior pain relief compared to pharmacotherapy and a favorable safety profile.20,21

Nevertheless, existing evidence from individual trials remains fragmented and limited by small sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity. At present, there are several systematic reviews that provide evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of AT for CPSP. However, the reporting quality, methodological rigor, and sufficiency of evidence provided by these reviews remain unclear. Consequently, there is a clear need for an up-to-date, methodologically rigorous umbrella review that synthesizes and evaluates current evidence on the efficacy and safety of acupuncture for CPSP. Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically assess and synthesise existing systematic reviews and/or meta-analyses (SRs/MAs) on AT for CPSP, thereby providing a higher-level evidence synthesis to inform clinical practice and guide future research in this challenging field.

Material and Methods

Protocol and Registration

The study protocol was developed following a framework established in a previous methodological study22 and registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251153198). This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement, eTable 1 (Supplementary File).

Data Sources and Search Strategy

The SRs/MAs included in this study were identified through comprehensive searches of the following eight databases: China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP Database for Chinese Technical Periodicals (VIP), Wanfang Database (WF), Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed), PubMed, Excerpta Medica Database (Embase), Web of Science (WOS), and Cochrane Library. In addition, gray literature was searched using Google Scholar, and citation tracking was performed.

The search period spanned from the inception of each database to December 29, 2025, across all databases and registries. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a statistician specializing in literature retrieval. The search terms combined MeSH items and free words and generally encompassed four key categories: (1) AT methods, including acupuncture, electrical stimulation, magnetic stimulation, and so on; (2) stroke, cerebrovascular accident, CVA, brain vascular accident, and so on; (3) central pain conditions, such as central pain, thalamic pain, and Dejerine Roussy; and (4) systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in eTable 2 (Supplementary File).

Criteria for Study Inclusion or Exclusion

Study Design

The studies included in this review consisted of published SRs/MAs of randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Clinical trials, overview of reviews, network meta-analyses, protocols, and animal studies were excluded. Additionally, we excluded duplicate publications, commentaries, conference abstracts, newspaper articles, and books. This review only included studies published in Chinese and English.

Participants

Eligible participants comprised patients with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke diagnosed according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) or other established criteria, who presented with central pain. Central pain was primarily defined as persistent, diffuse pain or hypersensitivity in the contralateral limb of the lesion. No restrictions were placed on the patient’s age, sex, nationality, race, or disease duration.

Intervention and Control

The intervention group used AT methods as the main treatment or main adjuvant treatment, including manual acupuncture (MA), moxibustion, acupressure, electroacupuncture (EA), warm acupuncture (WA), auricular needle, fire acupuncture, wrist-ankle needle. In addition, other common non-pharmacological physical interventions for CPSP, such as electrical stimulation or magnetic stimulation, were also included. The control group received pharmacotherapy (PT), placebo, or conventional treatment (CT).

Outcome Measure

This study focused on the post-treatment pain score, measured using the visual analog scale (VAS) or Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), as the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes included additional pain-related measures, limb function measures, psychological measures, quality of life measures, and AEs.

Study Screening Process

All reviewers in this study received professional training in advance. First, the search results were imported into the NoteExpress database (V3.8.0) for management, and duplicate papers were screened. Next, two researchers (J.D. and Y.Y.) identified the possible studies by reading the titles and abstracts back-to-back to ensure reliability. Studies that met the preliminary criteria were retrieved for full-text assessment. Studies that satisfied the eligibility criteria following a full-text review were included in the review, while excluded studies were documented with reasons for exclusion. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by adjudication from a third researcher (X.Z).

Data Extraction Process

Data entry was performed by two researchers (J.D. and Y.Y)., and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consultation or the third researcher’s (X.Z.) judgment. Data were obtained from the full texts and supplementary materials of the meta-analyses using a predesigned Excel form. If any required data were unavailable, the corresponding authors of the meta-analyses were contacted or extracted from the original RCT studies. In cases where multiple meta-analyses evaluated the same intervention and outcome, data were extracted from the largest available meta-analyses. The information collected included basic characteristics of the meta-analyses (first author, publication year, country, and funding sources), methodological details (number of included RCTs and participants, stroke type, intervention and control methods, and quality assessment tools), and results (types of outcomes, quantitative data, and main conclusions).

Quality Assessment

Assessment of Report Quality

Two independent reviewers (L.W. and B.Y.) assessed each included study using the 27-item PRISMA for Acupuncture (PRISMA-A) checklist, a PRISMA extension developed specifically for acupuncture SRs/MAs.23 Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (X.Z). Each item was scored as 1 (complete reporting), 0.5 (partial reporting), or 0 (no reporting). Total scores (maximum 27) were categorized as: 21.5–27 (relatively complete), 15.5–21 (certain defects), or 0–15 (serious defect).24

Assessment of Methodological Quality

Two reviewers (Y.Y. and X.L.) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using the A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR 2),25 with any discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (X.Z). The tool comprises seven critical items (Items 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15) and nine non-critical items, each rated as “yes,” “partial yes,” or “no” based on the degree of compliance. Based on these ratings, the overall methodological quality of each systematic review was classified as high (no or one non-critical shortcoming), moderate (more than one non-critical shortcoming), low (one critical flaw), or critically low (more than one critical flaw).

Assessment of Risk of Bias

Two reviewers (L.W. and B.Y.) independently assessed the risk of bias in the included SRs/MAs using the Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews (ROBIS) tool, with any discrepancies resolved through adjudication by a third reviewer (X.Z).26 The ROBIS tool examines four key domains: (1) study eligibility criteria; (2) identification and selection of studies; (3) data collection and study appraisal; and (4) synthesis and findings. Each domain is judged as “low,” “high,” or “unclear” risk of bias, ultimately leading to an overall risk of bias judgement for the SRs/MAs.

Evaluation of Quality of Evidence

The quality of evidence from the included studies was independently evaluated by two reviewers (Y.Y. and X.L.) using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system.27 Disagreements were resolved through adjudication by a third reviewer (X.Z). The GRADE approach classifies the quality of evidence into four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low, based on an evaluation across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other considerations.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analyses

Data for synthesis and analyses were obtained from eligible SRs/MAs supplemented by original RCTs where necessary. The findings of the meta-analyses were synthesized extensively. Where the SRs/MAs did not distinguish between AT methods, we reanalyzed the outcome data from the original RCTs using RevMan 5.4. For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) were used for analyses, whereas for continuous outcomes, the mean difference (MD) was preferred, with standardized mean difference (SMD) employed when appropriate. All effect estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was evaluated with I2 and Chi-square tests, with I2 < 50% and p > 0.10 indicating low heterogeneity, and I2 ≥ 50% or p ≤ 0.10 indicating high heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was applied for low heterogeneity; otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Consistency between the two reviewers was assessed using SPSS 25.0, with the Chi-square test, and was measured using the Kappa statistic. Kappa values range from −1 to +1, where values > 0 indicate statistical significance. The ranges of Kappa values are defined as follows: Kappa ≥ 0.80 for extremely high consistency, 0.60 < Kappa ≤ 0.80 for high consistency, 0.40 < Kappa ≤ 0.60 for moderate consistency, and 0 < Kappa ≤ 0.40 for low consistency.28 Finally, the sensitivity analyses was conducted on the primary outcome that exhibit heterogeneity, and the funnel plot was used to assess publication bias in meta-analyses involving more than 10 RCTs by RevMan 5.4.

Patient and Public Involvement

Neither the patients nor the public were involved in the design or conduct of this study, nor were they involved in reporting or dissemination.

Results

Searching Result

There were 845 records in the initial literature search. In total, 549 records were obtained after eliminating duplicates. By reviewing the titles and abstracts, we obtained 24 records that initially met the requirements. We then downloaded the corresponding articles from the 24 records for full reading and excluded 15 unqualified articles. Finally, 9 SRs/MAs met our study criteria.29–37 The screening process is illustrated in Figure 1. The detailed reasons for the exclusion of 15 studies can be found in eTable 3 (Supplementary File).

Studies Description

Nine eligible SRs/MAs published between 2023 and 2025 were included in this study.29–37 Of these, two were published in Chinese29,37 and seven in English.30–36 Except for one study conducted in Portugal,35 all other SRs/MAs were performed in China.29–34,36,37 All trials included in the nine SRs/MAs were RCTs, with a median of 11 trials (range: 5–16). The median number of patients included SRs/MAs was 781 (range: 119–1076). Regarding stroke type, only two SRs/Mas31,34 specified the inclusion of patients with both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. Interventions involved acupuncture and acupuncture-related techniques such as MA, EA, WA, repeated transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) — either alone or in combination with PT or CT. The control group received PT, CT, or a placebo. All SRs/MAs assessed the risk of bias of the included RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, three applied the GRADE system to evaluate the quality of evidence,30,31,35 and only one used the Pedro scale to appraise methodological quality.34 Two SRs/MAs did not report funding sources,35,37 while the remaining seven provided funding declarations: six received government support,29–34 and one reported no funding.36 Detailed characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Reporting Quality

The PRISMA-A assessment revealed median scores of 23.5 (range: 17.5–26.5) across the nine SRs/MAs (Figure 2 and eTable 4 in Supplementary File). Three SRs/MAs29,35,37 were rated as having defects in reporting quality, whereas the remaining six were considered relatively complete. The most frequently omitted items included: study characteristics (88.89%), summary of evidence (66.67%), protocol and registration (44.44%), search (33.33%), and risk of bias across studies (33.33%). The consistency test for reporting quality assessment showed extremely high consistency (kappa = 0.879, p < 0.001) (eTable 5 in the Supplementary File).

Abbreviation: PRISMA-A, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Acupuncture.

Methodological Quality

Based on the AMSTAR 2 assessments (Figure 3 and eTable 6 in the Supplementary File), only one SR/MA was rated as having moderate methodological quality.34 Among the remaining eight reviews, four were classified as low quality31,33,35,36 and four as critically low quality.29,30,32,37 Regarding critical items, four SRs/MAs29,30,32,37 did not provide a pre-registered protocol or trial registration (Item 2). None of the SRs/MAs conducted a comprehensive literature search (Item 4). Only one SR/MA34 adequately justified the exclusion of studies (Item 7). In Items 11 and 13, one SR/MA29 failed to account for heterogeneity in the results and did not discuss the potential impact of including RCTs with a high risk of bias on the findings. Regarding non-critical items, notable limitations were identified in items 3, 10, and 12. Seven SRs/MAs29–32,34,35,37 did not explain the rationale for restricting inclusion in RCTs (Item 3). None of the SRs/MAs reported funding sources for the included RCTs (Item 10). Furthermore, all but one SR/MA35 performed a meta-analyses; however, none assessed how data from RCTs with a moderate or high risk of bias might affect the overall effect estimates (Item 12). The consistency test for methodological quality assessment showed extremely high consistency (kappa = 0.882, p < 0.001) (eTable 5 in the Supplementary File).

Risk of Bias

Figure 4 shows the results of the risk of bias assessment. In the domain of study eligibility criteria, one SR/MA32 (11.11%) was rated as high risk due to the overly broad inclusion criteria for disease conditions. Under the identification and selection of studies, three SRs/MAs29,32,37 (33.33%) were judged to be at high risk owing to incomplete supplementary search methods, and one35 (11.11%) due to language restrictions. Regarding synthesis and findings, one SR/MA29 (11.11%) was deemed to be at high risk because of the absence of a pre-registered protocol and failure to adequately explore the substantial heterogeneity in the results. For the risk of bias in the review, three SRs/MAs29,31,37 were deemed high-risk owing to inadequate justification or discussion of the aforementioned high-risk concerns. Further details are provided in eTable 7 (Supplementary File). The consistency test for the risk of bias assessment showed extremely high consistency (kappa = 0.904, p < 0.001) (eTable 5 in the Supplementary File).

Confidence in Study Outcomes

One study35 did not conduct data analyses because of a lack of homogeneity in the data. Therefore, we summarized the confidence in the findings of another eight studies in eTable 8 (Supplementary File). These eight SRs/MAs collectively yielded 32 pieces of evidence for AT in CPSP. Among these, four were graded as moderate (12.50%), ten as low (31.25%), and 18 as very low (56.25%), with no evidence rated as high. Across all domains, publication bias (75.00%) and risk of bias (68.75%) were the most prevalent reasons for downgrading the evidence, followed by inconsistency (62.50%), imprecision (46.88%), and indirectness (0%).

Pain-Related Outcomes

As shown in Table 2, all SRs/MAs used the VAS or NRS as primary outcomes to evaluate the effect of AT methods on CPSP. Compared with PT, MA (fifteen RCTs, MD=−1.11, 95% CI [−1.41,-0.81], p<0.00001), MA + PT (five RCTs, MD=−0.97, 95% CI [−1.13,-0.82], p<0.00001), and rTMS + PT (four RCTs, MD=−1.51, 95% CI [−1.83,-1.20], p<0.00001) all resulted in significant improvements in VAS/NRS scores. But, WA did not show a statistically significant benefit (one RCT, MD=−1.07, 95% CI [−2.60,0.46], p=0.17). Compared with placebo or placebo + PT, significant improvements were observed for rTMS (six RCTs, MD=−0.58, 95% CI [−0.79,-0.36], p<0.00001), rTMS + PT (two RCTs, MD=−1.85, 95% CI [−2.27,-1.43], p<0.00001), and tDCS (two RCTs, MD=−1.60, 95% CI [−2.51,-0.69], p=0.0006).

Safety-Related Outcomes

Seven SRs/MAs30–33,35–37 summarized the AEs associated with the six AT methods for CPSP (Figure 5). However, the definitions, collection methods, and reporting formats of AEs varied considerably across studies, which precluded a quantitative meta-analysis. Therefore, we present a descriptive summary of AEs. Further review of the seven SRs/MAs and their included RCTs revealed that rTMS was associated with the highest incidence of AEs (61.08%), followed by tDCS (36.36%), and MA+PT (15.22%). The most frequently reported AEs were headache, pain at the stimulation site, and fatigue, all of which are usually caused by rTMS. It is noteworthy that two serious AEs (infection necessitating hospitalisation and ischaemic stroke) were reported after rTMS treatment, both of which were considered unrelated to the intervention according to the original RCT. All the other reported AEs were transient and mild. Further details regarding the AEs are provided in eTable 9 (Supplementary File).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses of primary outcome were conducted. After excluding three large-sample RCTs (n > 80) and two RCTs with unclear random sequence generation (eTable 10 in the Supplementary File), the I2 value decreased to 25%. Furthermore, compared with PT, MA significantly improved the VAS or NRS scores (ten RCTs, MD=−0.78, 95% CI [−1.00,-0.56], p<0.00001) (eFigure 1 in Supplementary File).

Publication Bias

Only one meta-analyses met the criteria for assessing publication bias, involving the comparison of MA and PT in terms of the primary outcome. The funnel plot indicates that most points were evenly distributed at the top of the plot, but there are slightly more points on the left side than on the right side. Although this asymmetry may suggest a minor publication bias, it could also be influenced by the generally small sample sizes. In addition, a few points fall outside the boundaries of the two dashed lines, suggesting potential heterogeneity between these studies (eFigure 2 in Supplementary File).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first umbrella review to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of AT for CPSP. We synthesized the existing results of SRs/MAs, critically evaluated their reporting and methodological quality, and performed supplementary quantitative analyses using data from the original RCTs to provide a comprehensive review.

Main Findings

The principal findings of this umbrella review indicate that AT, particularly MA and EA, whether used alone or adjunctively with PT, demonstrate statistically significant benefits in reducing CPSP-related pain, as measured by the VAS or NRS, when compared with PT or placebo. However, the primary outcome of the comparison between MA and PT may be affected by a minor publication bias. This may be attributable to a small-study effect, potentially leading to an overestimation of the therapeutic effect of MA. Therefore, more high-quality and large-scale clinical trials are needed to provide proof. Furthermore, rTMS showed substantial analgesic effects in both the PT and placebo controls. The benefits extended beyond primary pain relief to improvements in certain secondary pain outcomes (PRI, PPI, MPQ and β-EP), limb function (MRS and FMA), and quality of life (SF-36 and PSQI). However, evidence for the efficacy of AT on psychological outcomes (HAMA, HAMD, HADS-A, HADS-D and BDI) remains inconclusive, largely due to the limited number and small sample sizes of available studies. The safety profile of AT appears to be generally acceptable, with all AEs being transient and mild; however, rTMS was associated with the highest incidence of AEs, mainly manifested as headache.

Concurrently, this review highlights significant concerns regarding the robustness of the current evidence-based research. The methodological quality of the included SRs/MAs was predominantly low or critically low according to AMSTAR 2, primarily due to the absence of pre-registered protocols, non-comprehensive literature searches, and inadequate explanations for study exclusion. Correspondingly, confidence in the outcomes, as graded by the GRADE system, was predominantly low or very low for all synthesized evidence, with the risk of bias and publication bias being the most frequent reasons for downgrading. Finally, although the reporting quality of most SRs/MAs was relatively satisfactory, further improvements are warranted. The above results suggest that the therapeutic effect of AT may have been overestimated. Nevertheless, the direction of the effect is consistent across multiple comparisons and outcome domains, supporting the potential clinical utility of AT for pain relief, functional improvement, and quality of life in CPSP. However, given the current evidence limitations, definitive quantitative conclusions should be drawn with caution.

Potential Mechanism

The pathogenesis of CPSP involves dysfunction of the entire spinal-thalamo-cortical pathway.19 Key mechanisms include the dysregulation of specific neural pathways, such as impaired thalamic reticular nucleus-ventrobasal thalamus inhibitory projections,38 disruption of the mediator complex subunit 1/ brain-derived neurotrophic factor/ tropomyosin receptor kinase B pathway.39 At the molecular level, a range of signals and receptors act through neuroinflammatory cascades, glial activation, and disrupted inhibitory transmission to collectively drive central sensitization and maintain pain hypersensitivity in CPSP.40–42 Additionally, cellular contributors, including activated glial cells and hypersensitive sensory fibers, drive nociceptive abnormalities.39,43,44 Ultimately, these combined factors led to CPSP.

Direct evidence from CPSP animal models supports that AT exerts its analgesic effects through multiple pathways. Experimental research has indicated that it reduces neuronal apoptosis and suppresses abnormal astrocyte activation to alleviate CPSP.45 Acupuncture also appears to attenuate autophagy associated with CPSP in the hippocampus by downregulating key proteins within the β-catenin/COX-2 signalling axis.46 Furthermore, other studies have shown that acupuncture alleviates CPSP through the upregulation of silent information regulator 1, inhibition of inflammasome activation, downregulation of interleukin-18 expression, and suppression of adenylate cyclase 1 protein upregulation.47,48 Together, these findings suggest that acupuncture is a promising multitarget therapy that warrants further investigation of its underlying mechanisms.

Comparison with Previous Studies

Our findings corroborate and extend the conclusions of previous individual SRs/MAs reporting positive effects of AT on CPSP.29–37 Notably, a recent network meta-analysis49 that evaluated multiple noninvasive therapies for CPSP also supported the potential role of rTMS and tDCS in pain relief, while highlighting the safety concerns associated with rTMS, a finding consistent with our safety results. More recently, another network meta‑analysis50 specifically compared acupuncture with drug therapy for CPSP, identifying Xingnao Kaiqiao acupuncture combined with pregabalin as the most effective intervention. While these studies provide valuable insights into the comparative efficacy of AT, our umbrella review differs in several key aspects. First, we synthesised existing SRs/MAs, providing a higher-level appraisal of the evidence base. Second, we assessed not only efficacy but also methodological quality, reporting quality, and safety profiles across a broader range of ATs, including traditional acupuncture methods and non‑pharmacological physical interventions. Third, our rigorous quality assessment revealed that the underlying evidence remains of low or very low certainty, which contextualises the findings of previous network meta‑analyses and highlights the need for more robust original studies. Taken together, our study offers a crucial higher-order appraisal that was lacking in earlier syntheses. While previous reviews have suggested efficacy, our rigorous assessment revealed that their conclusions were based on methodologically weak evidence. The near-universal failure to conduct comprehensive searches (including gray literature and trial registries) and adequately justify the exclusion of studies concerns the potential for reporting bias and the reliability of the pooled estimates. Furthermore, no prior synthesis has systematically observed the efficacy of different AT methods (eg., MA vs. EA vs. rTMS) or comparatively evaluated their safety profiles, as presented here. Our finding that rTMS had the highest incidence of AEs adds a critical safety dimension to the existing literature, which has often underreported or insufficiently analysed harms.

Outlook and Recommendations

Future research should address the critical methodological flaws identified in this umbrella review. We recommend that future RCTs and subsequent SRs/MAs consider the following practices: First, prospective registration for all research is advisable to ensure transparency and mitigate selective outcome reporting; as such, registration or prior publication of protocols can reduce research waste, clarify predefined research plans and registered questions, and further boost the quality of subsequent implementation and reporting.51 Second, for SRs/MAs, it would be prudent to adopt exhaustive search strategies, rigorously justify study selection, apply appropriate tools (eg., GRADE) to assess evidence certainty, and conduct predefined sensitivity and subgroup analyses to explore heterogeneity. Third, adherence to reporting guidelines such as PRISMA is highly recommended for more standardized outputs.52 Fourth, it would be valuable to conduct more high-quality RCTs to evaluate the long-term efficacy of AT, its impact on psychological comorbidities, and the comparative efficacies of different AT methods.

In clinical practice, while AT shows promise as a relatively safe option for CPSP, clinicians may wish to interpret the current positive efficacy data with caution, given the low certainty of the existing evidence. MA and EA may be considered part of a multimodal treatment approach for CPSP, whereas the use of rTMS requires careful risk-benefit consideration because of its higher rate of associated AEs.

Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of our review lies in its comprehensive and systematic approach. It is the first to apply rigorous methodological and reporting quality assessments (AMSTAR 2, PRISMA-A, and ROBIS) and evidence certainty grading (GRADE) to the entire corpus of SRs/MAs in AT for CPSP. Supplementary reanalyses of the RCTs data provides valuable comparative insights. However, a few limitations must be acknowledged. First, the findings were constrained by the inherent limitations of the primary studies that included SRs/MAs, such as small sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity. Second, although we performed extensive searches, some unpublished, non-English, or non-Chinese studies may have been excluded. Third, the quantitative syntheses were based on aggregated data from SRs/MAs and a limited number of original RCTs; therefore, they should be considered exploratory. Furthermore, heterogeneity in AEs definitions and reporting across studies limited the ability to perform meta-analysis of safety outcomes, which were therefore summarized descriptively. Finally, the generalizability of the findings may be limited as most of the included studies were conducted in China.

Conclusion

AT significantly alleviates pain and improves limb function and quality of life in patients with CPSP, although it shows no consistent evidence of benefit on the psychological status. In addition, mild and transient AEs have been observed, most of which are associated with rTMS, and warrant close attention from researchers and clinicians. These findings highlight AT as a potentially valuable non-pharmacological option for CPSP, while underscoring the urgent need for high-quality studies to confirm its efficacy, clarify its role in psychological outcomes, and better define the risk profile of rTMS.
