Use of electronic point-of-care resources by early-career general practitioners and associations with their use during consultations: A cross-sectional analysis of consultation data.
Authors: Ben Mitchell, Parker Magin, Mieke L van Driel, Amanda Tapley, Anna Ralston, Andrew Davey, Elizabeth Holliday, Jason Dizon, Dominica Moad, Alison Fielding, Katie Fisher, Lisa Clarke, Geoffrey Spurling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine the use, frequency and factors linked to the use of any electronic point of care resources (ePOC resources) used by early-career general practitioners (GPs in training, otherwise known as GP residents or registrars) during consultations; and the frequency, and factors linked to the use of evidence-based clinical summaries.DesignCross-sectional analysis of data collected as part of the Registrar Clinical Encounters in Training (ReCEnT) project from 2018 to 2022. Every 6 months, GP trainees record 60 consecutive consultations, including information about their use of resources.SettingAustralian training general practices.Participants3024 GP trainees in community-based vocational training.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the use of ePOC resources, and the secondary outcome was the use of evidence-based ePOC summaries.ResultsA total of 3024 GP trainees accessed electronic resources during patient encounters for 67 651/628 855 (10.8%) of diagnoses/problems. Use of ePOC resources increased 4% per year over the study period. Therapeutic Guidelines was accessed most often (27 435/79 536, 34.7% of all ePOC use) followed by Australian Medicines Handbook (7507, 9.4%) and HealthPathways (6965, 8.7%). Various factors were associated with increased use of ePOC resources, including increasing patient age, diagnosis/problem type, increasing years of experience prior to GP training and stage of training. GP trainees rarely accessed dedicated evidence-based clinical summaries.ConclusionsAustralian GP trainees use a range of resources to answer their clinical questions, mostly from Therapeutic Guidelines and prescribing compendiums, but also system specific resources that are free to access.

Introduction

Medical knowledge continues to expand at a rapid rate and remains impossible to keep up with.1 2 Information is updated exponentially, and 20% of guideline recommendations become out of date after 3 years.3 Knowledge can be acquired outside of the consultation room, and increasingly, will also be required during consultations.

While all doctors have information needs, early-career general practitioners (GPs), or GPs in specialist vocational training, have extra information needs given their career stage and the broad range of presentations they see.4 Furthermore, trainees are at a stage of their careers when practice patterns are being established, and there is evidence that practice patterns tend to be stable over time.57 Many services are now in place to provide information at the point of care (POC), including dedicated evidence-based syntheses.8 A systematic review of questions raised by clinicians in the context of patient care found the mean frequency of questions raised was 0.57 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.77) per patient, but just over half were pursued, and 78% of those were answered.9 However, there is little published literature regarding what electronic point of care (ePOC) resources doctors actually use, particularly as older studies will not reflect the current range of ePOC availability,10 and the qualitative methods of more recent work are not able to answer the questions of how often or where doctors search for information.1113 While we have previously (2010–2013) examined GP trainees’ in-consultation information seeking more generally,14 there is no literature regarding ePOC-specific information seeking for primary care doctors.

We sought to establish what type of ePOC resources are used by early-career GPs, their frequency of use, and determine factors associated with ePOC use.

Methods

Study design

We used a cross-sectional design nested within the Registrar Clinical Encounters in Training (ReCEnT) project.15

ReCEnT

ReCEnT is an ongoing, multisite inception cohort study conducted across several regional training areas in Australia, involving 44% of all Australian GP trainees during the period of this substudy.16 GP trainees undertake a minimum of three 6 month (full-time equivalent) training terms in community GP, under an apprenticeship-like model. Trainees see patients independently but are overseen by senior general practitioners who provide teaching and assistance (GP supervisor). GP trainees can seek help when needed, but practise with considerable autonomy, including prescribing and test ordering privileges identical to senior GPs. The full ReCEnT study protocol is available elsewhere.15

Briefly, ReCEnT-participating trainees complete a questionnaire immediately before they commence in-consultation data collection. This elicits demographic and other characteristics of the registrar and of their current practice. Immediately after questionnaire completion, they commence recording the details of 60 consecutive consultations (a ‘trainee-round’) on three occasions (once per mandatory 6 month training term). This is via individual consultation case report forms (CRFs). The CRFs elicit characteristics of the patient and details of the consultation content (including resources accessed). Trainees enter data at the time of consultation via a paper-based CRF (prior to 2020) or, from 2020, an online electronic CRF. Individual resource use is linked to the diagnosis/diagnoses that prompted it. Diagnoses/problems are coded according to the International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC)−2 PLUS. Noting that if registrars are not confident that they can make a specific diagnosis (eg, myocardial infarction), they are asked to record a problem formulation (ege, ‘chest pain’).

We analysed data from a 5 year period, 2018–2022, to capture data that best reflects current behaviour and currently available ePOC resources.

Outcome factors

The outcome factor in primary analyses was ePOC resources being used. The outcome factor in secondary analyses was dedicated evidence-based clinical summaries being used. Dedicated evidence-based clinical summaries were defined following the model proposed by Haynes’ evidence-based healthcare pyramid 5.017 and used by other authors,8 18 defined as: Point-of-care information summaries offer predigested syntheses of medical research intended to be used when the patient and physician interact (ie, point-of-care). In our analysis, we included BMJ Best Practice, DynaMed, Therapeutic Guidelines and UpToDate. While other resources are evidence-based, such as STI Guidelines Australia, these are not comprehensive resources that aim to cover a broad range of conditions and therefore did not meet our definition.

Independent variables

Included independent variables are described in online supplemental table 1. These are related to the trainee, patient, practice and consultation. Trainee factors included age, gender, training term, work sessions per week and place of basic medical qualification. Patient factors included age, gender and being new to the practice or to the trainee. Practice factors included rurality (using Modified Monash Model),19 the socioeconomic status of the practice location (using Socio-Economic Index for Area-Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (SEIFA-IRSD)),20 the size of the practice (full-time equivalents) and if the practice routinely bulk-billed (no out-of-pocket cost for a consultation). Consultation factors included duration of the consultation, the diagnosis/problem dealt with (by ICPC-2 PLUS chapter) and trainee help-seeking from supervisors, books, other human sources or electronic resources. ‘Consultation action’ factors included learning goals generated (self-identified specific learning need), medicines prescribed, and imaging and pathology ordered. For each information source (including ePOC), whether use was for diagnosis or management, or both, was recorded.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in this study.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of data from 2018 to 2022.

The analysis unit was diagnosis/problem.

Proportions of diagnoses/problems for which trainees accessed ePOC resources and accessed dedicated evidence-based clinical summary ePOC resources were calculated with 95% CIs.

Descriptive statistics included frequencies for categorical variables and mean with SD for continuous variables. The main analyses used univariable and multivariable logistic regression. Logistic regression was used within the generalised estimating equations (GEE) framework to account for repeated measures within trainees. An exchangeable working correlation structure was assumed. An augmented backwards selection process was followed to select explanatory variables for inclusion in the final multivariable model for each outcome. Variables in the model with p values >0.2 were tested for removal. A variable was removed if the resulting model did not have any substantively different (<10%) effect sizes than the previous model.

To examine our research questions, two models were built for each of our primary and our secondary outcomes. For our primary analysis, to examine the associations of an ePOC being used, patient-, practice-, trainee- and consultation-level independent variables were entered into an initial regression model. Then, to examine associations with subsequent trainee actions, the above independent variables were entered in a second regression model, along with consultation action-level independent variables. The rationale for building two models was that whether a trainee uses an ePOC is plausibly influenced by patient, practice, trainee and consultation variables, but evaluation of these influences may be compromised by inclusion in the multivariable model of factors operating once the ePOC is accessed.

The regressions modelled the log-odds that the relevant ePOC resource was used for a diagnosis/problem (any ePOC vs no ePOC for the primary analysis, and dedicated evidence-based clinical summary ePOC vs all other ePOC in the secondary analysis). Effects are expressed as ORs with 95% CI. ORs of independent variables in the regression models expressed the relative odds of ePOC use for the respective independent variable. Significance was declared at the conventional 0.05 level, with the magnitude and precision of effect estimates also used to interpret results.

We also calculated the proportion of total e-Resource use contributed by each of the most commonly used e-Resources; the ICPC-2 PLUS chapters of presenting diagnoses/problems that generated the most frequent use of electronic POC resources; and the ICPC-2 PLUS chapters with the highest ‘degree of difficulty’ (ie, those that generated the highest proportion of use of ePOC resources per diagnosis/problem).

Statistical analyses were programmed using STATA 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and SAS V9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). This study has ethics approval from the RACGP National Research and Evaluation Ethics (HREA ID-KM03269), and University of Newcastle HREC (H-2009–0323), ratified by The University of Queensland Ethics Committee (2023/HE000277).

Prevalence of information seeking

For 67 651 diagnoses/problems managed (10.8%), the trainee accessed an ePOC. For 55 513 (82.1%) diagnoses/problems encountered, the trainee accessed a single electronic resource, for 9390 (13.8%) of diagnoses/problems two resources were accessed, and three or more resources were accessed for 1376 (2.0%) of these diagnoses/problems. Trainees accessed ePOC resources for management-type clinical questions more often than diagnostic questions (86.5% and 32.9%, respectively). Twenty-seven per cent were both diagnostic and management questions. Trainees were less likely to access a supervisor for questions (5.3% of diagnoses/problems) compared with accessing an electronic resource (10.8%) but did use both in 1.4% of diagnoses/problems. Physical books were accessed rarely (0.31%) and using both books and ePOC rarer still (0.19%).

The most common ePOC resources used are presented in table 2. The most searched electronic resources were Therapeutic Guidelines (34.5% instances of all ePOC use), Australian Medicines Handbook (9.4%) and HealthPathways (local health services’ clinical support sites) at 8.7%. Problem or population-specific sources such as the Royal Children’s Hospital (4.6%) and RACGP (3.6%) websites were next most common. PubMed/literature searching was performed a total of 21 times (0.02%) and Cochrane was searched a total of 4 times (0.005%).

We found that the evidence-based clinical summaries represented a total of 38.4% of ePOC use. However, when excluding Therapeutic Guidelines, this figure reduced to 3.8% for the combined group of DynaMed, BMJ Best Practice and UpToDate ePOC resources.

The ICPC-2 PLUS chapters of presenting diagnoses/problems that generated the most frequent use of electronic POC resources were respiratory (13.6%), skin (13.4%) and general and unspecified (12.9%). The next most common were musculoskeletal (8.4%), psychological (8.1%) and endocrine, metabolic and nutritional (7.6%). The chapters with the highest ‘degree of difficulty’, that is, those that generated the highest proportion of use of resources per diagnosis/problem, were male genital (19.1%), ear (15.6%) and female genital (15.0%). Primary analysis: associations with ePOC use

The logistic regression model results with the outcome factor of ‘ePOC use’ are presented in table 3. Goodness of fit, linearity and C-statistic were satisfactory (see Supplementary information—‘Regression analysis’). For each year in this study, the odds of ePOC use increased annually by an estimated 4% (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.07). Among patient-related factors, trainee use of ePOC resources decreased with increasing patient age and was lowest in the higher age group (75+, aOR 0.44, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.47) compared with the referent youngest age group (0–14 years). For ‘practice’-related factors, significant associations were found for different training regions; however, there were no significant associations of rurality, socioeconomic status of the practice location or practice size. Associations of several trainee-level factors were statistically significant: the number of years working clinically before GP training (aOR 0.97 per additional year, 95% CI 0.95 to 0.99), and female gender (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.24). GP trainees who obtained their medical qualification in Australia were less likely than those who qualified overseas to use ePOC resources (aOR 0.76, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.87). Later training terms were associated with less ePOC use (term 2 aOR 0.84, 95% CI 0.80 to 0.88; term 3 aOR 0.64, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.69) compared with term 1.

Secondary analysis: associations with dedicated evidence-base clinical summary ePOC use

Evidence-based ePOC resources (compared with other ePOC resources) had no annual change in use over time (OR 1.0, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.03). ICPC-2 PLUS chapters were all statistically significantly associated with evidence-based ePOC use compared with the referent ‘general and unspecified’ group (see online supplemental table 3). Nearly all chapters had stronger associations when examining evidence-based ePOC resources compared with the primary outcome (figure 1), such as ear (aOR 6.76), urological (aOR 4.88), eye (aOR 4.07) and skin (aOR 4.39) with the strongest association. The exceptions were pregnancy/childbearing/family planning (aOR 0.78), male genital (aOR 2.40) and social problems (aOR 0.47).

Discussion

Main findings

We found that in 10.8% of diagnoses/problems, trainees sought information in an electronic resource, more often than seeking help from their supervisors (5.3%). Eighty per cent of ePOC use entailed 14 electronic resources, with over one-third of access dedicated to Therapeutic Guidelines, an online, Australian independent not-for-profit organisation that publishes clinical guidance aimed at GPs and hospital doctors. Use of ePOC resources increased 4% year on year over the 5 year study period; however, there was no increase in the proportion of evidence-based ePOC syntheses used. This may reflect a general trend towards online versions of traditional printed guidelines, or the greater availability and accessibility of electronic websites. Further research is required to answer this question.

Use of ePOC resources declined with training time, years of previous experience and increasing patient age. Type of diagnosis/problem (ICPC-2 PLUS disease chapter), seeking help from supervisors, or trainees developing a learning goal were also strongly associated with use of ePOC resources. We do not know from this data how much of this ePOC use represents ‘checking’ behaviours (such as drug dosages), desire to reassure clinical decisions, or to learn information on the run (‘just in time learning’).21 Increased use of ePOC by doctors trained outside Australia may represent a desire to reflect local practices rather than a need to fill a clinical knowledge gap. Similarly, female gender being associated with higher ePOC use may represent a relative over-confidence from male GP trainees, rather than subjective clinical doubt in female trainees. The ICPC-2 PLUS disease chapter associated with higher rates of ePOC use may reflect lack of familiarity with the subject, perhaps as these are uncommon pre-GP training terms in Australian hospitals, or exposure to diagnoses/problems that present primarily in primary care, rather than in secondary or tertiary care. A decrease in ePOC use with increasing training term is expected as trainees progress in their training and reflects known patterns of uncertainty in GP trainees and help seeking.22 23

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to quantitatively detail the ePOC use of any type of doctor anywhere, not just GP trainees in Australia. Second, by including ICPC-2 chapters, we are the first to account for case-mix in the multivariable analysis for trainee clinical activities. Thirdly, this study uses a dataset that is both recent and relevant to currently available ePOC resources. The response rate in this study was high (95.6%) and includes over 40% of all GP trainees currently in training in Australia, from a wide range of locations and at all stages of training. The findings are therefore likely to be generalisable to GP trainees across Australia and other countries with similar GP-gatekeeper health systems and apprenticeship-like training programmes. The dedicated data recording and tight linkage of data (including assistance/information-seeking) to specific diagnoses/problems are strengths (compared with use of routinely collected clinical electronic medical record data).

The study has some limitations. Association, but not causation, can be inferred from our cross-sectional analysis. The specific prompt of information seeking for each diagnosis/problem is not explicit in our data, though we record whether information-seeking is for diagnosis, management or both. Trainees entered their electronic resource with free text, and some mis-categorisation of ePOC types could have occurred. Measurement error is possible for our independent variables, but this is expected to be modest—registrars’ self-report of their personal and practice characteristics will be reliable, and the contemporaneous recording of in-consultation variables minimises recall bias, and reliability of these measures is anticipated to be good. As with any epidemiological analyses, unmeasured confounding is possible. A large number of potential confounders is measured in ReCEnT, guided by considerable clinical and educational expertise of the investigators. But some possibly relevant factors are not measurable. For example, practice culture and access to in-consultation supervisory support that may affect the relative recourse to in-consultation ePOC information.

Finally, this data provides information about the ePOC use of GP trainees for diagnoses/problems seen but is not able to answer how well these needs were met by the ePOC resource.

Comparison with other literature and implications for practice

Kortekaas,24 in 2015, attempted to answer how often and how GP trainees (n=82) in the Netherlands search for answers to clinical queries by asking third-year trainees to keep a log of consecutive patients for 8 days. The most common resources in this study were from human sources (colleagues or supervisors 28% of the time) and written resources, such as national GP guidelines (26%); however, it is not reported how often electronic resources were used. In contrast, our study found that seeking supervisor help was a relatively uncommon activity, and ePOC use (which included online guidelines) was relatively common.

Meaning of the study: possible mechanisms

We found that, similar to our previous study of trainees’ general in-consultation information seeking,14 in-consultation use of ePOC was strongly associated with subsequent generation of learning goals. This suggests that ePOC resources are not being used only as a pragmatic immediate response to a knowledge gap, but are also a substrate for longer-term learning.

In Australia, Therapeutic Guidelines is widely accessible during both medical school and hospital training, and so likely familiar to GP trainees. Funding for access is also provided by many GP training providers in Australia, and by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners for GPs in training. Free-to-access resources are a feature of the most accessed electronic POC resources, with nine of the top ten resources either free to use or provided by training providers to GP trainees.

Aside from Therapeutic Guidelines, evidence-based ePOC resources were used infrequently. UpToDate, DynaMed and BMJ Best Practice were used a total of 2982 times (3.75%). This is despite the finding from Viegas Dias18 in 2022, who found over 90% of clinical questions from primary care doctors could be answered by these three resources. This suggests that rather than content, barriers such as access (cost), usability, or even poor familiarity and awareness might be reasons why these resources are not used more often. Recent work suggests lack of awareness may be a barrier for adoption, at least for some ePOC resources.25 It may be that Therapeutic Guidelines is providing much of the evidence-based ePOC needs for our participants. Further research is required to answer this question.

Similarly, accessing primary literature is rarely practised during a consultation. In the aforementioned study of GP trainees in the Netherlands,24 PubMed accounted for 1% of all resources used (including human and other non-electronic resources), compared with 0.02% of electronic resources from our study. This result, and the even rarer use of the Cochrane Library, may reflect the strong preference for GP trainees to use pre-appraised evidence,26 their pragmatic approach, and how both time and belief in finding an answer are major determinants of POC resource use.27 This is in keeping with the results of Galbraith and colleagues, who described a ‘real world approach’ to evidence-based medicine in general practice,26 including the use of pre-appraised evidence-based resources, and therefore has implications for those providing evidence-based medicine training to GP trainees.

Australian GP trainees use ePOC resources frequently at the time of consultation to answer clinical questions. There is an increasing use of ePOC over time; however, use of dedicated evidence-based syntheses designed for clinical use is relatively uncommon, apart from Therapeutic Guidelines which may explain the low use of other resources from this category. However, trainees use many resources to meet their in-practice clinical needs.

Unanswered questions and future research

We suggest further research focus on determining the ePOC resource needs of doctors in training and how well current resources meet these needs. These results show a wide range of resources are accessed by registrars, suggesting that single evidence-based POC tools are insufficient. The interaction between trust, previous use of an ePOC tool and ability to meet clinical need may provide a deeper understanding of these results. Further exploration from factors associated with ePOC use such as country of training, male vs female gender and ICPC-2 PLUS category could be explored to understand the associations found in this study.

Implications

Training providers, medical educators and publishers of ePOC resources may consider these results carefully in making decisions on access to quality, evidence-based resources. Medical educators may revisit the evidence based-medicine (EBM) syllabus to include evidence at the POC, reflecting the frequency and behaviour that registrars answer their clinical questions. Training providers and practice owners may reflect on these data in choosing which of these tools to make available (and free to access), and incorporate into training to increase their adoption, augmenting the free resources that registrars already use. It is yet unknown how the current rise of AI-based tools aimed at point of care medical support would affect these results. Compilers and publishers of ePOC resources may consider how doctors use different tools for meeting different needs, and further research is needed to further detail how type of information, information presentation and user interface determine how well they meet doctors’ clinical needs.
