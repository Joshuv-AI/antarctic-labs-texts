Assessment of gait speed in older adults using a short–distance walking test with smartphone motion sensors and machine learning
Authors: Thiago André Carniel, Fátima Kremer Ferretti, Felipe Mega Masson, João Paulo Eckert, Clodoaldo Antônio De Sá
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Classical gait speed tests rely on the existence of a flat surface of 4 to 15 meters, distance that is not always available in clinical settings. To address this space constraint and to preserve the well–reported clinical outcomes associated with the gait speed test, the present work investigates the potential of a set of machine learning models to predict the gait speed in older adults, but by means of a short–distance test. To enhance screening, the present framework adopts a risk–stratification model, categorizing gait speed into four strata rather than treating it as a continuous variable. Motion data were obtained with a smartphone from over two hundred older adults. Feature extraction consisted of time– and frequency–domain quantities derived from the acceleration and angular velocity signals based on the discrete wavelet transform and power spectral density. Feature selection was investigated by the ReliefF algorithm. Among the several model setups evaluated, the k–Nearest Neighbors classifier is the one that provided the best predictions (median [95% CI] – accuracy: 0.969 [0.963–0.974]; F1-score: 0.971 [0.964–0.975]; AUC: 0.980 [0.975–0.984]). Based on these figures, the proposed strategy is a promising approach to provide clinicians with a reliable and straightforward gait speed stratification in cases where the classical test is not feasible due to space constraints.

Introduction

Considered by many to be a vital sign of mobility and functional capacity, gait speed has been widely reported over the past few decades as a measure associated with a broad range of health outcomes in older adults, such as sarcopenia, frailty, falls, cognitive decline, hospitalization, stroke, chronic obstructive lung disease, cardiovascular disease, mortality, among others1–13. The well–documented relations of the gait speed with major health–related outcomes make this measure an important and reliable indicator for geriatric assessment in clinical settings3. For example, the recent world guidelines for falls prevention and management for older adults strongly recommend (GRADE 1A) the gait speed test for predicting fall risk14.

The gait speed test is a simple protocol that consists of walking on a flat and rigid surface while taking a time interval within a predetermined distance. The average gait speed is calculated from the ratio between the walking distance and the time interval. In general, the protocols differ by the walking pace (usual or fast) and the predetermined distance, which usually ranges from 4 to 15 meters8. Among these test variations, walking at the usual pace through a 4–meter timed zone (with 1–2 meters acceleration and deceleration zones) is the most commonly used, being generally referred to as the 4–meter gait speed test (4MGS)1,5,8,10.

As previously mentioned, the first requirement to perform the gait speed test relies on the existence of a flat surface of 4 to 15 meters, such as a large room or corridor. Although it has been argued that the gait speed test is suitable for use in clinical settings, this does not always reflect reality, since the vast majority of clinicians work in rooms that do not meet the aforementioned space requirement, which also applies to assessments conducted at home. In such cases, the gait speed test cannot be performed, leaving this important data out of the patient’s record and related clinical evaluation (diagnosis and monitoring).

Motivated by these facts, the present work was driven by the following question: is it possible to predict the values of the 4MGS test from a considerably shorter walking test designed for use in small spaces, such as those commonly found in clinic rooms and homes? The existing literature in this field encompasses a substantial body of work employing sensor-based techniques that integrate machine learning (ML) for gait assessment in different contexts and health conditions15–20. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous work has reported a methodology to estimate 4MGS values from a short-distance walking test.

Based on this, the present work contributes to this field by presenting a ML modeling strategy to predict the gait speed retrieved from the 4MGS test, but using motion data recorded from a short–distance walking test as a predictor. This novel test, namely the 2–meter 3–turn test (2M3T), consists of 3 consecutive turns around 2 cones spaced 2 meters apart, with motion data recorded by attaching a smartphone to the ankle. Features on the time– and frequency–domain are extracted from the acceleration and angular velocity signals by means of multiresolution analysis based on the discrete wavelet transform (DWT). The DWT has been successfully applied in ML activity recognition frameworks based on motion signals21–25. In addition to the DWT, a set of frequency–domain features is also obtained from the power spectral density (PSD)26.

This study was guided by the a priori hypothesis that kinematic patterns captured by the accelerometer and gyroscope of a smartphone during a short–distance turning test could accurately predict the gait speed obtained in the 4MGS. The rationale for adopting the 2M3T stems from its feasibility in small clinical offices or home environments, where the standard 4-meter corridor is not available. By combining multiple turns and short-distance walking, the 2M3T allows for the capture of sufficient data while preserving the fundamental biomechanical characteristics of gait necessary for robust ML predictions.

In order to enhance screening, the present machine learning framework adopts a risk–stratification classification approach (i.e., a multiclass problem), modeling four gait–speed strata (low risk, moderate risk, at risk and high risk) rather than treating gait speed as a continuous outcome. Moreover, a dimensionality reduction (feature selection) approach is proposed based on the ReliefF algorithm27,28.

Several model setups were evaluated to find the best combination for the present problem: machine learning algorithms, wavelet types and number of features ranked by the ReliefF. Inferences about the performance and reliability of the models have been made in a statistical manner.

Gait speed tests

The well–reported 4–meter gait speed test (4MGS), with 8–meter long total distance, was the one employed in this study1,5,8,10. Briefly, the participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace over an 8–meter flat and rigid laboratory surface. The central 4–meter used for the measurements was delimited by 2 cones, being the first and last 2 meters the acceleration and deceleration zones, respectively (Fig. 1a). In order to mitigate measurement bias on the time interval by using a manual stopwatch, the walking of each participant was recorded in sagittal plane in order to assess the time instants when one foot passes in front of the cones that delimited the central 4–meters measuring zone. In this protocol, the average gait speed (in m/s) is computed by ratio between the 4 meters and the time interval in seconds. The test was repeated three times for each participant and the mean gait speed values were used as the target variables for the machine learning training.

The proposed short–distance gait test, namely 2–meter 3–turn test (2M3T), consists of 3 consecutive turns around 2 cones spaced 2 meters apart (Fig. 1b). The 3 turns are required not only to achieve a usual pace, but also to get a proper amount of data. In this test, motion data is recorded by attaching a smartphone to the right ankle, just above the lateral malleolus (Fig. 1b and d). As shown in Fig. 1d, the smartphone must be attached to the right ankle, ensuring its triaxial orientation aligns with the IMU sensors. For most smartphones, this means positioning the device upright with the screen facing outwards from the ankle.

Particularly, the data from the accelerometer and gyroscope sensors was used in this study, which was properly processed to further extract a set of features (input variables) for the machine learning predictions. Preprocessing

The raw acceleration and angular velocity components (X, Y, Z) were acquired at 100 Hz from the accelerometer and gyroscope sensors of a smartphone (Moto G8 Plus, Motorola). The triaxial orientation of the sensors in relation to the ankle is illustrated in Fig. 1d. From each 2M3T test, a 12 s time interval was extracted from the original signal, from the beginning of the walking period. This preprocessing step was carried out manually into the Matlab code. To mitigate potential missing data from the IMU and ensure a consistent sampling rate, the data were resampled at the recording rate (i.e., 100 Hz). A Butterworth filter (15 Hz cutoff) was applied to all signals in order to reduce high frequency noise; and another Butterworth filter (0.3 Hz cutoff) was employed only on the Y–acceleration to deal with gravitational effects29,30. Data augmentation and multiresolution analysis

In the present work, a data augmentation approach based on a sliding window technique was employed31. A 6 s window with 50% overlap was used to segment each one of the processed signals of 12 s (3 samples per signal), resulting in a set of 24 samples for each 2M3T test (for instance, see the X components plotted in Fig. 3a).

The gait speed is the target variable for the machine learning modeling, variable that may present important features embedded into the signals, not only in the time–domain, but also in the frequency–domain. Motivated by this assumption, it is proposed to extract features from both the time and frequency domains by means of multiresolution analysis based on the discrete wavelet transform (DWT). Briefly, the DWT performs filtering and down sampling operations (by factor of 2) using filter banks (high–pass and low–pass filters) to decompose the original signal into sub–bands with approximation and detail coefficients, where different wavelet types can be used in this regard. This allows a detailed resolution of the signals in both time and frequency domains at low and high frequencies. Further information about DWT can be found elsewhere21,25.

Accordingly, each of the 6 s samples obtained by the windowing was decomposed into 6 levels by the DWT, where the approximation and detail coefficients were obtained. Examples of the levels reconstructed from the DWT coefficients are shown in Fig. 3b for the Y and Z components of the acceleration and angular velocity, respectively. Furthermore, in order to extract frequency features from the sub–bands, the power spectral density (PSD) was estimated for each level by the Welch’s method26, where examples of the PSDs are shown in Fig. 3b for each DWT level reconstructed.

Feature extraction and selection

Time–domain features were extracted directly from each of the resulting levels obtained from the DWT, and for both the approximation and detail coefficients. Frequency–domain features, on the other hand, were extracted from the related PSDs computed from the previous mentioned DWT levels. It is well–established in the artificial intelligence (AI) field that a large number of features does not always leads to better predictions and/or model robustness, and the reasons for that are broad, e.g.: overfitting, redundant data, noise, among others. To deals with this dimensionality issue, several algorithms have been proposed with the aim to select (rank) the features that, in theory, may present a high predictive power without compromising the performance28,32.

Among these class of dimensionality reduction techniques, this work investigated the ability of the ReliefF, which is a robust algorithm capable of dealing with incomplete and noisy data, presenting low bias and may capture local dependencies27,28. Machine learning modeling

Modeling strategy

The main idea of the present work consists in predicting the gait speed retrieved from the 4MGS test, but using motion data recorded from a short–distance test (2M3T test) and a machine learning approach. Based on this, the first modeling choice naturally leads one to a regression problem, where the gait speed data obtained from the different tests can be compared pointwise. However, the machine learning modeling proposed herein was framed within a risk stratification approach, i.e., a classification problem, and the rationale behind this choice is twofold. First, a wide range of health outcomes related to gait speed in older adults are usually stratified based on cutoff points, which generally range from 0.6 to 1.0 m/s, being 0.8 m/s the most reported3,10,12–14. Second, a risk stratification approach provides clinicians with a straightforward and convenient assessment in clinical settings.

In order to achieve a further screening on the prediction, rather than a simple binary classification, it is proposed four strata for the gait speed (gs) based on the distribution of Fig. 1c. The three model setups that outperform the others in the train–validation process, based on the previously mentioned analyses, were the candidates for the best model. Therefore, in order to select the best one, the reliability of them was assessed in a statistical manner as follows. Each model was evaluated 50 times by randomly dividing the entirely dataset into 85% for train–validation and 15% for test. In this case, the performance metrics (accuracy, F1–score and AUC) was assessed only in the test sets (unseen data).

It is important to mention that all preprocessing and feature selection tasks were embedded within the cross–validation folds to prevent data leakage. ReliefF ranking and feature normalization were computed exclusively on the training subsets then applied to validation/test folds.

Statistical inferences on the models performance were accomplished based on the Kruskal–Wallis test. Moreover, confidence intervals were estimated by the bootstrap approach.

The modeling goal of this study is to select the best combination of ML algorithms, wavelet types and number of features ranked by the ReliefF. The first step in this selection concerns the latter, i.e., finding a proper number of features ranked by the ReliefF for the problem at hand. In this regard, the performance of the ML algorithms in the train–validation process was evaluated by increasing the number of the ranked features in increments of 50, the results of which are shown in Fig. 4. Recent studies employing smartphone–based gait assessment approaches have consistently reported high performance metrics, aligning with our findings. Research specifically focusing on older adults further demonstrates strong validity and reliability across various sensing methods37. Other investigations based on single–sensor smartphone frameworks for continuous gait speed estimation also show high agreement with reference systems38. Similarly, deep–learning models applied under controlled treadmill conditions have achieved robust results39. While these studies primarily used regression–based estimation rather than the classification–based risk stratification adopted here, their consistently strong outcomes reinforce the feasibility and reliability of smartphone–based gait analysis approaches, supporting our results.

The high performance observed in the best model setups could be closely related to feature engineering and gait biomechanics. It is likely that a small subset of features (most likely in the frequency domain and certain wavelet levels) could achieve reasonable performance and plausibly relate to classic gait events. This could be addressed in a follow-up study that investigates feature importance using explainable AI methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)40.

One may argue that the gait speed could be directly assessed from the 2M3T test by using any kind of wearable device equipped with inertial measurement units (IMU) and a suitable signal processing algorithm. In fact, there are cases where such an approach has been considered as a gold standard, e.g., to access free–living gait41. However, the proposed 2M3T test is very short. In comparison with the 4MGS test, it involves changes of direction, different stride counts, and varying frequency (cadence). Therefore, these features are unlikely to resemble the exact same gait speed observed in the 4MGS test, leading this study to a more robust (ML) modeling approach.

In terms of clinical applicability, the 2M3T test and the associated model demonstrated great potential for accurately stratifying gait speed and mobility risk, classifying patients into categories such as low, moderate, at risk, and high risk for mobility decline. This stratification can help healthcare professionals monitor and intervene early in patients with changes that may compromise their independence and health. The model provides a quick and reliable assessment, particularly useful in situations where the traditional 4MGS test cannot be performed due to space limitations.

Despite the promising results, one important limitation of this study was the development of the model based on a local sample of community–dwelling older adults. Firstly, it is worth highlighting that this work was developed with a relatively small sample and imbalanced classes. To extend it further to account for geriatric conditions that affect gait, handling minority classes is fundamental to achieving a reliable model. For instance, a recent work combining data balancing with generative augmentation has been shown to stabilize gait classification in rare–disease or nonstandard movement settings, improving minority–class performance42. This approach could be explored within the present modeling context.

Secondly, although the best models were statistically evaluated and all performance metrics were reported only in test sets (unseen data), this does not guarantee generalization. In fact, additional testing is needed in larger and more heterogeneous populations (with different characteristics), such as institutionalized individuals or those with comorbidities. Moreover, it is important to emphasize that regardless of the technique used to assess gait speed in clinical or laboratory settings, its correlation with various aspects of daily life functioning is necessary to draw meaningful conclusions about the functional gait characteristics of the population under investigation. In addition, to further enhance the comprehensiveness of geriatric assessment regarding functional capacity and mobility, the outcomes from the gait speed assessment may be potentially integrated with those of the widely used Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, thereby enabling a more complete analysis of the different domains of functional capacity. Future studies could explore other machine learning techniques, such as deep learning, as well as investigate different combinations of algorithms and feature extraction techniques to further improve the precision and clinical applicability of the developed models.

To mitigate overfitting given the high dimensionality, we implemented a set of safeguards: (a) dimensionality reduction via ReliefF feature ranking, selecting ~350 features from an initial pool of ~1,150–1,300 based on systematic performance profiling; (b) 30–fold cross–validation for all training tasks; (c) strict prevention of data leakage by embedding all preprocessing and feature selection within the cross–validation folds, with ReliefF ranking and normalization computed exclusively on the training subsets; and (d) statistical assessment through 50 repeated 85/15 train–test splits, computing performance metrics only on held–out test sets and reporting medians with 95% confidence intervals. The observed attenuation from train–validation to test performance (F1-score: 0.992 vs. 0.971) supports adequate generalization without evidence of severe overfitting. Confusion matrices and per–class metrics (Table 4) further corroborate consistent performance across all strata. Nevertheless, we reinforce that external validation on independent, heterogeneous population is required to confirm the generalization of the model and clinical applicability.

The proposed ML pipeline can be translated into real–world geriatric care as a rapid, space–efficient, mobile device–based screening tool. The predicted gait speed strata (low, moderate, at risk, high risk) provide clinicians with useful information. For example, individuals classified as “at risk” (0.8 < gs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 1 m/s) may be referred for strength and balance training, while those in the “high risk” category (gs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 0.8 m/s) may require additional functional assessment or referral for physical therapy treatment. On the other hand, “low-risk” older adults (gs>1.2 m/s) can maintain their current levels of physical activity and routine monitoring.

From a practical standpoint, the model could be integrated into a mobile application running on a smartphone equipped with accelerometer and gyroscope sensors. The application would automatically pre-process the recorded motion data (filtering, segmentation, and feature extraction) to generate gait speed strata without manual intervention. All calculations could be performed locally or in secure cloud environments, ensuring anonymous data processing and compliance with data protection regulations. This implementation would allow physicians or healthcare providers to obtain an immediate reading of functional mobility, increasing the accessibility and scalability of geriatric assessments.

Conclusions

The main goal of the present work consisted in predicting the gait speed retrieved from the 4MGS test, but using motion data recorded from a short–distance test (2M3T test) and a machine learning approach framed within a risk stratification approach. Several model setups were evaluated to find the best combination of machine learning algorithms, wavelet types and number of features ranked by the ReliefF. After a comprehensive investigation on the problem at hand, the k–Nearest Neighbors classifier with 350 features extracted using the biorthogonal wavelet (bior3.1) and ranked by the ReliefF is the one that outperform the others: (median [95% CI] – accuracy: 0.969 [0.963–0.974]; F1–score: 0.971 [0.964–0.975]; AUC: 0.980 [0.975–0.984]). Although the achieved results are promising and the machine learning modeling has proven to be robust, further research is needed in larger and more diverse populations to ensure the generalization in a clinical context.

In practical terms, this modeling strategy could be incorporated into a mobile application for smartphones, enabling rapid assessment in clinical or home settings. Minimum data requirements include a brief 12-second recording of ankle sensor movement, automatic signal preprocessing, and secure data storage. This implementation aligns with digital health trends and could facilitate large-scale monitoring of mobility and functional capacity among older adults, thereby supporting preventive and personalized care.
