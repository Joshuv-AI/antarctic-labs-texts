Suzetrigine as a novel treatment for headache disorders: A case series
Authors: Molly Fensterwald, Jordan Johnson, Angel Moreno, Shawn Conger‐Love, Charles Flippen, Chelsea Hesterman, Mollie Johnston, Joshua Kamins, Karla Minota, Peony Pak, Salman Hashim, Thanh‐Liem Huynh‐Tran, Noelle Kobayashi, Bradley Shane, Andrew Charles, Sinifunanya Nwaobi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuzetrigine is a selective voltage‐gated sodium channel blocker that was recently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of acute pain. We retrospectively evaluated the efficacy and tolerability of suzetrigine for the treatment of different headache disorders in patients refractory to standard treatments in an academic headache center. These patients had a variety of headache and facial pain diagnoses, including chronic migraine, medication‐overuse headache, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, nummular headache, occipital neuralgia, and persistent idiopathic facial pain. Some did not clearly meet any diagnostic criteria. The treatment was generally well tolerated, and most patients reported improvement as measured by patient global impression of change. Suzetrigine is a promising new therapeutic approach that warrants further study for the treatment of headache disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Despite remarkable recent therapeutic advances, there is an ongoing need for new therapeutic approaches for those with headache disorders for whom current treatments are ineffective or poorly tolerated. Sodium channels are well established as a therapeutic target for pain, but the role of sodium channels in headache disorders remains poorly understood. The efficacy of intravenous lidocaine for some headache disorders provides indirect evidence for sodium channel involvement, 1 , 2 , 3 as does the efficacy of other therapies that have sodium channel blockade, including topiramate, lamotrigine, and lacosamide, as one of their mechanisms of action.

Suzetrigine is a selective blocker of the NaV1.8 type of sodium channels that are expressed primarily in peripheral sensory neurons. 4 Suzetrigine was approved in 2025 for the treatment of acute pain based on results of two randomized, placebo‐controlled studies for postoperative pain. 5 Here, we provide a retrospective review of off‐label suzetrigine use specifically for the treatment of different headache disorders. All patients had either inadequate response to or poor tolerability of standard treatments for their specific headache disorder prior to suzetrigine treatment.

METHODS

Standard protocol approvals and ethical considerations

The University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) Institutional Review Board (IRB) deemed this study to be exempt from review (IRB‐25‐2154) given the retrospective design. De‐identified patient data was utilized when possible and all data was accessed on secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act–compliant platforms to maintain patient confidentiality and data security.

Data sources and study cohort

This study utilized the UCLA health system electronic health record (EHR) for cohort identification and data collection. EHR data was queried from December 1, 2024, to February 5, 2026, for all patients prescribed suzetrigine (N = 1394); data query preceded US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for practical reasons. The cohort of interest was refined to patients treated in the UCLA Goldberg Migraine Program with confirmed use of suzetrigine for the treatment of headache and facial pain diagnoses and had documentation of suzetrigine response (N = 80).

Study design, variables, and statistical analyses

We extracted Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) scores 6 , 7 from clinical documentation in response to the question, “Since starting suzetrigine, how is your pain?”. Scores were collapsed into response categories of “Improved” (0–2), “No change” (3), or “Worsened” (4–6). Demographic data, prior prescribed acute and preventive headache medications, headache and facial pain diagnoses, and reported side effects were also extracted. A full list of the headache medications and diagnoses used in groupings are provided in supplemental figures (Table S1–, S3). We present counts and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviation for continuous variables. A chi‐squared test was used to determine significant differences in the frequency of PGIC scores.

Treatment response to suzetrigine

Most patients reported improvement with suzetrigine use (N = 54/80, 67.5%, collapsed grouping). Five patients (N = 5/80, 6.3%) reported worsening (Figure 1A,B). The distribution of responder groups was statistically significant (X 2(2) = 46.83, p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis of equal distribution of responder outcomes. PGIC responder rates were evaluated within each headache category (Figure 1C). The highest rates of improvement (83.3%) occurred in trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (N = 6); lowest rates of improvement (40.0%) were observed in medication‐overuse headache (MOH) (N = 5). These small sample sizes precluded any inferential analysis of suzetrigine response across headache disorders. Mild side effects were reported in 15 patients and included pruritus, lightheadedness, and constipation (Table S4).

Representative cases

We selected three representative cases with differing pathophysiology that highlight the potentially broad utility of suzetrigine in the management of refractory headache disorders. In each case, appropriate imaging and lab studies revealed no cause of secondary headache. The patients described in the cases provided written informed consent to have their cases published.

Case 1: Hemicrania continua

A 65‐year‐old woman with history of migraine presented with 4 years of continuous right‐sided periauricular pain distinct from prior headaches. The pain was burning and squeezing with prominent cranial allodynia, worsening in the evenings and associated with ipsilateral cranial autonomic symptoms. Prior preventive medications included amitriptyline, topiramate, and oxcarbazepine without benefit. Prior procedural interventions of occipital and greater auricular nerve blocks and onabotulinumtoxinA injections were ineffective. She reported relief with ibuprofen and meloxicam, but both had adverse effects.

Given a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, celecoxib was started (in lieu of indomethacin) and reduced headache severity by 75%. Additional preventive therapies were tried including memantine, melatonin, duloxetine, lamotrigine, nortriptyline, candesartan, lacosamide, galcanezumab, and noninvasive vagal nerve stimulation. Suzetrigine twice daily was added to celecoxib. Within 2 weeks, she experienced marked reduction in pain severity and meaningful improvement in quality of life, reporting that “more improved pain control the rest of the day has done wonders for my morale.”

Case 2: Medication‐overuse headache

A 60‐year‐old woman with a long‐standing history of headache presented for 2 years of daily, constant bifrontal pain associated with nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. Daily use of rizatriptan along with frequent intake of caffeine, acetaminophen, and combination analgesics resulted in transient improvement. She was diagnosed with chronic migraine without aura with superimposed probable medication‐overuse headache. Prior medication trials included multiple triptans and gepants, propranolol, nortriptyline, topiramate, galcanezumab, gabapentin, eptinezumab, and onabotulinumtoxinA chemodenervation, without sustained benefit.

Suzetrigine was prescribed as short‐term bridging therapy with a caffeine and analgesic‐weaning schedule to facilitate reduction of overuse. Within 3 days, the patient reported no headaches and stated that the medication “helped me immensely with getting off the caffeine pills and the [Acetaminophen/aspirin/caffeine].”

Case 3: Hypnic headache

A 76‐year‐old man with cardiovascular risk factors, stage III chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, thoracic aortic aneurysm, and a remote history of migraine presented with 1 year of nocturnal headaches. Pain occurred nightly around 3 or 4 A.M., causing nocturnal awakenings. Quality was pressure‐like in the bilateral frontal and occipital regions with associated cervicalgia, photophobia, and phonophobia without cranial autonomic features. Symptoms improved within 15 min of getting out of bed. Pain could persist on awakening.

Nocturnal caffeine was partially beneficial. Gabapentin and melatonin caused daytime sedation. Nortriptyline was ineffective. Lithium and indomethacin were avoided due to chronic kidney disease. A 2‐week course of suzetrigine twice daily resulted in approximately 80% improvement in headache severity, allowing return to sleep at night without reported adverse effects.

DISCUSSION

Multiple sodium channel blockers, particularly intravenous lidocaine, have been used to treat refractory headache and facial pain disorders. 1 , 2 , 8 , 9 This treatment is problematic, however, because of the possibility of significant adverse effects and requirement for intensive monitoring during infusion as well as inconsistent efficacy and durability. 1 , 10 Attempts to mimic the effects of lidocaine with oral therapies such as mexiletine have been largely ineffective. Other therapies that have sodium channel inhibition as a mechanism of action, such as carbamazepine and derivatives, lamotrigine, and lacosamide, do not have consistent efficacy and may have poor tolerability. In contrast to these relatively nonselective sodium channel blockers, suzetrigine is selective for the NaV1.8 channel and therefore does not affect cardiac or central nervous system sodium channels. 11

Our results indicate that suzetrigine shows potential benefit for patients with a variety of headache disorders who demonstrate inadequate efficacy or poor tolerability to currently available standard therapies. Some patients with marked improvements had headache diagnoses, such as short‐lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache and nummular headache, for which established therapeutic options are limited. It was encouraging that some patients with MOH responded to suzetrigine. Whereas the potential for MOH with suzetrigine remains unknown, its mechanism as an ion channel blocker is highly distinct from other therapies that cause MOH, such as receptor agonists like opioids, triptans, or barbiturates.

Suzetrigine is FDA approved for the acute treatment of moderate to severe pain, but it has not been specifically studied for headache disorders. A study of chronic suzetrigine use for the treatment of painful diabetic neuropathy is underway (ClinicalTrials.gov/study/NCT06628908). Regardless of the efficacy outcome, this study should provide useful information regarding long‐term tolerability and safety. In our cohort, suzetrigine was well tolerated, and we did not observe any serious adverse events. This study is limited by its retrospective nature, small sample size, lack of a placebo control group, and the broad range of headache disorders that were evaluated. Despite these limitations, our data suggest that suzetrigine is a promising new therapy and that further studies are warranted to better evaluate its efficacy, safety, and long‐term tolerability in the treatment of headache and facial pain disorders.
