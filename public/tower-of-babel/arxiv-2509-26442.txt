Extensions of Robbins-Siegmund Theorem with Applications in Reinforcement Learning
Xinyu Liu xinyuliu@virginia.edu, Zixuan Xie xie.zixuan@email.virginia.edu, Shangtong Zhang shangtong@virginia.edu

Abstract
The Robbins-Siegmund theorem establishes the convergence of stochastic processes that are almost supermartingales and is foundational for analyzing a wide range of stochastic iterative algorithms in stochastic approximation and reinforcement learning (RL). However, its original form has a significant limitation as it requires the zero-order term to be summable. In many important RL applications, this summable condition, however, cannot be met. This limitation motivates us to extend the Robbins-Siegmund theorem for almost supermartingales where the zero-order term is not summable but only square summable. Particularly, we introduce a novel and mild assumption on the increments of the stochastic processes. This together with the square summable condition enables an almost sure convergence to a bounded set. Additionally, we further provide almost sure convergence rates, high probability concentration bounds, and L p L^{p} convergence rates. We then apply the new results in stochastic approximation and RL. Notably, we obtain the first almost sure convergence rate, the first high probability concentration bound, and the first L p L^{p} convergence rate for Q Q -learning with linear function approximation.

Introduction

Supermartingale is an important concept in stochastic processes and the celebrated Doob’s supermartingale convergence theorem
(
Doob 1953
)
establishes the almost sure convergence of supermartingales and is arguably one of the most important results in the field of martingales.
However,
many stochastic processes of interest are not supermartingales and, as a result, Doob’s theorem does not apply.
Instead,
they are only very close to supermartingales and adopt some noisy perturbations from supermartingales.

Robbins and Siegmund 1971
call them almost supermartingales.
The Robbins-Siegmund theorem establishes the almost sure convergence of almost supermartingales under mild conditions and has been widely used in stochastic approximation and reinforcement learning
(
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Barakat and Bianchi 2021
;
Sebbouh et al. 2021
;
Xin et al. 2021
;
Qian et al. 2024
)
.

However,
the Robbins-Siegmund theorem has a significant limitation.
It requires the zero-order term of the almost supermartingales to be summable.
When applying the Robbins-Siegmund theorem to analyze stochastic approximation
(
Benveniste et al. 1990
;
Kushner and Yin 2003
;
Borkar 2009
;
Liu et al. 2025a
)
and reinforcement learning
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
algorithms (e.g.,
Q
-learning
(
Watkins 1989
)
with linear function approximation
(
Sutton 1988
)
),
this zero-order term is sometimes derived from the learning rate,
which is required to satisfy the Robbins-Monro condition
(
Robbins and Monro 1951
)
,
i.e.,
it is not summable and is only square summable.
This is apparently incompatible with the summable condition in the Robbins-Siegmund theorem.

This incompatibility calls for extensions of the Robbins-Siegmund theorem.
However,
to our knowledge,
all existing extensions still require the summable condition.
For example,

Liu and Yuan 2022
;
Karandikar and Vidyasagar 2024
derive almost sure convergence rates for the Robbins-Siegmund theorem under the summable condition.
This work instead aims to directly reconcile this incompatibility.
Specifically,
we make two contributions.

1.

We introduce a novel and mild assumption on the increments of almost supermartingales.
This together with the Robbins-Monro condition enables an almost sure convergence to a bounded set.
Additionally,
we further provide almost sure convergence rates, high probability concentration bounds, and
L^{p}
convergence rates,
all to a bounded set.

2.

Building on the new results concerning almost supermartingales,
we deliver new convergent results of general stochastic approximations with time-inhomogeneous Markovian noise,
which is then used to analyze
Q
-learning with linear function approximation,
yielding the first almost sure convergence rate,
the first high probability concentration bound,
and the first
L^{p}
convergence rate,
all to a bounded set.

Background

This section reviews the classical Robbins-Siegmund theorem and some latest extensions.
We consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\Pr)
equipped with a filtration
\quantity{\mathcal{F}_n}_{n\geq 0}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{n}
represents the information available up to time
n
. For any random variable
z
, we denote the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
by
\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z]\doteq\mathbb{E}\quantity[z |\mathcal{F}_n]
.
The Robbins-Siegmund theorem is concerned about the convergence of a non-negative real-valued random sequence
\quantity{z_n}_{n\geq 0}
satisfying

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z_{n+1}]\leq(1+a_{n})z_{n}+x_{n}-y_{n}\quad\mbox{a.s.,}\quad

(RS)

where
\quantity{z_n}_{n\geq 0}
,
\quantity{a_n}_{n\geq 0}
,
\quantity{x_n}_{n\geq 0}
, and
\quantity{y_n}_{n\geq 0}
are
non-negative
real-valued random sequences adapted to
\quantity{\mathcal{F}_n}_{n\geq 0}
.
This non-negative condition is always imposed in the rest of the paper.

Robbins and Siegmund 1971
call such
\quantity{z_n}
almost supermartingales.
Indeed,
if
a_{n}=x_{n}=0
,
then
\quantity{z_n}
immediately becomes a supermartingale.
1
1

1

This additionally requires to verify that
\quantity{z_n}
is bounded in
L_{1}
, which is an easy exercise given (
RS
) and the non-negativity of
\quantity{z_n}
.

We now present the Robbins-Siegmund theorem.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 1 of
Robbins and Siegmund 1971
)
.

Consider
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS
).
Assume

(A1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}<\infty
a.s.;

(A2)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}<\infty
a.s.

Then

1.

\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}
exists and is finite a.s.;

2.

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}y_{n}<\infty
a.s.

Here, the zero-order term in Section
1
refers to
\quantity{x_n}
and the summable condition refers to (A2).
Lemma
1
is an asymptotic result, which is recently extended to a non-asymptotic version by
Liu and Yuan 2022
;
Karandikar and Vidyasagar 2024
,
under some additional assumptions.

Lemma 2

(Theorem 2 of
Karandikar and Vidyasagar 2024
)
.

Consider
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS
).
Let the non-negative real-valued random sequence
\quantity{b_n}_{n\geq 0}
be such that
y_{n}=b_{n}z_{n}
.
For some
\eta\in(0,1)
, assume that

(A1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}<\infty
,
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}<\infty
,
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}=\infty
a.s.;

(A2)

There exists some
n_{1}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq n_{1}
,
b_{n}\geq\frac{\eta}{n}
a.s.;

(A3)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+1)^{\eta}x_{n}<\infty
,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(b_{n}-\frac{\eta}{n})=\infty
a.s.

Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\eta}z_{n}=0
a.s.

Here (A3) is still a summable condition on the zero-order term.

Sometimes practitioners
(
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Barakat and Bianchi 2021
;
Sebbouh et al. 2021
;
Xin et al. 2021
;
Qian et al. 2024
)
consider a special identification
of
\quantity{a_n, x_n, y_n}
in (
RS
).
Namely,
they consider a deterministic sequence
\quantity{T_n \in(0, 1)}_{n\geq 0}
, some positive scalars
\alpha,\xi
, and use

a_{n}=0
,
x_{n}=\xi T_{n}^{2}
, and
y_{n}=\alpha T_{n}z_{n}
,
yielding

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z_{n+1}]\leq(1-\alpha T_{n})z_{n}+\xi T_{n}^{2}.

(1)

The
\quantity{T_n}
usually results from the learning rates of stochastic approximation and reinforcement learning algorithms so it satisfies the Robbins-Monro condition,
i.e.,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}=\infty,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}^{2}\leq\infty
.
Then naturally,
the zero-order term
\xi T_{n}^{2}
is summable.
However,
many important RL algorithms (see Section
6
for examples) do not reduce to the nice form above.
For those algorithms, the corresponding zero-order term is
T_{n}
instead of
T_{n}^{2}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z_{n+1}]\leq(1-\alpha T_{n})z_{n}+\xi T_{n}.

(RS-Special)

Unfortunately,
the zero-order term is no longer summable and is only square summable under the Robbins-Monro condition.
Since
T_{n}\geq T_{n}^{2}
,
this situation essentially allows more noise,
making
\quantity{z_n}
further away from being a supermartingale.
To our knowledge,
no existing extension of the Robbins-Siegmund theorem is able to handle this situation.
This is the gap that this paper aims to bridge.

Notations.

For a real number
z
, we denote its positive part by
z^{+}\doteq\max(z,0)
. Note that the operator
(\cdot)^{+}
satisfies

\displaystyle\textstyle\absolutevalue{z_1^+ - z_2^+}\leq\absolutevalue{z_1 - z_2}\quad\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

This inequality can be easily proved via the identity
z^{+}=\frac{1}{2}z+\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{z}
and the triangle inequality.
For an interval
Z\subset\mathbb{R}
,
we denote
d(z,Z)\doteq\inf_{\tilde{z}\in Z}\absolutevalue{z - \tilde{z}}
as the distance from
z
to
Z
.

3
Asymptotic Extensions of Robbins-Siegmund Theorem

We now proceed to analyzing (
RS-Special
)
under the Robbins-Monro condition
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}=\infty
and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}^{2}<\infty
.
Consider a special case where
z_{n}
is deterministic.
Then (
RS-Special
) degenerates to

\displaystyle z_{n+1}\leq(1-\alpha T_{n})z_{n}+\xi T_{n}.

(3)

Under the Robbins-Monro condition,
it is trivial to show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(z_{n},[0,\xi/\alpha])=0
,
i.e.,

\quantity{z_n}
converge to a bounded interval.
Building on this special case,
one would then easily optimistically conjecture that under the Robbins-Monro condition,
the
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS-Special
) also converge to a bounded interval almost surely.
Unfortunately,
this is impossible.
Below we provide an example where the
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS-Special
) diverges to infinity almost surely even when the Robbins-Monro condition holds.

Example 3

(Divergence with Robbins-Monro Condition)
.

Define
T_{n}\doteq(n+1)^{-3/4}
.
Let
\quantity{X_{n+1}}_{n\geq 0}
be independent with

\displaystyle X_{n+1}\doteq\begin{cases}A_{n}&\text{with probability }p_{n},\\
0&\text{with probability }1-p_{n},\end{cases}\qquad n\geq 0,

(4)

where
A_{n}\doteq(n+1)^{1/4},p_{n}\doteq\frac{T_{n}}{A_{n}}=\frac{1}{n+1}
.
Starting from
z_{0}=0
and with filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
,
define recursively

\textstyle z_{n+1}=(1-T_{n})\,z_{n}+X_{n+1},\qquad n\geq 0.

(5)

Then

1.

\quantity{z_n}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z_{n+1}]=(1-T_{n})z_{n}+T_{n}
, i.e., (
RS-Special
);

2.

\limsup_{n\to\infty}z_{n}=\infty
a.s.

Proof.

\quantity{T_n}
apparently fulfills the Robbins-Monro condition.
Since
X_{n+1}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathbb{E}_{n}[X_{n+1}]=p_{n}A_{n}=T_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z_{n+1}]=(1-T_{n})z_{n}+T_{n}
.
That is, (
RS-Special
) holds with
\alpha=\xi=1
.

Define the events

\Xi_{n}\doteq\quantity{X_{n+1}=A_n}
.
They are independent with

\Pr(\Xi_n)=p_{n}=1/(n+1)
and
\sum_{n}\Pr(\Xi_n)=\infty
. By the second
Borel-Cantelli lemma,
\Pr(\qty{\Xi_n \, \text{infinitely often}})=1
. On
\Xi_{n}
,

\displaystyle\textstyle z_{n+1}=(1-T_{n})z_{n}+A_{n}\geq A_{n}=(n+1)^{1/4}.

(6)

Since
\Xi_{n}
occurs infinitely often almost surely,

z_{n+1}>(n+1)^{1/4}
occurs infinitely often almost surely,
forcing
\limsup_{n\to\infty}z_{n}=\infty
almost surely.
∎

Example
3
shows that the Robbins-Monro condition alonely does not prevent
the potential divergence of
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS-Special
).
Intuitively, the divergence in Example
3
results from the spikes of
{X_{n+1}}
.

X_{n+1}
is mostly 0 but occasionally spikes to an increasingly large value
A_{n}
.
Between the spikes, the factor
1-T_{n}
contracts
z_{n}
.
But the spikes occur infinitely often,
making
z_{n}
grows unbounded almost surely.
This motivates us to introduce additional assumptions to control such spikes,
or equivalently,
control the growth of
z_{n+1}
from
z_{n}
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS-Special
) with

\quantity{T_n\in(0,1)}_{n\geq 0}
being a sequence of strictly decreasing deterministic real numbers.
Suppose

(A1)

\sum_{n}T_{n}=\infty
and
\sum_{n}T_{n}^{2}<\infty
;

(A2)

There exists some constant
B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}\geq\alpha
, such that for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{z_{n+1} - z_n}\leq B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}T_{n}(z_{n}+1)\quad\mbox{a.s.}\quad

(7)

Then there exists some constant
B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}}}
, such that

\displaystyle\textstyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d\quantity(z_n, [0, B_\tref{thm:bound}])=0\quad\mbox{a.s.}\quad

(8)

Proof.

We will identify
B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}}}=\frac{\xi}{\alpha}
.
Let
u_{n}\doteq d(z_{n},[0,\frac{\xi}{\alpha}])=\quantity(z_n -\frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{+}
. Now we consider two cases.

(1) When
u_{n}>0
.

Then
u_{n}=z_{n}-\frac{\xi}{\alpha}
,
we have

\displaystyle\textstyle u_{n+1}^{2}=\quantity(\qty(z_{n+1} - \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^+)^{2}\leq\quantity(z_{n+1} - \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}=\quantity((z_{n+1}-z_n)+z_n-\frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}=\quantity((z_{n+1}-z_n)+u_n)^{2}.

(9)

Thus

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}[u_{n+1}^{2}]

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[\qty(u_n+(z_{n+1}-z_n))^2]

(By (
9
))

\displaystyle\textstyle=u_{n}^{2}+2u_{n}\,\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[z_{n+1}-z_n]+\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[(z_{n+1}-z_n)^2]

(10)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq u_{n}^{2}+2u_{n}\quantity(-\alpha T_n z_n + \xi T_n)+B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}T_{n}^{2}\quantity(z_n + 1)^{2}

(By (
RS-Special
) and Theorem
4
(A2))

\displaystyle\textstyle=u_{n}^{2}+2u_{n}\cdot\quantity(-\alpha T_n u_n)+B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}T_{n}^{2}\quantity(u_n + \frac{\xi}{\alpha} + 1)^{2}

(Since
u_{n}=z_{n}-\frac{\xi}{\alpha}
)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq u_{n}^{2}-2\alpha T_{n}u_{n}^{2}+2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}T_{n}^{2}u_{n}^{2}+2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}T_{n}^{2}\quantity(\frac{\xi}{\alpha} + 1)^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\textstyle=\quantity(1 - 2\alpha T_n + 2B_{\tref{thm:bound},1}^2 T_n^2)u_{n}^{2}+2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}\quantity(1 + \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}T_{n}^{2}.

(12)

(2) When
u_{n}=0
.

Then
z_{n}\leq\frac{\xi}{\alpha}
. By (
2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\textstyle\absolutevalue{u_{n+1}}=\absolutevalue{u_{n+1}- u_n}\leq\absolutevalue{(z_{n+1}-\frac{\xi}{\alpha})-(z_n-\frac{\xi}{\alpha})}\leq\absolutevalue{z_{n+1}-z_n}.

(13)

Thus

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[u_{n+1}^2]\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[\abs{z_{n+1}-z_n}^2]

(By (
13
))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}\quantity(1 + z_n)^{2}T_{n}^{2}

(By Theorem
4
(A2))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}\quantity(1 + \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}T_{n}^{2}

(Since
z_{n}\in\quantity[0, \frac{\xi}{\alpha}]
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle\quantity(1 - 2\alpha T_n + 2B_{\tref{thm:bound},1}^2 T_n^2)u_{n}^{2}+2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}\quantity(1 + \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}T_{n}^{2}.

(Since
u_{n}=0
)

Combining two cases we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\quantity[u_{n+1}^2]\leq\quantity(1 - 2\alpha T_n + 2B_{\tref{thm:bound},1}^2 T_n^2)u_{n}^{2}+2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}\quantity(1 + \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}T_{n}^{2}.

(14)

We now proceed to apply Lemma
1
. We identify in (
RS
)

\textstyle z_{n}\leftrightarrow u_{n}^{2},a_{n}\leftrightarrow 2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}T_{n}^{2},x_{n}\leftrightarrow 2B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}},1}^{2}\quantity(1 + \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^{2}T_{n}^{2},y_{n}\leftrightarrow 2\alpha T_{n}u_{n}^{2}.

(15)

As
\sum_{n}T_{n}^{2}<\infty
, we have
\sum_{n}a_{n}<\infty
and
\sum_{n}x_{n}<\infty
a.s. Applying Lemma
1
then gives: 1.
\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}^{2}
exists and is finite almost surely; 2.
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}y_{n}<\infty
a.s. That is,
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}u_{n}^{2}<\infty
a.s.

We now illustrate that the only possibility is
\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}^{2}=0
a.s. For a fixed sample path
\omega
, consider
u_{n}(\omega)^{2}
as a deterministic sequence (abbreviated as
u_{n}^{2}
).
If the limit
\zeta_{0}\doteq\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}^{2}
exists and
\zeta_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, then there exists
N
such that
u_{n}^{2}\geq\zeta_{0}/2
for all
n\geq N
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}T_{n}u_{n}^{2}\geq\frac{\zeta_{0}}{2}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}T_{n}=\infty,

(16)

where the last equality holds because
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}=\infty
. This contradicts the convergence of
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}u_{n}^{2}
.
By definition, we have
\zeta_{0}\geq 0
. Therefore, the only possibility is
\zeta_{0}=0
. That is,
0=\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\quantity(\qty(z_n - \frac{\xi}{\alpha})^+)^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}d\quantity(z_n, \qty[0, \frac{\xi}{\alpha}])^{2}
. Denoting
B_{\text{\ref{thm:bound}}}\doteq\frac{\xi}{\alpha}
then completes the proof.
∎
Notably, (A2) of Theorem
4
prescribes that the growth of
z_{n+1}
from
z_{n}
can at most be linear of
z_{n}
,
gated by
T_{n}
.
This additional structure on the growth excludes the pathological spikes in Example
3
.
In Section
6
,
we demonstrate that this additional assumption indeed trivially holds for important reinforcement learning algorithms such as
Q
-learning with linear function approximation.

We now apply the same idea to study (
RS
).
Similarly,
we want to relax the summable assumption (A2) in Lemma
1
.
To this end,
we additionally introduce a square-summable control on the growth between consecutive steps and an overall drift that is non-positive once the process is overly large.

Theorem 5
.

Consier
\quantity{z_n}
in (
RS
). Suppose that

(A1)

\sum_{n}a_{n}<\infty
a.s.;

(A2)

\absolutevalue{z_{n+1} - z_n}\leq b_{n}(z_{n}+1)
a.s.,
where
\quantity{b_n}_{n\geq 0}
is a non-negative real-valued random sequence adapted to
\quantity{\fF_n}_{n\geq 0}
and satisfies
\sum_{n}b_{n}^{2}<\infty
a.s.;

(A3)

There exists some constant
B_{\text{\ref{thm:rs general}}}
such that

\displaystyle z_{n}>B_{\text{\ref{thm:rs general}}}\implies x_{n}-y_{n}\leq-c_{n}(z_{n}-B_{\text{\ref{thm:rs general}}}),

(17)

where
\quantity{c_n }_{n\geq 0}
is a non-negative real-valued random sequence adapted to
\quantity{\fF_n}_{n\geq 0}
and satisfies
\sum_{n}c_{n}=\infty
a.s.

Then

\displaystyle\textstyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d\quantity(z_n, [0, B_\tref{thm:rs general}])=0\quad\mbox{a.s.}\quad

(18)

The proof follows a similar idea as in Theorem
4
and is provided in Appendix
A.1
.
We now elaborate on the connection between Theorem
5
and Theorem
4
.
When reducing to (
RS-Special
) from (
RS
), we set
a_{n}=0
so Theorem
5
(A1) is absent in Theorem
4
.
Instead,
Theorem
4
(A1) is the Robbins-Monro condition.
The requirement
\sum_{n}T_{n}=\infty
ensures that
1-T_{n}
can generate enough contraction drift over the time,
which corresponds to Theorem
5
(A3),
particularly,
\sum_{n}c_{n}=\infty
.
The requirement
\sum_{n}T_{n}^{2}<\infty
ensures that the growth rate is square summable,
which corresponds to Theorem
5
(A2),
particularly,
\sum_{n}b_{n}^{2}<\infty
.

Notably,
we are not claiming that Theorems
4
&
5
are stronger than Lemma
1
.
Indeed,
we remove the summable assumption at the cost of introducing new assumptions and the convergence is to a bounded set instead of a point.
Our key contribution and novelty lies in that we are the first to obtain convergent results for almost supermartingales without the summable assumption.
And according to Example
3
,
our results might be the best that one can hope.
Additionally,
we do demonstrate in Section
6
that our results have significant applications in reinforcement learning.

4
Nonasymptotic Extensions of Robbins-Siegmund Theorem

Having established asymptotic extensions of the Robbins-Siegmund theorem,
we in this section further establish nonasymptotic convergence rates.
We start with (
RS-Special
) and an almost sure convergence rate.

Theorem 6
.

Consider
\quantity{z_n}_{n\geq 0}
in (
RS-Special
) and
let all conditions in Theorem
4
hold.
Additionally, assume there exists some constant
\eta>0
such that

(A1)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}nT_{n}>\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}
;

(A2)

\sum_{n}(n+1)^{\eta}T_{n}^{2}<\infty
.

Then

\textstyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\eta/2}d\quantity(z_n, [0, B_\tref{thm:bound}])=0\quad\mbox{a.s.}\quad

(19)

Similar to the proof of Theorem
4
where we construct auxiliary sequences to invoke Lemma
1
,
we prove Theorem
6
by constructing auxiliary sequences to invoke Lemma
2
,
which is provided in Appendix
B.1
.

If
T_{n}
decays in an inverse linear manner,
we can further obtain high probability concentration bound and

5
Stochastic Approximation

We now use Theorems
6
&
7
and Corollary
8
to derive new convergent results for stochastic approximations.
Specifically,
we consider stochastic approximation algorithms in the form of

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=w_{t}+\alpha_{t}H(w_{t},Y_{t+1}).

(SA)

Here
\quantity{w_t \in\mathbb{R}^d}
is the stochastic iterates,
\quantity{\alpha_t}
is a sequence of learning rates,
\quantity{Y_t}
is a
time-inhomogeneous Markov chain evolving in a finite space
\mathcal{Y}
, and
H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the function that
generates the incremental update.
Importantly,
we consider the setting where the transition kernel of
\quantity{Y_t}
is controlled by
\quantity{w_t}
itself.
This setting adopts important applications such as linear SARSA
(
Rummery and Niranjan 1994
;
Zou et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
and linear
Q
-learning
(
Baird 1995
;
Meyn 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
)
.
In such settings,
the convergence of
\quantity{w_t}
to a point is usually not attainable (see, e.g.,
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Meyn 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
) unless strong assumptions are made (see, e.g.,
Zou et al. 2019
).
We, therefore,
aim to establish the convergence to a bounded set instead.
The high level idea of our approach is to fit (
SA
) into the template (
RS-Special
).
To this end,
we use the skeleton iterates technique from
Qian et al. 2024
to work with the Markovian nature of
\quantity{Y_t}
and use a novel error decomposition from
Liu et al. 2025b
to work with the time-inhomogeneous nature of
\quantity{Y_t}
.
One of our main contributions lies in a new analysis that successfully controls the complex bias terms arising from this integration, making these two powerful techniques compatible for the first time.
After obtaining (
RS-Special
),
our new results can then take over.

This contribution is crucial because
Qian et al. 2024
have to assume that
\quantity{Y_t}
is time homogeneous so they can use the original Robbins-Monro theorem, while

Liu et al. 2025b
have to take total expectation on both sides of (
RS-Special
) to derive the rate at which the second moments of
\quantity{z_t}
converges to a bounded interval.

Liu et al. 2025b
thus miss the opportunity to derive an almost sure converge rate, a high probability concentration bound, and an
L^{p}
convergence rate.
We now list our assumptions,
many of which are adapted from
Qian et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
.

Assumption LR1

(Assumption LR1 in
Qian et al. 2024
)
.

The learning rate has the form

\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha_{t}=\frac{C_{\alpha}}{(t+3)^{\nu}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\nu\in\left(\frac{2}{3},1\right].

(23)

Assumption LR2

(Assumption LR2 in
Qian et al. 2024
)
.

The learning rate has the form

\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha_{t}=\frac{C_{\alpha}}{(t+3)\ln^{\nu}(t+3)}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\nu\in(0,1).

(24)

The two forms of learning rates are from the skeleton iterates technique of
Qian et al. 2024
.
Assumption
LR1
is a standard choice of learning rate other than that
\nu
cannot be smaller than
2/3
,
which is an inherent limitation of the skeleton iterates technique.
This learning rate will be used for obtaining an almost sure convergence rate of
\quantity{w_t}
by invoking Theorem
4
.
Assumption
LR2
is specially designed such that the resuling
T_{n}
in (

) can verify Assumption
4.1
.
This learning rate will be used for obtaining high probability concentration bound and
L^{p}
convergence rate by invoking Theorem
7
and Corollary

.
We refer the reader to
Qian et al. 2024
for more discussion about the two learning rates.

Assumption 5.1

(Assumption 3.1 & 3.2 in
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

There exists a family of parameterized
transition matrices
\Lambda_{P}\doteq\quantity{P_w \in\mathbb{R}^{\abs{\mathcal{Y}} \times\abs{\mathcal{Y}}}\mid w \in\mathbb{R}^d}
such
that
\operatorname{Pr}(Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t})=P_{w_{t}}(Y_{t},Y_{t+1})
. Furthermore, let
\bar{\Lambda}_{P}

denote the closure of
\Lambda_{P}
. Then for any
P\in\bar{\Lambda}_{P}
, the time-homogeneous Markov chain induced by
P
is irreducible
and aperiodic.

Assumption
5.1
looks prohibiting but as can be seen soon in Section
6
,
this assumption can be trivially verified in applications.
Under Assumption
5.1
, for any
w
, the Markov chain induced by
P_{w}
has a unique stationary distribution,
which we denote as
d_{\mathcal{Y},w}
. This allows us to define the expected update as

h(w)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim d_{\mathcal{Y},w}}\quantity[H(w, Y)]
.
Additionally, by Lemma 1 of
Zhang et al. 2022
, Assumption
5.1
implies
that the Markov chains in
\Lambda_{P}
mix both geometrically and
uniformly.
In other words,
there exist constants
C_{\text{\ref{asp:markov chain}}}>0
and

\tau\in[0,1)
, independent of
w
, such that

\displaystyle\textstyle\sup_{w,y}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{P_w^n(y, y') - d_{\mathcal{Y},w}(y')}\leq C_{\text{\ref{asp:markov chain}}}\tau^{n}.

(25)

We then define

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}\doteq\min\quantity{n \geq 0 \mid C_\tref{asp:markov chain}\tau^n \leq\alpha}

(26)

to denote the number of steps that the Markov chain needs to mix to an accuracy
\alpha
.
We now introduce the norm we will work with in the rest of the paper.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be an arbitrary inner product for vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\norm{\cdot}
to be the norm induced by this inner product (i.e., the inner product norm for vectors and the corresponding induced norm for matrices).
For a non-negative real number
r
, we denote the closed ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at
0
with radius
r
by
\mathcal{B}(r)\doteq\quantity{x \in\mathbb{R}^d \mid\norm{x} \leq r}
.

Assumption 5.2

(Assumption A3 in
Liu et al. 2025b
)
.

There exists a constant
C_{\text{\ref{asp:P_lip'}}}
such that for
\forall w_{1},w_{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle\norm{P_{w_1} - P_{w_2}}\leq\frac{C_{\text{\ref{asp:P_lip'}}}}{1+\norm{w_1}+\norm{w_2}}\norm{w_1 - w_2}.

(27)

Assumption 5.3

(Assumption A2 & A3 in
Liu et al. 2025b
)
.

There exists some constant
L_{h}
, such that for
\forall w_{1},w_{2},y

\displaystyle\norm{H(w_1,y) - H(w_2,y)}\leq L_{h}\norm{w_1 - w_2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\norm{H(0,y)}\leq L_{h}.

(28)

Assumption
5.2
is stronger than the standard Lipschitz continuity assumption (cf. Assumption
5.3
) and is the key to handle the time-inhomogeneous nature of
\quantity{Y_t}
.
Similar idea dates back to at least
Konda 2002
.

Assumption 5.4
.

There exist some deterministic constants
C_{\text{\ref{asp:bound inner}},1}>0
and
C_{\text{\ref{asp:bound inner}},2}
such that

\displaystyle{\left<w,h(w)\right>}\leq-C_{\text{\ref{asp:bound inner}},1}\norm{w}^{2}+C_{\text{\ref{asp:bound inner}},2}.

(29)

Assumption
5.4
ensures that the expected update direction

will decay
\norm{w}
at least when
w
is overly large.
The factor
C_{\text{\ref{asp:bound inner}},2}
is usually 0 if
\quantity{Y_t}
is time-homogeneous.
But for a time-inhomogeneous
\quantity{Y_t}
,
it is usually strictly positive
(
Meyn 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
)
.
This is one of the main difficulties in analyzing stochastic approximations with time-inhomogeneous Markovian noise.

We rely on the skeleton iterates technique from

Instead of examining
\quantity{w_t}
along the natural time scale

we examine a subsequence
\quantity{w_{t_m}}
indexed by
\quantity{t_m}

The subsequence

For the case where

t_{m}<t-\tau_{\alpha_{t}}

In both scenarios, this meticulous case analysis successfully demonstrates that the bias is bounded within
\mathcal{O}(T_{m}^{2})

6
Applications in Reinforcement Learning

In this section,
we apply the results in Section

) algorithms.
We focus on linear

-learning,
though our results can also be applied to linear SARSA
(

)
to advance the understanding of the long standing “chattering” problem of linear SARSA
(

)
.

Q
-learning
(
Watkins and Dayan 1992
)
is one of the most celebrated RL algorithm.
When combined with linear function approximation,
the resulting algorithm, linear
Q
-learning,
is largely believed to suffer from possible instability for decades,
i.e.,
the iterates from linear
Q
-learning can possibly diverge to infinity.
This issue exercises the deadly triad in RL
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
Zhang 2022
)
.
Numerous efforts have been spent in prior works to tweak the linear
Q
-learning algorithm to achieve stability
(
Melo et al. 2008
;
Lee and He 2020
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Carvalho et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Gopalan and Thoppe 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Han-Dong and Donghwan 2024
;
Meyn 2024
;
Fan et al. 2020
;
Xu and Gu 2020
;
Cai et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
.
See the related work section and Table 1 of
Liu et al. 2025b
for a more detailed survey.

However, recent breakthroughs
(
Meyn 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
)
confirm that linear
Q
-learning is guaranteed to be stable when paired with an
\epsilon
-softmax behavior policy with an adaptive temperature,
without any algorithm tweaks or strong assumptions.
This work builds upon and improve over
(
Meyn 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
)
.
We now present our new convergent results for linear
Q
-learning.

Problem Setup.

We consider an infinite horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP,
Bellman 1957
;
Puterman 2014
) with a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
, a finite action space
\mathcal{A}
, a reward function
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, a transition function
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, an initial distribution
p_{0}:\mathcal{S}\times[0,1]
,
and a discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
.
At time
0
,
an initial state
S_{0}
is sampled from
p_{0}
.
At time
t
,
an agent at a state
S_{t}

7
Conclusion

We extend the classical Robbins-Siegmund theorem to handle the critical regime where the zero-order term has divergent summation but is square summable,
a setting that arises naturally in many modern stochastic approximation and RL algorithms. Our extended framework provides precise almost sure convergence rates,
high-probability concentration bounds,
and
L^{p}
convergence rates,
enabling fine-grained analysis of algorithms previously beyond theoretical reach.

As a key application, we establish the first complete convergence characterization of linear
Q
-learning, including almost sure convergence rates, maximal concentration bound with exponential tails, and
L^{p}
convergence rates,
demonstrating that linear
Q
-learning converges with explicit, quantifiable rates despite that it was deemed instable for decades.

Our framework opens several promising directions: extending the analysis to nonlinear function approximation, handling non-Markovian noise models, and developing adaptive algorithms that exploit the precise convergence characterization to optimize learning rates. More broadly, our results suggest that many algorithms previously thought to be theoretically intractable may yield to careful analysis using our extended framework.
