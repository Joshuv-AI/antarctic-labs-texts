An hourly climate projection and renewable energy generation dataset for power system modeling in China
Authors: Ruijie Chen, Benjamin F Hobbs, Zongxiang Lu, Yury Dvorkin, Ying Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs renewable energy deployment expands, power systems are increasingly sensitive to climate variability. Global Climate Models (GCMs) are commonly used to assess climate change impacts on future power systems. However, GCM outputs are typically only available at daily or coarser temporal resolutions, insufficient for the hourly granularity required by power system models, as both demand and renewable output show strong diurnal variations affecting system operation and planning. To address this, we present an hourly climate projection and renewable energy generation dataset for China, developed using an analog-based temporal downscaling method at a 0.5° spatial resolution. Our dataset ensures physically consistent meteorological variables and coherent daily statistics across multiple GCMs and socioeconomic pathways (SSPs), providing physically credible representations of plausible future climates rather than precise forecasts. Covering 2021–2060, it includes projections from five GCMs under four SSPs. The meteorology and renewable power dataset support studies on renewable energy potential, power system reliability, and energy transition pathways under future climate conditions, bridging the gap between climate projections and energy system modeling.Subject terms: Solar energy, Wind energy, Energy modelling

Background & Summary

Electric power systems are rapidly evolving to incorporate increasing shares of renewable energy to achieve climate mitigation goals1. With the growing dominance of variable renewable energy sources such as wind and solar photovoltaics, electricity supply is becoming more dependent on meteorological conditions2,3. Simultaneously, electricity demand from air conditioning and electric heating is susceptible to weather, especially as electrification in end-use sectors increases. As a result, the future balance between electricity supply and demand will be increasingly influenced by weather conditions. This requires power system planners to account for climate change impacts to ensure reliable and cost-effective transition pathways.

Recently, power system planners have increasingly relied on meteorological datasets to generate multi-decadal time series of renewable energy supply and electricity demand, enabling analyses using power system planning and policy models over multi-year and even multi-decadal time horizons4. For example, Chen et al. simulated China’s electricity transition pathways using wind and solar PV generation simulated from NASA reanalysis data5. Perera et al. assessed the effects of climate change and extreme events on energy systems using renewable generation and demand time series derived from 13 climate scenarios, combining various global circulation models and representative concentration pathways (RCPs)6. Wenz et al. explored future European electricity consumption using daily temperature data from the WATCH forcing dataset to represent warming trends7. Thus, linking long-term meteorological data with power system models has become a widely used approach to assess and manage climate risks in planning.

Historical datasets, including reanalysis products, are commonly used to simulate coincident renewable generation and electricity demand. However, they cannot capture key aspects of long-run climate uncertainties, such as scenarios, model, and internal variability8. These uncertainties have important implications: electricity demand depends on temperature, but future temperature increases are unclear9. Similarly, projected wind speed and solar radiation vary across models and scenarios, resulting in uncertain renewable resource potentials10,11. As a consequence, the adequacy of a given resource plan to meet load will differ under alternative climate projections, raising concerns about the reliability of systems designed using only historical data12. Thus, there is a pressing need for climate projection datasets that support future-oriented energy system modeling.

Ensembles of global climate models (GCMs) and emission scenarios have become the standard approach to represent the plausible range of future climate trajectories. Rather than predicting specific and precise weather realizations, these ensembles aim to capture the spread and central tendency of physically consistent climate evolutions, which enhances credibility when assessing long-term climate change impacts. Such ensemble-based approaches have been widely adopted across fields such as water resource management13, climate extremes14, and energy studies15. Recently, the energy–meteorology community has outlined several essential criteria for climate projection datasets to support power system simulations under climate change4,16,17:

-Hourly resolution, to capture short-term variability in renewable output and demand, matching the temporal resolution of power system models.

Although many meteorological datasets exist, few meet all of these criteria. Reanalysis datasets such as ERA5 and MERRA-2 are widely used to create multi-year renewable generation and demand time series to guide energy transition planning18,19. For example, Zhu et al. used ERA5 data to simulate wind and solar PV generation over multiple decades to study China’s energy transition20, while Wang et al. employed MERRA-2 for similar purposes21. While these datasets provide high temporal resolution and internal consistency, they lack future climate information. In contrast, climate projection ensembles—such as those from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report (IPCC6)—offer insights into future electricity demand and resource potential. For instance, Sundar et al. identified robust decarbonization pathways for the western United States using daily simulations from 100 ensemble members of the LENS2 dataset22. However, their daily temporal resolution prevents them from being applied in hourly power system simulations23.

To overcome this limitation, downscaling methods are widely used to produce high-resolution climate projections24. These techniques include: (1) dynamical downscaling, which applies regional climate models (RCMs) to simulate fine-scale weather patterns, and (2) statistical downscaling, which uses historical data to establish empirical relationships between large- and small-scale climate variables25,26. Dynamical approaches are resource-intensive and require modeling expertise, whereas statistical methods are more efficient and accessible.

Various statistical techniques are available for hourly downscaling, including interpolation, delta mapping, analog methods, and machine learning27. Interpolation uses historical daily temperature shapes to interpolate between projected maximum and minimum temperatures, but cannot be applied across variables28. Delta mapping modifies historical hourly time series using statistical change factors between baseline and projected periods, but may break inter-variable coherence29,30. Analog methods disaggregate daily projections by matching them with similar historical days, requiring long-term reference datasets31. This approach preserves daily statistics and cross-variable correlations inherited from reference datasets, thereby providing physically consistent representations of climate projections for power system modeling. Machine learning approaches, which learn cross-scale relationships from data, show promise but demand technical expertise and computational resources32. Some datasets already employ these methods, including the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s sup3rcc dataset (machine learning-based) and regional projections for Northwestern Europe (delta-based)29,32.

Here, we present a new dataset of hourly climate projections and variable renewable energy (VRE, referred to as wind and solar PV in this work) generation time series for mainland China. The dataset consists of two components: climate projections and renewable generation time series. Hourly meteorological and generation projections from 2021 to 2060 are derived from the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project (ISIMIP) using a modified analog approach, covering five GCMs under four IPCC6 scenarios: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. The resulting dataset reflects a plausible range of future climate evolutions consistent with CMIP6 projections, and the generated hourly series maintains physical realism across variables and timescales. VRE generation time series are produced using both the WFDE5 reanalysis dataset (1979–2019) and the downscaled projections (2021–2060). These data are not intended as precise forecasts but as physically credible realizations of possible climate trajectories, enabling consistent evaluation of climate impacts on energy systems. The dataset can be used for wind and solar resource assessment, power supply adequacy and reliability analysis, power system planning modeling, and designing China’s electricity system decarbonization pathway. By providing hourly resolution under physically consistent multi-model ensembles, this work bridges the gap between climate projections and power system modeling, supporting robust analyses of system reliability, flexibility, and planning under climate uncertainty.

Input data

Historical hourly and projected daily climate variables (listed in Table 1) serve as inputs for the downscaling process. Historical data are sourced from the WFDE5 reanalysis dataset (WATCH Forcing Data methodology applied to ERA5), which provides bias-adjusted hourly meteorological variables from 1979 to 2019 at 0.5° spatial resolution33. The WFDE5 dataset is distributed by the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) through its Climate Data Store (CDS) as monthly files in NetCDF format and can be downloaded at 10.24381/cds.20d54e34 (C3S, 2020b). Future climate projections (2021–2060) are obtained from the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project (ISIMIP), which provides daily data from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) simulations. We use ISIMIP projections from 2021 to 2060 from five global climate models: GFDL-ESM4, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, and UKESM1-0-LL. These are evaluated under four shared socioeconomic pathway (SSP) scenarios: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. These SSPs represent different global development trajectories, defined in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (2021), indicating “Taking the Green Road”, “Middle of the Road”, “A Rocky Road”, and “Taking the Highway”, respectively34. All datasets are available at a 0.5° spatial resolution. The ISIMIP climate projection dataset is provided in NetCDF format and can be directly accessed in the ISIMIP Repository at https://data.isimip.org/.

The ISIMIP datasets are bias-corrected using a reanalysis dataset composed of WFDE5 over land and ERA5 over the ocean. Xu et al. used Taylor diagrams to evaluate each meteorological variable and showed that the bias-adjusted outputs of the five ISIMIP3b GCMs agree well with observations across China, confirming their reliability in simulating regional climate change and assessing future conditions35. Furthermore, we extend this evaluation to the joint distribution of the five meteorological variables. We performed the multivariate two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test for each pair of meteorological variables between GCM output and WFDE5 at each spatial grid cell, and calculated the mean p-values across all spatial grid cells, shown in Fig. 1. All p-values are higher than 0.9, indicating strong consistencies of each pair of meteorological variables between the ISIMIP model’s output and WFDE5.

These confirm that the WFDE5 reanalysis dataset is consistent with GCM projections from the ISIMIP3b archive, providing a consistent hourly reference data for the downscaling36. Additional datasets supporting the renewable energy simulations include land cover, elevation, slope, and nature reserve data provided in the Supplementary Information of Wang et al.21; wind turbine parameters described in the Data and Methodology section of Wang et al.37; photovoltaic system parameters provided in the Supplementary Information of Zhu et al.20; and wind shear coefficients derived from the ERA5 dataset (10.24381/cds.adbb2d47)38.

To convert daily climate projections to hourly resolution, we employ a modified analog technique termed the extended analog method. This method identifies meteorologically similar days in the historical record to reconstruct sub-daily variability for future projections. For each spatial grid cell and target future day D, we consider the sequence of three consecutive future days (D-1, D, D + 1) and search for the most similar three-day sequence (d-1, d, d + 1) in the historical dataset based on daily values of multiple climate variables. Downscaling method

This section presents a modified analog method (termed the extended analog method) developed to downscale daily climate projections into hourly data. This method is executed for each spatial grid cell. The purpose of the extended analog method is to improve inter-day continuity of simulated VRE generations (defined as the difference between the 24th hour, previous day, and the 1st hour, current day), which is highly related to the power system ramping requirement. Let ξi,j,tH represent the value of climate variable i (e.g., wind speed, temperature) at spatial grid cell j and hour t from the WFDE5 historical dataset, which has a 1-hour temporal resolution. Similarly, let ξi,j,dP denote the daily climate projection value i at spatial grid cell j and day d.

The key modification of the extended analog method lies in its use of three-day sequences rather than single-day comparisons of the existing analog method31. The existing analog method follows the same 3 steps, but is applied independently to each day at Step 2 (Δ = 0), which can produce discontinuities between consecutive days. Technical Validation

This section validates the extended analog downscaling method by comparing its values to distributions of historical data and by calculating estimation error statistics. We use the hourly WFDE5 data from 1979 to 2006 as the reference to downscale the daily WFDE5 data from 2007 to 2019, and the results are then compared with the hourly WFDE5 data from 2007 to 2019. Subsequently, we use the hourly WFDE5 data from 1979 to 2019 to generate wind and solar PV generation data, and compare them with an open-access 1-year wind and PV generation dataset in 31 provinces of China61.

Meteorological variable validation

To evaluate the accuracy of the downscaling method, we compare hourly meteorological variables reconstructed from downscaled WFDE5 daily data (2007–2019) with the original hourly WFDE5 data. This validation aims to assess how well the temporal downscaling algorithm reproduces the observed sub-daily variability from daily inputs. For each meteorological variable and each grid cell, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between the downscaled time series and the observed time series over the period 2007–2019, and their average values across China are presented in Table 6. Most meteorological variables show high correlation (>0.97), with lower values for wind speed due to its greater intra-day variability. The extended analog method slightly outperforms the conventional analog method across all metrics31. To further ensure the spatial and temporal distributions of the Pearson correlation coefficients, two complementary validation procedures were conducted. We further examine the spatiotemporal distribution of Pearson correlation coefficients between the downscaled and reference meteorological variables during 2007–2019. Specifically, Pearson correlation coefficients between downscaled and reference time series are computed for each grid cell and shown in Fig. 3. This step evaluates the spatial consistency of reconstructed hourly patterns across China. For each hour during 2007–2019, correlation coefficients between the downscaled and the reference spatially distributed data across 3,848 grid cells are also calculated and averaged to the same hour-of-day in Fig. 4. This complementary hourly correlation analysis examines how accurately the downscaled fields reproduce the diurnal evolution of spatial patterns. Together, these two analyses provide a comprehensive validation of the downscaling method in both spatial and temporal dimensions.

To compare the joint distributions of each pair of climate variables between downscaled data and reference data, we further assess the consistency of joint distributions using the multivariate two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test62, applied to all pairs of climate variables. This non-parametric test evaluates whether two multivariate samples originate from the same underlying probability distribution by quantifying the maximum distance between their empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs). It does not assume any specific distributional form, making it well-suited for testing the statistical similarity of meteorological variables that exhibit non-Gaussian and correlated characteristics.

Since meteorological variables may vary periodically with the hour and season, this test is performed separately for each hour and month across all grid cells. Specifically, for each spatial grid cell, month, and each hour of the day, the paired samples were constructed by collecting grid-level time series of two climate variables (e.g., temperature–radiation, wind speed–temperature, etc.) from the downscaled dataset and from the reference WFDE5 data over the period 2007–2019. The multivariate two-sample K–S statistic and its corresponding p-value were then calculated for each pair, representing the degree of joint distributional similarity. The resulting statistics were averaged across months and hours.

Table 7 summarizes the average test statistics and p-values. All p-values remain close to 1, indicating strong distributional consistency. In other words, the null hypothesis, the downscaled and reference datasets follow the same joint distribution for each variable pair, cannot be rejected at any significance level, confirming that the downscaling preserves the physical consistencies among variables. These findings confirm the suitability of the dataset for simulating physically consistent renewable energy generation and temperature (highly related to load demand).

Overall, the extended analog method performs better than the analog method in terms of correlation coefficient, MAE, MAE of ramping rate, and MAE of inter-day continuity. The ramping rate is used to capture the variability dynamics and reflect the power system’s flexibility demand, defined as the hour-to-hour change in aggregated power output (rt = Pt-Pt-1, where Pt is the aggregated power output at hour t). We further evaluate its MAE between the downscaled and reference time series. The MAE of ramping rate therefore measures how well the downscaled data reproduce the short-term fluctuations and variability intensity observed in the original WFDE5-based power generation.

In addition, the inter-day continuity metric evaluates the smoothness of day-to-day transitions by comparing the generation value at the end of one day with that at the start of the next. The MAE of inter-day continuity between downscaled and reference datasets indicates whether the temporal downscaling introduces artificial discontinuities at daily boundaries.

Since we introduce multi-day information in the downscaling process, the inter-day continuity of the extended analog method performs significantly better. This improvement demonstrates that including adjacent-day analogs effectively reduces discontinuities and preserves realistic daily transitions in renewable generation profiles. This confirms that the key characteristics of renewable energy, including generation and ramping rate, are well preserved.

Uncertainty discussion

In this section, we review and quantify three major sources of uncertainty in our dataset. These include uncertainties in input data provided by the GCMs and their scenarios, the analog downscaling method, and the conversion from meteorological data to renewable generation.

Overall, our analysis reveals that: (1) input data uncertainty is primarily associated with the spread among GCMs and SSPs, leading to consistent warming trends but diverse changes in wind speed and solar radiation; (2) downscaling uncertainty arises from the choice of the day-of-year (DOY) time window, where excessively wide windows introduce noise and distort seasonal features, while an optimized DOY window minimizes the mean absolute error; and (3) renewable generation uncertainty mainly stems from the assumption of a constant wind shear exponent, which can significantly bias the diurnal wind power profiles. Quantitative comparisons demonstrate that adopting time-varying parameters effectively reduces bias and improves the physical realism of the derived dataset Figs. 8–11.

Downscaling process uncertainty: time window selection

We explored how the selection of the day-of-year (DOY) window affects downscaling performance, as shown in Fig. 14. Daily WFDE5 data is downscaled using both analog and extended analog methods across various DOY windows. We use the mean absolute error (MAE) to find the optimal DOY.

Sensitivity of MAE to DOY window.

Overall, both analog and extended analog downscaling approaches exhibit a tendency for the MAE to stabilize as the time window increases. This is because an excessively large time window introduces reference days that have seasonal characteristics different from the target day, which do not provide additional useful information for the downscaling process. At the same time, certain meteorological variables, such as solar radiation in the extended analog method, and temperature and wind speed in the analog method, display a V-shaped curve with increasing time window. This indicates that indiscriminately increasing the time window can introduce unnecessary information, thereby increasing the uncertainty of the downscaled results and reducing the quality of the downscaled dataset. Considering the impact of different time window parameters on the result uncertainty, we selected N = 12 (corresponding to the extremum of the V-shaped curve for radiation) for downscaling the meteorological forecast dataset.

Renewable energy generation uncertainty: wind shear coefficient

When calculating wind turbine power output, it is necessary to convert near-surface wind speeds to hub-height wind speeds. This conversion is typically based on the wind shear exponent. Previous studies have shown that the wind shear exponent exhibits distinct diurnal and seasonal variations64. Therefore, using a single, time-invariant wind shear exponent, such as the commonly adopted value of 1/7 or the spatially varying but temporally static maps provided by the Global Wind Atlas65, may introduce biases in the estimated wind power generation. To quantify the uncertainty in wind power output arising from the variability of the wind shear exponent, we designed four scenarios, as illustrated below:

Scenario 1 (time-varying)

For each spatial grid cell, different wind shear exponents are applied for each month and each hour of the day. These exponents are derived from ERA5 data by calculating the wind shear between 10 m and 100 m wind speeds during 2015–2019, averaged over all years for the same month and hour of the day.

Scenario 2 (hour-invariant)

For each spatial grid cell, a distinct wind shear exponent is used for each month, without considering diurnal variations. The exponents are obtained by averaging the monthly wind shear coefficients between 10 m and 100 m wind speeds from ERA5 during 2015–2019.

Scenario 3 (month-invariant)

For each spatial grid cell, a distinct wind shear exponent is applied for each hour of the day, ignoring monthly variations. The exponents are calculated from ERA5 data using the 10 m and 100 m wind speeds for the same hour of the day during 2015–2019.

Scenario 4 (time-invariant)

For each spatial grid cell, a constant wind shear exponent is used, calculated as the mean of the wind shear values between 10 m and 100 m wind speeds from ERA5 for 2015–2019.

Using 2019 as an example, 100 m wind speeds were derived under each scenario, and corresponding wind power outputs were calculated. The results were then averaged to obtain both monthly and hourly mean power generation curves, as shown in Figs. 15, 16. Significant differences can be observed in the diurnal patterns of power output. Because the wind shear exponent typically exhibits lower values during the daytime and higher values at night, applying a fixed diurnal wind shear exponent tends to preserve the strong “high-day–low-night” pattern of the 10 m wind speed at 100 m height, leading to a distorted diurnal profile of wind power generation. In contrast, the seasonal variation in wind speed, characterized by higher values in spring and autumn and lower values in summer and winter, and combined with the relatively small inter-month variation in wind shear exponent, results in only minor differences among the monthly mean wind power outputs across the four scenarios. We therefore recommend not using a time-invariant wind shear exponent when estimating wind power generation from near-surface wind speeds, as doing so can substantially distort the simulated diurnal power generation patterns Figs. 14–16.
