Ayahuasca drinking using a two-bottle choice procedure in male mice
Authors: Natali D Kisaki, Yasmim A Serra, Isa R S Rodrigues, Kallyane S O Silva, Caio Jovita-Farias, Gérson L Alves, Marcus T M Bezerra, Isabelle Gaburro-Ribeiro, João P C Leite, Nailton M S Jesus, Alexandre J Oliveira-Lima, Lais F Berro, Eduardo A V Marinho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAyahuasca has been proposed as a treatment for substance use disorders. However, because of its hallucinogenic properties, studies investigating its abuse potential are needed. The aim of the present study was to investigate voluntary ayahuasca drinking in male mice using a two-bottle choice procedure. Male mice were exposed to two bottles, one of water and one of ayahuasca (0.01, 0.03 or 0.1 mg/ml), for 15 h/day, under 3 protocols (acquisition): (1) every other day access; (2) access every 3 days; (3) access every 5 days. Animals were then submitted to a 14-day drug-free period, followed by 3 re-exposure phases (same conditions as during acquisition), with 7 drug-free days between each. Regardless of the ayahuasca concentration, animals showed a preference for ayahuasca over water when exposed to ayahuasca every other day during the acquisition and re-exposure phases. Extending the period between ayahuasca exposures changed the expression of ayahuasca preference, with the longest break (every 5 days) being associated with preference for water over ayahuasca (i.e., ayahuasca aversion), an effect that was more predominantly observed at higher ayahuasca concentrations. A significant interaction was observed between frequency of exposure to ayahuasca and ayahuasca concentration for total ayahuasca intake during the later re-exposure phase. Our findings show that both the frequency of exposure and the ayahuasca concentration are critical when determining ayahuasca preference in a two-bottle choice model in mice, which can help guide therapeutic/ritualistic ayahuasca use.Keywords: Ayahuasca, Two-bottle choice, Preference, Drinking, MiceSubject terms: Preclinical research, Experimental models of disease

Introduction

Psychedelics have gained increased attention in recent years as emerging research continues to show their therapeutic application for the treatment of mental disorders (for review see1. In this regard, accumulating evidence suggests that ayahuasca may have therapeutic potential for the treatment of substance use disorders (SUD)2. Ayahuasca is a hallucinogenic brew prepared from the decoction of the plants Banisteriopsis caapi, which contains β-carbolines, and Psychotria viridis, which contains N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT)3,4. Pre-clinical studies from our laboratory have shown that ayahuasca blocks the abuse-related behavioral effects of alcohol and stimulants in mice3–6. These findings are corroborated by human studies showing a decreased prevalence of substance use disorders among ritualistic ayahuasca users2,7.

While the clinical evidence is encouraging, psychedelic research progress has been hindered by concerns over the abuse potential of these drugs. The perception that psychedelics may lead to abuse and induce dependence contributed to their strict scheduling and regulated use, including in research8. However, decades of research on psychedelic abuse liability have consistently shown that the abuse potential of these drugs is lower compared to other drugs of abuse, including in pre-clinical models (for review see9. A further understanding of the potential for abuse of psychedelics is particularly important with the renewed interest in these drugs for the treatment of SUD.

We have shown previously that mice develop conditioned place preference to ayahuasca4,5, suggesting that this substance may have rewarding effects in rodents. The emergence of ayahuasca-induced conditioned place preference was dose-dependent, as only an intermediate dose, but not a low or a high doses, induced this effect4. Importantly, the conditioned place preference model involves repeated non-contingent administration of a drug within a short period of time, which might not closely mimic ayahuasca use patterns in real world scenarios. In fact, Fábregas and colleagues10 have shown that ritualistic ayahuasca use, with a mean frequency of ritual attendance of six times/month, was not associated with the common deleterious psychosocial effects observed for other drugs of abuse. Therefore, it becomes important to investigate the abuse potential of ayahuasca using other pre-clinical models, with a particular focus on whether different patterns of use may contribute to its abuse-related behavioral effects.

The aim of the present study was to investigate voluntary ayahuasca drinking in male mice using a two-bottle choice procedure. Separate groups of mice were exposed to different concentrations of ayahuasca under 3 protocols that varied in the frequency of exposure to ayahuasca (every other day, every 3 days or every 5 days). With these studies, we aimed to elucidate whether ayahuasca concentration and/or frequency of exposure can contribute to the development and expression of ayahuasca preference using a two-bottle choice procedure in male mice.

Results

Data for the mean intake of ayahuasca and water solutions across the days of the experimental protocol (including acquisition and re-exposure phases) are reported in the Supplementary Material (Tables 1 and 2, respectively).

Ayahuasca preference

Initially, ayahuasca preference data were averaged across all days of each phase of the two-bottle choice protocol (acquisition vs re-exposure), and analyzed by ayahuasca concentration. A mixed-measures ANOVA of the preference (%) for the ayahuasca bottle with frequency of exposure (every other day, every 3 days or every 5 days) as between-subject factor and phase (acquisition vs re-exposure) as within-subject factor showed a significant effect of frequency of exposure for all concentrations: 0.01 mg/ml [F(2,127) = 10.28; p < 0.0001]; 0.03 mg/ml [F(2,142) = 8.398; p < 0.001]; 0.1 mg/ml [F(2,210) = 16.66; p < 0.0001]. For the concentrations of 0.03 and 0.1 mg/ml, ANOVA also showed a significant interaction between frequency of exposure and phase: 0.03 mg/ml [F (2,84) = 7.439; p < 0.01]; 0.1 mg/ml [F(2,210) = 3.278; p < 0.05].

For the 0.01 mg/ml ayahuasca concentration (Fig. 1A), exposure to ayahuasca every other day led to a preference for the ayahuasca bottle (> 50%) both during the acquisition (65.33 ± 2.88) and the re-exposure (64.65 ± 3.07) phases. For the other protocols, animals showed either a slight preference for ayahuasca (ayahuasca exposure every 3 days: acquisition 57.11 ± 3.01, re-exposure 54.44 ± 2.25; ayahuasca exposure every 5 days: acquisition 53.78 ± 3.16) or preference for the water bottle (< 50%) (ayahuasca exposure every 5 days: re-exposure 45.01 ± 3.87). Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons tests showed that animals exposed to ayahuasca every 5 days showed a significantly lower preference for ayahuasca compared to animals exposed to ayahuasca every other day, both during the acquisition phase (p < 0.05) and the re-exposure phase (p < 0.001). No significant within-subject differences were observed between the different phases (p > 0.05).

Ayahuasca intake

In addition to evaluating ayahuasca preference, we also evaluated ayahuasca intake (g/kg) during the two-bottle choice protocol. Ayahuasca intake data were also averaged across all days of each phase of the procedure (acquisition vs re-exposure), and analyzed by ayahuasca concentration. A mixed-measures ANOVA of the ayahuasca intake data with frequency of exposure (every other day, every 3 days or every 5 days) as between-subject factor and phase (acquisition vs re-exposure) as within-subject factor showed a significant interaction effect between frequency of exposure and phase for all concentrations: 0.01 mg/ml [F(2,77) = 7.323; p < 0.01]; 0.03 mg/ml [F(2,84) = 5.588; p < 0.01]; 0.1 mg/ml [F(2,78) = 16.42; p < 0.0001].

However, this same group (ayahuasca exposure every 5 days) showed a decreased ayahuasca intake during the re-exposure phase compared to itself during the acquisition phase (p < 0.01).

Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to elucidate whether ayahuasca concentration and/or frequency of exposure can contribute to the development and expression of ayahuasca preference using a two-bottle choice procedure in male mice. Our findings show that both frequency of exposure and concentration influenced the expression of preference for an ayahuasca solution compared to water. In general, animals exposed to ayahuasca more frequently (every other day) showed a preference for ayahuasca regardless of the concentration of ayahuasca in the bottle; however, this effect was more pronounced at lower concentrations. On the other hand, animals who had a longer break in between ayahuasca exposures (every 5 days) generally showed indifference (initial acquisition) or, most predominantly, lack of preference/aversion (late acquisition and re-exposure) for ayahuasca regardless of ayahuasca concentration, an effect that was associated with greater ayahuasca drinking compared to other groups. However, a switch in intake pattern is observed towards late acquisition and at re-exposures, during which a decrease in ayahuasca intake is accompanied by the emergence of ayahuasca aversion. Together, these findings suggest that the frequency of exposure to ayahuasca had a major effect on whether animals exhibited preference or aversion for ayahuasca.

In addition to the frequency of exposure, the concentration of ayahuasca in the bottle also contributed to the emergence of preference vs aversion. At the lowest concentration tested (0.01 mg/ml), animals exposed to ayahuasca every other day or every 3 days showed preference for ayahuasca in the averaged data, as well as at most time-points during acquisition and re-exposure. On the other hand, the same frequency of exposure engendered a mixture of preference, indifference or aversion at higher concentrations (depending on the study time-point), with the most pronounced difference observed for 0.03 mg/ml ayahuasca. Of note, ayahuasca is well known for having a bitter and sour taste11. In drinking models for other drugs, such as alcohol, it is common to use the addition of bitter solutions (e.g. quinine), to decrease alcohol intake and/or evaluate motivation for alcohol drinking12. Therefore, some of our findings showing ayahuasca aversion could be a result of ayahuasca’s aversive taste.

Ayahuasca aversion could also be a pharmacological consequence of intake of greater doses of this compound. The effects of ayahuasca seem to be mediated primarily by DMT’s action as a serotonin 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C receptor agonist4,6. We have proposed that the rewarding and aversive effects of ayahuasca are determined by serotonin receptor occupancy depending on the ayahuasca dose, with intermediate doses that primarily target 5-HT2A receptors having rewarding effects, while higher doses that also target 5-HT2C receptors exerting aversive effects 4. In fact, this pharmacological difference may have contributed to the ayahuasca aversion observed during the acquisition phase in animals exposed to ayahuasca every 5 days, as that effect was accompanied by a greater ayahuasca intake compared to animals that had access to ayahuasca every other day. However, lack of preference for ayahuasca, or aversion, was also observed during the re-exposure phase for animals that had access every 5 days, and ayahuasca intake was significantly lower during that phase, including in comparison to animals exposed to ayahuasca every other day (0.01 mg/ml concentration). These findings further indicate that total ayahuasca intake alone cannot completely explain our findings. Instead, we propose that ayahuasca intake and frequency of exposure to ayahuasca both influenced the emergence of ayahuasca preference and aversion. In fact, based on our statistical analyses, we observed significant effects of both frequency of exposure and ayahuasca concentration on ayahuasca preference and intake during late acquisition and re-exposure, with a significant interaction between these two factors observed for ayahuasca intake during the last re-exposure phase (R3).

Discussing the frequency of ayahuasca exposure is an extremely important topic when determining the patterns of use of ayahuasca that would retain therapeutic effectiveness while minimizing its potential for abuse. Of note, ritualistic ayahuasca use is normally regulated by religions or retreat organizations, and it is uncommon for participants to use ayahuasca more than twice a week10, with religious users usually limiting intake to twice a month13. Despite the low frequency of use, studies have shown that ritualistic use of ayahuasca under controlled conditions (retreats or religious use) can improve overall subjective well-being, empathy and creative thinking14, affect and cognition15, mindfulness-related capacities16, and symptoms of anxiety and depression17, in addition to decreasing SUD prevalence7. Therapeutic effects can be observed even after a single ayahuasca administration14. However, studies suggest that regular ayahuasca use is associated with better psychosocial and mental health outcomes and less use of other drugs compared to non-regular ayahuasca use18. Therefore, while the present findings suggest that a low frequency of ayahuasca use may decrease potential for abuse, future studies are needed to identify patterns of use that also maintain therapeutic efficacy while minimizing the frequency of exposure to this drug. Also, with an increase in the recreational use of ayahuasca worldwide in recent years, our findings showing that frequent (every other day) use of this drug may be associated with the emergence of abuse liability also emphasize the need for a regulated use.

In summary, our study shows that male mice may show preference for ayahuasca over water in a two-bottle choice procedure; however, this effect is dependent on the frequency of exposure to ayahuasca. This substance has been used for centuries by traditional indigenous communities in Latin and South America, and the mounting evidence suggests that it may have important therapeutic effects for a variety of mental disorders. Our findings suggest that the current pattern of ayahuasca use in ritualistic contexts, in which the frequency of use is relatively low, should provide a safe approach for ayahuasca therapy. Of note, a limitation of the present study was that, because we controlled for the number of ayahuasca exposures (i.e., all groups were exposed to the same number of sessions of ayahuasca drinking during acquisition and re-exposure), temporal differences between protocols could have contributed to our results (i.e., some animals were in study longer due to longer intervals between ayahuasca exposures). Also, the present study reports findings in males only, and identifying potential sex differences in ayahuasca intake is needed in future investigations. As the interest in the therapeutic use of ayahuasca grows, future studies investigating different ayahuasca use protocols in clinical settings will be extremely important to determine ayahuasca use safety and minimize its potential for misuse and abuse, particularly in the context of SUD treatment.

Methods

Animals

Ninety 3-month-old Swiss male mice from our own colony were used in this study. Animals weighing 35–40 g were group-housed (10 per cage) for most of the study, except during drinking sessions, during which animals were single-housed for 15 h every other day (see description below and Fig. 7 for experimental design). When in groups, animals were housed in polypropylene cages (32 × 42 × 18 cm) under controlled temperature (22–23 °C) and light (12 h light, 12 h dark; lights on at 06h30) conditions. Food and water were available ad libitum throughout the experiments. Animals were maintained in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th Edition, revised 2011) and the Brazilian Law for Procedures for Animal Scientific Use (#11,794/2008), and the authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of UESC approved the experimental procedures.

Timeline for the experiments investigating voluntary ayahuasca drinking in male mice using a two-bottle choice procedure. Male mice were exposed to two bottles, one of water and one of ayahuasca (0.01, 0.03 or 0.1 mg/ml), for 15 h/day, under 3 protocols (acquisition): (1) every other day (EOD) access; (2) access every 3 days (E3D); (3) access every 5 days (E5D). Animals were then submitted to a 14-day drug-free period, followed by 3 re-exposure phases (R1, R2 and R3, same conditions as during acquisition), with 7 drug-free days between each re-exposure.

Ayahuasca

A batch of ayahuasca was obtained from a member of the Santo Daime Church in Brazil. The batch was lyophilized and quantified to obtain concentrations of its main constituents, as previously described: DMT: 0.4 mg/100 mg; tetrahydroharmine; 3.07 mg/100 mg; harmine: 3.85 mg/100 mg; harmaline: 0.17 mg/100 mg3. After lyophilized, ayahuasca was dissolved in water to the concentrations used in the study (0.01, 0.03 or 0.1 mg/ml).

Two-bottle choice ayahuasca drinking and experimental design

Mice were given the opportunity to drink ayahuasca using an intermittent access, two-bottle choice procedure, adapted from previous studies with alcohol in our laboratory6. A timeline for the experimental design for these studies is illustrated in Fig. 7. The protocol was divided into 2 phases: acquisition and re-exposure. During both phases, when animals were not in the individual drinking cages, animals remained group-housed in their home cages. Food was available ad libitum during all sessions.

Acquisition

During acquisition sessions, animals were housed individually in polypropylene boxes (30 × 19 × 13 cm) for 15 h (17h00-08h00) and given access to two bottles, one containing 15 mL of water and the other containing 15 mL of ayahuasca solution at one of three concentrations: 0.01, 0.03 or 0.1 mg/ml. Therefore, the majority of the 15-h ayahuasca access took place during the animals’ dark (active) phase. In addition to different concentrations of ayahuasca, animals were exposed to ayahuasca following 3 distinct schedules: access every other day, access every 3 days or access every 5 days. Therefore, 9 different groups were formed (N = 8–10/group): access every other day at the 3 concentrations (3 groups), access every 3 days at the 3 concentrations (3 groups) and access every 5 days at the 3 concentrations (3 groups). Although animals were exposed to ayahuasca at different frequencies, the number of ayahuasca exposures was the same across groups, with all animals being submitted to 15 acquisition sessions. Therefore, for animals exposed to ayahuasca every other day, the acquisition phase lasted 29 days; for animals exposed every 3 days, the acquisition phase lasted 43 days; and for animals exposed every 5 days, the acquisition phase lasted 71 days.

Re-exposures

Immediately after the last day of acquisition, animals were submitted to a 14-day drug-free period, after which all animals had 3 sessions of 15 h ayahuasca access (re-exposure) under the same conditions of acquisition (same ayahuasca concentration and exposure frequency). The last re-exposure session was followed by a 7-day drug-free period, after which another 3 re-exposure sessions were conducted under the same conditions as the first. Finally, animals were submitted to one final 7-day drug-free period and 3 re-exposure sessions.

Consumption from each bottle (water or ayahuasca) was measured at the end of each session (acquisition or re-exposure). Bottles were refilled with fresh water or fresh ayahuasca solution before each session. Bottle sides were switched at every session. Each bottle of water or ayahuasca was weighed on a precision scale before and after the session to determine the consumption of water/ayahuasca in g. Animals were also weighed daily. To ensure data were not affected by liquid loss due to bottle leaks or evaporation, two bottles were left in an empty cage for one week during each session, during which time liquid loss was measured and found to be less than 0.1 mL/day. After obtaining the final weight of each bottle, ayahuasca intake in g/kg was calculated for each session using the following formula:

Ayahuasca intake in mg/kg = [Amount of ayahuasca consumed (g) x ayahuasca concentration in the solution (mg/ml)] / [animal’s weight (g)] × 1000.

To calculate each animal’s preference for the ayahuasca bottle vs the water bottle, the following formula was used:

Preference = [Amount of ayahuasca consumed (g) / total consumption (water + ayahuasca solution) (g)] × 100.

Statistical analysis

All variables were checked for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). Data for the acquisition phase were averaged across the entire acquisition period, and data for the re-exposure phase were also averaged across all re-exposure sessions. Statistical analyses were performed using repeated measures (RM) analysis of variance (ANOVA), and sphericity was assumed for all our tests. Data were analyzed separately for each ayahuasca concentration. For multiple comparison analyses, frequency of exposure (every other day, every 3 days or every 5 days) was categorized as between-subjects factor, and phase (acquisition vs re-exposure) was defined as within-subjects factor. Additional two-way ANOVAs were conducted including frequency of exposure and ayahuasca concentration (0.01, 0.03 or 0.1 mg/ml) as between-subjects factors. Multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni tests. All analyses and graphic representations were performed using GraphPad Prism (v. 10.0.2). A probability of p < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.
