Rice LAI estimation using UAV-based multi-parameter fusion at the booting stage
Authors: Fulang Cen, Shengxi Chen, Lang Liu, Song Zhang, Lei Tu, Jianquan Qin, Chang Zheng, Zhenran Gao, Quanzhi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe leaf area index (LAI) is a key parameter for characterizing crop growth and water use efficiency. Therefore, efficient and accurate monitoring of LAI is essential for precision rice management. To overcome the limitations of traditional LAI measurement methods, which are time consuming, labor intensive, and difficult to scale, this study proposes an inversion framework that integrates multi-source UAV remote sensing features with machine learning models. The framework incorporates color indices (CIs) derived from RGB imagery, vegetation indices (VIs) derived from multispectral data, texture features (TIs), and texture feature indices (TFIs), and employs six machine learning algorithms to develop optimized LAI estimation models for the rice booting stage. The results indicate that at a flight altitude of 30 m, the CNN model integrating CIs and TIs achieved an accuracy of R2 = 0.815. At 60 m, the RF model combining VIs and TFIs showed superior performance, with an R2 of 0.866. Further integration of CIs, VIs, and TFIs at 30 m produced the best results, increasing R2 to 0.901, reducing RMSE to 0.273, and raising RPD to above 3.0. These findings demonstrate that TFIs significantly enhance the spectral-spatial representation capability of multispectral data, thereby improving model accuracy. The combined use of CIs and VIs across different sensors compensates for the inherent limitations between spectral and spatial information, while the integration of multi-resolution TIs and TFIs effectively overcomes the constraints of single-source data. Overall, the proposed approach provides a robust and efficient solution for high-precision LAI estimation during critical growth stages of rice, offering strong support for precision agricultural management.Keywords: Rice, LAI, UAV, RGB, Multispectral, Machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Leaf area index (LAI) is a fundamental biophysical parameter that reflects crop canopy structure, photosynthetic capacity, and water use efficiency, serving as a critical link between plant physiological processes and remote sensing observations1,2. Accurate estimation of LAI is therefore essential for crop growth monitoring, yield prediction, and precision agricultural management. However, traditional LAI measurement methods, which rely on destructive sampling or manual instruments, are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale to large agricultural areas. As a result, there is a growing demand for efficient, non-destructive, and high-throughput LAI estimation approaches.

UAV-based monitoring of rice LAI typically employs sensors such as RGB cameras, multispectral and hyperspectral imagers, and LiDAR systems to collect image, spectral, and point cloud data. From these data sources, color indices, spectral indices, and texture indices can be extracted and used to construct LAI estimation models. For instance, several studies have estimated rice LAI using color indices3,4 and texture indices4 derived from UAV-based RGB imagery, while others have established relationships between LAI and multispectral vegetation indices (VIs), such as the modified triangular vegetation index (MTVI2), to achieve LAI estimation5.

However, despite these advances, each sensing modality exhibits inherent limitations that restrict estimation accuracy and robustness. UAV RGB imagery is highly sensitive to illumination conditions and solar incidence angles, and color-based vegetation indices are prone to saturation under dense canopy conditions, resulting in reduced sensitivity to LAI variation6,7. Similarly, models relying solely on spectral information are affected by spectral saturation and soil background interference. Although hyperspectral imaging provides detailed spectral resolution, its high cost and complex data processing limit its practical applicability8–10. LiDAR, while effective for structural characterization, lacks spectral sensitivity and cannot directly retrieve physiological parameters such as chlorophyll content.

To overcome these limitations, recent studies have explored the integration of multi-source information. In particular, texture features, which capture canopy spatial heterogeneity and structural patterns, have been shown to effectively complement spectral and color features. The combined use of RGB-derived color indices, multispectral vegetation indices, and texture features has been demonstrated to significantly improve LAI estimation accuracy11,12. In addition, factors such as UAV flight altitude, crop variety, and data fusion strategy have been reported to influence estimation performance13,14.

Nevertheless, several critical research gaps remain. First, although multi-source data fusion has shown promising results, the synergistic integration of spectral and texture information is still not fully understood, particularly in terms of how to construct effective texture feature indices (TFIs) that couple spectral and spatial characteristics. Second, the influence of UAV flight altitude on feature-LAI relationships and model performance has not been systematically quantified. Third, the comparative performance of different machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models under multi-parameter fusion scenarios remains insufficiently explored, especially under limited sample conditions.

Spectral and texture features are the core input variables for LAI inversion models. Existing approaches can be broadly classified into empirical and physical models. Empirical models typically establish regression relationships between LAI and vegetation indices or physiological parameters, while physical models simulate canopy-radiation interactions to estimate LAI. However, due to the strong nonlinearity of crop growth processes and the cross-scale attenuation effects from leaf to canopy level, traditional models often fail to adequately capture the complex relationships underlying LAI inversion15.

Machine learning (ML) methods have demonstrated strong capability in modeling nonlinear relationships and improving LAI estimation accuracy. Algorithms such as random forest (RF)16, support vector machines (SVM)17, partial least squares regression (PLSR), and backpropagation neural networks (BPNN)18 have been widely applied in this field. In particular, RF shows strong robustness due to its feature selection and regularization capabilities. Deep learning (DL) methods, including convolutional neural networks (CNN)19 and long short-term memory networks (LSTM)], further enhance feature extraction and nonlinear modeling capabilities. However, the performance of DL models is often constrained by data scale and input feature representation, especially in small-sample agricultural scenarios12,21, Wang & Cao et al., 2022). Therefore, developing multi-parameter-driven models that effectively integrate spectral and structural information remains a key challenge.

Based on the above considerations, this study aims to address the limitations of existing approaches by developing a UAV-based multi-parameter fusion framework for accurate LAI estimation during the rice booting stage. Specifically, RGB and multispectral imagery were acquired at two flight altitudes, and multiple feature types-including color indices (CIs), vegetation indices (VIs), texture features (TIs), and texture feature indices (TFIs)-were extracted. Furthermore, four machine learning algorithms and two deep learning models were systematically compared under both single-parameter and multi-parameter fusion scenarios.

The main contributions of this study are as follows: (1) the construction of a multi-parameter feature system integrating spectral and texture information, including the development of TFIs; (2) a systematic analysis of the effects of UAV flight altitude on LAI estimation performance; and (3) a comprehensive comparison of ML and DL models under multi-source feature fusion conditions, leading to the identification of an optimized LAI estimation model. These findings provide important theoretical and technical support for high-precision crop monitoring and precision agricultural management.

Materials and methods

Study area and experimental design

The experiment was conducted at the Rice Experimental Station of the College of Agriculture, Guizhou University, in Xingyi City, Guizhou Province, China, in 2023 (104°51′–104°58′E, 24°58′–25°42′N; Fig. 1). The study area lies at an elevation of approximately 1,200 m. The region has an annual sunshine duration of 1,436–1,648 h and a mean annual temperature of 17 °C, with summer temperatures typically ranging from 21 °C to 26 °C. The mean annual precipitation is 1,531.6 mm. These climatic conditions, characterized by abundant sunshine, favorable temperatures, and sufficient precipitation, are conducive to rice cultivation.

Geographical overview of the study area. (a) Guizhou Province, (b) Xingyi City, (c) Layout of experimental plots.

A total of 30 rice varieties were selected for the experiment. Seedlings were mechanically transplanted at a spacing of 17 cm × 30 cm. Nitrogen was applied at a total rate of 180 kg N ha−1, with a split ratio of 5:2:3 among basal, tillering, and panicle stages, respectively. Phosphorus and potassium were applied at rates of 96 kg P₂O₅ ha−1 (entirely as basal fertilizer) and 135 kg K₂O ha−1, respectively, with potassium applied at a 5:5 ratio between basal and panicle stages. Standard high-yield management practices were followed for irrigation and pest control. Two nitrogen treatments were established: conventional nitrogen application (180 kg N ha−1) and a no-nitrogen control (0 kg N ha−1), resulting in a total of 61 experimental plots.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

UAV data collection and preprocessing

A DJI M300 RTK unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), equipped with a Zenmuse H20T camera and an MS600 Pro multispectral sensor, was used for data acquisition (Fig. 2).

Radiometric calibration was performed using pre-flight images of a calibrated reflectance panel to derive band–specific linear coefficients, thereby converting raw digital numbers into surface reflectance. Subsequently, image mosaicking was performed using Yusense Map software to generate georeferenced orthomosaics. Based on these orthomosaics, plot boundaries were delineated using ground control points, and pixel values within each plot were averaged to obtain mean reflectance for multispectral bands, mean digital numbers for RGB channels, and mean texture metrics derived from gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) analysis. These plot–level averaged values were then used to calculate vegetation indices, color indices, and texture indices according to their respective formulas.

Ground data collection

Leaf area index (LAI) measurements were simultaneously obtained using a TRAC-III (Tracing Radiation and Architecture of Canopies) instrument. Prior to measurement, the instrument was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. During data acquisition, the probe was positioned beneath the rice canopy with the sensor maintained in a horizontal orientation. Measurements were conducted across each plot at a constant speed of 0.3 m s−1. Each plot was measured three times, and the mean of the three readings was used as the representative LAI value.

(2)Extraction of RGB Texture Features (TIs)

Extraction of RGB Texture Features (TIs)

Texture features were extracted using the gray–level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) method implemented in ENVI 5.6. A 3 × 3 moving window, 64 Gy levels, and a pixel offset of d = 1 were adopted to balance spatial detail and computational efficiency. Directional averaging over 0°, 45°, 90°, and 135° was applied to achieve rotation invariance. For each of the R, G, and B channels, eight texture metrics were calculated: mean (Mea), variance (Var), homogeneity (Hom), contrast (Con), dissimilarity (Dis), entropy (Ent), second moment (Sec), and correlation (Cor). For example, at a flight altitude of 30 m, the eight texture features derived from the B channel were denoted as 30 m-B-Mea, 30 m-B-Var, 30 m-B-Hom, 30 m-B-Con, 30 m-B-Dis, 30 m-B-Ent, 30 m-B-Sec, and 30 m-B-Cor, respectively.

The 14 texture feature indices (TFIs) were selected based on spectral band relevance and the sensitivity of texture features to canopy structure (Table 5). The near-infrared (NIR) and red-edge (RE1 and RE2) bands were prioritized because the NIR band is highly sensitive to vegetation biomass, whereas the red-edge bands are closely associated with chlorophyll content. The green (G) band was also included because of its sensitivity to leaf chlorophyll content. To ensure parsimony and robustness, only the mean texture feature (MEA) was employed, as it provides a stable and interpretable representation of canopy spatial heterogeneity. This selection strategy helps ensure that the constructed TFIs are both biophysically meaningful and computationally efficient for LAI estimation during the rice booting stage.

Modeling methods and accuracy validation

The overall technical framework of this study is illustrated in Fig. 3. To systematically evaluate the estimation performance of algorithms based on RGB and multispectral data integration, six color indices (CIs), six vegetation indices (VIs), two texture features (TIs), seven texture feature indices (TFIs), and their combinations were used as input features at flight altitudes of 30 m and 60 m. Six models were developed using both machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) approaches, including Random Forest (RF)38,Support Vector Machine (SVM)39, Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR)40, Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN)41, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) (LSTM)42, and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)43.

Technical workflow.

To mitigate overfitting caused by high-dimensional inputs and limited sample size, several strategies were adopted. First, correlation-based feature selection was applied to remove redundant variables and retain the most informative predictors. All input features were then standardized prior to model training to ensure comparable scales. Model robustness was evaluated using both calibration–validation splitting and five-fold cross-validation. Model parameters were optimized through iterative tuning.

In the RF model, the number of trees and the minimum number of samples per leaf were set to 500 and 5, respectively. In the SVM model, the penalty parameter was set to 3 using a radial basis function (RBF) kernel.

For the PLSR model, all input variables were standardized prior to modeling. The optimal number of latent variables was determined within the training set using five-fold cross-validation by minimizing the root mean square error of cross-validation (RMSECV). The number of latent variables ranged from 1 to 10, and the final model was established using the optimal value.

The BPNN model was implemented as a feedforward neural network with an input layer corresponding to the number of retained features, a hidden layer with 10 neurons, and a single output neuron for LAI prediction. The hidden layer used the ReLU activation function, while the output layer used a linear activation function. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer with a maximum of 200 epochs and early stopping based on validation performance.

The LSTM model was constructed using reshaped feature vectors as input sequences for regression. The network consisted of a sequence input layer, an LSTM layer with 50 hidden units, a fully connected layer, and a regression output layer. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 8, and a maximum of 200 epochs. Early stopping and dropout (rate = 0.5) were applied to improve generalization.

The CNN model consisted of two convolutional layers (32 and 64 filters, kernel size = 3, ReLU activation) with max pooling, followed by a fully connected layer (128 neurons, ReLU activation, dropout = 0.5) and a regression output layer. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001) with a batch size of 8 and early stopping, with L2 regularization applied. The model was trained on tabular feature vectors rather than raw images, improving suitability for small datasets.

Prior to modeling, the dataset was randomly shuffled, with 66% of the samples used for training (Cal) and 33% for validation (Val). Based on this partitioning, additional analyses were conducted on the best-performing models using RGB features, multispectral features, and their fusion. Specifically, a 4:1 data split and five-fold cross-validation were applied to further evaluate model stability and generalization. All models were developed and analyzed using MATLAB R2021a.

Model performance was evaluated using three statistical metrics: the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and residual predictive deviation (RPD). Their definitions are given in Eqs. (7)–(9).

In these equations, n denotes the number of samples in the training or validation set; xᵢ and x̄ denote the predicted values and their mean values, respectively; yᵢ and ȳ denote the observed values and their mean values, respectively; and SD denotes the standard deviation of the observed values. These metrics collectively assess model accuracy and predictive stability. Specifically, higher R2 and RPD values, together with lower RMSE, indicate improved model performance.

Correlation analysis between feature variables and LAI

Correlation analysis between RGB features and LAI

The correlations between color indices (CIs) and LAI at two flight altitudes are shown in Fig. 4. At 30 m, the CIs exhibited strong negative correlations with LAI, ranked in descending order of absolute correlation strength as follows: EXGR (-0.80) > EXB (-0.80) > RGBVI (-0.79) > EXG (-0.78) > NDI (-0.78) > (r-g-b)/(r + g) (-0.78). All correlation coefficients had absolute values of at least 0.78, indicating a strong and consistent relationship between CIs and LAI at this altitude. At 60 m, however, the correlations between CIs and LAI were notably weaker than those at 30 m. The strongest correlation was observed for NDI (-0.76), whereas the weakest was observed for EXG (-0.65). This reduction can be attributed to the decline in spatial resolution with increasing flight altitude, which affects the ability of color indices to accurately characterize canopy traits. At 30 m, higher–resolution imagery minimizes soil–canopy mixing within pixels, allowing color indices to more accurately reflect canopy characteristics. In contrast, at 60 m, lower spatial resolution increases pixel heterogeneity and amplifies soil background interference, thereby significantly weakening the relationship between color indices and LAI.

Correlation between RGB color indices and LAI.

As shown in Fig. 5, except for correlation (COR), the texture features derived from imagery acquired at 30 m exhibited higher correlation coefficients with LAI than those derived from imagery acquired at 60 m. Among all features, B-Mea showed the strongest correlation with LAI at both altitudes, with coefficients of -0.80 and -0.75 at 30 m and 60 m, respectively, followed by G-Mea, which showed correlations of -0.73 and -0.68, respectively. Both B-Mea and G-Mea were more strongly correlated with LAI than the other texture features, most of which had absolute correlation coefficients below 0.60. The stronger correlations of these features can be attributed to the strong absorption of blue and green light by chlorophyll in crop leaves, which makes the mean values of the blue (B) and green (G) bands highly sensitive to the physiological status of the canopy. Because LAI directly reflects leaf area density and canopy structure, B-Mea and G-Mea effectively capture variations in canopy structure and coverage. Therefore, among the 24 texture variables analyzed, B-Mea and G-Mea at both altitudes were selected as input variables for model construction.

Correlation analysis between multispectral features and LAI

Among the constructed multispectral vegetation indices, those with correlation coefficients greater than 0.84 with LAI were selected for further analysis, as shown in Fig. 6. For the same vegetation index, only minor differences in correlation with LAI were observed between the 30 m and 60 m flight altitudes, with the differences ranging from 0.01 to 0.02 and slightly higher correlation coefficients at 60 m. Across the six vegetation indices, strong correlations with LAI were observed at both flight altitudes, with r = 0.84–0.86 at 30 m and r = 0.85–0.87 at 60 m, indicating that these indices effectively characterize rice canopy status. The absence of substantial differences in correlation between the two altitudes may be attributed to the high canopy cover and uniform canopy structure of the rice fields. Although higher flight altitude reduces spatial resolution, the imagery still retained sufficient canopy information to maintain a stable relationship between the vegetation indices and LAI.

Correlation between multispectral vegetation indices and LAI.

The correlation analysis between the 48 multispectral texture features and LAI is illustrated in Fig. 7. The relationship between texture features and LAI was strongly affected by flight altitude. At 30 m, texture features such as R-ENT (r = -0.55), RE1-COR (r = -0.56), RE2-COR (r = -0.60), and NIR-COR (r = -0.51) exhibited relatively weak correlations with LAI (|r|≤ 0.60). However, at 60 m, the correlations became markedly stronger, with features such as B-DIS (r = -0.81), B-ENT (r = -0.81), R-MEA (r = -0.82), and R-ENT (r = -0.82) showing highly significant negative correlations (p < 0.01, |r|> 0.80). This difference may be attributed to the distinct ways in which different spatial resolutions capture canopy structural information. Although the high-resolution imagery at 30 m can resolve fine canopy details, it is also more susceptible to local noise, such as gaps between leaves and shadow effects, which may weaken the overall relationship between texture features and LAI. In contrast, the coarser-resolution imagery at 60 m may integrate canopy information more effectively through pixel mixing, thereby strengthening the correlation between texture features and LAI. Based on these findings, the four texture features most strongly correlated with LAI were selected as input variables for modeling at each altitude: R-ENT, RE1-COR, NIR-COR, and RE2-COR for 30 m, and B-DIS, B-ENT, R-MEA, and R-ENT for 60 m.

To overcome the limitations of single-band texture features in capturing both spectral and structural information for LAI estimation, 14 texture feature indices (TFIs), including NDTI, DTI, and RTI, were constructed using mean texture features (MEA) derived from multiple bands, including the near-infrared (NIR), green (G), and red-edge bands (RE1 and RE2). Seven indices with strong correlations with LAI were selected as input variables at both flight altitudes (Fig. 8). Among them, RTIMEA(N)-MEA(RE2), RTIMEA(N)-MEA(RE1), NDTIMEA(N)-MEA(RE2), NDTIMEA(N)-MEA(RE1), and RTIMEA(N)-MEA(G) were common to both altitudes, whereas RTIMEA(RE2)-MEA(G) and RTIMEA(RE1)-MEA(G) were specific to 30 m, and DTIMEA(N)-MEA(RE1) and RTIMEA(N)-MEA(G) were specific to 60 m. NDTIMEA(N)-MEA(RE2) exhibited the highest correlation coefficient at both altitudes (0.88), and all selected indices maintained consistently high correlations with LAI (0.82–0.88). These results indicate that single-band texture features mainly capture structural characteristics within individual spectral bands, whereas NDTI, DTI, and RTI effectively integrate spectral and structural information.

LAI estimation based on machine learning and deep learning

LAI estimation using RGB features

The training performance of six models for estimating rice LAI using single and fused RGB-derived features is shown in Fig. 9. At both 30 m and 60 m, the CNN model achieved the highest accuracy with the CIsTIs feature combination (R2 > 0.80), followed by the RF model (R2 > 0.78). At 30 m, compared with models using CIs or TIs alone, the CIsTIs features increased R2 by 3.4%–5.2% and 12.4%–15.6%, respectively, while reducing RMSE by 3.1%–8.2% and 3.9%–4.0%. At 60 m, R2 increased by 4.7%–7.2% and 13.7%–16.4%, while RMSE decreased by 4.4%–5.9% and 3.0%–16.7%. Overall, accuracy was higher at 30 m than at 60 m, indicating the importance of spatial resolution for spectral and texture features. Color indices were more sensitive to rice LAI than texture features, and model performance ranked as follows: CIsTIs > CIs > TIs.

Discussion

Performance of different input parameters in rice LAI estimation

RGB imagery offers high spatial resolution, enabling precise extraction of canopy texture features such as leaf arrangement and pore distribution. This capability effectively compensates for the limited spectral information available from visible bands44. Integrating texture features derived from RGB data can significantly enhance the accuracy of LAI inversion‌45. However, because RGB sensors rely solely on the visible spectrum, they suffer from low spectral information density and saturation effects. During the panicle initiation and heading stages, when the rice canopy becomes dense and closed, vegetation indices such as ExG and NGRDI tend to saturate, thereby reducing the accuracy of LAI estimation46. In contrast, multispectral imagery contains a greater number of spectral bands, providing richer spectral information and generally enabling a more detailed representation of vegetation growth status47. However, it should be noted that multispectral features are also subject to saturation effects, particularly under dense canopy conditions in later growth stages, which may reduce their sensitivity to LAI variations. This limitation is consistent with the residual analysis results in this study, where a tendency toward underestimation was observed in high LAI conditions. Multispectral data maintain relatively stable performance in LAI estimation across different spatial resolutions, whereas the accuracy of RGB-derived estimates decreases markedly with coarser resolution. This trend is consistent with the findings of the present study: as flight altitude increased from 30 to 60 m, the correlation between CIs and TIs and LAI declined significantly, resulting in weaker estimation performance at 60 m compared with 30 m. The inclusion of narrow spectral bands such as near-infrared and red-edge in multispectral sensors makes their spectral reflectance less sensitive to flight altitude changes48. Consequently, even at 60 m, vegetation indices derived from multispectral data can maintain stable correlations with LAI. By integrating RGB and multispectral data, it is possible to leverage the high spatial resolution of RGB imagery together with the rich spectral content of multispectral data, thereby increasing the diversity of input features and enhancing model estimation accuracy49–51). Because individual features contain limited information about crop growth, multi-feature fusion can substantially improve the accuracy of crop parameter estimation49,50). Specifically, combining multiple vegetation indices (VIs) with texture features has been shown to effectively enhance the precision of rice LAI estimation52,53). Previous research also demonstrated that integrating RGB and multispectral data improved the accuracy of potato LAI estimation54.

Nevertheless, a major challenge in crop parameter estimation is that redundancy among VIs can negatively affect both computational efficiency and accuracy55. Therefore, constructing optimized multi-parameter feature combinations is critical for LAI estimation, as it allows the complementary strengths of different features to be fully utilized to improve prediction performance. In the present study, for example, the RF model using CIs, VIs, and TFIs as input parameters at 30 m achieved an R2 that was 10% and 0.9% higher than that obtained using CTs and TFIs alone, respectively, while RMSE decreased by 18.6% and 11.3%. These findings are consistent with previous research results.

Texture feature indices (TFIs) significantly enhance LAI estimation accuracy

Texture features contain rich spatial information about crop canopy surfaces, capturing critical structural characteristics. However, many individual texture features show relatively weak correlations with LAI56,52,53. The results of this study demonstrate that, compared with single-band texture features, texture feature indices (TFIs) derived from combinations of spectral bands exhibit significantly stronger correlations with LAI. For instance, under the 30 m flight altitude, the RF model using TFIs improved the estimation accuracy by 12.1% in terms of R2 and reduced RMSE by 20.4% relative to the original single-band texture features. TFIs effectively integrate diverse and comprehensive texture information57, mitigating the influence of soil background, terrain variation, and shadow interference58. The mean texture feature, which represents the average value of target and background pixels within a moving window, helps to smooth image noise and minimize background disturbance(Wang & Si et al., 2022). By enhancing the relationship between texture features and target biophysical variables, TFIs substantially improve the sensitivity and robustness of model predictions59. In this study, constructing TFIs and integrating them with vegetation indices (VIs) and color indices (CIs) effectively enhanced the estimation accuracy of rice LAI, underscoring the advantage of texture–spectral feature fusion in UAV-based crop monitoring.

Performance of different model algorithms in LAI estimation

Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods have shown strong capability in modeling complex and nonlinear relationships in crop growth monitoring, thereby improving the accuracy of LAI estimation60,61. Integrating ML and DL with remote sensing parameters can further enhance the reliability of LAI estimation62. However, their performance is highly dependent on data characteristics and model suitability.

In this study, the relatively small sample size limited the effectiveness of deep learning models. Although CNN demonstrated strong fitting ability, its performance was constrained by the use of structured feature vectors rather than spatially organized image data, which restricted its capacity to fully exploit spatial information63. In addition, deep learning models generally require large datasets to adequately learn complex nonlinear patterns64, 65, and under small-sample conditions, they are prone to overfitting or underfitting66. Similarly, the LSTM model did not perform well, as it is primarily designed for temporal sequence modeling67. while the input data in this study lacked temporal characteristics. These limitations reduced the advantages of deep learning approaches under the current experimental conditions.

In contrast, the Random Forest (RF) model exhibited more stable and reliable performance. This can be attributed to its ensemble learning mechanism, which enhances generalization ability and reduces the risk of overfitting, particularly under small-sample conditions68,69. Moreover, RF can automatically evaluate feature importance and handle high-dimensional and redundant features, making it well suited for multi-source data fusion scenarios.

Other models showed comparatively weaker performance for specific reasons. SVM performs well in small-sample and high-dimensional settings but is highly sensitive to kernel selection and parameter tuning70, which may limit its effectiveness in complex datasets. PLSR, as a linear method, has limited ability to capture nonlinear relationships, resulting in reduced estimation performance in high-dimensional systems71. BPNN, due to its shallow architecture, suffers from limited representation capacity and convergence instability, and may easily fall into local optima24,25.

Overall, the results indicate that model performance is strongly influenced by both data characteristics and algorithm suitability. Under the conditions of limited sample size and multi-source feature fusion, traditional machine learning methods, particularly RF, may provide more stable and robust performance than deep learning approaches.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although this study demonstrates promising performance in LAI estimation using multi-source UAV data and machine learning models, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the analysis was primarily conducted at the rice booting stage, and the applicability of the proposed framework across other growth stages remains to be further validated. In addition, the experiments were carried out at a single study site with limited varieties and nitrogen treatments, which may restrict the generalizability of the model. Environmental factors such as illumination conditions and atmospheric variability were relatively stable during data acquisition; therefore, the robustness of the model under more complex and dynamic conditions requires further investigation. Second, flight altitude was found to influence model performance due to its impact on spatial resolution and feature representation72. Although lower flight altitude generally improved estimation accuracy, it may reduce operational efficiency in large-scale applications73. Therefore, future studies should further explore the trade-off between spatial resolution and operational efficiency, as well as optimize multi-altitude data fusion strategies. Furthermore, this study primarily relied on data-driven approaches. Recent advances in vegetation parameter inversion have increasingly focused on integrating mechanistic models with machine learning, as well as physics-informed AI and differentiable mechanistic models74,75. These approaches can enhance model interpretability, improve generalization ability, and reduce dependence on large training datasets. Incorporating such hybrid modeling frameworks into UAV-based LAI estimation is a promising direction for future research.

Although model-specific feature selection methods such as recursive feature elimination (RFE) may further optimize predictor subsets, we adopted correlation-based pre-screening in this study because of the limited sample size and the need to maintain a unified and interpretable feature selection framework across multiple models. Future work will compare correlation-based screening with model-specific selection strategies such as RFE and embedded importance-based methods.

In addition, future work should focus on multi-temporal and multi-location data collection to improve model robustness and transferability. The integration of multi-source data, including hyperspectral, LiDAR, and thermal data, may further enhance the accuracy and stability of LAI estimation. Developing more generalized and scalable models will be essential for supporting precision agricultural management under diverse environmental conditions.

Conclusion

This study established an LAI estimation framework that integrates UAV-derived multi-parameter features with six modeling algorithms and evaluates the performance of RGB and multispectral (MS) imagery at flight altitudes of 30 m and 60 m during the rice booting stage. The fusion of features derived from RGB and MS data substantially improved LAI estimation accuracy, following the trend RGB + MS > MS > RGB. Texture indices derived from combinations of spectral bands—particularly near-infrared (NIR) and red-edge (RE1, RE2)—showed stronger correlations with LAI than single-band texture features. The integration of CIs, VIs, and TFIs (CIsVIsTFIs) further enhanced model performance compared with individual features or partial combinations (e.g., CIsVIs). At both 30 m and 60 m, the CNN model using CIsVIsTFIs achieved the best performance (R2 = 0.87), followed by the RF model. These results provide a useful approach for UAV-based crop monitoring and support precision agricultural management.
