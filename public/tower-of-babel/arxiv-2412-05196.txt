Exponential Speedups by Rerooting Levin Tree Search
Laurent Orseau, Marcus Hutter, Levi H. S. Lelis

Abstract
Levin Tree Search (LTS) ( Orseau et al., 2018 ) is a search algorithm for deterministic environments that uses a user-specified policy to guide the search. It comes with a formal guarantee on the number of search steps (node visits) for finding a solution node that depends on the quality of the policy. In this paper, we introduce a new algorithm, called lts \sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}} (pronounce root-LTS), which implicitly starts an LTS search rooted at every node of the search tree. Each LTS search is assigned a rerooting weight by a rerooter , and the search effort is shared between all LTS searches proportionally to their weights. The rerooting mechanism implicitly decomposes the search space into subtasks, leading to significant speedups. We prove that the number of node visits that lts \sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}} takes is competitive with the best decomposition into subtasks, at the price of a factor that relates to the uncertainty of the rerooter. If LTS takes time T T , in the best case with q q rerooting points, lts \sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}} only takes time O ⁡ ( q ​ T q ) O(q\sqrt[q]{T}) . Like the policy, we expect that the rerooter can be learnt from data, making lts \sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}} applicable to a wide range of domains.



Keywords

informed search, planning, efficiency guarantees, anytime algorithms, rerooting, Sokoban

Introduction

We are interested in tree search algorithms for deterministic domains.
Tree search algorithms such as all variants of best-first search — including A*
(
Hart et al., 1968
)
, Weighted-A* (WA*), and Greedy Best-First Search (GBFS)
(
Doran et al., 1966
)
— and variants of MCTS — such as UCT
(
Kocsis and
Szepesvári, 2006
)
, AlphaGo, AlphaZero and other variants
(
Silver et al., 2016
;
Silver et al., 2017b
;
Silver et al., 2017a
)
— explore the search tree starting at the root and can visit a node only if its parent has been visited first.
These algorithms are often guided by some side information, such as with cost-to-go heuristic function for A*, WA* and GBFS, a reward/value function for UCT and AlphaZero, or a policy for AlphaZero, Levin Tree Search (LTS)
(
Orseau et al., 2018
)
, and Policy-Guided Heuristic Search
(
Orseau and Lelis, 2021
)
.

Such algorithms also sometimes come with different types of guarantees:
A* and WA*, with an admissible heuristic function —
i.e.
, a function that never overestimates the optimal cost-to-go — are guaranteed to return a solution
that is cost-optimal (A*) or bounded-suboptimal (WA*), while UCT and AlphaZero are guaranteed to (eventually) have low regret in terms of cumulative reward during the search.
LTS is guaranteed to return a solution within a number of node visits that depends on the quality of its policy.
In this paper, we consider the latter type of guarantee,
on the efficiency of the search process depending on the quality of the side information.

To explain the main concepts of this paper, let us consider a kind of side information we call
clues
: some nodes are clue nodes, and a node can be known to be a clue node only when reaching it.
A clue may be
helpful
if it is on the path toward a solution node,
or
misleading
otherwise.

Policy-guided algorithms like LTS use a probability distribution over the available actions at any node of the tree:
LTS is a best-first search with visits the nodes in increasing order of their cost
n\mapsto d(n)/\pi(n)

On

— see

Reward-based algorithms such as AlphaZero are greedy in the sense that they spend most of their search time in the subtree rooted in the first high-reward node they visit.
This greedy behavior can lead to taking double-exponential time (with the solution depth) to recover from misleading rewards/clues
(

In this paper, we propose the
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
algorithm (pronounced root-LTS), which solves the kind of clue environments described in
Example

1
and much more.

\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
uses both a policy and a new guiding construct called
rerooter
.
The rerooter assigns a
rerooting weight
to each visited node.
This weight can depend on whatever information is available when visiting the node, including whether the node is a clue node, or how many clue nodes have been visited up to this node, or any other feature of the current and previously-visited nodes.
Intuitively, the rerooting weight of a node informs
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
what fraction of its search time should be devoted to the tree rooted in this node.

\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
comes with formal guarantees on the number of node visits required to reach a solution node, depending on the quality of the policy and of the rerooter.
Like the LTS policy, we expect that the rerooter may be learnt from data or designed by the user, but this is beyond the scope of this paper.

While
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
does not specifically require clues since only the rerooting weights matter, clues are still a useful concept both for intuition and perhaps to design rerooters.
We envision that clues could be given whenever the search is ‘on the right path’.
This is related to rewards and shaping rewards in reinforcement learning
(
Ng et al., 1999
;
Sutton and
Barto, 1998
)
,
landmarks in classical planning
(
Hoffmann et al., 2004
)
.
In automated theorem proving
(
Loveland, 2016
)
, a clue may be given when a hopefully-helpful lemma is found.
In constraint satisfaction programming
(
Tsang, 2014
)
, a clue could be given when
some difficult constraints are satisfied.
More generally, a clue could be given whenever substantial progress is made on some scoring function, or when a bottleneck has been passed, or when a subtask has been solved.
Clues may not all have the same meaning either, and rerooting weights need not be equal for all clues.

3
Lower Bound

First we prove a general lower bound on the number of node visits that any search algorithm, randomized or deterministic,
using any kind of heuristic guide,
must perform before visiting a solution node in the presence of clues.
We will also use this set of environments as a running example and to show that some improvements to
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
are impossible in general.
In the following result, for simplicity we assume that the algorithm can test whether a node is the solution node
n^{*}
only upon visiting it.

Theorem 3
.

Consider an infinite perfect binary tree.
Choose
a,q\in{\mathbb{N}}_{1}
.
Let
\mathcal{N}_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
be the set of clue nodes,
where
q=|\mathcal{N}_{q}|
is the number of clue nodes.
The root is a clue node.
For every other clue node
n
,
there exists a clue ancestor
\overline{n}
at relative depth at most
a
:

1\leq d(n)-d(\overline{n})\leq a
.
Optionally, communicate
a,q,\mathcal{N}_{q}
to the search algorithm.
Let
\mathcal{N}_{q,a}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{q}}\{\underline{n}:n\preceq\underline{n},d(\underline{n})-d(n)\leq a\}
be the set of nodes of depth at most
a
relative to any clue node of
\mathcal{N}_{q}
.
The solution node
n^{*}
is chosen uniformly at random among
\mathcal{N}_{q,a}
.

Then, for all such
a,q,\mathcal{N}_{q}
,
every search algorithm must visit at least
(q+1)2^{a-1}
nodes on average before visiting
n^{*}
— and thus also in the worst case.
∎

Note that instead of communicating
\mathcal{N}_{q}
to the search algorithm, a less informed alternative is to communicate a clue membership function

n\mapsto\left\llbracket{n\in\mathcal{N}_{q}}\right\rrbracket

which can only be applied to generated nodes (see
Eq.

2
) — the result still holds in this case.

Proof.

For a given set
\mathcal{N}_{q}
, let us place the clues
in the tree, one by one, in increasing order of their depths.
First, the root is a clue node.
Since the solution node
n^{*}
can descend from any clue node at relative depth at most
a
,
there are for now
2^{a+1}-1
possible placements.
The second clue node is at depth at most
a
relative to its closest ancestor clue node (the root, in this case).
The solution node
n^{*}
can now also be placed among any of the closest
2^{a+1}-1
descendants of the second clue node.
But, among these, all (and only) the nodes at depth at most
a
relative to the first clue node have already been counted.
This overlap is the largest when the second clue node is a child of
the first clue node (rather than a more distant descendant), in which case the cardinality of the overlap is
2^{a}-1
.
Hence the number of
new
places for
n^{*}
is at least
2^{a+1}-1-(2^{a}-1)=2^{a}
, and the cumulative number of places at this stage is
at least
2^{a+1}-1+2^{a}
.
After repeating the process until all
q
clues are placed, the number of possible places
|\mathcal{N}_{q,a}|
for
n^{*}
is thus at least
2^{a+1}-1+(q-1)2^{a}=(q+1)2^{a}-1
.
(Ordering the clue nodes by their depth ensures that when placing a new clue node
n
, no descendant of
n
is already a clue node, and thus the overlap to consider is only with respect to its closest clue ancestor.)
Therefore,
by randomizing the location of
n^{*}
,
any deterministic algorithm needs at least
(q+1)2^{a}/2
node visits on average to visit
n^{*}
,
and by a standard argument this holds also for any randomized algorithm.

∎

Theorem

3
means that, for the considered set of environments,
if there are
m
clue nodes on the path from the root to the solution node
n^{*}

— these are clue nodes that any tree search algorithm must visit before visiting
n^{*}
— it is not possible to achieve an upper bound of

O(m2^{a})
node visits (on average or in the worst case) if
m=o(q)
.
For example, if every path from the root has at most
\sqrt{q}
clue nodes,
then
m\leq\sqrt{q}
, and so an algorithm with an upper bound of
O(m2^{a})=O(\sqrt{q}2^{a})
does not exist as this contradicts
Theorem

3
.
Similarly, if it happens that the clues are at depths at most
b<a
relative to their closest clue ancestors (and the solution node is still at relative depth
a
to its closest clue ancestor), it is not possible to achieve an upper bound of
O(q2^{b})
in general, as taking
b=a-\ln a
would contradict
Theorem

3
, even if
b
is communicated to the search algorithm (since
Theorem

3
allows for
\mathcal{N}_{q}
to be communicated).

4
Self-Counting Cost Functions

In this section, we first define self-counting cost functions and show their relation to the BFS
steps at which nodes are visited.
Next, as a side contribution, we improve the self-counting cost function of LTS.
Then, we show how to compose self-counting cost functions into a single one.

Definition 4

(Self-counting cost function)
.

A cost function
c:\mathcal{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
self-counting
if, for all
\theta\geq 0
,
the number of nodes of cost at most
\theta
is itself at most
\theta
:

\displaystyle|\{n\in\mathcal{N}:c(n)\leq\theta\}|\leq\theta\,.

Note that non-monotone cost functions may still be self-counting.

Orseau et al. (2018)
;
Orseau et al. (2023)

use the monotone cost function
\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)=d(n)/\pi(n)
to guide the BFS of Levin Tree Search.
They prove that
|\{n:\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)\leq\theta\}|\leq 1+\theta
for all
\theta\geq 0
.
But because BFS is invariant to cost translation,
BFS with the cost function
\tfrac{d}{\pi}(\cdot)
is equivalent to BFS with the cost function
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(\cdot)
.
Since
|\{n:1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)\leq 1+\theta\}|\leq 1+\theta
,
which is equivalent to
|\{n:1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)\leq\theta\}|\leq\theta
,
the cost function
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(\cdot)
is a self-counting cost function.

The following result strongly links the number of BFS steps with the cost of a monotone self-counting cost function.

Lemma 5
.

A monotone cost function
c
is self-counting if and only if
t\leq c(n_{t})
for all
t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{1}
, where

n_{t}
is visited at step
t
of the BFS with the cost function
c
.∎

Proof.

Assume
c
is self-counting.
Since
c
is monotone, BFS enumerates the nodes in order of increasing costs,
so for all
t
we have

t~=~|\{n_{1},\dots n_{t}\}|~\leq~|\{n:c(n)\leq c(n_{t})\}|~\leq~c(n_{t})

as required, where the last inequality follows from the self-counting property.

For the other direction,
we assume that
t\leq c(n_{t})
for all
t
.
For any
\theta
,
choose the largest
\tau
such that
c(n_{\tau})\leq\theta
.
Since
c
is monotone, all nodes visited before
\tau
have cost at most
\theta
, and all nodes visited after
\tau
have cost strictly more than
\theta
,
that is,

|\{n:c(n)\leq\theta\}|~=~|\{n_{1},\dots n_{\tau}\}|~=~\tau~\leq~c(n_{\tau})~\leq~\theta

and thus
c
is self-counting.
∎

Hence, for example, since the cost function
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)
is self-counting and monotone,
the number of steps
T
before BFS (with this cost function) visits the node
n_{T}
is bounded by
T\leq 1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{T})
— as was shown by
Orseau et al. (2018)
.
Additional examples and remarks can be found in
Appendix

C
.

4.1
Slenderness Cost Function

Now we define a variant of the cost function
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(\cdot)
with tighter guarantees.
Indeed, as a self-counting cost function,
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(\cdot)
is a little loose, as shown by the following example.

Example 6

(
\tfrac{d}{\pi}
double counts)
.

Consider a chain of nodes where each node has exactly one child with conditional probability 1 — see
Figure

2
.
Since
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}
is self-counting,
the number of nodes of cost at most the cost of
n_{a}
is

|\{n:1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)\leq 1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{a})\}|\leq 1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{a})=1+d(n_{a})
, and indeed
there are exactly
d(n_{a})+1
such nodes.
Now suppose that
n_{a}
has 2 children
n_{b}
and
n_{c}
, each with conditional probability 1/2.
Then
1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{b})=1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{c})=2d(n_{a})+3
,
which is indeed also an upper bound on the number of nodes of cost at most the cost of
n_{b}
.
However, this upper bound is loose because there are in fact only
d(n_{a})+3
such nodes.
This is because the ancestors of
n_{b}

and the ancestors of
n_{c}
are counted separately, leading to double-counting.
∎

Indeed, the factor
d(n)
appears in the ratio between the upper and lower bounds
(
Orseau et al., 2023
, Theorem 2)
.
We develop a tighter self-counting cost function
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)
that avoids this double counting.
The cost function
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)
is based on the quantity
\lambda(n)
which counts what fraction of its ancestors the node
n
is ‘responsible’ for.
If a node
n
holds a share
\lambda(n)
of its ancestors (including itself),
then a child
\underline{n}
of
n
holds a share
\lambda(n)\pi(\underline{n}|n)

of the ancestors of
n
, and thus a share

\lambda(n)\pi(\underline{n}|n)+1
of the ancestors of
\underline{n}
.
We call
\lambda:\mathcal{N}\to[1,\infty)
the
slenderness
of a node,
which, for a given policy
\pi
,
is defined as
\lambda(n_{1})\!=\!1
and for each
n
,

\displaystyle\forall\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n):\quad\lambda(\underline{n})\ =\ \lambda(n)\,\pi(\underline{n}|n)+1\,.

(3)

It follows that for every node
n
,
1\leq\lambda(n)\leq 1+d(n)
.
Then, for a finite tree,
the sum of the slenderness of the leaves should be a tighter count of the number of nodes in the tree than naively summing the depths of the leaves.
From this we define a tighter self-counting cost function.

Define the
slenderness cost function

\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}:\mathcal{N}\to[1,\infty)
, for all
n
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)\ =\ \frac{\lambda(n)}{\pi(n)}\ =\ \tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(\mathrm{par}(n))+\frac{1}{\pi(n)}

(4)

which shows that
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}
is a monotone cost function.
We can deduce the useful formula:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)\ =\ \sum_{\overline{n}\preceq n}\frac{1}{\pi(\overline{n})}\,.

(5)

Since
\pi(n_{1})=1
and
\pi(\overline{n})\geq\pi(n)
and
d(n)=|\mathrm{anc}(n)|
,
this implies that
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)\leq 1+\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n)
.
The slenderness cost function is self-counting,
with a tight lower bound
if the policy is proper:

\displaystyle\text{for all }\theta\geq 0:\quad\quad\frac{\theta-1}{B}~<~|\{n:\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)\leq\theta\}|~\leq~\theta

(6)

where
B
is the average branching factor in the tree
\{n:\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)\leq\theta\}
.
The proof is in
Appendix

B
.
The ratio between the upper and lower bounds for
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}
is tighter by a factor
d(\cdot)
than the one obtained by
Orseau et al. (2023, Theorem 2)
for
\tfrac{d}{\pi}
.

Example

We show how self-counting cost functions can be composed,
which is a central idea of our algorithm.
But first, suppose that we have

After a total of

While we already have an interesting result, a few points remain to be addressed.

Tie breaking.

It turns out that running BFS with
\tilde{c}
is equivalent (up to some tie breaking) to running BFS with
c^{\mathrm{max}}
: Indeed, a node with low
\tilde{c}
-cost can be visited only once its maximum
\tilde{c}
-cost ancestor has been visited, which forces BFS with
\tilde{c}
to behave as if it was BFS with
c^{\mathrm{max}}
.
The main difference between
c^{\mathrm{max}}
and
\tilde{c}
is that many nodes with different
\tilde{c}
-costs have the same
c^{\mathrm{max}}
-costs.
In essence, the non-monotonicity of
\tilde{c}
induces a tie-breaking rule for nodes that all have the same
c^{\mathrm{max}}
-costs.
While it can be shown that
\tilde{c}
also enjoys the bound of
Eq.

13
,
we can take advantage of the induced tie-breaking to obtain a refined bound.
So we now put
c^{\mathrm{max}}
aside and focus on
\tilde{c}
, which will require strengthening the analysis to account for non-monotonicity.

Unnormalized weights.

From now on, the weights
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots\}
need not sum to 1.
Not only is this more convenient, but it also allows for obtaining bounds that are not possible with normalized weights — see for example
Mourtada and
Maillard (2017)
.
In a nutshell, the normalizer of the weights is a linear
factor in the cost function,
and since BFS is invariant to rescaling of the cost function, the normalizer can be omitted.

Conclusion

We have proposed a new search algorithm for deterministic domains, called
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
, which uses a rerooter to start the policy-based LTS at various
nodes in the search tree, potentially speeding up the search significantly.
The rerooter can use side information that the policy does not, such as ‘clues’.

\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
comes with theoretical guarantees on the number of search steps (node visits) depending on the quality of the policy and of the rerooter.

Clues appear similar in nature to shaping rewards and landmark heuristics, and
knowledge from these fields could be leveraged to design domain-specific rerooters and derive guarantees.

While many search domains are deterministic in nature (
e.g.
, theorem proving, many games, program synthesis, etc.), it could be valuable to extend
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
to tackle stochastic domains.

We are eager to see how
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
performs on real-world applications,
when both the policy and the rerooter are learnt,
in particular since LTS already works well
(
Orseau et al., 2018
;
Orseau et al., 2023
;
Orseau and Lelis, 2021
)
.
We hope that the theory we have proposed can provide a solid foundation for designing faster search algorithms and solving challenging search problems.

Appendix A
Clues and Classic Algorithms

Expanding on the claims made in the introduction, we show that classical algorithms (A*, WA*, LTS and MCTS variants) all struggle to make good use of clues.

A.1
Admissible A* Cannot Use Clues

A*
[
Hart et al., 1968
;
Dechter and
Pearl, 1985
]
is a best-first search algorithm whose priority queue is sorted according to the function
f(n)=g(n)+h(n)
, for each node
n

g

n

is an estimate of the number of actions to go.
We denote as

the minimum number of actions to connect

A* is guaranteed to find depth-optimal solutions if
h(n)\leq h^{*}(n)
for all
n

Weighted A*
[
Pohl, 1970
]
is a generalization of A* that uses a weighted version of the heuristic

to ensure
g

1+\varepsilon

\varepsilon\geq 0
.

Regarding the specific clue environment of
Example

1
of the Introduction, it is actually possible to design an admissible heuristic function for A* that solves the problem as efficiently as
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
:
for every node at depth 50 or depth 100 that is not a clue node, set the heuristic to infinity.
This effectively prunes all these nodes.
However, this ‘rerooting-by-pruning’ behaviour is not feasible in general while guaranteeing depth-optimality of the solution.
For example, if there is even a small chance that the solution node
does not descend from a clue node of depth 50, then no single path can be pruned by any admissible heuristic and A* must search everywhere to guarantee depth-optimality of the returned solution.
The following theorem implies that no algorithm (including A* and Weighted A*) can be claimed to guarantee
1+\epsilon

g
-optimality for all monotone cost functions

while also making efficient use of clues.

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{1-p}+\dots\frac{1}{(1-p)^{r-1}}+\frac{1}{(1-p)^{r}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}+\dots\frac{1}{p^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-p)^{r}}\left(\frac{1-p}{p}+\frac{1}{1-p}\frac{1}{p^{\ell}}-\frac{p}{1-p}\right)-\frac{1-p}{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-p}\frac{1}{(1-p)^{r}p^{\ell}}\,.

The case
p\leq 1-p
is proven similarly.
∎

With a bit more work,
using
1+1/p+\dots 1/p^{\ell}\leq 1+\ell/p^{\ell}
, the bound can be improved to

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n)\leq\max\left\{\min\left\{r+2,\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{left}}}\right\},~\min\left\{\ell+2,\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{right}}}\right\}\right\}\frac{1}{\pi(n)}\,,

where
p_{\mathrm{left}}=p=1-p_{\mathrm{right}}
.

Example 21

(Depth-dependent arity)

Consider an infinite tree where the number of children of any node

B.2
Proof of the Slenderness Bounds

Refer to
Example

21
for some intuition about why
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}
‘counts’ the number of nodes in a tree.

Before proving the bounds of
Eq.

6
,
we build an intermediate concept we call the
complementary policy
with respect to a tree,
and we prove that it satisfies some useful properties.

Recall that a set
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
of nodes is a tree
rooted in some node
n_{a}

if for every node in the set, its parent is also in the set, except for the root
n_{a}
:
\forall n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus\{n_{a}\},\mathrm{par}(n)\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
.

Definition 22

(Complementary policy)
.

For a given tree
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
rooted in
n_{1}
, the
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
-complementary policy
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi

is, for all
n\in\mathcal{N}
,

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(n)\ =\ \pi(n)-\sum_{\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(\underline{n})\ =\ \pi(n)\left(1-\sum_{\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(\underline{n}|n)\right)\,.

The quantity
\pi^{\prime}(n)
is the amount of the path probability
\pi(n)

that is
not
passed down to children of
n

within

\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
.
That is,
\pi^{\prime}(n)
is ‘lost’ (or ‘leaked’) by
n
from
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
.
This also accounts for the fact that the policy may not be proper
— which is a form of ‘leakage’ too.
Note that a leaf of
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
loses all its path probability
\pi(n)
, that is,
\pi^{\prime}(n)=\pi(n)
.
Then we show that the total path probability that is lost is exactly 1 (the path probability of the root).

Lemma 23
.

For any tree
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
rooted in
n_{1}
, the
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
-complementary policy
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi^{\prime}(n)=1\,.

Proof.

Using
Definition

22
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi^{\prime}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(n)-\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\ {}\sum_{\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(\underline{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(n)-\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus\{n_{1}\}}\pi(n)\ =\ \pi(n_{1})\ =\ 1\,.\qed

In the next result,
the quantity
\frac{\pi^{\prime}(n)}{\pi(n)}\lambda(n)
corresponds to the
fraction
of the share
\lambda(n)

that is not passed down to its children within
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, that is,
the fraction that is ‘lost’.
The next result shows that every share is eventually lost, either partially by some nodes of
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
,
or in full by the leaves of
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
(since they pass down no share at all within
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
).
Hence,
since the share
\lambda(n)
represents a fraction of the number of ancestors of
n
(included),
when summing over all nodes of
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, the cumulative fraction of the lost shares
is equal to the number of nodes in
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 24
.

For any tree
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
rooted in
n_{1}
, the
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
-complementary policy
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
satisfies

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\frac{\pi^{\prime}(n)}{\pi(n)}\lambda(n)\,.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\frac{\pi^{\prime}(n)}{\pi(n)}\lambda(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(n)\left(1-\sum_{\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(\underline{n}|n)\right)\frac{\lambda(n)}{\pi(n)}

(19a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\lambda(n)-\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\sum_{\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\pi(\underline{n}|n)\lambda(n)

(19b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\lambda(n)-\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\sum_{\underline{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}(\lambda(\underline{n})-1)

(19c)

\displaystyle=\lambda(n_{1})+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus\{n_{1}\}}1

(19d)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|\,,

(19e)

with

(a)

from the definition of
\pi^{\prime}
in
Definition

22
,

(b)

from the definition of
\lambda
in
Eq.

(c)

by cancellation of every

and dealing with the

(d)

since

∎

We can now easily prove the upper bound of

For any given

Proposition 25

(
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}

B.3
Telescoping Property

We show that the root-dependent cost
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}
function
satisfies a form of ‘telescoping’ property.

Lemma 28

(
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}
telescopes)
.

For all
n_{a},n_{b},n\in\mathcal{N}

such that
n_{a}\preceq n_{b}\preceq n
,

\frac{\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n;n_{a})-1}{\pi(n_{a})}=\frac{\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{b};n_{a})-1}{\pi(n_{a})}+\frac{\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n;n_{b})-1}{\pi(n_{b})}\,.

∎

This is of the form
f_{a}(n)=f_{a}(n_{b})+f_{b}(n)
.

Proof.

From
Eq.

7
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n;n_{a})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{a}\preceq\overline{n}\preceq n}\frac{1}{\pi(\overline{n}|n_{a})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{a}\preceq\overline{n}\preceq n_{b}}\frac{1}{\pi(\overline{n}|n_{a})}+\sum_{n_{b}\prec\overline{n}\preceq n}\frac{1}{\pi(\overline{n}|n_{a})}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{b};n_{a})+\frac{1}{\pi(n_{b}|n_{a})}\sum_{n_{b}\prec\overline{n}\preceq n}\frac{1}{\pi(\overline{n}|n_{b})}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{b};n_{a})+\frac{1}{\pi(n_{b}|n_{a})}\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n;n_{b})-1\right)\,,

and the result follows by subtracting 1 on each side, then dividing by
\pi(n_{a})
.
∎

Note that, by contrast, the rooted
\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n;n_{a})=(d(n)-d(n_{a}))/\pi(n|n_{a})
does not have a similar telescoping form (with the offending term in red):

\displaystyle\frac{d(n)-d(n_{a})}{\pi(n|n_{a})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(n_{b}|n_{a})}\frac{d(n)-d(n_{b})}{\pi(n|n_{b})}+\frac{d(n_{b})-d(n_{a})}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi(n|n_{b})}\pi(n_{b}|n_{a})}\,,

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n;n_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(n_{b}|n_{a})}\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n;n_{b})+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{1}{\pi(n|n_{b})}}\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{b};n_{a})\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n;n_{a})}{\pi(n_{a})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n;n_{b})}{\pi(n_{b})}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{1}{\pi(n|n_{b})}}\frac{\tfrac{d}{\pi}(n_{b};n_{a})}{\pi(n_{a})}\,.

Appendix C
Self-Counting Cost Functions

Example 29

(Simple monotone self-counting cost functions)
.

In a binary tree, the cost function
c(n)=2^{d(n)+1}-1
is monotone self-counting — it is also quite tight for perfect infinite binary trees.
The cost function
c(n)=3^{d(n)+1}
is also monotone self-counting, even though the it grossly overestimates the number of nodes of cost at most a given bound: for a given node
n^{*}
there are far fewer than
3^{d(n^{*})+1}
nodes of cost at most
3^{d(n^{*})+1}
— since there are only
2^{d(n^{*})+1}-1
of them.
In a binary tree, the cost function
c(n)=2000
for the 1023 nodes of depth at most 9 and
c(n)=\infty
otherwise is also monotone self-counting.
In a chain (every node has only one child), the cost function
c(n)=d(n)+1
is also monotone self-counting.
∎

Example 30

(Simple non-monotone self-counting cost-function)
.

In a binary tree, the cost function
c(n)=1024
for all the 1024 nodes at depth 10, and
c(n)=\infty
for all the other nodes is self-counting but is not monotone. Hence
Lemma

5
does not apply.
Indeed, the root has infinite cost, but it is still the first node enumerated by BFS.
∎

Remark 31

(Inverse probability SCCF)
.

The inverse of a probability distribution over the nodes is a self-counting cost function.
Indeed, for all
\theta\geq 0
,

\left|\left\{n:\frac{1}{p(n)}\leq\theta\right\}\right|=|\{n:1\leq p(n)\theta\}|=\sum_{n:1\leq p(n)\theta}1\leq\sum_{n:1\leq p(n)\theta}p(n)\theta\leq\theta\,.

However, a self-counting cost function is not necessarily the inverse of a probability distribution over the nodes.
∎

Remark 32

(Harmonic composition of SCCF)
.

It is tempting to attempt to use the harmonic mean instead of the minimum so as to accumulate the weights for nodes that are shared.
Unfortunately, here is a simple counter-example:
The nodes
n_{a}
and
n_{b}
are children of the root
n_{1}
.
The costs are
c_{1}=(1,2,3)
for nodes
n_{1},n_{a},n_{b}
, and
c_{2}=(1,3,2)
.
We take
w_{1}=w_{2}=1/2
.
Then
c(n_{a})=c(n_{b})=1/(1/2\cdot 1/2+1/2\cdot 1/3)=12/5<3
,
and thus
|\{n:c(n)\leq 12/5\}|\not\leq 12/5
, so
c
is not self-counting.
However, if
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are inverse probability distributions (and thus are self-counting, see
Remark

31
),
then their composition using a harmonic mean is
also the inverse of a probability distribution and thus is also self-counting.
∎

Appendix D

First we build a couple of tools to deal with non-monotone cost functions,
and compositions of non-monotone self-counting cost functions.

The following lemma holds for any cost function

(Tree-to-path)

\displaystyle\max_{j<k\leq t}c(n_{k})~=~\max_{n_{j}\prec n\preceq n_{t}}c(n)\,.

Define

.
Since
n_{j}\in Q_{j}
(by

1
),
by

2
the priority queues

to

Appendix E
Comparison between LTS and
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}

To be able to compare the bounds of LTS and
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
(the simplified bound of
Corollary

12
), we can rewrite the LTS bound as a ‘subtask decomposition’.
Let
n_{T}
be the node found at step
T
by
either LTS or
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
using the cost function
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(\cdot)
, then for any subtask decomposition
\{n_{T_{i}}\}_{i<m}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\text{LTS:}\quad T\

\displaystyle\leq\ \lambda(n_{T})\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\prod_{i<m}}\,\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi(n_{T_{i+1}}\mid n_{T_{i}})}}\,,

\displaystyle\text{$\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}${}:}\quad T

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\max_{1\leq i<m}\frac{w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}}{w_{T_{i}}}}\,\frac{\lambda(n_{T_{i+1}};n_{T_{i}})}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi(n_{T_{i+1}}\mid n_{T_{i}})}}\,.

(Note that the value of
T
may be differ depending on the algorithm.)
On first reading, omit the terms
\lambda(\cdot)
which are usually small
compared to the other terms — and recall that
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(\underline{n};n)\leq d(\underline{n})-d(n)
.
Each term
\pi(\cdot|\cdot)
is a product of ‘action’ probabilities,
so
1/\pi(n_{T_{i+1}}|n_{T_{i}})
can easily be exponential with the depth of
n_{T_{i+1}}
relative to
n_{T_{i}}
.
Now, LTS features a
product
of these terms,
which can itself be exponential with the number of ‘subtasks’
m-1
in the considered decomposition.
By contrast,
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
features a
maximum
, which is a significant improvement over the product in LTS, at the price of the factor
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}/w_{T_{i}}
.
A (very) good rerooter could set
w_{T_{i}}=1
for all
i<m
on the path toward
n_{T}
,
and
w_{t}=0
everywhere else. Then
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}=m-1
, and the
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
bound improves exponentially over the LTS bound.

Of course, the rerooter may not be very good.
In particular, in
Section

6
, it is explained that the factor
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}
may be linear with
T
, leading to vacuous bounds, and a transformation of input rerooting weights is proposed to ensure that the bounds do not become vacuous.

Observe also that setting
w_{1}=1
and
w_{t}=0
everywhere else makes the
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
and LTS bounds coincide.

Section

First, we need this simple result:

Lemma 39
.

Let

(possibly with

\displaystyle f(b)-f(a)~\geq~(b-a)\min_{x\in[a,b]}f^{\prime}(x)\,.

Proof.

Follows directly from the mean value theorem.
∎

Now, given arbitrary

Appendix G
Speeding Up the Calculation of the Cost

Recall from
Eq.

14
that the cost of a node
n
for

is

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{r}}(n)=\min_{n_{t}\prec n}\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}(n)}{w_{t}}\,.

The naive calculation of this cost — as in

1
(p.
1

This may not be particularly problematic if other elements of the search require heavier computation (such as simulating the environment or querying a neural network).
But, using the following result, which exploits a property of

Lemma 41
.

Let

be two ancestors of a node

.
If

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{a}(n_{t})}{w_{a}}\leq\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n_{t})}{w_{b}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\pi(n_{a})}{w_{a}}\leq\frac{\pi(n_{b})}{w_{b}}

then for all

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{a}(n)}{w_{a}}\leq\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n)}{w_{b}}\,.

This implies that
c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n)/w_{b}
never plays a role in the minimum calculation of
c^{\mathrm{r}}(n)
.

Proof.

From
Lemma

28

(where we substitute

,
n_{b}\leadsto n_{t}
)
it follows that, for all

,

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{a}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n})}{\pi(n_{a})}=\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{a}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n_{t}})}{\pi(n_{a})}+\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}t}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n})}{\pi({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n_{t}})}\,,

and similarly for
b

Multiplying all sides by
\pi(n_{a})/w_{a}
and using the assumptions we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{a}(n)}{w_{a}}\ =\ \frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{a}(n_{t})}{w_{a}}+\frac{\pi(n_{a})}{w_{a}}\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}(n)}{\pi(n_{t})}\ \leq\ \frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n_{t})}{w_{b}}+\frac{\pi(n_{b})}{w_{b}}\frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{t}(n)}{\pi(n_{t})}\ =\ \frac{c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n)}{w_{b}}\,.

Remark 43

(Numerical stability)
.

Because the quantities
\pi(\cdot)
can become exponentially small with the depth,
some care needs to be taken to avoid numerical instability when calculating the costs of the nodes.
Recall that, from
Eq.

14
and
Eq.

4
, for all
n_{b}\prec n_{k}\prec n_{t}
with
n_{t}\in\mathcal{C}(n_{k})
,

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n_{t})=c^{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(n_{t})+\frac{1}{\pi(n_{t}|n_{b})}\,.

Define

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(n_{t};n_{b})=c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n_{t})\pi(n_{t}|n_{b})\,.

We recommend maintaining the quantity
\lambda^{\prime}(n_{t};n_{b})
in linear space, and the quantity
\pi(n_{t}|n_{b})
in log space.
The quantities can be updated from a node
n_{k}
to one of its children
n_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(n_{t};n_{b})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}(n_{k};n_{b})\pi(n_{t}|n_{k})+1\,,

\displaystyle\ln\pi(n_{t}|n_{b})

\displaystyle=\ln\pi(n_{k}|n_{b})+\ln\pi(n_{t}|n_{k})\,.

Then the cost can also be calculated in log space — recall that best-first search is invariant to monotone increasing transformations of the cost function:

\displaystyle\ln c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n_{t})

\displaystyle=\ln\lambda^{\prime}(n_{t};n_{b})-\ln\pi(n_{t}|n_{b})\,,

\displaystyle\ln c^{\mathrm{r}}(n_{t})

\displaystyle=\min_{n_{b}\prec n_{t}}\ln c^{\mathrm{r}}_{b}(n_{t})-\ln w_{b}\,.

Moreover, the condition

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(n_{a})}{w_{a}}\ \leq\ \frac{\pi(n_{b})}{w_{b}}\quad\text{is equivalent to}\quad\frac{1}{w_{a}\pi(n|n_{a})}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{w_{b}\pi(n|n_{b})}

for all
n\in\mathrm{desc}_{+}(n_{a})\cap\mathrm{desc}_{+}(n_{b})
.
The latter form (in log space) can be more numerically stable as most of the time it requires less precision.

Appendix H
Detailed Example: Sokoban

Figure 5
:
A simple level of Sokoban. The player (the pumpkin)
can move in all 4 directions (up, down, left, right) and
must push all 4 boxes (brown squares)
onto one goal spot each (blue diamond cells).
Boxes cannot be pulled.

To make our ideas a little more concrete, we make some back-of-the-envelope calculations to analyse the behaviour of
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
on a level of Sokoban — see
Figure

5
.
The reader must keep in mind that this serves only illustrative purposes.
In particular, this level of Sokoban has been designed to demonstrate some interesting features of our results, and most of the numbers we present are likely largely overestimated.
Both the rerooter and the policy we design are simplistic for the sake of clarity.

A
state
is one configuration of the board.
Multiple nodes in the search tree may correspond to the same states.
There are 89 non-wall cells, 4 boxes, 4 goal spots and 1 player.
That is, there are 89 possible places for the 5 moving objects and,
since the 4 boxes are not distinguishable,
the number of possible states is at most (but close to)
89{}!/(1!4!(89{}-4-1)!)=207\,538\,210
.
With this kind of Sokoban level configuration, breadth-first search
with transposition tables
— to ensure visiting each state only once — likely takes about half this number of node visits on average.

For the level displayed in
Figure

5
,
we consider the following solution path.
Starting at the root, the player takes the following actions:

down left left up up left up up left,

right right up up,

right right down down left,

right down right down down left left,

and ends up in a solution state at a node
n_{T}=n^{*}
, where all 4 boxes are on goal spots.
In the sequence above, commas are placed each time a box has been pushed on a goal spot.
The number of actions (= depth of the solution) in this sequence is 25.

We define the policy to be uniform among the actions that lead to states
that differ from the current state and from the previous state.
In particular, this avoids undoing the previous action, such as moving down after moving up — unless a box has been pushed.
Note that this is significantly weaker than if we had used full transposition tables,
as it does not prevent states from being visited multiple times,
but this allows us to read the policy’s probabilities directly from the picture.
Let us rewrite the sequence of actions above,
with each action preceded by the number of child nodes of positive policy probability:

(3)down (3)left (2)left (3)up (2)up (1)left (1)up (1)up (2)left,

(2)right (2)right (2)up (2)up,

(4)right (2)right (3)down (2)down (3)left,

(2)right (3)down (1)right (3)down (2)down (3)left (2)left.

Multiplying all these numbers together and taking the inverse gives the path probability
\pi(n^{*})
.
We can calculate
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n^{*})=\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{T})=195\,879\,469
.
This is an upper bound on the number of steps that LTS with this policy needs to perform to find this particular solution node.

Now let us design a simple rerooter.
For the sake of the argument, we assume that no state is visited twice —
which, as we mentioned earlier, could be enforced at the policy level.
We consider 3 ‘types’ of clues, and each type will have a different associated rerooting weight.
A clue node is of clue type
z\in\{1,2,3\}

if exactly
z
boxes are on a goal spot,
and a box has just been pushed on a goal spot.

Let us make some quick estimates.
There are
N=89
non-wall cells.
For clues of type 3,
if exactly 3 boxes (chosen out of 4) are on goal spots,
then the remaining box can be in any of the
N-5
cells (removing the 4 goal positions and the player’s position).
For each such positioning of the boxes, the player can be in at most one of 3 cells surrounding each box on a goal spot, so a total of 9 positions.
So our estimate of the number of clues of type 3 is
\binom{4}{3}\binom{N-5}{1}\times 9
.

More generally, for clues of type
z\in\{1,2,3\}
, we estimate the number of clues to be

M_{z}=\binom{4}{z}\binom{N-5}{4-z}\times z\times 3\,.

where
z\times 3
is the number of possible positions of the player around one of the
z
boxes on their goal spots.
We obtain

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=1\,143\,408\,,

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=125\,496\,,

\displaystyle M_{3}

\displaystyle=3\,024\,.

If
n_{t}
is a clue node of type
z
then we set
w_{t}=1/M_{z}
,
otherwise
w_{t}=0
.
We also set
w_{1}=1
.
Note that

w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\leq w_{1}+\sum_{z\leq 3}\text{number of clues of type }z\times 1/M_{z}\leq 4\,.

Now let us estimate a bound on the step
T
at which
n^{*}=n_{T}
is visited for the example of
Figure

5
and the sequence of actions above.

Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
be the steps at which
\sqrt{\mathrm{\textnormal{{lts}}}}
visits
the 3 clue nodes (of types
1,2,3
in order) of the solution trajectory.

Theorem

11
and
Corollary

12
allow us to choose any convenient decomposition into subtasks to calculate a bound,
for a given sequence of actions and the corresponding resulting node
n_{T}
,
for the analysis, without changing the algorithm
.
So first, let us choose to not decompose at all,
with the subtask decomposition
\{n_{1},n_{T}\}
.
Then, as in
Eq.

17
we have

T\leq w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{T})\leq 4\times 195\,879\,469\,.

Obviously, this is not a great bound, as it is a factor 4 worse than the LTS bound.

Second, let us choose a decomposition
\{n_{1},n_{t_{1}},n_{t_{2}},n_{t_{3}},n_{T}\}

on all 3 clues of each type visited on the solution path.
Then, from
Corollary

12
,

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\leq w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\max\left\{\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{1}}),\ {\mathchoice{\makebox[60.55553pt]{$\displaystyle M_{1}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{2}};n_{t_{1}})$}}{\makebox[60.55553pt]{$\textstyle M_{1}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{2}};n_{t_{1}})$}}{\makebox[49.90277pt]{$\scriptstyle M_{1}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{2}};n_{t_{1}})$}}{\makebox[43.81981pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle M_{1}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{2}};n_{t_{1}})$}}},\ M_{2}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}};n_{t_{2}}),\ M_{3}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{T};n_{t_{3}})\right\}

\displaystyle=w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\max\left\{{\mathchoice{\makebox[30.62321pt]{$\displaystyle 733$}}{\makebox[30.62321pt]{$\textstyle 733$}}{\makebox[27.8339pt]{$\scriptstyle 733$}}{\makebox[26.46124pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle 733$}}},\ 1\,143\,408\times 31,\ {\mathchoice{\makebox[66.54922pt]{$\displaystyle 125\,496\times 229$}}{\makebox[66.54922pt]{$\textstyle 125\,496\times 229$}}{\makebox[58.47203pt]{$\scriptstyle 125\,496\times 229$}}{\makebox[54.649pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle 125\,496\times 229$}}},\ 3\,024\times 393\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 141\,782\,592\,.

While this bound is an improvement over the previous one, as it matches roughly the LTS bound, it still does not offer any advantage.
The culprit is the large number
M_{1}
:
There are too many clues of type 1, that is, these clues are not informative enough.
So let us choose a decomposition
\{n_{1},n_{t_{2}},n_{t_{3}},n_{T}\}

that ‘skips over’ the clue node of type 1, which gives:

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\leq w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\max\left\{\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{2}}),\ {\mathchoice{\makebox[58.8889pt]{$\displaystyle{}M_{2}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}};n_{t_{2}}){}$}}{\makebox[58.8889pt]{$\textstyle{}M_{2}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}};n_{t_{2}}){}$}}{\makebox[48.23615pt]{$\scriptstyle{}M_{2}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}};n_{t_{2}}){}$}}{\makebox[42.15318pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{}M_{2}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}};n_{t_{2}}){}$}}},\ M_{3}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{T};n_{t_{3}})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathchoice{\makebox[19.97792pt]{$\displaystyle 4$}}{\makebox[19.97792pt]{$\textstyle 4$}}{\makebox[13.8285pt]{$\scriptstyle 4$}}{\makebox[10.93312pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle 4$}}}\max\left\{{\mathchoice{\makebox[30.62321pt]{$\displaystyle{}7213{}$}}{\makebox[30.62321pt]{$\textstyle{}7213{}$}}{\makebox[27.8339pt]{$\scriptstyle{}7213{}$}}{\makebox[26.46124pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{}7213{}$}}},\ 125\,496\times 229,\ 3\,024\times 393\right\}

\displaystyle=114\,954\,336\,.

This bound improves a little bit on the previous one, but not significantly.

M_{2}
is also too large a number.
While there are fewer clue nodes of type 2 than of type 1,
the information that clue nodes of type 2 provide is not enough
to compensate for the difficulty to find the next clue node (of type 3) from the clue node of type 2.
Let us try one more decomposition
\{n_{1},n_{t_{3}},n_{T}\}
that skips over the first two clue nodes:

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\leq w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\max\left\{{\mathchoice{\makebox[31.66672pt]{$\displaystyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}})$}}{\makebox[31.66672pt]{$\textstyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}})$}}{\makebox[25.5834pt]{$\scriptstyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}})$}}{\makebox[22.08362pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{t_{3}})$}}},M_{3}\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{T};n_{t_{3}})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathchoice{\makebox[19.97792pt]{$\displaystyle 4$}}{\makebox[19.97792pt]{$\textstyle 4$}}{\makebox[13.8285pt]{$\scriptstyle 4$}}{\makebox[10.93312pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle 4$}}}\max\left\{795\,181,\ 3\,024\times 393\right\}

\displaystyle=4\,753\,728\,.

This time we obtain a bound that improves over the LTS bound by a factor 40
— and, since the clues of types 1 and 2 are always too numerous, it is natural to remove them altogether to save another factor 2 in
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}
.

Alternatively, for a clue of type
k
at node
n_{t}
we could assign
a rerooting weight
w_{t}=1/[(1+q_{k,t})\ln(1+M_{k})]
where
q_{k,t}
is the number of clues of type
k
seen up to step
t
(included).
Then we would still have
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\leq 4
, but early clues of type
k
would have a significantly higher weight than
1/M_{k}
.

If
M_{k}
is unknown or hard to estimate accurately, we can also simply set
w_{t}=1/(1+q_{k,t})
, leading to
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\leq 1+\sum_{k}\ln(1+q_{k,T})\leq 1+3\ln(1+\sum_{k}q_{k,T}/3)
(using Jensen’s inequality) and thus in the worst case
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}\leq 1+3\ln(T/3)
.
And, indeed, a numerical simulation (
without
transposition tables) shows that, with this rerooter, the Sokoban level is solved at step
T=1\,853
,
with
w{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{${}_{<T}$}}<3.83
and the numbers of visited clue nodes
is 8 for type 1, 1 for type 2, and 1 for type 3.
Using this information, we can calculate the bound on the number of visits by decomposing into 4 subtasks:

\displaystyle T\leq 3.83\max\{733,\ 9\times 31,\ 2\times 229,\ 2\times 393\}\leq 3\,011\,.

Observe that this bound is not even a factor 2 away from the actual number of node visits, and is less than a factor 6 of the cube root of the LTS bound
\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}(n_{T})=195\,879\,469
.
