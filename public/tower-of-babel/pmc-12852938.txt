In vitro evaluation of Bacillus subtilis isolated from Apis mellifera for potential use as a probiotic feed additive in honeybees
Authors: Chi Young Hwang, Eui-Sang Cho, Hyo Jung Lim, Myung-Ji Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe honeybee Apis mellifera plays a crucial role in global agriculture but faces significant threats from pathogens, pesticides, and nutritional stress in the beekeeping industry. In this study, we evaluate the potential application of Bacillus subtilis MBLB2796 isolated from the honeybee gut. Solid-state fermentation of soybean meal with MBLB2796 reduced allergenic proteins and anti-nutritional factors, enhancing its nutritional quality. Strain MBLB2796 also exhibited high hydrolytic enzyme activities (15.5 mm of protease, 13.5 mm of amylase, and 15.0 mm of cellulase), strong antimicrobial activity (20.5 mm) against Paenibacillus larvae, and antioxidant activity against DPPH and ABTS radicals (66.2% and 62.3%, respectively). Strain MBLB2796 demonstrated a strong survival rate after freeze-drying up to 94.4%, excellent stability during long-term storage, and superior acid and bile salt tolerance compared to commercial strains. Adhesion to Caco-2 cells was also comparable to the probiotic reference and commercial strain. Optimization of culture conditions using a one-factor-at-a-time approach identified 2.0% of yeast extract, 1.0% of galactose, and 0.25% of K2HPO4 as major medium components, increasing about 2.6-fold of biomass production in the flask cultivation system. Batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor achieved an 11.8 g/L DCW, which was a 5.1-fold increase in biomass yield compared to basal flask cultivation. These results suggest that the industrial potential of MBLB2796 as a probiotic starter for honeybee feed additive, offering both functional and economic benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33832-8.Keywords: Honeybee, Bacillus subtilis, Probiotic, Paenibacillus larvae, Feed additiveSubject terms: Applied microbiology, Industrial microbiology

Introduction

Apis mellifera (honeybee) plays a crucial role in agriculture by pollinating numerous crops essential for both human and animal consumption. Despite their importance, global honeybee colony numbers have failed to keep pace with growing demand due to pathogen infections, pesticide exposure, climate change, and nutritional stress1,2. Honeybee colonies face significant threats from various pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi, that cause serious diseases such as American foulbrood, which is caused by Paenibacillus larvae3.

Recent research suggests that the honeybee microbiota plays an essential role in maintaining host health by stabilizing the gut environment, protecting against pathogens and parasites, supporting digestion and detoxification, and modulating immune responses4,5. As awareness of the importance of gut microflora in honeybee health increases, the use of probiotics in apiculture is gaining recognition as a safe and effective alternative to traditional methods, such as antibiotics, for promoting colony health and enhancing disease resistance6. Given the essential role of gut bacteria in honeybee health, the development of probiotics specifically tailored for honeybee applications has become increasingly important7,8. For instance, lactic acid bacteria are recognized probiotics due to their antimicrobial properties and potential as dietary supplements9,10.

Bacillus spp. also exhibit antimicrobial activity against various bacteria and fungi by producing compounds such as bacilysin, ericins, fengycin, iturin, subtilins, and surfactin11–13. These can function as effective protective mechanisms and competitive factors in the establishment and maintenance of dominance within a given ecological niche14. Bacillus spp. exhibit remarkable resistance to heat, radiation, desiccation, enzymatic degradation, and acidic conditions15. Endospores, in particular, withstand a wide range of physicochemical stresses encountered during food and feed production, storage, and application. For example, pelleting processes for bacilli spores can involve temperatures up to 90 °C, yet more than 90% of spores remain viable after heating in feed samples16. In addition, there is a growing database of scientific evidence showing that some Bacillus strains can have health-promoting effects on eukaryotic hosts, and the application of different beneficial bacterial strains for human and animal health, including bacilli probiotics, is rapidly growing in scope and scale14,17,18. In particular, studies on bee probiotics have demonstrated that the use of Bacillus spp. confers multiple benefits, including improved survival rates, enhanced growth and development, greater colony strength, and increased immunity19.

Beekeepers strive to maintain optimal colony health, which is largely influenced by management practices8. During periods of pollen scarcity, they often rely on artificial pollen substitutes to sustain colony strength20. Various diet formulations have been developed as substitutes for natural pollen, often incorporating protein-rich ingredients such as soy, corn gluten, yeast, casein, and egg to supply essential amino acids21. For example, an artificial diet using pea flour was found to be an effective and economical supplementary feed for honeybee colonies during dearth periods, significantly improving brood production, colony strength, and honey yield compared to sugar syrup22. Composed of watermelon juice, legume flours, yeast, sugar, and oil, it was identified as a promising and effective artificial diet for supporting Apis mellifera colony health, immunity, and productivity during dearth periods23. Especially, soybean remains a major component in many commercial bee diets due to its affordability and accessibility24. However, soybeans contain protease inhibitors, such as trypsin inhibitors, which exert significant anti-nutritional effects25,26. Additionally, allergenic proteins such as glycinin and β-conglycinin have been identified in soybeans, and their presence can impair nutrient digestion and absorption in both humans and animals27,28. To address these limitations, microbial solid-state fermentation using bacteria and fungi has been applied to reduce anti-nutritional factors and improve the nutritional quality of soybean-based feed29,30. For example, B. subtilis L5 effectively reduced anti-nutritional components while increasing protein content and free amino acid concentration in fermented soybean meal31. In another study, 24 h of soybean meal fermentation with B. subtilis BS12 resulted in a reduction of glycinin and β-conglycinin by about 90%, respectively32.

In this study, we aimed to develop a probiotic feed additive for honeybees by utilizing B. subtilis MBLB2796, a strain exhibiting strong antimicrobial activity against P. larvae as well as high hydrolytic enzymatic activities33. Soybean meal was fermented using MBLB2796 to reduce anti-nutritional factors and improve nutrient digestibility for honeybee consumption. In addition, standard probiotic assessments were conducted to evaluate its potential as a honeybee probiotic, and culture conditions were optimized to establish MBLB2796 as an efficient probiotic starter strain for industrial-scale application.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and bacterial isolation

The honeybee (Apis mellifera) was bred in the Incheon National University apiary in Incheon, Republic of Korea, and twelve-day-old nurse bees were collected during the summer (June 2021) for bacterial isolation. Bacterial species isolation was performed in previous studies33. Briefly, the surface of each bee was sterilized with 100% ethanol, after which the entire intestinal tract (from the esophagus to the rectum) was carefully dissected. The dissected intestines were suspended and serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The resulting suspensions were spread onto tryptic soy agar (TSA; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Colonies exhibiting distinct morphological features, such as differences in color, shape, size, and surface texture, were selected and repeatedly re-streaked at least three times on fresh TSA plates to obtain pure isolates. The final selected strain, designated MBLB2796, was identified as Bacillus subtilis.

Bacterial culture conditions and solid-state fermentation

For Bacillus species cultivation, tryptic soy broth (TSB, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and TSA were used. To compare the functional evaluation of solid-state fermentation, Bacillus subtilis ST and Bacillus siamensis BK were isolated from Soytide and Bulkeeper, which are currently commercialized feed probiotics for honeybees. For lactic acid bacteria cultivation, de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth (KisanBio, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and MRS agar with 1.5% agarose were used. For the probiotic test, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG was used in this study as the control strain.

To evaluate the suitability of the isolated strain as probiotics for honeybee feed, solid-state media (SSM) was prepared using defatted soybean meal as a substrate. The SSM with a moisture content of 60% (w/v) was made, followed by sterilization by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. The strain was pre-cultured in a 14 mL Falcon tube with 5 mL of TSB at 30 °C for 24 h, and then 10% (v/w) inoculated into the SSM. Further, SSM was carried out at 30 °C for 72 h to produce a solid-state fermented product (SSFP). To determine the colony-forming unit (CFU), SSFP was mixed with 1 × PBS solution, serially diluted, and then spread onto TSA plates. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, and the CFU was measured.

SDS-PAGE for confirmation of allergen and digestion interference factors

To confirm the degradation of protease inhibitors and allergenic proteins by microbial fermentation, protein extraction was performed34. Briefly, 0.5 g of the SSFP was suspended in 5 mL of 8 M urea with 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and subjected to ultrasonication (power 40%, pulse 30, total duration 3 min) to facilitate protein release. Following sonication, the lysate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was carefully collected. For protein precipitation, 450 µL of the extracted supernatant was mixed with 50 µL of 6 N trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and incubated at − 20 °C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was washed three times with 500 µL of cold acetone, with each wash followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. After the final wash, residual solvents were evaporated by heating the pellet at 95 °C using a heat block35. The dried pellet was resuspended in D.W. for subsequent analysis. The stacking and separating gels were prepared with acrylamide concentrations of 5% and 12%, respectively. Protein samples were denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min before electrophoresis. Approximately 20 μL of each protein sample was loaded into individual wells. After electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 to visualize protein bands.

Protease, amylase, and cellulase activities of SSFP

For the hydrolytic enzyme activity test, 0.25 g of SSFP was suspended in 1 mL of 1 × PBS solution. The suspension was then filtered using a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate membrane filter. Subsequently, 20 μL of the filtrate was dispensed onto a paper disk for testing. Selective solid agar plates containing specific substrate components that react specifically with each enzyme were used. For the protease activity method, skim milk agar plates based on TSA containing 1.0% (w/v) skim milk (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) were employed36. The protease activities were evaluated by the well diffusion method to measure the clear zone. For the α-amylase activity method, TSA with 0.2% (w/v) soluble starch (Samchun Pure Chemical, Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea) was used. The α-amylase activities were evaluated to measure the clear zone by staining Lugol’s iodine solution, containing 0.5% (w/v) potassium iodine and 0.25% (w/v) iodine. For the cellulase activity method, 1.0% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), 1.0% (w/v) glucose, and 0.5% yeast extract (w/v) were added to an agar plate and used. The cellulase activities were evaluated by 0.3% (w/v) congo red solution for 15 min, and the clear zone was measured after rinsing with 1 M NaCl37.

Antibacterial activity against P. larvae

The pathogen P. larvae responsible for American foulbrood disease (AFB) used in this study was obtained from the Korean Agricultural Culture Collection (KACC) with accession number KACC 11540. P. larvae was cultured aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h in MYPGP broth containing 1.0% (w/v) of Mueller–Hinton broth (KisanBio, Seoul, Republic of Korea), 1.5% (w/v) of yeast extract (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), 0.3% (w/v) of K2HPO4 (Samchun Pure Chemical, Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea), 0.1% (w/v) of sodium pyruvate (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan), and 0.2% (w/v) of glucose (Samchun Pure Chemical, Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea)38. Then, 100 μl of the cultured suspension was spread onto MYPGP agar plates. For antimicrobial activity testing, 0.25 g of SSFP was suspended in 1 ml of 1 × PBS, filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate filter using a syringe, and then 20 μl of the filtrate was dispensed onto paper discs for analysis. For the positive control, 20 μl of tetracycline solution at a concentration of 100 ppm was used. After treatment, all plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, and antimicrobial activity was determined by measuring the diameter of the clear zone of inhibition39. All experiments were performed at least three times independently.

DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging assay

Antioxidant activity was assessed using a colorimetric 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2′-Azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) tests were determined using the colorimetric microplate assay with simple modifications40,41. Briefly, 100 μL of 0.2 mM DPPH (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, US) solution dissolved in ethanol was mixed with 100 μL of SSFP suspension in a 96-well microplate and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader. For the ABTS assay, 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (Duksan, Seoul, South Korea) was mixed with 7 mM ABTS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio using 20 mM sodium acetate buffer and incubated in the dark for 14–16 h. The resulting solution was diluted with 70% ethanol to an absorbance of 0.7 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. For the assay, 100 μL of ABTS⁺ solution was mixed with 100 μL of SSFP suspension and incubated for 7 min at room temperature in the dark. Antioxidant activity was measured by the reduction in absorbance at 734 nm and expressed as the percentage inhibition of ABTS*⁺ oxidation. Each scavenging activity was calculated as: Scavenging activity (%) = [1 – (Asample – Ablank) / Acontrol] × 100.

Survival rate after freeze-drying

The SSFP was suspended in D.W. (10% w/v on a wet basis) and vortexed to get a homogenous suspension. The suspension sample was aliquoted to a 1.5 mL EP tube and frozen quickly in a − 80 °C deep freezer. After, freeze-dried was performed for 48 h using a lab-scale freeze dryer (IlSinBioBase, Yangju, Republic of Korea). Dried samples were stored at room temperature for a period of up to 34 weeks. For calculating the survival rates, samples were rehydrated to the original volume with D.W. and then spread onto the TSA plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the CFU was measured. The survival rate was calculated as follows: Survival rate (%) = [Samplefreeze-dried (log CFU/ml)/Samplecontrol (log CFU/ml) × 100].

Survival to simulated gastric and intestinal juices

To evaluate acid resistance, an acid tolerance assay was performed42. The OD600nm of the liquid-cultured strain was adjusted to 0.6 ± 0.02, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was completely removed, and the cell pellet was washed twice with 1 × PBS by repeating centrifugation under the same conditions. To prepare an acidic buffer, 1 × PBS was adjusted to pH 2.0 and 4.0 using 1 M HCl. The washed cells were resuspended in the acidic PBS, vortexed briefly, and incubated at 37 °C for up to 4 h, with samples collected at 1 h intervals. After incubation, the cells were again centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was washed twice with 1 × PBS as previously described. Finally, serial dilutions were prepared using 1 × PBS, and viable cell counts were determined by plating on TSA agar. For the control group, cells were treated with 1 × PBS (neutral pH) instead of pH 2.0 and 4.0 PBS, respectively.

To evaluate bile salt resistance, a bile tolerance assay was conducted43. The OD600nm of the liquid-cultured strain was adjusted to 0.6 ± 0.02, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was completely removed. The cells were then washed twice with 1 × PBS by repeating centrifugation under the same conditions. Bile salt solutions at concentrations of 0.3% and 1.0% (w/v) were prepared using Bile salts (Sigma, B-3883) in 1 × PBS. The washed cells were resuspended in the respective bile salt solutions, vortexed, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged again at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed twice more with 1 × PBS under the same conditions. Finally, serial dilutions were prepared in 1 × PBS and plated on TSA agar for CFU enumeration. For the control group, cells were treated with 1 × PBS without bile salts. The survival rates were calculated as follows: Survival rate (%) = [Sampletreated (log CFU/ml)/Samplecontrol (log CFU/ml) × 100].

Blood hemolysis and phospholipase assay

The hemolytic activity was determined by using TSA containing 5% (w/v) sheep blood (KisanBio, Seoul, Republic of Korea), and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the hemolytic activity of isolated strains was evaluated and classified on the basis of lysis of red blood cells in the medium around the colonies44. Phospholipase production screening was performed on TSA plus 5% (w/v) egg yolk (KisanBio, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Plates were incubated at 37 °C aerobically for 48 h. Phospholipase activity was confirmed by detecting the clear zone around colonies45.

Determination of antibiotic susceptibility

A 100 µL of culture broth with an optical density (OD600nm < 0.1) was spread onto TSA plates. To determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of selected isolates, commercial antibiotic E-test strips (Hi-Media Ltd., Mumbai, India) were applied. Each strip contained a concentration gradient ranging from 0.016 to 256 µg/mL. The test was conducted using eight clinically relevant antibiotics: vancomycin, gentamicin, kanamycin, streptomycin, erythromycin, clindamycin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol. Isolates were classified as resistant or susceptible according to the guidelines46,47.

Adhesion assay to Caco-2 cell lines

The Caco-2 cell used in this study was obtained from Korea Cell Line Bank (KCLB) with accession number KCLB 30,037.1. To assess cell surface hydrophobicity related to intestinal adhesion ability, the Caco-2 cell line was used48. Caco-2 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with FBS at a 9:1 (v/v) ratio until confluency in a T-75 flask. The cells were then seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow for cell attachment. The OD600nm of the liquid-cultured strain was adjusted to 0.4–0.5, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS by repeating centrifugation under the same conditions. The washed cells were then resuspended in 1 × PBS, and 1 mL of the suspension was added to each well of the 24-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2 h, and after incubation, non-adherent bacteria were removed by washing the wells three times with 1 × PBS. To lyse the Caco-2 cells and release the adhered bacteria, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (Sigma, S3443) in 1 × PBS was added and incubated for 10 min. The lysates were serially diluted in 1 × PBS and plated on TSA agar to determine CFUs49. For the control, bacterial suspension was added to wells without Caco-2 cells and processed identically. The adhesive rate was calculated as follows: Adhesive rate (%) = [Sampleadhesion (log CFU/ml)/Samplecontrol (log CFU/ml) × 100].

Culture optimization for increasing the biomass production

To enhance industrial applicability and economic feasibility for probiotics honeybee feed, culture conditions were optimized to improve biomass production efficiency. For cultivation, 100 mL of culture medium was added to a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask and incubated aerobically at 30 °C for 48 h at 200 rpm. The optimization was carried out by modifying the composition of the TSB medium. The optimization strategy followed a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach to identify the most suitable carbon sources, nitrogen sources, and inorganic salts, as well as their optimal concentrations. The nitrogen sources tested included yeast extract, beef extract, soytone, peptone, and tryptone at a concentration of 2.0% (w/v). Carbon sources tested included glucose, galactose, fructose, maltose, sucrose, lactose, and soluble starch at a concentration of 0.25% (w/v). For inorganic salts, K2HPO4, KH2PO4, KCl, MgSO4, and MgCl2 were evaluated at a concentration of 0.25% (w/v). To determine the optimal initial concentrations of nitrogen sources, carbon sources, and inorganic salts, experiments were conducted within the ranges of 1.0–6.0% (w/v) for nitrogen sources, 0.25–5.0% (w/v) for carbon sources, and 0.25–1.0% (w/v) for inorganic salts. Therefore, the optimized fermentation medium (OFM) was confirmed.

Scale-up fermentation for biomass production

To validate the OFM for large-scale production, strain MBLB2796 was cultured in a 5-L jar fermenter (KoBioTech, Incheon, South Korea). The strain was pre-cultured in a 14 mL Falcon tube with 5 mL of TSB at 30 °C for 24 h. For the preparation of the seed culture, 2% (w/v) of pre-culture was inoculated and cultivated in two 500-mL Erlenmeyer flasks, each containing 100 mL of optimized medium for 12 h. The seed cultures were then aseptically transferred into the fermenter containing 2.8 L of OFM. Agitation was initiated at 200 rpm and adjusted when the dissolved oxygen (DO) level dropped below 30%. The aeration rate was maintained at 1.0 vvm (volume of air per volume of medium per minute). The pH was controlled within the range of 6.7–7.3 using 28% (v/v) ammonia solution (Samchun, Pyeongtaek, Korea) and 6 N HCl. Antifoam 204 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added as needed to suppress foam formation. The fermentation process was carried out under the optimized conditions for 30 h.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates, each measured in triplicate. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test to determine significant differences between groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.4 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Microbial counts after solid-state fermentation

Microbiological monitoring of the SSFP was inoculated with individual MBLB2796, B. subtilis ST, and B. siamensis BK (Fig. 1a). After 72 h of fermentation, the viable count of the SSFP fermented by MBLB2796 was 2.31 × 1010 CFU/g, which was higher than that of the SSFP fermented by B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK, with the viable count of 9.67 × 109 CFU/g and 2.13 × 108 CFU/g, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The results indicated that MBLB2796 could serve cost-effective probiotic starter for honeybees compared to the previous commercial strain.

SDS-PAGE analysis using SSFP

In soybean, β-conglycinin accounts for approximately 40% of the total protein content and is primarily composed of α-, α′-, and β-subunits with molecular masses of 73 kDa, 68 kDa, and 52 kDa, respectively50. Glycinin, another major soybean storage protein, consists of acidic and basic subunits with molecular masses of 37 kDa and 20 kDa, respectively51. Trypsin inhibitor, a proteinaceous anti-nutritional factor found in soybean, has a molecular mass of about 21.5 kDa52. Compared to the unfermented control by SDS-PAGE, SSFP fermented by MBLB2796 showed a reduction in protein bands corresponding to β-conglycinin, glycinin, and trypsin inhibitor, similar to commercial species (Fig. 1b). These results indicate that allergenic proteins and anti-nutritional factors in soybean meal are effectively reduced through microbial solid-state fermentation, enhancing its suitability as a feed additive.

SSFP capable of protease, amylase, and cellulase activities

The hydrolysis enzyme activity values calculated from the clear zones on agar media supplemented with skim milk, available starch, and CMC indicate that MBLB2796 exhibits higher hydrolytic enzyme activity compared to the previous commercial strains (Table 1). The hydrolysis capacity (mm) of MBLB2796, which was calculated by the radius of the clear zone, was 15.5 mm of protease, 13.5 mm of amylase, and 15.0 mm of cellulase. The B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK showed lower, in which the values were 13.0 mm and 12.0 mm of protease, 13.0 mm and 11.0 mm of amylase, and 13.0 mm and 11.0 mm of cellulase, respectively.

Antimicrobial activity of SSFP against P. larvae

After 48 h of incubation using the paper disc assay, a clear inhibition zone was confirmed on P. larvae-inoculated agar plates when suspended and filtered SSFP samples were applied (Fig. 2a). SSFP fermented by MBLB2796 and other commercial strains exhibited antimicrobial activity against P. larvae. Their activities were lower than that of the positive control (100 ppm tetracycline), which showed a clear inhibition zone of 25.0 mm, but among the tested strains, MBLB2796 exhibited the largest inhibition zone (F value, 1,530; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2b). The diameter of the clear inhibition zone produced by SSFP fermented with MBLB2796 against P. larvae was 20.5 mm. In comparison, the commercial strains B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK exhibited inhibition zones of 19.0 mm and 16.5 mm, respectively.

Image of antimicrobial activity of fermented SSFP against P. larvae (a) and evaluation of clear zone (b). All experiments were performed at least three times independently. Different lowercase letters indicate statistical differences at P ≤ 0.05.

Antioxidant activity of SSFP against free radicals

The DPPH assay results demonstrated that SSFP fermented by MBLB2796, as well as by commercial strains, exhibited measurable radical scavenging activity. All activities were lower than that of the LGG (80.2%). MBLB2796 showed the highest antioxidant activity among the tested strains, with a scavenging rate of 66.3% (F value, 44.63; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3a). In comparison, B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK exhibited lower activities of 47.7% and 56.6%, respectively. Similarly, the ABTS assay showed that the scavenging levels against ABTS radical were 70.4%, 40.6%, 52.2%, and 62.3% when the samples were LGG, ST, BK, and MBLB2796 (F value, 43.58; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3b). These results suggest that the SSFP suspension exhibits antioxidant activity, with the formulation fermented by MBLB2796 demonstrating the highest effectiveness compared with commercial strains.

In vitro antioxidant activity of fermented SSFP against DPPH (a) and ABTS (b). All experiments were performed at least three times independently. Different lowercase letters indicate statistical differences at P < 0.05.

Viability and storage stability of freeze-dried SSFP

SSFP fermented with MBLB2796 maintained a maximum survival rate of 94.4% immediately after freeze-drying, even without the addition of protective agents, indicating that cell viability was not significantly affected by freezing conditions (Fig. 4). Similarly, B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK exhibited survival rates of up to 98.7% and 84.4%, respectively, confirming that members of the B. subtilis possess inherent advantages for survival following freeze-drying. During storage at room temperature for up to 34 weeks, SSFP fermented with MBLB2796 retained a high level of viability, with a minimum survival rate of 93.7%, demonstrating excellent long-term stability. Likewise, B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK maintained minimum survival rates of 96.0% and 83.0%, respectively, throughout the storage period, further supporting the freeze-drying stability of Bacillus strains. These results indicate that MBLB2796 exhibits comparable or superior survival and storage stability to commercial strains, even in the absence of added cryoprotectants.

Survival rates of bacterial species from immediately after freeze-drying up to 34 weeks of storage, compared to pre-freeze-drying levels. All experiments were performed at least three times independently.

Resistant test of strain MBLB2796

The ability of probiotics to withstand stomach acid and bile is a critical determinant of their survival in the gastrointestinal tract. In the acid tolerance test, the survival rate of all strains showed a decreasing trend with increasing exposure time to low pH conditions (Fig. 5a). All Bacillus spp. used in the experiment exhibited weaker acid resistance compared to LGG. MBLB2796 maintained a survival rate of over 60% when exposed to low pH conditions for up to 3 h, but the survival rate decreased to 55.0% after 4 h of exposure. B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK exhibited slightly lower resistance to low pH, with survival rates remaining below 60% throughout the entire exposure period. Similar results were obtained under acidic conditions (pH 4), which mimic the intestinal environment of honeybees (Fig. 5b). All strains exhibited a gradual decline in viability over time, with LGG maintaining the highest survival across all time points and BK the lowest. ST and MBLB2796 displayed comparable survival trends, with MBLB2796 maintaining viability of approximately 84.4%, 77.4%, 72.1%, and 70.2% at an interval of 1 h.

Survival rates of bacterial species following exposure to pH 2 condition (a), pH 4 condition (b), and bile salt concentrations (c). All experiments were performed at least three times independently.

At the 0.3% bile salt conditions, MBLB2796 showed similar survival rates to B. subtilis ST and LGG. MBLB2796 showed good tolerance with a survival rate of 83.3% after 2 h incubation (Fig. 5c). Although this is lower than that of LGG, it is higher than the survival rates of B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK. Under 1.0% bile salt conditions, the CFU of the MBLB2796 decreased to 74.9% compared to that under 0.3% bile salt, but this value remained higher than those of the other comparison strains, except for LGG.

In vitro biosafety assessment

In the hemolysis assay, MBLB2796 did not exhibit any clear or greenish zones on the agar plate, confirming that the strain demonstrates γ-hemolysis (non-hemolytic activity). In the phospholipase assay, MBLB2796 showed weak enzymatic activity.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that MBLB2796 was sensitive to eight clinically relevant antibiotics, with minimum inhibitory concentrations (µg/mL) as follows: vancomycin (0.38), gentamicin (0.5), kanamycin (2), streptomycin (8), erythromycin (0.75), clindamycin (0.75), tetracycline (1.5), and chloramphenicol (2).

Cell surface hydrophobicity for adhesion

The present study evaluated the adhesion capacity of Bacillus spp. to Caco-2 cell lines. All Bacillus strains exhibited lower adhesion levels compared to LGG. However, MBLB2796 demonstrated a 74.4% adhesion rate, which was not significantly different from that of the control strain LGG (76.2%) on Caco-2 cells (F value, 13.11; p = 0.0043) (Fig. 6). The commercial strain B. siamensis BK also showed no significant difference in adhesion (71.8%) compared to MBLB2796. In contrast, B. subtilis ST displayed a significantly lower adhesion capacity than the experimental strains used in this study. These results suggest that MBLB2796 has strong potential as a probiotic starter for honeybee applications, surpassing previously used commercial strains.

Culture conditions optimization for biomass production of MBLB2796

The optimal nitrogen source, carbon source, and inorganic salt for MBLB2796 were identified using the OFAT approach, and the growth of strain MBLB2796 was further optimized according to the concentration of media components. When 1.7% (w/v) casitone and 0.3% (w/v) soytone in the basic TSB medium were replaced with various nitrogen sources at 2.0% (w/v), biomass production was higher with yeast extract and soytone (F value, 88.45; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7a). Two nitrogen sources were significantly proper source compared to beef extract, tryptone, and peptone. Although yeast extract did not result in significantly higher biomass compared to the basic TSB medium, it yielded the highest biomass of 2.3 g/L dry cell weight (DCW) among the tested nitrogen sources. Therefore, it was selected for further evaluation of biomass production based on nitrogen source concentration. The efficiency of biomass production reached 2.7–3.0 g/L DCW at an initial yeast extract concentration of 3.0–4.0% (w/v), which was significantly higher than other concentrations (F value, 128.7; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7b). This increase represented up to 29% higher biomass production compared to the culture with 2.0% yeast extract. However, yeast extract concentrations below or above this range did not significantly enhance biomass production during the initial growth phase of MBLB2796.

Optimization of biomass production using a one-factor-at-a-time approach. The effects of nitrogen sources (a), yeast extract concentrations (g/L) (b), carbon sources (c), galactose concentrations (g/L) (d), inorganic salts (e), and K₂HPO₄ concentration (g/L) (f) on biomass production were evaluated under shake flask conditions at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h. All experiments were performed at least three times independently. Different lowercase letters indicate statistical differences at P < 0.05.

The biomass production was significantly increased when basal TSB (0.25% glucose) was replaced with 0.25% galactose, fructose, and lactose (F value, 49.68; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7c). Especially, galactose supplement achieved the highest biomass production about 1.44 times (3.0 g/L DCW) compared to TSB cultivation. Biomass production exceeded 4.7 g/L DCW when the initial galactose concentration was greater than 0.5% (F value, 182.5; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7d). No statistically significant differences in biomass production were observed across the galactose concentration range of 0.5–2.0%, but when supplementation with 1.0% galactose resulted in approximately 80% higher biomass production (5.5 g/L DCW) compared to the culture supplemented with 0.25% galactose.

Biomass production of MBLB2796 in response to different inorganic salts was highest when 0.25% K2HPO4, as included in the standard TSB medium, was added (F value, 698.3; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7e). In contrast, media containing other potassium or magnesium salts resulted in significantly lower biomass levels compared to the original medium, except for the medium supplemented with KCl. Furthermore, varying the concentration of K2HPO4 did not lead to significant changes in biomass production, indicating that 0.25% was sufficient for optimal growth (F value, 0.3718; p = 0.7788) (Fig. 7f). Through the OFAT-based optimization, biomass production of MBLB2796 cultured in OFM was enhanced by approximately 2.6-fold compared to the original TSB medium.

Large-scale fermentation of MBLB2796 as a probiotic feed additive starter

Batch fermentation under OFAT conditions was conducted to evaluate the feasibility of applying the OFM for large-scale biomass production. MBLB2796 was successfully transferred to a 5-L fermentation using OFM (Fig. 8a). Biomass production increased rapidly during the logarithmic growth phase up to 6 h, followed by a steady growth phase from 6 to 15 h, reaching a maximum 11.8 g/L DCW at 18 h and decline in biomass production was observed after 18 h (Fig. 8b). The initial galactose concentration was completely consumed by 12 h of fermentation, during which DO levels dropped sharply in the logarithmic growth phase and gradually increased after galactose depletion. Finally, batch fermentation using the OFM resulted in a 30-h reduction in cultivation time and a 5.10-fold increase in biomass production compared to flask culture in TSB medium over 48 h. Moreover, biomass production was 2.12-fold higher than that achieved in flask-level culture using OFM, demonstrating the successful scale-up of MBLB2796 fermentation.

Discussion

The gut microbiota plays a fundamental role in honeybee growth, development, immune regulation, and defense against pathogen invasion53. A balanced microbial community is essential for maintaining bee health. Moreover, the composition of the gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a biomarker of colony well-being7,54. The use of probiotics in apiculture is gaining traction as a sustainable approach to enhance colony health, especially in the face of increasing environmental stressors and disease pressure. In this study, B. subtilis MBLB2796, a strain isolated from the gut of Apis mellifera, demonstrates considerable promise as a probiotic candidate tailored for honeybee systems. In social insects, such as the honey bees, the intestinal bacteria are transmitted and shared by colony members through oral–fecal and trophallactic transmission55. By supporting a wide range of aspects, strain MBLB2796 can contribute to healthier hives, which is crucial for consistent pollination services and the production of high-quality bee products such as honey, royal jelly, and propolis56,57. For instance, bees inoculated with beneficial microbes like B. subtilis may act as vectors of microbial dispersal, potentially introducing probiotic species to floral environments and plant surfaces58. This could have effects on plant–microbe interactions, pollinator-associated microbiomes, and even soil health, suggesting a symbiotic bridge between managed bee health and environmental microbiology59. Its potential extends not only to improving honeybee performance but also to contributing to the sustainability of broader agroecological systems.

SSFP using MBLB2796 effectively reduces allergenic proteins and anti-nutritional factors in soybean meal, enhancing its suitability as a feed additive. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed degradation of major soybean allergens, including β-conglycinin, glycinin, and the trypsin inhibitor, following fermentation with MBLB2796. These results align with previous studies demonstrating that microbial fermentation can diminish such proteins, thereby improving the nutritional quality of soybean meal60,61. This degradation is primarily attributed to proteolytic enzymes produced by Bacillus species, which hydrolyze complex proteins into smaller peptides and amino acids, reducing allergenicity and enhancing digestibility62. Additionally, the reduction of trypsin inhibitors facilitates better protein utilization, as these compounds can impair digestive enzyme function63. The fermentation process also enriches soybean meal with bioactive peptides and free amino acids, improving both palatability and nutritional value. Such enhancements are particularly beneficial in animal nutrition, where feed efficiency and nutrient absorption are critical64.

SSFP fermented by MBLB2796 exhibited high extracellular enzymatic activity, notably in the degradation of protein and carbohydrate substrates, which was greater than or comparable to the activities of B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK. These enzyme activities are advantageous for a probiotic intended to support digestion. Bacillus species are well known for secreting diverse hydrolases that break down complex macromolecules65,66. These enzymes can improve nutrient availability and digestion in the host. For example, B. subtilis in the honeybee gut has been shown to produce amylase, contributing to nectar sugar breakdown67. The enhanced amylolytic and proteolytic activities of MBLB2796 suggest it may help honeybees more efficiently utilize components of supplemental feed, such as pollen substitutes or sugar syrups.

Although the exact antimicrobial metabolites of MBLB2796 were not identified in this study, extensive evidence suggests that Bacillus-derived lipopeptides are the primary contributors to antagonistic activity against P. larvae. In particular, surfactins and iturins have been shown to disrupt bacterial membranes and inhibit honeybee pathogens68,69. Moreover, Bacillus strain antagonistic to P. larvae has been repeatedly associated with lipopeptide-mediated inhibition70. Taken together, these support the hypothesis that the antimicrobial activity observed for MBLB2796 is most likely attributable to the production of lipopeptides such as surfactin and iturin.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that microbial fermentation significantly improves the antioxidant properties of soybean-based substrates. This is largely attributed to the microbial conversion of inactive or bound forms of phytochemicals into more bioavailable and bioactive compounds during fermentation71,72. In particular, fermentation has been shown to increase the total phenolic content and isoflavone aglycone levels, both of which are known for their strong antioxidant properties27,73. Enzymes secreted by microbes, such as amylases, proteases, and cellulases, play a key role in degrading complex macromolecules and breaking down plant cell walls, thereby releasing bound phenolics and other bioactive molecule74. Previous studies have shown that B. subtilis fermentation of soybeans significantly increases total phenolic content, along with notable rises in total flavonoid content, particularly within the free phenolic fraction75,76. In our study, SSFP using B. subtilis MBLB2796 showed notably higher antioxidant activity than that of commercial Bacillus strains. These results are in agreement with previous reports that demonstrate a substantial rise in DPPH radical scavenging activity following Bacillus-mediated soybean fermentation75,76. The enhanced antioxidant activity observed in SSFP fermented with MBLB2796 may be attributed to the strain’s superior enzymatic capability. Further studies on long-term feeding trials and in vivo antioxidant effects will be essential to validate the functional potential of MBLB2796-fermented SSFP.

A fundamental criterion for probiotics is surviving the transit through the hostile gut environment. Although honeybees lack stomach acid and bile systems comparable to those of mammals, they are nonetheless exposed to acidic conditions within fermented pollen (bee bread), which arises from lactic acid fermentation by symbiotic microbes. Additionally, honeybees encounter various plant secondary compounds derived from nectar and pollen. Rangeland plants produce a wide range of secondary metabolites that can exert deleterious or beneficial effects on foraging organisms, including pollinators77. Elevated levels of these metabolites in plant tissues often correlate with their concentrations in nectar, potentially increasing the chemical exposure78. Certain plant-derived substances can affect bee health by altering immune responses or causing toxicity at high concentrations77,79. Moreover, several studies have reported that the pH of bee bread ranges from 3.49 to 4.04, indicating a notably acidic environment due to microbial fermentation80. While the commercial strains B. subtilis ST and B. siamensis BK also survived under these conditions, MBLB2796 demonstrated a superior survival rate in acidic conditions, both pH 2 and pH 4. These results are consistent with the spore-forming nature of B. subtilis, as spores can be resistant to extreme acidity and other stresses81,82.

The adhesion capacity of probiotic strains to intestinal epithelial cells is a critical determinant of their colonization efficiency and subsequent health benefits to the host. In this study, B. subtilis MBLB2796 exhibited a notable adhesion rate of 74.4% to Caco-2 cells, which was statistically comparable to the well-established probiotic strain LGG (76.2%). These results suggest the potential of MBLB2796 as a viable probiotic candidate, especially considering its performance relative to commercial strains. Several studies have shown that Bacillus strains with higher hydrophobicity and auto-aggregation capacities tend to exhibit stronger adhesion to epithelial cells83,84. Hydrophobic interactions facilitate the initial contact between bacterial cells and the mucosal surface. Spore-forming Bacillus species also add another dimension to their adhesion properties. Spores are known to adhere more robustly to surfaces, including epithelial cells, compared to their vegetative counterparts85,86. This enhanced adhesion is attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the spore surface and the presence of specific structural components87. Therefore, generally spore-forming B. subtilis may confer additional advantages in terms of persistence and colonization within the gastrointestinal tract. Further studies are necessary to validate this adhesion capacity to honeybees and explore the probiotic potential of MBLB2796.

From an industrial application, the viability of a probiotic after formulation is crucial88. Strain MBLB2796 was similar to or higher than the survival rates of commercial strains without cryoprotectant. The addition of a cryoprotectant during freeze-drying increases both production cost and process complexity89. The high survival rate of MBLB2796 without the need for a cryoprotectant suggests potential for process simplification and cost reduction in future industrial applications. This is in agreement with the inherent robustness of Bacillus spores. Wild-type B. subtilis spores are known to survive multiple freeze-drying cycles with minimal loss of viability90. In our study, a single freeze-drying cycle reduced the viable counts only marginally for all strains, and MBLB2796 in particular maintained viability well within the acceptable range. This excellent stability means that MBLB2796 can be processed into a dry formulation and stored for extended periods without significant loss of effectiveness. For beekeepers or feed manufacturers, this translates to a reliable shelf life and ease of handling in the field. Additionally, the resilience of MBLB2796 suggests that it could be incorporated into various feed formats and remain viable until consumed by the honeybees.

Efficient and scalable biomass production of probiotic strains is critical for their commercial application, particularly as feed additives. In this study, the optimization of culture medium components for B. subtilis MBLB2796 was systematically carried out using an OFAT approach, resulting in substantial improvements in biomass production and process efficiency. Among nitrogen sources, yeast extract was identified as the most effective, consistent with previous reports on Bacillus spp. Yeast extract contains peptides, amino acids, and growth factors that facilitate rapid microbial growth and secondary metabolite production91,92. Soytone also supported high biomass production, aligning with earlier studies that highlight the advantages of mixed organic nitrogen sources over inorganic alternatives91. These results emphasize the crucial role of peptide-rich organic nitrogen sources in optimizing Bacillus growth.

Previous studies have demonstrated that galactose significantly enhances extracellular enzyme production, including cellulase and protease. For instance, B. subtilis exhibited a 22% contribution of galactose to total cellulase activity in a defined medium, while B. stearothermophilus showed improved growth and protease production when cultured with 0.75% galactose93,94. In addition to serving as a preferred carbon source, galactose also acts as a regulatory molecule that promotes enzyme expression, facilitates exopolysaccharide synthesis for enhanced colonization and stress tolerance, and modulates transcriptional pathways associated with cell wall remodeling and quorum sensing95–97. The Leloir pathway enables galactose catabolism by converting it into UDP-galactose98. Galactose is first phosphorylated by GalK to form galactose-1-phosphate, which is then converted to UDP-galactose by GalT, producing glucose-1-phosphate as a byproduct. This glucose-1-phosphate can enter glycolysis after conversion to glucose-6-phosphate, thereby contributing to energy metabolism. B. subtilis can utilize galactose not only for cell growth but also for various biosynthetic pathways99.

Inorganic salts are also essential for microbial physiology, contributing to osmoregulation, enzyme activation, and phosphate metabolism. In this study, K2HPO4 was identified as the most effective inorganic salt for biomass production of MBLB2796, with maximum growth observed at 0.25% supplementation. This is consistent with previous findings where K2HPO4 enhanced keratinase production in B. subtilis, particularly when combined with MgSO4, suggesting synergistic effects in enzyme biosynthesis100. Although K2HPO4 was not the primary growth determinant in orthogonal studies of compound Bacillus strains, it still contributed positively when used in conjunction with optimized carbon and nitrogen sources.

The successful scale-up of B. subtilis MBLB2796 using OFM in a 5-L batch bioreactor demonstrated high adaptability and productivity of the strain under controlled conditions. Moreover, biomass yield was more than doubled relative to OFM flask cultivation, indicating improved mass transfer and environmental control at a large scale. Batch fermentation remains a widely adopted strategy for industrial B. subtilis applications due to its operational simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and reproducibility. For instance, pullulanase production by B. subtilis reached 896 U/mL in batch culture using inexpensive substrates such as corn starch and corn steep liquor101. Similarly, batch fermentation was effectively applied in the production of keratinase and nattokinase, achieving notable yields through medium optimization97,102. These examples demonstrate the practical viability of batch systems for high-yield bioproduction in Bacillus species and can suggest successful application to probiotic biomass production. However, batch fermentation is inherently limited by substrate depletion and product inhibition. To further enhance biomass production and process efficiency, fed-batch operation could be considered.

Conclusion

B. subtilis MBLB2796 demonstrated promising in vitro characteristics associated with probiotic functionality, showing comparable or superior performance to commercial strains across multiple assays. SSFP fermented by MBLB2796 exhibited strong biological activity, including robust hydrolytic enzyme production and effective degradation of allergenic proteins, suggesting improved nutritional quality and digestibility. The strain also showed notable antimicrobial activity against P. larvae and enhanced antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, MBLB2796 displayed high survival under acidic and gut-mimicking conditions, strong adhesion to Caco-2 cells, and excellent stability after freeze-drying without cryoprotectants. Its suitability for scale-up through optimized cultivation further supports its industrial applicability. Collectively, these in vitro findings indicate that MBLB2796 is a promising probiotic candidate for future evaluation in honeybees; however, in vivo studies are required to confirm its efficacy and functional benefits at the individual and colony levels.
