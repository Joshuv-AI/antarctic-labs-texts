Perinatal mental health management level of obstetric healthcare workers and influencing factors: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Qian Fang, Zhen Luo, Lu Lin, Aiying Jin, Huimin Guo, Li Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstetric healthcare workers (OHWs) play an important role in managing perinatal mental health (PMH) problems of perinatal women. This study aims to examine the characteristics of different obstetric healthcare workers subgroups in terms of PMH management level and identify its influencing factors to enhance the prevention and treatment efforts of OHWs.MethodsFrom March to May 2021, a total of 1010 OHWs with professional qualification certificates (obstetricians, obstetric nurses, and midwives) from 34 hospitals participated in this cross-sectional study. The response rate was 93.35%. Latent characteristics and their influencing factors were analyzed using latent profile analysis and multivariable logistic regression with SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3.ResultsThe level of PMH management of obstetric health care workers in this study could be divided into a Low PMHQ group and a High PMHQ group with a two-profile model. The level of PMH management in the Low PMHQ group (81.78%) was relatively low and accounted for a large proportion. The high PMHQ group (18.22%) had a high level of PMH management. There were significant differences in the scores of PMH management between the two groups, especially in the three dimensions of the Perinatal Illness Perception Scale. Perinatal mental health training is an essential factor affecting the PMH management level of obstetric healthcare workers (OR = 0.662, P=0.019).ConclusionsMost obstetric healthcare workers had a relatively low level of PMH management, especially since their knowledge of perinatal mental illness was weak. Medical authorities must focus on strengthening the training of obstetric healthcare workers and improving the management level of obstetric healthcare workers.

Introduction

Perinatal mental health (PMH) refers to the state of mental health from pregnancy to within one year after childbirth [1]. Perinatal mental health problems (PMHPs) refer to a series of psychological problems that may be encountered by women during this period, ranging from mild anxiety and depression to severe mental health problems [1, 2].

Studies have shown that PMHPs can increase the incidence of obstetric diseases and even lead to adverse birth outcomes [3, 4] and impaired mother-child attachment [5]. It will impose a heavy burden on mothers and babies, families, health systems, and society. Therefore, timely identification, treatment, and management of PMHPs are essential. However, many perinatal women do not realize that they have PMHPs or that they need treatment; they are unwilling to receive treatment for reasons such as fear of separation from their children and discrimination [6, 7]. At the same time, relatives and friends of perinatal women lack the relevant knowledge and skills to provide practical help and support to perinatal women [6, 8].

Obstetric healthcare workers (OHWs) are healthcare providers with whom perinatal women have regular contact. They have a better opportunity to identify and provide support for their mental health problems [9]. Studies have proven that perinatal women are more likely to receive mental health support from OHWs than professional mental health service providers [10]. Therefore, OHWs need to play an essential role in the prevention and treatment of PMHPs: perform screening and assessment to identify high-risk groups [11]; provide counselling services to help perinatal women learn about the support channels and resources available [12]; provide early intervention and proper referral for women with PMHPs [13], etc. The effective performance of these roles requires OHWs to have the awareness, knowledge, and ability to identify and manage PMHPs.

Currently, relevant studies in China and abroad have focused on investigating the level of knowledge and identification ability about common perinatal mental illnesses (postpartum depression, schizophrenia) among OHWs [14–17]. The lack of investigation into the awareness and knowledge regarding PMHPs and the perception of professional issues associated with the provision of PMH care of OHWs in a broader sense has led to the vast majority of managers and practitioners in healthcare institutions failing to pay sufficient attention to PMHPs or lacking the evidence to promote relevant changes. The qualitative research findings of Bains et al. indicated that barriers to PMH services are related to healthcare personnel’s awareness of PMH services [18]. Therefore, understanding their awareness level and influencing factors will be conducive to improving PMH services.

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) is a robust mixed-model technique commonly used to identify subtypes of homogeneous latent classes or subgroups in large heterogeneous populations [19]. In recent years, LPA has been applied in different fields, such as psychology, and medicine, to identify unobserved subpopulations [20]. Therefore, LPA was used in this study to explore the characteristics of the level of PMH management in OHWs.

In summary, this study aimed to address the following research questions: (a) What are the characteristics of the level of PMH management in OHWs (in terms of perinatal mental illness knowledge, related professional issues, and perinatal mental health awareness)? (b) What are the influencing factors for different categories of PMH management levels? By addressing these questions, we aim to provide a basis for improving the PMH management levels of OHWs and developing targeted intervention strategies or policies.

Methods

Design, setting and procedure

From March to May 2021, OHWs (obstetricians, obstetric nurses, and midwives) from 34 hospitals in Jiangsu Province and Shanghai participated in this cross-sectional study. The sample size should be 15 ~ 20 times the number of items, with a 10% increase to account for potential attrition. Therefore, the minimum sample size was estimated to be 690 ~ 920 [21]. Sojump.com (Questionnaire Star, a professional online survey, evaluation, and voting platform) was used to administer the survey. The questionnaire link was distributed via work email to the OHWs. Participants were fully aware of the purpose of the study and volunteered to participate. All questionnaires in this study were collected anonymously, and the data were used only for this study, which has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (No. SUDA20200225H09). Participants were required to complete all questions before submission, with an average completion time of approximately 15 min.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) obstetricians, obstetric nurses, and midwives with professional qualification certificates and (ii) informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. Staff on leave during the survey period, interns, and trainees were excluded. To ensure the quality of the questionnaires, we excluded those completed within 5 min. A total of 1080 OHWs were surveyed, with 1010 valid, resulting in an effective response rate of 93.35%.

Measurements

Sociodemographic questionnaire

The sociodemographic questionnaire included sex, age, occupation, years of working in obstetrics, professional title, experience of PMHPs of relatives or friends or oneself, and experience of caring for patients with PMHPs, etc.

Chinese version of perinatal mental health questionnaire (PMHQ-C)

The PMHQ-C consists of the Perinatal Illness Perceptions Scale (PIPS) [22], the Professional Issues in Maternal Mental Health Scale (PIMMHS) [23], and the Perinatal Mental Health Awareness (PMHA) [24]. This study used the Chinese version of PMHQ, previously translated by our research team [25, 26]. The results of reliability and validity test showed that the PMHQ-C had good construct validity, content validity, internal consistency and test-retest reliability, making it a reliable tool for comprehensively evaluating the level of PMH management among OHWs [25, 26].

The perinatal illness perceptions scale (PIPS)

PIPS [22] comprises three sub-scales: Causes, Consequences (Mother), and Consequences (Baby). Each item was assessed on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3. The higher the score, the higher the knowledge level of PMHPs. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the PIPS was 0.873.

The professional issues in maternal mental health scale (PIMMHS)

PIMMHS [23] was used to assess the participants’ views on professional issues while engaging in perinatal health care services. The PIMMHS scale comprises seven items, including the PIMMHS-emotion and training sub-scales. The higher the score, the lower the emotional burden related to their current knowledge and skills, and the feeling that the training they have received is enough to support them to carry out perinatal mental health care services, which will have a positive impact on the provision of appropriate perinatal mental health care and support. The items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 to 4. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the PIMMHS scale was 0.810.

The perinatal mental health awareness scale (PMHA)

PMHA [24] was designed to assess the PMH awareness of PMH service providers. The PMHA scale consists of 9 items and includes three correlated sub-scales: (a) knowledge, (b) confidence in identifying and (c) confidence in managing PMHPs. It uses a 4-point Likert scale with a score ranging from 0 to 3. The higher the score, the higher the level of perinatal mental health awareness. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the PMHA scale was 0.945.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 26.0 was used for descriptive statistics. Using Mplus 8.3, 8 subscales of 3 dimensions of the PMH management level of the OHWs were used as explicit variables for latent profile analysis. The smaller the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and Adjusted BIC (ABIC), the better the model fit [27]. The closer the entropy value is to 1, the better the fitting degree of the model is [28]. Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) and Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) can compare the fitting effect of two adjacent models, and P < 0.05 indicates that the k model has a better fitting effect than the k-1 model, with k equaling the number of classes [29]. In addition, the proportion of categories should be considered, and the proportion of people in each category should be at least 5%. Otherwise, the classification is not reasonable [30].

Cohen’s d illustrates the difference in the standardized amplitude of PMHQ-C dimensions in the LPA profile, and >0.80 indicates a large effect [31]. When analyzing the factors that influence the profile of PMH management level, the χ2 test was used for univariate analysis first, and the statistically significant factors in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Profile model selection

In this study, latent profile models of 1 to 5 classes were fitted based on the latent profile analysis of the eight subscales of the PMHQ. As shown in Fig. 1, the values of AIC, BIC, and ABIC became smaller as the number of categories increased, but the two-profile model decrease the most in AIC, BIC, and ABIC. Meanwhile, the Entropy value of the two-profile model was the highest (0.972), and both LMR and BLRT were significant, indicating that it had good classification accuracy and fitting. The two-profile model accounted for more than 5% of each category. Therefore, the two-profile model was considered the best potential profile model for PMHQ-C. Meanwhile, the two class probabilities of the two-profile model patterns were 99.5% and 98.0%, respectively, indicating good discrimination and reliable results of LPA analysis.

Scores of perinatal mental health management level

Table 2 shows the specific scores of each dimension of the overall PMH management level of the OHWs. In general, the level of PMH management of the OHWs was medium. The scores of each subscale in the High PMHQ group were significantly greater than those in the Low PMHQ group, with a large effect size, especially in the PIPS dimension (d2−1= 31.485 ~ 48.760).

Influencing factors of perinatal mental health management level

Univariate analysis showed that the two profiles of PMHQ were statistically different in terms of age, occupation, professional title, hospital grade, hospital nature, whether it was a teaching hospital, whether it had received training related to perinatal mental health, and experience in caring for patients with perinatal mental illness (Table 1). These variables were included in the multivariate analysis, with the Low PMHQ group as the reference group, as shown in Table 3, only whether they had received training related to perinatal mental health still had a significant effect, indicating that trained OHWs had a higher level of perinatal mental health (P = 0.019).

Discussion

This study is the first to apply LPA in a large sample of Chinese OHWs, using a validated Chinese version scale (PMHQ-C) to comprehensively assess PMH management competence across perinatal mental illness knowledge, related professional issues, and perinatal mental health awareness. The role of perinatal mental health training in improving OHWs’ awareness and perception of PMH health service providers and the perception of professional issues related to the delivery of PMH care was highlighted. This study will help improve OHWs’ PMH management level and provide a basis for more scientific and reasonable formulation of relevant intervention strategies or policies.

Category 1 was the Low PMHQ group, accounting for 81.78% of the OHWs in this study, and its scores in the eight subscales of three dimensions of PMH management were lower than those of category 2 High PMHQ group (only 18.22%). This showed that the overall level of OHWs’ PMH management was relatively low (high and low here were relative; low referred to low scores in the Low PMHQ group relative to the High PMHQ group in this study sample), that awareness and knowledge of PMH and professional issues related to the provision of PMH care need to be further improved, and that these findings are generally consistent with previous research [3, 10, 12, 13, 32].

There were significant effect differences between the two groups (d2−1=4.830 ~ 48.760), especially in the three dimensions of PIPS, which include the causes, consequences (Mother), and consequences (Baby) of perinatal mental illness. This indicated that the Low PMHQ group had incomplete cognition of perinatal mental illness, ignored the role of some risk factors in PMHPs, and lacked an understanding of the harm that PMHPs may cause to mothers and infants. In contrast, OHWs in the High PMHQ group had more comprehensive knowledge, allowing them to identify high-risk mothers earlier and provide timely management. This is generally consistent with previous studies [3, 13, 33]. A systematic review of 20 studies showed that screening for PMHPs is hampered by poor understanding of risk factors [34], which may lead to underdiagnosis of perinatal women with risk factors, thereby affecting the timely implementation of preventive measures. Lack of understanding of the consequences of PMHPs will hinder the prioritization of PMH in clinical practice for OHWs, leading to further patient deterioration [13].

The results of the multivariate analysis showed that only the training related to perinatal mental health still had a significant effect, which was consistent with the differences in PIPS scores. Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which emphasizes the reciprocal interplay among cognitive factors, environmental influences, and self-efficacy [35], provides a useful framework for understanding such variations. In the context of this study, OHWs’ behavior in delivering PMH services is shaped by environmental influences (e.g., whether they have received relevant training), cognitive factors (their perception of PMHPs), and self-efficacy (their confidence in providing PMH services). Specifically, the Low PMHQ group often lack systematic training, resulting in limited awareness of PMHPs and reduced self-efficacy, which lowers their confidence in identifying and managing PMHPs in clinical practice. By contrast, the High PMHQ group usually receive relevant training, which enhances their knowledge, strengthens their self-efficacy, and increases their willingness and capability to provide PMH management in daily work. The results affirmed the role of PMH training in improving perinatal mental health and were similar to previous findings [12, 36, 37]. A review showed that midwives’ lack of training, knowledge, and confidence in perinatal mental health is one of the barriers to providing perinatal mental health care [38]. Training, expanded scope of practice, and collaborative care are central to successful screening, management, and appropriate and timely referral for perinatal mental health conditions [34]. Therefore, hospitals should place greater emphasis on developing competencies in PMH management and provide tiered, targeted training according to the actual PMH management levels of OHWs. For the Low PMHQ group, training should focus on basic knowledge dissemination, risk identification, and clinical skills. The training content should include methods for recognizing and assessing common PMHPs, as well as communication skills, referral procedures, and practical cases of multidisciplinary collaboration. Regular lectures, simulation exercises, and case-based discussions can effectively improve OHWs’ ability to identify risks and their confidence in interventions. For the High PMHQ group, they should play a demonstrative and leading role in clinical practice. In addition, hospitals should integrate PMH management into routine maternal healthcare services and establish standardized screening and referral pathways to facilitate the early identification and intervention for high-risk women. Such measures may enhance the quality of PMH services and ultimately improve the psychological well-being and quality of life of perinatal women.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths stemming from using validated instruments to examine OHWs’ PMH management level. Additionally, LPA was applied to reveal heterogeneity within the groups which can provide a basis for designing stratified intervention strategies. However, this study still has the following limitations. Firstly, the vast majority of the respondents in this study were from a province and a municipality directly under the central government in East China, so the generalizability of the findings was limited to a certain extent. Secondly, the participants of this study are predominantly female, with a significant gender bias in the sample composition. This limits the applicability of the study results across different gender groups to a certain extent and may make it difficult to widely generalize the findings to a more comprehensive population that includes males. Future studies may adopt multi-center stratified sampling to address the insufficient representativeness caused by geographical barriers. Meanwhile, the proportion of male participants should be increased to enhance the generalizability and representativeness of the research.

Conclusion

This study identified the characteristics of the Low PMHQ group and the High PMHQ group based on LPA. There were significant differences in the scores of perinatal mental health between the two groups, especially in the three dimensions of PIPS. We highlighted the importance of perinatal mental health training in improving OHWs’ level of PMH management. It strongly supports the development of more scientific and reasonable intervention strategies or policies. This was of great significance in ensuring the health of mothers and babies and improving the quality of obstetric healthcare services.
