HeLP-BAG score: a novel data-driven scoring system for predicting post-operative 30-day mortality using 24-hour preoperative data
Authors: Hyun Sik Kim, Hyo Seop Shin, Jinhyong Goh, Seung Ho Jun, Chang Hyun Lee, Joo Heung Yoon, Choon Hee Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate preoperative risk stratification remains challenging, as existing scoring systems are often complex, invasive, or limited to specific patient populations. We aimed to develop a simple, interpretable, and broadly applicable risk score to screen for 30-day postoperative mortality using routinely available variables.MethodsWe developed the HeLP-BAG score using three large surgical cohorts. To reduce demographic bias, cohorts were matched by age and sex, yielding 24,617 male and female patients per dataset. Clinical data collected within 24 h before surgery were used as inputs, and 30-day postoperative mortality was defined as the outcome. Candidate variables were selected based on clinical relevance, statistical separation, monotonicity, noninvasiveness, and general applicability. Change points were identified using a change-point detection algorithm and refined through clinical consensus. Model performance was evaluated in a combined validation cohort and compared with ASA-PS, seven established scoring systems, and five machine learning models using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). ICU admission was additionally assessed using a post-hoc weighting strategy.ResultsTen variables were included in the final score: ICU admission, age, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, hemoglobin, lactate, arterial pH, blood urea nitrogen, albumin, and glucose. The combined validation cohort consisted of 98,468 patients, with a 30-day mortality rate of 1.47%. The HeLP-BAG score achieved an AUROC of 0.873, showing comparable performance to ASA-PS (0.865; p = 0.120) and higher performance than SAPS3 (0.831). Performance was particularly robust in high-risk subgroups, including emergency surgery (AUROC 0.843; p < 0.001 vs. ASA-PS) and older patients aged ≥ 65 years (0.866; p = 0.004 vs. ASA-PS). Mortality increased monotonically with higher scores, and web-based calculator was developed to facilitate bedside implementation.ConclusionsThe HeLP-BAG score is a simple and interpretable tool for screening 30-day postoperative mortality, demonstrating consistent performance across diverse surgical populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03907-8.

Introduction

Postoperative mortality remains a critical concern in safeguarding patient safety and optimizing the management of surgical outcomes. The surgical mortality rate is a crucial indicator for hospitals and healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2]. Accurately predicting and reducing postoperative mortality remain central objectives for physicians and hospitals.

In the intensive care unit (ICU) setting, several scoring systems have been proposed to predict mortality. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) is the most widely known model with using first 24-hour data in the ICU stay [3]. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score is also a widely recognized scoring system [4]. In some settings, the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) uses fewer variables than APACHE while maintaining predictive performance [5, 6]. The Mortality Probability Model (MPM) and the Multiple Organ Dysfunction Score (MODS) have also been proposed [7, 8]. These scoring systems were primarily developed under the assumption of multi-organ failure. However, postoperative clinical deterioration can also occur rapidly in patients outside the ICU, including those in general wards, an area that remains relatively underexplored. In such settings, the American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS) and the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) can be used depending on the clinical context [9, 10]. Scoring systems such as the American College of Surgeons-National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) have been developed to estimate postoperative mortality risk. However, these and others require detailed information on the type of surgery and therefore cannot be applied to patients whose surgical procedure has not yet been determined [11–13]. A simpler approach, the Surgical Apgar Score (SAS) has been proposed but it uses an estimated blood loss which can be difficult to measure accurately [14].

Therefore, we aim to develop and validate a risk assessment tool that estimates mortality risk using only a small number of readily measurable variables, independent of the specific surgical procedure and the data collection process. This approach is intended to efficiently screen high-risk patients for postoperative mortality in emergency situations.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The datasets used in this study were derived from publicly available sources: first, the reference cohort was the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-4, version 3.1), which was used for statistical analysis and selection of variables and cutoff values. The validation cohort was the combined set of two external datasets, Informative Surgical Patient dataset for Innovative Research Environment (INSPIRE, version 1.3), and Medical Informatics Operating Room Vitals and Events Repository (MOVER, single version) [15–19].

To minimize dataset-driven bias and control for age- and sex-related effects, the three datasets were randomly matched by age group and sex using the smallest group size across datasets, resulting in 24,617 men and 24,617 women per dataset. The MIMIC-4 dataset includes data from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), covering ICU and emergency admissions from 2008 to 2022 [17]. After preprocessing, the mean age was 61.31 ± 14.24 years; 6,183 patients (12.56%) died within 30 days, 32,029 (65.06%) underwent emergency surgery, and all patients were admitted to the ICU. The INSPIRE dataset includes surgical data from the Seoul National University Hospital from 2011 to 2020 [18]. After preprocessing, the mean age was 59.14 ± 14.22 years; 339 patients (0.69%) died within 30 days, 5,067 (10.29%) underwent emergency surgery, and 9,598 (19.49%) were admitted to the ICU. Among ICU patients, 280 (82.60% of all deaths) died within 30 days. The MOVER dataset includes surgical data collected at the University of California, Irvine Medical Center, from 2015 to 2022 [19]. After preprocessing, the mean age was 61.08 ± 14.17 years; 1,109 patients (2.25%) died within 30 days, 24,476 (49.71%) underwent emergency surgery, and 32,298 (65.60%) were admitted to the ICU. Among ICU patients, 1,357 (93.72% of all deaths) died within 30 days. Overall study process is provided in Fig. 1, and the number of patients by age group and comparisons of variable values for each dataset are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Data preprocessing

Clinical data within 24 hours before surgery were extracted from three datasets. When multiple measurements were available during this window, the value closest to the surgery start time was selected. All variables and matching column names in three datasets are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Logical Observation Identifier Names and Codes (LOINCs) were used for preprocessing the MOVER dataset. The LOINCs applied to each variable are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. Existing scoring systems (ASA-PS, SOFA, MODS, MPM III, SAS, SAPS2-3, and APACHE IV) were then computed using these preoperative values for comparison. ASA-PS scores were directly extracted from labeled fields in the datasets. For the remaining scoring systems, calculations were performed based on publicly available formulas, which are detailed in Supplementary Table 5. Some scores required information on comorbidities; these were assessed using International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes as summarized in Supplementary Table 6. If required values for a score were missing, no points were assigned, under the assumption that missing measurements reflected clinical stability and no indication for testing.

The combined validation cohort was stratified into emergency and non-emergency cases using the “Emergency operation” and “Emergency occurrence” labels. In addition, patients were stratified by age (< 65 and ≥ 65 years) to examine differences in risk according to age. Final performance evaluations were conducted across five groups: emergency, non-emergency, age-stratified, and overall validation population.

Primary variable selection

As shown in Table 1, variables were categorized into five groups: anthropometry, arterial blood gas analysis (ABGA), vital signs, complete blood cell count (CBC), and others. Variable selection was guided by the following criteria: (1) a substantial difference between survivors and non-survivors; (2) a monotonic or consistent association with mortality; (3) noninvasive procedure with minimal influence from external devices; (4) simple, intuitive, and widely used; (5) applicability across a broad range of diseases; and (6) bidirectionality, such that both abnormally low and high values are clinically relevant. Criteria (1) and (2) were determined based on statistical analyses, criteria (3) through (5) were based on clinical expertise, and criterion (6) was determined using both statistical results and clinical judgment. Variables requiring clinical judgement (e.g., GCS) were excluded, since it requires manual decision of clinical expert. When evaluating substantial differences for criterion (1), variables with a p value ≥ 0.001 in the t-test comparing survivors and non-survivors were excluded. To further assess trends for criterion (2), the following procedure was applied.

Cutoff determination

Cutoffs were initially estimated from the data and subsequently refined through clinical consensus. Specifically, changepoints of mortality (reference cutoffs) were automatically detected from the scatterplot described at the above section. Change points where the datapoint showed abrupt shifts were detected using the Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) algorithm [20]. To reflect clinical severity, extreme values were preferentially selected. For non-bidirectional variables, the mean or median of the reference cutoffs was used when clinically appropriate. For bidirectional variables, cutoffs were included only when the direction of mortality change was consistent between data-driven analysis and clinical consensus (e.g., when values below a cutoff were clinically meaningful and were also associated with higher mortality in the data). Cutoffs were rounded for usability and adjusted based on prior studies or established clinical guidelines.

External validation

The HeLP-BAG was compared with ASA-PS, seven established scoring systems (SOFA, MODS, MPM III, SAS, SAPS2-3, and APACHE IV) and five conventional machine learning tree models (Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost) [21–24]. Machine learning models used the same variables as the HeLP-BAG score. Model performance for predicting 30-day mortality was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).

Post-hoc analysis of ICU admission

ICU admission is a binary variable closely associated with patient prognosis, and direct incorporation may represent the most objective approach. However, it may also be influenced by institutional policies and clinician judgment rather than solely reflecting the patient’s clinical status. As the MIMIC dataset consists exclusively of ICU patients, its ability to capture the independent effect of ICU admission is limited. Therefore, a post-hoc analysis was exceptionally performed, in which the weight assigned to ICU admission was adjusted within the validation cohort to achieve the best validation performance.

Statistical analysis

A chi-square test and a t test were conducted on the reference dataset to compare variables between non-survivors and survivors. The Python language Pandas and NumPy libraries were used for data preprocessing and variable selection, whereas Matplotlib and Seaborn libraries were used for visualization. The Ruptures and Scikit-learn libraries were utilized for implementing PELT algorithm, and machine learning models, respectively. The statistical analysis utilized SciPy library [25].

Results

Statistical analysis

Table 1 provides the chi-square and t test results for the reference cohort. The chi‑square test showed that emergency operations were more frequent in the non-survivors (72.4%, 4,475 of 6,183) than in the survivors (64.0%, 27,554 of 43,051). The t‑test identified significant differences between the two groups in several clinical variables. Notably, non‑survivors exhibited higher blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (32.51 ± 27.74 mg/dL vs. 24.06 ± 18.22 mg/dL; p < 0.001), lower albumin (2.89 ± 0.75 g/dL vs. 3.14 ± 0.76 g/dL; p < 0.001), and higher lactate (2.74 ± 2.36 mmol/L vs. 2.11 ± 1.82 mmol/L; p < 0.001) compared with survivors. Variables with p-values exceeding 0.001, including weight, PaCO2, platelet count, and bilirubin, were excluded from further analysis.

Bidirectionality was considered for arterial pH, MAP, glucose, and heart rate, as each is associated with well-established pathological states at both extremes (acidosis / alkalosis, hypotension / hypertension, hypoglycemia / hyperglycemia, and bradycardia / tachycardia, respectively). However, bidirectional cutoffs were ultimately selected only for arterial pH and glucose. For MAP, mortality did not reverse even when higher cutoffs were applied; mortality remained higher in lower-value ranges (hypotensive states) compared with higher-value ranges (hypertensive states). For heart rate, consistency was lost in the bradycardia range, where mortality was higher at relatively higher values within the bradycardic range, indicating a lack of monotonicity. Therefore, bidirectional cutoffs were adopted only for arterial pH and glucose, where mortality was consistently elevated at both values below the lower cutoff and above the upper cutoff. For MAP and age, a single cutoff was used because observations at extreme values were limited, as shown in Fig. 2. Introducing additional cutoffs to capture these sparse extremes could lead to unstable estimates. Therefore, the median value was used as the cutoff. For similar reasons, additional lower cutoffs were not introduced for lactate or BUN. The reference cutoffs, final cutoffs, and mortality rates corresponding to candidate cutoffs considered during the decision process are summarized in Supplementary Table 9. Scatter plots for variables not selected for the final score are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

External validation

The combined validation cohort consisted of 98,468 records, including 1,448 patients (1.47%) who died within 30 days. The mean age was 60.11 ± 14.23 years; 29,543 (30.00%) underwent emergency surgery, and 41,896 (42.55%) were admitted to the ICU. Among ICU patients, 1,357 patients (93.72% of all deaths) died within 30 days.

The scoring system was named HeLP-BAG based on its component variables: heart (mean arterial pressure, heart rate) and hemoglobin (He), lactate (L), arterial pH (P), BUN (B), albumin (A), and glucose (G). Table 3 presents the finalized scoring system, and Table 4 shows the distribution of scores and corresponding mortality rates in the validation cohort. 30-day mortality increased monotonically with increasing scores, except for the score 13, which included only 19 cases. Table 5; Fig. 3 show the performance of the proposed score compared with other methods across the validation cohort. For clarity, Fig. 3 displays only the five best-performing models in each subset, while the complete results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Supplementary Fig. 3 presents the calibration curves and decision curve analysis based on the logistic regression–derived probabilities of the models [26]. The calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, and decision curve analysis showed a higher net benefit than both the treat-all and treat-none strategies across clinically relevant threshold probabilities.

In the total cohort, the proposed score (AUROC 0.873) showed comparable performance to ASA-PS (0.865) and SAPS3 (0.831). Because the AUROC values of the proposed score and ASA-PS were similar, statistical comparison was performed using DeLong’s test (Supplementary Table 10) [27]. However, significant differences were observed in the emergency and older subsets. Post-hoc analysis of ICU admission

As described in the Methods, the weight assigned to ICU admission was adjusted in a post-hoc manner during external validation. Alternative weights ranging from 0 to 5 points were evaluated. Supplementary Table 11 presents the AUROC values with confidence intervals for HeLP-BAG-ASA, HeLP-BAG, and ASA across different ICU weights. When ICU weight was set to 0 (i.e., ICU admission excluded), the proposed score showed significantly lower performance than ASA. When a weight of 1 was applied (i.e., unadjusted ICU admission), the proposed score also showed lower performance without statistical significance. Based on these findings, a weight of 3 was selected for ICU admission. As this adjustment was derived from the validation dataset, it should be interpreted as dataset-specific. In future applications, the optimal weight for ICU admission may be recalibrated according to the characteristics of individual institutions.

Clinical application

To improve accessibility and usability, we developed a web-based calculator for the HeLP-BAG score, which can be accessed at https://help-bag.me. This tool allows users to input clinical variables with just a few clicks and receive immediate results. It was developed by the authors and follows the scoring rules and cutoff definitions presented in the manuscript; all cutoff values were directly adopted from the results shown in Table 3. Because mortality rates did not increase strictly monotonically at the extreme score ranges due to limited sample sizes, mortality estimates were recalculated using a maximum score of 12 points as the reference. In other words, for scores ≥ 12, mortality was estimated using the cumulative mortality of all patients with scores ≥ 12. This approach was used to stabilize mortality estimates in sparsely populated extreme score ranges. Patients scoring 8 points or more (at 10.07% mortality) are categorized as intermediate risk, whereas those with 11 points or more (at 22.33% mortality) were classified as high risk. The calculator’s design was inspired by Mdcalc, ensuring a user-friendly experience [28]. The examples of the interactive web application interface are depicted in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a novel data-driven perioperative scoring system, HeLP-BAG, as a screening tool to identify patients at high risk of 30-day postoperative mortality. This system also strived to minimize inter-clinician variability by utilizing measured values and demonstrated superior performance compared to various scoring systems and machine learning models.

Subjective variables such as ASA-PS and the GCS were excluded from the score despite their high discriminatory performance, because they are vulnerable to inter-rater and intra-rater variability. Previous studies have reported disagreements in ASA-PS classification between surgeons and anesthesiologists (Cohen’s kappa, κ = 0.53) [29, 30], as well as inconsistency upon reassessment by anesthesiologists themselves (κ = 0.48 [0.29–0.68]) [31]. Although the GCS generally shows higher agreement (κ > 0.7 in 77% of studies) [32], disagreement has been reported for the verbal response component (Intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC = 0.40 [0.20, 0.60]), which has contributed to variability in APACHE scoring (ICC = 0.90 [0.84, 0.94]) [33]. Such inconsistency should be minimized since it is associated with poor patient outcome [29]. However, this interpretation requires caution as the overall reliability of the APACHE score is not necessarily low. Moreover, the clinical judgment remains valuable. Supplementary Table 10 presents the AUROCs of a combined score obtained by averaging the normalized values of the ASA-PS and HeLP-BAG scores. This combined approach demonstrated superior performance compared with ASA-PS across all subsets (AUROC = 0.913). Therefore, the HeLP-BAG score can serve as a complementary tool to enhance ASA-PS.

Complex scoring systems can be as challenging as clinical judgment. Scoring systems that incorporate comorbidities require prior diagnoses. It depends on history taking, clinical findings, imaging, and physical examination, and are ultimately determined by physicians. Although medical histories may be reviewed or provisional diagnoses inferred, their reliability poses an additional concern [34]. The use of numerous variables and complex calculations further limits practicality. For example, APACHE IV incorporates eight comorbidities and more than 30 variables, with over 100 cutoff points, making manual calculation impossible. Moreover, controversies remain regarding the timing and methods of data collection [35]. Standardizing variable units, measurement timing, and calculation procedures is often unrealistic in routine clinical practice. Therefore, many scoring systems are primarily used in retrospective research, while preoperative risk assessment in real-world relies on simpler tools such as the GCS or ASA-PS. However, excessive reliance on subjective clinical judgment may increase criminal charges for medical errors, particularly in high-volume healthcare systems, such as those in South Korea [36, 37]. Therefore, there remains a need for an efficient and accurate risk score. In this context, risk scores have been generally criticized [38, 39]. As highlighted in previous study, a clinically applicable scoring system that is free from complex calculations is essential [38].

The clinical validity of the model cannot be fully examined in this study. Its value will be determined through real-world utilization. Nevertheless, the proposed model has the advantage of being explainable and implementable. The variables included in the model have been used in previous studies for predicting 30-day mortality. For example, lactate (AUROC 0.839–0.870) [40, 41], arterial pH (AUROC 0.92–0.94) [42], elevated BUN and low albumin (AUROC 0.793–0.894) [43–46] have been used, as well as machine learning approaches of these variables [47–49]. However, prior usage or reported technical performance does not fully justify the clinical rationale for variable selection. For instance, fatal arrhythmias cannot be fully detected by a single heart rate measurement, and sinus tachycardia is commonly observed even in healthy conditions, leading to false-positive interpretations. Although artificial intelligence (AI) or clinician judgment could be incorporated, such approaches would compromise the explainability and implementability of the model. The proposed score focuses on extreme conditions and is intended to support consideration of operability in high-risk situations. While this study is primarily data-driven, it also reflects commonly accepted clinical thresholds. For example, a hemoglobin level of 7–8 g/dL is generally regarded as severe anemia warranting consideration of perioperative transfusion [50, 51], and recommended target ranges include a MAP of 60–70 mmHg [52] and glucose levels of 80–180 mg/dL [53]. Although clear consensus is lacking for some other variables, this large-scale retrospective study may contribute to expanding future clinical discussions.

Another issue to note for developing a complex clinical decision supporting tool is that the dataset selection, cutoff detection, and cutoff adjustment inevitably involved subjective decisions. AI could be considered to address these limitations; [54] however, data-driven, or AI-based models are prone to dataset bias and overfitting [55, 56]. Models trained on a single dataset may show good internal performance but often deteriorate when applied to external data [54]. Supplementary Table 12 shows AUROCs obtained using an 80:20 train–test split of the reference cohort. Five tree-based models were trained with maximum depths (hyperparameter) from 1 to 12. Most tree-based models showed substantially lower validation AUROCs than training or test AUROCs, rarely exceeding 0.8, highlighting limited generalizability.

Current study has a few limitations. First, the developmental cohort was based on a single center (BIDMC) data cohort. However, as mentioned earlier, this model has been externally validated with two other large-sized multicenter cohorts. However, the post-hoc analysis of the ICU admission variable may represent an important limitation. Model evaluation should be performed using an untouched validation dataset. One possible approach would have been to transform ICU admission from a binary variable into a probabilistic estimate using imputation or clustering. However, ICU admission differs from other variables in that it is strongly influenced by institutional policies, resource availability, and clinician judgment, making it inherently institution dependent. Extensive preprocessing of this variable could therefore lead to overfitting to institution-specific decision patterns. Nevertheless, ICU admission remains a readily identifiable clinical factor that reflects patient severity. In future applications, if model performance differs across institutions, the weight assigned to ICU admission may be locally recalibrated (fine-tuning) while keeping other objective variables unchanged. As a practical alternative, the model may be applied without post-hoc tuning of the ICU weight by assigning a value of 1 and combining it with ASA-PS. This approach is supported by Supplementary Table 11, in which HeLP-BAG-ASA (average of normalized values) showed minimal performance differences between ICU weights of 1 and 3.

Another limitation of this study is the handling of missing values, which were treated as non-risk states. In clinical practice, though, missingness may reflect higher severity due to delayed or omitted testing, potentially leading to risk underestimation in some patients. To evaluate the potential impact of both missingness and cutoff refinement, additional analyses were performed. We compared the original analysis with two alternative settings: (1) median imputation of missing values for each variable (e.g., heart rate, BUN) and (2) the use of unadjusted cutoffs detected by the PELT algorithm. The results are presented in Supplementary Table 13. For HeLP-BAG-ASA, six combinations were evaluated depending on how HeLP-BAG and ASA were derived (original, median, or unadjusted). The analysis showed that model performance tended to decrease when median imputation was applied and improved when adjusted cutoffs were used (M2 > M2m, M2 > M2u, M1om > M1mm, M1om > M1um, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that the adopted strategy—retaining the original dataset without imputation while applying manually refined cutoffs—provided more favorable discrimination performance in this dataset.

Conclusions

We developed and validated a data-driven, parsimonious, and interpretable scoring system HeLP-BAG, to screen for postoperative mortality risk in urgent clinical settings. This score provides practical utility at the bedside, particularly in situations where time and resources are limited.
