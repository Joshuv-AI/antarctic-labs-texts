A weighted casebase framework for outcome-dependent two-phase sampling designs
Authors: Karina Kwan, Nicholas Cheng, Philip Awadalla, Celia M T Greenwood, David Soave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutcome-dependent two-phase sampling designs permit cost-effective measurement of expensive covariates in large prospective cohorts. Weighted Cox regression is commonly used for these designs, but applications requiring smooth hazard estimation or direct absolute risk prediction motivate a complementary parametric approach.MethodsWe extend the casebase framework, which represents hazard regression as logistic regression on sampled person-moments, by combining the casebase sampling offset with inverse-probability weights for phase-two inclusion. We formulate the estimator as a participant-level M-estimator, establish consistency and asymptotic normality under standard conditions, and develop a computationally efficient dfbeta-based jackknife/sandwich variance estimator. Background

Large prospective cohort studies are central to modern epidemiology, providing longitudinal follow-up and enabling estimation of disease risk and identification of environmental, clinical, or genomic determinants of health. The Ontario Health Study (OHS) [1], with over 225,000 participants, exemplifies such efforts. While such cohorts collect detailed baseline and outcome data, resource constraints often limit the measurement of expensive exposures such as genomic, proteomic, metabolomic, or other biomarker measurements on all participants. Outcome-dependent two-phase sampling designs provide an efficient strategy in these settings by measuring costly covariates in a subset of participants while retaining information from the full cohort. Case-cohort sampling, in which a random subcohort and all incident cases are selected, represents one commonly used example [2].

Standard analyses of case-cohort and related two-phase designs are typically conducted using weighted Cox proportional hazards models, where inverse-probability weights correct for unequal inclusion probabilities [3]. This approach is well established and remains the natural default when the primary target is a relative hazard parameter and the baseline hazard can remain unspecified. However, in many applications involving risk prediction, calibration, or decision support, researchers require estimates of hazard, survival, or absolute risk over clinically relevant time horizons. In these settings, the baseline hazard is not merely a nuisance quantity; it is part of the target of inference. Weighted Cox regression can also produce absolute-risk estimates after estimating the baseline cumulative hazard, but these estimates are typically step functions tied to observed failure times. The weighted casebase approach is therefore most attractive when investigators want a directly parameterized smooth hazard or risk curve, for example for calibrated 5- or 10-year risk prediction, interpolation between observed event times, or decisions based on absolute-risk thresholds.

The casebase framework offers a reformulation of hazard regression as logistic regression applied to sampled person-moments [4, 5]. In full cohort settings, this approach provides a convenient way to fit parametric hazard models using standard generalized linear model (GLM) software, with an offset term accounting for the sampling of person-moments. The resulting model can accommodate parametric or flexible functions of time, allowing smooth estimation of hazards and absolute risks. However, extension to outcome-dependent two-phase designs requires two additional considerations: inverse-probability weighting for the individual-level sampling design and appropriate variance estimation when individuals contribute repeated sampled person-moments.

Recent methodological work has emphasized unified modeling strategies for complex survival data structures, including clustering, cure fractions, and competing risks [6]. The problem considered here is complementary. We focus on outcome-dependent two-phase sampling designs, such as those used for expensive covariates, where the main inferential challenge is to combine individual-level sampling weights with the person-time sampling structure of the casebase representation.

In this paper, we extend the casebase framework to outcome-dependent two-phase sampling designs, including case-cohort and related stratified or frequency-matched sampling schemes when the relevant inclusion probabilities are known or consistently estimable. The proposed estimator combines two sampling adjustments that operate at different levels: the casebase offset corrects for sampling of person-moments from the observed at-risk person-time used to generate the base series, whereas inverse-probability weights correct for unequal sampling of individuals into the phase-two sample. This separation is useful in applications where investigators require smooth estimates of hazard, survival, or absolute risk functions using standard GLM software. The goal is not to replace weighted Cox regression, which remains the natural default for estimating relative hazard parameters without specifying the baseline hazard, but to provide a complementary approach when the baseline hazard or absolute risk function is itself part of the estimand.

Our main contributions are as follows. First, we formulate a weighted casebase estimating equation for outcome-dependent two-phase samples with known or consistently estimable inclusion probabilities. Second, we express the estimator as an individual-level M-estimator that allows repeated sampled person-moments within participants. Third, we develop a computationally efficient variance estimator based on aggregation of dfbeta diagnostics from weighted logistic regression, corresponding to an individual-level sandwich covariance estimator. Fourth, we evaluate finite-sample performance relative to weighted Cox regression through simulation studies that include correctly specified univariate settings as well as targeted sensitivity scenarios involving multiple covariates, baseline hazard misspecification, independent random censoring, and estimated inclusion probabilities. Finally, we illustrate implementation using data from a biomarker substudy within the Ontario Health Study.

The OHS analysis is intended as an illustrative implementation rather than as a definitive matched-design epidemiologic analysis. The original biomarker substudy used frequency matching, and a fully design-consistent analysis would require matching-stratum-specific inclusion probabilities, conditional or stratified methods, or other analyses explicitly incorporating the matching structure. We use this example to demonstrate the weighted casebase workflow, including estimated inclusion probabilities, repeated sampled person-moments, and smooth absolute risk prediction.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The Methods section develops the weighted casebase estimator, its large-sample justification, and variance estimation. The Simulation studies section presents simulation studies evaluating finite-sample performance under both primary and sensitivity scenarios. The Illustrative implementation using an Ontario Health Study biomarker substudy section provides an illustrative implementation using OHS biomarker data. The Discussion section provides a discussion of the implications and limitations, followed by the conclusions in the Conclusions section.

Methods

This section develops a weighted casebase estimator for outcome-dependent two-phase sampling designs. We first review the standard casebase representation of hazard regression as logistic regression on sampled person-moments. We then extend this representation to two-phase samples by combining the casebase offset, which accounts for sampling of person-moments, with inverse-probability weights, which account for unequal sampling of individuals. We finally describe the large-sample justification and a computationally efficient individual-level variance estimator based on aggregated dfbeta diagnostics.

Practical implementation

The estimator can be implemented using any weighted logistic regression routine that permits an offset and record-level weights inherited from source individuals. A practical implementation proceeds as follows. After the phase-two sample is identified, compute , generate the base series from observed phase-two at-risk person-time using the sampling mechanism in the Weighted casebase estimator for two-phase designs section, construct the case-series and base-series records, and assign each record the supplied inverse-probability weight of its source individual, or . Then fit the weighted logistic regression with offset , extract unstandardized dfbeta diagnostics, aggregate those diagnostics by source individual, and compute the cross-product in (9). This requires only one model fit and avoids repeatedly refitting the model after deleting each individual. The casebaseweights R package implements this workflow for fitting weighted casebase models and computing the individual-level dfbeta aggregation variance estimator.

When inclusion probabilities are estimated, the dfbeta-based estimator remains conditional on the estimated weights. To propagate uncertainty from weight estimation, the stacked estimating-equation sandwich is the natural default when the inclusion probabilities are obtained from a standard parametric model fitted to the phase-one data, because it avoids repeated resampling and refitting. A subject-level bootstrap is useful when the weight-construction procedure is complex or multistep but can be reproduced in each replicate, or as a sensitivity analysis. In each bootstrap replicate, individuals are resampled at the subject level, respecting phase-two sampling strata when applicable; the inclusion probabilities are re-estimated; and are recomputed; the base series is regenerated; and the weighted logistic regression is refit. Thus, K bootstrap replicates require K complete analysis workflows, whereas the stacked sandwich uses the original inclusion-probability and weighted casebase model fits. If weights are supplied externally and their estimation procedure cannot be reproduced, inference remains conditional on the supplied weights.

Simulation studies

We conducted simulation studies to evaluate the finite-sample performance of the weighted casebase estimator compared to weighted Cox regression under outcome-dependent two-phase sampling. The primary objectives were to examine bias, variance estimation, confidence interval coverage, and convergence across different covariate types, cohort sizes, and sampling-weight magnitudes.

The primary simulations reproduce the original correctly specified univariate setting. To address additional operating characteristics relevant to applied two-phase studies, we added targeted sensitivity simulations involving multiple covariates, baseline hazard misspecification, independent random censoring, and estimated inclusion probabilities. These additional scenarios were designed to address specific limitations of the primary simulations without creating a full factorial expansion that would obscure interpretation.

Aggregation of simulation results.

For each estimator, summary statistics were computed using simulation replicates with finite coefficient and standard error estimates for that estimator. This estimator-specific aggregation prevents isolated numerical failures in one model from propagating to other estimators in the same scenario. In the settings considered here, this affected only a small number of weighted Cox replicates in the most extreme log-normal scenarios.

Primary simulation results

For binary covariates (Tables 4–7 in Appendix A), weighted casebase regression and weighted Cox regression performed similarly across all cohort sizes. Bias was small for both weighted estimators, estimated standard errors were close to empirical standard errors, and coverage remained close to nominal even when the control weight increased to 499. In contrast, naive two-phase analyses that ignored inverse-probability weights were substantially biased and had poor coverage, illustrating the need to account for the outcome-dependent sampling design.

For normally distributed covariates (Tables 8–11 in Appendix A), weighting reduced the large selection bias seen in the naive analyses, but finite-sample performance was less stable than in the binary-covariate setting. As the cohort size increased while the number of sampled controls remained fixed, the inverse-probability weights became larger without increasing the amount of phase-two covariate information. Both weighted Cox and weighted casebase estimators showed upward bias and under-coverage in these settings, indicating that the instability reflects the weighted two-phase design rather than the casebase formulation specifically.

For log-normal covariates (Tables 12–15 in Appendix A), performance was again affected by the combination of skewed covariates, fixed phase-two sample size, and large inverse-probability weights. Weighted casebase and weighted Cox regression showed broadly similar qualitative behaviour, although the most extreme scenarios exhibited greater numerical instability, particularly for weighted Cox regression. These results support the interpretation that the main finite-sample limitation is weight variability under sparse phase-two sampling, not the casebase representation itself.

Simulation summary

Weighted casebase and weighted Cox regression had similar finite-sample performance across the scenarios studied. Both performed well for binary covariates and in the targeted multivariable, baseline-misspecification, censoring, and estimated-weight sensitivity analyses, whereas continuous or highly skewed covariates combined with fixed phase-two sample sizes and large weights produced instability for both methods. These findings support weighted casebase regression as a complementary approach when smooth hazard or absolute-risk estimation is required, rather than as a replacement for weighted Cox regression when the baseline hazard may remain unspecified.

Illustrative implementation using an Ontario Health Study biomarker substudy

We illustrate the computational implementation of the weighted casebase methodology using data from a biomarker substudy within the Ontario Health Study (OHS). The OHS is a prospective cohort of over 225,000 Ontario residents designed to investigate environmental, lifestyle, and genetic factors associated with chronic disease development [1].

This analysis is intended as an illustrative implementation of the weighted casebase workflow rather than as a definitive epidemiologic analysis of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) methylation and breast cancer risk. The original biomarker substudy used an outcome-dependent frequency-matched sampling design. A fully design-consistent analysis of that study would require stratum-specific inclusion probabilities, conditional or stratified methods, or other analyses explicitly incorporating the matching structure. Because those features are not fully available in the present analysis, we focus on demonstrating how the proposed weighted casebase estimator can be implemented with estimated inverse-probability weights, repeated sampled person-moments, and smooth absolute risk prediction. The resulting estimates should therefore be interpreted as a methodological illustration rather than as definitive evidence about breast cancer risk prediction.

Study design and data

The biomarker substudy selected 80 women who developed breast cancer after enrollment and 70 women who remained cancer-free from OHS cohort participants with available plasma samples. The original study employed frequency matching on age in 5-year categories, sex, date of plasma collection in 6-month windows, ethnicity, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Because all participants in this substudy were women, sex was fixed in the analytic sample.

For each participant, cfDNA was extracted from stored plasma samples and methylation levels were measured across 1,760,313 binned genomic regions profiled using cfMeDIP-Seq. Following quality control and preprocessing, the analysis focused on 1,000 CpG sites with the largest variance across samples. These measurements were summarized using principal component-based dimension reduction, with candidate models considering up to five methylation principal components.

Controls were selected through frequency matching, yielding an outcome-dependent two-phase sample. Inclusion probabilities were not directly observed from the original sampling scheme. We therefore used the estimated inclusion probabilities reported by Cheng et al. (2023) [9], which were derived using age-specific cumulative breast cancer incidence rates from the Canadian Cancer Registry and all-cause mortality rates in Ontario reported by Statistics Canada. These estimated inclusion probabilities were used to construct inverse-probability weights. The same weights were incorporated consistently for parameter estimation, risk prediction, and performance evaluation throughout this illustrative analysis.

Because the original frequency-matching strata were not explicitly incorporated into the weighted casebase likelihood, the analysis should be viewed as a simplified weighted two-phase analysis. Available matching variables were included as adjustment covariates where possible, but this adjustment is not equivalent to a fully matched or stratified design-consistent analysis.

Analysis methods

The dataset was partitioned into a training set and a test set. The training set consisted of samples from batches 1 through 7, while the test set consisted of samples from batch 8. This split was chosen because batch 8 was analyzed later. The training set contained 124 samples, and the test set contained 26 samples. The test set was held out during model tuning and was used only after final tuning parameters were selected.

Given the high dimensionality of the methylation data relative to the sample size, we used supervised principal components for dimension reduction. In the training set, univariate weighted Cox score statistics were computed for individual methylation regions. Regions with score statistics exceeding a threshold θ were retained, and principal components were then computed using the retained regions. Candidate values were for the number of principal components and for the score statistic threshold.

Tuning was performed using repeated 10-fold cross-validation within the training set. Each fold contained a balanced mix of cases and controls, with fold sizes ranging from 11 to 13 individuals. For each candidate pair , the supervised PCA procedure was performed within the training data for each cross-validation split. Results

Cross-validation within the training set selected two principal components and a score statistic threshold of θ =1.89, yielding 24 methylation regions for the final analysis. These tuning parameters were then used to fit both the weighted Cox and weighted casebase models.

Table 21 in Appendix A presents weighted concordance values for the training and test sets. Both methods achieved identical discrimination in the training set, with concordance equal to 0.70. In the test set, the weighted casebase model had a slightly higher point estimate of concordance than the weighted Cox model, 0.68 versus 0.65. However, the test set contained only 26 individuals, so this difference should not be interpreted as evidence that the casebase model has superior predictive performance. The more relevant conclusion is that the weighted casebase workflow produced predictions with discrimination comparable to the weighted Cox workflow in this small illustrative example.

The weighted Brier scores shown in Figure 2 provide a time-varying descriptive assessment of prediction error. Across follow-up time, the weighted Cox and weighted casebase models produced very similar Brier score curves in both the training and test sets. This suggests comparable overall predictive performance in this dataset, while reinforcing that the primary distinction between the methods is not discrimination but the form of the fitted risk function.

Figure 3 compares predicted absolute risks with weighted Kaplan-Meier estimates. For this display, individuals were stratified by the median fitted risk score, using the Cox model risk score for consistency across methods. The casebase model produced smooth estimated risk curves over time, whereas the Cox model produced step functions tied to observed event times through its estimated baseline cumulative hazard. Both methods showed reasonable agreement for the lower-risk groups. In the higher-risk groups, both methods tended to overestimate risk, particularly in the test set.

These calibration patterns should be interpreted cautiously. The test set was small, the supervised PCA procedure may have introduced overfitting despite internal cross-validation, and the matching structure of the original study was only partially addressed through covariate adjustment and inverse-probability weights. In addition, the instability of the weighted Kaplan-Meier estimate at later follow-up times reflects the limited number of individuals remaining at risk, especially in subgroup-specific calibration plots.

Overall, this application demonstrates the practical implementation of the weighted casebase framework in a realistic biomarker substudy with estimated inverse-probability weights. The analysis illustrates how weighted casebase regression can be embedded in a high-dimensional prediction workflow, how repeated person-moments can be handled through individual-level variance aggregation, and how smooth absolute risk curves can be obtained from a GLM-based hazard model. The application should not be interpreted as establishing improved prediction for cfDNA methylation markers or as a complete matched-design analysis of the original OHS substudy.

The weighted casebase analysis was implemented using the casebaseweights R package, available at https://github.com/karinakwan/casebaseweights/. The package provides functions for fitting weighted casebase models and computing the individual-level dfbeta aggregation variance estimator described in this paper.

Discussion

We developed a weighted extension of the casebase framework for outcome-dependent two-phase survival designs. The proposed approach combines two distinct sampling adjustments: the casebase offset accounts for sampling of person-moments from observed person-time [4, 5], whereas inverse-probability weights correct for unequal sampling of individuals into the phase-two sample [3]. This yields a GLM-based implementation of parametric or flexible-parametric hazard regression under outcome-dependent sampling.

The methodological contribution is not that weighted casebase regression replaces weighted Cox regression. Rather, the contribution is to extend the casebase representation to settings in which individuals are selected through outcome-dependent two-phase sampling and may then contribute repeated sampled person-moments. This creates a two-level sampling structure: individuals are sampled into the phase-two dataset, and person-moments are sampled from the observed follow-up time of those individuals. By formulating the estimator as an individual-level M-estimator, the repeated person-moments can be aggregated at the source-individual level for inference.

The proposed dfbeta-based jackknife/sandwich variance estimator follows from this individual-level influence representation. By aggregating observation-level dfbeta diagnostics within individuals, the estimator accounts for dependence among repeated sampled person-moments from the same participant and provides a computationally efficient fixed-weight sandwich covariance estimator. This is useful when the casebase dataset contains many sampled person-moments or when models are embedded within cross-validation or high-dimensional workflows. When inclusion probabilities are estimated rather than known design quantities, the default dfbeta-based estimator should be interpreted as conditional on the supplied weights. Uncertainty from estimating inclusion probabilities can be incorporated using a stacked estimating-equation sandwich estimator or by using a subject-level bootstrap in which the inclusion probability model is re-estimated within each bootstrap replicate.

The simulation studies showed that weighted casebase regression had finite-sample behaviour broadly comparable to weighted Cox regression in the settings examined. In the primary correctly specified simulations, both methods performed well for binary covariates, with negligible bias and approximately nominal coverage even when sampling weights were large. For continuous covariates, increasing weight magnitude led to greater finite-sample instability for both weighted casebase and weighted Cox regression. This pattern is consistent with the known sensitivity of inverse-probability weighted estimators to extreme weights [11], and indicates that the observed instability reflects the weighting structure rather than a feature unique to the casebase formulation. In these scenarios, the number of sampled controls was fixed while the full cohort size increased, so larger cohorts increased the control weights without increasing the amount of phase-two covariate information. In our simulations, this was most evident for weighted Cox regression under log-normal covariates, where occasional numerical instability and heavy-tailed robust variance estimates were observed.

The additional sensitivity simulations were included to assess settings closer to those encountered in applied two-phase studies. These simulations considered multiple covariates, baseline hazard misspecification, independent random censoring, and estimated inclusion probabilities. In these sensitivity analyses, weighted casebase and weighted Cox regression again showed similar coefficient performance, with small bias, close agreement between empirical and estimated standard errors, and near-nominal coverage. The misspecification analysis also showed that flexible time bases can be used within the casebase framework to reduce reliance on a strict Weibull baseline specification. These additional scenarios are important because the casebase approach requires specification of the time component of the hazard model, whereas Cox regression leaves the baseline hazard unspecified.

The main practical distinction between weighted Cox and weighted casebase regression therefore concerns the role of the baseline hazard. Weighted Cox regression remains the natural default when the scientific goal is estimation of relative hazard ratios and the baseline hazard can be treated as a nuisance function. Weighted casebase regression is complementary: it is useful when smooth estimation of the hazard, survival function, or absolute risk function is itself part of the inferential target. This feature is particularly relevant in risk prediction, calibration, or decision-support settings, where absolute risks over clinically meaningful time horizons are often more directly interpretable than relative hazards alone.

The Ontario Health Study biomarker substudy illustrates this practical distinction. The weighted Cox and weighted casebase analyses produced similar discrimination and Brier score patterns in this small example, while the casebase model yielded smooth absolute risk curves through its parametric time specification. However, this application should be interpreted as a methodological illustration rather than as a definitive epidemiologic analysis of cfDNA methylation and breast cancer risk. The original substudy used frequency matching, and a fully design-consistent analysis would require stratum-specific inclusion probabilities, conditional or stratified methods, or another approach that explicitly incorporates the matching structure. In addition, the test set was small, the supervised PCA workflow may have introduced overfitting despite internal cross-validation, and uncertainty in the externally estimated inclusion probabilities was not fully propagated in the main analysis.

A natural extension is to stratified two-phase designs. If base person-moments are sampled separately within design stratum h, records in that stratum use the offset and the corresponding stratum-specific first-order inclusion weight; participant-level influence contributions are then aggregated as above, with the variance calculation respecting dependence induced by the sampling design. Fully matched designs are less direct: matched-set membership and set-specific selection probabilities must enter , and conditional or stratified nuisance components may be required. Because a purely conditional analysis removes the baseline component needed for absolute-risk prediction, absolute risks would additionally require calibration of the baseline hazard from the full cohort or an external source. Formal development and evaluation of this matched-design extension are beyond the present scope.

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, as with other inverse-probability weighted methods, very large sampling weights may lead to instability, particularly for continuous and highly skewed covariates, where we observed occasional numerical failures and heavy-tailed variance estimates in weighted Cox regression [11]. In practice, this may be mitigated by increasing the number of sampled controls, improving the sampling design, using stabilized or calibrated weights, or conducting sensitivity analyses for influential weights. Second, weighted casebase regression requires specification of the time component of the hazard model. Flexible time bases can reduce sensitivity to strict parametric assumptions, but misspecification can affect absolute risk estimation. Third, when inclusion probabilities are estimated, variance estimation should account for the weight-estimation step if uncertainty in those probabilities is material. Fourth, all simulations were low dimensional: the primary scenarios used one covariate, and the multivariable sensitivity analysis used two. They therefore do not establish performance when the number of candidate predictors is large relative to the phase-two sample size, under regularization, or with complex nonlinear and interaction structures. Fifth, the current development focuses on right-censored survival outcomes under two-phase sampling; extensions to time-dependent covariates, competing risks, recurrent events, and more complex clustered survival settings remain useful directions for future work. Recent work on unified survival models for clustered data, cure fractions, and competing risks highlights the broader need for methods that remain computationally accessible while accommodating complex survival-data structures [6].

Conclusions

Weighted casebase regression provides a theoretically grounded and computationally practical approach to parametric or flexible-parametric hazard modeling under outcome-dependent two-phase sampling. Its finite-sample performance was broadly comparable to weighted Cox regression in the scenarios studied, while offering direct smooth estimation of hazards and absolute risks within a familiar GLM framework. The method should be viewed as a complementary tool rather than a competitor to weighted Cox regression: Cox regression is preferable when the baseline hazard can remain unspecified, whereas weighted casebase regression is useful when the baseline hazard or absolute risk function is part of the scientific target.
