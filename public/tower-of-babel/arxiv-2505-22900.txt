A closer look at Chapoton’s q q -Ehrhart polynomials Thanks: We are grateful to Serkan Hoşten and Dusty Ross for helpful remarks.
Matthias Beck, Thomas Kunze

Abstract
. If 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is a lattice polytope (i.e., 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is the convex hull of finitely many integer points in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} ), Ehrhart’s famous theorem (1962) asserts that the integer-point counting function | t ​ 𝒫 ∩ ℤ d | |t\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}| is a polynomial in the integer variable t t . Chapoton (2016) proved that, given a fixed integral form λ : ℤ d → ℤ \lambda:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z} , there exists a polynomial cha 𝒫 λ ⁡ ( q , x ) ∈ ℚ ⁡ ( q ) ​ [ x ] \operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)[x] such that the refined enumeration function ∑ 𝐦 ∈ t ​ 𝒫 q λ ⁡ ( 𝐦 ) \sum_{\mathbf{m}\in t\mathcal{P}}q^{\lambda(\mathbf{m})} equals the evaluation cha 𝒫 λ ⁡ ( q , [ t ] q ) \operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,[t]_{q}) where, as usual, [ t ] q := q t − 1 q − 1 [t]_{q}:=\frac{q^{t}-1}{q-1} ; naturally, for q = 1 q=1 we recover the Ehrhart polynomial. Our motivating goal is to view Chapoton’s work through the lens of Brion’s Theorem (1988), which expresses the integer-point structure of a given polytope via that of its vertex cones. It turns out that this viewpoint naturally yields various refinements and extensions of Chapoton’s results, including explicit formulas for cha 𝒫 λ ⁡ ( q , x ) \operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x) , its leading coefficient, and its behavior as t → ∞ t\to\infty . We also prove an analogue of Chapoton’s structural and reciprocity theorems for rational polytopes (i.e., with vertices in ℚ d \mathbb{Q}^{d} ).

1.
Chapoton Polynomials

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
lattice polytope
, i.e., the convex hull of finitely many points in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The
Ehrhart polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined via
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(t):=|t\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

[
11
]
. It is an important invariant of
\mathcal{P}
and enjoys numerous applications in various mathematical fields (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
).
Chapoton
[
9
]
initiated the study of

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,:=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in t\mathcal{P}}q^{\lambda(\mathbf{m})}

where
\lambda:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}
is a fixed integral form. Naturally, for
q=1
we recover the Ehrhart polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
, but the algebraic structure of
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)
is a priori not clear.
Chapoton’s main theorem
[
9
]
is as follows.
We define, as usual,
[t]_{q}:=\frac{q^{t}-1}{q-1}
and
we call two vertices
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}
of
\mathcal{P}

adjacent
if they form an edge of
\mathcal{P}
.
We say that
\lambda
is
generic and positive with respect to

\mathcal{P}
if
\lambda(\mathbf{v})\neq\lambda(\mathbf{w})
whenever
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
are adjacent vertices, and
\lambda(\mathbf{v})\geq 0
for any
vertex
\mathbf{v}
.

Theorem 1

(Chapoton)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with
respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then there exists a polynomial
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)[x]
such
that

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}\left(q,[t]_{q}\right)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)

for all positive integers
t
.
The degree of
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)
is
m:=\max\{\lambda(\mathbf{v}):\,\mathbf{v}\text{
\rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}\}
, and all the poles of the coefficients of
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)

are roots of unity of order
\leq m
.

So the
q
-Ehrhart polynomial
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)
(naturally) contains more information than
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
but still retains some
polynomial structure (as opposed to, say, the multigraded Hilbert series of the cone over
\mathcal{P}
). Chapoton’s
work has been applied, e.g., to poset enumeration invariants
[
12
,
13
]
and graph coloring
[
2
]
.

Example 1
.

Let
\Delta
be the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
, and
(0,1)
, and let
\lambda=(1,2)
. Then (as we will compute below in Example
2
)

\operatorname{cha}_{\Delta}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\,\frac{q^{3}}{q+1}\,x^{2}+\frac{q(2q+1)}{q+1}\,x+1\,.

It turns out that, in several special cases, Chapoton polynomials have been computed in the
(sometimes distant) past; Section
2
contains some of these instances and
connects them to the underlying polyhedral geometry.

Our motivating goal is to view Chapoton’s Theorem
1
through the lens of Brion’s Theorem
[
7
]
, which expresses the
integer-point structure of a given polytope via that of its vertex cones.
It turns out that this viewpoint naturally yields various refinements and extensions of
Chapoton’s work. We describe some highlights next; the accompanying proofs are in Section
3
.

Given a vertex
\mathbf{v}
of
\mathcal{P}
, let
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
be the conical hull of all edge directions at

\mathbf{v}
, that is,

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}\,:=\sum_{\mathbf{w}\text{ adjacent to }\mathbf{v}}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v})\,.

(The affine cone
\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
is known as the
vertex cone
of
\mathcal{P}
at
\mathbf{v}
; we
will use this nomenclature for both
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
and
\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
.)
Given a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its
integer-point transform
as

\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z})\,:=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}

where
\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}:=z_{1}^{m_{1}}z_{2}^{m_{d}}\cdots z_{d}^{m_{d}}
.
When
S
is a rational cone,
\sigma_{S}(\mathbf{z})
evaluates to a rational function, and so we may
define, for an integral form
\lambda
,

(1)

\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)\,:=\,\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},\ q^{\lambda_{2}},\ \dots,\ q^{\lambda_{d}}\right),

a rational function in
q
.
Our first result gives an explicit formula for Chapoton’s
q
-Ehrhart polynomials.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)\bigl((q-1)[t]_{q}+1\bigr)^{\lambda(\mathbf{v})}

where
\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)
is the rational function defined in (
1
).

Thus

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{\lambda(\mathbf{v})},

and so Theorem
2

immediately recovers the first two statements in Theorem
1
; we also refine the
third one, as follows. Given a vector
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we denote by
g(\mathbf{m})
the vector
\mathbf{m}
scaled down by the
gcd of the entries of
\mathbf{m}
. Thus when
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
are adjacent vertices of
\mathcal{P}
, then
g(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v})
is
the primitive edge direction from
\mathbf{v}
to
\mathbf{w}
.

Theorem 3
.

Each pole of
\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)
(defined in (
1
)) is an
n^{\text{th}}
root of unity where
n=|\lambda(g(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}))|
for some adjacent vertex
\mathbf{w}
.

Theorem
2
can easily be restated in terms of the coefficients of the Chapoton
polynomials:

Corollary 4
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\sum_{k=0}^{\max\lambda(\mathbf{v})}(q-1)^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex
of }\mathcal{P}}\binom{\lambda(\mathbf{v})}{k}\,\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)\,x^{k}

where
\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)
is the rational function defined in (
1
).

Corollary 5
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with respect to

\mathcal{P}
, then the leading coefficient of
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)
is
(q-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{v})}\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)
where
\mathbf{v}
is the vertex of
\mathcal{P}
that maximizes
\lambda(\mathbf{v})
.

In
[
9
, Section 4.1]
, Chapoton suggests to compute
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,\frac{1}{1-q})
(and does so in the case when
\mathcal{P}
is an order polytope), as an analogue of computing
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)
in the limit as
t\to\infty
.
Theorem
2
immediately yields this evaluation:

Corollary 6
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}\left(q,\frac{1}{1-q}\right)\,=\begin{cases}\rho_{\mathbf{0}}^{\lambda}(q)&\text{ if }\mathbf{0}\text{ is a vertex of }\mathcal{P},\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

We remark that the interpretation
t\to\infty
is reflected in this formula: in the first case,
\mathcal{P}

becomes
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{0}}
as
t\to\infty
, whereas in the second case,
\mathcal{P}
wanders off into the
horizon.

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)

and an application of the

.

Corollary 7
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and

is an integral form that is generic and positive with respect to

, then the constant term of
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)
is

.

Chapoton
[
9
]
also proved the following combinatorial reciprocity theorem, which lifts
the classic Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity theorem
[
14
]
into the
q
-world. We will
show that it easily follows from Theorem
2
.

Theorem 8

(Chapoton)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a lattice polytope and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with
respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then

(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}\left(\tfrac{1}{q},[-t]_{\frac{1}{q}}\right)\,=\,\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}^{\lambda}\left(q,[t]_{q}\right).

Equivalently,

(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}\left(\tfrac{1}{q},-qx\right)\,=\,\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,.

Ehrhart counting functions can be considered also for
rational
polytopes, whose vertices
are in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Ehrhart
[
11
]
proved that in this case
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
is a

quasipolynomial
, i.e., there exist
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(kp+r)
is a
polynomial in
k
, for
0\leq r<p
.
(These polynomials are the
constituents
of the quasipolynomial
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
.)
Moreover, one can choose
p
to be the
denominator
of

\mathcal{P}
, i.e., the smallest
p
such that

is a lattice polytope.
We prove the following rational analogues of Theorems
1
and
3
:

Theorem 9
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a rational polytope with denominator
p
, and
\lambda
is an integral form that is generic and positive with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then there exist polynomials
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda,r}(q,x)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)[x]
such that

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda,r}\left(q,[k]_{q}\right)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,kp+r)

for all
integers

and all
0\leq r<p
.
The degree of
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda,r}(q,x)
is
\max\{\lambda(p\mathbf{v}):\,\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}\}
.
Each pole of a coefficient of
\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda,r}(q,x)
is an
n^{\text{th}}
root of unity where
n=|\lambda(g(p(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v})))|
for some adjacent vertices
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
.

Finally, we offer the following rational version of Theorem
8
.

Theorem 10
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a rational polytope with denominator

, and
\lambda
is an integral form which
is generic and positive with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, then for any

and

(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda,r}\left(\tfrac{1}{q},[-k]_{\frac{1}{q}}\right)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}^{\lambda}(q,kp-r)\,.

2.
Connections

We now develop both a toolbox of examples of Chapoton polynomials and give a glimpse of history
where they had previously appeared (naturally with different nomenclature).
For these examples, as well as the proofs in Section
3
, we make the simple but crucial observation that, by definition of
[t]_{q}
,

(2)

q^{kt}\,=\,\bigl((q-1)[t]_{q}+1\bigr)^{k},

and so
q^{kt}
can be expressed as a degree-
k
polynomial in
[t]_{q}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[q]
.

2.1.
The unit cube and Euler–Mahonian statistics

Let
\Box=[0,1]^{d}
and
\lambda=(1,1,\dots,1)
. Then

(3)

\operatorname{ehr}_{\Box}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,=\,[t+1]_{q}^{d}\qquad\text{ and so }\qquad\operatorname{cha}_{\Box}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\,(1+qx)^{d}.

The quantity
[t+1]_{q}^{d}
famously appears in the
Carlitz identity

[
8
]
(though with some effort one can derive it from the works of MacMahon
[
15
, Volume 2, Chapter IV, §462]
)

(4)

\sum_{t\geq 0}[t+1]_{q}^{n}\,x^{t}\,=\,\frac{\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}x^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)}q^{\operatorname{maj}(\pi)}}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}\left(1-xq^{j}\right)}\,.

The numerator on the right is known as an
Euler–Mahonian polynomial
due to the relation
with Euler’s work on
\operatorname{des}(\pi):=|\operatorname{Des}(\pi)|
and MacMahon’s original introduction of the major index

\operatorname{maj}(\pi):=\sum_{j\in\operatorname{Des}(\pi)}j

for a permutation

\pi\in S_{n}
, where
\operatorname{Des}(\pi):=\{j:\,\pi(j)>\pi(j+1)\}
.

The vertices of
\Box
are of the form
\mathbf{e}_{I}:=\sum_{j\in I}\mathbf{e}_{j}
, where
I\subseteq[d]
and

\mathbf{e}_{j}
is the
j
th standard unit vector. Each vertex lies in
d
edges; if
j\in I

then
-\mathbf{e}_{j}
is an edge direction at
\mathbf{e}_{I}
, otherwise
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is an edge direction. Since each vertex cone of
\Box
is unimodular,

\rho_{\mathbf{e}_{I}}^{\lambda}(q)\,=\,\frac{1}{(1-q)^{d-|I|}(1-\frac{1}{q})^{|I|}}\,=\,\frac{(-q)^{|I|}}{(1-q)^{d}}

and so Theorem
2
gives

\operatorname{cha}_{\Box}^{\lambda}(q,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\left(d\atop j\right)\frac{(-q)^{j}}{\left(1-q\right)^{d}}\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{j}.

One can apply the binomial theorem to recover the arguably simpler expression in (
3
).

For general
\lambda>0
,

\operatorname{ehr}_{\Box}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,=\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}[t+1]_{q^{\lambda_{j}}}\,=\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{q^{\lambda_{j}(t+1)}-1}{q^{\lambda_{j}}-1}\,=\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{q^{\lambda_{j}}\left((q-1)[t]_{q}+1\right)^{\lambda_{j}}-1}{q^{\lambda_{j}}-1}\,,

and this function appears in a multivariate refinement of (
4
)
[
3
]
.
We can compute its incarnation via Theorem
2
as above: with

\lambda_{I}:=\sum_{j\in I}\lambda_{j}
, we have

\operatorname{cha}_{\Box}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\sum_{I\subseteq[d]}\frac{(-1)^{|I|}q^{\lambda_{I}}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1-q^{\lambda_{j}}\right)}\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{\lambda_{I}}.

2.2.
The standard simplex and Sylvester waves

Let
\Delta=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}:\,x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{d}=1\right\}
, the
convex hull of the
d
standard unit vectors, and assume that the coefficients of
\lambda
are
distinct.
Then

\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in t\Delta}q^{\lambda_{1}m_{1}+\lambda_{2}m_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{d}m_{d}}

is the generating function of the number of partitions with exactly
t
parts in the set
\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d}\}
.
This is a refinement of sorts of the
restricted partition function

p_{\Lambda}(n)
, the
number of partitions of
n
with parts in
\Lambda
. This function is classically decomposed into

Sylvester waves
and is intimately connected to the
Frobenius coin-exchange problem

(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Chapter 1]
).

The vertex cone at
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is again unimodular, with edge directions
\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
, so that

\rho_{\mathbf{e}_{j}}^{\lambda}(q)\,=\,\frac{1}{\prod_{k\neq j}\left(1-q^{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}\right)}\,,

and Theorem
2
yields

\operatorname{cha}_{\Delta}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\prod_{k\neq j}\left(1-q^{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}\right)}\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{\lambda_{j}}.

2.3.
Unimodular simplices and integer partitions

Let
\Delta=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{d}\leq 1\right\}
,
another unimodular
d
-simplex, and let
\lambda=(1,1,\dots,1)
.
Then

\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in t\Delta}q^{m_{1}+m_{2}+\dots+m_{d}}\,=\,\left[{t+d}\atop d\right]_{q}\,,

the (very classical) generating function of the number of partitions with at most
d
parts, each of which
\leq t
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). Thus

\operatorname{cha}_{\Delta}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\,\frac{1}{[d]_{q}!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(q^{j}x+[j]_{q}\right).

Along with the origin, the vertices of
\Delta
are
\mathbf{e}_{d},\mathbf{e}_{d-1}+\mathbf{e}_{d},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{1}+\dots+\mathbf{e}_{d-1}+\mathbf{e}_{d}
. As
\lambda(\mathbf{e}_{d-k+1}+\dots+\mathbf{e}_{d})=k
,
Theorem
2
gives

\operatorname{cha}_{\Delta}^{\lambda}(q,x)\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{1}{\prod_{k\neq j}\left(1-q^{k-j}\right)}\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{j}.

2.4.
Lecture hall simplices/partitions

Our last family of examples is given by the
lecture hall simplex

\Delta_{n}\,:=\,\left\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n}:\,x_{1}\leq\frac{x_{2}}{2}\leq\frac{x_{3}}{3}\leq\dots\leq\frac{x_{n}}{n}\right\},

first studied in
[
10
]
in
conjunction with the famous
lecture hall partitions
, which we may view as points in
the cone

\mathcal{K}_{n}\,:=\,\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\,x_{1}\leq\frac{x_{2}}{2}\leq\frac{x_{3}}{3}\leq\dots\leq\frac{x_{n}}{n}\right\}

—a lecture hall partition of
m
is an integer point in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
(where
n
equals the number
of parts of the partition) whose coordinates sum to
m
.
Lecture hall partition first saw the light of day in
[
6
]
and have been the subject of
active research;
[
16
]
gives a survey.

The lecture hall cone
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is the vertex cone of
\Delta_{n}
at the origin.
With
\lambda=(1,1,\dots,1)
, the rational function
\rho_{\mathbf{0}}^{\lambda}(q)
(in the
language of (
1
)) is precisely the rational generating function of all lecture
hall partitions with
\leq n
parts.

The lecture hall simplex
\Delta_{n}
is rational (with denominator
n
), and so the recursion

(5)

\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta_{n}}^{\lambda}(q,jn+i)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta_{n}}^{\lambda}(q,jn+i-1)+q^{jn+i}\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta_{n-1}}^{\lambda}(q,j(n-1)+i-1)\,,

for any
j\geq 0
and
1\leq i\leq n
,
which follows from first principles
[
10
]
, might historically be the earliest
occurrence of a
q
-Ehrhart
quasi
polynomial.
In terms of the Chapoton constituents, the recursion can be translated as follows.
Define
\operatorname{cha}_{n,i}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)[x]
via

\operatorname{cha}_{n,i}\left([j]_{q}\right)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta_{n}}^{\lambda}(q,jn+i)

for
j\geq 0
and
1\leq i\leq n
, and define
\operatorname{cha}_{n,0}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)[x]
via

(6)

\operatorname{cha}_{n,n}(x)\,=\,\operatorname{cha}_{n,0}(1+qx)\,;

this last definition is motivated by

\operatorname{cha}_{n,n}\left([j]_{q}\right)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta_{n}}^{\lambda}(q,jn+n)\,=\,\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta_{n}}^{\lambda}(q,(j+1)n)\,=\,\operatorname{cha}_{n,0}\left([j+1]_{q}\right).

Thus we can start with

(7)

\operatorname{cha}_{1,0}(x)\,=\,1+qx\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\operatorname{cha}_{1,1}(x)\,=\,1+q+q^{2}x\,,

and (
5
) yields

(8)

\operatorname{cha}_{n,i}(x)\,=\,\operatorname{cha}_{n,i-1}(x)+q^{i}\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{n}\operatorname{cha}_{n-1,i-1}(x)

for
j\geq 0
and
1\leq i\leq n
.
We suspect that the equations (
6
), (
7
), and
(
8
) completely determine the family of polynomials
\operatorname{cha}_{n,i}(x)
, but it
is now clear how to prove this. Even more subtle is the determination of the actual polynomials;
we note that they were not computed in closed form in
[
10
]
.

We conclude this section by noting that, by Corollary
6
,
\operatorname{cha}_{n,0}(q,\frac{1}{1-q})
gives yet another incarnation of the rational generating function of all lecture hall partitions with
\leq n
parts.

3.
Proofs

Our main ingredient is the following.

Theorem 11

(Brion)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a rational polytope, we have the following identity of rational functions:

\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{z})\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}(\mathbf{z})\,.

We are ready to prove our refinement of Chapoton’s Theorem.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Brion’s Theorem
11
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,t)\,

Proof of Theorem
3
.

It follows from first principles (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 3.3]
) that the rational-function
form of
\sigma_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathbf{z})
, where
\mathcal{K}
is a
simplicial
rational cone (i.e.,
\mathcal{K}

has dimension many generators), has denominator

\prod_{\mathbf{g}}(1-\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{g}})
where the product is taken over all generators of
\mathcal{K}
.
Because every cone can be triangulated without introducing new generators, the same is true for a
general rational cone
\mathcal{K}
.

The generators of the vertex cone
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
of a given polytope are
g(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v})
for all
vertices
\mathbf{w}
that are adjacent to
\mathbf{v}
.
Thus specializing the integer-point transform of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
according to
(
9
) yields the denominator of

\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)

as

\prod_{\mathbf{w}\text{ adjacent to }\mathbf{v}}\left(1-q^{\lambda(g(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}))}\right).\qed

Next we prepare the ingredients for the proof of Corollary
7
.
Given a face
\mathcal{F}
of the polytope
\mathcal{P}
, the
tangent cone
of
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}\,:=\,\left\{\mathbf{x}+\lambda(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}):\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F},\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{P},\ \lambda\geq 0\right\}.

In particular, the tangent cone of a vertex
\mathbf{v}
equals
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Given a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote its indicator function by

[S](\mathbf{x})\,:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\mathbf{x}\in S,\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

The Brianchon–Gram relation (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Section 3.7]
) gives an identity among the
indicator functions of the tangent cones of a given polytope.

Theorem 12

(Brianchon–Gram)
.

For any polytope
\mathcal{P}
,

[\mathcal{P}]\,=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\text{ \rm face of }\mathcal{P}}(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{F}}\,[\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}]\,.

Proof of Corollary
7
.

Translate the given lattice polytope
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the (rational) polytope
\mathcal{Q}
so that the
barycenter of
\mathcal{Q}
is the origin; note that two corresponding vertices of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}

come with identical cones
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Because
\mathcal{Q}
is a rational, we can further shrink it such that for each of it vertices,

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,=\,\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Note that this implies
\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
We now apply the Brianchon–Gram Theorem
12
to integer-point transforms:

(11)

1\,=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}[\mathcal{Q}](\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}\,=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\text{ \rm face of }\mathcal{Q}}(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{F}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}[\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}](\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}\,=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\text{ \rm face of }\mathcal{Q}}(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{F}}\sigma_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{z})\,.

However, the rational generating function
\sigma_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{z})
is zero when

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}
contains a line (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Exercise 4.34]
), and so (
11
) simplifies to

1\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ vertex of }\mathcal{Q}}\sigma_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}}(\mathbf{z})\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ vertex of }\mathcal{Q}}\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}(\mathbf{z})\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}(\mathbf{z})\,.

On the other hand, by Corollary
4
, the constant term of

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,x)
equals

\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\rho_{\mathbf{v}}^{\lambda}(q)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},\ q^{\lambda_{2}},\ \dots,\ q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)\,=\,1\,.\qed

For our proof of Theorem
8
, we will need to following reciprocity
theorem
[
17
]
. We denote the relative interior of
\mathcal{K}
as
\mathcal{K}^{\circ}

and write
\frac{1}{\mathbf{z}}:=(\frac{1}{z_{1}},\frac{1}{z_{2}},\dots,\frac{1}{z_{d}})

Theorem 13

(Stanley)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a rational cone. Then, as rational functions,

(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{K}}\sigma_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\mathbf{z}}\right)\,=\,\sigma_{\mathcal{K}^{\circ}}(\mathbf{z})\,.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

The variant of Brion’s Theorem
11
for
\mathcal{P}^{\circ}
(see, e.g.,

[
4
, Exercise 11.9]
) says

\sigma_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}(\mathbf{z})\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}^{\circ}}(\mathbf{z})\,.

Thus

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}^{\lambda}\left(\tfrac{1}{q},t\right)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}q^{-t\lambda(\mathbf{v})}\,\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}^{\circ}}\left(q^{-\lambda_{1}},q^{-\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{-\lambda_{d}}\right)

and we can apply Theorem
13
to the above summands to deduce

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}^{\lambda}\left(\tfrac{1}{q},t\right)\,=\,(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{P}}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}q^{-t\lambda(\mathbf{v})}\,\sigma_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:vertexconespecialization}}}{{=}}\,(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,[-t]_{q})\,.

The second claim follows with

[-t]_{\frac{1}{q}}\,=\,\frac{1-q^{t}}{1-\frac{1}{q}}\,=\,q\,\frac{1-q^{t}}{q-1}\,=\,-q\,[t]_{q}\,.\qed

Proof of Theorem
9
.

By Brion’s Theorem
11
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,kp+r)\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in(kp+r)\mathcal{P}}q^{\lambda(\mathbf{m})}\,=\,\sigma_{(kp+r)\mathcal{P}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{(kp+r)\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}q^{k\,\lambda(p\mathbf{v})}\,\sigma_{r\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right).

With (
2
), this yields

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda}(q,kp+r)\,=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{r\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)\bigl((q-1)[k]_{q}+1\bigr)^{\lambda(p\mathbf{v})},

which can be viewed as the rational version of Theorem
2
.
So the polynomial we are after is

\operatorname{cha}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\lambda,r}(q,x)\,=\,\sum_{\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{r\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}}\left(q^{\lambda_{1}},q^{\lambda_{2}},\dots,q^{\lambda_{d}}\right)\bigl((q-1)x+1\bigr)^{\lambda(p\mathbf{v})},

which has degree
\max\{\lambda(p\mathbf{v}):\,\mathbf{v}\text{ \rm vertex of }\mathcal{P}\}
.

Our proof of Theorem
3
extends almost verbatim to the rational case, as the
generators of

are still
\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}
for all vertices
\mathbf{w}
that are adjacent
to
\mathbf{v}
; the only difference is that their integral version is now

.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

We start as in our proof of Theorem

:
