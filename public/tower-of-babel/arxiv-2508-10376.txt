Lower Bounds on Tree Covers
Yu Chen, Zihan Tan, Hangyu Xu

Abstract
Given an n n -point metric space ( X , d X ) (X,d_{X}) , a tree cover 𝒯 {\mathcal{T}} is a set of | 𝒯 | = k |{\mathcal{T}}|=k trees on X X such that every pair of vertices in X X has a low-distortion path in one of the trees in 𝒯 {\mathcal{T}} . Tree covers have been playing a crucial role in graph algorithms for decades, and the research focus is the construction of tree covers with small size k k and distortion. When k = 1 k=1 , the best distortion is known to be Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) . For a constant k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , the best distortion upper bound is O ~ ​ ( n 1 k ) \tilde{O}(n^{\frac{1}{k}}) and the strongest lower bound is Ω ⁡ ( log k ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log_{k}n) , leaving a gap to be closed. In this paper, we improve the lower bound to Ω ⁡ ( n 1 2 k − 1 ) \Omega(n^{\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}}) . Our proof is a novel analysis on a structurally simple grid-like graph, which utilizes some combinatorial fixed-point theorems. We believe that they will prove useful for analyzing other tree-like data structures as well.

1.1
Our result

In this paper, we make progress towards closing this gap. Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For every
k\geq 1
, there is an
n
-point metric, such that any size-
k
tree cover has stretch
\Omega_{k}(n^{\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}})
.

Specifically, when
k=2
, our result implies a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, nearly matching the upper bound of
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
in
[
BFN22
]
. For constant
k
, we show a polynomial lower bound, refuting the possibility of
\mathrm{poly}\log n
stretch. Therefore,
Theorem

1
provides an answer to the above questions by Bartal, Fandina, Neiman, and Le.

For proving
Theorem

1
, we employ a novel approach. At a high-level, we construct a grid-like structure, compute its triangulation, label each point according to its location in the tree cover, and derive the stretch lower bound by Tucker’s Lemma. Our proof reveals a close connection between tree covers and the celebrated fixed-point theorems in combinatorial topology, and we believe that this connection will be useful in analyzing tree covers in different settings and other tree-like data structures.

The stretch upper bound of
\tilde{O}(n^{1/k})
in
[
BFN22
]
is actually achieved by Ramsey tree covers, a restricted type of tree covers that require each vertex to choose a home tree to preserve its distances to all other vertices (formal definition in
Section

5
). They also showed a stretch lower bound of
\Omega(n^{1/k})
for Ramsey tree covers.
As a side result, we give an alternative proof of this near-optimal lower bound. Our hard instance is a high-dimensional cyclic grid, which is structurally simpler than the recursive construction in
[
BFN22
]
. Our proof is similar to the proof of
Theorem

1
and also makes use of a variant of Tucker’s Lemma.

Technical Overview

In this section, we provide a high-level overview of our techniques, with the focus on the connection between tree covers and combinatorial topology.

Case
k=1
: examining a tree-based tour on a cycle

We start by reviewing the proof of the
\Omega(n)
distortion lower bound for the case
k=1
. We present a proof sketch similar to the one in
[
RR98
]
, and an alternative proof can be found in
[
Gup01
]
.

Consider an unweighted cycle
(0,1,2,\ldots,n-1,0)
and a dominating tree
T
on the same set of vertices. We may assume without loss of generality that for each edge
(i,j)
in
T
(with
i<j
), its length/weight is the cycle-distance between vertices
i
and
j
, namely
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(i,j)=\min\left\{j-i,n-(j-i)\right\}
, as otherwise we can decrease the length of this edge without hurting the distortion of
T
. To reveal the topological difference between a tree and a cycle, we “interpolate” their edges as follows.

1.

For each edge
(i,j)
in
T
, we subdivide it by
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(i,j)-1
new nodes (called
interpolating nodes
), which are then labeled by consecutive integers connecting
i,j
in the natural order. For example, edge
(3,6)\to(3,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}5},6)
, and edge
(1,n-3)\to(1,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n-1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n-2},n-3)
. Edges
(3,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4}),({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}5})({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}5},6)
are called
interpolating edges
. See
Figure

1
for an illustration.

2.

For each
i
, we define
\Pi_{i}
as the sequence of all nodes (including the old ones and the interpolating ones) we encounter while walking along the path from the old
i
to the old
i+1
in
T
. Observe that the tree-distance between old nodes
i,i+1
is
|\Pi_{i}|
, the number of interpolating edges in
\Pi_{i}
.
Here
\Pi_{n-1}
connects the old node
n-1
to the old node
0
in the tree.

3.

Finally, we define
\Pi
as the circular concatenation of sequences
\Pi_{0},\Pi_{1}\ldots,\Pi_{n-1}
, identifying, for each
i
, the last node of
\Pi_{i}
and the first node of
\Pi_{i+1}
(both being the old
i+1
), so
\Pi
is a closed circular tour.

It is immediate to verify that all
\Pi_{i}
and
\Pi
are continuous, in that any integer

is sided by either
j+1
or
j-1
. The key to the lower bound proof is to
examine the continuous behaviors of
\Pi
and
\Pi_{i}
’s, which are extracted from the tree, on the cycle
.

The shortest way to go continuously from
i
to
i+1
on the cycle is to directly use the edge
(i,i+1)
.
We say that
\Pi_{i}
is
obedient
2

2

There is a notion in combinatorial topology called “degree of a mapping”, and our notion “obedient” is similar to their “degree-
0

, if the number of
(i,i+1)
appearances in
\Pi_{i}
exceeds that of
(i+1,i)
by
1
.
For example,
\Pi_{2}=(2,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}3},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4},5,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4},3)
is obedient, but
(2,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n-1}\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4},3)

Intuitively,
\Pi_{i}
is obedient if it “essentially travels on the short side of cycle”.
See
Figure

1

The following two properties are easy to prove:

•

if
\Pi_{i}
is obedient, the number of
(j,j+1)

(j+1,j)
for each

; and

•

if
\Pi_{i}
is non-obedient, it contains all
n

The crucial observation is that, if some
\Pi_{i}
is non-obedient, then the distortion of

1

|\Pi_{i}|\geq n-1

\Pi_{i}

. From the first property of obedient

i

To summarize, we interpolate the tree edges, mapping the cycle into a continuous tree-tour
\Pi

\Pi_{0},\ldots,\Pi_{n-1}
, and examine their behaviors from the topological perspective. The key point is that a cycle must contain an edge
(i,i+1)
whose corresponding trajectory
\Pi_{i}
is non-obedient, and such an edge, which we call an

, is the cause of
\Omega(n)
distortion.

Case
k=2
: the torus grid

The best distortion upper bound for the
k=2
case is
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})

[
BFN22
]
. Therefore, a natural first graph to try proving a matching lower bound on is the
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
grid. However, with a delicate design, two trees can actually cover the grid with distortion
O(1)
. See
Appendix

A

According to the lesson from the
k=1
case, to make it harder for trees to cover, we should plant more cycles on the grid. Specifically, we connect each first-row vertex to their corresponding last-row vertex, and connect each first-column vertex to their corresponding last-column vertex, so the resulting graph is a torus grid. As we show next, this torus grid poses a
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
distortion lower bound for two trees.

We focus on the following two types of non-contractible cycles: vertical/horizontal cycles, which are cycles that go around the grid in the vertical/horizontal way, respectively. See
Figure

\sqrt{n}
.

We denote the trees by
T_{1},T_{2}
. To prove the
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
distortion lower bound, it suffices to find a pair of vertices that are
O(1)
-close in the torus grid but
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
-far in both trees, which is done as follows.

1.

Every vertical cycle contains a non-obedient edge for
T_{1}
. Therefore, the union of all such edges must form a “horizontal cut” that hits all vertical cycles (see
Figure

2
).

2.

Similarly, every horizontal cycle contains a non-obedient edge for
T_{2}
. Therefore, the union of all such edges must form a “vertical cut” that hits all horizontal cycles.

3.

The above horizontal cut and vertical cut must intersect in the torus grid. At their intersection we can find the desired pair of vertices. Specifically, as illustrated in
Figure

2
, a
T_{1}
-non-obedient edge
(v,u_{1})
and a
T_{2}
-non-obedient edge
(v,u_{2})
share a common endpoint
v
, so
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{1}}(v,u_{1})=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
and
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{2}}(v,u_{2})=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.
Then

•

if
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{2}}(v,u_{1})=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, then
v,u_{1}
are
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
-far in both trees;

•

if
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{1}}(v,u_{2})=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, then
v,u_{2}
are
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
-far in both trees;

•

if
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{2}}(v,u_{1}),\mathrm{dist}_{T_{1}}(v,u_{2})=o(\sqrt{n})
, then by triangle inequality,

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{1}}(u_{1},u_{2})\geq\mathrm{dist}_{T_{1}}(v,u_{1})-\mathrm{dist}_{T_{1}}(v,u_{2})=\Omega(\sqrt{n}),

and similarly

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, and so

u_{1},u_{2}
are
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
-far in both trees.

General
k
: the role of Tucker’s Lemma

Based on our approach in the
k=2
case, we can set up a similar plan for proving lower bounds for general
k
: construct a
k
-dimensional grid with length
n^{1/k}
, make it cyclic by connecting
i
-dimensional antipodal points for each dimension
i
, find the “dimension-
i
cut” consisting of
T_{i}
-non-obedient edges, which is essentially a
(k-1)
-dimensional plane, and finally analyze the intersection of all
k
planes.

The issue resides in the very last step (Step 3 in the
k=2
case): we are only guaranteed that, around the intersection, for each
i
, some pair of points cross the dimension-
i
cut, but that does not guarantee the existence of a pair of points simultaneously crossing all dimension-
i
cuts.
Specifically, we consider the
k
-dimensional cube that represents the intersection of all dimension-cut planes. We associate with each vertex a vector in
\left\{+1,-1\right\}^{k}
as follows. For each vertex, for each
i
, if it lies on the left side (left and right sides can be dictated in an arbitrary way) of the dimension-
i
cut, then we set the
i
-th coordinate of its associated vector
v
as
v_{i}=+1
, otherwise we set
v_{i}=-1
. Then, among all vectors in the cube,

•

what we can prove (via the results in
[
Mus14
]
): for each
i
, there exists a pair of vertices whose associated vectors
u,v
satisfy that
u_{i}=-v_{i}
(such a cube is called a
centrally labeled
cube); and

•

the goal is to show: there is a pair of vertices whose associated vectors
u,v
satisfy that
u_{i}=-v_{i}
for all
i
(such a pair of vertices are said to form a
complementary edge
).

In fact, what we can prove does not necessarily imply the goal for
k\geq 3
. For example, the
8
vectors in a
3
-dimensional cube can be

\left\{(1,1,1)\times 5,(1,-1,-1),(-1,-1,1),(-1,1,-1)\right\}.

So the cube is indeed a centrally labeled cube, but there are no complementary edges.

Luckily, the existence of a centrally labeled cube is enough to derive a
\Omega(n^{1/k})
lower bound for Ramsey tree covers with
k
trees. We present the details of this proof in
Section

5
.

But to prove lower bound for general tree covers, we have to find complementary edges, and this is where we need Tucker’s lemma, which states that, in a triangulated domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in a labeling of its vertices by integers in
\left\{-d\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,d\right\}
that is antipodally symmetric on the domain’s boundary, there is a complementary edge. Since the number of possible labels in our setting is
2^{k}
(the size of set
\left\{+1,-1\right\}^{k}
), we have to significantly increase the number of dimensions in our construction from
k
to
d=2^{k-1}
, which reduces the length of our grid-like structure from
n^{\frac{1}{k}}
to
n^{\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}}
. Therefore, we are only able to prove an
\Omega(n^{\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}})
stretch lower bound for general tree covers with this approach. Some additional modifications to our grid-like construction are needed in order to achieve the antipodally symmetric property required in Tucker’s lemma.
We present the details in
Section

4
.

Preliminaries: Simplices, Triangulations, and Tucker’s Lemma

A set
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
k
-dimensional simplex
(or
k
-simplex
) if it is the convex hull
{\mathrm{conv}}(A)
of a set
A
of
k+1
affinely independent points.
Vertices
of
\sigma
, denoted as
V(\sigma)
, refer to points in
A
. Simplices of the form
{\mathrm{conv}}(B)
where
B\subseteq A
are called
faces
of
\sigma
.

Our proof is based on some combinatorial fixed-point theorems. To formally state them, we need the notion of triangulation and its antipodal symmetry.

Definition 2

(Triangulation)
.

Let
X
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is homeomorphic to the
\ell_{2}
-norm unit ball. A finite family
\Gamma
of simplices is a
triangulation of

X
, iff
\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Gamma}\sigma=X
, and the following two properties hold:

•

For each simplex
\sigma\in\Gamma
, all faces of
\sigma
are also simplices in
\Gamma
.

•

For any two simplices
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Gamma
, the intersection
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
is a face of both
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.

We define the vertices of
\Gamma
as
V(\Gamma)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Gamma}V(\sigma)
.
We define the
boundary
of
\Gamma
to be the triangulation
\partial(\Gamma)\subseteq\Gamma
containing all simplices in
\Gamma
that lie entirely in the boundary
\partial(X)
of
X
.

Definition 3

(Antipodal Symmetry)
.

A triangulation
\Gamma
is said to be
antipodally symmetric on the boundary
if, for all simplices
\sigma\in\partial(\Gamma)
, the opposite simplex
-\sigma
(
-\sigma=\left\{-v\mid v\in\sigma\right\}
) also lies in
\partial(\Gamma)
.

Definition 4

(Labeling of Triangulation)
.

A labeling of triangulation
\Gamma
is an assignment
\ell:V(\Gamma)\rightarrow L
that assigns an integer of a set
L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
to each vertex of
\Gamma
.

With the above definitions, we can now state Tucker’s lemma (originally in
[
Tuc45
]
; see
[
Meu06
]
for a survey) and its variant.

Theorem 5

(Tucker’s Lemma)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a triangulation of the domain
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is antipodally symmetric on the boundary. Let
\ell:V(\Gamma)\rightarrow\{-d,\ldots,-2,-1,1,2,\ldots,d\}
be a labeling such that
\ell(v)=-\ell(-v)
for all
v\in\partial(X)
. Then
\Gamma
contains a complementary edge, that is, an edge
(v,v^{\prime})
with
\ell(v)=-\ell(v^{\prime})
.

We can further use
2^{d}
labels, instead of
2d
labels in Tucker’s lemma, to obtain the following extension of Tucker’s lemma (Theorem 1.9 in
[
GM13
]
):

Theorem 6

(
[
GM13
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a triangulation of
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is antipodally symmetric on the boundary of the domain
X
. Let
\ell:V(\Gamma)\rightarrow\left\{+1,-1\right\}^{k}
be a labeling such that
\ell(v)=-\ell(-v)
for all
v\in\partial(X)
. Then
\Gamma
contains a neutral simplex, that is, a simplex
\sigma
such that for all
1\leq i\leq n
, there exist
v,v^{\prime}\in V(\sigma)
such that
\ell_{i}(v)=-\ell_{i}(v^{\prime})
, where
\ell_{i}(v)
is the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
coordinate of
\ell(v)
.

Theorem

6
can be proved by either applying Borsuk-Ulam Theorem
[
MBZ03
]
(a topology approach) or by extending the framework of Ky Fan
[
Fan52
]
to more possible labelings
[
GM13
]
(a combinatorial approach).
We refer the interested readers to
[
GM13
]
for a survey of relevant approaches.

Proof of
Theorem

1

In this section, we provide the proof of
Theorem

1
.
We will construct a metric
(X,d_{X})
, and show that for any
k
dominating trees
T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}
on
X

u,v
in

\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\left\{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{i}}(u,v)\right\}=\Omega\bigg(\frac{n^{\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}}}{2^{k-1}}\bigg)\cdot d_{X}(u,v).

4.1
Construction of the Hard Instance

We first define an edge-weighted graph
\tilde{G}
as follows.

•

Vertex set:
V(\tilde{G})=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:||x||_{1}\leq r\}
;

•

Edges set:
E(\tilde{G})=E_{0}\cup E_{\mathrm{sp}}
, where edges in
E_{\mathrm{sp}}
are called
special edges
, and

–

E_{0}=\{(u,v):||u-v||_{1}=1\}
,

–

E_{\mathrm{sp}}=\{(u,v):u=-v,||u||_{1}=r\}
;

•

Edge weights:
w_{e}=\begin{cases}1,\textnormal{ if }e\in E_{0};\\
0,\textnormal{ if }e\in E_{\mathrm{sp}}.\\
\end{cases}

Our hard instance
G
is the graph obtained by contracting all the edges in
E_{\mathrm{sp}}
within
\tilde{G}
. Specifically, we denote the two vertices contracted by the one with positive value at the first non-zero coordinate.
By choosing
d=2^{k-1}
and
r=\Theta(n^{1/d})
, we can ensure that
|V(G)|=n
.
The metric
(X,d_{X})
is simply the shortest path distance metric on all vertices of
G
.

4.2
Reduction to Tucker’s Lemma

Lemma 7
.

There is a triangulation
\Gamma^{\prime}
of
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:||x||_{1}\leq r\}
, such that
V(\Gamma^{\prime})=V(\tilde{G})
, and for every edge
(u,v)
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
,
||u-v||_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let
\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\right\}
be the standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

5
Lower Bound for Ramsey Tree Covers

Given a metric space
(X,d_{X})
on
|X|=n
points, a
Ramsey tree cover
is a collection
{\mathcal{T}}=\left\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\right\}
of (edge-weighted) trees on
X
, such that

•

for each
1\leq i\leq k
and every pair
x,y
of vertices in
X
,
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{i}}(x,y)\geq d_{X}(x,y)
(that is, each
T_{i}
is a
dominating tree
); and

•

for every vertex
x
in
X
, there exists a tree
T_{i}\in{\mathcal{T}}
(called the
home tree
of
x
), such that, for every vertex
y\in X
,
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{i}}(x,y)\leq\alpha\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
.

k=|{\mathcal{T}}|
is called the

of

and
\alpha\geq 1
is called the
distortion
or
stretch
of
{\mathcal{T}}
.

In this section, we provide an alternative proof of the near-optimal bound of
\Omega_{k}(n^{1/k})
for Ramsey tree covers of size

similar to the one introduced in

4

k
dominating trees

, there is a vertex

in

We now show that the two trees
T_{1},T_{2}
constructed above cover the grid with distortion
O(1)
.

Consider a pair
u,v
of vertices in the grid. Let
c
be the center of largest-indexed layer in
T_{1}
that has an area containing both
u
and
v
.
Recall that the
c
-tree is divided into
8
regions.
We distinguish between the following cases.

Case 1.
u,v
lie in non-adjacent regions

(e.g., regions I and VII in
Figure

7
).
Let
\theta
denote the angle between vectors
\vec{uc}
and
\vec{vc}
, then
\pi/4\leq\theta\leq 7\pi/4
. Denote by
C
the constant hidden in
14
, by basic trigonometry,

\displaystyle d(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d^{2}(c,u)+d^{2}(c,v)-2\cos\theta\cdot d(c,u)d(c,v)}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{d^{2}(c,u)+d^{2}(c,v)-\sqrt{2}d(c,u)d(c,v)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2}}}{2}\cdot\bigg(d(c,u)+d(c,v)\bigg).

Denote
C^{\prime}=\frac{2C}{\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2}}}
. Then the tree distance in
T_{1}
between
u
and
v
satisfies

\displaystyle d_{T_{1}}(u,v)

\displaystyle=d_{T_{1}}(u,c)+d_{T_{1}}(c,v)\leq C\cdot(d(u,c)+d(c,v))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C}{\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2}}}\cdot d(u,v)=C^{\prime}\cdot d(u,v),

where
d(u,v)
denotes the grid distance between
u
and
v
.

Case 2.
u,v
lie in adjacent regions.

There are two possibilities: diagonally neighboring (e.g., regions I and VIII) or orthogonally neighboring (e.g., regions I and II). In the first case, observe that
u
and
v
belong to the same area in the next layer of
T_{1}
, a contradiction to the assumption that
c
is the center of largest-indexed layer in
T_{1}
that has an area containing both
u
and
v
. In the second case, they belong to the same area in the next layer of
T_{2}
. We may then zoom in that layer, further distinguish between the cases where they lie in adjacent or non-adjacent regions, and repeat our analysis.

To sum up, for a pair
u,v
of vertices: either

•

they lie in non-adjacent regions at some layer, in which case by similar analysis in Case 1 we can show that their grid distance is preserved up to factor
O(1)
; or

•

they lie in the same area in the last layer of
T_{1}
or
T_{2}
, which is a subtree of
O(1)
size and therefore preserves grid distances within
O(1)
factor.
