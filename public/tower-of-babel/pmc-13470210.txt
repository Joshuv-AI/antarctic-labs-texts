The normal distance from soft tissue nasion to the arbitrary axis-orbital plane is similar in pediatric, adolescent, and young adult samples
Authors: Aleš Čelar, Stefan Lettner, Erwin Jonke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnatomical facebows establish the arbitrary axis-orbital plane by assessing both the hinge axis points and landmark orbitale. The estimated point orbitale results from the facebow’s nasion relator height of 25.4 mm. This height was investigated in adults but it is unclear if their anthropometric dimensions adequately translate to children and adolescents. The present cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the validity of this height retrospectively for the ages 6 to19.9 years. We used lateral cephalometric radiographs of 420 individuals (210 males, 210 females,) and divided the sample into 7 age groups. A single investigator measured the perpendicular distance of soft tissue nasion to a line connecting the averaged arbitrary hinge axis point and the averaged point orbitale. Data were analyzed using an ordinary least squares linear regression model (p < 0.05). The perpendicular distances ranged from 11 to 33.5 mm (mean 22.8, standard deviation 3.5). There were no significant differences of the distance between age groups, or sex within age groups, or sex between age groups. The use of a fixed 25.4 mm nasion relator height in children and adolescents was not associated with more instrumental error in assessing point orbitale than in the adult age group. The present study’s data did not preclude application of this nasion relator height from age 6 years onward.Keywords: Arbitrary facebow, Anatomical facebow, Axis-orbital plane, Nasion relator height, OrbitaleSubject terms: Anatomy, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Facebows are commonly used in prosthetic dentistry, orthodontics, and orthognathic surgery. A kinematic facebow precisely records the 3D relationship between maxillary dentition and kinematic hinge axis of the mandible1–3. However, correct recording of the kinematic axis and its transfer to an articulator are time-consuming procedures.

In a quicker approach, anatomical facebows estimate the transverse hinge axis points by attaching ear pieces to the external auditory meati. The third point for establishing the arbitrary axis-orbital plane (AOP) results from assessing point orbitale with a nasion relator. Its height of 25.4 mm represents the perpendicular distance between soft tissue nasion and arbitrary AOP4. This height need not match the individual anatomical distance in children and adolescents. Although deviation from the actual dimension affects proper facebow transfer of the AOP inclination, the correctness of this nasion relator height was not studied during growth.

The aim of the present investigation was to evaluate if a 25.4 mm nasion relator height is appropriate for determining point orbitale in individuals aged 6 to 19.9 years. We intended to measure the perpendicular distance between soft tissue nasion and arbitrary AOP on lateral cephalometric skull radiographs. Our null hypotheses stated no differences of this distance between 7 age groups as well as between males and females.

Methods

Study design

The institutional review board approved the present retrospective, cross-sectional study (Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna, Austria, vote nr. 1326/2020). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations including the protection of personal data.

Setting

We recruited records of children, adolescents, and young adults, who attended the Vienna University Dental Clinic for orthodontic treatment planning from 2008 until 2023. They had come on referral or own initiative. All participants had undergone a standard diagnostic protocol including medical history, impressions for casts or intraoral scanning, clinical examination, facial and intraoral photography, as well as panoramic and lateral cephalometric radiography.

Participant selection

Eligibility criteria included presence of digital lateral cephalometric radiographs of 6- to 19.9-year-old males and females. Exclusion criteria were previous orthodontic therapy, orthognathic surgery, cleft lip and palate, orofacial syndrome, orofacial trauma, neoplasia, rickets, and growth hormone therapy.

We used a random generator to select 420 young individuals from the patient pool of the university clinic. The sample was primarily of European descent (n = 371), complemented by individuals from the Middle East (n = 49). The sample’s mean age was 12.7 years with a standard deviation (SD) of 4.1. All participants had provided informed consent for orthodontic treatment planning. The informed consent statement for minor participants had been received from parents or legal guardians. We divided the sample into 7 biennial age groups, representing ages 6–7.9, 8–9.9, 10–11.9, 12–13.9, 14–15.9, 16–17.9, and 18–19.9 years. Each group consisted of 30 males and 30 females.

Study size

The sample size calculation was based on the study of Wilcox et al.4, who investigated the accuracy of locating orbitale in adults. We considered α = 0.05, a SD of 3.5 mm, and a clinically remarkable difference of 3 mm between the groups. The slightly smaller SD than Wilcox et al.’s 3.87 took into account that we expected smaller dimensions of children’s faces. The calculation yielded 30 males and 30 females in each group and a resulting power of 0.9.

Variables, data sources, measurements

All lateral cephalometric radiographs had been taken at the university clinic using a Philips Optimus 50 x-ray unit (Philips Healthcare, Eindhoven, Netherlands, film-focus distance 4 m). Fuji FCR XG-1 software rendered the digital image (Fuji Photo Film Co., Tokyo, Japan). The printed radiographs measured 26.5 cm × 20 cm and displayed a 50 mm scale. A single, experienced orthodontist identified the following landmarks:

soft tissue nasion: most concave point of the skin between forehead and nose,

orbitale: most inferior point of the averaged orbital rim,

posterior condyle: most posterior condylar point of the averaged mandibular condyle; this point

represented the arbitrary hinge axis in this study,

arbitrary axis-orbital plane: line from the averaged posterior condyle point to averaged orbitale,

perpendicular distance of soft tissue nasion to AOP: normal linear distance of soft tissue nasion at a

90° angle to the arbitrary AOP.

The investigator delineated the arbitrary AOP and its perpendicular line passing through soft tissue nasion on the anonymized radiograph (Fig. 1). He measured the perpendicular distance between soft tissue nasion and AOP with a vernier caliper (Zürich model; Schweickhardt Co, Seitingen-Oberflacht, Germany). Because of radiographic magnification, he determined the actual millimeter value by transferring the caliper jaws directly onto the radiograph’s millimeter scale. He read the value to the nearest 0.3 mm. Two months later the same investigator repeated the tracings and measurements of the perpendicular distance on 20 randomly selected and newly printed radiographs for determination of the measurement error.

Bias and missing data

Efforts to counteract potential sources of bias encompassed researcher blinding, random sampling, use of a standardized data collection protocol and standardized instruments, plus validation of registered data for the reduction of typing errors. The data set was complete, i.e. there were no missing data.

Statistics

We assessed measurement error by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Dahlberg’s error. A linear model estimated the perpendicular distance from soft tissue nasion to orbitale as dependent variable and sex and age group as independent variables. We visually checked approximate normality of residuals by means of a quantile–quantile plot. Differences in age and gender were evaluated using Tukey-corrected pairwise t-tests, calculated conditionally from an ordinary least squares model (p < 0.05). Differences of estimated marginal means of the perpendicular distance are presented pairwise for all age groups.

We entered the data into IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (Armonk, New York, USA). Sample size, descriptive statistics, ICC, Dahlberg formula, and inferential statistics were calculated in R statistical software (R version 4.4.2, R Core Team 2024_R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing_. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Measurement error

The ICC of 20 repeated measurements of the perpendicular distance between soft tissue nasion and arbitrary AOP yielded 0.89 (95% confidence interval from 0.86 to 0.93). The same 20 repeated measurements resulted in a Dahlberg error of 0.88 (95% confidence interval from 0.6 to 1.3).

Descriptive and inductive statistics

Overall, the perpendicular distances ranged from 11 to 33.5 mm and averaged 22.8 ± 3.5 mm (Table 1). The middle 50% of the data represented a range from 21 to 25 mm. The means differed between the age groups by 0 to 1.5 mm (medians 0 to 1.8 mm). Figure 2 displays a comparison of data using box-and-whisker plots across the age groups. Data did not show relevant deviation from normal distribution in the quantile–quantile plots.

Discussion

We found similar perpendicular distances from soft tissue nasion to the estimated AOP at the means and all quartiles from 6 to 19.9 years of age. Similar means and quartiles indicated that this distance was fairly stable from age 6 years onward. Further, there were no significant differences between the age groups same as between the sexes. Consequently, anatomical facebows can most likely be used from age 6 years onward with similar errors as in adults.

The complete range of our measurements (11 to 33.5 mm) agrees with the results of Wilcox et al. They quantified the distance from soft tissue nasion to the Frankfort horizontal on cephalometric radiographs of 114 individuals aged 18 years or above4. In Wilcox et al.’s investigation, the minimum was 15.9 mm and the maximum was 39.4 mm. Due to different methodology, their minimum, maximum, and mean of 26.8 mm are not directly comparable with the present study. However, there was a similar range in a clearly older sample.

The findings of our study do not align with the common expectation of smaller anatomical dimensions in children. Between nasion and orbitale, the eyes and the orbits largely contribute to the height of the upper midface. Human orbits attained 86–96% of the adult dimension at 7 to 8 years of age 5,6. In addition, orbital height increased by age without significant difference between males and females7. Orbits and neurocranium grow faster than the lower midface or the mandible. The latter reach adult size later. For instance, the perpendicular distance from nasion to the palatal plane increases until age 19 years8.

Problems of incorrect facebow transfer concern all reference points of the AOP, i.e. the condylar hinge axis points and orbitale. The transverse condylar axis and the maxillary dental arch represent the patient’s gnathic geometry in the articulator. A correctly transferred geometry enables articulators to replicate the patient’s arc of closure for accurate reproduction of functional occlusal contacts3,9–11. Incorrect rotational condylar axis positions cause aberrant arcs of closure. They affect the occlusion of restorations, the maxillary incisor’s vertical position, biting force, or masticatory efficiency10,12–14. The resulting error could necessitate considerable occlusal adjustment or a redesign of incisor restorations, particularly in patients with reduced or deficient anterior guidance15. Nagy et al.’s method of axis location should be applied clinically for the prevention of such failure. Their technique determined the transverse axis with an ear-piece facebow within 2 mm to the kinematic axis in 96%16.

Incorrect transfer of point orbitale raises or lowers the anterior maxillary cast17. When predicting orthognathic surgery outcomes, aberrations of dental arch orientation aggravate the synthesis of diagnostic findings from mounted casts with those from clinical examination, lateral cephalometric radiographs, and profile photographs18,19.

Condylar axis deviations of up to 5 mm from the kinematic hinge axis produced clinically acceptable occlusal errors of 0.2 mm3. Correspondingly, aberrations of point orbitale of up to 5 mm could be acceptable. Yet, in the present study some individuals showed substantial deviation from the average perpendicular distance between soft tissue nasion and arbitrary AOP of up to 11 mm. In such instances, use of an adjustable nasion relator should be considered for customized facebow transfer4,20. Face scans or lateral cephalometric radiographs provide data for individual nasion relator adjustment.

Limitations of the present study refer to restriction of 3D structures to 2D x-ray images. All radiographs originated from a single institution and were measured by a single investigator. This setting may represent sources of bias despite randomized selection of patient records from a period of 16 years. The cephalometric data contained averaged landmarks of the mandibular condyles and orbits. The most posterior point of the averaged mandibular condyle defined the arbitrary axis. Its deviation from the kinematic hinge axis was not evaluated in the present work. Further, the cross-sectional nature of the data does not represent individual longitudinal growth but characterizes estimates of a single point in time. In terms of generalizability, the results of this study refer to Europeans and individuals from the Middle East. Further research should evaluate morphological metrics of Asians and Africans.

Within these limitations, the present study failed to reject both null hypotheses. The perpendicular distance from soft tissue nasion to AOP did not differ significantly between the studied age groups and between the sexes. We conclude that using an anatomical facebow with a nasion relator height of 1 inch from age 6 years onward is not associated with greater errors in estimating point orbitale than in adults.
