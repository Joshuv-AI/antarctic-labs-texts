Relationship between nanoscale assembly and cytotoxicity in the piperazine amides of betulinic acid: an in vitro study
Authors: Martina Wimmerová, Miroslav Šlouf, Lucie Rárová, Marie Kvasnicová, David Šaman, Zdeněk Wimmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Six new piperazine amides of betulinic acid, partly decorated with a glycine unit, were synthesized and purified and their structures were elucidated. This study explored whether relationships could be identified among the molecular structures, nanoscale self-assembly, and cytotoxicity of the synthesized compounds. Because of the limited quantities of the target compounds finally available for the analytical methods, their selection had to be very careful. NMR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry were used to elucidate the molecular structures, while transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to characterize the supramolecular structures formed after dissolution in methanol and water. The target compounds reproducibly displayed several basic morphologies depending on the solvent type (methanol vs. water) and the time in solution (1 h vs. 48 h). Several characteristic morphologies were observed, including cloud-like assemblies, isometric nanoparticles of various sizes, fibrous nanostructures with thicknesses below 10 nm, and combinations of these structures, depending on the compound, solvent, and time in solution. The target compounds (6 and 8), the parent compound (1) and the intermediates (2 and 4) were subjected to cytotoxicity assays in the HeLa and MCF7 cancer cell lines while using normal human fibroblasts (BJ) as the reference cells. Cisplatin (cis-diaminedichloroplatinum(ii), CDDP), a pharmacologically used anticancer agent, was used for comparing the cytotoxicity levels and cytotoxicity profiles of the studied compounds. Compounds 6 and 8 were specifically examined for possible associations between nanoscale self-assembly and cytotoxicity studied in vitro. The results provided initial insights into the complex relationship among the molecular structures, self-assembly, and cytotoxicity of the studied compounds.

Introduction

Small molecules based on natural plant products have often been subjected to nanoscale assembly. The nanostructured materials display various physicochemical characteristics. They are applicable in various fields of investigation, namely, in pharmacology, medicine, and different technical areas.1 The capability of a nanoscale assembling of triterpenoids, secondary plant metabolites, selected among many plant products, has been studied in different organic solvents and in aqueous media.2–4 The semisynthetic derivatives of triterpenoids have also been included in this investigation.2–4 Subsequently, the investigation of the ability to display biological activity usually represented an accompanying objective in the complex studies of the above plant products and of their semisynthetic derivatives.2–4 During the current investigation program, numerous natural triterpenoids and their different semisynthetic derivatives were studied in searching for the convenient compounds capable of displaying required characteristics.2 Based on the experience resulting from a recent investigation, the derivatives of ursolic acid seem to be the most potent ones so far in terms of their capability for nanoscale assembly among the compounds derived from other frequently studied pentacyclic triterpenoids.4 In contrast, only a limited number of the studied derivatives of betulinic acid has displayed these characteristics thus far.2 However, different biological activities, such as cytotoxicity, antiviral activity and/or antimicrobial activity, have been displayed by betulinic acid derivatives quite often.5–8 We have already documented a relationship between the cytotoxicity and the dynamic nanoscale assembly of the studied compounds.9 Generally, nanoscale materials have already been proven to affect the biological activity of drugs either positively or negatively, and they may positively assist in the targeted transport of biologically active drugs to the active sites of receptors to augment and accelerate the mode of action of the drugs.10,11

Betulinic acid [(3β)-3-hydroxylup-20(29)-en-28-oic acid; 1; Scheme 1] is the pentacyclic triterpenoid of the lupane family, and it belongs among the secondary plant metabolites.

Synthetic procedure: (i) acetic anhydride, DIPEA, DMAP, and THF; (ii) (a) oxalyl chloride and DCM; (b) N-Boc-protected piperazine, DIPEA, and DCM in an argon atmosphere; (iii) HCl (gas) in 1,4-dioxane; (iv) N-Boc-glycine, HOBt, NMM, EDC·HCl, and DCM in an argon atmosphere; and (v) LiOH·H2O, and methanol.

Betulinic acid and its wide spectrum of semisynthetic derivatives have been reviewed several times in recent years.12–15 These investigations have documented the spectrum of biological effects of betulinic acid and its derivatives, but there are still relatively rare reports on their physicochemical characteristics, including nanoscale assembly.16 Piperazine belongs to the class of polyamines. This cyclic diamine is often considered a biologically active molecule, such as in functional antibiotics or serotonin antagonists acting at the serotonin receptors17,18 or in the recently designed cytotoxic drugs and compounds showing the topoisomerase I inhibition effect.19–21 Glycine is the simplest, achiral and stable proteinogenic amino acid. It displays different biological effects, including the effect of the neurotransmitter inhibitor in the central nervous system or the effect in the postsynaptic potential.22 It often forms conjugates with both natural compounds, for increasing their solubility, and xenobiotics, for their detoxification.23

The investigation reported herein involved betulinic acid, piperazine and glycine to search for novel structures capable of nanoscale assembly and displaying cytotoxicity. A potential application of the prepared nanostructured materials consists of a combination of their ability to form nanoscale assemblies and their ability to display biological activity. Therefore, the objectives of this investigation consisted of several partial tasks: (a) synthesis and structure elucidation of the piperazine conjugates with betulinic acid, partly decorated by the glycine unit, the incorporation of which brings an additional amide bond to the target molecules; (b) the investigation of the nanoscale assembly of the target compounds 6 and 8 by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) performed in two independent solvents at the time of 1 h and 48 h from the point of complete dissolution of the target compounds 6 and 8; (c) cytotoxicity screening tests involving the target compounds and the intermediate products obtained during the synthetic procedure to confirm potential advantages of the complex target structures on cytotoxicity; and (d) the evaluation of the relationship between the nanoscale assembly of the target compounds 6 and 8 on their observed cytotoxicity.

Results and discussion

Synthetic procedure

The synthesis of the target compounds started with the protection of the C(3)–OH group of 1 by converting it into the C(3)-acetate derivative 2 using acetic anhydride in THF in the presence of a base (DIPEA) and a reaction accelerator (DMAP; path i; Scheme 1). The new amide bond in the C(17)–COOH group was then formed in a two-step reaction. The carboxyl group was converted into its acyl chloride form using oxalyl chloride in DCM. The acyl chloride was then reacted with the N-Boc-protected piperazine derivative in DCM using DIPEA as a base, yielding 3 (path ii). The Boc-protecting group in 3 was removed using a 4 M solution of HCl (gas) in 1,4-dioxane, affording 4 (path iii). The free amino group of 4 was reacted with the N-Boc-protected glycine using 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt) and N-methylmorpholine (NMM) in dichloromethane (DCM), followed by the addition of N-ethyl-N′-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl) under an argon atmosphere, affording product 5 (path iv). The next step of this synthesis consisted of removing the Boc-protecting group present in 5 using the procedure described above (path iii), yielding 6, one of the target products. The other target product 8 was also obtained from the intermediate product 5. It was first treated with lithium hydroxide in methanol to liberate the C(3)–OH group, yielding 7 (path v). The Boc-protecting group in 7 was removed using a 4 M solution of HCl (gas) in 1,4-dioxane (path iii), yielding 8. Most of the synthetic methods described above have already been used in a combination with several other plant triterpenoids during the previous investigation performed by this research team.4,24

Nanoscale assembly

The ability of both the target compounds (6 and 8) to form nanoscale assemblies was monitored by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Compounds 6 and 8 were soluble in both methanol and water, and the TEM studies were performed in both these solvents (Fig. 1). Methanol was selected due to its miscibility with organic solvents and water, and water was selected due to its compatibility with the physiological environment and suitability for potential pharmacological and/or medicinal applications. During the TEM sample preparation, the dissolved compounds were deposited on the electron-transparent carbon film, and the excess solvent was removed instantly using the fast-solvent removal protocol, which resulted in the reproducible fixing of the nanoscale assemblies on the film, as documented in several previous studies.25–27

TEM micrographs showing the formation of nanoscale assemblies of 6 and 8 in methanol and water. (a) 6, methanol, 1 h; (b) 6, methanol, 48 h; (c) 6, water, 1 h; (d) 6, water, 48 h; (e) 8, methanol, 1 h; (f) 8, methanol, 48 h; (g) 8, water, 1 h; and (h) 8, water, 48 h. The time of imaging (1 h and 48 h) was measured from the point of complete dissolution of compounds 6 and 8.

Compound 6 formed free, diffuse, cloud-like nanoscale assemblies combined with isometric nanoparticles (200–400 nm) that were found in methanol at the time of 1 h (Fig. 1a). They were slowly transformed into a mixture of isometric nanoparticles and long fibrous nanostructures with thicknesses below 10 nm at the time of 48 h (Fig. 1b). In water, compound 6 formed a mixture of free cloud-like nanoscale assemblies and long fibrous nanostructures at the time of 1 h (Fig. 1c). The appearance of isometric nanoparticles was rare. At the time of 48 h in water, compound 6 existed as a nice fibrous nanomaterial (Fig. 1d). The behaviour of compound 8 was different. In methanol, it showed a mixture of cloud-like nanoscale assemblies and long fibrous nanostructures at the time of 1 h (Fig. 1e), which was transformed into more pronounced cloud-like nanoscale assemblies connected by less clearly visible fibrous nanostructures at the time of 48 h (Fig. 1f). In water, compound 8 showed an analogous mixture of big isometric nanoparticles (∼200 nm) and fibrous nanomaterials at the time of 1 h (Fig. 1g). The morphology later changed in favour of more isometric nanoparticles but with much smaller sizes (20–40 nm) connected by less intensively pronounced long fibrous nanostructures at 48 h (Fig. 1h). For additional details, refer to the SI (section 1.2. and Fig. S1 and S2).

Cytotoxicity screening tests

The cytotoxicity screening tests involved the target compounds 6 and 8, the intermediate products 2 and 4 and the parent compound betulinic acid (1). The spectrum of the compounds subjected to cytotoxicity assays also included the pharmacologically used anticancer agent cisplatin (cis-diaminedichloroplatinum(ii), CDDP; Table 1). The results of cytotoxicity assays performed in the HeLa (cervical cancer) and MCF7 (breast adenocarcinoma) cancer cell lines showed that the cytotoxicity of compound 8 surpassed that of compound 6 in both cancer cell lines (Table 1). In our previous studies that were focused on different amide derivatives of several triterpenoids, mostly less polar compounds displayed higher cytotoxicity than the more polar ones, as documented recently.4 Compound 6 showed a better cytotoxicity profile than cisplatin (CDDP). Betulinic acid (1) was inactive within the tested range of concentrations of the studied compounds. In turn, both intermediate compounds (2 and 4) showed cytotoxicity. Although 2 displayed mild cytotoxicity in both cancer cell lines, it was not cytotoxic in normal fibroblasts, and 4 showed cytotoxicity values comparable with those displayed by 8 and CDDP (Table 1). The cytotoxicity results showed the advantage of introducing the glycine unit into the target molecule 8.

Relationship between nanoscale assembly and cytotoxicity in vitro

Finally, a relationship between the nanoscale assembly and cytotoxicity of compounds 6 and 8 was identified using 6 in the MCF7 cancer cell line (SI, Table S1). A difference in the observed cytotoxicity in replication 1 of the experiment (IC50 = 15.2 μM) in comparison with the cytotoxicity in replications 2 and 3 of the experiment (IC50 = 33.0 μM and IC50 = 33.1 μM, respectively) was observed at weekly intervals. This supports our hypothesis that the morphology of the nanoscale assemblies changed during a single replication of the experiment, i.e., in the period between the replications 1 and 2 of the experiment. The conditions during the TEM imaging and cytotoxicity assays were different. A longer time period is applied for cytotoxicity assays between the application of the samples to the cells and the monitoring of the biological experiment (72 h) than during the TEM imaging, which was monitored at a time of 1 h and at a time of 48 h since the sample dissolution. When comparing the micrographs of 6 obtained from the water solution recorded at 1 h (Fig. 1c) and 48 h (Fig. 1d), the development of nanofibers is clearly visible, and this may serve as indirect evidence that the hypothesis is correct. Nevertheless, it is necessary to stress that the nanoscale assembly at the cell wall may be different. However, nanoscale assembly may enable, facilitate or reduce the penetration of the nanostructures formed from the potentially biologically active compounds into the cells, resulting in cytotoxicity found experimentally. Small variations in the IC50 values are observed in the HeLa and BJ cells as well; however, the variations in the IC50 values are too small to make any conclusion on their basis. In turn, compound 8 displayed higher cytotoxicity than 6, which made tracing the irregularities in the observed cytotoxicity values of 8 much more difficult. Nevertheless, in the past, when other triterpenoid derivatives were studied, we observed variable cytotoxicity, which indicated a possible effect of the nanoscale assembly on the cytotoxicity of the studied compounds that time.9 Therefore, the observation made with 6 when tested in the MCF7 cancer cells seems not to be stray and out of the ordinary behaviour. Additional and preliminary information on the extra reference compound S1 can be found in the SI, Tables S2 and S3 and Fig. S1 and S2.

Conclusions

The nanoscale assembly and cytotoxicity of a series of piperazine amides of betulinic acid, partly decorated with a glycine motif, were studied. The TEM imaging of the nanoscale assemblies of 6 and 8 in methanol and water resulted in several observations. In methanol, compound 6 formed diffuse, cloud-like nanoscale assemblies and isometric nanoparticles that were later transformed into a mixture of isometric assemblies with long fibrous nanoscale structures. In water, a mixture of the cloud-like nanoscale assemblies with long fibrous nanoscale structures appeared at the time of 1 h, while nice nanoscale fibres appeared at the time of 48 h. Compound 8 formed a mixture of the cloud-like nanoscale assemblies and fibres at the time of 1 h, and this morphology changed only slightly with time. In water, compound 8 formed an analogous image as in methanol at the time of 1 h, which also contained isometric nanoparticles of sizes of ∼200 nm. The morphology changed with time to much smaller isometric nanoparticles (20–40 nm) connected by less intensively visible long fibrous nanoscale structures. In cytotoxicity studies, compound 8 was the most active in all tested cells.

Experimental

General

The NMR measurements were performed on a Bruker AVANCE II 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm TCI cryoprobe in a 5 mm tube in different solvents. The 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded at 600.13 MHz and 150.90 MHz, respectively, in CDCl3 (at 293 K) or in DMSO-d6 (at 373 K) using tetramethylsilane (δ = 0.0 – CDCl3) or the signal of the solvent (δ = 2.50 or 39.50 for 1H/13C – DMSO-d6) as internal references. The 1H NMR data are presented in the following order: chemical shift (δ) expressed in ppm, multiplicity (s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; q, quartet; m, multiplet), number of protons, coupling constants in Hertz. For the unambiguous assignment of both 1H and 13C signals, 2D NMR 1H,13C gHSQC and gHMBC spectra were measured using standard parameter sets and pulse programs delivered by the producer of the spectrometer. Infrared spectra (IR) were measured using a Nicolet iS5 FT-IR spectrometer. Mass spectra (MS) were measured with a Waters ZMD mass spectrometer, mostly in a positive ESI mode (coin voltage, CV = 10 to 20 eV). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed with a Tecnai G2 Spirit Twin 12 microscope (FEI, Brno, Czech Republic). TLC was carried out on silica gel plates (Merck 60F254), and the visualization was performed by both UV detection and spraying using a methanolic solution of phosphomolybdic acid (5%), followed by heating. For column chromatography, silica gel 60 (0.063–0.200 mm) from Merck was used. All chemicals and solvents of analytical grade were purchased from regular commercial sources, and the solvents were purified by general methods before use. Betulinic acid were purchased from Dr. Jan Šarek – Betulinines (https://www.betulinines.com). The scanned 1H and 13C NMR spectra of the prepared compounds, and the calculated 1H and 13C NMR spectra (ChemBioDraw Ultra, version 12.0) are presented in the SI (Fig. S3–S9).
