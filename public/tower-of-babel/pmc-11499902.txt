Elevated intestinal fatty acid-binding protein levels as a marker of portal hypertension and gastroesophageal varices in cirrhosis
Authors: Satoshi Miuma, Hisamitsu Miyaaki, Naota Taura, Yasuko Kanda, Satoshi Matsuo, Kazuaki Tajima, Kosuke Takahashi, Yasuhiko Nakao, Masanori Fukushima, Masafumi Haraguchi, Ryu Sasaki, Eisuke Ozawa, Tatsuki Ichikawa, Kazuhiko Nakao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe measured intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) levels, a useful marker of small intestinal mucosal injury, in patients with cirrhosis to determine their relationship with liver function and complications. This cross-sectional study included 71 patients with cirrhosis admitted for treatment of cirrhotic complications or hepatocellular carcinoma (cohort A) and 104 patients with cirrhosis who received direct-acting antiviral therapy for HCV (cohort B). I-FABP levels, measured by ELISA, were evaluated relative to hepatic reserve and compared with non-invasive scoring systems for diagnostic performance in cirrhotic complications. The median I-FABP level in both cohorts were significantly elevated in patients with reduced hepatic reserve (CTP grade A/BC cohort A, 2.33/3.17 ng/mL, p = 0.032; cohort B, 2.46/3.64 ng/mL, p = 0.008) and complications with gastroesophageal varices (GEV; GEV (-)/(+) cohort A, 1.66/3.67 ng/mL, p < 0.001; cohort B, 2.32/3.36 ng/mL; p = 0.003). Further, multiple logistic regression analysis identified I-FABP as the only factor contributing to GEV presence in both cohorts, which outperformed non-invasive scoring systems for GEV diagnosis (sensitivity 84.6%; specificity 84.2%; sensitivity 69.6%; specificity 63.8%, respectively). In conclusion, elevated small-intestinal mucosal injury in patients with cirrhosis was related to reduced hepatic reserve and GEV presence. Introduction

Cirrhosis is commonly understood as a liver disease associated with hepatocellular damage and fibrotic changes. Portal hypertension often leads to life-threatening complications, such as gastroesophageal varices (GEV), ascites, and encephalopathy, depending on the underlying liver disease and the duration of the illness1,2. This condition also triggers a wide range of gastrointestinal abnormalities, including the small intestine. Given the anatomical connection that allows blood to flow to the liver through the superior mesenteric and portal veins, the small intestine may serve as an indicator of compromised liver health. Therefore, the assessment of small-intestinal mucosal injuries in patients with cirrhosis is crucial. However, mucosal injury in the small intestine, which constitutes the largest interface between the body and external environment, remains largely unexplored because diagnosis is required for either small bowel endoscopy or capsule endoscopy. Both types of endoscopies are available at a limited number of facilities and are difficult to perform routinely.

Intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP), also called FABP-2, has a molecular weight of 14–15 kDa, is located on chromosome 4, and is expressed specifically in duodenal and small-intestinal cells3,4. Like other fatty acid-binding proteins, it plays a role in the absorption, transport, and metabolism of fatty acids in the intestinal tract5. Clinically, blood levels of I-FABP have been reported to be extremely useful markers of small-intestinal mucosal injury, such as ischemic changes, and reflect intestinal permeability6–8. Compared with small-intestinal endoscopy and capsule endoscopy, evaluation of small-intestinal mucosal injury by measuring I-FABP levels is considered clinically useful because it allows timely evaluation and includes potential mucosal injury that cannot be obtained by endoscopy.

This study was aimed to evaluate small-intestinal mucosal injury in patients with cirrhosis by measuring I-FABP levels and elucidating its relationship with liver reserve function and cirrhotic complications using a cross-sectional design. This study clarifies the clinical utility of measuring I-FABP levels and may lead to the development of a new tool to assess the pathogenesis of liver cirrhosis.

Methods

This cross-sectional study using two cohorts was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagasaki University Hospital (approval no. 23022011) and conducted in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of Nagasaki University Hospital waived the requirement for written informed consent for this study, which uses residual specimens and does not involve invasiveness towards patients. Instead, the study protocol was published, and guaranteed opt-out opportunities were advertised on our hospital’s website in accordance with institutional review board instructions.

Study participants

Two cohorts (Cohort A and B) were set up for analysis.

Cohort A included 71 patients with cirrhosis who were hospitalized for cirrhotic complications or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in our department between January 2020 and December 2021. The inclusion criteria for Cohort A were as follows: (i) age 18 years or older; (ii) diagnosis of cirrhosis; and (iii) any complication of cirrhosis and/or HCC. Patients requiring emergency hospitalization due to active hemorrhage, acute exacerbation of hepatic encephalopathy, or severe infection, and those hospitalized for the induction of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy for hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection were excluded from Cohort A.

Cohort A consisted of cirrhosis cases that required hospitalization. Therefore, it was assumed that this group would be significantly skewed towards cases of decompensated cirrhosis and HCC. In this study, we also included Cohort B, set as a group of entirely different cirrhosis cases, to better analyze I-FABP levels in cirrhosis cases with caution. The inclusion criteria for Cohort B were as follows: (i) age 18 years or older; (ii) diagnosis of cirrhosis with HCV; and (iii) receiving DAA therapy between September 2014 and December 2021 in our department as either outpatients or inpatients.

Serum samples were extracted on admission in cohort A or prior to the initiation of DAA therapy in cohort B and stored in a deep freezer until analysis. The clinical background, complications, and blood examination data at admission (Cohort A) and at the start of DAA therapy (Cohort B) were retrospectively collected from the medical charts. Child–Turcotte–Pugh (CTP) grade, model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score, and other non-invasive scoring systems, including the alanine aminotransferase ratio (AAR), aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI), fibrosis index (FI), fibrosis-4 (Fib-4) index, fibrosis quotient (FibroQ), Goteborg University Cirrhosis Index (GUCI), King’s score, Lok index, and albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) scores were determined based on these data (Supplementary Table S1). Five cases each from cohorts A and B were excluded from the determination of the CTP grade and the MELD score because accurate international normalized ratio (INR) values could not be obtained owing to the use of warfarin or direct oral anticoagulant treatment. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) using transient elastography (TE) (FibroScan system) was performed within six months prior to admission or at the start of DAA therapy.

Diagnosis of cirrhosis and definition of decompensated cirrhosis

In both cohorts, the presence of cirrhosis was diagnosed by pathological evaluation (METAVIR F4) in patients who underwent liver biopsy and was diagnosed by LSM > 15.0 kPa, development of common cirrhotic complications (ascites, varices, and encephalopathy), and morphological evaluation (blunted nodular edge and splenomegaly) using imaging tests (abdominal ultrasonography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging) in patients who did not undergo liver biopsy.

The definition of decompensated cirrhosis was based on cirrhosis cases meeting any of the following criteria: (i) progression to CTP grade B or C, (ii) presence or history of ascites, (iii) presence or history of GEV bleeding, including prophylactic endoscopic treatment, and (iv) cirrhosis cases undergoing medication for the remission of these complications.

Assessment of common cirrhosis complications

Cases with any sized GEV observed by the latest upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, with a history of bleeding of the GEV, and with a history of endoscopic prophylactic treatment were classified as the presence of varices. Among them, medium to large GEVs, GEVs with a positive RC sign or those with a history of endoscopic prophylactic treatment were categorized as ‘high-risk GEV,’ while others were classified as ‘small GEV’. Cases with ascites visible on the latest imaging test or with medications for remission of ascites (such as diuretic therapy) were classified as the presence of ascites. Cases with history of overt hepatic encephalopathy or with medications (such as lactulose and rifaximin) were classified as the presence of hepatic encephalopathy.

Measurement of serum I-FABP and serum soluble CD14 (sCD14) levels

I-FABP levels were measured using a Human FABP2/I-FABP Quantikine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. In Cohort A, serum soluble CD14 (sCD14), an indirect serum marker of intestinal permeability, was measured using a human CD14 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate photometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages), and continuous data are presented as medians (1st -3rd interquartile range [IQR]). To compare the differences between the two groups, the chi-square test with Yates’ continuity correction was used for categorical data and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data. To compare differences among more than three groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test (KW test) was used for continuous data. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation between two continuous datasets. To identify factors affecting I-FABP levels, multiple regression analysis was performed using continuous variables. During the analysis, variables that showed significant differences in the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test were transformed into normally distributed variables using logarithmic transformation, and data found to be significant in the Smirnov-Grubbs test were excluded as outliers. Contributing factors were evaluated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to confirm the predictive performance of blood examination data and non-invasive scoring systems for GEV presence. The optimum cut-off values were calculated using the Youden index. Data were analyzed using SPSS v.22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics and I-FABP levels

The characteristics of the 71 patients with cirrhosis in Cohort A and the 104 patients in Cohort B are presented in Table 1. Regarding Cohort A, the median age was 68 years (male/female: 44/27). The underlying liver diseases were hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related in 8 patients, HCV-related in 15 patients, and non-HBV/HCV-related in 48 patients. All HCV-related cases achieved SVR with DAA therapy. The reasons for hospitalization were as follows: 17 patients for pre-transplant comprehensive evaluations, 35 patients for HCC treatment, 13 patients for GEV treatment, 3 patients for refractory ascites treatment, and 3 patients for portal vein thrombosis (PVT) treatment. Among the 17 patients admitted for pre-transplant comprehensive evaluations, liver transplantation was subsequently performed in 12 patients, with a median period from admission to liver transplantation of 28 days. Among the 54 patients hospitalized for reasons other than pre-transplant comprehensive evaluations (such as those hospitalized for HCC treatment, or for the treatment of liver failure symptoms), only one patient died within 90 days after hospitalization. The CTP grades A/B/C were 32 (48.5%)/21 (31.8%)/13 (19.7%), respectively, with 52 cases (73.2%) of GEV, 32 cases (45.1%) of ascites, 25 cases (35.2%) of hepatic encephalopathy, 49 cases (69.0%) of HCC, and 11 cases of PVT. Most of the patients (80.3%) were diagnosed with decompensated cirrhosis. The median I-FABP level in Cohort A was 2.80 ng/mL (IQR, 1.85–5.08 ng/mL).

Regarding Cohort B, the median age was 74 years (male/female: 44/60). All cases were enrolled before the induction of DAA therapy, and all cases tested positive for HCV RNA. There were no deaths within 90 days after DAA therapy induction. The CTP grade A/B/C were 69 (66.3)/25 (25.3)/5 (4.8), with 46 cases (44.2%) of GEV, 14 cases (13.5%) of ascites, 6 cases (5.8%) of hepatic encephalopathy, and 6 cases (5.8%) of HCC, with no cases of PVT. More than half of the patients (55.8%) had compensated cirrhosis. The median I-FABP level in Cohort B was 2.75 ng/mL (IQR, 1.78–3.88 ng/mL).

When comparing Cohort A and Cohort B, Cohort A had a higher proportion of cases with cirrhosis complications and HCC, and more cases were diagnosed with decompensated cirrhosis. Additionally, CTP scores and MELD scores were significantly higher in the former. In Cohort A, the blood test data reflected decreased liver function, with INR, total bilirubin, and ammonia being significantly higher, while albumin was significantly lower (Table 1).

I-FABP levels stratified by the hepatic reserve

I-FABP levels stratified by CTP grade and MELD score are shown in Fig. 1, and their correlation with CTP and MELD score is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Regarding Cohort A, I-FABP levels were significantly higher in patients with CTP grade B/C (median I-FABP levels, 3.17 ng/mL) than those with grade A (median I-FABP levels, 2.33 ng/mL) (p = 0.032) (Fig. 1a). In the stratification by MELD score, I-FABP levels showed a significant increase with increasing MELD score (median I-FABP levels: 1.85 ng/mL for MELD score 6–7, 2.67 ng/mL for MELD 8–10, and 3.16 ng/mL for MELD ≥ 11; p = 0.018, KW test) (Fig. 1b). Similar to Cohort A, in Cohort B, I-FABP levels were significantly higher in patients with CTP grade B/C (median I-FABP levels, 3.64 ng/mL) than in those with CTP grade A (median I-FABP levels, 2.46 ng/mL; p = 0.008) (Fig. 1c). In the stratification by MELD score, the I-FABP level showed a significant increase with increasing MELD score (median I-FABP levels: 2.35 ng/mL for MELD 6–7, 2.64 ng/mL for MELD 8–10, and 3.89 ng/mL for MELD ≥ 11; p = 0.004, KW test) (Fig. 1d). Both CTP and MELD score were positively correlated with I-FABP levels in Cohort A (Supplementary Fig. 1a and d), Cohort B (Supplementary Fig. S1b and e), and the combined Cohort A and B (Supplementary Fig. S1c and f).

Correlation between I-FABP and sCD14 levels in cohort A

To evaluate whether I-FABP levels reflected intestinal permeability, the correlation between I-FABP levels and sCD14, an indirect marker of intestinal permeability, was analyzed. There was no significant correlation between I-FABP and sCD14 levels (r = 0.143, p = 0.235) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The correlations between sCD14 levels and other blood test data were also analyzed. sCD14 levels showed a weak correlation with the CTP score (r = 0.360), albumin (r=-0.301) and Cr (r = 0.344), and moderate correlation with C-reactive protein (r = 0.532) (Supplementary Table S2).

I-FABP levels stratified by the presence or absence of cirrhotic complications

The involvement of cirrhotic complications in I-FABP levels is shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3. I-FABP levels were significantly higher in patients with GEV (median I-FABP levels: GEV (-)/(+), 1.66/3.67 ng/mL; p < 0.001) in Cohort A, and were the particularly the highest in high-risk GEV (small/ high-risk GEV, 2.89/ 3.77 ng/mL) (Fig. 2a and b). Contrastingly, I-FABP levels tended to be higher in patients with ascites (ascites (-)/(+), 2.41/2.94 ng/mL), however there was no significant differences (p = 0.066) (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Additionally, there was no significant difference in I-FABP levels between patients with and without hepatic encephalopathy (encephalopathy (-)/(+), 2.61/2.89 ng/mL; p = 0.686) (Supplementary Fig. S3b). As for Cohort B, I-FABP levels were significantly higher in patients with GEV (median I-FABP levels: varices (-)/(+), 2.32/3.36 ng/mL; p = 0.003), and were particularly the highest in high-risk GEV (small/ high-risk GEV, 3.02/ 3.38 ng/mL) (Fig. 2c and d), consist with the results from Cohort A.

Diagnostic performance of I-FABP levels for the presence or absence of GEV

The diagnostic performance of I-FABP levels for GEV was evaluated. In Cohort A, the I-FABP cutoff value of 2.22 ng/mL, as calculated by ROC analysis, showed the best discrimination performance (AUROC 0.86, sensitivity 84.6%, specificity 84.2%) for GEV than other blood examination data, LSM, and non-invasive scoring systems (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S3). Furthermore, we conducted a logistic regression analysis to examine the predictive ability for the presence of GEV. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, in the univariate analysis, age, sex, the presence of HCC, the levels of INR, total bilirubin, albumin, and I-FABP, ALBI score, FI, Lok index, and MELD score were identified as contributing factors to the presence of GEV. In the multivariate analysis, I-FABP emerged as the only independent factor contributing to the presence of GEV in any multivariate analysis model (model 1, odds ratio [OR] 2.689, p = 0.015; model 2, OR 3.206, p = 0.007; model 3, OR 3.201, p = 0.007; model 4, OR 2.781, p = 0.014) (Table 3). As for ascites, conversely, the I-FABP cutoff value of 5.44 ng/mL did not show good discrimination performance (AUROC 0.63, sensitivity 34.4%, specificity 89.7%) (Supplementary Fig. S4a). In addition, for hepatic encephalopathy, the I-FABP cut-off value of 2.20 ng/mL did not show good discrimination performance (AUROC 0.59, sensitivity 84.0%, specificity 58.7%) (Supplementary Fig. S4b).

Diagnostic performance of I-FABP levels for the presence or absence of GEV, excluding cases with PVT

In Cohort A, 11 patients with PVT were observed, and all of which were accompanied by GEV. Additionally, the I-FABP levels in patients with PVT were significantly higher than in those without PVT (Supplementary Fig. 5). This raises questions regarding whether I-FABP levels truly contribute to GEV, as they may simply be associated with the presence of PVT. Therefore, we also evaluated the diagnostic performance of I-FABP levels for GEV using logistic regression analysis in 60 patients from Cohort A excluding those with PVT. As shown in Supplementary Table S6, in the univariate analysis, age, sex, the presence of HCC, the levels of INR, total bilirubin, albumin and I-FABP, ALBI score, Lok index and MELD score were identified as contributing factors to the presence of GEV. In the multivariate analysis, I-FABP emerged as the only independent factor contributing to the presence of GEV in any multivariate analysis model (model 1, OR 2.733, p = 0.012; model 2, OR 3.021, p = 0.010; model 3, OR 2.766, p = 0.014) (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

Here, small-intestinal mucosal injury was analyzed in patients with cirrhosis using two different cirrhotic cohorts with varying etiologies and proportions of decompensated cirrhosis. Two notable findings emerged. First, small-intestinal mucosal injury increased with decreased hepatic functional reserve. Second, assessing the extent of small-intestinal mucosal injury by measuring I-FABP levels can assist in predicting the presence of GEV. There have been few analyses of I-FABP levels in patients with cirrhosis and no reports revealing its clinical usefulness in the diagnosis of GEV.

In the present study, I-FABP levels were shown to increase with decreased hepatic reserve. Kanda et al. identified a cutoff value of I-FABP (> 3.1 ng/mL) indicative of mucosal injury at the level of small bowel ischemia9. Based on this value, 29 cases (40.8%) in Cohort A, and 42 cases (40.4%) in Cohort B exhibited levels suggesting small-bowel ischemia. The differences in clinical background between the low and high I-FABP groups with a cut-off value of 3.1 ng/mL are shown in Supplementary Tables 11 and 12. Both Cohorts A and B demonstrated that the high I-FABP group exhibited a higher ratio of CTP grade B/C, MELD score, ratio of GEV, ratio of decompensated cirrhosis, and INR levels than the low I-FABP group. Several studies have reported that patients with cirrhosis often exhibit portal hypertensive enteropathy (PHE), which is characterized by small intestine lesions such as erythema, erosions, and angioectasia10–13. Otani et al. identified PHE in 131 of 363 patients (36.1%) with cirrhosis13, and it is reported that the strong involvement of decreased hepatic reserves, such as CTP grades B and C, and the presence of esophageal varices or collateral vessels in PHE12,13. These findings are common in the background of the elevated levels of I-FABP observed in the present study. Our study did not directly diagnose PHE using endoscopy; however, measuring I-FABP levels may be a valuable tool for assessing PHE in patients with cirrhosis, potentially identifying cases missed by capsule endoscopy.

Next, we examined the correlation between I-FABP levels and sCD14 levels. sCD14 is a glycoprotein that functions as a co-receptor together with the Toll-like receptor 4/myeloid differentiation factor 2 complex for bacterial liposaccharides, which is known to indicate a leaky gut14,15. This study found no correlation between I-FABP and sCD14 levels (r = 0.143), suggesting that I-FABP is not a reliable indicator of leaky gut in patients with cirrhosis. Leaky gut is typically caused by multiple disorders that affect barrier homeostasis, including altered tight junctions of the intestinal mucosa, intestinal bacterial imbalances, and weakened mucosal immunity16–18. Therefore, relying solely on I-FABP levels, which reflect intestinal mucosal ischemia, may not be sufficient to diagnose leaky gut.

Notably, we found that the evaluation of small-intestinal mucosal injury using I-FABP levels had the best ability to discriminate the presence of GEV in patients with cirrhosis. Although there have been many reports on the diagnostic performance of LSM and various non-invasive scoring systems for GEV19–22, measuring I-FABP showed the best diagnostic performance and was superior to those in the present study. To the best of our knowledge, only one report has examined the relationship between GEV and I-FABP levels23. The authors found that I-FABP levels were significantly higher in patients with cirrhosis with variceal bleeding (n = 41) than in those without variceal bleeding (n = 43). Our study is the first to report the high diagnostic performance of I-FABP levels in patients with cirrhosis.

I-FABP levels were highly diagnostic of GEV but not of ascites or encephalopathy. In addition, I-FABP levels were found to be highest in high-risk GEV, which is considered to have higher portal pressure compared to small GEV. Furthermore, in Cohort A, 11 out of 71 cases had PVT. Comparing I-FABP levels in cases with or without PVT, those with PVT had significantly higher I-FABP levels than those without PVT (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results suggest a strong link between small-intestinal mucosal injury detected by I-FABP measurement and portal hypertension, which often accompanies GEV. In alternatively, I-FABP measurement may be useful for monitoring the severity of portal hypertension. A strong correlation between small-intestinal mucosal injury and portal venous pressure has been reported in a study using capsule endoscopy in patients with cirrhosis24. Furthermore, Harki et al. reported that gastrointestinal mucosal ischemia was observed in patients with cirrhosis using visible light spectroscopy25. Presumably, I-FABP reflects small-intestinal ischemia induced by portal hypertension. As a background mechanism, ischemia could be due to circulatory insufficiency in the small-intestinal mucosa caused by mesenteric vein congestion resulting from portal hypertension and vascular dysfunction caused by imbalances in circulating vasoactive mediators, including endothelin and nitric oxide synthase, induced by cirrhosis.

Contrastingly, the diagnostic performance for GEV was inferior in Cohort B compared to that in Cohort A. Our unpublished data shows that I-FABP levels decrease after SVR in patients with HCV cirrhosis. In the present study as well, we observed that I-FABP levels were significantly lower in HCV cirrhosis cases of Cohort A (all post-SVR) compared to those of Cohort B (all pre-DAA treatment) in CTP grade A cases where portal hypertension is expected to be less advanced (Supplementary Fig. 6). We surmised that aside from portal hypertension, HCV infection might also influence I-FABP levels in Cohort B, potentially reducing its diagnostic accuracy for GEV. This observation could explain why no significant difference in I-FABP levels was observed between Cohort A and B, despite Cohort A having more cases of decreased hepatic reserve. Further detailed data accumulation is necessary to clarify this point in future studies. Additionally, in cirrhosis cases, I-FABP levels are influenced by factors such as WBC, albumin (negatively), and creatinine (positively). Regarding creatinine in particular, Okada et al. found that I-FABP levels negatively correlate with estimated glomerular filtration rate in chronic and end-stage kidney disease on hemodialysis26. Elevated I-FABP levels in these conditions require careful interpretation.

The findings reported herein should be considered in light of the following limitations. First, the number of participants in this study was not large. Larger sample sizes are needed to obtain clearer results. Second, the assessment of the GEV was based on the results of the latest endoscopy performed within the past six months in this study. Owing to the timing differences, I-FABP levels may differ from those at the time of endoscopy, which should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, prospective rather than retrospective studies are needed.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that marked small-intestinal mucosal injury can be predicted by I-FABP levels and is associated with reduced hepatic reserve and varices in patients with cirrhosis. Specifically, we identified a potential link between I-FABP levels, unknown behavior in patients with cirrhosis, and the progression of portal hypertension. As I-FABP levels exhibit minimal correlation with other blood examination data, they are promising novel biomarkers for evaluating cirrhotic conditions in clinical settings and could provide new strategies for treating patients with cirrhosis.
