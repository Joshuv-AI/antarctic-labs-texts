Healthcare decision-makers’ perspectives on evaluating conflict management training in paediatric healthcare: a utilisation-focused qualitative study
Authors: Juliette Phillipson, Sarah Barclay, Esse Menson, Oscar Lyons
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConflict is prevalent across healthcare settings but is especially common in paediatrics, where high emotional stakes and parental expectations often intensify disagreements. Conflict can lead to negative outcomes for hospitals, staff and patients. Effective conflict management training can mitigate these impacts, but evaluating such training programmes remains challenging due to a lack of standardised tools and best practices.MethodsThis qualitative study aimed to explore healthcare decision-makers' perspectives on what key areas should be evaluated in conflict management training programmes in paediatric healthcare settings, employing Patton’s utilisation-focused evaluation approach. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 13 healthcare decision-makers and key stakeholders from various healthcare and charitable organisations. Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis by Braun and Clarke to identify key themes for evaluation.ResultsFour primary themes were generated regarding the key areas that healthcare decision-makers believe should be evaluated in conflict management training. These were experience of training sessions, staff competency and well-being, patient/family experiences of conflict and impact on staff time and on clinical resources. Interviewees identified that the evaluation of training sessions should focus on participant engagement and satisfaction. Codes relating to staff competency and well-being included the acquisition and long-term retention of conflict management skills. Codes relating to patient/family experience focused on the quality of communication and support during conflicts. The theme of resource utilisation included codes relating to the time and cost implications of conflict.ConclusionThe study identified essential evaluation areas that align with and expand on Kirkpatrick’s framework, suggesting the need for both qualitative and quantitative data and long-term follow-up. Tailoring evaluation frameworks to specific programme contexts can enhance their relevance and utility, contributing to improved conflict management in both paediatric and wider healthcare settings.

Background

Conflict is a multifaceted phenomenon characterised by negative emotional reactions stemming from perceived disagreements between two or more parties.1 Conflict is widely viewed as inevitable within healthcare settings given the stressful context, real or perceived resource scarcity, communication issues and workload pressures.15 Paediatric healthcare settings are prone to a high prevalence of conflict, primarily driven by emotional intensity, communication breakdowns, disagreements over treatment plans and the often unrealistic expectations of families navigating complex medical decisions.6 If not managed appropriately and promptly, conflict can lead to detrimental outcomes for hospitals (including legal expenses, staff costs, higher staff turnover and reduced productivity), for staff (such as burnout, poor team cohesion and reduced well-being) and most critically for patients and families (resulting in medical errors, poor therapeutic relationships and worse prognoses).1712

Training in conflict management has proven effective in mitigating the negative impacts of conflict on hospitals, staff and patients/families.137 10 12 13 To enhance the application of training in practice, there have been calls for the development and assessment of structured conflict management processes.11417 The Conflict Management Framework (CMF) developed by the Medical Mediation Foundation (MMF)18 for use in paediatric settings is one of the few formalised approaches to managing healthcare conflict. The CMF has shown promise in reducing the frequency of conflicts and in improving staff self-reported scores of burnout.17 19 The time healthcare staff spend managing conflicts represents a significant resource investment. Reducing time spent on conflicts has the potential to save considerable time and, consequently, reduce costs.7

There is no established single best practice for evaluating the impact of a conflict management training intervention. Previous studies of conflict management training in healthcare settings have adopted several different approaches, including the use of surveys, interviews, observations and focus groups, and collecting data relating to a range hypothesised outcomes from conflict.1 For instance, surveys and interviews have been used to gauge participants' perceptions and self-reported changes in behaviour, while observational studies and focus groups have provided deeper insights into the dynamics and real-world application of conflict management strategies. However, there is yet to be a consensus on the relative utility of each approach to studying conflict management training, and there is a paucity of validated or standardised tools for comparable and repeatable evaluation.

This study aimed to answer the following research question: ‘What are the evaluation needs of healthcare decision-makers regarding conflict management training in paediatric healthcare settings?’ We conducted a qualitative study, rooted in the principles of utilisation-focused evaluation,20 to answer this question. Our aim was to develop an evaluation of a complex multi-site, multi-year conflict management training programme and to increase the utility of data collected through that evaluation. By focusing on the specific needs and contexts of healthcare decision-makers, we aimed to maximise both practicality and relevance of the evaluation, ultimately contributing to a better understanding of the impact of conflict management training within healthcare settings.21

Methods

The purpose of evaluation as outlined by Patton is to provide information to support decision-making. Using Patton’s utilisation-focussed evaluation approach, ‘a focus on intended use by intended users undergirds and informs every design decision in the evaluation’.21 With this in mind, we first identified, organised and engaged primary intended users of the evaluation of conflict management training; then identified primary intended uses of evaluation data by establishing the evaluation’s priority purposes.2022

Interviews were arranged with key stakeholders identified at each of the UK sites chosen for this study (the Great North Children’s Hospital in Newcastle, Leeds Children’s Hospital, Oxford Children’s Hospital and Oxford’s Helen and Douglas House Hospice), along with key stakeholders from the True Colours Charitable Trust, which had funded the training programme, the Together for Short Lives Charity and with parents who volunteered to contribute their insights. Interviews were conducted by a medical doctor who was independent of any of the training, healthcare or charitable organisation involved in the programmes or interviews and who has expertise in programme evaluation research. They were conducted over video-call or telephone, lasted for 30–60 minutes and were recorded and professionally transcribed for analysis. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of each interview. Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Ethical approval

This study was classified as a service evaluation as it sought to gather information on how an existing conflict management programme should be evaluated to meet the needs of healthcare decision-makers. In accordance with NIHR guidelines, service evaluation focuses on assessing the effectiveness and outcomes of a current service, which was the intent of this study.

Data analysis

Interviews were analysed by two authors (JP and OL) in duplicate through reflexive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke.23 This involved (1) independently reading all the material to become familiar with the data; (2) systematically reviewing the transcription line by line to identify meaningful codes and (3) grouping and summarising groups of codes to themes. The authors then met to compare and discuss their initial themes. Themes were reviewed, refined and re-organised to ensure they accurately reflected the data and captured all relevant aspects. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. The final themes were then interpreted and analysed to formulate the results.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, recruitment or conduct of this study. Although the study engaged parents as stakeholders to provide their perspectives during interviews, their involvement was limited to participation and did not extend to study design or recruitment. The results will not be directly disseminated to the study participants, but findings may be shared through academic publications and presentations.

Results

A total of 12 interviews were completed with 13 healthcare decision-makers. One interview was conducted with two True Colours Charitable Trust stakeholders simultaneously, at the request of the participants. The other interviewees consisted of four nurses, four doctors, two parents and the director of Programmes for the Together for Short Lives Charity. The nurses and doctors were all in decision-making positions including co-chair of the clinical ethics advisory group, medical director, lead nurse for education, associate director of nursing for children and young people, quality and safety lead, deputy head of nursing, and mental health and well-being lead for critical care.

Four themes were generated from the interviews regarding the evaluation of conflict management training. These were experience of training sessions, staff competency and well-being, patient/family experiences of conflict, and impact on staff time and on clinical resources. Experience of training sessions

Interviewees highlighted that the evaluation of training programmes should focus on staff members’ experience of the training.

I think, whether the approach… [whether] it was easy, whether they found the information easy to absorb and use and put into practice, that kind of thing. Because you're really trying to sell that as a model, aren't you? - Charitable organisation Programme Director

We'll look at the numbers of staff we've got, the number of hours we think it'll need, the number of sessions that we need to put on, how often do we need to get new key trainers, when have we reached a saturation point? - Nurse (Deputy head of nursing)

Interviewees suggested relevant data might include the number of sessions, number of attendees from various demographics (particularly different disciplinary roles), engagement, satisfaction, perceived relevance to work, recommendations for improvement and financial cost of sessions.

The importance of longevity of the training and retention of skills gained was frequently mentioned, with emphasis on culture change and sustainability.

I think one of the things that I'd like to achieve with it is that it’s not standalone training. That it’s a real… for me that it’s part of our everyday… it’s part of our everyday training that it’s not just a standalone training that it’s really recognised in the Children’s Hospital. - Nurse (Lead nurse for education of the nursing team)

Staff competency and well-being

This theme included both skill development and the psychological impact of conflict and conflict management training on staff, as well as their collaborative experiences. Interviewees drew attention to the importance of assessing whether the programme successfully equipped healthcare staff with the necessary skills to both identify and manage conflict. They also highlighted utility in measuring (1) staff confidence in using conflict management skills, (2) the frequency of use of conflict management skills and (3) long-term retention of those skills.

I hope that the people will come out with a range of skills and thoughts on how to communicate with difficult situations and have strategies to deal with difficult situations. And I guess we're all individuals and we all will deal with a situation differently, but I guess it’s giving a consistent set of principles to the staff – Nurse (Associate director of nursing for children and young people)

I think one of the issues that would be good and would be important, would be to show that people don't just walk out of the training and say, Yes, yes. I know lots of things now. It’s wonderful. but that six months later, that they're using those skills, they can recognise cases of conflict, that they have something to do when they encounter them. – Doctor (Consultant in newborn intensive care and co-chair of the clinical ethics advisory group)

Interviewees frequently discussed the detrimental effects of conflict on staff. By providing better skills to manage conflict, they reasoned the training programme could lead to a reduction in stress and burnout and an improvement in general well-being. Interviewees suggested that such impacts on staff would be important outcomes from the training and therefore worth evaluating.

Patient/family experiences of conflict

Interviewees emphasised that the evaluation of training programmes should include the perspectives of patients and their families. Suggested outcomes with respect to patient and family experiences included the parental experience of healthcare conflict, whether families felt supported, and the impact of conflict-associated stress on well-being.

Do they [parents] feel free, psychologically safe, to ask all those questions? Is there that safety that they can go into a dialogue and not be concerned whether that’s going to be detrimental to the care of the child, whether they're going to be seen as difficult, and do that in a way that’s helpful to their understanding? They might be frustrated, stressed, all those things. – Doctor (Paediatric intensive care consultant)

Interviewees highlighted that effective communication is essential for facilitating shared decision-making. They felt that evaluation outcomes relating to family experiences of their relationship with healthcare staff and the communication received would be valuable.

That the family’s voices are heard. That would be the bottom line, that they are actually involved in the decision-making process. – Parent

But in a lot of those cases, we're aware that it’s a communication problem. It’s often communicating bad news with families, and if that’s not done well, families don't quite understand what’s going on and all this disagreement and conflict occurs. – Charitable organisation Programme Director

Interviewees identified that poorly managed conflict is likely to have a negative impact on patient care, particularly as paediatric patients are a vulnerable population. Including direct measurement of patient outcomes in the evaluation of training programmes was suggested.

But in a way, the child is paramount. And we never quite measure it. We know that, through other stats, if there is a conflict there’s increased errors, increased safety [concerns], increased dissatisfaction in the care given. – Doctor (Paediatric nephrologist and quality and safety lead)

Impact on staff time and on clinical resources

Interviewees commonly discussed the potential for conflict to consume large amounts of healthcare workers’ time. Interviewees especially highlighted the value of quantifying this as a metric for securing funding for conflict management training long-term, as a reduction in time spent on conflict could result in a reduction in overall healthcare costs.

I think one of the very significant issues about conflicts of this kind is the time that they take because clinicians' time within a stretched NHS is an exceptionally precious resource. And these cases are incredibly time consuming, disproportionately so, and far out of proportion with the political complexity. And they're all complex cases by their nature, but they take, often, five times more time than any other patient on the ward. – Doctor (Consultant in newborn intensive care and co-chair of the clinical ethics advisory group)

I'd be disappointed if we didn't see something on savings and clinical time hours and if we can quantify that in terms of the cost. - True Colours Charitable Trust stakeholder

Interviewees suggested a range of outcome measures related to utilisation of resources. A direct measure of the incidence of conflict would provide valuable insight into the efficacy of the training framework. This could be a comparison of incidence before and after training or between groups who have had training versus those who have not.

A gold standard way of evaluating something like this would be to measure the numbers of the different types of conflict as a function of the number of patients over a period of time, and to show that the high-level conflict was reduced, for example. – Doctor (Consultant in newborn intensive care and co-chair of the clinical ethics advisory group)

Other quantifiable conflict outcomes suggested by participants included the number/seriousness of complaints, litigation and high-profile cases.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a thematic analysis of interviews with healthcare decision-makers to guide the evaluation of a paediatric healthcare conflict management programme and to determine the types of data that would allow stakeholders to assess the programme’s success. Four key themes were generated as specific areas for evaluation: experience of training sessions, staff competency and well-being, patient/family experiences of conflict, and impact on staff time and on clinical resources. Our findings provide insights into the outcomes that healthcare decision-makers consider important when evaluating conflict management training. These areas align with existing frameworks but also highlight additional factors that are unique to the context of paediatric healthcare.

Kirkpatrick’s framework, developed in 1960, remains the most popular approach to evaluating training programmes in organisations.2427 Kirkpatrick categorised training programme outcomes into a four-tier hierarchy, designed as a guide for evaluation rather than a fixed protocol. Many evaluation themes identified in this study align with each of the tiers of Kirkpatrick’s framework. For example, suggested measures of staff reaction to the training programme included engagement, satisfaction, perceived relevance to work and recommendations for improvement, all of which align with Kirkpatrick level 1: ‘Reaction’. Learning outcomes, such as skills gained in identifying and managing conflict and staff confidence, align with Kirkpatrick level 2: ‘Learning’. The frequency of use of newly learnt skills reflects Kirkpatrick level 3: ‘Behaviour’. Kirkpatrick level 4: ‘Results’ encompasses many suggestions such as the incidence of conflict, patient outcomes, reduction in time spent on conflict, reduction in conflict-related costs, complaints, litigation and high-profile cases.

Some researchers have pointed out flaws in Kirkpatrick’s framework, discussing the possibility of neglecting important intangible and soft outcomes.28 29 Our findings align with these identified shortfalls as certain themes for evaluation cannot be stratified into the framework including reduction in staff stress or burnout and the improvement in general well-being. Similarly, family experiences such as perceived parental support, experiences of communication with the medical team, parental stress and well-being require a broader evaluation framework.

Several adaptations of Kirkpatrick’s framework have been developed for evaluation purposes.2932 Despite critiques over the years, Kirkpatrick’s framework offers a helpful starting point for establishing the scope of important evaluation outcomes, especially when combined with modern mixed-methods approaches. The current study has highlighted the utility of evaluation planning interviews in identifying important areas for evaluation that may not be captured within a traditional framework.

Limitations of this study include the sample population of 13 individuals from selected children’s hospitals or services, which are context-specific and may limit the generalisability of our findings to all healthcare settings or other types of conflict management programmes. Strengths of the study include the range of stakeholders interviewed (nurses and doctors in decision-making roles, parents, charity representatives), capturing a wide array of perspectives on conflict management training. Using Patton’s utilisation-focused evaluation approach ensures that the evaluation framework is tailored to the specific needs and contexts of healthcare decision-makers, enhancing its practical relevance and utility.

This study provides insights into evaluation measures to include in the design of conflict management training programmes for paediatric healthcare professionals. Our results suggest that an effective utilisation-focused evaluation of a conflict management programme will include qualitative and quantitative data, information from healthcare professionals and patients/families, and long-term follow-up. We recommend that future programmes incorporate a similar methodology in planning programme evaluations to ensure that the design is tailored to the specific aims of the programme and the relevant context.
