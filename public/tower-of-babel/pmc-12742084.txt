Palliative care consultation for end-of-life decision-making in hospitalised patients: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ghazal Haddad, Henry Ajzenberg, F Daniel Davis, Patricia A Fogelman, Karen Korzick, Mary Faith Marshall, Douglas Naylor, Sandra M Swoboda, Julie Reid, Simon Oczkowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract:IntroductionHospitalised patients nearing the end of life (EOL) often face complex treatment decisions, leading to potential conflicts among care teams, patients and families. Palliative care consultations may enhance decision-making processes, improve satisfaction and reduce unnecessary interventions. This systematic review will assess the impact of palliative care consultations on treatment decisions, family and patient satisfaction, and psychological outcomes in hospitalised adults.Methods and analysisWe will include randomised controlled trials comparing palliative care consultations to standard care in hospitalised adults. The primary outcomes will include decisions to withhold or withdraw treatments, patient and family satisfaction with EOL decision-making, and psychological outcomes such as anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder. Secondary outcomes will include intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, utilisation of potentially non-beneficial treatments, and the use of institutional policies or legal actions. Databases including MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane CENTRAL and PsycINFO will be systematically searched from inception to September 2025. Two independent reviewers will screen studies and extract data using Covidence. Meta-analyses will use random-effects models to generate pooled estimates for primary and secondary outcomes. Risk of bias will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, and evidence certainty will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach. Subgroup analyses will explore variations by ICU versus non-ICU settings, cancer versus non-cancer diagnoses and default versus clinician-initiated consultations.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this review. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420250624190.

Introduction

Hospital-based deaths account for approximately 35% of all deaths in the USA, often requiring complex end-of-life (EOL) decision-making.1 Patients at the EOL often experience a constellation of physical symptoms—such as pain, dyspnoea and nausea—alongside cognitive dysfunction, psychological distress and unmet spiritual needs.2 3 These challenges demand a multifaceted approach to ensure that care is patient-centred, goal-concordant and directed at relieving suffering.

Palliative care consultations have emerged as a transformative intervention in the landscape of EOL care. Evidence consistently demonstrates their role in enhancing communication between care teams and families, improving satisfaction with care and optimising symptom management.4 5 Beyond addressing intrusive symptoms and suffering encountered by patients at the EOL, these consultations are instrumental in guiding decisions about the appropriateness of life-sustaining treatments, such as mechanical ventilation and intensive pharmacologic interventions.6 7 In the intensive care unit (ICU), palliative care has been associated with reductions in hospital and ICU length of stay, as well as lower healthcare costs, highlighting its potential for system-level benefits.8

Despite these documented advantages, the impact of palliative care consultations outside the ICU remains less well understood. Hospitalised patients on general medical or surgical wards may face distinct challenges, such as delayed recognition of EOL trajectories or fewer opportunities for multidisciplinary consultation.9 This gap in evidence creates uncertainty about how best to extend the benefits of palliative care to broader hospital populations.

Importantly, the outcomes of palliative care extend beyond clinical measures. Psychological sequelae, such as anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), are increasingly recognised among both patients and their families during and after EOL experiences.10 Addressing these outcomes is critical for a holistic approach to care, as unresolved psychological distress can undermine the perceived quality of EOL care.

This systematic review aims to synthesise the available evidence on the impact of palliative care consultations on EOL decision-making for hospitalised adults, both within and outside ICUs. PICO question: In hospitalised adult patients nearing the EOL (P), how do palliative care consultations (I), compared with standard care without palliative involvement (C), affect decisions to withhold or withdraw treatment, patient and family satisfaction, psychological outcomes (anxiety, depression, PTSD), ICU and hospital length of stay, utilisation of potentially non-beneficial treatments, and institutional or legal actions (O)?

Methods and analysis

Eligibility criteria

In this study, we will include data from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) published in English that compare palliative care consultations addressing decision-making, with or without symptom management, with standard care or alternative forms of clinical support without palliative care involvement. We chose to include only RCTs to ensure the highest level of evidence and minimise bias in evaluating the impact of palliative care consultations. While other designs may offer useful context, our focus is on synthesising causal evidence. We limited inclusion to studies published in English due to resource constraints that preclude translation and screening of non-English studies. While this may introduce language bias, the decision was made to ensure accurate data extraction and interpretation. Additionally, we define the intervention of interest—palliative care consultation—as a formal consultation delivered by a specialist palliative care team that includes at least one clinician (physician or nurse) with advanced training or certification in palliative medicine. While the structure and composition of palliative care teams may vary across institutions, eligible interventions will be restricted to those provided by dedicated teams with explicit palliative care expertise, rather than generalist providers. Comparator groups may include standard care without palliative involvement or alternative forms of non-palliative clinical support. The patient population will include hospitalised adults (≥16 years). We will not include conference abstracts unless they provide sufficient methodological details and outcome data to allow for reliable assessment of eligibility, risk of bias and data extraction. If a conference abstract appears eligible but lacks essential information, we will attempt to contact the corresponding author to determine whether a full-text publication is available or whether additional unpublished data can be shared. Studies for which adequate information cannot be obtained will be excluded and reported in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram and the list of excluded studies, along with reasons for exclusion.

Search strategy

We will perform a systematic search of the databases MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane CENTRAL and PsycINFO from inception to September 2025, using tailored search strategies developed with a medical librarian. All geographical locations will be included. Our Embase search strategy is provided below as an example, and the remaining search strategies are included in online supplemental document 1. Search results from all databases will be provided by an experienced medical librarian. These results will be uploaded into Covidence, which will automatically identify and remove duplicate citations using its built-in deduplication algorithm. Bibliographies of included studies will be reviewed to identify additional studies.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this review will include clinical endpoints that assess the concordance between patients’ preferences and treatments they received, decisions to withhold or withdraw treatments, and patient and family satisfaction with EOL decision-making. Psychological outcomes, such as symptoms of anxiety, depression and PTSD, will also be considered primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes will focus on metrics such as ICU and hospital length of stay, the utilisation of potentially non-beneficial treatments and the use of institutional policies or legal actions. These outcomes will be analysed to provide comprehensive insights into the efficacy of palliative care consultations.

Data extraction

We will use predefined standardised sheets to capture study characteristics and outcomes. From each included study, we will extract detailed information on author and year of publication, study design (eg, RCT, cluster RCT) and study setting, including hospital type and geographic location. Sample size, including total participants and group distributions, will be recorded. We will describe the intervention in detail, specifying the elements of palliative care consultations provided, and provide details on the control group, such as the type of usual care or alternative intervention. Demographic data, including mean age, age range, sex distribution, race/ethnicity and religious/spiritual affiliation, will be collected. We will also extract information about the reason for hospital admission, admission location (ICU vs ward), prevalent comorbidities, goals of care stated at admission and life-limiting conditions identified. Outcomes data will include primary outcomes such as the concordance between patients’ preferences and treatments received, decisions to withhold or withdraw treatments, satisfaction with EOL decision-making and psychological outcomes (eg, anxiety, depression, PTSD). Secondary outcomes will include length of stay, use of potentially non-beneficial treatments, the use of institutional policies or legal actions, and additional relevant measures.

In cases where multiple reports appear to represent the same study population, we will examine all reports to identify the most comprehensive dataset. The report with the most complete data will be included in the analysis, and additional reports will be used for supplemental information where relevant. We will clarify and resolve duplication by cross-referencing trial identifiers, study characteristics and author groups.

Data will be extracted independently by two reviewers, with conflicts resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis will summarise study characteristics and outcomes. Meta-analyses will use random effects models to calculate pooled ORs for dichotomous outcomes and standardised mean differences for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic.

Subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore potential effect modification by care setting (ICU vs non-ICU), patient population (oncology vs non-oncology) and timing of palliative care consultation (early vs late), based on biological plausibility, clinical relevance and heterogeneity observed in prior studies. These criteria were selected a priori to better contextualise the intervention’s effect across care environments and patient trajectories.

The GRADE approach will assess evidence certainty for each outcome, considering study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.

Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool will evaluate the risk of bias in RCTs. Two reviewers will independently assess each study, with a third reviewer resolving disagreements.

Meta-bias assessment

We will assess the potential for publication bias by visually inspecting funnel plots if at least 10 studies are available per outcome. We will perform Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry. To evaluate selective outcome reporting within studies, we will compare published outcomes with trial registries or protocols when available. We will also record any discrepancies between the methods and results sections of the included studies.

Reporting guideline compliance

This protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines. A completed PRISMA-P checklist is included as an online supplemental document 2.

Ethics and dissemination

As a systematic review of published data, this study does not require ethical approval. Any concerns regarding research integrity may be directed to the McMaster University Manager, Academic and Research Integrity (masokim@mcmaster.ca). Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations to advance understanding of palliative care’s role in EOL decision-making.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design of this systematic review and will not be involved in its conduct, reporting or interpretation. However, we plan to disseminate the findings through presentations to relevant patient advocacy groups to ensure accessibility of results.
