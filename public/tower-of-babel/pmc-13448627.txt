Streptococcus anginosus-associated kynurenic acid drives PD-1 blockade resistance through an ITGA2-mTOR-CTSV axis in gastric cancer
Authors: Yingxin Ren, Xiang Yu, Jiaqiang Jiang, Jinlong Luo, Cuiyin Zhao, Xinyuan Mao, Yijie Xi, Junting He, Chao Yu, Yanfeng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe tumor microbiota critically shapes responses to immunotherapy; however, the mechanisms by which specific microbial species drive immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) resistance in gastric cancer (GC) remain poorly defined.MethodsStreptococcus anginosus enrichment was assessed in ICB-unresponsive GC tissues from patients and multiple preclinical models. Orthotopic, subcutaneous, and germ-free mono-colonized mouse models were employed to evaluate the impact of S. anginosus on antitumor immunity and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade efficacy. Integrated multi-omics analyses were performed to identify microbiota-derived metabolites, and mechanistic studies investigated their effects on CD8+ T-cell function. Therapeutic targeting of integrin α2 (ITGA2) was tested to assess restoration of cytotoxic T-cell activity and sensitization to PD-1 blockade.ResultsS. anginosus was markedly enriched in ICB-non-responsive GC tissues. Colonization with S. anginosus impaired antitumor immunity and promoted PD-1 blockade resistance by suppressing CD8+ T-cell effector function. Multi-omics analyses identified kynurenic acid (KA) as a microbiota-derived metabolite selectively enriched following S. anginosus colonization. KA induced ITGA2 expression and inhibited an mTOR-dependent signaling cascade, sustaining cathepsin V expression and attenuating interferon-γ and granzyme B production in CD8+ T cells. Therapeutic inhibition of ITGA2 restored CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and sensitized tumors to PD-1 blockade.ConclusionsA microbiota-metabolite-immune signaling axis involving S. anginosus, KA, and ITGA2 drives immunotherapy resistance in GC. Therapeutic inhibition of ITGA2 represents a potential strategy to overcome ICB resistance in GC.

Introduction

Immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of multiple cancers. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, which inhibit immunosuppressive receptors such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), activate T cells and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment.1 2 However, an immunosuppressive milieu remains a major barrier to effective immunotherapy in gastric cancer (GC). While the CheckMate 649 trial reported an objective response rate of 59% for combined ICB and chemotherapy,3 anti-PD-1 monotherapy in the KEYNOTE-062 trial achieved only a 15.5% response rate,4 highlighting the urgent need to identify novel regulators of the gastric tumor immune microenvironment and actionable therapeutic targets.

Emerging evidence indicates that the gut microbiome not only contributes directly to tumor progression but also exerts systemic control over host immunity.5–7 Specific microbial signatures have been linked to ICB efficacy across multiple malignancies.8–11 Certain microbes promote cancer development via direct interactions with tumor cells, including Helicobacter pylori in GC,12 13 Porphyromonas gingivalis in pancreatic cancer,14 15 and Fusobacterium nucleatum in colorectal cancer.16 17 Importantly, many gut microbes influence tumor progression primarily by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Preclinical studies and clinical trials have identified bacterial taxa associated with ICB responses, such as Bifidobacterium, Collinsella, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Parabacteroides, which enhance the activity of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment.18–22 Given the unique acidic environment of the stomach, the role of gastric-resident microbiota in shaping antitumor immunity remains largely unexplored.

Streptococcus anginosus is a member of the S. anginosus group and is recognized as an opportunistic pathogen associated with a broad range of human diseases, including oral and periodontal infections, deep abscess formation, hepatobiliary infections, and bacteremia. Increasing evidence has linked S. anginosus to gastrointestinal malignancies, particularly esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and GC, where its enrichment correlates with chronic mucosal inflammation, epithelial injury, and tumor progression. In addition to its potential tumor-promoting effects, S. anginosus has been reported to induce pro-inflammatory cytokine production and remodel local immune responses, suggesting that it may contribute to cancer-associated inflammation and immune dysregulation.23–26 However, whether S. anginosus directly shapes the gastric tumor immune microenvironment and influences the response to ICB remains unknown.

Bacteria can regulate antitumor immunity through multiple mechanisms. They can present microbial antigens or mimic tumor-associated epitopes to activate antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, promoting recognition and elimination of cancer cells.27 28 Additionally, microbial metabolites, including bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, branched-chain amino acids, and trimethylamine, can modulate natural killer and regulatory T-cell function, thereby influencing the tumor immune landscape.29–33 Understanding the key microbial metabolites and their targets in GC is critical for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy.

Here, by integrating microbiome profiling in mouse models with validation in human gastric tumors, we demonstrate that S. anginosus functionally contributes to immunotherapy resistance rather than merely representing a tumor-enriched commensal. Mechanistically, S. anginosus promotes the accumulation of kynurenic acid (KA) within the tumor microenvironment, which suppresses CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and limits the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. We further show that KA directly interacts with and induces integrin α2 (ITGA2), a collagen receptor integrin implicated in immune cell adhesion, migration, and intracellular signaling, in CD8+ T cells, leading to inhibition of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway and downstream regulation of lysosomal cathepsin V (CTSV), ultimately driving T-cell dysfunction. Importantly, genetic or pharmacologic targeting of ITGA2 restores CD8+ T-cell and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-CD8+T cell cytotoxicity and synergizes with PD-1 blockade to enhance antitumor efficacy in vivo. Together, our findings establish a causal link between intratumoral bacteria, metabolite-mediated immune suppression, and therapeutic resistance, and identify a tractable microbiome-metabolite-integrin axis as a promising target for immunomodulatory intervention in GC.

Materials and methods

Materials and resources

All of the items used in this study, including reagents and antibodies, are detailed in online supplemental table 1.

Cell lines

The MFC murine GC cells and two human GC cell lines (AGS and SNU-216) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Mice

C57BL/6J and NOD-SCID mice (20~25 g) were procured from Zhuhai Baishitong Biotechnology (Zhuhai, China), while 615 mice were supplied by Shulaibao Biotechnology (Chengdu, China). Atp4b-iCre (T054674) and Tff1-flox (T016018) mice, both on a pure C57BL/6 background, were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). Myc-LSL (#035557) mice were imported from The Jackson Laboratory. To establish the GC mouse model, Atp4b-iCre, Tff1-flox, and Myc-LSL mice were intercrossed to generate Atp4b-iCre±, Tff1flox/flox, and Myc LSL/− (ATM) mice. All mice were maintained in specific-pathogen-free barrier rooms under a strict 12 hours light-dark cycle at 22±2°C and 50–60% relative humidity. Mice were group-housed on autoclaved corn-cob bedding and received sterile standard chow and acidified water ad libitum. Cages were changed weekly inside a Class II biosafety cabinet to minimize microbial drift. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (approval no. IACUC-LAC-202500307-003).

Human samples

To independently validate bacterial taxa inferred from 16S rRNA sequencing (Sangon), we collected clinical GC surgical specimens at Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). A total of 46 surgically resected samples were obtained, including 32 from treatment-naïve patients and 14 from patients who had received neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Surgically resected tumor specimens were processed for histological analysis and embedded according to standard pathological procedures. 4 µm sections were cut for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) using rRNA-targeted oligonucleotide probes. The study protocol was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Detailed clinicopathological parameters, including age, sex, Lauren classification, tumor, node, metastases stage, PD-L1 status, and treatment history, are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Bacteria and culturing

S. anginosus was streaked onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl, 1.5% agar) supplemented with 0.1% glucose and 0.02% cysteine. After 16 hours at 37°C under 5% CO2, a single colony was inoculated into 5 mL liquid LB and allowed to acclimate for two sequential 12 hours passages without shaking. The second passage was then diluted 1:100 into 50 mL fresh LB and grown to mid-exponential phase. Bacterial cells were harvested, washed once in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended to 1×109 colony-forming units (CFU) mL−1 in PBS for downstream infection assays.

GC patient-derived organoids

Patient-derived GC organoids were established from freshly resected tumor tissues. Macroscopically viable tumor fragments (2–4 mm3) were washed with ice-cold PBS, minced using sterile scalpels, and enzymatically dissociated for 60 min at 37°C in a solution containing 1 mg/mL Collagenase IV (Gibco) and 0.5 mg/mL Dispase. The dissociated cells were filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer, centrifuged at 400×g for 4 min, and embedded in Matrigel (Corning) with GC organoid culture medium (Novoprotein). The organoids were maintained under standard three-dimensional organoid culture conditions, with medium replacement every 3 days and passaging at a 1:3 ratio every 7 days using 0.5 mM EDTA in PBS.

CD8 T-cell isolation and activation

Peripheral blood was layered onto Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Solarbio) and centrifuged at 400×g for 30 min. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected, washed two times with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended at 5×107 cells/mL for downstream assays. CD8 T cells were positively selected with EasySep Human CD8+ Kit II (STEMCELL). Isolated cells were cultured in X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Lonza) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and anti-CD3/28 (Gibco, Amecica). After 72 hours at 37 °C/5% CO2, expanded CD8+ T cells were harvested, counted, and used immediately for co-culture assays.

ITGA2 knockdown and CTSV overexpression in CD8+ T cells

CD8+ T cells were transfected or transduced using lentiviral systems with ITGA2-shRNA or CTSV-overexpression plasmids, respectively. After culture in X-VIVO 15 medium and puromycin selection (2 µg/mL, 72 hours). Knockdown or overexpression efficiency was verified by western blot or flow cytometry. Plasmid sequences for ITGA2 knockdown and CTSV overexpression are listed in online supplemental table 3.

CAR-claudin 18.2+ CD8+ T-cell generation

To generate laudin 18.2+ CAR-CD8+ effectors, negatively selected CD8+ T cells (>95% purity) were primed for 48 hours with TransAct (Miltenyi) at 1:100 dilution in TexMACS medium. Activated cells were then seeded at 3×105 cells per well of a RetroNectin-coated 24-well plate. Lentiviral particles encoding a second-generation CAR were pre-concentrated by ultracentrifugation (100,000×g, 2 hours, 4°C) and applied at an multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 in the presence of 10 µg/mL Polybrene. Plates were spinoculated (250×g, 32°C, 50 min) and returned to 37°C/5% CO2. 16 hours later, vectors were removed and cultures were expanded for an additional 72 hours in cytokine-rich medium before functional assays. Transduction efficiency routinely exceeded 45% as determined by flow cytometry.

Data statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean±SD and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism V.9. Groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test or one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc analysis. Survival curves were constructed with Kaplan-Meier estimates and analyzed through the log-rank test. P value of <0.05 was deemed significant.

Results

S. anginosus is enriched in GC tissues and impairs the efficacy of immunotherapy

To investigate microbial features associated with immunotherapy response, 16S rDNA sequencing was performed on gastric tumor tissues from orthotopic mouse models treated with or without anti-PD-1 therapy. Non-metric multidimensional scaling and principal coordinate analysis revealed distinct microbial community structures between the two groups (online supplemental figure 1A–D). Linear discriminant analysis effect size identified S. anginosus as one of the most significantly enriched taxa in untreated tumors (figure 1A and online supplemental figure 1E,F). Functional profiling indicated that these microbial shifts were accompanied by alterations in metabolic pathways, suggesting a potential link between S. anginosus abundance and immunotherapy response (online supplemental figure 1G,H).

FISH analysis further validated the spatial distribution of S. anginosus within gastric tumors. Bacterial fluorescence signals were detected in tumor samples from both patients with GC and mouse models prior to anti-PD-1 treatment, whereas tissues collected after treatment exhibited reduced signals (figure 1B,C and online supplemental figure 1J,K). Consistently, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis confirmed a significantly higher abundance of S. anginosus in untreated GC tissues compared with samples obtained following anti-PD-1 therapy (figure 1D and online supplemental figure 1L). Furthermore, among patients receiving immunotherapy, both FISH and RT-qPCR analyses demonstrated that S. anginosus levels were significantly elevated in non-responders compared with immunotherapy responders (figure 1E–H), indicating a robust negative association between intratumoral S. anginosus burden and clinical response to PD-1 blockade. Collectively, these results demonstrate that S. anginosus enrichment is associated with a poor response to PD-1 blockade, implicating this bacterium as a potential microbial driver of immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments that attenuate immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Moreover, to evaluate the impact of S. anginosus on immunotherapy efficacy, we employed both orthotopic (ATM) and subcutaneous (MFC) GC mouse models. Mice were pretreated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail for 2 weeks to facilitate S. anginosus colonization, which effectively cleared gut microbiota by >95% (online supplemental figure 1I). Following bacterial colonization and anti-PD-1 treatment (figure 1I), mice treated with S. anginosus developed significantly larger orthotopic gastric tumors compared with controls, and the bacterium markedly attenuated the antitumor efficacy of PD-1 blockade (figure 1J,K). Histological and immunohistochemical analyses revealed increased cellular proliferation, evidenced by elevated Ki-67 positivity in S. anginosus-treated tumors (figure 1L,M), without significant alterations in body weight (figure 1N). To further characterize the microbial landscape at the experimental endpoint, we additionally performed 16S rRNA sequencing of gastric tumor tissues from orthotopic ATM mice in both PBS and S. anginosus-gavaged groups (n=5 per group). Microbiota profiling demonstrated successful and sustained enrichment of S. anginosus in the experimental group at the endpoint, whereas the control group exhibited relatively low abundance of S. anginosus (online supplemental figure 1M,N). Consistently, in subcutaneous models, S. anginosus colonization accelerated tumor growth, increased tumor weight, and shortened survival compared with controls (figure 1O–T). Collectively, these results indicate that S. anginosus colonization drives tumor progression and diminishes the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade.

The culture supernatant of S. anginosus promoted the proliferation of GC cells

To determine whether S. anginosus exerts direct protumorigenic effects, human (AGS, SNU-216) and murine (MFC) GC cells were treated with varying concentrations of S. anginosus culture supernatant (S.a SN). Cell viability assays revealed a concentration-dependent increase in proliferation across all three cell lines after 48 hours, with a significant effect observed at S.a SN compared with control medium (figure 2A–C). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that S.a SN treatment induced a pronounced accumulation of cells in the S and G2/M phases, indicative of enhanced cell-cycle progression and active proliferation (figure 2D,E). Similarly, treatment of human GC organoids with S.a SN resulted in markedly increased tumor spheroid size relative to controls, observable by day 3 and further pronounced by day 7 (figure 2F,G and online supplemental figure 2A.B). Consistent with these findings, EdU incorporation assays revealed elevated DNA synthesis activity, as reflected by a higher proportion of EdU-positive nuclei in S.a SN treated cells compared with controls (figure 2H,I). Taken together, these findings show that S. anginosus may release soluble factors that directly enhance GC cell proliferation.

S. anginosus suppresses CD8+ T-cell function and impairs antitumor immunity

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of S. anginosus, murine PBMCs were co-cultured with ATM tumor cells in the presence of S.a SN. Treatment markedly reduced the frequencies of CD8+ and CD8+ interferon (IFN)-γ-producing subsets (figure 3A–E). Intriguingly, this immunosuppressive pattern persisted even in the absence of tumor cells: PBMCs exposed to S.a SN alone exhibited similarly reduced CD8+ T-cell frequencies (figure 3F–J). These results further demonstrate that S. anginosus suppresses CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor responses, paralleling its proliferative effects on GC cells. Notably, the suppression of CD8+ T cells was consistently more pronounced than that of CD4+ T cells, indicating preferential targeting of cytotoxic T cell.

To further assess direct effects on CD8+ T cells, CD8+ T cells from the murine splenic and human peripheral blood were isolated and treated with S.a SN. Treatment significantly attenuated IFN-γ and granzyme B expression relative to control medium (figure 3K–N). CD8 T-cell depletion experiments further demonstrated that the protumor effect of S. anginosus is largely dependent on CD8+ T-cell mediated immunity. Efficient CD8+ T-cell depletion (>95%) was confirmed by flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood (online supplemental figure 3A). In immunocompetent (IgG-treated) mice, S. anginosus significantly accelerated tumor growth compared with PBS controls; however, this effect was markedly attenuated following CD8 T-cell depletion, with no significant difference observed between groups under CD8-depleted conditions (figure 3O,P).

Furthermore, to assess the in vivo consequences of S. anginosus colonization, we employed the ATM GC mice model pretreated with antibiotics to deplete endogenous microbiota (online supplemental figure 3B–N). Beginning at 10 weeks of age, mice were orally administered live S. anginosus (1×109 CFU) or PBS two times a week for 8 weeks (figure 3Q). Bioluminescence imaging revealed markedly increased gastric photon flux in the S. anginosus group at week 8 compared with controls, indicating accelerated tumor formation (figure 3R,S). Gross anatomical examination confirmed enlarged and thickened gastric tissues, accompanied by significantly increased stomach weight (figure 3T,U). Histological evaluation revealed higher-grade neoplastic lesions and extensive epithelial hyperplasia in S. anginosus-colonized mice (figure 3V), consistent with enhanced tumor progression. And, flow-cytometric analysis of gastric immune infiltrates revealed that S. anginosus colonization profoundly reshaped the tumor immune landscape. The frequency of CD8+ T cells was markedly reduced, whereas CD4+ T-cell proportions were increased in S. anginosus-colonized mice (figure 3W). In parallel, CD45+CD11C+ dendritic cell populations were also significantly diminished (figure 3X). By contrast, no significant alterations were detected in the proportions of pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophages (CD86+F4/80+) or anti-inflammatory (M2) macrophages (CD206+F4/80+) macrophages (figure 3Y). Functionally, intratumoral CD8+ T cells from S. anginosus-colonized mice exhibited reduced IFN-γ and granzyme B expression (figure 3Z), confirming that bacterial colonization suppresses cytotoxic T-cell function in vivo.

S. anginosus-associated kynurenic acid accumulation suppresses CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity

To identify the microbial metabolite mediating the immunosuppressive effects of S. anginosus, untargeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) metabolomic profiling was first performed on fecal samples from S. anginosus-colonized and PBS-treated ATM mice. Metabolomic analysis revealed a distinct metabolic reprogramming in the tumor environment, characterized by a significant increase in KA, a tryptophan-derived immunomodulatory metabolite, in the S. anginosus group (figure 4A,B and online supplemental figure 4A–D).

To further determine whether S. anginosus directly contributes to KA production, untargeted metabolomic profiling of bacterial culture supernatants was performed. Compared with LB medium, S. anginosus conditioned medium exhibited significantly elevated KA abundance (online supplemental figure 4I), indicating an intrinsic metabolic capacity of S. anginosus to promote KA generation. Consistently, in vivo validation using an orthotopic GC mouse model demonstrated that S. anginosus colonization significantly increased intratumoral KA levels as measured by ELISA (figure 4C). Metagenomic analysis further confirmed successful bacterial engraftment, as evidenced by marked enrichment of S. anginosus in colonized mice (online supplemental figure 4E–H), supporting a direct link between bacterial colonization and KA accumulation in the tumor microenvironment.

In clinical GC samples, ELISA-based measurements showed higher intratumoral KA levels in patients without anti-PD-1 therapy, a trend that was also evident in immunotherapy non-responders (figure 4D,E). Moreover, quantitative PCR-based analysis of matched clinical samples demonstrated a positive correlation between S. anginosus abundance and intratumoral KA levels (figure 4F), further supporting an association between microbial colonization and KA accumulation in the tumor microenvironment. To further exclude the confounding effects of antibiotic-induced microbiota perturbation, we established a germ-free (GF) mouse model for S. anginosus mono-association (figure 4G). Targeted LC-MS/MS analysis demonstrated that S. anginosus colonization alone significantly increased fecal KA levels in GF mice compared with uncolonized controls (figure 4H). In a subcutaneous tumor model established in GF mice, S. anginosus mono-association also significantly promoted tumor growth, as evidenced by increased tumor burden and higher tumor weights, without significantly affecting body weight (figure 4I–L). Moreover, flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed that S. anginosus mono-association markedly impaired CD8+ T-cell effector function and altered immune cell activity under microbiota-free conditions (figure 4M–S). Collectively, these findings indicate that S. anginosus is sufficient to drive KA accumulation and immunosuppressive remodeling in vivo.

To further evaluate the physiological relevance of KA, targeted LC-MS/MS analysis demonstrated that intratumoral KA concentrations ranged from 3.1 to 7.5 µM in patients without immunotherapy and from 0.18 to 2.98 µM in patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy (online supplemental figure 4J). The immunological impact of KA was then evaluated by treating purified human CD8+ T lymphocytes with increasing concentrations of KA (0–4 μM). KA induced a dose-dependent reduction in IFN-γ and granzyme B production without significantly affecting cell viability (figure 4T,U and online supplemental figure 4K–M). Notably, 2 µM KA represented the lowest concentration that consistently induced robust suppression of CD8+ T-cell effector function while maintaining T-cell survival and was therefore selected for subsequent mechanistic and co-culture assays. Functionally, KA pretreatment significantly impaired the tumor-killing capacity of CD8+ T cells against AGS and SNU-216 GC cells, as evidenced by reduced tumor-cell apoptosis (figure 4V,W and online supplemental figure 4N). In contrast, KA treatment alone did not directly induce apoptosis in GC cells (online supplemental figure 4O–Q), indicating that the observed effects were primarily mediated through suppression of T-cell cytotoxic function rather than direct tumor-cell toxicity.

The inhibitory effect of KA further extended to engineered immune therapies. Claudin 18.2-targeted CAR-T cells with transduction efficiencies exceeding 45% (online supplemental figure 4R) exhibited markedly reduced cytotoxicity against claudin 18.2-positive AGS and SNU-216 cells (online supplemental figure 4S–U). At an optimized effector-to-target ratio of 8:1, KA significantly impaired CAR-T-mediated tumor lysis and decreased IFN-γ and granzyme B production, consistent with functional exhaustion rather than non-specific cytotoxicity (online supplemental figure 4V,W). Similarly, in patient-derived GC organoid co-culture systems, KA markedly attenuated CAR-T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against human GC organoid, further confirming its immunosuppressive effects in a physiologically relevant human model (figure 4X,Y). Fluorescence co-localization analysis confirmed the spatial interaction between CAR-T cells and GC organoids (figure 4Z). In addition, supplementary in vivo experiments further demonstrated that KA promoted tumor growth in a subcutaneous tumor model established using ATM-derived gastric cancer cells (online supplemental figure 4XAC). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that elevated KA levels driven by S. anginosus colonization suppress both endogenous CD8+ T cells and CAR-T cell-mediated antitumor immunity, thereby promoting immune evasion and tumor progression in GC.

ITGA2 mediates KA-induced suppression of CD8+ T-cell function

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying KA-mediated T-cell suppression, transcriptomic profiling was performed on human CD8+ T lymphocytes exposed to KA for 48 hours. Principal component analysis and differential gene expression revealed a distinct transcriptional landscape compared with control cells (online supplemental figure 5A,B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analyses highlighted cell adhesion and integrin signaling among the most significantly altered pathways (online supplemental figure 5C,D). Notably, ITGA2 emerged as one of the top upregulated genes following KA exposure (figure 5A,B). Flow cytometry and western blot validation confirmed robust ITGA2 induction in KA-treated CD8+ T cells, without affecting cell viability (figure 5C and online supplemental figure 5E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of GC tissues further demonstrated elevated ITGA2 expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells from individuals who had not received immunotherapy (figure 5D).

To determine whether ITGA2 contributes functionally to the impaired CD8+ T-cell response, lentiviral knockdown of ITGA2 was established and confirmed at the protein level (figure 5E and online supplemental figure 5F). ITGA2 silencing markedly restored IFN-γ and granzyme B expression in human CD8+ T cells (figure 5F), indicating that ITGA2 plays a causal role in KA-induced functional suppression. Consistent with this, ITGA2 knockdown in CAR-claudin 18.2+ CD8+T cells significantly enhanced their ability to induce apoptosis and lysis of GC cells in co-culture assays (figure 5G,H). Furthermore, when ITGA2-deficient CD8+ T cells were treated with KA, the suppressive effects of KA on IFN-γ and granzyme B production were largely abrogated (figure 5I), confirming that ITGA2 acts downstream of KA to mediate effector T-cell inhibition. To further investigate the interaction between KA and ITGA2, we performed Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) together with a photoaffinity probe-based pull-down assay. CETSA demonstrated a reproducible increase in the thermal stability of ITGA2 following KA treatment across a range of temperatures compared with vehicle control, consistent with ligand-associated protein stabilization. Furthermore, the photoaffinity probe KP1 specifically enriched ITGA2 following ultraviolet (UV) crosslinking and streptavidin pull-down, whereas minimal ITGA2 signal was detected in the negative control probe KP2. Importantly, enrichment of ITGA2 by KP1 was markedly reduced in the presence of excess unlabeled KA, supporting the specificity of the interaction (online supplemental figure 5G,H). Collectively, these complementary biochemical approaches support a specific physical association between KA and ITGA2.

ITGA2 inhibition enhances immunotherapy efficacy in GC mice

To evaluate whether ITGA2 blockade enhances immunotherapy efficacy, we employed three complementary GC mouse models: (1) the ATM orthotopic GC model, (2) the MFC subcutaneous tumor model, and (3) a subcutaneous model established by implanting mouse primary GC cells derived from chemically induced tumors (figure 5J). Administration of the ITGA2 inhibitor E7820 (30 mg/kg) significantly suppressed tumor progression across all models compared with vehicle-treated controls. Notably, combination treatment with anti-PD-1 antibody (100 µg, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection). further enhanced tumor suppression, achieving the most pronounced inhibition of gastric tumor growth (figure 5K,L and online supplemental figure 5I,J).

To characterize the immune contexture underlying these effects, we profiled ITGA2 distribution and tumor-infiltrating immune cells. High-dimensional spectral flow cytometry revealed that ITGA2 was broadly expressed across multiple immune populations, including CD8+ T cells and tumor-associated macrophages (online supplemental figure 5K). Given the central role of CD8+ T cells in antitumor immunity, downstream analyses focused on this compartment. Consistently, E7820 showed minimal direct cytotoxic effects on GC cells in vitro (online supplemental figure 5L), suggesting that its antitumor activity is primarily immune-mediated. Immunophenotypic analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed that ITGA2 inhibition significantly increased the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and restored their effector functions, as evidenced by elevated IFN-γ and granzyme B production. Concomitantly, the proportion of M1-like macrophages was increased, whereas M2-like macrophages were reduced within the tumor microenvironment (figure 5M–U and online supplemental figure 5M–V). Consistent with these immune changes, Ki-67 staining analyses demonstrated that the tumor-cell proliferation was decreased following ITGA2 blockade, either alone or in combination with PD-1 inhibition (figure 5L, online supplemental figure 6A,B and online supplemental figure 6S,T). Furthermore, in subcutaneous xenograft models, combined ITGA2 and PD-1 blockade synergistically decreased tumor volume and weight without affecting body weight (figure 5V–X and online supplemental figure 6C–R). Importantly, dual therapy significantly prolonged overall survival compared with either monotherapy, confirming the therapeutic synergy between ITGA2 inhibition and PD-1 blockade (online supplemental figure 6U).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that targeting of ITGA2 effectively restores antitumor immunity and amplifies the response to PD-1 blockade across multiple in vivo models of GC.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which ITGA2 modulates CD8+ T-cell immunity, we performed co-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (Co-IP/MS) on lysates of human CD8+ T cells (figure 6A). Molecular docking of the top 20 candidate proteins from the mass spectrometry analysis identified CTSV and SON DNA-binding protein (SON) as the highest-ranking interactors with ITGA2 (figure 6B,C). Subsequent Co-IP and western blot validation confirmed a robust ITGA2-CTSV interaction, whereas ITGA2-SON association was weaker and inconsistent across replicates (figure 6D,E). Silencing ITGA2 in CD8+ T cells led to a marked reduction in CTSV protein expression, while SON levels remained largely unchanged (figure 6F and online supplemental figure 7A,B), suggesting CTSV as a primary downstream effector of ITGA2 signaling.

Given that ITGA2 is a plasma membrane integrin and CTSV is predominantly localized to lysosomes,34 35 a direct regulatory mechanism is unlikely, suggesting the involvement of intermediate signaling pathways. Integrin signaling is known to converge on multiple intracellular pathways, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and NF-κB cascades, which can influence cellular metabolism and immune function.36 37 To systematically identify the pathway linking ITGA2 to CTSV regulation, we first examined a panel of signaling pathways following ITGA2 knockdown in CD8+ T cells. Western blot analysis revealed that ITGA2 depletion induced activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, as evidenced by increased PI3K-P85 expression and elevated phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR. In contrast, signaling components from parallel pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB modules, exhibited minimal changes (figure 6G–K and online supplemental figure 7C–L).

To determine whether the ITGA2-dependent regulation of CTSV is mediated through PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, we next analyzed pathway activity under four experimental conditions: Ctrl, ITGA2 KD, pharmacological inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling using dactolisib, and combined ITGA2 KD plus dactolisib treatment. As expected, ITGA2 knockdown increased AKT and mTOR phosphorylation, which was efficiently suppressed by dactolisib (figure 6L–P). Importantly, inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling partially restored CTSV protein levels that were reduced by ITGA2 knockdown, supporting a pathway-dependent regulation of CTSV downstream of ITGA2 (figure 6R and online supplemental figure 7O).

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed clear subcellular co-localization of mTOR (red) and CTSV (green) within CD8+ T cells, supporting a potential regulatory interaction at the lysosomal compartment (figure 6Q). Consistent with this observation, Co-IP experiments demonstrated that mTOR physically associates with CTSV, as well as with ITGA2, indicating that mTOR may act as a molecular scaffold linking membrane ITGA2 signaling to lysosomal CTSV stability (online supplemental figure 7M,N).

Furthermore, we investigated whether CTSV functions as a downstream effector of the ITGA2-PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis to mediate KA-induced immunosuppression in CD8+ T cells. Overexpression of CTSV in human CD8+ T cells significantly reduced IFN-γ and granzyme B production (figure 6S–U and online supplemental figure 7P,Q). Conversely, ITGA2 knockdown restored effector cytokine expression and cytotoxicity, and this rescue was partially reversed by CTSV co-overexpression, indicating that CTSV functions downstream of ITGA2 to mediate T-cell dysfunction. To directly assess the functional relevance of this axis, claudin 18.2-targeted CAR-T cells were preconditioned with combinations of KA exposure, ITGA2 silencing, and CTSV overexpression prior to co-culture with GC cells. Functional assays demonstrated that KA treatment or CTSV overexpression independently suppressed CAR-T cytotoxicity, whereas ITGA2 knockdown markedly enhanced tumor-cell lysis. Importantly, CTSV re-expression largely abolished the protective effect of ITGA2 silencing, confirming CTSV as a critical downstream effector linking KA-ITGA2 signaling to impaired T-cell function (figure 6V,W).

Collectively, these findings delineate a KA-ITGA2-PI3K/AKT/mTOR-CTSV signaling cascade through which S. anginosus-induced metabolic remodeling undermines CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity, providing a mechanistic basis for microbial-driven immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance in GC.

Disruption of the ITGA2-CTSV axis restores CAR-T efficacy in vivo

To determine whether inhibition of the KA-ITGA2-mTOR-CTSV axis could rescue CAR-T cell cytotoxicity in vivo, we established a subcutaneous ATM-luc GC xenograft model in NSG mice by implanting luciferase-labeled ATM tumor cells into the left axilla (figure 7A). CAR-Claudin 18.2+ CD8+ T cells were preconditioned with ITGA2 knockdown (sh-ITGA2) and/or CTSV overexpression (OE-CTSV) prior to intravenous infusion. Bioluminescence imaging demonstrated that CAR-T cells with ITGA2 silencing markedly suppressed tumor progression, as indicated by a significant reduction in total photon flux compared with control CAR-T cells (figure 7B,C). Conversely, enforced CTSV expression largely abolished the antitumor effect of ITGA2 knockdown, restoring tumor growth to levels similar to controls. Consistently, survival analysis over a 55-day observation period demonstrated that mice receiving sh-ITGA2 CAR-T cells exhibited significantly prolonged survival, whereas CTSVv overexpression substantially reversed this survival benefit (online supplemental figure 6V). Throughout treatment, body weight and hematologic indices, including white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), platelets (PLT), haemoglobin (HGB) and neutrophils (Neu), remained stable among all groups (figure 7D–I). Likewise, biochemical markers of hepatic and renal function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), urea (UREA) and creatinine (CREA) were unaffected (figure 7J–M), and H&E staining of major organs (liver, heart, spleen, lung, kidney) revealed no pathological abnormalities (figure 7N), indicating that ITGA2-CTSV modulation did not induce systemic toxicity.

Discussion

Although accumulating evidence links gastrointestinal dysbiosis to GC, the role of intratumoral microbes in tumorigenesis and immune regulation remains poorly understood. In this study, through integrated microbiome, metabolomic, transcriptomic, and immunological analyses, we demonstrate that S. anginosus is consistently enriched within gastric tumor tissues and functions as a critical microbial driver of immunotherapeutic resistance. Functional studies across orthotopic, subcutaneous, and GF mono-colonization models further establish that S. anginosus accelerates tumor progression, suppresses CD8+ T-cell effector function, and attenuates the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade. These findings extend previous observations linking S. anginosus to gastric carcinogenesis by further supporting its role in shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and limiting antitumor immunity.

S. anginosus, a gram-positive bacterium commonly colonizing the oral cavity, has recently been identified as a gastric pathobiont capable of establishing persistent colonization in the murine stomach. It induces acute inflammatory responses characterized by robust upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, followed by chronic gastritis associated with parietal cell atrophy, mucinous metaplasia, and epithelial dysplasia hallmarks of gastric precancerous lesions.38–40 Moreover, S. anginosus infection reshapes the gastric microbiota, leading to the enrichment of pathogenic oral commensals such as Prevotella and Aggregatibacter, along with the depletion of beneficial B. pseudolongum.41 Fu and colleagues have reported that S. anginosus promotes gastric tumorigenesis through direct interaction with gastric epithelial cells via the TMPC-ANXA2-MAPK signaling axis.25 Consistent with this, we confirmed the protumorigenic potential of S. anginosus using both orthotopic GC mouse models and in vitro experiments involving exposure of tumor cells to bacterial supernatants. Beyond its intrinsic oncogenic effects, our integrative analysis of gut microbiota from patients with immunotherapy-treated GC revealed that S. anginosus is selectively enriched in non-responders and correlates with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, S. anginosus impairs CD8+ T-cell cytotoxic function, thereby attenuating the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. Importantly, CD8 T-cell depletion experiments suggest that the protumor effects of S. anginosus are at least partly dependent on CD8+ T-cell related immune pathways, highlighting a key role of cytotoxic T-cell immunity in shaping its tumor-promoting activity. Together, these findings establish S. anginosus not only as a microbial driver of gastric tumorigenesis but also as a key regulator of antitumor immunity and immunotherapeutic resistance in GC.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying S. anginosus-mediated immunosuppression, we focused on bacterial metabolites and identified KA as a key functional mediator. Colonization with S. anginosus increased KA in feces, bacterial supernatants, and intratumoral tissues in both mouse models and human GC samples. GF mono-colonization confirmed that S. anginosus alone suffices to elevate KA, accelerate tumor growth, and impair CD8+ T-cell effector function. Mechanistically, physiologically relevant KA levels suppressed IFN-γ and granzyme B production without affecting tumor-cell viability, highlighting immune-mediated effects. KA also inhibited endogenous CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and claudin 18.2 CAR-T-cell activity, including reduced killing of patient-derived GC organoids, underscoring translational relevance. These findings establish a direct link between intratumoral bacterial burden and local accumulation of immunomodulatory metabolites. KA has been previously reported to exert tissue-protective effects in multiple preclinical models of organ ischemia and is considered a cardioprotective factor.42 However, recent work by Zhou et al demonstrated that KA, recognized as an immunosuppressive metabolite, can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor to promote the expansion and infiltration of immunosuppressive neutrophils within the tumor microenvironment, thereby facilitating the progression of oral squamous cell carcinoma.43 Despite these findings, the precise role of KA in tumor progression and metastatic dissemination remains incompletely defined. In our study, although KA did not directly affect GC cell proliferation, it indirectly promoted tumor growth by functionally suppressing CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity.

KA, a key metabolite of the kynurenine pathway, is an endogenous ligand of hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 3 (HCAR3).44 Interestingly, our transcriptomic analysis did not reveal significant changes in HCAR3 expression; instead, we observed a marked upregulation of ITGA2. Transcriptomic profiling revealed prominent induction of ITGA2 in KA-treated CD8+ T cells, and functional experiments demonstrated that ITGA2 silencing restored cytokine production and cytotoxicity in both conventional CD8+ T cells and claudin 18.2 CAR-T cells. Notably, CETSA together with the pull-down assay consistently supported a specific physical association between KA and ITGA2, suggesting that ITGA2 is a potential molecular target through which KA modulates CD8+ T-cell function. Previous studies have shown that ITGA2 overexpression promotes proliferation and invasion in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma via FAK/AKT signaling and enhances pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and invasion through STAT3-mediated upregulation of PD-L1.45 46 While the protumorigenic role of ITGA2 is established, its immunoregulatory function remains largely unexplored. Here, high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry revealed broad ITGA2 expression across tumor-infiltrating immune populations, including CD8+ T cells and tumor-associated macrophages. Notably, KA exerted a pronounced suppressive effect on CD8+ T cells. Pharmacologic inhibition of ITGA2 with E7820 restored CD8+ T-cell infiltration and effector function, synergized with PD-1 blockade in multiple GC models, and had minimal direct effects on tumor cells. These findings position ITGA2 as a potential immunoregulatory checkpoint downstream of microbial metabolic remodeling.

KA interacts with ITGA2 on the surface of CD8+ T cells, subsequently triggering inhibitory signaling within these cytotoxic lymphocytes. To elucidate downstream effectors of ITGA2, we performed mass spectrometry-based protein interaction analyses and identified CTSV as a key target. Functional rescue experiments confirmed that CTSV mediates ITGA2-dependent modulation of CD8+ T-cell activity. CTSV exhibits context-dependent roles in cancer, with its expression variably promoting or suppressing tumor progression across different cell types.47–49 Elevated CTSV expression has been reported in multiple malignancies, including breast cancer, squamous cell carcinoma, and colorectal cancer.50–52 However, its expression and regulatory functions within immune cells remain largely unexplored. Here, manipulating CTSV levels in CAR-engineered CD8+ T cells demonstrated that CTSV critically regulates their cytotoxic activity, highlighting a translationally relevant axis for enhancing adoptive T-cell therapies in cancer.

Given that ITGA2 is a plasma membrane integrin whereas CTSV is predominantly localized to lysosomes, a direct spatial interaction is unlikely. Instead, our data support a signaling-mediated regulatory mechanism. Integrin signaling converges on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, a key regulator of lysosomal function. Consistently, ITGA2 knockdown selectively activated PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, while pharmacologic inhibition of this pathway partially restored CTSV expression. Moreover, the subcellular co-localization and biochemical association between mTOR and CTSV suggest that mTOR may act as an intermediary linking ITGA2 signaling to lysosomal CTSV regulation. Although the precise molecular intermediates remain to be defined, these findings provide functional evidence that the ITGA2-PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis governs CTSV-dependent suppression of CD8+ T-cell activity. Importantly, disruption of the ITGA2-CTSV axis significantly restored CAR-T therapeutic efficacy in vivo and prolonged survival in NSG xenograft models without inducing systemic toxicity, supporting the translational potential of targeting this pathway to overcome metabolite-driven immune suppression.

In summary, our study delineates a causal axis linking S. anginosus colonization, metabolite-mediated suppression of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, and gastric tumor progression. We demonstrate that S. anginosus-derived KA acts upstream to induce ITGA2 expression on CD8+ T cells, thereby engaging PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and promoting CTSV-dependent T-cell dysfunction. Through this metabolite-integrin-mTOR-protease axis, cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell activity is attenuated, facilitating tumor immune escape. These findings highlight the importance of integrating microbial ecology with tumor immunology to better understand therapeutic resistance and develop microbiota-informed strategies for improving immunotherapy and CAR-T efficacy in GC. Targeting this metabolite-integrin-protease axis represents a promising strategy to restore antitumor immunity and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in GC.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Although our metabolomic analyses, GF mono-colonization experiments, and clinical correlation studies collectively support a contributory role of S. anginosus in driving KA accumulation, host-derived tryptophan metabolism may also participate in shaping intratumoral KA levels. In addition, definitive genetic evidence would require the construction of a KA synthesis-deficient S. anginosus mutant. However, stable genetic manipulation systems for clinical S. anginosus isolates remain technically challenging in our current experimental setting, particularly for precise editing of tryptophan metabolic pathways. Future studies using genetically engineered KA-deficient strains, isotope-tracing approaches, or bacterial metabolic flux analyses will therefore be important to further dissect the relative contribution of microbial versus host-derived KA within the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, while ITGA2 has known roles in regulating cell adhesion, migration, and tissue localization, these aspects were not investigated in the present study focused on the KA-ITGA2-AKT/mTOR axis in CD8+ T-cell dysfunction. Despite these limitations, our findings establish a mechanistic link between a tumor-associated bacterium, microbial metabolite-mediated immune regulation, and ICB resistance. This study highlights the importance of microbiome-derived metabolites as regulators of antitumor immunity and provides a potential therapeutic framework for overcoming immunotherapy resistance in GC.
