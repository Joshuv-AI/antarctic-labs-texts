Circularly polarized luminescence optical waveguides in stretchable photonic crystal fibers for advanced human motion monitoring
Authors: Tian-Tian Hao, Yan Guan, Yuan-Yuan Qu, Jia-Wei Qi, Dong-Ping Liu, Ming-Zhu Wu, Jiang Huang, Pengfei Duan, He-Lou Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible optical waveguides are vital for next-generation optoelectronics, yet conventional organic soft waveguides lack the dynamic, real-time modulation required for wearable systems. Here, we report mechanically programmable chiroptical waveguides utilizing stretchable photonic crystal (SPC) fibers. By establishing a rigorous quantitative framework relating helical pitch evolution to circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) dissymmetry factors (glum), we achieve a linear modulation of glum from 0.153 to near zero via mechanical stretching. These fibers exhibit an exceptionally low optical loss coefficient (~0.0922 dB/mm), ensuring long-range signal integrity. Crucially, the glum signal provides a “self-calibrating” sensing mode inherently robust against excitation fluctuations and environmental scattering—overcoming fundamental limitations of standard intensity-based sensors. This work provides a deeper understanding of strain-regulated photonic architectures, offering a robust platform for high-precision, interference-free human motion monitoring and multi-channel optoelectronic communication.

Introduction

Optical waveguides are widely recognized for their high information capacity, efficient signal transmission, and broad utility in sensors, lasers, and communication systems1–3. However, conventional rigid or passive waveguides lack the dynamic, stimulus-responsive modulation required for emerging intelligent wearable and flexible devices4,5. The growing demand for real-time, quantifiable transduction of mechanical stimuli in applications such as human motion monitoring, personalized healthcare6, electronic skin7, soft robotics8, and advanced prosthetics9 has driven interest in soft, stretchable platforms that can convert deformation into multidimensional signal outputs. Currently, these devices are mainly dependent on precise electrical response signals, which enable the simultaneous visual identification, monitoring, and quantification of stimulus sources10,11. Although electronic sensors provide high accuracy and sensitivity, they encounter challenges including limited biocompatibility from metallic components, miniaturization difficulties, and vulnerability to electromagnetic interference and electrical safety hazards12–15. In contrast, the sensors based on optical waveguides transmit signals via light, which offers enhanced safety for wearable and implantable applications, particularly in electrically sensitive environments such as medical imaging suites or human–machine interfaces16–18. However, developing wearable and implantable optical waveguide materials (OWMs) remains challenging due to difficulties in controlling light on curved and biological interfaces19–21.

Circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), characterized by its angle-independent polarization and quantified by the luminescence dissymmetric factors (glum), offers inherent advantages for encoding high-density, low-crosstalk optical information22,23. In particular, CPL signals are intrinsically ratiometric and far less susceptible to artifacts from viewing angle, ambient lighting variations, or scattering limitations that often compromise the reliability of mechanochromic structural color or unpolarized fluorescence intensity readouts in practical wearable scenarios. Despite these benefits, most reported CPL-active optical waveguides rely on rigid chiral organic crystals or static low-dimensional systems24–28, which cannot achieve rapid, reversible CPL switching under mechanical stimuli or integrate multi-modal optical responses (CPL, color, intensity, and waveguiding) in a single deformable element29–33. On the other hand, stretchable photonic crystal (SPC) based on chiral liquid crystal elastomers provides a promising soft material platform, as their self-organized helical superstructures enable switchable selective reflection, high glum values, mechanochromism, and elastic deformation34–38. While previous studies have demonstrated mechanochromic CLCE fibers and films with strain-tunable reflection or basic photonic properties39–43, these systems have primarily focused on static or single-modal optical outputs. Critically, they have not achieved reversible mechanical tuning of CPL intensity and glum within a low-loss optical waveguide architecture, nor demonstrated that CPL provides a functionally superior, interference-robust signal for quantitative strain sensing compared to conventional optical readouts.

In this work, we present SPC fibers fabricated from chiral luminescent liquid crystal elastomers that integrate mechanically tunable CPL, mechanochromic visualization across the full visible spectrum, and highly efficient circularly polarized photonic waveguiding. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we designed stretchable SPC fibers as optical sensing elements, incorporating a fluorescent monomer (DCS) and mechanochromic molecules (Rh-AC) as a Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) donor and acceptor, respectively44,45 (the detailed synthesis and characterization of Rh-AC are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 3). By deliberately matching the photonic bandgap to the emission band of an embedded fluorescent acceptor in a FRET pair, we achieve tunable CPL with a glum value from 0.153 to zero. Meantime, uniaxial stretching progressively unwinds the chiral helix, suppressing CPL polarization while preserving low-loss waveguiding even under large strains or tight bending. Results

Preparation and characterization of CPL-active OWMs based on SPC fibers

The SPC fibers were prepared using a facile anisotropic deswelling method, integrating two-stage thiol-acrylate Michael addition and photopolymerization reactions43,46. The lab-made fluorescent monomer DCS and Rh-A, commercial liquid crystal monomer 2-methyl-1,4-phenylene bis(4-(3-(acryloyloxy) propoxy) benzoate) (RM257) and the chiral monomer(3 R, 3aS, 6aS)-hexahydrofuro [3,2-b] furan-3,6-diylbis (4-(4-((4-(acryloyloxy) butoxy) carbonyloxy) benzoyloxy) benzoate) (LC756), chain extender 2,2’-(ethylenedioxy)diethanethiol) (EDDET), and crosslinker tetrakis (3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP) were utilized to prepare SPC@x%LC756 fibers through the thiol-acrylate-Michael addition reaction (x represents the LC756 molecule monomer mass make up x wt.% of the total monomer mass.) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 2, and Supplementary Table 1). It is noted that the lab-made double-bond-functionalized Rh-AC molecule in its ring-closed (c-Rh-AC) and the ring-opened Rh-AC (o-Rh-AC) showed typical stimulus responsiveness (Supplementary Fig. 4). The chemical structures of SPC@x%LC756 fibers were comprehensively characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Fig. 2b). The absence of characteristic absorption peaks for sulfhydryl (–SH) at 2577 cm−1, olefinic C═C at 1637 cm−1 and alkyl double-bond at 989 cm−1 unequivocally indicated that complete reaction of the -SH and acrylate group. These findings indicated the successful synthesis of SPC@x%LC756. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of SPC@x%LC756 fibers revealed that all SPC fibers exhibited excellent thermal stability, with 5% weight loss occurring above 400 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Detailed quantitative data derived from the curves are presented in Supplementary Table 2, Supporting Information. Subsequently, we systematically investigated the photophysical properties of SPC@0%LC756 and SPC@4.5%LC756. We examined the emission of SPC@0%LC756 and SPC@4.5%LC756 under 365 nm excitation (Supplementary Fig. 7a). These results showed no significant change in the fiber emission band after doping with different contents of LC756. The absorption spectra of SPC@0%LC756 and SPC@4.5%LC756 were measured in thin films (Supplementary Fig. 7b). The results revealed that the absorbance spectrum of TFA-treated SPC films exhibited double peaks at ≈490 and 530 nm. The positions of these peaks were consistent with those observed for the o-Rh-AC molecule characterized in solutions. Additionally, the absorption of the fiber after doping with LC756 did not show any significant change. The quantum efficiencies of c-Rh-AC, o-Rh-AC, SPC@x%LC756 were measured to be 2.60%, 22.8%, 13.0%, 12.9%, 12.7%, and 13.5%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7c). These values indicated that o-Rh-AC with ring-open significantly enhanced the emission efficiency of the materials.

Interestingly, the SPC fibers exhibited remarkable dynamic mechanochromic behaviors at room temperature. For instance, the red-reflecting SPC fiber demonstrated a continuous color change from red to blue upon mechanical stretching (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9, and Supplementary Movies 1–6). It is important to note that the entire process is fully reversible. To quantitatively assess the recovery speed, we employed a high-speed camera (180 fps, with an effective slow-motion factor of 3.6 times speed) to capture the fiber’s relaxation upon rapid manual release from a stretched state (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Movie 7). A frame-by-frame analysis reveals that the recovery time from a fully stretched to a fully relaxed state is approximately 355.56 ms, with an elastic recovery rate of 97.06%. This quantitative assessment confirms that the response speed is sufficiently rapid for practical wearable sensing applications, as typical human motion occurs on timescales of hundreds of milliseconds. Furthermore, stretching-releasing cycles were performed over 100 times (Supplementary Fig. 11), without any noticeable degradation in performance. Cross-sectional SEM images showed the SPC fiber revealing a helical structure with a helical pitch (p) of 420 nm in the initial red state (Supplementary Fig. 12). CPL optical waveguide behavior of SPC fibers

The CPL behaviors for the SPC with different doping contents of LC756 were investigated using a CPL spectrometer. As shown in Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 15, all samples exhibit clear CPL with a significant glum value, demonstrating effective chirality induction by LC756 in SPC@x%LC756. Notably, both the CPL signal intensity and glum increased initially with increasing LC756 content, reaching a maximum at 4.5% doping, before decreasing at higher concentrations. The SPC@4.5%LC756 sample displayed the highest CPL signal and glum value, with its POM image revealing a characteristic oily texture (Supplementary Fig. 16a–c). Notably, the POM textures of SPCs deform along the mechanical stretching direction, but when the strain exceeds a critical value (Strain > 230%), the helical structure collapses, causing the POM textures to become blurred and resulting in a loss of color uniformity (Supplementary Fig. 16d–f). In the meantime, the results of the cross-sectional SEM experiment clearly show the changes in the helical pitch under tensile strain. As the fiber undergoes stretching, the helical pitch decreases from an initial value of 420 to 323 nm after stretching (Supplementary Fig. 12b, c). This provides direct evidence of the structural changes occurring under strain, which is consistent with the unwinding of the helical configuration. Transmittance spectra of SPC@x%LC756 (Fig. 3c) showed that varying the LC756 content modulated the photonic bandgap, resulting in distinct reflective colors under natural light. For SPC@4.5%LC756, the reflection band overlapped with the emission band of Rh-AC (Fig. 3d), indicating optimal CPL performance. Based on the aforementioned findings, SPC@4.5%LC756 exhibited the highest glum and excellent mechanical stretchability, making it the optimal candidate for investigating tunable CPL. The glum, defined as glum = 2(IL-IR)/(IL + IR), reached the maximum glum of SPC@4.5%LC756, which was 0.153 (Supplementary Fig. 17). Tensile deformation significantly altered the SPC samples' structure from its initial state, impacting its CPL properties. To explore this, we compared CPL signals of SPC@4.5%LC756 before and after stretching under identical conditions (λex = 365 nm). As shown in Fig. 3e, f, both CPL signals and glum values substantially decreased with progressive stretching. Specifically, the glum value diminished to near zero, indicating that external mechanical stress suppressed the CPL signal in SPC@4.5%LC756. These results demonstrated that external mechanical stimulation could lead to the disappearance of the CPL signal in SPC@4.5%LC756. Stretch-induced CPL suppression was performed over ten cycles (Supplementary Fig. 18), with no noticeable degradation observed, demonstrating the material’s robustness. The three-dimensional chiral architecture of SPC fibers demonstrates reversibility, enabling consistent structural integrity and performance through repeated cycles of tensile stretching and stress relaxation. The combined analysis of mechanochromic property, reflection behavior, and the glum values demonstrates that under elevated tensile strain, the chiral structure undergoes disruption. This disruption leads to the unwinding of the helical configuration into a stratified arrangement, which consequently reduces the glum to nearly zero (Fig. 3g). Using the Bragg law (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\lambda }}_{max }=n*p$$\end{document}λmax=n*p), we calculate that helical pitch p decreases from 416.3 ± 5 nm to 283.4 ± 4 nm. Plotting p vs. glum fit with a linear equation (glum = −0.384 + 0.00129*p, R2 = 0.961), proving that helical unwinding (reduced p) is the direct cause of glum reduction, rather than non-specific structural damage (Fig. 3h–j). Thus, a quantitative structure-property relationship is established in which macroscopic strain regulates the helical pitch of SPC fibers, synchronously tuning both the reflection color/spectrum (via Bragg wavelength shift) and the glum value (via helical unwinding). This provides a general design principle for mechanically programmable chiroptical materials, enabling controllable chiral optical performance through strain-mediated modulation of chiral structures.

SPC has been documented to possess mechanochromic properties. Hence, we hypothesized that mechanoresponsive CPL emission could be achieved by mechanically tuning the photonic bandgap. Here, SPC@4.5%LC756 fiber was selected to investigate mechanically tunable CPL. Unexpectedly, uniaxial stretching induced mechanochromism but also resulted in an observable CPL phenomenon. To characterize the emitted light, a linear polarizer placed before a CCD detector was rotated to various angles θ, and the luminescence intensities of the ±45° linearly polarized light signals from the SPC@4.5%LC756 fiber tip were recorded per 30° intervals under different strains (Fig. 4b, c, e, f, h, i and Supplementary Fig. 20a, b, d, e, g, h, j, k, m, n). The relationship between the maximum luminescence intensity (occurring at approximately 600 nm) and polarization angle θ is shown in Fig. 4d, g, j and Supplementary Fig. 20c, f, i, l, o. With increasing strain, the polarization directions of the +45° and −45° linearly polarized lights from the SPC@4.5%LC756 fiber tip transitioned to nearly unpolarized luminescence. This mechanoresponsive CPL behavior can be attributed to the unwinding of the helical nanostructure into a monodomain structure resembling a 1D Bragg reflector under uniaxial stretching, which eliminated the intrinsic circular polarization characteristics and resulted in unpolarized luminescence (Supplementary Fig. 21). These findings highlight the critical need to preserve the helical nanostructure during stretching to achieve mechanically regulated CPL effectively.

Human motion monitoring based on CPL optical waveguide

The performance of the interactively full-color changeable CPL fiber sensor allows its use as a skin-attachable wearable device for interactive real-time human motion monitoring across various body parts (Fig. 6a). During body movement, changes in CPL intensity and reflection spectra are readily converted into digital signals by instrumentation, while the accompanying color changes allow intuitive visual perception. These signals correlate with the motion state and quantify the deformation. Notably, the integration of CPL into this motion monitoring system is crucial due to its advantages over conventional optical signals with only mechanochromic color or unpolarized fluorescence intensity alone. While mechanochromic color provides a direct visual readout, it is highly sensitive to the viewing angle and ambient lighting. Even slight shifts in observation position or lighting conditions can alter the perceived color, leading to misinterpretation of strain magnitude. In contrast, the CPL signal, quantified by the glum, is an inherent emission property of the SPC fiber’s chiral structure, making it much less susceptible to these external interferences. This robustness ensures that the CPL-derived digital signal is more reliable for quantitative strain analysis compared to the qualitative readout of structural color. The sensor was attached to a finger subjected to different stretching, bending, and pressing, with the optical signals varying concurrently (Supplementary Fig. 27). However, when testing the fluorescence intensity of the SPC fiber without the polarizer, it was found that the fluorescence intensity remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 28). The glum value serves as a ratiometric metric that normalizes these fluctuations. For example, the glum of SPC@4.5%LC756 can be precisely tuned through mechanical stretching. This tunability ensures high precision even under varying ambient conditions, maintaining a stable correlation between glum and applied strain. The consistent signal changes over multiple cycles indicate excellent stability and repeatability, with the structural color shifting from red to blue, indicating robust user-interaction visualization. Figures 6c–e displays the CPL intensity response to repeated bending of the elbow and knee joints, respectively. Similarly, stable signal response and color-switching caused by elbow and knee joint motions were also displayed. These results implied that the sensor showed excellent ability to interactively detect large deformation motions of the human body. Beyond quantitative precision, CPL also provides an independent information channel that complements structural color and unpolarized optical signals, forming a multi-modal sensing system. In complex motion sensing scenarios, the combination of multiple orthogonal modalities, i.e., structural color for intuitive visualization, fluorescence intensity as a simple analog signal, and CPL as a stable, quantitative digital signal, greatly enhances the system’s versatility and information richness. This multi-modal approach allows for cross-verification, ensuring reliable performance under diverse conditions where reliance on a single signal could be limiting. Such versatility is especially valuable for wearable applications, where environmental conditions are often variable. Similarly, when fixed to a finger, the sensor exhibited consistent responses to stretching, bending, and pressing (Fig. 6b). A stepwise decrease in CPL intensity and a corresponding structural color shift (from red to green) were observed with incrementally increasing bending angles, alongside stable optical characteristics at fixed deformation states. Finally, we evaluated the potential of multifunctional OWMs devices based on SPC fibers for human motion monitoring. Owing to the intrinsic flexibility, these multifunctional OWMs devices can be attached to the finger joints and maintain stable signal transmission even at a bending degree of 100°, allowing for integration with flexible sensors for wearable applications (Supplementary Fig. 29). As a proof of concept, we integrated the multifunctional OWMs devices into smart wearable textiles for human motion. Body movements generated distinct color patterns in the devices, visible to the naked eye, allowing strain visualization. These patterns, reflecting rich structural colors, effectively indicated motion status. The exercise intensity signals were directly transmitted to the OWMs devices for real-time processing (Fig. 6f). Notably, after resting, CPL intensity signals returned to their normal state, with structural color changes spontaneously reverting within 10 min. The adaptive response to exercise intensity ensured efficient, long-term monitoring of CPL signals, providing timely alerts for motion-related issues. The direct visibility of optical signals allowed the development of a remote wireless communication method leveraging synergistic optical waveguide and CPL signals. For instance, a device attached to a finger was utilized as communication equipment. By applying Morse code principles, we defined small bending (~30°) as “dots” and large bending (~60°) as “dashes”. This allowed information transmission through sequential finger bending. As a demonstration, the message “C P L L C E” was encoded using CPL intensity signals and corresponding structural color changes, enabling intuitive interpretation (Fig. 6g). This study presents a paradigm for secure optical communication by exploiting multiple, interdependent optical dimensions, such as intensity, reflection wavelength (color), and circular polarization signature (glum), for information encoding. This multi-parameter encryption approach, which is difficult to intercept or replicate, offers significantly enhanced protection against eavesdropping. These results show that biomimetic, mechanically tunable CPL-active OWMs have advantages for intelligent and interactive applications.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a multifunctional interactive OWMs fiber that seamlessly integrates smart information processing with dynamic structural color changes, advancing wearable electronics and intelligent human motion monitoring. The OWMs device exhibited adaptability, responding to CPL and optical waveguide signals while displaying vivid, tunable colors across the visible spectrum (632–439 nm) over a wide strain range (0–230%). These signals were detectable by both instruments and human eyes during stretching and releasing cycles, enabling precise strain quantification and visualization. The tunable glum value for CPL (0.153–0) further enhanced its versatility. Real-time monitoring of human movements, including finger, elbow, and knee joint bending, underscored its practicality. These attributes position the OWMs strain sensor as a promising candidate for application in human–machine interface, electronic skin, health monitoring, and beyond.

Characterizations

The 1H NMR spectra were obtained using a Bruker ARX 400 MHz spectrometer, with tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard and dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 as the solvent. The structure of the synthesized mechanochromic molecule Rh-AC was confirmed by ¹H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3). Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization tandem flight time mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) was performed on a Bruker Daltonics BIFLEX Ш MALDI-TOF analyzer. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a PE Spectrum FT-IR spectrometer using powder samples. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) experiments were performed on a TA DSC Q100 calorimeter. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted on a TA SDT 2960 thermogravimetric analyzer under a nitrogen atmosphere at a rate of 20 °C/min. Tensile measurements were carried out on a CREE-8003B tensile testing machine (CREE-8003B, Kerui Instruments, Dongguan, China) according to the ISO 527-2 standard. Tensile fatigue measurements were carried out on DBX-500 tensile testing machine (DBX-500, Guangdong, China). A dumbbell-type sample was measured at a crosshead speed of 2.0 cm/min. The LCE texture of the sample was investigated using a Leica DMLM-P polarized light microscope equipped with a Mettler FP82HT hot stage. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded on a Cary 60 spectrophotometer using a flashing xenon lamp as the light source. Fluorescence lifetime measurements were obtained using a Horiba Deltaflex spectrometer equipped with a microsecond flash-lamp, and the data were analyzed using DAS6 Analysis software. Photoluminescence spectra were recorded on MFS F-4600 spectrometer using a quartz cuvette and right-angle geometry. The absolute fluorescent quantum yields in the solid state were determined using an integrating sphere on a Nanolog FL3-2iHR fluorescence spectroscopy equipped with R928 photomultiplier as detector. X-ray scattering was performed with the Ganesha system (SAXSLAB, U.S.) equipped with a multilayer focused Cu Kα radiation as the X-ray source (Genix3D Cu ULD) and a two-dimensional semiconductor detector (Pilatus 100 K, DECTRIS, Switzerland). Optical waveguide measurements were performed using a 365 nm laser light source (intensity set to 36 mW, CH-FL03) and a CHM2000 Pro CCD spectrometer. Circular polarized luminescent spectra were measured with JASCO CPL-300.
