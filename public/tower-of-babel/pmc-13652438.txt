Introduction

Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome (KTS) is a rare congenital malformation characterized by capillary and venous malformations and overgrowth of soft tissues and bones, with or without lymphatic involvement. The primary clinical hallmarks include capillary malformations, varicose veins, and tissue hypertrophy[1]. Current evidence suggests that KTS results from sporadic gene mutations occurring in utero. The primary genetic marker has been identified as a mutation in the PIK3CA gene, which regulates cell growth and proliferation[2]. Differential diagnosis from Parkes Weber syndrome (PWS) is crucial; unlike KTS, PWS necessarily includes arteriovenous fistulas and is associated with mutations in the RASA1 gene[3]. This study presents a clinical observation of a patient with KTS followed for a period of more than 20 years. During this period, the patient underwent two open surgical procedures and had three normal vaginal deliveries (NVDs). Such a long follow-up period is rare for case reports and provides an opportunity to evaluate the long-term outcomes of surgical interventions, as well as the impact of significant vascular stresses such as pregnancy and childbirth. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines.

Case presentation

Patient: Female, born in 1978. Diagnosis: KTS; congenital venous malformation of the right lower extremity, characterized by predominant deep venous hypoplasia and superficial venous varicosities; grade III chronic venous insufficiency. Pelvic varicosity. Superficial venous varicosities of the left lower extremity. The diagnosis of KTS was established based on the presence of the complete clinical triad and findings from ultrasound examinations. The absence of arteriovenous fistulas allowed us to rule out PWS.

Complaints: The patient reports a dull, aching pain in the right lower extremity occurring even after short periods of standing, sitting, or lying on the right side.

Medical history: Capillary malformations, manifesting as “port-wine stains” on the skin of the right lower leg, thigh, and buttock, have been present since birth. Venous varicosities have progressed since puberty (1992). Slight hypertrophy of the right lower extremity, compared to the left, has been observed.

In 2003, the patient was hospitalized for inpatient treatment, during which simultaneous angiography was performed. A vessel was visualized at the location of the peroneal artery, extending from the distal third of the lower leg to the dorsal surface of the foot, with no contrast opacification observed within its lumen. Phlebography demonstrated that the deep veins and the great saphenous vein (GSV) of the right lower extremity were patent; however, moderate reflux was noted in the deep femoral vein and the femoral vein.

Given the patient’s venous insufficiency and plans for her first pregnancy, it was decided to perform a surgical intervention in 2003 consisting of a combined open varicose vein procedure. This included a Babcock intraluminal wire stripping of the GSV trunk, Narath phlebectomy of the varicose tributaries through micro-incisions, and Cockett subfascial ligation of incompetent perforating veins in the lower leg. The estimated blood loss (EBL) was approximately 100 mL, within expected limits for open phlebectomy. Intraoperative hemostasis was achieved through direct ligation of the venous stumps and temporary packing with compression; the procedure was completed uneventfully.

Following her two pregnancies and NVDs in 2005 and 2007, during which she consistently wore compression stockings on both legs, she nevertheless developed varicose veins of the left lower extremity and experienced a recurrence of varicosities on the right side.

The 2009 duplex ultrasound (DUS) findings revealed the following: the deep veins of both lower extremities were patent, with reflux lasting 0.5 seconds in the popliteal veins. Right side: A history of hemangioma and tortuous varicosities in the inguinal region was noted. The GSV trunk was not visualized in the proximal third, whereas ectasia and valvular insufficiency were present in the middle and distal thirds of the thigh, with associated varicose dilatation. Left side: The GSV trunk demonstrated competent valves in the proximal third of the thigh but was hypoplastic in the middle and distal thirds; venous drainage occurred via varicose subcutaneous tributaries. Bilateral incompetent perforating veins were identified in the lower legs.

Based on DUS findings indicating disease recurrence and valvular insufficiency, surgical intervention was scheduled for the patient; however, it was postponed due to her third pregnancy. In 2010, the patient had her third NVD. Although she wore compression stockings continuously throughout this pregnancy and childbirth, the event was nevertheless associated with further deterioration of the venous system in both extremities.

Consequently, in 2012, the patient underwent an open surgical procedure using the Narath and Cockett techniques. Similar to the first operation, the EBL was approximately 100 mL, and the intraoperative course was completely uneventful, with identical hemostatic methods.

Since that time, the patient has strictly adhered to a compression regimen, consistently wearing an elastic compression stocking (class II, 23–32 mmHg) on the right lower extremity, maintaining regular daily walking (approximately 10 000 steps), and regularly completing semiannual courses of venotonic medications.

In 2019, a DUS examination was performed, revealing valvular insufficiency with prolonged reflux in the popliteal vein, alongside recurrent varicose veins in the right GSV territory without signs of thrombosis. Additionally, insufficiency of the ostial valve and of the valves along the right GSV trunk was noted. In the left lower extremity, ultrasound demonstrated varicose veins in the GSV territory and terminal valve insufficiency.

By 2026, follow-up DUS demonstrated significant clinical progression in both extremities compared with the 2019 findings. In the right lower extremity, pathological reflux had extended proximally to involve the common femoral vein, with the recurrence clearly driven by insufficiency of the GSV stump, an accessory ostium, and tortuous tributaries. In the left lower extremity, marked deterioration was observed as the GSV trunk became significantly dilated, with valvular incompetence confirmed along its entire length.

The patient noted an improvement in her condition following the first surgery. However, three NVDs led to clinical deterioration, and a second surgical intervention failed to provide the expected relief. Due to the high risk of postoperative complications, it was decided to pursue further conservative treatment using compression therapy. This approach allows for the maintenance of a satisfactory clinical status and prevents rapid disease progression. At present, she wears compression stockings not only as prescribed but also because even brief periods without compression cause a sensation of edema and venous congestion in the limb affected by KTS. Discussion

KTS follows a progressive course. Limb imaging reveals unilateral varicose veins, persistent embryonic veins, anomalies of the superficial and deep venous systems, low-flow veno-lymphatic malformations, and hypertrophy of bone and soft tissues[4,5].

Pregnancy in women with KTS is classified as a high-risk condition. The primary threats include thromboembolic events and significant obstetric hemorrhage, particularly in the presence of pelvic or vaginal varicosities[6]. However, the incidence of fetal growth restriction and fetal malpresentation in women with KTS is similar to that of the general population[7].

Management of KTS is predominantly conservative, as no definitive cure exists. The use of compression stockings on the affected limb remains the cornerstone of management[1,8–10]. When combined with regular low-impact exercise such as walking, elastic compression synergistically activates the calf muscle pump. As demonstrated by hemodynamic studies, ambulation under graduated compression significantly enhances venous return, reduces residual volume fraction, and lowers ambulatory venous hypertension, thereby preventing severe blood pooling and microvascular damage in the malformed veins[10,11]. When necessary, minimally invasive procedures, such as foam sclerotherapy, radiofrequency ablation, or vein embolization, may be performed. Therefore, open surgical interventions, which were widely utilized as recently as 20 years ago, are now reserved only for patients who are not suitable candidates for minimally invasive procedures[1,12,13].

Conclusion

The clinical case presented herein demonstrates that open surgical interventions in KTS can cause recurrences. Long-term conservative management, utilizing elastic compression therapy combined with consistent daily walking and regular courses of venotonics, may restrain disease progression in the absence of major provoking factors such as pregnancy and childbirth.
