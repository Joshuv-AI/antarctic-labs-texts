Interstitial lung disease in metastatic breast cancer: a landscape review using SEER-Medicare
Authors: Laura C. Pinheiro, Anjile An, Chrystal A. Landry, Emily Tonorezos, Kerri I. Aronson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

In the United States, there are 170,000 adults living with metastatic breast cancer (mBC) [1]. Given the incurable prognosis for a mBC diagnosis, treatment regimens involve delaying progression, reducing symptom burden, optimizing health-related quality of life (HRQOL), and extending overall survival. There are a wide array of systemic treatment options for mBC including chemotherapy, hormone therapy, targeted therapy, and, more recently, immunotherapy and antibody-drug conjugates. Based on tumor characteristics such as hormone-receptor and HER-2 status, patients often begin a specific treatment regimen and remain on that treatment until the cancer progresses or unacceptable toxicity develops, at which point an alternative regimen is chosen.

An important goal of mBC treatments is to balance the clinical outcomes of progression-free survival and overall survival with symptom burden and quality of life. Interstitial lung disease (ILD), manifested as inflammation and/or scarring of the lung parenchyma, is a serious side effect of certain mBC treatments, including most targeted therapies [2]. Patients prescribed these treatments who develop symptomatic ILD typically present with dyspnea and cough [3]. While symptoms may be mild, some patients present with rapidly progressive respiratory failure. Clinical imaging, including the pattern seen on imaging, along with a supportive temporal history of drug exposure will provide support of the diagnosis of ILD [3, 4], which is often confirmed once other etiologies such as infection, pulmonary edema, progressive cancer, or alternative etiologies of ILD are ruled out [5]. The development of ILD can have profound deleterious effects on a person’s HRQOL. Studies outside of mBC have shown that adults with ILD experience not only the physical symptoms of dyspnea, cough, and fatigue but also contend with reduced physical functioning and negative impacts on the psychological and social domains of HRQOL [6, 7].

Over the last decade, there has been increasing use of targeted therapies for mBC resulting in significant improvements seen in both progression-free and overall survival. Oral targeted CDK-4/6 inhibitors (ribociclib, abemaciclib, palbociclib) are now used routinely in both advanced hormone receptor-positive metastatic and high-risk, early-stage breast cancer. PI3-kinase inhibitors (capivasertib, alpelisib) and mTOR inhibitors like everolimus are used in later lines of mBC. Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy with pembrolizumab is recommended in first-line treatment of PDL-1 positive triple negative mBC. More recently, antibody-drug conjugates have changed the landscape of early lines of mBC treatment [8]. Trastuzumab emtansine was the first antibody-drug conjugate approved for mBC treatment in 2013, and newer Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan-nxki is highly efficacious for both HER2-positive and HER2-low mBC. Sacituzumab-govitecan-hziy and even more recently datopotamab deruxtecan-dlnk are used to treat both hormone receptor positive and triple negative mBC. Some of these therapies carry small, but-real risks of ILD [6, 7, 9]. To date, few studies have reported on the incidence and drivers of ILD among mBC patients, especially outside of clinical trials [10]. Among the studies that have reported on ILD in mBC, all have focused on individuals receiving HER2 + targeted treatments [2, 11]. As such, the objective of the current study was to characterize the incidence of ILD among a large, population-based cohort of people with mBC diagnosed between 2002 and 2017, insured by Medicare fee-for-service through 2018, and who received any cancer-directed treatment.

Methods

Dataset

We used national cancer registry from SEER linked to Medicare fee-for-service claims (SEER-Medicare) [12] for beneficiaries aged 65 + years of age who were diagnosed in a SEER registry region. SEER includes cancer cases diagnosed across 26 registries from 2002 to 2017 [13]. Medicare Parts A, B, and D claims were available through 2018. This study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board at Weill Cornell Medicine.

Inclusion

We included females and males aged 66 + years who were diagnosed with Stage IV breast cancer (as their first cancer) between January 1, 2002-December 31, 2017. As in other SEER-Medicare analyses, sixty-six years of age was chosen so that all individuals had continuous Medicare Part A and B coverage for 12 months before diagnosis in order to capture comorbid conditions, including a previous diagnosis of ILD. The sample was also limited to those with continuous Medicare Part A and B coverage for at least 6 months after mBC diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria

Medicare beneficiaries: (1) diagnosed with cancer from an autopsy or death certificate, (2) with a prior history of cancer, or (3) with a history of ILD before mBC diagnosis (codes listed in Supplemental Table 1), and (4) who died within 180 days of their mBC diagnosis.

Primary outcome

An incident ILD diagnosis after mBC diagnosis among older adults without evidence of ILD in the 12-months before their mBC diagnosis date. To identify an incident ILD diagnosis, we used codes shown in Supplemental Table 2. The table of codes was developed from those employed in prior Medicare claims analyses for ILD to be consistent with existing literature [14], including (1) those that describe potential ILD, (2) a non-etiology specific ILD (e.g. not idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or connective tissue disease related ILD) or (3) codes that included typical imaging features seen in ILD and pneumonitis. The codes used for this definition (in comparison) to the exclusionary codes listed in Supplemental Table 1, were deemed more specific. This decision was made to help limit to ILD cases on imaging rather than other lung-related side effects or non-specific imaging abnormalities [14]. Our primary outcome was operationalized as binary outcome (yes/no).

Covariates

We examined individual-level characteristics from SEER including age at mBC diagnosis in years, sex, a composite of race and ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic/Latinx, Non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander, Non-Hispanic Other), and clinical characteristics (breast sub-type, hormone-receptor status). From the Medicare fee-for-service files, we included dual eligibility for Medicaid as an indicator of socio-economic status, Charlson Comorbidity Score (0, 1, 2+), receiving a pharmacologic treatment that carries risk of ILD (Supplemental Table 3) receipt of surgery (yes/no), receipt of hormone therapy (yes/no), receipt of chemotherapy (yes/no), and receipt of radiation therapy (yes/no). Treatments were treated as fixed variables with at least one Medicare fee-for-service claim indicating receipt.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study sample with respect to demographic and clinical factors of interest. Continuous variables are represented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables are represented as n (%). Where appropriate, the Chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to examine the association between demographic/clinical factors of interest and ILD diagnosis (yes/no). Clinically relevant variables were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to evaluate independent effect on ILD diagnosis based on observed differences in Table 1. We also adjusted for calendar year (2002–2017) to capture differences in treatment availability and prescribing patterns over these 15. years. Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated from the multivariable model. All p-values are two-sided with statistical significance evaluated at the 0.05 alpha level. All analyses were performed in R Version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Cohort characteristics

Our exclusion cascade is presented in Fig. 1. Among a cohort of 14,343 adults diagnosed with mBC between 2002 and 2017 who had no evidence of ILD in the year prior to mBC diagnosis, the median age at diagnosis was 75 (IQR 70, 82) years, 98% were female, 6% were Hispanic/Latino, 3.6% were Non-Hispanic Asian, 10% Non-Hispanic Black, 80% Non-Hispanic White, and < 1% were ‘other’ race. Overall, 1,968 (13.7%) received a treatment that carries risk for ILD; the median time from diagnosis to receiving that treatment was 72 (IQR 40–162) days (Table 1).

ILD diagnosis after cancer

Among the 14,343 patients with mBC and no evidence of ILD at baseline, 1,806 (12.6%) had an ILD diagnosis after their mBC diagnosis. Figure 2 shows the temporal trends in ILD diagnosis between 2002 and 2017 (Fig. 2). There was no significant difference in proportion of patients who received a pharmacologic treatment that carries the risk of ILD between patients who received an ILD diagnosis and those who did not (14% vs. 12%). Individuals with an ILD diagnosis were more likely to receive hormone therapy (28% vs. 20%), chemotherapy (56% vs. 44%), radiation (63% vs. 55%) and surgery (70% vs. 58%) compared to individuals without a diagnosis of ILD (all p < 0.001). Those with an ILD diagnosis also had a greater comorbidity burden with 29% have 2 + comorbidities compared to 25% among the group without an ILD diagnosis (p < 0.001).

Predictors of receiving an ILD diagnosis

Race and ethnicity were independently associated with an incident ILD diagnosis after adjustment for age, Medicaid eligibility (an indicator of socio-economic status), Charlson comorbidity score, receipt of ILD-associated treatment, chemotherapy, and radiation. In these fully adjusted models, Hispanic/Latino and Non-Hispanic Black patients had lower odds of an incident ILD diagnosis compared to Non-Hispanic White patients (aOR 0.74; 95% CI 0.58–0.95 and aOR 0.78; 95% CI 0.64–0.94, respectively). Individuals who were eligibility for Medicaid (indicating lower socio-economic) status were more likely to be diagnosed with ILD (aOR 1.15; 95% CI 1.02–1.26). Greater comorbidity burden was significantly associated with having an ILD diagnosis, with those having 2 + comorbidities having 61% (95% CI 1.41–1.84) higher odds of ILD diagnosis compared to individuals without any comorbidities. Receipt of chemotherapy (aOR 1.28; 95% CI 1.13–1.46) or radiation therapy (aOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.11–1.41), compared to no chemotherapy or radiation therapy, was also independently associated with increased odds of ILD diagnosis (Table 2).

Discussion

In this SEER-Medicare cohort of more than 14,000 older adults living with mBC in the US, 12.6% had an incident ILD diagnosis in the 6 months following their mBC diagnosis. Of note, we did not observe a significant difference in an incident ILD diagnosis (after mBC diagnosis) between adults who received therapies that carry the risk of ILD or not. Factors associated with an incident ILD diagnosis included Non-Hispanic White race, greater comorbidity burden, and receipt of chemotherapy or radiation.

Incidence of ILD appears to be a concern beyond any specific treatment regimen, as our 12% incidence reflects a group of patients receiving a variety of different cancer therapies. A 2022 study of people with mBC receiving HER2 directed therapies reported a 9% incidence of ILD, but this study included a sample where 16% had pre-existing ILD at the time of their cancer diagnosis [11]. Our estimate is within the wide range of 2.4–21.4% reported in a systematic review of 18 studies of anti-HER2 therapies but reflects a sample that is not limited to individuals receiving HER2 + therapy [2].

Prior studies have indicated that incident ILD among mBC patients is often associated with treatment dose interruptions, reductions, and discontinuations [2], which can limit the treatment’s ability to control tumor progression [15]. These complications can also limit future available treatment options for patients. To date, only two anti-HER2 treatment protocols (i.e., trastuzumab deruxtecan and trastuzumab combined with paclitaxel and either everolimus or placebo) [16] provide specific ILD management guidelines; however, we found that ILD among people with mBC is significantly more common than previously described. This suggests that broader, treatment-agnostic ILD management guidelines may be warranted, though the development and validation of such guidelines will require dedicated prospective study.

According to NCCN clinical management guidelines [17], all cancer patients should be evaluated for pre-existing lung conditions before initiating high-risk therapies. Guidelines also recommend “regular monitoring” for ILD mBC patients, but no additional details regarding frequency or modality of follow-up are specified. While our study was not designed to evaluate screening strategies or early detection programs, our findings demonstrate that ILD is more common in this population than previously recognized. This underscores the need for future research in the area. Specifically, prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether diagnostic approaches such as pulmonary function testing or dedicated chest imaging could facilitate earlier ILD identification and, ultimately improve patient outcomes in the mBC setting. There have been efforts at individual academic institutions to develop monitoring and screening protocols for specific pharmaceutical agents in mBC treatment [18, 19]. Multi-disciplinary, cross-institutional collaboration between oncology, pulmonology, and primary care at the national level may be necessary to develop more broad and uniformly applicable guidelines and recommendations.

If ILD is identified and addressed early on, there is the potential to minimize treatment disruptions and reduce symptom burden. The potential clinical value of early ILD detection is supported by analogous approaches already adopted in oncology. For example, patients on anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody therapy are required to undergo echocardiogram monitoring every 3 months while on treatment. Rates of cardiac toxicity observed in real-world studies of patients on monotherapy with trastuzumab are reported at 7.6%, with most consisting of asymptomatic drops in left ventricular function and recovery frequently observed with treatment pause [20]. Whether a similarly structured approach could be applied to ILD surveillance in mBC patients remains and important and unanswered question. Guidelines highlighting multidisciplinary collaboration and early identification of ILD are currently being explored other fields such as rheumatology and pulmonology, where there is an increased risk of development of ILD with systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease [21, 22].

Limitations

We included stage IV breast cancer patients aged 66 + years old with continuous Part A and B Medicare enrollment for 12 months before and 6 months after diagnosis, potentially limiting our generalizability to younger mBC adults and those with other insurance types. The cohort was nearly all female, limiting our ability to generalize to males. Additionally, although we excluded individuals with an ILD diagnosis in the year before their mBC diagnosis, it is possible that individuals had undiagnosed ILD. We do not have access to radiographic imaging to make this determination. Given that our ILD diagnosis was identified in claims, we were unable to examine specific ILD subtypes or patterns of ILD. Additionally, we are unable to account for unmeasured confounders that may potentially affect ILD treatment selection such as smoking history, underlying lung disease, pulmonary metastases, prior thoracic radiation, and performance status. Our evaluation period of January 1, 2002-December 31, 2017, does not include patients treated with newer targeted therapies; for example, trastuzumab deruxtecan was first FDA approved for mBC treatment in December 2019. Therefore, ILD rates reported here surely underestimate current rates seen in clinical practice. HER2 status was not available in SEER Medicare until 2010, so individuals diagnosed between 2002 and 2009 have missing values for HER2 status. Finally, our treatment covariates were fixed (yes/no) and not considered as time-varying covariates.

Conclusion

In this large cohort of older adults living with mBC using SEER-Medicare data, 12.6% were found to have incident ILD diagnosis in the 6 months following mBC diagnosis. With the development of more novel targeted therapies over the last decade and their use in later lines of therapy for mBC, ILD is likely more pervasive than these numbers reflect. Newer antibody-drug conjugates and immune checkpoint inhibitors are being used more broadly and in earlier lines of therapy in recent years, with fatal cases of ILD reported in phase I/II studies.23 In addition to the obvious clinical significance of these therapy-related fatalities, an ILD diagnosis and associated complications can significantly limit future therapeutic options in mBC treatment and also be life-limiting. If ILD is identified and addressed early on, there is the potential to minimize treatment disruptions and reduce symptom burden. Oncology guidelines for monitoring for this highly intrusive and potentially fatal complication are sparse and non-specific. In clinical trials, high-resolution CT monitoring at baseline and every 6 weeks has been employed and can be considered along with use of pulmonary function testing and oxygen saturation monitoring at routine visits. Patient education regarding heightened awareness of symptoms of ILD and engaging our colleagues in primary care and pulmonary medicine in both multidisciplinary ongoing clinical care and more broad guideline creation will improve patient outcomes and quality of life.
