Enhanced mechanical and thermal properties of polyester/glass/sheep wool fiber hybrid composites by successful replacement glass with wool
Authors: Mohsen Sadroddini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to examine the possibility of partially substituting glass fiber (GF) with sheep wool fiber (SWF) with the goal of developing environmentally sustainable hybrid polyester composites. This study investigated the mechanical properties (tensile, flexural, and impact), thermal properties, water absorption, and microstructures of hybrid composites. The targeted composites were fabricated via compression molding and contained a total fiber weight fraction of 10% (with varying proportions of GF and SWF). Initially, the surface chemistry, morphology, tensile properties, and thermal stability of SWFs were analyzed individually. In the case of the composites, the highest tensile strength was recorded for the 9GF1SWF (comprising 9 and 1 wt% GF and SWF, respectively) hybrid composite, which reached 14.09 MPa (7.31% higher than 10GF0SWF). The 9GF1SWF sample also presented the highest amount of absorbed flexural energy prior to failure, 166 MJ/m3 (19.42% higher than 10GF0SWF), and the highest impact strength, 11.15 kJ/m2 (11.05% higher than 10GF0SWF). The TGA results revealed that the incorporation of SWF reduced the rate of thermal degradation as well as improved thermal stability of the hybrid composites, especially at high temperatures. The microstructural characteristics of the composites and their correlation with the mechanical properties, along with the various reinforcing mechanisms, were examined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In terms of water absorption, both the hybrid and polyester/glass composites exhibited comparable behavior, with similar absorption levels. In brief, the findings of this study suggest that sheep wool fibers may serve as a viable alternative to synthetic glass fibers.

Introduction

In recent years, glass fiber-reinforced polymer (GFRP) composites have increasingly replaced metals, primarily because of their suitable mechanical properties, light weight, and cost-effectiveness. These composites are used in various industries, such as automotive, aerospace, transportation, construction, marine, and wind turbines. In this regard, the predominant thermoset resin matrices are polyester and epoxy, whereas the most commonly employed fiber is glass fiber1. Despite their superior mechanical properties, glass fibers present several environmental challenges2,3. Its spinning process demands high energy consumption and temperatures (up to 1400 °C)4. Additionally, the release of glass fibers into the air, water, and soil and ultimately into the human and animal bodies and respiratory systems of living organisms leads to significant health hazards5. Hence, replacing glass fibers in GFRP composites with natural fibers for the purpose of developing biocomposites is valuable6.

Moreover, natural fiber-reinforced composites (NFRCs) have emerged as promising solutions owing to their three significant attributes of cost effectiveness, reduced weight, and minimal environmental impact7,8. Their lighter weight can contribute to a sustainable economy by reducing fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions and, ultimately, decreasing reliance on petroleum products9,10. Plant fibers such as jute, sisal, kenaf, flax, and lotus fibers have been extensively researched11–13. However, one significant drawback of NFRCs is their relatively low mechanical properties14. One promising approach to enhance their mechanical attributes is fiber hybridization15. Hybridizing natural and synthetic fibers can provide synergistic properties, effectively addressing the drawbacks associated with using only one type of fiber16,17. The majority of research conducted thus far has focused on hybridizing synthetic and plant fibers18,19, whereas studies focusing on animal fibers remain limited.

Sheep wool fiber (SWF) is highly important among all animal fibers because of its unique properties, such as good insulation properties, high flame resistance, low density, and biocompatibility20,21. Sheep wool fibers contain five main elements: carbon (50%), oxygen (22–25%), hydrogen (7%), nitrogen (16–17%), and sulfur (3–4%). It has a tensile strength of 160–390 MPa, elongation at break of 25–35%, and a Young’s modulus of 2.3–3.4 GPa22. Annually, a significant quantity of sheep wool fibers, particularly those with black and dark brown colors that are unsuitable for textile applications, are disposed or burned, potentially leading to environmental concerns. Burning them can cause air pollution and the remaining ash can be problematic. Furthermore, their disposal would result in the loss of a valuable natural reinforcing fiber that could have many potential applications, particularly in polymer composites and building materials23.

Straznicky et al.24. investigated the impact of adding 3 vol% sheep wool fiber to thermosetting epoxy and polyurethane matrices. Their results revealed that the addition of more wool fibers results in additional cost savings without affecting the mechanical properties. In the research conducted by Manivannan et al.25., the tensile strength of polyester was increased by adding 20%, 30%, and 40 weight fractions of wool fibers. Interestingly, the tensile and flexural properties of the composite containing 30% wool were the lowest, which was attributed to the presence of voids and weak interactions. This contradiction seems slightly puzzling. In addition, the highest flexural and impact strengths were achieved in the composites with 40% and 30% wool fibers, respectively. The reason for this increase was attributed to better fiber‒matrix interfacial adhesion.

To the best of our knowledge, few studies have investigated the use of sheep wool fibers combined with synthetic fibers, specifically glass fibers. Bharath et al.26. investigated the influence of a stacking sequence of biomass sheep wool fibers and woven glass fibers on the physical and chemical properties of reinforced epoxy. The results showed that placing 21 layers of glass fabric (30%) as the composite core between 2 layers of wool fibers (30%) as the composite skin resulted in better mechanical and chemical properties. Ogaili et al.27. examined the mechanical, thermal and electrical properties of epoxy reinforced with woven glass fibers and sheep wool fibers. The hybrid composite (25% glass + 25% wool, in volume fraction) was compared with the composite containing glass (50%) and the composite containing wool (50%). The mechanical properties of the hybrid composite were intermediate between those of the other two composites. Abede et al.28. studied the mechanical and water absorption properties of polyester hybrid composites reinforced with sheep wool fabric (20%) and E-glass fiber mat sheets (20%). Their results indicated improved tensile, flexural, and impact properties of the hybrid composites, as well as water absorption of less than 1%.

This study investigated the effects of hybridization of sheep wool fiber/glass fiber on the mechanical and thermal properties of reinforced hybrid polyester composites. Its purpose is the possibility of substituting a portion of glass fibers with sheep wool fibers in the abovementioned composites. The novelty of the present study can be summarized as follows: First, very few studies have been conducted on glass fiber/sheep wool fiber hybridization. Second, in the present study, the total percentage of fibers was kept constant, whereas similar studies have mainly kept the percentage of synthetic fibers constant and added natural fibers in varying amounts26–28. Third, we tried to use wool fibers without chemical modification to eliminate the complexities of the modification process in terms of cost, time, and labor. This leads to ease of manufacturing of hybrid composites, which is an important advantage. Overall, despite the weaker properties of wool fibers than glass, the improved mechanical and thermal performance of hybrid composites was obtained. This indicates a successful replacement of glass fibers with sheep wool fibers. In this regard, hybrid polyester composites containing different amounts of GF and SWF were fabricated via hot compression molding. The mechanical and thermal properties of the composites were measured via tensile, flexural, impact, and thermogravimetry tests. In brief, the findings suggest the beneficial role of sheep wool fiber as an effective replacement for glass fiber, resulting in superior mechanical and thermal properties compared to composites composed solely of glass fibers. Notably, this outcome was attained despite the fact that the properties of sheep wool fiber are significantly inferior to those of glass fibers. These underscore the advantageous impact of successful hybridization facilitated by the inclusion of sheep wool fiber.

Materials

An ortho-type unsaturated polyester resin (UPE) designed for general-purpose applications under the trade name RESITAN UP-747/7 was purchased. An E-glass fiber (GF) chopped strand was obtained with the trade name YN-CSBMC6 from JUSHI Company. Clean and washed sheep wool fibers (SWFs), which are free of dust and contaminants, were prepared from local areas in/around West Azerbaijan province, Iran. The collected SWFs were long fibers that were then cut to the desired length. The physical properties of the fibers used are presented in Table 1.

Preparation method

The first step was to determine the required quantities of both glass and sheep wool fibers for the fabrication of composites. The basis was a composite made up of 10% (by weight% or wt%) GF. Then, SWF was added to replace some of the GF to create hybrid composites. The relative amounts of the fibers in the composites and their designations are given in Table 2. With respect to the wt% SWF, the values of 1, 2 and 3% are apparently low. However, owing to the very low density of wool, the volume occupied by wool is much greater than that occupied by glass fibers. Therefore, the use of higher amounts of SWF will lead to a sharp decrease in matrix-fiber wetting.

Figure 1 shows the fabrication of the composites in a schematic manner. First, the polyester resin was mixed with the accelerator (1 g of cobalt naphthenate 1% per 100 g of resin). The required amounts of fibers were subsequently added to the resin and thoroughly mixed. Next, the curing agent (1.5 g of 50% MEKP per 100 g of resin) was incorporated. Owing to the exothermic nature of the curing reaction combined with the mixing process, numerous bubbles formed. If bubbles are not eliminated from the mixture, they can adversely affect the mechanical properties of the final composites. The composite samples after mechanical testing are displayed in Fig. 2. All the composites were manufactured via the conventional hot compression molding process, wherein the temperature was maintained at 150 °C for 30 min while a compressive force of 25 metric tons was used. To ensure the complete curing of the polyester resin, the post-curing process was completed at a temperature of 120 °C for 2 h in an oven.

Results and discussion

Properties of sheep wool fiber

TGA

By employing TGA, the thermal stability of sheep wool fibers was investigated. Figure 4 represents the TGA and DTGA of the sheep wool fiber. As depicted, the heating process resulted in three stages of weight loss (or thermal degradation). Several research groups have documented three-stage weight loss in wool fibers from sheep, goat, and horse25,29. The first stage of thermal degradation is identified in the temperature range of 34–138 °C, with a weight loss of approximately 5.3%. This is mostly ascribed to the elimination of water present in the wool fibers. Research has identified three types of water, namely, free water, chemically bonded water, and loosely bonded water30. The second and most important thermal degradation stage occurred within the temperature range of 220–450 °C and was characterized by a substantial weight loss of 53.6%. This is attributable to the breakdown of the microfibril structure and molecular chains of wool fibers, primarily due to the cessation of disulfide and keratin bonds29. The third stage of degradation, albeit minor, transpired within the temperature range of 620–630 °C, which may be linked to the degradation of the peptide side chains of the wool fibers31. The TGA results revealed that the thermal stability of the sheep wool fiber was preserved up to 220 °C. The thermal degradations mentioned earlier are also represented by three peaks in the DTGA at temperatures of 59 °C, 325 °C, and 630 °C.

Morphology

The surface morphology of the sheep wool fibers was examined by scanning electron microscopy. Figure 5 presents SEM images of the sheep wool fibers. Employing the “ImageJ” image processing program, the diameter of the fibers was determined to range from 15 to 50 microns, with a mean diameter of approximately 26 microns. Measurements were conducted across various sections of numerous fibers to ensure enhanced precision. The diameter of sheep wool fiber may vary with several influencing factors, including the breed of sheep, the season of fiber picking, and the diet of the sheep25,32. As illustrated in Fig. 5b, sheep wool fibers possess a rough and irregular surface that resembles fish scales (known as a scale-like morphology). This unique morphology has also been documented by other researchers33,34. These irregular surfaces may facilitate the formation of a more robust and intimate interface with the polymer matrix and adjacent fibers, thereby enhancing the mechanical properties of the hybrid composites.

Tensile properties

Figure 3b displays the tensile stress‒strain curve for a single sheep wool fiber. The overall tensile behavior of the SWF is comparable to that of plastically deformed materials, and it has an initial linear curve that is well known as Hookean behavior35. The elastic modulus can be calculated from the linear slope of this area. Changes in the bond angle result in ‘Hookean’ deformation. Further stretching of the fibers leads to the yield region being reached, which is also accompanied by an increase in strength. The region mostly originates from the beginning of the transition from the α- to the extended chain β-lattice in wool microfibrils36. Further stretching of the fiber reduces its diameter, which in turn compacts the microfibril structure within the fiber. Consequently, strain hardening behavior occurs, and ultimately, fiber rupture occurs. The average tensile properties of the SWF are given in Table 3. The results obtained in the present study are in good agreement with the values (tensile modulus and strength are 4.8 ± 0.7 GPa and 160.9 ± 23 MPa, respectively, with 27.7% elongation at break) ​​reported by Das et al.22.

Tensile properties are considered among the most important mechanical properties of materials in the elastic mode. Figure 7a shows the tensile stress‒strain curves of the composites. All the composites exhibit linear elastic behavior except for the 7GF3SWF composite, which consists of a combination of linear and nonlinear behavior. This may be attributed to various factors, such as the higher wool fiber content, the more ductile nature of wool fibers than glass fibers, the lower inherent strength of wool fibers, and the reduced mechanical interlocking between wool and glass fibers. This will be discussed further below.

Figure 7b presents the tensile strength and tensile modulus of the composites. The 9GF1SWF composite has the highest tensile strength, which is 7.31% greater than that of the 10GF0SWF composite. This clearly reveals that wool fibers may be a suitable replacement for glass fibers. Owing to the scale-like structure of sheep wool fiber (see Fig. 5), its mechanical interlocking and physical engagement with adjacent wool and glass fibers and matrix. In addition, the proper bonding between the matrix/wool fibers is also a constructive factor. All three mechanisms are well supported by SEM images (see Fig. 11). may play a constructive role in improving the tensile strength of hybrid composites. This phenomenon has also been observed in hybrid composites of epoxy/jute/hemp and natural rubber/sisal/palm that help transfer stress between fibers38,39. In hybrid biocomposites reinforced with wool fibers, biochar particles, and ammonium polyphosphate (APP), Das et al. reported that mechanical interlocking between the pores on the surface of biochar particles and the polypropylene matrix was essential for improving properties. With increasing wool fiber content in the 7GF3SWF composite, the tensile strength decreases. This may be due to two reasons: first, the inherent mechanical strength of sheep wool fibers is lower than that of glass fibers, and second, the high content of sheep wool fibers (since their density is very low, their volume fraction in the composite is very high), which results in insufficient resin to wet the fiber surface and voids22,40. In other words, there was an optimal amount of wool fiber at which the mechanical properties were optimized. This phenomenon has been reported in polyester/date palm/sheep wool fibers and polyester/jute/hemp hybrid composites. The mechanical properties of the composites decreased as the quantity of wool fibers exceeded a certain threshold38,41. Notably, the insufficient wetting of the SWF surface is due to the high content of SWF fibers and not necessarily to poor polymer‒fiber interface interactions. This is evidenced by the tensile strengths of the 9GF1SWF and 8GF2SWF composites, which were almost equal to or greater than that of the 10GF0SWF composite.

The tensile modulus of the composites was calculated via the slope of the stress‒strain curve in the linear elastic region. With increasing wool fiber content, the tensile modulus of the composites gradually decreases. The tensile modulus of the 9GF1SWF and 8GF2SWF composites are 15.21% and 22.28% lower than that of the 10GF0SWF composite, respectively. The 7GF3SWF composite has the lowest tensile modulus. The tensile modulus of wool fibers (approximately 3.47 GPa) is lower than that of glass fibers (approximately 72 GPa). In contrast, the 9GF1SWF composite still demonstrates satisfactory performance and can be employed in the same manner as glass fiber-reinforced composites.

Flexural properties

Figure 8a presents the flexural force‒extension curves of the composites. All the composites are broken without yielding. Compared with the hybrid composites, the 10GF0SWF composite broke earlier in terms of strain at break but had the highest flexural strength and modulus.

Figure 8b shows the flexural strength and flexural modulus of the composites. The 10GF0SWF sample has the highest flexural strength. The flexural strengths of the 9GF1SWF, 8GF2SWF and 7GF3SWF composites are 15.71%, 17.92%, and 16% lower than that of the 10GF0SWF composite, respectively. This may be attributed to the lower inherent flexural strength of sheep wool fibers than glass fibers42. The higher flexural flexibility of wool fiber than glass fiber is also an effective factor in reducing the flexural strength of composites38,43. With respect to the flexural modulus of the composite, the lower inherent modulus of wool fibers than glass fibers are the main reason why hybrid composites possess a reduced flexural modulus. Notably, our results are in agreement with the results obtained by Ogaili et al. in an epoxy/glass fiber/wool fiber hybrid composite, as well as in polyester/jute and glass fabric composites27.

The maximum absorbed flexural and tensile energy, which is defined as the energy needed to cause fracture in a sample, is an important factor in evaluating fiber-reinforced composites. It is derived from the area under the flexural and tensile stress‒strain curves. Table 5 shows the absorbed flexural and tensile energy of the composites. All the hybrid composites presented greater absorption of flexural energy than did 10GF0SWF; 9GF1SWF (19.42%), 8GF2SWF (6.47%), and 7GF3SWF (3.59%). The greater flexibility of sheep wool fibers and their scale-like structure may be responsible for this, which requires greater flexural energy. In the bending test, the inner surface of the sample is under compression, and the outer side is under tension44. Owing to their high flexibility and rough surface, sheep wool fibers under compressive loading can be more entangled. This leads to improved absorption of flexural energy. The decrease in energy absorbed with increasing wool fiber loading may be explained by SWF agglomeration, as well as the incomplete wetting of the fiber surface with the polymer matrix38,45. The 10GF0SWF and 9GF1SWF composites perform equally in terms of absorbed tensile energy, whereas the other two hybrid composites exhibit lower performance. In tensile mode, it appears that the physical interlocking of wool and glass fibers, which play a significant role in the flexural mode, weakens some of its constructive effect. Therefore, we found a decrease in the absorbed tensile energy, especially at higher wool fiber loadings.

Impact properties

Impact tests are widely used to determine the energy required to fracture a material under dynamic stress and are highly important for fiber-reinforced composites. Figure 9 shows the impact energy and impact strength of the composites. The changes in the impact energy and impact strength of the composites followed the same trend. Interestingly, the impact strength of some hybrid composites is greater than that of 10GF0WF. This clearly reveals the effective role of hybridization with sheep wool fibers. This may be due to several reasons. First, the greater brittleness of the glass fiber and 10GF0SWF composites than wool fiber and hybrid composites (see Fig. 7) results in a lower impact strength. Other studies have also explicitly stated that this is the reason for the low impact strength of hybrid composites40,46. Second, sheep wool fiber has greater flexibility than glass fiber. The greater the flexibility is, the greater the impact strength. Third, the rough surface of sheep wool fibers leads to improved physical entanglement between adjacent fibers and polymers, as well as between wool and glass fibers. This enhances the impact strength through energy absorption at the interface of entangled fibers. Abdellah et al. attributed the increase in the impact strength of polyester/date palm fiber/sheep wool fiber hybrid composites to the interfacial interlocking between date palm and sheep wool fibers41. However, if there is proper impregnation of fibers in the matrix, a higher wool fiber content will result in a greater fiber/matrix interface area. Thus, higher fiber loading leads to greater and more effective bearing of the impact load.

Microstructure of the composites

The microstructural characteristics of the hybrid composites after failure were examined by scanning electron microscopy, and their corresponding micrographs are shown in Fig. 11. It is clear from the images that the GF and SWF are uniformly distributed throughout the matrix. Various phenomena can be discerned within the images, including mechanical interlocking of fibers, fiber pull-out, fiber fracture, fiber/matrix bonding, and fiber agglomeration. All of the above mechanisms are involved in all hybrid composites and consequently affect their mechanical performance. This contribution varies in different composites, being stronger in one and weaker in another. Mechanical interlocking is a very important mechanism that influences mechanical strength44,52, with SEM images corroborating the findings of mechanical evaluations, particularly tensile strength and impact strength in 9GF1SWF. While in the 7GF3SWF composite, due to the high number of wool fibers, the physical interlocking of wool with glass, which is considered a strengthening mechanism, is decreasing. In other words, the wool fibers are more interlocked with themselves than with the stronger glass fiber. Additionally, the presence of voids and fiber agglomeration especially in the 7GF3SWF composite, have a detrimental effect on the mechanical properties53. The presence of voids may be attributed primarily to two factors: first, the entrapment of air during the composite fabrication process, and second, the formation of cracks within the matrix38. At lower amounts of wool fibers (9GF1SWF), a good distribution of them within the matrix is ​​observed. The fibers are well covered by the matrix and good bonding is observed. Therefore, the contribution of a mechanism such as fiber breakage is greater here. While at higher amounts of wool (7GF3SWF), there is not enough resin to wet the fiber surface, so fiber agglomeration is observed in the microscopic images. This has led to a decrease in mechanical properties. An additional mechanism observed is fiber pull-out, which invariably results in a loss of mechanical energy but may also signify weak matrix/fiber interactions46. In addition, the micrographs clearly demonstrate that fiber fracture is a significant failure mode. Without the fibers being pulled out, the wool fibers fail because their surface is well bonded and covered in the matrix. Conversely, fibers have achieved their maximum load-bearing capacity, which has resulted in an enhancement of the mechanical properties of the composites. This mechanism has also been described by other researchers in composites reinforced with polyester/wool fibers54, epoxy/coir fibers55, and polyester/jute/hemp fibers38.

Water absorption

Figure 12 displays the water absorption of the composites. It is observed that water absorption gradually increases with increasing amount of sheep wool fibers. The 7GF3SWF composite has the highest degree of water absorption, and both the 9GF1SWF and 8GF2SWF hybrid composites display equal performance and are closely aligned with the 10GF0WF composite. Various mechanisms have been proposed for water absorption in fiber-reinforced composites, including (a) formation of micro-cracks due to fiber swelling, (b) penetration and capillary flow of water molecules through the microcracks, and (c) de-bonding of the fiber-matrix interface56,57. Here, the increase in water absorption with increasing wool fibers may be attributed to the following factors: (a) presence of abundant polar hydrophilic functional groups on SWF surface (corresponding to FTIR result)44, (b) voids due to microcracks (corresponding to Fig. 11), (c) entrapment of water molecules in the porous and rough surface of sheep wool fibers (corresponding to Fig. 5). It is noteworthy that the above-mentioned mechanisms are intensified with increasing wool fibers. Accordingly, water absorption follows an upward trend with the gradual addition of wool fibers. But nevertheless, the water absorption values of these composites are acceptable and even lower than those reported in other studies55,58.

Comparative brief discussion of sustainability aspects

A quantitative study of this issue requires further investigation. But in qualitative comparison, from raw material extraction to the factory gate, producing 1 tonne of glass fiber fabric in Europe generates, on average, greenhouse gas emissions of 1.7–2.2 tonnes of CO2-Eqs59,60. The majority of the impact is driven by the production of glass fiber, which accounts for 89% of the carbon footprint of the final product. Whereas natural fibers only have a carbon footprint of about 0.5–0.7 tonnes CO2-eq per 1 tonne of natural fiber. This is only one-third the carbon footprint of glass fiber. Conversely, the initial advantage of natural fibers over glass fiber is diminished in the final product as a result of additional processing steps that counteract their carbon footprint. Nonetheless, when compared to glass fiber composites, natural fiber composites have a 20–50% lower carbon footprint61,62. Fiberglass is recyclable, but it is not a straightforward process. It requires a lot of energy and sophisticated equipment. In terms of biodegradability, depending on environmental conditions, microorganisms, and physical characteristics, glass fibers only begin to degrade after about 50 years, which may take hundreds of years. The complicated process of sheep wool fiber biodegradation is driven by particular enzymes released by bacteria, fungi, and microorganisms that are naturally found in soil63. All this while sheep wool fibers have been shown to degrade after a year of exposure to soil64. Finally, it can be concluded that replacing part of the glass fibers with sheep wool fibers may be a suitable solution for environmentally friendly hybrid composites.

Conclusion

Hybrid composites of polyester reinforced with glass and sheep wool fibers were successfully fabricated. In summary, the findings confirmed better mechanical properties and thermal stability the hybrid composite, namely 9GF1SWF, than the polyester/glass fiber composites. Notably, it offered the highest tensile strength, absorbed flexural energy, and impact strength. These favorable results might be attributed to the synergistic effect between sheep wool (due to their distinct scale-like structure) and glass fibers. To summarize, the advantageous of hybridization of sheep wool fibers might be utilized as an efficient approach to partially replace glass fibers. This is a significant step forward in the development of more environmentally friendly hybrid polymer composites. These could be a good alternative to many common real-world applications of fiberglass parts.
