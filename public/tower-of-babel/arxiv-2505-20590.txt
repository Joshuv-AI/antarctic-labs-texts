Scaling Law of Urban Individual Tour Behavior
Xu-Jie Lin, Yitao Yang, Wei-Peng Nie, Xiao-Yong Yan

Abstract
Analysing and modeling urban individual tour behavior are of great significance for a wide range of applications such as transportation management and urban planning. However, the urban tour length distribution has long been neglected in individual mobility models that can be found in recent literature. To fill in this gap, we analyse Foursquare user check-in data and find that urban human tour length distribution follows a power-law with exponential truncation. This law also appears in China’s urban heavy truck trajectory data which can reflect commercial vehicle mobility. To reproduce the universal scaling law on tours of urban human and truck mobility, we introduce a tour terminate-continue model. Our model can not only reproduce the urban tour length distribution, but also the Heaps’ law and Zipf’s law in human and truck mobility, providing a new perspective for characterizing individual human mobility. Although there is currently no model that reproduces the distribution of tour length, a variety of models have been developed to capture the statistical properties of individual mobility provide us with valuable insights. The early models use Lévy random walk to model human mobility and accurately replicate the observed statistical characteristics

While these models can reproduce the statistical characteristics of human mobility using simple rules, they do not consider the tour generation mechanism. Lenormand et al. propose a new model considering individuals’ choice of terminating the search process of shopping, and successfully reproduce the distribution of shopping trip distance
[
29
]
. Although not originally designed for modeling individual tour behavior, its framework highlights the critical role of tour termination choice process in human mobility.

In terms of modeling the tour termination choice process, several models that have been proposed for modeling commercial vehicle tour behavior provide us with valuable insights. Hunt and Stefan propose a trip chain generation framework using discrete choice model to predict the next stop purpose of commercial vehicles and generate trip chains
[

[

13

14

15

The purpose of this work is to uncover urban tour length distribution and develop model to reproduce the statistical characteristics of tour-based individual human mobility such as tour length distribution, Heaps’ law and Zipf’s law. We start by presenting the Foursquare user check-in dataset and intra-city heavy truck trajectory dataset that we use in the statistical analysis. These two datasets contain information on the trajectory of Foursquare users and heavy trucks which respectively represent the intra-city human and truck mobility. Inspired by researches on individual human mobility, we introduce a model considering the tour termination mechanism and individuals’ tendency to revisit location. The model generates tour length distribution, Heaps’ law and Zipf’s law that mimic the empirical ones and can explain the observed tour length distribution of human and heavy trucks .

Data

In this study, we use two large-scale empirical datasets to analyze urban individual tour behavior. The first dataset consists of Foursquare user check-in records in New York and Los Angeles including 221,128 tips generated by 49,062 users
[
23

. Foursquare user check-in data directly reflects voluntary visits to locations, making it particularly valuable for analyzing individual human mobility. The second dataset consists of intra-city heavy truck GPS trajectories in Shanghai, and Beijing, collected over a two-week period in 2018
[
21
]
. This dataset includes 1,753,957 trips generated by 75,135 heavy trucks, which can reflect individual truck mobility. Each record in these two dataset contains an individual ID, timestamp, and geographic coordinates. We identify the most frequently visited location for each individual as their base location, and extract tours from their trajectory data. Here, we filtered out individuals with trips less than two. 4
Model

We observe that the tour length distributions in both human mobility within U.S. cities and truck mobility within Chinese cities universally follow a truncated power-law. But why do individuals with different travel modes in different environments exhibit similar patterns? This suggests that there may be a common underlying mechanism governing the generation of tours in both human and truck mobility. To the best of our knowledge, the tour length distribution has received limited attention in the study of individual human mobility. To investigate the capability of traditional human mobility models in reproducing tour length distributions, we adopt the EPR model and the SHMU model as benchmark models. Figure
1
demonstrates that EPR (triangle) model and SHMU (square) model are unable to reproduce the empirically observed distribution of tour lengths. This limitation arises from the fact that the EPR model primarily captures individuals’ exploration of new locations and preferential return to previously visited ones, while the SHMU model focuses solely on the memory effect associated with location visit frequency. Crucially, both models neglect the tour generation processes. Although tours can be extracted from the simulated trajectories, the tour length distributions generate by both models exhibit substantial discrepancies when in comparison with empirical observations. To fill in this gap, it is necessary to capture the key factors influencing individuals’ decisions to terminate a tour.

Here, we propose a tour terminate-continue (TTC) model, as shown in Fig.
2
. In TTC model, we assume that an individual begins his or her tour at a base location. At each step, the individual must decide whether to terminate the tour and return to base location (see Fig.
2
(a)). This tour generation process is modeled through the relationship between the probability of terminating the tour and the length of the current tour. Figure
3
shows the empirical relationship between the probability of terminating the tour and the length of current tour in New York (a), Los Angeles (b), Shanghai (c), and Beijing (d). We find that the probability of terminating the tour decreases with the increase in the current tour length, which is consistent in trend with the findings of Hunt and Stefan
[
12
]
. Moreover, we observe a negatively correlated scaling relationship between the probability of terminating a tour
{P}_{\text{term}}
and the current tour length
l

{{P}_{\text{term}}}=\rho{{l}^{-\gamma},}

(2)

where
\rho
denotes the initial terminate probability, and
\gamma
characterizes the degree to which this probability decreases as the tour length increases. Therefore, the probability of continuing the tour can be expressed as

{{P}_{\text{cont}}}=1-\rho{{l}^{-\gamma}},

(3)

The parameter
\rho
represents the initial probability of an individual choosing to terminate the tour. The greater the value of
\rho
, the higher the terminate probability
P_{\text{term}}
. The parameter
\gamma
captures the extent to which the current tour length
l
influences the terminate probability. When
\gamma=0
, the terminate probability is independent of the current tour length
l
. When
\gamma>0
, the terminate probability decreases with the increase of the current tour length
l
, following a power-law form.

2
(b)). To capture the individual’s preference for returning to previously visited locations, we introduce a parameter
\theta
that characterizes the competition between visiting new and old locations. We assume that the probability of an individual exploring a new location is

{{P}_{\text{new}}}=\frac{\theta}{\theta+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
k\neq k^{\prime}\end{subarray}}{{{m}_{k}}}},

(4)

where

is the number of visits to location
k
,

represents the index of base location, and
k^{\prime}
represents the index of last visited location. Accordingly, the probability of an individual revisiting an old location
k
(not base location) is

{{P}_{\text{old, }k}}=\frac{{{m}_{k}}}{\theta+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
k\neq k^{\prime}\end{subarray}}{{{m}_{k}}}}.

(5)

Note that the new location must be different from both the base location and the last visited location. After selecting the next location to visit, the process returns to the first stage (TTC model) to determine whether to terminate the tour. When the exploration parameter
\theta=0
, the individual will not explore any new locations but selects the next destination among previously visited locations, with the probability proportional to their visit frequencies. When the exploration parameter
\theta=1
, the individual will only explore new locations rather than return to any previously visited non-base locations. When the exploration parameter
\theta
lies between 0 and 1, higher value of
\theta
indicated a greater tendency for individuals to explore new locations.

5.2
Model prediction

We focus on the following statistical properties of urban tour-based human mobility: (1) the relationship between terminate probability and the current tour length, (2) tour length distribution, (3) the relationship between the number of locations visited and the number of trips, (4) location visit frequencies. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed model, we selected two classical models of individual human mobility as benchmarks for comparison, namely, the EPR model and the SHMU model. The parameters of all models are calibrated using the method described in the Model Calibration section.

5.3
Model features

We start by the analytical investigation of tour length distribution and the relationship between the number of locations visited and the number of trips. In TTC model, the negatively correlated scaling relationship between terminate probability and the length of the current tour (see Eq.
2
) serves as the core component in modeling the tour generation mechanism. A possible explanation for this phenomenon is that as individuals visit an increasing number of intermediate stops, they have a higher likelihood of encounter new opportunities, such as emerging user demands or new place of interests, making them less likely to return to base location. This scaling relationship reveals regularity of individuals’ choice of terminating the tour, providing a foundation for our modeling and analysis.

Accordingly to TTC model, the probability that a tour has a length of
l
can be formulated as

P(l)={{P}_{\text{term}}}(l)\prod\limits_{i=1}^{l-1}{{{P}_{\text{cont}}}(i)},

(7)

2

P(l)=\rho{{l}^{-\gamma}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{l-1}{(1-\rho{{i}^{-\gamma}})},

6
Discussion

In summary, we observed a universal scaling law in urban individual tour behavior: the tour length distribution follows a truncated power-law in Foursquare user check-in data and heavy truck trajectory data. To the best of our knowledge, This scaling law has been largely neglected in the existing human mobility literature.
To reproduce this characteristics, we propose a TTC model according to the scaling law between the terminate probability and the current tour length, as well as a competitive mechanism for choosing new or previously visited locations. Our model not only accurately reproduces the empirical tour length distributions observed in four major cities, but also successfully reproduces Heaps’ law and Zipf’s law, offering a unified framework for understanding individual human mobility.

While the proposed model effectively captures several key statistical characteristics in individual human mobility, it is not without limitations. First, our statistical analysis is based on data from only four cities, using Foursquare check-in data and heavy truck trajectories data. It will be important to evaluate our hypothesis and model performance using mobility data from a broader range of travel modes, such as private cars, taxis, and light trucks
[
31
;
32
]
. Second, although our model assumes a negative scaling relationship between tour terminate probability and current tour length, the underlying cause of this scaling relationship remains unclear. Future research should aim to uncover the mechanisms that drive the observed decrease in terminate probability as tour length increases. Third, our model currently does not incorporate spatial information such as travel distance. Extending the framework to include spatial constraints and distance-dependent location choice mechanisms may help us reproduce additional statistical characteristics
[
7
;
29
]
, including the distribution of total tour distances.

Additionally, our study have significant implications for urban transportation systems by understanding how individuals structure their tours. For example, by integrating real-time order demand with current truck locations, freight tours can be dynamically matched to logistics needs, enhancing delivery efficiency. Such an approach not only reduces the number of trucks required but also alleviates traffic congestion, and mitigates environmental impacts such as greenhouse gas emissions.
