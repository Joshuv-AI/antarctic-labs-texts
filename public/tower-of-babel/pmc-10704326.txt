“There's no time limit on grief:” Women's perspectives on a novel reproductive grief screening tool
Authors: Jennifer J Bute, Maria Brann, Cara Buskmiller, Michaelene Fredenburg
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWomen who have experienced reproductive loss (i.e., miscarriage, stillbirth, abortion) evaluated the usefulness of a novel screening tool, Reproductive Grief Screen (RGS), to identify patients struggling with ongoing, complicated grief.MethodsThis mixed-methods study involved U.S. women who had experienced reproductive loss. Online data collection resulted in 27 interviews and 282 surveys completed. Perceptions of and preferences about RGS were thematically analyzed. Chi square analyses assessed relationships between demographics and tool preferences.ResultsRGS validated women's experiences with grief after reproductive loss. Women noted their providers may be unaware of their loss(es). Participants requested periodic screening using RGS beginning shortly after a loss (or during new patient intake) and occurring regularly (e.g., annually). Overall, women preferred completing RGS online before an appointment, though preferences varied by demographics (i.e., age, time since loss). Participants want providers to compassionately discuss RGS results with them and offer appropriate resources.ConclusionThe RGS can help identify largely ignored grief after reproductive loss.InnovationFindings from group and individual interviews and a survey of women who have coped with reproductive loss suggest that use of a brief RGS tool could reshape clinical practice to aid women who might be facing complicated grief. Moreover, women expressed clear preferences for how to implement use of the RGS in clinical contexts.Keywords: Reproductive loss, Reproductive grief, Miscarriage, Stillbirth, Abortion

Introduction

Up to two million U.S. women experience pregnancy loss each year [1]. Such losses can take a variety of forms, including early miscarriage, stillbirth, selective reduction, or termination [2]. This pervasive type of loss has been associated with delayed or prolonged grief [3], sometimes referred to as a form of complicated grief [4]. In addition to grief, patients might face additional mental health challenges after reproductive loss, including posttraumatic stress, anxiety, depression [5,6], suicidal behaviors [7], and profound threats to identity [8].

Women have reported ongoing grief symptoms years or even decades after experiencing a loss [3]. Yet, the grief surrounding reproductive loss is frequently ignored or disenfranchised, meaning women often lack access to therapeutic assistance and other resources (e.g., support groups) that promote adaptive coping [3,9]. As such, they may turn to maladaptive responses, such as substance abuse, that contribute to strain in interpersonal relationships and challenge attainment of personal goals, such as education or workplace ambitions [3,8]. Although research suggests that women benefit from emotionally focused care to improve psychological adjustment [10], the need for such care can be missed if health care providers are not privy to complicated grief symptoms or warning signs of poor coping.

To better assess patients' experiences with grief and their need for services such as social support, mental health counseling, and so on, Authors [2] developed and validated a brief screening tool, the Reproductive Grief Screen (RGS, see Appendix), to assess patients' experiences with complicated grief after loss of a pregnancy. The 5-item RGS was designed specifically for clinical use, including implementation in OB/GYN and family physician practices, as well as mental health contexts. Although another perinatal grief screening tool, the Perinatal Grief Scale, exists, this 33-item instrument is best-suited for research purposes and is too long for use in clinical practice [2]. Moreover, the unique nature of reproductive loss necessitates the development of items that are specific to the context of those loss, such as items that assess avoidance of baby-related items and events [2].

The RGS asks respondents to assess the extent to which day-to-day living and emotional well-being is affected by reproductive loss and ongoing grief, and its concise design makes is feasible for use in clinical practice [2]. In initial testing of the RGS [2], the mean RGS score was 4.02 (IQR 2.27–5.77) out of a possible 10 points. Cronbach's alpha was 0.77 (95% CI 0.69–0.83). The goodness of fit indices of the model meet the Fornell-Larcker criterion (RMSEA = 0.167, CFI = 0.89, SRMR = 0.06). The AVE for RGS was 0.46 and the CR was 0.78. The correlation between the RGS and PGS was 0.95, suggesting that they measure the same symptoms of grief after reproductive loss. However, this study also showed the RGS measured symptoms that overlapped with trauma, or that the RGS may be too general when assessing post-traumatic stress symptoms.

Our goal in the present study was to employ a patient-centered approach to determine best practices for implementation of the RGS in clinical settings. Using focus groups and individual interviews with women who had experienced one or more losses and a survey instrument designed to assess women's opinions of the screening tool, this mixed methods study explored women's preferences for administration of the tool, offering specific recommendations for use of the screening tool in clinical practice.

Methods

Participants

Interviews

After receiving institutional review board approval, we recruited women who had experienced reproductive loss from online postings of the study on reproductive loss support websites and social media sites between October 2021 and February 2022. To be eligible for the study, women had to be at least 18 years old, able to speak and read English, have access to Zoom, and have experienced some type of pregnancy loss. Of the 27 women who participated in the online interviews, 23 participated in a focus group discussion with women who experienced similar types of reproductive loss and four women participated in an individual interview.

Fourteen women (51.9%) participated in one of four miscarriage focus groups, and nine women (33.3%) participated in one of three stillbirth focus groups. All four women (14.8%) who participated in an individual interview experienced an abortion. Because of the sensitivity and stigma associated with abortion, regardless of the reason for termination, and because of the lack of women available to participate in a focus group with other women who had an abortion, the four willing women were interviewed individually. After each interview session, we emailed participants a $50 electronic gift card to a national retailer for their participation. Data collection

Interviews

Interested participants contacted one of the researchers to determine eligibility, and if eligible, was scheduled for an online interview. Another researcher conducted all interview sessions, including indvidual and foucs group interviews, via Zoom videoconferencing. This researcher asked participants, in both group and individual interviews, similar questions about their experience with pregnancy loss; interactions with health care providers; and perceptions of the RGS. She shared the RGS [2] with participants and solicited feedback about scale items and preferences for wording and administering the assessment as part of ongoing assessment of the tool. The current manuscript focuses on women's preferences for adminstration and general opinions of the RGS.

Survey

Following a thematic analysis of qualitative focus group sessions and individual interview data regarding women's preferences for ongoing screening after pregnancy loss, we created a survey to administer to a larger sample of women to glean more generalizable results for administering a complicated grief assessment tool. The online survey was hosted by the Qualtrics platform. At the end of the survey, interested participants had the opportunity to submit their email address, separate from their responses, into a drawing to receive one of thirty $25 electronic gift cards to a national retailer for their participation. After closing the survey, we cleaned the data to discard unusable data (e.g., bot-generated responses, incomplete surveys) before conducting any statistical analysis.

Data analysis

Qualitative

Our approach to qualitative data analysis was guided by a phroentic, interative approach [11], which emphasizes the value of qualitiatve data for solving real-world problems and outlines an approach to analysis that is firmly grounded in the wisdom of research participants. Before analyzing data, members of the research team reviewed all Zoom-generated transcripts, correcting any errors and adding pseudonyms. Two researchers read through all the transcripts to familiarize themselves with the data before systematic coding. We then conducted a thematic analysis by first engaging in open coding [12]. After compiling initial themes from this primary-cycle coding into a codebook, we divided the transcripts in half and each engaged in secondary-cycle coding of our assigned data with the agreed-upon codes to determine if they existed in the dataset as a whole [11]. We data conferenced with each other and experts in reproductive loss regularly about emergent themes to verify the trustworthiness of our data analysis. All members of the research team are women who are deeply commited to improving the well-being of women coping with reproductive giref and, as such, remained particulary sensitive to privileging women's voices throughout the analysis process. In addition, the researcher who conducted the interviews wrote detailed field notes after each session to reflect on the emotional responses she had as someone who has experienced pregnancy loss. These notes, along with conversations among the team ensured that our commitment to better grief care after pregnancy loss was supported by the emergent themes from our data.

Quantitative

We performed descriptive statistics on demographic data to assess frequencies of characteristics of the sample and chi-square tests of independence to examine relationships between different demographic variables and screening tool preferences using IBM SPSS Statstics (Version 29).

Results

Data from individual interviews, focus groups interviews, and survey respondents indicated that women viewed the RGS as potentially useful in a variety of health care settings, including OB/GYN offices, primary care clinics, and mental health contexts. Women overwhelmingly expressed the need for communication about pregnancy loss and thought this screening tool could promote communication about grief with providers. One woman said, “It would maybe open the door for the patient to talk with the physician if the physician would be open to talking with that patient” (Camille, 51, abortion).

The majority of women in the survey sample (72%) believed that it would be helpful for a health care provider to check in with them about how they are coping after a pregnancy loss, and several focus group participants called for a “standard of care” in managing reproductive loss. Women indicated a variety of ways the RGS could be helpful, including validating the emotional pain trigged by loss. As one woman explained, “When I read it, it felt normalizing” (Mandy, 40, stillbirth). In addition, women thought the screening tool would be useful for tracking prolonged grief and adjustment over time, even years after the loss.

Potential challenges in using the RGS

Health care providers must know that a patient has experienced a loss in order to administer the RGS. Of the 282 women who completed the survey, 208 (73.8%) stated that their current health care provider knows about their loss(es). This means that more than a quarter of women did not think their provider knew about their loss (18.1%) or were unsure if they knew (7.8%). As one woman explained,

One thing I know happens, and I filled out a bunch of forms like this, is they'll ask you how many pregnancies you've had and how many live births. But there are ways that medical professionals can find out if there's been a loss, whether there's been miscarriage, stillbirth, or abortion that could be added to a medical form in some way so that they would learn more about how that loss occurred.

(Linda, 70, abortion)

This observation supported the survey data, which suggested that the most common way for health care providers to know about previous pregnancy losses was through the medical record (35.8% noted this exclusively and an additional 7.4% marked this along with other ways of knowing). Nearly 15% of women (14.9%) exclusively noted that they told their health care provider about their loss with an additional 5.6% noting this was one of multiple ways their provider knew.

Although women found the questions on the RGS helpful in validating their emotions, women in the focus group sessions and interviews commented that the tool must be administered with care because the items could be “triggering” or “loaded.” Indeed, women were concerned that the tool could be harmful if a health care provider is not trained to administer and discuss it in a compassionate way. “I think that they just have to know how to use the tool,” said Betty (50, multiple miscarriages), “and that is not just ‘okay here's some questions.’ So they have to be aware that they need to be empathetic and everything while they're talking to you about it.” Women called for training so that providers could use the tool without causing unintended harm: “My only concern would be if there's not training so that they know what to do with it. Because it would be devastating to complete it, acknowledge that you're having quite a hard time, and then not have anything happen” (Heather, 36, miscarriage).

Results from the interviews and survey supported offering women encouragement and resources, as needed, after they complete the tool. One participant said, “If I'm answering these questions, I'd want feedback so that way, I know that they're not just probing me as another data point or statistic” (Kimberly, 34, miscarriage). Ways providers could support women include not only discussing the results with the patient but also discussing different support options and providing resources (see Table 3). Having a conversation about the results and offering support are ways to address some of the potential challenges of using a screening tool.

Preferences for administration of the screening

Women offered feedback via interviews, focus groups, and the survey about preferences for administering the RGS. Offering an option for women to choose whether to complete the tool could support women's autonomy and comfort. For instance, women could receive a text or email giving them the option to complete the screening prior to an upcoming appointment.

Women might need time and space after a loss, particularly if they are in the midst of embodied grief, such as physical changes that occur after loss. “It took me, maybe a few weeks to where I was finally grasping what happened,” explained Jessica (26, miscarriage), “because I know, in the heat of the moment, I felt like, if I were to see that questionnaire I would just automatically be like, ‘of course I'm feeling all these feelings because it's just happened.’”

In terms of timing and frequency of administration, the screening tool could be used at intervals during the first year after a loss and yearly thereafter. Survey results suggested that women preferred that they be screened more frequently during the first year following loss (35.1% of women recommended monthly) and then annually (47.5%) or at the discretion of the health care provider (46.8%) after the first year following the loss. Timing could be dependent on which provider is using the tool. For example, OB/GYNs might administer the RGS at annual check-ups, but a therapist might not find it useful to use the screening tool at every appointment. Ongoing use of the tool was found to be important given the complicated grief associated with loss, which might ebb and flow along with milestone dates, like an anticipated due date or the anniversary of the loss, and life events, like the birth of a child or grandchild. As one participant said, “I think it needs to be throughout the patient's life, even if it's just at their yearly exam to inquire of the patient ‘How are you doing mentally? How are you doing emotionally?’” (Camille, 51, abortion).

Additionally, most women preferred to complete the RGS prior to an exam but were hesitant about completing it in a waiting room because some items could be triggering, and they feared having an emotional response in a public space. More than three-fourths of women surveyed preferred to complete the screening electronically either before arriving to the health care facility (57.4%) or in the examination room (21.3%). Women suggested completing it online via a text message with a link (27.3%) or via a patient portal (30.1%): “I find it very convenient especially if it's on your phone. It's not taking me away from anything,” said Mandy (40, stillbirth). Holly (32, multiple types of loss) echoed, “Because everything is going to apps these days, and then you could think about it more, instead of filling it out in the waiting room where you might be triggered by just being there.”

If health care providers elect to use the RGS in the office as opposed to prior to an appointment, several options could be used to introduce the screening tool. Based on the survey, slightly more women (39%) preferred that a health care provider state that they would like to see if there are any “lingering concerns” related to the loss, but other wording options were fairly evenly preferred (see Table 4). Women were asked what language they would like a health care provider to use if they became aware of their loss and decided to check-in on the patient's mental health. Slightly more than a third of women (37.2%) preferred that they be asked about whether they found their grief to be distressing (see Table 5).

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Based on a mixed-methods approach designed to assess women's perspectives on a novel reproductive grief screening tool, we found that the RGS shows promise in being a useful way to assess and discuss grief in clinical settings. Our study highlights the voices of patients who have experienced reproductive loss(es) to offer patient-centered insights into clinical use of the screening tool. Women offered specific feedback on the timing and administration of the tool and offered practical considerations for training providers to use the tool. Their suggestions supported previous research that advises providers to demonstrate empathy and allow time and space for patients to process their experience when discussing reproductive loss with a patient [13]. Based on our qualitative and quantitative data, health care providers should assess grief initially after a loss and periodically as time progresses. Of the women who did not think the screening tool would be helpful (17.4%), the most common reason why focused on time since loss (e.g., “it was a long time ago”) and the ability to have a live birth after loss (e.g., “I have since had my rainbow baby”). As previous research has noted, grief, despair, and difficulty coping are most intense in the first three months following a loss but may lessen as the years since the loss pass [14]. The results of this study reiterate the importance of early screening shortly after the loss and then tailoring ongoing screening to the patient's needs.

In terms of administration, most women in our samples indicated a preference to complete the assessment prior to an appointment using electronic means, such as a text message or via an online patient portal. However, older women who had experienced the loss >16 years ago were less likely to prefer electronic administration, as were women of mixed race. As such, administration could be tailored to the needs and practices of particular health care systems (e.g., via patient portal for large clinics or using pencil and paper for solo practice providers, like therapists) and to the preferences of the patient when possible. In Wanyonyi et al.'s [15] meta-analysis of tailored messaging, they found that “knowledge of the personal characteristics of patients and the subsequent tailoring of health messages may result in positive effects” (p. 354). Thus, recognizing individual preferences, especially for sensitive topics like complicated grief, can help meet individuals' health and psychosocial needs. Regardless of means of administration, women preferred to be given a choice about whether they wanted to complete the screening tool, giving them some autonomy in a difficult and uncontrollable situation.

A primary strength of our approach was focusing on the voices of women in assessing the implementation of a novel reproductive grief screening tool, which will ensure that the delicate nature of this topic can be approached with careful consideration. However, our sample for the study could have been expanded in both size and diversity to assess the perspectives of women from a broader range of backgrounds to understand how the RGS should be adapted for other languages and to account for cultural and religious differences in coping with reproductive grief. These are important sites for future research. Moreover, the authors are still refining wording of items and conducting additional psychometric testing, so the version of the RGS offered here represents a work in progress.

Innovation

This novel screening tool, the RGS, offers an innovative approach to screening patients for complicated grief specific to reproductive loss. Our findings, which are centered on the voices of patients, suggest a need for improved systems of identifying patients' reproductive losses to distinguish patients who might benefit from grief screening and access to mental health resources. One reason that women might be missed in current practice relates not only to the absence of a brief screener, a need that the RGS can fulfill, but our findings also reveal the fact that providers might not always know that patients have experienced a past loss. Given that 25.9% of women stated that either their provider did not know about their loss(es), or they were unsure if their provider knew about their loss, it is important to establish mechanisms for making sure that these women do not “slip through the cracks.” Effective and judicious administration of the new RGS necessitates that providers are aware of the type and number of losses, including patient intake forms that ask for more detail than simply number of pregnancies and/or number of births. Patients should be able to respond to detailed questions about number, type, and time since loss(es).

In addition, use of this novel screener necessitates that the current practices for caring for patients who have had any form of pregnancy loss are reformed. Providers will need training in administering, interpreting, and discussing the RGS with patients. Further research is warranted to determine the precise nature of this training; though, at a minimum, health care providers should be prepared to discuss the loss with the patient and offer her appropriate options for support (e.g., counseling referral, support group information, online resources). All in all, our findings point to the ways providers and health care systems can reimagine care for patients coping with reproductive grief.

Conclusion

Implementation of the RGS [2] in clinical settings has the potential to recognize disenfranchised, complicated grief in a large group of patients who might be suffering in silence. Our results suggest that women coping with pregnancy loss viewed the RGS favorably and expressed clear preferences that can guide clinical implementation of this important screening tool.
