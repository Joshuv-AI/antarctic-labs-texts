Balanced spanning trees of the 2 2 -by- N N grid
Makenzie Gallagher, Kristopher Tapp

Abstract
. We obtain an exact formula for the probability that a uniformly random spanning tree of the 2 2 -by- n n square grid is “balanced” in the sense that it has an edge whose removal partitions its vertices into two sets of equal size. We compute the exact limit of this probability as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
primary 05C05; secondary 05C90

1.
Introduction

Recent advances in computational redistricting have sparked interest in
balanced
spanning trees. Several algorithms have been used in court cases this decade to argue that voting maps are biased, including ReCom (
[
DDS20
,
CDRR22
]
), Forest ReCom (
[
ACH+23
]
) and SMC (
[
MI23
]
). Each of these algorithms is capable of generating an ensemble of thousands or millions of random nonpartisan maps by repeatedly performing a step that involves drawing a random spanning tree of a graph and identifying a
balanced cut edge
(an edge whose removal partitions the vertices into two sets of approximately equal weight), if one exists. A better theoretical understanding of this likelihood could help to understand the performance of these algorithms and improve upon them.

A significant step was recently taken in this direction. The authors of
[
CLLV23
]
proposed a provably fast-mixing algorithm for sampling not-necessarily-balanced partitions of the vertices of a graph into connected pieces. In the case of grids, they conjectured that a sufficiently large portion of such a sample would be balanced. In the redistricting context, this amounts to building an ensemble of voting maps whose districts are not even approximately equipopulous, and hoping that enough are balanced to yield a sufficiently large subsample of legal maps. Their conjecture was proven by the authors of
[
CPTF24
]
, who established that for fixed
k
, the portion of spanning trees of the
n
-by-
m
square grid that are
k
-
balanced
is at least
1/\text{poly}(m,n)
, where “
k
-balanced” means there exist
k-1
edges whose removal partitions the vertices into
k
sets of equal sizes.

The goal of this paper is to obtain exact results in the simplest nontrivial case: the
2
-by-
n
grid, which we denote as
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Let
T_{n}
denote the number of spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Let
S_{n}
denote the number of
balanced
spanning trees; i.e., trees that contain an edge (called a
balanced cut edge
) whose removal partitions the vertices into two sets of equal size. Our main result is an exact formula for the ratio
\frac{S_{n}}{T_{n}}
, from which the following limits follow.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer.

(1)

(Odd case)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{2m+1}}{T_{2m+1}}=\frac{3+\sqrt{3}}{9}\approx 0.53

(2)

(Even case)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{2m}}{T_{2m}}=\frac{1+4\sqrt{3}}{6\sqrt{3}}\approx 0.76
.

In Section
6
, we additionally consider the probability that a spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is balanced if, instead of choosing
T
uniformly (that is, choosing it from the UST “uniform spanning tree” distribution), it is constructed as a minimal spanning tree with respect to random edge weights (that is, it is drawn from the MST “minimal spanning tree” distribution). Surprisingly, for the even choices of
n
that we computed, the MST probabilities are greater than the UST probabilities.

2.
Related work

The literature cited in the previous section motivates and/or addresses general questions about the probability that a random spanning tree is balanced. In this section, we mention some results in the literature about the structure of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
(the
2
-by-
n
grid).

The author of
[
Raf08
]
derived the following recursive formula for the number of possible spanning trees over a
2
-by-
n
grid:

(1)

T_{n+2}=4\cdot T_{n+1}-T_{n},

and showed that the generating function of the sequence
T_{n}
is

\frac{x}{1-4x+x^{2}}
. His recursive formula is the starting point of our work.

More recently, the authors of
[
BZ25
]
proved that the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
of the portion of the vertices of a uniformly random spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
which are leaves (degree
1
) equals

\frac{14\sqrt{5}}{5}-6\approx 0.26
.

Set up

Let
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{n}
denote the dual graph of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Let
v_{\infty}
denote the vertex of
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{n}
that corresponds to the unbounded face of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. For example, Figure
1
shows the vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{11}
in red and the vertices of
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{11}
in yellow (
v_{\infty}
is not explicitly shown in the figure).

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
denote the set of spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, so
T_{n}=|\mathcal{T}_{n}|
. By convention, we set
T_{0}=1
. It is well known that the spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
correspond one-to-one with the spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{*}
. For any
T\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let
T^{*}
denote the corresponding spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{*}
. With respect to the natural one-to-one correspondence between the edges of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and the edges of
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{n}
, the edges of
T^{*}
are exactly the ones corresponding to edges of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
that do not belong to
T
.

Removing an edge of
T
corresponds to adding an edge to
T^{*}
, which creates a unique cycle
\gamma
in
T^{*}
. Note that all cycles of
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{*}
contain
v_{\infty}
, so we can regard
\gamma
as a path from
v_{\infty}
to
v_{\infty}
. For example, Figure
2
shows a spanning tree
T
of
\mathcal{G}_{11}
(in grey), the corresponding spanning tree
T^{*}
of
\mathcal{G}_{11}^{*}
(in brown), and the cycle
\gamma
induced by removing the highlighted edge of
T
(highlighted in purple).

4.
Counting balanced trees

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
denote the set of balanced spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, so
S_{n}=|\mathcal{S}_{n}|
. The goal of this section is to derive the following explicit formula for
S_{n}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer.

(1)

(Odd case):
If
n=2m+1
, then

S_{n}=n+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(6+4i)\cdot T^{2}_{m-i}.

(2)

(Even case):
If
n=2m
, then

S_{n}=n+2\cdot T^{2}_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(4+4i)\cdot T^{2}_{m-i}.

Proof.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, removing the balanced cut edge of a tree
T\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
results in a unique loop
\gamma
in
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{n}
at
v_{\infty}
. Our strategy is to first count the possibilities for
\gamma
and then count the possibilities for
T\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
associated with each possibility for
\gamma
.

We claim first that there are exactly
n
possibilities for
\gamma
(loops in
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{*}
that divide the vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
equally) which can be easily enumerated. Figure
4
illustrates in purple the possibilities for
\gamma
when
m=5
(including
n=11
on the left and
n=10
on the right). Each “
\times 2
” multiplier symbol in the figure indicates that the reflection of the configuration over the horizontal centerline is an additional option for
\gamma
. It is straightforward to generalize this scheme to arbitrary
m
.

Enumerate the configurations for
\gamma
(modulo horizontal symmetry) as
\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{m}
, where the meaning of the index
i
of
\gamma_{i}
is as exemplified in the figure. We claim that the
i^{\text{th}}
configuration is associated with exactly

b_{i}\cdot\text{length}(\gamma_{i})\cdot T_{m-i}^{2}

balanced trees in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, where
b_{i}\in\{1,2\}
is the multiplier; that is,
b_{i}=2
if
\gamma
is different from its horizontal reflection. Here the factor
\text{length}(\gamma_{i})
counts the number of placements of the balanced cut edge, while the factor
T^{2}_{m-1}
counts the number of ways to draw spanning trees in end blocks (which are shaded cyan in Figures
3
and
4
). In summary,

S_{n}=\text{length}(\gamma_{m})+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}b_{i}\cdot\text{length}(\gamma_{i})\cdot T_{m-i}^{2}.

Note that
\text{length}(\gamma_{m})=n
, while for
i\in\{0,\dots m-1\}
we have

\text{length}(\gamma_{i})=\begin{cases}2i+3&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}\\
2i+2&\text{if }n\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

Making these substitutions completes the proof.
∎

n

(left) and

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The solution to Equation
1
(with initial conditions
T_{1}=1
,
T_{2}=4
) is

(2)

T_{k}=a\left(r^{k}-r^{-k}\right),\qquad\text{ where }a=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}},\text{ and }r=2+\sqrt{3}.

In this section, we combine Lemma
4.1
with Equation
2
to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

(Odd case):
We first consider the odd case
n=2m+1
. For each fixed index
i\in\{1,...,m\}
, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{T_{m-i}^{2}}{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=a\cdot\frac{\left(r^{m-i}-r^{-(m-i)}\right)^{2}}{r^{2m+1}-r^{-(2m+1)}}

\displaystyle=a\cdot\frac{r^{2m-2i}+r^{-2m+2i}+2}{r^{2m+1}-r^{-2m-1}}

\displaystyle=a\cdot\frac{1+r^{-4m+4i}+2r^{-2m+2i}}{r^{2i+1}-r^{-4m+2i-1}}\rightarrow\frac{a}{r^{2i+1}}

where “
\rightarrow
” indicates the limit as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Combining this with Lemma
4.1
gives:

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}}{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{T_{n}}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\underbrace{(6+4i)\cdot\frac{T_{m-i}^{2}}{T_{2m+1}}}_{\text{denoted }Y(i,m)}\rightarrow\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(6+4i)\cdot\frac{a}{r^{2i+1}}=\frac{a}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(6+4i)\cdot r^{-2i}.

Setting
x=r^{-2}=7-4\sqrt{3}
, this sum converges to:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(6+4i)x^{i}=\frac{6}{1-x}+\frac{4x}{(1-x)^{2}}=\left(2\sqrt{3}+3\right)+\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)=2\sqrt{3}+\frac{10}{3}.

Making this substitution and simplifying yields the desired result for the odd case.

It remains to justify the limit claim in Equation
3
, which asserts that

(4)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i\geq 0}Y(i,m)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}Y(i,m),

where we can consider all sums to be infinite by setting
Y(i,m)=0
if
i\geq m
.
To justify Equation
4
via the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we must construct a sequence
M_{i}
such that
\sum_{i}M_{i}<\infty
and
Y(i,m)<M_{i}
for all
i\geq 0
and all
m\geq 1
. The sequence
M_{i}=\frac{6+4i}{3^{2i+1}}
fits the bill because

(5)

\frac{T^{2}_{m-i}}{T_{2m+1}}\leq\frac{1}{T_{2i+1}}\leq\frac{1}{3^{2i+1}}.

Equation
5
follows from the more general fact that
T_{a}\cdot T_{b}\cdot T_{c}\leq T_{a+b+c}
, which can be justified algebraically or by observing that there are multiple ways to add edges to a triple in
\mathcal{T}_{a}\times\mathcal{T}_{b}\times\mathcal{T}_{c}
to generate a tree in
\mathcal{T}_{a+b+c}
.

(Even case):
We next consider the even case
n=2m
. Similar to the previous argument, for each fixed index
i\in\{0,...,m\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{T_{m-i}^{2}}{T_{n}}\rightarrow\frac{a}{r^{2i}}=a\cdot x^{i}.

Combining this with Lemma
4.1
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}}{T_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{T_{n}}+2\cdot\frac{T_{m}^{2}}{T_{n}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(4+4i)\cdot\frac{T_{m-i}^{2}}{T^{n}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2a+a\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(4+4i)\cdot x^{i}

\displaystyle=2a+a\left(\frac{4x}{1-x}+\frac{4x}{(1-x)^{2}}\right),

which simplifies to the desired result.
∎

6.
Conclusion and future directions

We have obtained explicit formulas and limits for the probability that a uniformly random spanning tree of the
2
-by-
n
grid
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is
2
-balanced. In other words, for this simple class of graphs, we obtained (in the
k=2
case) exact solutions for the probabilities for which bounds were obtained in
[
CPTF24
]
.

Future research could study the probability that a spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is
k
-balanced for
k>2
, or could consider the
3
-by-
n
grid.

Another future avenue is to study the same question with respect to the MST (minimal spanning tree) distribution on the set of spanning trees of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. In other words, what is the probability that a spanning tree
T
of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is balanced if, instead of choosing
T
uniformly (from the “UST distribution”), it is constructed one edge at a time, with each next edge uniformly randomly selected, along the way rejecting additions that would create cycles? This is the same as applying Kruskal’s algorithm to find a minimum spanning tree with respect to random edge weights.

Table
1
summarizes for each
n\in\{2,...,19\}
the portion of trees that are balanced with respect to both the UST and MST distribution. The UST probabilities in this table are computed as exact fractions via Equation
1
and Lemma
4.1
, and then rounded to
6
decimals. The MST probabilities for
n\leq 5
are computed as exact fractions by enumerating all permutations of the edges of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and tallying the portion of these permutations (considered as edge weights) for which Kruskal’s algorithm yields a balanced tree. The “
\sim
” symbol indicates that for
n\geq 6
, the MST probabilities are
approximated
from a sample of one million permutations of the edges of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
(because there are too many permutations to loop over all of them).

It is interesting that, in both the even and odd cases, the UST probabilities converge so quickly to the limit values reported in Theorem
1.1
.

For odd
n
, the MST values in the table are perhaps unsurprising. For example, when
n=3
, the exact MST probability of balance (
4/7
) is a bit smaller than the exact UST probability of balance (
6/10
). The numerical results are consistent with the guess that
MST<UST
for all odd
n\geq 3
. It is not clear whether the MST and UST distributions have the same limit in the odd case as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

For even
n
, the MST values in the table are quite surprising. In the case
n=4
, the exact MST probability of balance (
248/315
) is
larger
than the exact UST probability of balance (
11/14
). Moreover, given the very large sample size of 1 million permutations, the table provides overwhelming statistical evidence that
MST>UST
for all
n\in\{6,8,10,12,14,16\}
as well (with
p
-values smaller than
10^{-100}
). The table might lead one to guess that the MST limit is strictly larger than the UST limit in the even case as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

This data is surprising because the prevailing expectation was that the MST probability of balance should be
\leq
the UST probability of balance, at least for natural classes of graphs like grids.
