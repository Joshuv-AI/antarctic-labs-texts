A Simplified Proof for the Edge Density of 4-Planar Graphs
Aaron Büngener

Abstract
A graph on n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 vertices drawn in the plane such that each edge is crossed at most four times has at most 6 ​ ( n − 2 ) 6(n-2) edges – this result proven by Ackerman is outstanding in the literature of beyond-planar graphs with regard to its tightness and the structural complexity of the graph class. We provide a much shorter proof while at the same time relaxing the conditions on the graph and its embedding, i.e., allowing multi-edges and non-simple drawings.

Introduction

A drawing
\Gamma
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is an embedding in the plane such that each vertex
v\in V
is mapped to a distinct point and each edge
e\in E
to a Jordan arc connecting the corresponding endpoints. We assume that (i) no vertex is an interior point of an edge, (ii) two edges have only finitely many intersection points, which are either proper crossings or a common endpoint, and (iii) at most two edges cross at a single point. We allow parallel edges and loops in
G
, if they are non-homotopic in
\Gamma
, i.e., they form a closed curve with at least one vertex inside and one outside. We denote by
P(\Gamma)
the planarization of
\Gamma
, i.e., the vertices and crossing points of
\Gamma
are the vertices of
P(\Gamma)
, while the edges of
P(\Gamma)
are the crossing-free segments in
\Gamma
, which are bounded by vertices and crossing points.

A given graph
G
is
k
-planar, if it has a drawing
\Gamma
, where each edge is involved in at most
k
crossings. They find direct application in the well-known Crossing Lemma
[
4
,
7
,
10
]
. For
k\leq 3
tight (up to a constant) upper bounds on the edge density, i.e., the maximum number of edges a graph
G
on
n
vertices can have, were shown (
k=1
:
4(n-2)

[
11
]
;
k=2
:
5(n-2)

[
5
,
10
]
;
k=3
:
5.5(n-2)

[
5
,
7
,
9
]
).
For
k=4
, an explicit proof of the tight upper bound
6(n-2)
has been given only for the case where
G
is simple
[
2
]
. Unfortunately, the proof relies on a massive discharging argument with lengthy case analysis.
Here, we considerably simplify the proof and extend it to the non-simple case.

Recently, the known upper bound for the edge density of simple 5-planar graphs has been drastically improved from
\approx 8.3n
to
\approx 6.94n

[
6
]
. A key ingredient for the simplification of the case analysis was a separate treatment of empty triangular faces described by three pairwise crossing edges in the drawing.
We apply this idea to the 4-planar setting. For 4-planar graphs, it is known that the upper bound of
6(n-2)
can be achieved by a planar hexagonalization where each hexagon
h
is enhanced by nine further crossing edges inside
h
, see
\cref
fig:hexagon-def.
We specify this pattern
H^{*}
in
\cref
sec:def and will show that we can assume that an empty triangular face is always part of an
H^{*}
(
\cref
pro:0-triangle).

Another source for simplification compared to
[
2
]
is the fact that just a few types of faces can exist in
P(\Gamma)
(
\cref
pro:small-faces,pro:fill,pro:cr-minimal,pro:cr-minimal-2) – which only holds in our more general setting. This leads to a short proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Any connected 4-planar graph
G
on
n\geq 3
vertices has at most
6(n-2)
edges.

Proof of
\cref
th:4-planar

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be a 4-planar drawing of an edge-maximal
n
-vertex graph
G
that has the maximum number of
H^{*}
configurations and is under this condition crossing-minimal.

We start by specifying which types of faces can occur in
P(\Gamma)
.
For that, we prove the theorem by induction on the number of vertices (this is needed for
\cref
pro:2-connected),
so observe that for the base case
n=3
at most six non-homotopic edges are possible (three loops and three non-loop-edges). The next proposition is inspired by
[
2
, Proposition 2.1]
, but we have to adapt it to the non-simple case.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
P(\Gamma)
has a vertex
x
that is a crossing in
\Gamma
so that
P(\Gamma)\setminus{x}
is not connected. Then
G
has at most
6(n-2)
edges.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\Gamma}
be the drawing of the graph
\tilde{G}
obtained by replacing the crossing
x
by a vertex. Thus, this adds one vertex and two edges. Let
\tilde{\Gamma}_{1},...,\tilde{\Gamma}_{k}
be the connected components of
\tilde{\Gamma}\setminus\{x\}
with corresponding graphs
G_{1},...,G_{k}
. Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the drawing induced by the vertices of
G_{1}
and
x
, and let
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
be the drawing induced by the vertices of
G_{2}\cup...\cup G_{k}
and
x
. Let
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}
be the graphs corresponding to
\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
. Note that both have vertex
x
.

By this procedure, no multi-edges of
G^{\prime}
or
G^{\prime\prime}
are homotopic in
\Gamma^{\prime}
or
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
, because the vertex
x
separates the same Jordan arcs in
\Gamma^{\prime}
that were separated in
\Gamma
by vertices of
G^{\prime\prime}
and vice versa.

Let
n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}
be the number of vertices of
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}
. By construction, we have
n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}=n+2\geq 5
and w.l.o.g.
n^{\prime}\geq 3
and
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
. For
n^{\prime\prime}=2
, we observe that
n=n^{\prime}
while
G^{\prime}
has one edge more than
G
. This contradicts that
G
is maximally dense. For
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 3
, we can apply induction and conclude that
G
has at most
6n^{\prime}-12+6n^{\prime\prime}-12-2=6(n+2)-26\leq 6n-12
edges.
∎

Thus, if the boundary of a face is not a simple cycle, then it is a vertex of
G
that appears multiple times.

Proposition 3
.

For each face
f\in P(\Gamma)
, we have
|f|\geq 3
.

Proof.

A 0-1-gon implies a self-crossing edge, which is forbidden as the edges are Jordan arcs. A 1-1-gon is created by an homotopic loop and a 2-2-gon by homotopic multi-edges, which both are not allowed. In the case that
f
is a 0-2-gon or a 1-2-gon, we have a contradiction to the crossing-minimality of
\Gamma
by swapping the two edge segments forming
f
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

A face
f\in P(\Gamma)
with
v(f)>2
is a 3-triangle.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
be the (not necessarily distinct) vertices of
f
. If
|f|\geq 4
, then not all edges
v_{i}v_{j}
with
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
are present on the boundary of
f
. Therefore, we can insert such an edge (which is not homotopic to any other edge), contradicting that
G
is edge-maximal.
∎

Combining this with
\cref

The non-simple 2-triangles have exactly one vertex on its boundary, which is connected by a loop. There is no need to handle this type separately, as all following arguments work the same for the two types of 2-triangles. Non-simple 3-triangles can be treated the same way.

Proposition 7
.

Every 0-triangle

is part of an

Proof.

Assume that

is not part of an

be the three edges that form

be the endpoints of

may appear multiples times as an endpoint of edges

. Then we choose an arbitrary tree

are connected in

. Now define

as the region that closely surrounds

and by 4-planarity, there are at most nine of them. Thus, by this procedure we do not lower the number of edges and find a drawing with a larger number of

\ch(f)=|f|+v(f)-4

Case 1: There is a 2-triangle that is a vertex-neighbor of
f
.
W.l.o.g. let
f_{0}
be a 2-triangle. If
\ch_{3}(f_{0})\geq\frac{7}{9}
, then
f_{0}
has an excess of
\frac{1}{9}
and
\ch_{4}(f)\geq 0
. If
\ch_{3}(f_{0})<\frac{7}{9}
, then
f_{0}
contributes charge in
Step 2

f_{4}

e_{3}
,
f_{1}
is also a 2-triangle. So
\ch_{2}(t_{1})=\frac{2}{9}
and
f
contributes only
\frac{1}{9}
charge to
t_{1}
in
Step 3
. Thus,
\ch_{3}(f)\geq 0
.

In the following, we can assume that wedges of all
t_{i}
contain at most one 0-quadrilateral, as otherwise one vertex–neighbor of
f
is a 2-triangle and we can refer to Case 1.

f

.

4
Open questions

We demonstrated how the case analysis in the original proof by Ackerman
[
2
]
can be shortened. So one may ask if the complexity of this proof can be reduced further. Applying the concept of the density formula might help
[
8
]
. Further, our method may open up new possibilities to characterize optimal 4-planar graphs. This new approach might provide a path to improving the density bounds also for k-planar graphs for larger
k\geq 5
, and also to better constants in the Crossing Lemma.
