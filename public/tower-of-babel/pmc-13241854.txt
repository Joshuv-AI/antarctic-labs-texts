Changing Pattern of Peritoneal Dialysis-Related Fungal Peritonitis: A 21-Year Single-Center Experience
Authors: Hao Yan, He Zhang, Yue Qian, Xinyu Su, Yiwei Shen, Jiaying Huang, Zhaohui Ni, Leyi Gu, Wei Fang
Published: 2026

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is an important modality of kidney replacement therapy for patients with kidney failure. PD-related peritonitis continues to be a significant cause of mortality and technique failure.1 Although fungal peritonitis (FP) represents only a small proportion of overall PD-related peritonitis,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 it is associated with an extremely poor prognosis, characterized by high mortality rates, substantial PD technique failure, and a considerable increase in treatment burden.1 Minimizing the risks of development of FP is a key priority to improve patient outcomes, and identifying factors that predict FP-associated death is also a significant concern in treatment.

In the 1996 edition of the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD) recommendations on peritonitis,11 the topic of antifungal prophylaxis was introduced for the first time. In the subsequent updates in 2005 and 2010, it was suggested that antifungal prophylaxis might prevent some cases of Candida peritonitis in programs with high rates of FP.12,13 In the latest editions in 2016 and 2022, it has been recommended to co-prescribe an antifungal agent whenever PD patients receive an antibiotic course (evidence level 1B).14,15 However, debates on whether to follow the recommendation are still ongoing, and there are tremendous variations in actual practice at both the facility and country levels. As reported by the Peritoneal Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study, uptake of the strategy was remarkably low in programs across multiple countries and regions.16

Since 2016, we have adhered to the recommendation of antifungal prophylaxis as a secondary prevention in our program. As per protocol, oral fluconazole 100 mg daily during any antibiotic treatment is mandatory for PD patients without contraindications. The present retrospective observational study evaluates the incidence of FP over the last 2 decades at our center, with particular interest in the effects of antifungal prophylaxis on the development of FP and risk factors for mortality after FP occurrence.

Methods

Study Population

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at Ren Ji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All study procedures were in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ren Ji Hospital (LY2025-358-B). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of deidentified information.

The study included all adult PD patients who experienced FP between January 2004 and December 2024. It was further dichotomized into 2 periods, 2004-2015 and 2016-2024, to evaluate the longitudinal changes of FP incidence over time, particularly the diversity of the disease patterns before and after adoption of the antifungal prophylaxis policy. The information collected included the patients’ demographic features, etiology of kidney failure, comorbid condition status, PD vintage, residual kidney function measured within 3 months before FP onset, use of antibiotics before FP, bacterial peritonitis occurrence before FP, clinical manifestations, laboratory and microbiology characteristics, treatments, and outcomes. Residual kidney function was calculated as the mean of renal urea and creatinine clearance from a 24-hour urine collection. The adapted Charlson comorbidity index for patients with kidney failure17 was applied to indicate overall comorbid condition burden.

Diagnosis and Definitions

Diagnosis criteria and definitions related to peritonitis followed the ISPD guidelines.15 FP was verified by at least one of the following: a positive fungal culture from PD effluent or catheter, discovery of yeast by effluent smear, or fungus detected by peritoneum pathologic study. Secondary FP was defined as a fungal episode occurring during an antibiotic course for either bacterial peritonitis or other bacterial infections or within 3 months after completion of the course. Otherwise, an episode was classified as primary FP, including those in which fungus was the only organism or in which fungus and bacteria were simultaneously identified from the very first effluent culture after peritonitis onset. FP-associated death was in accordance with the ISPD definition,15 namely, mortality within 30 days of FP onset or during hospitalization due to FP.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the longitudinal changes in FP incidence, namely, the rate of overall FP and the rates stratified by primary and secondary FP. The temporal relationship between the incidence of FP and the adoption of the antifungal prophylaxis strategy was considered. The clinical course and outcomes of the FP cases as well as the risk factors for FP-associated death were also investigated.

Statistical Analyses

Data are presented as number (percentage) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation and median (interquartile range [IQR]) for normally and nonnormally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Trends of overall peritonitis rates and FP rates over time were analyzed using linear by linear association. Rates of FP, primary FP, and secondary FP were compared between 2004-2015 and 2016-2024 using Poisson regression. Comparisons of percentages were conducted by χ2 test. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify risk factors for FP-associated death, and variables with P values <0.05 in the univariable analyses were eligible for the stepwise multivariable model. Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS version 25.0, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Trends of Peritonitis and FP Incidence

From 2004 to 2024, there were 1,497 episodes of PD-related peritonitis among 2,482 patients. The overall peritonitis rate was 0.157 episodes/patient-year, showing a significant decline over time (P = 0.001) (Fig 1A). Specifically, there was a pronounced downward trend in annual rates during 2004-2015 (P = 0.015), whereas no remarkable change was observed during 2016-2024 (P = 0.799), but the peritonitis rate was significantly lower in the more recent period (0.134 episodes/patient-year vs 0.212 episodes/patient-year, P < 0.001; risk ratio, 0.63; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.51-0.78).

(B) Annual fungal peritonitis rate significantly declined during 2004-2024 (P = 0.019); (C) Annual secondary fungal peritonitis rate significantly declined during 2004-2024 (P = 0.012). (D) Annual primary fungal peritonitis rate remained stable during 2004-2024 (P = 0.902). Trends in rates were analyzed using linear by linear association.

A total of 62 FP episodes in 62 individuals were identified, representing 4.1% of the total peritonitis events. The characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. No concurrent fungal exit-site or tunnel infections were diagnosed. Most of the patients were receiving manual PD, except for 1, who was receiving automated PD. Conventional glucose-based PD fluids were used in all the patients. The overall FP rate was 0.0065 episodes/patient-year, and the annual rates ranged between 0.0013 and 0.0171 episodes/patient-year, presenting a discernible decrease over the entire study period (P = 0.019) (Fig 1B). Similar to the trends in peritonitis incidence, annual FP rates significantly decreased during 2004-2015 (P = 0.031) and then stabilized during 2016-2024 (P = 0.752). The average FP rate was marginally lower during 2016-2024 than during 2004-2015 (0.0058 episodes/patient-year vs 0.0087 episodes/patient-year, P = 0.096; risk ratio, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.42-1.07).

The series consisted of 32 primary FP episodes (52%) and 30 secondary FP episodes (48%). The incidence of secondary FP significantly attenuated throughout the entire study period (P = 0.012; Fig 1C), and the average secondary FP rate decreased from 0.0052 episodes/patient-year during 2004-2015 to 0.0022 episodes/patient-year during 2016-2024 (P = 0.016; risk ratio, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.21-0.86). Secondary FP accounted for 17 of 28 FP episodes before 2016, and 13 of 34 FP episodes from 2016 onward (61% vs 38%, P = 0.078). There was no marked change in primary FP incidence over the study period (P = 0.902; Fig 1D), and the average primary FP rates during 2004-2015 and 2016-2024 were 0.0035 and 0.0036 episodes/patient-year (P = 0.943; risk ratio, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.47-2.23), respectively. Etiology of FP

Among the primary FP cases, 20 episodes (63%) were attributed to violations of aseptic technique during PD exchange, 2 episodes (6%) were caused by intestinal perforation, and the causes remained indistinct in the other 10 episodes (31%). Concurrent identification of bacteria from the effluent cultures was verified in 5 primary FP cases (16%): 2 occurred before 2016 and 3 after 2016. The identified bacteria included coagulase-negative Staphylococci in 2 cases, Burkholderia cepacia in 1 case, and multiple enteric bacteria in 2 cases, which were due to intestinal perforation.

Among the secondary FP cases, 18 occurred during the treatment of bacterial peritonitis, 9 developed as recurrent peritonitis after bacterial peritonitis, and 3 developed after use of antibiotics for non-PD-related infections. The preceding bacterial peritonitis included 15 Gram-positive, 9 Gram-negative, 1 miscellaneous, and 2 culture-negative episodes. The median interval between diagnosis of secondary FP and the onset of preceding bacterial infections was 7 (IQR, 4-10) days. Antifungal prophylaxis was prescribed in none of the secondary FP episodes before 2016. It was not used in 5 of the 13 secondary FP episodes since 2016, and the reasons included prolonged QTc on electrocardiogram (n = 2), impaired hepatic function (n = 1), and initial treatment at other facilities (n = 2).

Treatments and Outcomes

Fluconazole was the most frequently used drug (n = 54) for initial antifungal therapy. Subsequent adjustments included amphotericin in 15 cases, voriconazole in 13 cases, caspofungin in 7 cases, and itraconazole in 1 case. Fifty-one patients (82%) had catheter removed 1 (IQR, 0-3) day after FP was diagnosed, and 10 patients (16%) died before PD catheter removal could be performed.

FP-associated death eventually occurred in 21 (34%) patients, with a median course of 20 (IQR, 13-44) days. Sepsis was the leading cause of mortality (n = 18), followed by myocardial infarction, sudden death, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage (n = 1 for each). For the survivors, the median antifungal duration after catheter removal was 25 (IQR, 20-40) days. One patient was cured with catheter in situ without interruption of PD, 1 terminated dialysis due to kidney recovery after catheter removal, and the remaining 39 (63%) patients were switched to permanent hemodialysis.

Risk Factors for FP-Associated Death

Univariable analyses revealed that age, PD vintage, Charlson comorbidity index, residual kidney function, C. parapsilosis as the sole causative fungus, and catheter removal within 1 day after FP diagnosis were related to FP-associated death. In multivariable analysis, a greater Charlson comorbidity index score (odds ratio [OR], 1.74; 95% CI, 1.10-2.74) was linked to FP-associated death, while C. parapsilosis as the sole causative fungus (OR, 0.17; 95% CI, 0.03-0.94) was associated with a lower risk of mortality (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study illustrates the longitudinal changes in incidence of PD-related FP, involving 62 cases at a large center across the past 21 years. There was a significant attenuation in FP incidence over time, which mirrored the trend seen in overall peritonitis incidence, and it was primarily attributed to the reduction in secondary FP. The results indicate a fundamental change in the disease pattern. In addition, our findings revealed that greater comorbid condition burden might increase the risk of FP-associated death, while C. parapsilosis as the sole causative fungus was associated with a decreased risk of death.

Although the study period was artificially divided into 2 segments, with the shift to antifungal prophylaxis as the cut-point, the process of improvement remains coherent. In more detail, both the incidences of peritonitis and FP declined substantially during 2004-2015 and then stabilized during 2016-2024 but at more favorable levels than in the previous period. Furthermore, the changes in incidence were clearly distinguishable between secondary and primary FP. The former exhibited a pronounced downward trend, especially after the implementation of the prophylactic strategy, whereas the latter showed no obvious fluctuation. The findings suggest that it is plausible that reducing the overall peritonitis rate and adhering to the antifungal prophylaxis strategy are 2 essential factors in reducing FP rates, particularly for secondary FP.

Conventionally, it is theorized that the development of FP involves fungal overgrowth in the gastrointestinal tract and compromised peritoneal defense, especially during peritonitis.18 Previous studies observed FP being commonly associated with recent antibiotic therapy, which selectively suppresses bacteria but promotes gastrointestinal fungal proliferation. Goldie et al19 showed that among 55 FP cases, 35 episodes were secondary to antibiotics within a month, while it was 48 episodes within the previous 6 months, primarily for bacterial peritonitis. Auricchio et al10 reported that only 21% of FP cases were not preceded by bacterial peritonitis. A registry data analysis demonstrated that the shorter the duration between an earlier peritonitis episode and a second one, the greater probability the second episode was of fungal origin.2

Although observational studies yield conflicting results, 2 randomized trials20,21 and a meta-analysis22 support the benefit of co-prescribing antifungal agents when antibiotics are administered, either oral nystatin or fluconazole, on the prevention of subsequent FP. This evidence led to ISPD recommendations on antifungal prophylaxis. However, substantial deviation from ISPD guidelines is common, and it is perceived that antifungal prophylaxis may be less beneficial in programs with inherently low baseline rates of FP.16

The proportion of FP in overall peritonitis events at our center (approximately 4%) aligns closely with contemporary studies.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 However, compared with rates reported by others, which typically ranged from 0.012 to 0.18 episodes/patient-year, we achieved an average FP rate of 0.0065 episodes/patient-year, which is among the lowest ever reported. After the introduction of antifungal prophylaxis, the incidence of FP dropped from an already low level of 0.0087 episodes/patient-year to a more favorable level of 0.0058 episodes/patient-year. This decrement was predominantly due to the decrease in secondary FP from 0.0052 episodes/patient-year to 0.0022 episodes/patient-year. Our results oppose the perception mentioned above, advocating that antifungal prophylaxis is effective in reducing the risk of FP, particularly secondary FP, even when the baseline FP rate is low.

Fluconazole is a widely used antifungal agent. There is currently no consensus on its prophylactic prescription. The randomized controlled trial by Restrepo et al21 used 200 mg every other day, and observational studies have suggested various dosages (50 mg or 200 mg every day, with or without a loading dose).23, 24, 25 We have applied a moderate dose of 100 mg per day, which has proven to be effective and safe. Daily usage may be helpful to improve adherence. A recent meta-analysis also supports prescribing fluconazole once a day to prevent secondary FP.26 So far, no increase in fluconazole-resistant species has been observed.

On the other hand, the primary FP rates did not show a significant change, appearing to be uninfluenced by the decline in overall peritonitis or the antifungal prophylaxis strategy. This implicates diversity in mechanisms underlying primary and secondary FP. Our center is located in a warm and moist area where fungal species are disseminated in the environment, and as we found, C. parapsilosis, a skin commensal, was the most frequent causative fungus. Violations of aseptic technique and contamination during PD exchanges may, at least partially, account for the development of FP.27,28 This emphasizes the importance of following relevant aseptic techniques to prevent FP. Furthermore, in line with the prespecified definition, we included 2 FP cases due to intestinal perforation, which, according to the latest ISPD guidelines,15 should be grouped as enteric peritonitis in the primary category. It highlights the complexity of the etiology of FP, and further research into the comprehensive mechanisms of primary FP is warranted.

The mortality in the present study remained significant, which is comparable to the other reports from East Asia.9,29 Our results underpin the importance of comorbid condition burden and causative organisms on FP-associated death. Nadeau-Fredette et al6 revealed that nonsurvivors of FP had a greater Charlson comorbidity index. The relatively larger sample size of our study allowed us to verify the result by multivariable analysis. It is reasonable to assume connections between comorbid condition status and other potential factors contributing to death, including aging, dialysis vintage, and residual kidney function. Moreover, in our series, for those who died without catheter removal, the decision to leave the catheter in situ was usually based on their excessive number of comorbid conditions. Lo et al30 reported that C. parapsilosis infection increased the risk of death; however, this result could be confounded by limited sample size. C. parapsilosis exhibits the lowest biofilm production capacity, an essential virulence factor of fungi, among various Candida spp.,27 which may explain the relatively benign outcomes.

There are limitations to this study. First, although the decline in secondary FP incidence after the implementation of antifungal prophylaxis was definite, causality cannot be established given the retrospective design. However, we provide a relevant example showing that adherence to the strategy is effective even when the baseline FP rate is low. Second, there may be disagreement on the definitions of primary and secondary FP. As authoritative criteria for discrimination are still lacking, we defined these subtypes according to whether there was exposure to antibiotics within 3 months before FP onset to assure uniqueness in terminology, and the sophisticated follow-up database enabled us to obviate major incompleteness in data collection and misclassification. Third, potential factors related to FP-associated death, such as details regarding use of antifungal medications,31 were absent in the analysis; in addition, due to the single-center design and rarity of FP, the sample size and the number of mortality events in the study were limited, which may increase the risk of overfitting and unstable coefficient estimates in the multivariable model. Therefore, the analysis was of an exploratory nature, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted.

In conclusion, the incidence of FP declined remarkably at our center over 21 years. This reduction was primarily attributable to the decrease in secondary FP. The results underline the importance of antifungal prophylaxis, even in programs with a low baseline FP rate. In contrast, primary FP remains an issue, outweighing secondary FP in recent years. Elucidation of the underlying mechanisms and establishment of effective preventive measures are warranted. The mortality after FP occurrence is still significant. In addition to timely catheter removal and targeted, prolonged antifungal therapy, more attention should be given to patients with a high comorbid condition burden and those cases complicated by infections with certain pathogens to improve outcomes.
