Differential geometry of curves in dual space
Rafael L’́opez*

Abstract
. We introduce the Frenet theory of curves in dual space 𝔻 3 \mathbb{D}^{3} . After defining the curvature and the torsion of a curve, we classify all curves in dual plane with constant curvature. We also establish the fundamental theorem of existence in the theory of dual curves, proving that there is a dual curve with prescribed curvature and torsion. Finally we classify all dual curves with constant curvature and torsion.

Primary 53A10; Secondary 53C21, 53C42

1.
Introduction and the problem of reparametrization

The study of curves in Riemannian spaces is one of the main topics in classical differential geometry. The geometry of these curves is studied thanks to its Frenet frame and the notion of curvature. In
3
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the curvature and the torsion of a curve determine, up to rigid motions of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the shape of the curve. In this paper we extend this theory to curves in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
.

Dual numbers were introduced by William Clifford in 1873
[
7
]
. The set of dual numbers is the algebra
\mathbb{D}=\{x+\varepsilon y\colon x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}
where
\varepsilon^{2}=0
. Eduard Study used dual numbers to represent lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as follows. Let
\mathbb{D}^{3}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the space of dual vectors. Let
L=p+\langle v\rangle
denote a line of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a point of
L
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the direction vector of
L
, which we assume unitary,
|v|=1
. We associate to
L
the dual vector
v+\varepsilon v^{*}\in\mathbb{D}^{3}
, where
v^{*}=p\times v
is the moment of
L
. Notice that
v^{*}
does not depend on the choice of the point
p
of
L
. Moreover,
v
and
v^{*}
are orthogonal vectors
[
17
]
. Also Study represented the relative displacement and orientation of two lines in space by dual numbers. So, if
\theta
is the angle between their direction vectors and
d
is the shortest distance between them, both numbers are formally written as the dual number
\theta+\varepsilon d
. Thanks to this type of representations of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, some properties of the kinematics of curves can be studied in terms of dual numbers. This has attracted the interest in CADG, robotics and analysis of the mechanic of mechanisms: see for example,
[
1
,
3
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

In this paper, we study smooth curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
from the viewpoint of differential geometry. For a smooth curve, we mean a differentiable map
\gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{D}^{3}
. If we write
\gamma
in its real and dual part, then
\gamma(t)=\alpha(t)+\varepsilon\beta(t)
, where
\alpha,\beta\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
are smooth curves and the derivative of
\gamma
is
\gamma^{\prime}(t)=\alpha^{\prime}(t)+\varepsilon\beta^{\prime}(t)
. The metric in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
is defined by extending the Euclidean metric
\langle,\rangle
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by bilinearity and using
\varepsilon^{2}=0
,

\langle a_{1}+\varepsilon b_{1},a_{2}+\varepsilon b_{2}\rangle=\langle a_{1},b_{1}\rangle+\varepsilon(\langle a_{1},b_{2}\rangle+\langle b_{1},a_{2}\rangle),

where
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
i=1,2
.
The purpose of this paper is to give a theory of Frenet frame which it allows a definition of curvature and torsion for a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. Recently, there is a certain literature in the study of curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
from this viewpoint. However, and in opinion of the author, some of these studies suffer some confusion about what variable is being considered in the curve and we now explain in detail.

The problem finds if one considers curves in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
of real variable or curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
whose variable is a dual number, that is, of dual parameter. Both objects are different. In the first case we are in the situation of an immersion of an interval
I
of
\mathbb{R}
in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. This corresponds with a pair of curves of real variable in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In the second case, since a dual parameter corresponds to a pair of real numbers, we have an immersion of a domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. In this case, a curve of dual parameter represents a pair of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In consequence, the concept of differentiability used in each case is different. In the first situation, differentiability is the usual notion of a function of real variable which extends in a natural way to the real and dual parts of the curve. However, if we consider curves with dual parameter, the differentiability is completely different.
The notion of differentiability for functions defined in domains of
\mathbb{D}
follows the same steps that holomorphic functions and similar Cauchy-Riemann equations appear when this theory is developed
[
18
]
.

We can compare the last situation of curves with dual parameter if we replace the set of dual numbers by the set of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
,
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}
,
i^{2}=-1
. Differentiability of functions between a domain of
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{C}^{3}
means that the functions are holomorphic. The study of holomorphic curves in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
is now attracting mathematicians since the papers of Calabi and Lawson
[
5
,
6
,
13
]
. We also point out a remarkable paper of Bryant where he studied holomorphic curves in the
6
-sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{6}
by means of a type of holomorphic Frenet frame defining a curvature and torsion of holomorphic curves
[
4
]
. Notice that curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
with dual parameter are not the object of this paper.

Coming back again with curves in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
, surely the source of misunderstanding finds in
[
18
]
, when the author reparametrizes a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
of real variable in order to have unitary speed vectors. More precisely, following
[
18
, p. 147]
, the problem arises when a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
of real variable is not parametrized by arc-length. Let
\gamma(s)=\alpha(s)+\varepsilon\beta(s)
,
s\in I\subset\mathbb{R}
a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
where
|\alpha^{\prime}(s)|=1
for all
s\in I
. However
|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|=1+\varepsilon\langle\alpha^{\prime}(s),\beta^{\prime}(s)\rangle
. By repeating here the computations done in
[
18
]
, the author defines the so-called ‘dual arc-length parameter’
\tilde{s}
by

\tilde{s}=\int_{0}^{s}|\gamma^{\prime}(\sigma)|\,d\sigma=\int_{0}^{s}(1+\varepsilon\langle\alpha^{\prime}(\sigma),\beta^{\prime}(\sigma)\rangle\,d\sigma=s+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{s}\langle\alpha^{\prime}(\sigma),\beta^{\prime}(\sigma)\rangle\,d\sigma.

(1.1)

But this identity does not define a dual variable because
\tilde{s}
is a function of
s
,
\tilde{s}=\tilde{s}(s)
. In the simple case that
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
are orthogonal, we have
\tilde{s}=s+\varepsilon
and
\tilde{s}(s)
only describes the line
y=0
of
\mathbb{D}
. Other example shows the same problem. If, for instance,
\gamma(s)=(\cos s,\sin s,0)+\varepsilon(s,0,s)
, then
\tilde{s}=s+\varepsilon\sin s
, which it does not cover
\mathbb{D}
and thus it is not a dual variable. This confusion has been followed by many authors attempting a Frenet theory for functions that initially were of a real variable and next, after (
1.1
), these functions were transformed of dual variable: see for example,
[
8
,
14
,
15
,
21
,
22
]
.

In order to establish a theory of Frenet frame for curves in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
the problem finds in those dual curves that are not parametrized by arc-length. The issue is that not every dual curve
\gamma(t)=\alpha(t)+\varepsilon\beta(t)
can be reparametrized by arc-length and extra conditions to
\alpha
and
\beta
must be assumed. This is explained at the beginning of Sect.
2
. The conditions of a dual curve to have reparametrizations by arc-length are the following:

(1)

The real part
\alpha
of the curve must be a regular curve.

(2)

The velocities vectors
\alpha^{\prime}(t)
and
\beta^{\prime}(t)
must be orthogonal for all
t\in I
.

Although the second condition may be restrictive, we have shown that orthogonality is natural in the context of kinematics for the description of lines because the director vector
v
of a line and its moment
v^{*}
are orthogonal vectors. Therefore, the theory of Frenet curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
will be developed under both conditions.

The organization in this paper is the following. In Sect.
2
, we show the problem of reparametrizations by arc-length and we give how to solve up. Once the objects of study have been well established, we define the Frenet frame of a curve in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. This allows to give the definition of curvature and torsion. With both concepts, it is natural to propose similar questions motivated by the classical theory of curves in Euclidean space. In Sect.
3
, we classify the curves with constant curvature in the dual plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, proving that the real part is a circle and the dual part is a curve of spiral type (Thm.
3.1
). In Sect.
4
, we establish a theorem of existence of dual curves when the curvature and the torsion are prescribed. More precisely, we prove that given two differentiable functions
k_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
defined in some interval of
\mathbb{R}
, there exists a dual curve of curvature and torsion
k_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
, respectively (Thm.
4.1
). We show some explicit examples in Sect.
5
as for examples, curves with zero torsion. Uniqueness cannot be obtained as in Euclidean space, that is, non-congruent curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
in the Euclidean sense, may have the same curvature and torsion. Finally in Sect.
6
, we obtain explicitly the parametrizations of all dual curves with constant curvature and constant torsion (Thm.
6.1
).

A last remark. Along this paper we will call
dual curves
to refer curves of real variable in the dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. A more appropriate terminology may be a
curve in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
such as it appears in the title of this paper. A dual curve would be a term employed for curves with dual parameter. However, it is common in the literature the use of terms as spherical curves, hyperbolic curves or Lorentzian curves to indicate curves of real variable whose image is include in spheres, hyperbolic spaces or Lorentzian spaces, respectively. For this reason we also employ the terminology of dual curve as curve in dual space.

2.
The Frenet frame of dual curves

In this section, we assign a Frenet frame for a curve in dual space and, consequently, we will define the curvature and the torsion of a curve. For basic properties of dual numbers, we refer to the reader to
[
18
]
. This first part of this section holds for curves of real parameter on
n
-dimensional space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
.

We show the problem of reparametrization by arc-length of a dual curve. Let
\gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{D}^{n}
be a differentiable map,
\gamma=\gamma(t)
. Let us write
\gamma=\alpha+\varepsilon\beta
, where
\alpha,\beta\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the real and dual parts of
\gamma
, respectively. Then

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=\alpha^{\prime}(t)+\varepsilon\beta^{\prime}(t)
. For convenience, we drop the variable of
\gamma
if it is understood. We will assume that
\gamma
is a regular curve, that is,
\gamma^{\prime}(t)\not=0
for all
t\in I
. In order to define the Frenet frame, we need that the curve is parametrized by arc-length. We say that
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length if
\langle\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rangle=1
for all
t\in I
. Since

\langle\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rangle=\langle\alpha^{\prime}(t),\alpha^{\prime}(t)\rangle+2\varepsilon\langle\alpha^{\prime}(t),\beta^{\prime}(t)\rangle,

this implies

\langle\alpha^{\prime}(t),\alpha^{\prime}(t)\rangle=1,\quad\langle\alpha^{\prime}(t),\beta^{\prime}(t)\rangle=0,\quad t\in I.

(2.1)

Therefore necessary conditions for a curve
\gamma
to be parametrized by arc-length is that
\alpha
is a regular curve and that the velocities of the real and dual parts must be orthogonal. If the curve is not parametrized by arc-length, then we reparametrize
\gamma
in order to get it. As usually, a reparametrization of
\gamma
is a change of the parameter
t
by
s
by means of a diffeomorphism
\phi\colon J\subset\mathbb{R}\to I
between intervals of
\mathbb{R}
,
t=\phi(s)
. A reparametrization of
\gamma
is the curve
\tilde{\gamma}(s)=\gamma(\phi(s))
,
s\in J
. If we impose the condition that
|\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)|=1
for all
s\in J
, the chain rule gives

\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(s)\gamma^{\prime}(\phi(s))=\phi^{\prime}(s)\left(\alpha^{\prime}(\phi(s))+\varepsilon\beta^{\prime}(\phi(s))\right).

(2.2)

Using that
\phi^{\prime}\not=0
, the condition
\langle\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\rangle=1
is equivalent to

\phi^{\prime}(s)^{2}\langle\alpha^{\prime}(\phi(s)),\alpha^{\prime}(\phi(s))\rangle=1,\quad\langle\alpha^{\prime}(\phi(s)),\beta^{\prime}(\phi(s))\rangle=0.

As a consequence, we see that a reparametrization of
\gamma
allows to get unitary tangent vector for the real part of
\gamma
if
\alpha^{\prime}\not=0
, that is, if
\alpha
is regular. However, the orthogonality condition (
2.1
) remains invariant and it must be assumed from the beginning. The study of dual curves from a differential geometry viewpoint makes necessary both conditions. We summarize the above calculations in the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{D}^{n}
be a curve,
\gamma(t)=\alpha(t)+\varepsilon\beta(t)
. If
\alpha
is a regular curve and
\langle\alpha^{\prime}(t),\beta^{\prime}(t)\rangle=0
for all
t\in I
, then there is a reparametrization of
\gamma
by arc-length.

Example
1
.

The following curves cannot be parametrized by arc-length.

(1)

\gamma(t)=(0,0,1)+\varepsilon(t,0,0)
. The real part is not a regular curve even though the dual part is a regular curve.

(2)

\gamma(t)=(t,0,0)+\varepsilon(t,t,0)
. The vectors
\alpha^{\prime}(t)=(1,0,0)
and
\beta^{\prime}(t)=(1,1,0)
are not orthogonal.

Example
2
.

Let
\gamma(t)=(\cos t^{2},\sin t^{2},0)+\varepsilon(0,0,\cos t)
,
t\in I=(0,\infty)
. We have

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=2t(-\sin t^{2},\cos t^{2},0)-\varepsilon(0,0,\sin t)
. The real part
\alpha
is regular because
|\alpha^{\prime}(t)|=2t
. Moreover,
\langle\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\rangle=0
. Thus
\gamma
satisfies the condition of Prop.
2.1
. We reparametrize
\alpha
by arc-length obtaining
\phi(s)=\sqrt{s}
. Then the desired reparametrization of
\gamma
is

\tilde{\gamma}(s)=\gamma(\sqrt{s})=(\cos s,\sin s,0)+\varepsilon(0,0,\cos(\sqrt{s})).

Proposition
2.1
says that if a curve
\gamma
is not parametrized by arc-length, then reparametrizing only its real part, we have a reparametrization by arc-length of the dual curve
\gamma
. This reparametrization of
\gamma
also reparametrizes the dual part
\beta
. However it is not possible, in general, that the curve
\beta
is parametrized by arc-length, that is, there is not a common parameter
s
which it is the arc-length parameter for both curves
\alpha
and
\beta
. Only in this case, we have the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A curve
\gamma
in the dual space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
parametrized by arc-length is said to be normalized if
\beta
is parametrized by arc-length.

We begin with the Frenet theory for dual curves. Let
\gamma
be a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{n}
parametrized by arc-length. Define the
tangent vector
of
\gamma
as

T(s)=\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\alpha^{\prime}(s)+\varepsilon\beta^{\prime}(s).

As usually, the unit normal vector of
\gamma
is defined by differentiating
\langle T(s),T(s)\rangle=1
, obtaining
\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s),T(s)\rangle=0
. As in the Euclidean case, the normal vector is defined once we discard the case
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=0
for all
s\in I
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\gamma
be a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{n}
parametrized by arc length. Then
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=0
for all
s\in I
if and only if
\gamma
is a straight line of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
. Moreover, the straight lines
\alpha
and
\beta
are orthogonal.

Proof.

Since
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=\alpha^{\prime\prime}(s)+\varepsilon\beta^{\prime\prime}(s)
, the condition
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=0
is equivalent to
\alpha^{\prime\prime}(s)=0
and
\beta^{\prime\prime}(s)=0
everywhere, that is,
\alpha
and
\beta
are straight lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, hence the result. The last statement is immediate.
∎

From now, we will consider curves in
3
-dimensional dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. Let
\gamma
be a dual curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
such that
|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)|\not=0
everywhere. Define the
normal vector
of
\gamma
by

N(s)=\frac{1}{|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)|}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s).

Definition 2
.

If
\gamma
is a dual curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
parametrized by arc-length, the
curvature
of
\gamma
is defined by

\kappa(s)=|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)|.

Finally we define the binormal vector and the torsion.

Definition 3
.

If
\gamma
is a dual curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
parametrized by arc-length, the
binormal vector

B
of
\gamma
is defined by
B(s)=T(s)\times N(s)
and the torsion
\tau
by

\tau=\langle N^{\prime},B\rangle.

Here
\times
is the cross product in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
which is defined by bilinearity from the cross product of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
18
, p. 142]
. Notice that
|B|=1
and
B
is orthogonal to
T
and
N
. The basis
\{T,N,B\}
is orthonormal and it is called the
Frenet frame
of
\gamma
. It is also immediate the Frenet equations

\begin{split}T^{\prime}(s)=&\kappa(s)N(s),\\
N^{\prime}(s)=&-\kappa(s)T(s)+\tau(s)B(s),\\
B^{\prime}(s)=&\qquad-\tau(s)N(s).\end{split}

(2.3)

We can express the normal and binormal vectors of
\gamma
as well as its curvature and torsion in terms of that of
\alpha
because
\alpha
is also parametrized by arc-length. We denote the Frenet frame of
\alpha
by
\{T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}\}
and
\kappa_{\alpha}
and
\tau_{\alpha}
stand for its curvature and torsion respectively. The following result is a consequence of straightforward computations.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\gamma=\alpha+\varepsilon\beta
be a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
parametrized by arc-length. Then

\begin{split}T&=T_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\beta^{\prime},\\
N&=N_{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\left(\beta^{\prime\prime}-\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle N_{\alpha}\right),\\
B&=B_{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\left(T_{\alpha}\times\beta^{\prime\prime}+\kappa_{\alpha}\beta^{\prime}\times N_{\alpha}-\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle B_{\alpha}\right),\\
\kappa&=\kappa_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle,\\
\tau&=\tau_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\frac{1}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\left(\langle\beta^{\prime\prime\prime}-\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\beta^{\prime\prime}+\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}\beta^{\prime},B_{\alpha}\rangle-\tau_{\alpha}\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle\right).\end{split}

(2.4)

In case that
\gamma
is a normalized curve, the vectors
\beta^{\prime}
,
\beta^{\prime\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime\prime\prime}
can be expressed in terms of the Frenet frame
\{T_{\beta},N_{\beta},B_{\beta}\}
of
\beta
and of its curvature
\kappa_{\beta}
and torsion
\tau_{\beta}
.

Corollary 2.3.1
.

Let
\gamma=\alpha+\varepsilon\beta
be a normalized curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. Then

\begin{split}T&=T_{\alpha}+\varepsilon T_{\beta},\\
N&=N_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\frac{\kappa_{\beta}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\left(N_{\beta}-\langle N_{\alpha},N_{\beta}\rangle N_{\alpha}\right),\\
B&=B_{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\left(\kappa_{\beta}T_{\alpha}\times N_{\beta}+\kappa_{\alpha}T_{\beta}\times N_{\alpha}-\kappa_{\beta}\langle N_{\alpha},N_{\beta}\rangle B_{\alpha}\right),\\
\kappa&=\kappa_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\kappa_{\beta}\langle N_{\alpha},N_{\beta}\rangle,\\
\tau&=\tau_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\frac{1}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\langle(\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}-\kappa_{\beta}^{2})T_{\beta}+\kappa_{\alpha}(\frac{\kappa_{\beta}}{\kappa_{\alpha}})^{\prime}N_{\beta}+\kappa_{\beta}\tau_{\beta}B_{\beta},B_{\alpha}\rangle\\
&-\varepsilon\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\kappa_{\beta}\langle N_{\beta},N_{\alpha}\rangle\end{split}

Example
3
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(\cos s,\sin s,0)+\varepsilon(0,0,s)
. Then
\gamma
is normalized and we have

\begin{split}T(s)&=(-\sin s,\cos s,0)+\varepsilon(0,0,1),\\
N(s)&=(-\cos s,-\sin s,0),\\
B(s)&=(0,0,1)+\varepsilon(\sin s,-\cos s,0).\end{split}

Then
\kappa=1
. Since
N^{\prime}(s)=(\sin s,-\cos s,0)
, we have
\tau=\langle N^{\prime},B\rangle=\varepsilon
. This curve has constant curvature and torsion. Moreover,
\kappa
is a real number and
\tau
is a pure dual number.

Example
4
.

Let
\alpha
be a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
parametrized by arc length and consider the dual curve
\gamma(s)=\alpha(s)+\varepsilon\alpha^{\prime}(s)
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}+\varepsilon\alpha^{\prime\prime}
, then
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length because
\langle\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\rangle=0
. A computation of the Frenet frame gives

\begin{split}T&=T_{\alpha}+\varepsilon T_{\alpha}^{\prime},\\
N&=N_{\alpha}+\varepsilon N_{\alpha}^{\prime},\\
B&=B_{\alpha}+\varepsilon B_{\alpha}^{\prime}.\end{split}

It is then immediate

\begin{split}\kappa&=\kappa_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\kappa_{\alpha}^{\prime},\\
\tau&=\tau_{\alpha}+\varepsilon\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}.\end{split}

Example
5
.

Let
\gamma(s)=\alpha(s)+\varepsilon\alpha^{\prime}(s)
, where
\alpha
is the circular helix
\alpha(s)=(r\cos(s/m),r\sin(s/m),hs/m)
, where
r,h\in\mathbb{R}
,
r>0
and
m=\sqrt{r^{2}+h^{2}}
. We know
\kappa_{\alpha}=r/m^{2}
and
\tau=h/m^{2}
. For the dual curve
\gamma(s)=\alpha(s)+\varepsilon\alpha^{\prime}(s)
we have
\kappa=\kappa_{\alpha}
and
\tau=\tau_{\alpha}
.

Remark 1
.

Formulas (
2.4
) show that the dual curvature and dual torsion have a behaviour with respect to dilations similar as in Euclidean space. To be precise, let
\lambda>0
. Given a dual curve
\gamma
, consider

\tilde{\gamma}(s)=\lambda\gamma(\frac{s}{\lambda})=\tilde{\alpha}(s)+\varepsilon\tilde{\beta}(s)=\lambda\alpha(\frac{s}{\lambda})+\varepsilon\lambda\beta(\frac{s}{\lambda}).

Then
\tilde{\gamma}
is also parametrized by arc-length.
Since
\tilde{\beta}^{(n)}(s)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{n-1}}\beta^{(n)}(s/\lambda)
, then it is immediate

\kappa_{\tilde{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\kappa_{\gamma},\quad\tau_{\tilde{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\tau_{\gamma}.

3.
Planar curves with constant curvature

Once we have established a theory of Frenet frame for curves in dual space, it is natural to give results of classification according the curvature and the torsion. For example, we can cask for those curves with constant curvature or with constant torsion. In Euclidean space, there are not explicit parametrizations of curves with constant curvature or curves with constant torsion. See for example
[
2
,
16
,
19
]
. In the case of dual curves, the problem is more difficult. For instance, if the dual curvature
\kappa
is constant, then the real part of the curve has constant curvature, which it is already a problem in Euclidean space. Moreover, we need to find the dual part of the curve.

However, and as in the Euclidean space, we can restrict the problem to curves in the dual plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. If in the Euclidean plane the classification is well known (circles), in this section we solve the analogous problem in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
determining explicitly the dual part, which it will be a type of spiral curve.

The theory developed until here for curves in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
also holds for curves in the dual plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
after the convenient changes. In such a case, there is no a notion of binormal vector and torsion. We find the classification of the planar curves with constant curvature in the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
be a curve parametrized by arc-length. If its curvature
\kappa
is constant, then up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\alpha
is a circle of radius
r>0
,

\alpha(s)=r(\cos\frac{s}{r},\sin\frac{s}{r}).

(3.1)

Moreover, the dual part
\beta
is parametrized by

4.
Theorem of existence of dual curves

In the theory of curves in Euclidean space it is known that given two smooth functions
k_{0},\tau_{0}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
, with
k_{0}>0
, there is a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose curvature is
k_{0}
and torsion is
\tau_{0}
. Moreover, two curves with the same curvature and torsion coincide up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It is natural to ask if the same situation occurs for dual curves. We have pointed out in Rem.
2
that uniqueness in the Euclidean sense is not true, that is, if two curves
\gamma_{i}(s)=\alpha_{i}(s)+\varepsilon\beta_{i}(s)
,
i=1,2
, in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
have the same curvature, they are not necessarily congruent in the sense that there is a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
carrying
\alpha_{1}
in
\alpha_{2}
and
\beta_{1}
in
\beta_{2}
. The same observation holds for curves in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
.

The question of existence is the following: given two smooth functions
k_{0},\tau_{0}\colon I\to\mathbb{D}
, with
\textrm{Re}(k_{0})>0
, is there a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
whose curvature and torsion are
k_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
, respectively?

Since the real parts of
\kappa
and
\tau
in (
2.4
) coincide with the curvature and the torsion of the real part
\alpha
of
\gamma
, then
\alpha
can be found by the classical theory of Euclidean curves. However, the problem acquires an extra difficulty in finding the dual part because the curve
\beta
has the property to be orthogonal to
\alpha
. We look the problem in the dual plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

Let
k_{0}=k_{1}+\varepsilon k_{2}
be a smooth function in
\mathbb{D}
such that
k_{1}>0
. We ask for the existence of a dual curve in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
with curvature
k_{0}
. According to (
2.4
), we must have
\kappa_{\alpha}=k_{1}
and
k_{2}=\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle
. Thus,
\alpha
is a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with curvature
k_{1}
. The existence of
\alpha
is assured by standard theory. Once we have the curve
\alpha
, we know its normal vector
N_{\alpha}
. It remains to solve the equation

\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle=k_{2}.

(4.1)

Notice that (
4.1
) is an ODE whose solution is assured. However, this is not enough because it may occur that
\beta
is not orthogonal to
\alpha
, as it is required. To be precise, since (
4.1
) is of second order, the solution has integration constants which must be chosen in order to ensure orthogonality of
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
. We see an example.

Example
6
.

Let
k_{0}=1+\varepsilon
. For the curve
\gamma=\alpha+\varepsilon\beta
, we know
\alpha(s)=(\cos s,\sin s)
. Since
\beta^{\prime}
is orthogonal to
T_{\alpha}
, then
\beta^{\prime}=\lambda N_{\alpha}
for some function
\lambda
. Then
\beta^{\prime\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}N_{\alpha}-\lambda T_{\alpha}
. Thus

\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle=1
writes as
\lambda^{\prime}=1
and this gives
\lambda(s)=s+c
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies

\beta^{\prime}(s)=-(s+c_{2})(\cos s,\sin s),\quad c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

An integration of this equation gives the parametrization (
3.2
).

This example shows the strategy to follows. In the case that
\gamma
is a planar dual curve,
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
, it is possible to give an explicit constructive method to find the curve
\gamma
, answering to the initial question of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
k_{0}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function with
\textrm{Re}(k_{0})>0
, then there exist a curve
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
whose curvature is
k_{0}(s)
. The real part of
\gamma
is unique up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

If
k_{0}(s)=k_{1}(s)+\varepsilon k_{2}(s)
, using (
2.4
), we require to solve the system

\begin{split}\kappa_{\alpha}&=k_{1},\\
\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle&=k_{2}.\end{split}

(4.2)

From the first equation, we consider a curve
\alpha\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose curvature
\kappa_{\alpha}
is
k_{1}
. This curve is determined up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The solution of the first equation is given after initial conditions of
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
at a given point
s=s_{0}\in I
. Furthermore, from the local theory of Euclidean planar curves, it is known that if we define

\theta(s)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}k_{1}(u)\,du,

the curve
\alpha
defined by

\alpha(s)=\left(\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\cos\theta(u)\,du,\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\sin\theta(u)\,du\right)

(4.3)

has curvature
\kappa_{\alpha}=\theta^{\prime}
. Once we know the curve
\alpha
we are going to find
\beta
. Because
\beta^{\prime}
is orthogonal to
\alpha^{\prime}
, we write

\beta^{\prime}(s)=\lambda(s)N_{\alpha}(s),

where
\lambda
is determined by the second equation of (
4.2
). Indeed,
\beta^{\prime\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}N_{\alpha}-\lambda k_{1}\alpha^{\prime}
. Thus the condition
\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle=k_{2}
is

\lambda^{\prime}(s)=k_{2}(s).

This gives
\lambda(s)=\int^{s}_{s_{0}}k_{2}(u)\ du
. Finally the curve
\beta
is obtained by solving the ODE

\beta^{\prime}(s)=\left(\int^{s}_{s_{0}}k_{2}(u)\ du\right)N_{\alpha}(s).

(4.4)

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Given differentiable functions
k_{0}=k_{0}(s)
, with
\textrm{Re}(k_{0})>0
and
\tau_{0}=\tau_{0}(s)
,
s\in I\subset\mathbb{R}

\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{D}^{3}

is the arc length and

and
\tau_{0}
are the curvature and torsion of

is unique up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

Proof.

Let

k_{0}(s)=k_{1}(s)+\varepsilon k_{2}(s),k_{1}(s)>0,\qquad\tau_{0}(s)=\tau_{1}(s)+\varepsilon\tau_{2}(s).

(4.5)

If
\gamma=\alpha+\varepsilon\beta
is a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
with curvature
k_{0}
and torsion
\tau_{0}
, by the formulas (
2.4
), we deduce that
\alpha
is a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose curvature and torsion are
\kappa_{\alpha}=k_{1}
and
\tau_{\alpha}=\tau_{1}
, respectively. This determines the real part of
\gamma
. Moreover,
\alpha
is unique up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, proving the last statement of theorem.

Following (
2.4
), the curve
\beta
that we are looking for satisfies

\left\{\begin{split}k_{2}&=\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle,\\
\tau_{2}&=\frac{1}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\left(\langle\beta^{\prime\prime\prime}-\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\beta^{\prime\prime}+\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}\beta^{\prime},B_{\alpha}\rangle-\tau_{\alpha}\langle\beta^{\prime\prime},N_{\alpha}\rangle\right).\end{split}\right.

(4.6)

Since

is orthogonal to

for all

, then there are two functions

and

to determine such that

A computation of
\beta^{\prime\prime}
and

gives:

\begin{split}\beta^{\prime\prime}&=-\kappa_{\alpha}x\,T_{\alpha}+(x^{\prime}-\tau_{\alpha}y)N_{\alpha}+(x\tau_{\alpha}+y^{\prime})B_{\alpha},\\
\beta^{\prime\prime\prime}&=(-2\kappa_{\alpha}x^{\prime}-x\kappa_{\alpha}^{\prime}+\kappa_{\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}y)T_{\alpha}+(x^{\prime\prime}-x\left(\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\right)-2\tau_{\alpha}y^{\prime}-y\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime})N_{\alpha}\\

Computing (
4.6

\left\{\begin{split}k_{2}&=x^{\prime}-y\tau_{\alpha},\\
\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}\tau_{2}&=\kappa_{\alpha}y^{\prime\prime}+\kappa_{\alpha}\left(\tau_{\alpha}x^{\prime}+x\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}\right)-\kappa_{\alpha}^{\prime}\left(\tau_{\alpha}x+y^{\prime}\right)+\kappa_{\alpha}^{3}y.\end{split}\right.

) after initial conditions are given.
If

and

are solutions of (
4.8

\beta
is obtained by solving (
4.7
). This concludes the proof.

7.
Conclusions and outlook

This work presents the Frenet theory of curves of real parameter in dual space
\mathbb{D}^{3}
. In the Introduction, we have shown some misleading in the literature concerning to the reparametrization by arc-length in the sense that it is not possible to reparametrize a curve in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
of real parameter and to convert it in a curve with dual parameter. Specially, we have emphasized that the study of curves in
\mathbb{D}^{3}
with dual parameter is completely different and, possibly, more difficult according with its analogy of holomorphic curves.

For dual curves with real parameter, not every dual curve can be reparametrized by arc-length and an orthogonality condition between the velocities of the real and dual parts has to be imposed. The Frenet theory of dual curves allows to obtain some extension of some results of Euclidean curves. In general, this type of results will be more difficult than their analogs in Euclidean space because of the existence of the dual part as well as the orthogonality condition with the real part. This is clearly shown in Thm.
4.1
when we assuming that the torsion of the curve is zero everywhere where it was not possible to determine explicitly the dual part of the curve. In contrast, this shows that the theory of dual curves is richer than its analogue of Euclidean spaces.

The present work also confirms that a theory of differential geometry of dual curves can be developed in future works. For example, it would be important to have a result of uniqueness of Thm.
4.2
in terms of dual geometry.
