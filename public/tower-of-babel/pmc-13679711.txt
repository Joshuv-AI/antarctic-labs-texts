Tapping into the micro-sites in multiwalled carbon nanotube sheets: nanofibers versus trunk deagglomeration for electrodes in redox flow batteries
Authors: Ibrahim Mustafa, Maryam AlNahyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We demonstrate an enhanced micro-site exposure capability to tap into the nanofibers as opposed to trunks consisting of Multiwalled Carbon Nanotube (MWCNT) bundles. We show this by accelerating the replenishment of the under exposed material within the active sonication zone during MWCNT sheet fabrication. Because high intensity acoustic cavitation (sonication) generates transient collapsing microscopic bubbles, control of deagglomeration quality becomes challenging. We herein demonstrate the ability of hydrodynamic optimization in providing improved deagglomeration, whilst reducing the MWCNT quality degradation (typically induced by sonication processes). The true value of this hydrodynamic investigation is revealed in the optimized electrochemical kinetics and enhanced apparent diffusion, which points to an optimized and un-collapsed porous network. Optimized electrodes achieved up to a 2.5-fold higher BET surface area (280.0 m2 g−1) and up to a 10.33-fold higher roughness factor (259.4). Consequently, charge transfer resistance dropped 5.9-fold for the negative (V3+/V2+) and 1.9-fold for the positive (VO2+/VO2+) half-cell. Kinetic rate constants increased 2.0-fold for V3+/V2+ and up to 4.9-fold for VO2+/VO2+, alongside a 3.9-fold rise in positive species diffusivity.

Introduction

The global demand for scalable and high-efficiency energy storage technologies has accelerated the development of next-generation electrochemical systems, notably vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs), supercapacitors, and fuel cells.1–4 The performance of these electrochemical conversion devices is governed by the intrinsic properties of their electrodes, which must simultaneously provide excellent electrical conductivity, rapid charge transfer, and an accessible electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) to enable reactions of active species and their continuous replenishment within the bulk and at the interface of the electrodes.5,6

Among candidate materials, MWCNT are one-dimensional nanostructures that manifest as large cylindrical molecular structures with tube diameters ranging from 0.7 to 50 nm.7 While MWCNT are recognized for their exceptional electrical conductivity and large surface area,8–17 their utilization in simple powder form presents significant practical limitations for use in high-performance electrodes. When processed into macroscopic, free-standing assemblies—such as bucky papers or flexible MWCNT sheets—they construct highly interconnected, continuous three-dimensional porous networks. These networks are well-suited for demanding electrochemical processes, such as the redox reactions of VRFB negative and positive half-cells.18–22

Despite their theoretical promise, translating the extraordinary microscopic properties of individual MWCNT into high-performance macroscopic electrodes remains a manufacturing bottleneck. Due to their high aspect ratios and strong, intrinsic van der Waals forces (500 eV µm−1), raw MWCNT naturally exist as highly entangled, dense bundles and macroscopic agglomerates.23 These bundles form trunk structures that severely restrict electrolyte infiltration, induce spatial electronic inhomogeneities, and drastically minimize the accessible ECSA, especially at high current densities.20 In order to overcome these cohesive forces, liquid-phase deagglomeration is required prior to electrode fabrication.24–26 Sonication, which is a form of high intensity acoustic cavitation, is the industrial gold standard for this process. During sonication, the propagation of high-frequency acoustic waves generates transient microscopic bubbles that violently collapse. This implosion releases intense localized shockwaves, micro-jets, and severe hydrodynamic shear stresses that untangle the cohesive MWCNT ropes.24 However, this process frequently exceeds the mechanical failure threshold of the nanotubes themselves. Prolonged, unoptimized static sonication induces structural degradation, including lattice fragmentation, amorphization, and MWCNT tube scission.26 This structural degradation shortens the aspect ratio of the fibers, compromises the continuous electronic conduction pathways, and introduces an uncontrolled density of defects that can destabilize the electrode/electrolyte interface.27–32 This issue has been investigated by researchers in the literature in different ways33,34 including a hydrodynamic cavitation method35 and a molecular wedging method.36 Herein, we investigate a flow processing method that can be adapted to the preferred fabrication techniques without compromising scalability of fabrication.

In the tape casting fabrication method of MWCNT, achieving a specific minimum suspension viscosity is generally accepted as a prerequisite for generating a uniform, high-quality cast, as the achievable viscosity can be related to the particle size reduction and homogeneity. Since high-intensity acoustic cavitation is inherently violent, this macro-scale rheological benchmark entirely masks the concurrent, micro-scale structural degradation occurring within the liquid medium.

This study addresses this fundamental limitation by establishing a highly scalable, hydrodynamically optimized fabrication framework utilizing continuous flow sonochemistry. Rather than subjecting a static batch of material to prolonged, localized acoustic energy, this methodology continuously circulates the MWCNT suspension through a dedicated flow-through acoustic chamber at variable volumetric flow rates (50 to 3000 ml min−1). This architecture introduces an innovative operational freedom: it decouples the global processing time from the localized acoustic residence time. The core hypothesis of this work is that the primary limiting factor governing effective macroscopic deagglomeration is the physical replenishment rate of the unexposed material within the active cavitation zone, rather than a prolonged residence duration under intense acoustic shear. By utilizing higher suspension circulation flow rates combined with minimized sonication exposure times, we demonstrate that macro-scale fluid homogeneity and target rheological profiles can be fully satisfied while reducing sonication-induced structural degradation effects. Ultimately, this work addresses a precise and scalable manufacturing roadmap designed to reduce MWCNT processing-induced defects for the engineering of lower resistance and higher surface area MWCNT sheet electrodes as a step toward next-generation electrochemical devices.

Experimental

Chemicals

MWCNT flakes (Applied Nanostructured Solutions, L.L.C., USA) produced by a chemical vapor deposition process at atmospheric pressure with an average length of 30 µm 37 were utilized in this study. Vanadium(iv) oxide sulfate powder (VOSO4, 97%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 96.2%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were prepared in different molarities by dilution in deionized (DI) water (resistivity >18.0 MΩ × cm, Purite Select Fusion water purification system, Thame, UK), to prepare electrolytes for various experiments. The molarities utilized throughout this study were 0.05 M VOSO4 in 3 M H2SO4 for the 3-electrode cell setup experiments and 1.7 M VOSO4 in 3 M H2SO4 for the positive half-cell of full VRFB cell experiments. To prepare the negative half-cell electrolyte, a VRFB cell was charged at a constant potential of 1.6 V until the charging current dropped below 7 mA, and the resulting electrolyte was diluted to achieve the required molarities in 3 M H2SO4 solution.

Fabrication methods

The fabrication methodology is shown in Fig. 1(a–d) and the compositions corresponding to the different process parameters are tabulated in Table 1. First, 800 mg of ground MWCNT, 100 ml DI water, and 100 ml ethanol were added to form a suspension in a beaker. The suspension was then sonicated at a fixed amplitude of 39% using a ½″ diameter probe attached to an ultrasonication device (VCX 750, Sonics, USA) within a flow setup to enable circulation of electrolyte. The flow compartment's height is 230 mm, with a diameter of 89 mm and an internal volume of 65 ml. The length of the probe is 139 mm which is fully immersed and sealed in the flow compartment. The connecting pipes have a total length of 70 cm and the total beaker volume is 300 ml. The circulation tube utilized was MasterFlex 35 (inner diameter = 7.9 mm) and pump model was a BT600L peristaltic pump adapted with an Easy-Load II MasterFlex pump head (Cole-Parmer, USA). Flow rates were controlled ranging from 50 to 3000 ml min−1. In this setup, both the single-pass residence time and the cumulative exposure time were calculated and are presented in Table S1. The thermal history of the experiments is provided in Table S2, and the acoustic power calibration is provided in Table S3. The dispersions were circulated through a flow sonication setup over different flow rates and sonication times, with regular continuous monitoring of the viscosity of the suspension. Once the desired cutoff viscosity was achieved, the sonication was stopped and the suspension was then poured over a copper sheet (9 µm, Libgroup, China) and tape cast at 5 mm of casting blade thickness. The layers were then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 8 h.

Physical and chemical characterization

The physical, structural, chemical, and electronic properties of the fabricated MWCNT electrodes were evaluated as follows. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed for high magnifications. Samples for TEM characterization were prepared by sectioning 1 mg of the respective as-fabricated MWCNT sheet, followed by sonication in 30 ml of 50% ethanol for 1 min. A 5 µl droplet of the suspension was drop-cast onto a 400-mesh, Cu, ultrathin C Type-A support film. The samples were examined under high vacuum and at 200 kV in bright field imaging mode. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Quanta3D 450, FEG, USA) was employed at moderate magnifications. For SEM imaging, a section cut from the respective as-fabricated MWCNT sheet was mounted onto an aluminum stub with double-sided conductive carbon tape. All samples were examined under high-vacuum conditions with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV, a spot size of 2.5, and a working distance of 10 mm. Specific surface area and pore metrics were evaluated by nitrogen (N2) physisorption at 77 K utilizing a commercial surface area analyzer (Anton Paar Nova 800). Prior to the isotherm measurements, all samples were degassed at 100 °C for 8 h. The specific surface area was subsequently calculated via the multipoint Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) framework, extracting data points exclusively within the linear relative pressure regime (P/P0 = 0–0.35) of the desorption isotherm. Raman spectroscopy (Qontor, Renishaw) was performed at 50% power and 1 accumulation, with a 532 nm laser excitation source. Raman mapping was performed over a selected area of 1 mm × 1 mm, generating 2601 scans for every sample analyzed. Samples were prepared by cutting a small piece of the sheet and directly subjecting it to Raman spectroscopy for analysis. Surface functional groups on the MWCNT surfaces were detected using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; VERTEX 80 V, Bruker Optik GmbH, Germany). The measurements were executed in transmittance mode using compressed solid pellets fabricated from a homogeneous blend of the active sample and potassium bromide (KBr) powder at a fixed 1 : 180 mass ratio. Sessile drop contact angle measurements were performed using a goniometer (Biolin Scientific, Attension Theta Flow), deploying a controlled deionized water droplet volume of 5 µL. The electrical conductivity of the free-standing sheets was quantified at ambient temperature using a specialized four-probe system (Ossila, Sheffield, UK). Suspension particle size detection and distribution was performed using a wet dispersion unit connected to a particle detector over a slit (Fritsch, Analysette 22 NeXT). Viscosity measurements were conducted using a rheometer (Discovery Series, TA HR30). In the control experiments, suspensions were prepared by mixing the same composition (800 mg of MWCNT in 50% ethanol solution) and circulating it at the highest flow rate (3000 ml min−1) for 45 min with no sonication. The samples were then allowed to settle for 24 hours prior to sampling for rheometric and particle size detection analysis.

Electrochemical characterization

Cyclic voltammetry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were performed in a three-electrode-cell configuration, utilizing a sheet of the investigated electrode as a working electrode, along with an Ag/AgCl reference electrode and a 1.11 cm2 platinum sheet counter electrode. The electrolyte molarity was 0.05 M V3+ and 0.05 M VO2+ in 3 M H2SO4 for the negative and the positive electrolyte experiments respectively. Measurements were performed using a potentiostat (Biologic, VMP300). The cyclic voltammetry methods employed were based on a potentiostatic staircase method over the respectively investigated potential range and were compensated for iR-drop. The EIS results were acquired at the formal potentials with frequencies ranging between 200 kHz and 100 mHz and at an amplitude of 10 mV RMS. To control the working area of the investigated MWCNT sheets, molten wax was applied over the nonworking areas, controlling the geometric surface area of the working electrode. Full cell experiments were performed using no-gap architecture hardware with an interdigitated flow field and an electrode area of 5 cm2 employed in each half-cell. The electrolyte molarity was 1.7 M V3+ and 1.7 M VO2+ in 3 M H2SO4 for the negative and positive half-cells respectively. 2 layers of as-fabricated MWCNT electrode sheets were used in each half-cell. Charge discharge experiments were performed at constant current densities with cutoff values of 1.68 V and 0.82 V. Full-cell charge discharge tests were performed at stepped current densities of 30, 45, and 60 mA cm−2 across 12 cycles. Extended cycling was performed for 100 cycles at 60 mA cm−2.

Results and discussion

Rheometric analysis and particle size distribution

The rheological behavior of the MWCNT suspensions was evaluated via viscosity measurements across various flow rates at fixed sonication intervals, as shown in the SI (Fig. S1) and the particle size distribution was measured across various sonication times and flow rates, as shown in the SI (Fig. S2). First, a control sample (circulated within the system for 45 min with no sonication) was tested, confirming that any observed increase in viscosity is likely due to the effects of sonication acoustic waves on the MWCNT dispersion. For each sonication duration (t = 5, 10, 15 min), higher flow rates consistently yielded higher final viscosities. This approach allows engineers to achieve the required degree of deagglomeration while minimizing exposure to excessive, localized sonication energy, which can otherwise induce shear stress and damage the structural integrity of the MWCNT fibers. Likewise, for each flow rate (Q = 50, 200, 800, 3000 ml min−1), higher sonication times yielded higher final viscosities. As illustrated in the SI (Fig. S3), the choice of the respective flow rates and sonication times for each of the four samples studied resulted in attaining similar viscosity and apparent particle distribution profiles. Suspensions sonicated at non-optimized/low sonication times and/or low flow rates resulted in low viscosities, making it impossible to fabricate a good quality electrode sheet. Because the tape casting method requires a minimum viscosity threshold in order to achieve a thick-enough wet cast, it was not possible to fabricate a freestanding sheet from suspensions achieving a viscosity lower than 1 Pa s ± 0.2 (at a shear rate of 80 1 s−1). It is important to note here that macroscopic viscosity and particle size distribution detectable do not necessarily reflect the intrinsic structural properties of the constituent MWCNT fibers, and further analysis is required.

In order to understand the effect of varying sonication times and flow rates on the MWCNT agglomerate size, particle size distributions of the four MWCNT suspensions were plotted as shown in SI Fig. S3. All four suspensions resembled narrower and similar peak shapes, as compared to the control sample (45 min with no sonication) which resembled a broader peak distribution, indicating that the observed narrowing distribution effect was related to the sonication effects. The apparent particle size distributions are in accordance with results published in the literature for similar sonication times assuming that the measured diameters of MWCNT agglomerates were a result of projections of the agglomerates.38 Interestingly, the samples exhibited a notable shift in their peak sizes, which consistently increased as MWCNT residence time in the sonication chamber decreased. We attribute the increasing shift in peak sizes to the possibly longer length of MWCNT fibers which become more apparent in samples exposed to lower residence time. Recall that the TEM results showed a larger population of longer MWCNT fibers (1–2 µm) in samples sonicated with faster flowrates and shorter sonication times, as compared to their rivals. Similar behavior and trends were achieved with methods that utilized different deagglomeration techniques; Fuji et al.35 demonstrated similar shifts in particle peak sizes and attributed the results to the increased MWCNT fiber lengths.

Microscopy analysis

To evaluate the impact of flow sonochemistry on the microstructural topology of the fabricated electrodes, SEM and TEM imaging (Fig. 2) were conducted. The electron micrographs reveal a highly interconnected, three-dimensional porous network composed of interwoven MWCNT fibers across all samples. A contrast in the degree of bundle deagglomeration is apparent; in sheets fabricated via higher flow rates and lower sonication times (Q3000/t15), a reduction in the number of trunks (MWCNT bundles denoted by red arrows in Fig. 2a–d) is clearly observed, as compared to sheets fabricated via lower flow rates and higher sonication times (Q50/t45). The increased amount of debundling implies the ability of hydrodynamic optimization to expose more intrinsic surface between the nanofibers, tapping into micro-sites within the bulk of MWCNT sheets. The improvement in bundle deagglomeration (Q3000/t15) can be attributed to the effect of processing of MWCNT suspensions at relatively higher flow rates, reducing residence time and accelerating fluid replenishment within the active cavitation zone during sonication. Moreover, TEM imaging revealed a wall and length damaging phenomenon; defected wall sites were consistently more apparent in samples processed at batch/lower flow rates and higher sonication times (Fig. 2e), as compared to samples processed at higher flow rates and lower sonication times (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, across the sample studies, a larger population of shorter MWCNT protrusions (0.1–1 µm) was counted in samples processed at lower flow rates and higher sonication times (Q50/t45 and Q200/t43), while a larger population of longer MWCNT protrusions (1–2 µm) was counted in samples processed at higher flow rates and lower sonication times (Q800/t30 and Q3000/t15), as shown in Fig. 2g, and S4–S7. These observed phenomena may be attributed to the likelihood of the low flow rate and high sonication times processing methodology in generating localized energy hot spots and possible over-exposure to sonication, possibly damaging exposed (protruded) MWCNT fibers while leaving core bundles (trunks) untouched in some locations. Conversely, the reduction in residence time under sonication results in faster replenishment which maximizes the net utilization of cavitation-induced shockwaves and micro-jets to directly counteract the strong innertube van der Waals cohesive forces.

Surface area and pore size analysis

Surface area and pore size distribution were investigated and analyzed for all sheets (Fig. 3a and b), showing that the Q3000/t15 sheet exhibited a surface area of 280 m2 g−1. This was significantly larger than those of the Q50/t45, Q200/t43, and Q800/t30 sheets, exhibiting surface areas of 112.79, 112.85, and 200.85 m2 g−1 respectively. As compared with surface areas of MWCNT sheets reported in the literature for a wide range of fabrication methods and different MWCNT sources (184.3–278.9),39,40 our results are reasonable. More specifically, our results exceed the results of those fabricated via similar means (tape casting methods).20,41 Overall, the results imply that as the residence time of MWCNTs exposed to sonication decreases, the surface area increases, and this is in accordance with SEM observations that revealed that an improved trunk debundling and larger microsite exposure are achievable in samples processed at higher flow rates and lower sonication times. Moreover, BJH analysis (Fig. 3b) reveals that the most recurrent pore sizes for all sheets appeared at 25.5 ± 0.2 nm and 3.7 ± 0.4 nm. Interestingly, the pore size analysis results show similar pore size distributions but with higher recurrence, indicating that manipulating MWCNT residence time under sonication is unlikely to affect the overall physical structure layering layout, but rather likely to affect the deagglomeration and the opening up of channels within the intrinsic bulk structures of the MWCNT fibers.

Wettability

The surface wettability of the MWCNT sheets was evaluated via water contact angle measurements, as presented in Fig. 3c. The contact angle progressively increased from 118.8° to 126.3° as the volumetric flow rate was increased from 50 to 3000 ml min−1 and the sonication exposure time was simultaneously decreased from 45 to 15 min. These trends demonstrate that surface wettability decreases in high-flow, low-sonication processing regimes. The higher wettability observed at longer sonication times is attributed to the effects of prolonged residence time of MWCNT fibers exposed to acoustic energy, which induces structural defects and increases the density of open edge sites along the MWCNT walls (as apparent from TEM analysis). These edge sites could have possibly facilitated the formation of hydrophilic, oxygen-containing functional groups upon exposure to ambient moisture or processing chemical environments.

Raman spectroscopy was performed for all sheets (Fig. 3e) and shows that characteristic MWCNT features were exhibited: the D band (1336–1347 cm−1), G band (1583–1589 cm−1), and 2D band (2687–2689 cm−1). Raman spectroscopy mapping was further performed (see Fig. S8 and S9) over an area of 1 mm × 1 mm, and the results were analyzed to calculate mean ID/IG and I2D/IG ratios (Fig. 3f). Overall, the results show that all sheets had a higher modest increase in the mean ID/IG ratio (0.85–0.89) as compared to that of the raw MWCNT flakes tested. Moreover, sheets fabricated at higher flow rates and lower sonication times (Q3000/t15) featured a modestly lower mean ID/IG and a higher mean I2D/IG ratio across the area studied, compared to those produced with lower flow rates and longer sonication times (Q50/t45). The results indicate a plausible apparent decrease in density of defects in the Q3000/t15 sample, which is in accordance with our TEM analysis showing that the higher flow rate and lower sonication time processing method can result in fewer wall defects, as compared with the lower flow rates and higher sonication time processing method. This possible reduction in structural defects is attributed to the shorter residence time of the fibers within the active sonication chamber (see Table S1), which minimizes exposure time to the aggressive, damaging shear stresses of the sonication process. Ultimately, these results demonstrate that manipulation of residence time through hydrodynamically optimized flow sonochemistry is a promising method to limit over-exposure during MWCNT deagglomeration.

Electrochemistry

Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) shown in Fig. S10 and S11 were recorded for the as-fabricated MWCNT sheets to evaluate their electrochemical performance toward the V3+/V2+ and VO2+/VO2+ redox couples. All measurements were conducted with iR-compensation and are reported in the 3rd cycle to ensure that the electrode/electrolyte interface had reached a stable, steady-state condition. Across all electrodes studied toward the V3+/V2+, cathodic (jpc) and anodic (jpa) peak current densities, shown in Table 2, scale with increasing scan rates (v = 12.5 to 200 mV s−1). At a scan rate of 200 mV s−1, electrode Q3000/t15 achieves the highest current response, with jpc = −181.69 mA cm−2 and jpa = 169.04 mA cm−2, compared to −120.46 mA cm−2 and 106.47 mA cm−2 for Q50/t45. Moreover, peak potential separation (ΔEp) widens at higher scan rates, where Q800/t30 exhibits the lowest ΔEp of 134.54 mV at 200 mV s−1 (compared to 216.47 mV for Q50/t45), while Q200/t43 attains a minimal ΔEp of 29.51 mV at 12.5 mV s−1. Furthermore, the peak current ratio (Ipa/Ipc) across all samples peak between 0.928 and 0.953 at 100 mV s−1.

The enhanced surface accessibility in Q3000/t15 translates directly into the observed mass transport and reaction kinetics for both vanadium redox couples (Fig. 4a, c and Table 4). For the negative half-cell redox couple (V3+/V2+), the apparent diffusion coefficient (Dapp) more than doubles from 29.9 × 10−5 cm2 s−1 for Q50/t45 to 65.0 × 10−5 cm2 s−1 for Q3000/t15, accompanied by an increase in the heterogeneous rate constant (k0app) from 4.46 × 10−3 cm s−1 to 9.11 × 10−3 cm s−1. In the positive half-cell redox couple (VO2+/VO2+), Dapp increases nearly four-fold from 1.72 × 10−5 cm2 s−1 (Q50/t45) to 6.64 × 10−5 cm2 s−1 (Q3000/t15). Meanwhile, k0app for the positive reaction reaches 16.0 × 10−3 cm s−1 for Q800/t30 and 7.17 × 10−3 cm s−1 for Q3000/t15, both significantly outperforming Q50/t45 (3.28 × 10−3 cm s−1). The notable boost in Dapp across both redox couples highlights how the deagglomerated network of Q3000/t15 mitigates diffusion limitations within the electrode matrix.

Interestingly, while Dapp in both half-cells redox couples increase with higher flow rates and shorter sonication times, the heterogeneous rate constant for the positive redox couple (k0app for VO2+/VO2+) displays a distinct trend. k0app reaches a peak value of 16.0 × 10−3 cm s−1 for the Q800/t30 electrode before decreasing to 7.17 × 10−3 cm s−1 for Q3000/t15. The behavior may be related to a delicate interplay between mass transport accessibility and defect density availability.

To contextualize the electrochemical performance of our hydrodynamically optimized electrodes, the apparent heterogeneous rate constants (k0app) were compared against those of benchmark carbon-based electrodes reported in the literature. For the V3+/V2+ redox couple, the peak rate constant of 9.11 × 10−3 cm s−1 under Q3000/t15 exceeds values reported for standalone MWCNT buckypaper (5.69 × 10−3 cm s−1),43 carbon paper (1.13 × 10−3 cm s−1),44 pristine carbon felt (7.0 × 10−4 cm s−1),45 and glassy carbon (5.4 × 10−4 cm s−1).44 Similarly, for the positive half-cell reaction (VO2+/VO2+), our optimized Q800/t30 condition yielded k0app = 16.0 × 10−3 cm s−1, outperforming untreated carbon felt (9.8 × 10−4 cm s−1),45 carbon paper (1.04 × 10−3 cm s−1),44 and unmodified MWCNT buckypaper (9.52 × 10−3 cm s−1).43

Full-cell charge–discharge evaluations, incorporating our best performing electrode pair (Q800/t30 electrode in the positive half-cell and the Q3000/t15 electrode in the negative half-cell), performed in 1.7 M vanadium in 3 M H2SO4 across current densities of 30, 45, and 60 mA cm−2 are shown in Fig. 4g and h. Across all applied current densities, coulombic efficiency (CE) increases with higher current rates which can be attributed to shorter charge–discharge durations that limit active species crossover. At 30 mA cm−2 (cycles 1–4), Q800/t30 and Q3000/t15 achieve voltage efficiencies (VEs) of 76.2% and 75.4% and energy efficiencies (EEs) of 64.7% and 62.7%, respectively, outperforming Q50/t45 (VE = 70.0%, EE = 62.2%). As the current density is raised to 60 mA cm−2 (cycles 9–12), Q800/t30 retains the highest voltage efficiency (VE = 67.8–69.2%) and energy efficiency (EE = 60.3–61.1%), followed by Q3000/t15 (VE = 65.6–67.0% and EE = 58.3–59.1%). The relative discharge capacity retention (Fig. 4h) shows that at 60 mA cm−2 (12th cycle), the cell preserves 64.2% of its discharge capacity. This improved capability and capacity retention in Q800/t30 and Q3000/t15 is related to their lower charge transfer resistances (Rct) and improved mass transport properties established in our half-cell analyses.

Extended cycling for Q800/t30 at a constant current density of 60 mA cm−2 over 100 cycles is shown in Fig. 4i. The overall energy efficiency transitions from 75.3% in cycle 1 to 71.6% at cycle 50 and 64.7% after 100 cycles. This decay in voltage and energy efficiency over extended cycling reflects progressive concentration polarization and mass transfer resistance accumulated during continuous operation.

When benchmarked against literature,46–51 our electrode is entirely freestanding and operates without auxiliary supports (e.g., graphite felt or carbon felt). Nonetheless, this substrate-free design poses inherent challenges for rapid electrolyte transport; the overall full-cell energy and voltage efficiencies recorded for this class of MWCNT materials remain lower than those of state-of-the-art vanadium redox flow batteries using carbon felt or highly porous carbon paper substrates, with EE performances reaching 85–91% in cases where additives are employed.52–56 This performance gap stems from fundamental structural differences: commercial carbon felt possesses highly open, macro-scale porous networks that enable convective electrolyte flow and rapid liquid turnover throughout the electrode depth. In contrast, the class of free-standing MWCNT sheet materials consists of densely packed nanotube networks with sub-micrometer and nanoscale pores, imposing high hydraulic resistance that can severely limit bulk electrolyte forced convection through the electrode thickness over cell-operational current densities. While MWCNT sheets face intrinsic mass transport constraints regarding electrolyte penetration, the continuous-flow sonication strategy presented here (Fig. 5) demonstrates a step/pathway leading toward improving mass transport and electrocatalytic activity within fully free-standing carbon architectures, yielding reasonable and competitive metrics within the class of free-standing MWCNT sheets.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a scalable continuous-flow processing strategy to tap into microsites in MWCNT sheets, through improved deagglomeration capability. We show that prolonged acoustic residence time at low volumetric flow rates and high sonication times induces aggressive shear stresses that may introduce defects (as plausible from TEM and Raman mapping), whereas higher flow rates and lower sonication times can result in improved deagglomeration and larger surface areas, along with higher roughness factors, primarily by accelerating the replenishment of the under exposed material within the active sonication zone.

The value of this hydrodynamic optimization is revealed in the electrochemical kinetics, which points to an improved and un-collapsed porous network that maximizes the available electrochemical surface area.

Optimized electrodes achieved up to a 2.5-fold higher BET surface area (280.0 m2 g−1) and up to a 10.33-fold higher roughness factor (259.4). Consequently, charge transfer resistance dropped 5.9-fold for the negative (V3+/V2+) and 1.9-fold for the positive (VO2+/VO2+) half-cell. Kinetic rate constants increased 2.0-fold for V3+/V2+ and up to 4.9-fold for VO2+/VO2+, alongside a 3.9-fold rise in positive species diffusivity.

Q3000/t15 shows substantial improvements in apparent diffusion coefficients (Dapp) for both V3+/V2+ (65.0 × 10−5 cm2 s−1) and VO2+/VO2+ (6.64 × 10−5 cm2 s−1) redox couples. While negative half-cell kinetics (k0app and Rct) scale directly with total surface area and mass transport accessibility, the positive reaction kinetics (k0app) peak under intermediate processing conditions (Q800/t30, 16.0 × 10−3 cm s−1).
