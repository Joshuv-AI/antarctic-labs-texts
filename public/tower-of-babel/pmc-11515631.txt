New insights into the phylogenetic relationships within the Lauraceae from mitogenomes
Authors: Yu Song, Qun-Fei Yu, Di Zhang, Li-Gang Chen, Yun-Hong Tan, Wen Zhu, Hua-Long Su, Xin Yao, Chao Liu, Richard T. Corlett
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe family Lauraceae is subdivided into six main lineages: Caryodaphnopsideae, Cassytheae, Cryptocaryeae, Hypodaphnideae, Laureae, and Neocinnamomeae. However, phylogenetic relationships among these lineages have been debatable due to incongruence between trees constructed using nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) sequences and chloroplast (cp) genomes. As with cp DNA, the mitochondrial (mt) DNA of most flowering plants is maternally inherited, so the phylogenetic relationships recovered with mt genomes are expected to be consistent with that from cp genomes, rather than nrDNA sequences.ResultsThe mitogenome of Machilus yunnanensis, with a length of 735,392 bp, has a very different genome size and gene linear order from previously published magnoliid mitogenomes. Phylogenomic reconstructions based on 41 mt genes from 92 Lauraceae mitogenomes resulted in highly supported relationships: sisterhood of the Laureae and a group containing Neocinnamomeae and Caryodaphnopsideae, with Cassytheae being the next sister group, followed by Cryptocaryeae. However, we found significant incongruence among the mitochondrial, chloroplast, and nuclear phylogenies, especially for the species within the Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae lineages. Time-calibrated phylogenetic analyses showed that the split between Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae dated to the later Eocene, around 38.5 Ma, Laureae originated in the Late Cretaceous, around 84.9 Ma, Cassytheae originated in the mid-Cretaceous around 102 Ma, and Cryptocaryeae originated in the Early Cretaceous around 116 Ma. From the Late Cretaceous to the Paleocene, net diversification rates significantly increased across extant clades of major lineages, and both speciation rates and net diversification rates continued steady growth towards the present.ConclusionsThe topology obtained here for the first time shows that mt genes can be used to support relationships among lineages of Lauraceae. Our results highlight that both Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae lineages are younger than previously thought, likely first diversifying in the Eocene, and species in the other extant lineages of Lauraceae dates in a long-time span from the Early Cretaceous to the Eocene, and the climate of a period of about 90 million years was relatively warm, while the extant species of Lauraceae then continuously diversified with global cooling from the Eocene to the present day. Background

In plants, both the mitochondria and chloroplasts contain DNA. The chloroplasts are the site for photosynthesis, while the mitochondria are the main locus of respiratory carbon metabolism and adenosine triphosphate synthesis (Raven [1]). The chloroplast (cp) genome ranges in length from 115 to 165 kb [2, 3] while the mitochondrial (mt) genome ranges from 66 kb [4] to 11.7 Mb [3]. Unlike the typical quadripartite structure of the plastome, the mitogenome contains many repeated sequences that undergo frequent recombination [3, 5]. These recombination events give rise to alternative structures for the mitogenome, such as circles, head-to-tail concatemers, and over-lapping linear fragments [5, 6]. The repeats, especially the large ones (> 250 base pairs), have brought challenges for assembly and annotation of mitogenomes [7].

Although the plant mt genome is extremely complex, many recent studies have focused on the significance and role of mt genes in phylogenetic analysis [3, 8–11]. These studies show that the mt tree may provide useful evidence for cytonuclear incongruence [12–14] and its maternal pattern of inheritance could provide additional information in phylogenetic and evolutionary studies [15–18]. In the plant mitogenome, atp1, matR, cox1, rps2, rps3, and nad5 loci were widely used for phylogenetic studies twenty years ago [19–22]. For example, Parkinson et al. [19] used three mtDNA markers (small subunit mtSSU, cox1 and rps2), nuclear SSU rDNA, and cpDNA rbcL to reconstruct the early history of flowering plants and showed a monophyletic Laurales including Calycanthus floridus and Laurus nobilis with high maximum parsimony bootstrap value (MP-BS 90%). Qiu et al. [20] used the mtDNA markers atp1 and matR, nuclear barcoding marker 18S rDNA, and cpDNA markers atpB and rbcL to reconstruct a monophyletic Lauraceae including Camphora officinarum, Cryptocarya meissneriana, and Laurus nobilis, with low support (MP-BS 73%). Barkman et al. [21] constructed another phylogenetic tree for the earliest angiosperms using the mtDNA markers atpA, cox1, and matR, as well as the nuclear sequences 18S rDNA and the cpDNA barcoding markers atpB and rbcL. The result showed a monophyletic Laurales (NJ-BP 100%) including Calycanthus floridus and an unidentified Cinnamomum species. Qiu et al. [22] used a larger sample size and mtDNA markers atp1, matR, nad5, and rps3 to show a monophyletic Lauraceae, including Laurus nobilis and Cryptocarya meissneriana, with low support (ML-BS 47%). However, these studies had limited ability to resolve phylogenetic problems at the genus level or within some families.

The family Lauraceae comprises over 50 genera and nearly 3500 living species. It is one of the largest groups of the magnoliids and has recently attracted increasing attention because of the uncertain phylogenomic relationships within it. Previous phylogenetic studies have used chloroplast genome sequences to construct robust trees, with the six tribes Cassytheae, Caryodaphnopsideae, Cryptocaryeae, Hypodaphnideae, Laureae, and Neocinnamomeae in the family forming well-supported monophyletic clades [23]. A following study used the nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) sequences to reconstruct a monophyletic Lauraceae including five main clades [2]. Both studies found that incongruence between chloroplast genome and nuclear ribosomal datasets remained in the position of the genera Caryodaphnopsis and Neocinnamomum, Camphora and Cinnamomum, Lindera and Litsea, and Actinodaphne and Neolitsea, respectively. Apart from the genera in the family Lauraceae, decades of studies have raised controversy on the reliability of plastome phylogenomics in the Asteraceae [13], Ceratophyllaceae [24], Magnoliaceae [25], and Malvaceae [26], and hybridization and incomplete lineage sorting may cause problematic plastid phylogenies.

Compared to plastomes, mitogenomes exhibit a lower substitution rate, making them less informative but more important [3]. A growing number of studies have utilized mitochondrial data to infer deep phylogenomic relationships within plant lineages [22, 27, 28], and several studies have consistently identified partial congruences between mitochondrial and nuclear data [29, 31]. Data from mt genomes may help to resolve conflicts between phylogenetic inferences from cp genomes and nrDNA sequences, and our understanding of lineage relationships in Lauraceae would be enhanced if phylogenetic inferences were reconstructed with plant genomic datasets from many sources, including sequence data from mitochondria, chloroplast, nuclear ribosomal cistrons, and low-copy nuclear genes.

With the development of sequencing technologies, nearly seven hundred complete mt genomes of land plants now are available [3]. However, for magnoliids, the third largest group of angiosperms, only 13 species have assembled mitogenomes [5, 9], and there has not been a comprehensive Lauraceae phylogeny reconstructed with mitogenome sequences. In this study, we therefore first sequenced, assembled, and annotated the complete mitogenome in the family Lauraceae, for Machilus yunnanensis. Machilus yunnanensis provides important timber which is highly valued because of its brilliant color and remarkable durability. This was then used as a reference for the identification of mt coding genes in other Lauraceae species. We compiled a dataset composed of 41 mt genes from 91 Lauraceae taxa, representing 29 genera spanning the five core clades identified in the family. The aims of this study are (1) to obtain a well-supported mitogenome phylogeny; (2) to identify phylogenetic discordance between the mt genes and the cp genes or the nrDNA data matrixes; and (3) to elucidate the temporal framework of lineage origin.

Results

General features of the Machilus yunnanensis mitochondrial genome

A total of 33 Gb raw reads of 150 bp in length and 43 Gb single-molecule real-time DNA reads were generated for Machilus yunnanensis from Illumina paired-end sequencing and PacBio Sequel platforms, respectively. We assembled the whole mitogenome using PacBio long reads along with Illumina short reads, resulting in a circular mitogenome with a length of 735,392 bp (accession number: PP968710). The overall base composition of the entire mitogenome is: A: 26.4%, T: 26.5%, G: 23.4%, C: 23.7% (47.1% GC content). The mitogenome of M. yunnanensis contains a total of 63 unique genes, including 41 protein-coding genes, 19 tRNA genes (trnP-UGG has three copies), and 3 rRNA genes (Fig. 1A). Among the functional genes, 10 protein-coding genes contain introns (Additional file 1: Table S1).

In the M. yunnanensis mitogenome, 1312 interspersed repeat sequences were identified. Forward (691 repeats, 52.67%) and palindromic repeats (571 repeats, 43.52%) were the most common types (Additional file 1: Table S2). There were 93 SSRs discovered in the M. yunnanensis mitogenome. Consistent with all observed species, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant SSR type (64 A/T repeats and 6 C/G repeats). In addition, there were 13 dinucleotide and 6 trinucleotide repeats, respectively. However, there were no hexanucleotide repeats identified in the M. yunnanensis mitogenome (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Mitochondrial genome comparisons

We performed synteny and rearrangement analyses between the mitogenomes of M. yunnanensis, Liriodendron tulipifera, and Magnolia biondii. Dozens of rearrangement events among the three mitogenomes were detected in both coding regions and noncoding regions (Additional file 2), and we only identified three conserved intergenic regions, atp4-nad4L, nad3-rps12, and sdh4-cox3-atp8 (Fig. 1B). For the protein-coding genes, the gene orders of atp4, nad4L, ccmFN, rps1, rps4, sdh4, cox3, and atp8 are extensively conserved among the three genomes, one gene inversion event occurred between nad3 and rps12, and gene rearrangements were relatively common in the other 31 protein-coding genes (Fig. 1B). In addition, the percent variability was calculated to represent the genetic variation of the 41 protein-coding genes among 92 Lauraceae samples (Fig. 2). Mitochondrial phylogeny

Using the mitogenome of M. yunnanensis as a reference, 97 mt contig datasets were re-annotated and parsed into 41 protein-coding genes. After removing ambiguously aligned positions, the mt matrix had an alignment length of 34,240 bp, with 2,410 variable sites and 1776 parsimony-informative sites (Table 1). In the Bayesian Inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML) trees of the mt matrix (Fig. 3A, Additional file 3), the Lauraceae were strongly supported as monophyletic (ML-bootstrap support [BS] = 100%, BI posterior probability [PP] = 1.00). Sisterhood of Lauraceae and a clade containing two Illigera species was highly supported (ML-BS = 100%, BI-PP = 1.00), with another clade containing Chimonanthus and Calycanthus species as the next sister group (ML-BS = 100%, BI-PP = 1.00), followed by the outgroup including Liriodendron and Magnolia species (ML-BS = 100%, BI-PP = 1.00). Both phylogenetic analyses identified five main lineages in the Lauraceae. The first-diverging lineage, Cryptocaryeae (ML-BS = 100%, BI-PP = 1.00), comprises 18 species from 5 genera, Eusideroxylon Teijsm. & Binn., Cryptocarya R.Br., Endiandra R.Br., Syndiclis Hook.f., and Beilschmiedia Nees. The second lineage, Cassytheae (ML-BS = 84%, BI-PP = 1), includes three species of Cassytha L. The third lineage, Laureae (ML-BS = 98%, BI-PP = 1.00), includes 64 species from 21 genera, Persea Mill., Alseodaphne Nees, Alseodaphnopsis H.W.Li & J.Li, Nothaphoebe Blume, Dehaasia Blume, Phoebe Nees, Machilus Nees, Nectandra Rol. ex Rottb., Sassafras Nees, Kuloa Trofimov & Rohwer, Camphora Fabr., Cinnamomum Schaeff., Lindera Thunb, Litsea Lam, Dodecadenia Nees, Iteadaphne Blume, Laurus L., Sinosassafras H. W. Li, Parasassafras D. G. Long, Actinodaphne Nees, and Neolitsea Merr. The fourth and fifth lineage have three Neocinnamomeae (ML-BS = 100%, BI-PP = 1.00) and three Caryodaphnopsideae species (ML-BS = 100%, BI-PP = 1.00), respectively. Sisterhood of the Laureae and a clade containing Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae was highly supported (ML-BS = 98%, BI-PP = 1.00). Chloroplast and nrDNA phylogenies

To better understand the phylogenetic relationships of species in the family Lauraceae, we assembled nrDNA sequence and cp gene datasets. The matrixes had aligned lengths of 6189 bp and 60,893 bp (Table 1), respectively. These analyses of nrDNA and cp gene sequences resolved phylogenetic relationships among the main clades and most genera in the family Lauraceae, and most resolved relationships had high internal support (Fig. 3B, C, Additional file 3). The five high-supported lineages were Cryptocaryeae (nrDNA ML-BS = 98%, nrDNA BI-PP = 1.00, cp ML-BS = 100%, and cp BI-PP = 1.00), Cassytheae (nrDNA ML-BS = 92%, nrDNA BI-PP = 0.93, cp ML-BS = 100%, and cp BI-PP = 1.00), Neocinnamomeae (nrDNA ML-BS = 93%, nrDNA BI-PP = 0.94, cp ML-BS = 100%, and cp BI-PP = 1.00), Caryodaphnopsideae (nrDNA ML-BS = 98%, nrDNA BI-PP = 0.99, cp ML-BS = 94%, and cp BI-PP = 1.00), and Laureae (nrDNA ML-BS = 93%, nrDNA BI-PP = 0.94, cp ML-BS = 94%, and cp BI-PP = 1.00). The nrDNA and cp trees were largely consistent with the topologies of the mt tree, with differences only in the positions of the Neocinnamomeae and Caryodaphnopsideae, both of which are slightly weaklier supported than the others. The plastome phylogeny indicated that Caryodaphnopsideae is sister to Laureae (ML-BS = 94%, BI-PP = 1.00), while the nrDNA phylogeny showed a well-supported clade composed of Laureae and Neocinnamomeae (ML-BS = 93%, BI-PP = 0.94).

Estimates of divergence times and rates

Our Bayesian molecular dating analyses suggested an Early Cretaceous stem age for Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae of 125.32 Ma (138.90–113.72 Ma in 95% highest posterior density) (Fig. 5A). The divergence time between Cryptocaryeae and its sister clade was estimated to be 116.04 Ma (129.49–110.10 Ma), Cassytheae diverged from its sister clade around 102.04 Ma (123.77–71.32 Ma), Laureae diverged around 84.88 Ma (112.85–61.52 Ma), and Neocinnamomeae diverged with Caryodaphnopsideae around 38.52 Ma (81.17–10.13 Ma) (Table 2, Fig. 5A, B). These lineages of Lauraceae originated successively during a period of about 90 million years from 125.32 to 38.52 Ma (Fig. 5A, B). The speciation rates for the lineages, as reconstructed by BAMM, indicated a slow increase (< 0.84) from 118.47 to 73 Ma and a high increase (> 0.89) from 23 Ma to the present (Fig. 6A, B). Discussion

The previous proposed Lauraceae phylogenies was based on analyses using cp DNA and nrDNA sequences [2, 23, 33, 34]. Our phylogenetic analyses using chloroplast, mitochondrial, and nrDNA sequences of the same 91 species in the family Lauraceae yielded a hypothesis with strong supports for monophyly of Caryodaphnopsideae, Cassytheae, Cryptocaryeae, Laureae, and Neocinnamomeae. The mitochondrial genome data reveal support for different phylogenetic relationships among Caryodaphnopsideae, Laureae, and Neocinnamomeae and highlight that both Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae lineages are much younger than previously thought.

Mitogenome size and structure

This study produced a complete mitogenome for the tree species Machilus yunnanensis. The M. yunnanensis mitogenome has a length of 735,392 bp, while other published mitogenomes in the magnoliids range in length from 328,934 bp in Aristolochia gigantea to 1,916,195 bp in Hydnora visseri, both in the Aristolochiaceae [5]. Comparative genomic analyses indicated that frequent recombination events were present between the mitogenome sequences of a species of Lauraceae and two different species of Magnoliaceae (Fig.1), and three conserved fragments were found among the three genomes. The first fragment consists of atp4, nad4L, and their intergenic region, the second consists of atp8, cox3, sdh4, and their intergenic regions, and the third inversion fragment consists of nad3, rps12, and their intergenic region (Fig. 1). In the eight published mitogenomes of Aristolochiaceae species and one of Saururaceae, the three conserved fragments have also been identified [5], which suggests that the mt genes in the clusters are tightly linked among the species in the magnoliids. In the published 28 mitogenomes of monocots and eudicots, two conserved clusters, atp4-nad4L and rps12-nad3, were identified, while in those of eudicots, the conserved cluster cox3-sdh4 was also reported [32], suggesting that the magnoliids and eudicots share more conserved mt gene clusters than the monocots.

Phylogenetic relationships

The Lauraceae has six tribes in our previous study [23]. Following the initial divergence of the Hypodaphnideae, relationships among the five main Lauraceae lineages have been elaborated using cp and nrDNA data [2, 33, 34]. Our analyses of 41 mt protein-coding genes for 91 plant species, representing 29 genera of Lauraceae, further confirmed the five monophyletic lineages: Cassytheae, Caryodaphnopsideae, Cryptocaryeae, Laureae, and Neocinnamomeae (Fig.4). The topology obtained here showed for the first time that mt genes can provide significantly supported relationships among main lineages of Lauraceae. Three of the five lineages are consistent with the published plastid phylogenomic relationships [2, 24, 33, 34]. However, the mt, cp, and nrDNA data reconstructed different phylogenetic placements for Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae (Fig.3). Significant incongruence between the mitochondrial and chloroplast phylogenies has raised doubts about uniparental inheritance of chloroplast or mitochondrial DNA in Lauraceae, and incomplete lineage sorting or ancient hybridization may be the cause of the discordances detected between nrDNA and mt or cp trees [3]. It might be added that the weakly supported species relationships within the main Lauraceae lineages have raised controversy on establishing relationships at the species level solely based on mitochondrial data.

Divergence timing

According to the results of our molecular clock analyses, the 38.52 Ma date for the origin of Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae lineages is much younger than the ca. 76.1 Ma date proposed by Yang et al. [34], the ca. 83 Ma date proposed by Li et al. [35], the ca. 92 Ma date proposed by Nie et al. [36], and the ca. 142 Ma date proposed by Chanderbali et al. [37], which illustrates that molecular dating estimates based on different phylogenetic trees are often associated with large uncertainties. Our 84.88 Ma date for the origin of the stem clade of Laureae is close to the ca. 76.1 Ma date proposed by Yang et al. [34], the ca. 83 Ma date proposed by Li et al. [35], and the ca. 92 Ma date proposed by Nie et al. [36], but is much younger than the ca. 142 Ma date proposed by Chanderbali et al. (2001). Our 102.04 Ma date for the stem clade of Cassytheae approximates the ca. 90.9 Ma date proposed by Yang et al. [34] and the ca. 91 Ma date proposed by Li et al. [35]. The stem node of Cryptocaryeae is dated to 116.04 Ma, which is near to the ca. 100.3 Ma date proposed by Yang et al. [34], the ca. 98 Ma date proposed by Li et al. [35], and the ca. 100 Ma date proposed by Nie et al. [36], but tend to be much younger than the ca. 158 Ma date proposed by Chanderbali et al. [37]. Based on the mt genome data, the diversification of the extant lineages in the family occurred from the Cretaceous to the Eocene (125.32 to 38.52 Ma), which is too recent to be associated, as previously suggested, with the history of the breakup of the ancient supercontinent Gondwana [37].

Conclusions

As one of the largest groups of the magnoliids, the Lauraceae has recently attracted increasing attention because of disagreements about the phylogenomic relationships within it. Here, we provide a complete mitogenome for Machilus yunnanensis through a new assembly strategy. Frequent recombination events generated diversity within the mitogenomes in both Magnoliaceae and Lauraceae species. The phylogenetic analyses based on mt, cp, and nrDNA data confirmed the five monophyletic lineages of Lauraceae but reconstructed different phylogenetic placements for Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae. Divergence times reconstructed with the mt gene matrix showed a much younger origin of Caryodaphnopsideae and Neocinnamomeae lineages than previously suggested.

Methods

Plant materials

The ingroup comprised 91 species of 29 genera from five main lineages (Hypodaphnis zenken lacks sequence data) in the Lauraceae for molecular analysis. For the outgroup, six representatives from Illigera, Calycanthus, Chimonanthus, Magnolia, and Liriodendronwere obtained according to the previous molecular and morphological studies [23], 38]. The voucher specimens for the 92 sampled plants (Cassytha larsenii had two individuals) were deposited at the Herbarium of Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS, Additional file 1: Table S4).

DNA extraction and sequencing

In September 2019, we collected young leaves of Machilus yunnanensis from one tree growing in the Kunming Institute of Botany (KIB), CAS, for genomic DNA extraction using the modified CTAB protocol (Doyle and Dickson [39]). High-quality DNA of Machilus yunnanensis was sent to the Novogene Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China) for library construction and sequencing. For PacBio Sequel sequencing, 20 μg of genomic DNA were prepared to generate sequencing libraries with 20-kb DNA inserts on the Pacific Biosciences RSII instrument, and a total of 43 Gb single-molecule real-time DNA reads were acquired. For Illumina HiSeq sequencing, libraries were constructed with fragments of approximately 300 bp according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States), and the 92 samples were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2,500 at KIB, CAS.

Assembly and annotation

GetOrganelle v.1.7.5 [40] was used to assemble the mt genomes, nrDNA sequences, and chloroplast genomes. For assembling the complete mitogenome ofM. yunnanensis, the Illumina sequencing data were initially assembled using GetOrganelle with the following parameters: -F embplant_mt -k 25,41,61,83,105,127. GetOrganelle first looked for reads with similarity to mt references using an extension algorithm (Additional file 4), and then it assembled these paired-end reads by calling SPAdes v.3.13.0 [41]. After the subreads result files of Pacbio third-generation sequencing ofM. yunnanensiswere obtained, the subset of sequences that were related to mitochondria in the third-generation sequences was obtained by aligning to the scaffolds assembled by GetOrganelle using BLAST 2.5.0 + [42], with -evalue 1e − 100. Subsequently, these long reads and the mitochondria-related short reads that were extended by GetOrganelle were used together for hybrid assembly, which was performed by Unicycler pipeline v.0.4.8 [43]. After the polishing step performed by Pilon, we used bam file visualization tool within UGENE v.39.0 [44] to examine the alignment files (Additional file 5), to ensure that the short reads coverage of the assembly is normally distributed (average coverage: 1162).

Mitochondrial genomes were annotated using GeSeq v.2.03 [45], with Liriodendron tulipifera (KC821969) and Magnolia biondii (MN206019) as references. Subsequently, a detailed annotation was performed with references in Geneious Prime [46], and the annotation of M. yunnanensis was modified manually by comparing to the available plants mt annotations downloaded from NCBI using PhyloSuite v.1.2.2. The tRNA genes were also detected by the online tRNAscan-SE server (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/) for corroboration of tRNA boundaries. The circular mitogenome map was visualized using OGDRAW [47].

Sequence analysis

Long repetitive sequences were identified using REPuter web-service program (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer). Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected using the MISA-web service (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/). To reveal rearrangement events in mitogenome evolution, Circos v.0.69.8 [48] was used to compare the mitogenome ofM. yunnanensis with those of Liriodendron tulipifera and Magnolia biondii.Data matrices were extracted and constructed manually based on the newly generated sequences and the published genomes [2, 49, 50] by BioEdit v.7.2.5 and Geneious Prime. Three different datasets were generated, from mitochondria, chloroplast and nrDNA sequences: (1) the mitochondria dataset included 41 mt protein coding genes with all start and stop codon treated as missing data; (2) the chloroplast dataset included 72 cp protein coding genes; (3) the structure of nrDNA dataset included 18S, ITS1, 5.8S, ITS2, and 26S. A summary of the datasets generated for this study is shown in Table 1. Each dataset was aligned individually using MAFFT v.7.505 [51]. Following alignment, BioEdit was used to manually adjust poorly aligned regions.

Phylogenetic analysis

We built phylogenetic trees for each genomic region (mitochondrial, nrDNA, and chloroplast dataset) separately. Phylogenetic analyses were carried out using IQ-TREE 2 [52] searching for the best partition scheme for the mt dataset [53], and using standard model selection for cp and nrDNA datasets, followed by maximum likelihood (ML) tree inference and 1000 bootstrap replicates. Bayesian inference (BI) was undertaken using MrBayes v.3.2.7a. The optimal models for Markov chain Monte Carlo were run in MrBayes for 10,000,000 generations and sampling trees every 1000 generations, and the first 25% were discarded as burn-in. The parameters of the best-fit substitution models for the three datasets were calculated by Jmodeltest v.2.1.7 [55]. The phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited in FigTree v.1.4.2 [56]. To explore possible phylogenetic conflicts, we utilized SplitsTree v.4.18.1 [57], with the Neighbor-Net algorithm and GTR-distances options on to generate the split tree network.

Divergence time estimation and diversification rate analysis

Divergence times were estimated using BEAST v.2.6.3 [54], with the mt gene matrix. The relaxed clock and birth–death models were selected in the speciation process with 1000 steps of 10,000,000 generations. We then generated a time-calibrated tree with nine reliable calibration points. The stem ages of the magnoliids (Da) and Laurales (Db) followed Massoni et al. [58], the stem age of the Lauraceae (Dc) followed Silvestro et al. [59], the stem ages of the Cryptocaryeae (Dd), Cryptocaryineae (De), and the extant triplinerved Cryptocaryaspecies (Df) followed Song et al. [60], the stem age of the Perseineae (Dg) followed Li et al.[61], the stem age of the Cinnamomineae (Dh) followed Huang et al. [62], and the stem age of the Lauriineae (Di) followed Qin et al. [63]. Details of these analyses are provided in Table3. We evaluated diversification in the Lauraceae using the Bayesian Analysis of Macroevolutionary Mixtures program (BAMM) v.2.5.0 and subsequently analyzed with R package BAMMtools v.2.1.10 [64]. Average global temperatures over the last 160 million years were recorded from the PhanSST database (http://dx.doi.org/10.1126/science.aay1323).
