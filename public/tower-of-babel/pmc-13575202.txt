Reinforcement learning-guided multi-objective trajectory planning for obstacle avoidance in robotic manipulators
Authors: Zhenlong Zhao, Shuang Hao, Bin Jin, Lei Liu, Zhongfeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic manipulators operating in cluttered environments require collision-free trajectories that remain executable under kinematic and dynamic constraints. This paper proposes a reinforcement learning (RL)-guided multi-objective trajectory planning framework, termed RL-MOP-HNE, for a 6-DOF UR5 manipulator. The planning model simultaneously minimizes path length, energy consumption, and execution time while satisfying collision-avoidance, kinematic, and dynamic constraints. A tabular SARSA agent is embedded into the evolutionary search to adaptively select search behaviours according to the current optimization state. To improve the balance between exploration and exploitation, a Gaussian-perturbation adaptive hybrid crossover operator is integrated with a hierarchical neighborhood evolution (HNE) strategy, enabling progressive population refinement throughout the search process. The proposed method is evaluated in three representative environments with increasing planning complexity, including single-obstacle, narrow three-obstacle, and irregular five-obstacle scenarios, and is compared with MOEA/D, MOPSO, MSCLPSO, NSGA-II, and RL-NSGA-II. Experimental results show that RL-MOP-HNE generates feasible trajectories in all test cases and achieves the lowest dynamic-stability-prioritized composite scores among the compared algorithms. The planned trajectories exhibit smoother joint motion and lower velocity fluctuations, although these improvements are generally accompanied by longer execution times. Complementary analyses, including time scaling, manipulability, clearance evaluation, statistical significance tests, and ablation studies, further explain the performance characteristics of the proposed framework and quantify the contribution of its key components. The proposed framework is therefore well suited to robotic applications where motion stability and dynamic executability are of greater importance than minimum-time operation.

Introduction

With the development of intelligent manufacturing, flexible assembly, and human–robot collaboration (HRC), industrial manipulators are increasingly used in narrow, unstructured1,2, and obstacle-dense workspaces3,4. In these environments, trajectory planning must generate collision-free motion while also considering execution time, energy consumption, joint-motion smoothness, and dynamic feasibility5,6. For high-DOF manipulators, the nonlinear mapping between joint space and task space is coupled with obstacle-avoidance constraints, velocity and acceleration limits, and several performance requirements. Therefore, manipulator trajectory planning is a high-dimensional, constrained, and multi-objective optimization problem (MOP).

A feasible end-effector path is not necessarily a practical robot trajectory. If the path is obtained through abrupt joint corrections or an overly compressed time allocation, the manipulator may show high velocity peaks, large acceleration changes, or increased actuation effort. These effects are important in precision assembly, medical robotics, and HRC, where stable motion can be more valuable than simply finishing the task as fast as possible. For this reason, trajectory planning should be evaluated not only by geometric length and arrival time, but also by dynamic executability indicators such as velocity fluctuation, manipulability variation, obstacle clearance, and computational cost.

Recent obstacle-avoidance studies can be broadly grouped into local reactive methods, sampling-based planners, and hybrid learning-planning methods. Improved sampling planners, including FMT and improved RRT* variants, improve path-search feasibility in cluttered, narrow, or redundant workspaces7–9. DRL and replay-based planners have also been used to improve adaptability in dynamic environments and reduce simulation cost during manipulator trajectory generation10–13. However, many of these methods still focus mainly on geometric feasibility, path length, or local collision avoidance. They do not fully integrate execution time, energy consumption, and joint dynamic smoothness into a unified optimization model, and the generated paths often require additional smoothing, time re-parameterization, or dynamic checking before industrial execution.

To handle conflicting trajectory objectives, MOEAs have been widely used as global search frameworks. Recent manipulator trajectory-planning studies introduce acceleration-continuity constraints, jerk, vibration, energy, torque, or impact evaluation14,15, and improved evolutionary operators to balance trajectory quality and dynamic feasibility16,17. Recent comprehensive-learning PSO and RL-guided NSGA-II studies also show the value of adaptive learning strategies in evolutionary search18–20. Nevertheless, MOEA-based planners still face two difficulties in high-dimensional joint spaces with strong obstacle coupling. First, convergence speed and population diversity are difficult to maintain simultaneously, which may lead to premature convergence or discontinuous non-dominated solution sets. Second, fixed operators and preset parameters cannot always respond effectively to changes in the current search state.

RL provides an online decision-making framework for learning state-action mappings through environmental interaction, and on-policy TD learning can provide stable local search guidance in noisy or constrained optimization processes21. Recent studies have applied RL or DRL to simulation-efficient manipulator control and adaptive evolutionary search management22,23. These studies show the adaptability of RL, but pure RL still requires many samples, is sensitive to reward design and hyperparameters, and may lack explicit safety verification20,24. Moreover, many RL planners combine path quality, energy consumption, and execution time into a single scalar reward, which can reduce solution diversity and decision interpretability.25,26

These limitations suggest that learning should be used as a search-guidance component rather than as an isolated planner. A lightweight RL module can adjust operator selection or perturbation behavior according to the current search state, while the evolutionary population still keeps multiple candidate trajectories for trade-off analysis. This structure is useful for constrained manipulator planning because it allows local corrections near obstacles without removing the multi-objective interpretation of path length, energy consumption, and execution time. It also keeps the learning process reproducible, which is difficult when a high-capacity policy network is trained for every planning scene.

Based on the above discussion, standalone MOEAs and standalone RL methods are both limited when applied to complex multi-objective manipulator trajectory planning. RL-assisted evolutionary optimization has recently received attention because policy learning or value learning can be used to adapt operators, parameters, or selection strategies during population evolution27,28. This combination is suitable for constrained trajectory planning: RL can provide state-aware search guidance and reduce blind evolutionary search, while MOEAs can preserve solution diversity and generate non-dominated candidates under multiple objectives. Therefore, an RL-guided MOEA can make use of both the adaptive decision ability of RL and the multi-objective search ability of evolutionary optimization.

Another issue is the interpretation of algorithmic performance. A planner may appear better on execution time because it allows sharper joint motion, whereas a stability-oriented method may choose a longer duration to reduce velocity fluctuation. Direct comparison of a single metric can therefore be misleading. In this study, the primary objectives are kept separate from auxiliary evaluation metrics, and the experimental section further reports normalized profiles, convergence variability, time-scaling checks, supplementary trajectory-quality indicators, statistical tests, and ablation results. These analyses are included to make the reported trade-offs clearer and to show where the proposed method is useful and where its limitations remain.

In this paper, a 6-DOF UR5 manipulator is considered in complex obstacle environments. A three-objective trajectory planning model is established by considering path length, energy consumption, and execution time. A reinforcement learning-guided multi-objective trajectory planning algorithm, named RL-MOP-HNE, is proposed to improve dynamic executability, convergence stability, and search robustness in constrained spaces. To evaluate the proposed method, RL-MOP-HNE is compared with MOEA/D, MOPSO, MSCLPSO, NSGA-II, and RL-NSGA-II under three representative obstacle configurations. The comparison is conducted in terms of trajectory geometry, joint dynamic quality, normalized performance profiles, convergence behavior, and ablation performance. Differential kinematics and kinematic feasibility

The pose mapping alone is not sufficient for multi-objective trajectory planning. The differential relation between joint velocity and EE velocity should also be considered. For a given joint velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{q}$$\end{document}, the resulting end-effector velocity depends on the current value of J(q). This effect is important in obstacle avoidance, because local corrections near obstacles may require faster joint-level responses than the slower overall motion from the start pose to the goal pose.

At the same time, Eq. (9) limits how quickly these local corrections can be made. The planning problem thus contains both local, constraint-limited adjustments and a longer task-level motion over the full duration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_f-t_0$$\end{document}.

The dynamic model adds another layer to this coupling by linking acceleration to torque, energy consumption, and feasible execution time. For this reason, obstacle-avoidance planning is treated here as a dynamic optimization problem rather than only a geometric path-search problem. The SARSA-guided search is used to adjust the evolutionary operators according to the current state of the search, so that broad exploration and local correction can be balanced during optimization.

Dynamic modeling and energy characterization

In addition to kinematic feasibility and collision avoidance, trajectory quality also depends on the actuation effort required for execution. In this study, the joint-space dynamic model of the UR5 is established using the Euler–Lagrange formulation:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tau (t)=M(q)\ddot{q}(t)+C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}(t)+G(q), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Equation (10) shows that the same Cartesian path may lead to different \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{q}(t)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ddot{q}(t)$$\end{document}, and torque requirements under different time parameterizations. Under uniform time scaling from T to α T, the joint velocity scales with 1/α and the joint acceleration scales with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/\alpha ^2$$\end{document}. If the trajectory is compressed too strongly in time, the inertial and velocity-related torque terms can increase sharply and may approach or exceed actuator limits. Execution time is therefore not only a scheduling variable; it also affects dynamic feasibility. Thus, evaluating a trajectory only from its geometric shape is insufficient.

Based on the dynamic model, the instantaneous mechanical power of the manipulator at time t is defined as11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(t)=\sum _{i=1}^{6}|\tau _i(t)\dot{q}_i(t)|. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The total energy consumption of the trajectory is then expressed as12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E=\int _{t_0}^{t_f} P(t)\,dt \approx \sum _{k=1}^{N} |\tau _k^T\dot{q}_k|\Delta t. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Equation (12) links the dynamic model in this section to the energy objective formulated in Section 3. The energy objective is not equivalent to the time objective. Although reducing execution time can improve task efficiency, it often increases joint velocity and acceleration, which further increases τ (t) and P(t). Similarly, sharp local detours caused by obstacle avoidance may increase the actuation demand through Eqs. (7) and (10). Therefore, path length, energy consumption, and execution time are conflicting objectives and should be coordinated within a unified multi-objective framework. In the numerical implementation, M(q), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C(q,\dot{q})$$\end{document}, G(q), and the inverse-dynamics torque τ (t) are computed using the Robotics Toolbox for MATLAB30. Torque evaluation is performed with the recursive Newton–Euler algorithm, whose computational cost is linear in the number of joints for a serial manipulator. The energy integral in Eq. (12) is then approximated by trapezoidal quadrature over the same N=100 sampled trajectory points for all algorithms. This continuous-modeling and discrete-evaluation strategy keeps the physical meaning of the model and remains compatible with the subsequent RL-guided multi-objective evolutionary algorithms.

Multi-objective optimization problem

In manipulator trajectory planning, the MOP provides a structured framework for generating efficient and feasible trajectories. Based on the kinematic and dynamic models established in Section 2, any candidate joint trajectory Q(t) can be mapped to the corresponding EE path, obstacle distances, joint states, and driving torques. Under this unified representation, path length, energy consumption, and execution time can be evaluated consistently. The coordination of these conflicting objectives is essential for improving system performance in industrial automation, robotic surgery, and space applications. In this study, obstacle-avoidance trajectory planning for the UR5 manipulator is formulated as an MOP, where RL is integrated with NSGA-II to obtain non-dominated candidate solutions under multiple constraints. The RL module is used to generate diverse initial trajectories, while NSGA-II performs global optimization to balance path length, energy consumption, and execution time. The MOP is defined as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \min _{Q(t)} F(Q) = [f_1(Q), f_2(Q), f_3(Q)]^T, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q(t) = [q_1(t), q_2(t), \dots , q_6(t)]^T$$\end{document} denotes the joint trajectory, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_1(Q)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_2(Q)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_3(Q)$$\end{document} correspond to path length, energy consumption, and execution time, respectively. The optimization is subject to kinematic, dynamic, and collision-avoidance constraints:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} q_i^{\min } \le q_i(t) \le q_i^{\max }, \\ |\dot{q}_i(t)| \le \dot{q}_i^{\max }, \\ |\ddot{q}_i(t)| \le \ddot{q}_i^{\max }, \\ d_{i,\ell ,a}(q(t)) \ge d_{\textrm{safe}}, \forall i,\forall \ell ,\forall a, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{i,\ell ,a}(q(t))$$\end{document} is the signed distance defined in Section 2, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\textrm{safe}}$$\end{document} denotes the safety margin. By combining RL-based initialization with NSGA-II-based evolution, a set of non-dominated solutions is obtained, providing multiple candidate trajectories for different application requirements.

Path length

Path length is a basic measure for evaluating the geometric efficiency of the EE motion in task space. A shorter path reduces unnecessary movement, which helps decrease mechanical wear and improves motion stability. Let the EE position be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(t) = [x(t), y(t), z(t)]^T$$\end{document}. The path length is defined as15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_1(Q) = L = \int _{t_0}^{t_f} \left\| \frac{dp(t)}{dt} \right\| dt \approx \sum _{k=1}^{N-1} \Vert p_{k+1} - p_k\Vert , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N is the number of sampling points. This discrete approximation is suitable for numerical computation and can be directly combined with kinematic constraints. Minimizing L leads to compact trajectories and is particularly useful in cluttered environments.

Energy consumption

Energy consumption describes the actuation effort required during trajectory execution and is an important indicator for system efficiency. High energy consumption may cause overheating, increased power demand, and accelerated component degradation. The instantaneous power is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(t) = \sum _{i=1}^{6} |\tau _i(t) \dot{q}_i(t)|$$\end{document}, and the total energy consumption is given by16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_2(Q) = E = \int _{t_0}^{t_f} P(t) dt \approx \sum _{k=1}^{N} |\tau _k^T \dot{q}_k| \Delta t. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Minimizing this objective requires coordinated adjustment of joint velocities and accelerations. This objective is coupled with both path length and execution time, and excessive reduction of one objective may increase the others.

Execution time

Execution time represents the efficiency of task completion and directly affects system productivity. In applications such as automated assembly or emergency operations, shorter execution time is preferred, but excessive acceleration may introduce vibration and reduce accuracy. The execution time is defined as17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_3(Q) = T = t_f - t_0. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The optimization of T is achieved by adjusting time parameterization under velocity and acceleration constraints. Since this objective conflicts with path length and energy consumption, multi-objective trade-off analysis is required to obtain feasible solutions. The three objectives in Eq. (13), namely path length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_1$$\end{document}, energy consumption \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_2$$\end{document}, and execution time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_3$$\end{document}, are the quantities optimized during the evolutionary search. Other indicators used in the experiments, including joint velocity variance, maximum joint velocity, and average joint range, are evaluation metrics rather than direct objectives. They describe the dynamic executability of the selected trajectories. For instance, reducing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_3$$\end{document} may raise joint velocities, whereas lowering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_2$$\end{document} often discourages abrupt acceleration changes. These additional indicators therefore help interpret the trade-off obtained from the primary objectives. The proposed framework combines RL and NSGA-II to explore the high-dimensional search space efficiently and to generate trajectory sets that balance path length, energy consumption, and execution time under practical constraints.

Reinforcement learning-based trajectory planning

In robotic manipulator trajectory planning, reinforcement learning (RL) can improve search adaptability by learning from interaction with the environment. Instead of relying only on a fixed optimization rule, the planner can update its trajectory strategy according to the current state, obstacle distribution, and optimization feedback. In this study, an RL-based planning framework is developed for the UR5 manipulator in spherical-obstacle environments. The SARSA algorithm is used as the policy update method and is combined with NSGA-II to support autonomous obstacle-avoidance trajectory planning.

SARSA

SARSA is used in this study because its on-policy update is well matched to safety-oriented trajectory search. In the proposed planner, a search action may change an individual toward a shorter path, lower energy, or shorter time, but it may also move the trajectory closer to an obstacle, a joint-position limit, or a velocity and acceleration bound. Therefore, the learning process should evaluate the behavior that is actually followed during exploration, including the constraint risk caused by that behavior.

As an on-policy temporal-difference method, SARSA updates the action value using the next action selected by the current \varepsilon -greedy policy. This means that the value estimate reflects the exploratory action that the planner will really execute, rather than only the best action under a greedy update. This property is useful in noisy and constrained trajectory optimization, because collision penalties, near-limit joint states, and population randomness can produce unstable reward feedback. By learning from the actually executed policy, SARSA gives a more cautious value estimate and reduces the chance that a near-boundary action is reinforced only because it gives a temporary improvement in path length, energy, or execution time.

Q-learning is also an effective off-policy method for many discrete decision problems. However, its update is based on the greedy next action, which is less directly tied to the exploratory behavior used during the current search. Since the present work gives priority to collision avoidance and constraint feasibility during evolutionary guidance, we selected SARSA to keep the learned search preference consistent with the current policy and its actual safety consequences. The compact state description and discrete action set used in RL-MOP-HNE also allow SARSA to be implemented in tabular form, making the learning module simple and reproducible within the multi-objective evolutionary framework. This policy-based update is useful for manipulator trajectory planning, where the search process must consider feasibility, obstacle avoidance, and motion quality at the same time.

At time step t, the agent observes the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t$$\end{document} and selects an action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_t$$\end{document}. After executing the action, it receives a reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_t$$\end{document}, moves to the next state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{t+1}$$\end{document}, and selects the next action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t+1}$$\end{document}. The SARSA update rule is written as18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q(s_t, a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t, a_t) + \alpha [r_t + \gamma Q(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1}) - Q(s_t, a_t)], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This update rule reflects the behavior generated by the current policy and is used to guide posture adjustment and operator selection during trajectory search. In this study, SARSA is trained through repeated episodes to learn feasible trajectory distributions under collision-avoidance constraints, which are then used to support the initialization and guidance of NSGA-II.

RL environment

An RL environment is constructed to describe the interaction among the manipulator, obstacles, and target point. The environment is built based on the UR5 kinematic and dynamic models. It includes joint constraints, spherical obstacles, state observation, action execution, and reward evaluation. Through repeated interaction with this environment, the agent gradually improves its planning strategy.

Observation and action spaces

The observation space should contain the main information required for obstacle avoidance and goal-directed motion. In this study, the state vector is defined as19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_t = [q_1, \dots , q_6, \dot{q}_1, \dots , \dot{q}_6, d_{\text {obs}}, d_{\text {goal}}], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{q}_i$$\end{document} are the angle and angular velocity of the ith joint, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\text {obs}}$$\end{document} denotes the distance from the end effector (EE) to the nearest obstacle, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\text {goal}}$$\end{document} is the Euclidean distance between the EE and the target point. This state design combines joint-space and task-space information, allowing the agent to consider both motion feasibility and obstacle clearance.

The action space is represented by joint-angle increments:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} a_t = [\Delta q_1, \Delta q_2, \dots , \Delta q_6], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta q_i$$\end{document} is the adjustment of the ith joint angle at the current step. Its range is limited by the joint velocity and acceleration constraints. In practical implementation, the action can be described by either discrete angular steps or continuous commands. This work uses a discrete action space to keep SARSA learning stable and easy to reproduce. Each action chooses a joint-angle increment from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta q_i \in \{-\delta , 0, +\delta \}$$\end{document}, where δ is selected so that the resulting motion can satisfy the velocity and acceleration limits after interpolation. The discrete increments are converted into continuous joint trajectories by fifth-order B-spline interpolation. This interpolation gives sufficient smoothness for the joint position, velocity, and acceleration profiles, and helps avoid torque discontinuities caused by abrupt changes between sampled points.

RL-integrated multi-objective trajectory planning

A single optimization method is often insufficient for trajectory planning with multiple conflicting objectives and strong constraints. To improve both search adaptability and convergence-diversity balance, this study proposes an RL-integrated multi-objective trajectory planning algorithm, named RL-MOP-HNE. The method combines SARSA-based RL guidance with an enhanced NSGA-II. A Gaussian-perturbation adaptive hybrid crossover operator and a hierarchical neighborhood evolution (HNE) mechanism are introduced to improve convergence, population diversity, and solution stability. The overall procedure of RL-MOP-HNE is shown in Algorithm 1.

To clarify the interaction among modeling, learning, and evolutionary optimization, the computational flow of RL-MOP-HNE is illustrated in Fig. 1. The framework contains an outer evolutionary loop and an inner RL feedback loop. The outer loop evaluates path length, energy consumption, execution time, dynamic feasibility, and obstacle avoidance, while the inner loop abstracts the current search state and uses SARSA to select the search action for the next generation.Fig. 1Algorithmic flowchart of RL-MOP-HNE.

Algorithmic flowchart of RL-MOP-HNE.

As shown in Fig. 1, the top-down trunk represents the main optimization path, and the two side modules represent SARSA-based search control and HNE-based population interaction. The non-dominated rank and crowding distance obtained from NSGA-II assessment are used both as state information for SARSA and as the basis for HNE stratification.

The feedback arrows in Fig. 1 indicate that infeasible offspring are filtered before reward updating, preventing collision-violating or constraint-violating solutions from being reinforced. SARSA adaptively controls crossover, perturbation, mutation, and hierarchical interaction, while HNE assigns elite refinement, neighborhood exploitation, and global exploration roles to different population layers. This closed-loop structure helps coordinate convergence, diversity, and dynamic feasibility during constrained trajectory optimization.

SARSA operator design

To improve the adaptability of the evolutionary search, a SARSA operator is embedded into RL-MOP-HNE. Through the state-action-reward-next state-next action process, the operator updates the value function and guides the selection of evolutionary operations. The update rule follows Eq. (18). In this process, the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t$$\end{document} describes the current trajectory individual, including path length, energy consumption, and obstacle clearance. The action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_t$$\end{document} represents the selected evolutionary operation, such as crossover or mutation. The reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_t$$\end{document} is calculated from the non-dominated rank and crowding distance of the individual. By learning the contribution of different operations during evolution, the SARSA operator helps the algorithm select more suitable search actions at different stages and improves the efficiency of the optimization process.

The SARSA policy also helps connect the search process with the different planning conditions discussed in Section 2. When the population is diverse and convergence is still weak, the learned policy tends to support broader crossover and perturbation operations. When the search is closer to an obstacle boundary or convergence pressure increases, mutation and local refinement become more useful. In this way, operator selection changes with the search state instead of following a fixed schedule.

Gaussian-perturbation adaptive hybrid crossover operator

To balance exploration and exploitation during evolution, a Gaussian-perturbation adaptive hybrid crossover operator, termed GA-SBX, is designed. The operator combines simulated binary crossover (SBX) with Gaussian perturbation. An adaptive coefficient is used to control the search range, so that the algorithm maintains stronger diversity in the early stage and gradually focuses on local refinement in the later stage. The operator is defined as22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x'_{i} = x_i + \beta (x_j - x_i) + \mathcal {N}(0, \sigma ^2), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_j$$\end{document} are parent individuals, β is an adaptive coefficient, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (t) = \sigma _0 \exp (-\lambda t / T_{\max })$$\end{document} decreases with the iteration process. At the beginning of optimization, a larger perturbation improves global exploration. As the search proceeds, the perturbation gradually decreases, allowing the population to refine solutions near promising regions. In the UR5 trajectory planning task, GA-SBX helps improve trajectory-node continuity, reduce random energy fluctuations, and preserve obstacle-avoidance feasibility.

Hierarchical neighborhood evolution mechanism

In complex MOPs, the algorithm should maintain diversity while improving convergence. Excessive exploration may slow convergence, whereas excessive exploitation may cause premature convergence and diversity loss among non-dominated solutions. To address this issue, an HNE mechanism is introduced. HNE combines population stratification with neighborhood-guided evolution, enabling different search behaviors for individuals with different qualities. It is integrated into the NSGA-II framework and is suitable for high-dimensional, non-convex, and multimodal trajectory-planning problems.

(a) Layering principle

At the tth iteration, the current population is defined as23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P^t = \{x_1^t, x_2^t, \dots , x_N^t\}, \quad x_i^t \in \mathbb {R}^D, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N denotes the total number of individuals in the population, while D represents the dimensionality of the optimization variables associated with the joint trajectory control points. For each individual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i^t$$\end{document}, the Pareto rank \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_i^t$$\end{document} is obtained by non-dominated sorting, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_i^t=1$$\end{document} denotes the first front. The crowding-distance metric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i^t$$\end{document} evaluates the distribution density of an individual among solutions belonging to the same Pareto front. To divide the population into different layers, a composite fitness index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i^t$$\end{document} is defined as24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_i^t = \omega _1 R_i^t - \omega _2 D_i^t, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where a smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_i^t$$\end{document} indicates better convergence, and a larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i^t$$\end{document} indicates better diversity. Therefore, the negative sign before \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i^t$$\end{document} ensures that individuals with better convergence and better distribution are assigned lower composite fitness values. The weight coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _2$$\end{document} satisfy25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \omega _1 + \omega _2 = 1, \quad \omega _1, \omega _2 > 0, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and are adjusted during evolution to balance convergence and diversity. According to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i^t$$\end{document}, the population is divided into three layers:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} P_E^t = \{ x_i^t \mid F_i^t \le \theta _1 \}, & \text {Elite layer} \\ P_D^t = \{ x_i^t \mid \theta _1 < F_i^t \le \theta _2 \}, & \text {Development layer} \\ P_G^t = \{ x_i^t \mid F_i^t > \theta _2 \}, & \text {Global exploration layer} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The dynamic thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _2$$\end{document} are defined as27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \theta _1 = \mu _F - \kappa _1 \sigma _F, \\ \theta _2 = \mu _F + \kappa _2 \sigma _F, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _F$$\end{document} denotes the average composite fitness of the current population, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _F$$\end{document} represents the corresponding standard deviation, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa _1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa _2$$\end{document} are positive parameters that control the proportions of the three layers. This statistical stratification responds to the current population distribution, but it should not be interpreted as a fixed rule that always assigns a certain number of individuals to each layer. When the initial population is highly homogeneous, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _F$$\end{document} is small and the thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _2$$\end{document} in Eq. (27) move close to the population mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _F$$\end{document}. In this case, the layer assignment is still determined by the relative composite fitness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i^t$$\end{document}, which includes both Pareto rank and crowding distance. Thus, small but useful differences among individuals are retained, rather than forcing the whole compact population into the elite layer.

If the population is almost identical, the threshold equation alone cannot create new diversity. For this reason, HNE also keeps explicit diversity-recovery channels. The global layer performs random exploration, the development–global interaction includes a Gaussian perturbation term, and the early-stage inter-layer weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _G(t)$$\end{document} gives more participation probability to global-search individuals. These mechanisms allow diversity to recover even when the initial standard deviation is low. As the population becomes more dispersed, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _F$$\end{document} increases, the thresholds separate more clearly, and the algorithm can gradually shift from broad exploration to focused refinement.

This three-layer structure directly mirrors the multi-time-scale regimes discussed in Section 2. The global exploration layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_G^t$$\end{document} contains the most diverse individuals and promotes broader trajectory-level search across different path geometries, corresponding to the slower task-level horizon. The elite layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_E^t$$\end{document} consists of the best-converged individuals, which undergo fine-grained neighborhood refinement that produces fast corrective adjustments in joint space, corresponding to the faster local dynamic regime. The development layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_D^t$$\end{document} serves as an intermediate bridge, receiving guidance from elite individuals while maintaining sufficient diversity to support global search. By aligning each layer’s search behavior with a specific temporal regime, HNE provides a structured mechanism for adapting the optimization process to the multi-rate nature of the trajectory planning problem.

(b) Neighborhood-guided evolution

To promote information exchange among layers, HNE introduces a neighborhood learning model. The neighborhood of individual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i^t$$\end{document} at generation t is defined as28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {N}(x_i^t) = \{ x_j^t \mid \Vert x_i^t - x_j^t \Vert < \rho _t \}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _t$$\end{document} is the dynamic neighborhood radius. It decreases with the iteration index t:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \rho _t = \rho _0 \exp \left( -\lambda _\rho \frac{t}{T_{\max }} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _0$$\end{document} is the initial radius, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\rho > 0$$\end{document} is the decay rate, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\max }$$\end{document} is the maximum number of iterations. A larger neighborhood in the early stage promotes global information exchange, while a smaller neighborhood in the later stage supports local refinement. This radius decay also interacts with the multi-time-scale structure: a broad early-stage neighborhood operates over the slower task-level horizon, allowing information to propagate across different trajectory geometries, while the shrinking late-stage neighborhood concentrates on fast, localized joint-space corrections that are typical of obstacle-avoidance maneuvers. The decay rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\rho $$\end{document} controls how quickly the search transitions between these two temporal regimes. Based on this neighborhood structure, different update strategies are applied to different layers.

1. Elite layer → development layer (guided transfer)

Elite individuals guide development-layer individuals according to30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x_j^{t+1} = x_j^t + \eta _1 (x_e^t - x_j^t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_e^t \in P_E^t$$\end{document} is the best elite individual in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {N}(x_j^t)$$\end{document}, selected according to the composite fitness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i^t$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _1 \in (0,1)$$\end{document} is the learning coefficient. This operation transfers high-quality search information to suboptimal individuals and accelerates local convergence in the development layer.

2. Development layer ↔ global layer (exploratory transfer)

Information exchange between the development and global layers is performed as31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x_k^{t+1} = x_k^t + \eta _2 (x_d^t - x_k^t) + \mathcal {N}(0, \sigma _d^2), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_d^t \in P_D^t$$\end{document} is a representative individual in the development-layer neighborhood, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _2 \in (0,1)$$\end{document} controls the learning intensity, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {N}(0, \sigma _d^2)$$\end{document} is a Gaussian perturbation term. This update introduces useful information from the development layer while preserving diversity, which helps avoid premature convergence. The perturbation term is especially useful when the population is initially homogeneous, because it provides a direct way to generate new local variations instead of relying only on the existing spread of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i^t$$\end{document}.

3. Global-layer self-update (random exploration)

Individuals in the global layer are updated by random exploration:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x_g^{t+1} = x_g^t + \xi _t (x_r^t - x_g^t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_r^t$$\end{document} is a randomly selected individual from the whole population or the global layer, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi _t \sim U(0,1)$$\end{document} is a uniformly distributed random variable. This operation helps the algorithm leave the current search region and explore new potential areas. Together with the development–global perturbation, this global-layer update prevents a compact initial population from being refined only around one narrow search region.

Through these differentiated neighborhood interactions, HNE allows elite knowledge to diffuse downward while maintaining active exploration in lower layers. As a result, the population can obtain a more diverse and higher-quality non-dominated solution set in complex trajectory-planning problems.

(c) Dynamic adjustment of inter-layer weights

To further balance exploration and exploitation, the inter-layer weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _E(t)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _D(t)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _G(t)$$\end{document} are introduced to represent the probabilities that individuals from the elite, development, and global layers participate in crossover and mutation. These weights are adjusted according to the generation index t:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \phi _E(t) = \frac{t}{T_{\max }}, \\ \phi _D(t) = 1 - \left| \frac{2t}{T_{\max }} - 1 \right| , \\ \phi _G(t) = 1 - \frac{t}{T_{\max }}, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\max }$$\end{document} is the maximum number of iterations. This mechanism follows the search pattern of early exploration, middle-stage balance, and late exploitation. In the early stage, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _G(t)$$\end{document} is large, and the global layer plays the main role in maintaining diversity. This early emphasis is another safeguard for homogeneous initial populations, because it gives exploratory updates enough opportunity before elite refinement becomes dominant. In the middle stage, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _D(t)$$\end{document} reaches its maximum value, supporting a balance between convergence and diversity. In the late stage, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _E(t)$$\end{document} becomes dominant, and the search focuses on refining high-quality solutions.

The HNE operator can be expressed in a probabilistic form:34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x_i^{t+1} = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} x_i^{t+1}(P_E^t), & \text {with probability }\ \phi _E(t) \\ x_i^{t+1}(P_D^t), & \text {with probability }\ \phi _D(t) \\ x_i^{t+1}(P_G^t), & \text {with probability }\ \phi _G(t) \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i^{t+1}(P^t)$$\end{document} denotes the next-generation individual generated by the neighborhood interaction rule of the corresponding layer, as given in Eqs. (30)–(32). Through dynamic weight adjustment, HNE allocates search effort according to the evolutionary stage and improves convergence-diversity balance in nonlinear and high-dimensional constrained problems.

(d) Theoretical rationale and applicability

The HNE mechanism follows the ideas of layered search and cooperative co-evolution. It decomposes a complex multi-objective search process into several heterogeneous sub-search tasks, so that local convergence can be improved without losing global exploration. The mechanism has three main features.

First, the composite fitness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i^t$$\end{document} combines non-dominated rank \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_i^t$$\end{document} and crowding distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i^t$$\end{document}, providing a balance between convergence and diversity. Second, the neighborhood radius \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _t$$\end{document} decays exponentially, which supports a coarse-to-fine search process. Third, the inter-layer update rules in Eqs. (30)–(32) form a knowledge-diffusion process, allowing useful search directions to spread from high-quality individuals to other layers.

When the learning coefficients satisfy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0< \eta _1, \eta _2 < 1$$\end{document} and the neighborhood radius decreases with the iteration process, the update step tends to become smaller in the late stage. This behavior can be expressed as35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \lim _{t \rightarrow T_{\max }} \Vert x_i^{t+1} - x_i^t\Vert = 0, \quad \forall i \in P^t. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This relation indicates that the population gradually reaches a stable state in the late stage, reducing oscillation and unnecessary search variation. In practical trajectory optimization, HNE provides stage-dependent search behavior: broad exploration in the early stage, diversity maintenance in the middle stage, and local refinement in the final stage. This behavior is suitable for multi-objective manipulator trajectory planning with high-dimensional decision variables, nonlinear constraints, and conflicting objectives. Therefore, HNE can improve solution diversity, convergence stability, and algorithm robustness in complex obstacle environments.

The above modeling and algorithmic design lead to several experimentally verifiable expectations. The kinematic and collision-avoidance model should be reflected in feasible EE trajectories without obstacle penetration; the dynamic model and objective formulation should be reflected in path length, energy consumption, execution time, and joint-velocity indicators; the SARSA module should improve search direction and reduce unnecessary detours; and the HNE mechanism should improve convergence stability and motion smoothness. Section 5 is therefore organized to evaluate these claims through case-based trajectory comparisons, normalized performance profiles, convergence curves, and ablation tests.

Experiments and results analysis

To evaluate the performance of the proposed RL-MOP-HNE in cluttered environments, a series of comparative experiments is carried out on the MATLAB simulation platform. Comparative algorithms

For comparative evaluation, six representative multi-objective optimization algorithms were selected as benchmark methods, including MOEA/D, MOPSO, MSCLPSO, NSGA-II, RL-NSGA-II, and the proposed RL-MOP-HNE algorithm. Specifically, MOEA/D decomposes a multi-objective optimization task into several scalar optimization subproblems that are optimized collaboratively, while MOPSO is a swarm intelligence-based optimizer designed for handling multiple conflicting objectives. MSCLPSO extends the conventional particle swarm framework by introducing a comprehensive learning strategy to enhance population diversity and global search capability. NSGA-II, as one of the most classical Pareto-based multi-objective evolutionary algorithms, employs non-dominated sorting and crowding distance mechanisms to maintain convergence and diversity. RL-NSGA-II further incorporates SARSA-based reinforcement learning guidance into NSGA-II to improve adaptive search behavior. In contrast, the proposed RL-MOP-HNE integrates SARSA learning with the HNE optimization mechanism to improve convergence stability, solution distribution, and joint-motion smoothness.

Each algorithm is executed 30 times independently under identical conditions. These repeated runs are used for convergence-variability and statistical analyses. Cross-case comprehensive discussion

Across the three trajectory scenarios, the dominant challenge changes from local detour, to narrow-passage coordination, and then to coupled multi-obstacle robustness. MOEA/D shows a direct decomposition-driven preference and often preserves efficient geometric progress. MOPSO now appears as an aggressive turning strategy: it creates visibly different trajectories, but this preference leads to larger velocity peaks and fluctuation. MSCLPSO benefits from population diversity and gives exploratory paths, although this often requires larger joint motion. NSGA-II provides a conservative baseline with wider detours, while RL-NSGA-II improves geometric directionality but is still sensitive to local transitions. RL-MOP-HNE is not the fastest planner in these cases. Its main advantage is that it accepts a longer execution duration in exchange for smoother joint-angle and joint-velocity profiles. This trade-off is more suitable for tasks where controllable motion is more important than minimum-time execution, such as precision assembly, medical robotics, and human–robot collaboration.

Cross-case comprehensive comparison

The performance profiles, convergence curves, and quantitative metrics across the three cases show that the algorithms have different strengths under different environmental properties. In the single-obstacle case, MOPSO and related swarm methods can exploit local shortcuts effectively. In the narrow-passage case, MOEA/D and RL-NSGA-II show good geometric efficiency, but dynamic smoothness becomes harder to maintain. In the irregular multi-obstacle case, diversity-oriented and learning-guided methods become more useful for path compactness, yet their dynamic fluctuation remains higher. NSGA-II provides a conservative baseline but loses competitiveness as constraints become coupled. Across all three cases, RL-MOP-HNE obtains the lowest dynamic-stability-prioritized composite score, with S=0.050, 0.050, and 0.087 in Cases I–III, respectively. This cross-case trend indicates that the proposed method maintains the most stable dynamic behavior, while accepting a longer execution duration in exchange for lower joint-velocity peaks and fluctuations. Therefore, RL-MOP-HNE should be understood as a dynamically stable planner rather than an algorithm that simply dominates every single objective. A time-scaling check was also conducted to separate path geometry from time allocation. In Case I, all trajectories were rescaled to the same duration of 4.54 s. Under this common time budget, NSGA-II reduced its velocity variance from 0.212 to 0.026 and its maximum velocity from 0.76 to 0.26, which are lower than the corresponding RL-MOP-HNE values after scaling (velVar=0.072, maxVel=1.04). This result indicates that part of the smoothness advantage of RL-MOP-HNE comes from its conservative time parameterization rather than from path geometry alone. Its practical value is that this time allocation is selected automatically during optimization, instead of being imposed manually after planning. The current experiments use spherical obstacles to keep the geometry simple and the comparison reproducible. More realistic workspaces may include non-convex objects such as benches, fixtures, shelves, or containers. The framework can be extended to such cases by replacing the signed-distance collision check with a mesh-based, occupancy-map, or signed-distance-field evaluator, while keeping the RL-guided evolutionary search unchanged.

Statistical significance results and discussion

The statistical results show that RL-MOP-HNE has significant advantages over No-RL and No-HNE in path length and energy consumption. At the 0.05 significance level with n=30 runs, the path-length improvements are significant at p<0.01 for both ablation comparisons, and the energy-consumption improvements are significant at p<0.05. The smoothness cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_s$$\end{document} also shows significant improvement at p<0.05, indicating that the reduction in joint-velocity fluctuation is unlikely to be caused by random variation alone. These results confirm that both RL guidance and HNE contribute to multi-objective search performance. The complete method also improves trajectory smoothness, while the planned execution time does not differ significantly among the three ablation configurations.

From a computational viewpoint, tabular SARSA adds little storage demand, while HNE introduces \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(N\log N+N|\mathcal {N}|)$$\end{document} operations per generation. The measured CPU time is therefore scenario dependent. It is close to NSGA-II in Case I, but about 4.3 and 4.2 times the NSGA-II time in Cases II and III, respectively. This should be considered when applying the method to dense obstacle environments. Overall, the ablation results and statistical tests show that the performance gains of RL-MOP-HNE are not caused by random variation. They mainly come from the combined effect of RL-based search guidance and HNE-based population evolution.

Conclusion

This paper presented RL-MOP-HNE, a reinforcement learning-guided multi-objective trajectory planning framework for obstacle avoidance by a 6-DOF UR5 manipulator. The trajectory planning problem was formulated as a three-objective optimization task that simultaneously considers path length, energy consumption, and execution time under kinematic, dynamic, and collision-avoidance constraints. Within the proposed framework, a SARSA-based reinforcement learning strategy adaptively guides the evolutionary search according to the current optimization state, while a Gaussian-perturbation adaptive hybrid crossover operator and hierarchical neighborhood evolution (HNE) progressively improve population refinement and search efficiency. Experimental evaluation was conducted in three representative environments with increasing planning difficulty. RL-MOP-HNE successfully generated collision-free trajectories in all scenarios, including the most constrained Case III, where a positive minimum clearance of 0.002 m was maintained throughout the planned motion.

Compared with the five baseline algorithms, RL-MOP-HNE achieved the lowest dynamic-stability-prioritized composite score across all three benchmark cases. The planned trajectories generally exhibited lower joint-velocity peaks and reduced velocity fluctuations, leading to smoother robot motions. These improvements, however, were accompanied by longer execution times and, in more complex environments, increased computational cost. Rather than providing the best performance for every objective, the proposed framework offers a balanced trade-off by favouring motion stability and dynamic executability over minimum-time execution. This observation is consistently supported by the convergence behaviour, performance indicators, statistical analysis, and ablation studies. The results further show that reinforcement learning mainly improves search direction during evolution, whereas HNE enhances solution refinement in the later optimization stage. Removing either component leads to noticeable degradation in trajectory quality, energy-related performance, and motion smoothness.

Additional trajectory-quality analyses provide further insight into the behaviour of the proposed framework. Across all test environments, RL-MOP-HNE maintains the smallest variation in manipulability while preserving positive obstacle clearance, indicating more gradual changes in kinematic conditioning along the planned trajectory. Although localized jerk peaks remain in some trajectory segments, the overall motion is smoother and more suitable for practical robotic execution. These findings suggest that RL-MOP-HNE is particularly appropriate for applications where stable and dynamically feasible motions are more critical than achieving the shortest possible execution time, such as precision assembly, medical robotics, and human–robot collaboration.

The present study adopts spherical obstacles to establish a reproducible benchmark for algorithm evaluation. The proposed optimization framework is not restricted to this collision representation and can be extended to more realistic industrial environments by incorporating mesh-based collision models, occupancy maps, or signed-distance-field representations without changing the overall optimization framework. Future work will focus on validation using a physical UR5 platform, extension to higher-DOF and dual-arm manipulators, handling dynamic obstacles and end-effector orientation constraints, incorporating uncertainty-aware planning, and integrating more comprehensive trajectory-quality metrics, including DCSV-based evaluation criteria.
