Precomputing Multi-Agent Path Replanning using Temporal Flexibility: A Case Study on the Dutch Railway Network
Issa Hanou, Eric Kemmeren, Devin Wild Thomas, Mathijs de Weerdt

Abstract
Executing a multi-agent plan can be challenging when an agent is delayed, because this typically creates conflicts with other agents. So, we need to quickly find a new safe plan. Replanning only the delayed agent often does not result in an efficient plan, and sometimes cannot even yield a feasible plan. On the other hand, replanning other agents may lead to a cascade of changes and delays. We show how to efficiently replan by tracking and using the temporal flexibility of other agents while avoiding cascading delays. This flexibility is the maximum delay an agent can take without changing the order of or further delaying more agents. Our algorithm, FlexSIPP, precomputes all possible plans for the delayed agent, also returning the changes for the other agents, for any single-agent delay within the given scenario. We demonstrate our method in a real-world case study of replanning trains in the densely-used Dutch railway network. Our experiments show that FlexSIPP provides effective solutions, relevant to real-world adjustments, and within a reasonable timeframe.

Introduction

When executing in a multi-agent environment, delays frequently occur, which can result in conflicts and disrupt the previously safe execution of the plan.
Resolving these execution conflicts often requires deciding which agents will have their plans disrupted and by how much.
As conflicts compromise the safe execution, a decision must be made swiftly, while replanning takes time.

In this paper, we address the multi-agent path replanning problem, where a single agent needs to be replanned (e.g., because of a delay), and our objective is to recover a safe plan as quickly as possible.
Full replanning of all agents can be computationally expensive, so we consider the subset of these problems where other agents may only be delayed, not rerouted, and this delay must not introduce additional conflicts.
These constraints are inspired by the application of railway networks.
We quantify an agent’s ability to absorb a delay as its
temporal flexibility
, which is the maximum delay that it can take without changing the order in which agents use a resource, and without further delaying more agents.
The temporal flexibility is easily precomputed from a given plan.
With this, we can identify the set of
tipping points
for the delayed agent, which are the extent of the delays causing a conflict with another agent at which the ordering should change (i.e., when the other agent should go first).

This work is most closely related to Multi-Agent Execution Delay Replanning (MAEDeR)
(
Hanou et al. 2024
)
, which addresses a stricter subset of delay replanning problems, where the originally delayed agent must recover without affecting the plans of any other agents.
Like MAEDeR, our approach uses the Safe Interval Path Planning (SIPP)
(
Phillips and Likhachev 2011
)
state space to encode when resources are not used by other agents.
With this problem representation, optimal single-agent plans for all start times can be efficiently computed
(
Thomas et al. 2023
)
, by treating other agents as moving obstacles.

Hanou et al. 2024
use this to shift replanning to be ‘in advance’, and recover from some execution conflicts instantly by looking up a new plan for the delayed agent in its any-start-time plan.
While the resulting multi-agent plan is safe, MAEDeR does not necessarily provide an optimal solution, as it is based on the assumption that the plans of agents other than the delayed agent cannot be modified.

In comparison to MAEDeR, because we allow for the delay of other agents, we can reduce the overall delay.
This method, called FlexSIPP, still provides near-instant delay replanning, and the required precomputation runs in polynomial time.
FlexSIPP was originally described by
Kemmeren 2025
as a railway-specific approach.
In this paper, we formalize FlexSIPP and generalize it to include the multi-agent path replanning setting with continuous time, demonstrating that we can efficiently replan a single agent, either because it is delayed or because its initial plan fails for other reasons.

To evaluate the effectiveness of FlexSIPP, we analyze a real-world case study of a defective high-speed line and the process of rerouting the affected trains in the regular network in the Netherlands.
We identify the tipping points in relation to the regular trains in the network so that we can plan the rerouted train in between them.
As the Dutch railway network is densely used, we show the need for using agents’ temporal flexibility to find a feasible solution.

Our main contribution is the FlexSIPP algorithm for quickly replanning a delayed agent in a multi-agent pathfinding context.
We present a method for determining the flexibility of agents and use tipping points to illustrate the replanning trade-off between delaying other agents or further delaying or rerouting a delayed agent.
A case study of the railway network in the Netherlands is used to demonstrate the benefits of automatically identifying tipping points and using these in real-world situations.

Background

We begin by describing the state space representation of Safe Interval Path Planning (SIPP); Any-Start-Time Planning, which is the single-agent optimal search used by our precomputation; and Multi-Agent Execution Delay Replanning (MAEDeR), which is the most closely related formulation of the multi-agent delay replanning problem.

Any-Start-Time Planning

The original SIPP algorithm is an
A^{*}

Any-start-time planning (@SIPP) generalizes SIPP to plan for all start times by searching with earliest arrival-time functions (ATFs), rather than scalar

(
Thomas et al. 2023

An ATF maps the departure time of the agent, when it begins traversing an edge, to the earliest arrival time at the end of the edge.
A SIPP graph

\langle t_{s}^{s_{2}},t_{e}^{s_{2}}\rangle

This ATF

\displaystyle\zeta=t_{s}^{s_{1}}

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \beta=\min(&t_{e}^{e},t_{e}^{s_{1}},t_{e}^{s_{2}}-\delta(s_{1},s_{2}))\end{aligned}

Multi-Agent Execution Delay Replanning

We build on the work done by
Hanou et al. 2024
that applied Any-Start-Time Planning to solve a subset of multi-agent delay replanning problems.
They defined the Multi-Agent Execution Delay Replanning (MAEDeR) problem, which is a single-agent problem in the multi-agent setting.
A MAEDeR problem is defined by the tuple
\langle N,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}\rangle
.
Here,
N
is the set of components that can be represented as a graph with edges between locations (e.g., a railway network),
\mathcal{A}
is the set of agents (e.g., trains) navigating through the network, and
\mathcal{C}
holds the problem characteristics on how agents interact with the network and each other.
A solution to MAEDeR is a function
\mathcal{M}
that takes an agent
{a}^{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathcal{A}
and a positively delayed start time
{t}^{\mathrm{d}}
, and returns the shortest safe plan for this delayed agent not affecting the plans of other agents in
\mathcal{A}
, or a failure if no such feasible plan exists.

Hanou et al. 2024
describe two algorithms to solve MAEDeR problems.
First, Replanning SIPP (rSIPP) performs a SIPP search for the delayed agent, resulting in a set of SIPP graphs that can be generated before execution.
However, these SIPP graphs still need to be searched for a new safe plan during execution.
Second, @MAEDeR precomputes the SIPP graphs and already transforms them into @SIPP graphs (with ATF edges).
These are searched before execution using the RePEAT algorithm
(
Thomas et al. 2023
)
, which repeatedly searches for optimal plans for monotonically increasing start times.
The resulting set of plans can be rapidly queried for an optimal plan corresponding to any departure time.
Essentially, @MAEDeR replans in advance, so that at execution time, replanning just involves querying the any-start-time plan of the delayed agent.
While they find the optimal solution provided only the delayed agent is replanned, the resulting multi-agent plan is not necessarily optimal.
Sometimes, a lower-cost overall plan can be found by also replanning other agents.

Problem Description: Flexible Multi-Agent Delay Replanning

Consider a multi-agent path planning problem and a solution to this problem consisting of trajectories for all agents, and assume that the plan of one agent cannot be safely executed (e.g., the agent is delayed); we want to replan that agent, based on the temporal flexibility of the other agents.
We start with the model of this replanning problem as an @SIPP graph
(
Thomas et al. 2023
)
.
This graph is created from the perspective of the delayed agent, which means that the safe intervals are safe for the delayed agent to not conflict with the movements of all the other agents.
Later, we explain how to extend this graph with the temporal flexibility of the other agents.

Formally, a Flexible Multi-Agent Delay Replanning problem is defined as the tuple
\langle\mathcal{A},{a}^{\mathrm{d}},N,\delta,x_{o},x_{g},t_{o},t^{\mathrm{max}},\Pi\rangle

•

to configuration
x_{2}

Search

We find the any-start-time plan for agent
{a}^{\mathrm{d}}
using the RePEAT algorithm on the @SIPP graph with the additional flexible ATFs.
These ATFs define the flexibility that can be used during the search.
To ensure feasible plans for the other agents, we track the flexibility used by agent
{a}^{\mathrm{F}}
.
A flexible ATF
{A}^{\mathrm{F}}=\langle{\zeta}^{\mathrm{F}},{\alpha}^{\mathrm{F}},{\beta}^{\mathrm{F}},{\Delta}^{\mathrm{F}}\rangle
allows some agent
{a}^{\mathrm{F}}\neq{a}^{\mathrm{d}}
to be delayed in some configuration
{x}^{\mathrm{d}}
by at most
f_{{a}^{\mathrm{F}}}({x}^{\mathrm{d}},{t}^{\mathrm{d}})
.
This delay has an impact on every configuration-time pair in the single-agent plan
\pi_{{a}^{\mathrm{F}}}
that is visited after configuration
{x}^{\mathrm{d}}
.
Using a flexible ATF restricts the use of other agents’ flexibility.
The delay impacts the availability of the ATFs that were bound by the occupation of agent
{a}^{\mathrm{d}}
.

For all configurations
\overline{x}
that agent
{a}^{\mathrm{F}}
visits after it visits
{x}^{\mathrm{d}}
, these configurations were originally safe during some interval
\langle\underline{t_{s}},\underline{t_{e}}\rangle
(before agent
{a}^{\mathrm{F}}
visits), and also safe during some interval
\langle\overline{t_{s}},\overline{t_{e}}\rangle

{a}^{\mathrm{F}}
visits).
These intervals are then updated such that the first interval gets
\langle\underline{t_{s}},\underline{t_{e}}+f_{{a}^{\mathrm{F}}}({x}^{\mathrm{d}},{t}^{\mathrm{d}})\rangle
and the second interval is updated to
\langle\overline{t_{s}}~+~f_{{a}^{\mathrm{F}}}({x}^{\mathrm{d}},{t}^{\mathrm{d}}),~\overline{t_{e}}\rangle
.
The exact update of the arrival time functions is domain-dependent based on the agent’s kinematics and size.

Using the search procedure described above, we now show that we find the best replanning solution given the agent’s flexibility in polynomial time (Proposition

The flexibility is derived in polynomial time (Proposition
1
), and the construction of the @SIPP graph is also done in polynomial time.
Each configuration adds a safe interval to the problem, and each agent splits such an interval as it passes through. Therefore, the problem size scales linearly with the number of configurations (the size of the network
N

O(|N|\cdot|\mathcal{A}|^{2})

The branching factor in the

If a replanning solution exists that involves only delaying one of the other agents according to their flexibility (Definition

During search, we track the flexibility used, as well as any delay encountered for some agent

Case Study: Rotterdam to Schiphol

To evaluate the effect of using the flexibility of other agents, we consider the example of rerouting trains in a busy railway network.
When disturbances occur in railway network operations, trains are delayed, and in some cases, they must be rerouted.
If the tracks are already occupied close to full capacity, it is very difficult to fit this rerouting into the schedule.
Therefore, it becomes necessary to adjust the schedule slightly to accommodate delayed trains.

Specifically, we consider the tight schedule in the Netherlands, where train services are frequent and are expected to become even more metro-like in the next few years
(
ProRail 2024
)
[p.21].
The railway network in the Netherlands is mostly double-track, and in some cases, it is even quadruple-track, allowing trains to be rerouted along an alternative track.
In that case, the replanning must consider the trains originally planned to use that track, preferably without significantly disturbing their planned service.

A high-speed line (HSL) runs between the stations of Rotterdam Central and Schiphol Airport to facilitate easy travel between two of the largest cities: Amsterdam and Rotterdam.
This line is also used for international trains to Brussels, Paris, and London.
However, the high-speed line is particularly prone to issues: train punctuality is only 69% on this line, compared to 89% on the rest of the network
(
NS 2024a
)
.
When the HSL is unavailable due to large disturbances, trains scheduled to use it must be rerouted over the regular network, through Leiden and The Hague (see Figure
3
).
In this case, the previously discussed algorithm (@MAEDeR) for the Multi-Agent Execution Delay Replanning problem falls short because the schedule is so tight that no complete path can be found between the regular trains.
Therefore, we must slightly delay some of the regular trains to make room for the HSL-rescheduled trains; otherwise, they cannot be scheduled at all.

In practice, railway companies (in the Netherlands) use
train handling documents
, which can be understood as a flow chart saying what the appropriate action is if a train traveling a specific route is delayed
(
NS 2024b
)
.
These documents thus specify the tipping points, when to delay the other trains, and when the delayed train should allow other trains to proceed first.
For example, in the case of the Rotterdam-Leiden-Schiphol line, the tipping point used in industry for regular intercity and sprinter trains is 7 minutes; regular trains on the route between The Hague and Leiden can thus be delayed by 7 minutes to allow another train to proceed first
(
Kemmeren 2025
)
[p.14].
Currently, these documents are created manually, although they are not specified for every situation.
For example, there is no tipping point defined for this Eursotar case.
So, FlexSIPP provides a safe and automated alternative to the industry.

Railway Replanning with Flexibility

The railway network is divided into
blocks
, and only one train is allowed inside a block at a time.
A common safety procedure uses a trackside signal to inform train drivers whether a block is occupied.
To calculate when a block is free to enter, the railway industry uses
blocking times
, which can be calculated precisely using the length of the block, the speed of the train, the reaction time of the driver, and the time it takes the train to clear the block
(
Goverde, Corman, and D’Ariano 2013
)
.
The blocking times can be visualized over the travel distance as in Figure
4
.
In this example, two trains follow each other on the same route.
As long as the blocking times do not overlap, no conflict arises between these two trains.
We find the minimum separation of two trains, called the
headway
, when two trains directly follow each other in at least one block, which is referred to as the critical block.
The flexibility of trains is thus easily visualized as the gaps between their blocking times, in industry referred to as
buffer time
, which also depends on the flexibility in the next block.

Now, we show how to model the Railway Replanning Problem as a Flexible Multi-Agent Delay Replanning problem.
Each block is a configuration
x
, allowing one agent to enter a block at a time.
We represent the railway network in a routing graph, connecting blocks based on whether a train travels directly from one to another
(
Kemmeren 2025
)
.
The blocking times are used to calculate the traversal times
\Delta
between blocks.
From a given timetable, we generate the @SIPP graph for the delayed train and extract the trajectories of other trains, which are the dynamic obstacles.
Based on this timetable, we then compute the flexibility of the other trains, given by the blocking time computations.
Finally, we also allow the delayed agent to be a completely new agent that was not included in the original plan
\Pi
.
In this case, the @SIPP graph that is given as input for Flexible Multi-Agent Delay Replanning has safe intervals that avoid conflicts with all agents from the original plan.

Evaluation

To demonstrate our method, we evaluate it on our case study. Moreover, we compare it to the other methods mentioned in the Background section: rSIPP (the replanning algorithm) and @MAEDeR (replanning in advance), answering the following research questions.

Q1

Can FlexSIPP provide tipping points for the case study of replanning a high-speed train over the regular tracks in the Netherlands?

Q2

Does FlexSIPP provide better plans for the case study than rSIPP and @MAEDeR in terms of minimizing the overall delay?

Q3

How do rSIPP, @MAEDeR, and FlexSIPP compare in terms of runtime?

Experimental Setup

FlexSIPP is implemented in two stages: the domain-specific implementation (written in Python) that processes the infrastructure and computes the @SIPP graph, and the search stage (written in C++) that takes an @SIPP graph and computes the any-start-time plan for the delayed agent with the amount of flexibility used.
The search algorithm is domain-independent and generalizes to any @SIPP graph, offering flexibility in terms of safe intervals.
The experiments were run on an AMD Ryzen 5600X with 32GB of RAM and a time limit of 1800 seconds for the search algorithm.

Related Work

Our method uses flexibility to find a single-agent plan in multi-agent environments, so we discuss the Multi-Agent Pathfinding (MAPF) problem, where multiple agents need to reach their goal locations without creating conflicts with each other in their paths
(
Stern et al. 2019
)
.
More specifically, our application relates to replanning in MAPF settings, such as rerouting a specific agent, as well as planning for new agents within a previously planned scenario.
Previous work on MAPF already used an extended problem formulation allowing new agents to appear
(
Švancara et al. 2019
)
, using several methods: 1) replanning one new agent without influencing other agents, 2) replanning several new agents at once without influencing other agents, 3) replanning all agents, and 4) planning a route for the new agent, ignoring other routes and resolving conflicts while replanning. The first two methods do not allow changes to the plans of other agents, similar to MAEDeR’s problem formulation. The third method is very inefficient as it ignores previous information and has long computation times. The last method is more efficient and uses prior knowledge, but may result in suboptimal solutions.
FlexSIPP strikes a new balance between computational efficiency and optimality by keeping the routes of all other agents, but adjusting their delays to create an opening for the new agent to plan (represented by an extended @SIPP graph).

Our SIPP representation allows for continuous time solutions, relating to Continuous MAPF approaches, such as Continuous Conflict-Based Search (CCBS)
(
Andreychuk et al. 2019
)
.
Their method is a full multi-agent approach, using an adopted SIPP algorithm as a low-level algorithm for path generation.
However, their method focuses on planning and does not allow for replanning or flexibility.

In Lifelong MAPF, new goals are continuously added to the current scenario during execution.
So, the plan must constantly be updated to accommodate the current scenario.
While different strategies for replanning are used, such as replanning all agents, or just the affected agents
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, these methods do not reuse the knowledge from solving the scenario in earlier timesteps.
FlexSIPP could also be applied here to more efficiently use the information at different points in time and find a better overall solution.

Our approach solves a replanning problem for a delayed agent.
We could use this to iteratively build a multi-agent plan by adding one agent at a time, which is closely related to Prioritised Planning
(
Morag et al. 2025
)
.
However, this would require deciding on the priority of the agents, which affects the flexibility of the agents as well.

In terms of replanning in railway networks, many methods use Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) models
(
Fang, Yang, and Yao 2015
)
.
Most often, the goal is to minimize the sum of all delays or maximize the robustness of the network, where the latter aims to find a timetable in which delayed trains are less likely to propagate and impact the scheduled times of other trains.
FlexSIPP is able to quickly return the precomputed any-start-time plan with flexibility, or determine right away if no such plan can be found.

Conclusion

We introduce a new approach to replan a single delayed agent in a multi-agent context.
Inspired by the railway operations, we allow other agents to be safely delayed, based on the temporal flexibility present in their original plans.
The delayed agent can now have a start time previously considered unsafe, which becomes safe by safely delaying another agent, allowing us to use this inherent flexibility that was previously unused.
As flexibility is based on their original plans, ensuring no conflicts occur with the added flexibility, we guarantee that the replanning of the delayed agent does not further impact any other agents.
So, we can reorder a delayed agent and other agents from the original plan.
We demonstrated that our method is effective in real-world settings, transforming detailed railway infrastructure into a simplified model using safe interval path planning.
While able to handle the continuous time component and different speed profiles of agents, our method is also effective in simpler grid-world settings, such as Multi-Agent Pathfinding (e.g., our running example).
Our method brings the single-agent replanning approach using any-start-time planning closer to a true multi-agent pathfinding approach.
