Dissecting the transcriptomic signature of porcine oocyte during primordial follicle formation from single cell resolution
Authors: Xin-Hao Zhang, Kai Zhang, Yu Tian, Gui-Li Ruan, Fa-Li Zhang, Ke-Xin Zhu, Ai-Ying Li, Wei Shen, Jun-Jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian reserve, refers to the number of primordial follicle (PF) in mammals, is a decisive factor affecting the reproductive lifespan of females. However, the molecular mechanism of PF formation has not been fully elucidated, especially in large mammals. In the study, the ovarian tissues at embryonic day 95 (E95) and postnatal day 0 (PD0) were collected for single-nucleus transcriptome sequencing, attempting to investigate the transcriptome dynamic of porcine oocyte during PF formation at a single-cell level.ResultsIn the results, we identified in detail three germ cell clusters with different transcriptional modes. Moreover, through a comparative analysis of developmental trajectory in pig, mouse and monkey, several conserved regulatory genes that associated with the developmental fate of germ cells were identified. By employing SCENIC software, we identified 27 transcription factors specific to developmental stages of germ cells. In addition, a protein interaction network between transcription factors and conserved genes was constructed and it discovered a key transcription factor, enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), which is expressed exclusively in oocytes during PF formation, and there is the asynchrony of its expression in germ cells between the medulla and cortex regions. Further, during the in vitro culture of mouse ovaries, the inhibition of EZH2 expression significantly decreased the number of oocytes and leading to the decline of protein expressions that essential for PF formation.ConclusionsThis study provides a single-cell map of pig ovary in the late embryonic development and newly born periods, and enriches the public understanding of primordial follicle formation in mammals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02126-6.

Introduction

The primordial follicle (PF) is the basic functional unit of reproduction in female, consisting of a single oocyte and surrounding granulosa cells. In mammals, the limited ovarian reserves determine their reproductive lifespan. Generally, once the PF pool established, it never grow up in number, and the insufficient number of PFs during gestation can also lead to primary ovarian insufficiency, after puberty, the periodic recruitment and development of PFs lead to a continuous consumption of PF pool, it is eventually exhausted and menopause occurs in animals [1]. PF formation process mainly occurs during mid- and late-pregnancy in mammals, the molecular mechanism of which is largely unknown due to multiple reasons.

Currently, the mechanism study of PF formation process is most concentrated on rodents. As early as embryonic (E) 11.5 day, the primordial germ cells (PGCs) in mice arriving in genital ridges begin mitosis, the cyst containing an average of approximately 30 germ cells is formed because the incomplete cytoplasmic division [2]. Subsequently, the germ cells enter meiosis and eventually arrest in the diplotene stage of prophase I [3]. And at E16.5, the germline cyst begins to disintegrate due to the invasion of pre-granulosa cells, in this process, the germ cells in a cyst face two fate choices, nurse cell and oocyte, the former transport substances such as mitochondria, Golgi bodies and mRNA to the dominant oocyte through intercellular bridges and the latter develop into oocyte enclosed with pre-granulosa cells in PF [4, 5]. A growing researches suggested that the nurse cells tend to occur apoptosis, autophagy and necrosis [6], and in the recent publication, it uncovered that nurse cells undergo acidification-dependent programmed cell death [7]. Eventually at PD5, accompanied by massive germ cell lost, only about one-fifth of the germ cells survive and are assembled into PFs by pre-granulosa cells [8]. In addition, there were two waves of PF formation process, which occur at medullary and cortical regions, the former develops rapidly and contribute to the onset of puberty, the latter sustain fertility in post-puberty lifespan. To be noteworthy, there are great differences between mice and other large mammals (such as human) in physiological structure, especially gestation time, which imply that the PF formation process in mice is completed in a shorter period of time, while humans have more time to proceed, so there must be huge differences in the regulatory mechanisms between them.

As we all know, pigs are also an important animal and biomedical model. However, the related study of early ovarian development in pig is largely scant. It has been demonstrated that pig PGCs firstly appear about E12 [9], which migrate to the genital ridges at E18 [10], and undergo mitosis to form germ cysts from E20 to E42 [11], these germ cells initiated meiosis around E40 [12–14], then cyst dissociates and forms the first wave of follicles on E56-68 [15, 16], and the second wave of primordial follicles on a larger scale is assembled around E90, until the cyst structure in the ovary is basically completely dissociated after birth [17]. To better deeply understand the regulatory mechanism of PF formation, it’s urgent to carry the related study in pigs.

In the latest study, the single cell transcriptome was applied in ovarian development and bring some novel insights of regulatory mechanism concerning PF formation. Recently, several reports uncovered the heterogeneity of early ovarian tissue in mammals using the single-cell transcriptome technology. In Niu’s report, the female meiosis is divided into seven relevant stages, that is pre-meiotic, pre-leptotene, leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene and dictyate, and two waves of primordial follicle formation are supported by bipotential pregranulosa (BPG) and epithelial pregranulosa (EPG) cells [18]. And in Wang’s study, some developmental stage-specific genes in oocytes were identified during follicle formation stage [19], and these genes may be involved in the biological events of follicular assembly process. In He’s report, the germ cells from newborn (PD0.5) ovaries are subjected to single-cell RNA-Seq, which are classified into germ cell cyst, cyst breakdown and follicle stages, and their results suggested that ID1 and ID2 from the same family have a regulatory effect on the PF formation [20]. In addition, the first and second wave pre-granulosa cell populations that support follicle formation in human were found to be similar to those in mice. In Ge’s report, the conservative programs of gene expression and DNA methylation during early oogenesis between pigs and humans were revealed, highlighting conservative spatial dynamics of germ cells during early oogenesis in both species [21]. However, the molecular mechanism of PF formation in pig is still largely unknown.

In this study, the single-cell transcriptome technology was employed to investigate the ovarian development at E95 and PD0 in pig. Firstly, we identified several clusters of different transcriptional states in germ cells and their transcriptomic dynamic was analyzed. Then a comparative analysis in pig, mouse and monkey during PF formation was performed, the developmental stage specific transcription factors in porcine ovaries were identified by SCENIC. Among, the in vitro experiment revealed that inhibition of EZH2 during PF formation in mice resulted in a significant decrease of oocytes in number. Thus, we highlight the role of EZH2 in regulating PF formation, which enrich our understanding for the molecular mechanism of PF formation in mammals.

Materials and methods

Pig embryos collection

The operating procedures of animal experiments were approved by the requirements of the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Agricultural University (approval No. 2022-021). For collection, euthanasia was performed on pigs before embryo removal and gonad separation. We collected female and male fetal gonads with intact morphology and reasonable cell viability from E95 and PD0 embryos.

Mouse experiment

The experimental mice, ICR strain, were purchased from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co. Ltd (Jinan, China). The mice were kept in cages at an ambient temperature of 24℃, with a 12-hour day and night, and with adequate water and food. The estrus female mice were mated with adult male mice at 5 pm, and the presence of a plug in the vagina on the next morning indicated a successful mating. The mice were humanely killed by cervical dislocation, female and male fetal gonads with intact morphology and reasonable cell viability were collected from E16.5, PD0 and PD3 embryos. All the experimental operations on mice have been approved by Animal Care and Ethical Committee of Qingdao Agricultural University (approval No. 2023-017).

Pig gonad single-nuclei isolation and sequencing

Nuclei isolation, 10× Genomics library construction, and sequencing were performed by Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) as previous published paper [22, 23]. Briefly, the frozen samples were chopped or homogenized, and the cell membranes were lysed in a buffer to prepare a cell nucleus suspension, and trypan blue staining and microscopic examination of cell nucleus integrity were performed. Subsequently, the cell suspension was loaded onto a Chromium Single Cell Chip (Chromium Single Cell G Chip Kit, 10× Genomics, PN-1000120) according to the manufacturer’s introduction of Chromium Single Cell 3’ GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10× Genomics, PN-1000268). In the progress, the captured cells were lysed and the released RNA was barcoded through reverse-transcription in individual single-cell gel beads in the emulsion (GEMS). In each droplet, the complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated and amplified through reverse-transcription, cDNA concentration and quality were assessed using Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent), respectively. Then, the library was constructed by Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd following the 10× Genomics process. After that, the insert size in the library was detected using an Agilent 2100. Once the insert size met expectations, the qPCR was performed to accurately quantify the effective concentration of the library (effective concentration is defined as not less than 10 nM) and the lectures meeting quality standards were sequenced using the Illumina platform with sequencing strategy of PE150.

The upstream analysis of single-cell transcriptome data

With the produced FASTQ raw data from sequencing, Cell Ranger “count” pipeline (version 7.1.0) was applied, and reads were aligned to the pig reference genome (version Sscrofa11.1, GCA_000003025.6), and the download data of mouse and monkey were their own reference genome: Macaca mulatta, (GCA_049350105.2) and Mus musculus (GCA_000001635.9), which produces the gene expression matrix.

Cell identification and clustering analysis

The R package Seurat (version 5.0.2) was applied for data processing, including dimensionality reduction and cell clustering analysis [24]. Firstly, the single cell downstream data were loaded in R (version 4.3.2) and then “CreateSeuratObject” function was used to construct Seurat object. Meanwhile, the low-quality cells and genes in the samples were filtered out what is gene expression less than 200 features for cell and gene detected in at least 3 cells. Also, the DoubletFinder package (version 2.0.3) is used to remove the cells of potential doublet with the default parameters [25]. Next, we used “FindIntegrationAnchors” and “IntegrateData” functions to combine E95 and PD0 single cell datasets, after normalization and data scaling, the cells were executed with dimensionality reduction and clustering by using “FindNeighbors”, “FindClusters” and “RunUMAP” functions. Following a series of processing steps, high-quality cells were clustered and grouped into germ cells (DDX4, DAZL, SYCP2, ZP3), granulosa cells (WNT6, AMHR2, FSHR, CHST8), stromal cells (NR2F2, C7, COL3A1), endothelial cells (PECAM1, CDKL1, CD34, RASGRP3), smooth muscle cells (ACTA2, RGS5, EDNRA) and immune cells (CD163, CD36, SLC11A1, NLRP3) based on the average expression levels of well-known markers.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

The “FindMarkers” function from Seurat is used to calculate the differentially expressed genes between groups, which were determined by the values of “avg_log2FC > 1” and “p_val_adj < 0.05”.

The construction of gene trajectory

GeneTrajectory package (version 1.0.0) was used to construct gene trajectory of the germ cells data set [26], including pig, mouse and monkey. Firstly, we used “FindVariableFeatures” function of Seurat package to get 2000 high variable genes of germ cells data set, which were served as sorting object of GeneTrajectory. Secondly, “RunDM” and “GetGraphDistance” functions were applied to compute gene-gene distances. Then, we obtained two gene trajectories in each species using “GetGeneEmbedding”, “ExtractGeneTrajectory” and “scatter3D” functions in GeneTrajectory package. Lastly, we used “RunALRA” function of SeuratWrappers package (version 0.3.5) and “AddGeneBinScore” of GeneTrajectory package to divide the gene trajectory into five bins and score them, and “FeaturePlot” function of Seurat package was employed to draw gene bin plots of two gene trajectory in three species.

Transcriptional regulatory network analysis

The R package SCENIC (version 1.3.1) was applied to infer the transcriptional regulatory network for germ cells [27] with only the pre-PF GC, PF oocyte, and PrF oocyte clusters of pig. Briefly, the raw counts and a list of known were used as inputs for network inference, and the co-expressed genes for each TF were constructed with GENIE3 software (version 1.24.0), then the “runSCENIC” program was used to generate the regulons. The activity score of candidate regulons in the individual cell was quantified using AUCell software (version 1.24.0). Given the lack of TFs database in pig, the humans (hg19) database implanted in SCENIC was applied for the screening of TF in germ cells.

Gene enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape [28], which is available at website of http://metascape.org. After the submit the gene list on the Metascape website, the enrichment results would be produced following Custom Analysis Module.

Protein interaction network construction

To construct protein interaction network, the multiple proteins option in the STRING website (https://string-db.org) was selected. After the submit of gene list, proteins without any interacting partners in the STRING database were filtered out, and the protein interaction network was obtained. And Cytoscape (version 3.10.2) software was employed to visualize the network.

The in vitro culture of mouse ovaries

The isolated ovaries from PD0 newborn mouse were cultured in 3D culture model as reported previously [22, 29]. The culture medium (taking 25 mL as an example) consists of 11 mL α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT), 11 mL DEME/F12 (Gibco, C11330500BT), 2.5 mL fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10099141, Australia), 0.25 mL Penicillin-Streptomycin (HyClone, SV30010), and 0.25 mL sodium pyruvate (HyClone, SH40003-12). And the ovaries were placed on agar block in each well of 24-well plate (NEST, 702001, China). The ovarian tissues were cultured in an incubator at 37℃, 5% CO2, and saturated humidity.

The Ezh2 knockdown assay

The knockdown experiment was performed according to the manual of GenePharma as previously reported [30]. Briefly, during the in vitro culture of mouse ovaries, the culture medium was replaced with transfection system including 4 µL 20 mM Ezh2 mRNA, 4 µL lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, L3000015, Waltham, USA) and 392 µL basal medium (GenePharma, Shanghai, China, sense: 5’-CAACACCCAACACAUAUAATT-3’), and the control group was designed with the negative control (sense: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’). The basal medium is the culture medium without FBS and Penicillin-Streptomycin. The ovaries were cultured in basal medium for 6–8 h and then transferred to complete medium without transfection vector. The culture medium was changed by half every two days, and samples were harvested after the culture of a total of three days.

Immunohistochemical staining of ovarian sections

Firstly, the paraffin samples were prepared with ovarian sections at 5 μm in thickness. Following a standard rehydration procedure, after gradient rehydration and antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4℃. The following day, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies for 45 min at 37℃, after that, the slides were counterstained with propidium iodide Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, P0133, China) for 6 min and sealed. Double immunostaining was performed with DDX4 antibody from mouse (Abcam, ab27591) or rabbit (Abcam, ab13840) with EZH2 (ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, R24813, China) antibody, followed by secondary antibodies of donkey anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 555, Abcam, ab150106, China) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, Abcam, ab150077), Hoechst 33,342 was used for nucleus counterstaining. The statistical method for oocyte counting was consistent with that in previous publication [22].

Western blot

The western blot method was consistent with previous reports [30, 31]. Firstly, six ovaries in each sample were collected, and the corresponding volume of RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013C) was added to extract the total protein. Following, Sodium Dodecyl Sulphate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed, and the target protein was transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, Millipore, ISEQ00010, USA), which was blocked with TBST buffer containing 5% bovine serum albumin (TBS containing 1% Tween-20), and then the incubation with EZH2 (CST, 5246T), DDX4 (Abcam, ab13840), GAPDH (Affinity, AF7021, China), LHX8 (Abcam, ab137036), SOHLH1 (Biorbyt, orb158448), LAMP1 (CST, C54H11), p62 (ABclonal, A19700) and LC3 antibody (Abcam, ab51520) was performed at 4℃ overnight. On the next day, after washing 3 times, incubate with enzyme-labeled goat anti-rabbit antibody (Beyotime, A0208) at room temperature for 1.5 h. Finally, chemiluminescence was prepared using the BeyoECL Plus Kit (Beyotime, P0018) and exposed (Tanon5200, China).

Statistical Analysis

All experimental results are shown as mean ± SD from 5 independent experiments. A two-tailed Student’s t test was performed to calculate P values, in which P < 0.05 (*) is considered as difference between groups is statistically significant and P < 0.01 (**) is extremely significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad).

Results

The single cell transcriptome identified single cell atlas in perinatal period ovaries of pig

To dissect the molecular mechanism of primordial follicle formation in pig, the single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and bioinformatic analyses was conducted with porcine ovarian samples at E95 and PD0 (Fig. 1A). In the study, 13,135 and 10,358 cells were generated from E95 and PD0 samples, respectively, after datasets pre-processing, integration, quality control, and linear transformation, dimensional reduction using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), 23,493 high-quality cells were retained for analyses (Fig. 1B), and 16 clusters were identified (Fig. 1C), based on marker gene expression, six cell types were identified as shown in Fig. 1D and S1 including germ cells (clusters 0 to 4, DAZL and DDX4), granulosa cells (clusters 5 to 9, WNT6 and FSHR), stromal cells (clusters 10 and 11, NR2F2 and COL3A1), endothelial cells (clusters 12 and 13, PECAM1 and RASGRP3), smooth muscle cells (cluster 14, ACTA2 and RGS5), immune cells (cluster 15, CD36 and NLRP3). The statistical results of cell types proportions in dataset showed that the ratio of germ cells decreased from E95 to PD0, while the ratio of granulosa cells increased (Fig. 1E). Subsequently, the differentiated fate was examined mainly focusing on germ cells, the major participant for primordial follicle formation.

Fine scale analysis of germ cell during primordial follicle formation process

To delve deeper into the transcript program of germ cell development, we conducted a refined UMAP analysis focusing solely on the germ cell clusters. We identified 9 clusters in the germ cell dataset (Fig. 2A), based on the total amount of RNA as previously reported [7], the cell population can be divided into germ cells (GC) and nurse cells (NC, Figs. 2B, S2A and S2B), therefore, cells in clusters from 1 to 3 are germ cells (above dashed line) and clusters range from 4 to 9 are nurse cells (below dashed line). Then we checked the expression of oocyte-stage-specific genes during folliculogenesis in the nine clusters, and germ cells in cluster 3 highly expressed genes involving in sister chromatid homologous recombination (DMC1), and synaptonemal complex formation (SYCP1, SYCP2 and SYCP3) during meiosis [18, 32], which occur in germ cells at the pre-stage of PF formation (pre-PF GC), genes such as LHX8 and NOBOX [19, 33] that essential for PF formation were mainly expressed in cluster 2 cells (PF oocyte), MOS and RSPO2 were mainly expressed in developing follicles, and were only present in primary follicles (PrF oocyte, cluster 1). Consequently, the UMAP clustering of germ cell population generated three developmental stages and they were considered as PrF oocyte, PF oocyte and pre-PF GC (Fig. 2D). Importantly, several new genes coupled with developmental stages of germ cells including pre-PF (i.e., TCHH, LUZP4 and MS4A13), PF (i.e., NRG2, SHROOM3 and ZFHX4), and PrF (i.e., SPATA16, HECW1 and EGFL6) stages were identified (Figs. 2E and S2C).

Identification of the development stage-specific genes in oocyte

To reveal the transcriptome changes of oocytes during early folliculogenesis, we performed differential expressed genes (DEGs) analysis on oocytes at the three stages. The results of DEGs analysis between pre-PF GC and PF oocyte showed that pre-PF GC had 737 highly expressed genes and PF oocyte had 880 (Fig. 3A). In pre-PF GC, the Gene Ontology (GO) of genes were mainly enriched in biological processes related to meiosis, such as “regulation of MAPK cascade”, “regulation of chromosome organization”, and “DNA damage response”, that were consistent with the cell status of pre-PF GC. In the PF oocyte, the main enriched terms were “extracellular matrix organization”, “actin cytoskeleton organization”, and “phospholipid metabolic process” (Fig. 3B). In the DEGs results between PF and PrF oocytes, 275 genes were highly expressed in PF oocyte and 319 genes were enriched in PrF oocyte (Fig. 3C). The GO enrichment results of DEGs showed that the main terms in PF oocyte were “negative regulation of hippo signaling”, “neurotrophin TRK receptor signaling pathway”, and “female gamete generation”, while in the PrF oocyte, they were “response to metal ions”, “regulation of secretion”, and “regulation of ERK1 and ERK2 cascade” (Fig. 3D).

From the PF oocyte stage, oocytes begin the biological process of corresponding metal ions and the PrF oocyte stage is specifically enriched in purine metabolism (Fig. 3E).

Gene trajectory analysis reveals species-conserved genes in early folliculogenesis

To reveal conserved transcriptional signatures of early folliculogenesis, we compared our data with previously published single-cell data from female mouse gonads from E16.5 to PD5 [7, 19] and rhesus fetal tissue from gestational week 5 to postnatal 6 months [34]. Here, we performed cell type identification on the mouse and monkey datasets, two germ cell clusters were identified, including pre-PF GC, PF oocyte (Figs. S3 and S4). Afterwards, the pre-PF GC and PF oocyte clusters in pig, monkey and mouse were extracted for subsequent analysis. Based on UMAP analysis using pre-PF GC and PF oocyte clusters (Fig. 4A, B and C), we obtained two trajectories in the three species, trajectory 1 mainly contains genes in meiosis stage, such as DMC1 and RAD51AP2, while trajectory 2 contains genes in a relatively later period of oocyte development, such as ZAR1 and GDF9 (Fig. S5A, S5B and S5C). We then mapped trajectory 1 and 2 both in two datasets to UMAP plots, and it’s observed that trajectory 1 is mainly expressed in pre-PF GC, and trajectory 2 is mainly expressed in PF oocyte (Fig. 4D, E and F). In trajectory 1, we found a total of 10 conserved ortholog by combining pairwise conserved genes of pig-monkey and pig-mouse comparisons, and the heat map showed they were highly expressed at pre-PF stage (Fig. S5E). The intersections of genes in trajectory 2 yielded 44 and 98 conserved orthologs for the pig-monkey and pig-mouse comparisons, respectively (Fig. 4G), their expressions were showed in heatmaps, as 98 genes in Fig. 4H and 44 in Fig. S5F. GO enrichment results of 102 conserved genes of trajectory 1 and trajectory 2 in pig and monkey showed that these genes are involved in cell-matrix adhesion, cell recognition, oocyte development, protein domain specific binding, regulation of phosphorylation (Fig. 4I and Supplementary Table S11). And GO enrichment results of 54 conserved genes of trajectory 1 and trajectory 2 in pig and mouse showed that these genes are involved in biological process of blastocyst development, positive regulation of cell migration, response to electrical stimulus, muscle structure development (Fig. S5G and Supplementary Table S12).

Identification of oocyte stage-specific transcription factors

To investigate the regulatory factor of oocyte development, SCENIC software was applied to screen the transcription factors (TFs) activity of oocyte with dataset at pre-PF, PF and PrF stages in pig. We obtained a total of 27 oocyte developmental stage-specific TFs, which were divided into three categories (TF1, TF2, TF3) according to their regulatory activities at different stages (Fig. 5A and B), which contain 15 (such as EZH2, CTCF and EP300), 5 (such as FOXO3 and GABPB1), and 7 (such as FOXN3 and PHOX2A) transcription factors, respectively. To establish the regulatory relationship of these TFs with the 131 conserved genes that identified above, a protein interaction network analysis of these 27 transcription factors with conserved genes were performed (Fig. 5C), and it suggested that the proteins such as EZH2, EP300, RUNX1 and CREB1 have more interactions. Given the fact that part TFs have been reported, and from the UMAP result, EZH2 has regulatory activity in each development stage of oocyte during PF formation (Fig. 5D), therefore, EZH2 was concentrated on in the following study.

EZH2 served as an important regulatory factor of PF formation both in pig and mouse ovary

Firstly, the expression patterns of EZH2 both in porcine and mouse ovaries were detected with double immunofluorescence staining of DDX4 and EZH2. In ovarian sections, EZH2 is only expressed in oocytes both in pig (E95 and PD0, Fig. 5E) and mice (from E16.5 to PD3, Fig. 6A). Specially, EZH2 is primarily expressed in oocytes at the medulla region in mice, and it partially expressed in germ cells in a cyst, particularly noticeable in the cortical region from E16.5 to PD3. Subsequently, we used western blot to detect the expression level of EZH2 from E16.5 to PD3, the results showed that the levels of EZH2 at E16.5 and PD0 were almost the same, but increased sharply at PD3 (Fig. 6B). To investigate the effect of EZH2 on primordial follicle formation, we simultaneously knocked down Ezh2 by siRNA during the in vitro culture of PD0 ovaries in mice (Fig. 6C). The statistical results of ovarian section showed that compared with the control group, Ezh2 knockdown significantly reduced the number of oocytes per section (Fig. 6D and E), but did not change the ratio of oocyte within follicles and cysts (Fig. 6F). And the expression level of DDX4 significantly decreased after Ezh2 knockdown, meanwhile, LHX8 and SOHLH1 which involved in the PFs formation also reduced significantly (Fig. 6G). In addition, the autophagy related proteins, including LC3 and LAMP1, were significantly increased and p62 was significantly decreased in siEzh2 group compared to that of Ctrl group (Fig. 6H). The results implied EZH2 might affect the size of PF pool through autophagy, and it suggested to be an important regulatory factor of PF formation.

Discussion

In mammals, the process of PF assembly began from germ cell cyst (or nest) and end up with the finite ovarian reserve (also termed primordial follicle pool), which is precisely arranged both temporally and spatially. Although much efforts have been paid, there are still many unresolved questions about the PF assembly, such as the fate decision factors of future follicular oocyte, and the role of ovarian somatic cells in follicular assembly process. On the one hand, the study of PF formation mechanism in large mammals is fairly scant. In this study, the single-cell transcriptome profile of porcine ovaries was utilized to reveal the transcriptome dynamic of germ cells during PF formation process. Significantly, our results suggested that EZH2 is likely a pivotal regulator of PF formation. This work provides new insights into PF formation and a valuable data resource for future related research in mammalian.

Germ cells were included in the bioinformatics analysis as the highest priority. Firstly, by employing the method as previously reported [7], the germ cell populations in the porcine ovaries at E95 and PD0 were divided into oocyte and nurse cell based on the total amount of RNA, and the different states of oocytes were generated, including pre-PF, PF and PrF stage oocytes, which underwent a depth analysis subsequently. In the results of gene differential expression analysis of oocyte at these three stages, the biological processes of “cell-cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules” and “neurotrophin TRK receptor signaling pathway” were enriched in PF oocyte. In previous studies, the role of cell adhesion (CDH) protein in the process of PF formation was investigated [35]. Here we enriched for other members of the CDH family, such as CDH7 and CDH8 (Supplementary Table S4). In addition, it has been reported that neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor type 2 (NTRK2) can facilitate follicle assembly in mouse ovary, Ntrk2-null resulted in a decrease in the number of PFs in the ovaries of postnatal mice and impaired early follicle development [36]. Furthermore, in the PrF oocyte, some biological processes related to metal ions were significantly enriched, indicating that some metal ion might involve in the activation of primordial follicles or follicle development after activation in pig.

In the study, several oocyte stage-specific transcription factors during primordial follicle assembly process were identified, such as RUNX1, WT1, GABPB1 and EP300. Interestingly, the expression patterns of RUNX1 in pig ovaries are different from those in mice. It’s reported that RUNX1 is specifically expressed in the pre-granulosa cells in mouse ovary [37], and in pigs, overexpression of RUNX1 in vitro was found to promote porcine oocyte maturation by affecting the BMP15/TGF-β signaling pathway [38]. In addition, WT1 is expressed in porcine oocytes, and downregulation of WT1 does not affect the maturation of porcine oocytes, but led to an increase in the apoptosis rate of early embryos after fertilization [39]. In mouse studies, WT1 is required for lineage specification of both granulosa cells and Sertoli cells during sex determination, the specific knockout of Wt1 in pregranulosa cells leads to their differentiation into steroidogenic cells [39]. Besides, WT1 is highly expressed in granulosa cells of primordial, primary, and secondary follicles, but significantly decreased in antral follicles, which is consistent with the fact that granulosa cells maintain adequate WT1 levels via autophagy to ensure them responsiveness to FSH during antral follicle formation in growing follicles [40–42]. Further, GABPB1 acts as a master transcription factor of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes [43]. And as PGC develops into PF, the number of mitochondria increases fourfold in oocyte [44]. It has been suggested that the increase in mitochondria is not only due to transport from nurse cells, but may also be due to its own synthesis [4]. In our data, GABPB1 has the highest activity score in PF oocytes, however, its role during PF formation still needs further investigation.

EZH2 is suggested to play an important role in PFs formation. Here, our results demonstrated that EZH2 has a conserved expression pattern both in pig and mouse ovaries during PF formation. In Drosophila, as germ cell development proceeds, the enhancer of zeste (E(z), a homologous gene of EZH2)-mediated H3K27me3 only exists in oocytes to maintain their fate, which gradually disappears in nurse cells. And in the absence of E(z), oocyte will transdetermine into nurse cell fate [45]. More important, the homozygous mutations of Ezh2 in mice would cause embryonic lethality [46], when Ezh2 mutates to its small isoform, the number of follicles at all developmental stages, with the exception of PFs, is significantly decreased, leading to a marked reduce of female mice in fertility [47]. Additionally, the growing evidence suggested that as a histone methyltransferase and a subunit of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), EZH2 is capable of driving the tri-methylation modification of H3K27 [48]. Recently, it has been reported that the oocyte loss during the establish of PF pool was influenced by lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) mediated autophagy through regulating of p62 gene and its H3K4me2 demethylase activity. Also, the epigenetic programming was proposed [49] to play a role in ovarian reserve formation including H3K27me3, In our study, the size of PF pool were was diminished and autophagy level was increased by the knockdown of Ezh2 in vitro. Thus, during the process PF formation, EZH2 likely affect oocyte in number via autophagy and methylation modification of H3K27.

It should be acknowledged that this study has certain limitations. Firstly, the single-nucleus transcriptome sequencing of porcine ovaries in this study was performed using one biological replicate per developmental stage (E95 and PD0). Due to the considerable difficulty in obtaining porcine embryonic ovarian tissues (including high costs of maintaining pregnant sows, limited time windows for collecting embryos at specific gestational ages, and stringent ethical approval requirements), we prioritized maximizing cell capture depth over biological replication. However, the lack of biological replicates limits our ability to assess inter-individual variability and statistically validate the identified cell clusters. Future studies incorporating multiple biological replicates will be necessary to confirm the robustness of the germ cell subtype classifications and the stage-specific gene expression patterns reported. In addition, the verification experiment relied solely on the results of in vitro culture of mouse ovaries, without any in vivo knockout data, and no experiments were conducted on pigs or monkeys given that the difficulty in obtaining samples. These undoubtedly weaken the reliability of the experimental conclusions. Therefore, more rigorous experiments should be carried out to strength our conclusion in future.

Conclusions

This study provides a single-cell map of pig ovary in the late embryonic development and newly born periods, and enriches the public understanding of primordial follicle formation in mammals.
