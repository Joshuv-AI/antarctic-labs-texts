A Bregman ADMM for Bethe variational problem
Yuehaw Khoo, Tianyun Tang, Kim-Chuan Toh

Abstract
In this work, we propose a novel Bregman ADMM with nonlinear dual update to solve the Bethe variational problem (BVP), a key optimization formulation in graphical models and statistical physics. Our algorithm provides rigorous convergence guarantees, even if the objective function of BVP is non-convex and non-Lipschitz continuous on the boundary. A central result of our analysis is proving that the entries in local minima of BVP are strictly positive, effectively resolving non-smoothness issues caused by zero entries. Beyond theoretical guarantees, the algorithm possesses high level of separability and parallelizability to achieve highly efficient subproblem computation. Our Bregman ADMM can be easily extended to solve the quantum Bethe variational problem. Numerical experiments are conducted to validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method. Based on this research, we have released an open-source package of the proposed method at https://github.com/TTYmath/BADMM-BVP .

Introduction

1.1
Graphical model and belief propagation

Graphical model
[
34
]
is a powerful framework for representing multivariate distributions, with a graph encoding the dependency structure among variables. It has applications in many fields including scientific computing
[
59
]
, machine learning
[
6
]
and statistical physics
[
58
]
. A key challenge in graphical models is probabilistic inference, which we describe in the next paragraph.

For a simple undirected connected graph
G=([n],\mathcal{E})
, consider a pairwise Markov random field (MRF) with the joint probability distribution function given by

P(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\prod_{k\in[n]}\Psi_{k}(x_{k}),

(1)

where
x_{k}
is the state of node
k
with
r
possible values,
\Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})
and
\Psi_{k}(x_{k})
are potential functions, and
Z
is a normalization constant. The goal of probabilistic inference is to determine the marginal distribution of a single random variable
x_{k}
. While the marginal distribution can be computed directly by summing over all other variables, this approach is generally NP-hard
[
16
]
due to the exponential growth in the state space as
n
increases.

Belief propagation.
In 1988, Pearl introduced belief propagation (BP)
[
57
]
as an approximate method to solve the probabilistic inference problem. For each edge
ij
(In this paper, we assume
i<j
when referring to
ij\in\mathcal{E}
.) in
\mathcal{E}
, BP defines message variables
m_{i\rightarrow j}(x_{j})
and
m_{j\rightarrow i}(x_{i})
, which are updated as follows:

\displaystyle m_{i\rightarrow j}(x_{j})\leftarrow\frac{1}{z_{i,j}}\sum_{x_{i}\in[r]}\Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\Psi_{i}(x_{i})\prod_{k\in N(i)\setminus\{j\}}m_{k\rightarrow i}(x_{i}),

(2)

\displaystyle m_{j\rightarrow i}(x_{i})\leftarrow\frac{1}{z_{j,i}}\sum_{x_{j}\in[r]}\Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\Psi_{j}(x_{j})\prod_{k\in N(j)\setminus\{i\}}m_{k\rightarrow j}(x_{j}),

(3)

where
z_{i,j}
and
z_{j,i}
are normalization constants that prevent the messages from diverging. This iterative process can be interpreted as passing messages along the network until convergence to a fixed point. Using these messages, the marginal distributions are approximated as:

\displaystyle b_{k}(x_{k}):=\frac{1}{\hat{z}_{k}}\Psi_{k}(x_{k})\prod_{i\in N(k)}m_{i\rightarrow k}(x_{k}),

(4)

\displaystyle b_{i,j}(x_{i},x_{j}):=\frac{1}{\bar{z}_{i,j}}\Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\prod_{k\in N(i)\setminus\{j\}}m_{k\rightarrow i}(x_{i})\prod_{k\in N(j)\setminus\{i\}}m_{k\rightarrow j}(x_{j}),

(5)

where
\hat{z}_{k}
and
\bar{z}_{i,j}
are normalization constants ensuring that
b_{k}
and
b_{i,j}
are valid probability distributions. The approximations
b_{k}
and
b_{i,j}
, often referred to as beliefs, provide estimates of the marginal distributions of the joint probability
P
. For tree-structured graphs, BP is guaranteed to converge to the exact marginal distributions in a finite number of iterations. However, for graphs with cycles, convergence is not guaranteed
[
54
]
. Nevertheless, BP often performs remarkably well as a heuristic in practice and has been successfully applied in various domains
[
1
,
47
,
53
]
.

1.2
Bethe variational problem

The practical success of BP has inspired extensive theoretical investigations. Yedidia, Freeman, and Weiss achieved a key breakthrough in their seminal works
[
83
,
84
,
82
]
, where they demonstrated that the fixed points of BP correspond to the critical points of the following Bethe variational problem (BVP)
[
73
]
:

\displaystyle\min\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{x_{i},x_{j}\in[r]}b_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\ln\frac{b_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})}{\Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})b_{i}(x_{i})b_{j}(x_{j})}+\sum_{k\in V}\sum_{x_{k}\in[r]}b_{k}(x_{k})\ln\frac{b_{k}(x_{k})}{\Psi_{k}(x_{k})}:

(6)

\displaystyle\forall k\in[n],\sum_{x_{k}\in[r]}b_{k}(x_{k})=1,\ \forall ij\in\mathcal{E},\ \sum_{x_{j}\in[r]}b_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})=b_{i}(x_{i}),\ \sum_{x_{i}\in[r]}b_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})=b_{j}(x_{j})\Bigg\},

where
b_{k}
and
b_{ij}
are the beliefs satisfying normalization and marginalization constraints. The objective function in (
6
), known as the Bethe free energy (BFE), was introduced by Hans Bethe in 1935
[
8
]
. It serves as a powerful approximation tool in statistical physics
[
46
]
, simplifying the computation of free energy in systems where exact calculations are computationally infeasible. Alternatively, the BFE can be interpreted as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
[
32
]
between a tree-like probability distribution

\frac{\prod_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}b_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})}{\prod_{k\in[n]}b_{k}(x_{k})^{d_{k}-1}},

(7)

where
d_{k}:=|N(k)|
is the degree of vertex
k
, and the joint distribution in (
1
). Since general probability distributions may not adhere to the form in (
7
), the minimizer of (
6
)—and by extension, the output of the BP algorithm—provides only an approximation to the original probabilistic inference problem.

BP can be viewed as a fixed-point method applied to the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions of (
6
), with the message variables corresponding to the Lagrange multipliers for the marginal constraints. The connection between BP and (
6
) offers an alternative approach to finding a fixed point of BP by directly solving the optimization problem (
6
) and obtaining its stationary point.

However, one important limitation of the earlier works by Yedidia et al.
[
83
,
84
,
82
]
is that they considered only the variables
b_{ij}
and
b_{k}
with strictly positive entries, thereby ignoring boundary points in the feasible set of (
6
). If the minimizer of (
6
) contains zero entries, it cannot serve as a fixed point for BP. However, boundary points cannot be disregarded when solving (
6
), as the non-convexity makes it challenging to ensure that local minima will not have zero entries. Furthermore, zero entries pose significant challenges for optimization algorithms because the objective function is not Lipschitz-continuous on the boundary. This observation motivates the following question:

Question 1.1
.

Do the local minima of (
6
) have only positive entries?

In Section
2
, we provide a positive answer to Question
1.1
by demonstrating that the entries of all local minima are bounded below by a positive constant, which depends only on the coefficients in (
6
). Our result not only addresses a gap in the theoretical analysis of the aforementioned works but is also critical for algorithmic design and convergence analysis, as it helps overcome the non-smoothness issue on the boundary of (
6
). In the next subsection, we summarize several classical algorithms for solving (
BVP
) and introduce our new algorithm, which offers several advantageous properties.

1.3
Algorithms for Bethe variational problem

In this subsection, we present algorithms for solving (
BVP
), starting with a discussion of several classical approaches.

Gradient descent method.
Welling and Teh
[
78
]
proposed a gradient descent (GD) method to find a stationary point of (
BVP
) for
binary
MRFs. The key insight is that for
r=2
, problem (
BVP
) can be reformulated as an unconstrained optimization problem with
n
variables, allowing GD to be applied. Shin refined this approach in
[
66
,
67
]
to get a polynomial time complexity bound for graphs with maximum degree
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Additionally, Leisenberger et al.
[
36
]
extended this idea using a projected quasi-Newton method to exploit second-order information. For binary pairwise MRFs with
submodular
cost functions, global minimizers of (
BVP
) have been explored using GD
[
77
]
. Despite its theoretical soundness, the GD method is limited to binary networks. For
r>2
, it is unclear how to reformulate the problem as unconstrained optimization, rendering GD inapplicable in such cases.

Double-loop algorithm.
Yuille
[
86
,
87
]
introduced a double-loop algorithm called the concave-convex procedure (CCCP) to solve (
BVP
) for any
r\geq 2
. The key observation is that the objective function of (
BVP
) is a difference of convex (DC) functions, enabling the application of DC programming techniques
[
26
]
. In each outer iteration of CCCP, the concave part is linearized to create a majorization. The obtained convex problem is then solved using a block coordinate minimization (BCM) method applied to its dual problem. Although CCCP can handle any
r\geq 2
, its efficiency is hindered by the iterative nature of BCM, as solving subproblems to high accuracy can be computationally expensive. Furthermore, while CCCP guarantees monotonic decreases in the objective function, the convergence of the variables remains unresolved, motivating the need for algorithms with more rigorous theoretical guarantees. BVP
) are summarized in Table
1
.

The limitations of classical algorithms for (
BVP
) motivate the following question:

Question 1.2
.

Can we design an algorithm for (
BVP
) that satisfies the following properties?

1.

Applicability to (
BVP
) for any
r\geq 2
.

2.

Convergence guarantee.

3.

Easy-to-solve subproblems.

Because the variables in (
BVP
) can be separated into two blocks,
b_{ij}
and
b_{k}
, a natural approach is to apply the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). However, the non-convexity and non-smoothness of the objective function in (
BVP
) present significant challenges for both the implementation and convergence analysis of ADMM. In Section
3
, we introduce a variant of the ADMM algorithm, called Bregman ADMM, which possesses all the desirable properties mentioned in Question
1.2
. The main innovations include the use of a KL-divergence as the penalty function and a nonlinear update of the Lagrange multiplier. These choices not only enable efficient updates but also ensure rigorous convergence guarantees in the non-convex setting.

Beyond its convergence guarantees, Bregman ADMM also features easily solvable subproblems. In Section
4
, we show that these subproblems are highly separable, comprising many independent, small-dimensional, strongly convex, and smooth optimization problems. This separability allows for the use of parallelizable methods, offering a significant advantage over CCCP, whose subproblems rely on non-parallelizable block coordinate descent. Furthermore, in order to obtain a closed-form solution, we propose incorporating a novel proximal term into the second subproblem.

1.4
Quantum Bethe variational problem

Akin to classical graphical models, Leifer and Poulin introduced quantum graphical models in
[
35
]
, with applications in quantum error correction
[
62
]
, simulations of many-body quantum systems
[
52
]
, and approximations of ground-state energy
[
63
]
. Hastings
[
24
]
further proposed quantum belief propagation (QBP) as a quantum counterpart to classical belief propagation for solving quantum graphical models. Recently, Zhao et al.
[
88
]
explored the connection between QBP and the quantum Bethe variational problem (
5.1
).

A related class of optimization problems involves semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations of quantum many-body problems via quantum embedding theories
[
69
,
21
,
31
,
29
,
30
]
. These models are similar to (
5.1
) in that they approximate a many-body quantum system by clustering accurate local densities and combining them using local consistency constraints. In particular, Lin and Lindsey
[
42
]
proposed tightening convex relaxations by intersecting local consistency constraints with the global semidefinite constraint. More recently, Khoo and Lindsey
[
28
]
developed an ADMM-type method to efficiently solve these SDP problems by exploiting the parallelizability of the subproblems. However, the method proposed there does not readily work with entropy, and also there is no convergence guarantee. Compared to the model in
[
42
]
, (
5.1
) does not impose a global semidefinite constraint but instead incorporates an additional entropy function. In this work, we focus on (
5.1
), though we believe extending our approach to solving SDP problems in quantum embedding theories is a promising direction for future research.

A separate class of convex optimization problems stems from two-electron reduced density matrix (2-RDM) theory
[
49
,
50
,
13
,
51
,
89
,
38
,
2
,
56
,
17
]
. This approach approximates the many-body density function using low-order moments
[
33
]
. The formulation of 2-RDM optimization problems differs significantly from both (
5.1
) and quantum embedding theories, so we do not pursue it in this work.

In Section
5
, we demonstrate how our algorithm, Bregman ADMM, can be extended to solve (
5.1
). Since our primary focus is on solving classical Bethe variational problems, we defer the detailed formulation of (
5.1
) and the adaptation of our algorithm to Section
5
. Numerical experiments in Section
6
will highlight the efficiency and robustness of our method compared to various state-of-the-art algorithms for both (
BVP
) and (
5.1
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider the following entropy regularized OT problem

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}_{+}}\left\{\left\langle C,X\right\rangle+\left\langle\log X,X\right\rangle:\ X{\bf 1}_{r}=u,\ X^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}=v\right\},

{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}u={\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}v=1.

(32)

where
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{r}

\displaystyle\|\lambda-\log u\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1.5\|C\|_{\infty}+\log r,

(33)

\displaystyle\|\mu-\log v\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1.5\|C\|_{\infty}+\log r.

(34)

In addition, we have that

\min_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\{X^{*}(i,j)\}\geq\frac{\big(\min_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\{u(i),v(j)\}\big)^{2}}{r^{2}\exp[4\|C\|_{\infty}]}.

(35)

Proof.

From Lemma 2 in
[
15
]
,we know that the optimal solution

of (

32

Note that the Lagrangian multipliers
\lambda,\mu
are not unique because for any

\mu^{\prime}:=\mu+\alpha{\bf 1}_{r}
also satisfy the equation (

\max_{i\in[r]}\{\lambda(i)\}=\max_{i\in[r]}\{\mu(i)\}.
Let
a,b\in[r]
such that
\lambda(a):=\max_{i\in[r]}\{\lambda(i)\},

\mu(b):=\max_{i\in[r]}\{\mu(i)\}.
From (
32
), we have that

1\geq X(a,b)=\exp[-C(a,b)+\lambda(a)+\mu(b)]\geq\exp[-\|C\|_{\infty}+\lambda(a)+\mu(b)],

from which we have

\max_{i\in[r]}\{\lambda(i)\}=\max_{i\in[r]}\{\mu(i)\}\leq\|C\|_{\infty}/2.

(36)

Also, we have that

1/r^{2}\leq\max_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\{X(i,j)\}\leq\exp\left[\|C\|_{\infty}+\max_{i\in[r]}\{\lambda(i)\}+\max_{i\in[r]}\{\mu(i)\}\right],

from which we have

\max_{i\in[r]}\{\lambda(i)\}=\max_{i\in[r]}\{\mu(i)\}\geq-\log r-\|C\|_{\infty}/2.

(37)

Now, from (

) and (

), we have that

\displaystyle u=\exp\left[-C+\lambda{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}+{\bf 1}_{r}\mu^{\top}\right]{\bf 1}_{r}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[\|C\|_{\infty}{\bf 1}_{r\times r}+\lambda{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}+\|C\|_{\infty}{\bf 1}_{r\times r}/2\right]{\bf 1}_{r}

\displaystyle=\exp(\lambda)\circ\left(r\exp(1.5\|C\|_{\infty}){\bf 1}_{r}\right).

Computing
\log(\cdot)
of the above inequality, we get

\log u-(\log r+1.5\|C\|_{\infty}){\bf 1}_{r}\leq\lambda.

(38)

From (
32
) and (
37
), we have that

\displaystyle u=\exp\left[-C+\lambda{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}+{\bf 1}_{r}\mu^{\top}\right]{\bf 1}_{r}=\exp(\lambda)\circ\left(\exp\left[-C\right]\exp[\mu]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp(\lambda)\circ\left(\exp\left[-\|C\|_{\infty}{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right]\exp[\mu]\right)\geq\exp(\lambda)\circ\left(\exp\left[-\|C\|_{\infty}{\bf 1}_{r}\right]\cdot\exp\left[\max_{i\in[r]}\{\mu(i)\}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp(\lambda)\circ\exp\left[-(1.5\|C\|_{\infty}+\log r){\bf 1}_{r}\right].

Computing
\log(\cdot)
of the above inequality, we get

\lambda\leq\log u+(1.5\|C\|_{\infty}+\log r){\bf 1}_{r}.

(39)

Combining (
38
) and (
39
), we get (
33
). Similarly, we can prove (
34
).

From (
32
), (
33
) and (
34
), we have that

\min_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\{X^{*}(i,j)\}\geq\exp\left[-\|C\|_{\infty}+2\left(-\log r+\log\left(\min_{1\leq i,j\leq r}\{u(i),v(j)\}\right)-1.5\cdot\|C\|_{\infty}\right)\right],

which implies (
35
).

3
Bregman ADMM

3.1
Algorithmic framework

In this subsection, we present our algorithm for solving (
BVPτ
). The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
20
,
22
]
is widely used in optimization due to its simplicity and broad applications
[
11
,
37
,
70
,
68
]
. The traditional ADMM algorithm for solving (
BVPτ
) is outlined in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

ADMM for (
BVPτ
)

Initialization
: Choose
\rho>0
,
{\bf q}^{1}\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{X}),{\bf Q}^{1}\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{Y}),\Lambda^{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{3}
.

for

t=1,2,\dots

do

1.
{\bf q}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{{\bf q}\in\mathcal{X}_{\tau}}\left\{\mathcal{F}({\bf q})-\left\langle\Lambda^{t},\mathcal{A}({\bf q})\right\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathcal{A}({\bf q})+\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t})\|^{2}\right\}

2.
{\bf Q}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{{\bf Q}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\mathcal{G}({\bf Q})-\left\langle\Lambda^{t},\mathcal{B}({\bf Q})\right\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathcal{A}({\bf q}^{t+1})+\mathcal{B}({\bf Q})\|^{2}\right\}

3.
\Lambda^{t+1}:=\Lambda^{t}-\rho(\mathcal{A}({\bf q}^{t+1})+\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t+1}))

end

for

While straightforward, Algorithm
1
uses quadratic penalty terms, which can be problematic because
\mathcal{F}({\bf q})
is strongly concave with its Hessian’s smallest eigenvalue having the order of
-\Omega(1/\tau)
in
\mathcal{X}_{\tau}
. When
\tau
is small,
\rho
must be chosen very large,
\rho=\Omega(1/\tau)
, to ensure that the subproblem in Step 1 remains convex, which makes the algorithm computationally inefficient. When the subproblem is non-convex, finding its global minimizer can be challenging. Even if specialized algorithms could succeed in identifying the global minimizer, the solution may lie on the boundary, which is not a critical point of (
BVPτ
).

In
[
75
]
, Wang and Banerjee proposed a Bregman ADMM, which uses the Bregman divergences
B_{\phi}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
B_{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot)
as penalty function and proximal term, as shown in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

Bregman ADMM with linear dual update for (
BVPτ
)
[
75
]

Initialization
: Choose
\rho>0
,
{\bf q}^{1}\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{X}),{\bf Q}^{1}\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{Y}),\Lambda^{1}={\bf 0}\in\mathcal{H}_{3}
.

for

t=1,2,\dots

do

1.
{\bf q}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\mathcal{X}_{\tau}}\left\{\mathcal{F}({\bf q})-\left\langle\Lambda^{t},\mathcal{A}({\bf q})\right\rangle+\rho B_{\phi}(-\mathcal{A}({\bf q}),\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t}))+B_{\psi}({\bf q},{\bf q}^{t})\right\}

2.
{\bf Q}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\mathcal{G}({\bf Q})-\left\langle\Lambda^{t},\mathcal{B}({\bf Q})\right\rangle+\rho B_{\phi}(\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}),-\mathcal{A}({\bf q}^{t+1}))\right\}

3.
\Lambda^{t+1}:=\Lambda^{t}-\rho(\mathcal{A}({\bf q}^{t+1})+\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t+1}))

end

for

In this paper, because of the special structure of (

), we choose the function
\phi:\mathcal{H}_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
and
\psi:\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{3}
are defined in (

\forall{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{3},\ \phi({\bf x}):=\left\langle{\bf x},\log{\bf x}\right\rangle,\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\ \psi({\bf y}):=\sum_{k\in[n]}(d_{k}-1)\left\langle y_{k},\log y_{k}\right\rangle,

(50)

where
d_{k}
is the degree of vertex
k.

Remark 3.1
.

From the definition of
\psi
in (
50
), it may not be strictly convex if
d_{k}=1
for some
k\in[n].
However, this won’t affect our theoretical analysis in this paper. The reason is that the Bregman divergence
B_{\phi}(-\mathcal{A}({\bf q}),\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t}))
is strongly convex with respect to
{\bf q}
.

In Algorithm
2
, the subproblem in Step 1 is convex as long as
\rho>0
, contrasting with the stricter requirements in Algorithm
1
. The convergence of Bregman-type methods such as the Bregman proximal gradient method
[
23
]
, stochastic
Bregman proximal gradient method
[
18
]
and Bregman proximal point method
[
81
]
has been well-studied in the literature. However, the theoretical analysis of Bregman ADMM is still in its infancy. Although Wang and Banerjee proved the convergence of their Bregman ADMM for convex problems, its extension to non-convex problems like (
BVPτ
) lacks a theoretical guarantee. While nonconvex ADMM variants have been studied
[
74
,
61
,
44
]
, they often use quadratic penalties, with the Bregman divergence appearing only as a proximal term. Here, we refer to a Bregman ADMM as an ADMM that uses Bregman divergence as the penalty function, and the convergence of such a method for non-convex problems remains an open question.

Theoretical challenges:
Analyzing the convergence of Algorithm
2
involves two key difficulties:

•

The asymmetry of the Bregman divergence penalty function
complicates the convergence proofs. In contrast, the convergence proofs of the usual ADMM that employs a quadratic penalty function do not encounter such difficulties. This fact alone hinders a direct adaptation of the proof in Wang and Banerjee
[
75
]
for the convergence of convex Bregman ADMM.

•

The marginal
\mathcal{G}({\bf Q})
defined in (
14
) is not Lipschitz-continuous on the boundary of
\mathcal{Y}
defined in (
12
). However, in existing analyses of non-convex ADMM
[
76
,
80
,
25
,
45
]
, the objective function (whose variable is optimized) in the second subproblem is typically assumed to have a Lipschitz-continuous gradient (When there are more than two blocks, this assumption is applied to the last function.), which ensures that the Lagrangian multiplier can be bounded in terms of the primal variables.

To address these challenges, we introduce a Bregman ADMM with nonlinear dual update as stated in Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

Bregman ADMM with nonlinear dual update for (
BVPτ
)

Initialization
: Choose
\rho>0
,
{\bf q}^{1}\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{X}),{\bf Q}^{1}\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{Y}),\Lambda^{1}={\bf 0}\in\mathcal{H}_{3}
.

for

t=1,2,\dots

do

1.
{\bf q}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\mathcal{X}_{\tau}}\left\{\mathcal{F}({\bf q})-\left\langle\Lambda^{t},\mathcal{A}({\bf q})\right\rangle+\rho B_{\phi}(-\mathcal{A}({\bf q}),\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t}))+B_{\psi}({\bf q},{\bf q}^{t})\right\}

2.
{\bf Q}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\mathcal{G}({\bf Q})-\left\langle\Lambda^{t},\mathcal{B}({\bf Q})\right\rangle+\rho B_{\phi}(\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}),-\mathcal{A}({\bf q}^{t+1}))\right\}

3.
\Lambda^{t+1}:=\Lambda^{t}-\rho\left(\nabla\phi(\mathcal{B}({\bf Q}^{t+1}))-\nabla\phi(-\mathcal{A}({\bf q}^{t+1}))\right)

end

for

Algorithm
3
modifies the dual update to a nonlinear form. This nonlinear dual update is more natural than the linear update in Algorithm
2
because it aligns with the optimality conditions of the second subproblem, i.e., the gradient of the function in the second subproblem is zero after replacing
\Lambda^{t}
by
\Lambda^{t+1}.
As far as we are aware of, such dual
update in ADMM has not been considered in the past.
The nonlinear dual update in Algorithm
3

\displaystyle{\rm dist}\left({\bf 0},\partial\Phi\left({\bf q}^{t+1},{\bf Q}^{t+1},\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t+1})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{2}\left(\|{\bf q}^{t}-{\bf q}^{t+1}\|+\|{\bf Q}^{t}-{\bf Q}^{t+1}\|+\|\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t+1})-\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t})\|\right).

(128)

Define

(129)

Define the following set

(130)

where

is defined in (

\mathcal{U}
is compact and every limit point of

is inside
\mathcal{U}.
Because

is constructed from linear functions, logarithm functions and

\mathcal{Y}

). Because

and a continuous concave function

such that for all

with

and all

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{\rm dist}(\mathcal{U},({\bf q},{\bf Q},\Lambda))<\epsilon,\Phi^{*}<\Phi({\bf q},{\bf Q},\Lambda)<\Phi^{*}+\eta\Big\},

(131)

one has,

\varphi^{\prime}(\Phi({\bf q},{\bf Q},\Lambda)-\Phi^{*}){\rm dist}({\bf 0},\partial\Phi({\bf q},{\bf Q},\Lambda))\geq 1.

(132)

Step 4.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime}(\Phi({\bf q}^{t},{\bf Q}^{t},\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t}))-\Phi^{*})}\leq{\rm dist}({\bf 0},\partial\Phi({\bf q}^{t},{\bf Q}^{t},\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t})))

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{2}\left(\|{\bf q}^{t-1}-{\bf q}^{t}\|+\|{\bf Q}^{t-1}-{\bf Q}^{t}\|+\|\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t})-\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\Lambda^{t-1})\|\right).

4
Implementation details

In this section, we discuss the implementation details of Algorithm
3
. While the framework outlined in Algorithm
3
and the conditions in Theorem
3.2
are primarily designed for theoretical convergence analysis, certain practical adjustments are necessary to enhance computational efficiency. These adjustments may not strictly adhere to the framework or conditions specified in the theorem. Although this creates a gap between theory and practice, we believe that strictly following the theoretical framework at the cost of significant efficiency loss would be overly conservative. Instead, our focus is on balancing theoretical rigor with practical performance.

4.1
Solving the subproblem in Step 1

The subproblem in Step 1 of Algorithm
3
can be written as follows for any
k\in[n]:

\displaystyle q^{t+1}_{k}:=\arg\min\Big\{\left\langle c_{k}-(d_{k}-1)\log q_{k}^{t},q_{k}\right\rangle-\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\left\langle\lambda_{kj}^{t},-q_{k}\right\rangle-\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\left\langle\mu^{t}_{ik},-q_{k}\right\rangle

(139)

\displaystyle+\rho\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\left\langle\log q_{k}-\log(Q_{kj}^{t}{\bf 1}_{r}),q_{k}\right\rangle+\rho\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\left\langle\log q_{k}-\log({Q_{ik}^{t}}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}),q_{k}\right\rangle:\ {\bf 1}^{\top}_{r}q_{k}=1,\ q_{k}\geq\tau\Big\}.

After some simplications, we get the following problem,

q^{t+1}_{k}:=\arg\min\Big\{\left\langle\hat{c}_{k}^{t},q_{k}\right\rangle+\rho\cdot d_{k}\left\langle q_{k},\log q_{k}\right\rangle:\ {\bf 1}^{\top}_{r}q_{k}=1,\ q_{k}\geq\tau\Big\},

(140)

where
\hat{c}_{k}^{t}
is defined as

\hat{c}_{k}^{t}:=c_{k}-(d_{k}-1)\log q_{k}^{t}+\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\lambda_{kj}^{t}-\rho\log(Q_{kj}^{t}{\bf 1}_{r})\right)+\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\mu^{t}_{ik}-\rho\log({Q_{ik}^{t}}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r})\right).

(141)

Problem (
140
) is a strongly convex optimization problem with simplex-type constraints, and it is independent for each
k\in[n]
. One can apply the semismooth Newton method
[
64
]
to effectively solve its dual problem iteratively.

In Algorithm
3
, the constraint
{\bf q}\geq\tau
is included primarily for convergence analysis, as it prevents the iterates from approaching zero. From Proposition
2.2
, this constraint becomes inactive at stationary points when
\tau
is sufficiently small. In our implementation, rather than choosing a very small
\tau>0
, we simplify the problem by ignoring the constraint
{\bf q}\geq\tau
and directly solving:

q^{t+1}_{k}:=\arg\min\Big\{\left\langle\hat{c}_{k}^{t},q_{k}\right\rangle+\rho\cdot d_{k}\left\langle q_{k},\log q_{k}\right\rangle:\ {\bf 1}^{\top}_{r}q_{k}=1,\ q_{k}\geq 0\Big\},

(142)

which has the following closed-form solution

q^{t+1}_{k}:=\exp\left[-\hat{c}^{t}_{k}/(\rho d_{k})\right]/\left({\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}\exp\left[-\hat{c}^{t}_{k}/(\rho d_{k})\right]\right).

(143)

Our numerical experiments indicate that the iterates
({\bf q}^{k})_{k\in[n]}
of the Bregman ADMM are consistently bounded away from zero, rendering the constraint
{\bf q}\geq\tau
unnecessary in practice. This simplification significantly enhances computational efficiency without compromising the algorithm’s robustness.

4.2
Solving the subproblem in Step 2

The subproblem in Step 2 of Algorithm
3
can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle Q^{t+1}_{ij}:=\arg\min_{Q_{ij}\geq 0}\Big\{\left\langle C_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle+\left\langle\log Q_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle-\left\langle\lambda_{ij}^{t},Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu^{t}_{ij},Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle

(144)

\displaystyle+\rho\left\langle\log(Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r})-\log q_{i}^{t+1},Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle+\rho\left\langle\log(Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r})-\log q_{j}^{t+1},Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle:\left\langle Q_{ij},{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right\rangle=1\Big\}.

After simplifications, this reduces to:

\displaystyle Q^{t+1}_{ij}:=\arg\min_{Q_{ij}\geq 0}\Big\{\left\langle\widehat{C}_{ij}^{t},Q_{ij}\right\rangle+\left\langle\log Q_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\rho\left\langle\log(Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}),Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle+\rho\left\langle\log(Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}),Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle:\left\langle Q_{ij},{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right\rangle=1\Big\},

(145)

where
\widehat{C}_{ij}^{t}
is defined as:

\widehat{C}_{ij}^{t}:=C_{ij}-\left(\lambda_{ij}^{t}+\rho\log q_{i}^{t+1}\right){\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}-{\bf 1}_{r}\left(\mu^{t}_{ij}+\rho\log q_{j}^{t+1}\right)^{\top}.

(146)

Problem (
4.2
) is a strongly convex optimization problem with an entropy regularizer and a simple affine constraint. It is essentially an entropy optimal transport (OT) problem, where the marginal constraints are replaced by KL-divergence penalties. Both Newton and gradient descent methods can be applied to solve its dual problem efficiently. Notably, in many Bethe variational problems derived from graphical models,
r
is small, making the computational cost of the Newton method small. Furthermore, since the subproblem (
4.2
) is independent for each
ij\in\mathcal{E}
, all such problems can be solved in parallel, unlike the subproblems in CCCP, which are not separable and require block coordinate minimization.

Although Newton or gradient descent methods ensure convergence, they are iterative, resulting in a double-loop structure for the overall algorithm. To create a single-loop variant of Algorithm
3
, we add a proximal term to linearize the penalty function in (
144
). Using the strong subadditivity (SSA) of the entropy function
[
40
]
, we define the following convex function
\varphi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
{\bf Q}\in\mathcal{Y},

\varphi({\bf Q}):=\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\Big(2\left\langle\log Q_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle-\left\langle\log(Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}),Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle-\left\langle\log(Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}),Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle\Big).

(147)

By adding the proximal term
\rho B_{\varphi}({\bf Q},{\bf Q}^{t})
in Step 2 of Algorithm
3
, the corresponding subproblem becomes:

Q^{t+1}_{ij}:=\arg\min_{Q_{ij}\geq 0}\left\{\left\langle\widetilde{C}_{ij}^{t},Q_{ij}\right\rangle+(1+2\rho)\left\langle\log Q_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle:\left\langle Q_{ij},{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right\rangle=1\right\},

(148)

where
\widetilde{C}_{ij}^{t}
is defined as:

\widetilde{C}_{ij}^{t}:=\widehat{C}_{ij}^{t}-\rho\Big(2\log Q_{ij}^{t}-\log(Q_{ij}^{t}{\bf 1}_{r}){\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}-{\bf 1}_{r}\log({Q_{ij}^{t}}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r})^{\top}\Big).

(149)

This modified problem (
148
) admits the following closed-form solution:

Q^{t+1}_{ij}:=\exp\left[-\widetilde{C}_{ij}^{t}/(1+2\rho)\right]/\left\langle\exp\left[-\widetilde{C}_{ij}^{t}/(1+2\rho)\right],{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right\rangle.

(150)

Remark 4.1
.

While we have established the convergence of Algorithm
3
with the proximal term
B_{\psi}({\bf q},{\bf q}^{t})
in Step 1, the convergence of Algorithm
3
when adding the proximal term
\rho B_{\varphi}({\bf Q},{\bf Q}^{t})
in Step 2 remains unproven. This is because the penalty parameter
\rho
appears in the proximal term, and the resulting error in (
54
) cannot be controlled by simply increasing
\rho
. To our knowledge, the convergence guarantee for non-convex ADMM with such a proximal term,
even in the Euclidean case, remains an open problem. Despite this, our numerical experiments show that the linearized Bregman ADMM with the additional
proximal term
\rho B_{\varphi}({\bf Q},{\bf Q}^{t})
performs significantly better than the original version, which requires an iterative solver to solve the second subproblem.

4.3
Termination criterion

In order to terminate the algorithm, we need an measurement of the stationarity. Suppose
({\bf q},{\bf Q},\Lambda)\in\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{X})\times\operatorname{ri}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{H}_{3}
is a primal-dual solution of (
BVP
), the KKT conditions are as follows:

\forall ij\in\mathcal{E},\ Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}=q_{i},\ Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}=q_{j},

(151)

\forall ij\in\mathcal{E},\ \exists\hat{z}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\,{\rm s.t.}\ C_{ij}+\log Q_{ij}-\lambda_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}-{\bf 1}_{r}\mu_{ij}^{\top}+\hat{z}_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r\times r}={\bf 0}_{r\times r},

(152)

\forall k\in[n],\ \exists z_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\ {\rm s.t.}\ c_{k}-(d_{k}-1)\log q_{k}+\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{kj}+\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{ik}+z_{k}{\bf 1}_{r}={\bf 0}_{r},

(153)

where
z_{k}
and
\hat{z}_{ij}
are the Lagrangian multipliers for the normalization constraints
{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}q_{k}=1
and
\left\langle Q_{ij},{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right\rangle=1
, respectively. We ignore the normalization constraints and nonnegative constraints in (
151
) because they are strictly satisfied in Algorithm
3
. We use the following KL-divergence to measure the violation of primal feasibility (
151
)

{\rm Resp}({\bf q},{\bf Q}):=\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\left\langle\log q_{i}-\log(Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}),q_{i}\right\rangle+\left\langle\log q_{j}-\log(Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}),q_{j}\right\rangle\right).

(154)

When
\lambda_{ij},\mu_{ij}
are given, (
152
) has the following unique solution as a probability density vector:

\widehat{Q}_{ij}:=\exp[-C_{ij}+\lambda_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}+{\bf 1}_{r}\mu_{ij}^{\top}]/\left\langle\exp[-C_{ij}+\lambda_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}+{\bf 1}_{r}\mu_{ij}^{\top}],{\bf 1}_{r\times r}\right\rangle.

(155)

We use the following KL-divergence to measure the violation of (
152
):

{\rm Resd}_{{\bf Q}}:=\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\left\langle\log Q_{ij}-\log\widehat{Q}_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle.

(156)

The verification of (
153
) is slightly more complicated. For any
k\in[n],
if
d_{k}>1,
then similar to (
155
), (
152
) has the following unique solution as a probability density vector:

\hat{q}_{k}:=\exp\left[\frac{c_{k}+\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{kj}+\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{ik}}{(d_{k}-1)}\right]/\left\langle\exp\left[\frac{c_{k}+\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{kj}+\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{ik}}{(d_{k}-1)}\right],{\bf 1}_{r}\right\rangle

(157)

We use the KL-divergence
\left\langle\log q_{k}-\log\hat{q}_{k},q_{k}\right\rangle
to measure the violation of (
153
) for
q_{k}.
When
d_{k}=1,
because
d_{k}-1=0,
we don’t have the
\log q_{k}
term in (
153
). In this case, we use the following residue

\Big\|(I_{r}-{\bf 1}_{r\times r}/r)\Big(c_{k}+\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{kj}+\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{ik}\mu_{ik}\Big)\Big\|/(1+\|c_{k}\|).

(158)

Combining these two cases, we use the following term to measure the violation of stationarity of
{\bf q}
:

\displaystyle{\rm Resd}_{{\bf q}}:=\sum_{k\in[n]\ d_{k}>1}\left\langle\log q_{k}-\log\hat{q}_{k},q_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k\in[n],\ d_{k}=1}\Big\|(I_{r}-{\bf 1}_{r\times r}/r)\Big(c_{k}+\sum_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{kj}+\sum_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{ik}\mu_{ik}\Big)\Big\|/(1+\|c_{k}\|).

(159)

With (
156
) and (
4.3
), we define the dual KKT residual as follows:

{\rm Resd}({\bf q},{\bf Q}):={\rm Resd}_{{\bf Q}}+{\rm Resd}_{{\bf q}}.

(160)

In our implementation, we use the following KKT residual

{\rm Res}({\bf q},{\bf Q}):=\max\{{\rm Resp}({\bf q},{\bf Q}),{\rm Resd}({\bf q},{\bf Q})\},

(161)

to measure the stationarity. When it is smaller than the required tolerance, we terminate our algorithm.

4.4
Penalty parameter

In our theoretical analysis of Algorithm
3
, we fix the penalty parameter
\rho
to be some sufficiently large number. However, in practice, the efficiency of ADMM is sensitive to the penalty parameter and a commonly used strategy is to adaptively update it to balance the primal and dual KKT residuals
[
43
,
70
]
. In our implementation, we update
\rho^{t}>0
as follows:

\rho^{t+1}:=\begin{cases}\max\{\rho^{t}/1.2,10^{-3}\}&\mbox{if ${\rm Resp}({\bf q}^{t+1},{\bf Q}^{t+1})<{\rm Resd}({\bf q}^{t+1},{\bf Q}^{t+1})/5$}\\
\max\{1.2\cdot\rho^{t},10^{3}\}&\mbox{if ${\rm Resp}({\bf q}^{t+1},{\bf Q}^{t+1})>5\cdot{\rm Resd}({\bf q}^{t+1},{\bf Q}^{t+1})$}\\
\rho^{t}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(162)

The updating scheme of the penalty parameter is not unique, we find that the strategy in
(
162
) works pretty well in numerical experiments. In order to avoid updating the penalty parameter too frequently and making our algorithm more stable, we check the KKT residual and update the penalty parameter once in every ten iterations.

5
Quantum extension

5.1
Quantum Bethe variational problem

The quantum Bethe variational problem is as follows:

\displaystyle\min\Big\{\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\left\langle\widehat{C}_{ij},Q_{ij}\right\rangle+\left\langle Q_{ij},{\rm logm}(Q_{ij})\right\rangle\right)+\sum_{k\in[n]}\left(\left\langle\hat{c}_{k},q_{k}\right\rangle-(d_{k}-1)\left\langle q_{k},{\rm logm}(q_{k})\right\rangle\right):

\displaystyle\forall ij\in\mathcal{E},\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij})=q_{j},\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{ij})=q_{i},Q_{ij}\in\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}}_{+},\forall k\in[n],q_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{r}_{+},\mathrm{Tr}(q_{k})=1\Big\}.

(QBVP)

In (
5.1
),
\widehat{C}_{ij}\in\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}},

\hat{c}_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{r}
are hermitian coefficient matrices. The operator
{\rm logm}(\cdot)
refers to the logarithm of a matrix such that for any matrix
A\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{+}

(the set of
p\times p
hermitian positive semidefinite matrix.) with eigenvalue decomposition
P{\rm diag}(d)P^{\top},

{\rm logm}(A)=P{\rm diag}(\log(d))P^{\top}.
Note that
{\rm logm}(A)
requires
A
to be positive definite. However, by defining
\left\langle A,{\rm logm}(A)\right\rangle:=\left\langle d,\log(d)\right\rangle,

A
can have zero eigenvalues. In this case, the domain of (
5.1
) is a compact set. Similarly, we use
{\rm expm}(\cdot)
to denote the matrix exponential such that
{\rm expm}(A)=P{\rm diag}(\exp(d))P^{\top}.
The mappings
\mathrm{Tr_{l}}:\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{r}
,
\mathrm{Tr_{r}}:\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{r}
are trace out operators such that for any
B\in\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}}
and
i,j\in[r],

\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(B)(i,j):=\sum_{k=1}^{r}B(i+(k-1)r,j+(k-1)r),

(163)

\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(B)(i,j):=\sum_{k=1}^{r}B((i-1)r+k,(j-1)r+k).

(164)

Here for simplicity, we only provide the optimization formulation of (
5.1
). For more bankground knowledge about quantum mechanics, please refer to
[
35
,
88
]
. (
5.1
) is an extension of (
BVP
) with the following changes

(1)

q_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{+},

Q_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}_{+}
are replaced by
q_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{r}_{+}
and
Q_{ij}\in\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}}_{+}
respectively.

(2)

\log(\cdot)
and
\exp(\cdot)
are replaced by
{\rm logm}(\cdot)
and
{\rm expm}(\cdot)
respectively.

(3)

Q_{ij}{\bf 1}_{r}
and
Q_{ij}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{r}
are replaced with
\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{ij})
and
\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij})
respectively.

(4)

{\bf 1}^{\top}q_{k}=1
is replaced by
\mathrm{Tr}(q_{k})=1.

Note that from the constraint
\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij})=q_{j},
we have that
\mathrm{Tr}(Q_{ij})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij}))=\mathrm{Tr}(q_{j})=1.
Thus, the trace of each matrix
Q_{ij}
is also equals to 1.

5.2
Quantum belief propagation

There are two types of quantum belief propagation (QBP) methods. The approach introduced by Leifer and Poulin in
[
35
]
is a direct extension of the classical belief propagation. However, it imposes stringent requirements on the quantum system and may not converge, even when the graph
G
is a tree. In this paper, we focus on the QBP method proposed by Zhao et al. in
[
88
]
, which is derived from the KKT conditions of (
5.1
) and is applicable to a broader class of problems.

For any
ij\in\mathcal{E},
let
\lambda_{ij},\mu_{ij}\in\mathbb{H}^{r}
be the Lagrangian multipliers of the marginal constraint
\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{ij})=q_{i},\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij})=q_{j}
, respectively. Define
\Psi_{k}:={\rm expm}(-\hat{c}_{k}),

\Psi_{ij}:={\rm expm}(-\widehat{C}_{ij}).
Let the messages be
M_{ij}^{\lambda}:={\rm expm}(\lambda_{ij}),

M_{ij}^{\mu}:={\rm expm}(\mu_{ij}).
For any
ij\in\mathcal{E}
, QBP updates the messages as follows:

Q_{ij}\leftarrow\frac{{\rm expm}(-\widehat{C}_{ij}+\mu_{ij}\otimes I_{r}+I_{r}\otimes\lambda_{ij})}{\mathrm{Tr}\left({\rm expm}(-\widehat{C}_{ij}+\mu_{ij}\otimes I_{r}+I_{r}\otimes\lambda_{ij})\right)},

(165)

M_{ij}^{\mu}\leftarrow\psi_{j}\odot\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\alpha j\in\mathcal{E},\alpha\neq i}\left(\mathrm{Tr_{l}}\left(Q_{\alpha j}\right)\odot(M_{\alpha j}^{\mu})^{-1}\right)\odot\displaystyle\prod\limits_{j\rho\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathrm{Tr_{r}}\left(Q_{j\rho}\right)\odot(M_{j\rho}^{\lambda})^{-1}\right),

(166)

M_{ij}^{\lambda}\leftarrow\psi_{i}\odot\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\alpha i\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathrm{Tr_{l}}\left(Q_{\alpha i}\right)\odot(M_{\alpha i}^{\mu})^{-1}\right)\odot\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i\rho\in\mathcal{E},\rho\neq j}\left(\mathrm{Tr_{r}}\left(Q_{i\rho}\right)\odot(M_{i\rho}^{\lambda})^{-1}\right).

(167)

In the above,
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product, and
the product
\odot
is defined as

A\odot B={\rm expm}\left({\rm logm}(A)+{\rm logm}(B)\right).

(168)

Here,
\displaystyle\prod\limits
represents the sequence of
\odot
-products. After each round of message updates, the marginal density operator
q_{k}
for any
k\in[n]
is recovered as follows:

q_{k}:=\begin{cases}\Big[\Psi_{k}^{-1}\odot\Big(\displaystyle\prod\limits_{kj\in\mathcal{E}}M^{\lambda}_{kj}\Big)\odot\Big(\displaystyle\prod\limits_{ik\in\mathcal{E}}M^{\mu}_{ik}\Big)\Big]^{-1/(d_{k}-1)}/z_{k},&d_{k}>1\\
\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ik})&\exists i\in[n],\ {\rm s.t.}\ ik\in\mathcal{E}\\
\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{kj})&\exists j\in[n],\ {\rm s.t.}\ kj\in\mathcal{E},\end{cases}

(169)

where
z_{k}>0
is a normalization constant to make sure
\mathrm{Tr}(q_{k})=1.
Although various algorithms have been proposed to solve the classical (
BVP
), the research
for the quantum case (
5.1
) is still in an early stage and we have not seen any optimization algorithms proposed to solve (
5.1
) in the literature. In the next subsection, we will show that our Bregman ADMM can be extended to solve (
5.1
).

5.3
Bregman ADMM for (

)

In this subsection, we discuss how to extend Algorithm
4
to solve (
5.1
). In order to facilitate our discussion, we need some notation and definitions that are similar to those presented in the beginning of
subsection
2.1

Define the following linear spaces

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}:=\displaystyle\prod\limits_{k\in[n]}\mathbb{H}^{r},\ \widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{2}:=\displaystyle\prod\limits_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}},\ \widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{3}:=\displaystyle\prod\limits_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{H}^{r}\times\mathbb{H}^{r}.

(170)

Let
\widehat{{\bf C}}:=(\widehat{C}_{ij})_{ij\in\mathcal{E}},

\hat{{\bf c}}:=(\hat{c}_{k})_{k\in[n]}.
Define the following
convex sets

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}:\ \forall k\in[n],\ q_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{r}_{+},\ \mathrm{Tr}(q_{k})=1\right\},

(171)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf Q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{2}:\ \forall ij\in\mathcal{E},\ Q_{ij}\in\mathbb{H}^{r^{2}}_{+},\ \mathrm{Tr}(Q_{ij})=1\right\},

(172)

and linear maps
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}:\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{3},

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}:\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{3}
such that for any
{\bf q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{1},

{\bf Q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{2}

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}({\bf q}):=(-q_{i},-q_{j})_{ij\in\mathcal{E}},\quad\widehat{\mathcal{B}}({\bf Q}):=(\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{ij}),\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij}))_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}.

(173)

Define the functions
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for any
{\bf q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}},

{\bf Q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}({\bf q}):=\left\langle\hat{{\bf c}},{\bf q}\right\rangle-\sum_{k\in[n]}(d_{k}-1)\left\langle q_{k},{\rm logm}(q_{k})\right\rangle,\ \widehat{\mathcal{G}}({\bf Q}):=\left\langle\widehat{{\bf C}},{\bf Q}\right\rangle+\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\left\langle Q_{ij},{\rm logm}(Q_{ij})\right\rangle.

(174)

Define the function
\hat{\psi}:\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for any
{\bf q}\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}},

\hat{\psi}({\bf q}):=\sum_{k\in[n]}(d_{k}-1)\left\langle q_{k},{\rm logm}(q_{k})\right\rangle.

(175)

Similar to the strong subadditivity (SSA) of Shannon entropy, von Neumann entropy also has SSA
[
41
]
. Thus, the following function
\hat{\varphi}:\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}({\bf Q}):=\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{E}}\Big(2\left\langle{\rm logm}(Q_{ij}),Q_{ij}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\left\langle{\rm logm}(\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{ij})),\mathrm{Tr_{r}}(Q_{ij})\right\rangle-\left\langle{\rm logm}(\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij})),\mathrm{Tr_{l}}(Q_{ij})\right\rangle\Big).

(176)

With the above notation, our Bregman ADMM for solving (

\rho^{1}>0
,

.

In Algorithm

q^{t+1}_{k}:=\arg\min\Big\{\left\langle\tilde{c}_{k}^{t},q_{k}\right\rangle+\rho^{t}\cdot d_{k}\left\langle q_{k},{\rm logm}(q_{k})\right\rangle:\ \mathrm{Tr}(q_{k})=1,\ q_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{r}_{+}\Big\},

where

.

From Table

and Table
3
, we observe that the behavior of JBP and GSBP is unstable. Both methods either converge after only a few iterations or diverge. While GSBP requires fewer iterations than JBP, JBP is significantly faster. This speed advantage arises because JBP updates the messages in parallel, whereas GSBP updates them sequentially. In our implementation of JBP in
Matlab
, we store all messages in an
r\times r\times m
3D array, allowing efficient tensor operations. In contrast, for GSBP, we use a cell array to store messages and rely on a for loop to update them. The use of for loops is known to be much slower than performing operations directly on tensors because
Matlab
leverages multi-core parallel computing for matrix and tensor computations.

The situation for BADMM and CCCP is similar. BADMM requires around 3 times fewer iterations than CCCP, and its running time is at least ten times, and sometimes even more than one hundred times, faster than that of CCCP. This demonstrates the critical importance of the parallelizability of an algorithm.

When comparing BADMM and BP, we observe that BP is indeed more efficient than BADMM when the former converges. However, the convergence of BP is not guaranteed
and its convergence is unpredictable. In contrast,
BADMM is much more robust as it successfully solves all instances, whereas JBP and GSBP solve only half of the instances. Inspired by the efficient yet unstable behavior of BP, a practical strategy is to initially run BP for a few iterations to check for convergence. If BP does not converge, switching to BADMM can be a viable alternative. Although BADMM is not as efficient as BP, it offers much greater stability.

One aspect worth mentioning is that we tested Algorithm
4
with Step 3 replaced by the linear dual update from Algorithm
2
. However, we observed that this modified algorithm failed to converge for any of the tested instances. Consequently, we do not present these results in the paper. This observation highlights that the nonlinear dual update in Algorithm
3
is not only critical for theoretical analysis but also essential in practice.

6.2
Example of (
BVP
): sensor network localization

In the second example, we test the Sensor Network Localization (SNL) problem
[
48
]
, which aims to estimate the locations of sensors based on noisy observations of pairwise distances. Traditional approaches treat SNL as a nonlinear regression problem and employ algorithms such as gradient descent
[
71
,
39
]
and semidefinite programming
[
9
,
14
]
to solve it. In
[
27
]
, Ihler et al. reformulated SNL as a graphical model and proposed the Nonparametric Belief Propagation (NBP) method as a
solver.

Specifically, consider
n
sensors
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
located in a planar region
[0,1]^{2}
. For any distinct
i,j\in[n]
, the noisy distance between sensors is observed with probability
P_{o}(x_{i},x_{j})
, given by:

d_{ij}=\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|+\nu_{ij},\ \nu_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),\ P_{o}(x_{i},x_{j}):=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}}{2R^{2}}\right),

(181)

where
N(0,\sigma^{2})
is a normal distribution with standard deviation
\sigma>0
, controlling the noise level, and
R>0
is a parameter determining the likelihood of an observation. Additionally, we assume the presence of
a
anchors
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{a}
, whose locations are known. The noisy distance between a sensor
x_{k}
and an anchor
y_{\ell}
is observed with probability
P_{o}(x_{k},y_{\ell})
, given by:

d_{k\ell}=\|x_{k}-y_{\ell}\|+\nu_{k\ell},\ \nu_{k\ell}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),\ P_{o}(x_{k},y_{\ell}):=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x_{k}-y_{\ell}\|^{2}}{2R^{2}}\right).

(182)

We construct a graph
G=([n],\mathcal{E})
, where
ij\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if the distance between
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
is observed. Similarly, let
\mathcal{E}_{1}\subset[n]\times[a]
, where
k\ell\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
if and only if the distance between
x_{k}
and
y_{\ell}
is observed. With these definitions, we consider the graphical model in (
1
) with potential functions defined as:

\forall ij\in\mathcal{E},\ \Psi_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j}):=P_{o}(x_{i},x_{j})\frac{\exp[-(d_{ij}-\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})]}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}},

(183)

\forall k\in[n],\ \Psi_{k}(x_{k}):=\prod_{k\ell\in\mathcal{E}_{1}}P_{o}(x_{k},y_{\ell})\frac{\exp[-(d_{k\ell}-\|x_{k}-y_{\ell}\|)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})]}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}.

(184)

Unlike previous graphical models with finite states, the potential functions in (
183
) and (
184
) are continuous functions. To apply our algorithm, we discretize
[0,1]^{2}
into a grid
\{0,1/t,2/t,\ldots,(t-1)/t,1\}^{2}
and restrict the domain of the potential functions to this discrete set. The resulting number of states is
r=(t+1)^{2}
. Note that this discretization introduces significant model error if
t
is too small and dramatically increases problem size if
t
is too large. Ihler et al.
[
27
]
addressed this issue by using NBP with stochastic approximation to solve the continuous model directly, avoiding discretization. Here, we use discretization solely to test the behavior of our algorithm. Extending Bregman ADMM to the continuous setting is an interesting direction for future research.

a=4

Matlab

7
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a Bregman ADMM to solve the Bethe variational problem and its quantum counterpart. Despite the non-smooth and non-convex nature of this optimization problem, we established the convergence properties of
our Bregman ADMM by leveraging the problem’s special structure. A key step in our analysis involves proving that the entries of local minima are bounded below by a positive constant, which prevents the iterates of the Bregman ADMM from approaching the boundary and enables the exploitation of the objective function’s smoothness. Extensive numerical experiments demonstrate that our Bregman ADMM is highly robust compared to the well-known belief propagation method, which does not have convergence guarantee for a general graph.
For future work, an intriguing direction would be to extend the theoretical results to the quantum Bethe variational problem. Another promising avenue is to apply our algorithm to tackle more challenging problems in scientific computing.
