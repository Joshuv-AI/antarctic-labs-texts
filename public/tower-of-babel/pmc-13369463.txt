Disrupting sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk via biomimetic nanovesicles to augment chemotherapy efficacy under chronic stress
Authors: Jingjie Liu, Jiaqi Qin, Wenxia Zheng, Xiaojuan Wang, Zhaohan Wei, Zixiang Xie, Tingwei Yan, Xin Li, Ziqiao Ding, Haojie Liu, Zhiheng Cai, Shanmiao Gou, Xiangliang Yang, Tuying Yong, Lu Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic stress significantly impacts cancer progression by activating the sympathetic nervous system, leading to increased tumor growth, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy. To address these challenges, we develop biomimetic hybrid nanovesicles (Pro@hNVs) by fusing M1 macrophage-derived vesicles with pH-sensitive liposomes (hNVs) to encapsulate the β-adrenergic receptor (ADRB) blocker propranolol (Pro). Leveraging the tumor-targeting properties of M1 macrophage-derived vesicles and their matrix metalloproteinase-mediated degradation of tumor extracellular matrix, Pro@hNVs effectively accumulate and deeply penetrate tumor tissues, followed by the release of Pro in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment. Pro subsequently inhibits the sympathetic nerve-cancer cell crosstalk by blocking ADRB2 signaling. Meanwhile, Pro@hNVs effectively reprogram adrenergic signal-induced M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) into the M1 phenotype through the released Pro and hNVs, thereby amplifying TNF-mediated neurotoxicity and effectively disrupting sympathetic nerve-macrophage crosstalk. This dual-action mechanism of Pro@hNVs significantly inhibits the sympathetic nerve function promoted by gemcitabine, resulting in the improved chemotherapy efficacy and enhanced antitumor immune response under chronic stress. These findings highlight the potential of Pro@hNVs as a promising strategy to enhance chemotherapy outcomes in cancer patients experiencing chronic stress, offering a viable therapeutic avenue for overcoming treatment resistance.Subject terms: Chemotherapy, Drug delivery, Cancer therapeutic resistance, Cancer microenvironment

Introduction

Chronic stress, often characterized by anxiety and depression, is highly prevalent in cancer patients and is exacerbated by factors such as lack of confidence in cancer diagnosis and treatment, as well as the adverse effects of therapies1–3. Clinical meta-analyses have demonstrated a strong correlation between chronic stress and both high cancer incidence and low survival rates4,5. Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that chronic stress can promote chemoresistance, reducing the therapeutic efficacy of agents like paclitaxel6, cisplatin6, doxorubicin7, and oxaliplatin8. Therefore, enhancing chemotherapy efficacy under chronic stress holds significant clinical importance.

Tumors secrete neurotrophic factors that attract nerve axons, including sympathetic nerves, thereby establishing sympathetic innervation9–11. Chronic stress activates the sympathetic nervous system, driving tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance3,12–14. Specifically, the activated sympathetic nerves enhance cancer cell proliferation15,16, induce angiogenesis17, and recruit macrophages to infiltrate tumor tissues and polarize them into the M2 phenotype18–20. In addition, these nerves promote collagen deposition in tumor tissues to inhibit drug accumulation and penetration, while decreasing cancer cell sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents, thus leading to drug resistance21–24. Notably, chemotherapy further increases sympathetic nerve infiltration in tumor tissues, exacerbating drug resistance7,25. Intervening in sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk, thus, may be an effective strategy to improve chemotherapy outcomes under chronic stress.

Under chronic stress, norepinephrine (NE) secreted by sympathetic nerves binds to β-adrenergic receptors (ADRB), particularly ADRB2 expressed by various cells in the tumor microenvironment, including cancer cells and macrophages2,26. The activation of ADRB2 promotes the crosstalk between sympathetic nerves and these cells, driving tumor progression and resistance27,28. Pharmacological blockade of ADRB2 decreased nerve infiltration within tumors16, reduced the number of macrophages in tumor tissues, and inhibited their polarization into the M2 phenotype18,19, thereby slowing tumor development and metastasis and enhancing chemotherapy sensitivity7,16,28,29. These findings highlight the pivotal role of ADRB2 in the crosstalk between sympathetic nerves and tumors. Targeting ADRB2 holds promise for disrupting the sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk and improving the efficacy of chemotherapy under chronic stress. Propranolol (Pro), a non-selective β1 and β2-adrenergic receptor blocker approved by the FDA, has been widely used to treat conditions such as arrhythmia, hypertension, anxiety, and infantile hemangiomas30. However, the short half-life and potential side effects of Pro, such as bradycardia, atrioventricular block and hypotension, limit its application in cancer therapy31. Thus, developing a delivery system that can overcome the dense tumor stroma exacerbated by chronic stress and achieve targeted delivery and responsive release of Pro in the tumor microenvironment could potentially block sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk, improving chemotherapy efficacy and reducing adverse effects.

Biomimetic hybrid nanovesicles, created by fusing cell-derived vesicles with liposomes, have garnered significant attention in drug delivery research by integrating the targeting ability and biological functions of cell-derived vesicles with the high drug-loading capacity and smart responsiveness of liposomes20,32,33. Our previous work has shown that macrophage-derived vesicles possess tumor-targeting property and inherit high matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) expression of macrophages, enabling them to degrade the tumor extracellular matrix (ECM)34. In addition, M1 macrophage-derived vesicles can effectively repolarize M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) to the M1 phenotype32,35.

In this work, the biomimetic hybrid nanovesicles (hNVs) by fusing M1 macrophage-derived vesicles with pH-sensitive liposomes (LIPs) are developed to load Pro (Pro@hNVs). Pro@hNVs efficiently target tumor tissues and degrade the tumor matrix to promote their accumulation and deep penetration within the tumor and then release Pro in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment. Combining the M2-like TAM repolarization by hNVs and the ADRB2 blockade in cancer cells and macrophages by the released Pro, Pro@hNVs effectively disrupts the sympathetic nerve-cancer cell crosstalk and sympathetic nerve-macrophage crosstalk to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis. Importantly, Pro@hNVs significantly reduce chemotherapy-triggered sympathetic nerve activity, thereby enhancing chemotherapeutic efficacy (Fig. 1a).

Results

Chronic stress promotes tumor progression and exacerbates tumor microenvironment in orthotopic Panc02 tumor-bearing mice

Emerging evidence underscores the critical role of nerves, particularly sympathetic nerves, in chronic stress-mediated tumor development, metastasis, and therapy resistance1–3,26. Compared to adjacent non-tumor pancreatic tissues, tumor tissues exhibited markedly elevated expressions of β3-tubulin (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, a sympathetic nerve marker, Supplementary Fig. 1a, c), and adrenergic receptor beta-2 (ADRB2, Supplementary Fig. 1d, e) by immunofluorescence, as well as higher NE levels by ELISA (Supplementary Fig. 1f), confirming sympathetic nerve enrichment in pancreatic tumor microenvironment.

To elucidate the potential link between chronic stress and tumor progression, an orthotopic pancreatic cancer model with chronic restraint stress (CRS) was established. CRS-exposed mice showed anxiety-like behaviors, characterized by reduced open-field center time/distance (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c), increased forced-swim immobility time (Supplementary Fig. 2d), and upregulated serum NE levels (Supplementary Fig. 2e). In orthotopic Panc02 tumor-bearing mice, CRS significantly increased tumor weight (Fig. 1b) and intestinal metastatic nodules (Fig. 1c), accelerating tumor development and metastasis. CRS also upregulated β3-tubulin (Fig. 1d, e) and TH (Fig. 1f, g) expressions, and increased NE (Fig. 1h) and nerve growth factor (NGF, Fig. 1i) levels in tumor tissues, suggesting enhanced sympathetic nerve infiltration. Furthermore, CRS elevated the proportion of TAMs (Fig. 1j), particularly the tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive M2 subtype (Fig. 1k), and upregulated ADRB2 in tumor tissues (Fig. 1l, m and Supplementary Fig. 3). Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis revealed an inverse correlation between ADRB2 expression and pancreatic cancer patient prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 4). Tumor Immune Estimation Resource 2.0 (TIMER2.0) analysis36 showed that ADRB2 expression in pancreatic tumors correlated positively with nerve-related markers (GAP43, DCX, NEFL, NTRK1, S100B, UCHL1, NGF; Supplementary Fig. 5a–g) and DBH, the rate-limiting enzyme for NE synthesis in sympathetic nerves (Supplementary Fig. 5h), as well as M2 macrophage infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that ADRB2 acted as a key regulator of sympathetic nerve activity and M2 macrophage polarization, both critical for pancreatic cancer progression. In addition, CRS increased tumor collagen content (Fig. 1n, o), and TIMER2.0 analysis36 revealed that stromal-related markers, including COL1A1, COL1A2, ACTA2 and FN1, strongly correlated with ADRB2 in pancreatic cancer (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d), suggesting chronic stress-mediated ADRB2 signaling may facilitate the formation of a denser stromal microenvironment, which could potentially impede drug delivery. These findings highlighted the critical role of CRS-mediated ADRB2 in exacerbating the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, developing nanotherapeutics that mitigate dense tumor stroma to inhibit ADRB2 activity holds great promise for disrupting sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk and improving therapeutic efficacy in stress-associated tumor progression.

Preparation and characterization of Pro@hNVs

M1 macrophage-derived nanovesicles (M1-NVs) were isolated from lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated RAW264.7 cells using sonication and extrusion through polycarbonate membrane filters, with a particle size of 161.2 ± 1.7 nm and zeta potential of − 12.2 ± 0.7 mV. However, M1-NVs showed increased size and polydispersity index (PDI) over time in phosphate buffer saline (PBS, Supplementary Fig. 8a–c), indicating poor colloidal stability. This prompted the development of a hybrid delivery system by fusing M1-NVs with LIPs to enhance Pro delivery. To develop Pro@hNVs, LIPs and Pro-loaded LIPs (Pro@LIPs) were synthesized via the thin-film hydration method using 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), 3β-Hydroxy-5-cholestene 3-hemisuccinate (CHEMS) and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)−2000 (DSPE-mPEG2000) as lipid components in the presence or absence of Pro. Subsequently, hNVs and Pro@hNVs were generated by hydrating LIPs and Pro@LIPs in PBS containing M1-NVs, followed by extrusion, respectively.

Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis showed that incorporating LIPs into DiI/DiD dual-labeled M1-NVs increased DiI fluorescence intensity and decreased DiD fluorescence (Fig. 2a), indicating successful fusion of LIPs with M1-NVs during hNV formation. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) revealed that the protein profiles of hNVs and Pro@hNVs matched those of M1 macrophages and M1-NVs (Fig. 2b). The presence of M1 macrophage marker CD86 in hNVs and Pro@hNVs further validated the successful fusion (Fig. 2c). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that both hNVs and Pro@hNVs had spherical vesicular structures (Fig. 2d). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis demonstrated a slight size increase in hNVs compared to LIPs (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 9), confirming M1-NVs fusion onto LIPs. Pro loading did not significantly affect hNV size (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 9). The zeta potential of LIPs, hNVs, Pro@LIPs, and Pro@hNVs was approximately − 11 mV (Fig. 2f). Pro@hNVs exhibited good reproducibility across five batches, with low coefficients of variation (CV) for size (4.07%), zeta potential (4.48%), and PDI (3.49%, Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis showed that Pro@hNVs had a drug encapsulation efficiency of 72% (Supplementary Fig. 11a) and drug loading capacity of 6% (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Pro@hNVs retained the pH-sensitive release behavior of LIPs, releasing approximately 72% of Pro at pH 6.5 versus 43% at pH 7.4 after 24 h (Fig. 2g). In addition, Pro@LIPs and Pro@hNVs exhibited robust colloidal stability, with no significant changes in size (Fig. 2h), zeta potential (Fig. 2i) and PDI (Fig. 2j) over 70 days in PBS, both with and without 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Pro@hNVs efficiently inhibit sympathetic nerve-cancer cell crosstalk under chronic stress

To investigate Pro@hNVs’ effects on sympathetic nerve-driven cancer cells under chronic stress, murine pancreatic carcinoma Panc02 cells were stimulated with isoproterenol (ISO) to mimic stress-induced sympathetic nerve activation, then treated with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at pH 7.4 or 6.5. ISO significantly promoted the proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 12a) and migration (Supplementary Fig. 12b–e) of Panc02 cells. LIPs or hNVs alone did not remarkably impact ISO-stimulated Panc02 cell viability (Fig. 3a). At pH 7.4, Pro@LIPs and Pro@hNVs exhibited minimal impacts on ISO-stimulated Panc02 cells. However, at pH 6.5, they significantly reduced ISO-stimulated Panc02 cell viability, with effects similar to those of free Pro (Fig. 3a). Importantly, Pro@hNVs did not affect Panc02 cell viability in the absence of ISO stimulation at pH 6.5 (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating inhibition of ISO-stimulated Panc02 cells via ADRB2 suppression. Furthermore, Pro@LIPs and Pro@hNVs markedly reduced ISO-stimulated Panc02 cell migration at pH 6.5, as demonstrated by decreased wound closure (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b) and transwell migration (Fig. 3b, c), likely due to pH-responsive Pro release. Beyond ISO, sympathetic nerves secrete NE to promote cancer cell activity. The supernatants from sympathetic neuron-like PC12 cells enhanced Panc02 viability (Supplementary Fig. 15a), while Pro@LIPs and Pro@hNVs reversed this effect (Supplementary Fig. 15b), confirming their inhibition of sympathetic nerve-stimulated cancer cell function. In addition, real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and ELISA analyses revealed that ISO upregulated NGF expression (Supplementary Fig. 16a) and secretion (Supplementary Fig. 16b) in Panc02 cells. Conversely, Pro@LIPs and Pro@hNVs downregulated NGF expression (Fig. 3d) and secretion (Fig. 3e) in ISO-stimulated cells at pH 6.5, indicating disruption of sympathetic nerve-stimulated cancer cell activity under chronic stress might further affect sympathetic nerves via NGF in the acidic tumor microenvironment.

Given the paracrine crosstalk between sympathetic nerves and cancer cells, we further examined whether Pro@hNVs-treated Panc02 cells affected sympathetic nerve cell function. Supernatants from ISO-exposed Panc02 cells pretreated with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs, or Pro@hNVs at pH 7.4 or 6.5 were used to incubate PC12 cells. As expected, supernatants from ISO-treated Panc02 cells enhanced PC12 cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 17a) and neurite outgrowth (Supplementary Fig. 17b, c). Supernatants from Pro@LIPs- or Pro@hNVs-treated ISO-stimulated cells had no effect on PC12 viability at pH 7.4 but significantly reduced it at pH 6.5 (Fig. 3f). They also markedly decreased PC12 neurite outgrowth at pH 6.5, with 41.8% and 44.5% reductions in length compared to the PBS-treated group (Fig. 3g, h), respectively. These results confirmed that Pro@hNVs efficiently inhibited sympathetic nerve-cancer cell crosstalk via acid triggered Pro release, impacting both cancer cell activity and sympathetic nerve function under chronic stress.

Pro@hNVs efficiently inhibit sympathetic nerve-macrophage crosstalk under chronic stress

Chronic stress promotes the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype. Given that Pro inhibits M2 macrophage polarization by targeting the ADRB2 receptor29, and M1 macrophage-derived vesicles repolarize M2 macrophages to M1 phenotype32,35, we investigated Pro@hNVs’ effects on M2 macrophage repolarization under chronic stress. ISO-stimulated RAW264.7 cells were treated with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at pH 7.4 or 6.5. Expectedly, ISO significantly downregulated the mRNA levels of Tnf (an M1-related marker), upregulated the mRNA levels of Arg1 and Mgl1 (M2-related markers), and decreased TNF content in RAW264.7 cells (Supplementary Fig. 18a–d), confirming ISO-induced M2 macrophage polarization. Both hNVs and Pro abrogated ISO-stimulated M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 4a–c), and Pro@hNVs exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect at pH 6.5 (Fig. 4a–c). Furthermore, Pro@hNVs significantly enhanced the ratios of CD80+ and CD86+ (M1-related markers, Fig. 4d, e) cells, decreased the ratios of CD206+ (M2-related marker, Fig. 4f) cells, and enhanced TNF secretion (Fig. 4g) in ISO-stimulated RAW264.7 cells at pH 6.5, confirming efficient inhibition of ISO-induced M2 macrophage polarization. These findings indicated that Pro@hNVs efficiently inhibited sympathetic nerve-regulated M2 macrophage polarization through released Pro and hNVs’ repolarizing action. Furthermore, the supernatants from ISO-exposed, Pro@hNVs-treated macrophages at pH 6.5 significantly inhibited PC12 cell viability (Fig. 4h) and reduced neurite outgrowth (Fig. 4i, j), these effects were abolished by TNF inhibitor etanercept (Etan, Fig. 4h–j) and restored by exogenous TNF supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 19a–c). Consistent results were obtained with primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs, Supplementary Fig. 20a–g). These results suggested that Pro@hNVs modulated macrophage function under acidic conditions to release TNF, which in turn affected sympathetic nerve cell activity, highlighting their potential to disrupt sympathetic nerve-macrophage crosstalk under chronic stress.

Pro@hNVs efficiently degrade tumor ECM to increase their tumor accumulation and penetration

Our previous work demonstrated that macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles, like macrophages, express MMPs to degrade tumor ECM34. Consistent with this, western blot analysis revealed MMP9 and MMP14 expression in hNVs and Pro@hNVs (Fig. 5a). MMP activity assay confirmed high MMP activity in both hNVs and Pro@hNVs (Fig. 5b), indicating Pro loading did not impact hNVs’ inherent MMP activity. Furthermore, collagen content determination showed that hNVs and Pro@hNVs efficiently broke down collagen (Fig. 5c), thereby further substantiating their MMP activity. To investigate their efficacy in vivo, CRS-exposed Panc02 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs. Immunofluorescence staining showed that hNVs and Pro@hNVs significantly reduced intratumoral collagen I expression (Fig. 5d, e), confirming their tumor ECM-degrading capacity.

Chronic stress exacerbates tumor ECM, restricting intratumoral drug accumulation and penetration23. Given that hNVs efficiently degraded tumor ECM, their tumor accumulation capacity was first evaluated in CRS-exposed Panc02 tumor-bearing mice following intravenous injection of IR780-labeled LIPs or hNVs. Whole-animal fluorescence imaging revealed significantly higher IR780 intensity in tumors of hNVs-treated mice than those treated with LIPs (Fig. 5f, g), indicating the enhanced tumor accumulation of hNVs. Ex vivo analysis at 48 h post-injection confirmed that hNVs achieved approximately 1.8-fold higher tumor accumulation than LIPs (Fig. 5h, i). This finding was corroborated by measuring intratumoral Pro levels in CRS-exposed Panc02 tumor-bearing mice at 24 h after intravenous administration of Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs pretreated with or without Batimastat (Bati), an MMP inhibitor34. The Pro@hNVs-treated group exhibited significantly higher intratumoral Pro levels, with approximately 5.0- and 1.7-fold increases compared to Pro and Pro@LIPs, respectively (Fig. 5j). Bati pretreatment significantly reduced the intratumoral accumulation of Pro@hNVs, but not Pro@LIPs (Fig. 5j), indicating that Pro@hNVs enrichment in tumor tissues depended on their MMP-mediated ECM degradation. For tumor penetration evaluation, CRS-exposed orthotopic Panc02 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously administered with IR780-labeled LIPs or hNVs. Confocal microscopy at 48 h revealed that IR780 fluorescence was predominantly localized within the Cy3-conjugated CD31-labeled tumor vessels in the LIPs-treated group (Fig. 5k, l), suggesting restricted diffusion. In contrast, the hNVs-treated group showed extensive IR780 fluorescence throughout the tumor interstitium, with minimal CD31 colocalization (Fig. 5k, l). These findings demonstrated that hNVs enhanced tumor accumulation and achieved deeper penetration, likely due to their ECM-degrading properties.

Pro@hNVs efficiently inhibit tumor growth and improve tumor microenvironment in Panc02 tumor-bearing mice under CRS exposure

Leveraging their ability to disrupt sympathetic nerve-cancer cell/macrophage crosstalk, enhance tumor accumulation and achieve deep penetration, Pro@hNVs’ therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in CRS-exposed Panc02 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6a). Pro@hNVs demonstrated superior anticancer efficacy compared to Pro and Pro@LIPs, reducing tumor weight by 74.3% and 57.5%, respectively (Fig. 6b, c), and decreasing mesentery metastatic nodules by 79.6% and 66.7%, respectively (Fig. 6d, e). Mice treated with Pro@hNVs also exhibited the longest survival time, extending lifespan by approximately 19 and 12 days compared to Pro and Pro@LIPs, respectively (Fig. 6f). Importantly, body weight monitoring (Supplementary Fig. 21) and serological analysis (Supplementary Fig. 22a–f) showed negligible toxicity of Pro@hNVs in CRS-exposed orthotopic Panc02 tumor-bearing mice.

Flow cytometric analysis showed that hNVs, Pro@LIPs, and Pro@hNVs significantly increased CD11c+ TAM ratios (Fig. 6g) and decreased CD206+ TAM ratios (Fig. 6h) in tumors, with Pro@hNVs exhibiting the strongest TAM reprogramming ability (Fig. 6g, h), which was confirmed by the highest intratumoral TNF content (Fig. 6i). Furthermore, Pro@hNVs efficiently enhanced intratumoral CD8+ T cell ratios (Fig. 6j) and most potently inhibited intratumoral sympathetic nerve density, as evidenced by the lowest expressions of β3-tubulin (Fig. 6k, l) and TH (Fig. 6m, n) via immunofluorescence. In addition, Pro@hNVs significantly reduced intratumoral NE (Fig. 6o) and NGF (Fig. 6p) levels, verifying their efficacy in impairing nerve function. These findings suggested that Pro@hNVs inhibited sympathetic nerve activity and modulated the tumor microenvironment to enhance anticancer outcomes.

To elucidate whether Pro@hNVs’ antitumor efficacy correlated with ECM degradation and M2-like TAM repolarization, CRS-exposed orthotopic Panc02 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with Pro@hNVs pretreated with or without Bati and/or intraperitoneal injection of Etan (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Bati pretreatment weakened Pro@hNVs-mediated ECM degradation (Supplementary Fig. 23b, c), and Etan decreased intratumoral TNF content (Supplementary Fig. 23d). Both Bati and Etan impaired Pro@hNVs’ ability to repolarize TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 23e–g) and increase intratumoral CD8+ T (Supplementary Fig. 23h), CD69+CD8+ T (Supplementary Fig. 23i), and GzmB+CD8+ T cell numbers (Supplementary Fig. 23j), and counteracted Pro@hNVs’ suppressive effects on tumor nerve density, as evidenced by elevated expression of β3-tubulin and TH (Supplementary Fig. 23k–n). Correspondingly, Bati and/or Etan pretreatment significantly reduced Pro@hNVs’ anticancer activity (Supplementary Fig. 23o–r), confirming its dependence on MMP-mediated ECM degradation and TNF-driven immunity. Given that sympathetic nerve signaling suppresses CD8⁺ T cell function37–39, whether Pro@hNVs could directly counteract this inhibition was investigated. In ISO-stimulated splenic T cell activation assays, Pro@LIPs and Pro@hNVs at pH 6.5 significantly increased the proportions of CD69⁺CD8⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 24a) and GzmB⁺CD8⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 24b) without altering total CD8⁺ T cell ratios (Supplementary Fig. 24c), demonstrating that the acid-triggered Pro release was sufficient to antagonize adrenergic inhibition of CD8⁺ T cells.

Pro@hNVs efficiently boost the therapeutic efficacy of Gemcitabine

Suppressing intratumoral nerve activity has emerged as a potential strategy to augment cancer therapies25,40–42. Gemcitabine (GEM), a first-line pancreatic cancer treatment43, significantly upregulated Adrb2 mRNA in Panc02 cells (Supplementary Fig. 25a) and ADRB2 expression in tumors of CRS-exposed Panc02 tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 25b, c), suggesting that GEM may promote sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk and treatment resistance. To explore Pro@hNVs’ ability to enhance GEM, CRS-exposed Panc02 tumor-bearing mice received intravenous injections of PBS, GEM, Pro@hNVs, or concurrent administration of GEM and Pro@hNVs (GEM + Pro@hNVs) (Fig. 7a). Notably, GEM+Pro@hNVs achieved the most significant outcomes (Synergy Index (SI) = 0.03), with 2 out of 6 mice showing complete tumor regression (Fig. 7b, c). This combination also markedly reduced mesentery metastasis, achieving a 75.9% reduction relative to GEM monotherapy (Fig. 7d, e). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that 25% of mice in the combination group survived beyond 80 days, outperforming other groups (Fig. 7f). Collectively, these results highlight the capacity of Pro@hNVs to significantly enhance the anticancer efficacy of GEM under chronic stress.

Discussion

Chemotherapy, a cornerstone in cancer treatment, is significantly impaired by chronic stress1–3,7. Strategies to enhance chemotherapeutic efficacy under chronic stress include behavioral interventions (e.g., exercise to boost cyclophosphamide sensitivity45,46), psychotropic drugs (e.g., fluoxetine and valproic acid to augment paclitaxel and temozolomide response47), and blocking stress-related receptor signaling, particularly β-adrenergic signaling1–3. However, these approaches face challenges, including limited efficacy of behavioral interventions, neurological side effects of psychotropics, and β-blocker-associated bradycardia31, highlighting the need for more effective and targeted strategies.

Chronic stress activates the sympathetic nervous system to enhance chemoresistance1–3, with ADRB2 playing a pivotal role6,7. NE secreted by sympathetic nerves binds to ADRB2 expressed by various cells in the tumor microenvironment, activating downstream signaling pathways that drive tumor progression and chemoresistance via multiple mechanisms11–14: regulating ERK1/2-JNK-MAPK to enhance proliferation48, upregulating anti-apoptotic (such as BCL-2, BCL-XL, and MCL-1) and inactivating pro-apoptotic (such as BAD) molecules6, stimulating cancer cell inhibin-βA secretion to induce collagen deposition and intratumoral barrier formation21. Importantly, recent studies, including our own findings, have shown that chemotherapeutic agents upregulated ADRB2 expression in cancer cells, further intensifying chemoresistance7. Moreover, ADRB2 activation in cancer cells promotes the secretion of neurotrophic factors, such as NGF and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), enhancing sympathetic nerve infiltration and axonal sprouting and establishing reciprocal sympathetic nerves-cancer cell crosstalk that drives tumor progression and treatment resistance16,49. Thus, targeted ADRB2 blockade might be a promising strategy to disrupt this crosstalk and improve chemotherapy under chronic stress.

In this work, we developed an ADRB blocker Pro delivery system (Pro@hNVs) via hybridizing M1-NVs with pH-sensitive LIPs. Leveraging M1-NVs’ intrinsic tumor targeting and MMP-mediated collagen degradation, Pro@hNVs efficiently accumulated in tumors and released Pro in the acidic tumor microenvironment. Pro@hNVs treatment at pH 6.5 significantly inhibited cancer cell proliferation and migration and reduced NGF expression and secretion, reciprocally inhibiting neural proliferation and neurite outgrowth. These results indicated that Pro@hNVs exerted anticancer effects likely by blocking NGF-mediated sympathetic nerve-cancer cell crosstalk under chronic stress. Future studies should explore whether Pro@hNVs affected other neurotrophic factors or axon guidance molecules. In addition, while chronic stress increases both intratumoral NE and circulating epinephrine (the latter was reported at negligible levels in breast cancer tissues and have minimal impact on tumor progression7), the potential of Pro@hNVs to target neurotransmitters beyond NE merits further investigation.

β-adrenergic signaling also mediates sympathetic nerve-immune cell crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment under chronic stress3,14. ADRB2 activation in cancer cells induced the secretion of monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP1) via the cAMP-PKA pathway to recruit TAMs50, while chronic stress-induced NE activated ADRB2 on TAMs to polarize them towards the M2 phenotype18,29. M2-like TAMs exacerbated the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, upregulated neuroprotective polyamines (e.g., spermine) for axonal repair51, and promoted nerve infiltration within tumors via SPP1 secretion and neurite outgrowth via neuronal mTORC2 signaling52. Conversely, M1 macrophages secreted TNF, IL-6, and CCL2 to induce peripheral nerve injury25,53. Our results also showed that chronic stress significantly increased the infiltration of M2-like TAMs. Pro@hNVs effectively repolarized these M2-like TAMs to M1 phenotype, likely through combined Pro-triggered ADRB blockade and M1-NV-driven M2-like TAM reprogramming. In addition, TNF secreted by Pro@hNVs-reprogrammed M2-like TAMs was responsible for decreased sympathetic nerve activity, as evidenced by the fact that the TNF inhibitor Etan rescued the inhibition of neuronal cell viability and neurite outgrowth induced by Pro@hNVs-reprogrammed M2-like TAMs, while exogenous TNF supplementation restored the neurotoxicity. Our in vitro data showed that Pro@hNVs directly enhanced CD8⁺ T cell activation via ADRB2 blockade. However, considering that Pro@hNVs outperformed Pro@LIPs in promoting intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and Etan treatment abrogated the Pro@hNVs-induced increase in intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell numbers and activation, implying an additional indirect mechanism involving TNF secretion by repolarized M2-like TAMs. Moreover, Pro@hNVs exhibited superior antitumor efficacy over Pro and hNVs alone, which was weakened by both Bati and Etan, confirming that the optimal antitumor activity of Pro@hNVs relied on hNVs-triggered tumor ECM degradation and immune modulation, as well as Pro-mediated sympathetic signaling blockade.

GEM enhances ADRB2 expression in pancreatic and breast cancer cells, potentially intensifying sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk and therapeutic resistance. Our findings indicated that Pro@hNVs combined with GEM improved chemotherapeutic efficacy across diverse stress models and cancer types with a favorable safety profile. However, critical challenges remain for clinical translation. First, the neuromodulatory effects of Pro@hNVs may vary significantly among different chemotherapeutic agents, with anthracyclines (e.g., DOX, epirubicin) and GEM upregulating ADRB2 expression and NGF secretion in cancer cells7,25, while albumin-bound paclitaxel disrupted intratumoral neural networks and oxaliplatin had minimal impact on neural components54. These insights underscore the necessity for personalized chemotherapy regimens tailored to specific nerve-tumor crosstalk. Second, while our work focused on Pro@hNVs’ role in blocking peripheral sympathetic nerve-tumor crosstalk, the peripheral neuronal plasticity may influence central nervous system (CNS) circuits10,55. Whether Pro@hNVs augmented antitumor effects through neuroplasticity-driven CNS modulation remained unclear. Third, without a systematic comparison of restraint durations, we cannot confirm Pro@hNVs’ efficacy across all stress intensities, and a milder protocol may prove sufficient. Future studies should systematically evaluate chronic stress across a range of severities to define the minimal effective condition, thereby optimizing experimental design. Fourth, given Pro@hNVs’ ability to reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment in pancreatic and breast cancer, combining them with immunotherapies may provide viable therapeutic avenues for “cold tumors” like pancreatic and prostate cancers that are rich in neural infiltration but resistant to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Finally, large-scale production and optimization of the manufacturing protocols and engineering strategies for Pro@hNVs represent critical steps towards clinical translation. Addressing these challenges is essential for the successful translation of Pro@hNVs for pancreatic cancer and other stress-exacerbated malignancies.

In summary, we develop Pro@hNVs to target tumors and release Pro for enhanced cancer chemotherapy under chronic stress. The released Pro effectively disrupts the sympathetic nerve-cancer cell and sympathetic nerve-TAM crosstalk in combination with the repolarization of M2-like TAMs by hNVs. This dual mechanism of Pro@hNVs significantly inhibits tumor growth, metastasis, and chemoresistance under chronic stress. This study highlights the potential of Pro@hNVs as a promising strategy to improve chemotherapy outcomes in chronic stress-affected cancer patients.

Cells, animals and human pancreatic tumor tissues

The Panc02 mouse pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell line was from BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China). The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell line, RAW264.7 mouse mononuclear macrophage cell line, THP-1 human leukemia monocytic cell line, B16F10 mouse melanoma cell line and PC12 rat pheochromocytoma cell line were from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). PC12 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% HS, 5% FBS, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Macrophages derived from THP-1 cells were generated with 100 ng mL-1 Phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h. M1 macrophages were obtained by stimulating RAW264.7 cells or THP-1-derived macrophages with 100 ng mL-1 LPS for 48 h. Murine BMDMs were obtained from the femurs and tibias of male C57BL/6 mice (5–6 weeks old) and differentiated for 5 days in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20 ng mL-1 recombinant mouse M-CSF. T cells were isolated from spleens of male C57BL/6 mice (5–6 weeks old) using MojoSort™ Mouse CD3 T Cell Isolation Kit (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20 ng mL-1 IL-2.

Male C57BL/6 J mice (18–20 g, 5-6 weeks old) and female BALB/c mice (16–18 g, 5-6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under standard conditions (21 ± 1 °C, 40–70% humidity, 12 h light-dark cycle) with free access to food and water. For the construction of orthotopic pancreatic cancer model, Panc02 cells (1 × 106 cells in 25 µL PBS per mouse) or KPC cells (2 × 105 cells in 25 μL PBS per mouse) were orthotopically transplanted into the head of the pancreas of male C57BL/6 J mice. For the construction of orthotopic breast cancer model, 4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells in 50 µL PBS per mouse) were orthotopically transplanted into the fourth mammary fat pad of female BALB/c mice. The subcutaneous melanoma model was established by injection of B16F10 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells in 100 μL PBS per mouse) into the right femoral region of male C57BL/6 J mice. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC-2024-4680). The maximum tumor volume permitted by our ethics committee was 1500 mm3, and this limit was not exceeded in any experiment.

Fresh pancreatic tumor tissues were obtained from adenocarcinoma patients undergoing surgical resection at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The study was authorized by the Clinical Trial Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (2025-S092), with informed consent from all participants.

Chronic stress mouse model

Mice were exposed to the CRS or CUMS model as previously described48,56,57. Briefly, to construct the CRS mouse model, mice were restrained in ventilated 50 mL centrifuge tubes for 6 h every other day, allowing slight backward and forward movement. For CUMS mouse model, mice received two randomly daily stressors (no consecutive repeats) from: cold swimming (3 min, 4 °C), tail pinch (5 min), food deprivation (12 h), water deprivation (12 h), 30° cage tilt (12 h), moist bedding (3-4 h), bedding removal (3-4 h), overnight illumination, restraint stress (2 h in 50 mL tubes), or intense illumination (30 min). Mice were subjected to chronic stress one week after cancer cell inoculation.

Behavioral tests

On day 17 of CRS or CUMS, anxiety-like behavior was evaluated with two sequential tests. For the open-field test (OFT), the mice were placed in the center of a 40 × 40 × 40 cm opaque arena (20 × 20 cm central zone), movements were tracked for 5 min with SMART 3.0 software (Panlab, Spain) to record central zone duration and total distance. The arena was wiped with 75% ethanol between animals. For the forced-swim test (FST), the mice were placed in a 25 cm-high, 10 cm-diameter cylinder filled with 25 °C water to a depth of 15 cm. After 2 min of habituation, immobility time (minimal movements to keep head above water) was scored for 4 min. Water was replaced after every trial.

Preparation of Pro@hNVs

M1-NVs were harvested as previously described with modifications32,58. Briefly, M1 macrophages were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (1 min, 37 °C), gently pipetted with complete culture medium, pelleted by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed three times with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in ice-cold PBS at a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells per mL. Cell suspensions were sonicated on ice (50 W, 1.5 s on / 2 s off) for 3 min, serially extruded 11 times through 0.8 and 0.4 μm polycarbonate membrane filters (Whatman, Buckinghamshire, UK) using a miniextruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabama, USA). The extruded suspensions were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The resulting supernatants were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 1 h to collect M1-NVs, stored at − 80 °C.

LIPs and hNVs were prepared by thin-film hydration. Briefly, DOPE, CHEMS, and DSPE-mPEG2000 at a molar ratio of 6: 4: 0.5 were dissolved in chloroform. The solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure at 37 °C to form a uniform film. The film was hydrated in PBS with or without M1-NVs at a M1-NVs/phospholipids mass ratio of 1: 10 at 37 °C for 1 h with stirring, vortexed, and then extruded 11 times through 0.2 μm polycarbonate membrane filters using a miniextruder to obtain homogeneously dispersed LIPs or hNVs. Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs were obtained by adding Pro at a Pro/phospholipids mass ratio of 1: 10 during hydration, followed by ultrafiltration using 100 kDa ultrafiltration tubes (Sigma-Aldrich) and washing three times. The prepared LIPs, hNVs, Pro@LIPs, and Pro@hNVs were stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of Pro@hNVs

To verify the incorporation of LIPs into M1-NVs, a FRET assay was conducted as previously described58. M1-NVs labeled with DiI (excitation/emission = 549/565 nm) and DiD (excitation/emission = 644/663 nm) were combined with LIPs for hydration and subsequently extruded through 0.2 μm polycarbonate membranes as per hNV preparation. The fluorescence spectra (535–750 nm) were recorded with excitation at 525 nm. To further evaluate the successful construction of Pro@hNVs, proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, and loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel. Proteins were visualized by silver staining using a fast silver stain kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Furthermore, the proteins from M1 macrophages, M1-NVs, hNVs and Pro@hNVs were subjected to western blot analysis using Rabbit-anti-CD86 (Proteintech, cat No 13395-1-AP, 1/1000 dilution) as the primary antibody and HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Proteintech, cat No SA00001-2, 1/5000 dilution) as the secondary antibody. Protein detection was performed using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Morphology of Pro@hNVs was examined using TEM (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Hydrodynamic diameter, zeta potential, and PDI of Pro@hNVs were assessed by DLS (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). The inter-batch CV was calculated as the overall standard deviation divided by the grand mean59. Stability of Pro@hNVs was evaluated by monitoring these DLS parameters over time in PBS with or without 10% FBS. Protein content in Pro@hNVs was quantified via the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Pro content was determined by HPLC (Agilent 1100, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) under the following conditions: C18 column (5 × 250 mm, 5 µm particle size); mobile phase: methanol and 0.02 M sodium acetate (70: 30, v/v) adjusted to pH 5.51 with acetic acid; detection wavelength: 229 nm; flow rate: 1.0 mL min−1; run time: 10 min per sample. The pH-responsive Pro release from Pro@hNVs was evaluated by dialysis in PBS at pH 7.4 and 6.5 at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. The amount of Pro released was measured at different time points using HPLC.

Proliferation and migration assay

Panc02 cells were treated with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at 5 µM Pro concentration in the presence of 5 µM ISO or PC12 cell supernatants at pH 7.4 or 6.5. The cell viability was determined by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) at 24 h post-treatment per the manufacturer’s protocol. For the wound healing assay, 3 × 104 Panc02 cells pretreated with the above formulations for 24 h were seeded in scratch test inserts. Cell migration was monitored at different time intervals using a Ti-2 inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the wound healing area was quantified by ImageJ. For the transwell assay, Panc02 cells were pretreated with the above formulations for 24 h and collected. 1.2 × 105 Panc02 cells were seeded in the upper chambers of transwell inserts with DMEM medium, while 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers. After 12 h, the inserts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 5% crystal violet. Migrated cells were observed using the Ti-2 microscope and quantified by ImageJ.

NGF expression and secretion

Panc02 cells were treated with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at a Pro concentration of 5 µM in the presence of 5 µM ISO at pH7.4 or 6.5. At 12 h after treatment, RNA was extracted from Panc02 cells using FreeZol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and subjected to real-time RT-PCR for Ngf expression analysis. The primer sequences used were as follows: mouse Gapdh (F: 5’-GTTCCTACCCCCAATGTGTCC-3′, R: 5′-TAGCCCAAGATGCCCTTCAGT-3’); mouse Ngf (F: 5’-TGATCGGCGTACAGGCAGA-3’, R: 5’-GCTGAAGTTTAGTCCAGTGGG-3’), which were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). To determine the content of NGF secreted by Panc02 cells, Panc02 cells were treated as described for 24 h, then incubated in fresh RPMI 1640 medium for an additional 24 h. The supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to remove the debris, and analyzed for NGF content using ELISA kits (MEIMIAN, Yancheng, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Macrophage polarization

RAW264.7 macrophages or BMDMs were treated with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at a Pro concentration of 5 µM in the presence of 5 µM ISO at pH 7.4 or 6.5. After 12 h, RNA was extracted from the treated macrophages for phenotype-related gene expression analysis by real-time RT-PCR. The primer sequences used were as follows: mouse Tnf (F: 5’-CCCACGTCGTAGCAAACCAC-3’, R: 5’-GCAGCCTTGTCCCTTGAAGA-3’); mouse Arg1 (F: 5’-CAAGACAGGGCTCCTTTCAG-3’, R: 5’-TGGCTTATGGTTACCCTCCC-3’); mouse Mgl1 (F: 5’-AGAAAACCCAAGAGCCTGGT-3’, R: 5’-GAGGCCCAGGGAGAACAG-3’), which were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For flow cytometric analysis, the cells were collected after 24 h treatment and stained with the following antibodies: Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD80, APC anti-mouse CD86 and Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse CD206, with the details shown in Supplementary Table 1. To determine TNF secretion, RAW264.7 macrophages were treated as described for 24 h, and then incubated in fresh RPMI 1640 medium for an additional 24 h. The supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to remove the debris, and analyzed for TNF content using ELISA kits (Dakewe Biotech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Neural cell proliferation and neurite growth assay

The supernatants from Panc02, RAW264.7 cells or BMDMs were collected after 24 h treatment with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at 5 µM Pro concentration in the presence of 5 µM ISO at pH 7.4 or 6.5. For the neural cell proliferation assay, PC12 cells were incubated with these Panc02 cell-derived supernatants or macrophage-derived supernatants supplemented with 50 ng mL-1 NGF in 96-well plates for 48 h. The neural cell proliferation was determined using CCK-8 per the manufacturer’s protocol. For the neurite growth assay, PC12 cells were seeded in PLL-coated 12-well plates for 12 h, then treated with the above supernatants for 72 h. Neurite outgrowth was visualized using a Ti-2 inverted fluorescence microscope and quantified using the simple neurite tracer (SNT) plugin of ImageJ.

T cell activation

T cells isolated from spleens of male C57BL/6 mice were treated with PBS, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at 5 µM Pro concentration in the presence of plate-bound anti-CD3ε antibody (5 µg mL−1, Biolegend, cat. No 100340), soluble anti-CD28 antibody (2 µg mL−1, Biolegend, cat. No 102116) and 5 µM ISO at pH 7.4 or 6.5. After 4 days, the cells were collected and co-incubated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences, cat. No 550583, 1/500 dilution) for 3 h, then stained with FITC anti-mouse CD8a, Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse CD69, and PE/Cyanine7 anti-human/mouse Granzyme B antibodies with the details shown in Supplementary Table 1. The stained cells were analyzed by the CytoFLEX S flow cytometry.

Degradation matrix capacity analysis

Western blot was used to determine the expression of MMP9 and MMP14 on Pro@hNVs. The MMP enzyme activity of Pro@hNVs was analyzed using a mouse MMP ELISA Kit (MEIMIAN, Yancheng, China). For in vitro degradation matrix capacity of Pro@hNVs, M1-NVs, LIPs, hNVs, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at a final concentration of 200 µg protein mL−1 were incubated with an equal volume of 200 µg mL−1 collagen type I (Corning, Massachusetts, USA) for different time intervals and then detected by the Sirius Red Total Collagen Detection Kits (Chondrex, Woodinville, USA). For in vivo ECM degradation capacity of Pro@hNVs, CRS-exposed orthotopic Panc02 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs or Pro@hNVs at the Pro dosage of 7 mg kg−1 every 2 days for 7 times. The tumors were harvested, fixed with 4% PFA and sectioned by cryotomy. The sections were incubated with anti-collagen I Rabbit antibody (Servicebio, cat. No GB11022-3-100, 1/200 dilution) as the primary antibody and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Servicebio, cat. No GB21303, 1/300 dilution) as the secondary antibody, visualized by fluorescence microscopy and then quantified by ImageJ.

In vivo biodistribution and tumor penetration

Four weeks after constructing Panc02 tumor-bearing mice under CRS exposure, the mice were intravenously injected with IR780-labeled LIPs or hNVs at an IR780 dosage of 1 mg kg−1 for fluorescence imaging analysis, or Pro, Pro@LIPs, Pro@hNVs, 0.6 mg mL−1 Bati-pretreatd Pro@LIPs, or Bati-pretreatd Pro@hNVs at a Pro dosage of 7 mg kg−1 for HPLC analysis. For fluorescence imaging analysis, IR780 fluorescence was captured using Caliper IVIS Lumina II in vivo imaging systems (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA) at various time points post-injection. After 48 h, the mice were sacrificed, and the major organs and tumor were collected for IR780 fluorescence imaging. Furthermore, the tumor tissue was fixed by 4% PFA, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and labeled with Cy3-conjugated CD31 antibody to visualize the distribution of IR780 relative to blood vessels using FV3000 confocal microscope. For HPLC assays, the major organs and tumors were collected at 24 h post-treatment, weighed, and homogenized in PBS. Pro was extracted from the homogenates using methanol and quantified by HPLC.

In vivo anticancer effects

One week after implantation of Panc02, 4T1 or B16F10 cells, or five days after KPC cell implantation, mice were intravenously administered PBS, LIPs, hNVs, Pro, Pro@LIPs, or Pro@hNVs at the Pro dosage of 7 mg kg−1 every other day for a total of 7 doses. Concurrently, chemotherapeutic drugs were intravenously injected at the following dosages: 20 mg kg⁻¹ GEM for the Panc02 and KPC pancreatic cancer models, 10 mg kg−1 GEM for the 4T1 breast cancer model every three days for a total of 5 doses; and 2 mg kg−1 Dox for the B16F10 melanoma model every other day for a total of 6 doses. Mice were continuously exposed to CRS or CUMS throughout the treatment period. Body weight, tumor size, and survival rates were monitored. Heart rate and mean arterial pressure in the KPC model were recorded under 1.5% inhaled isoflurane anesthesia using a BL-420N data acquisition and analysis system (TaiMeng, Chengdu, China). At the designed time intervals, a subset of mice was sacrificed, and tumors were collected for weighing and analysis. Drug synergy was assessed according to the Bliss independence model60. The expected additive effect (Eexpected) was calculated as Eexpected = EA + EB − EA × EB, where EA and EB are the fractional effects of the individual drugs. The synergy index (SI) was defined as the difference between the observed combined effect (Eobserved) and the expected effect (SI = Eobserved - Eexpected), with Eobserved and Eexpected derived from tumor growth inhibition measurements. According to the fundamental principle of the Bliss model, an SI > 0 indicates synergy. The number of intestinal metastatic nodules in the Panc02 and KPC models was counted. Tumors were sectioned and stained for immunohistochemical analysis of ADRB2 and immunofluorescent analysis of β3-tubulin, TH and collagen Ⅰ while remaining tissues were homogenized for ELISA analysis of NE, NGF, and TNF levels. The major organs were fixed with 4% PFA, sectioned and stained with H&E.

Tumor immune microenvironment analysis

Tumor tissues were collected, rinsed with PBS, and minced into small pieces. These pieces were placed in 6-well plates containing 4 mL of digestion solution composed of 0.8 mg mL−1 collagenase type I and 5 µg mL−1 DNase I, and incubated at 37 °C for 40 min. Digestion was terminated by adding 2 mL of RPMI 1640 complete medium. Single-cell suspensions were obtained through mechanical dissociation using a mesh cell strainer, followed by filtration through a 40 µm nylon mesh and erythrocyte lysis treatment. The single cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 700 (BD Biosciences, cat. No 564997, 1/1000 dilution) to differentiate live from dead cells. For myeloid cell analysis, the cells were first treated with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 for Fc blocking, then stained with the following antibodies: APC-Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45, FITC anti-mouse/human CD11b, PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD11c, Brilliant Violet 421 anti-mouse F4/80, Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD80, APC anti-mouse CD86, Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse CD206, PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse Gr-1, and PE anti-mouse Ly-6G. For lymphocyte analysis, the cells were co-incubated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences, cat. No 550583, 1/500 dilution) for 3 h, then stained with the following antibodies: APC-Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45, BB700 anti-mouse CD3e, Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse CD4, FITC anti-mouse CD8a, Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse CD69, and PE/Cyanine7 anti-human/mouse Granzyme B. The stained cells were analyzed by the CytoFLEX S flow cytometry. Antibody details are provided in Supplementary Table 1, and the gating strategy for flow cytometric analysis using FlowJo is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 42.

Human tumor slice culture

Tumor tissues from pancreatic adenocarcinoma patients undergoing surgical resection were sliced into thin sections (approximately 5 × 5 × 2 mm) and cultured in 6-well plates using RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Tumor slices were treated with PBS, Pro@hNVs from THP-1-derived M1-NVs, GEM, or Pro@hNVs+GEM at the Pro concentration of 5 µM and GEM concentration of 1 µg mL−1 at 37 °C for 4 days. To analyze nerve infiltration, the tumor slices were fixed by 4% PFA and stained with anti-β3-tubulin or anti-TH antibody. To assess cancer cell proliferation and macrophage phenotypic changes, tumor slices were digested with 0.8 mg mL−1 collagenase type I and 5 µg mL−1 DNase I, mechanically dissociated using a mesh cell strainer, and filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with the following antibodies: FITC anti-human CD45, PE anti-mouse/human CD11b, PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human CD14, PE/Cyanine7 anti-human CD80, APC anti-human CD206, and Brilliant Violet 421 anti-human Ki-67. For T cells analysis, the cells were co-incubated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences, cat. No 550583, 1/500 dilution) for 3 h, then stained with the following antibodies: FITC anti-human CD45, PE anti-human CD3, Pacific Blue anti-human CD8, APC anti-human CD69 and PE/Cyanine7 anti-human/mouse Granzyme B. Stained cells were analyzed using CytoFLEX S flow cytometry. Antibody details are provided in Supplementary Table 1, and the gating strategy for flow cytometric analysis using FlowJo is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 43.

Public datasets analysis

The association between ADRB2 expression and nerve-related genes (GAP43, DCX, NEFL, NTRK1, S100B, UCHL1, NGF, DBH) or stromal-related genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, ACTA2, FN1) in pancreatic and breast cancer was evaluated using TIMER2.036. The link between ADRB2 expression and M2 macrophage infiltration in pancreatic cancer was analyzed via TIMER2.036. Prognostic impacts of ADRB2 expression levels in cancer patients were examined using the Kaplan-Meier plotter61.

Statistical analysis

Data from all experiments are expressed as means ± standard deviation (s.d.). For comparisons between two groups, the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was utilized. For analyses involving multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, CA).
