Comparative Analysis of Surface Roughness and Design Features of Titanium Dental Implants on Primary Stability and Osseointegration
Authors: Ravindranath Dhulipalla, Ramanarayana Boyapati, Kishore K. Katuri, Yamuna Marella, Chaitanya Adurty, Lakshmikanth Kolaparthy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:This study aimed to evaluate the impact of the surface roughness and design features of titanium dental implants on primary stability and osseointegration.Methods:Sixty titanium dental implants were categorized into three groups based on their surface roughness: smooth (Subgroup A1), moderately rough (Subgroup A2), and highly rough (Subgroup A3). Each roughness category was further divided into three design subgroups: cylindrical (B1), tapered (B2), and hybrid (B3). Primary stability was assessed using insertion torque and resonance frequency analysis (RFA). Osseointegration was evaluated through histological and histomorphometric analyses, measuring bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area fraction occupancy (BAFO) at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-implantation in rabbit models.Results:Highly rough-surface implants (Subgroup A3) demonstrated significantly higher insertion torque and ISQ values than smooth (Subgroup A1) and moderately rough (Subgroup A2) implants (P < 0.001). Among the design features, tapered implants (Subgroup B2) exhibited the highest insertion torque and ISQ values (P < 0.001). Histological and histomorphometric analyses revealed that highly rough-surface implants (Subgroup A3) and tapered implants (Subgroup B2) had the highest BIC and BAFO percentages at all time points (P < 0.001).Conclusion:These findings indicate that both surface roughness and implant design significantly influence primary stability and osseointegration.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants have revolutionized dentistry by providing a reliable solution for tooth replacement with high success rates. The success of dental implants is predominantly determined by two key factors: primary stability and osseointegration. Primary stability refers to the mechanical stability of the implant immediately after placement, whereas osseointegration involves the biological process by which the implant becomes firmly anchored to the jawbone through the formation of new bone around the implant surface.[12]

The surface roughness of titanium implants has been extensively studied as a means of enhancing osseointegration. Rougher surfaces provide a larger surface area for bone contact and better mechanical interlocking between the implant and bone, facilitating quicker and more robust bone healing.[3] Various surface treatment techniques, such as sandblasting, acid etching, and plasma spraying, have been developed to modify the surface roughness of titanium implants and improve their osseointegration potential.[4]

In addition to surface roughness, design features of implants, such as shape and geometry, also play a crucial role in their primary stability and osseointegration. For instance, tapered implants are designed to mimic the natural root shape of teeth, allowing for better distribution of mechanical forces and enhanced stability in areas of varying bone density.[5] Cylindrical and hybrid designs each have their own advantages and limitations, influencing the choice of implant depending on the clinical scenario.[6]

Previous studies have shown that implants with rougher surfaces and tapered designs tend to exhibit higher primary stability and better osseointegration compared to those with smoother surfaces and cylindrical designs.[7]

METHODOLOGY

This in vitro experimental study, conducted over 12 months, involved 60 titanium dental implants categorized into two main groups based on surface roughness (Group A) and design features (Group B), each with three subgroups. Group A included smooth (A1), moderately rough (A2), and highly rough (A3) surfaces, while Group B comprised cylindrical (B1), tapered (B2), and hybrid (B3) designs. Each subgroup contained 10 implants, and the study aimed to assess the impact of these variations on primary stability and osseointegration. Surface roughness was measured using a profilometer and confirmed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Primary stability was evaluated using insertion torque measurements and resonance frequency analysis (RFA), with implants placed in artificial bone blocks simulating type II bone density.

For in vivo osseointegration assessment, titanium implants were surgically placed in the tibiae of 10 New Zealand white rabbits, with each rabbit receiving six implants across the experimental groups. Histomorphometric analysis at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-implantation was conducted to evaluate bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area fraction occupancy (BAFO). Data were analyzed using SPSS Version 23.0, with ANOVA used to compare primary stability metrics across groups, and T-tests used to assess osseointegration parameters, with a significance level set at P < 0.05 for all tests.

RESULTS

Implants with highly rough surfaces (Subgroup A3) demonstrated significantly higher insertion torque values (38.6 ± 3.5 Ncm) and ISQ values (68.7 ± 5.3) compared to smooth (Subgroup A1) and moderately rough surfaces (Subgroup A2), as well as superior bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area fraction occupancy (BAFO) percentages at 12 weeks (76.5 ± 6.4% and 70.3 ± 5.5%, respectively). Similarly, tapered implants (Subgroup B2) exhibited higher insertion torque (40.2 ± 3.2 Ncm), ISQ values (70.1 ± 5.4), and superior BIC and BAFO percentages (78.2 ± 6.2% and 72.1 ± 5.4%, respectively) compared to cylindrical and hybrid designs. These results underscore the critical influence of surface roughness and implant design on primary stability and osseointegration, with highly rough surfaces and tapered designs showing superior outcomes [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the effects of surface roughness and implant design on the primary stability and osseointegration of titanium dental implants. Implants with highly rough surfaces (Subgroup A3) exhibited significantly higher insertion torque and ISQ values compared to smooth (Subgroup A1) and moderately rough (Subgroup A2) surfaces. Rough surfaces enhance mechanical interlocking with the bone, improving primary stability.[12] Tapered implants (Subgroup B2) showed superior primary stability compared to hybrid (Subgroup B3) and cylindrical (Subgroup B1) designs. The tapered geometry allows for better mechanical engagement, especially in varying bone densities.[34]

Implants with highly rough surfaces (Subgroup A3) demonstrated the highest bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area fraction occupancy (BAFO) at all time points. Tapered designs (Subgroup B2) also showed superior osseointegration, likely due to the compressive forces generated during insertion, which stimulate bone remodeling.[56] Both surface roughness and implant design significantly influence implant performance. Optimal outcomes were observed with implants combining high surface roughness and a tapered design, indicating that an integrated approach is crucial for maximizing implant success.

Clinical implications

Understanding these factors aids clinicians in selecting appropriate implants for various clinical scenarios, particularly in cases of poor bone quality. The enhanced osseointegration observed with rough surfaces and tapered designs suggests reduced healing times and earlier loading, leading to improved patient satisfaction and treatment efficiency.

While in vitro tests and animal models provide valuable insights, they may not fully replicate human conditions. Future research should focus on long-term clinical studies and explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the bone response to surface roughness and implant design. Additionally, further investigation into coating materials and surface chemistry could enhance implant performance.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the critical role of surface roughness and design features in the primary stability and osseointegration of titanium dental implants. Implants with highly rough surfaces and tapered designs have demonstrated the most favorable outcomes, achieving superior mechanical stability and enhanced biological integration.
