Emergency neuroendoscopic evacuation of traumatic intraventricular hemorrhage: A case report
Authors: Seigo Kimura, Daiji Ogawa, Ryokichi Yagi, Norihito Fukawa, Keiichi Yamada, Hirokatsu Taniguchi, Masahiko Wanibuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Traumatic intraventricular hemorrhage (tIVH) is an uncommon but severe manifestation of traumatic brain injury and is associated with poor neurological outcomes, particularly when accompanied by acute hydrocephalus. While neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation has been reported to be effective for intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) secondary to spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, its role in tIVH remains unclear. We report a rare case of rapidly progressive tIVH complicated by acute hydrocephalus, successfully treated with emergency endoscopic hematoma evacuation. Notably, the bleeding source was directly visualized endoscopically, providing important insights into the underlying pathophysiology of tIVH.Case Description: An 81-year-old woman presented with mild consciousness disturbance after a fall. Initial head computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a small IVH in the right lateral ventricle. Despite conservative management, follow-up CT revealed rapid progression of cast-like IVH extending into the third and fourth ventricles, accompanied by acute hydrocephalus. Emergency neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation was performed. During surgery, active venous bleeding was directly visualized on the lateral wall of the right lateral ventricle and successfully controlled by coagulation. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy was also performed. Postoperative imaging confirmed adequate hematoma removal without rebleeding. Although delayed hydrocephalus required ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement, no vascular abnormalities were identified on postoperative CT angiography.Conclusion: Emergency neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation for tIVH enabled rapid decompression, direct identification of the bleeding source, and effective hemostasis. This case suggests that endoscopic surgery may be a useful therapeutic option in selected patients with rapidly progressive tIVH and acute hydrocephalus. Further accumulation of cases is required to clarify its impact on long-term neurological outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a serious clinical condition associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly when accompanied by acute obstructive hydrocephalus and elevated intracranial pressure. The presence and volume of intraventricular blood have been identified as independent predictors of poor neurological outcome in both spontaneous and traumatic brain injury.[5] External ventricular drainage (EVD) has traditionally been regarded as the standard surgical treatment for IVH-associated hydrocephalus, allowing cerebrospinal fluid diversion and intracranial pressure control. However, EVD does not directly remove intraventricular clot burden and is frequently complicated by catheter obstruction, infection, prolonged drainage, and shunt-dependent hydrocephalus.[2,6]In recent years, neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation has been increasingly applied to IVH secondary to spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage or thalamic hemorrhage. Several studies and systematic reviews have demonstrated that endoscopic evacuation may achieve more rapid hematoma clearance, reduce shunt dependency, and improve functional outcomes compared with EVD alone or EVD combined with intraventricular fibrinolysis.[1,4,7] In contrast, the role of neuroendoscopic surgery for traumatic IVH (tIVH) remains unclear. To the best of our knowledge, we could not find published reports specifically describing endoscopic evacuation for tIVH. Here, we report a rare case of rapidly progressive tIVH complicated by acute hydrocephalus, successfully treated with emergency endoscopic hematoma evacuation. Notably, the bleeding source was directly visualized endoscopically, providing important insights into the underlying pathophysiology of tIVH.

CASE DESCRIPTION

An 81-year-old woman was found collapsed on the street at approximately 4:00 AM with a contusion to the occipital region, prompting an emergency medical call. Her medical history was notable for hypertension and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. She was receiving oral medications, including amlodipine 5 mg, sacubitril/valsartan 100 mg, and aspirin 100 mg.

On arrival at our hospital, her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 14 (E4V4M6), with no focal neurological deficits, including limb weakness. She had repeated vomiting and a subcutaneous hematoma over the occipital region. Her vital signs showed a blood pressure of 149/91 mmHg and a heart rate of 90 beats/min. A head computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a right occipital subcutaneous hematoma and a small IVH in the body of the right lateral ventricle [Figure 1a and b]. No apparent skull fracture was identified. The patient was admitted and initially managed conservatively. Intravenous administration of carbazochrome sodium sulfonate (100 mg) and tranexamic acid (1g) was initiated, and systolic blood pressure was maintained below 140 mmHg using continuous intravenous infusion of nifedipine. Three hours later, the patient’s level of consciousness deteriorated to a GCS score of 13 (E3V4M6). A follow-up head CT performed 3 h and 4 min after the initial scan demonstrated a cast-like IVH extending from the anterior horn to the body and posterior horn of the right lateral ventricle, with further extension into the third and fourth ventricles [Figure 2a and b]. A small amount of IVH was also observed in the contralateral lateral ventricle. Associated ventricular dilatation suggested the development of acute hydrocephalus. Therefore, emergency endoscopic hematoma evacuation was performed. Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position, and a 3-cm skin incision was made approximately 3 cm anterior to the bregma and 3 cm to the right of the midline. A burr hole was created using a disposable perforator (Codman®, Integra LifeSciences, USA) and subsequently enlarged with a round cutter. A 10-Fr sheath was inserted toward the right lateral ventricle. Hematoma evacuation was performed using a rigid endoscope (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany). The procedure was performed mainly using a wet-field endoscopic technique with repeated irrigation using saline. Because intraventricular blood and clot markedly obscured visualization, hematoma evacuation was performed in a stepwise manner. First, the clot in the anterior horn of the right lateral ventricle was partially removed to create a working space and restore orientation. Repeated irrigation and suction were then used to improve visualization of the ventricular cavity and anatomical landmarks. After the foramen of Monro was identified, the endoscope was advanced into the third ventricle, where the hematoma was removed as completely as possible under continuous irrigation. Whenever the operative field became dark due to residual blood, irrigation was repeated, and clot removal was resumed only after the anatomical structures could again be recognized. Next, the floor of the third ventricle was bluntly perforated using a suction tube to create an endoscopic third ventriculostomy [Figure 3a]. The tuber cinereum was identified between the mammillary bodies and the infundibular recess. Subsequently, after returning to the right lateral ventricle to continue hematoma evacuation, venous bleeding was observed from the lateral wall of the ventricular body [Figure 3b]. Once the bleeding point became visible, hemostasis was achieved by electrocoagulation at the tip of the suction tube. After confirming adequate hemostasis, the ventricles were thoroughly irrigated with saline. A ventricular drain was placed, and the procedure was completed. Postoperative CT demonstrated successful removal of the hemorrhage without evidence of rebleeding [Figure 4a and b]. A head CT angiography performed on the following day to evaluate for underlying vascular lesions revealed no significant abnormalities. The patient’s level of consciousness remained at a GCS score of 13. She was able to follow simple verbal commands, greet others, and state her name, and communication by nodding was preserved. However, she required total assistance for activities of daily living. Progressive hydrocephalus subsequently developed, and a ventriculoperitoneal shunt was performed on postoperative day 63 [Figure 5]. The patient was transferred to a convalescent rehabilitation hospital on postoperative day 87 with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 5.

DISCUSSION

tIVH occurs in approximately 1.5–3% of all traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) and in up to 10% of severe TBI cases.[9] Patients with TBI complicated by tIVH generally have a poor prognosis,[9] and tIVH accompanied by early ventricular enlargement is considered particularly severe and associated with unfavorable outcomes.[8] Although the precise mechanisms underlying the poor prognosis remain incompletely understood, secondary pathophysiological processes related to tIVH, including acute hydrocephalus and increased intracranial pressure, are thought to play important contributory roles.

The mechanism underlying the development of IVH remains unclear.[9] Several pathophysiological mechanisms have been proposed. First, shearing stress may induce displacement of midline brain structures, leading to injury of the subependymal microvasculature draining into the ventricular walls, as well as damage to the fragile ependymal cells or the choroid plexus. Second, ventricular deformation (cavitation) caused by traumatic forces, particularly sagittal acceleration, may result in rupture of the subependymal veins within the ventricular wall as the ventricle recoils to its original shape and position. Third, IVH may be triggered by a sudden increase in intraventricular pressure or the propagation of mechanical vibrations. tIVH is more likely to occur following high-energy trauma. In our case, the head CT scan at presentation showed hemorrhage in the lateral ventricle body, with no evidence of intracerebral hemorrhage. The bleeding point confirmed endoscopically after hemorrhage expansion was located on the lateral wall of the right lateral ventricle [Figure 3b]. The tract of the sheath identified on postoperative CT [Figure 4a and b] spatially corresponded to the hematoma localization observed in the same region on preoperative CT [Figure 1a and b]. This location corresponded anatomically to the subependymal venous territory along the lateral wall of the body of the lateral ventricle, which is consistent with the known course of the posterior caudate vein. Ventriculostomy for tIVH and endoscopic hematoma evacuation for non-tIVH are commonly performed. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous reports have described endoscopic hematoma removal for tIVH. Furthermore, no studies have documented direct endoscopic visualization of venous bleeding, presumed to be the source of hemorrhage, in patients with tIVH. Although the surgical concept of neuroendoscopic clot evacuation may resemble that used for spontaneous IVH, the significance of the present case lies in its application to rapidly progressive tIVH, in which the indication for surgery was driven not only by hydrocephalus but also by concern for ongoing hemorrhagic progression. In addition, direct endoscopic visualization of active venous bleeding from the lateral ventricular wall provided a rare opportunity to support a pathophysiological mechanism of tIVH and achieve targeted hemostasis.

This patient was receiving aspirin therapy at presentation. After admission, she underwent blood pressure control and hemostatic management with intravenous administration of sodium carbazochrome sulfonate (100 mg) and tranexamic acid (1 g), along with continuous infusion of nifedipine. The initial CT scan on admission demonstrated only minimal IVH; however, follow-up CT revealed progressive expansion of the hemorrhage. Although a causal relationship between aspirin use and hemorrhage progression cannot be established in this single case, careful inpatient observation may be warranted in patients with tIVH receiving antithrombotic therapy, particularly in the early postinjury period.

In our case, the initial head CT on admission demonstrated only minimal IVH; however, follow-up CT revealed progression of the hemorrhage accompanied by ventricular enlargement. Considering the increasing hematoma volume, the need for early mass reduction, and the potential requirement for hemostasis at the bleeding source, emergency endoscopic hematoma evacuation was performed. Although third ventriculostomy was simultaneously attempted, delayed hydrocephalus subsequently developed. Early endoscopic hematoma evacuation offers several advantages, including rapid decompression of mass effect from hemorrhage and elevated intracranial pressure due to acute hydrocephalus, as well as the ability to directly identify the bleeding source. However, despite surgical intervention, the patient was transferred to a long-term care facility with an mRS score of 5, indicating that endoscopic hematoma evacuation itself does not necessarily guarantee a favorable neurological outcome. Long-term prognosis is likely influenced by multiple factors, including the overall severity of traumatic brain injury, encompassing both primary injury and secondary complications such as delayed hydrocephalus. Compared with simple ventricular drainage, endoscopic hematoma evacuation may be particularly advantageous in cases with rapid hemorrhagic progression or acute hydrocephalus, especially in terms of prompt decompression and visualization of the bleeding source. Nevertheless, further accumulation of cases is required to clarify whether this strategy can improve long-term neurological outcomes. A technical challenge of endoscopic evacuation for IVH is that intraventricular blood absorbs light and readily creates a dark operative field, making anatomical orientation difficult. In the present case, this problem was addressed by adopting a wet-field technique with repeated saline irrigation and sequential clot removal, which allowed gradual restoration of the ventricular landmarks and eventual identification of the bleeding point. Regarding hydrocephalus management, fenestration of the third ventricular floor was attempted during endoscopic surgery. Although cerebrospinal fluid flow into the prepontine cistern was observed, the fenestration was created using a blunt suction tube, resulting in an insufficient opening, which may have contributed to the subsequent development of delayed hydrocephalus. Balloon dilatation is considered effective for achieving an adequate fenestration;[3] however, balloons are not routinely stocked at our institution, and the procedure was performed as an emergency measure with the available instruments. Maintaining ready access to balloon catheters may enable more reliable third ventriculostomy and potentially reduce the risk of delayed hydrocephalus, which could influence long-term neurological outcomes. Maintaining ready access to balloon catheters may facilitate reliable third ventriculostomy in emergency settings and potentially contribute to improved long-term neurological outcomes.

CONCLUSION

We report a case of emergency endoscopic hematoma evacuation for tIVH. This technique enabled direct visualization of the bleeding source and rapid decompression of the mass effect. However, further accumulation of cases is necessary to determine whether this strategy can improve long-term neurological outcomes.
