Data-driven spatial metamodeling for non-Gaussian digital roughness mapping in precision machining
Authors: Prithbey Raj Dey, David Enke

ABSTRACT
AbstractTurning-based precision machining produces non-Gaussian spatial surface topographies, inherently defined by skewness () and kurtosis (), which govern surface quality, tribology, and functional performance of the machined workpiece. However, surface roughness modeling remains challenging due to process uncertainty, measurement variability, and the limited integration of and into spatial surface roughness analyses, despite their fundamental role in defining non-Gaussian topographies produced by turning operations. This paper presents a novel data-driven metamodeling framework–Spatial Non-Gaussian Roughness Metamodeling (SNGRM)–that integrates geospatial analysis and kriging interpolation to achieve spatial mapping of arithmetic mean roughness () under explicitly non-Gaussian surface conditions defined by and , thereby advancing multivariate characterization of non-Gaussian machined surfaces. For the interpolation of , ordinary kriging captures spatial variability inferred from the - domain, while universal kriging advances this metamodeling framework by incorporating machining parameters as external drift to explain systematic variations in surface roughness. Consequently, group-wise cross-validation demonstrates that universal kriging achieves superior predictive performance by more effectively capturing both non-Gaussian spatial variability and deterministic trends associated with machining parameters. The proposed framework directly exploits empirical machining data while retaining measurement variability arising from repeated observations, which is frequently averaged out or neglected in conventional roughness models. By jointly incorporating non-Gaussian spatial characteristics and machining parameters, SNGRM enables robust spatial interpolation of the roughness quality index, , with explicit quantification of associated uncertainty via kriging variance. Introduction

In precision engineering, surface texture analysis and predictive modeling play a significant influence, as surface roughness serves as a key quality index governing the performance and reliability of mechanical components. Hard turning is a high-precision machining process that reduces machining time, process steps, and energy consumption, while the resulting surface quality critically influences mechanical properties such as fatigue strength, surface hardness, and wear resistance1. Although more than 70 parameters exist to characterize surface roughness, the arithmetic mean deviation ()–internationally recognized as the standard quality index, primarily reflects Gaussian surface features and is therefore inadequate to represent non-Gaussian textures typically produced in turning operations2, underscoring the need for advanced analytical and predictive approaches. The selection of appropriate non-Gaussian surface descriptors requires a clear understanding of the relationship between roughness parameters and frictional dynamics. Sedlaček et al.3 reported lower coefficients of friction under boundary lubrication conditions for surfaces with negative skewness and higher kurtosis, indicating improved surface quality. However, despite such evidence, relatively limited research has systematically explored the role of skewness and kurtosis in predictive modeling of surface roughness.

Beyond statistical description, non-Gaussian descriptors, such as and , possess direct tribological significance. Negative skewness is associated with valley-dominated plateau-like surfaces that promote lubricant entrapment and reduce asperity contact under boundary and mixed lubrication conditions3,4, while kurtosis reflects the prominence of peaks and valleys that influence contact load distribution and film stability5. Experimental and analytical studies report reduced friction coefficients, improved running-in behavior, enhanced wear resistance, and superior lubricant retention capacity for surfaces exhibiting negative skewness and appropriate kurtosis4,6. These effects are critically important in industrial systems including honed engine cylinder liners7, slider bearings and textured tribo-surfaces8, compressor blade of an aero engine9, magnetic storage head–disk interfaces10, and mixed-lubrication wear applications11, thereby highlighting the practical engineering relevance of incorporating and in advanced surface characterization frameworks. In multi-axis machining environments, surface generation is strongly influenced by nonlinear kinematic errors arising from rotary axis motion and feed direction variability. The direction of feed motion significantly affects both the magnitude and orientation of nonlinear geometric errors12, while the interaction between tool center point path deviations and tool axis vector orientation errors complicates interpolation strategies and surface accuracy control13. Such kinematic effects alter local tool engagement conditions and consequently modify surface height distributions. Experimental studies further demonstrate that machining direction significantly influences skewness and kurtosis; for instance, face milling produces negative skewness with higher kurtosis on bottom surfaces, while the side surface yields positive skewness with kurtosis values closer to Gaussian behavior14. These findings indicate that and are sensitive to machining kinematics, feed direction, and tool-path characteristics, making them valuable descriptors for evaluating surface integrity in complex machining environments. Moreover, randomness and uncertainty arising from geometric inaccuracies and measurement errors hinder product quality15,16, underscoring the importance of adopting predictive approaches that can handle such uncertainties.

Despite the development of numerous predictive models and simulation techniques to capture the complexity of machining processes, most existing approaches remain deterministic17, as they may not explicitly incorporate empirical variability arising from repeated measurements. This may limit their predictive accuracy, often resulting in approximate point estimates without explicit quantification of associated prediction uncertainty associated with model estimates. Moreover, although machined surfaces inherently possess spatial structures in which roughness parameters exhibit correlation and interdependence18, these relationships have not been widely or systematically incorporated into predictive frameworks. Traditionally regarded as a spatial stochastic process model, Kriging (or Gaussian Process modeling) serves as an effective framework by inherently capturing microscale variability and measurement error, while also quantifying prediction uncertainty19,20. In parallel, spatial point pattern analysis offers an analytical basis for examining parameter interdependence and structural organization, which can be leveraged to characterize non-Gaussian surface roughness. However, realizing these capabilities requires an integrated analytical framework that unifies these complementary approaches. To this end, the SNGRM is introduced, integrating non-Gaussian roughness descriptors with spatial point pattern analysis and kriging-based interpolation of . The proposed metamodel is assessed through rigorous statistical and analytical validations, demonstrating the effectiveness of the integrated framework and comparing the predictive capabilities of alternative kriging approaches for .

Literature review

Non-Gaussian surface and empirical uncertainty in machining

Single-point machining processes, such as turning and shaping, generate non-Gaussian surface profiles21 which are crucial to consider in surface layer investigation and modeling. Static factors (machining parameters, cutting tool geometry, cooling conditions, and workpiece properties) together with dynamic factors (temperature, force, and vibration) govern the surface quality22. Recent studies further indicate that surface roughness can be strongly influenced by process mechanics, particularly elastic recovery and plastic side flow during cutting, which can reduce cutting forces and thereby improve surface finish23. Among machining parameters, depth of cut (), cutting speed (), and feed rate (f) are typically regarded as the critical factors for characterizing the roughness profiles of machined surfaces. In surface layer investigations, Myshkin et al.18 emphasized the need to treat surface profiles as spatial structures, while Yan and Liu1 highlighted the use of autocorrelation function (ACF) analysis to capture the periodicity and directionality of surface profiles, enabling reconstruction of Gaussian surfaces based on the variance of surface fluctuations. However, non-Gaussian surfaces require higher-order descriptors, particularly skewness and kurtosis24,25, which capture profile asymmetry and peak distribution as depicted in supplementary material (Appendix A), in addition to the variance and correlation length that define Gaussian characteristics. This is further supported by a recent study demonstrating that a set of twelve amplitude-based roughness parameters exhibits strong linear dependence on standard deviation for surfaces with pre-defined statistical properties, whereas skewness and kurtosis are essential for capturing deviations from Gaussian height distributions26. Skewness and kurtosis play a critical role in governing asperity interactions and lubrication performance in machined surfaces, particularly under non-Gaussian conditions27. Under mixed lubrication conditions, Wang et al.25 demonstrated that for simulated non-Gaussian surfaces, contact parameters–including real contact area and load ratio–exhibit a rapid increase, particularly within the skewness range of –2 to 2 and the kurtosis range of 2 to 4. Skewness and kurtosis are particularly sensitive indicators of abraded surfaces generated during machining processes28. This sensitivity is reflected in their decisive influence on the functional performance of machined components, affecting properties such as lubrication efficiency, sealing capability, friction and wear behavior, fatigue strength, corrosion resistance, and contact stiffness29. Yan et al.30 demonstrated that the interactive effects of these descriptors are critical, showing that an increase in skewness under high kurtosis level reduces fatigue life. In the turning of steel, feed rate has been shown to strongly influence skewness and to moderately affect kurtosis through its effect on the amplitude and frequency of surface peaks, whereas depth of cut has been reported to exert a more limited but sometimes comparable influence on both parameters31. Prior studies further indicate that negative and positive values are associated with a reduction in , highlighting the importance of interdependence among roughness parameters–particularly under hydrodynamic lubrication conditions4,32. However, empirical uncertainty can obscure the independence or correlation of these variables33, thereby limiting the effectiveness of correlation-based regression models.

The precision of machined surfaces and the reliability of machining processes are significantly influenced by generative chatter, which arises from the self-exciting dynamics of cutting and leads to uncertainties associated with tool vibration and accelerated tool wear34. Among the machining parameters, cutting speed is often identified as the dominant contributor to variability in both surface roughness and tool degradation35. In general, uncertainty stems from geometric and repeatability errors, measurement inaccuracies, thermal effects, and process variability36,37, and is typically quantified following the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM) framework or through Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS)38. Repeatability analysis is commonly employed to estimate empirical uncertainty by performing repeated measurements under identical machining conditions at a fixed measurement location in accordance with the GUM framework37. Kumar36 adopted repeatability analysis in micro-turning of copper alloys, deriving uncertainty from the standard deviation of three replicated measurements. In contrast, Farkas and Drégelyi-Kiss39 demonstrated its scalability using twelve repetitions. Moreover, inter-block variability has been reported to exceed intra-block variability by more than a factor of three37, highlighting that trial-to-trial fluctuations significantly influence the reliability of predictive models. Due to the propagation of process variability in machining systems, traditional feature selection approaches may not consistently and accurately identify key features that significantly impact quality indicators15. Collectively, these observations highlight the need to explicitly address measurement uncertainty or adopt modeling approaches that can inherently account for such variability to develop reliable surface roughness models.

State-of-the-art surface roughness modeling

Surface layer modeling, a central component of surface engineering, involves predicting surface geometry and profile characteristics based on machining parameters, environmental conditions, and measured roughness metrics.

The influencing variables can be classified according to their modeling complexity as either easy-to-model (e.g., feed rate, tool geometry, material hardness, vibration amplitude and frequency) or difficult-to-model (e.g., cutting speed, tool material, spindle speed, temperature, and coolant)40. Kuzinovski et al.40 further categorized geometric roughness profiles into dominant deterministic and stochastic structures, where deterministic profiles are primarily governed by controllable machining parameters, while stochastic profiles arise from inherent process variability and uncontrolled factors. In modeling such stochastic behaviors, linear mixed models have proven effective for capturing random effects arising from repeated trials and experimental variability37,41; however, correlated factors often introduce additional complexity due to conflicting objectives and coupled stochastic responses42. Surface roughness models can be broadly grouped into three categories43: (i) empirical models derived from experimental data through linear or non-linear mathematical formulations and design of experiments, (ii) advanced data-driven methods such as response surface methodology, neural networks, and expert systems, which are capable of handling large sets of impact factors, and (iii) kinematic–geometric models that mirror the tool’s cutting action to reconstruct surface topography. More recently, numerical simulation models have been used to replicate machining processes to analyze the impact of cutting conditions, material, and tool characteristics44, while artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches–spanning machine learning (ML) with parameters, image-based ML, and deep learning–have emerged for detecting and predicting surface roughness1. Although these approaches are effective in capturing non-linear relationships and complex interactions, they are generally formulated to predict roughness parameters as scalar responses and do not explicitly represent the spatial structure of surface profiles, which is essential for continuous surface characterization and digital roughness mapping.

Advanced surface reconstruction algorithms and simulation techniques have been developed for the numerical generation of non-Gaussian surfaces by directly synthesizing surface height fields. Earlier approaches employing finite impulse response (FIR) filtering45 or moving-average models with linear transformations46 aim to match prescribed ACFs and height distributions. More recent simulation frameworks combining fast Fourier transform (FFT), translation process theory, and the Johnson translation system have been proposed to generate non-Gaussian surfaces with specified ACFs, skewness, and kurtosis47,48, while modified extensions integrate spectral representation methods and iterative schemes to improve numerical stability when reproducing extreme skewness and kurtosis values2. Despite their effectiveness in surface synthesis, these methods typically rely on dense surface height measurements obtained from high-resolution optical or digital microscopy and are often based on spectral assumptions, which may increase computational demand. In addition, they are primarily developed for surface generation and do not explicitly incorporate machining or process parameters within the modeling framework.

Traditional statistical and design-of-experiment approaches have been widely employed to model roughness parameters (, , ) as response variables based on machining parameters49. While analysis of variance (ANOVA)-based analyses are commonly used to identify the decisive factors influencing surface roughness in hard turning and the Taguchi method has been widely employed for machining parameter optimization50,51, multiple linear regression analyses have been utilized to establish correlations between cutting parameters and roughness responses, with feed rate and cutting speed consistently emerging as dominant contributors52,53. Although feature selection plays a pivotal role in understanding and controlling quality indicators, Xu et al.15 highlight the limitations of traditional feature selection methods under variation propagation and advocates knowledge-enabled strategies that explicitly account for process variability. In randomized experimental designs, linear mixed models provide valid inference but may not explicitly capture spatially structured variability. In this context, deterministic and random process-induced deviations in multifactorial machining processes can be systematically accommodated within kriging frameworks, formulated using best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) to model spatially correlated variability and trend effects54,55. For defining the spatial domain that represents surface characterization, convex and concave hulls can be used, as they have been widely applied in pattern recognition, image processing, statistics, and classification tasks to describe the geometric extent of point distributions56. While convex hulls are commonly adopted due to their computational simplicity, they impose a convex boundary that can obscure concavities and internal voids, whereas concave hulls relax this constraint and can provide a more representative description of the underlying geometric shape of a dataset. Advanced concave hull algorithms, including k-nearest neighbor–based approaches57, α-concave hulls58, and extensions to n-dimensional datasets56, have been developed to quantify concaveness and construct graphs capturing complex spatial geometry, whose suitability can be systematically examined using spatial statistical measures. Within such spatially defined domains, kriging surrogate models offer a natural statistical framework for modeling spatially correlated variability and deterministic trend components, enabling the construction of spatial maps of surface quality.

In recent years, kriging-based surrogate models have been employed in machining to reliably predict technological responses by modeling the complex, non-linear interactions among process parameters. Regression kriging has been reported to outperform response surface models in multi-objective optimization of hard milling of AISI H13 steel, accurately predicting power, temperature, , and material removal rate using machining parameters such as depth of cut, feed rate, cutting speed, and material hardness59. Similarly, Nguyen et al.60,61 demonstrated the capability of kriging to effectively capture non-linear dependencies of specific cutting energy, surface roughness, energy efficiency, turning cost, and operational safety in both dry milling of SKD61 and rotary turning of hardened steel based on parameters including spindle speed, depth of cut, feed per tooth, tool nose radius, feed rate, cutting speed, and tool inclination angle. Extensions such as co-kriging have incorporated auxiliary variables like vibration acceleration alongside conventional parameters to enhance surface roughness modeling performance62, while hybrid strategies have combined kriging interpolation with sequential quadratic programming to optimize titanium alloy machining63. In addition, the simulation output of finite elements has been integrated with kriging to optimize tool geometry in relation to cutting stress and temperature64. Recently, Dey and Enke65 developed a kriging-based framework using the spatial structure induced by and , demonstrating their interdependence and correlation with ; however, the model did not consider non-Gaussian characteristics of the surface profile. Despite these advances in kriging-based roughness modeling and multi-objective optimization, existing approaches predominantly treat surface roughness as scalar responses and may not explicitly account for the spatial structure of non-Gaussian roughness characteristics, thereby limiting their applicability for spatial analysis and kriging-based interpolation.

Research gaps and proposed framework

The review of machining processes and modeling approaches reveals critical research gaps, particularly in the treatment of measurement variability and the limited integration of non-Gaussian spatial structures into predictive metamodeling frameworks. As discussed in Section Non-Gaussian surface and empirical uncertainty in machining, skewness and kurtosis are primary descriptors of non-Gaussian surfaces that vary significantly with machining parameters and shape the resulting roughness profile, yet they have not been systematically incorporated into metamodeling frameworks. In addition, although process uncertainty and measurement variability in machining processes have been well documented–through both analytical and simulation-based approaches–there remains limited explicit integration of these aspects into modeling or metamodeling frameworks using empirical data. These gaps underscore the need for predictive frameworks that explicitly incorporate higher-order descriptors and empirical variability derived from repeated measurements to improve the reliability of roughness characterization. As reviewed in Section State-of-the-art surface roughness modeling, Tomov et al.66 outlined general categories of roughness modeling but also noted key limitations, such as empirical models often rely on fixed constants and exponents that are valid only for specific experiments or narrow design spaces. Similarly, kinematical–geometrical models, although effective in replicating surface features, may exhibit limited accuracy, as they do not explicitly incorporate machining parameters through analytical formulations. Moreover, most predictive models are developed for individual roughness parameters, particularly and , yet these metrics may not fully or uniquely characterize the surface profile, especially for non-Gaussian surfaces that require skewness and kurtosis. Recommendations to account for interactions and correlations among roughness parameters through multivariate approaches have received comparatively limited attention. Furthermore, although surfaces exhibit spatial structure, existing predictive models have not adequately addressed this aspect. Collectively, these gaps highlight the need for an integrated framework that explicitly incorporates non-Gaussian descriptors, spatial correlation, and empirical variability into the predictive modeling of machined surfaces.

To address these identified gaps, this study proposes the SNGRM framework, which integrates non-Gaussian roughness descriptors for spatial domain formation, explicit modeling of spatial correlation and empirical variability, and machining parameters into the predictive modeling of machined surfaces. The proposed metamodeling framework unifies multiple modeling components within a coherent informational flow, enabling a structured and comprehensive representation of roughness behavior. Unlike advanced surface construction methods that aim to explicitly reconstruct surface height fields using high-resolution optical or digital microscopy, the present framework adopts experimentally measured roughness descriptors (, , and )–which represent summary statistics of the underlying height distribution–as the basis for spatial modeling. Within SNGRM, kriging is employed to model the systematic effects of machining parameters through an external drift, while also quantifying variability embedded in empirical data and incorporating it within the kriging framework. This strategy enables reliable and computationally efficient two-dimensional digital roughness mapping that is directly compatible with routine machining experiments.

Methodology and dataset

Spatial analysis and Kriging-based prediction

Spatial analysis enables the identification of patterns and trends within spatially distributed data, combining geostatistics with location-based inference. In the context of machined surface roughness, spatial analysis reveals how the roughness quality indicator varies spatially across a domain constructed from non-Gaussian descriptors, with point pattern analysis used to characterize the distribution and identify potential spatial clustering or regularity. The surface roughening process is closely related to the inhomogeneous deformation of grains, and the microscopic heterogeneity must be considered to predict surface roughness accurately67. To interpret such spatial heterogeneity, it is essential to distinguish between true and apparent contagion in point pattern processes68. Apparent contagion, driven by exogenous factors such as machining conditions, reflects spatial heterogeneity and is expressed through the first-order intensity function which defines the expected number of events per unit area at location x. In a homogeneous point process, the intensity remains constant across space, 69. In contrast, true contagion reflects intrinsic spatial dependence, quantified by the second-order intensity function , which measures the likelihood of observing an additional event at y given one at x. In practice, observed spatial clustering of surface roughness may result from a combination of first-order heterogeneity and second-order interaction70, motivating the use of both homogeneous and inhomogeneous spatial point pattern functions in the analysis.

Second-order functions that account for interpoint distances and spatial relationships, such as G and K functions, are employed to characterize spatial dependence in the roughness data. The nearest-neighbor–based G(r) function measures the cumulative distribution of nearest-neighbor distances, while Ripley’s K(r) function evaluates the degree of spatial interaction among points across multiple scales71; r denotes the interpoint distance used in computing the spatial point pattern functions. The homogeneous G(r) and K(r) functions are commonly used to compare observed spatial point patterns with theoretical expectations under complete spatial randomness (CSR), thereby identifying whether a pattern exhibits clustering or regularity. In contrast, the inhomogeneous functions and incorporate intensity reweighting based on a fitted first-order trend–such as in machining–to account for non-stationarity arising from underlying process variables. Empirical estimates of these functions are commonly evaluated against MCS–based envelopes to infer spatial patterns, such as random, clustered, or regular. In parallel, edge correction techniques are implemented to mitigate boundary bias and ensure reliable estimation near the edges of the observation window. The combined application of inhomogeneous functions and simulation envelopes provides a robust framework for characterizing spatial dependence while accounting for first-order heterogeneity.

Prior to interpolation using kriging-based spatial methods, the spatial dependence within the data is quantified through the empirical semivariogram, which represents the second-order interaction or spatial autocorrelation indicative of true contagion. The semivariogram expresses the relationship between semivariance and lag distance, where the semivariance γ (h) measures dissimilarity between pairs of observations separated by distance h, in contrast to covariance or correlation, which measure similarity. The semivariogram is characterized by three key parameters: the nugget (), sill (C), and range (a). The sill denotes the semivariance value at which the variogram stabilizes, corresponding to the limit of spatial autocorrelation; the range represents the lag distance at which this sill is reached, beyond which spatial dependence becomes negligible; and the nugget captures microscale variability and measurement error at distances smaller than the sampling interval. Spatial non-Gaussian roughness metamodeling

A metamodel, often referred to as a surrogate or a model of models, represents the underlying relationships between response and explanatory variables within a modeling system. Metamodeling formalizes critical and contextual information to establish a coherent conceptual foundation for model development, while enhancing reproducibility, adaptability, and comprehensibility73. A typical metamodeling framework comprises three stages74–design of experiment, selection of an appropriate metamodel, and fitting the model through analytical calibration. However, such frameworks may lack a formalized structure, which can be established by applying the principles of ontology and model-driven engineering through Object Management Group’s multilayer metamodeling architecture75. This model-driven approach enables the abstraction of SNGRM as a sequence of model transformations.

The SNGRM framework, illustrated in Fig. 1, provides a structured workflow for spatial characterization and interpolation of surface roughness. The process begins with a full factorial experimental design, where machining parameters and repeated roughness measurements (, , ) are collected. The non-Gaussian descriptors (, ) are used to define the admissible descriptor-based spatial domain for morphological characterization and spatial modeling of . Spatial autocorrelation is characterized using empirical semivariogram analysis to quantify second-order spatial structure within the descriptor-defined domain. For ordinary kriging, semivariograms are estimated directly from the observed values, whereas universal kriging first removes the deterministic machining-parameter trend, and semivariograms are subsequently estimated from the residual field to model the remaining spatial dependence. Ordinary kriging predicts using only spatial coordinates in the descriptor domain, while universal kriging additionally includes machining parameters as external drift. The resulting digital roughness maps are accompanied by kriging variance surfaces, enabling uncertainty-aware spatial interpretation.

Inferential analysis of empirical data

Before developing any spatial or predictive metamodel, it is crucial to evaluate whether the empirical surface roughness data exhibit non-Gaussian characteristics, as frequently observed in precision turning operations, and whether the observed deviations arise from intrinsic surface morphology rather than random measurement noise. These questions are addressed through an inferential analysis of replicated machining trials, focusing on the statistical consistency of higher-order roughness descriptors across trials. In idealized models of surface roughness, a Gaussian or normal distribution assumes symmetric amplitude distributions with zero skewness () and mesokurtic behavior characterized by a kurtosis of three (). However, in this study, surface profiles were measured using a stylus-type roughness tester, which provides summary statistics rather than raw amplitude height data. The roughness measures show consistent deviations from the Gaussian reference values–together with weak correlations between and the higher-order parameters, which provide compelling evidence that the surface features exhibit a non-Gaussian distribution.

Fig. 2a,b illustrates the distribution of surface samples in the – space for two independent machining trials under identical process conditions. In both trials, the data points are distributed away from the Gaussian reference coordinate , with no notable clustering in its vicinity. Instead, the observations occupy regions that systematically exclude the Gaussian reference, indicating asymmetric height distributions and platykurtic behavior. The reproducibility of this deviation across independent trials suggests that the observed non-Gaussian characteristics are not attributable to random measurement variability but rather reflect intrinsic properties of the machined surface morphology. This deviation is further supported by the statistical independence of and from other amplitude-based descriptors. As summarized in Fig. 2c, both and exhibit strong positive correlations with for both trials, while and remain weakly or negligibly correlated. These findings are consistent with those reported by Fecske et al.26, who noted that and serve as key descriptors of non-Gaussian surface shape, largely independent from amplitude parameters. This lack of collinearity affirms their distinct morphological role in surface characterization. Taken together, these results confirm that the observed non-Gaussian characteristics are both statistically significant and physically repeatable.

While the standard linear model (LM) accounts only for fixed effects, the linear mixed-effects model (LMM) incorporates both fixed effects–such as cutting speed (), feed rate (f), and depth of cut ()–and random effects due to trial-to-trial variability. To formally assess whether modeling this random variability improves model performance, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were computed for both LM and LMM formulations fitted to , , and , as shown in Fig. 3(d). Both AIC and BIC assess model performance by balancing goodness of fit with model complexity. Lower values indicate a better compromise between accurately capturing the data and avoiding overfitting, thereby suggesting a more efficient model. In every case, the LMM yielded substantially lower AIC and BIC values, reflecting improved model fit due to the inclusion of random effects. This supports the selection of LMM as the appropriate modeling strategy to account for trial-level uncertainty, thereby ensuring more reliable identification of significant process parameters in subsequent analyses. These results validate the adoption of trial-based modeling as the foundation for subsequent ANOVA and MANOVA, as it effectively accounts for trial-induced deviation and ensures more reliable estimation of fixed effects.

To validate the normality assumptions underlying the ANOVA and MANOVA, the distribution of residuals from the linear mixed-effects models (LMM) was examined using Q–Q plots (Supplementary material Fig. S2), as normality assessments should be conducted on model error rather than on raw observations80. The residuals of align closely with theoretical quantiles, while shows mild deviation despite remaining approximately symmetric and unimodal, consistent with the “Nearly Normal Condition” outlined by De Veaux et al.81. For , minor deviations from normality were observed, primarily due to a few influential points in the upper tail. However, given the robustness of ANOVA-type models to mild departures from normality and the balanced experimental design, these deviations are not considered critical. The dataset followed a full factorial design with three levels for each machining parameter and twelve replicates per combination, yielding a balanced structure with 27 combinations and 324 total observations. This balanced design enhances the robustness of ANOVA, while the MANOVA–performed on trial-wise sample means–also benefits from equal group sizes82, enabling reliable multivariate inference.

Spatial Characterization of non-Gaussian Surface

Following the inferential statistical analysis, spatial analysis was performed to examine how non-Gaussian roughness descriptors ( and ) delineate the spatial domain for subsequent interpolation modeling. Homogeneous G- and K-functions were first applied to evaluate the spatial organization and clustering of surface features, defining the analysis window under the assumption of stationarity. Inhomogeneous functions incorporating machining parameters were then used to assess process-induced trends, showing that part of the spatial heterogeneity followed a first-order deterministic pattern, while residual spatial dependence persisted. This residual correlation, representing second-order stochastic variability, was subsequently quantified through semivariogram modeling and incorporated into the kriging-based spatial metamodel.

Although and are response descriptors rather than physical spatial coordinates, the descriptor space constitutes a statistically structured feature domain rather than an arbitrary scatter plot. Admissible skewness–kurtosis combinations are bounded by the classical Pearson moment inequality (), which defines a mathematically feasible region for third- and fourth-order standardized moments. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, the experimental pairs lie within the statistically admissible region defined by the Pearson moment constraint. Moreover, Fig. 6b shows that these descriptor pairs align with the typical region of the turning-domain reported in surface metrology literature83, exhibiting a similar orientation and trend. This organized clustering reflects process-induced morphology constraints rather than random variation. Consequently, proximity within the – domain represents similarity in surface topography characteristics, allowing the descriptor space to be treated as a structured stochastic feature domain for spatial modeling.

To further quantify this spatial organization, homogeneous spatial point pattern analyses were carried out using G- and K-function diagnostics, as demonstrated in Fig. 8. Each column corresponds to a specific concavity level, where subplots (i)–(v) represent: (i) the smoothed residual field from the fitted homogeneous point process model, (ii) the empirical and simulated envelope of the G-function, (iii) the empirical and edge-corrected G-function, (iv) the empirical and simulated envelope of the K-function, and (v) the empirical and edge-corrected K-function. The smoothed residual maps (subplots 8(i)) evolve from diffuse intensity distributions at lower concavity levels (1–2) to sharper, more localized gradients at higher levels (3–4), indicating clearer spatial clustering of surface features.

The G-function simulation envelopes (subplots 8(ii)) further clarify this progression. For Concavity = 1, the empirical G-function (solid black line) remains mostly within the simulation envelope, suggesting no significant departure from complete spatial randomness (CSR). In Concavity = 2, small deviations appear near the upper boundary, indicating the onset of weak clustering. At Concavity = 3 and 4, however, the empirical G-function clearly rises above the envelope at shorter distances, reflecting significant local clustering behavior. These deviations indicate that point interactions become increasingly structured at higher concavity, consistent with a transition from random to clustered spatial organization. The edge-corrected G-functions (subplots 8(iii)) further reinforce this behavior, as increasing concavity leads to closer agreement between the edge-corrected and empirical curves while simultaneously increasing their deviation from the theoretical CSR line. This convergence indicates improved boundary correction and a more reliable characterization of short-range point interactions at higher concavity levels.

The K-function diagnostics (subplots 8(iv)–(v)) complement the G-function analysis by capturing clustering across broader spatial scales. For Concavity = 1, the empirical K-function lies close to or slightly below the theoretical CSR curve (red dashed line), indicating an approximately random spatial arrangement. As concavity increases, the empirical curves gradually rise above the simulation envelope, particularly for Concavity = 3 and 4, confirming the emergence of significant clustering at larger spatial lags. At lower concavity levels (1–2), the theoretical line lies marginally apart from the envelope, likely due to boundary asymmetry and mild non-stationarity in the observation window. However, as concavity increases, the theoretical and empirical curves converge toward the envelope, and the edge-corrected K-functions become more stable and consistent, suggesting reduced boundary effects and improved spatial coherence. Accordingly, Concavity = 4 was selected as the optimal spatial window, as it best represents both local and global clustering patterns within the non-Gaussian roughness domain. Although Concavity 4 encloses a larger spatial area, the presence of empty interior regions does not indicate insufficient sampling but instead reflects the intrinsic physics of turning-induced surface generation. Machined surfaces exhibit inherently non-Gaussian topographies, with empirical values concentrating predominantly in regions of positive skewness and platykurtic-to-mesokurtic kurtosis, leaving the Gaussian reference state sparsely populated or entirely absent.

To determine whether the observed spatial patterns were governed by machining parameters rather than random clustering, inhomogeneous Poisson point process models were developed using machining parameters as first-order intensity covariates, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Model performance was evaluated using 99 Monte Carlo simulations, which generated a 99% confidence envelope for the inhomogeneous G- and K-functions to assess the adequacy of the fitted model. The smoothed residual field (Fig. 9a) exhibits pronounced intensity gradients–red zones indicating underpredicted and blue zones indicating overpredicted regions–revealing structured spatial variability in the residual field. The inhomogeneous G-function (Fig. 9b) further demonstrates spatial dependence not fully captured by the deterministic trend, as the empirical curve extends beyond the simulation envelope at short distances, indicating localized clustering beyond the effects of machining parameters. Similarly, the inhomogeneous K-function (Fig. 9c) exhibits noticeable deviations at moderate spatial lags (r > 0.25), suggesting that the covariate-based model captures only part of the spatial variability. These results confirm that while machining parameters account for the first-order spatial heterogeneity, second-order spatial dependence persists, necessitating subsequent semivariogram analysis to quantify and integrate spatial autocorrelation within the kriging-based metamodeling framework.

Overall, these results demonstrate that increasing concavity enhances the detection of spatially coherent patterns within the roughness domain. The inhomogeneous Poisson analysis further revealed that while machining parameters effectively capture the first-order deterministic trend, a degree of second-order spatial dependence persists, indicating residual autocorrelation among the data points. The combined evidence from the homogeneous and inhomogeneous G- and K-function diagnostics thus confirms the presence of meaningful spatial structure within the – feature space. Consequently, the concavity level of 4 was selected as the statistically justified spatial domain that captures non-random clustering and machining-driven spatial trends, providing a robust foundation for SNGRM.

Spatial metamodeling to digital roughness mapping

Based on the preceding spatial characterization of non-Gaussian profile, spatial correlation analysis, variogram modeling and best-fit selection, and kriging-based interpolation using ordinary and universal kriging were conducted, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These steps enabled digital roughness mapping of within the – domain, accounting both local spatial dependence and machining-driven first-order trends, while quantifying prediction uncertainty through variance estimation.

The spatial correlation structure of surface roughness was analyzed through semivariogram modeling, as presented in Fig. 10, with complementary diagnostic plots (variogram clouds, binned semivariograms, and correlograms) provided in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S3). Although prior statistical analyses identified feed rate as the dominant machining parameter governing surface roughness variability, the first-order trend incorporated in the external drift component was constructed using all three machining parameters (, , and f) within the universal kriging framework. Preliminary analysis showed that a feed-rate-only trend yields semivariogram structures nearly identical to those obtained using all parameters (Fig. S4), with diagnostic plots indicating that f captures the dominant contribution to spatial variability. Nevertheless, the multivariate trend was retained to preserve physical interpretability and to explicitly reflect the known effects of cutting speed and depth of cut on surface roughness reported in the machining literature. Both ordinary and universal kriging models exhibit pronounced spatial dependence, with semivariance increasing with lag distance before reaching a sill near h = 0.40. The sill value for the OK semivariogram is higher (≈ 0.11) than that of the UK semivariogram (≈ 0.045), reflecting the use of constant-mean and external-drift residuals, respectively. This reduction indicates that incorporating machining-parameter-driven trends separates deterministic effects from residual spatial variability, thereby reducing unexplained variability and promoting a more stable spatial structure. In addition, the UK semivariogram exhibits a smaller nugget effect, suggesting that part of the short-range variability treated as random noise in the OK model is instead explained by process variables. This enhanced spatial coherence confirms that incorporating physically meaningful process covariates enables the model to capture both deterministic and stochastic components of surface variation, establishing UK as a more reliable framework for predictive surface roughness modeling.

Collectively, these results confirm that the universal kriging model more effectively captures both short- and medium-range spatial variability through the inclusion of physically meaningful process covariates.

To ensure unbiased evaluation and prevent data leakage, a group-wise 5-fold CV was adopted to compare the predictive performance of the OK and UK models, as illustrated in Fig. 12. The systematic evolution of the UK-based roughness fields across increasing feed rates (Fig. 13b(i–iii)) reflects the underlying kinematic mechanism of surface generation in turning. The dominant influence of the feed rate on spatial roughness evolution is physically rooted in the kinematic generation of tool marks and asperity spacing. Feed directly dictates the peak-to-peak distance and the theoretical height of the surface profile; as it increases, geometrically induced roughness amplitudes rise, creating more pronounced periodic features and higher values85. These higher feed rates amplify ridge formation, shifting the height distribution toward more positive skewness and reduced kurtosis, as observed across various cooling conditions86. This morphological transition–from plateau-like surfaces at low feed to peak-dominated textures at high feed–fundamentally reconfigures the surface organization in the – domain. Such restructuring ensures that the spatial clustering and intensity patterns captured in the Kriging-based maps reflect the convergence of similar magnitudes within distinct regions of the – domain. This interpretation is further supported by tribological studies, which associate increased skewness and reduced kurtosis with higher wear volumes and frictional responses, whereas lower feed rates promote superior contact and lubrication conditions87,88. Consequently, the statistically dominant role of the feed rate identified in this spatial framework is consistent with the underlying mechanics of tool–workpiece interaction and asperity formation in turning.

In addition to kriging performed directly on raw , a log-transformed was examined to assess whether improved approximate normality of the response variable would enhance kriging performance, given the positive skewness of the empirical roughness data (Supplementary Material Fig. S7). For kriging applied directly to raw , negative predictions were observed only in regions distant from measurement locations, while predictions within data-supported regions remained physically meaningful; such values were therefore truncated to zero89. Log-transformation ensures non-negative interpolation after back-transformation, remaining within the physically admissible range of the experimental observations. However, the application of log-transformation introduces extreme sensitivity of errors associated with back-transformation90, particularly in the interpretation of prediction uncertainty. When a direct exponential back-transformation was applied, the resulting variance maps exhibited spatial patterns consistent with classical kriging behavior, including reduced uncertainty in regions of high data density (Supplementary Material Fig. S9). In contrast, the bias-corrected lognormal back-transformation yielded variance fields that increased in regions of elevated predicted (Supplementary Material Fig. S10), reflecting the proportional effect whereby uncertainty scales with the local mean91. Although analytically correct, this behavior leads to variance patterns that are less intuitive for engineering interpretation. The group-wise CV results (Supplementary Material Table S2) further indicated that kriging applied directly to raw –especially under universal kriging–provided superior predictive accuracy in terms of MAE and RMSE. Accordingly, raw-data-based kriging is adopted as the primary modeling framework, while the log-transformed analysis is retained as a complementary robustness and sensitivity assessment, with detailed results presented in the Supplementary Material.

Conclusion and future scope

This study introduced a data-driven spatial metamodeling framework (SNGRM) for digital roughness mapping of non-Gaussian surfaces based directly on empirical measurements. Unlike conventional mean-based analyses that neglect measurement noise, the proposed approach incorporates both machining-induced uncertainty and measurement error as second-order spatial variability, integrating deterministic process trends with stochastic roughness behavior through universal kriging. In summary, this SNGRM integrates statistical inference, spatial diagnostics, and geostatistical interpolation to enable a unified understanding of surface generation mechanisms. The proposed SNGRM framework advances data-driven surface engineering by providing digital roughness maps with quantified uncertainty, laying the groundwork for precision machining and process optimization. The framework remains computationally viable for experimental datasets typical of precision machining research while offering structured spatial covariance modeling and explicit uncertainty quantification beyond conventional scalar prediction approaches. By explicitly linking system-level machining information (M0) with analytical modeling layers (M1) and metamodel-driven spatial inference (M2), SNGRM provides a coherent methodological pathway for integrating non-Gaussian surface characterization, spatial statistics, and kriging-based uncertainty quantification.

In the present study, the spatial window was identified through iterative assessment of homogeneous G- and K-function diagnostics. Future research will focus on the development of a more rigorous uncertainty quantification framework that integrates theoretical and knowledge-based modeling within the kriging process. Although kriging inherently accounts for measurement error and spatial variability through the semivariogram, incorporating a formal uncertainty propagation scheme would enhance reliability. The quantified uncertainty could also be leveraged to define an adaptive spatial window for improved metamodeling. Moreover, extending the SNGRM framework with sequential or adaptive metamodeling strategies may improve its scalability and establish a compatible workflow for spatial characterization and digital mapping of non-Gaussian surface roughness.
