Impact of School-Based Periodontal Health Education Programs on Gingival Health in Adolescents: A Cluster-Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Gaurav Gupta, Sonali Mallick, Abhishek Awasthi, Varsha Sharma, Sarvesh Bansal, Suman Kriti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to periodontal diseases due to inadequate oral hygiene practices and hormonal changes during puberty. School-based oral health education programs have emerged as a promising strategy for improving periodontal health by fostering awareness and encouraging positive behavior change.Materials and Methods:A cluster-randomized controlled trial was conducted among 480 adolescents aged 13–15 years from eight government schools in an urban district. The schools were randomly assigned into intervention (n = 240) and control (n = 240) groups. The intervention group received a bimonthly, interactive periodontal health education program over six months, including audiovisual modules, toothbrushing demonstrations, and peer-led activities. The control group received standard oral health pamphlets without active instruction. Gingival health was assessed using the gingival index (GI) and plaque index (PI) at baseline and after six months by calibrated examiners blinded to the allocation.Results:At baseline, there was no significant difference between the groups in mean GI and PI scores (P > 0.05). After six months, the intervention group showed a significant reduction in mean GI score from 1.89 ± 0.42 to 0.91 ± 0.27 (P < 0.001) and in PI score from 2.04 ± 0.35 to 1.03 ± 0.26 (P < 0.001). In contrast, the control group showed only minor improvements in GI (1.87 ± 0.40 to 1.72 ± 0.39; P = 0.09) and PI scores (2.01 ± 0.38 to 1.93 ± 0.34; P = 0.11). The between-group differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001).Conclusion:School-based periodontal health education programs significantly improve gingival and plaque scores in adolescents.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal diseases are among the most prevalent oral health problems affecting adolescents globally, with gingivitis being the most common early manifestation. These conditions are primarily driven by the accumulation of dental plaque and are exacerbated by inadequate oral hygiene practices and hormonal changes during puberty that increase gingival inflammation.[1] Adolescents often exhibit poor compliance with oral hygiene routines, making them a high-risk group for developing periodontal problems if preventive strategies are not implemented early.

School-based oral health programs provide a strategic platform for delivering preventive education, as they can effectively target large adolescent populations in a structured environment. Such programs have been shown to significantly influence oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors, leading to improved clinical outcomes.[23] Moreover, integrating interactive and peer-led models into school curricula enhances engagement and retention of oral hygiene practices among students.[4] These approaches not only empower adolescents but also foster a culture of oral health awareness that can extend to families and communities.

Despite existing evidence supporting the benefits of school-based oral health education, there is a scarcity of studies evaluating their specific impact on gingival health through rigorous study designs like cluster-randomized controlled trials. Given the increasing burden of periodontal conditions in youth and the cost-effectiveness of preventive care in school settings, it is essential to assess the real-world effectiveness of structured health education models on gingival and plaque indices.[5]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of eight government secondary schools were selected using stratified random sampling to ensure socioeconomic and geographic representation. The schools were randomly allocated into two groups: intervention (n = 4 schools) and control (n = 4 schools), using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Each school served as a single cluster to minimize cross-contamination.

The study included 480 adolescents aged 13 to 15 years enrolled in the selected schools. Inclusion criteria were: healthy school-going children, written parental consent, and willingness to participate. Students with a history of systemic illness, ongoing orthodontic treatment, or periodontal therapy within the last six months were excluded.

Students in the intervention group received a structured, school-based periodontal health education program over six months. The program included bimonthly interactive sessions covering topics, such as plaque control, proper brushing techniques, diet and oral hygiene, and the importance of routine dental visits. Teaching strategies included audiovisual presentations, model demonstrations, role-playing, and peer-led discussions. Toothbrushing demonstrations were conducted using standardized models and supervised practice was encouraged.

Participants in the control group were provided with routine oral health pamphlets containing general information about dental care, but no active instruction or follow-up was given during the study period.

Gingival health was assessed using two clinical indices: the gingival index (GI) by Löe and Silness and the PI by Silness and Löe. Examinations were carried out at baseline and after six months by trained and calibrated examiners blinded to group assignment. Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Gingival Index (GI)

At baseline, the mean GI score for the intervention group was 1.89 ± 0.42, and for the control group, it was 1.87 ± 0.40 (P = 0.63), indicating no significant difference. After six months, the intervention group showed a significant reduction in mean GI to 0.91 ± 0.27 (P < 0.001), whereas the control group had a marginal reduction to 1.72 ± 0.39 (P = 0.09). The difference between groups at follow-up was statistically significant (P < 0.001) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that a structured, school-based periodontal health education program significantly improved gingival and plaque scores among adolescents over a six-month period. The findings support the effectiveness of periodic, interactive oral health education in school environments, especially when delivered through a combination of visual, verbal, and participatory teaching methods.

The significant improvement observed in the GI and plaque index (PI) scores in the intervention group aligns with earlier studies that emphasized the positive impact of oral health education on periodontal outcomes in school-aged children.[12] Adolescents are at a critical developmental stage where behavior modification, including the adoption of proper oral hygiene practices, can be effectively implemented. Educating them during this phase not only addresses immediate health concerns but may also foster lifelong habits that prevent periodontal disease progression.[3]

The program’s bimonthly format, which included audiovisual materials, brushing demonstrations, and peer involvement, was likely a key factor in maintaining engagement and retention. Similar interactive approaches have been shown to be more effective than passive strategies, such as leaflet distribution alone.[4] The minor improvements seen in the control group, which received only pamphlets, highlight the limited impact of passive education and the need for more dynamic interventions.

Furthermore, the significant difference in outcomes between the two groups corroborates findings from cluster-randomized trials in other settings, where structured health education led to measurable improvements in plaque control and gingival health.[5] The use of peer-led discussions may have also contributed to the intervention group’s success, as peer influence is a known driver of behavioral change among adolescents.[67]

CONCLUSION

School-based periodontal health education significantly improves gingival and plaque scores among adolescents. Incorporating structured, interactive programs into school curricula can effectively promote long-term oral hygiene habits.
