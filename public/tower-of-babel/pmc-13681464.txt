Reverse Dorr Classification of Lateral Knee Radiographs: A Qualitative Assessment of Bone Quality in Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Matthew A Beilfuss, Micah MacAskill, Kyle R Wagner, Diane C Krueger, Sam Mosiman, Brian T Nickel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Dorr classification is the standard radiographic method for assessing proximal femur bone quality and guiding implant selection in total hip arthroplasty (THA); however, a similar method has not been established for total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Previous studies have demonstrated that radiographic parameters of the distal femur, specifically the ratio of the inner vs outer cortical diameter on lateral knee radiographs, are associated with bone mineral density. The aim of this study was to develop an efficient, reproducible, and qualitative classification system of the distal femur that correlates with bone mineral density to guide implant selection in TKA.MethodsA retrospective review of 200 TKA patients with preoperative dual-energy radiograph absorptiometry (DXA) within 1 year of TKA at a single institution was performed. Preoperative lateral knee radiographs were independently reviewed and the degree of anterior cortical taper of the distal femur between the trochlea and a point one patella height (Reverse Dorr 1 [RD1]) or two patella heights (RD2) proximal to the trochlea was graded as Reverse Dorr A, B, or C.ResultsThe Reverse Dorr classification system was associated with DXA T-score, intraoperative physician assessment, and demonstrated moderate to strong interobserver agreement. Reverse Dorr A femurs had a 59-70% predicted probability of normal bone, whereas Reverse Dorr C femurs had a 66-76% predicted probability of osteoporosis.ConclusionsThe Reverse Dorr classification system is an efficient and reproducible assessment of preoperative bone quality and may serve to inform implant selection in TKA.Keywords: Reverse dorr classification, Bone health, Cementless TKA, Cemented TKA

Introduction

The annual incidence of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) in the United States continues to rise as the population ages [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. With high demand and the average comorbidity burden of TKA patients increasing, it is imperative for arthroplasty surgeons to tailor implant selection to each patient to ensure long-term survivorship and reduce complications [5]. Cementless total hip arthroplasty (THA) has long been the fixation method of choice in the United States and remains the predominate mode of fixation in primary THA, annually reported as 95% by the American Joint Replacement Registry. On the contrary, nearly 80% of primary TKA in the United States use cemented components; however, cementless TKA continues to gain traction with nearly a 10-fold increase in the last decade [1]. Advantages of cementless fixation include biologic ingrowth, decreased operating time and blood loss, potential improved patient-reported outcome measures, and comparable, if not improved, long-term survivorship in younger patients with good bone quality than cemented TKA [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Cementless TKA also confers advantages in survivorship and infection rates in patients with a body mass index over 35 [12,13]. Despite these benefits, cementless TKA is associated with early tibial aseptic loosening and periprosthetic fracture, particularly in women over the age of 65 with poor bone quality [9,11,14]. Moreover, low preoperative bone mineral density (BMD) has been postulated to correlate with early fixation failure and periprosthetic fracture in cementless TKA [[14], [15], [16]]. Although much of the literature on cemented vs cementless fixation is mixed, there does appear to be a link between bone quality and fixation method with subsequent outcomes. Therefore, an accurate assessment of a patient's preoperative bone health may guide implant selection in TKA.

The Dorr classification of the hip is the gold standard for radiographically assessing proximal femur bone quality and guides fixation method in THA, with cementless fixation appropriate for Dorr A femurs, surgeon choice for Dorr B femurs, and cemented fixation for Dorr C femurs [17]. Similarly, quantitative assessments of distal femur bone quality have been developed. He et al [18] demonstrated that the cortical bone thickness of the distal femur correlates with BMD. Carlson et al [19] built upon this finding and demonstrated that the ratio of the cortical bone thickness at a point 1 patella height proximal to the trochlea divided by the cortical bone thickness at the level of the trochlea on lateral knee radiographs was highly correlated with local BMD. This ratio, termed the “Reverse Dorr (RD)” model, was shown to have high interobserver reliability and was a strong predictor of osteopenia in primary TKA. Although these quantitative indices are important for establishing the relationship between distal femur cortical thickness and BMD, it is time consuming for surgeons to calculate; thus, the clinical application of a quantitative system is limited.

Therefore, we aimed to develop an efficient, reproducible, and qualitative “RD” classification system of the distal femur that correlates with BMD to guide implant selection in TKA. We hypothesized the qualitative taper of the anterior cortical bone of the distal femur at a point 1 (RD1) or two patella heights (RD2) proximal to the trochlea compared to the level of the trochlea would be predictive of preoperative bone quality as defined by dual-energy radiograph absorptiometry (DXA) measured T-scores. Furthermore, a patient classified as RD A would be predictive of normal BMD, RD B would be predictive of osteopenia, and RD C would be predictive of osteoporosis.

Material and methods

Study participants

This study was conducted using retrospective data from 200 TKA patients at a single institution between July 2019 and August 2025. The sample included patients who participated in TKA related research studies or who were referred to the institution's Bone Health Optimization program to promote optimal bone health prior to TKA. This project was determined exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) review with all utilized research studies approved by the institutional IRB.

All eligible participants for this study had preoperative DXA scans and lateral knee radiographs within 1 year of their surgical date. Based on BMD T-scores, subjects were classified into one of four groups: normal, osteopenia, clinical osteoporosis, or osteoporosis. Subjects were classified using lowest BMD T-score from the lumbar spine, total hip, femoral neck, or the 1/3 radius. BMD diagnostic groups were defined as follows: normal = T-score ≥ −1.0 without clinical fracture, osteopenia = T-score −1.0 to −2.5 without clinical fracture, clinical osteoporosis = T-score −1.0 to −2.5 with low trauma fracture or Fracture Risk Assessment Tool risk ≥20% of major osteoporotic fracture or ≥3% risk of hip fracture, and osteoporosis = T-score ≤ −2.5 (Table 1). There were fifty patients from each group. Women represented 143 of the participants (71.5%) with most participants being White (96.5%). The mean age at time of surgery was 69.7 years old. The median age of participants in the normal diagnostic group was 69 (range, 54 to 81), osteopenia group was 67 (range, 56 to 86), and osteoporosis group was 72 (range, 52 to 87). The mean number of days between DXA and preoperative lateral knee radiograph was 58.5 days (range, 25.5 to 115) (Table 2).

Radiographic assessment

Three independent reviewers (a board-certified, fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon, arthroplasty fellow, and orthopedic surgery resident) qualitatively assessed the anterior cortical taper on preoperative lateral knee radiographs between a point 1 patella height (RD1) or two patella heights (RD2) proximal to the trochlea of the 200 study participants. The lateral knee radiograph chosen for review was the closest preoperative knee radiograph to the DXA date. A qualitative score of RD A, B, or C was given to each radiograph (Table 3). A RD A femur was defined as a distinct anterior cortical taper with at least double the cortical thickness at a point one or two patella heights proximal to the trochlea compared to the level of the trochlea. A RD B femur was defined as a slight taper that was less than double the cortical thickness at a point one or two patella heights proximal to the trochlea compared to the level of the trochlea. Finally, a RD C femur was defined as no anterior cortical taper between a point one or two patella heights proximal to the trochlea compared to the level of the trochlea (Figs. 1 and 2). All reviewers were blinded to a patient's BMD group.

Data analysis

Weighted and unweighted Fleiss Kappa scores were calculated to assess reviewer agreement among RD scores at 1 (RD1) and two patella heights (RD2) proximal to the trochlea. RD scores were then combined based on the most common score (ie, if at least two out of the three reviewers graded a femur as RD A, that patient was scored as RD A). In instances where there were no common scores, that patient was removed from the bone health analysis. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the relationship between RD Classification scores and age, lowest BMD T-score, total hip T-score, and intraoperative physician bone assessment (IPA) [20]. Weighted Kappa coefficients were also calculated to assess agreement between common RD scores and BMD diagnostic group (normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis). Finally, predicted probability models were used to assess the likelihood of an RD A femur predicting normal bone, RD B femur predicting osteopenia, and RD C femur predicting clinical or T-score osteoporosis. All statistical analyses were completed using SAS (SAS Institute Inc.) and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Reverse dorr classification agreement among reviewers

We found a moderate to strong level of agreement among reviewers at both one (RD1 weighted κ = 0.4) and two patella heights proximal to the trochlea (RD2 weighted κ = 0.7). In general, we found the highest level of agreement among RD A and C femurs in RD2 (RD A unweighted κ = 0.7 and RD C unweighted κ = 0.6) compared to RD1 (RD A unweighted κ = 0.4 and RD C unweighted κ = 0.3, respectively). The lowest level of agreement was found to be with RD B femurs (RD1 unweighted κ = 0.1 and RD2 unweighted κ = 0.5) (Table 4). When combining RD scores, there were 8 patients in the RD1 group with no common score (ie, the patient was scored as RD A, B, and C by the graders) and 0 patients without a common score in the RD2 group.

Reverse dorr classification is correlated with bone mineral density T-score and IPA

We found a fair to moderate correlation between RD classification and lowest BMD T-score in both RD1 and RD2. In general, we found RD2 to be more strongly correlated to BMD T-score and IPA than RD1, and the strongest correlation was with lowest overall T-score (rs = −0.494, P-value <.0001) (Table 5). Importantly, we did not find a correlation between assigned RD score and age (rs = 0.13, P value <.0761) (Table 6).

Reverse dorr classification and agreement with normal bone, osteopenia, and osteoporosis

We found a fair to moderate level of agreement between RD score (A, B, or C) and diagnostic group (normal bone, osteopenia, and osteoporosis) in both RD1 (weighted κ = 0.3 ± 0.06) and RD2 groups (weighted κ = 0.3 ± 0.05). In RD1, only 3.1% of patients with clinical or T-score osteoporosis were classified as RD A and 19.5% of patients with normal bone were classified as RD C. In RD2, 9% of patients with clinical or T-score osteoporosis were classified as RD A and only 2% of patients with normal bone were classified as RD C. The predicted probability that a RD A femur represented normal bone was 70% in RD1 and 59% in RD2, whereas the probability that a RD B femur represented osteopenia was 31% in RD1 and 29% in RD2. Finally, the probably a RD C femur represented osteoporosis was 66% in RD1 and 76% in RD2 (Table 7 and 8).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to establish an efficient and reproducible qualitative assessment of bone quality of the distal femur to guide implant selection in TKA. Building upon the work of Carlson et al who demonstrated a relationship between quantitative cortical indices of the distal femur to BMD, we created the Reverse Dorr (RD) classification system of the distal femur by qualitatively analyzing the degree of anterior cortical taper on lateral knee radiographs. We found that grading the anterior cortical taper of the distal femur at a point one and two patella heights proximal to the trochlea compared to the level of the trochlea was moderately correlated to BMD metrics, and using a point of reference of two patella heights proximal to the trochlea was found to be more strongly correlated to lowest T-score, total hip T-score, and IPA. Additionally, the RD grading scale was efficient and reproducible with moderate to high agreement between graders independent of level of training, particularly for RD A and RD C femurs. Interestingly, we found no correlation between RD score and age, which suggests that the RD classification system better correlates with BMD and IPA independent of age. The predicted probability of an RD A femur representing normal bone was 59-70%, RD B representing osteopenia was 29-31%, and RD C representing osteoporosis was 66-76%. Most importantly from a clinical standpoint, it was unlikely for a patient with osteoporosis to be graded as RD A and a patient with normal bone to be graded as RD C in RD2.

Patient selection for cemented vs cementless fixation in TKA remains controversial. Many factors influence the decision to use cemented vs cementless implants, including patient demographics, medical comorbidities, previous BMD T-scores, and IPA. These factors alone, however, are flawed. Not all patients have preoperative DXA. Furthermore, it can be challenging to pivot intraoperatively from a preoperative planned mode of fixation based upon a surgeon's intraoperative assessment of bone quality. Opening new bone preparation instruments or cement equipment may prolong operative time, thereby increasing the risk of adverse events such as wound dehiscence or surgical site infection [21]. A patient's preoperative bone quality is an important factor influencing the survivorship of TKA; however, there is no agreed upon qualitative radiographic assessment of bone quality of the knee to guide TKA implant selection. A lateral knee radiograph is universally obtained for all preoperative TKA patients and serves as an efficient and cost-effective data point to assess a patient's preoperative bone quality.

Several quantitative radiographic assessments of the distal femur currently exist. The Citak classification system classifies patients' distal femur anatomy as type A, B, or C depending on the 25th and 75th percentiles of the inner diameter of the femur at 20 cm proximal to the joint line for male and female patients on anteroposterior (AP) knee radiographs. Additionally, Citak et al developed a novel distal femur index based on the ratio of the inner diameter of the femoral canal at 20 cm proximal to the knee joint to the inner diameter of the medullary canal at 2 cm proximal to the adductor tubercle on AP knee radiographs. Although this is a reliable way to define the radiographic anatomy of the distal femur, no correlations were made to BMD [22]. Carlson et al [19] defined the RD ratio of cortical indices on distal femur lateral knee radiographs, which was strongly predictive of local bone density in the knee; however, the clinical application of a quantitative assessment of the distal femur is limited. The addition of our simple, efficient, and reproducible RD qualitative radiographic classification system of the distal femur allows the surgeon to quickly assess a patient's bone health, which may inform implant selection. In this study, assessing the anterior cortical taper of the distal femur at two patella heights proximal to the trochlea (RD2) had better interrater reliability as well as correlation to BMD and IPA. In RD2, it was rare for a patient with osteoporosis to be graded as RD A and a patient with normal bone to be graded as RD C. Therefore, a patient with an RD A femur would be more appropriate for cementless fixation, whereas a patient with an RD C femur would be appropriate for cemented fixation. We feel this score can guide surgical planning and operating room setup, similar to the Dorr classification of the hip. At our institution, we plan for cementless implants for RD A femurs, cementless implants with cement available in the room for RD B femurs, and cemented implants for RD C femurs.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of this study results in some variation in the timing of preoperative DXA and lateral knee radiograph. Because of this, there may be minor differences in BMD between the DXA date and the date of the lateral knee radiograph. Additionally, local disuse osteopenia caused by a painful arthritic joint may result in differences in local BMD that would impact RD classification but may not be captured by global BMD T-scores. Second, the RD classification is qualitative by nature. Therefore, a patient may be qualitatively graded as having good bone quality (RD A) when their bone quality is poor based on objective intraoperative assessment. This could result in an intraoperative change in planned fixation method, thereby increasing operative time, or improper fixation, thereby increasing complication risks. Additionally, the qualitative grading of the anterior cortical taper on lateral knee radiographs is dependent on the quality of the lateral knee radiograph. Differences in exposure, rotation, abduction, and adduction affect the cortical overlap, which may influence the anterior cortical taper. However, this is relatable to clinical practice as there is some variation in quality of preoperative knee radiographs. For the most part, radiographs included in this study were of high quality. Finally, a patient's DXA reported BMD assessment obtained at the lumbar spine and hip may not accurately reflect local BMD at the knee [23]. Intraoperative physician assessment of local bone quality will remain the gold standard to guide implant selection in TKA; however, the RD classification may be a useful adjunct in preoperative planning [20].

Conclusions

The RD classification is an efficient and reproducible qualitative radiographic assessment of the distal femur that is associated with preoperative bone quality. Considered with other patient factors such as age, sex, medical comorbidities, and intraoperative physician assessment, the RD classification serves as an additional data point to guide implant selection in TKA. This is vitally important to increase survivorship and decrease complications and healthcare costs with the current uptrend in cementless TKA in the United States. Future studies are necessary to evaluate how the RD classification affects current clinical practice with regards to cementless vs cemented fixation, both in a retrospective and prospective manner.
