Parallel Layer Normalization for Universal Approximation
Yunhao Ni 1 Yuxin Guo 1 Yuhe Liu 1 Wenxin Sun 1 Jie Luo 1 Wenjun Wu 1,2 Lei Huang 1,2 1 State Key Laboratory of Complex and Critical Software Environment (SKLCCSE), Beihang University, Beijing, China 2 Hangzhou International Innovation Institute, Beihang University, Hangzhou, China

Abstract
Layer Normalization (LN) is usually studied for its role in optimization, but it also acts as a multivariate nonlinearity between affine layers. We investigate how grouping LN units affects the approximation power of neural networks as width grows. In Parallel Layer Normalization networks (PLN-Nets), fixed-dimensional LN units are applied independently to groups between two affine layers. We prove that a shallow LN-Net with one LN unit is non-universal regardless of width, whereas PLN-Nets become universal as the number of parallel groups grows. The mechanism extends to deep networks, Root Mean Square Normalization (RMSNorm) variants, position-wise feed-forward networks, and convolutional networks under suitable grouping structures. Controlled MLP and physics-informed neural network (PINN) experiments support PLN’s practical viability, while CNN and ViT experiments show that group count shapes the finite-sample fitting envelope but does not act alone: the grouped axes, replacement site, and label structure lead to distinct fitting and generalization behavior.



1
Introduction

Normalization has become a fundamental component of modern neural networks.
Batch Normalization (BN;
Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015
) was originally introduced to
stabilize and accelerate the training of deep networks, while Layer
Normalization (LN;
Ba et al., 2016
) was developed to avoid dependence on
batch statistics. LN and its variants are now standard components of
Transformers
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
, and group-based normalization operators
are widely used in convolutional and structured architectures

(
Wu & He, 2018
)
. Most existing studies explain normalization through its
effects on optimization, including scale invariance, conditioning, and training
stability. Its contribution to the representation capacity of neural networks,
however, remains substantially less understood.

A recent study
(
Ni et al., 2024
)
initiated this representation
perspective by treating LN as a multivariate nonlinearity. It established
finite-sample classification results for deep LN-Nets and showed that grouping
can amplify LN’s nonlinearity. Finite-sample fitting and stronger nonlinearity,
however, do not establish universal approximation. It remains unclear whether
increasing the number of groups, rather than merely widening one LN unit, can
enable LN-Nets to approximate arbitrary continuous functions.

We answer this question first in multilayer perceptrons
(MLPs;
Cybenko, 1989
;
Hornik, 1991
). We define
Parallel Layer Normalization (PLN; Definition
3.2
) as a direct sum of
fixed-dimensional LN units placed between affine layers. A shallow network
with one LN unit is non-universal regardless of its width, whereas shallow
PLN-Nets become universal as the number of groups grows
(Theorems
4.1
–
4.3
). The same
construction yields deep-network approximation results with explicit bounds.
An exact function-class equivalence with Parallel Layer Scaling (PLS) extends
these results to Root Mean Square Normalization (RMSNorm)-based networks

(
Zhang & Sennrich, 2019
)
.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs;
LeCun et al., 2015
) and
Transformers
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
are more complex than MLPs, yet retain
the affine–nonlinear building blocks of MLPs.
We prove exact representation results for convolutional networks and
position-wise feed-forward networks: in both cases, affine layers share
parameters across positions while PLN acts independently on feature or channel
coordinates. Token and spatial dimensions also admit grouping domains that
span multiple positions, beyond the position-wise PLN structure covered by our
theory. We therefore investigate these other grouping schemes empirically.

We conduct regression experiments on MLPs to empirically demonstrate the
approximation capability of finite PLN-Nets, consistent with our
universal-approximation result. We also use PINN experiments to explore their
potential for physics-informed learning. We further investigate grouped normalization under different grouping schemes in
CNNs and Transformers, finding that its performance depends on the grouping
scheme and data structure. The detailed experimental settings and results are
presented in Section
6
.

2
Related Work

2.1
Universal Approximation and Neural Network Expressivity

The universal approximation property of neural networks has been studied for
more than three decades. Early results established that single-hidden-layer
networks with sigmoidal activations are universal approximators

(
Cybenko, 1989
)
, and were subsequently generalized to broad
classes of bounded nonconstant activations
(
Hornik, 1991
)
.
Later work characterized approximation rates
(
Barron, 1993
)
and
examined architectural constraints including arbitrary depth

(
Gripenberg, 2003
)
, bounded depth and width

(
Maiorov & Pinkus, 1999
)
, and minimal width

(
Park et al., 2020
;
Shin et al., 2025
)
. Expressivity
has also been studied through linear regions
(
Montufar et al., 2014
)
and VC
dimension
(
Bartlett et al., 2019
)
. Universal approximation analyses have
further been extended from MLPs to CNNs

(
Oono & Suzuki, 2023
)
, RNNs

(
Li et al., 2022
)
, and Transformers

(
Yun et al., 2020
)
.

Many constructive UAT proofs exploit properties specific to the activation.
For example, the piecewise-linear structure of ReLU permits localized
constructions and explicit approximation rates

(
Yarotsky, 2017
;
Yun et al., 2020
)
,
whereas smooth activations such as tanh require different error-control
arguments
(
De Ryck et al., 2021
)
. Normalization instead introduces a
coupled, multivariate nonlinear transformation
(
Ni et al., 2024
)
.
Consequently, most approximation analyses of structured architectures omit
normalization for analytical tractability. Our results treat normalization
itself as the intermediate nonlinear operator.

2.2
Theoretical Understanding of Layer and Group Normalization

Normalization has primarily been analyzed through optimization. Existing
theory attributes its effectiveness to scale invariance and stabilized
training dynamics
(
Arora et al., 2019
;
Ba et al., 2016
;
Huang et al., 2023
)
, as well
as improved conditioning of the optimization problem

(
Cai et al., 2019
;
Daneshmand et al., 2020
;
Ghorbani et al., 2019
;
Karakida et al., 2019
;
Lyu et al., 2022
;
Santurkar et al., 2018
)
.
These analyses explain important training benefits, but the role of
normalization in representation and function approximation remains less
understood.

From a representation perspective, prior work identified the intrinsic
multivariate nonlinearity of LN and showed that LN-Nets with three
neurons per layer and
O(m)
LN layers can shatter any set of
m
labeled
samples
(
Ni et al., 2024
)
. That result obtains classification capacity through depth. In
contrast, we study function approximation through width: we ask how arranging
multiple normalization units in parallel changes the representation class and
when normalization itself can provide universal approximation.

Grouping introduces an additional structural choice. Standard Group
Normalization (GN;
Wu & He, 2018
) partitions image channels and computes
each group’s statistics jointly over spatial positions. Other designs
alter the normalization domain: PONO normalizes channels independently at each
spatial position
(
Li et al., 2019
)
, while LN-G-Position partitions those
channels further
(
Ni et al., 2024
)
. Related position-wise grouped
RMS-style normalizers also appear in RetNet
(
Sun et al., 2023
)
and Mamba-2

(
Dao & Gu, 2024
)
, whereas temporal GN variants may pool statistics
across sequence positions
(
Baevski et al., 2020
;
Chi et al., 2023
)
.
Despite their similar names, these operators lack a unified naming convention and may perform substantially different computations.

We formalize PLN as a
direct sum of fixed-dimensional LN units whose number grows with width, derive
its representation consequences, and extend the construction to sequence and
image tensors.

3
Preliminaries and Parallel Layer Normalization

We first introduce the network notation used in the approximation results,
then define RMS normalization, LN, and their parallel forms, and finally
establish two structural properties of the resulting networks. We use lowercase letters for
scalars, boldface lowercase letters for vectors, and boldface uppercase letters
for matrices. The notation
[\rvx]_{i}
denotes the
i
-th coordinate of a vector.

3.1
Neural Networks

Affine layers.

An affine layer
\varphi:\sR^{d}\to\sR^{m}
is of the form

\varphi(\rvx)=\mW\rvx+\vb

\mW\in\sR^{m\times d}
and

Networks with intermediate operators.

We consider feed-forward networks obtained by composing affine layers with a
class of intermediate operators
\phi

Definition 3.1

(
\phi
-networks)
.

A
\phi
-network (or
\phi
-Net) of depth

f=\varphi_{L+1}\circ\phi_{L}\circ\varphi_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}\circ\varphi_{1},

(1)

where the

\phi_{\ell}:\sR^{d_{\ell}}\to\sR^{d_{\ell}}

Remark 3.1
.

If
\phi:\sR\to\sR
is an element-wise activation and

\rvx=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\top}\in\sR^{d}
, we write its coordinate-wise action as

\phi(\rvx):=(\phi(x_{1}),\ldots,\phi(x_{d}))^{\top}
. In contrast, a normalization
operator maps several coordinates jointly. Definition

applies
such fixed-dimensional normalization maps independently to multiple blocks.

Depth and Width.

The depth of
\Eqn
1

L
and its width is

\max\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{L}\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}(\phi;L,N)
the class of

\phi
-networks of depth
L
and width at most
N
. The case
L=1
is called
shallow. For simplicity, throughout the paper, function classes compared by
inclusion or equality are taken to have the same input and output spaces.

PLN partitions the feature coordinates into groups and applies LN to all groups

Definition 3.2

(Parallel Layer Normalization)
.

Let

\rvh=\rvh_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\rvh_{N}\in\sR^{d}

3.3
Structural Properties of PLN Networks

Following Definition
3.1
, networks using
\mathrm{LN}[n_{s}]
and

\mathrm{PLN}[n_{s}]
as intermediate operators are called LN-Nets and PLN-Nets,
respectively. The direct-sum structure in Definition

parallel composition of independently parameterized, fixed-dimensional
LN-Nets.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given

\hat{f}_{i}\in\gF(\mathrm{LN}[n_{s}];1,n_{s})
with

\hat{f}_{i}:\sR^{d_{x}}\to\sR^{d_{y}}
for

, there exists a

such that

f(\rvx)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{f}_{i}(\rvx),\qquad\forall\rvx\in\sR^{d_{x}}.

(4)

Conversely, any such PLN-Net can be decomposed into a sum of
N
shallow
LN-Nets with the same input and output dimensions.

4
Universal Approximation by PLN Networks

We now establish the central representation result for MLPs. A single LN is a
multivariate nonlinearity and can realize useful scalar activations after
composition with affine layers. However, a shallow network with only one
full-width LN remains non-universal, because increasing its width enlarges one
coupled nonlinear unit while the number of units remains one. PLN removes
this obstruction by placing fixed-dimensional LN units in parallel. We first
make this contrast precise and then prove universal approximation for both
univariate and multivariate functions.

\gF(\phi;L,N)\subseteq\gF(\mathrm{PLN}[n_{s}];L,n_{s}N),

Summary.

LN already induces nontrivial scalar nonlinearities, but a single full-width LN
unit remains non-universal in shallow networks. PLN converts fixed-dimensional
normalization units into a width-scalable direct sum, allowing classical
activation-network approximation results to transfer to PLN-Nets in arbitrary
input dimension. The exact PLN–PLS equivalence established in
Lemma
3.2
transfers these conclusions directly to PLS-Nets.
We next lift the PLN constructions to structured architectures.

5
Extensions to Structured Architectures

For sequence and image data, we identify PLN designs that preserve the
approximation guarantees established above.

Summary.

The shallow MLP experiments provide finite-network evidence consistent with
PLN’s universal-approximation results, while the PINN study suggests its
potential for physics-informed learning. Across CNNs and ViTs, comparisons
among LN, PLN, GN, and Mix-Norm show that performance depends not only on group
count but also on the grouped axes, replacement site, and evaluation task
(random-label fitting versus clean-label generalization).

7
Conclusion

This work identifies a distinction between two ways to scale
normalization-based networks: widening one LN unit does not make a shallow
LN-Net universal, whereas adding parallel fixed-dimensional units does. By
formalizing the latter as PLN, we establish shallow universal-approximation
results with explicit bounds and transfer approximation constructions to deep
PLN-Nets. The exact PLN–PLS equivalence extends these results to RMSNorm-based
networks. Besides, our representation results carry the local mechanism to
position-wise feed-forward and convolutional networks. Finite-model experiments
support PLN’s approximation behavior and show that normalization-domain choices
affect CNN and ViT performance.

Limitations and Future Work.

Our theorems cover affine–PLN compositions with normalization local to a
token or spatial position, but not cross-position domains or arbitrary
compositions with additional nonlinear activations. The experiments provide
finite-model evidence rather than empirical proof of universality, and the PINN
results remain preliminary. In future work, we will extend the theory and
experiments to broader tasks and cross-position normalization.

\displaystyle b_{0}\in\sR

The explicit width bound in Theorem

uses the
idealized zero-variance convention, because it represents the sign function

[\mathrm{LN}_{\varepsilon}(\rvh)]_{j}=\frac{h_{j}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\varepsilon}},\qquad\varepsilon>0,

and show that it can still represent the smooth activation

\displaystyle\phi(z)=z/\sqrt{1+z^{2}}
exactly. We use

\displaystyle\mathrm{PLN}_{\varepsilon}[n_{s}]
to denote the direct sum of

\displaystyle\mathrm{LN}_{\varepsilon}[n_{s}]

Lemma A.4

(Exact smooth activation under stabilized LN)
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi(z)=z/\sqrt{1+z^{2}}
and fix
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. For every

\displaystyle n_{s}\geq 3
, any width-one shallow
\displaystyle\phi
-Net can be represented exactly by a

\displaystyle n_{s}

Proof.

Let a width-one
\displaystyle\phi
-Net be

\hat{f}(\rvx)=\alpha\phi(\vw^{\top}\rvx+b)+\beta.

(77)

We construct the corresponding stabilized LN-Net in three steps.

Step 1: Construct the first affine layer.
For
\displaystyle n_{s}\geq 3

\rva=\sqrt{\frac{n_{s}}{2}}(1,-1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},\qquad\rvc=\sqrt{\frac{n_{s}}{6}}(1,1,-2,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}.

(78)

\bm 1^{\top}\rva=\bm 1^{\top}\rvc=0,\qquad\rva^{\top}\rvc=0,\qquad\|\rva\|_{2}^{2}=\|\rvc\|_{2}^{2}=n_{s}.

(79)

\displaystyle A=\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}
and
\displaystyle z=\vw^{\top}\rvx+b

(80)

\displaystyle\rva
and
\displaystyle\rvc
are centered, the mean of
\displaystyle\rvh(z)
is zero.

(81)

Let
\displaystyle\rvu=\rva/n_{s}
. By the orthogonality of
\displaystyle\rva
and
\displaystyle\rvc
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rvu^{\top}\mathrm{LN}_{\varepsilon}(\rvh(z))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{Az}{\sqrt{A^{2}z^{2}+1+\varepsilon}}=\frac{Az}{A\sqrt{z^{2}+1}}=\phi(z).

(82)

The output layer applies
\displaystyle\alpha\rvu^{\top}
and adds
\displaystyle\beta
. Therefore,

\alpha\rvu^{\top}\mathrm{LN}_{\varepsilon}(\rvh(\vw^{\top}\rvx+b))+\beta=\alpha\phi(\vw^{\top}\rvx+b)+\beta=\hat{f}(\rvx).

(83)

Thus, the stabilized LN-Net represents the width-one
\displaystyle\phi
-Net exactly.
This completes the proof.
∎

We next extend the construction from one activation unit to shallow and deep
networks. Applying Lemma
A.4
independently to
\displaystyle N

activation units and arranging the resulting LN blocks in parallel gives

\gF(\phi;1,N)\subseteq\gF(\mathrm{PLN}_{\varepsilon}[n_{s}];1,n_{s}N),\qquad n_{s}\geq 3.

(84)

For a deep
\displaystyle\phi
-Net, we apply the same construction to every activation
layer and combine the adjacent affine layers, as in the proof of
Lemma
4.3
. Hence the corresponding inclusion also
holds for arbitrary depth.

Since
\displaystyle\phi
is smooth and non-polynomial, standard shallow-network
universal-approximation results

(
Hornik, 1991
;
Pinkus, 1999
)
imply that stabilized
PLN-Nets are dense on compact domains. The multivariate approximation rate,
the deep-network approximation result, and the position-wise sequence and
convolutional representation results are all transferred through this exact

\displaystyle\phi
-representation. Therefore, these results continue to hold under the
stabilized convention.

The stabilized operator is continuous and does not represent the discontinuous
sign function exactly. Consequently, this argument does not assert the
explicit width bound

\displaystyle N=\floor{L/(2\epsilon)}+1
from
Theorem
4.2
for stabilized LN. Learnable scale and
shift parameters do not alter the conclusion because they can be absorbed into
the following affine layer.

\displaystyle 10^{-4}
