Related Dictyostelium G protein subunits Gα1 and Gα2 have different roles in chemotaxis and transcription factor regulation
Authors: Nicholas M. Kiger, Nicholas W. Mason, Md Ikram Rafid, Jeffrey A. Hadwiger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gα subunits provide important contributions to the specificity of signaling pathways that are regulated by G protein-coupled receptors. The Dictyostelium discoideum Gα2 subunit mediates chemotactic responses to cAMP including the activation of the atypical MAPK, Erk2, and translocation of the transcription factor GtaC but some of these responses partially occur in the absence of the Gα2 subunit suggesting a Gα2 subunit-independent mechanism. The Gα1 subunit is the most closely related paralog of the Gα2 subunit making it a possible candidate for overlapping functions. Heterologous expression of Gα1 in gα2− cells did not rescue cAMP chemotaxis or other gα2− phenotypes suggesting no functional overlap between these subunits. Loss of the Gα1 subunit resulted in a slight delay in multicellular development on nonnutrient agar and a growth increase on bacterial lawns in both the parental and gα2− strains. Both gα1− and gα2− cells grew to stationary phase in axenic medium earlier than their parental strain suggesting both subunits might mediate responses to nutrient limitations. The loss of Gα1 function resulted in a marginal impact on the translocation of GtaC but in gα2− cells the absence of Gα1 enhanced the translocation of GtaC. These results indicate that the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits are not functionally redundant but could possibly interact with other proteins common to both signaling pathways. Background

The amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum has been a leading model organism for understanding eukaryotic chemotaxis because of its well characterized chemotactic responses and the ability to genetically analyze the signaling mechanisms that regulate this cellular process [1–4]. G protein-coupled receptors and other common regulatory proteins function in both amoeba and animal cells to mediate chemotaxis and the signaling pathways involved are very similar based on genetic and biochemical analyses [1, 5]. In addition to directing cell movement, chemotactic signaling pathways can be important for developmental processes such as cell differentiation, and the simple multicellular developmental life cycle of this amoeba has been an asset in understanding connections between cell movement and differentiation [6]. Multiple chemoattractants have been identified for Dictyostelium but the responses to extracellular folate and cAMP and their impact on growth and development, respectively, are the best characterized [7, 8].

The fates of Dictyostelium responding to either folate or cAMP are very distinct. Folate stimulation promotes the search for additional food sources and continued growth as solitary cells whereas cAMP stimulation promotes the aggregation and differentiation of starved cells [8]. However, both chemotactic responses use G protein-coupled receptors to induce similar changes in cyclic nucleotide levels, activate downstream mitogen activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and translocate common transcription factors [9–14]. The number of G protein-coupled receptors responsive to each chemoattractant is different. Four closely related cAMP receptors (Car1-4) can be activated by cAMP and the genome encodes six other cAMP receptor-like receptors (CrlA-F), suggesting the number of receptors responding to cAMP could be much higher than four [15, 16]. A pair of cAMP receptors, Car1 and Car3, provide the ability of starved cells to aggregate and the other Car receptors play important roles during the multicellular stages of development based on phenotypic analysis [17–19]. For folate responses, a single G protein-coupled receptor, Far1, is essential for the chemotactic movement of cells toward bacterial food sources [20]. Both cAMP and folate stimulate the activation of an atypical mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK), Erk2, and the subsequent activation of a typical MAPK, Erk1, as a secondary response [13]. Remarkably, the activation of the MAPKs by both cAMP and folate are quite similar if not identical.

A single G protein Gα subunit, Gα4, is required to transduce signals from Far1 to downstream signaling proteins based on the absence of these responses in gα4− mutants [20, 21]. For cAMP responses, the Gα2 subunit is required for cAMP chemotaxis but some studies have shown that some cAMP responses can partially exist in the absence of Gα2 [12]. In particular, the activation of Erk2 and the Erk2-dependent localization of the GATA transcription factor, GtaC, can partially occur in gα2− cells [22, 23]. This distinction between the folate and cAMP responses is intriguing given that other responses such as cyclic nucleotide and chemotaxis appear to be so similar [24]. Given that multiple cAMP receptors are expressed in early development it is possible that multiple signaling pathways might be activated in response to cAMP and that these pathways might involve different Gα subunits.

Dictyostelium genome encodes over 60 G protein-coupled receptors and twelve conventional Gα subunits [15, 25, 26]. Only a single Gβ subunit and Gγ subunit have been identified and characterized [27, 28]. Loss of the Gβ subunit prevents chemotaxis to both cAMP and folate suggesting that this subunit associates with multiple Gα subunits [29]. Gβ subunits typically contain several WD40 repeats and while Dictyostelium expresses other WD40 repeat-containing proteins, it is not clear if any of these are associated with G protein-mediated signaling pathways [27, 30]. The multitude of receptors and Gα subunits suggest that these proteins are specific to different signal transduction pathways but that other G protein subunits and downstream regulatory proteins, such as nucleotide cyclases, phosphodiesterases, and MAPKs, are often common to multiple pathways. The specificity of these downstream signaling proteins can be difficult to determine because they often contain protein binding or docking motifs that allow for their participation in many pathways.

In this study, the role of the Gα1 subunit, as a paralog of the Gα2 subunit, is investigated to determine if the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits have functional overlap during early developmental processes. Structure and expression of these subunits were compared and the ability of heterologous expression of Gα1 in gα2− cells to rescue cAMP chemotaxis was examined. Growth phenotypes of gα1− cells are described and compared to gα2− cells. In addition, gα1−gα2− mutant cells were created and tested for the ability to eliminate cAMP chemotactic responses, including the Erk2-dependent translocation of the GtaC transcription factor. These analyses provide a better understanding of the possible relationships between the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits during the transition between growth and development.

Methods

Strains and cell culture

All strains were isogenic derivatives of the parental strain KAx3 except for the loci of gene disruptions. The gα2− strain was previously created and described [12]. The gα1− strain was created using a previously described gene disruption construct containing the thyA gene in the thymidine auxotrophic mutant strain JH10 (isogenic derivative of KAx3) [12]. The gα1−gα2− strain was created from the gα1− strain by disrupting the gα2 gene with a gene disruption construct conferring blasticidin resistance. Transformations of strains were conducted using electroporation as previously described [31]. Dictyostelium strains were grown in HL5 medium and transformants were selected in medium without thymidine supplements or in medium containing drugs (G418, blasticidin, or hygromycin (4–10 µg/ml) [31]. Alternatively, strains were grown in bacterial (Klebsiella aerogenes) lawns on SM+/3 agar plates as described [11].

Recombinant DNA constructs

The gα1 open reading frame was amplified by PCR and cloned as a BamHI-XbaI fragment adjacent to the act15 promoter in the Dictyostelium expression vector pDM358 (confers hygromycin resistance) [32]. To disrupt the gα2 locus in gα1− cells, a blasticidin resistance gene fragment (BamHI digest) was inserted into a unique BclI site within the gα2 open reading frame as previously described for another gα2 disruption construct [12]. This vector was digested with EcoRI and then transformed into gα1− cells. Clonal transformants were initially plated on bacterial lawns to screen for aggregation defects. Disruptions of the gα2 and gα1 loci were verified by PCR amplification (Fig. S1).

Phylogenetic analysis and structure predictions

Phylogenetic analysis of the Dictyostelium Gα subunits was conducted using MEGA12 program and the phylogeny was inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method and Jones-Taylor-Thorton [33]. The initial tree for the heuristic search was selected by choosing the tree with the superior log-likelihood between a Neighbor-Joining tree and a Maximum Parsimony tree [34]. The Neighbor-Joining tree was generated using a matrix of pairwise distances computed using the p-distance. The Maximum Parsimony tree had the shortest length among 10 tree searches; each performed with a randomly generated starting tree. The analytical procedure encompassed 12 amino acid sequences with 638 positions in the final dataset. Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA12 utilizing up to 4 parallel computing threads [35]. Predicted structures of the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits were generated using protein sequence data in Alphafold3 [36]. The superimposition of these structures was conducted using the Matchmaker tool in ChimeraX [37].

Development and chemotaxis

Cells were grown in fresh HL5 medium one day prior to development or chemotaxis assays. For synchronized starvation and development, cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed in phosphate buffer (12 mM NaH2PO4 adjusted to pH 6.1 with KOH) and then suspended at 2 × 107 cells/ml before plating on nonnutrient agar plates (1.5% granulated agar in phosphate buffer). Images of developing strains were obtained using a Nikon SMZ dissecting scope at times indicated. Chemotaxis assays to cAMP were conducted using above agar chemotaxis assays as previously described [38]. Cells were washed in phosphate buffer and suspended at 1 × 107 cells/ml and droplets were spotted on to nonnutrient plates. Droplets of 100 nM cAMP were spotted approximately 2 mm away from the cell droplets. Agar near the cell droplets was scarred using a needle and images were collected at the start of the assay at the same magnification after 3 h. The early and late images, aligned with agar scars, were used to measure the distance traveled by the leading edge of cells toward the source of cAMP.

Growth on bacteria assays

Cells were grown in fresh HL5 medium for one day prior to harvesting and washing cells in phosphate buffer. Cell suspensions of 1 × 108 cells/ml were plated as 1 µl droplets on a lawn of bacteria grown over SM+/3 plates. Images of Dictyostelium plaques were taken at the same magnification at the indicated times over several days. Plaque diameters were measured and graphed.

Transcription factor translocation assay

The translocation of the GFP-GtaCc−s reporter from the nucleus to the cytoplasm was assayed as previously described [10]. Mutant cells were transformed with the GFP-GtaCc−s reporter construct and selected in HL5 medium containing G418. Transformants were grown in fresh HL5 medium one day prior to plating on coverslips covering 10 mm holes in the center of 60 mm petridishes. Cell attachment was allowed for 15 min and the cells were washed 5 times in developmental buffer (DB, phosphate buffer with 2 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM CaCl2) before being overlayed with 100 µl DB. GFP fluorescence in the cells was monitored with timelapse imaging using an Olympus IX81 spinning disc confocal microscope with Cellsens software. Cells were stimulated with 10 nM cAMP. The nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio of fluorescence of individual cells (minimum of 40 cells using at least 2 assays) was analyzed using ImageJ (FIJI) software to determine mean pixel intensity. The data plotted represents the mean of ratios within the middle 50% of values to reduce the variability associated with cells moving in or out of focus.

Results

Dictyostelium Gα subunit paralogs

A previous study has indicated that the loss of Gα2 function leads to an impairment but not a complete loss of the Erk2-dependent translocation of the transcription factor GtaC [22]. This observation is surprising given that no other Gα subunits have been directly associated with cAMP receptors and that Gα2 function is the essential for chemotaxis to cAMP. Possible explanations for GtaC translocation in the absence of Gα2 subunit are that another Gα subunit can partially compensate for the loss of Gα2 or that a Gα subunit-independent mechanism can mediate the Erk2-dependent activity. If another Gα subunit can compensate for the loss of Gα2, then such a subunit would likely resemble Gα2 structurally. A phylogenetic analysis of the Dictyostelium Gα subunit family suggests that the Gα1 subunit is the most structurally similar subunit to the Gα2 subunit (Fig. 1A). As with most Gα subunits, many residues are conserved between Gα2 and Gα1 but less conserved regions occur primarily at the amino and carboxy termini and at an internal region (Gα1 residues 110–118, Gα2 residues 114–128) (Fig. S2). Modeling of Gα subunit structure using alphafold3 indicates a similar conformation for Gα1 and Gα2 subunits except for the less conserved regions. The superimposition of these Gα subunit structures revealed a similarity in the overall predicted conformation (Fig. 1B). This analysis of the predicted structures suggests that Gα1 and Gα2 subunits are closely related and that these subunits potentially could have similar or overlapping functions or interactions. However, this analysis does not rule out that differences in residues side chains, particularly in the less conserved regions, could potentially impact interactions with other proteins.

Expression of Gα1 and Gα2 subunits

Both Gα1 and Gα2 subunits are expressed during growth and during multicellular development as indicated by previous studies and data sets in dictyExpress (Fig. S3A) [39, 40]. The transcript of the gα2 gene has a significant rise during the aggregation phase of development (6–12 h post starvation) compared to the gα1 transcript expression and this expression pattern is consistent with the role of Gα2 in the mediation of cAMP chemotaxis and cell aggregation. Neither subunit gene has a strong bias for expression in prespore or prestalk cells based on data sets in dictyExpress (Fig. S3B) [40]. In general, the transcript data suggests that both these subunit genes are expressed in most if not all cells during early development but that gα2 expression is elevated compared to gα1 expression during the cell aggregation phase. Previous analysis of gα1− mutants has indicated that Gα1 function is not required for cell aggregation [12, 41]. Although, a role for Gα1 function in cAMP chemotaxis could be potentially masked by the abundant expression of the Gα2 subunit in gα1− cells during this phase of development and conversely the lower level of the Gα1 subunit expression might not be sufficient to allow chemotaxis in the absence of the Gα2 subunit.

Heterologous expression of Gα1 subunit does not rescue cAMP chemotaxis defects

To examine if increased expression of the Gα1 subunit can rescue Gα2 function with respect to cAMP chemotaxis, gα2− cells were transformed with a vector expressing the Gα1 subunit from the pseudo-constitutive act15 promoter. When starved these cells phenocopied gα2− cells in that they did not aggregate on nonnutrient agar (Fig. 2A). Likewise, these cells also did not aggregate when grown on bacterial lawns. However, cell aggregation is a complex process that relies on multiple cellular processes such as the release of cAMP and the chemotaxis toward cAMP. The gα2− cells, with the heterologous Gα1 subunit expression, were also assayed for cAMP chemotaxis in an above agar assay. These cells did not show any noticeable chemotaxis to cAMP and therefore phenocopied gα2− cells without the Gα1 vector (Fig. 2B). The lack of cAMP chemotaxis or the ability to aggregate indicates that the heterologous expression of Gα1 subunit is not sufficient to rescue Gα2 function, suggesting that the Gα1 subunit does not functionally overlap with the Gα2 subunit during early development.

Phenotypes of gα1− and gα1−gα2− double mutants

The inability of heterologous Gα1 expression to rescue Gα2-mediated chemotaxis suggests different functions for Gα1 and Gα2 subunits but this data does not exclude a possible overlap in the interactions of these subunits with other proteins. Such overlap could result in competitive interactions of these subunits with other proteins, and such interactions might impact Gα1 and Gα2 mediated signal transduction pathways. To further examine potential relationships between Gα1 and Gα2 subunits and their signaling pathways, a phenotype comparison was made between cells lacking either subunit or both subunits. Loss of the Gα1 subunit did not impact development when cells were starved on nonnutrient agar, except for a slight delay in the transition from loose mounds into mounds with tips (Fig. 3A). This slight delay has been observed in earlier studies of gα1− strains [41]. The gα2− cells, as expected from previous studies, and the gα1−gα2− cells were aggregation defective on nonnutrient agar plates. In axenic growth medium, gα1−, gα2−, and gα1−gα2− cells reached stationary phase before the parental strain and the gα1−gα2− double mutant was not capable of reaching the same cell density at the stationary phase as observed with the other strains (Fig. 3B). Growth and development of the strains were also examined on bacterial lawns because the consumption of bacteria results in a more rapid proliferation of Dictyostelium discoideum compared to growth in axenic medium. The development of gα1− cells was like that of the parental strain and both gα2− and gα1−gα2− mutants displayed no multicellular development on bacterial lawns. The growth rate of gα1− plaques on bacterial lawns was like that of wild-type cells except for a slight increase in the rate of plaque expansion (Fig. 3C and D). A small increase in plaque expansion rate was also observed for the gα1−gα2− double mutant compared to gα2− cells, although both these strains had much slower plaque expansions compared to the parental or gα1− strain. The small but significant increases in plaque expansion rates suggest that Gα1 function might have a role in restricting growth on bacteria.

Translocation of GtaC in response to cAMP

The developmental phenotypes of gα1−, gα2−, and gα1−gα2− cells did not indicate an overlap in function between these subunits but these developmental processes involve many signaling events and might not reveal subtle relationships between the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits. Therefore, these mutants were analyzed for the ability translocate GtaC in response to cAMP. To examine if both subunits contribute to the rapid translocation of GtaC, mutants were transformed with the GFP-GtaCc−s reporter. As previously reported, the parental KAx3 strain displayed a rapid translocation of the reporter in response to cAMP and the gα2− cells exhibited an impaired but significant translocation at a slower rate (Fig. 4). In contrast, gα1− cells responded to the cAMP with a translocation rate of the reporter than close to that of the parental strain. Surprisingly, the translocation of the reporter in the gα1−gα2− cells was more efficient than in gα2− cells, suggesting that the Gα1 subunit does not overlap with the Gα2 subunit to mediate this translocation.

Discussion

The relatedness of the Gα1 and Gα2 protein sequences and predicted similarity in protein conformation suggest that these two subunits are the most likely subunits to have overlap in function or interactions with other signaling proteins. The temporal and spatial overlap in expression of these genes also supports the possibility of these subunits operating in the same or related signal transduction pathways. However, loss of the Gα2 subunit has a severe impact on chemotaxis to cAMP and cell aggregation whereas loss of the Gα1 subunit has no detectable change in these processes. A delay in cGMP accumulation after cAMP stimulation has been previously reported for gα1− cells but the basis of this delay is not known [41]. Using a strong heterologous promoter to express the Gα1 subunit did not rescue loss of Gα2 function suggesting that the relative levels of these subunits is not the basis for the differences in phenotypes.

The loss of the Gα1 subunit had little impact with respect to development on nonnutrient agar but the enhanced growth of gα1− cells on bacterial lawns and the more rapid rise to stationary phase in axenic medium suggests Gα1 function might have a role in sensing nutrient limitations and preparing cells for starvation. An earlier study has suggested that the Gα1 subunit transmits signals from conditioned medium factors that accumulate with increases in cell density and decreases in nutrient availability [42]. This suspected role appears to be consistent with the increased growth of gα1− on bacteria and the more rapid attainment of stationary phase growth. The Gα2 subunit also functions to prepare cells for starvation but this appears to occur through cAMP stimulated chemotaxis during aggregation [12]. However, given the relatedness of the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits, it would not be surprising if both subunits evolved from a common ancestor that controlled responses to nutrient limitations. Regardless of this commonality, Gα1 and Gα2 functions appear to be distinct based on the inability of Gα1 overexpression to rescue defects in chemotaxis and aggregation associated with gα2− cells.

The small reduction in the efficiency of GtaC translocation in gα1− cells suggests that Gα1 might have a marginal role in cAMP responses, but this contrasts the increased efficiency of the translocation when gα1 is disrupted in gα2− cells. Therefore, the reduced efficiency of GtaC translocation in gα1− cells is not likely due to a direct role of Gα1 subunit in mediating this response. Given the similarity of Gα1 and Gα2 subunit structures it is possible both subunits have shared binding to one or more other signaling components (Fig. 5). Proteins that can typically interact with Gα subunits include receptors, Gbg dimers, regulators of G protein signaling (RGS), scaffold proteins or other proteins that act as downstream effectors. In the absence of the Gα1 subunit, more Gα2 subunit could bind to a shared interacting-protein, and this could possibly deplete some of the available Gα2 subunit for mediating the translocation of GtaC. It is unlikely that these Gα subunits compete for receptors given the existence of more than 60 G protein-coupled receptors in the genome compared to the 12 Gα subunits, but rather multiple receptors might interact with the same Gα subunit. A single Gbg dimer can interact with different Gα subunits but these dimers typical bind to inactive Gα subunits [29]. Overexpression of different Gα subunits typically result in distinctly different phenotypes suggesting that changes in the Gα subunit and Gbg dimer stoichiometry do not by themselves lead to phenotypic changes in signaling [41, 43, 44]. Five proteins with RGS domains have been identified in Dictyostelium and these presumably function to reduce the interaction of Gα subunits with downstream effectors by enhancing the intrinsic GTPase activity of the Gα subunit. One of these, RCK1, appears to function downstream in cAMP responses but it remains to be determined if it interacts directly with the Gα2 or Gα1 subunit [45]. Common interactions with other downstream signaling proteins cannot be ruled out but the identification and characterization of these proteins remains to be fully explored in Dictyostelium.

The explanation for the decreased efficiency of GtaC translocation in gα1− mutants does not account for the increased efficiency of GtaC translocation in gα1−gα2− cells compared to gα2− cells. If gα1 subunit is not mediating this process, then possibly a more distantly related Gα subunit could facilitate this process in response to cAMP. It is not clear if another Gα subunit couples to cAMP receptors but Dictyostelium has 10 other Gα subunits and perhaps one of these can interact with one of the four closely related cAMP receptors or the several other cAMP receptor-like receptors. The Gα4 subunit can mediate the translocation of GtaC but this subunit couples to the folate receptors and thus far has not been associated with cAMP receptors. The presence of another Gα subunit or pathway that can translocate GtaC only explains that this process can occur in the absence of Gα2 but it does not explain the increased efficiency of the translocation in the absence of both Gα1 and Gα2 subunits unless these subunits have an inhibitory role in regulating this alternative pathway. The data in this study suggests that the loss of the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits relieve the inhibition of this alternative pathway. These inhibitory roles could possibly be different for Gα1 and Gα2 and perhaps include competition for receptors that are stimulated by cAMP.

Conclusions

The Gα1 and Gα2 subunits do not have overlapping functions with respect to cAMP stimulated chemotaxis, development, and transcription factor translocation despite the structural similarity between these two subunits. Therefore, another Gα subunit or a Gα subunit-independent mechanism is responsible for the translocation of the GtaC transcription factor in the absence of Gα2 function. This lack of overlapping function does not exclude the possibility that the Gα1 and Gα2 subunits might interact with regulatory proteins shared by both signal transduction pathways.
