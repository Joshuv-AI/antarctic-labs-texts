General Recurrence Multidimensional Zeckendorf Representations
Jiarui Cheng a , Steven J. Miller b Sebastian Rodriguez-Labastida c , Tianyu Shen d , Alan Sun e , and Garrett Tresch f

Abstract
We present a multidimensional generalization of Zeckendorf’s Theorem (any positive integer can be written uniquely as a sum of non-adjacent Fibonacci numbers) to a large family of linear recurrences. This extends work of Anderson and Bicknell-Johnson in the multi-dimensional case when the underlying recurrence is the same as the Fibonacci one. Our extension applies to linear recurrence relations defined by vectors 𝐜 → = ( c 1 , c 2 , … , c k ) \vec{\mathbf{c}}=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}) such that c 1 ≥ c 2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ c k c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\cdots\geq c_{k} and where c k = 1 c_{k}=1 . Under these conditions, we prove that every integer vector in ℤ k − 1 \mathbb{Z}^{k-1} admits a unique 𝐜 → \vec{\mathbf{c}} -satisfying representation ( 𝐜 → \vec{\mathbf{c}} -SR) as a linear combination of vectors, ( 𝐗 𝐧 → ) n ∈ ℤ (\vec{\mathbf{X_{n}}})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}} defined for every n ∈ ℤ n\in\mathbb{Z} by initially by zero and standard unit vectors and then the recursion 𝐗 → n := c 1 ​ 𝐗 → n − 1 + c 2 ​ 𝐗 → n − 2 + ⋯ + c k ​ 𝐗 → n − k . \vec{\mathbf{X}}_{n}:=c_{1}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}+c_{2}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{n-2}+\cdots+c_{k}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{n-k}. To establish this, we introduce carrying and borrowing operations that use the defining recursion to transform any 𝐜 → \vec{\mathbf{c}} representation into a 𝐜 → \vec{\mathbf{c}} -SR while preserving the underlying vector. Then, by establishing bijections with properties of scalar Positive Linear Recurrence Sequences (PLRS), we prove that these multidimensional decompositions inherit various properties, such as the number of summands exhibits Gaussian behavior and summand minimality of 𝐜 → \vec{\mathbf{c}} -SRs over all all 𝐜 → \vec{\mathbf{c}} -representations.

Proofs of Main Results

There is a natural map that transforms linear combinations of truncated
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-recurrence vectors into the underlying
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-recurrence sequences.

Definition 2.1
.

For
n\geq k-2,S_{n}:\mathbb{Z}^{k-1}\xrightarrow{}[0,X_{n})
is the scalar product defined by

S_{n}(\vec{\mathbf{v}})\ =\ \vec{\mathbf{v}}\cdot(X_{n-1},\ \ldots,\ X_{n-k+1})\pmod{X_{n}}

where each
X_{n}
is defined as in Definition 1.1.

The following lemma follows by the same argument as in
[
AB-J
]
. For the sake of completeness and to highlight our specific case, its proof is included below.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have
S_{n}(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{n-i}
.

Proof.

When
1\leq i\leq k-1
, we have
\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-i}=\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
, by definition. Therefore,

S_{n}(\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-i})\ =\ X_{n-i}\pmod{X_{n}}\ =\ X_{n-i}.

When
i=k
,

\displaystyle S_{n}(\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-k})

\displaystyle\ =\ \vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-k}\cdot(X_{n-1},\ \ldots,\ X_{n-k+1})\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-j}}{c_{k}}\cdot(X_{n-1},\ \ldots,\ X_{n-k+1})\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{0-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}X_{n-j}}{c_{k}}\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{X_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}X_{n-j}}{c_{k}}\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ X_{n-k}\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ X_{n-k},

and when
i=k+1
,

\displaystyle S_{n}(\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-k-1})

\displaystyle\ =\ \vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-k-1}\cdot(X_{n-1},\ \ldots,\ X_{n-k+1})\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-1-j}}{c_{k}}\cdot(X_{n-1},\ \ldots,\ X_{n-k+1})\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{X_{n-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}X_{n-1-j}}{c_{k}}\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ X_{n-k-1}\pmod{X_{n}}

\displaystyle\ =\ X_{n-k-1}.

When
k+1<i\leq n-1
, according to the conditions previously obtained, it can be concluded through induction that

S_{n}(\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-i})\ =\ X_{n-i}.

Therefore, it’s true for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
that
S_{n}(\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-i})=X_{n-i}
. By linearity of
S_{n}
, the proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Each end complete
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR can be transformed into a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR by a finite sequence of carrying operations. Furthermore, by Remark
1.16
, this process only reduces the sum of the coefficients.

Proof.

We proceed by induction over the number of chunks of the representation. Let
a:=(a_{n})_{n=1}^{m}
be an end complete
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR with only 1 chunk and define
a_{0}=0
. Note that as
\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{0}=\vec{\mathbf{0}}
we can extend
a
to
(a_{n})_{n=0}^{m}
. In this case, note that
m=k
and

a_{1}\ =\ c_{1},\ \ldots,\ a_{k}\ =\ c_{k}.

Then, by carrying into
a_{0}
, we obtain the sequence
(b_{n})_{n=0}^{m}
, where
b_{0}=1
and
b_{i}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. Note that that
(b_{n})_{n=1}^{m}
is the zero vector which is, indeed, a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR.

Suppose that all end complete
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR’s with
\ell
chunks can be transformed into a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR. Let
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{m}
be an end complete
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR with
\ell+1
chunks. For each
1\leq i\leq\ell+1
denote
a_{n_{i}}
as the first coefficient of the
i^{\rm th}
chunk as in Definition
1.11
. By assumption we can carry into
a_{n_{\ell+1}-1}
. Let
b
be the resulting sequence of such carrying operation; then, we have exactly two cases to consider.

Case 1:

b
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR and we are done, or

Case 2:

b
is an end complete
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR and the desired result follows by induction.

Indeed, suppose that Case 1 does not hold; then by Definition
1.7
there must exist
s\in\{1,\ \ldots,k\}
such that

a_{n_{\ell}}\ =\ c_{1},\ \ldots,\ a_{n_{\ell}-1+s-1}\ =\ c_{s-1},\ a_{n_{\ell}-1+s}<c_{s}

and there exists
p\geq 0
such that
a_{n_{\ell}-1+s+i}=0
, for all
i\leq p
, and
n_{\ell}-1+s+p=n_{\ell+1}-1
. Notice that, because of Remark
1.16
,
b_{q}=0
for all
q\geq n_{\ell+1}
. If
p=0
then
n_{\ell+1}-1=n_{\ell}+s-1
, and so
b_{n_{\ell}-1+s}\leq c_{s}
; otherwise
b
is exactly

(a_{1},\ \ldots,\ a_{n_{\ell}}\ =\ c_{1},\ a_{n_{\ell}+1}\ =\ c_{2},\ \ldots,\ a_{n_{\ell}-1+s}\ <\ c_{s},0,\ \ldots,\ 0,\ b_{n_{\ell+1}-1}=1)

which is clearly a
\vec{c}
-SR.
Hence, the only possible way for
b
not to be a
\vec{c}
-SR is that
s=k
and
b_{n_{\ell}-1+s}=c_{k}
, in other words
b
is end complete.
∎

We now prove that weakly decreasing
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR’s can be transformed into satisfying representations. We note that the algorithm utilized in the proof of this result is similar to the algorithm from the appendix of
[
KKMW
]
that converts any decomposition of an integer over a PLRS into a legal decomposition. However, as we define the vectors using a backwards recursion, it is not the case that we could eventually borrow into zero terms. In fact, it is very possible in the case where
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
is not weakly decreasing for this process to never terminate (see Example
4.5
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Every
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR, where
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
is a weakly decreasing vector and
c_{k}=1
, can be transformed into a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR by a finite number of borrowing and carrying operations.

Proof.

Recall that if
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
is a weakly decreasing vector then
c_{i+1}\leq c_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ \ldots,\ k-1\}
.
Throughout this proof, the symbol
a^{i}
represents the
i
th
iteration of a process that consists of a finite number of borrows and carries.
Suppose that
a^{0}:=(a_{n}^{0})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR and let
c:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}
.

By Definition
1.12
there exist
p_{0}\geq 1
and
0\leq j_{0}<k
such that
I(a^{0})=n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}
and where

\displaystyle a_{n_{p_{0}}}\ =\ c_{1},\ \ldots,\ a_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}-1}\ =\ c_{j_{0}},\ a_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}\ \geq\ c_{j_{0}+1}.

(2.1)

Since
n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}
is the first overfilled element of
a
, we have that
(a_{n}^{0})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}-1}
is a
\vec{c}
-SR. We now proceed by case analysis to define
a^{1}
.

Case 1:
j_{0}=k-1
.
Then
(a_{n}^{0})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}
is end complete and so by applying Proposition
2.3
, a finite sequence of carries transforms
(a_{n}^{0})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}
into a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR say
(b^{0}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}
. Define
a^{1}
by

a_{n}^{1}\ =\ \begin{cases}b^{0}_{n},&1\leq n\leq n_{p_{0}}+j_{0};\\
a_{n}^{0},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In this case we have that
G(a^{1})\leq G(a^{0})-(c-1)
.

Case 2:
0\leq j_{0}<k-1
.
Note that in this case,
a_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}\ >\ c_{j_{0}+1}
. Borrow from
a_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}^{0}
to define
d^{0}
. In particular, define

d^{0}_{n}\ =\ \begin{cases}a_{n}^{0}+c_{i},&\text{if }n=n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}+i\ \text{for some }1\leq i\leq k;\\
a_{n}^{0}-1,&\text{if }n=n_{p_{0}}+j_{0};\\
a_{n}^{0}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
is weakly decreasing, then for all
1\leq i\leq k-j_{0}-1

\displaystyle d^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}+i}\ \geq\ c_{i}\ \geq\ c_{j_{0}+i+1}

and, thus,

\displaystyle d^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}+j}\ \geq\ c_{j+1}\ \text{for each }0\leq j\leq k-1.

(2.2)

Moreover, if we define
(e^{0}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+k-1}
by

e^{0}_{n}\ =\ \begin{cases}d^{0}_{n},&\text{if }1\leq n<n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}\ \text{ or }\ n_{p_{0}}+k\leq n;\\
c_{i+1},&\text{if }n=n_{p_{0}}+i\ \text{ for some }j_{0}\leq i\leq k-1,\end{cases}

then
(e^{0}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+k-1}
is end complete and so by applying Proposition
2.3
, a finite sequence of carries transforms
(e^{0}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+k-1}
into a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR, say
f^{0}:=(f^{0}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+k-1}
. Define
a^{1}
by

a_{n}^{1}\ =\ \begin{cases}f^{0}_{n},&1\leq n<n_{p_{0}}+j_{0};\\
d^{0}_{n}-c_{i+1},&\text{if }n=n_{p_{0}}+i\ \text{ for some }j_{0}\leq i\leq k-1;\\
d^{0}_{n},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular,
a^{1}
is obtained from
a^{0}
by a single borrow and then at least one carry. By Remark
1.16
this implies that
G(a^{1})\leq G(d)-(c-1)=G(a^{0})
.

If
a^{1}
is defined in either case is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR, then we are done. If not, then as above there exist
p_{1}\geq 1
and
0\leq j_{1}<k
such that
I(a^{1})=n_{p_{1}}+j_{1}
and where

\displaystyle a_{n_{p_{1}}}\ =\ c_{1},\ \ldots,\ a_{n_{p_{1}}+j_{1}-1}\ =\ c_{j_{1}},\ a_{n_{p_{1}}+j_{1}}\ >\ c_{j_{1}+1}.

(2.3)

There are two important properties we prove.

Claim 1:
We have that
n_{p_{1}}\geq n_{p_{0}}
and if
G(a^{1})=G(a^{0})
then
G_{n_{p_{1}}}(a^{1})\geq G_{n_{p_{0}}}(a^{0})+1
.

Proof of Claim 1.

If
a^{1}
is constructed through Case 1 then
a^{1}_{n_{p_{0}}+k-1}=a^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}+k-1}-c_{k}=0
and so the claim follows from the fact that
(b^{0}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}=(a^{1}_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR. Note that in Case 1, we have
G(a^{0})\neq G(a^{1})
.

Suppose instead that
a^{1}
is constructed through Case 2. It is immediate that
n_{p_{1}}\geq n_{p_{0}}
in the case where
n_{p_{0}}=1
and so we may suppose further that
n_{p_{0}}\geq 2
. If
j_{0}=0
then
n_{p_{1}}\geq n_{p_{0}}
follows from the fact that
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}-1}=(a_{n}^{1})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}-1}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR. If
j_{0}\neq 0
then
n_{p_{1}}\geq n_{p_{0}}
follows from the fact that
a^{1}_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}-1}=a^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}-1}-c_{j_{0}}=0
and since
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}=(a_{n}^{1})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{0}}+j_{0}}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR.

Note that by Remark
1.16
, if
G(a^{1})=G(a^{2})
then
a^{1}
is obtained from
a^{0}
by exactly one borrow from
a^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}}
and then one carry into
a^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}-1}
where
n_{p_{0}}-1\geq 1
. Thus, as single borrow from
a^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}}
does not effect
G_{n_{p_{0}}}
and a single carry into
a^{0}_{n_{p_{0}}-1}
increases
G_{n_{p_{0}}}
by 1, we have that

G_{n_{p_{1}}}(a^{1})\ \geq\ G_{n_{p_{0}}}(a^{1})\ =\ G_{n_{p_{0}}}(a^{0})+1

as desired.
∎

Since
n_{p_{1}}+j_{1}
is the first overfilled element of
a^{1}
, we have that
(a_{n}^{1})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{1}}+j_{1}-1}
is a
\vec{c}
-SR.

Inductively, if
a^{\ell}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-NSR and is defined for some
\ell\geq 1
, then we may define
n_{p_{\ell}}
and
j_{\ell}
such that
n_{p_{\ell}}+j_{\ell}
is the first overfilled element of
a^{\ell}
. Then we have that
(a_{n}^{\ell})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{\ell}}+j_{\ell}-1}
is a
\vec{c}
-SR and employ the same case analysis as above to construct
a^{\ell+1}
. If
a^{\ell+1}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR then we are done. Otherwise, we may define
n_{p_{\ell+1}}
and
j_{\ell+1}
such that
n_{p_{\ell}+1}+j_{\ell+1}
is the first overfilled element of
a^{\ell}
and have that
(a_{n}^{\ell})_{n=1}^{n_{p_{\ell}}+j_{\ell}-1}
is a
\vec{c}
-SR. Furthermore, by applying Claim 1,
n_{p_{\ell+1}}\geq n_{p_{\ell}}
and either
G(a^{\ell+1})<G(a^{\ell})
or
G_{n_{p_{\ell+1}}}(a^{\ell+1})>G_{n_{p_{\ell}}}(a^{\ell})
.

Claim 2:
There exists a
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
a^{q}
is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-SR.

Proof of Claim 2.

Suppose not. Let
\alpha=G(a^{0})
,
\beta:=\lceil\frac{\alpha}{c-1}\rceil
, and
Z:=\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}\ :\ G(a^{i})<G(a^{i-1})\}
. Note that
|Z|\leq\beta
. Indeed, if
|Z|>\beta
then there exists a subset
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{\beta+1}\}\subset Z
where
z_{j}<z_{j+1}
for each
1\leq j\leq\beta
. Then

\displaystyle G(a^{z_{\beta+1}})\ \leq\ G(a^{z_{\beta}})-(c-1)\ \leq\ G(a^{z_{\beta-1}})-2(c-1)\

\displaystyle\leq\ \cdots\ \leq\ G(a^{z_{1}})-\beta(c-1)

\displaystyle\leq\ \alpha-(\beta+1)(c-1)\ <\ 0.

Let
z:=\max\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}\ :\ i\in Z\}
and define
\gamma:=G(a^{z})
. Note that for all
j>z
it must be the case that
G(a^{j})=G(a^{j-1})
and
G_{n_{p_{j}}}(a^{j})\geq G_{n_{p_{j-1}}}(a^{j-1})+1
. However, if we examine
q=z+\gamma
then

\gamma\ <\ G_{n_{z}}(a^{z})+\gamma\ \leq\ G_{n_{p_{q}}}(a^{q})\ \leq\ G(a^{q})\ \leq\ G(a^{z})\ =\ \gamma.

Hence, we have the desired contradiction.

∎

The proof is complete as Claim 2 establishes that process must terminate in a finite number of iterations to a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}-SR
.

∎

We now prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

We need to show both existence and uniqueness of the desired representations.

Existence:
By definition,
\vec{\mathbf{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k-1}
has a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-satisfying representation. By induction we need only to show that if
\vec{\mathbf{v}}
has a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-satisfying representation then so does
\vec{\mathbf{v}}+\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
for any
1\leq i\leq k
. Indeed, all positive vectors can be obtained by adding linear combinations of the basis vectors
\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{i}=\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k-1
and then as
\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}-c_{i}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
, adding multiples of
\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
reaches all possible vectors. These inductive steps involve increasing a coeﬃcient in a

\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-satisfying representation by one. Either the resulting representation is already a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
-satisfying representation and we are done or it is a
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
NSR. Hence, existence follows from Proposition
2.4
.

Uniqueness:
Similar to the case of
[
AB-J
]
, Lemma
2.2
implies that
S_{n}
is one-to-one in representations satisfying
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-i}
. Hence, uniqueness follows from the uniqueness of a
legal
decomposition for a PLRS (see
[
KKMW
]
, Theorem 1.3).

∎

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
; we define

\displaystyle R_{n}^{\vec{c}}\ :=\ S_{n}^{-1}\Big[[X_{n},X_{n+1})\Big],

where
X_{n}
is defined as in Definition
1.1
, and
S_{n}
is as in Definition

.

This definition serves as the natural way of generalizing the interval

to higher dimensions; using Remark

to

also hold in the multidimensional analogue.

For clarity, we provide two concrete examples of properties that our multidimensional Zeckendorf representations inherit through the the map

3.1
Gaussian Convergence

Definition 3.2
.

(Associated Probability Space to a Positive Linear Recurrence Sequence)
[
MW1
]
. Let
(H_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a PLRS. For each
n
, consider the discrete outcome space

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}\ :=\ \{H_{n},\ H_{n}+1,\ \ldots,\ H_{n+1}-1\}

with probability measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(A)\ :=\ \sum_{\omega\in A}\dfrac{1}{H_{n+1}-H_{n}},\ A\subset\Omega_{n}.

In addition, define the random variable
K_{n}
by setting
K_{n}(\omega)
equal to the number of summands of
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
.

This definition is extended naturally to higher dimensions in the following way.

Definition 3.3
.

(Inherited Probability Space to a Positive Linear Recurrence Vector Sequence). Let
\vec{c}
be a weakly decreasing vector and
c_{k}=1
. Consider the discrete outcome space

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}^{\vec{c}}\ :=\ R_{n}^{\vec{c}},

with probability measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\vec{c}}(B)\ :=\ \mathbb{P}_{n}(S_{n}[B]),\ B\subset\Omega_{n}^{\vec{c}};

where
S_{n}
is defined in Definition
2.1
. We define the random variable
K_{n}^{\vec{c}}
by setting
K_{n}^{\vec{c}}(\omega)
equal to the number of summands of
\omega\in\Omega_{n}^{\vec{c}}
; equivalently
K_{n}^{\vec{c}}(\omega)=G(a)
, where
a
is the unique
\vec{c}
-SR of
\omega
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The map
{S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}}:R_{n}^{\vec{c}}\rightarrow[X_{n},X_{n+1})
is a bijection and
K_{n}^{\vec{c}}(w)=K_{n}(S_{n}(w))
.

Proof.

By Definition
3.1
we have that
S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}
is surjective.
Suppose that
\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2}\in R_{n}^{\vec{c}}
are such that
S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}(\vec{v}_{1})=S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}(\vec{v}_{2})
. Let
a=(a_{n})_{n=1}^{m_{1}}
and
b=(b_{n})_{n=1}^{m_{2}}
the unique representations for
\vec{v}_{1}
and
\vec{v}_{2}
respectively, that is

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{1}\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{m_{1}}a_{n}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-n}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \vec{v}_{2}\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{n=1}^{m_{2}}b_{n}\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{-n}.

Then, due to Lemma
2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}(\vec{v_{1}})\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{m_{1}}a_{n}X_{m_{1}-n}\ \ \text{and}\ \ S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}(\vec{v}_{2})\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{m_{2}}a_{n}X_{m_{2}-n}.

Notice that
(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a PLRS and both
\sum_{n=1}^{m_{1}}a_{n}X_{m_{1}-n}
and
\sum_{n=1}^{m_{2}}a_{n}X_{m_{2}-n}
are satisfying representations. Thus, by the uniqueness of a PLRS representation (
[
MW1
]
, [
Theorem 1.1
]) we have that
m_{1}=m_{2}
and
a_{n}=b_{n}
for all
n\leq m_{1}
. In other words,
a=b
, which proves that the function is indeed injective. With this we have proven that
S_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{R_{n}^{\vec{c}}}
is bijective. Lastly, let
\vec{v}\in R_{n}
and
a
be its unique
\vec{c}
-SR. We have

\displaystyle K_{n}^{\vec{c}}(\vec{v})\

\displaystyle=\ G(a)\ =\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n},

which is exactly the number of summands of
S_{n}(\vec{v})
.

∎

In the 1950s, Lekkerkerker
[
Lekk
]
answered the question:
On average, how many summands are needed in the Zeckendorf decomposition?

4

Sometimes it takes a while for papers to be widely seen, for example Lekkerkerker’s theorem on the number of summands in Zeckendorf decompositions was published 20 years before Zeckendorf’s paper!
He later proved that for every

, as
n\rightarrow\infty
the average number of summands needed is
n/(\phi^{2}+1)
, where
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is the golden ratio.
Since Zeckendorf’s theorem has been generalized one can naturally ask if Lekkerkerker’s theorem still holds for these various generalizations. In
[
MW1
]
it was proved that the Gaussian convergence is a property that holds in general for a PLRS. More specifically,
\mathbb{E}[K_{n}]
and Var
(K_{n})
are of order
n
, and as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
K_{n}
converges to a Gaussian (see
[
KKMW
]
,
[
BILMT1
]
and
[
Lekk
]
). This result is generalized as follows.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\{\vec{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be a
\vec{c}
-recurrence for a weakly decreasing vector
\vec{c}
where
c_{k}=1
. Then
\mathbb{E}[K_{n}^{\vec{c}}]
and
Var
(K_{n}^{\vec{c}})
are of order
n
, and as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
K_{n}^{\vec{c}}
converges to a Gaussian.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

3.2
Summand Minimality

We call a representation of
\vec{v}

summand minimal
if no other representations of
\vec{v}
uses fewer summands. We say that a Positive Linear Recurrence Vector
\vec{\mathbf{X}}
is
summand minimal

if its
\vec{c}-SR
is summand minimal for all
\vec{v}
. In
[
CHHMPV1
]
it is proved that a PLRS is summand minimal if and only if its recurrence vector is weakly decreasing. This result is now naturally generalizes as follows.

Theorem 3.6
.

A
\vec{c}
-recurrence with recurrence vector
(c_{1},\ \ldots,\ c_{k})
is summand minimal if and only if
c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\cdots\geq c_{k}
; i.e.,
\vec{c}
is weakly increasing.

Proof.

This follows directly from
[
CHHMPV1
]
, Theorem 1.1.
Indeed, all of the
\vec{c}
-SR translate into legal representations in
{\mathbb{Z}}
, where the theorem holds.

∎
