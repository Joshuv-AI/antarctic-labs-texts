Prevalence and Associated Factors of Frailty in Middle-Aged and Elderly Patients with Atrial Fibrillation: An Exploratory Machine Learning Analysis
Authors: Tingting Liao, Yanmei Gan, Lingfang Liu, Maoyuan Tang, Lu Gan, Gaoye Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty significantly increases the risk of adverse outcomes in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), particularly among middle-aged and elderly individuals. Despite its clinical importance, there is a lack of efficient, multidimensional identification tools specifically tailored for this population. This study aimed to identify clinical and psychological factors associated with frailty and evaluate machine learning-based identification frameworks for middle-aged and elderly AF patients.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study of 501 AF patients, the dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (80%) and an independent test set (20%). Within the training set, five-fold cross-validation was implemented for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection via LASSO penalized regression (λ1se). Seven machine learning algorithms were compared against the logistic regression model. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to identify key frailty-related factors and provide model interpretability. Performance was explicitly assessed on the independent test set using the Area Under the Curve (AUC), Brier score, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).ResultsFrailty prevalence was 36.73%. Smoking (OR=3.36), mild cognitive impairment (OR=2.04), valvular heart disease (OR=2.08), and depressive/anxiety symptoms were independently associated with frailty. On the independent test set, CatBoost achieved the highest AUC (0.836, 95% CI: 0.758–0.915), and Brier score (0.169), with a sensitivity of 84.4% and a specificity of 67.6% at the 0.5 threshold. In comparison, the simplified logistic model demonstrated a sensitivity of 85.9% and a specificity of 35.1% (AUC=0.784, P=0.125). SHAP and DCA validated model interpretability and net clinical benefit.ConclusionFrailty is prevalent among middle-aged and elderly AF patients and is associated with clinical and psychological determinants. While machine learning algorithms provide robust identification, a simplified regression framework offers comparable accuracy with lower clinical complexity. Given the cross-sectional design, external validation in prospective cohorts is essential before clinical application can be considered.Keywords: frailty, middle-aged and older adults, atrial Fibrillation, prevalence, machine learning

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is among the most prevalent clinical arrhythmias, with a frequency that increases markedly with age. It is frequently linked to high disability and mortality rates, representing a significant public health challenge globally and specifically within the aging Chinese population,1–3 where estimates indicate approximately 4.87 million affected adults aged 60 and above, and this burden is expected to grow further with the accelerating aging of the population.4 In this population, frailty, is a clinical recognizable state of increased vulnerability resulting from age-related decline in physiological reserve, and it is highly prevalent among patients with AF, has emerged as a critical concern.5 A multinational European study6 of AF patients in 250 cardiac centers showed that 59.6% were pre-frail and 21.3% were frail, translating to over 80% experiencing frailty or pre-frailty. More critically, frailty significantly elevates the risk of stroke and mortality in AF patients and prolongs their hospital length of stay.7 Therefore, timely identification of frailty status is of critical importance for stratifying patient risk and facilitating appropriate clinical management.

Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome associated with a complex interplay of diverse factors, encompassing demographic characteristics, comorbidities, physiological function, and psychosocial status.8 Aging is considered a primary factor, linked to a multisystem decline involving physiological reserves, muscle mass capillary density and oxidative capacity which collectively cause reduced muscle strength and functional disability.9 In addition, research10 indicates that women exhibit a higher prevalence of frailty compared to men, possibly reflecting differences in muscle mass and postmenopausal hormonal changes. In terms of clinical comorbidities, coronary heart disease accelerates frailty progression through mechanisms such as reduced physical mobility and aggravated chronic systemic inflammation.11 Furthermore, the burden of multiple chronic conditions is positively correlated with the frailty severity, as it imposes a greater physiological load and accelerates the depletion of functional reserves.12 In addition to physical diseases, behavioral and psychological factors also play a crucial role. Sleep disturbances impair physical recovery and may promote the frailty progression by disrupting metabolic processes and exacerbating inflammatory responses.13 Concurrently, psychological issues such as depression and anxiety often interact with reduced physical activity and inadequate nutritional intake, forming a vicious cycle that further elevates frailty risk.14

Although the clinical significance of frailty in patients with AF has gained increasing recognition, existing studies have largely focused on its prevalence and prognostic implications using traditional statistical methods. Most current AF-specific frailty tools rely on administrative codes or limited clinical markers, which often lack the integration of multidimensional patient-reported data, such as psychological and cognitive status. Consequently, their performance in accurately identifying concurrent frailty status remains limited.7 Several studies have demonstrated the potential of machine learning for frailty risk assessment. For instance, Isaradech et al15 developed a machine learning model for early frailty classification in Thai older adults, achieving robust performance with AUCs of 0.810 (internal validation) and 0.750 (external validation), suggesting its utility as a screening tool. Similarly, Cao et al16 constructed a frailty risk assessment model for patients with coronary heart disease using machine learning, attaining a comparable discriminative accuracy with an AUC of 0.803. In the context of AF, however, existing efforts to develop frailty identification models remain limited. Previous models often utilized traditional nomograms and were constrained by relatively small sample sizes, or failed to incorporate the non-linear associations inherent in geriatric syndromes.8 To date, the application of machine learning techniques for frailty risk identification specifically in middle-aged and elderly patients with AF has not been investigated. Given the high prevalence and substantial clinical burden of AF in this population, efforts to better identify and characterize frailty in this context are of clear clinical relevance.

In recent years, machine learning algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for medical risk assessment and factor identification.17 Rooted in data science, these approaches have been increasingly applied across diverse domains, including enterprise risk management,18 disease risk stratification,19 chronic disease surveillance,20 and surgical decision support.21 The rationale for using these approaches beyond traditional logistic regression lies in their capacity to manage high-dimensional data and uncover complex feature hierarchies that traditional linear paradigms may overlook.22 Machine learning facilitates the detection of non-linear patterns and interactions among clinical and psychosocial variables without the need for prior specification. As these algorithms represent different methodological paradigms, including tree-based, distance-based, and probabilistic approaches, and their comparative performance in the context of frailty in AF patients has not been systematically evaluated. Such an exploratory analysis allows for the identification of a most suitable identification framework, balancing discriminative performance and interpretability to inform future clinical decision-making.

Therefore, the present study utilized a cross-sectional design to address the following objectives and hypotheses. We hypothesized that (1) frailty would be prevalent among middle-aged and elderly Chinese patients with AF and would be associated with a combination of sociodemographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors; (2) machine learning algorithms would provide comparable or superior discriminative performance compared with traditional logistic regression in identifying factors associated with frailty. Accordingly, this exploratory cross-sectional study aimed to (1) estimate the prevalence of frailty in this population; (2) identify associated factors using both logistic regression and machine learning approaches; and (3) develop and compare seven machine learning models, with model interpretation facilitated by SHAP analysis to enhance clinical explainability.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study consecutively enrolled middle-aged and elderly patients with atrial fibrillation admitted to the cardiology ward of a tertiary hospital between February 2024 and August 2025. During the study period, all patients who met the eligibility criteria were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 45 years; (2) meeting the diagnostic criteria for AF based on medical history, physical examination, 12-lead electrocardiogram, and 24-hours Holter electrocardiogram; (3) ability to communicate effectively; (4) willingness to participate and provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) hearing impairment precluding completion of assessments; (2) diagnosed reading or writing disorders; (3) acute severe illness or clinical instability compromising cooperation; (4) current use of psychoactive medications or a documented history of chronic substance abuse.

A total of 516 questionnaires were initially screened. During the post-collection data cleaning phase, 15 cases were further excluded to ensure data integrity: (1) 7 cases were removed due to logical inconsistencies or a completion time of less than 5 minutes, and (2) 8 cases were excluded because of missing core biochemical markers in the electronic medical record system. This resulted in a final sample of 501 participants with 100% data completeness. A STROBE-style flow diagram detailing the patient selection process, including the number of patients screened, excluded with reasons, and finally included in the analysis, is presented in Figure 1. The reporting of this study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis-Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD-AI) statement.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a uniform electronic questionnaire administered through the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn). Access to the dataset was restricted to the research team via password protection to ensure confidentiality. Participants were invited to complete the questionnaires by scanning a QR code. Researchers who had received standardized training provided uniform instructions to assist them throughout the process. To ensure data integrity, the electronic questionnaire was configured to require responses for all mandatory items before submission, thereby preventing item-level missing data at the source. Printed questionnaires were available for those unable to use smartphones or who preferred a paper-based format. The completion time ranged from approximately 15 to 20 minutes. For paper-based questionnaires, researchers performed an immediate on-site inspection to ensure no items were left blank; if missing values were identified, participants were asked to complete them on the spot. Electronic submissions were automatically saved, while paper questionnaires were collected manually and subsequently double-entered into the database to minimize transcription errors. Following data collection, responses were exported to Excel and then imported into R for analysis, and all online records were subsequently deleted to maintain data security.

Measurement

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A comprehensive data collection form was utilized to gather participant information, comprising 2 main components: (1) Personal background data, including age, sex, Body Mass Index(BMI), educational level, marital status, smoking (defined as continuous smoking for ≥1 year (≥1 cigarette/day), including current and former smokers), alcohol (defined was defined as an average alcohol intake exceeding 100 mL per day for a minimum of one year, including current and former drinkers); (2) Clinical characteristics, such as New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, coronary heart disease, valvular heart disease, No. physical illness (Number of comorbidities) was defined as the presence of one or more documented chronic conditions. Based on clinical records, these mainly included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, stroke, and valvular heart disease), No. medications prescribed, history of interventional surgery, types of AF, Duration of atrial fibrillation, Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF), total cholesterol, triglyceride, serum creatinine, urea nitrogen, uric acid, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT pro-BNP). This data of Clinical characteristics was extracted from the medical electronic system. As these parameters are part of the standard clinical diagnostic protocol for AF patients at our institution, all included participants had complete medical records for these variables. Any cases with missing core clinical data during the initial screening were excluded as part of the data cleaning process to ensure a complete-case analysis.

The Chinese Version of FRAIL Scale

The FRAIL scale developed by the International Academy on Nutrition and Aging and the International Association of Gerontology and Geriatrics,24 is used to evaluate the physical frailty of patients. This scale contains five items: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illnesses and weight loss. A diagnosis of frailty is established when three or more of these criteria are met.25 The total score ranges between 0 and 5, and participants’ health status was classified as robust (score = 0), per-frailty (score = 1–2) or with physical frailty (score = 3–5). In the present study, to specifically identify individuals with clinically significant functional decline, participants were further categorized into two groups: the non-frailty group (score 0–2, comprising robust and pre-frail individuals) and the frailty group (score 3–5). This binary classification was adopted to focus on the identification of patients meeting the full diagnostic criteria for frailty, who typically exhibit markedly diminished physiological reserve and higher clinical vulnerability compared to those in the pre-frail or robust stages. The Chinese adaptation by Wei et al26 demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.826.

The Beijing Version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MoCA-BJ)

The MoCA-BJ was originally developed by Nasreddine et al27 and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Yang et al28 It has been widely used in China for assessing MCI. The scale has 12 items with a total score of 30. It comprises 12 items with a total score of 30, where scores ≥26 indicate normal cognition, 16–25 indicate MCI, and scores <16 suggest severe cognitive impairment. For individuals with ≤12 years of education, one point was added to the total score. The Cronbach’sα of this scale is 0.82.

The Chinese Version of The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 was developed by American researchers Spitzer et al29 is a widely used instrument for screening and assessing depressive symptoms. It consists of 9 items that evaluate the respondent’s experiences over the past two weeks. Each item was scored on a scale of 0–3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. Based on the total score, the severity of depressive symptoms is categorized as follows: none (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). A Chinese validation study by Bian et al30 confirmed the high internal consistency of the PHQ-9, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.857.

The Chinese Version of Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7)

The GAD-7 scale was originally developed by Spitzer et al31 then translated and tested by the Chinese scholars He et al32 It was used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms experienced by participants in the past two weeks. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the day; 3 = nearly every day). Each item was scored on a scale of 0–3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. According to the total score, anxiety is classified as none (0–4 points), mild (5–9 points), moderate (10–14 points), severe (15–21 points). The Cronbach’s α of this scale is 0.898.

Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) Score

The CONUT score was developed by Ignacio de Ulíbarri J et al33 in 2005 as a tool for nutritional screening and monitoring. The CONUT score was calculated based on the values of the three indicators: serum albumin, total cholesterol, and total lymphocyte count. The total score ranging from 0 to 12, categorizing nutritional status into four levels: normal (0–1), mild (2–4), moderate (5–8), and severe (9–12). A higher total score indicates an increased risk of malnutrition.34

Ethical Considerations

This observational study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (protocol 2023-K258-01). The research team included a cardiologist and four nursing researchers. Trained investigators explained the study’s purpose and procedures to potential participants, who provided written informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire. The study’s reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

Data cleaning and statistical validation were performed prior to analysis. After importing data directly from Excel into R (version 4.3.2), no statistical imputation was required as the final dataset consisted of 501 cases with 100% data completeness. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables, depending on their distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test alongside visual inspection of histograms. Inferential analyses included chi-square tests (for categorical variables), independent t-tests (for normally distributed continuous variables), and Mann–Whitney U-tests (for non-normally distributed) to evaluate univariate associations with the outcome of interest. Detailed categorization thresholds, and coding schemes for all analyzed variables are provided in Supplementary Table S1. In addition, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed for each candidate variable to obtain crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); the full results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Due to the limited number of frailty events in the training set (n = 147) relative to the number of candidate predictors (approximately 40 parameters after dummy coding, EPV ≈ 3.7), we used LASSO penalized logistic regression with 10-fold cross validation for variable selection. The optimal penalty parameter λ was chosen as λ.1se (the largest λ within one standard error of the minimum cross validated deviance) to ensure a parsimonious model. Variables with non zero coefficients were then entered into a standard logistic regression model (refitted on the training set) to obtain adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The final model contained 8 parameters (including dummy variables), resulting in an EPV of approximately 18.4, indicating adequate stability. Multicollinearity was evaluated via variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF<5 considered acceptable Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set, and calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and the Brier score.

Machine Learning Analysis

In this study, machine learning techniques were employed to analyze and identify the possible presence of frailty in middle-aged and elderly patients with AF. Machine-learning modelling was conducted only after completing the descriptive and inferential statistical analyses. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2. To ensure reliable model evaluation, the dataset was randomly split into 80% training and 20% test sets, stratified by frailty status. A random seed (2025) was set to ensure reproducibility. All subsequent preprocessing and modeling steps were performed exclusively on the training set to prevent data leakage. Specifically, continuous variables were standardized, and categorical variables were dummy encoded within each cross-validation fold. To address class imbalance within the training set, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) with k = 5 nearest neighbors was applied exclusively to the training data within each fold of the cross-validation process. The test set remained independent and was not subjected to any over-sampling to ensure an unbiased assessment of model generalizability. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a random search strategy with 10 candidate configurations per model, targeting the optimization of ROC-AUC. This process was conducted strictly within the training set using 5-fold cross-validation, with specific search ranges detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Final model performance was comprehensively evaluated using accuracy, precision, F1 score, log loss, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Pairwise comparisons of AUCs between models were performed using DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves. Model calibration was quantitatively assessed for all evaluated models using the Brier score. Furthermore, to explicitly evaluate practical applicability, calibration curves and DCA were constructed specifically to compare the best-performing machine learning algorithm (CatBoost) against the traditional logistic regression model. To enhance interpretability, feature importance was analyzed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values from the CatBoost model and Gini coefficient importance from the XGBoost model. Additionally, SHAP dependence plots were generated for the top-ranking features to characterize their specific marginal effects on the likelihood of frailty. This ensured that the role of each predictor was transparent and clinically understandable.

The following 7 classiffers were assessed: K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), XGBoost, Naive Bayes (NB), and CatBoost. KNN is a non-parametric supervised learning method applicable to classification and regression, which classifies a sample based on the majority vote of its K closest neighbors in the training set determined by similarity measures such as Euclidean distance; it is straightforward to implement on small, low-dimensional datasets without explicit training but sensitive to imbalanced distributions and the curse of dimensionality.35–37 In contrast, ensemble methods like RF employ bagging by constructing multiple decision trees from randomly sampled data and features, aggregating their predictions to reduce variance and overfitting.38 Probabilistic approaches such as the Naive Bayes classifier operate under feature independence assumptions, leveraging Bayes’ theorem to compute posterior probabilities for efficient classification, particularly in text domains.39,40 SVM seek an optimal separating hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes and can handle non-linear patterns via kernel tricks.41 Advancements in boosting have led to powerful sequential models: GBM builds a strong predictor by iteratively correcting residuals to capture complex nonlinearities;42 its enhanced variant XGBoost incorporates regularization, second-order gradient information, and parallel processing for improved accuracy and efficiency;43 while CatBoost, specifically designed for categorical features, utilizes ordered boosting and specialized encoding to mitigate overfitting and preprocessing overhead, ensuring robustness and scalability.43 This study selected seven complementary machine learning algorithms to adapt to clinical data characteristics, including KNN for local similarity, SVM for non-linear boundaries, RF for variance reduction, GBM as a boosting baseline, XGBoost for enhanced performance, CatBoost for categorical features, and NB for efficient high-dimensional data handling.

Results

Description of the Study Participants

In terms of education, 299 (59.68%) had junior high school or below, 124 (24.75%) had high school or secondary specialized school, and 78 (15.57%) had a college or above. Most participants were married (461, 92.02%). Regarding lifestyle, 196 (39.12%) had a history of smoking and 344 (68.66%) reported alcohol use. Psychologically, 248 (49.5%) had mild depressive symptoms, 109 (21.75%) had moderate to severe symptoms, and 144 (28.74%) had no depressive symptoms. Similarly, 247 (49.3%) had mild anxiety symptoms and 55 (10.98%) had moderate anxiety symptoms. Clinically, 224 (44.71%) had MCI. Common comorbidities included hypertension (261, 52.10%), diabetes (118, 23.55%), and valvular heart disease (191, 38.12%). A majority (352, 70.26%) had one to two comorbidities. Paroxysmal AF was the predominant type (423, 84.43%). Additional details are presented in Table 1.

Associations Between the Variables and Probable Frailty

The univariate analysis revealed (Table 1) and univariate logistic regression (Supplementary Table S2) statistically significant differences in multiple baseline characteristics between the frail and non-frail groups. Patients in the frail group were significantly older than those in the non-frail group (P = 0.003), and lower educational level was strongly associated with frailty (P < 0.001), with college-educated patients showing a markedly lower likelihood of frailty (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.14–0.50). Regarding lifestyle and behavior, a history of smoking was significantly more prevalent in the frail group (P < 0.001, OR=3.70). Clinically, the presence of MCI, hypertension, diabetes, valvular heart disease, and having 3–5 comorbidities (No. physical illness) were all associated with a significantly higher likelihood of frailty (all P < 0.05, see Supplementary Table S2 for detailed ORs). Furthermore, malnutritional risk and more severe depressive and anxiety symptoms demonstrated strong positive associations with frailty status (all P < 0.001). Conversely, no statistically significant differences or associations were observed for sex, BMI, marital status, AF type, LVEF, or biochemical markers including NT-proBNP and renal function indicators (all P > 0.05).

Machine Learning Model Performance Comparison

Table 3 and Figure 3 summarized the comparative performance metrics and corresponding ROC curves of the seven machine learning models for identifying the presence of frailty. Upon comprehensive comparison, the CatBoost model demonstrated superior overall performance. It achieved the highest AUC (0.836, 95% CI: 0.758–0.915) and the lowest log-loss (0.516). At the standard 0.5 probability threshold, CatBoost exhibited a well-balanced identification capability, with a sensitivity of 84.4% and a specificity of 67.6%. While the LASSO-logistic regression model achieved a comparable AUC (0.784, 95% CI: 0.694–0.874; p=0.125) and a slightly higher sensitivity (85.9%), its specificity was markedly lower (35.1%). Other models, including SVM (AUC = 0.809) and RF (AUC = 0.803), also showed good discriminatory efficacy, Collectively, the high AUC values across all models suggest that various machine learning approaches possess good discriminatory efficacy. However, DeLong’s tests revealed that CatBoost’s AUC was not significantly different from that of the highly parsimonious LASSO-logistic regression (AUC = 0.784, 95% CI: 0.694–0.874; p=0.125) or the other machine learning models (all p > 0.05, see Supplementary Table S4).

Model Calibration and Clinical Utility

In addition to discriminative metrics, this study evaluated the models’ calibration and clinical utility. As shown in the calibration curves (Supplementary Figure S1), the model-derived probabilities of both the CatBoost and LASSO-logistic models aligned with the observed frailty proportions, following the 45-degree reference line. CatBoost yielded a Brier score of 0.169, the lowest among the evaluated models, indicating stable probabilistic accuracy. Furthermore, DCA (Supplementary Figure S2) indicated that both the CatBoost and logistic regression models provided a positive net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (approximately 0.05 to 0.40) compared to “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies, demonstrating their practical value for clinical decision-making.

Discussion

This study found that 36.73% of middle-aged and elderly AF patients were frail, a prevalence similar to that described by Sanghai et al44 and consistent with findings from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis.5 The decline in multisystem functional reserve and the accumulation of inflammatory metabolic abnormalities that accompany aging are key contributors to the underlying biological pathway of frailty.9 Furthermore, AF related alterations in cardiac function, exercise tolerance, and hemodynamic stability may further predispose patients to the frailty syndrome.45 While these findings highlight the substantial epidemiological burden of frailty in this population, the translation of these epidemiological observations into routine clinical screening tools requires caution. Before advocating for systematic implementation, the clinical utility of our identification framework must be rigorously evaluated and confirmed through external validation in diverse, longitudinal cohorts.

Consistent with earlier evidence,46,47 both our machine learning feature importance ranking (SHAP) and multivariable LASSO analysis consistently identified smoking as the most critical behavioral factor associated with frailty (LASSO OR = 3.36; XGBoost Gain ranked 1st). This correlation may be mediated through tobacco induced chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which contribute to accelerated muscle loss and impaired vascular function.48 The synergistic action of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress accelerates frailty progression by exacerbating metabolic dysregulation, suppressing muscle protein synthesis while promoting muscle catabolism, and impairing vascular function and tissue perfusion.49 While identifying smokers within the AF population may help pinpoint individuals at heightened risk, the effectiveness of integrated interventions (such as smoking cessation counseling and physical training) in reversing or delaying frailty progression remains hypothetical and strictly requires prospective interventional trials for validation.

The significant impact of both valvular heart disease and MCI within our parsimonious model underscores the complexity inherent in managing multiple coexisting conditions. Studies50 have shown that the reduced cardiac output and consequent tissue hypoperfusion linked to valvular heart disease may initiate a cascade that includes loss of skeletal muscle mass, reduced strength, and fatigue. Concurrently, patients often report symptoms including palpitations, chest tightness, and dizziness, which are frequently associated with a reduction in physical activity levels.51 MCI also impacts key self-management behaviors, leading to poorer medication adherence, reduced ability to monitor and report symptoms, and lower engagement with sustained interventions like cardiac rehabilitation.52 The presence of both conditions may contribute to a higher risk of frailty, potentially through combined pathways of diminished hemodynamic or physiological reserve and reduced cognitive capacity for complex regimen adherence. Recognizing these comorbidities is essential for risk stratification; however, whether integrating cognitive support with valvular management actively improves frailty outcomes is an area that necessitates future longitudinal investigation.

Our findings emphasize the prominent contribution of psychological factors to the recognition of frailty, with depressive and anxiety symptoms showing particularly strong associations. Within the final multivariable LASSO framework, moderately severe depressive symptoms and moderate anxiety symptoms were confirmed as independent clinical correlates. Depressive symptom is a common psychiatric comorbidity of frailty, characterized by core symptoms including anhedonia, lack of motivation, and fatigue, which are frequently associated with reduced physical activity and diminished social participation in patients.53 Interestingly, while malnutritional status demonstrated a strong association with frailty in the univariate analysis, it was not retained in the final multivariable LASSO model. This suggests that the variance explained by malnutrition may be captured or mediated by these severe psychological symptoms, as depression and anxiety directly precipitate appetite disturbances and impaired self-care.54 These results indicate that psychological evaluations (eg., PHQ-9, GAD-7) yield critical discriminatory value in frailty screening. Nonetheless, incorporating tailored psychological or nutritional interventions into AF care pathways requires further outcome-based research to substantiate actual clinical benefit.

In evaluating the classification models, our methodology combined machine learning algorithms with established feature selection metrics including SHAP and gain-based importance. The analyses consistently identified smoking, anxiety, MCI, and valvular heart disease as the primary associated factors. The consistent identification of these variables across different algorithms supports their reliability for risk stratification. Additionally, the SHAP analysis captured the non-linear contributions of sociodemographic factors like educational level that were excluded during LASSO regularization. These findings demonstrate that machine learning provides specific exploratory advantages by mapping complex interactions within broad datasets, thereby offering a detailed hierarchy of feature importance that traditional linear approaches might not detect.55

In terms of classification performance, the CatBoost model demonstrated good discriminative ability and calibration, consistent with existing evidence supporting ensemble methods in clinical tasks.56 This performance not only surpasses models previously developed for geriatric or cardiovascular cohorts, such as those by Isaradech et al15 (AUC = 0.810) and Cao et al16 (AUC = 0.803), but also exceeds the identification accuracy for perceived frailty reported in the recent ETNA-AF-Europe study57 (AUC = 0.764). The enhanced identification performance possibly reflects our comprehensive integration of multidimensional risk factors. While earlier models predominantly relied on conventional clinical or administrative variables, such as age, body weight, and basic comorbidities, this study incorporated psychological symptoms (depression and anxiety) and MCI into the machine learning framework.

A primary objective of the current analysis was to compare these machine learning approaches against traditional penalized regression to inform practical screening strategies. Model stability was ensured by addressing potential multicollinearity through LASSO-penalized regression, with all variables retained in the final model exhibiting low variance inflation factors. However, this study indicated that the performance of the CatBoost model was not statistically superior to the LASSO logistic model, which is clinically relevant because it suggests that while machine learning algorithms are valuable for uncovering core indicators, a streamlined regression model incorporating only the key clinical and psychological variables is sufficient to capture the essential variance for frailty. The simplified regression approach therefore provides comparable accuracy alongside a reduced assessment burden, making it a highly practical tool for targeted pre-screening in routine busy outpatient environments.

To further evaluate the clinical applicability of these identification frameworks, the current study comprehensively assessed model calibration and decision-making utility. The calibration plots confirmed that the evaluated models provide accurate probability estimations rather than merely ranking patients. Furthermore, the DCA mathematically demonstrated that applying the identification models, including both the CatBoost and the logistic regression, yields a higher net clinical benefit for targeted frailty screening compared to default clinical strategies.58 Given the comparable discriminatory performance between the machine learning and logistic regression models, we propose a potential two-tier identification framework to optimize clinical workflow. The simplified five-variable logistic model serves as a practical first-tier tool for the rapid identification of frailty risk in busy outpatient settings. Subsequently, individuals identified as high-risk can undergo a second-tier of comprehensive clinical assessment or be evaluated using the full machine learning framework to inform further clinical decision-making and resource allocation. This strategic approach emphasizes clinical efficiency by utilizing the streamlined model for broad screening while reserving more complex analytical tools for high-risk subsets. While these frameworks strongly support internal validity and potential utility for pre-screening, external validation remains necessary to confirm these benefits across diverse healthcare settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the identified factors and frailty, only associations can be inferred. Second, while we employed consecutive enrollment, this study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to broader populations or other clinical settings. Third, depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales. Although these are well-validated screening instruments, they are self-reported measures rather than clinical diagnoses, which may introduce information bias. Fourth, despite adjustment for multiple covariates, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured variables (eg., inflammatory biomarkers, polypharmacy) or the lack of continuous dose–response measures for certain variables (eg., pack-years of smoking or exact daily alcohol intake, beyond their binary categorization). Fifth, this study lacks an external validation cohort. Although we employed rigorous internal validation (eg., 5-fold cross-validation and an independent test set) and assessed clinical utility via DCA, the robustness of the identification framework requires further evaluation in independent, multi-center cohorts. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to clarify causal pathways, employ consecutive sampling across multiple centers to enhance representativeness, and conduct external validation to confirm the clinical applicability of these machine learning-based tools.

Conclusion

This study identified multidimensional factors associated with frailty in middle-aged and elderly patients with AF and critically evaluated the performance of frailty identification models. A parsimonious set of variables including smoking, MCI, valvular heart disease, and symptoms of depression and anxiety was found to be associated with frailty status. Although the CatBoost algorithm achieved the highest discriminative performance, it was not statistically superior to the simplified five-variable logistic model. These results suggest the potential for developing efficient screening strategies using a limited number of clinical parameters. However, given the exploratory and cross-sectional nature of this study, these findings are not intended for immediate clinical application. Future longitudinal research and external validation in independent, multi-center cohorts are essential to establish the reliability and practical utility of these identification frameworks.
