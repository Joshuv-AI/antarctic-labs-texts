Tumor genomic landscape of older patients with metastatic breast cancer☆
Authors: H. Gupta, K.D. Brantley, T. Grinda, R.A. Freedman, A. Kodali, G.J. Kirkner, M.E. Hughes, A. Higgins, A.B. Newman, S. Avdulla, J. Files, G. Suggs, S.M. Tolaney, D. Dillon, L. Sholl, A.C. Garrido-Castro, A.D. Cherniack, N.U. Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetastatic breast cancer (MBC) in older patients has distinct clinical and histologic characteristics. Elucidating the genomic basis of MBC helps identify potential therapeutic targets to improve outcomes for older patients with MBC.Patients and methodsUsing a prospective database and targeted DNA sequencing (OncoPanel), we examined MBC’s genomic landscape in older patients (age ≥70 years at MBC diagnosis) and compared findings with those in younger (aged <50 years) and middle-aged (aged 50-69 years) patients. After classifying single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number variations (CNVs) as oncogenic (via OncoKB), the frequencies of SNVs and CNVs, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and oncogenic signaling pathways were compared by age group using Fisher’s exact tests. We estimated the association between continuous age at MBC diagnosis and mutations via multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusting for race, stage at initial diagnosis, subtype, histology, and sample tested (primary versus metastatic).ResultsOur study included 2379 patients [853 (35%) younger, 1311 (55%) middle-aged, and 215 (9%) older] who underwent OncoPanel testing between 2013 and 2020. The most frequent tumor alterations in older patients were SNVs in PIK3CA (44%), TP53 (33%), CDH1 (22%) and amplifications in CCND1 (18%). After adjustment, older age was associated with higher frequency of SNVs in CDH1 [odds ratio (OR) = 1.43, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.21-1.68, q < 0.001], MAP3K1 [OR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.10-1.54, q = 0.008], and PIK3CA [OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.11-1.31, q < 0.001] and fewer SNVs in TP53 [OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.77-0.91, q < 0.001]. Patients in the older group were more likely to have tumors with ≥10 mutations/megabase than the youngest patients (26% versus 17%, P = 0.003).ConclusionsIn this large cohort of patients with MBC, the tumor genomic landscape differed between older and younger patients even after accounting for tumor subtype. Older patients were more likely to have high-TMB and PIK3CA-mutated tumors, highlighting the importance of genomic testing for treatment applications in this population.

Introduction

One-third of patients with breast cancer (BC) are aged >70 years and nearly half of all BC deaths occur in those aged >70 years.1,2 The high proportion of deaths occurring at older ages is likely due to a combination of factors, including the frequent long latency between diagnosis and recurrence, the prolonged disease courses of metastatic breast cancer (MBC), and shorter survival times due to undertreatment, treatment-related toxicity, and variable disease biology. As an example, BC in older adults is associated with less triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive BC subtypes and more luminal tumors compared with younger patients.3,4 Recent work has shown that age influences tumor genomics across cancer types.5 However, because older patients are consistently underrepresented in clinical trials and translational studies, our knowledge of tumor genomics in older cohorts is limited.6

As new genomic identification capabilities facilitate the identification of resistant or sensitive clones for targeted or personalized therapy,7 it is increasingly important to understand age-specific tumor genomic differences to provide treatment options for improved prognosis. Limited data from early-stage tumors from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) have demonstrated that the mutational landscape differs by age, with fewer TP53 and GATA3 mutations and higher proportion of mutations in PIK3CA, CDH1, and MAP3K1 in older populations.8,9 Because of tumor evolution and the selective pressure exerted by treatment, the genomic alterations in MBC can differ substantially from those in the primary tumor.10, 11, 12, 13 In a recent study in a subsample of 1000 patients with MBC, the authors did not observe differences in mutational frequencies by age for PIK3CA and MAP3K1 and instead found age-related differences in mutational frequency of genes related to therapeutic resistance mechanisms.14 However, the low numbers of MBC samples from older patients (n = 98) precluded assessment across subtypes; a limited number of patients with de novo MBC were represented and findings of therapeutic resistance mechanisms may have been driven by timing of sample collection, which differed by age group.14

Here, we seek to provide additional evidence on age and tumor genomic associations in MBC using a large longitudinal translational cohort with genomic profiling using a targeted exome, tumor-only next-generation sequencing (NGS) platform (OncoPanel).

Methods

Patients and samples

We extracted clinical and pathological data from a prospectively maintained institutional database of patients with MBC treated at the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI) (the EMBRACE cohort: Ending Metastatic Breast Cancer for Everyone) from medical and pathological electronic health records. We included patients with MBC who received care at our center and had targeted OncoPanel sequencing for at least one tumor sample, collected from a metastatic tumor sample, or, if unavailable, from a primary tumor sample between 1 July 2013, and 31 December 2020 (N = 2379). Patients were classified according to their age at metastatic diagnosis in three groups: <50 years old, 50-69 years old, and ≥70 years (Supplementary Figure 1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). We used 70 years as the age cut-off for the oldest group here owing to the high proportion of BC incidence and deaths1,15; differences in BC presentation, treatment plans, and treatment adherence16, 17, 18, 19, 20; and lower survival observed in this age group.17,18,21 BC subtypes were determined on the basis of immunohistochemical analysis with or without FISH analyses carried out for the biopsy sample available at the time of earliest metastatic diagnosis (or at primary diagnosis, if metastatic unavailable) and the biopsy sample with OncoPanel testing (either metastatic or primary tumor). According to the College of American Pathologists-American Society for Clinical Oncology classification guidelines, tumor subtypes were classified as hormone receptor (HR)-positive/HER2-negative, HR-positive/HER2-positive (HR-positive/HER2), HR-negative/HER2-positive, and triple-negative.22,23 HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors were further classified as either luminal A-like or luminal B-like; tumors were considered luminal B-like if they were grade 3 or had <10% progesterone receptor staining. We also extracted data on additional patient and tumor variables, including gender, race, stage at initial diagnosis, and tumor histology.

Tumor sequencing

Targeted tumor sequencing was completed using the DFCI-Profile tumor-only NGS platform and the validated OncoPanel assay, which includes 277 (version 1), 302 (version 2), or 447 (version 3) cancer-associated genes.24 The genomic calling pipeline has been described in detail previously and captured focal copy number variations (CNVs) and single nucleotide variants (SNVs). Mutations from gnomAD25 and ClinVar26 databases were filtered if they were not found more than once in the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC)27 database. The OncoKB tool (Memorial Kettering Cancer Center, New York, NY)28 classified mutations as oncogenic. In addition, loss-of-function (LOF) mutations in tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) were identified. CNVs were determined as oncogenic if they were high amplifications (≥6 counts) for oncogenes or as a two-copy loss for TSGs. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was measured as the total number of mutations divided by total panel size. Tumors were further classified as hypermutated using segmentation analysis to determine an inflection point for the rapid increase in TMB; this was a TMB ≥10.29 The final set of genes included 239 common to all three versions of OncoPanel.

Statistical analysis

Participant demographics and tumor characteristics were described by age group at MBC diagnosis (<50 years, 50-69 years, and ≥70 years) with medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. TMB was compared by age group at MBC diagnosis continuously via the Kruskal−Wallis test and as high versus low (≥10 versus <10 mutations/megabase)30 via the Fisher’s exact test. Frequencies of SNVs and CNVs were described by age group and compared using Fisher’s exact test—overall and within tumor molecular subtype.

We compared the genomic landscape by age using multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for race (white, black, Asian, other, and unknown), TMB, tumor histology (invasive ductal, invasive lobular, mixed, and other/unknown), subtype of metastatic tumor or primary tumor if metastatic unavailable (luminal A-like, HER2-negative luminal B-like, HER2-positive, and TNBC), initial diagnosis stage (de novo MBC versus recurrent MBC), and sample type assessed (primary versus metastatic). Age was assessed categorically (<50 years as the referent group) and as a continuous variable, with units of 10-year increase represented. Multiple comparisons were accounted for using the Benjamini−Hochberg false discovery rate. We plotted P and q values and described significant results as P < 0.05 (corresponding q value cut-off = 0.10).

SNVs or CNVs that occurred in >5% of the entire cohort and that were enriched or depleted (P < 0.05) in at least one molecular subtype were included in primary analyses. Gene-level burden of SNVs and CNVs in ≥1% to <5% of the total population was assessed separately.

Pathway analysis

Genes were grouped into 10 known oncogenic signaling pathways defined within TCGA: RTK-Ras, PI3K, TP53, cell-cycle, Myc, Notch, Wnt, TGF-β, NRF-2, and Hippo.31 We tested the association between pathway variant frequency, defined as a combined measure of SNVs and CNVs with known gain-of-function or LOF consequences, and age at MBC diagnosis using Fisher’s exact test.

Sensitivity analysis

All analyses were repeated in the subset of individuals with metastatic biopsies sequenced (excluding those with sequencing on primary tumors). Where multiple metastatic biopsies existed, the first metastatic biopsy was selected. We also conducted all analyses in a subset of individuals known to harbor no germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants (via clinical testing) to account for imperfect germline filtering in our sequencing pipeline. Finally, to understand the influence of therapeutic resistance on the observed relationships, we tested differences in the proportion of SNVs and CNVs by age group among patients with recurrent MBC stratified by prior therapy status [endocrine therapy (ET), chemotherapy, anti-HER2 therapy].

Results

Patient, tumor, and specimen characteristics by age

This analysis includes 2379 patients with MBC who underwent OncoPanel testing and had known age of diagnosis (n = 2357 women, n = 22 men) divided into three age groups [n = 853 (36%) aged <50 years, n = 1311 (55%) aged 50-69 years, and n = 215 (9%) aged ≥70 years at MBC diagnosis] (Table 1). Median age at MBC diagnosis was 54.2 years (IQR 45.6-62.7years). Compared with other age groups, those aged ≥70 years at MBC diagnosis were more likely to be Caucasian (≥70 years: 93% versus 50-69 years: 89% versus <50 years: 84%, P < 0.0001), to be diagnosed at less advanced initial stage (stage I ≥70 years: 21% versus 50-69 years: 16% versus <50 years: 10%, P < 0.0001), to have lobular histology (≥70 years: 23% versus 50-69 years: 15% versus <50 years: 7%, P < 0.0001), and to have HR-positive/HER2-negative subtype (73% versus 62% versus 53%, P < 0.0001). Somatic genomic landscape of older patients with MBC

Among the older patient group, the most common SNVs across all subtypes were PIK3CA in 44.2%, TP53 in 33.5%, CDH1 in 22.3%, ESR1 in 9.3%, and ARID1A in 8.8% (Figure 1). CNVs included amplifications in CCND1 (18%), FGFR1 (7.4%), and MCL1 (7.4%). Mutational frequencies differed by subtype. For instance, SNVs in PIK3CA were present in 56% of those with luminal A-like and 75% of those with HR-negative/HER2-positive disease, whereas they were less common in HR-positive/HER2-positive (20%) and TNBC (13%) subtypes. SNVs in TP53 were most common in TNBC (74%) or HER2-positive tumors (50%), whereas the high frequency of SNVs in CDH1 was primarily driven by HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors. CCND1 amplifications were in 23% of HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors but only in 2.6% of TNBC tumors. Genomic differences by age group

Mutational frequencies of oncogenic drivers differed by age group (Figure 1) and within molecular subtypes (Figure 2; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). SNVs of PIK3CA were significantly more frequent in the older group compared with others within the HR-positive/HER2-negative subtype (≥70 years: 52.2% versus 50-69 years: 39.2% versus <50 years: 31%; P = 0.005 for≥70 versus 50-69 years; P = 1.02 × 10−5 for ≥70 versus <50 years) (Figure 2A). This pattern remained for both luminal A-like tumors (≥70 years: 56.4% versus 50-69 years: 45.2% versus <50 years: 36.3%; P = 0.09 for ≥70 versus 50-69 years; P = 0.009 for ≥70 versus <50 years) and for HER2-negative luminal B-like tumors (≥70 years: 48.7% versus 50-69 years: 33.6% versus <50 years: 27%; P = 0.03 for ≥70 versus 50-69 years; P = 0.001 for ≥70 versus <50 years). In contrast, among HR-positive/HER2-positive tumors, mutational frequency of PIK3CA was lower in older individuals (≥70 years: 20% versus 50-69 years: 37% versus <50 years: 28%), though differences were not statistically significant. Although the frequency of CDH1 SNVs was significantly higher in older HR-positive/ HER2-negative patients (≥70 years: 27% versus <50 years: 7.6%, P = 4.3 × 10−9), this association disappeared after restricting to those with invasive lobular histology only (≥70 years: 69% versus <50 years: 68%). Considering genes mutated in <5% in the total cohort, SNVs in NF1 and RUNX1 were more likely in tumors of patients ≥70 years at diagnosis compared with those <50 years at diagnosis (NF1: 7.7% versus 0.4%, P = 0.002; RUNX1: 6.4% versus 0.4%, P = 0.03). BRCA2 mutations were less common in older patients than in younger (≥70 years: 1.3% versus <50 years: 7.6%, P = 0.005). Based on recent findings suggesting age-specific differences in ERBB2 SNVs within lobular cases,14 we examined this in our study as well. Among women with HR-positive/ HER2-negative lobular MBC (≥70 years: n = 42, 50-69 years: n = 165, <50 years: n = 41), SNVs in ERBB2 were found in 4.8% of tumors among women aged ≥70 years, 7.9% of tumors among women aged 50-69 years, and in 0% of tumors of women aged <50 years, though differences were not statistically significant. This pattern was maintained in luminal B-like tumors (≥70 years: 10.5%, 50-69 years: 17%, <50 years: 0%) but not in luminal A-like tumors.

CNVs were less frequent in older patients (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.1085400). Notably, amplification of ERBB2 in clinically HER2-positive tumors was about half as frequent among older as among younger patients (≥70 years: 44% versus <50 years: 85%, P = 0.0005). This remained statistically significant for both HR-negative/HER2-positive tumors (≥70 years: 50% versus <50 years: 94%, P = 0.008) and HR-positive/HER2-positive tumors (≥70 years: 40% versus <50 years: 80%, P = 0.04). MYC amplifications were less common in older patients than in younger patients with luminal B-like tumors (≥70 years: 10% versus <50 years: 16%, P = 0.04) (Figure 2B).

Associations of somatic mutations with age at MBC diagnosis

After adjusting for race, TMB, molecular subtype, histology, stage at initial diagnosis, sample tested (primary versus metastatic), and subjecting results to multiple hypothesis testing, among genes with alterations in ≥5% of the cohort, SNVs in CDH1 [odds ratio (OR) = 1.43, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.21-1.68, q < 0.001], MAP3K1 (OR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.10-1.54, q = 0.008), and PIK3CA (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.11-1.31, q < 0.001) were significantly enriched with a 10-year increase in age, whereas SNVs in TP53 (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.77-0.91, q < 0.001) and GATA3 were enriched with decreasing age (OR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.76-0.98, P = 0.02, q = 0.07) (Figure 3A). Statistically significant associations with age were maintained for PIK3CA and CDH1 in luminal A-like tumors and HER2-positive tumors and for TP53 in HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors. TMB according to age and subtype

Older patients had a median TMB significantly higher than the youngest group of patients [≥70 years: 7.3 (IQR = 5.3-10.8) versus <50 years: 6.8 (IQR = 4.8-9.1), P = 1.9 × 10−6] (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). By subtype, older patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors and with TNBC had a significantly higher median TMB than the youngest group (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540); within HR-positive/HER2-negative subtype, this association remained for luminal B-like tumors only. Pathway analyses

Three oncogenic signaling pathways were less altered in older individuals than in younger individuals (RTK-RAS pathway q = 0.01, TP53 pathway q = 9.76 × 10−6, and the MYC pathway q = 0.0006). Alteration frequency of the PI3K pathway was higher in older individuals than in younger individuals (q = 7.7 × 10−5). By subtype, the PI3K pathway remained statistically significantly altered in older individuals compared with younger individuals with HR-positive/HER2-negative disease (q = 2.0 × 10−5). Age differences in the MYC signaling pathway were prominent for TNBC, whereas differences in RTK-RAS pathway genes were most prominent in HER2-positive tumors (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540).

Sensitivity analysis

Among 1607 patients with metastatic samples profiled, 151 (9.4%) were older, 908 (57%) were middle-aged, and 548 (34%) were young (Table 1). Frequency of SNVs and CNVs by age were similar to the overall cohort. In adjusted models, patterns remained the same (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). Differences in TMB by age were similar in both metastatic and primary samples (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). When restricted to individuals confirmed to have no germline P/LP variants via clinical tests, results remained consistent. Among 1044 patients with recurrent MBC and metastatic biopsies sequenced and prior treatment data, compared with younger individuals, older individuals were less likely to have chemotherapy (≥70 years: 69% versus <50 years: 91%, P < 0.001) and slightly more likely to have received ET (if HR-positive primary tumor, n = 781: ≥70 years: 91.6% versus <50 years: 83.2%, P = 0.09) (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). After restricting to individuals who received either ET or chemotherapy in response to primary BC, age-mutation associations observed in the overall cohort remained (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540). Interestingly, the inverse association between TP53 mutational frequency and older age only appeared in patients with recurrent MBC who had received prior chemotherapy (≥70 years versus <50 years: P = 0.006) or prior ET (P = 0.06). The positive association between PIK3CA mutational frequency and older age was limited to patients with recurrent MBC and prior ET (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108540).

Discussion

In this translational study from a large longitudinal cohort examining the somatic genomic landscape of 2379 patients with MBC according to age, we found that older age was significantly associated with SNVs in PIK3CA, CDH1, and MAP3K1. Additionally, older patients were more likely to have a high TMB compared with younger patients.

The mutational profile of our cohort was similar to that found in two large prospective studies with whole-exome sequencing (WES) in MBC. These studies previously identified the five most frequent oncogenic genes across all ages as TP53 (43%-47%), PIK3CA (30%-42%), ESR1 (14%-17%), GATA3 (11%-18%), and KMT2C (10%-11%).10,13 Here, we found a similar proportion of patients with SNVs in TP53 (47%), PIK3CA (31%), and GATA3 (9%) but we observed a lower proportion of ESR1 mutations (7% versus 14%-17% in WES studies). As ET is known to increase ESR1 mutational frequency,32 this may be a result of earlier sample collection (at the time of primary or earliest metastatic biopsy) in the setting of less prior ET exposure.

A recent study conducted at Memorial Sloan Kettering (MSK), also examined tumor genomics within age groups using targeted sequencing.14 The investigators found enrichment in PIK3CA and CDH1 mutations in elderly patients (defined as those aged ≥65 years) for HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors, consistent with our findings. These associations have also been noted in patients with early-onset disease.8,9 In both our study and the MSK study, lobular histology was more common among elderly patients and explained the majority of the observed associations between age and CDH1 SNVs.14 In our study frequency of CDH1 mutations in patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative lobular carcinoma was more similar by age group (68% for those aged <50 years versus 69% for those aged ≥70 years), compared with the MSK study (70% for those aged <45 years versus 83% for those aged ≥65 years), though this may be due to slightly different age group comparisons. Age-specific PIK3CA mutational differences, which have been observed in the early setting and which persist throughout the evolution from primary tumor to metastasis,8,9,12,14,32,33 were not explained by the higher frequency of HR-positive/HER2-negative disease in the older age group. The association between PIK3CA mutations and older age remained significant when restricted to individuals with HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors in our study and the MSK study.14 We further showed that this association was consistent for both luminal A-like and luminal B-like subtypes. Interestingly, the relationship between older age and higher frequency of PIK3CA mutations was specific to patients with recurrent MBC who had received prior ET, though a null finding in the non-ET group may be because of small numbers. Although age-specific differences in PIK3CA mutational frequency lost significance when restricted to individuals with HER2-positive disease (≥70 years: 44% versus 50-69 years: 34% versus <50 years: 30%, P > 0.05), we found opposite trends in mutational frequency by age, dependent on HR status among these patients with HER2-positive disease (HR-positive/HER2-positive: ≥70 years: 20% versus <50 years: 28%; HR-negative/HER2-positive: ≥70 years: 75% versus <50 years: 31%).

We observed a potential difference in ERBB2 amplification across age groups among patients with HER2-positive tumors, with fewer ERBB2 amplifications in older patients. Although this finding suggests that older patients with HER2-positive disease may derive less benefit from anti-HER2 therapy, these results must be interpreted with caution given the very low numbers of older patients with HER2-positive tumors (n = 18). Anti-HER2 therapy has been shown to work best in cases with highly-amplified HER2 in the early setting,34,35 and small studies suggest a reduced response rate to trastuzumab-based treatments in the metastatic stage for patients with low-amplified HER2.36,37 This may explain the contrasting results of anti-HER2 therapies in older patients with MBC.38, 39, 40 In the MSK study, investigators did not observe differences in any CNVs by age group, including ERBB2 amplifications, though the very small absolute numbers of elderly patients with HER2-positive tumors in this study (n < 10) likely precluded their ability to detect significant findings.14 Age-associated enrichment in ERBB2 mutations in lobular HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC was seen in the MSK study.14 In our study, higher ERBB2 mutational frequency by age was seen specifically in lobular HER2-negative luminal B-like tumors (10% in those aged ≥70 years versus 0% in those aged <50 years), whereas no differences were observed for luminal A-like subtypes. As ERBB2 mutations are associated with specific targeted therapy options such as tucatinib and neratinib,41,42 our findings further support the use of routine NGS testing in older patients to uncover treatment options that spare the toxicities of cytotoxic chemotherapy.

Our study also highlights two Food and Drug Administration-approved targetable biomarkers significantly more common in older patients: PIK3CA and high TMB. As noted, PIK3CA mutations were more frequent in older individuals, especially those with HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors. Alpelisib and capivasertib are two inhibitors of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway approved for patients with PIK3CA-mutated tumors.43,44 Although age-specific data were not reported for capivasertib,43,44 in the phase III SOLAR-1 trial for alpelisib (NCT02437318), older patients (12% aged ≥75 years) experienced more gastrointestinal toxicity and hyperglycemia compared with other groups.45,46 Increased glucose levels, including hyperglycemia, are an expected side effect of PI3K1 inhibition47 but it is more difficult to tolerate in a more frail older population with comorbidities. New allosteric, mutant-selective PIK3CA inhibitors such as RLY-2608, ETX-636, and STX-478 that target only mutant PIK3CA could potentially reduce this toxicity. Given that more than half of older patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors harbor PIK3CA mutations, our findings highlight the critical importance of enrolling older patients in clinical trials of novel agents to fully understand the toxicity profile in this group.48

Pembrolizumab received tumor-agnostic approval for metastatic solid tumors with a high TMB (TMB ≥10 mut/Mb).29,49, 50, 51 In our study, the prevalence of high TMB was 21% in older patients with MBC. This is higher than reported in previous studies (with high TMB typically ranging from 5% to 8.2%)29,49, 50, 51 and may be overestimated due to targeted gene panels rather than whole-genome sequencing.52,53 Our finding of significantly higher TMB in patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors conflicts with results shown in the MSK study, where the difference was minimal and nonsignificant.14 However, our finding that high TMB increased with age (17%, 23%, and 26% in young, middle-aged, and older patients, respectively) is consistent with analyses of a large cohort of 100 000 human cancer genomes, independent of cancer type, showing a 2.4-fold difference in TMB between 10 and 90 years.54 Although TMB represents a promising biomarker in older patients with MBC, the effect of age on the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has not been elucidated owing to the low enrollment of older patients in clinical trials.55,56 Moreover, their tolerance threshold is lower, and the rate of ICI discontinuation due to immune-related adverse events is higher with increasing age in large cohorts, regardless of tumor type.57,58 Thus, development of more tolerable ICIs that are likely to be effective in older populations is an important avenue for future research.

Although our study consisted of a relatively small proportion of elderly patients (n = 215, 9% of the population tested), statistically significant differences emerged for several tumor characteristics. However, subtype analyses relied on increasingly small patient groups in HER2-positive and TNBC subsets, which limits our conclusions beyond those of the HR-positive/HER2-negative subtype and necessitates further studies with larger populations of older patients with MBC. Results may not be generalizable to the larger population given a single institution was used. We assessed mutations at a single timepoint here, precluding our ability to investigate evolution of mutations over time. Although the inclusion of samples obtained from both metastatic biopsies and primary tumors in our study represents a potential limitation, we addressed this limitation by conducting sensitivity analyses restricted to metastatic samples only, which revealed findings similar to our overall results. Mutations may reflect treatment resistance mechanisms; however, we found age-mutation associations remained consistent when restricted to individuals with prior treatments, suggesting that age-specific distribution of prior therapies did not influence results. Our analysis was limited to a targeted sequencing panel that covered 277-447 genes, depending on the version. A more comprehensive analysis using whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing may reveal additional coding and noncoding alterations that are differentially enriched across age groups. In addition, our approach does not capture complex structural variations or changes at the RNA level, which may also play an important role in the molecular landscape according to age.

In this comprehensive genomic characterization of a large sample of 2379 patients with MBC, including 215 individuals aged ≥70 years at diagnosis, we observed a significantly higher TMB and a significantly higher frequency of PI3KCA mutations in older individuals with HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors. These genomic alterations may be targeted in older patients to improve patient outcomes and pave the way for a specific precision medicine approach for older patients with MBC, a population expected to exponentially increase in the next decade.
