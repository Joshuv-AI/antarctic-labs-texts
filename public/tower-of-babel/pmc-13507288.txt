Physician adoption patterns of AI-driven clinical decision support systems in urinary tract infection management
Authors: Meitar Ben Moshe Gaash, Gabriel Chodick, Roni Romano, Shira Greenfeld, Ben Gedassi, Uri Lerner, Joseph Azuri, Shirley Shapiro Ben David
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2021, Maccabi Healthcare Services (MHS) introduced “UTI Smart-Set” (UTIS), an AI-driven decision support system (DSS) based on a machine-learning (ML) algorithm, to optimize antibiotic treatments for UTIs. UTIS reduced antibiotic mismatch–defined as pathogen resistance to prescribed empiric antibiotic based on culture—by ~ 30%, yet ~ 33% of physicians did not follow its recommendations. We aimed to characterize physicians according to UTIS implementation. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using MHS data of UTI encounters between 9/2023 and 3/2024. Analysis included 626 physicians and 15,033 encounters. We examined correlations between physicians’ characteristics and implementing UTIS recommendations, accounting for patient- and encounter-level variables. Results indicted that physicians with younger patients population (odds ratio [OR], 0.952 per year, 95% CI 0.922–0.983), diagnose more UTIs (OR 1.021 per case, 95% CI 1.007–1.035), and work within group practices (OR 1.542, 95% CI 1.02–2.333), were more likely to follow UTIS recommendations. Conversely, older physicians (OR 1.034 per year, 95% CI 1.012–1.056), Arabic sector (OR 3.474, 95% CI 1.709–7.062), and a higher volume of patients (OR 1.027 per 100 patients, 95% CI 1.003–1.052) were less likely to implement UTIS recommendations. Introduction

Machine learning (ML) algorithms have become increasingly essential in various medical applications, including diagnostics and decision-support tools such as antimicrobial resistance prediction1. Their adoption is driven by the growing availability of large-scale clinical data, enabling the development of robust, data-driven tools2. Traditionally, clinical decisions relied on guidelines and physicians’ individual experience, but modern patient care demands a multidisciplinary, data-intensive approach. By analyzing diverse patient features, ML algorithms can synthesize complex information to support clinical decision-making3. Unlike rule-based systems or inexperienced clinicians, advanced ML models, such as deep learning, are well-suited to manage the heterogeneity of patients’ characteristics3. In 2021, “Urinary Tract Infection Smart-Set” (UTIS), a ML-based AI-driven decision-support tool that we based our study on, was embedded in Maccabi Healthcare System (MHS), the second largest outpatient Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) in Israel4. UTIS designed to support empiric antibiotic treatment of urinary tract infections. UTIs are among the most common indications for outpatient antibiotic prescribing, and empiric treatment is frequently required because microbiological culture results are not available at the point of care. However, rising antimicrobial resistance, particularly to commonly used agents such as fluoroquinolones, complicates empiric decision-making. UTIS is intended to address this challenge by integrating patient-level factors with local resistance patterns to reduce the risk of antibiotic mismatch and improve adherence to guideline-concordant therapy. This approach enhanced the appropriateness of antibiotic therapy, reduced the risk of developing antibiotic resistance, lowered the risk of adverse effects, and improved patient outcomes4,5. A previous study found that by implementing UTIS’ recommendations, the measured resistance to antibiotics prescribed to patients, known as the antibiotic mismatch rate, was decreased by 30%4,6. However, the study showed that about 33% of physicians do not integrate the UTIS’s recommendations into their practice, and this unacceptance rate of UTIS recommendations was stable during the study period (the lowest monthly value was 28%, the highest was 39%, and the overall variance was < 0.1%)4,6. Thus, it is revealed that some barriers make it more challenging for some physicians to implement these DSS and use them correctly. A study examining physicians’ opinions on algorithms of this type as a decision-making aid found that 94% of physicians considered the tool effective, and 89% believed that implementing the tool would make their work easier1. Various studies have suggested potential barriers from physicians that may hinder the adoption of ML algorithms1,2,7,8. At least part of the relationship between these barriers and the patterns of implementation of DSS can be explained by the characteristics of the physicians7,9. This study aimed to identify physician’s characteristics correlating with high or low probability of implementing UTIS’s recommendation. By understanding those characteristics, implementation of DSS may become more effective and optimize antibiotic treatment.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study utilizing the database of MHS, a state-mandated health provider operating nationwide in Israel, which covers more than 2.7 million members nationwide, with over 6000 physicians and approximately 22 million medical encounters annually. MHS’ central data repository retains patient demographic data, physician data, and laboratory results using each patient’s unique national identification number. All healthcare providers within MHS use a unified electronic medical record (EMR) system. The study period was between September 2023 to March 2024.

UTIS was applicable only to encounters with urinary tract infections identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) codes. Accordingly, we identified all medical encounters with a physician diagnosis of urinary tract infection using ICD-9 code 599.0.

Inclusion criteria

The study population consisted of any physician with at least 10 medical encounters where UTIS was opened, and antibiotics were prescribed. The purpose of setting this medical encounter-based threshold was to obtain a reliable estimate of each physician’s average probability of implementing the DSS, which served as the basis for our statistical analysis.

In addition, according to UTIS definitions, the DSS was only available for individuals aged 18 and older, therefore the minimum patient age was 18.

Variable definitions

Variables included demographic and clinical variables (e.g., age, sex, specialities) related to physicians, patients, and each medical encounter. Physicians’ variables were physicians’ age, physicians’ sex, group practice affiliation, number of UTI diagnoses over the study period, mean age of attributed patients, volume of attributed patients, physician’s sector and physician’s specialty. Patients’ variables were age and sex. Medical encounter’s variables were location, is it took place at the patient’s home clinic and is it took place at urgent care settings. It was important for us to account for these variables, as we considered them likely confounders in the final analysis assessing the association between physician characteristics and the implementation of the algorithm’s recommendations.

Physicians’ variables

Specialty Included physicians were primary care physicians without formal residency (general practitioners) or with formal residency training in: family practice, internal medicine, or pediatrics.

Most of the Pediatricians (92%) did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 8% were excluded from the study due to missing values.

Interns of family medicine physicians are family medicine residents who provide supervised clinical care across a broad spectrum of primary care settings while progressively acquiring independent clinical responsibility. In this study, family medicine residents were classified separately from board-certified family physicians, reflecting differences in clinical experience, scope of independent decision-making, and level of training.

Internalists are Internal medicine specialists working as primary care physicians. Those are board-certified physicians in internal medicine who practice in community-based outpatient clinics and provide comprehensive primary care services.

Family medicine residents, family physicians working in urgent care settings, and emergency medicine physicians treating adults, were combined into a single category due to a limited number of cases in each category.

Group practice setting Group practice is a healthcare environment where multiple primary care physicians work together in the same practice or facility.

Physician’s patient population To account for changes in physician’s patient population over time, the analysis was adjusted for the total number of patients seen by the physician at each calendar quarter (three-month period) during the study period. In addition, we calculated for each physician the average age of patients and the number of medical encounters at each calendar quarter.

Physician’s sector Physicians’ sector affiliation was inferred using geospatial intelligence derived from geographic information system (GIS) analysis provided by Points Location Intelligence Ltd., a commercial geospatial analytics company. Points Intelligence facilitates advanced mapping and geographic system to identify the spatial distribution of physicians. This approach relies on highly detailed demographic data collected at the level of statistical areas.

Medical encounter’s variables

Medical encounters in urgent care setting Medical encounter variables included the care setting—either a clinic or an urgent care center—and, for clinic medical encounters, whether the encounter occurred at the patient’s home clinic or at a nonassigned clinic.

Urgent care centers provide after-hours medical services and operate outside of regular clinic hours. They are equipped with imaging, laboratory, and other diagnostic resources to facilitate timely medical care.

Patient’s home clinic defined as the clinic where the patient’s assigned physician practices.

These variables were considered important potential confounders in the final analysis evaluating the association between physician characteristics and the implementation of the algorithm’s recommendations.

Statistical analysis

We first used hierarchical logistic regression modeling (HLM) at the medical encounter-level to calculate the adjusted average predicted probability for implementing UTIS’s recommendation for each physician, accounting for patient-level and medical encounter- level characteristics. We utilized HLM to account for clustering encounters within physicians treated as a cluster (Supplementary Fig. S1). There were missing values in three physician-level variables for which multiple imputation couldn’t be perform: mean age of attributed patients of each physician, volume of patient of each physician and number of medical encounters taken by each physician. Physicians with missing values in these variables were excluded from the final analyses. Most of the physicians excluded from the analysis due to missing values were males (Supplementary Table S2).

To assess whether exclusions due to missing data occurred at random, we conducted a logistic regression analysis with missingness (excluded physicians vs. included physicians) as the outcome. Physician sex, physician age, being part of a group practice, and physician specialty were significantly associated with missing data, indicating that missingness was not completely at random (Supplementary Table S3). This finding is consistent with the observed higher proportion of male physicians among those excluded.

To assess the robustness of the final analysis, sensitivity analyses were performed. Full-sample sensitivity analysis was performed in which all physicians, including those with missing data, were retained in the analytic sample, and variables with substantial missingness were excluded from the regression models. Results of these analyses were compared with the primary findings. The final analysis included 15033 medical encounters and 626 physicians. Subsequent analyses were conducted at the physician level. Univariate analyses were performed to examine associations between physicians’ charactersitics and implemetation probability. The normality of data distribution was checked with histograms, Q–Q Plots and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Chi-square test was used to compare two categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney test was used to compare continuous variables between two groups. We utilized multivariable logistic regression models aimed to find correlations between physician’s characteristics and implementation patterns of UTIS. The outcome in the first multivariable model was aimed at predicting physician belonged to the highest quartile of implementation probability, while the second model was aimed at the lowest quartile. Because we described the variability between physicians by the ordinary logistic regressions, we didn’t treat physicians as random effect factor in the HLM. To reduce the degrees of freedom and increase statistical significance, four categories of the categorical variable ‘Physician’s Specialty”, which had very low prevalence in the study population, were collapsed into a single category. In addition, the variable ‘number of medical encounters taken by each physician’ was excluded from the final analysis due to collinearity. The goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression models was assessed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Statistical significance was set at p value < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM-SPSS version 29.0.1.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The artwork was created using RStudio version 2025.05.0 Build 496 for Mac (Posit Software, PBC).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is unique in examining the characteristics of physicians associated with the adoption or non-adoption of ML–based AI-driven DSS recommendations in clinical practice.

Although many studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of such algorithms10,11, there is a disparity between these positive outcomes and infrequent implementation of their recommendations in clinical practice1,7,8. Various studies have suggested potential barriers that may hinder the adoption of ML algorithms, including concerns about safety, responsibility for errors and legal issues, reduction of patient-doctor interactions, developing technological dependency and inadequate working habits, and favoring treatment based on the physician’s clinical experience and hesitating to alter known practices1,7,8. It is reasonable to assume that at least part of the relationship between these barriers and the implementation patterns of algorithm’s recommendations may be mediated by characteristics of physicians.

There are potential explanations for the association we have found between physicians’ characteristics and the DSS’ recommendation implementation patterns. Older physicians, who may encounter greater difficulties in working consistently with new technologies (due to lower digital literacy), and have established working habits with significant clinical experience that may be harder to change, are less likely to implement the algorithm’s recommendations. Physicians managing a higher volume of patients have adapted to specific workflows, resulting in reduced time for incorporating innovations and limited capacity to explore the processes underpinning their decision-making. Consequently, this makes it more challenging for them to modify their established practices. Given the reasons discussed above, we believe that a targeted investment from healthcare organizations is required to facilitate the integration of such an effective tool among physicians belonging to these populations.

Conversely, physicians working in group practices are more likely to adopt DSS recommendations because they can share responsibility for any hesitancy or consult with a colleague in the event of uncertainty in decision-making, which makes them more comfortable with the technology. Additionally, group practices often encourage continuous learning and updates on new software, making it easier for physicians to integrate new tools into their practice. Physicians with more experience with UTI diagnoses are more comfortable using the algorithm because they are familiar with clinical guidelines and know when to rely on the algorithm’s recommendations and when to rely on their clinical experience. In addition, beyond providing patient-specific, guideline-concordant antibiotic recommendations, the algorithm streamlines administrative processes by facilitating the issuance of urine tests, cultures, and sick leave certificates. Consequently, physicians adhering to the algorithm receive positive reinforcement through the documentation of UTI diagnoses in electronic health records by enhancing workflow efficiency. This administrative advantage may contribute to the observed correlation between a higher number of UTI diagnoses and increased adherence to the algorithm’s recommendations.

Interestingly, while UTIs are more prevalent among elderly patients12, our findings suggest that physicians treating younger patients are more likely to follow UTIS’s recommendations. This observation indicates that the reason for this correlation does not stem from the fact that physicians treating younger patients are more familiar with UTI cases, as we noted for those with more UTI diagnoses in their clinical records. Instead, this correlation might be explained by the characteristics of younger patients, who typically have less complex medical history and are less likely to experience disease complications. This enables physicians to manage their care with greater confidence, feeling comfortable adhering to standard protocols without deviating from the algorithm’s recommendations. In this context, the algorithm provides an additional layer of support, allowing physicians to manage the treatment with less concern for unexpected complications.

In general, the intended role of decision-support algorithms, is to serve as an aid in the decision-making process for physicians, rather than as a tool that makes decisions on their behalf4. The goal of DSS was to enhance decision-making by supporting clinicians without undermining their autonomy or compromising patient safety. If clinicians follow DSS’ recommendations strictly without applying their clinical judgment, there could be a risk of over-reliance on technology, potentially leading to suboptimal patient outcomes, as physicians are best suited to evaluate each patient’s unique context. To address these risks, DSS was designed as a supportive tool to assist clinical judgement, not replace it. Conversely, disregarding the DSS’s recommendations could result in missed opportunities for improved patient care, given that the system is designed to provide valuable insights according to a comprehensive evaluation of a patient beyond specific professionality, scanning all available patient features and synthesizing these data to help decision-making using a trained algorithm4.

Limitation

The dataset and the research upon which the current study is based on, are exclusively from MHS. The organizational structure and behavior, and their impact on physicians’ work patterns, significantly influence the implementation or non-implementation of decision-supporting technologies, such as UTIS. Nonetheless, MHS is a large and diverse organization that includes many physicians nationwide with different backgrounds, ages, ethnicities, etc., and thus our findings are likely to be generalizable to other public care providers. In Addition, Missing data were observed in several physician-level variables, leading to the exclusion of a subset of physicians from the primary analyses. Importantly, missingness was not completely at random and was associated with specific physician characteristics, including sex, age, specialty, and group practice affiliation. Sensitivity analyses were based on alternative model specifications rather than imputation-based approaches, and therefore residual bias related to non-random missingness cannot be entirely excluded. Nevertheless, full-sample sensitivity analysis demonstrated overall consistency of the findings. Furthermore, our analysis was limited to administrative data, and there may be other important confounders (such as beliefs, attitudes, personality or prior experience) associated with physicians’ adoption of virtual health care that we were unable to consider. In addition, this is a data-driven study, and therefore, it is impossible to delve into the clinical considerations of the physician at each medical encounter. Further research will be required to understand the broader factors involved and whether additional elements are influencing the implementation or non-implementation of such systems, beyond the characteristics of the physician. Follow-up research on this topic is planned.

Conclusion

Our study found specific physician characteristics strongly associated with their likelihood of adopting or not adopting ML based DSS, such as physician’s age, patient population’s charactersitics, sector affiliation and number of UTI diagnoses. The enhanced integration of DSS represents a critical imperative for future healthcare delivery models. Identifying correlates for algorithm implementation could potentially enable healthcare organizations to strategically target interventions toward practitioner subpopulations requiring additional support for optimal DSS integration.
