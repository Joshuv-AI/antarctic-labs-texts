Integrated chest-tube analgesia after thoracoscopic surgery: rethinking the role of chest tubes
Authors: Ji-Yoon Jung, Tae-Yun Sung
Published: 2026

Postoperative pain remains an important barrier to enhanced recovery after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Although VATS is less invasive than open thoracotomy, the indwelling chest tube continues to represent one of the most persistent and clinically relevant sources of postoperative pain. In particular, the indwelling chest tube is not simply a passive drainage device. Its contact with the parietal pleura and intercostal structures may provoke nociceptive and neuropathic pain, interfere with coughing and deep breathing, and impede early mobilization (1). Accordingly, contemporary enhanced recovery pathways for lung surgery increasingly recognize chest tube–related pain as a specific therapeutic target, emphasizing multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia, regional anesthetic techniques, optimized chest-drain management, and early drain removal to facilitate functional recovery (1-3). Despite these advances, however, chest drainage remains indispensable after lung surgery, making chest tube–related pain an unresolved challenge rather than a problem that can simply be avoided.

Against this background, Tang et al. evaluated a drainage-analgesia integrated (DAI) chest tube designed to deliver local anesthetics directly into the pleural space after thoracoscopic lung resection (4). The device incorporates a dedicated infusion lumen and multiple lateral outlets within the wall of the drainage tube, thereby combining pleural drainage and localized drug delivery in a single system. In this prospective, single-center, open-label randomized trial, 47 patients were allocated to receive either the DAI chest tube or a conventional chest tube. Both groups received the same systemic analgesic regimen, including intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with a continuous background infusion of sufentanil, whereas the DAI group additionally received a mixture of ropivacaine and lidocaine through the integrated infusion channel. The DAI group had lower resting and coughing pain scores at 24 and 48 hours, fewer PCA activations, and higher Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) scores at 48 hours and at chest-tube removal. However, cumulative sufentanil consumption, drainage outcomes, chest-tube duration, and postoperative length of stay did not differ significantly between groups.

The principal significance of the DAI system is conceptual as much as technical. Chest tubes have traditionally been regarded as unavoidable generators of postoperative pain, with their adverse effects mitigated primarily by reducing tube size and number and removing the tube as early as clinically appropriate (2). Rather than simply introducing another analgesic technique, the DAI system challenges the conventional view of the chest tube as a passive drainage device and redefines it as a therapeutic interface capable of targeting pain at its source. Intrapleural administration of local anesthetics is not a new strategy. Previous clinical studies suggest that intrapleural local anesthetics can reduce cough-evoked or chest tube-related pain and may decrease supplementary analgesic requirements after thoracoscopic surgery (5-7). More recently, Tang et al. showed that the concentration of intrapleural ropivacaine may influence analgesic efficacy after uniportal VATS (8). The principal innovation of the current study therefore lies not in the analgesic route itself, but in integrating that route into an otherwise necessary drainage device.

This integration may offer meaningful practical advantages. Conventional intrapleural analgesic techniques can require a separate catheter, repeated manipulation of the drainage system, or additional infusion equipment (5-7,9). By integrating a drug-delivery lumen into the chest tube itself, the device eliminates the need for an additional puncture while avoiding the placement and management of a separate catheter. More importantly, this design may extend the role of the chest tube beyond postoperative analgesia, allowing it to serve as a potential therapeutic platform for targeted intrapleural delivery of anti-inflammatory agents, antibiotics, fibrinolytics, or pleurodesis agents. However, conceptual elegance and technical convenience do not necessarily translate into meaningful clinical benefit. Ultimately, adoption of any new perioperative device should be driven not merely by statistically significant reductions in pain scores, but by demonstrable improvements in patient-centered outcomes, including opioid exposure, pulmonary recovery, functional rehabilitation, complications, and cost-effectiveness.

The observed reductions in pain scores at 24 and 48 hours provide a promising preliminary efficacy signal. These time points are relevant because patients remain engaged in coughing exercises, ambulation, and pulmonary rehabilitation while the chest tube is still in place. The accompanying improvement in QoR-15 scores also suggests that the intervention may have affected the broader patient experience rather than pain intensity alone. However, the magnitude and robustness of this benefit require cautious interpretation. The study included only 47 patients, of whom 22 received the investigational device. A trial of this size cannot provide precise estimates of treatment effects or adequately characterize uncommon device-related adverse events. Furthermore, because the appearance and connections of the two chest tubes differed, patients, clinicians, and ward staff were not blinded. This limitation is particularly relevant when the primary and secondary endpoints include self-reported pain, PCA activation, and quality-of-recovery scores, all of which are potentially susceptible to expectation and performance bias.

The opioid-related findings deserve particular attention. Although the number of PCA activations was statistically lower in the DAI group, the absolute difference was modest, averaging 0.1 vs. 0.9 activations during the first 48 postoperative hours. Indeed, 14 of 25 patients in the control group did not activate the PCA device at all. Fewer activation attempts may indicate reduced perceived breakthrough pain; however, the statistically significant difference should not be equated with a clinically substantial opioid-sparing effect. More importantly, both groups received a continuous background infusion of sufentanil at 2 μg/h. Consequently, cumulative sufentanil exposure was driven predominantly by the programmed background infusion rather than by patient demand. This design likely reduced the ability of the study to detect a true difference in systemic opioid requirements and may explain why total sufentanil consumption remained similar despite lower pain scores in the DAI group. Future trials should consider demand-only PCA or protocolized rescue opioids without a mandatory background infusion.

Another unresolved question is how integrated intrapleural analgesia should be positioned relative to contemporary regional techniques. Procedure-specific recommendations for VATS support basic analgesia with acetaminophen and a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug or cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor, unless contraindicated, and recommend thoracic paravertebral or erector spinae plane block as first-choice regional techniques (3). In the study by Tang et al., regional blocks were not routinely incorporated, and postoperative nonopioid analgesia consisted mainly of paracetamol. The trial therefore does not establish whether the DAI system would provide additional benefit within a comprehensive contemporary pathway that already includes regional blockade and scheduled multimodal analgesics. At present, it should not be considered a replacement for established regional techniques. Its potential role may instead be complementary, although this possibility requires direct evaluation in patients receiving contemporary regional analgesia.

The local anesthetic regimen also warrants closer pharmacological evaluation. The infusion solution contained 100 mg of ropivacaine and 400 mg of lidocaine diluted to a total volume of 300 mL, with an initial 20-mL bolus followed by programmed 20-mL doses every 4 hours. No clinical manifestations of local anesthetic systemic toxicity were reported, which is reassuring but insufficient to establish safety. The pleural surface permits systemic absorption, and repeated intrapleural administration may increase systemic exposure. In a pharmacokinetic study of repeated intrapleural ropivacaine, plasma concentrations were higher after repeated dosing than after a single administration, although no serious toxicity was observed in the small study population (10). Because lidocaine and ropivacaine were administered in combination, the potential for additive systemic toxicity should be considered. Future studies should report cumulative dose relative to body weight and include serial plasma concentration measurements (10). They should also prespecify neurological and cardiovascular surveillance and management pathways for systemic toxicity (11). The incremental advantage of combining lidocaine with ropivacaine, rather than using a single agent at the minimum effective concentration, also remains to be established.

Tang et al. should be commended for subjecting a practical device innovation to randomized evaluation rather than presenting technical feasibility alone. The study suggests that localized drug delivery through an integrated chest tube can be implemented without interfering with routine drainage and may improve early pain-related outcomes. It also introduces a useful change in perspective: a device that contributes to postoperative pain may simultaneously serve as a route for targeted treatment. However, the current findings should be considered exploratory. Adequately powered multicenter trials should incorporate concealed allocation, blinded outcome assessment, standardized multimodal analgesia, and comparators that reflect current regional-anesthesia practice. Outcomes should extend beyond pain scores at isolated time points to include demand-driven rescue-opioid consumption, dynamic pain during functional recovery, pulmonary outcomes, patient-reported recovery, and device- and local-anesthetic-related safety.

Whether integrated chest tubes will ultimately become part of routine thoracic practice remains uncertain. Larger comparative trials against contemporary multimodal analgesic pathways are clearly required. Nevertheless, Tang et al. have accomplished something equally important by encouraging us to reconsider the chest tube as a potential therapeutic platform rather than merely a drainage device. If future studies confirm its clinical value, the chest tube may no longer be viewed simply as the cause of postoperative pain, but also as part of its solution.
