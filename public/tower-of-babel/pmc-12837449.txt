The association of frailty and mechanical power with hospital mortality in critically ill patients: a retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV and eICU database
Authors: Jiacheng Shen, Kun Fang, Yu Qiu, Li Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty, characterized by diminished physiological reserve, and high mechanical power are well-established risk factors for mortality in critically ill patients. However, the potential synergistic impact of these two factors on clinical outcomes remains insufficiently explored.MethodsBaseline data from critically ill patients were extracted from the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD databases. Frailty was evaluated using the Laboratory-based Frailty Index (FI-Lab), and mechanical ventilation intensity was quantified by mechanical power (MP). Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) and logistic regression analyses were employed to assess the associations between FI-Lab, MP, their interaction, and hospital mortality.ResultsA total of 17,881 critically ill patients were included—13,740 from MIMIC-IV and 4,141 from eICU. Both MP (MIMIC-IV: OR 1.126; 95% CI 1.093–1.160; eICU: OR 1.165; 95% CI 1.094–1.241) and FI-Lab (MIMIC-IV: OR 1.169; 95% CI 1.158–1.179; eICU: OR 1.240; 95% CI 1.215–1.266) showed significant associations with hospital mortality in both cohorts. The effect estimates for MP were consistent across databases (I2 = 0%; Cochran’s Q test, P = 0.34), whereas FI-Lab results demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 96.3% [89.9%–98.7%]; Cochran’s Q test, P < 0.001). To account for this variability, a GLMM was used to pool estimates across datasets. Each 5 J/min increase in MP was associated with a 7.7% higher risk of hospital mortality (OR 1.077; 95% CI 1.045–1.109), while each 0.05-unit increase in FI-Lab corresponded to an 18% increased risk (OR 1.180; 95% CI 1.171–1.190). Importantly, a statistically significant interaction was observed between MP and FI-Lab (OR 1.011; 95% CI 1.009–1.013), indicating that their combined effect exceeds the sum of individual effects.ConclusionsFI-Lab and mechanical power are independently associated with hospital mortality, and their interaction further amplifies the risk. These findings suggest that integrating frailty assessment with mechanical power monitoring may enhance risk stratification in critically ill patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03736-4.Keywords: Frailty, FI-Lab, Mechanical power, Critically ill patients, Critical care

Introduction

Mechanical power (MP) is a key metric that quantifies the energy delivered by mechanical ventilation to the lungs [1–3]. As a comprehensive parameter integrating multiple ventilatory variables, MP has garnered growing interest for its potential to guide and optimize ventilator settings [4]. However, while mechanical ventilation provides essential respiratory support, inappropriate configurations or excessive energy delivery can contribute to ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) [5, 6] and increase the risk of weaning failure [7], thereby worsening clinical outcomes.

The injurious effects of MP are amplified when lung compliance is reduced. Low-compliance lungs, particularly the “baby lung” in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are prone to increase the frequency of cyclic recruitment and derecruitment (R/D). These reopening events are transient and spatially confined, but they concentrate mechanical stress in vulnerable regions, resulting in localized high-energy impacts that markedly increase the risk of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) [8]. Thus, reduced compliance magnifies the regional stress burden imposed by a given level of MP.

Frailty may represent a critical biological substrate that further modulates this relationship. Defined as a syndrome of diminished multisystem physiological reserve [9, 10], frailty can be objectively and automatically assessed from electronic health records using the laboratory-based frailty index (FI-Lab) [11] and it is a well-established independent predictor of mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients [12]. Frail patients frequently exhibit respiratory muscle weakness, impaired diaphragmatic function, altered lung and chest wall mechanics, and small-airway dysfunction with premature closure [13–17]. These pathophysiological alterations reduce effective compliance, elevate critical opening pressures, and increase the likelihood of R/D cycles. Consequently, for any given level of MP, frail patients are more susceptible to concentrated energy deposition, greater VILI risk, and worse ICU outcomes.

Despite these converging pathways, existing studies have largely evaluated MP and frailty independently [18–21]. Whether frailty modifies the association between ventilatory energy exposure and mortality—specifically, whether it acts as an effect amplifier that enhances the detrimental impact of higher MP—remains unclear. Clarifying this impact may facilitate the development of individualized ventilation targets and promote earlier consideration of diaphragm-protective strategies. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate whether frailty modifies the harmful effects of MP in critically ill patients. Specifically, it examines the interaction between MP and FI-Lab in relation to hospital mortality.

Methods

Study population

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) v3.0 and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU) are large-scale, publicly available databases designed to support research on critical care practices and outcomes across the United States. MIMIC-IV contains data from a single academic medical center, whereas eICU aggregates information from multiple healthcare institutions. The MIMIC-IV dataset includes ICU and hospital admissions from 2008 to 2019, while the eICU dataset covers patients admitted to the ICU between 2014 and 2015. Both databases employ comprehensive and standardized data collection methods, incorporating electronic health records, patient monitoring systems, and clinician documentation. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) admission to an intensive care unit; and (3) receipt of mechanical ventilation for more than 24 h. Patients for whom MP or FI-Lab could not be calculated were excluded (see below for details).

Baseline variables

Data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases using Structured Query Language (SQL) in PostgreSQL 11.2. Collected baseline variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, race), clinical severity scores-sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), acute physiology score III (APSIII), Oxford acute severity of illness score (OASIS), Glasgow coma scale (GCS), and comorbidities such as myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, and malignancy. For missing values in demographic variables, severity scores, and comorbidity data, median imputation was applied.

Mechanical power

Mechanical power (MP) refers to the amount of energy delivered to the respiratory system per unit time during mechanical ventilation. It is a composite metric that integrates key ventilator settings: tidal volume (VT), respiratory rate (RR), peak pressure (Ppeak), plateau pressure (Pplat), and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). In this study, MP was calculated based on a previously validated comprehensive formula [1, 21]: MP (J/min) = 0.098 × VT × RR × (Ppeak − ½ × ΔP), where ΔP = Pplat − PEEP.

This formula offers a significant advantage in clinical practicality compared to the geometric gold-standard method. Although the geometric method is considered the reference standard for measuring delivered mechanical energy, its calculation is cumbersome, requiring determination of the area under the dynamic pressure–volume curve—a process that depends on specialized software and data acquisition systems not routinely available on most intensive care ventilators—thus limiting its applicability in everyday clinical practice. Furthermore, this comprehensive formula provides improved accuracy relative to the simpler surrogate formula, which prioritizes ease of use by eliminating the need for inspiratory hold maneuvers [22]. Therefore, the comprehensive formula achieves a favorable balance between clinical usability and precision.

Ventilator data were extracted at all available time points, starting from the initiation of mechanical ventilation and ending on day 3, with timestamps recorded every 4 h. The average MP over each 4-h interval was computed by weighting individual MP values by their duration of use, summing these products, and dividing by 4 h.

To minimize the influence of extreme outliers, MP values below the 2.5th percentile or above the 97.5th percentile were winsorized at those thresholds. Due to inconsistent documentation-ventilator settings are often recorded only during initiation or adjustments-there was temporal asynchrony between MP-related parameters, leading to intermittent missing data. To address this, the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was used to impute missing MP-related values with the most recent prior measurement. Cases where MP could not be calculated at specific timepoints due to insufficient data were excluded from analysis.

Frailty assessment

FI-Lab was used to assess frailty on the first day of ICU admission. The index was derived from 33 variables: 3 vital signs, 21 blood tests, 3 arterial blood gas measurements, and 6 urine tests [23]. Specifically, the components included: systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, white cell count, platelet count, hemoglobin, total bilirubin, alanine transaminase, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, lactate dehydrogenase, urea nitrogen, creatinine, glucose, potassium, sodium, calcium, phosphorus, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio (INR), partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, troponin-T, pH, PO₂, PCO₂, lactate, urine leukocytes, erythrocytes, protein, glucose, ketones, and bilirubin. Each variable was binarized (0 or 1) based on deviation from reference ranges, and FI-Lab was calculated using the cumulative deficit model (Supplementary Table 1). Patients with more than 12 missing items were excluded. Given substantial missingness of urine data in the eICU database (urine leukocytes were retained), urine parameters were omitted from FI-Lab calculation for eICU patients; those with more than 7 missing non-urine items were excluded.

Model construction

Hospital mortality was the primary binary outcome. Within each database, four nested logistic regression models were fitted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs): Model 1 adjusted for MP, FI-Lab, and duration of mechanical ventilation (hours from initiation); Model 2 additionally included age, sex, and race; Model 3 further adjusted for predefined comorbidities; and Model 4 added the interaction term (MP × FI-Lab) to Model 3. Effect modification by frailty was assessed by comparing Model 3 and Model 4 using a Type III likelihood-ratio test (ANOVAs for nested models). Heterogeneity across databases was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistics. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was then employed to pool estimates across datasets, treating hospital source as a random effect to account for inter-center variability. To further explore the MP × FI-Lab interaction, interaction plots and difference-in-differences (DID) analyses were conducted to visualize and quantify effect modification.

Nonlinear model

Given potential nonlinear associations between MP and mortality, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to model MP within fully adjusted GLMMs. Models with 3–7 knots were tested, and the optimal number was selected based on Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and log-likelihood. When assessing interaction, interaction terms were included alongside the spline basis functions for MP.

Sensitivity analysis

Two complementary modeling approaches were used to validate the robustness of the interaction findings. First, accelerated failure time (AFT) models with Weibull distribution were fitted to hospital survival time (censored at discharge or death), including MP, FI-Lab, and their interaction term, along with the same covariates as in primary models. Results were reported as time ratios (TR) with 95% CIs. Second, generalized propensity score with inverse probability weighting (GPS-IPW) was applied to create a pseudo-population balanced on baseline characteristics. GPS was estimated for MP levels, and inverse probability weights were generated using the R package CBPS.

Robustness was further evaluated through three sensitivity checks: (i) Extreme-value sensitivity: models were refitted after winsorizing observations with MP values outside the 1st–99th percentiles; (ii) Imputation strategy sensitivity: ventilator parameters were re-imputed using linear interpolation instead of LOCF, and all analyses were repeated; (iii) Component adjustment: to determine whether MP and its interaction with FI-Lab remained significant after accounting for established ventilatory variables, driving pressure (Pplat − PEEP), VT, and respiratory rate were added to the final model.

Stratified analyses

To examine potential effect modification, stratified analyses were performed according to four clinically relevant variables: age (≤ 40, 40–65, > 65 years), sex, presence of ARDS, and sepsis. These strata were selected because age and sex are well-known determinants of ICU outcomes. Stratification by ARDS status was particularly important given the markedly reduced lung compliance in these patients, which may amplify the impact of mechanical power. Similarly, sepsis was included as a subgroup due to its systemic inflammatory effects on both respiratory and circulatory systems, enabling a more refined interpretation of MP-frailty interactions in distinct pathophysiological contexts.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. Group comparisons were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Patients and study cohorts

Among the 17,881 patients included in this study, 13,740 were from the MIMIC-IV database and 4,141 from the eICU database (the detailed patient selection flowchart is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1). In the MIMIC-IV cohort, FI-Lab values across the three quantiles were categorized as < 0.476 (mild frailty), 0.476–0.581 (moderate frailty), and ≥ 0.581 (severe frailty) (Supplementary Table 1 lists the laboratory variables and their binarization thresholds used in the cumulative deficit model; patterns of missing data for these items are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2). The median MP was 12.418 J/min, with first and third quartiles at 8.811 and 18.000 J/min, respectively. Patterns of missing MP data are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 3. MP calculation was frequently delayed at the initiation of mechanical ventilation due to incomplete early recording of key parameters (Pplat, VT, PEEP, RR, and Ppeak). Consequently, the proportion of missing MP values decreased over time as documentation improved and LOCF imputation was applied. This trend differed significantly between databases: in MIMIC-IV, the missing rate dropped below 10% by 16 h, whereas in eICU, it remained around 50% after the same period. For clinical interpretability, MP was categorized into four levels-Quartile1, Quartile2, Quartile3, and Quartile4-based on rounded quartile thresholds. MP levels increased with the severity of frailty but gradually declined over time.

Baseline characteristics of critically ill patients according to frailty severity are presented in Table 1. Patients with higher frailty levels had longer hospital and ICU stays, higher hospital mortality, more severe SOFA, APS III, and OASIS scores, lower GCS scores, and a greater prevalence of liver and renal disease. Female patients exhibited lower frailty levels compared to males.

The impact of frailty and mechanical power on hospital mortality

Model fit progressively improved with increasing covariate adjustment in both databases (Supplementary Table 2). In MIMIC-IV, the inclusion of demographics and comorbidities significantly enhanced model fit, and the addition of the MP × FI-Lab interaction term yielded a further significant improvement (Model 4 vs. Model 3, p < 0.001), with Model 4 exhibiting the lowest AIC and BIC values. Similar results were observed in the eICU database, where the interaction term also significantly improved model fit (Model 4 vs. Model 3, p = 0.017). As shown in Fig. 1, ORs for MP, FI-Lab, and their interaction were derived from Model 4. In the fully adjusted multivariate logistic regression using MIMIC-IV data, the OR for MP per 5 J/min increase was 1.126 (95% CI 1.093 − 1.160), for FI-Lab per 0.05-unit increase was 1.169 (95% CI 1.158 − 1.179), and for the interaction term was 1.012 (95% CI 1.010 − 1.015). Validation using the eICU database showed generally consistent directional effects, though effect sizes varied (MP: OR 1.165, 95% CI 1.094 − 1.241; FI-Lab: OR 1.240, 95% CI 1.215 − 1.266; interaction: OR 1.007, 95% CI 1.001 − 1.012). There was no significant heterogeneity for MP across databases (I2 = 0%, Cochran’s Q test p = 0.335). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed for FI-Lab (I2 = 96.3% [89.9–98.7%]; Cochran’s Q test, p < 0.001), precluding conventional meta-analysis. Therefore, the GLMM was employed to pool estimates, incorporating hospital-level random effects to account for inter-center variability. In the GLMM, the OR for MP per 5 J/min was 1.077 (95% CI 1.045 − 1.109), for FI-Lab per 0.05-unit increase was 1.180 (95% CI 1.171 − 1.190), and for the interaction was 1.011 (95% CI 1.009 − 1.013). Variance inflation factors were low to moderate, indicating no multicollinearity or model instability (Supplementary Table 3). Residual diagnostics-including residual vs. fitted plots and QQ plots-revealed no major deviations from model assumptions and no influential outliers (Supplementary Fig. 4). Compared with the fixed-effects logistic model, the GLMM achieved a higher log-likelihood (− 148,424 vs − 149,580) and lower AIC/BIC (AIC 296,887 vs 299,197; BIC 297,088 vs 299,387). Discrimination also improved slightly: AUC 0.726 (95% CI 0.724–0.728) for the GLMM versus 0.719 (95% CI 0.717–0.721) for the fixed-effects model. DeLong’s test for two correlated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves confirmed that the difference in area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) between the two models was statistically significant (Z = 24.215, p < 0.001). Interaction plots (Supplementary Fig. 5) demonstrated a monotonic increase in hospital mortality with rising MP, with steeper slopes at higher FI-Lab levels-indicative of positive effect modification, consistent across both databases. Difference-in-differences (DID) analyses (Supplementary Tables 4–6) confirmed that FI-Lab modified the association between MP and mortality. Within each FI-Lab stratum, higher MP was associated with greater risk, and the effect size of MP increased with FI-Lab level. In the GLMM comparing MP (Knot2) vs. MP (Knot4) across FI-Lab levels, DIDs were − 0.011 (95% CI − 0.015 to − 0.007), − 0.072 (− 0.086 to − 0.058), − 0.098 (− 0.112 to − 0.084), and − 0.148 (− 0.159 to − 0.136) from low to high FI-Lab, all Tukey-adjusted p < 0.001. The progressive decline in DID magnitude indicates that FI-Lab amplifies the adverse impact of higher MP on hospital mortality.

Interaction and nonlinearity of mechanical power across frailty levels

Subgroup analysis by frailty severity (Table 2) revealed a significant modifying effect of frailty on the MP–mortality relationship (Bonferroni-adjusted p for interaction < 0.001). A consistent pattern was observed across all frailty strata, supporting the robustness of the main findings. In the mild frailty group, MP in Quartile1 was associated with significantly lower odds of mortality (OR 0.756, 95% CI 0.719–0.794, p < 0.001) compared to Quartile2, while Quartiles 3 and 4 showed progressively higher risks (OR 1.143, 95% CI 1.088–1.200, p < 0.001; OR 1.812, 95% CI 1.718–1.912, p < 0.001). A similar trend was observed in the moderate frailty group: Quartile1 had lower risk (OR 0.748, 95% CI 0.713–0.786, p < 0.001), while Quartiles 3 and 4 showed elevated risks (OR 1.238, 95% CI 1.184–1.296, p < 0.001; OR 2.143, 95% CI 2.046–2.244, p < 0.001). In the severe frailty group, Quartile1 also had reduced risk (OR 0.725, 95% CI 0.692–0.760, p < 0.001), with increasing odds in Quartiles 3 and 4 (OR 1.331, 95% CI 1.277–1.387, p < 0.001; OR 2.463, 95% CI 2.366–2.564, p < 0.001). These results indicate that the strength of the MP–mortality association increases with frailty severity, underscoring the importance of frailty-based risk stratification.

Sensitivity analysis

In Supplementary Fig. 6 A, higher MP and FI-Lab levels were associated with shorter survival times (time ratio < 1) in AFT models across eICU, MIMIC-IV, and pooled analyses. The MP × FI-Lab interaction indicated that higher frailty amplified the detrimental effect of MP on survival, resulting in a steeper mortality gradient. Forest plots confirmed consistent time ratios below 1 for MP, FI-Lab, and their interaction, with significant estimates and minimal inter-database variation. In Supplementary Fig. 6B, after GPS-IPW, covariate balance improved, and effect estimates for MP, FI-Lab, and their interaction remained stable in direction and magnitude. The steeper MP-mortality slope at higher FI-Lab levels persisted, suggesting that the observed interaction is not attributable to residual confounding. Alternative imputation using linear interpolation instead of LOCF yielded nearly identical main and interaction effects (Supplementary Fig. 6 C), confirming result stability. In outlier-trimming sensitivity analysis (1st–99th percentiles, Supplementary Fig. 6D), interaction estimates remained robust and directionally consistent. Finally, when models were adjusted for core ventilatory components—driving pressure, VT, and RR—the association between MP and mortality persisted with only modest attenuation. However, the MP, FI-Lab, and interaction effects remained statistically significant and directionally unchanged, confirming that the observed associations are independent of traditional ventilatory parameters (Supplementary Fig. 6E).

Stratified analyses by age, sex, ARDS, and sepsis

Stratified analyses demonstrated that the effect of MP on hospital mortality was significantly modified by frailty, age, sex, and clinical conditions (Fig. 3). Age-related differences were notable: among younger patients (≤ 40 years), mortality risk remained relatively stable with increasing MP in the mild frailty group but followed an L-shaped curve in those with moderate to severe frailty, rising sharply at higher MP levels. In contrast, older patients (> 65 years) exhibited a consistently steep MP–mortality gradient regardless of frailty status, reflecting heightened vulnerability. Sex also influenced this risk. Specifically, females exhibited higher overall mortality in the severe frailty, and males showed a more rapid risk increase in the moderate frailty. ARDS patients consistently displayed a U-shaped curve with the lowest risk occurring at MP levels between 12 and 18 J/minute. For both sepsis and non-sepsis patients, mortality increased with MP and the sepsis patients demonstrated a lower mortality risk than the non-sepsis patients at comparable MP levels.

Discussion

The present study is the first to report the combined effects of MP and frailty on outcomes in critically ill patients. Frailty significantly modified the association between MP and hospital mortality, with a progressively stronger effect observed as frailty severity increased. Higher MP levels were associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes, particularly among patients with moderate to severe frailty.

A number of previous studies have showed that frailty occurs frequently in the ICU and impairs both short-term and long-term outcomes [24–27]. Moreover, frailty has been shown to adversely affect respiratory diseases [28]. At the same time, recent research has reconsidered VILI in terms of energy delivery, showing that elevated MP, particularly its early or accumulated levels and duration exceeding potential limits, correlates with higher ICU mortality in patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, ARDS, and general ventilated groups [21, 29]. Although prior research has established that frailty is independently associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation duration, higher mortality, extubation failure, and increased tracheostomy rates in critically ill patients [30, 31], these investigations largely overlooked whether frailty modifies tolerance to MP. The present study builds on this foundation by directly assessing the interplay between frailty and MP, providing robust evidence that frailty status modulates the impact of mechanical ventilation. From an energy-based perspective, these findings enrich the understanding of how frailty shapes ventilatory tolerance and offer novel insights for individualized ventilatory management in critical care.

This study demonstrates that the detrimental effect of MP increases with the severity of frailty, and the interaction between MP and frailty further elevates ICU mortality risk. Several interconnected biological mechanisms may offer hypotheses for explaining this synergistic effect.

First, respiratory muscle weakness and reduced lung compliance: Frail patients often exhibit weakened respiratory muscles and decreased lung compliance [32, 33], a pattern supported by our finding of a significant negative linear relationship between FI-Lab and static lung compliance (Estimate = − 0.765, P < 0.001; Pearson’s r = − 0.047, P < 0.001). These physiological impairments increase the demand for higher levels of MP and prolong mechanical ventilation duration, thereby raising the risk of VILI and promoting further respiratory muscle atrophy—a self-reinforcing cycle of progressive deterioration. In addition, the reduced lung compliance leads to higher local power density and more frequent R/D cycles [8]. This exposes frail patients to greater risk of VILI and poorer ICU outcomes.

Second, immunosenescence: Frailty is associated with chronic systemic inflammation, known as immunosenescence [32]. This condition heightens susceptibility to acute stressors, including the inflammatory response triggered by mechanical ventilation, which may accelerate organ dysfunction and increase mortality risk.

Third, oxidative stress: Frail individuals have diminished cellular antioxidant capacity and are more vulnerable to oxidative damage [34]. Mechanical ventilation, particularly under high oxygen conditions, can exacerbate oxidative stress [35, 36], leading to injury of alveolar epithelial and vascular endothelial cells, impairing gas exchange and contributing to pulmonary inflammation.

Finally, cardiovascular strain: Frailty is frequently accompanied by compromised cardiovascular reserve. High MP alters intrathoracic pressure, affecting cardiac preload and afterload, thus increasing myocardial workload and the risk of hemodynamic instability or heart failure. At the same time, reduced physiological reserves-particularly in cardiopulmonary function-make frail patients more dependent on mechanical support. However, elevated MP may worsen lung injury, further deteriorating their clinical status and deepening frailty, creating a vicious cycle of escalating physiological decline.

Mortality risk varies across subgroups defined by above factors. Frailty reflects biological rather than chronological age [37], enabling identification of high-risk individuals even among younger patients. In younger adults (≤ 40 years), robust physiological reserves confer resilience to MP, resulting in minimal association with mortality. In contrast, older patients (> 65 years) show a steep rise in mortality with increasing MP due to age-related declines in organ function and adaptive capacity. Sex differences are evident, consistent with prior studies [38]. For ARDS patients, the nonlinear relationship between MP and adverse outcomes aligns with previously reported thresholds of 16–18 J/min [21, 39]; however, our observation of a relatively low-risk range at 12–18 J/min requires prospective validation. In sepsis, lower mortality at comparable MP levels may reflect aggressive, protocol-driven resuscitation strategies that rapidly stabilize patients and enhance tolerance to mechanical ventilation. Conversely, non-sepsis patients often lack standardized care pathways, potentially rendering them more susceptible to the injurious effects of mechanical power despite similar exposure.

FI-Lab distinguishes itself through the use of objective laboratory measurements, thereby minimizing potential biases associated with subjective clinical evaluations or patient-reported questionnaires. This methodology enhances measurement consistency, particularly when analyzing large-scale datasets derived from electronic health records [40]. By not relying on self-reported data or requiring direct clinical interventions, FI-Lab is well suited for retrospective research and routine clinical environments where laboratory testing is routinely conducted. In the ICU, its practicality—eliminating the need for specialized geriatric assessments—enables real-time, dynamic monitoring that can effectively support clinical decision-making. Despite its advantages, FI-Lab's dependence on multiple laboratory results presents a potential limitation, as incomplete or missing data may impede its application or compromise measurement accuracy. Furthermore, it does not provide a comprehensive assessment of a patient's overall health status, given that it cannot serve as a substitute for comprehensive geriatric assessments (CGA) or capture physical and cognitive domains with the same depth as established tools such as the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) or Frailty Index (FI) [41].

Although this study is retrospective design, performing a sample size estimation is a valuable approach to evaluate the reasonableness and statistical power of the model. This can confirm that the sample sizes used are sufficient to detect the observed effects reliably. The sample size calculated based on the method described by Hsieh et al. [42]. Specifically, the α was 0.05 and the power was 0.8. Given that this study leverages large-scale retrospective data from these databases, both sample sizes far exceed these thresholds.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. As an observational analysis, it cannot establish causality, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. The potential influence of unmeasured confounders on the results cannot be ruled out. Additionally, data incompleteness-particularly missing values necessary for calculating MP or the FI-Lab may have introduced selection bias due to the exclusion of patients with incomplete records. Both datasets (MIMIC-IV and eICU) are subject to potential coding and measurement errors (e.g., documentation artifacts, device recording irregularities), which may introduce misclassification. In addition, both cohorts predominantly represent ICUs of US, with the MIMIC-IV database specifically reflecting a single-center design; differences in case-mix, resources, and ventilatory practices in other health systems may limit external generalizability. Finally, a notable discrepancy was observed in the association between ventilation duration and mortality across the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases, a finding that warrants further validation in prospective cohorts. Despite these constraints, this study offers a robust framework for understanding how frailty modulates the adverse effects of mechanical power, thereby supporting the advancement of individualized ventilatory strategies in critical care.

Conclusion

Frailty significantly modifies the effect of mechanical power on hospital mortality. FI-Lab, mechanical power, and their interaction are independently associated with hospital mortality. Early identification of frailty may help clinicians identify high-risk patients requiring mechanical ventilation. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and evaluate whether frailty-guided ventilatory strategies can improve outcomes in critically ill patients.
