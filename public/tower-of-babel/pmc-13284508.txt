Transition to adult diabetes care for young adults with type 2 diabetes: Challenge and opportunity
Authors: Giovanna F Klimovitsky, Sandy C Liu, Anne E Bantle, Tasma Harindhanavudhi, Megan O Bensignor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransition from pediatric to adult care is a critical period for young adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study included a retrospective chart review of 139 patients aged 17–25 years with T2DM followed in pediatric endocrinology between 2021 and 2022. Patients were categorized as transitioned to adult care, remained in pediatric care, or lost to follow-up. Of the cohort, 33.8% transitioned to adult care, 27.3% remained in pediatric care, and 38.9% were lost to follow-up. Among those who transitioned to adult care, 48.9% established care with family medicine, 27.7% with adult endocrinology, and 23.4% with internal medicine. The mean time to transition to adult care was 19.9 ± 19.3 months, and mean HbA1c at first adult visit was 8.9 ± 3.1%. Nearly 40% of young adults were lost to follow-up, and transitions to adult care were characterized by prolonged gaps in care, suggesting the need for structured transition pathways.

Background

Youth-onset type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasingly recognized as a distinct clinical condition with more aggressive progression and earlier complications than adult-onset disease [[1], [2], [3]]. Longitudinal studies show that youth diagnosed with T2DM face disproportionately early microvascular and macrovascular complications, along with increased mortality [[4], [5]]. As more adolescents with T2DM transition to adulthood, continuity of care during this developmental shift becomes crucial. Professional guidelines identify the pediatric-to-adult transfer as a vulnerable period, especially when coordination is lacking [[6], [7]].

Despite these recommendations, transition in youth-onset T2DM is often discontinuous. Prior studies report delayed transfers, high attrition rates, and low adherence to follow-up among young adults with T2DM [[6], [8]]. The baseline metabolic status before transfer does not consistently predict whether patients engage in adult care. Instead, structural factors, such as healthcare access and social determinants, often influence engagement [[7], [9]].

Existing data also suggest that young adults with early-onset T2DM frequently establish care in primary settings rather than endocrinology [[10], [11]]. While guidelines, such as those from the current ADA (American Diabetes Association) and ISPAD (International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes), emphasize early planning and structured handoffs [[12], [13]], real-world adoption is variable [6]. To address these gaps, we evaluated the timing, follow-up status, and provider distribution among youth with T2DM transitioning within a large integrated health system.

Research design and methods

We performed a retrospective chart review of electronic health records from a tertiary care health system providing both pediatric and adult diabetes services. All follow-up encounters captured in this analysis occurred within a single large tertiary academic health center; care delivered outside this system, including federally qualified health centers or independent community practices, could not be ascertained. At the time of data collection, no formal structured transition program was in place at the pediatric endocrinology clinic. This study therefore represents baseline data preceding the implementation of a coordinated transition process, which has since been initiated. Eligible patients had type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), were aged 17–25 years, and were evaluated in pediatric endocrinology between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2022. Patients with type 1 diabetes, positive autoantibodies, or steroid-induced hyperglycemia were excluded.

Patients were classified into three mutually exclusive groups based on care status: Transitioned to Adult Care (≥1 documented diabetes-related visit with an adult provider), Remained in Pediatric Care (continued follow-up with pediatric endocrinology and no adult visit), and Lost to Follow-Up (no documented pediatric or adult diabetes visit within 12 months preceding chart review). For those who transitioned, adult provider specialty and the interval between the last pediatric and first adult visit were recorded.

Extracted variables included demographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity, language preference), age at last pediatric visit, most recent pediatric hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and HbA1c at the first adult visit when available.

Descriptive statistics summarized outcomes. Group comparisons were performed using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. Analyses were conducted using Stata 18. The study was approved by the institutional review board.

Patients in the Remained in Pediatric Care group had the highest mean age at last pediatric visit (18.8 ± 1.5 years), compared with the Transitioned to Adult Care group (17.3 ± 1.8 years) and the Lost to Follow-Up group (17.1 ± 1.7 years; p < 0.001). Gender, race, and language preference did not differ significantly among groups. However, a significantly lower proportion of patients in the Lost to Follow-Up group identified as non-Hispanic compared with those in the Transitioned to Adult Care group (25.9% vs 61.7%; p = 0.003).

Baseline glycemic control, assessed by last pediatric HbA1c, did not differ among the three groups (mean 8.4 ± 2.9%; p = 0.36). In multinomial logistic regression using Remained in Pediatric Care as the reference group, baseline HbA1c was not significantly associated with likelihood of Transitioned to Adult Care or Lost to Follow-Up (p = 0.13).

Among the 47 patients in the Transitioned to Adult Care group, 13 (27.7%) established care with adult endocrinology, 23 (48.9%) with family medicine, and 11 (23.4%) with internal medicine (Table 2). Mean time to transition was shortest for adult endocrinology (14.1 ± 16.3 months), compared with family medicine (20.5 ± 21.0 months) and internal medicine (25.6 ± 18.3 months). Median time from last pediatric to first adult visit was 11 months (IQR 2–15) for adult endocrinology, 11 months (IQR 8–25) for family medicine, and 25 months (IQR 11–41.5) for internal medicine (p = 0.35). First adult HbA1c values were 8.7 ± 3.5%, 10.0 ± 3.0%, and 7.5 ± 2.3%, respectively. Across adult care specialties, the proportion meeting guideline-based follow-up (every 3–6 months) remained low.

Discussion

Youth-onset type 2 diabetes represents a high-risk clinical phenotype marked by accelerated β-cell decline and earlier onset of diabetes-related complications compared with adult-onset disease [[1], [2], [3]]. Contemporary longitudinal data demonstrate that individuals diagnosed in adolescence or early adulthood experience disproportionately early microvascular and macrovascular complications and premature mortality [[4], [5]]. As increasing numbers of adolescents with T2DM enter adulthood, continuity across this developmental transition becomes central to long-term disease management. Professional societies recognize the transfer from pediatric to adult services as a vulnerable period, particularly when preparation and coordination are limited [[6], [7]].

In this cohort, transition was frequently characterized by discontinuity. Nearly 40% of patients did not establish documented adult diabetes care, and baseline glycemic control during pediatric follow-up did not predict subsequent care trajectory. These findings align with prior reports demonstrating high rates of attrition and suboptimal adherence to clinical practice guidelines among youth with T2DM [[6], [8]]. The absence of association between pediatric HbA1c and later engagement suggests that system-level and access-related factors may exert greater influence on continuity than pre-transfer metabolic status alone. Broader literature highlights the impact of structural barriers, including healthcare access, cost, and social determinants, on engagement in young adults with diabetes [[7], [9]].

Adult follow-up most commonly occurred in primary care settings rather than endocrinology, reflecting patterns reported in national and international cohorts of early-onset T2DM [[10], [11]]. Although this study was not designed to determine specialty-based differences in long-term outcomes, variation in time to first adult encounter underscores the importance of clearly defined referral pathways. Current ADA and ISPAD recommendations emphasize early transition planning, readiness assessment, and structured transfer processes to reduce care fragmentation [[12], [13]]. However, implementation of these structured models remains inconsistent across health systems [6].

Given the rapid progression and early onset of complications in youth-onset T2DM, even temporary disengagement during early adulthood may delay therapy intensification, complication surveillance, and cardiovascular risk management [[1], [14]]. Data from landmark youth cohorts demonstrate rapid progression of hypertension, dyslipidemia, microalbuminuria, and retinopathy within a few years of diagnosis [[15], [16], [17]]. Moreover, early-onset T2DM is associated with excess cardiovascular mortality compared with youth-onset type 1 diabetes [[15], [16]]. Within this context, transition represents not simply a transfer of care but a modifiable health system process with measurable implications for long-term outcomes.

This study provides detailed health system–level characterization of follow-up status, transition timing, and adult provider distribution within an integrated care setting. Limitations include its retrospective design, single-system scope, inability to capture external care utilization or psychosocial determinants, and the absence of insurance status data, which precluded a formal analysis of socioeconomic factors as predictors of transition outcomes. Treatment regimen data, including medication type, intensification, and adherence, were not captured in this analysis. Data collection was limited to the first adult diabetes-related visit; subsequent visit attendance, retention in care, and concurrent primary care establishment were not captured and remain important questions for future investigation. Data beyond the first adult clinic visit were not available; therefore, longitudinal glycemic trajectories following transition cannot be assessed in this cohort. Notably, patients who established care with family medicine had the highest mean HbA1c at first adult visit (10.0 ± 3.0%); while the small sample size limits formal subgroup analysis, this observation warrants further investigation in larger prospective cohorts. Future studies should incorporate treatment regimen variables, Area Deprivation Index scores derived from zip codes, and other validated social determinants of health measures to more fully characterize clinical management, structural barriers to transition, and high-risk subgroups within this population. Nonetheless, these findings add to growing evidence that transition in youth-onset T2DM remains an area of unmet need requiring structured, coordinated, and specialty-informed approaches [[6], [12]].

Conclusions

Transition from pediatric to adult care in youth-onset T2DM was frequently accompanied by delayed linkage and incomplete follow-up within this cohort. These findings suggest that the transfer period remains a vulnerable phase in care continuity for adolescents and young adults with early-onset disease. Given the early and progressive complication burden described in youth-onset T2DM [[3], [4]] strengthening transition processes may help support sustained engagement during emerging adulthood. Future prospective studies are needed to evaluate structured, disease-specific transition strategies and their impact on long-term clinical outcomes. Our institution has since initiated a structured transition program, and prospective data collection is planned to evaluate its impact.
