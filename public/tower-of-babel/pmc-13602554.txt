Application of an AI-enabled personalized medical education model for improving clinical thinking among cardiovascular surgery trainees
Authors: Yuehang Yang, Li Sheng, Liang Huang, Qiannan Guo, Yuting Hu, Jiawei Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCardiovascular surgery involves complex clinical scenarios and high demands on clinical judgment. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled personalized teaching model in cardiovascular surgery training.MethodsFrom February to June 2025, 158 trainees in the department of cardiovascular surgery at our hospital were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 79) or a control group (n = 79). The control group received conventional teaching, including lectures, bedside teaching, and case discussions. The experimental group received a personalized teaching model integrating an intelligent question bank, staged case-based teaching, and AI-based standardized patient training. Outcomes included theoretical examination scores, the Chinese version of the California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV), clinical interview performance, and teaching satisfaction.ResultsThe experimental group achieved significantly higher theoretical examination scores than the control group (84.56 ± 5.07 vs. 77.95 ± 9.04, P < 0.001). CTDI-CV results showed significantly higher total scores and higher scores in truth-seeking, open-mindedness, analyticity, systematicity, and inquisitiveness in the experimental group (all P < 0.05). In the clinical interview assessment, the experimental group scored significantly higher in completeness of information collection, communication skills, diagnostic reasoning, and overall performance (all P < 0.001), while the between-group difference in empathy was not significant (P = 0.328). Teaching satisfaction in the experimental group was high, with an overall satisfaction score of 4.68 ± 0.39.ConclusionThe AI-enabled personalized teaching model may represent a feasible and effective approach for cardiovascular surgery education. It was associated with higher theoretical examination scores, better critical thinking disposition, higher scores in several dimensions of simulated clinical interview performance, and high learner satisfaction. Introduction

Cardiothoracic surgery is a key discipline in clinical medicine characterized by high specialization, demanding technical requirements, and substantial clinical risk. It encompasses a wide range of complex cardiovascular diseases, including congenital heart disease, valvular heart disease, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, and great vessel disorders [1]. Compared with other clinical specialties, patients undergoing cardiothoracic surgery are often critically ill and experience rapid disease progression. In addition, the incidence of perioperative complications in this population is relatively high, frequently involving the cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and neurological systems, which further contributes to the complexity and dynamic nature of their clinical conditions [2–4]. These characteristics require cardiovascular surgery trainees not only to have a solid theoretical foundation, but also to rapidly integrate patients’ symptoms, physical signs, monitoring data, and test results to make accurate clinical judgments and provide timely and effective interventions. However, for cardiovascular surgery trainees with limited clinical experience, promptly recognizing changes in patients’ conditions in the complex setting of cardiovascular surgery remains challenging, which may adversely affect subsequent clinical management and outcomes. Conventional training systems for cardiovascular surgery trainees still have notable limitations in addressing the demands of such complex clinical education. First, traditional group-based teaching typically follows standardized content and a uniform pace, making it difficult to fully accommodate individual differences among trainees in baseline knowledge and learning progression. Second, clinical skills training relies heavily on real patient cases, particularly in the teaching of critically ill and rare conditions. Owing to limited case availability and lack of reproducibility, it is often difficult to achieve an optimal balance between patient safety and adequate training opportunities [5]. In addition, teaching case design and assessment tool development have long relied on the individual clinical experience of instructors, which may limit teaching efficiency and make it difficult to ensure standardization and consistency [6]. Therefore, developing a more targeted teaching model tailored to the characteristics of cardiovascular surgery to enhance cardiovascular surgery trainees’ clinical thinking and overall response capacity has become an important issue in specialty medical education.

In recent years, with the continued development and application of artificial intelligence in healthcare, its role in medical education has attracted increasing attention [7]. This shift highlights the need for medical education to help trainees critically and appropriately use artificial intelligence (AI)-supported tools in clinical learning and practice [8]. Generative AI-based educational applications have shown particular promise in areas such as instructional content generation and clinical scenario simulation [9]. However, the application of artificial intelligence in medical education remains in an exploratory stage. ChatGPT, a generative AI application introduced by OpenAI in November 2022, has demonstrated potential for supporting personalized learning and simulating interactive clinical scenarios. Since its release, it has been increasingly adopted by students and professionals in health-related fields [10, 11]. Emerging evidence suggests that ChatGPT performs well in addressing practical clinical questions, supporting clinical case reasoning, and predicting the risk of cardiovascular disease [12, 13]. In addition, previous studies have suggested that ChatGPT-assisted teaching models may improve both theoretical knowledge acquisition and clinical skills, and have generally been well received by students [14]. Although AI has been increasingly explored in medical education, clearer pedagogical frameworks, rigorous evaluation, and integration into authentic teaching contexts remain needed [15]. Recent reviews have emphasized that AI in medical education should be evaluated with careful attention to transparency, supervision, validity of assessment, and potential risks such as hallucinations, bias, and overreliance on AI-generated content [16]. In this intervention, staged case presentation was used to organize complex cardiovascular surgery scenarios into sequential learning steps, which may help reduce cognitive load. Repeated AI-standardized patient (AI-SP) practice was designed to support deliberate practice in history taking and communication, while question-bank practice with individualized feedback was intended to promote self-regulated learning by helping trainees identify knowledge gaps. Nevertheless, generative AI may also produce inaccurate information, reflect biases in training data, and lack full transparency in its reasoning process. Therefore, expert review and teacher supervision remain essential when applying AI tools in medical education. Based on these considerations, this study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of an AI-enabled personalized clinical teaching model for cardiovascular surgery trainees, with a focus on its potential to improve clinical thinking and overall competence.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted from February to June 2025 and enrolled 158 trainees from the department of cardiovascular surgery at our hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) registered physicians or nurses currently in practice; (2) completion of the basic surgery rotation and entry into the specialized training stage in cardiovascular surgery; and (3) voluntary participation in the study with written informed consent provided. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior participation in similar AI-based medical education programs; and (2) inability to complete the full semester of teaching due to rotation arrangements (Fig. 1).

Study design and flow chart of the AI-enabled personalized teaching model and conventional teaching model for cardiovascular surgery trainees

Study design and randomization

This was a single-center randomized educational intervention study. Eligible trainees were assigned to the experimental or control group at a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random sequence. Group allocation was completed before the teaching intervention began. Because of the nature of the educational intervention, participants and instructors could not be blinded to group assignment. However, the simulated interview assessments were performed independently by two senior instructors who were blinded to group allocation.

Sample size and power consideration

The sample size was determined by the number of eligible trainees during the study period, and no formal prospective sample size calculation was conducted. A sensitivity power calculation showed that with 79 trainees per group, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and 80% power, the study could detect a standardized mean difference of approximately 0.45. For the theoretical examination score, this corresponded to a minimal detectable difference of approximately 3.32 points, while the observed between-group difference was 6.62 points.

Instructional implementation

Based on relevant cardiovascular surgery guidelines and classic case materials, ChatGPT-4o was used to assist in the generation of practice questions, staged case materials, and AI-SP communication scenarios. The practice questions covered different disease categories, including aortic dissection, complex coronary artery disease, and valvular disease, as well as varying levels of difficulty and multiple competency domains, including knowledge recall, clinical reasoning, and decision-making. Standardized prompt templates were developed before the intervention and are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. Each item in the intelligent question bank consisted of a clinical stem, answer options, and the correct answer. The items were classified according to disease category and difficulty level. Subsequently, a review panel consisting of two cardiovascular surgery experts with associate senior titles or above evaluated the generated items in terms of clinical accuracy, difficulty appropriateness, and consistency with the teaching syllabus. Items with inaccurate, ambiguous, or overly simplified content were revised or excluded to ensure the scientific validity and consistency of the teaching content. The design and reporting of this AI-supported teaching intervention were guided by the IDEAS framework for digital interventions [17].

Staged case-based teaching

Introductory module

The introductory module was developed based on the principles of problem-based learning and focused on common clinical scenarios in cardiovascular surgery, such as acute chest pain, postoperative low cardiac output, and postoperative bleeding. ChatGPT-4o was used to integrate case materials, including medical history, physical examination findings, laboratory results, and imaging data, into structured clinical cases. Interactive questions were embedded throughout the learning process to guide trainees in selecting appropriate next-step examinations, identifying critical diagnoses, and making initial management decisions. Key recommendations and explanatory notes based on relevant clinical guidelines were also provided to support learning.

Advanced module

Building on the introductory module, the advanced module incorporated more complex clinical scenarios commonly encountered in cardiovascular surgery, including perioperative multiple organ dysfunction, acute respiratory distress syndrome, postoperative low cardiac output complicated by malignant arrhythmias, acute kidney injury, and stroke. ChatGPT-4o was used to construct comprehensive case scenarios containing multiple clinical variables and selected distracting information. Trainees were required to perform patient assessment, identify major clinical problems, and formulate diagnostic and therapeutic plans. This module was intended to strengthen their ability to synthesize clinical information and make appropriate decisions in complex clinical settings.

Pre-teaching preparation

Before the formal teaching sessions, instructors provided training on the use of ChatGPT-4o and guided students in history-taking, disease explanation, and communication practice with AI-simulated patients. AI-SP interactions were conducted within predefined clinical scenarios and were used only for history-taking and communication training. Trainees were instructed not to regard AI-generated responses as authoritative clinical guidance. Instructors supervised the training process and provided feedback after the simulations to correct inaccurate or inappropriate AI-generated responses. Through iterative practice, students gradually adjusted their interviewing strategies and communication approaches to improve information-gathering and communication skills across different clinical scenarios. All participants completed standardized platform-use training before the intervention to ensure consistency in the implementation of the teaching process.

Teaching implementation

The control group received the conventional cardiothoracic surgery teaching model, which included didactic lectures on theoretical knowledge, bedside teaching with case demonstrations and analyses, and small-group discussions based on instructor-developed cases. In contrast, the experimental group received a comprehensive teaching model integrating an intelligent question bank, staged case-based teaching, and AI-SP training. Specifically, (1) theoretical thinking training: students completed clinical case modules in cardiovascular surgery and participated in online question discussions through the teaching platform on a regular basis; (2) communication skills training: students participated in one AI-SP history-taking simulation session per week; and (3) feedback and guidance: instructors regularly reviewed students’ learning data and provided targeted guidance and feedback during weekly group discussions, focusing on common problems and individual areas of weakness. The overall teaching period lasted for one semester, and all participants completed the full set of learning tasks and the final assessment.

Assessment methods

(1) Theoretical knowledge acquisition: theoretical knowledge acquisition was assessed using a standardized examination with a maximum score of 100 points. The examination covered the key principles of diagnosis and treatment for common diseases in cardiovascular surgery. All examination items were reviewed by the teaching team before use to avoid duplication with practice questions. (2) Clinical thinking ability: clinical thinking was assessed using the Chinese version of the California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV) [18]. The CTDI-CV consists of 70 items covering seven dimensions: truth-seeking, open-mindedness, analyticity, systematicity, critical thinking self-confidence, inquisitiveness, and cognitive maturity, and has demonstrated good reliability and validity. The total score ranges from 70 to 420, with higher scores indicating a stronger disposition toward critical thinking. A total score > 280 was considered indicative of an overall positive critical thinking disposition, while a subscale score > 40 was used as the reference standard for a positive disposition in the corresponding dimension. In this study, it was applied as one of the instruments reflecting cognitive dispositions related to clinical thinking, together with theoretical examination scores and simulated history-taking performance. (3) Clinical interview performance assessment: a self-developed Clinical Interview Performance Assessment Scale was used to evaluate trainees’ clinical interview performance across five dimensions: completeness of information collection, communication skills, diagnostic reasoning, empathy, and overall performance. Each dimension was rated on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating better performance (Supplementary Appendix 2). Two senior instructors blinded to group allocation independently assessed the simulated interviews, and the mean of their scores was used for analysis. Inter-rater reliability for the Clinical Interview Performance Assessment Scale was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), because the scale scores were treated as continuous variables. The scale was used as an exploratory tool to evaluate simulated interview performance. (4) Teaching satisfaction: teaching satisfaction was assessed only in the experimental group using a self-developed questionnaire that included items on stimulation of learning interest, degree of knowledge internalization, perceived improvement in clinical thinking, and perceived enhancement of communication skills. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale with reference to previous studies, and both the overall satisfaction score and individual item scores were calculated (Supplementary Appendix 3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples t test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. When the assumptions for independent-samples t tests were not adequately met, Mann–Whitney U tests were additionally performed as sensitivity analysis. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test. Scale scores were analyzed as continuous variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the CTDI-CV and clinical interview subdomain analyses. Therefore, these subdomain findings were considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously. All analyses were performed using the complete dataset, with no missing outcome data.

Theoretical examination performance

Following completion of the teaching cycle, all participants underwent a standardized staged theoretical examination. The experimental group achieved a significantly higher mean score than the control group (84.56 ± 5.07 vs. 77.95 ± 9.04, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Comparison of theoretical examination scores between the experimental and control groups. Bars represent group means, individual points represent trainee-level scores, and error bars represent 95% confidence intervals

CTDI-CV results

In the CTDI-CV assessment, the experimental group scored significantly higher than the control group in the total score as well as in the sub-dimensions of truth-seeking, open-mindedness, analyticity, systematicity, and inquisitiveness (all P < 0.05). Among these dimensions, inquisitiveness had the highest mean score in the experimental group, reaching 46.23 ± 4.32 points, followed by open-mindedness (45.88 ± 4.76 points) and analyticity (43.95 ± 4.03 points). In contrast, critical thinking self-confidence (41.20 ± 4.11 points) and cognitive maturity (40.11 ± 4.86 points) showed relatively lower mean scores, although both remained above the 40-point threshold. Truth-seeking had the lowest mean score in the experimental group (41.62 ± 4.58 points), despite still indicating a positive disposition. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the dimensions of critical thinking self-confidence and cognitive maturity (Table 2). These findings suggest that the AI-based personalized teaching model was particularly effective in improving students’ dispositions toward open-mindedness, analyticity, systematicity, and inquisitiveness, while its effects on critical thinking self-confidence and cognitive maturity appeared to be less pronounced.

Clinical interview performance

Clinical interview performance was independently assessed by two senior instructors who were blinded to group allocation, using the Clinical Interview Performance Assessment Scale. The inter-rater reliability for the overall performance score was good, with an ICC of 0.84. On this exploratory scale, the experimental group scored higher than the control group in completeness of information collection, communication skills, diagnostic reasoning, and overall performance (all P < 0.001). No statistically significant between-group difference was observed in empathy (Table 3).

Teaching satisfaction

Teaching satisfaction was generally high in the experimental group. The overall satisfaction score was 4.68 ± 0.39, with a positive response rate of 97.5% (77/79). Among the specific items, the highest mean score was observed for “This teaching model improved learning interest and initiative” (4.72 ± 0.45), followed by “AI-SP training improved confidence and skills in doctor–patient communication” (4.70 ± 0.46) and “I would recommend this model for future teaching practice” (4.66 ± 0.42) (Table 4). The positive response rates for these items were 96.2%, 96.2%, and 97.5%, respectively (Fig. 3). In addition, high levels of positive responses were also found for the intelligent question bank, staged case-based teaching, and personalized feedback, indicating that trainees generally recognized the value of this AI-based personalized teaching model in enhancing learning motivation, supporting clinical thinking, and improving communication skills. These results were therefore interpreted as indicators of learner acceptability and perceived usefulness, rather than as independent evidence of teaching effectiveness.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced artificial intelligence into the teaching process for cardiovascular surgery trainees and developed a personalized teaching model integrating an intelligent question bank, staged case-based teaching, and AI-SP communication training. Compared with the conventional teaching approach, this model was associated with better performance in theoretical knowledge assessment, clinical thinking-related outcomes, and simulated interview performance, together with high learner satisfaction. The present study aimed to evaluate the overall effectiveness of an integrated AI-enabled personalized teaching model in a real teaching setting, rather than to isolate the independent effect of AI from other interactive and practice-based teaching components.

The higher examination scores in the experimental group suggest that the intelligent question bank-supported training model was associated with better theoretical learning. The 6.62-point difference on a 100-point scale indicates not only statistical significance but also a potentially meaningful improvement in theoretical assessment performance. However, this finding should not be equated with clinical readiness or better real-world clinical performance, which requires further evaluation using workplace-based and patient-related outcomes. Compared with conventional teaching, in which content delivery and assessment design often depend on individual instructors’ experience, the intelligent question bank-supported training model enabled continuous updating of training materials based on current cardiovascular surgery guidelines and representative clinical cases, with expert review to ensure scientific rigor and clinical relevance. Accordingly, the training content may have been more closely aligned with current clinical practice. Moreover, the intelligent question bank-supported training model also enabled targeted practice based on individual performance, which may have helped trainees address specific knowledge gaps more efficiently. This advantage may be particularly relevant in cardiovascular surgery, where trainees are required to master a large and continually evolving body of knowledge within a limited training period.

Clinical reasoning develops through repeated exposure to clinical scenarios and reflective engagement in the decision-making process. Conventional case discussions are often based on static medical records, with limited trainee participation in diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making; consequently, learning tends to focus more on outcome interpretation than on the reasoning process itself [19]. In this study, staged case-based teaching may have supported clinical reasoning by presenting clinical information progressively and guiding trainees through key steps such as information gathering, problem identification, differential diagnosis, and management planning. By dividing complex cardiovascular surgery scenarios into sequential learning steps, this approach may reduce cognitive load and provide more opportunities for active reasoning. This may be particularly relevant in cardiovascular surgery, where trainees need to integrate clinical, imaging, hemodynamic, and perioperative information in complex and time-sensitive situations.

The CTDI-CV is widely used to assess critical thinking disposition in medical education and reflects multiple dimensions of learners’ thinking during clinical problem analysis and decision-making [18]. The experimental group showed higher overall CTDI-CV scores and higher scores in several dimensions related to active inquiry, analytical thinking, and systematic reasoning. These findings suggest that the intervention may support more structured engagement with clinical problems. The CTDI-CV total score difference also appears educationally relevant, as the experimental group exceeded the commonly used positive disposition threshold of 280, whereas the control group remained below this threshold. This suggests a shift toward an overall positive critical thinking disposition, although the CTDI-CV does not directly measure cardiovascular surgery-specific reasoning ability. However, the lack of significant differences in critical thinking self-confidence and cognitive maturity indicates that some dimensions of critical thinking may require longer clinical exposure and may be less responsive to short-term educational intervention.

Given the complexity of patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery, effective doctor-patient communication and accurate clinical information gathering are essential, yet these skills may receive insufficient emphasis in conventional teaching [20]. AI-SP training provided trainees with repeated opportunities to practice history taking and communication in a safe simulated environment. The higher scores observed on this exploratory, unvalidated simulated interview assessment suggest that AI-SP training may support selected aspects of clinical interview training and serve as a useful complement to conventional teaching. However, the non-significant difference in empathy indicates that AI-SP training may have limited effects on affective and interpersonal aspects of communication, which may still require real patient encounters, human standardized patients, and instructor-guided reflection. The high satisfaction scores further suggest good learner acceptability, although these findings should be interpreted as perceived usefulness rather than direct evidence of improved clinical communication in real practice.

The application of artificial intelligence may reduce teachers’ workload in tasks such as item development and routine formative assessment, thereby allowing them to devote more attention to learning process analysis and targeted instruction [21]. With the support of learning data feedback, teachers can identify both common difficulties and individual weaknesses in a more timely manner, which may improve the precision and interactivity of teaching. By enabling more timely and targeted feedback, this approach may encourage teachers to focus more on the development of learners’ clinical competence and less on routine knowledge delivery. AI can improve the efficiency of teaching resource development, provide opportunities for repeated practice, and support individualized learning [16]. These advantages may be particularly useful in specialty training, where learners often have different educational needs. However, the role of AI in medical education should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Recent studys have also emphasized that AI-supported medical education should be interpreted with caution because AI-generated content may contain hallucinations, bias, limited transparency, and insufficient clinical nuance, and therefore requires expert supervision before being used in teaching [22, 23]. The application of commercial AI platforms in medical education also requires attention to ethical and governance issues, including protection of personal and educational data, transparency about AI limitations, and the need for institutional policies to define appropriate use, faculty supervision, and accountability. Moreover, although case-based learning, question-bank training, repeated practice, and feedback are not new educational approaches, generative AI may make these strategies easier to organize and deliver in a more individualized manner. AI-based simulation also offers trainees repeated opportunities to practice cardiovascular surgery scenarios in a safe setting. However, it cannot replace real clinical encounters, human standardized patients, or expert-developed cases, particularly for empathy training and complex interpersonal communication. Thus, AI should be viewed as a useful supplement to conventional clinical teaching. The present study only showed improvements in short-term educational and simulation-based outcomes, and further research is needed to determine whether these improvements can lead to better clinical decision-making or patient outcomes in real practice. Beyond educational effectiveness, wider implementation of this model would also require consideration of practical resources. Faculty members would need training in prompt design, AI-assisted teaching supervision, and content review, and institutions would need stable technological support and mechanisms for ongoing quality control. These factors may affect cost, workload, and scalability, but they were not formally assessed in the present study. In addition, the present study was based on ChatGPT-4o, and its findings may not be directly generalizable to other large language models, which may generate different responses or require different prompting strategies. Future studies should evaluate the applicability of this teaching model across different AI platforms and examine whether the prompt templates developed in this study can be transferred to other models.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, its findings may have limited generalizability to other settings and to trainees at different stages of training. Second, only short-term educational and simulation-based outcomes were assessed. We did not evaluate real patient encounters, perioperative decision-making, diagnostic accuracy in clinical practice, complication rates, or patient-centered outcomes. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of improved clinical performance or patient care. Third, the Clinical Interview Performance Assessment Scale was self-developed and has not been externally validated. In addition, participants and teaching instructors could not be blinded because of the nature of the intervention, although predefined teaching schedules, standardized control teaching content, and blinded assessment were used to limit potential bias. Fourth, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the CTDI-CV and clinical interview subdomains. These subdomain findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Future multicenter studies with larger samples are needed to confirm these findings. The inclusion of more comprehensive assessment methods, such as the Objective Structured Clinical Examination, may further strengthen the evaluation of teaching effectiveness [24]. Finally, it may also be valuable to explore the combined use of artificial intelligence with technologies such as virtual reality to further optimize the clinical teaching system in cardiovascular surgery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the AI-enabled personalized teaching model may represent a feasible and effective approach for cardiovascular surgery education. In this study, trainees in the experimental group showed higher theoretical examination scores, better overall critical thinking disposition, and higher simulated clinical interview scores in information collection, communication skills, diagnostic reasoning, and overall performance. High satisfaction scores also suggested good learner acceptability and perceived usefulness. Further multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm the broader applicability and sustained educational value of this model.
