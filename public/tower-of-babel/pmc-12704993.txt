Effectiveness of the newly designed WASH implementation strategy on WASH practices, diarrheal diseases, respiratory illness, and absenteeism among ashram school children
Authors: Sarika U. Dakhode, Abhay M. Gaidhane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Poor WASH practices are associated with various infections such as diarrhea, worm infestations (STH), and skin diseases among children. WinS is involved in Sustainable Development Goals, where SDG-6 is to escalate safe WASH accessibility universally. However, implementation of WASH module in residential schools which are established for tribal community is rare.Objective:To study the effectiveness of the WASH implementation strategy on WASH practices, diarrheal diseases, respiratory infection, and absenteeism among students residing in ashram schools.Methods:This intervention study was conducted in eight ashram schools meant for adiwasi community of Wardha district. Four ashram schools were allocated randomly in each beneficiary and control arm. 471 students of IV-VI standard were divided into the beneficiary group (251) and control group (220). The WASH implementation model consisting of safe WASH practices was designed. Local engagement of school administration, teachers, and students as the WASH committee was the main approach for execution of the model. Interactive sessions, demonstration, video playing, and so on were the modes of delivery. Relative risk is assessed to compare the WASH practices, illness, and attendance.Results:There was positive change in WASH practices among students attending the ashram schools of beneficiary than the compared group, except washing hands after using urinals, brushing, and bathing practices. The average risk of unhygienic practices is reduced up to the extent of 56%. There was a significant decrease in diarrhea, respiratory infection, and absenteeism up to the extent of 57%, 56%, and 65%, respectively, among students attending the ashram schools of beneficiary than comparison schools.Conclusion:This present model seems to be effective in improving the WASH practices and reducing the absenteeism, diarrhea, and respiratory illness among the beneficiary group. Such a strategy can be explored to other schools for wider coverage.

Introduction

Poor WASH is associated with various infections such as diarrhea, worm infestations, and skin diseases among children.[1] These diseases are attributed to absenteeism in school-going children, which leads to substandard academic performance. Review and researches showed that improved WASH practices at the school level had an impact on children health outcome in the form of a decrease in morbidity of diarrheal diseases, respiratory infections, and an increase in school attendance.[23456]

WinS involved in SDG-6 is to escalate safe WASH accessibility universally and SDG-4 is to ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all, and the indicator is provision of safe WASH facilities in schools.[7] A review suggested that adequate resources, information, and accountability are necessary to implement the sustainable WASH services, but most of the countries fail to fulfil these requirements combined at the optimal level.[8] Most schools in developing countries lack WASH facilities. To address this gap, UNICEF with a number of partners calls on decision-makers to increase investments and to plan and act in cooperation. Regarding WASH in schools, national monitoring systems are often weak and many countries do not have even basic data.[9] Hence, the comprehensive package is designed to strengthen monitoring systems.

The Government of India has started many programs and policies since the past 50 years for providing safe WASH at the school level.[1011121314] Expenditure on education is the last priority for the tribal community in India because of poverty. Traditional patterns of involving the children in labor and earning again keep them away from the educational opportunity. Ashram schools in the tribal community or remote settings are one of such inputs specially designed to suit these underprivileged children from extremely poor families to uplift the children. Communal eating, sleeping, drinking water, sanitation, and hygiene arrangements in boarding schools are factors for disease transmission. Improvements to school WASH conditions may reduce contamination, which could be sufficient to reduce related illness and school absenteeism in some contexts in residential schools. However, implementation of WASH module in ashram schools which are established for the tribal community is found to be equal to none. If it is rarely planned and implemented, evaluation for its effectiveness in the context of ashram schools is the last step which remained untouched. As one of the key components of SDGs is WASH in school; in recognition of the importance of WASH in present setting, we planned this study.

Methodology

Study design and setting: This was a school-based intervention study conducted during 2020–23 in Ashram schools of Wardha (Maharashtra, India).

Study Participants: The study participants were students of IV to VI standard residing in Ashram school boarding and attending school education. Students who did not participate or discontinued intervention activities due to migration of family or shifted to other schools were excluded from analysis.

Sample size: By considering an intra-cluster correlation of 0.05 and a design effect of 2, the proportion of outcome from the control group (p1) and intervention group (p2) is 29% and 17%, respectively.[15] The sample size at 95% CI and 0.05 level of significance was 395. By considering 10% loss to follow-up, the total sample is 435. We enrolled 471 students in the study.

Sampling: All Ashram schools (eight) meant for the tribal community in a district were enrolled in the study. Four schools in the intervention group and other four in the control group were allocated randomly. 251 and 220 students in the intervention and control groups, respectively, get allocated. Principals and teachers of schools were explained the research objectives, intervention (only for one group), potential benefits, and risk of the study.

Data Collection Tools and Technique: This was prepared by using the WHO standards for WASH in schools in low-cost settings[7] and the Monitoring Package developed by UNICEF and recommended by JMP for WASH in schools. This is adaptable in developing countries.[9] This consists of three modules: EMIS Module, Survey Module, and Children’s Monitoring Module. By referring these modules, we prepared data collection tools: Basic information sheet, Observational Checklist, and Interview questionnaire suitable for the present study setting and objectives.

Baseline data: All schools, with intervention and control groups, were visited for the baseline assessment before the implementation of the model.

Endpoint data: Data similar to baseline were collected after intervention, except for basic information and sociodemographic information of children from both groups.

The impact of intervention on health outcomes like diarrheal diseases, respiratory infections, and eye infection reported by students may lead to subject bias.[16] In the present study, to overcome such type of bias, health-related measures from the school medical records, which consists of medical consultation details, have been collected, and also, we conducted interview of the students.

Intervention design: After baseline collection of information, students’ needs for safe WASH were assessed. By keeping in view that the model should be adopted by local settings, a replicable, scalable, and low-cost WASH implementation model was designed. Use of local resources along with local engagement of school administration, teachers, and students was the main approach for execution of the model. Ethics clearance: IEC approval is obtained from DMIMS University, Wardha, in Aug-Sep 2018 (Ref. No.- DMIMS (DU)/IEC/2018-19/7356).

Informed consent: We have obtained the written consent from the school Principal and teacher for participation in the study. Informed consent for students was taken from the teacher as a Guardian.

Statistical analysis: Chi-square test and relative risk (RR) were applied to compare the outcome among the intervention and control groups. The number needed to treat (NNT) is estimated for achieving the respective effective size of the particular variable.

It was noticed that unhygienic drinking water practices among students from the intervention arm were decreased by 58% (1-RR) compared to the control one. The risk of unhygienic drinking water practices dropped up to the extent of 42% (RR = 0.42) in the statistically significant range (CI = 0.3243–0.5364, P = 0.001).

Unsanitary urination practices among students from the intervention group were reduced by 39% (1-RR, CI = 0.4816–0.7785) compared to the control group, highlighting that the risk of unsanitary practices reduced up to the extent of 61% (RR = 0.61, P = 0.0001). The NNT reflects the number needed to treat 5.5 students with this intervention to reduce unsanitary urination practices in one student. The risk of unsanitary defecation practices in the intervention group is decreased up to the extent of 56% (RR = 0.56, CI = 0.3877–0.7994, P = 0.001). In order to reduce the unsanitary practices in one student, we need to provide intervention to nine students (NNT = 9).

Post intervention, the risk of unhygienic practices for hand washing before meal in the intervention group is decreased by 49% (1-RR) as compared to the control group. The risk of unhygienic practices for hand washing before meal is significantly declined up to the extent of 51% (RR = 0.51, CI = 0.3974–0.6544, P = 0.0001). The projected NNT is 4.08 to reduce such unhygienic hand washing practices in one student. The risk of unhygienic practices for washing hand after defecation in the intervention group was significantly cut down up to the extent of 68% (RR = 0.68, CI = 0.5603-0.8256, P = 0.0001) as compared to the control group (1-RR = 32%), and the NNT is estimated at 5.5. The risk of unhygienic practices for hand washing after using urinals in the intervention group is reduced by 3% (RR = 0.97, CI = 0.8712-1.0900, P = 0.6) only compared to the control group.

At the end point, the possibility of unhygienic hand washing practices, that is, without using soap or other material, is shrunk up to the extent of 58% (RR = 0.58, CI = 0.4461-0.7742, P = 0.05) in the intervention group. It was observed that oral hygiene practices were improved among beneficiary compared to comparison group children. However, the difference in proportion is not significant (RR = 1.07, CI = 0.8894-1.2973, P = 0.45). Discussion

Drinking Water practices: A large-scale survey was undertaken by WaterAid India in 2016 and covered 453 schools of villages in 34 districts of nine states and observed that 15% schools did not have access to safe drinking water facilities due to unavailability of water treatment facilities to make it safe for drinking. Also, students reported unpleasant taste and muddy appearance. About 15% schools did not cover the stored water container properly. Water was withdrawn from the container in an unhygienic way by the 45% students.[17]

Conducting competitions of posters, essay, role play, and displaying audio-visual messages on WASH practices leads to active involvement and interaction of students as such messages can be listened and perceived attentively, which are given by friends rather than teachers. In the present study, these factors played a catalytic role during the intervention process for a student’s involvement. After implementation of the WASH model, the pattern of drinking of water has been significantly improved in intervention compared to control schools. Good practices of drinking water such as using own reusable vessels and shared drinking vessels after washing have been improved significantly among intervention school students (P < 0.05). Drinking water using shared vessels without washing or directly from the faucet/hand pump spout was assessed to be significantly decreased in intervention as compared to the control arm (P < 0.05).

Sanitation Practices: Sanitary defecation practices improved among intervention school students (84.86%) against control school (71.82%), where the risk of unsanitary defecation practices decreased up to the extent of 56% (RR-0.56). These intervention activities also improved sanitary urination practices significantly (RR-0.51) in studied schools. Similar improvement was observed in public schools of Philippines after implementation of operation and maintenance packages on utilization and cleanliness of toilets. Accessibility and utilization of functional toilets were increased by 11% among intervention compared to control schools.[18] The SMART health education model was tried in rural area schools of Pune, and it was found to be effective in updating the knowledge and attitude of 5th and 6th class students for sanitation practices.[19]

Hygiene Practices: In the present study, at the endpoint, 76.49% students followed hand wash practices using soap/other material in intervention schools as compared to 60% in control schools. In schools of Mirzapur (UP), WASH intervention noted positive impact in the form that 89% students followed handwashing practices with soap after using toilet, whereas the rest of the students use alternate cleaning materials such as ashes and soil.[20]

The achievement of the present WASH intervention project was improvement in hygienic behavior of students in intervention schools by 33.07% for hand washing before meal in beneficiary schools (43.43% baseline, 74.50% endpoint), as compared to control schools, by 8.18% (41.82% baseline, 50% endpoint). The next behavior outcome achieved that 22.31% raised in number of students for washing hands after defecation in hygienic manner in beneficiary schools compared to control schools by 5.91%. Pupil hand washing behavioral outcome was improved by 23.9% when evaluated by Anna N. Chard et al.[21] in 2015 for UNICEF WinS intervention project in public schools of Laos.

Direct association between students’ hand washing behavior and compliance of teachers’, parents’, and peers’ suggestions (β = 0.09) and indirect association between students’ knowledge with hand washing behavior (β = 0.04) were expressed.[22] This suggests that children behavior was influenced by teachers’, peers’, and parents’ attitude and inputs. Hence, in our study, the intervention package focused on a participatory approach where teachers and students were involved in implementation of WASH activities by forming school and class WASH committees.

Campaign of ‘School of 5’ was evaluated by in Patna, Bihar. The overall impact of intervention on children of the treatment group for hand washing was positive compared to the control group. A higher proportion of children in the beneficiary group (35.2%) reported hand washing with soap than the control group (20.1%), [RR = 1.77, P = 0.003]. School level impact was greater than home level as this intervention was delivered in school.[23]

No significant improvement in teeth brushing practices and bathing practices was reported in beneficiary schools as compared to the control one in the present study.

Impact of WASH on children anthropometry, morbidity, and mortality was systematically reviewed for 41 trials with WASH-related intervention in LMICs, and strong evidence was found that only hygiene-related intervention had reduced the risk of respiratory infections by 24% (RR-0.76; CI-0.59-0.98) compared to combined WASH intervention.[24]

Diarrhea, Respiratory Infection, and Absenteeism: Anna Chard et al.[25] evaluated the impact of improvement in school WASH on infectious diseases among the pupils attending schools in Mali. They observed significant low prevalence of antibodies for both food- and water-borne enteric diseases (P = 0.001) as well as person-to-person transmitted enteric diseases (P = 0.01) among pupils attending experimental schools compared to comparison schools.

The ITT analysis found increasing the number of WASH target achievements by the particular school was negatively associated with prevalence of diarrhea (OR = 0.71, P-trend < 0.01) and respiratory symptoms (OR = 0.75, P-trend < 0.01) among students in matched control trials in Mali. However, the number of WASH target achievements by the schools has no association with absenteeism recorded on roll call (OR = 0.93, P = 0.38) or reported by the students (OR = 1.23, P < 0.01). This work highlighted that WASH intervention has a positive impact on health outcome in view of reducing diarrheal diseases and respiratory illness but no significant effect on school attendance of students as there may be other many other reasons for absenteeism compared to the illness or poor WASH at school.[26]

In the present study, the relative risks for diarrheal diseases and respiratory infection were RR = 0.57 and RR = 0.56, respectively. This means the relative risk reduction (RRR) for diarrhea and respiratory illness was 43% and 44%, respectively. RRR for diarrhea and respiratory infection was 25.3% and 15%, noted after implementing HWWS in Mumbai.[3]

A systematic review of 14 cluster RCTs evaluated impact of hand hygiene interventions at school as well as domestic settings in LMICs, and these interventions were beneficial in reducing the ARI morbidity among children.[27]

Mohan Kumar Sharma in schools of Nepal noted that after implementation of improved WASH services, significantly high numbers of students were regular in classes, where a significantly less number of students were absent in beneficiary schools (20%) as compared to control schools (42%) (P < 0.001).[28] Similarly, research across the globe revealed that an increase in school attendance was observed in those schools where WASH practices improved.[3620293031] Contrast findings like WASH interventions had no impact on school attendance among students were also reported.[516263233] In the present study, there was improvement in class attendance.

Safe WASH practices in schools are a part of primary and essential care. Inculcation of safe WASH practices among school children is the beginning of adoption of such behavior among them. When these children visited to home during holidays or vacation, they can transfer these knowledge and practices among their family members. This ultimately brings change in attitude and practice in the community.

Conclusion

In the study setting, on the basis of school environmental survey and students’ assessment, a structured implementation strategy for WinS was developed for accountable delivery of WASH services in ashram schools for the tribal community. This strategy is low cost, replicable, and scalable to other schools. It was driven by student bodies with support from rectors and teachers. The content of intervention package was comprehensive, which focused on all components of WASH. The implementation mode included interactive sessions, face-to-face communication, demonstrations, and hands-on training along with participatory approaches which determine the sustainability of behavior change.

At the end point, progressive behavior change among students in regard to drinking water practices, toilet utility, and hand hygienic practices was the achievement in target schools. However, improvement in practices for personal hygiene such as regular brushing and bathing habits was found difficult to inculcate within the said period. Health outcome in concern with diarrheal diseases and respiratory illness was reduced significantly in intervention schools. The intervention also demonstrated positive collateral benefits in the form of improved class attendance and capacity building of school teachers and staff through training of safe WASH practices. Constant supervision, monitoring, evaluation, and gap analysis required for identifying the barriers and facilitators for consistency of WASH services.
