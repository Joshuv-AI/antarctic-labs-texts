Relaxin‐2: Shaping the Proteomic Landscape of Skeletal Muscle Physiology, Glucose Trafficking, and Mitochondrial Function in Rat
Authors: Xocas Vázquez‐Abuín, Lucía Gómez‐Cibeira, Javier García‐Seara, Pablo de la Fuente‐López, Carlos Minguito‐Carazo, Susana B Bravo, Manuel Otero‐Santiago, Oreste Gualillo, José Ramón González‐Juanatey, Francisca Lago, Sandra Feijóo‐Bandín, Alana Aragón‐Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRelaxin‐2 is a hormone with robust beneficial effects on the heart and blood vessels and potential as a therapy for cardiovascular (CV) disease. Considering the interorgan communication between skeletal muscle and heart, and the relation between muscle quality/composition and CV events, we hypothesize that relaxin‐2 may regulate skeletal muscle physiology and metabolism. We aim to evaluate the impact of relaxin‐2 on the proteome of skeletal muscle from healthy Sprague–Dawley rats. Animals were treated with 0.4 mg/kg/day of serelaxin (recombinant form of human relaxin‐2) or vehicle (PBS) for 2 weeks employing subcutaneous osmotic minipumps. Skeletal muscle protein identification and quantification were performed by LC–MS/MS using a Data‐Independent Acquisition (DIA)‐Sequential Window Acquisition of All Theoretical Fragment Ion Spectra (SWATH) method. SWATH/MS quantitative analysis identified that relaxin‐2 significantly decreased 95 proteins and significantly increased 32 proteins in rat skeletal muscle when compared to control rats. From these, 34 proteins were associated with muscle function, myogenesis, muscle differentiation and/or regeneration, 20 are mitochondrial proteins (six from the complexes of the electron transport chain), and 10 proteins participate in glucose metabolism. Qualitative data‐dependent workflow analysis identified 35 proteins exclusive to the skeletal muscle of the relaxin‐2‐treated group: eight proteins related to processes of skeletal muscle function (size, ion homeostasis or organization of caveolae structures and cytoskeleton) and myogenesis, and two proteins involved in muscle differentiation. Our work highlighted for the first time the role of relaxin‐2 in crucial processes of muscle physiology and energetic metabolism, which could influence several processes involved in myopathy and CV.Keywords: energetic metabolism, glucose transport, mitochondrial respiration, myogenesis, proteome, proteomics, relaxin‐2, serelaxin, skeletal muscle

Introduction

Relaxin‐2 is a 6 kDa peptide hormone which belongs to the insulin‐like peptide superfamily and constitutes a pleiotropic hormone and paracrine factor widely synthesized in different tissues of the human body of both sexes [1]. This hormone participates in innumerable physiological mechanisms besides reproduction, mainly through its cognate receptor, the relaxin/insulin‐like family peptide receptor 1 (RXFP1), although this hormone could also signal through the RXFP2 and the glucocorticoid receptor [1, 2, 3]. Relaxin‐2, and its recombinant form serelaxin, have been highlighted as a promising clinical therapy for cardiovascular (CV) and cardiometabolic diseases owing to its solid beneficial effects in the heart and blood vessels, including the modulation of the cardiac energetic metabolism and lipidome, as well as vasodilatation and angiogenesis properties [4, 5]. However, despite significant advancements and knowledge in the CV system, the comprehension of relaxin‐2 effects on musculoskeletal tissue is limited.

There exists only a small piece of evidence suggesting the influence of relaxin‐2 at the musculoskeletal level, mainly focused on its potent anti‐fibrotic ability, favoring muscle regeneration during skeletal muscle healing [6, 7, 8, 9]. Relaxin‐2 stimulates nitric oxide synthase in rat skeletal muscles [10], promotes the growth and differentiation of C2C12 myoblast cells, and upregulates the expression of myogenic markers, such as myf‐5 and desmin, as well as the number of multinucleated myotubes in these cells [8, 11]. In myoblasts transfected with transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β1, relaxin‐2 inhibits their proliferation and decreases their α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) expression, suggesting a disruption of the fibrotic activity typically manifested in these cells [8]. Relaxin‐2 also stimulates muscle regeneration, increases the population of satellite cells and the activity of the matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) MMP‐2 and MMP‐9, reduces fibrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration, and improves muscle strength in injured muscle [8, 11]. Other works have deciphered additional roles of relaxin‐2 in muscle healing at the vascular level, including increased angiogenesis and revascularisation in injured muscle, augmented skeletal muscle capillary density in high‐fat‐fed mice [11, 12], and stimulation of rapid, transient vasodilation in the terminal arteriolar network [13].

It is well described an interorgan communication between skeletal muscle and heart or blood vessels through the production of myokines and miRNAs, which are carried and transported by extracellular vesicles, and that induce protective effects regulating cardiac and vascular function and structure [14], while muscle density, composition and quality could be useful for stratifying CV risk and reducing CV events [15, 16, 17]. Several works have demonstrated that relaxin‐2 can help undifferentiated skeletal myoblasts to establish mechanical and electrical coupling with neighboring cardiomyocytes, suggesting a more advantageous intercellular exchange of signals between the coupled cells and providing evidence for a role for cardiomyocytes in controlling intercellular communication among myoblasts [18]. In this direction, considering that the myocardium shares several characteristics with skeletal muscle [19], and that we have previously demonstrated the metabolic effect of relaxin‐2 on cardiac muscle [5, 20]; it is reasonable to speculate that skeletal muscle is another target of relaxin‐2 under certain conditions.

Since the influence of relaxin‐2 on the metabolism of skeletal muscle has not been deciphered yet, we aimed to investigate the function of relaxin‐2 in the regulation of skeletal muscle physiology and metabolism employing a proteomic approach, a handy bioanalytical screening tool that allows the identification and quantification of several proteins in a living organ or system under a specific treatment or condition. Our work outlines how relaxin‐2 modulates the skeletal muscle proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiles in healthy Sprague–Dawley rats.

Materials and Methods

All reagents used are from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise indicated. This work is a complementary examination of the effects of relaxin‐2 (serelaxin) in rat skeletal muscle from our previous studies in heart, liver, and visceral adipose tissue [5, 21, 22].

Ethical Approval

Our study protocol was authorized by the Galician Clinical Research Ethics Committee (2007/304), and experimental approaches employing animals are described following the Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments ARRIVE guidelines. All animals were housed and euthanized applying protocols validated by the Animal Care Committee of the University of Santiago de Compostela (USC) (Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, Spain) in agreement with European Union Directive 2010/63/EU for animal research (Approval No: 15005 AE/12/FUN01/FIS02/RNP2). The number of animals used in the experimental approaches was the minimum necessary to develop our objectives and to ensure enough statistical power.

Animals

We used male Sprague–Dawley rats (200–250 g, 8 weeks old) (RRID: RGD_70508), which were purchased from the Animal Facility Centre of the USC (Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, Spain). Because female sex hormones may influence relaxin‐2 receptor expression at the musculoskeletal level, only male rats were used to minimize potential hormonal confounding and ensure a more consistent response to relaxin‐2 in the target tissue [23]. Rats were housed with ad libitum access to a standard chow diet and tap water and were accommodated in individual cages under regulated conditions of room temperature (RT) (22°C ± 2°C), relative humidity (40% ± 5%), and illumination (12 h light/dark cycle, lights on at 08:00 and lights off at 20:00) in the Animal House of the Medicine Faculty of the USC (Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, Spain). Rats were distributed in two randomly assigned experimental groups with a size (n) of 10 individuals per group: a control or sham‐operated group and a relaxin‐2‐treated group. This group size referred to individual values and was established to ensure a suitable statistical analysis. We measured body weight, fat mass, or lean mass in rats together with energy expenditure, respiratory quotient, locomotor activity, food intake, and plasma levels of triglycerides, cholesterol, high‐density lipoproteins (HDL), low‐density lipoproteins (LDL), glutamic‐pyruvic transaminase (GTP), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) as previously reported [5]. After the sacrifices of the animals by decapitation (performed during the morning at 09:00) in the fed state, skeletal muscle from the gastrocnemius was collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Gastrocnemius muscle was employed because it exhibits a heterogeneous composition with a predominance of fast‐twitch type II fibers, which display different glycolytic and oxidative properties and represent a suitable tissue for the assessment of metabolic alterations [24, 25].

In Vivo Serelaxin Treatment

Human recombinant relaxin‐2 or serelaxin was kindly supplied by the Foundation for Research on Relaxin in Cardiovascular and other Diseases (Prosperius Institute, Florence, ITA) and was obtained from the identical batch synthesized for Connetics USA (San Diego, CA, USA) and used in preceding clinical trials on scleroderma [26]. For in vivo treatments, osmotic minipumps (Model 2ML2; Alzet DURECT Corporation, Cupertino, CA, USA) were filled under sterile conditions with a serelaxin solution at a dose of 0.4 mg/kg/day or vehicle (phosphate buffer saline, PBS) for the relaxin‐2‐treated group and the control group, respectively. The pumping rate of relaxin‐2 or vehicle was 5.0 μL/h during the 2 weeks of the treatment. This dose of serelaxin was established regarding the in vivo treatments of anterior works [27, 28]. The subcutaneous osmotic minipumps were surgically implanted into the subcutaneous space of the back of the animal under sterile conditions, and the animals were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (85 mg/kg) (Imalgene 1000; Boehringer Ingelheim AG, Ingelheim am Rhein, Germany) and xylazine (8 mg/kg) (Rompun; Bayer AG, Leverkusen, Germany) through intraperitoneal injection. The guarantee of suitable effectiveness of anesthesia was detected by the absence of pedal withdrawal reflex to noxious pinch. During the postoperative period, the rats were placed in a heating blanket to maintain their physiological temperature until the animals began to awake from anesthesia.

Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐PCR)

RNA was treated with DNase to eliminate genomic DNA and was extracted using a NucleoSpin kit, following the manufacturer's instructions (Macherey‐Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany). RNA quality and quantity were determined employing a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For relative quantification, we performed a reverse‐transcription reaction with 1 μg of RNA utilizing the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) following the manufacturer's instructions. Real‐time PCR was performed employing 2× Perfect Master Mix SYBR Gr Kit and specific primers supplied by QIAGEN (Hilden, Germany) for rat Relaxin Family Peptide Receptor 1 (Rxfp1) (RefSeq NM_201417.2) (RRID: SCR_003496), Relaxin Family Peptide Receptor 2 (Rxfp2) (RefSeq NM_001012475.2) (RRID: SCR_003496) and Relaxin Family Peptide Receptor 3 (Rxfp3) (RefSeq NM_001008310.1) (RRID: SCR_003496) and 18S ribosomal RNA (Rn18s) (RefSeq NR_046237.1) (RRID: SCR_003496). All these primer pairs (forward and reverse) were intron‐spanning and carefully designed, optimized, and validated by Qiagen (Germany). We performed RT‐PCR using the Stratagene Mx3000P Real‐Time PCR System following the manufacturer's instructions (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_020526). We ran 45 cycles of PCR, including control RT reactions, and PCR was validated by melting curves.

Protein Identification and Quantification by LC–MS/MS Using a DIA‐SWATH Method

Protein Extraction and Digestion

Freeze skeletal muscle tissue (100 mg) from the different samples was homogenized in RIPA buffer (200 mmol/L Tris/HCl [pH 7.4], 130 mmol/L NaCl, 10% [v/v] glycerol, 0.1% [v/v] SDS, 1% [v/v] Triton X‐100, 10 mmol/L MgCl2) with antiproteases and antiphosphatases (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in a TissueLyser II (QIAGEN, Tokyo, Japan) (RRID: SCR_018623). The homogenate was centrifuged at 14 000 g at 4°C for 20 min. Protein concentration was measured in the samples using an RC‐DC kit (Biorad Lab., Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Protein aliquots of 100 μg were concentrated in a SDS‐PAGE single band [29, 30] and submitted to a manual digestion as described elsewhere [31]. Finally, the peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid for further analysis.

Phosphoproteome Analysis

For fosfoproteome analysis, phosphopeptides were enriched using the High‐Select TiO2 Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Enriched fractions were then analyzed by LC–MS/MS using the SWATH method [21, 32].

Mass Spectrometric Analysis (DDA Acquisition) [21, 32]

Digested peptides (over 4 μg of each sample) were separated using Reverse Phase Chromatography. A gradient was created using a micro liquid chromatography system (Eksigent Technologies nanoLC 400; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) coupled to a high‐speed Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) with a micro flow source. The analytical column was a silica‐based reversed‐phase column Eksigent C18 150 × 0.30 mm, 3 mm particle size and 120 Å pore size (Eksigent; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA). The trap column was a YMC‐TRIART C18 (YMC Technologies, Teknokroma Analítica S.A., Sant Cugat del Vallès, Barcelona, Spain) with a 3 mm particle size and 120 Å pore size, switched on‐line with the analytical column. The loading pump delivered a solution of 0.1% formic acid in water at 10 μL/min. The micro‐pump generated a flow rate of 5 μL/min and was operated under gradient elution conditions, using 0.1% formic acid in water as mobile phase A, and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile as mobile phase B. Peptides were separated using a 90‐min gradient ranging from 2% to 90% mobile phase B.

Data acquisition was performed by a TripleTOF 6600 System (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) using a data‐dependent analysis (DDA) workflow. Source and interface conditions were the following: ionspray voltage floating (ISVF) 5500 V, curtain gas (CUR) 25, collision energy (CE), 10 and ion source gas 1 (GS1) 25. The instrument was operated with Analyst TF 1.7.1 software (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA). Switching criteria were set to ions greater than mass to charge ratio (m/z) 350 and smaller than m/z 1400 with a charge state of 2–5, mass tolerance of 250 ppm and an abundance threshold of more than 200 counts per second (cps). Previous target precursor ions were excluded for 15 s. The instrument was automatically calibrated every 4 h using tryptic peptides from PepCalMix as an external calibrant.

Data Analysis

After MS/MS analysis (MS2 data), data files were processed using ProteinPilot 5.0.1 software from Sciex (Redwood City, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_018681), which uses the algorithm Paragon for database search and Progroup for data grouping. Data were searched using a Rattus‐specific Uniprot database (RRID: SCR_002380) specifying iodoacetamide at cysteine alkylation as a variable modification and methionine oxidation as a fixed modification. False discovery rate (FDR) was performed using a nonlinear fitting method, displaying only those results that reported a 1% global false discovery rate or better [33].

Relative Quantification: Spectral Count

Scaffold (version Scaffold‐5.2.2 or Scaffold DDA 6.8.0; Proteome Software Inc., Portland, OR, USA) (RRID: SCR_014321) was used to validate MS/MS‐based peptide and protein identifications and to perform a semiquantitative analysis by spectral counting [34]. Peptide identifications were accepted if they could be established at > 97.0% probability to achieve a FDR < 1.0% by the Scaffold Local FDR algorithm. Protein identifications were accepted if they could be established at > 99.0% probability to achieve a FDR < 1.0% and contained at least two identified peptides. Protein probabilities were assigned by the Protein Prophet algorithm. The Fisher's exact test was used for quantification and fold change determination. Proteins were considered differentially expressed when the p value < 0.05. It should be noted that to quantify changes in spectral counts, we estimated fold changes as proposed by Beissbarth et al. [35] for serial analysis of gene expression (SAGE) data, which avoids the discontinuity seen in simple count ratios when a protein shows a spectral count of 0 in one of the samples. Proteins with a FC = 0 or FC = Infinity were considered present only in one sample type.

Functional and Pathway Analysis

We conducted pathway analysis using STRING database (https://string‐db.org/) (RRID: SCR_005223), applying a minimum required interaction score of PPI = 0.7 (protein–protein interaction) and an FDR < 0.05. Venn diagram and biological processes pathway analysis were generated using FunRich: Functional Enrichment analysis tool (http://www.funrich.org/, Melbourne, Australia) (RRID: SCR_014467) for data‐independent acquisition analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using Scaffold software (Proteome Software Inc., Portland, OR, USA) (RRID: SCR_014321).

Protein Quantification by SWATH (Sequential Window Acquisition of All Theoretical Mass Spectra) [21, 32]

SWATH Spectral Library Creation

In order to construct the MS/MS spectral libraries, the peptide solutions were analyzed by a shotgun data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) approach by micro‐LC–MS/MS. To get a good representation of the peptides and proteins present in all samples, pooled vials of samples from each group were prepared using equal mixtures of the original samples. Four microliter (4 μg) of each pool was separated into a micro‐LC system Ekspert nLC425 (Eksigent, Dublin, CA, USA) using a column Chrom XP C18 150 × 0.30 mm, 3 mm particle size and 120 Å pore size (Eksigent; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) at a flow rate of 5 μL/min. Water and ACN, both containing 0.1% formic acid, were used as solvents A and B, respectively. The gradient run consisted of 5%–95% B for 30 min, 5 min at 90% B and finally 5 min at 5% B for column equilibration, for a total run time of 40 min. When the peptides eluted, they were directly injected into a hybrid quadrupole‐TOF mass spectrometer, Triple TOF 6600 (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA), operated with a data‐dependent acquisition system in positive ion mode. A Micro source (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) was used for the interface between micro‐LC and MS, with an application of 2600 V voltage. The acquisition mode consisted of a 250 ms survey MS scan from 400 to 1250 m/z followed by an MS/MS scan from 100 to 1500 m/z (25 ms acquisition time) of the top 65 precursor ions from the survey scan, for a total cycle time of 2.8 s. The fragmented precursors were then added to a dynamic exclusion list for 15 s; any singly charged ions were excluded from the MS/MS analysis.

The peptide and protein identifications were performed using Protein Pilot software (version 5.0.1; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_018681). Data were searched against a Rattus‐specific UniProt database (RRID: SCR_002380), specifying iodoacetamide as Cys alkylation. The FDR was set to 1% for both peptides and proteins. The MS/MS spectra of the identified peptides were then used to generate the spectral library for SWATH peak extraction using the add‐in for PeakView Software (version 2.2; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_015786), MS/MSALL with SWATH Acquisition MicroApp (version 2.0; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA). Peptides with a confidence score above 99% (as obtained from the Protein Pilot database search) were included in the spectral library. To improve the spectral libraries and increase the number of quantified proteins, additional pools corresponding to the same tissue were incorporated into the library generation process. Furthermore, during the SWATH analysis, the extracted ion areas obtained from both spectral libraries were jointly used to improve pathway analysis and enhance the identification and characterization of dysregulated proteins [36].

Relative Quantification by SWATH Acquisition

SWATH (Sequential Window Acquisition of all Theoretical Mass Spectra)—MS acquisition was performed on a TripleTOF 6600 LC–MS/MS system (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA). Samples were analyzed using a data‐independent acquisition (DIA) method (30 total samples). Each sample (4 μL from a mg/ml solution) was analyzed using the LC–MS equipment and LC gradient described above for building the spectral library, but instead using the SWATH‐MS acquisition method. The method consisted of repeating a cycle that consisted of the acquisition of 100 TOF MS/MS scans (400–1500 m/z, high sensitivity mode, 50 ms acquisition time) of overlapping sequential precursor isolation windows of variable width (1 m/z overlap) covering the 400–1250 m/z mass range with a previous TOF MS scan (400–1500 m/z, 50 ms acquisition time) for each cycle. Total cycle time was 6.3 s. For each sample set, the width of the 100 variable windows was optimized according to the ion density found in the DDA runs using a SWATH variable window calculator worksheet from Sciex (Redwood City, CA, USA).

Data Analysis

The targeted data extraction of the fragment ion chromatogram traces from the SWATH runs was performed by PeakView software (version 2.2; Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA; RRID: SCR_015786) using the SWATH Acquisition MicroApp (version 2.0). This application processed the data using the spectral library created from the shotgun data. Up to 10 peptides per protein and seven fragments per peptide were selected, based on signal intensity; any shared and modified peptides were excluded from the processing. Five‐minute windows and 30 ppm widths were used to extract the ion chromatograms; SWATH quantitation was attempted for all proteins in the ion library that were identified by ProteinPilot (RRID: SCR_024414) with an FDR below 1%. The retention times from the peptides that were selected for each protein were realigned in each run according to the iRT peptides spiked in each sample and eluted along the whole time axis. The extracted ion chromatograms were then generated for each selected fragment ion; the peak areas for the peptides were obtained by summing the peak areas from the corresponding fragment ions. PeakView computed an FDR and a score for each assigned peptide according to the chromatographic and spectra components; only peptides with an FDR below 5% were used for protein quantitation. Protein quantitation was calculated by adding the peak areas of the corresponding peptides.

The integrated peak areas (processed. mrkvw files from PeakView) were directly exported to the MarkerView software (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_027371) for relative quantitative analysis. The export will generate three files containing quantitative information about individual ions, the summed intensity of different ions for a particular peptide and the summed intensity of different peptides for a particular protein. MarkerView has been used for analysis of SWATH‐MS data reported in other proteomics studies [37, 38] because of its data‐independent method of quantitation. MarkerView uses processing algorithms that accurately find chromatographic and spectral peaks direct from the raw SWATH data. Data alignment by MarkerView compensated for minor variations in both mass and retention time values, ensuring that identical compounds in different samples are accurately compared to one another. Unsupervised multivariate statistical analysis using principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to compare the data from the different samples. A most‐like ratio (MLR) [39] normalization was performed after statistical analysis to control for possible uneven sample loss across the different samples during the sample preparation process. The average MS peak area for each protein was derived from each sample.

Functional and Pathway Analysis

We performed pathway analysis using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/) (RRID: SCR_005223), applying a minimum required interaction score of PPI = 0.7 (protein–protein interaction) and an FDR < 0.05. The most functionally enriched pathways were represented employing String diagrams. Volcano plots were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_002798).

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium (RRID: SCR_004055) via the PRIDE [40] (Pride‐asap; RRID: SCR_012052) partner repository with the dataset identifier ID: PXD078619 via http://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride.

Statistics

All the experimental data results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of at least three individual measurements per group, with n established as independent animal individuals. Regarding gene expression analysis, the fold change versus control in the expression of target genes was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Comparisons between relaxin‐2‐treated groups versus control were performed with the nonparametric test, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, employing the software GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_002798). The regulation of skeletal muscle proteome was analyzed by the paired Student's t‐test in the normal distribution data using Markerview software (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_027371). Significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Randomization and Blinding

Rats were acquired from the Animal Facility Centre of the USC (Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, Spain) and were randomly distributed in the experimental groups when they were housed in the Animal House of the Medicine Faculty of the USC (Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, Spain). All analyses were performed blinded, such that the researchers performing the experiments and the data analysis were unaware of the experimental group to which the animals belonged.

Attrition

All the animal subjects included in our approach finished the study and were employed in all the experiments of this work; consequently, there was no attrition.

Results

General Features of the Animals

The present study constitutes an additional evaluation of the effects of serelaxin in rat skeletal muscle from our preceding studies in heart, liver and visceral adipose tissue [5, 21, 22]. Relaxin‐2 treatment was not able to cause modifications in body composition and the metabolic parameters of food intake, respiratory quotient, energy expenditure, locomotor activity, or plasma levels of cholesterol, HDL, LDL, triglycerides, GTP, and GGT (data not shown) and did not modify insulin plasma levels of treated rats when compared to controls (mean of insulin plasma levels ± SD: 3.91 ± 2.23 ng/mL in relaxin‐2‐treated rats vs. 3.61 ± 2.26 ng/mL in control rats, n = 10), as we have reported before [5, 22]. To ensure the proper liberation of relaxin‐2 from osmotic minipumps during the treatment, we determined human relaxin‐2 levels in rat plasma samples (mean of human relaxin‐2 plasma levels ± standard deviation [SD]: 135.05 ± 90.42 ng/mL, n = 10), as previously reported [5]. Moreover, relaxin‐2 did not change glucose plasma levels in treated rats when compared to controls (mean of glucose plasma levels ± SEM: 157.50 ± 4.94 mg/dL [lower 95% CI of mean = 141.8; upper 95% CI of mean = 173.2] in relaxin‐2‐treated rats vs. 153.50 ± 3.80 mg/dL [lower 95% CI of mean = 141.4; upper 95% CI of mean = 165.6] in control rats, n = 4) [21].

Relaxin Receptor Expression in Rat Skeletal Muscle

Rat skeletal muscle mRNA expression for the relaxin receptors Rxfp1, Rxfp2, and Rxfp3 was checked by RT‐qPCR. We have found low mRNA levels of the three relaxin receptors Rxfp1, Rxfp2, and Rxfp3 in rat skeletal muscle (Table 1). The Ct values for Rxfp1 and, especially, Rxfp2 genes suggest a slight quantity of these genes and, consequently, minimal expression levels of Rxfp1 and Rxfp2 in rat skeletal muscle (Table 1). Rxfp3 skeletal muscle mRNA levels were higher in samples from relaxin‐2‐treated rats, but we did not find homogeneous duplicates for gene expression analysis in control rats. We have performed an RT‐qPCR analysis on a total of 10 animals (n) per experimental group. However, we have not obtained Ct values in the control group for Rxfp1, Rxfp2, and Rxfp3 in 50%, 40%, and 70% of the samples, respectively, and in the relaxin‐2‐treated group in 40% for Rxfp1, 60% for Rxfp2, and 70% for Rxfp3. The duplicates for the control group were not acceptable in 30% of the samples for Rxfp1 and in 50% for Rxfp2 and Rxfp3. In the relaxin‐2‐treated rats, RT‐qPCR duplicates were unacceptable in 50%, and 10% of the samples for Rxfp1, Rxfp2, respectively.

Proteomic Qualitative (DDA) Analysis in Rat Skeletal Muscle

The qualitative proteomic analysis enabled us to identify proteins present in each experimental group. Among those present only in rat skeletal muscle of the relaxin‐2‐treated group, we identified a total of 35 proteins, while in the control group, we determined 47 proteins (Table 2, Figure 1a). From these, in the relaxin‐2‐treated group, eight proteins: sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase 3 (AT2A3), transcription activator BRG1 (SMCA4), dystrophin (DMD), EH domain‐containing protein 2 (EHD2), prostaglandin E2 receptor EP1 subtype (PE2R1), glutamine synthetase (GLNA), potassium voltage‐gated channel subfamily H member 1 (KCNH1), and breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein homolog (BRCA1) were mainly related to specific processes of muscle function (regulation of skeletal muscle size and ion homeostasis, organization of caveolae structures and cytoskeleton, or regulation of major kinase signaling cascades) and myogenesis, while two proteins: inositol 1,4,5‐trisphosphate receptor type 1 (ITPR1) and GRIP1‐associated protein 1 (GRAP1) were involved in muscle differentiation and regeneration (Table 2). In the skeletal muscle, the relaxin‐2‐treated group showed more proteins related to ATP metabolic processes, sarcomere organization, and skeletal muscle contraction when compared to the control group (Figure 1b).

Proteomic Quantitative (DIA) Analysis in Rat Skeletal Muscle

To evaluate the quantitative differences in protein profiles, rat skeletal muscle was analyzed by LC–MS/MS mass spectrometry, and proteins were quantified using the SWATH‐MS quantification method to identify the proteins with differential expression in skeletal muscle between control and relaxin‐2‐treated rats. One hundred and twenty proteins showed significantly altered expression between control and relaxin‐2‐treated rats. Table 3 displays the proteins with differential expression between the two groups expressed as fold‐change and log2 (fold‐change) for the comparison between relaxin‐2‐treated rats versus control. Specifically, the levels of 89 proteins were significantly decreased, while 31 proteins were significantly increased in rat skeletal muscle after the treatment with relaxin‐2 when compared to control rats (Table 3). The volcano plot of Figure 1c represents the global quantification of the proteome in rat skeletal muscle, showing the downregulated and upregulated proteins for the comparison of relaxin‐2 versus control. From all the proteins regulated by relaxin‐2, we highlighted that 34 proteins are associated with normal muscle function, myogenesis, muscle differentiation and/or regeneration, while 20 proteins are specific to the mitochondria, with six of them being distinct subunits of the different complexes of the electron transport chain (ETC) (Table 3). Additionally, 10 proteins regulated by relaxin‐2 in the skeletal muscle are involved in glucose metabolism, mainly in glucose uptake (Table 3).

Phosphoproteomic Analysis in Rat Skeletal Muscle

Quantitative proteomic analysis comparing the relaxin‐2‐treated group with the control group of rats identified seven phosphorylated proteins with significantly altered levels in healthy rat skeletal muscle, as displayed in Table 3 and in the volcano plot representing the phosphoproteome in Figure 1d. From these, relaxin‐2 treatment decreased chloride intracellular channel protein 1 (CLIC1), which preserves skeletal muscle excitability and adaptation to physiological or harmful events [41], apolipoprotein A‐IV (APOA4) involved in glucose uptake in skeletal muscle [42] and annexin A6 (ANXA6), which is highly relevant for muscle membrane repair [43] (Table 3). Conversely, relaxin‐2 increased the phosphorylation of the protein apolipoprotein E (APOE), which mediates the coupling of myogenesis and lipid metabolism in skeletal muscle [44] (Table 3).

Discussion

Skeletal muscle, the largest organ in the body, is an essential organ regulating whole‐body energy metabolism, specifically in insulin‐stimulated glucose uptake and oxidative metabolism, which possesses relevant metabolic flexibility in response to hormonal stimulation [45, 46]. Usually, skeletal muscle dysfunction and myopathy are present in patients with heart failure (HF) and involve reduced muscle mass, increased intermuscular adiposity, shifted fiber type and atrophy, mitochondrial dysfunction, remodeling, and bioenergetic abnormalities, which have been associated with a decline in exercise capacity and muscle function in these patients [47]. This is the first study demonstrating that the cardioprotective hormone relaxin‐2, proposed as a therapeutic strategy for HF, acts as a potent modulator of numerous proteins involved in muscle physiology and glucose trafficking, as well as distinct subunits from the different complexes of the ETC in the mitochondria of skeletal muscle.

Although we have identified a low expression of relaxin‐2 receptors Rxfp1, Rxfp2, and Rxfp3 in skeletal muscle from healthy rats, in accordance with the database The Human Protein Atlas, low levels of RXFP1 mRNA expression are detected in human skeletal muscle [48], while in human myoblasts, the RXFP1 gene is expressed [11], which indicates that the effect of relaxin‐2 on human skeletal muscle may be associated with the RXFP1 pathway. In rats, previous reports have described that phosphatidylinositol‐3‐kinase (PI3K) and protein kinase C zeta are involved in the adenylate cyclase signaling mechanism of relaxin action, although this activation does not involve RXFP1, and the specific receptor is, at present, unknown, but may be of the serpentine receptors distinct from RXFP1 or receptors of the tyrosine kinase types [49, 50]. Elevated relaxin‐2 plasma levels have been correlated with joint laxity and with a higher incidence of musculoskeletal injuries in women, possibly owing to the presence of relaxin receptors in multiple joints that activate MMPs, which locally degrade collagen and gelatin [51]. In our proteomic profile in skeletal muscle, the most remarkable outcome is that relaxin‐2 modulates 44 proteins directly related to the process of myogenesis, muscle differentiation and regeneration, which is in concordance with the previously established evidence of relaxin‐2 as a regulator of muscle homeostasis [6, 7, 8, 9, 11]. First of all, the master protein dystrophin (DMD), implicated in a myriad of mechanisms of muscle physiology: modulation of major kinase and G‐protein signaling cascades, the organization of caveolae structures, the regulation of skeletal muscle size and hypertrophy and ion homeostasis, cytoskeletal organization, as well as neuromuscular transmission [52]; is exclusively expressed in skeletal muscle from relaxin‐2‐treated rats. Among other relevant proteins, relaxin‐2 reduced the cell surface glycoprotein MUC18, which is considered a novel marker for myogenic progenitors in human fetal muscle and whose downregulation promotes myoblast fusion, which aligns with the previously described role of relaxin‐2 on muscle regeneration [53]. During development, skeletal muscles express unique myosin isoforms, such as embryonic and neonatal myosin heavy chains, coded by the myosin heavy chain 3 (MYH3) and MYH8 genes, respectively [54]. These myosins are re‐expressed during muscle regeneration and serve as specific markers of regenerating fibers in a pathological setting, with myosin‐3 being a crucial myogenic regulator during development that regulates the size, quantity, and type of muscle fibers [55, 56]. The reduction in myosin‐8 and myosin‐3 levels in skeletal muscle observed by relaxin‐2 could be explained because our animal model consists of an adult healthy rat, although further studies in animals with skeletal muscle injury are needed. Moreover, it has been previously described that forkhead box O1 (FOXO1) could regulate bovine skeletal muscle cells differentiation by modulating PI3K‐Akt or relaxin signaling pathways, and target MYH3 for transcriptional inhibition [57]. BH3‐interacting domain death agonist or BID is required for myoblast fusion and, together with caspase‐2, also controls myoblast differentiation by activating the mitochondrial death pathway [58], while in our approach, relaxin‐2 negatively influenced BID protein content as well as alpha‐crystallin B chain (CRYAB), a small heat shock protein that plays an important role in muscle homeostasis [59]. Relaxin‐2 also decreased the levels of programmed cell death 6‐interacting protein (PDC6I) or Alix in skeletal muscle, a multifunctional protein involved in myogenic differentiation and muscle metabolism that regulates the levels and distribution of the mitochondrial solute carrier Slc25A4, which is essential for ATP production [60, 61]. Skeletal muscle cells highly depend on ATP to perform their contraction and relaxation functions, and curiously, we have found that relaxin‐2 reduced the mitochondrial protein Slc25A4 (also known as ADP/ATP translocase 1 or ANT1), considered a central point of control for oxidative metabolism with a major role in proton leak in skeletal muscle mitochondria [62]. Even though relaxin‐2 downregulated a high number of proteins involved in muscle development and regeneration, we have also identified that it can increase the levels of casein kinase II subunit alpha (CSK21) and C‐terminal‐binding protein 1 (CTBP1), involved in the activation of the muscle‐specific gene program [63] and in skeletal muscle cell fusion and development into myotubes [64], respectively, as well as histone H1.5 (H15) which seem to be critical for skeletal myogenesis [65]. Remarkably, rats treated with relaxin‐2 showed an exclusive expression in skeletal muscle of the transcription activator BRG1 (SMCA4), a component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex and a pivotal factor that enables the gene expression responsible for myogenesis and differentiation [66]. Relaxin‐2 upregulated skeletal muscle levels of neurogranin, which regulates the affinity of calmodulin for calcium, being able to inhibit calcineurin signaling, myoblast differentiation, and fusion [67]. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) phosphorylated levels are strongly upregulated by relaxin‐2, and this apolipoprotein is tightly involved in coupling myogenesis and lipid metabolism in skeletal muscle [44].

We have deciphered that relaxin‐2 also shapes the levels of many proteins in skeletal muscle tightly related to glucose metabolism, especially glucose trafficking and GLUT4 translocation. Skeletal muscle is essential for glucose homeostasis, being responsible for 80% of postprandial glucose uptake from the circulation, and as the principal organ of insulin‐stimulated glucose disposal, it is also considered the primary driver of whole‐body insulin resistance [68]. Other researchers and we have previously established the role of relaxin‐2 in regulating glucose metabolism in other muscle cells, such as cardiomyocytes [20, 69], so it is comprehensible that its effect also occurs in skeletal muscle. Curiously, many of these proteins related to glucose trafficking are also involved in myogenesis and muscle regeneration in concordance with the notion that glucose restriction prevents myoblast differentiation [70]. Kinesin‐1 heavy chain (KINH) is significantly decreased in skeletal muscle of relaxin‐2‐treated rats, while it is reported that the microtubule motor protein kinesin‐1 enhances translocation of the glucose transporter GLUT4 to the cell surface for glucose uptake and is crucial for myogenic differentiation and regeneration [71]. An impairment in kinesin‐1 activity led to a reduction in mitochondrial ATP synthesis necessary for the myogenic program [72]. Alpha‐actinin‐4 (ACTN4), which increased the promoter activities of myogenic genes [73], is diminished by relaxin‐2 in skeletal muscle in our study and is probably involved in tethering GLUT4 vesicles to the cortical actin cytoskeleton [74]. Similarly, relaxin‐2 downregulated the levels of actin‐related protein 2/3 complex subunit 1B (ARC1B) and actin‐related protein 2/3 complex subunit 5‐like protein (ARP5L) in skeletal muscle, two components of the Arp2/3 complex related to the cytoskeleton that mediates actin polymerization and is essential for insulin‐mediated GLUT4 vesicle insertion in muscle cells [75] and to organize desmin to crosslink myofibrils for nuclear movement [76]. Calpain‐2 catalytic subunit (CAN2), related to the calpain system involved in cytoskeletal remodeling and the regulation of muscle mass and glucose transporter GLUT4 turnover [77], is also diminished by relaxin‐2 in skeletal muscle. Calpains are also the substrate of many spectrins, essential cytoskeleton proteins involved in the organization and function of membrane integral proteins, such as spectrin beta chain, non‐erythrocytic 2 (SPTN2) [78], which we have also found reduced by relaxin‐2. The protein 6‐phosphofructo‐2‐kinase/fructose‐2,6‐bisphosphatase 3 (F263), involved in promoting the glucose‐sensing mechanism in myocytes [79], is exclusively identified in skeletal muscle from relaxin‐2‐treated rats, and the VAC14 component of PIKFYVE complex (VAC14), essential for systemic glucose homeostasis and insulin‐regulated glucose uptake/GLUT4 translocation in skeletal muscle [80], is increased by relaxin‐2 in skeletal muscle. Most remarkably, contraction‐stimulated glucose transport and insulin‐stimulated glucose uptake in glycolytic skeletal muscle from mice are influenced by the serine/threonine‐protein kinase PAK2 [81], which we have found decreased in skeletal muscle by relaxin‐2. Importantly, and related to all these outcomes, evidence in humans and rodents supports the idea that the actin cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix (ECM) may be relevant to muscle glucose uptake and GLUT4 transport in response to insulin‐, contraction‐ and tension‐mediated pathways [82]. Indeed, we have shown here that relaxin‐2 shaped the expression of 16 proteins related to structural integrity and organization, and the cytoskeleton in rat skeletal muscle.

Since muscle has high energetic demands, its development and function critically rely on mitochondrial homeostasis [83]. Skeletal muscle has a high mitochondrial density, and mitochondrial function is closely associated with myogenic differentiation and energy provision [84]. Consequently, skeletal muscle mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with several diseases, including muscular dystrophies, atrophy, aging, sarcopenia, and insulin resistance [83]. Through our proteomic approach, we have demonstrated here that relaxin‐2 strongly regulates energetic metabolism, myogenesis and muscle differentiation in the skeletal muscle of healthy rats as discussed above. Regarding oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), relaxin‐2 modulates the levels of proteins/subunits from the different complexes of the ETC in skeletal muscle, decreasing the levels of NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] flavoprotein 2 (NDUV2), succinate dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] flavoprotein subunit (SDHA), ATP synthase F(0) complex subunit B1 (AT5F1), ATP synthase subunit alpha (ATPA), cytochrome b‐c1 complex subunit 1 (QCR1) while increasing the levels of NADH–ubiquinone oxidoreductase 75 kDa subunit (NDUS1), and being the ATP synthase subunit g (ATP5L) exclusively detected in the skeletal muscle of relaxin‐2‐treated rats. It has been reported that an altered proteome and post‐translational modifications of mitochondrial proteins in the skeletal muscle, including many complexes of ETC, modulate human muscle physiological and pathological processes [85, 86]. Mitochondrial damage during muscle diseases also involves altered calcium homeostasis and oxidative stress, among other mechanisms [87, 88]. Interestingly, breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein homolog (BRCA1) is expressed in skeletal muscle (in mitochondria, nucleus and cytoplasm) and is critical for the maintenance of mitochondrial bioenergetics and metabolic function in this tissue [89, 90]. Here, relaxin‐2 caused an exclusive expression of BRCA1 in skeletal muscle. Muscle mitochondrial function is regulated by L‐lactate dehydrogenase B chain (LDHB), a glycolytic enzyme that is decreased by relaxin‐2 in skeletal muscle, which catalyzes the interconversion of pyruvate and lactate, critical fuel metabolites of skeletal muscle, especially during exercise [91]. cGMP‐dependent 3′,5′‐cyclic phosphodiesterase (PDE2A2), involved in the regulation of mitochondrial cAMP levels and respiration, mitochondria morphology/dynamics and apoptotic cell death [92], is upregulated by relaxin‐2 in skeletal muscle, while heat shock protein HSP 90‐beta (HS90B) levels are downregulated by relaxin‐2 in skeletal muscle, a protein that arbitrates skeletal muscle plasticity modulating substrate use, including glucose and fatty acids, in physiological and disease conditions [93]. Aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDH) contribute to skeletal muscle homeostasis in healthy, aging, and Duchenne muscular dystrophy patients, and play a critical role in detoxification [94]. In our approach, relaxin‐2 decreased the levels of ALDH isoenzyme AL9A1 and increased AT1A2, the subunit alpha‐2 of the Na, K‐ATPase in skeletal muscle, whose tight regulation is essential to maintain whole‐body homeostasis and muscle function, including myogenesis and angiogenesis [94, 95].

Relaxin‐2 also diminished the levels of annexin A6 (ANXA6) as well as its phosphorylated form in skeletal muscle, which is relevant for muscle membrane resealing, forming a repair cap at the site of injury [43]. ANXA6 possesses a complementary role in sarcolemma membrane repair with EH domain‐containing protein 2 (EHD2) [96], exclusively identified in the skeletal muscle of relaxin‐2‐treated rats and required for myogenesis and muscle repair [97]. Additionally, skeletal muscle sarcomere organization requires actin scaffolding by EHD2 and clathrin heavy chain [98, 99], the last one reduced by relaxin‐2, while triadin (TRDN) levels are increased in skeletal muscle from relaxin‐2‐treated rats. TRDN protein is localized in the triad of the skeletal muscle and in the whole sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), contributes to the regulation of lumenal Ca2+ release and efficient muscle contraction, and is associated both with ryanodine receptor (RyR1) in the triad and with the inositol 1,4,5‐trisphosphate receptor (ITPR1) in longitudinal SR [100]. In relation to these results, ITPR1 (exclusively identified in skeletal muscle from relaxin‐2‐treated rats) is required for normal skeletal muscle activity, regulating gene expression, energy metabolism, and mitochondrial activity, as well as myoblast differentiation [101]. Rats treated with relaxin‐2 express other proteins related to calcium handling/signaling and regulation of SR in skeletal muscle, vital for the proper function of this organ [87]; such as sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase 3 (AT2A3), calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase type II subunit beta (KCC2B), and can diminish the levels of proteins junctophilin‐2 (JPH2) and regucalcin (RGN).

Similarly to our results presented here, other sexual hormones, such as estrogens, participate in muscle regeneration and control energy balance in skeletal muscle through the regulation of glucose metabolism and the activation of mitochondrial energy metabolism, including fatty acid oxidation, the TCA cycle, and oxidative OXPHOS, as well as mitochondrial biogenesis [102, 103]. Indeed, regarding TCA, we have described that relaxin‐2 reduced the levels of isocitrate dehydrogenase [NADP] mitochondrial (IDHP), which regulates the TCA cycle and is highly expressed in the heart and skeletal muscle, playing roles in glucose sensing, glutamine metabolism, and protection against oxidative stress [104]. Moreover, insulin‐like peptide 3 (INSL3), a relaxin‐like peptide mainly produced by Leydig cells of the testis, also plays a significant role in maintaining skeletal muscle function by increasing myotube size and myosin heavy chain protein synthesis [105], which is related to many insights caused by relaxin‐2 discussed before. Interestingly, we have found that relaxin‐2 can increase skeletal muscle levels of the adipokine nesfatin‐1 or nucleobindin‐2 (NUCB2), which stimulates GLUT4 membrane translocation in skeletal muscle [106]. Our group has previously reported that NUCB2 shares common pathways with relaxin‐2 related to glucose uptake in cardiac muscle cells by activating extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) 1/2, AKT, and the Akt substrate of 160 kDa (AS160) [20, 107].

There are several limitations to this work. This research has been focused on defining relaxin‐2 function on skeletal muscle at the physiological level, and the main limitation was that our in vivo approach employed healthy rats, which hinders the possible effect of serelaxin on the skeletal muscle proteomic profile since these animals did not show any pathology or injury that could alter this tissue. Considering that many of the proteins modulated by relaxin‐2 in the present study appear to be involved in biological pathways/mechanisms that are dysregulated in many musculoskeletal disorders, further studies should contemplate the therapeutic effect of serelaxin in experimental models of muscular atrophy, metabolic dysfunction affecting skeletal muscle (such as streptozotocin‐induced diabetes or diet‐induced insulin resistance), models of skeletal muscle fibrosis (e.g., dystrophin‐deficient models or overuse injury) and even CV diseases that courses with muscle atrophy and/or sarcopenia including skeletal muscle mitochondrial abnormalities such as HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Moreover, our investigation of the role of relaxin‐2 in the skeletal muscle showed some limitations in the literature since most of the reports did not delineate the relaxin‐specific receptor in this tissue.

Overall, our work has deciphered for the first time the regulatory role of relaxin‐2 on crucial processes of muscle physiology, including myogenesis, muscle differentiation and reparation, glucose metabolism, OXPHOS, mitochondrial function, and cytoskeletal organization, among others, in skeletal muscle employing a proteomic approach. Taking into account that these mechanisms constitute common physiopathological features in skeletal muscle remodeling and dysfunction during CV diseases, especially HF [47], our study generates novel outcomes regarding the possible use of serelaxin as an opportunity for the management of common states of skeletal muscle injury, metabolic disorders, and HF, for example. Notwithstanding, it remains to be addressed the effect of relaxin‐2 on the skeletal muscle proteome in pathological states, and we consider that further research should include this issue, which might open new therapeutic panoramas in the area of myopathies and cardiometabolic diseases, but that is beyond the scope of this work.
