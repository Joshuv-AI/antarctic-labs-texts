Comparative effectiveness of single-stage versus two-stage revision for hip prosthetic joint infection: emulation of the INFORM target trial using electronic health record data
Authors: Changqing Zhang, Yusheng Li, Chi Su, Yun Shen, Shengdi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the comparative effectiveness of single-stage versus two-stage revision for hip prosthetic joint infection (PJI) in Chinese tertiary hospitals by emulating the INFORM randomized trial using the target trial emulation framework.DesignRetrospective cohort study emulating a target trial, reported per the TARGET guideline. The protocol and analysis plan were written before analysis but were not prospectively registered.SettingThree tertiary hospitals in China. Index revision surgeries were performed between 1 January 2018 and 30 June 2022, with follow-up data locked on 31 December 2023, ensuring a minimum potential follow-up of 18 months for every patient.Participants754 adults with confirmed hip PJI undergoing revision surgery (280 single-stage; 474 two-stage), identified from electronic health records and confirmed by manual chart review. Treatment groups reflected the intended surgical strategy documented before or at the index operation.Main outcome measuresPrimary outcome: Harris Hip Score (HHS) at 18 months. Secondary outcomes: surgically treated reinfection, reoperation, mortality, and complications. The primary analysis used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) on the full cohort with multiple imputation of missing covariate and outcome data; 1:1 propensity score (PS) matching (280 pairs) was confirmatory. Patients who died before an assessment were excluded from the functional analysis at that assessment rather than having a score imputed, so the functional estimand is restricted to survivors (727 of 754 at 18 months). No a priori sample size calculation was performed because all eligible patients were included; precision is reported in place of retrospective power.ResultsMean age was 60.6 (SD 14.4) years; 511 of 754 patients (67.8%) were male. Avascular necrosis was the leading indication for the primary arthroplasty (390/754, 51.7%). The adjusted mean difference in HHS at 18 months was 1.68 points (95% confidence interval [CI] − 0.12 to 3.48; P = 0.07), favoring single-stage but not reaching statistical significance; the interval is small relative to the within-person minimal clinically important improvement (MCII) for the HHS reported after primary hip arthroplasty (approximately 16 to 18 points), but that threshold describes improvement within an individual patient rather than a difference between groups, and no minimal important between-group difference has been established for this instrument in revision surgery, so we do not claim to have excluded a clinically important difference. At 3 months, single-stage was associated with a higher HHS (mean difference 5.82, 95% CI 1.43 to 10.21; P = 0.01); this early difference is attributable in part to many two-stage patients not yet having undergone reimplantation. Surgically treated reinfection was similar in frequency (13.2% v 11.8%; risk ratio [RR] 1.12, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.74). Fewer intraoperative adverse events were recorded for single-stage (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.82), but this comparison is confounded by unequal operative exposure, because two-stage patients underwent two operative episodes and single-stage patients one, and it should not be read as a safety advantage of single-stage surgery. Mortality did not differ significantly (2.9% v 4.0%). The direction of the estimated difference was consistent across sensitivity analyses and no analysis was statistically significant, but the magnitude was not stable (1.18 to 2.48 points). Findings were directionally consistent with the INFORM trial, although HHS and WOMAC are distinct instruments and the estimates are not numerically comparable.ConclusionsIn this observational emulation, an intended single-stage strategy was associated with 18-month functional outcomes that did not differ significantly from an intended two-stage strategy. The comparison is statistically inconclusive for small differences and does not demonstrate equivalence, which was not formally tested. Because treatment was not randomized, the protocol was not prospectively registered, the magnitude of the estimate varied across model specifications, outcome ascertainment was incomplete, and the functional comparison is conditional on survival, these findings are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective, adequately powered studies before informing practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00402-026-06503-0.Keywords: Prosthetic joint infection, Target trial emulation, Hip arthroplasty, Single-stage revision, Causal inference

Introduction

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) after total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a devastating complication, occurring after 1% to 2% of primary arthroplasties [1]. It carries substantial morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, elevated mortality, and considerable economic cost [2, 3]. Hip arthroplasty volume in China has risen rapidly over the past decade [4]. The optimal surgical strategy for treating established hip PJI remains contested.

Two approaches dominate clinical practice. Single-stage revision removes the infected prosthesis, debrides the wound, and reimplants a new prosthesis in one operation. Two-stage revision separates removal from reimplantation by an interim period during which the patient lives with an antibiotic spacer while receiving targeted antibiotic therapy [1]; in the INFORM trial the interval between stages was permitted to range from two weeks to twelve months [5]. Two-stage revision has been the prevailing approach worldwide for decades. In a multicentre Beijing series it accounted for approximately 75% of hip PJI revisions [6], and in a national survey of 34 leading Chinese arthroplasty centres only seven (20.5%) reported offering single-stage revision for hip PJI [7].

The INFORM trial, published in 2022, was the first randomized controlled trial (RCT) to compare these two strategies [8]. It randomized 140 patients across 15 UK and Swedish centers and found no difference in Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores at 18 months (mean difference 0.13, 95% confidence interval [CI] − 8.20 to 8.46; P = 0.98). Single-stage revision was associated with better function at 3 months (mean difference 11.53, 95% CI 3.89 to 19.17; P = 0.003) and with fewer intraoperative adverse events (8% v 27%; P = 0.01), and was cost-effective. The trial was not powered for infection outcomes; at 18 months at least one marker of possible ongoing infection was recorded in 14% and 11% of the single-stage and two-stage groups respectively (P = 0.62) [8]. It enrolled predominantly White European patients with osteoarthritis-related THA and excluded no patients on the basis of organism or sinus tract status [5, 8].

Whether these findings generalize to Chinese hospitals is uncertain. Chinese hip PJI patients are younger and predominantly male, and the indication profile for the index arthroplasty differs, with avascular necrosis a leading indication for primary THA in China [4]. The microbiological profile also differs: in a 34-centre mainland Chinese series, methicillin-resistant staphylococci accounted for 25.6% of PJI cases [9]. These differences may modify the comparative effectiveness of single-stage versus two-stage revision.

Target trial emulation (TTE) provides a framework for estimating causal treatment effects from observational data by explicitly specifying and emulating the protocol of a hypothetical randomized trial [10, 11]. This approach has been increasingly adopted in pharmacoepidemiology [12] but remains virtually unused in orthopedic surgery, and its application in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) and global health settings faces recognized barriers [13]. We used the TTE framework to emulate the INFORM trial using electronic health record (EHR) data from three Chinese tertiary hospitals. We hypothesized that the comparative effectiveness of single-stage versus two-stage revision for hip PJI in Chinese practice would be broadly consistent with the INFORM trial’s findings, with no clinically meaningful difference in functional outcome at 18 months, despite the distinct patient population and surgical context.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study emulating the target trial (the INFORM trial) comparing single-stage with two-stage revision for hip PJI. Data were extracted from EHRs at three tertiary hospitals in China. The EHR query window spanned 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2023; eligible index revision surgeries were restricted to those performed between 1 January 2018 and 30 June 2022 so that every included patient had the opportunity to reach 18-month follow-up before the data lock on 31 December 2023. The target trial protocol (Table 1) was specified following the Hernán-Robins framework [10, 11], the TARGET reporting guideline [14], and the implementation framework of Ren et al. [15]. Time-zero alignment followed the recommendations of Fu et al. [16].

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hosting hospital, and by the institutional review board of each participating hospital. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. We did not obtain informed consent from participants because we used anonymized data compiled from EHRs and the registry; this waiver was approved by the ethics committees owing to the retrospective design.

Protocol registration and designation of analyses

The protocol of this target trial emulation was submitted to Ethics Committee of the hosting hospital. The target trial protocol and statistical analysis plan were specified in writing before the analysis was undertaken, but were not prospectively registered in a public repository before the investigators accessed outcome data. The TARGET statement asks authors to report whether, when, and where a study protocol was registered [14], and harmonized protocol templates for real-world evidence studies recommend prospective registration to constrain analytical flexibility [17]. Because registration is absent, the distinction between analyses specified in advance and those added later cannot be independently verified; we therefore label every analysis explicitly, as recommended for observational research [18]. The primary IPTW analysis, the confirmatory PS-matched analysis, the per-protocol analysis, the negative control outcome analysis, and the subgroup analyses were specified in the written analysis plan before analysis. The precision calculation, the benchmarking of the estimate against the minimal clinically important improvement, and the assessment of collinearity and separation in the propensity score model were added after peer review and are designated post hoc throughout.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were directly involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this study. Dissemination of results to participating hospitals and clinical teams is planned.

Target trial emulation

Table 1 details the seven components of the target trial protocol and their emulation. Eligible adults (≥ 18 years) with confirmed hip PJI requiring revision surgery were classified into treatment groups according to the intended surgical strategy, defined at time zero (the date of the index revision operation; for two-stage, the date of the first-stage surgery). This corresponds to the scenario described by Fu et al. [16], in which eligibility is met once and strategies are distinguishable at baseline, eliminating the need for cloning or sequential trial designs.

Eligibility criteria and treatment strategies

Patients were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes (T84.5x) combined with ICD-9-CM-3 procedure codes for revision arthroplasty (81.53, 00.70–00.72) (eTable 2). PJI diagnosis was confirmed by manual chart review applying Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) 2011/International Consensus Meeting (ICM) 2013 criteria [19]. Exclusion criteria are detailed in eMethods 1. Patients whose baseline records were too incomplete to permit propensity score estimation (n = 213), the majority of whom were inter-hospital transfers, were excluded; because transfer status may itself be associated with case complexity and with treatment choice, this exclusion is treated as a potential selection mechanism rather than a neutral restriction and is examined in the Discussion.

Treatment groups were defined by the intended strategy at time zero rather than by what was ultimately accomplished. The intended strategy was ascertained from the documented preoperative surgical plan (admission notes, multidisciplinary team records, and the planned-procedure field of the operative booking) and was therefore available at, and not after, time zero. A single-stage intention was defined as a documented plan for prosthesis removal, debridement, and reimplantation under one anesthetic. A two-stage intention was defined as a documented plan for staged removal-then-reimplantation with an interim antibiotic spacer. Procedures planned as single-stage but converted intraoperatively to a staged approach (e.g., because of unexpected bone loss or soft-tissue compromise; n = 11) were retained in the single-stage group for the intention-to-treat analysis and counted as protocol deviations in the per-protocol analysis (eMethods 3). Intraoperative findings were therefore not used to assign baseline treatment group, which avoids classifying patients on the basis of post-baseline information. Residual potential for misclassification of intention is addressed in the Discussion.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was Harris Hip Score (HHS) at 18 months, adapted from the INFORM trial’s WOMAC because HHS is the standard functional measure in Chinese orthopedic practice [8]. The two instruments assess overlapping constructs and are strongly correlated in formal validation cohorts (for example, r = − 0.88 between HHS and WOMAC, the negative sign reflecting their opposite scoring directions) [20], with correlations between a modified HHS and WOMAC or the Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score ranging from − 0.47 to − 0.91 [21]; this supports the substitution while not rendering the scales numerically interchangeable. The MCII for the HHS is approximately 16 to 18 points, derived in a cohort of 2,667 patients undergoing primary hip arthroplasty [22]. Two features of that threshold constrain its use here. First, it is an anchor-based estimate of the smallest improvement that an individual patient perceives as beneficial, that is, a within-person change threshold, and not an estimate of the smallest difference in mean score between two groups that would be clinically important; these are conceptually distinct quantities, and a threshold derived at the individual level cannot be transferred directly to a group comparison [23, 24]. A between-group mean difference smaller than the within-person MCII may still reflect a worthwhile shift in the distribution of outcomes, whereas a between-group difference exceeding it does not guarantee that individual patients perceive benefit. Second, no MCII for the total HHS has been established for revision hip arthroplasty. We therefore use the MCII only as an informal descriptive reference for the scale of the estimates, and not as a threshold against which clinical importance is judged; no minimal important between-group difference was defined, none having been established for this instrument in this population. The appropriate group-level use of a within-person threshold is a responder analysis comparing the proportion of patients improving by at least the MCII, which was not prespecified here and is proposed as future work. Secondary outcomes included surgically treated reinfection, any reoperation, all-cause mortality, intraoperative adverse events, postoperative complications, and serious adverse events (eMethods 2). For two-stage patients, intraoperative adverse events were ascertained across both operative stages, whereas single-stage patients were exposed to a single operative episode; this difference in operative exposure is considered when interpreting the results. A negative control outcome specified in advance, new-onset hypertension during follow-up, was analyzed to detect residual confounding not attributable to the treatment comparison [25]. Outcomes were extracted from outpatient follow-up records at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months (± 6-week window at 18 months).

Statistical analysis

Two causal contrasts were estimated: an intention-to-treat (ITT) effect comparing groups as classified at time zero, and a per-protocol effect comparing patients who adhered to their assigned strategy [11]. The primary analysis used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) with stabilized weights on the full eligible cohort (n = 754). Propensity scores (PS) were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including 32 baseline covariates across eight domains (eTable 3) [26].

Covariates were selected as confounders or predictors of outcome rather than by statistical significance, and variables associated only with treatment were avoided [27]. Several inflammatory markers in the model (C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, fibrinogen, D-dimer) are mutually correlated. Because the propensity score is a balancing score used for prediction rather than for causal interpretation of individual coefficients, collinearity among covariates does not compromise its balancing property [26, 28]; in a post hoc check the model was inspected for numerical instability and converged with finite coefficients and standard errors, with no evidence of complete or quasi-complete separation, for which penalized (Firth) estimation would otherwise have been applied [29]. With 280 patients in the smaller treatment group and 32 covariates, the ratio of treated patients to covariates was approximately 8.8:1, marginally below the conventional 10:1 rule of thumb; model adequacy was therefore judged primarily by achieved covariate balance rather than by model fit [30].

Missing data in both baseline covariates and the HHS outcomes were handled together using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with 20 imputed datasets; imputation preceded PS estimation, PS models and weights were derived within each imputed dataset, and estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules [31, 32]. Thus the primary analysis was not a complete-case analysis and used all 754 patients; missing 18-month HHS values (110/754, 14.6%) for patients still under observation were imputed rather than excluded. Missingness was not completely at random: the proportion of missing values differed between treatment groups for several variables (for example, D-dimer 11.1% versus 14.6%, and fibrinogen 6.8% versus 11.8%, in the single-stage and two-stage groups respectively; eTable 4) and was related to observed covariates, so a missing-completely-at-random mechanism was rejected [33]. The primary analysis therefore assumed data were missing at random conditional on a rich imputation model that included treatment, all analysis covariates, outcomes at every time point, and auxiliary variables. Because the missing-at-random assumption cannot be verified from the observed data [34, 35], a tipping-point (δ-adjusted) analysis was used to assess departures from it. Stabilized weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance [30]. Positivity was assessed by inspecting the PS distribution for adequate overlap across treatment groups (eFigure 1).

As a confirmatory analysis, we performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor PS matching (caliper = 0.2 standard deviations of the logit PS), yielding 280 matched pairs (n = 560). The primary outcome was analyzed using weighted linear regression of HHS at 18 months on treatment group, adjusted for baseline HHS. Binary outcomes were analyzed using log-binomial regression (risk ratios) and risk difference models. Kaplan-Meier survival curves with log-rank tests were constructed for time-to-event outcomes. For the per-protocol analysis, patients were censored at protocol deviation and inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) were applied using pooled logistic regression conditional on baseline and monthly time-varying covariates [11, 36].

Truncation of the functional outcome by death. Twenty-seven patients (8 single-stage, 19 two-stage) died before the 18-month assessment. For these patients the 18-month HHS is not missing but undefined, because a functional score cannot exist for a patient who has died; imputing one would generate a value with no counterpart in reality [37, 38]. Their scores were therefore not imputed. The primary functional estimand is accordingly survivor-restricted, being the difference in mean HHS at 18 months among patients alive at that assessment, and the outcome model was fitted in the 727 patients (272 single-stage, 455 two-stage) alive at 18 months. Of these, 644 had an observed score and 83 (11.4%) were alive with no recorded score and were imputed; the 110 patients with no recorded 18-month HHS therefore comprise 27 deaths, which were excluded, and 83 survivors, who were imputed (eTable 9). Eight patients whose vital status was unknown were assumed alive. The same principle was applied at the 3-, 6- and 12-month assessments, the analysis population at each comprising patients alive at that assessment. Death was analysed separately as an outcome in its own right and was not combined with function into a composite. In the terminology of the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework, death is an intercurrent event and this analysis corresponds to a while-alive strategy rather than to a composite or principal-stratum (survivor average causal effect) strategy [39, 40]; the resulting contrast is conditional on survival, and its interpretation is considered in the Discussion.

Sensitivity analyses included: PS matching (n = 560); adjustment for residual covariate imbalance using doubly robust estimation; a complete-case analysis; a tipping-point (δ-adjusted) analysis relaxing the missing-at-random assumption for the primary outcome; adjustment for surgeon and hospital as fixed effects; E-value calculation for unmeasured confounding [41]; and the negative control outcome analysis. Subgroup analyses examining PJI classification, organism type, difficult-to-treat organisms, age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, and hospital were exploratory. The study was not powered for interaction testing and no formal tests of interaction were performed; accordingly, subgroup-specific estimates are presented descriptively, and a difference in statistical significance between subgroups is not interpreted as evidence of effect modification, which requires a formal interaction test [42, 43]. This was not a noninferiority study, and no clinical equivalence margin was defined in advance; secondary outcomes were exploratory and were not adjusted for multiplicity. All analyses used R version 4.3 (packages: WeightIt, cobalt, MatchIt, mice, survey). Further details are in eMethods 3.

Sample size and precision (post hoc)

No a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. The study included every patient meeting the eligibility criteria within the fixed accrual window, so the sample size was determined by the size of the eligible source population rather than by a target effect size. Retrospective (observed) power was deliberately not computed, because observed power is a one-to-one deterministic function of the P value and conveys no information beyond the confidence interval [44]; precision is reported instead [45]. For the primary outcome the achieved standard error was 0.92 HHS points, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval half-width of 1.80 points. This precision corresponds to approximately 80% power to detect a true between-group difference of about 2.6 HHS points at a two-sided α of 0.05. For orientation only, the within-person MCII for the HHS after primary hip arthroplasty is approximately 16 to 18 points [22]; because that threshold applies to change within an individual rather than to a difference between groups [23, 24], it cannot be used to define what this study was or was not able to exclude, and we make no such claim. What can be stated is that the analysis was not designed to resolve between-group differences smaller than approximately 2.6 points. A non-significant result is interpreted as an absence of evidence of a difference, and not as evidence of equivalence, which was not formally tested and for which no margin was prespecified [46]. This calculation was performed after peer review and is post hoc.

Results

Study population

Of 2263 patients screened, 754 met eligibility criteria after exclusions (Fig. 1; eTable 1). Of these, 280 (37.1%) were treated with an intended single-stage strategy and 474 (62.9%) with an intended two-stage strategy, consistent with the two-stage–dominant practice pattern reported in Chinese centres [6, 7]. The confirmatory PS matching yielded 280 matched pairs (n = 560, 74.3% of the eligible cohort); the 194 unmatched two-stage patients were excluded from the matched analysis but retained in the primary IPTW analysis. Twenty-seven patients (8 single-stage, 19 two-stage) died before the 18-month assessment. Because a functional score is undefined after death, these patients were excluded from the functional analysis rather than having a score imputed, leaving 727 patients (272 single-stage, 455 two-stage) in the primary functional analysis population (eTable 9). Of these, 644 (88.6%) had an observed 18-month HHS and 83 (11.4%) were alive with no recorded score and were imputed; 618 patients had both an observed 18-month HHS and complete baseline covariate data and constituted the complete-case sensitivity analysis.

Baseline characteristics

Table 2 presents baseline characteristics with standardized mean differences before and after IPTW. Mean age was 60.6 (SD 14.4) years; 511 of 754 patients (67.8%) were male; mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.6 (SD 3.6). Avascular necrosis was the most common indication for the primary arthroplasty (390/754, 51.7%). Chronic PJI accounted for 592 of 754 infections (78.5%), and 209 (27.7%) were culture-negative. Before weighting, SMDs exceeded 0.1 for several covariates; after IPTW, all were below 0.1 (Fig. 2; Table 2). PS distributions showed adequate overlap across both treatment groups, with no evidence of practical positivity violations (eFigure 1).

Primary outcome

In the primary IPTW analysis, fitted among the 727 patients alive at the 18-month assessment with multiple imputation for the 83 survivors lacking a recorded score, the adjusted mean HHS at 18 months was 78.6 (SE 0.7) in the single-stage group and 76.9 (SE 0.5) in the two-stage group (Table 3). The weighted mean difference was 1.68 points (95% CI − 0.12 to 3.48; P = 0.07), favoring single-stage revision but not reaching statistical significance. The 95% confidence interval half-width was 1.80 points: the data are compatible with anything from a 0.12-point advantage of two-stage revision to a 3.48-point advantage of single-stage revision. This interval is small relative to the within-person MCII for the HHS reported after primary hip arthroplasty (approximately 16 to 18 points) [22], which gives an informal sense of scale; because that threshold refers to improvement within an individual patient rather than to a difference between groups, and because no minimal important between-group difference has been established for the HHS in revision surgery, it does not license a claim that a clinically important between-group difference has been excluded [23, 24]. The comparison remains statistically inconclusive for small differences and no equivalence margin was prespecified. The confirmatory PS-matched analysis (n = 560) yielded a similar estimate (mean difference 1.98 points, 95% CI − 0.25 to 4.21; P = 0.08). For context, the INFORM trial also reported no significant difference at 18 months (mean difference 0.13, 95% CI − 8.20 to 8.46; P = 0.98) [8], although HHS and WOMAC are distinct instruments and the two estimates are not directly comparable numerically.

Secondary outcomes (exploratory)

Single-stage revision was associated with a higher HHS at 3 months (mean difference 5.82 points, 95% CI 1.43 to 10.21; P = 0.01) (Fig. 3). This early difference attenuated by 6 months and was no longer significant at 12 or 18 months. It should be interpreted with caution: at 3 months from time zero, many two-stage patients had not yet undergone reimplantation and were living with an antibiotic spacer, so the difference is attributable in part to the inherent timeline and treatment burden of the two-stage strategy rather than necessarily to superior biologic recovery after single-stage revision. Functional scores during the spacer interval are characteristically lower and improve after reimplantation [47, 48]. Surgically treated reinfection was similar in frequency between groups: 13.2% (single-stage) versus 11.8% (two-stage) (risk ratio [RR] 1.12, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.74; P = 0.58) (Figs. 4 and 5A). All-cause mortality was numerically lower in the single-stage group (2.9% v 4.0%) but not statistically significant (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.80; P = 0.33) (Fig. 5B). Reoperation-free survival was similar (hazard ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.33; P = 0.22) (eFigure 4). Fewer intraoperative adverse events were recorded for single-stage revision (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.82; Fig. 4; Table 3); however, the denominator for two-stage patients encompassed two operative episodes (first-stage removal and second-stage reimplantation), whereas single-stage patients underwent one, so this difference partly reflects cumulative operative exposure and should not be interpreted as evidence that single-stage surgery is intrinsically safer. These secondary outcomes were exploratory, were not adjusted for multiplicity, and should be interpreted accordingly.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The direction of the primary estimate was consistent across all sensitivity analyses, and no analysis reached statistical significance; the magnitude, however, was not stable, with point estimates ranging from 1.18 to 2.48 HHS points, a roughly two-fold spread (Table 4; eFigure 3). The estimate attenuated from 1.68 to 1.28 points when surgeon and hospital were entered as fixed effects, indicating that part of the observed difference may be attributable to centre- and surgeon-level factors rather than to the operative strategy itself. The per-protocol analysis, censoring patients with protocol deviations (12.1% single-stage; 7.8% two-stage) and applying IPCW, produced the largest estimate (2.48 points, 95% CI − 0.64 to 5.60; P = 0.12) (eTable 7), and the doubly robust analysis the smallest (1.18 points, 95% CI − 0.63 to 2.99; P = 0.20). The complete-case analysis (n = 618) and the tipping-point analysis relaxing the missing-at-random assumption produced materially similar estimates. In every specification the upper confidence bound remained small relative to the within-person MCII; as above, this is offered as a description of scale and not as a formal exclusion of a clinically important between-group difference [23, 24]. The E-value for the point estimate was 1.32; however, because the 95% CI for the primary outcome already includes the null, the E-value for the confidence-bound nearest the null is 1.0, meaning no unmeasured confounding is required to shift the result to non-significance. This reinforces a cautious interpretation.

Exploratory subgroup estimates are presented in eTable 5 and eFigure 2. Point estimates favoured single-stage revision in 15 of 16 subgroups. No formal tests of interaction were performed and the study was not powered to detect effect modification; the nominally significant estimates in the chronic-PJI and Hospital B strata should therefore not be read as showing that these subgroups differ from their comparators, because a difference in statistical significance between two subgroups is not a test of interaction [42, 43]. With 16 strata examined, one or more nominally significant estimates would be expected by chance alone. Benchmarking against the INFORM trial showed directional consistency for the outcomes that could be compared (eTable 6).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this target trial emulation using EHR data from three Chinese tertiary hospitals, an intended single-stage strategy for hip PJI was associated with 18-month functional outcomes that did not differ significantly from those of an intended two-stage strategy. The point estimate favoured single-stage revision by 1.68 HHS points, but the comparison was statistically inconclusive for small differences. The estimate and its confidence interval are small relative to the within-person improvement threshold reported for the HHS, which conveys a sense of scale; because no minimal important between-group difference has been established for this instrument in revision surgery, we do not claim to have excluded a clinically important difference, and we did not test equivalence. The functional comparison is additionally conditional on survival to the assessment. Single-stage was associated with faster early recovery at 3 months and fewer recorded intraoperative adverse events, though both findings are exploratory and subject to important interpretive caveats discussed below. Surgically treated reinfection was similar in frequency between groups. The overall direction of findings was broadly consistent with the INFORM trial [8], despite substantial differences in patient demographics, indication profile, microbiology, and surgical practice between the Chinese and UK/Swedish settings.

Comparison with other studies

The INFORM trial remains the only RCT comparing these two strategies [8]. Our findings are directionally consistent with its principal result of no significant difference in functional outcome at 18 months, although the use of different instruments (HHS versus WOMAC), the observational design, and the distinct study population mean the two studies cannot be equated and our results do not constitute a formal replication. The substitution of HHS for WOMAC is supported by evidence that the two instruments capture overlapping constructs: cross-cultural validation of the HHS reported a correlation of r = − 0.88 with WOMAC (the negative sign reflecting the opposite scoring directions of the two scales), with high internal consistency and test–retest reliability [20], and validation of a modified HHS against WOMAC and the Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score reported correlations between − 0.47 and − 0.91 [21]. Strong correlation does not, however, make the scales numerically interchangeable, and we have avoided direct numerical comparison of effect sizes throughout. The early functional advantage for single-stage in our data is consistent with the pattern observed in INFORM, in which two-stage patients lacked a definitive prosthesis during the interstage interval. The magnitude we observed was smaller (5.8 versus 11.5 points), which may reflect the different instrument and the frequent use of articulating spacers in our cohort, which permit better interim function than static spacers [47, 48].

Our estimates of surgically treated reinfection (13.2% single-stage; 11.8% two-stage) cannot be directly compared with the corresponding INFORM figures, because the INFORM publication reports markers of possible ongoing infection at 18 months (14% v 11%) rather than a surgically treated endpoint; the definitions and ascertainment differ, and we therefore make only a qualitative comparison. Neither study found a significant between-group difference. The rates in our cohort may partly reflect the microbiological profile of Chinese PJI [9]. Ji et al. reported favourable infection control in a large Chinese single-stage series with broad inclusion criteria [49]. A recent meta-analysis of 14 studies and 1429 patients found no significant difference in reinfection between strategies for hip PJI (odds ratio 1.34, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.93; P = 0.12) [50].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the first to apply the TTE framework to orthopedic surgery in Chinese hospitals, addressing a recognized gap in the global health causal inference literature [13]. The protocol was structured using recent methodological advances: the TARGET guideline [14], the Ren et al. framework [15], and the Fu et al. time-zero alignment criteria [16]. Treatment groups were defined by intended strategy at time zero from the documented preoperative plan, follow-up time was aligned at the index operation, and the eligibility window was restricted to ensure complete potential follow-up, which together avoid immortal-time and related biases. Missing covariate and outcome data were handled by multiple imputation using all 754 patients, and the IPTW analysis was complemented by a confirmatory PS-matched analysis, a per-protocol IPCW analysis, a negative control outcome, and multiple sensitivity analyses.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, treatment was not randomized, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as surgeon experience, bone stock quality, and soft-tissue condition, cannot be excluded despite IPTW adjustment; the modest E-value (1.32) and a confidence interval that already crosses the null mean even slight unmeasured confounding could explain the result. Second, no a priori sample size calculation was performed and the analysis was powered by the size of the eligible population rather than by design; the achieved precision permits exclusion of differences of the magnitude usually regarded as clinically important but leaves the comparison inconclusive for differences smaller than approximately 2.6 HHS points, and the finding should not be read as demonstrating equivalence [46]. Third, although the direction of effect was consistent across specifications, the magnitude varied roughly two-fold (1.18 to 2.48 points) and attenuated when surgeon and hospital were modelled as fixed effects, indicating sensitivity to modelling choices and a possible contribution of centre- and surgeon-level factors. Fourth, the protocol and analysis plan were not prospectively registered before outcome data were accessed, so protection against analytical flexibility rests on internal documentation rather than on independent time-stamping [14, 17]. Fifth, 213 patients were excluded because baseline records were too incomplete for propensity score estimation, and most were inter-hospital transfers; transfer to a tertiary centre is plausibly associated both with greater case complexity, and therefore with a higher likelihood of a two-stage strategy, and with worse expected outcomes, so this exclusion is a selection mechanism rather than a neutral restriction and may bias the estimate in either direction [51]. Sixth, the propensity score model included 32 covariates against 280 patients in the smaller treatment group (approximately 8.8 treated patients per covariate), marginally below the conventional 10:1 heuristic; although balance was achieved on every covariate and collinearity does not impair the balancing property of a propensity score [26, 28], a more parsimonious specification might yield slightly different weights.

Seventh, the clinical-importance benchmark referred to throughout (an MCII of approximately 16 to 18 HHS points) is doubly limited. It was derived in patients undergoing primary hip arthroplasty [22], whereas baseline function, ceiling effects and expectations differ after revision surgery, and no validated value exists for the total HHS in revision arthroplasty; and, more fundamentally, it is a within-person improvement threshold rather than a minimal important between-group difference, so it cannot serve as a criterion for judging the clinical importance of a difference in group means [23, 24]. We therefore use it only to convey scale, and a responder analysis comparing the proportion of patients improving by at least the MCII, which would be the appropriate group-level use of such a threshold, was neither prespecified nor performed. Eighth, the functional comparison is restricted to survivors. Twenty-seven patients died before the 18-month assessment; their functional outcome is undefined rather than missing, and restricting the comparison to survivors conditions on a post-baseline event and can induce selection bias when mortality differs between groups [51]. Deaths were more frequent in the two-stage group (19 v 8; 4.0% v 2.9%). If the patients who died would have had below-average function had they survived, their exclusion raises the two-stage survivor mean and biases the single-stage advantage downward, making the reported estimate conservative; the opposite assumption would bias it upward. The absolute difference in mortality was 1.2% points and deaths represented 3.6% of the cohort, which limits but does not eliminate the scope for such bias. Survivors-only analyses of functional outcomes are common but can mislead, and a principal-stratum (survivor average causal effect) analysis would be the formal causal alternative [37, 39]. Ninth, although treatment group was assigned by intended strategy from the documented preoperative plan, the plan may occasionally have been incompletely recorded, leaving some residual potential for misclassification of intention; the 3-month and intraoperative-event comparisons are additionally shaped by the inherent two-stage timeline and by cumulative operative exposure across two stages, respectively, and should not be read as direct measures of biologic superiority. Tenth, the primary outcome was HHS rather than the INFORM trial’s WOMAC; although both are validated 0–100 hip-function scales, they weight domains differently and are not numerically interchangeable, and HHS was extracted retrospectively from clinic records, introducing measurement variability and a 15–21% rate of missing functional data that imputation can mitigate but not eliminate. Eleventh, surgically treated reinfection captures only infectious failures that led to a further operation; patients managed with chronic suppressive antibiotics, persistent low-grade drainage treated nonoperatively, or those not offered further surgery, may be missed, so true infectious failure may be underestimated (see Outcomes and eMethods 2). Twelfth, three tertiary referral centers may not represent lower-volume district hospitals where single-stage expertise is less available. Finally, secondary and subgroup outcomes were exploratory and unadjusted for multiplicity, no interaction tests were performed, and the study was neither randomized nor designed as a noninferiority trial with a prespecified margin.

Clinical implications

These findings are hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. At three high-volume Chinese tertiary centres, patients treated with an intended single-stage strategy had 18-month functional outcomes that did not differ significantly from those treated with an intended two-stage strategy, and the confidence interval was narrow, although for the reasons given above we do not translate that width into a claim about clinical importance. We deliberately refrain from specifying the clinical characteristics that might identify patients suited to single-stage surgery. The factors that actually governed strategy selection in routine practice, intraoperative assessment of bone stock and soft-tissue quality, the adequacy of debridement, and individual surgeon judgement, were not recorded in the electronic record and could not enter the propensity score. Defining a “suitable” subgroup from these data would therefore restate that unmeasured selection process as though it were an empirical finding, which is precisely the threat to validity identified above. What can reasonably be concluded is narrower: within the range of patients whom experienced Chinese surgeons already judged appropriate for either strategy, the choice of strategy was not associated with a detectable difference in 18-month function among those surviving to that assessment. Determining which patients benefit from each approach requires prospective studies in which the selection factors are measured explicitly, ideally randomized or formally designed as noninferiority comparisons with prespecified margins.

Methodologically, this study demonstrates that causal inference from Chinese EHR data is feasible when the analytical framework is carefully specified, while also illustrating its limits. China lacks a nationwide arthroplasty registry, making large-scale surgical RCTs difficult; well-designed TTE studies, potentially leveraging the national hospital quality monitoring system covering over 1,000 tertiary hospitals, could generate larger-scale evidence to inform future PJI treatment guidelines. Future emulations should register the protocol and analysis plan prospectively, define the estimand explicitly including the strategy for handling death as an intercurrent event, prespecify a minimal important between-group difference together with a responder definition, and record the intraoperative and surgeon-level determinants of treatment choice, which were the principal unmeasured confounders in the present study.

Conclusions

In patients treated at three tertiary Chinese hospitals, an intended single-stage revision strategy for hip PJI was associated with 18-month functional outcomes that did not differ significantly from those of an intended two-stage strategy, and the confidence interval made a difference of the magnitude usually regarded as clinically important unlikely in either direction. This does not establish equivalence, which was not formally tested. The comparison remains statistically inconclusive for small differences, the magnitude of the estimate varied across model specifications, outcome ascertainment was incomplete, treatment was not randomized, and the protocol was not prospectively registered. These results are therefore hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective, adequately powered studies before they can inform changes in clinical practice.
