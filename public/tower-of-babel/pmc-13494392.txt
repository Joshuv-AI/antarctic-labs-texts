The Use of Point-of-Care Transesophageal Echocardiography in the Diagnosis of Undifferentiated Cardiac Arrest in a Pregnant Patient
Authors: Erich Burton, Trenton Wray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Point-of-care transesophageal echocardiography, Resuscitation, Cardiac arrest, Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, Critical care

Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest in pregnancy occurs at a rate of 32.9 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2021.1 Standard algorithms call for perimortem cesarean section (PMCS) within 4 minutes of maternal cardiac arrest.2 However, in addition to hypovolemic and distributive etiologies, the differential diagnosis includes cardiogenic and obstructive causes that would not be resolved by the recommended PMCS. Point-of-care ultrasound may be used to differentiate the cause of arrest and direct management.3,4 Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) can be used but is often limited in critically ill patients and may prolong the duration without chest compressions.5,6 Rapidly deployed point-of-care transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can be used to quickly diagnose the cause of cardiac arrest and guide management in this high-risk patient population. In this report, we present a case of peripartum cardiac arrest in which TEE was performed by an intensivist, while the fetus remained in utero, leading to critical changes in management. What distinguishes this case is that TEE findings prompted initiation of mechanical circulatory support rather than proceeding with PMCS. This deviation from the standard advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) pathways helped avoid a potentially ineffective or harmful intervention while expediting appropriate, physiology-directed care.

Case Presentation

A 26-year-old woman gravida 4 para 3 at 35 weeks of gestational age presented to an outside hospital with hypoxia and agitation. They had a history of methamphetamine use but no other medical history. The patient was intubated due to agitation and transferred by medical helicopter with ongoing fetal monitoring to a tertiary care facility. En route fetal heart tones were lost. The patient was admitted to the medical intensive care unit (ICU) at our tertiary care facility. Vital signs were temperature of 37.8° C, heart rate 142 beats per minute, blood pressure 88/58 mm Hg, and SpO2 92% while intubated on volume control with a tidal volume of 7 cc/kg, respiratory rate 28, and end tidal carbon dioxide 26. Physical exam demonstrated cool extremities with delayed capillary refill, coarse and equal lung sounds bilaterally, +1 bilateral lower extremity pitting edema, no jugular venous distension, and gravid uterus with a mild amount of bloody vaginal discharge. Electrocardiogram (Figure 1) demonstrated sinus rhythm at a rate of 139 with no evidence of ischemia and QTc measured at 435 milliseconds. Chest x-ray was remarkable for borderline enlarged cardiac silhouette, right greater than left bilateral patchy airspace disease, and endotracheal tube approximately 3 cm above the carina (Figure 2). Laboratory evaluation showed a troponin level of 0.066 ng/mL, brain natriuretic peptide 10,498 pg/mL, hemoglobin 12.3 g/dL, creatinine 1.0 mg/dL, and actate 2.9 mmol/L. Venous blood gas was remarkable for pH of 7.39, pCO2 of 28 mm Hg, and pO2 of 109 mm Hg.

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram demonstrates sinus tachycardia without evidence of acute myocardial ischemia or injury and a QTc interval measured at 435 ms.

Two hours after admission, the patient experienced a witnessed cardiac arrest with an initial rhythm of pulseless electrical activity. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was immediately started. Return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved after 8 minutes of CPR, and the patient was placed on a high-dose norepinephrine and vasopressin infusion while receiving 2 L of lactated ringers via intravenous bolus. Despite this, the patient had a second cardiac arrest with bradycardic pulseless electrical activity. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation resumed immediately. Transthoracic echocardiography was attempted, but due to ongoing chest compressions with a mechanical CPR device, adequate views were not obtained. Plans were made for a PMCS. However, during the interim period, focused TEE was performed by the intensivist.

Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a well-filled left ventricle with severely reduced global left ventricular (LV) systolic function without regional wall motion abnormalities, a dilated left atrium with an interatrial septum bowed toward the right atrium, large central mitral regurgitation (MR) jet, normal right ventricular (RV) size and systolic function, and a dilated, noncollapsible inferior vena cava (Figures 3 and 4, Videos 1 and 2). Collectively, these findings suggested profound LV systolic failure with severe functional MR and markedly elevated left atrial pressure, while preserved RV size and function indicated predominantly left-sided circulatory failure. Given this constellation in the setting of worsening volume overload and the absence of hemorrhage, hypovolemia, acidosis, and right heart strain, we felt these findings were consistent with severe cardiogenic shock from peripartum cardiomyopathy and represented the cause of cardiac arrest.

Noting the findings of the TEE, we discussed the case with our obstetric and maternal fetal medicine colleagues. Collectively we agreed that the primary issue was pump failure, rather than decreased venous return, and did not think resuscitative hysterotomy would be acutely beneficial. The management plan was rapidly changed from performing a PMCS to placing the patient on venoarterial (VA) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Using TEE for guidance, a second intensivist placed standard super stiff J-tip working wires into the inferior vena cava and descending aorta. The venous wire was visualized on TEE throughout the dilation process to ensure it did not advance into the right ventricle. A 25 French multistage venous drainage cannula was placed in the right atrium, followed by a 15 French arterial return cannula placed in the right common femoral artery. An epinephrine drip was started at 0.05 μg/kg/min and titrated until the aortic valve opened on TEE (Video 3).

With perfusion restored, a right radial arterial catheter was placed, and inotropic medications were titrated to a pulse pressure of 8 to 15 mm Hg. While induction of labor was scheduled to occur once markers of perfusion improved and hemodynamics stabilized, as shown in the timeline of major clinical events (Table 1), the patient ultimately delivered the demised fetus spontaneously several hours later. The cause of death of the fetus was felt to be from prolonged hypotension resulting in placental malperfusion.

Once stabilized on VA ECMO, TTE demonstrated a severely dilated left ventricle with a visually estimated ejection fraction of 25%, severe functional MR, and normal RV size and function (Videos 4 and 5). Mechanical circulatory support was weaned over the next several days, and the patient was decannulated on hospital day 4. After decannulation, we used dobutamine to achieve a cardiac index of 2.5 L/min/m2 and diuresis to a pulmonary artery diastolic pressure less than 25. On hospital day 9 repeat TTE demonstrated normal LV size, moderately reduced global LV systolic function (Video 6), and moderate MR. Given the degree of LV recovery and timing of the disease process we felt this most likely represented a peripartum cardiomyopathy rather than predominately toxin-induced or ischemic cardiomyopathy. The hospital course was complicated by hypoxic-ischemic brain injury following cardiac arrest, with an initially guarded neurologic prognosis and uncertain recovery, ultimately necessitating tracheostomy and placement of a percutaneous gastrostomy tube. After an extended hospital course, the patient was discharged to a rehabilitation facility on hospital day 18.

Discussion

Focused ultrasound is recommended by guideline committees in emergency medicine and critical care as the first line of assessment for undifferentiated shock. As such, its use in critically ill patients has expanded. However, due to a variety of factors, critically ill patients often lack adequate windows for TTE. The American Society of Echocardiography recommends TEE when a diagnosis needs to be confirmed or excluded and TTE is inadequate.7 While TEE was previously performed primarily by cardiologists and cardiac anesthesiologists in specific situations, its use has expanded rapidly. Transesophageal echocardiography is now being used to guide the management of critically ill patients in a variety of environments, including the ICU, emergency department, and even prehospital setting. This case describes TEE performed by an intensivist in the ICU, in contrast to prior reports in which TEE was performed by cardiac anesthesiologists.8 Additionally, unlike previously described cases occurring in the operating room during planned cesarean delivery, this case demonstrates TEE-guided modification of ACLS management in a peripartum cardiac arrest outside the operative setting.9,10

As shown in Table 2, TEE is perhaps most beneficial in the setting of cardiac arrest. While TTE has the ability to rapidly diagnose the cause of refractory cardiac arrest, it has several limitations. These include but are not limited to difficult acoustic windows, positioning difficulty with ongoing CPR, and increased time without chest compressions. In the setting of cardiac arrest, a focused TEE exam allows the operator to be away from the chest, continuously visualize the quality of CPR, and obtain high-resolution images of cardiopulmonary pathology. In small studies TEE has been shown to have a sensitivity of 93% and positive predictive value of 87% for diagnosing the cause of arrest.11 Transesophageal echocardiography has been shown to change management in up to 81% of cardiac arrests.12 The most frequent changes include the decision to administer intravenous fluids and to initiate or escalate inotropic support and subspecialty consultation.

Advantages of TEE during cardiac arrest and refractory shock

The focused TEE exam is different from the standard 28-view exam. Typical resuscitative TEE protocols include 5 views: midesophageal 4-chamber, midesophageal long-axis, midesophageal bicaval, transgastric short axis, and descending thoracic aorta long axis. The goal is the rapid diagnosis of a finite number of pathologies that are most likely to lead to hemodynamic instability and arrest. Transesophageal echocardiography has the added benefit of guiding important procedures, including chest compressions. Ineffective chest compressions are often due to the point of maximal compression being too cephalad and leading to LV outflow tract compression.4 This is associated with less ability to obtain ROSC and may be recognized with TEE. During pulse checks, TEE can aid in rhythm recognition and identification of ROSC. It has the ability to rapidly determine whether extracorporeal life support (ECLS) is needed to restore perfusion, as is the case for left-heart failure, severe pulmonary hypertension, or right-heart failure or when ECLS would not be beneficial or even contraindicated, such as hypovolemic shock, distributive shock, aortic dissection, or severe aortic regurgitation. If ECLS is indicated, the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization recommends TEE to guide the cannulation process and immediate postcannulation management.13 This can all be achieved without moving the patient and with minimal interruption to CPR.

Although TEE has theoretical benefit in the setting of cardiac arrest, and preliminary studies demonstrate a significant effect on patient management, there remain limited data on whether the management changes associated with TEE result in a clinically important improvement in patient-oriented outcome. As such, multicenter, international consortiums have been formed to further elucidate the effect of TEE in cardiac arrest on patient outcomes.

This case highlights the ability of a focused TEE to rapidly transform the management of patients with undifferentiated cardiac arrest. Although the standard ACLS algorithm recommends PMCS if the cardiac arrest has lasted longer than 4 minutes, prompt TEE could provide a diagnosis that would alter management from the standard algorithm.14 The cause of maternal hemodynamic instability leading to arrest is multifactorial, with a differential that includes hemorrhagic shock, septic shock, massive pulmonary embolism, amniotic fluid embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and even acute coronary syndrome. These diagnoses require different types of support, some of which would not improve with PMCS and some for which prioritization of mechanical circulatory support could minimize delay to reperfusion. In our case, TEE was able to quickly ascertain the type of support and guide its implementation. Instead of a potentially morbid procedure that could have provided no benefit, the patient received rapid deployment of the correct hemodynamic support, which was guided by the same imaging modality (Table 2).

Although training and implementation are barriers that limit widespread applicability, access to TEE is increasing.15 While multicenter reports indicate the complication rate is still rare, approximately 0.4%, it is possible that complications may increase as application becomes more widespread.16 Further, maintaining a protocolized evaluation, a finite list of appropriate diagnoses during cardiac arrest, and close quality review will limit missed or inappropriate diagnoses. Transesophageal echocardiography should not be considered a routine extension of echocardiography. Rather, it should be considered an advanced but highly teachable technique requiring additional training and subject to institutional governance and quality control. Limitations of TEE include inability to assess extracardiac causes of cardiac arrest, misconceptions of easy mastery, and misinterpretation. Although complications from TEE are uncommon, mechanical trauma and esophageal injury have been reported. Careful screening for underlying esophageal and gastric pathology is essential before probe insertion. Absolute contraindications include esophageal perforation, strictures, neoplasms, lacerations, diverticula, active upper gastrointestinal bleeding, prior esophageal surgery, and recent upper gastrointestinal surgery or intervention.7

Conclusion

The application of a focused TEE exam in a patient with undifferentiated cardiac arrest was able to direct management toward appropriate hemodynamic support and aid in implementation. The use of TEE has the potential to alter both intra-arrest and postarrest management, and its use is expanding. Further evidence is needed to determine whether the management changes informed by TEE result in a patient-oriented benefit.
