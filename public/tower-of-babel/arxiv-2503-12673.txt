Part I
Group theory and its connections to logic

1.
Algorithmic questions around the Tits alternative

The 2023
NZRMI summer school
took place in Nelson, New Zealand. It was organized by Jeroen Schillewaert and Matthew Conder, both based in Auckland. NZMRI means “
New Zealand Mathematical Research Institute
”. There were six three-hour tutorials, by Emmanuel Breuillard (Oxford), Martin Bridson (Oxford), Benson Farb (U. Chicago), Nicolas Monod (EPFL), Anne Thomas (U. Sydney), and Amie Wilkinson (U. Chicago). Here, Andre Nies will discuss some aspects of Breuillard’s lectures.

They started out from the Tits alternative.

Theorem 1.1

(Tits, 1972)
.

Let
K
be a field, and let
\Gamma\leq\GL_{d}(K)
be finitely generated. Then either
\Gamma
contains a non-abelian free subgroup, or
\Gamma
has a solvable subgroup of finite index (i.e.,
\Gamma
is virtually solvable).

This result implies that every f.g. torsion linear group is finite, because this is the case for solvable groups as proved easily by induction on the solvability rank.

Breuillard in 2008 obtained a
strong Tits alternative
: given
d
there is
N(d)\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for each field
K
and each finite subset
S
of
\GL_{d}(K)
closed under inversion, either
\langle S\rangle
is virtually solvable, or
S^{N(d)}
contains elements
a,b
generating a free non-abelian subgroup.

Breuillard mentioned the following open problem, which essentially asks whether one can decide which side of the Tits alternative holds.

Question 1.2
.

Given a field
K
and
d\geq 3
, is there an algorithm to decide whether non-commuting matrices
A,B\in\GL_{d}(K)
generate a free group?

The question appears to be open even for
K={\mathbb{Q}}
. This question can be interpreted literally if
K
is a computable field. It is a bit harder to make sense of it for
K=\mathbb{C}
, the main case of interest. In line with computable analysis, the answer will have to be computed from approximations to
A,B
, such as matrices with entries that are in
{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
.

1.1.
Torsion in
\GL_{d}(K)

Here is a potentially simpler question:
Is there an algorithm to decide whether
A\in\GL_{d}(K)
is a torsion element?

For algebraic number fields, the paper

Deciding finiteness of matrix groups
by Babai, Beals, Rockmore in ISSAC 93 provides an algorithm that decides the question in polynomial time, and contains another algorithm that does it in Monte Carlo polynomial time with a more practical exponent.

Let
\chi_{A}
denote the characteristic polynomial
\chi_{A}(t)=\det(A-tI)
. Let
\Phi_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
be the
k
-th cyclotomic polynomial. Thus,
\Phi_{k}
is the minimal polynomial over
{\mathbb{Q}}
of a primitive
k
-th root of unity (such as
\exp(2\pi i/k)
) and has degree
\varphi(k)
, where
\phi
is the Euler totient function. The following is known.

Fact 1.3
.

A\in\GL_{d}({\mathbb{C}})
is torsion
\Leftrightarrow

\chi_{A}
is a product of cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{k}
, where necessarily
\varphi(k)\leq d
.

Since one can compute
\Phi_{k}
, and
\sqrt{k/2}\leq\varphi(k)
for each
k\geq 1
, this gives the desired algorithm when the coefficients of
A
are taken from some computable field
K
that is a subfield of
{\mathbb{C}}
(i.e.,
K
is computable and has characteristics
0
): on input
A\in\GL_{d}(K)
,

1.

compute
\chi_{A}
.

2.

see if it can be written as a product of
\Phi_{k}
’s where
k\leq 2d^{2}
. If so answer yes.

Note that this also gives a crude upper bound on the torsion as a function of
d
, something like
2^{d}\cdot(d^{2d})
. Knowing that, as an algorithm to determine whether
A
is torsion, we can try the various exponents up to this bound. Note that this is far from efficient.

Is there a better bound on the torsion?

This 1997
paper by Shmuel Friedland
shows that up to conjugation, the group O(d) of orthogonal
d\times d
matrices over the integers is the maximal finite subgroup of
GL_{d}({\mathbb{Q}})
, for large d. Also, its order
2^{d}d!
is the maximal order of a finite subgroup, with the exception of
d=2,4,6,7,8,9,10
. In the exceptional cases, the maximum has been obtained by Feit, based on Weisfeiler’s tight estimate for the Jordan function, in a preprint called “The orders of finite linear groups”, 1995, which has not appeared. See these
2006 notes by Serre
, where the work of Weisfeiler-Feit is called “incomplete”.

If it works, then for
d=3,5
or
d>10
, the maximum order is
2L(d)
, where
L(d)
is the maximum order of an element of the symmetric group
S_{d}
. (This follows because
O(d)\cong C_{2}^{d}\rtimes S(d)
.) For the value of
L(d)
, see this
paper by William Miller
. It surveys= a proof that

\log L(d)\sim\sqrt{d\log d}
,

where
\log
is the natural logarithm. Very roughly, this says that
L(d)
is the square root of the size of
S(d)
, which is about
\exp{(d\log d)}
.

2.
Khelif,Wilson: On ultraproducts of alternating groups

Gabriel Sabbagh asked whether an infinite pseudo-finite group can be finitely generated. For some background see
[
8
, Section 6]
.

This section is based on notes by Anatole Khelif related to this problem. John Wilson and Nies discussed these by email. The full explication below is due to Wilson.
Note that pseudofinite groups are precisely the groups elementarily equivalent to an ultraproduct of finite groups. The main result is:

Theorem 2.1
.

No infinite finitely generated group is elementarily equivalent to an ultraproduct of a family of finite alternating groups.

To prove this,
we require two lemmata.
For the definition and properties of bi-interpretability we refer the reader to
[
13
, p. 222]
. We write
P_{n}
for the power set of the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
n\geq 9
, the alternating group
A_{n}
and the three-sorted structure

(A_{n},\{1\dots,n\},P_{n})
, with the group operations on
A_{n}
, the action of
A_{n}
on
\{1\dots,n\}
, the element relation between
\{1\dots,n\}
and sets in
P_{n}
and the Boolean algebra operations on
P_{n}
, are bi-interpretable, in a uniformly way, without parameters.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
(A_{n},\{1\dots,n\},P_{n})
is uniformly interpretable in
A_{n}
.

For a subset
X
of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
write
A_{X}
for the subgroup of
A_{n}
that acts as the alternating group on
X
and fixes the complement
X^{\prime}
pointwise. For
u\in A_{n}
write
B_{u}
for the support
\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\mid ui\neq i\}
of
u
and
C_{u}
for the centralizer
{\mathrm{C}}_{A_{n}}(u)
of
u
in
A_{n}
. We write
C_{u}^{\prime}
for the derived group (commutator subgroup) of
C_{u}
, and
C_{u}^{\prime\prime}
for the derived subgroup of
C_{u}^{\prime}
.

We say that
u
has the
V
-property if
u
has order
2
and exactly four elements of
{\mathrm{C}}_{u}
commute with all of their conjugates under elements of
C_{u}
. We claim that
u
has the
V
-property if and only
u
has order
2
and
|B_{u}|=4
, and that if this holds, then
C_{u}^{\prime\prime}=A_{B_{u}^{\prime}}
. Since the elements
u
of order
2
are products of disjoint transpositions and are determined up to conjugacy by
B_{u}
, it suffices to consider permutations
u=(1,2)(3,4)\dots(4k-1,4k)
for positive integers
k
. An element
A_{n}

lies in
C_{u}
if and only if it permutes the sets
\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{4k-1,4k\}
. Therefore each product of an even number of the transpositions
(1,2),(3,4),\dots,(4k-1,4k)
lies in
C_{u}
and commutes with all conjugates by elements
C_{u}
. Since there are
k(2k-1)
products with two factors, if
u
has the
V
-property then
k=1
and
|B_{u}|=4
.

Now let
u=(1,2)(3,4)
.
Let
D
be the dihedral subgroup
\langle(1,3,2,4),(1,2)\rangle
of order
8
in
S_{n}
, and
S
the subgroup of
S_{n}
fixing
\{1,2,3,4\}
pointwise. Then
S\cong S_{n-4}
and, since
{\mathrm{C}}_{S_{n}}(u)
maps
\{1,2,3,4\}
to itself,
C_{u}
is the subgroup of
D\times S
consisting of pairs
xy
with
x\in D,y\in S
and
x,y
both even or both odd.
Since
S_{n-4}
has no non-trivial abelian normal subgroups, consideration of the projection map from
C_{u}
to
S
shows that the elements of
C_{u}
commuting with all conjugates in
C_{u}
lie in
D
, and, being even, they are the elements of
\langle(1,2)(3,4),(13),(2,4)\rangle
. Thus
|B_{u}|=4
and
u
has the
V
-property. Moreover,
C_{u}^{\prime}=D^{\prime}\times A_{\{1,2,3,4\}^{\prime}}
and the second derived group
C_{u}^{\prime\prime}
of
C_{u}
is
A_{\{1,2,3,4\}^{\prime}}
. Our claim follows.
We note also that, since every element of
A_{n-4}
is a commutator,
C_{u}^{\prime\prime}
is the set of iterated commutators
[[x_{1},x_{2}],[x_{3},x_{4}]]
with

x_{1},\dots,x_{4}
in
C_{u}
and so
C_{u}^{\prime\prime}
is definable in
A_{n}
with parameter
u
, uniformly in
n
.

Let
u,v
be elements of
A_{n}
with the
V
-property. From above,
\langle C_{u}^{\prime\prime},C_{v}^{\prime\prime}\rangle
fixes the
elements of
B_{u}\cap B_{v}
and indeed it easy to check that it is exactly the subgroup of
A_{n}
fixing the elements of
B_{u}\cap B_{v}
; that is,

\langle C_{u}^{\prime\prime},C_{v}^{\prime\prime}\rangle=A_{(B_{u}\cap B_{v})^{\prime}}.

We claim next that the above subgroup is definable with parameters
u,v
uniformly in
n
. From above we can assume that
B_{u}\neq B_{v}
,
and therefore that
|B_{u}\cap B_{v}|\leq 3
.
For
H\subseteq A_{n}
write
{\mathrm{C}}(H)
for
{\mathrm{C}}_{A_{n}}(H)
. Note that if
X\in P_{n}
and
|X|\geq 5
then
{\mathrm{C}}(A_{X})=A_{X^{\prime}}
. Now

{\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{u}^{\prime}})\cap{\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{v}^{\prime}})=A_{B_{u}}\cap A_{B_{v}}=A_{B_{u}\cap B_{v}}.

So
{\mathrm{C}}({\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{u}^{\prime}})\cap{\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{v}^{\prime}}))={\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{u}\cap B_{v}})
contains
A_{(B_{u}\cap B_{v})^{\prime}}
and is contained in the direct product of the symmetric group on
(B_{u}\cap B_{v})^{\prime}
and a group of permutations of
B_{u}\cap B_{v}
. Since
S_{3}
is soluble of derived length
2
and for
m\geq 5
every element of
A_{m}
is a commutator, it follows that

A_{(B_{u}\cap B_{v})^{\prime}}=\{[[x_{1},x_{2}],[x_{3},x_{4}]]\mid x_{1},\dots,x_{4}\in{\mathrm{C}}({\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{u}^{\prime}})\cap{\mathrm{C}}(A_{B_{v}^{\prime}}))\}.

Our claim follows.

The maximal proper subgroups of
A_{n}
of the form
A_{(B_{u}\cap B_{v})^{\prime}}
are exactly the point stabilizers in
A_{n}
. They can be identified with the corresponding elements of
\{1,\dots,n\}
and then the conjugation action of
A_{n}
on the stabilizers becomes the natural action of
A_{n}
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
. The elements of
P(\{1,\dots,n\})
can be defined as the fixed-point sets of elements of
A_{n}
or, in the case of subsets with complement containing at most two elements, the union of two such sets.
The lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group and let
L
be an atomic infinite Boolean algebra with least element
0
on which
G
acts as lattice automorphisms. Assume that the restriction of the action to the set of atoms is transitive and that for all
a,b\in L
, there is
g\in G
such that

ga\leq b
or
gb\leq a
.
Then
({\mathbb{N}},\leq)
is interpretable in
(G,L)
with parameters (the parameters being a finite generating set for
G
).

Proof.

Let
S
be a finite symmetric generating subset of
G
.
We say that an element
a\in L
is
connected
if for all
c,d\neq 0
with
c\wedge d=0

and
c\vee d=a
there are atoms
u
,
v
and
s\in S
with the following property:
u\leq c
,
v\leq d
and
sc=d
.

We define a preorder on
L
as follows: for
a,b\in L
write

a\preceq b
if and only if there exists
g\in G
such that

ga\leq b
. Then
\preceq
induces an equivalence relation
\sim
on
L
and a total order
\leq
on the quotient set
L/{\sim}
: for
a,b\in L
we have

a\sim b
if and only if
a\preceq b
and
b\preceq a
; and for equivalence classes
{\bf c},{\bf d}
we have
{\bf c}\leq{\bf d}
if and only if there are
c\in\bf c
and
d\in\bf d
such that
c\leq d
.

Let
s
and
t
be atoms of
L
.
Since
G
acts transitively on the atoms, there exists a connected element
a\in L

containing finitely many atoms such that
s\leq a
and
t\leq a
.
Then we can consider a minimal (for
\leq
) connected element
a\in L
such that
s\leq a
and
t\leq a
.
Such an element can be regarded as a finite sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
such that for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, there is
s_{i}\in S
such that
s_{i}(a_{i})=a_{i+1}
. Since
G
is finitely generated and acts transitively on the (infinitely many) atoms of
L
, such minimal sequences can be arbitrarily long.

We claim next that if
u,v
are elements of
L
that contain only finitely many atoms, then they are equivalent if and only if they have the same number of atoms.
Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
gu\leq v
for some
g\in G
. It is obvious that
v
contains at least as many atoms as
u
.
If
u
and
v
are equivalent, by symmetry we deduce that they contain the same number of atoms.
Conversely, if
u
and
v
contain the same (finite) number of atoms then
v\setminus gu
does not contain any atom. Since
L
is atomic, it follows that
gu=v
. Our claim follows.

We conclude that the set of equivalence classes of such minimal connected elements with the order induced by
\leq
is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{N}},\leq)
.
∎

Proof of the theorem.

We suppose that the theorem is false.
Let
G
be a group elementarily equivalent to an infinite ultraproduct of alternating groups
A_{n}
; then the integers
n
are unbounded on each set in the underlying ultrafilter. By Lemma 1, this group acts transitively on the atoms of an infinite atomic
G
-definable Boolean algebra (that is elementarily equivalent to an ultraproduct of sets
P_{n}
with
n
unbounded on sets in the ultrafilter).

By Lemma 2, if
G
is also finitely generated then
({\mathbb{N}},\leq)
is interpretable in
G
.
Since
({\mathbb{N}},\leq)
is a finitely axiomatisable infinite
structure, a pseudofinite group cannot interpret it. This is a contradiction.
∎

3.
Khelif, Nies: f.g. groups acting on Boolean algebras

This section contains another version of the second lemma in Khelif’s notes, already discussed as Lemma
2.3
above. The proof of this version is explicated by Nies.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a f.g. group acting from the left on an infinite atomic Boolean algebra
A
, canonically identified with a subalgebra of the powerset of the set of atoms. Suppose that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the action is transitive for elements of cardinality
n
.

Then
({\mathbb{N}},\leq)
can be interpreted in
(G,A)
with parameters.

In particular,
G
fails to be pseudofinite.

Proof.

Let
S
be a finite symmetric generating set of
G
. We use letters
a,b,c,d
for elements of
A
, and
u,v,w
for atoms. We write

a\preceq b\Leftrightarrow\exists g\in G\,[g\cdot a\leq b]
.

If
a
is finite then by the transitivity hypothesis,

|a|\leq|b|\Leftrightarrow a\preceq b
.

We say that
a
is
connected
if for each
c
such that
0<c<a
, there are
s\in S
and and atom
u\leq c
such that
su\wedge c=0
.
Given
a\in A
, let
\Gamma_{a}
be the graph on the elements of
a
(i.e., atoms below
a
) with the edge relation

Euv\Leftrightarrow\exists s\in S[s\cdot u=v]
.

Note that the graph
\Gamma_{1}
, where
1
is the largest element of
A
, is connected since
G
is generated by
S
and the action on the atoms is transitive.

Claim 3.2
.

Let
a
be finite. Then
a
is connected
\Leftrightarrow

\Gamma_{a}
is connected.

For the implication right to left: one can choose atoms
v\leq c
and
w\leq a\setminus c
. There is a path from
v
to
w
, which at some point leaves
c
.

For the implication left to right: if
\Gamma_{a}
is not connected, there is an atom
v\leq a
such that its connected component does not contain some atom
w
. Since
a
is finite, its connected component
c
exists in
A
, and satisfies
c<a
. But there can be no atom
u\leq c
as in the definition of an element being connected.

Let
a
be connected and suppose that
u,v\leq a
are atoms. We say that
a
is
minimal connected for
u,v
if
a\preceq b
holds for any connected
b
such that
u,v\leq b
.

Claim 3.3
.

Let
u,v
be atoms. Let
d
be the set of atoms in a path between
u
and
v
of shortest length in the whole graph
\Gamma_{1}
.

(a)

The set
d
is minimal connected for
u,v
.

(b)

If
a
is minimal connected for
u,v
then
|d|=|a|
.

For (a), note that clearly
d
is connected. If
|c|<|d|
and
u,v\in c
then by Claim 2 and the assumption that the path was shortest,
c
is not connected for
u,v
. Hence for any connected
c
such that
u,v\in c
, we have
d\preceq c
because
c
contains at least
|d|
many atoms.

For (b), note that by definition we have
a\preceq d
and hence
|a|\leq|d|
. By (a) this implies that
|a|=|d|
. This shows the claim.

Now let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of
a
that are minimal connected for some atoms
u,v
. Then
\mathcal{F}
contains only finite sets, and contains sets of each finite cardinality because
A
is infinite. So the quotient partial order of the pre-order
(\mathcal{F},\preceq)
, which can be interpreted in
(G,A)
with parameters from
S
, is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{N}},\leq)
.
∎

4.
Nies: The meet groupoid of an oligomorphic group

Definition 4.1
.

A
meet groupoid
as defined in
[
19
]
is a groupoid
(H,\cdot,{}^{-1})
that is also a meet semilattice
(H,\wedge,\emptyset)
with least element
\emptyset
, and
satisfies the following conditions, where
\sqsubseteq
denotes the induced partial order given by
A\sqsubseteq B:\Leftrightarrow A\wedge B=A
.

(1)

(least element)

\emptyset
is an idempotent such that
\emptyset\cdot A
and
A\cdot\emptyset
are only defined for
A=\emptyset
.

(2)

(compatibility)

U\wedge V\neq\emptyset
for all idempotents
U,V\neq\emptyset
.

(3)

(monotonicity of inverse operation)

A\sqsubseteq B

\Leftrightarrow A^{-1}\sqsubseteq B^{-1}
.

(4)

If
A_{i}\cdot B_{i}
are defined for
i=0,1
and
A_{0}\cap A_{1}\neq\emptyset\neq B_{0}\cap B_{1}
, then

(A_{0}\cap A_{1})\cdot(B_{0}\cap B_{1})=(A_{0}\cdot B_{0})\cap(A_{1}\cdot B_{1}).

In the following let
G\leq_{c}S_{\infty}
be an oligomorphic closed subgroup of the symmetric group
S_{\infty}
.
The meet groupoid
\mathcal{W}(G)
of
G
is a variant of the coarse group
\mathcal{M}(G)
of
G
introduced in
[
21
]
, which consists of the open cosets with a ternary relation for
AB\subseteq C
. The structure
\mathcal{W}(G)
was briefly introduced in
[
21
, Remark 2.16]
, where it was noted that
\mathcal{M}(G)
and
\mathcal{W}(G)
are interdefinable in a uniform way.

Definition 4.2
.

Given an oligomorphic group
G
, the domain of the
meet groupoid

\mathcal{W}(G)
consists of the open cosets, together with
\emptyset
. The groupoid product of open cosets
A\cdot B
is defined iff
A
is a left coset of a subgroup that
B
is a right coset of. The meet is the usual intersection as a binary operation.

4.1.
A model theoretic view of
\mathcal{W}(G)

We show that arbitrarily large canonical pieces of
\mathcal{W}(G)
can be interpreted in
M_{G}
, where
M_{G}
is the canonical structure for
G
.

The structure of imaginaries for an
\omega
-categorical structure
M

Let
M
be a countable
structure with domain
\omega
. By “definable” we mean first-order definable in
M
without parameters. The structure
M^{eq}
of imaginaries over
M
, going back to Poizat and Shelah, has sorts
S=D/E
for any
k\geq 1
, nonempty definable
D\subseteq M^{k}
, and definable equivalence relation
E
on
D
. We will use
\alpha,\beta,\ldots
for elements of
M^{eq}
. The atomic relations of
M^{eq}
are the atomic relations of the given structure
M
(viewed as relations on the “home sort”
M

For an oligomorphic group

For a countable structure

is the orbital structure of

Proposition 5.1
.

Let

be oligomorphic and 1-transitive. Let

be the normaliser of

in
S_{\infty}
, and let

be its centraliser. Then

is homeomorphic to an oligomorphic group.

The hypothesis that

is 1-transitive is for simplicity only. Note here that both

and

are closed subgroups of

, and that

is a normal subgroup of

. So

, the subgroup

is declared to be open iff

. (See
[

, Prop. 2.2.10]

is finite because each of its elements is

naturally embeds

. However

can be much bigger than

on

via the left regular presentation. Then

contains the right regular presentation, and thus an isomorphic copy of

.

Remark 5.2
.

Suppose

is a left action of a group

for the orbit equivalence relation of the subaction of

.

Proof of Prop.
5.1
.

In the notation above, let

.

Claim 5.3

The action of

To see this, suppose

Let

Given a Polish group

(a)

for each

We say that a faithful action

is topological.

Equivalently, the action is continuous, and for each open set

(b)

for each open

.

Most results in this post have been obtained in co-work with either Gianluca Paolini

We discuss how to put a natural Polish topology on

for

is t.d.l.c. for each oligomorphic group

6.1.
A Polish topology on
\Aut(G)
with continuous action on
G

A
Polish space
is a separable topological space with a topology that is induced by a complete metric. A
Polish group
is a topological group based on a Polish space.

Question 6.1
.

For which Polish group
G
can one topologise
\Aut(G)
as a Polish group so that the action
\Aut(G)\times G\to G
is continuous?

We develop a criterion for when the question has an affirmative answer. We will apply it to the locally Roelcke precompact non-Archimedean groups
[
25
,
32
]
. This class is large in the sense that is contains the disparate classes of t.d.l.c. as well as oligomorphic groups.

The setting is this: Suppose that there is a countable basis
\mathcal{W}(G)
for the topology of
G
such that
\mathcal{W}(G)
is invariant under the natural action of
\Aut(G)
on the open subsets of
G
. Then the action yields a monomorphism
\Aut(G)\to\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{W}(G))
, where
\mathrm{Sym}(X)
is the group of permutations on a set
X
. The group
\mathrm{Sym}(X)
for countable
X
is Polish via the topology of pointwise convergence: the pointwise stabilisers of finite subsets of
X
form a neighbourhood basis of the identity, consisting of open subgroups.

Criterion 6.2
.

Suppose the basis
\mathcal{W}(G)
is as above, and the range of the induced monomorphism
\Aut(G)\to\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{W}(G))
is closed. Then Question
6.1
has an affirmative answer via the topology
\tau
on
\Aut(G)
that makes the embedding a homeomorphism. Thus, one declares as open the subgroups of the form

(6.1)

\{\Phi\in\Aut(G)\colon\forall{i=1\ldots n}\ \Phi(A_{i})=A_{i}\},

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{W}(G)
.

Proof.

Note that
\Aut(G)
with this topology is non-Archimedean, and hence Polish. An action of a Polish group on a Polish space is continuous iff it is separately continuous (see
[
12
]
). So it suffices to show that for each
g\in G
, the map
\Aut(G)\to G
given by
\Phi\mapsto\Phi(g)
is continuous. Suppose then that
\Phi(g)\in B
where

. Let

. Then

. If
\Theta\in\Aut(G)
is such that
\Theta(A)=\Phi(A)
, then
\Theta(g)\in B
.
∎

We next characterise the topology on
\Aut(G)
obtained, in the case that

is totally disconnected. Recall that a topological space

is Baire if the intersection of countably many dense open sets is dense. Each Polish space is Baire.

Suppose that the elements of

.

(ii)

Proof.

(i) Every Baire measurable homomorphism from a Baire group

. (Here, to be Baire measurable means that the pre-image of every open set in

Definition 6.4

Theorem 6.17

(Thm. 2.7 in
[

)

Given a Roelcke precompact group closed subgroup

Proof.

We follow the proof for oligomorphic groups
[

form a groupoid (a small category for which all morphisms are invertible). For subgroups

Claim 1.

To see this, first note that

is finite because each coset in it is a double coset of

A fact in the theory of Polish groups states that each

be its meet groupoid defined in

The Chabauty space

We discuss the Chabauty space of a locally compact group

Using the notation of

Remark 7.1
.

If

for

is nonempty, and open in the subspace topology on

for each

.

A group

is called

Ideals in a meet groupoid

G

It is immediate that

\Leftrightarrow

.

Proof.

\Leftarrow:
Immediate.

\Rightarrow:
Let

Fractal dimensions and profinite groups

By
X^{*}
we denote the set of tuples over a set
X
, seen as a rooted tree where the successor relation is given by appending one element. Let
T\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
be a finitely branching subtree (i.e., set of strings closed under initial segments) such that each node has at least two successors. We view the path space
[T]
as an ultrametric space. We will use the point-to-set principle in metric spaces of Lutz et al.
[
16
]
to show that lower box dimension and Hausdorff dimension are equal for certain “regular” subsets of
[T]
, namely path spaces
[S]
where
S
is a subtree of
T
such that the number of successors is equal across each level.

We apply this to profinite groups by giving a new proof of Barnea and Shalev
[
3
, Thm. 2.4]
.
Let the group
G=\varprojlim_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\,(C_{n},p_{n})
be the inverse limit of an inverse system of finite groups, where
p_{n}\colon C_{n+1}\to C_{n}
are onto homomorphisms.
G
is a compact group with a compatible ultrametric, where the distance between distinct elements
f,g
of
G
is
1/|C_{n}|
for the least
n
such that the projections of
f
and
g
into
C_{n}
are different.
The lower box dimension of a closed subgroup
U
of
G
is easily seen to be

\liminf_{i}(\log u_{i}/\log|C_{i}|)
, where
u_{i}
is the size of the projection of
U
into
C_{i}
.
The result
[
3
, Thm. 2.4]
of central importance for their paper says that the Hausdorff dimension of a closed subgroup
U
of
G
equals its lower box dimension. They rely on Abercrombie
[
1
, Prop. 2.6]
for the nontrivial part that the box dimension is no larger than the Hausdorff dimension. Abercrombie proved this via means specific to profinite groups, including Haar measure. We show here that it is purely geometric fact that doesn’t need the group structure, but is merely exploiting the regularity of a description of the subgroup as a subtree of the tree given by the inverse system.

8.1.
Preliminaries

We begin by describing constructive dimension in computable metric spaces, and the point-to-set principle for both Hausdorff and packing dimension in that context. Here we mostly follow Lutz et al.
[
16
]
. We restrict ourselves to the standard gauge family
\theta_{s}(\alpha)=\alpha^{s}
, which has a precision family in their sense by
[
16

Suppose we are given a computable metric space
M
. Thus, there is dense set
D\subseteq M
, called the set of special points, and there is an onto function
r\colon\{0,1\}^{*}\to D
such that the distance between special points

is uniformly computable in

as a real number. (One says that

determine a
computable structure
on

we write

for
\min\{C(u)\colon r(u)=w\}
.

Definition 8.1

(
[
16

For

\alpha>0

one defines the

The
algorithmic dimension
of

(8.2)

\textit{dim}(x):=\liminf_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}C_{\alpha}(x)/\log(1/\alpha).

The
strong algorithmic dimension
of

(8.3)

\textit{Dim}(x):=\limsup_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}C_{\alpha}(x)/\log(1/\alpha).

Let

denote the packing dimension of

. See e.g.
[

, Section 2]
for the detailed definition.
The

determine the Hausdorff and packing dimension of a

(8.4)

\displaystyle\dim_{H}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{A}\sup_{x\in X}\textit{dim}^{A}(x),

(8.5)

\displaystyle\dim_{P}(X)

\displaystyle=

8.2.
The space of paths on a rooted tree.

Recall that

Suppose

be the computable bijection that is an isomorphism between the length lexicographical orderings on

and

be a path on

is computable.

Recall the definition of

in (
8.1
). We will relate it to the initial segment complexity of paths.

Proposition 8.4
.

Suppose that

is a computable tree such that each node has at least two successors. Let

define a computable structure as above. Let

. For each

such that
|T_{n-1}|^{-1}>\alpha\geq|T_{n}|^{-1}
, we have

C(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})-2\log n\leq^{+}C(f\!\upharpoonright_{n}\mid n)\leq^{+}C_{\alpha}(f)\leq^{+}C(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})
.

Proof.

The leftmost inequality is standard. For the middle inequality,
suppose

and

. Given
u\in\{0,1\}^{*}
such that
r(u)=w
and

, we can compute

, which has to equal
f\!\upharpoonright_{n}
since

. Thus

.

For the rightmost inequality, given a string

we compute the string

such that

. ∎

8.3.
Constructive dimension on path space
[T]

We express the values

and

(Def.

in terms of its initial segment complexity. This is of course well known at least for the case of a binary tree

Proposition 8.5
.

Suppose each node on

has at least two successors. For any

we have

\textit{dim}(f)=\liminf_{n}\frac{C(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})}{\log|T_{n}|}=\liminf_{n}\frac{K(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})}{\log|T_{n}|}

\textit{Dim}(f)=\limsup_{n}\frac{C(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})}{\log|T_{n}|}=\limsup_{n}\frac{K(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})}{\log|T_{n}|}

Proof.

We only verify the first assertion; the argument for the second assertion is almost identical. By hypothesis we have
\log|T_{n}|\geq n
, and so
\lim_{n}\log n/\log|T_{n}|=0
. For the left equality, using Prop.
8.4
we have

\dim(f)=\liminf_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}C_{\alpha}(f)/\log(1/\alpha)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}C(f\!\upharpoonright_{n})/\log|T_{n}|
.

For the right equality, note that for each

and each

, we have

.
∎

8.4.

[S]
.

For a tree

without leaves, the path space

carries the uniform measure

given by
\mu_{S}[S]=1

is defined, then
\mu_{S}([\sigma\,\widehat{\ }\,i]\cap[S])=\mu_{S}([\sigma]\cap[S])/k_{\sigma}
where

is the number of successors of

on

and the function

are computable, then

is computable. For such

, one can generalise the Levin-Schnorr Theorem (see, e.g.,
[

let

be the open subset of

described by

Let

R_{S,b}=[\{\sigma\in S\colon K(\sigma)\leq-\log\mu_{S}[\sigma]-b\}]^{\prec}_{S}.

Lemma 8.6
.

(R_{S,b})_{b\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a universal ML-test on
[S]
.

Proof.

Clearly the open sets

are
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
uniformly in

. Given

, let

be the set of prefix minimal strings

such that

1\geq\sum_{\sigma\in D}2^{-K(\sigma)}\geq\sum_{\sigma\in D}2^{\log\mu([\sigma])+b}.

This implies that
\mu_{S}[D]^{\prec}_{S}\leq 2^{-b}
.

Universality is shown as in the proof for Cantor space
2^{{\mathbb{N}}}
; see
[
20
, 3.2.9]
. To follow that proof, one first needs to write down a machine existence theorem for strings in
{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.
∎

While we are mainly interested in the case that

is uncountable, the argument above also works for countable

.
Note that in the countable case only the empty set is null, so every path is ML-random on

.

8.5.
Box dimensions of bounded closed subsets.

The lower and upper box dimension of a bounded set

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{B}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log N_{\alpha}(X)}{\log(1/\alpha)}

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}(X)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log N_{\alpha}(X)}{\log(1/\alpha)}

Now let

for a tree

for a unique subtree

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{B}([S])

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log|S_{n}|}{\log|T_{n}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log|S_{n}|}{\log|T_{n}|}.

9.
Bhojraj: the Ergodic condition on cylinders and Borel sets

Let
A
be a finite alphabet, let

be the set of strings of length

from

and let

be the set of infinite sequences from

be the shift operator on

which deletes the first element of a sequence, let
\mathcal{B}
be the Borel sigma algebra of

(i.e.,

Part IV

10.
Lam and Nies: Whitehead’s problem and the category of sets

SET is the category of sets with maps in between them. Ka Ho Lam, a former student of Nies, asked in 2019 whether there are properties of this category that are not decided by ZFC. Here we sketch how to obtain such a property, which is first order in the language of SET. We want the sentence to be “natural” from the point of view of categories; in particular, it should be invariant under equivalence of categories. This notion is due to MacLane, and will be recalled below. Speaking semantically, our result implies that there are two countable models
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}
of ZFC such that their respective categories SET
M
and SET
N
are not equivalent.

An abelian group
A
is called a
Whitehead group
if each extension of
A
by
{\mathbb{Z}}
splits. This means that for each exact sequence
0\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to E\to A\to 0
, there is a morphism
A\to E
inverting
E\to A
, or briefly
\Ext(A,{\mathbb{Z}})=0
. Clearly each free group is a Whitehead group.
The Whitehead problem asked whether the converse holds. By a result of Stein
[
29
]
, each countable Whitehead group is free, so a counterexample wouild necessarily be uncountable.
Shelah
[
26
,
27
]
, proved that this statement is independent of ZFC. If
V=L
then the conjecture holds. If
MA\,\land\,\lnot CH
holds then there is a Whitehead group that is not free. (Here MA is Martin’s axiom, and CH is the continuum hypothesis.)
Three years later Shelah
[
28
]
showed that the problem can have a negative answer even in the presence of CH.

To obtain a sentence in the language of the category of sets which ZFC cannot decide, we will proceed as follows. We first phrase Whiteheads problem by a first-order sentence about the category of abelian groups. Then we first-order interpret this category in the category of sets. The sentence will be invariant under equivalence of categories.

Another way to obtain such a sentence is to express the generalized continuum hypothesis GCH in the category of sets. We need a subobject classifier
\Omega
, namely a 2-element set (which can be characterized up to isomorphism by a certain pullback property, as is well known in topos theory). We want to talk about monomorphisms
\alpha\colon U\to\mathrm{Mor}(X,\Omega)
, which express subsets of
\mathcal{P}(X)
. We need to replace these by maps
\overline{\alpha}\colon U\times X\to\Omega
and express that
\alpha
is a monomorphism via a first order diagrammatic property of
\overline{\alpha}
.
Also see “Sheaves in geometry and logic by Moerdijk and MacLane, pp. 277 ff.

The present solution makes the result reliant on Shelah’s work
[
28
]
but not the fact that CH is independent of ZFC.

10.1.
Background on categories, and first-order logic

We view categories as generalised structures where the domain can be a class (consisting of the morphisms); they only have a ternary relation
R
for composition of morphisms.
Rfgh
means that
fg
is defined and equals
h
; henceforth, we also write this as
fg=h
. A morphism
f
is an identity morphism if
\forall g[\forall h(Rfgh\to h=g)\wedge\forall h(Rgfh\to h=g)]
. Objects are identified with the corresponding identity morphisms, so we don’t need a special sort for them. In practice we use the usual letters
A,B,C,\ldots
for them, and write
1_{A}
if we want to view
A
as morphism, e.g., in writing things like
1_{A}f=f
. Given a category, there are first-order definable functions
\sigma
and
\tau
that assign to a morphism its source and target objects, respectively. We have

\sigma(f)=h
if
h\text{ is identity }\land hf=f

\tau(f)=\ell
if
\ell\text{ is identity }\land f\ell=f
.

We write

{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{B}
for
\sigma(f)=A\land\tau(f)=B
.

We sweep under the rug some logical complications that ensue because the domain of the “structure” usually is a proper class. We merely note that saying that a subclass, or a function, is first-order definable via a formula means that ZFC proves the equivalence of the original definition of the function (which was given in the usual language of set theory) with what the defining formula (which is in the language of the category) expresses.

Two categories
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
are
equivalent
(in the sense of MacLane) if there is a functor
T\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
which is

•

full and faithful, i.e. for any two objects
A,B
of
\mathcal{C}
, the map

\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)\to\mathrm{Hom}(TA,TB)

induced by
T
is surjective and injective;

•

essentially surjective (or dense), i.e. each object
B
in
\mathcal{D}
is isomorphic to an object of the form
TA
, for some object
A
in
\mathcal{C}
.

Freyd
[
11
]
(also see
this site
) characterised the first-order properties of categories that are invariant under equivalence.
These are the ones that are diagrammatic (essentially: can be defined by a finite sentence in a language of diagrams and using the quantifiers
\forall,\exists
). For instance, requiring the existence of a pushout of a diagram with three objects is diagrammatic; saying that there are two distinct objects that are isomorphic is not.

10.2.
A statement about SET that is undecidable in ZF, and invariant under equivalence of categories

Note that we write composition of maps in SET in the natural order,
(fg)(x)=g(f(x)
. In ZF (without requiring choice) it is much easier to obtain an invariant sentence in the language of SET that cannot be decided.
We say that AC holds in a
category
if each epimorphism has a left inverse. That is, if
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{B}
is an epimorphism then there is
{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}{A}
such that
gf=1_{B}
. It is well known that in ZF, the axiom of choice for SET is equivalent to the usual axiom of choice. The only extra trouble is due to the fact that we want the property to be invariant under equivalence. The next two lemmas verify this directly, without recurring to Freyd’s work.

Claim 10.1
.

Let
T\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be an equivalence of categories. Suppose that
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{B}
is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
. Then

f
is an epimorphism in
\mathcal{C}

\Leftrightarrow

T(f)
is an epimorphism in
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow:
Suppose
{T(B)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\widetilde{g}_{i}}}{{\to}}{D}
in
\mathcal{D}
for
i=0,1
, and
T(f)\widetilde{g}_{0}=T(f)\widetilde{g}_{1}
. Since
T
is dense, there is an object
C\in\mathcal{C}
and an isomorphism
{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}{T(C)}
. Since
T
is full, there are morphisms
{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g_{i}}}{{\to}}{C}
in
\mathcal{C}
such that
T(g_{i})=\widetilde{g}_{i}p
. Since
fg_{0}=fg_{1}
we have
g_{0}=g_{1}
, and hence
\widetilde{g}_{0}=\widetilde{g}_{1}
.

\Leftarrow:
This implication holds for any faithful functor.

∎

Fact 10.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be equivalent categories. Then

AC holds in
\mathcal{C}

\Leftrightarrow

AC holds in
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Since
T
has an inverse that is also an equivalence of categories, we only need to show
\Leftarrow
. So suppose
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{B}
is an epimorphism in
\mathcal{C}
. Then in
\mathcal{D}
there is
{T(B)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\widetilde{g}}}{{\to}}{T(A)}
such that
\widetilde{g}T(f)=1_{T(B)}
. Since
T
is full,
\widetilde{g}=T(g)
for some
{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}{A}
. Then
gf=1_{B}
since
T
is faithful. ∎

10.3.
The category
Ab

Recall that an abelian group
A
is free if and only if it is projective: for any epimorphism
{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}{V}
and any morphism
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h}}{{\to}}{V}
there is
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{U}
such that
fg=h
. The direction left to right holds for any algebraic structure: if
A
is freely generated by a set
X
, let
f(x)
for
x\in X
be a preimage of
h(x)
in
U
, and extend. For the direction right to left, one uses that every subgroup of a free abelian group is itself free.

The advantage of saying “projective” instead of “free” is that being projective is first-order definable in a diagrammatic way.

Proposition 10.3
.

The class of Whitehead groups is first-order definable in a diagrammatic way in the category
Ab
(over ZFC).

Proof.

1. Given
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{B}
one can express that
f=0
: this holds exactly when
fg_{0}=fg_{1}
for each
B
and each pair of morphisms
{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g_{i}}}{{\to}}{B}
.

2.
{\mathbb{Z}}
(up to isomorphism) is the only projective object not isomorphic to a proper direct product.

3. A sequence
{\mathbb{Z}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}Q
is exact exactly if the monomorphism
{{\mathbb{Z}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}{A}
is maximal such that
fg=0
;

that is, if there is a proper monomorphism
{{\mathbb{Z}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h}}{{\to}}{M}
and monomorphism
{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\widetilde{f}}}{{\to}}{A}
such that
f=h\widetilde{f}
, then
\widetilde{f}g\neq 0
. ∎

So there is a first-order sentence
\theta
in the language of
Ab
expressing that some Whitehead group is not projective.

Proposition 10.4
.

The statement
\theta
is preserved under equivalences of categories in the sense of MacLane.

This is because the definitions are diagrammatic in the sense of Freyd as discussed above.

Remark 10.5
.

We conclude that the first-order theory of
Ab
is not determined by ZFC, and (for Platonists) that there are two countable models of ZFC such that their categories
Ab
are not equivalent.

10.4.
The category
SET

First-order interpretations of one struxture in another are fundamental in model theory; see the model theory books by Hodges (Ch. 5), or Tent and Ziegler. It is fairly straightforward to extend this notion to “structures” with domain a proper class. We say that an interpretation is diagrammatic if the involved first-order formulas are diagrammatic.

Proposition 10.6
.

There is a diagrammatic interpretation of the category
Ab
in the category
SET
.

Proof.

An object of
Ab
is represented by an 8-tuple of morphisms of SET

(A,S,p_{0},p_{1},a,\ell,m,s)

where
A,S
are objects (identity morphisms),
a\colon S\to A
represents the binary group operation,
\ell\colon A\to A
is the inverse operation,
m\colon 0\to A
, and the
p_{i}\colon S\to A
are the projections. One can say in a first-order diagrammatic way that
S,p_{0},p_{1}
has the universal property describing the product
A\times A
. One requires that the morphism

s\colon S\to S

switches the “components” of the direct product:
sp_{i}=p_{1-i}
for
i=0,1
. One can now formulate the abelian group axioms via diagrams, similar to the case of Hopf algebras in
Dominik Roje’s Honours thesis,
pages 19-21. For instance, commutativity is expressed by
sa=a
.

A morphism
g\colon A\to B
is then just a set morphism that satisfies some “commuting diagram property” expressing that it preserves the group operations. Also see Boersuk, Handbook of categorical algebra.
∎

Proposition 10.7
.

Suppose that there is a diagrammatic interpretation
\Gamma
of a category
\mathcal{C}_{0}
in
\mathcal{D}_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{0}
be equivalent to
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
be the category obtained as
\Gamma(\mathcal{D}_{1})
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

Thus, the internal categories
SET
relative to the pair of countable models of set theory obtained in Remark
10.5
are not equivalent.

11.
Copilot is trying to solve the Basel problem

Here’s a LaTeX code snippet for a proof of the result that the sum of the reciprocals of the squares of the positive natural numbers equals
\pi^{2}/6
. This is known as the Basel problem, and one of the classic proofs involves using the Fourier series.

We want to show that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

Consider the Fourier series of the function
f(x)=x^{2}
on the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
. The Fourier series expansion is given by:

f(x)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n^{2}}\cos(nx).

Evaluating this at
x=\pi
, we get:

\pi^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n^{2}}\cos(n\pi).

Since
\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^{n}
, the series becomes:

\pi^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n^{2}}(-1)^{n}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Subtracting
\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}
from both sides, we get:

\pi^{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}=4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Simplifying the left-hand side:

\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}=4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Dividing both sides by 4, we obtain:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.
