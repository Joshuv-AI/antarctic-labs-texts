Acceptance of Parents toward Hand-over-mouth Exercise and Other Behavior Management Techniques for Pediatric Dental Care in the 21st Century: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies
Authors: Ashwin Jawdekar, Farheen Tafti, Sayali Deolikar, Laresh Mistry
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand-over-mouth exercise (HOME) is an aversive technique for child behavior management in a dental office. HOME has been omitted from various guidelines and certain teaching curricula due to legal and ethical issues. This systematic review meta-analysis (SRMA) was undertaken to understand the acceptance of parents toward HOME in comparison with that of other behavior management techniques (BMTs).ObjectivesThis SRMA compared parental acceptance for HOME and other BMTs from observational studies reported in the literature from 1st January 2000, to date.MethodologyTwo authors independently searched data from the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, and others. Twenty-one studies remained in the systematic review, of which eleven could be included in the meta-analysis. Based on the summary findings of 1,034 participants from 10 studies, we compared parents’ acceptance for HOME vs other BMTs [voice control (VC), tell-show-do (TSD), physical restraint-active (PR-A), physical restraint-passive (PR-P), parental separation (PS), conscious sedation (CS), general anesthesia (GA), oral premedication (OP), and positive reinforcement (PR)]. Data analyses were carried out using RevMan 5.3. The Joanna Briggs Risk of Bias (JB-ROB) tool for cross-sectional analytical studies was used for bias assessment. A funnel plot was used to detect publication bias.ResultsOf 20 studies, nine studies reported higher acceptance for GA than HOME; seven reported higher acceptance for HOME than GA, and the remaining four appeared inconclusive. The meta-analysis results (eleven studies) based on forest plots with fixed effects models in terms of OR (CI) indicated no statistically significant difference in the acceptance for HOME vs GA [1.03 (0.84, 1.25)], CS [0.77 (0.60, 1.00)], and PR-P [1.21 (0.92, 1.59)]. In comparison with the acceptance for HOME, CS (NO) [0.65 (0.51, 0.84)], VC [0.48 (0.39, 0.59)], TSD [0.04 (0.03, 0.05)], PR-A [0.65 (0.50, 0.84)], PS [0.28 (0.22, 0.36)], OP [0.55 (0.39, 0.78)], distraction [0.11 (0.09, 0.15)], and PR [0.06 (0.04, 0.08)] were preferred; these being statistically significant. Heterogeneity values (I2) for all comparisons were high (above 80%), with the exception of one (comparison of HOME vs OP) having 50%. ROB across studies was judged to be moderate. The publication bias using the funnel plot analysis for all studies on the basis of SE [Log (OR)] showed marked and fairly equal dispersion on either side of the central line.ConclusionNo statistically significant difference was seen in the acceptance for HOME vs GA, CS, and PR-P. BMTs such as TSD, VC, PR-A, PS, CS (NO), OP, distraction, and PR were preferred to HOME; the difference in acceptance being statistically significant. The differences in acceptance between HOME vs CS (NO) and PR-A did not remain statistically significant when random effects models were used. Background

Hand-over-mouth exercise (HOME) (placing a hand on a crying child's mouth firmly so as to gain control of the uncooperative child) is an aversive technique for child behavior management in a dental office that has been practiced for over 100 years since it was first advocated by Evangelin Jordan in 1920.1,2

In a survey of program directors offering advanced training in pediatric dentistry, reported by Davis and Rombom in 1979, 89% of the programs included training and practice of HOME, including 31% accepting the use of airway restraint along with HOME, that is, hand-over-mouth airway restriction (HOMAR).3 Acs and Burke (1990) reported a marked reduction in the acceptance of HOMAR during the decade after the Davis and Rombom survey; however, no decrease was reported in the acceptance of HOME.3,4 In an article published in 2001, “A 20-Year Perspective on the Changing Use of Hand Over Mouth and Restraint in Post-Doctoral Pediatric Dental Education,” it was reported that between 1989 and 1999, 50% of previous HOME users discontinued its use.5 HOME has been omitted from various guidelines and certain teaching practices due to legal and ethical issues (Acs et al., 2001; AAPD, 2020).5,6

Parental approval and preferences for HOME and various other behavior management techniques (BMT) have been investigated by various authors. Desai et al. (2019) reported the least acceptance of HOME compared to tell-show-do, positive reinforcement, and modeling techniques of behavior management.7 In another study (Eaton et al., 2005) comparing all BMTs, the mean parental acceptance rating was in the acceptable range for all other techniques such as tell-show-do, nitrous oxide sedation, passive restraint, voice control, oral premedication (sedation), active restraint, and general anesthesia, with the exception of HOME.8 In another study by Muhammad et al. (2011), comparing the acceptance of various BMTs by parents, positive reinforcement, effective communication, tell-show-do, distraction, modeling, and nonverbal communication were the most acceptable; hypnosis and parental separation were moderately acceptable, and voice control, nitrous oxide sedation, protective stabilization (physical restraint), general anesthesia, HOME, and conscious sedation were the least acceptable techniques.9 Similar to these studies, several studies have reported varying degrees of acceptance of HOME and other BMTs by parents.10,14

This research has been undertaken to understand the acceptance of parents toward HOME in comparison with other BMTs based on the findings from the recent literature. The research question was framed as follows: How is the acceptance of parents for HOME and other BMTs reported in the literature from the year 2001 onwards?

Variables of Interest

Percentage acceptance for each technique was the main variable of interest for comparison. The acceptance of parents for HOME was compared with other techniques individually. Meta-analyses were attempted for all possible comparisons of HOME with other techniques.

Analysis Issues

Analyses were based on dichotomous data comparison using OR and CI. Because of heterogeneity across studies, we used both fixed-effects and random-effects models for all comparisons.

Dealing with Missing Data

Since studies reported parental acceptance of HOME with different techniques, all available variables and data reported in the individual studies (with HOME being a mandatory inclusion) were recorded. Studies and data were grouped for pairwise comparisons (such as acceptance for HOME vs GA, HOME vs TSD, etc.). No attempt was made to add values to any missing or empty cells in the data tables.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in the results of the trials was assessed by inspecting a graphical display of the results and by the formal tests of heterogeneity, I2.

Assessment of Reporting Biases

A funnel plot was used to assess the publication bias across all studies (11 in total). The results are reported in Figure 2.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

Data analyses were carried out using RevMan 5.3 software. For the meta-analysis, odds ratios and confidence intervals (OR and CI) were used for the analyses. A total of 10 different comparisons were made, with HOME being the constant outcome in comparison with other BMTs (Plots 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7I, 7II, 8, 9, 10). Only one additional analysis was carried out for CS (NO) because two studies reported CS and NO as distinct variables.

Results

Description of Studies

Dates of publication for the search ranged from 2000 to 2021. Using different combinations of keywords, a total of 5,712 records were identified in the preliminary search from search engines: PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar; from which 5,633 studies were excluded as being irrelevant. After assessing titles and abstracts, 79 studies were found to report parental acceptance of HOME and other BMTs, from which articles reporting acceptance of children and studies other than observational (comparison of child and parent acceptance) were excluded. This left us with 37 articles. In addition, an extra 10 studies could be retrieved after searching through another search engine (Semantic Scholar) and checking cross-references. After excluding studies that did not record parental acceptance in percentages, only 21 studies remained in the final selection for the systematic review, of which 12 could be included in the meta-analysis. One study (Gupta and Khajuria) was found to be a duplicate of a previously reported paper (Acharya et al.) and was therefore removed from the meta-analysis. Thus, the number for the meta-analysis was 11 studies. The stepwise selection and exclusion of studies (with reasons) are summarized in a flowchart (Fig. 1).

Data extraction was begun by FT and SD (postgraduate students) and completed by AJ and LM (the two senior investigators). After the data extraction, studies were segregated based on the parameters of comparison of acceptance for HOME and other techniques of behavior management.

The two variables for comparison were percentage acceptance for HOME and other techniques of behavior management, and visual analog scale (VAS) scores pertaining to the acceptance of HOME and other techniques of behavior management. Studies related to the VAS scores could not be included unless percentage acceptance could be calculated. Table 1 includes the description of studies that were considered for comparison.

From the data available in Table 1, it is evident that out of twenty studies, nine studies reported higher acceptance of parents for GA than for HOME; seven reported higher acceptance of parents for HOME than for GA, and the remaining four appeared inconclusive.

Characteristics of the Participants

A total of 20 studies were included in the systematic review (SR). The participants/respondents were predominantly mothers of children (age range 20–50 years) belonging to the age-group of 2–15 years. In the SR, eight studies were reported from Southeast Asia (six from India, one from Malaysia, and one from Thailand), seven were from the Middle Eastern region (four from Iran, one from Kuwait, one from Saudi Arabia, and one from Jordan), two studies were from Europe (one from Spain and one from Greece), two studies were from South America (both from Brazil), and only one study was from North America (United States).

Eleven studies (1,034 participants) remained in the meta-analysis (MA). Six studies were reported from Southeast Asia (five from India and one from Thailand), four were from the Middle Eastern region (two from Iran, one from Kuwait, and one from Jordan), and one study was from Spain.

Characteristics of Outcome Measures

The main outcome measure was acceptance of HOME by parents. The acceptance of HOME was compared with that of each of the other techniques of behavior management, all recorded in percentages (ratio variable).

The mean percentages and standard deviations (SDs) for the variables were as follows: HOME, VC, TSD, PR-A, PR-P, GA, PS, CS, D, OP, and PR. The quantitative assessment with the meta-analysis (MA) was based on the calculation of risk ratios (RR) and confidence intervals (CI) from the available data.

Discussion

“Systematic reviews aim to appraise and synthesize the available evidence addressing a specific research question; a meta-analysis is a statistical summary of the results from relevant studies.”30

The preferences for certain BMTs employed by dentists can be assessed only with questionnaire-based observational studies; hence, we chose to attempt a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies on the pertaining topic.

Meta-analysis of observational studies can be useful because observational studies not only provide additional information to RCTs but also, for many research questions, are the most reliable and the only source of available evidence.31 Furthermore, meta-analyses of observational studies are being used for the synthesis of evidence to fulfill the compelling need for timely and informed decisions in public health and clinical practice.32

In a scoping review of recommendations for the meta-analysis of observational studies based on 93 articles, Mueller et al. (2018) reported a lack of consensus and conflicting recommendations on various aspects of the process, such as the choice of study designs, quality scales to assess the risk of bias, and the choice of model (fixed or random effects, etc.).33

Therefore, we attempted to make use of the appropriate checklists and methods for the assessment of risk of bias, such as the JB ROB Tool for Cross-sectional Analytical Studies, which was used for the studies included in the SR, and the Survey Assessment Framework, which was used for the studies included in the MA.15,29 The JB ROB Tool is used in the MA of BMTs by Massignan et al. (2020).34

A common observation across most of the studies was that there was a lack of standardized, prevalidated, or piloted study tools. This can introduce bias in the measurements of the parameters of interest.

A prerequisite for the meta-analysis is the identification of a common variable of interest across the studies. In the present SRMA, acceptance of various BMTs was the main variable of interest. Several studies reported this in percentage acceptance, whereas a few studies reported acceptance based on VAS scores. The studies reporting only VAS scores could not be included unless the percentage acceptance was obtainable for a few reasons. VAS is often used as a categorical scale. At times, VAS scores are also reported as mean and SD; however, the scale range varies from 0 to 10 or 0 to 100.

Though we compared the acceptance of HOME to several BMTs that are commonly employed, we feel that the most noteworthy comparison is HOME vs GA. GA for child behavior management is often considered the last resort, and there may be significant resistance from parents to accepting the same due to the fear associated with the risks involved. We found another MA of observational studies on BMTs by Massignan et al. (2020); however, the MA differs in three ways: firstly, there were no comparisons made; secondly, it was not time-specific; and thirdly, it did not focus on the acceptance of a certain technique such as HOME in view of changing trends over the past few decades.

Questionnaire-based studies on the selection of BMTs have a limitation in that the questions may not adequately portray a real-life situation, that is, how the child is behaving while undergoing treatment and how the dental team is making an effort to manage the same. Al Zoubi et al. (2021) compared acceptance for techniques during the normal course and emergencies and found higher acceptance of pharmacological methods, PR-P, and PR-A (did not, however, include HOME) in emergency situations.35 A few studies also reported acceptance of modeling and hypnosis, such as that reported by Peretz and Zadik (2013); however, these techniques were not consistently represented in other studies.36 Ramos et al. (2005) (Brazil) on 400 children aged 4–10 years was omitted because of the inclusion of children with clefts.37

We could not assess the impact of parents’ age, gender, children's age, socio-economic status, and other factors such as the number of children, order of the child, and marital factors on the preferences in the selection of BMTs due to the unavailability of such information from all or most of the studies.

Results of our meta-analysis indicate low acceptance of HOME, comparable only to GA, passive restraints, and CS. All other BMTs were preferred to HOME by parents. According to the recent reference manual of AAPD, Behavior Guidance Techniques (BGTs) are classified as basic and advanced.6 The manual omits HOME from the BGTs. Our study, however, finds parental acceptance of HOME comparable to GA, passive restraints, and CS. Thus, judicious use of HOME with parental consent can still be a choice, at least in the settings where the law permits the same. This is in agreement with the views mentioned by Jawdekar (2010).37

Findings based on the selected studies in this SRMA have limited generalizability due to several reasons. Firstly, most studies were conducted in academic institutes and not in private settings. Secondly, there were no studies from North America, most of Europe, Australia-New Zealand, China, and Africa. The majority of the studies came from Middle Eastern and Southeast Asian countries, possibly because the Western world and many countries have already parted with the HOME technique and accepted pharmacological management as the standard of care for child behavior management.

The selected studies in the SRMA exhibit diverse methodological variations. Regarding the explanation of the BMTs to parents, a few studies included explanations through videos, while others showed photographs. In a few studies, BMTs were explained by the pediatric dentists or dentists, while in others, the same was done by staff other than the dentists.

Quality of the Evidence

Using the two tools, the risk of bias was judged to be moderate. The publication bias was found to be minimal, too. The evidence can be considered low quality owing to high heterogeneity and also because the MA is based on observational studies.

Conclusion

Based on the quantitative analyses (meta-analyses with forest plots using fixed effect models), we conclude that,

No statistically significant difference in the acceptance of parents was seen for HOME vs. GA, CS, and PR-P.

No statistically significant difference in the acceptance of parents was seen for HOME vs. GA, CS, and PR-P.

VC, TSD, PS, PR-A, CS (NO), OP, distraction, and PR were preferred to HOME; the difference in acceptance being statistically significant.

VC, TSD, PS, PR-A, CS (NO), OP, distraction, and PR were preferred to HOME; the difference in acceptance being statistically significant.

However, the above interpretations should be accepted with caution due to high heterogeneity among the studies. The differences in acceptance between HOME vs. CS (NO) and PR-A did not remain statistically significant when random effects models were used.

The risk of bias across the studies was moderate, with inconsistencies in the measurements of parameters in a standardized manner and the use of prevalidated or piloted tools. The publication bias was found to be minimal.

Implications for Practice

This review is the first of its kind to uncover the acceptance of parents to HOME and compare that with other BMTs. In the absence of other evidence, this can help develop guidance for dentists on BMTs, including HOME, based on parental approval. Our findings, however, have limited generalizability due to the limited number of studies included (not adequately representative) and cultural, social, and legal differences in the recommendations, training, and practice of BMTs across the globe.

Implications for Research

Preferences of parents (and thereby of children and dentists) toward various BMTs may vary over time. Periodic assessment of acceptance of various BMTs by all stakeholders (dentists, parent/child dyads, professional bodies, and policymakers) is therefore essential. More primary research is needed in different settings (public/private setups, general dental/specialty practices, etc.) and different social and cultural contexts in view of the same.
