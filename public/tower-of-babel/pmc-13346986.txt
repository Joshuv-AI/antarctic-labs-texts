Facile synthesis of Sarcochlamys pulcherrima mediated silver nanoparticles for antibacterial, antibiofilm, and network pharmacology evaluation
Authors: Anuva Samanta, Adarsha Ghosh, Southik Nandi, Soumyadeep Ghosh, Akash Gupta, Arijit Mondal, Rudranil Saha, Pranee Roy, Dibyarupa Pal, Arindam Maity
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen synthesis of nanoparticles (NPs) has transpired as aneconomical, efficient and environmentally friendlycompared to conventional methods. In this study, Sarcochlamys pulcherrima leaf extract was made to be used as a natural reducing and capping agent to fabricate silver nanoparticles (sp-AgNPs), which were further evaluated for antibacterial and antibiofilm activities. The sp-AgNPs were synthesized via two ways; Heating at 80 °C and Non-heating methodsto evaluate the particle size difference on the antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy.The sp-AgNPs were characterized using standard techniques ofUV–Vis spectroscopy, Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDX), Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS), Zeta Potential, and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). The colour change pre and post synthesis and subsequent characteristic UV–Vis peaks confirmed successful nanoparticle formation. Zeta and SEM analyses of sp-AgNPs revealed average sizes of 37.6–45.1 nm for Heat and 44.7–53.9 nm for Non-heatwith spherical, needle-like morphology. EDX confirmed the presence of silver ionin sp-AgNPs. Antibacterial assays against B.subtilis, S.aureus, P.aeruginosa, and E.coli showed inhibitory zones with both synthesis methods. The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Maximum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) for sp-AgNPs wereevaluated and antibiofilm activity performed.Superior biofilm inhibition of 67.51–83.14%was demonstrated by Non-heatingsynthesismethod compared to 50.93–73.76% reduction bythe heatmethod.Further, the Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectroscopy (LC-MS/MS) data and Network pharmacology identified key antibacterial phytoconstituents targeting immune and metabolic pathways like AKT1, STAT3, and CASP3. Overall, this study demonstrates a facile green synthesis of sp-AgNPs with potent antibacterial and antibiofilm potential which can be harnessed for future biomedical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51567-y.Keywords: Sp-AgNPs, Antibacterial, MIC, MBC, Biofilm inhibition, LC-MS/MS, Network pharmacologySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nanoparticles (NPs), typically ranges from 1 to 100 nanometres in size, exhibit distinct physical, chemical and biological properties that render them invaluable across diverse applications ranging from drug delivery, disease diagnostics, cancer therapy and tissue engineering to agriculture1–3. Compared to bulk materials, nanoscale materials possess high surface area-volume ratio, enhanced surface energy, with distinct physical properties like surface plasmon resonance, all of which contributes to antimicrobial efficacy4–6. However conventional NPs synthesis methods involvesthe use of harmful chemical reducing agents and stabilizers raising concerns regarding environmental safety, sustainability and biomedical applicability. Alternately, green synthesis approaches utilizing plant extracts have emerged as sustainable, cost effective, stable, superior and safer while leaving no harmful residue7–14. Earlier studies have superbly demonstrated nanoparticle biosynthesis utilizing different plant parts like leaves, fruit pulp, bark, roots, shoots, flowers, and seeds15–19. The Indian subcontinent harbours rich diversity of medicinal plants which plays a pivotal role in traditional medicine systems. They are widely recognized as effective therapeutic agents in modern medicine20–23, with World Health Organization (WHO) reporting that 80% of the global population relies on herbal remedies for primary healthcare24.

Among metallic NPs, silver NPs (AgNPs) have garnerednotable attention due to their potent biological properties. The green synthesis of AgNPs with various plant extracts containing key phytochemicals like alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, amides and aldehydes, facilitates reduction of Ag+ ions and facilitate formation of NPs25–28. The antimicrobial activity of AgNPs is well documented; attributed to their high surface area-volume ratio which enhances the interaction with microbial cells29,30. Synthesis parameters like temperature strongly influence the size and morphology of NPs thereby modulating antimicrobial behaviour31–35.Various studies have demonstrated the efficacy of plant-mediated AgNPs against both Gram+positive and Gram-negative bacteria including the multi-drug resistant bacteria like methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis,Vibrio cholerae, Klebsiella pneumoniae,Micrococcus luteus,Staphylococcus epidermidis, Proteus vulgaris,and Salmonella typhimurium are among other bacteria that are effectively killed by AgNPs36–40. With the advent of widespread antimicrobial resistance which has severely limited the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics, metal NPs offerssafer alternative with reduced possibility of microbial resistance development.

Sarcochlamys pulcherrima (Roxb.) Gaud.,a small evergreen tree native to tropical forests of Sikkim, Bhutan, Indonesia, Myanmar, and Thailand. The plant also thrives abundantly in hilly terrains and lowland plains of North-eastern India, widely used by numerous ethnic tribes and communities as a food source and therapeutic agent for treating various ailments like diarrhoea, dysentery, fever blisters, boils and itching of the eyes. Current studies have validated the antioxidant, antidiabetic and antimicrobial effects41 whereas immunological investigation has revealed myricitrin as modulator of browning of white adipose tissue in obese experimental models through the upregulation of the AMPK/SIRT1/UCP1 signalling pathway42.

Despite these promising findings, the bioactive molecules responsible for antibacterial efficacy were not ascertained in previous studies. Our study demonstrates for the first time the use of Network pharmacology approach in conjunction with Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectroscopy (LC-MS/MS) data to identify such bioactive molecules. Instead of traditional “one drug–one target” approach, Network pharmacology integratessystems biology, bioinformatics, and pharmacology to elucidate the bioactive compoundsand molecular targets involved in antibacterial and antibiofilm activity. The protein-protein network (PPI) construction, Hub gene identification and functional enrichment analysis (GO and KEGG) provides an idea of the key molecular targets and signalling pathways involved43,44.

The network pharmacology analysis in the current research was focused on completing the green synthesised silver nanoparticles (sp-AgNPs) system and not as an independent study on phytochemicals. The bioactive phytoconstituents detected by the IMPPAT database & LC-MS/MS have a dual role of reducing and capping agent during the manufacturing of nanoparticles and thus create a biologically active surface corona of sp-AgNPs. Such surface-bound phytochemicals should regulate the nano- bio interface and play an important role in the observed antibacterial and antibiofilm activity. Thus, to explain the possible molecular targets and pathways that may be altered by these phytochemical-functionalized nanoparticles, the network pharmacology approach was used to give a mechanistic linkage between the synthesis of nanoparticles and their effects on the biological process.

S. pulcherrima has proven ethno medicinal value and preliminary bioactivity data, very limited studies has investigated its potential for silver nanoparticles synthesis. Comprehensive network-based understanding on the antibacterial action of the plant was also not performed in earlier investigations. The effect of temperature on nanoparticle synthesis was also unexplored. To fulfil the lacunae, our study has explored three objectives: (i) sustainable preparation and characterization of sp-AgNPs, (ii) to assess antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy, (iii) network pharmacology approach to offer a mechanistic rationale for biomedical use of S. pulcherrima.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the plant extract

Fully grown and fresh leaves of Sarcochlamys pulcherrima were collected with the permission of local authorities from the West Khasi Hills, Meghalaya, India (latitude 25° 33’ 36” N and longitude 91° 36’ 53” E). The plant specimen was authenticated by the Central National Herbarium, Botanical Survey of India (Howrah, West Bengal, India). The specimen (AS–L1) was tentatively identified as Sarcochlamys pulcherrima (Roxb.) Gaudich. (Family: Urticaceae) vide letter no. CNH/Tech.II/2023/139 dated 10 August 2023, issued by Scientist ‘E’ & Head of Office. The plant utilized in the study was the subject of experimental research that conforms to all applicable institutional, national, and international regulations. For the study, fresh and healthy leaves of S. pulcherrima leaves were washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove adhered dust.Then it was dried in the shade away from direct sunlight for a period of almost 15–20 days. After the leaves were completely dried out, they were crushed into a powder using a grinder and then the storage was done in an airtight container. For the preparation of plant extract, 100 mL of deionized water was mixed with 20 g of fine powdered S. pulcherrima leaves, and the mixture was heated to 100 °C for 60 min. The extract was cooled at room temperaturebefore getting filtered using Buckners funnel to remove all the insoluble fractions.The resulting extract was then refrigerated at 4 °C until it was needed again.

Preparation of AgNPs with S. pulcherrima leaf extract (spAg-NPs)

A stock solution of 1 M silver nitrate (AgNO3) was prepared. From the already prepared stock solution, 0.1 M AgNO3 solution was taken along with the 1%(w/v) aqueous solution of leaf extract. Firstly, 0.1 ml of 1% (w/v) aqueous solution of plant extract was added to 2.6 ml of HPLC grade water and similarly five other varied volumes of plant extract solution were prepared and mixed with calculated volume of HPLC grade water (Table 1). Then a constant volume of 0.3 ml 0.1 M solution of AgNO3 was added and then all the six sets of solutions were heated at a temperature of 80 °C, with constant stirring for 60 min which is represented in Fig. 1. Similarly, another six sets of solutions were prepared without heating technique. The gradual change in the colour of the solution from deep green to yellowish-brown, indicating the process of reduction of silver. Then the UV-VIS spectrophotometer was used to evaluate the formation of this silver nanoparticle (sp-AgNPs) at various time intervals.

Standard procedures for confirmation of sp-AgNPs formation

The formation of sp-AgNPs was confirmed using various characterization techniques like UV–Visible spectroscopy, Zeta potential, FE-SEM, and Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis. The UV–Visible spectroscopy was used initially employed to confirm the formation of sp-AgNPs optimised by both heating and non-heating techniques.

The FE-SEM technique helped to study the surface morphology and structural features of the sp-AgNPs over a wide scanning range. The EDX analysis and zeta potential measurement also confirmed the elemental composition with the presence of silver with a characteristic peak and confirming the colloidal stability optimised by both the techniques respectively. DLS analysis, determined the hydrodynamic particle size distribution and polydispersity index of the sp-AgNPs optimised by both the methods.

Antimicrobial activity of sp-AgNPs

By well-diffusion method

The antibacterial activity of the sp-AgNPs extract against Gram-positive(P. aeruginosa and Bacillus subtilis) and Gram-negative bacterial strains (E. coli and S. aureus) was tested using the well diffusion method. After inoculating a loop full of bacterial cultures in nutrient broth from the stock stored at 4 °C, the cultures were incubated in a shaking incubator for the overnight at 37 °C and shaking condition in order to revive active bacterial cultures. By spread plate technique each bacterial culture was spread on nutrient-agar plate and 8 mm well was bored in it with cork borer. The sp-AgNPs at various concentration (333.33, 266.66 and 200 µg/ml) prepared by Heating and Non-heating method were added to the well and keptfor incubation overnight at 37 °C. The lowest values of sp-AgNPs, in this well diffusion study was not taken into account. Next day, the zone of inhibition was observed and compared to commercially available antibiotic Ampicillin.

By broth dilution (MIC and MBC) method

The technique provided in the CLSI (2012) recommendation was incorporated to calculate the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of the synthesized AgNPs. For the broth microdilution method, a 96 well microtiter plate under UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Shitmaztu-1800) at 595 nm was used to carry out the experiment. To perform the MIC determination test, a microbial inoculum concentration of 106 CFU/mL was established initially. The sp-AgNPs stock solution from each synthesized sample (Heating and Non-heating) was mixed with diluted bacterial inoculums and combined with 100 µL of nutrient broth, commencing from column 3 to column 12 in first row and from 15 to 24 column of 2nd row of the 96 well plates. Serial dilution of the AgNPs from stock solution was carried out to produce range of concentrations in µg/mL to determine the MIC and MBC. Column 3 of the microtiter plate contained the highest concentration of AgNPs (333.33 µg/mL), while the 24th column contained the lowest concentration (0.00127 µg/mL). The negative control for the test consisted of only the Nutrient broth medium (column 1) and the positive control (column 3)with the broth and bacterial inoculums, represented in Fig. 2. At 37 °C, the microtiter plate for both Heat and Non-heat synthesis method was incubated for 24 h. The OD value was measured at 595 nmusing microtiter plate reader (Shimadzu, Japan) which indicated inhibition of bacterial growth. All the experimental evaluation was conducted in triplicate throughout the study period.

By inhibition of biofilm formation

The two-biofilm producing bacterium, E. coli and S. aureuswas chosen for this study. The synthesized sp-AgNPs were diluted at different concentrations based on their sub-MICs from ¼ to 2×MIC. These concentrations were added to respective 12 well plates containing the bacterial culture. S. aureus was supplemented separately with 2% glucose and 10% sodium chloride to initiate biofilm formation. After overnight incubation at 37 0Cfor 48 h, the planktonic bacteria were discarded carefully without disturbing the formed biofilm. Each well wasgently washed twice using distilled water for removing any non-adherent cells. Subsequently, 2 mL of methanol was added simultaneously to each well to fix the biofilm, followed by 15 min incubation. After fixation, methanol was discarded and the well were air-dried completely. To stain the biofilm, 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet solution was freshly prepared and added to each well and to enable the dye to stain the biofilm matrix, the plate was incubated for 20 min. The excess stain in the well was then discarded, and the wells were rinsed gently with distilled water twice to remove unbound dye. The stained biofilm will next be dissolved by adding 30% (v/v) glacial acetic acid to each well.The plate was incubated for 30 min, after which the optical density (OD) of the solubilized stain was measured at 550 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The experiment was performed three times independently, and the mean OD values were calculated.

Identification of active compounds in Sarcochlamys pulcherrima and forecasting potential targets

The literature review37, LC-MS/MS profile, and Indian Medicinal Plants, Phytochemistry And Therapeutics Database (IMPPAT, https://cb.imsc.res.in/imppat/basicsearch/phytochemical) were used to identify Sarcochlamys pulcherrima compounds with valuable pharmacokinetic properties and their corresponding protein targets based on the criteria of oral bioavailability and toxicity profile45,46.

The identification and selection of bioactive compounds in Sarcochlamys pulcherrima were achieved using the combination of LC-MS/MS profiling, literature and the IMPPAT database. In order to achieve pharmacological relevance, the identified compounds were further filtered according to the major pharmacokinetic characteristics such as oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 0.5), toxicity profile, and drug-likeness properties. Those compounds that matched these requirements were taken into consideration as targets in SwissTargetPrediction. Besides, network topology analysis was used to put more emphasis on compounds possessing higher values of degree, which denote a stronger biological connectivity and therapeutic significance. The multi-step filtering methodology was used to make sure that only pharmacokinetically and biologically relevant compounds entered the network pharmacology analysis.

Evaluation of antibacterial targets and discovery of common targets

A systematic search was conducted in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/).

and OMIM database (https://www.omim.org/) using ‘Antibacterial’ as the search term to pinpoint relevant targets. Following the removal of duplicate entries, a distinct collection of antibacterial targets was preserved. Following this, the Venny 2.1 tool was utilised to conduct an intersection analysis between the targets of bioactive compounds from Sarcochlamys pulcherrima and the identified antibacterial targets, aiming to pinpoint common targets that may exhibit antibacterial activity47–49.

Developing a network of active phytoconstituents and their corresponding targets

We integrated all biologically active components and projected targets to enhance the efficacy of Sarcochlamys pulcherrima in treating bacterial diseases. Cytoscape (version 3.10.3) (https://cytoscape.org/) was utilised to construct a network based on bioactive chemicals, projected target networks, and pathways50,51. The top active compounds exhibiting the highest degree values which were identified as core components for the subsequent validation studies. This strategy seeks to identify essential molecules in network pharmacology, establishing a scientific foundation for subsequent research and drug development.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) & Hub genes analysis

An essential approach to comprehending the intricate functions of proteins in biochemical processes involves the examination of protein-protein interactions (PPIs). Exploring protein-protein interactions is essential for comprehending the intricate role of proteins in various biological processes. The research analysed the complex network of associated proteins using the advanced virtual screening platform STRING 12.0 (https://string-db.org/)52,53. The careful incorporation of relevant genetic elements into the STRING database yielded significant insights into complex protein-protein interactions (PPIs). The PPI network was specifically designed for “Homo sapiens,” with criteria for evaluating the accuracy of interactions between target proteins refined to ensure a confidence level surpassing the standard of 0.9. In the complex network representation, each node signifies a distinct protein, and the connecting edges depict the intricate relationships among various protein entities. Additionally, CytoHubba was utilised within Cytoscape (Cytoscape 3.10.3) to evaluate the involvement of hub genes54,55.

Analysing the KEGG and GO pathways

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed utilising ShinyGo version 0.85 (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) software47. GO offers a systematic compilation of standardised terminology related to molecular operations, biological processes, and cellular components. The text includes the expected and selectively chosen gene expressions obtained from these phrases across different species. Gene Ontology (GO) is advantageous for pathway enrichment analysis. This facilitates the identification of key biological processes involved in antibacterial and antibiofilm activity. The metabolic pathways and activities of the chemicals and genes within the network were analysed in accordance with research utilising KEGG56.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of sp-AgNPs

To systematically evaluate the effect of phytochemical concentration on sp-AgNP synthesis, several amounts of 1% (w/v) leaf extract were used with a set of Ag salt concentration. The presence of bioactive phytochemicals in plant extracts acts as effective reducing and capping agents to stabilize NP formation aiding in the reduction of Ag⁺ to Ag⁰. The variation in plant extract volumes affects NP formation. Lower concentrations of plant extract leads to incomplete reduction and generation of bigger, polydisperse nanoparticles whereas smaller, and monodisperse NPsare formed at ideal concentrations. Temperature is a critical parameter in nanoparticle synthesis, as it influences the nucleation rate and crystal growth.When the temperature rises, the reduction process accelerates, which leads to the smaller NPs formation of uniform size range. At non-heat conditions, delayed reduction kinetics cause inadequate conversion of Ag⁺ to Ag⁰ or the creation of bigger NPs with a wider size dispersion57,58.

Initial sp-AgNPs formation was deliberated by a distinct change in colour from greenish yellow to yellowish brown colour after 1–2 h. Thereafter a variety of analytical methods like UV-Visible spectrophotometer, FE-SEM, EDX, Zeta potential was used to evaluate the particle size, surface morphology, elemental composition, and surface stability of the synthesized aqueous sp-AgNPs.

UV–Visible spectrophotometer analysis

The primary investigation for the synthesis of sp-AgNPs was carried out by UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-1800) with a scanof 200–800 nm for preliminary confirmation. Moreover, the surface plasmon peaks depends on the size, contour, structure and composition of the synthesized NPs59,60. The spectrum analysis revealed that for heat and non-heat methods, the peak of sp-AgNPs was found in the 410–427 nm regions which confirmed the initial formation of NPs61. Similar observations were reported by Giri et al.., 202262 while using bioinspired AgNPs with Eugenia roxburghiiextract. Al-Rajhi, et al.., 202226 also observed similar peak within 400–450 nm range using Kei-apple fruit extract inspired AgNPs, confirming the SPR band for Ag formation. The surface morphology of green synthesised silver NPs was studied by using FE-SEM micrographs. The micrographs were obtained using scanning electron microscopes at magnifications of almost 30,000x and 50,000x. The FE-SEM image demonstrated a spherical to nanoneedle-like shape for the sp-AgNPs optimized by both strategies, namely Heating and Non-heating methods, as illustrated in Figs. 4a and b. No aggregation of sp-AgNPs was seen in the FE-SEM images; the nanoparticles exhibited a generally homogeneous morphology with a restricted size distribution.According to the literature review, silver NPs synthesized from different plant extracts exhibit variations in shape and size depending on the various chemical composition of the extract63. Irregular, polyhedral, hexagonal and other morphology of green synthesized AgNPs were observed by Garcidueñas-Piña et al.., 202328 utilizing Beta vulgaris and Ocimum basilicum as green part. Taheri et al.., 2015 observed needle like nanoparticle structure using shallot and apricot tree gum as a reducing and stabilizing agent with 50–60 nm diameter64. The micrographs revealed the presence of all potential spherical needle structures of sp-AgNPs.The synthesised nanoparticle with common grain size was determined to be between 37.6 nm and 45.1 nm for Heat synthesized sp-AgNPs and 44.7 nm to 53.9 nm for Non-heat synthesized sp-AgNPs.

Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDX)

Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDX) operates on the principle of the particle characteristics of light. Within the X-ray wavelength range, the energy of photons is sufficient to generate a detectable, oscillating X-ray.

The EDX analysis was performed using the JEOL JS 7500 F. The Fig. 5a and b displays the energy dispersive X-ray spectrum analysis of the S. pulcherrima leaf extract, which reveals that, alongside the oxygen derived from the bioorganic part of the extract, there are multiple other inorganic ions present.It is confirmed that silver has been accurately identified because the identification lines for its primary emission energies are shown and matches the peaks in the spectrum. The resulting silver nanoparticles exhibited an optical band peak at approximately 2.983 KeV for both Heat (6H_AgNPs) and Non-heat (6NH_AgNPs), thereby validating its presence.

A sharp zeta potential peak was observed for Heat and Non-heat synthesizedsp-AgNPs with peaks in the aqueous extract as −26.18 mV and − 30.95 mV respectively, visualized in Fig. 6a and 5b. AgNPs remained dispersed in the medium due to their negative charge. A highly negative value indicates strong stability, preventing aggregation by causing the particles to repel each other.Similar, zeta potential values were observed by Ariskiet al., 202434 while using fruit peel-based NPs.

Analysis of antibacterial activity

The antimicrobial efficacy of sp-AgNPs was evaluatedby measuring the zone of inhibition against two Gram-negative bacteria, Escherichia coli (ATCC 8739)and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 2488) and Gram-positive bacteria,Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 65Q8) and Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633) with results compared to standard antibiotic Ampicillin (Figs. 8 and 9). A clear distinctiveness is observed on antibacterial potential based on NP synthesis method (Table 2). For Gram-negative bacteria, the nanoparticles synthesized using the heat method (6H_AgNPs) demonstrated superior antibacterial activity compared to the non-heat method (6NH_AgNPs), producing the largest zones of inhibition against E. coli (15.33 ± 0.58 mm). Gram-positive bacteria demonstrated reduced ZOI against B. subtilis (14.66 ± 0.58 mm) and S. aureus (14.33 ± 0.58 mm). This suggests that the application of heat produced NPs with altered physicochemical properties, dictates their mechanism of action against different types of bacteria but significantly effective in both cases.

Application of heat during synthesis created smaller sizesp-AgNPs with higher surface areawhich effectively penetrated the complex outer lipopolysaccharide layerin Gram-negative bacteria.In Gram-positive bacteria cascade of interactions involving generation of Reactive oxygen species, release of silver ions (Ag⁺),membrane deformation and increasing permeability at the cell interface effectively killed bacterial cells.In contrast, the non-heat synthesis method performed well against Gram-positive bacteria, yielding maximum inhibition zones against B. subtilis (14.93 ± 0.58 mm) and S. aureus (14.33 ± 0.58 mm). Notably, the absence of an outer membrane in Gram-positive bacteria indicates once the sp-AgNPs breach the peptidoglycan layer successfully, they encounter less structural resistance, allowing ROS and Ag+ ions to penetrate more readily. This explains the reason behind antibacterial efficacy of both heat-synthesized and non-heat-synthesized sp-AgNPs against Gram-positive species, as the critical barrier is the peptidoglycan layer rather than the more selective outer membrane found in Gram-negative bacteria.Thus the heat method for NP synthesis demonstrated better results for Gram-negative bacteria whereas the non-heat method of sp-AgNPs exhibited better results against both bacterial types. In accordance with our findings, similar result was reported by earlier researchers where plant-mediated synthesized AgNPs demonstrated potent antibacterial activity against Gram-negative bacteria in contrast to Gram-positive bacteria36,37,39,41. Thus sp-AgNPs with heat method generated average small particle size compared to the Non-heat method and showed better efficacy in antibacterial action. Our results corroborate similar reports by earlier researchers where lesser nanoparticle size generated better inhibition zone as they diffuse easily through the bacterial membrane35,65,66.

Evaluation minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

To determine the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC), Micro broth dilution method was performed in 96-well plate against each bacterial species following standard guidelines. MIC demonstrates the lowest concentration of AgNPs that inhibits bacterial growth.Sp-AgNPs concentration was varied from 333.33 µg/ml to 0.00127 µg/ml and thereafter the bacterial inhibition was tested in ELISA reader at 550 nm. The concentration of the bacterial inoculums in all the wells were adjusted to 106 CFU/mL. Starting from column 3–12 and column 15–24, 100 µL of the synthesized AgNPs stock solution were varied in diluted concentration with the respective bacterial inoculums for the MIC test. Sp-AgNP concentrations were highest in column 3 of the microtiter plate and lowest in column 24 wherasColumn 1 represents negative control (only medium) and Column 2 is positive control (medium and bacterial inoculums).The results indicate that MIC for Heat synthesized sp-AgNPs was 1.30 µg/mL for B. subtilis, P. aeruginosa, E. coliand 0.65 µg/mL for S. aureus. Similarly, Non-Heatsp-AgNPs demonstrated varied MIC (1.3 µg/mLforPseudomonas, 0.65 µg/mLforE. coli, 5.20 µg/mL for S. aureus and 2.60 µg/mLforBacillus). For the Gram-positive bacteria(S. aureus and B.subtilis), MICs determined were 20.75 and 2.59 µg/mL respectively whereas for the Gram-negative bacteria, the MIC value was 2.59 µg/mL (Figure. 8).

The MBC is determined as the minimum concentration of the antimicrobial agent needed to kill a particularbacterium within a specified period, is typically observed by bacterial growthin Nutrient agar plate. The test was performed by plating the diluted bacterial suspension from each microtiter well into NA plate, incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. No bacterial growth indicated MBC value, represented in Table 3. In concurrence with our result, Swidan et al., 2022 also observed MIC value of 37.64 and 725.7 µg/ml, with ginger and cinnamon based AgNPs respectively65. Similar results were observed by Giri et al., 202262, where 240 µg/mL of Eugenia roxburghii based nanoparticle solution was the best concentration to inhibit bacterial growth. Whereas Goswami et al., 202467 observed MIC value of 0.78 µg/mL utilizing the S.pulcherrima synthesized AgNPs against P. aeruginosa and MBC of 1.56 µg/mL and 25.0 µg/mL for P. aeruginosa and B. subtilis.

The ratio of the MBC to MIC serves as an indicator to evaluate the nature of the antibacterial effect exhibited by sp-AgNPs, represented in the Table 3. If the value of MBC/MIC ratio is equal to 1 or 2, the compound is considered to exhibit a bactericidal effect, indicating the ability to kill the bacterial cells. In contrast, a ratio ≥ 4 or 16 is indicative of a bacteriostatic effect, implying that the compound inhibits bacterial growth without necessarily causing cell death. This ratio is a critical parameter in determining the antibacterial potential of AgNPs against the tested bacterial strains. In our study, all the bacterial strains evaluated exhibited an MBC/MIC ratio of either 1 or 2, indicating the bactericidal effect of sp-AgNPs.

Analysis of sp-AgNPs on biofilm formation

This study evaluated the antibiofilm efficacy of sp-AgNPs at sub inhibitory concentrations (1/4 to 2 MIC), against S. aureus and E. coli. The results demonstrated concentration dependent inhibition of Biofilm in both the bacteria, which was more pronounced against S. aureus. The synthesis method of sp-AgNPs highly influenced their efficacy. Nanoparticles produced via Non-heat method, which were larger in size exhibited superior antibiofilm activity compared to those from Heat method. At the highest concentration tested, (2 MIC) the Non-heat sp-AgNPs demonstrated 83.14% inhibition against S. aureus and 67.51% against E. coli compared to 73.76% and 50.93% respectively for the Heat synthesized particles (Fig. 10a and b). Furthermore sp-AgNPs prevented biofilm formation, against E. coli(5.8 µg/ml) and S. aureus(41.5 µg/ml) at varied concentrations. The enhanced performance of the larger Non-heatsp-AgNPs is likely to be attributed either to the strong bactericidal effect or towards more effective disruption of proteins involved in biofilm matrix formation. More pronounced antibiofilm activity for Gram-negative bacteria could be explained by their thin LPS layer whereas the thick mucopeptide wall structure of Gram-positive bacteria obstructed penetration by the NPs. This finding contradicts the study conducted by Singh et al.., 202168 using Cedecea sp. inspired AgNPs, where biofilm inhibition activity was obtained at much lower concentration (0.25× MIC). Our results corroborate with the findings of Swidan et al.., 202269 where different synthesis methods also yielded NPs with varying antibiofilm efficacies. His study reported 18 µg/ml of green and chemical synthesized NPs prevented urinary Enterococcus pathogens biofilm formation by 39.14% and 65.32% respectively. In our study though the Heat synthesized NPs displayed superior antibacterial activity (low MICs), the Non-heat method demonstrated stronger antibiofilm effect. Thus, it is perceived that antibiofilm activity does not correlate with antibacterial efficacy, but rather critically influenced by size, shape and morphology of the synthesized nanoparticle70–75.

GO functional and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Notably, some of the enriched Gene Ontology terms and KEGG pathways that were found in this study are directly related to the host defense response to bacterial infection. The biological process enrichment that exemplifies molecule-response to molecules of bacterial origin, lipopolysaccharide, chemical stimulus and oxygen-containing compound (Fig. 13a) demonstrates the role of innate immune signalling and inflammatory response, which plays a significant role in antibacterial defense. These processes are reported to control the processes of macrophage activation, cytokine generation and oxidative stress responses to bacterial invasion.

(d) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis generated using ShinyGO v0.85 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) based on KEGG database (Kanehisa Laboratories, https://www.kegg.jp/). The enrichment of the cellular component (Fig. 13b), such as vesicle, vesicle lumen, secretory granule, and perinuclear region of the cytoplasm, indicates involvement of intracellular trafficking, phagolysosomal processing, and secretion of the antimicrobial mediators. These sub-cellular constituents are implicated in bacterial recognition, phagocytosis, intracellular bacterial clearance.

Moreover, the molecular functionality analysis (Fig. 13c) showed great enrichment in same protein binding, enzyme binding, nuclear receptor activity, catalytic activity on a protein, and ligand-activated transcription factor activity. Immune responses, inflammation, and apoptosis in bacterial infection rely on these functions as a core of signal transduction pathways.

The relevance of the identified targets to antibacterial purposes is also supported by further analysis of the KEGG pathway enrichment (Fig. 13d). Significantly, activation of immune and stress-response networks associated with host defense against pathogens is indicated in paths like apoptosis, HIF-1 signaling, Th17 cell differentiation, PD-L1 checkpoint pathway, and infectious disease-related pathways such as shigellosis, hepatitis B and coronavirus disease. It is worth noting that apoptosis is majorly used in destroying the infected host cells and limiting the survival of bacteria in the cell.

It should be mentioned that, the biological activity of sp-AgNPs cannot be directly connected to the metallic silver core but it is actively dependent on the adsorbed phytochemical moieties on the surface of nanoparticles. It is possible that these phytoconstituents have a synergistic effect with AgNPs to regulate host immune responses and intracellular signalling pathways. The discovered network pharmacology in the given study is therefore the functional contribution of the phytochemical corona that forms the main interface in the interactions between the nanoparticles and the biology.

Recognition of HUB genes

Identifying hub genes is a crucial step in determining the significant genes which plays a role in the process. The target genes have been integrated into the STRING website. Additionally, we have identified the 10 hub genes by employing the CytoHubba plugin using the Degree method. Figure 13e illustrates the HUB genes identified, which include 10 genes: AKT1, STAT3, CASP3, EGFR, ESR1, PTGS2, MMP9, TLR4, HSP90AA1, and MTOR. According to degree method (Table 5) among these 10 genes, top three genes are AKT1, STAT3 & CASP3.All the genes are thoroughly documented regulators of host-directed antibacterial immunity. TLR4 is used to recognize bacterial lipopolysaccharide, AKT1 and MTOR are needed to control survival and autophagy of immune cells, STAT3 is needed to regulate immune responses led by cytokines, and CASP3 is needed to regulate infected cell apoptosis. Taken together, these findings imply that the antibacterial effect in the present study might have been mediated by the processes of regulation of the host immune, inflammatory, and apoptotic mechanisms, as opposed to direct bactericidal effects.

Conclusion

In this present study, the green synthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were successfully integrate utilizing the aqueous leaf extract of Sarcochlamys pulcherrima through both heating and non-heating methods, where the phytoconstituent acted as reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents for nanoparticle formation. This green synthesis approach markedly influenced the morphology and biological activity of the resulting sp-AgNPs, with needle-like structures exhibiting distinct antibacterial and antibiofilm properties. The sp-AgNPs produced via the heating method demonstrated enhanced bactericidal action against Gram-negative bacteria, whereas those obtained through the non-heating method showed superior inhibition of biofilm formation. Complementary network pharmacology analysis further elucidated the antibacterial mechanisms of S. pulcherrima. An integrated network pharmacology and protein-protein interaction (PPI) methodology was used in the current study to explain the molecular mechanism behind the observed antibacterial activity. Enrichment of the Gene Ontology showed strong participation of biological processes connected with the reaction to bacterial molecules, chemical stimuli, and oxidative stress, which emphasize the activation of the innate immune system and inflammatory processes.Cellular component analysis indicated that it was enriched in vesicles and secretory components, which means that it plays a role in intracellular traffic of proteins and processes of pathogen in the cell, but molecular function analysis indicated protein binding, enzymatic regulation and transcription factor activity which is significant in immune signalling. The further analysis of the data with KEGG pathway showed that there was an enrichment of immune and infection associated pathways, including but not limited to the apoptosis and HIF-1 signalling, Th17 cell differentiation, as well as pathogen-associated disease pathways, that played a pivotal role in defencing host mechanism against bacterial infections. The discovery of the main hub genes, including AKT1, STAT3, CASP3, TLR4 and MTOR, highlights their central functions in controlling the immune activation processes, the inflammatory responses, autophagy and apoptosis in bacterial challenge. All these results support the shared conclusion that an antibacterial effect is mostly effected via host-guided processes, comprising regulation of immune, inflammatory, and apoptotic systems as opposed to direct bacteria-killing. This host-targeted mechanism of action offers a mechanistic explanation of the potential of the investigated system to be antibacterial and justifies its applicability as a promising way of treating bacterial infections, especially in the background of antimicrobial resistance. Together, the experimental and computational results highlight the immensepotential of S. pulcherrima as a sustainable biogenic source for nanoparticle synthesis with potent antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy, which can be harnessed further in biomedical and therapeutic applications.
