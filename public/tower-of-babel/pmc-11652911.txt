Groundwater flow system of the Abijata-Langano-Ziway lakes basin, Ethiopia
Authors: Debele Abera, Behailu Berehanu, Tenalem Ayenew
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Abijata-Langano-Ziway Lakes Basin (ALZLB) is situated in the Central part of the Main Ethiopian Rift. The availability and dynamics of groundwater in the Abijata-Langano-Ziway Lakes Basin (ALZLB) are primarily controlled by its geological and hydrogeological characteristics, shaped by volcanic-tectonic and sedimentary processes. The basin faces significant challenges, including drastic change in land use pattern, rapid population growth sustained by subsistence farming, over-extraction of water resources, and vulnerability to climate change and fragile ecosystems. These issues emphasize the urgent need for effective water resource management.To understand this complex system, a numerical groundwater flow model was employed to characterize the groundwater flow system within the ALZLB and examine its interaction with surface water bodies. The MODFLOW model translates the conceptual understanding of the basin's hydrogeology into a mathematical representation, allowing for numerical analysis. The model incorporates input parameters such as hydraulic conductivity and boundary conditions representing groundwater inflow and outflow. Steady-state numerical calculation was used to characterize the qualitative hydrogeological conceptual model into numerical representation and thereby describe the groundwater system.The calibrated model exhibited excellent agreement between simulated and observed groundwater levels. Statistical measures indicated a strong correlation (R2 = 0.98) and high efficiency (NSE = 0.97) in replicating the observed data. Additionally, the Mean Error (ME) of −8.3 m suggests minimal bias in the simulations. Further analysis of the histogram residuals revealed that a significant portion of the simulated values (65 % and 82 %) fell within ±20 m and ±30 m of the observed groundwater levels, respectively. This revealed the model's accuracy in capturing the groundwater system's behavior.The model identified groundwater recharge and constant head boundaries as the primary sources of groundwater inflow, contributing 445 million cubic meters per year (MCM/year) and 90 MCM/year, respectively. Conversely, constant head boundaries represented the most significant outflow pathway, with a simulated discharge of 519 MCM/year. The calibrated balance between inflow and outflow (discrepancy of −0.75 %) confirms that the model effectively simulates steady-state groundwater flow conditions. The calibrated model demonstrates the model's capability to accurately represent the basin's groundwater system.Keywords: Water budget, Volcano-tectonic, Finite difference, groundwater hydrology, Water resources

Introduction

Numerical systems modeling can reveal the complex interactions between groundwater and surface water bodies, including rivers, lakes, wetlands, and estuaries. These models demonstrate how groundwater discharges contribute to baseflow, sustaining surface water during arid periods. Conversely, surface water bodies can act as recharge zones for underlying aquifers via infiltration processes [1]. A thorough understanding of groundwater-surface water interaction is paramount for effective water resource management [2]. This knowledge facilitates the development of sustainable water allocation strategies, the evaluation of groundwater pumping impacts on surface water availability, and the implementation of measures to safeguard and restore surface water bodies reliant on groundwater inputs.

Groundwater flow systems underpin the health and function of aquatic ecosystems. They act as a perennial source of water for surface water bodies, maintaining stable flow regimes and suitable habitat conditions for aquatic organisms [3]. Groundwater discharge also influences temperature regulation, nutrient availability, and the overall ecological integrity of surface water ecosystems, ultimately shaping species distribution and fostering diverse aquatic communities [4]. However, climate change presents a significant challenge to water resources globally [5]. Alterations in precipitation patterns, temperature, and hydrological cycles can disrupt the availability and timing of groundwater recharge and discharge, consequently impacting surface water availability [6,7].

The effect of climate change on the basin under investigation has been addressed by numerous studies and showed variable results. For instance, a decline in runoff and streamflow was reported [[8], [9], [10]]. On the contrary, up scaling in future precipitation projection results were published by numerous authors [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Even some authors revealed mixed results, both rising and falling in trends of precipitation in the basin [15,16]. Modeling groundwater flow systems is imperative for anticipating the implications of climate change on water resources and subsequently, its impacts on aquatic ecosystem dynamics, in the basin where the optimal balance between human and ecological water uses is adequately addressed.

The groundwater flow system has been widely used to examine the interaction of surface water and subsurface water [17]. Besides, the response of aquifer under fluctuating rainfall condition groundwater flow system in fracture controlled hydraulic system [18] and topographically controlled groundwater flow system are among the others [19]. Similarly, there are numerous groundwater modeling studies that have been published in the region with multiple objectives [[20], [21], [22], [23]].

In the study basin, groundwater is a crucial source of water supply for agricultural, domestic and industrial uses. The water supply for villages and towns predominately relies on groundwater sources and also more than 25 % of water demand for irrigation has been directly abstracted from aquifers in the basin [24,25]. Groundwater may be manifested as springs, streams, and wetlands. The perennial streams would not have permanent flow without groundwater contribution as baseflow [1]. Besides this, it has a great role in sustaining water resources in the basin. For instance, groundwater is believed to have a connection with the chain of lakes located in the rift floor of the basin and surrounding ecosystems. The highlands and plateau areas are mainly considered as potential recharge areas. On the contrary, the rift floor is the region where groundwater is found at shallow depth and is regarded as a discharge zone in the basin. The baseflow is considered as groundwater discharged to the streams and a simple baseflow separation method shows that its contribution to Ketar and Mike rivers flows [26,27].

There is a continuously increasing demand for groundwater development that may lead to over-exploitation of groundwater resources and growing impacts of human activities on aquifers, such as a decline in hydraulic heads and degrade groundwater quality. The basin supports a growing human population. These inhabitants depend critically on the basin's ecosystem services, primarily for agricultural production, livestock grazing, and fisheries. However, these anthropogenic activities present a potential threat to the basin's sustainability, potentially leading to water quality deterioration, biodiversity decline, and land degradation.

The study basin has also been facing the challenges of satisfying demands for irrigation, domestic and minimum water flows to sustain the ecological health of the basin [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Hence, the continuity of water resources is severely challenged in the phases of stressors such as population growth, increased demand for agricultural and urban uses, the need to maintain adequate environmental flows to sustain healthy ecology, and potential impacts of mankind-induced modifications such as land use change and climate change.

Agricultural constituted the key pillar in supporting the economy in the basin and moreover, there has been sharp growing of irrigation based investments in the basin [32]. Furthermore, the escalation of unregulated irrigation practices causing excessive flooding is resulting in the salinization of soil [33]. As a consequence, there has been a growing trend of converting forested areas into agricultural land as farmers abandon the salt-affected lands and search for new, unaffected lands [34].

The decrease in the size of Lake Abijata is attributed to the extraction of water from rivers and lakes upstream, which is further intensified by climate change and land development [8,35]. This reduction in water availability has led to a decline in the area coverage of Lake Abijata from 179.2 km2 in 1972 to 131.8 km2 in 2015 [36]. As a result, there has been a loss of biodiversity in the freshwater ecosystems due to human impacts on water and the extraction of water is often carried out without a proper understanding of the complex hydrological and hydrogeological systems and the delicate nature of the rift ecology [3].

Furthermore, the study conducted in the basin provides a concise overview of the key water-related issues that require effective water resource management. These include the unsustainable extraction of water, which poses a significant threat to the local ecosystem and leads to intense competition for water resources without proper differentiation in their usage; the decline in water quality, affecting both irrigation and domestic purposes; the heavy dependence of the population on water resources to sustain their livelihoods [37].

So far, a few studies on a numerical groundwater model have been carried out with the objective mainly focusing on its potential [38] and groundwater interaction with the lakes [39]. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to bridge notable research gaps regarding how the groundwater flow systems are configured spatially across the basin and its interaction with surface water using model-based approach. Moreover, model-based informed decision-making tool is very critical for water resource management given the present dynamic situation in the basin.

Characterization of the study basin

Description of the study basin

The Abijata-Langano-Ziway Lakes Basin (ALZLB) is located in the central section of the Central Main Ethiopian Rift valley, which is part of Great East African Rift Valley System. It encompasses part of Oromia and Southern Nations and Nationalities and Peoples’ Administrative Regions. It is a part of the Ethiopian Rift Valley Lakes Basin, and it is bounded by geographic coordinates of 414500–545180 mE longitude and 797930-93592 mN latitude (Fig. 1) using projection of Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM), Adindan N37 zone. The Great East African Rift Valley splits the Ethiopian highland into northern and southern parts, creating a rift floor inhabited by rows of lakes with different sizes, hydrological and hydrogeological settings [40].

Study area, (a) Ethiopia, (b) Rift valley basin, (c) ALZLB.

The basin endowed by Ziway, Langano and Abijata lakes lodging in the axis of the rift and on both sides of the basin, there are numerous persistent streams (Meki, Ketar, Gedemso, Lepsi, and Huluka) draining into the lakes. The basin supports a population of more than 2 million whose livelihoods mainly rely on subsistent farming [41] with the annual growth rate of 3 % [42]. According to Ref. [32], more than 83,000 ha of lands was irrigated using surface and groundwater sources and about 1.8 million of livestock [43]. The basin covers an area of more than 10, 000 km2.

The basin is characterized by humid to sub-humid in the plateaus and semi-arid in the rift floor. The mean yearly precipitation over the entire basin is around 900 mm, showing high spatial variations as no more than 700 mm/year and as high as 1200 mm/year in the rift floor and highlands respectively [44].

The Rift floor region of the central rift valley basin is occupied by a row of lakes: Ziway, Langano, and the terminal lake Abijata (shown in Fig. 1), while their areal extension, depth and volume in the 1ate 1990s’ (Table 1). However, the current size of the Lake Abijata has been modified to the large extent. As of 2020, the estimated area of the lake from the image of Google Earth was 166 km2. This variation may be associated with a temporal change of the lake area coverage. These lakes and perennial rivers are thought to have existential connections with the groundwater system of the basin because of its geological and hydrogeological frameworks (see Table 2).

Geological and hydrogeological settings

The geology of the basin is constituted by three major rock categories documented in Refs. [[45], [46], [47]] in the basin: (i) Basement and Mesozoic Sedimentary rocks; rarely outcropped in few localities in the western part of the basin; (ii) Volcanite and pyroclastic rocks and older lacustrine deposits and (iii) Quaternary Sedimentary Deposits.

The volcanite and pyroclastic groups of rocks comprise several units and cover about 71 % of the basin and in age ranging from Oligocene to Middle Pleistocene. These groups of rocks have great significance in determining the hydrological and hydrogeological flow regimes of the basin. The volcanite unit is made of basalts and rhyolitic ignimbrites and tuffs. Especially in the western part of the basin where the Meki River originated, the flat-lying plateau and the strongly faulted major plateau – rift escarpment in the basin are dominantly constituted by Pliocene age ignimbrites and tuffs. These rocks are less permeable, and are used as a groundwater divider on the western border of the basin. They are formed from thick beds of flood basalts, with rare beds of basic tuffs, agglomerates, and volcanic breccia, associated with some interlayered silicic volcanic rocks. The upper unit is dominantly rhyolite and trachyte flows with minor basalts, while flood basalts with minor silicic flows constitute the lower parts.

Arsi Shield Volcanoes (Eastern side) is the Late Miocene to Pliocene age forms shield volcanoes that largely contains basalts, mugearites and minor trachyte, and phonolites, but they also contain inter-bedded basaltic breccia (agglomerates). They serve as groundwater divide in the eastern boundary and have a significant influence on meteorological and hydrological parameters, because of their occurrence at the highly elevated Eastern part of the basin and are characterized by low permeability. Rift Floor Ignimbrites unit is composed of peralkaline-rhyolitic- ignimbrites and loosely fused ignimbrites and pumice with subordinate lava flow of basaltic composition. This group of rocks is found abundantly in low lying rift floor region. They also occupy the Wonji Fault Belt (WFB) and the Siltie-Debre Zeyit Fault Zone (SDZFZ) faulting zones. Hence, this unit could be a zone of high groundwater potential that has significant control over the groundwater flow regime. In addition, it significantly influences the hydrochemistry of the basin because of the rhyolitic composition of rocks.

Quaternary Sedimentary Units, Late Pleistocene to Late Holocene a complex sequence of lacustrine and fluvial deposits, and recent colluvial and fluvial deposits exposed at the foot of ridges (alluvial fans) occupy the rift floor. These groups of rocks have limited exposure but where they occur, they can influence groundwater flow regimes and strongly determine the hydrochemistry. Besides, there are other rock units that occupy a relatively small portion of the basin that control groundwater movement locally.

The hydrogeology of the basin is made up of the dominant and vast aquifer-forming groups of rocks, which are volcanic and volcanoclastic and Quaternary sedimentary units. These volcanics and volcanoclastic rocks comprise rocks of alkali trachyte, rhyolites, ignimbrite, tuffs and basalts that are found on the eastern and western escarpment and plateau of the basin. These were highly affected by rift faulting and intensively fractured and jointed and these structural features facilitate fast infiltration into the groundwater system [39].

Quaternary sedimentary units occur in the central (lakes region) and low-lying part of the basin and it is the second largest unit in surface area coverage. These units, in places where, there are secondary porosity and have an enormous capacity to store and transmit the groundwater. In contrast to these, they may have an impermeable layer that affects the region's hydrologic and hydrogeologic characteristics in some places [28].

Conceptual model of the study basin

Developing a conceptual model for the groundwater-flow system in the ALZLB Basin requires the definition of the hydrogeological framework: the identification of hydrogeological units within the lacustrine and alluvial deposits and volcanic units that are characterized as aquifer systems. The top layer is considered as an unconfined aquifer whereas the bottom layer is considered a confined aquifer system. The second part of the conceptual model consists of the hydrologic behavior of the flow systems in terms of shallow local flow and deep local flow, and deep regional groundwater flows. The presence of faults, especially in the escarpment of the basin, affects the groundwater flow system largely.

The flow systems are characterized by sources of water (recharge, movement of water from surface water to groundwater, release of water from storage) and sinks (discharge to surface waters, addition of water to storage). Springs emerged at lithological contacts and faulted zones. There are varying, amounts of sources and sinks in the case of lakes that may have a large influence on water levels and water movement in the basin. The third part of the conceptual model element is the connection of groundwater and surface water. The extracting of groundwater can appreciably alter the natural pattern of local and regional discharge into the lakes in the region. This, in turn, affects the levels of lake water. Finally, the calibration was carried out against the observed borehole data using the manual method through the trial-and-error method.

The following assumptions were made based on the above schematic conceptual model (Fig. 2). The source water (groundwater, spring and rivers) in the basin is from direct rainfall and rainfall on both sides of the escarpments and plateau. The occurrence of groundwater in the western and eastern escarpment is because of the hydrological and hydrogeological properties within various geomorphological units and lakes: The groundwater recharge zone is considered the western and eastern mountains and flows towards the lakes’ region; Butajira Crescent is assumed to be recharged from runoff coming from the escarpment, and direct rainfall on the plain; Lake Ziway receives water form groundwater, surface water, and direct rainfall on the Lake Surface and outflows groundwater at its south-eastern sides and through Bulbula River; and Lake Langano gain water from groundwater, surface water, and direct rainfall on the Lake surface and outputs through Horakelo River and groundwater. In addition to these, the groundwater flow direction is directed towards the lakes. However, as it approaches the lakes, the flow is directed to the south to the Lake Shalla on the eastern side and the main persistent rivers are believed to have links to groundwater.

Boundary and initial conditions

The model boundary conditions are delineated by defined hydraulic heads, flux and head dependent flux. It determines the flux of water enters and leaves the model domain. Similarly, in the study basin, the groundwater flow system is mainly controlled by geologic and hydrologic boundaries. Hence, a no-flow boundary is used to determine the geographic extent of the groundwater flow system and inflows and outflows along the model boundary and the general head boundary is used to represent the groundwater outflow and inflow depending on the head at the boundary in the southern part of the model domain.

Model design (model domain and Specification)

In order to put the conceptual model in a suitable form of numerical model, the model designing step is mandatory. This step comprises the layout of the grid cells, simulation time steps, boundary and initial conditions, and preliminary selection of values of aquifer parameters and hydrologic stresses. The Finite-Difference grid model consisted of two layers made up of 90 rows-oriented East to West and 95 columns-oriented North to South with equal dimension of 1500 m grid-cell. The thicknesses of the aquifers vary from 150 m to 200 m, and 250 m–300 m in the rift floor to the highland for the top layer and bottom layer respectively.

Model parameter

Hydraulic properties

During groundwater modeling, it is a very challenging activity to obtain realistic hydraulic conductivity values at appropriate spatial distribution in the field, preferably by pumping tests. So that, hydraulic conductivity is often the most important and variable modeling parameter [53]. The initial horizontal hydraulic conductivity of the basin is compiled from different literature [38,54]. Based on the above studies, the horizontal hydraulic conductivity map of the basin was prepared. The highest hydraulic value is assigned to the ignimbrite unit at the eastern escarpment and followed by and the rift floor alluvial-lacustrine deposits where primary porosity plays a significant role in groundwater storage and transmission.

The least value was given to balchi with insignificant areal coverage. The vast Oligocene-Early Pliocene Volcanites and Late Miocene-Pliocene Volcanites groups of rocks located on the highlands on both sides (at Eastern and western) have been assigned values of 5–10 m/day and 0.1–1 m/day at initial stage and later will be changed through calibration processes. Groundwater recharge zones

Groundwater recharge rate is one of the most variable and yet the most important parameter in groundwater modeling, despite its difficulty to accurately measure and estimate it because it occurs in a wide spatio-temporal dimension. In the case of the study basin, the major source of recharge to the aquifer comes from precipitation and river channel losses.

As the main recharge mechanism, direct or diffuse recharge is supposed to take place in almost areas of the basin with exception to where low permeable lacustrine soil (diatomite) exists in the rift floor, around Lake Ziway [32]. The mean annual recharge estimated using catchment soil-water balance mode with 50 mm soil moisture-holding capacity is about 10 % and 13 % of precipitation for Ketar and Meki subbasins respectively [40]. Model calibration

The study basin groundwater flow model was calibrated using the trial-and-error technique by altering initial estimates of aquifer properties, hydraulic conductivities, recharge, and discharge to become the best fit among simulated hydraulic heads and observed water levels. It was calibrated under steady-state conditions. The overall mean variance between simulated and observed heads was expressed, as given in Ref. [49] using statistical comparison. These are commonly used statistical comparison criteria: Mean Error (ME), Absolute Mean Error (AME), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Nash-Sutcliff coefficient (NSE), and Coefficient of Determination (R2).

Model sensitivity analysis

Following successful model calibration, a sensitivity analysis was undertaken to quantify the uncertainties associated with input variables, stresses, and boundary conditions [49]. This analysis served to reveal the model's response to variations within the calibrated parameter ranges, thereby identifying those parameters that exert the most significant influence on the simulated outcomes. These insights can be employed to guide future data collection efforts, ultimately minimizing model errors. The model's sensitivity to a particular parameter is then determined by evaluating the relative change in the output variable in response to a unit change in the input parameter holding all the others constant. A greater change in the output signifies a more responsive model, indicating a higher degree of influence from that specific parameter on the overall results.

In this study, a sensitivity analysis of the groundwater model was conducted to assess the overall model efficiency, with a specific focus on aquifer parameters. The analysis involved systematically increasing or decreasing the horizontal hydraulic conductivity and groundwater recharge rates by a predetermined percentage. The resulting changes in hydraulic heads were then compared against observed data and established model performance metrics to evaluate the sensitivity of the model to each parameter.

The sensitivity analysis revealed that ±20 % perturbations in either the hydraulic conductivity values or the recharge rates exerted a significant influence on the distribution of groundwater heads within the basin. The model demonstrated sensitivity to both increases and decreases in these parameters; however, a greater responsiveness was observed for increased recharge flux compared to equivalent increases in hydraulic conductivity (Fig. 11).

Groundwater balance of model domain

This study employs water budget analysis to assess the efficiency of the groundwater flow model. The model calculates the total volume of water entering and exiting the aquifer systems across the entire modeling domain. This approach serves as an indirect validation technique, where each component of the water budget (inflow and outflow terms) is evaluated relative to the simulated total inflows and outflows. Under natural conditions, the inflow components encompass constant head boundaries, general head boundaries, and river leakage. Conversely, outflows consist of constant head boundaries, general head boundaries, river leakage, and well abstractions. The model results indicate a high degree of consistency between inflow and outflow terms, signifying a steady-state modeling condition with a minimal discrepancy of −0.75 % (Table 6). The calibrated findings from the steady state model water balance indicated that the modeled groundwater recharge stood at approximately 445 million cubic meters (MCM). In contrast, the initial estimate for groundwater recharge was 555 MCM. The primary inputs into the groundwater system were groundwater recharge and the fixed head boundary, contributing 445 MCM/year and 90 MCM/year, respectively. Conversely, the principal outflow (discharge) from the groundwater system was through the fixed head boundary, with a simulated rate of 519 MCM/year.

Research limitations

The study basin is made up of volcano-sedimentary rock groups highly modified by volcano-tectonic events since its formation to now. This creates variable hydrogeological and hydrological parameters. Besides limitation of data availability, the numerical groundwater model is a device intended to approximate the field scale conditions of groundwater with a reasonable limit. It may not reproduce the complexity of nature as a whole. Hence, the following limitations may apparently exist: hydrogeological and hydrological properties, variability, boundary conditions, simplification used in conceptual models, spatial discretization de-emphasizes local variability and simultaneous calibration of fluxes and conductivities may bring uncertainties.

Conclusions

The Abijata-Langano-Ziway-Lake Basin (ALZLB) exhibits a geologically complex setting within the Ethiopian Rift Valley, leading to highly variable aquifer properties and intricate hydrogeologic characteristics. To elucidate these complexities, a numerical groundwater flow model was constructed utilizing MODFLOW software. Model parameters and boundary conditions were accurately selected to align with the established conceptual model of the basin. Calibration of the model outputs confirmed its efficacy in accurately representing aquifer properties, resulting in a strong fit. Notably, the water balance components derived from the calibrated model exhibited closely consistent with the conceptual model and associated assumptions, with a minimal discrepancy of −0.75 %.

This result suggests that the model effectively replicates the basin's natural water balance. Furthermore, the model simulations revealed a significant contribution of the basin's groundwater system to sustaining lakes, wetlands, and river ecosystems. It is imperative to acknowledge the ALZLB's status as one of Ethiopia's most environmentally sensitive areas, characterized by fragile ecosystems. Given its designation as a closed hydrological basin, even relatively minor interventions in water resource management strategies have the potential to induce far-reaching ecological consequences. A key outcome of this study is the establishment of a robust groundwater flow model. This model incorporates critical hydrogeological parameters, including hydraulic conductivity, aquifer characteristics, and recharge rates, to provide a comprehensive understanding of the basin's groundwater system.

The model's outputs provide valuable insights for water resource management and planning. These include the direction and magnitude of groundwater flow, identification of recharge and discharge zones, and potential areas susceptible to contamination or depletion. Furthermore, the model's predictive capabilities allow for the simulation of various scenarios, such as changes in pumping or land use practices. This enables informed decision-making towards sustainable groundwater management, mitigating potential adverse impacts. In essence, the model serves as a cornerstone for effective groundwater resource assessment and management, providing a scientific foundation for sustainable water practices.
