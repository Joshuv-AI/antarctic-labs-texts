Constitutive and inducible oleoresin defenses share genetic architectures and mechanisms in Pinus taeda
Authors: Mallory M Morgan, Jared Westbrook, Christopher Dervinis, Tania Quesada, Salvador Gezan, Robert Sykes, Timothy S Johnson, Gabriela Madrid, Matthew Lane, Alice Townsend, Thomas A Colquhoun, John M Davis, Daniel Jacobson, Gary F Peter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary The oleoresin defense system of loblolly pine (Pinus taeda) protects trees from insects and pathogens and is an important source of renewable biofuels and chemicals, but the genetic basis of oleoresin production is poorly understood. We characterized the genetic architecture of oleoresin flow, resin canal number, stem wood terpene content, and monoterpene composition in two clonal populations of P. taeda.We used quantitative genetic analyses, genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), multiplex network learning, and gene expression profiling to elucidate shared gene networks underlying defense traits and to identify high‐quality candidates for breeding and engineering loblolly pine.Genetic analyses revealed polygenic inheritance and trait‐to‐trait correlations provide strong evidence for shared genes regulating constitutive and induced oleoresin flow. We identified 236 single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with oleoresin flow, resin canal number, and terpene composition and highlight candidate genes likely involved in terpene biosynthesis, cambial meristem reprogramming, and pathogen perception and immune signaling. Fourteen GWAS candidates were methyl jasmonate‐responsive in tissues where resin canals initiate and terpene production occurs.Integrating quantitative genetics, GWAS, gene expression, and multiplex network analyses enabled the prioritization of high‐quality candidate genes. This work advances the development of more resilient loblolly pine optimized for ecological performance, renewable chemical, and biofuel production. Keywords: genome‐wide association study, loblolly pine, oleoresin flow, plant immunity, quantitative genetics, systems biology, terpene

Introduction

In North America, conifer forests coevolved with bark beetles and their associated fungi, whereby attacked trees exhibit constitutive and inducible defenses to resist the beetle‐fungus complex (Paine et al., 1997; Franceschi et al., 2005; Schiebe et al., 2012). In loblolly (Pinus taeda) and slash pine (Pinus elliottii var. elliottii), constitutive and induced oleoresin defenses are critical for physical and chemical resistance to southern pine beetle (Dendroctonus frontalis) and their associated fungi (Hodges et al., 1985; Strom et al., 2002; Klepzig et al., 2005). Oleoresin is a complex mixture of volatile monoterpene olefins and nonvolatile diterpenoid resin acids that are synthesized constitutively in needles and wood and are induced in response to wounding, fungal infection, and herbivory. In stems, oleoresin is synthesized and stored in the lumens of a specialized network of secondary resin canals in the secondary xylem and phloem (Trapp & Croteau, 2001; Zulak & Bohlmann, 2010). The chemical composition of oleoresin also makes it a renewable source of biofuels and chemicals that can be harvested from live tree stems (Peter, 2018). Resin canals are maintained under positive pressure, and the viscous oleoresin flows to wounds, physically pushing out insects and covering them and their associated fungi with toxic terpenes. Oleoresin also crystallizes and seals the wound site after biotic attack or mechanical damage, forming a physical barrier that protects the stem from further infection (Trapp & Croteau, 2001).

Oleoresin chemical composition, pressure, flow rate, and crystallization rate are associated with increased tree resistance to beetles and fungi (Paine et al., 1997). High oleoresin flow rate is linked to increased constitutive resistance in conifer trees (Hodges et al., 1979; Bridges, 1987; Nebeker et al., 1992; Strom et al., 2002; Klepzig et al., 2005), suggesting increased resistance to insects and pathogens is positively correlated to traits that enhance oleoresin flow. Constitutive oleoresin flow rate and resin canal number in loblolly pine (Westbrook et al., 2013, 2015), terpene composition and constitutive oleoresin yield in masson pine (Pinus massoniana) (Huanhuan et al., 2018), and wood terpene composition in slash pine (Zhang et al., 2016) are all under moderate genetic control and are inherited quantitatively. These findings support the idea that increasing wood oleoresin to enhance resistance to stem boring insects and fungal pathogens, as well as for renewable biofuel production, can be synergistically achieved through breeding and genetic engineering.

It is hypothesized that the extensive constitutive development of secondary resin canals in wood of Pinus evolved in response to selective pressure from bark beetles. Pines, which have their center of diversity in subtropical climates of North America (Perry et al., 1998), are hosts to bark beetles that undergo several generations of reproduction in 1 yr, whereas constitutive resin canal development is less extensive in Picea and Abies from cooler climates with slower rates of beetle reproduction (Matson & Hain, 1985; Christiansen et al., 1987; Franceschi et al., 2005). Constitutive oleoresin flow is related to oleoresin pressure, the reservoir of available terpene, and terpene composition (Fig. 1). In addition to constitutive defense, oleoresin production and secondary resin canal formation are induced by wounding, fungal inoculation, and treatment of stems with chemical inducers including jasmonic acid (JA) and ethylene (Stubbs et al., 1984; Franceschi et al., 2002; Martin et al., 2002) (Fig. 1). The induction of terpene synthesis after jasmonate treatment occurs at first (0–7 d) as a result of the increased expression of terpene biosynthetic enzymes (Martin et al., 2002; Zulak et al., 2009) and later (3–4 wk) as a result of the development of traumatic resin canals in xylem (Nagy et al., 2000; Franceschi et al., 2002; Luchi et al., 2005; Schmidt et al., 2011).

Summary of current state of knowledge for factors influencing oleoresin flow. Oleoresin flow in loblolly pine (Pinus taeda) is a complex, polygenic trait influenced by developmental and metabolic traits as well as environmental factors. Terpene synthesis determines the mono‐ and diterpene composition in oleoresin that influences oleoresin viscosity and crystallization rate. Axial and radial resin canals form an interconnected network of resin canals, which determine terpene supply and overall storage capacity of wood oleoresin. Oleoresin pressure is influenced by the water status of the tree, which includes transpiration rate and soil water content. Terpene synthesis and traumatic resin canal formation can be induced via the hormones ethylene and jasmonic acid, reactive oxygen species (ROX), insects, pathogens, and wounding, or a combination of these. We hypothesize that constitutive and induced oleoresin flow are positively correlated and share genetic regulatory elements because jasmonate stimulates the formation of new traumatic axial resin canals in the vascular cambial zone (VCZ), which are also produced constitutively in Pinus spp. However, it remains unknown if inducible defenses are genetically regulated and to what extent they share a genetic architecture with constitutive oleoresin defenses. A better understanding of the genetic architecture regulating important constitutive and induced defense traits is therefore needed to enable breeding and engineering efforts that enhance resistance to insect and pathogen pressures and increase terpene supply for renewable biofuels and chemicals.

To investigate this hypothesis, we characterized the genetic architecture governing constitutive and induced oleoresin flow and stem wood terpene content with quantitative genetic analyses and genome wide association studies (GWASs) in two clonal populations of loblolly pine (ADEPT2 and CCLONES) (Fig. 2). We estimated heritability and trait‐to‐trait correlations for seven oleoresin‐related traits and identified high‐ and low‐yielding genotypes to validate our quantitative genetic models. To identify candidate genes underlying oleoresin flow, resin canal number, and terpene composition, we performed GWAS using multiple single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sets and models. We applied multiplex network learning algorithms Multiplex Embedding of Networks for Team‐Based Omics Research (MENTOR) and Random Walk with Restart‐Shortest Paths (RWR‐SP) to mechanistically prioritize GWAS hits and integrate them into functional gene modules, which were experimentally supported by gene expression analyses. Our approach links quantitative genetics with systems biology to uncover the shared genetic basis of constitutive and inducible defense traits and to identify regulatory targets for breeding pines with enhanced resistance and increased terpene yield for renewable biofuel production.

Methodologies and conceptual workflow. We phenotyped two loblolly pine (Pinus taeda) populations for oleoresin traits across metabolic, developmental, and integrated scales. From the ADEPT2 population, we collected mono‐ and diterpene phenotypes as well as constitutive and methyl jasmonate (MeJA)‐induced oleoresin flow phenotypes. From CCLONES, we collected constitutive oleoresin flow, resin canal number, and resin canal density phenotypes. We characterized trait genetic architectures, including heritability estimates, genetic correlation estimates, and we calculated best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) and best linear unbiased estimators (BLUEs). We used BLUEs from each trait as phenotypes for genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) to identify candidate genes relevant for breeding and engineering. We assigned significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from GWAS to loblolly pine genes that were then mapped to Populus trichocarpa orthologs to enable multiplex network learning analyses. Multiplex Embedding of Networks for Team‐based Omics Research (MENTOR) and Random Walk with Restart‐Shortest Paths (RWR‐SP) were used to mechanistically contextualize and prioritize candidate genes. We performed a MeJA induction experiment on cambial tissues and secondary xylem to capture gene expression data from tissues forming new resin canals and terpenes to provide experimental support for our prioritized candidate genes. Materials and Methods

Populations

The Allele Discovery of Economic Pine Traits 2 (ADEPT2) association population consists of 425 unrelated wild, unimproved loblolly pine genotypes collected from counties across the natural species range (Cumbie et al., 2011). In 2010, rooted cuttings were established at a single common garden site in Gainesville, FL, USA (29.727°N/82.417°W). Between two and six ramets for each of 405 clones were randomized within six replicates in an α‐lattice incomplete block design (Patterson & Williams, 1976).

The Comparing Clonal Lines ON Experimental Sites (CCLONES) loblolly pine population was developed from a circular diallel mating design of 54 parents, consisting of 71 full‐sib families propagated as clones (Baltunis et al., 2007; Munoz et al., 2014). The CCLONES trial series contained six field sites with c. 1000 clones per site. Clonal assignments within replicates were randomized using an α‐lattice design with four replications per site, each with a single ramet per clone. Clones from c. 15 progeny per family were planted at each site. Oleoresin traits were measured at Nassau, FL and Palatka, FL sites (Westbrook et al., 2013, 2015).

Constitutive and induced oleoresin flow collection

In the ADEPT2 population, we simultaneously measured 24‐h constitutive oleoresin flow and 24‐h methyl jasmonate (MeJA)‐induced oleoresin flow on 405 genotypes (1396 individuals) during the eleventh (August 2020) and twelfth (June 2021) growing seasons (Supporting Information Table S1). We induced oleoresin flow by pipetting 0.50 ml of 400 mM MeJA in 95% EtOH into a 6.35 mm diameter × 3.8 cm‐deep borehole drilled at a downward 15‐degree angle using a portable drill and Drill Master angle drill guide 24 h before exposing xylem for constitutive and inducible oleoresin flow measurements. Approximately 24 h post induction, we used an arch punch to expose a circle of xylem measuring 1.27 cm in diameter (Strom et al., 2002; Roberds et al., 2003; Westbrook et al., 2013) immediately above the borehole wound (inducible) and another on the opposite side of the stem c. 180° from the borehole (constitutive). Immediately after xylem exposure, taps with 15 ml tubes were attached to the stem over the exposed xylem and oleoresin samples were collected after 24 h. Samples were freeze‐dried to remove rainwater contamination, which also removed the oleoresin monoterpene fraction (Westbrook et al., 2013), and their dry mass (dm) was averaged across years 2020 and 2021.

Constitutive wood mono‐ and diterpenoid collection and quantification

Two to six ramets of 405 genotypes (1414 individuals) from the ADEPT2 population were sampled for wood cores in July 2013 when trees were in their fourth growing season. Two 2 mm in diameter and 15 mm in length wood cores per tree were radially extracted from stems with Trephor microcorers (Rossi et al., 2006). Wood microcores were stored in microcentrifuge tubes on dry ice in the field to reduce volatilization of monoterpenes.

Monoterpenes were extracted by heating wood core samples at 80°C for 2 h in 1.5 ml borosilicate vials on dry heating blocks. Volatile monoterpenes were adsorbed onto 30 mg of porapak fiber within volatile collection traps (Analytical Research Systems, Gainesville, FL, USA). Volatiles were eluted with 1 ml of methylene chloride containing 3.375 × 10−3 g ml−1 of nonyl‐acetate as an internal standard. Elution vials were hermetically sealed and stored at −80°C before analysis. Wood core samples were then dried at 65°C for 48 h and weighed to the nearest 0.1 mg to normalize monoterpene concentrations to wood dry mass.

Monoterpenes were quantified with gas chromatography‐flame ionization detection on an Agilent 7890A gas chromatograph equipped with a DB‐5 column. Monoterpenes were identified in a subset of elutions using GC‐mass spectrometry by comparison with authentic standards (Sigma Aldrich) and the NIST 05 database. Response factors were estimated for 14 of 18 monoterpenes identified, and contents were calculated as mg per gram of wood dry mass (Methods [Link], [Link], Equation S1). Full instrument parameters and integration settings are provided in the Methods S1.

Wood diterpenoid content was measured from dried, ground wood core samples using pyrolysis molecular beam mass spectrometry (pyMBMS) (Davis et al., 1999). Diterpenoid ions were monitored at specific mass‐to‐charge ratios (Harman‐Ware et al., 2016), and total ion current was normalized across samples (Methods S1).

Quantitative genetic analysis of oleoresin traits

Variance components of constitutive oleoresin flow, induced oleoresin flow, α‐ and β‐pinene content, d‐limonene content, and total monoterpene and diterpenoid content were estimated using a univariate mixed model in ASReml‐R (Butler et al., 2023) with where genotype was fit as a random effect (Methods S2, Equation S2). Broad‐sense heritability (H 2) for each trait was estimated as the ratio of genotypic variance to phenotypic variance and tested with likelihood ratio tests comparing full models to null models with genotypic variance fixed at zero (Methods [Link], [Link], Equation S3). Genetic correlations (r g) between trait pairs were estimated from bivariate mixed models using a correlated heterogeneous covariance structure for all random effects and the residual (Methods S1, Equation S4).

High and low oleoresin‐yielding genotype selection

Best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) from Equation S2 were used to identify high and low oleoresin‐yielding genotypes based on oleoresin measurements taken in 2020 and 2021. We selected the five highest oleoresin yielding genotypes (n = 6 per clone) and three lowest yielding genotypes (n = 6 per clone) and collected 24‐h constitutive and induced oleoresin flow from these clones in June 2022 (n = 48). Oleoresin fresh mass (fm) was measured by collecting resin vials on ice to prevent monoterpene volatilization. Significant differences between high and low‐yielding genotypes were determined using Welch's two‐sample t‐test in R.

Genome‐wide association studies

ADEPT2 population

Best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) were calculated for each of the seven oleoresin‐related traits (constitutive oleoresin flow, induced oleoresin flow, α‐ and β‐pinene content, d‐limonene content, total monoterpenes, and total diterpenoids) using a univariate mixed model in ASReml‐R where genotype was fit as a fixed effect (Methods S1, Equation S5). We performed an F‐test for equality of variances (var.test in R) on the BLUEs for constitutive and induced oleoresin flow to compare phenotypic variability. BLUEs for all traits were used as phenotype responses for association tests with a single locus mixed linear model (MLM) (Yu et al., 2006) and multilocus MLMM (Segura et al., 2012), FarmCPU (Liu et al., 2016), and BLINK (Huang et al., 2019) models in the Gapit v.3 R package (Wang & Zhang, 2021).

Two different SNP sets were used for GWAS in the ADEPT2 population. In the first analysis, exome capture and sequencing on 375 loblolly pine genotypes was aligned to v.1.01 of the loblolly pine reference genome (Lu et al., 2016) yielding 2.8 M SNPs, which were filtered for quality and linkage disequilibrium to 473 666 high‐quality variants across 298 genotypes (Methods S1) using Vcftools (Danecek et al., 2011) and Plink v.1.9 (Purcell et al., 2007; Chang et al., 2015). BIC‐based model selection in Gapit v.3 (Model.selection = TRUE) determined the optimal number of PCs to use to account for population structure and a VanRaden kinship matrix was used to account for residual relatedness. In the second association study, whole‐genome resequencing from 10 loblolly pine individuals was used to design a customized 635 k SNP array aligned to genome v.2.01 (De La Torre et al., 2019), which was filtered to 49 526 high quality across 275 genotypes (Methods S1).

CCLONES population

Westbrook et al. (2013, 2015) previously reported heritabilities and significant SNPs for constitutive oleoresin flow, resin canal density, and resin canal number in the CCLONES population using 4854 SNPs. Here, we repeated GWAS on these traits using an expanded capture‐seq SNP set. A custom panel of 20 000 probes was designed and used to genotype 834 CCLONES clones at RAPiD Genomics (RAPiD Genomics©, Gainesville, FL, USA) (Methods S1). The resulting 223 717 SNPs were filtered to 13 883 high‐quality variants for association testing.

BLUEs for constitutive oleoresin flow (508 genotypes) and for resin canal number and density (370 genotypes) were calculated using multisite univariate mixed models in ASReml‐R that included fixed site and genotype effects and random replicate‐within‐site effects (Methods S1, Equations S6, S7). Association testing used MLM, MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK models in Gapit v.3 with a VanRaden kinship matrix.

Candidate genes associated with significant SNPs were identified using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of ≤0.2. Annotation files (gff and gtf) from loblolly pine genome v.1.01 and 2.01 were used to assign significant SNPs to genes, based on the specific genome version in which the SNP was identified. Given the highly fragmented nature of the loblolly pine genomes and their annotation files, we restricted gene assignments to cases where the SNPs were located within annotated gene regions (e.g. introns, exons) as specified by the annotation file. SNPs outside annotated gene regions were not assigned to the nearest gene due to the risk of inaccuracies arising from fragmented scaffolds, where the nearest gene might reside on a different scaffold altogether.

Multiplex network learning analyses

To prioritize candidate genes identified by GWAS, we leveraged multiplex network learning analyses using the well‐curated multiplex network available for Populus trichocarpa (Populus) (Cope et al., 2023). Though loblolly pine lacks a multiplex network, Populus provides a biologically relevant model as a well‐annotated woody perennial with extensive transcriptomic, regulatory, and interactome resources. Recent results show a substantial conservation of transcriptional programs, defense signaling pathways, and secondary metabolism with woody perennials, including conifers (Wilkinson et al., 2019), supporting our approach.

The Populus multiplex network integrates nine gene–gene network layers encompassing protein–protein interactions, transcription factor (TF) networks, co‐expression, metabolic pathways, and predictive expression networks (Zhang et al., 2010; Jin et al., 2015; Oellrich et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2018; Cliff et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2020) (Methods S1). To enable these analyses, we performed a Blastp search using Phytozome (Goodstein et al., 2012) to align protein sequences from loblolly pine genes containing significant SNPs to Populus proteins. In cases where multiple Populus proteins matched a single loblolly pine gene, the best hit was determined by selecting the match with the highest bit‐score. Only matches with an E‐value ≤ 0.02 were retained for downstream analyses using the Populus multiplex network (Table S2).

We used MENTOR (Sullivan et al., 2024) to mechanistically cluster the Populus orthologs mapping to loblolly pine candidate genes from GWAS. MENTOR uses an RWR algorithm on multiplex networks to quantify topological relationships among genes in a gene set, where the resulting clusters, or clades, represent groups of functionally related genes based on gene expression, protein–protein interactions, and metabolomic evidence. To explore the lines of evidence driving mechanistic connections within each MENTOR clade, we used RWR‐SP which calculates the pairwise shortest path within a gene set using the multiplex topology and returns the edges connecting pairs of genes and the specific network layers containing these edges (Kainer et al., 2025).

Gene expression

We performed an RNA‐sequencing experiment between control and MeJA‐induced 1‐yr‐old loblolly pine seedlings. Three (3) seedlings treated with 50 mM MeJA in 95% EtOH by painting 1 ml volume of solution on the bark, and three control seedlings were painted with 1 ml 95% EtOH only. After 7 d, the bark was peeled, and the surface of the woody stems were scraped with a single edge razor to collect the nonlignified VCZ tissue and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen (N2). After scraping, the woody stems were cut into 0.5‐mm‐thick cookies and frozen in liquid N2 as the woody secondary xylem tissue. All tissues were ground under liquid N2. Total RNA was extracted using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)‐based protocol (Chang et al., 1993), and barcoded Illumina TruSeq2 libraries were sequenced on one HiSeq‐2500 lane with paired‐end 100 base reads. Raw reads were quality‐assessed with FastQC (Andrews, 2010) and trimmed with Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014) and mapped to P. taeda v.1.01 and v.2.01 genome assemblies available on TreeGenes (Wegrzyn et al., 2008) with Salmon (Patro et al., 2017). Transcript‐level quantifications were imported into R using tximport (Soneson et al., 2015) and summarized to gene level when a transcript–gene map was available. Differential gene expression analyses were performed using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014). Full RNA extraction and library preparation details are provided in Methods S1.

Results

Quantitative genetics of oleoresin traits

Oleoresin traits exhibited moderate to strong genetic control. The average dry mass (g) of induced oleoresin flow was 4.2 times greater than constitutive flow in ADEPT2 (Fig. S1). Broad‐sense heritability estimates for constitutive and induced oleoresin flow were both 0.33 ± 0.03, indicating these traits are under moderate genetic control (Table 1). Constitutive and induced oleoresin flow had a significant strong positive genetic correlation of 0.78 ± 0.05 (Table 2), suggesting substantial overlap in their genetic architectures. Additionally, significant positive genetic correlations with diameter at breast height (DBH) were observed for both oleoresin flow traits, with correlation estimates of 0.37 ± 0.06 for constitutive flow and 0.44 ± 0.06 for induced flow (Table 2).

α‐Pinene and β‐pinene were the dominant monoterpenes out of the 18 monoterpenes identified in volatile collections from wood core samples, comprising 67.8% and 24.6% of total monoterpene volatiles, respectively (Fig. S2). Broad sense heritability estimates for these monoterpenes were 0.25 ± 0.03 for α‐pinene and 0.56 ± 0.03 for β‐pinene, with a significant negative genetic correlation (−0.24 ± 0.08) between them (Table 2). Additionally, α‐pinene content was negatively correlated with d‐limonene (−0.29 ± 0.08), whereas β‐pinene was positively correlated with d‐limonene (0.35 ± 0.05) (Table 2). d‐limonene itself, though much less abundant than α‐ and β‐pinene (Fig. S2), had the highest heritability (0.70 ± 0.02) among all traits.

For wood oleoresin content, total monoterpene had a heritability estimate of 0.23 ± 0.03, and total diterpenoid content a heritability of 0.17 ± 0.03 (Table 1), indicating low to modest genetic control.

Genome‐wide association studies

A total of 236 SNPs (FDR‐adjusted P‐value ≤ 0.2) were identified across all 10 traits in both populations, with 112 assigned to 101 pine genes (Table S2). In the ADEPT2 population, we identified 112 unique significant SNPs across all traits from the SNP set corresponding to the v.1.01 genome assembly using 473 666 SNPs and 66 unique significant SNPs across all traits from the SNP corresponding to the v.2.01 genome assembly using 49 526 SNPs (Table 4). We assigned significant SNPs to a total of 63 pine genes across all traits using both SNP sets in ADEPT2. Although induced oleoresin flow exhibited moderate heritability and a strong genetic correlation with constitutive flow, we did not detect SNPs significantly associated with this trait. Two SNPs (scaffold123756_7575 (v.2.01) and scaffold78800_31626 (v.2.01)) were significantly associated with both α‐pinene content and total monoterpene content, representing shared loci across monoterpene traits. However, these SNPs were not in the gene space within the annotation files and therefore not assigned to genes (see the Materials and Methods section). Of all the traits studied, we found that SNPs associated with d‐limonene and resin canal number had the largest effect sizes (Table 5).

Candidate gene prioritization using multiplex network learning analyses

From the 236 significant SNPs in our GWASs, we identified 101 pine protein sequences with orthology to Populus genes for use in multiplex learning analyses MENTOR and RWR‐SP. Ninety‐three (93) of these poplar protein sequences were found in the Populus multiplex network. Figure 3 provides a system‐level view of the mechanistic architecture underlying oleoresin‐related traits in loblolly pine. Using MENTOR, GWAS‐implicated genes (mapped to Populus orthologs) were hierarchically clustered based on multiplex network topology, revealing four mechanistically related clades with 34, 24, 11, and 26 pine genes, respectively. The accompanying heatmap shows binary associations between each Populus ortholog and the six phenotypes: d‐limonene, β‐pinene, α‐pinene, constitutive oleoresin flow, resin canal density, and resin canal number (Fig. 3). While only four genes were associated with more than one phenotype, the broader pattern of the heatmap demonstrated strong regulatory and mechanistic connectivity. Associations were not confined to isolated regions of the dendrogram; instead, each clade contained genes tied to multiple distinct traits. For instance, genes associated with resin canal traits and terpene composition frequently appear in the same clade, despite limited direct gene overlap. This suggests that distinct traits may be regulated by different components of shared biological modules, rather than by entirely separate gene networks. Taken together, Fig. 3 illustrates that oleoresin traits, although genetically complex and phenotypically diverse, are embedded within an interconnected regulatory landscape. The limited gene overlap underscores the importance of moving beyond one‐trait‐one‐gene interpretations. By leveraging multiplex network learning, MENTOR reveals how trait‐specific GWAS signals map onto overlapping functional contexts, providing insight into how complex defense phenotypes are integrated at the network level.

Gene expression in MeJA‐treated vascular cambium and secondary xylem

To help prioritize and experimentally support candidate genes from our GWAS, we conducted gene expression analyses with VCZ and secondary xylem tissues using bulk RNA sequencing. Secondary axial and radial resin canals form constitutively in a small subset of the ray cell files within the VCZ during normal growth (Larson, 1994; Zulak et al., 2009). We identified 34 genes whose mRNA abundance differed significantly between VCZ and secondary xylem tissue (P adj ≤ 0.1, log2FC ≥ |0.5|) that also contained SNPs significantly associated with oleoresin traits in our GWAS (Table 6). Eighteen (18) of these genes were constitutively more abundant in the VCZ, including NAM/ATAF/CUC (NAC), INDUCER OF CBF EXPRESSION 1 (ICE1), and MADS‐Box family TFs, genes encoding a coiled‐coil nucleotide‐binding site leucine‐rich repeat (NLR) protein, and a DEVELOPMENTALLY REGULATED PLASMA MEMBRANE POLYPEPTIDE (DREPP) (Table 6). Sixteen (16) genes were constitutively more abundant in the secondary xylem tissues, which are enriched in resin canals, including genes encoding putative 2 terpene synthases, 2 cytochrome P450s, a 5NG4‐like protein, and a cucumisin‐like serine protease (Table 6).

Discussion

Genetic architecture and breeding implications of oleoresin traits

Our findings demonstrate that oleoresin flow and composition are quantitative, polygenic traits with moderate to strong genetic control, reinforcing previous studies that identified heritability in constitutive oleoresin production (Squillace & Bengston, 1961; Roberds et al., 2003; Westbrook et al., 2013). The strong genetic correlation (0.78) between constitutive and induced oleoresin flow suggests that the same genes largely regulate both traits, supporting our original hypothesis that these defenses are not genetically uncoupled in pines. Although Lombardero et al. (2000) did not directly examine genetic architecture, their finding of a positive association between axial resin canal number and wound‐induced increases in oleoresin flow in loblolly pine suggests a functional linkage between constitutive and induced defenses. The genetic and functional linkage of these defenses is likely due to evolutionary pressures, which have shaped species‐level investment into constitutive and inducible defenses in Pinaceae. In pines, the persistent and strong selection pressure from bark beetles likely favors an integrated defense strategy with relatively high investment into constitutive oleoresin defenses and into rapidly inducible oleoresin defenses. Importantly, our evidence for the shared genetic basis means that selection enhancing constitutive defenses will also improve the inducible response, minimizing the cost of maintaining separate pathways.

Despite the strong genetic correlation between constitutive and induced oleoresin flow, we did not detect significant SNPs for MeJA‐induced oleoresin flow in our GWAS analyses of the ADEPT2 population. This is not uncommon for complex traits in plants, where GWAS frequently yield no significant associations due to polygenic architectures and the small effect sizes of individual loci (Clauw et al., 2025). Additionally, the 16 SNPs (two in genes) detected for constitutive oleoresin flow in the ADEPT2 population each had small effect sizes, and induced flow BLUEs exhibited substantially greater phenotypic variance than constitutive flow BLUEs F = 2.75, P < 2.2 × 10−16. If genetic effects on induced flow are comparable in magnitude, they would explain an even smaller proportion of the larger phenotypic variance, reducing the likelihood of reaching significance thresholds. Moreover, the genetic correlation estimated from our clonal population captures the total genetic covariance between traits, including nonadditive effects such as dominance and epistasis, whereas GWAS tests primarily for additive effects at individual loci. Thus, shared genetic architecture driven in part by nonadditive effects would not be detected by our GWAS, and the genetic correlation therefore captures the shared polygenic signal across the genome even when individual loci do not reach genome‐wide significance for one or both traits (Bulik‐Sullivan et al., 2015; Mackay & Anholt, 2024).

Our gene expression analyses provide independent functional evidence supporting this shared architecture. Fourteen (14) GWAS‐implicated genes were significantly MeJA‐responsive in the VCZ or secondary xylem, including three genes specifically associated with constitutive oleoresin flow (Table 6). This overlap indicates that many loci influencing constitutive oleoresin traits also participate in the induced defense program, reinforcing the genetic correlation results and supporting a shared, integrated genetic architecture for constitutive and inducible oleoresin defenses.

This shared genetic architecture is also reflected at the whole‐genotype level. High‐yielding genotypes selected by BLUPs produced an average of 14 times more constitutive oleoresin (dm) than low‐yielding genotypes and an average of 4.8 times more induced oleoresin (dm) than low‐yielding genotypes, consistent with the strong genetic correlation between these traits. The high‐yielding genotypes also exceeded population averages by 2.6‐fold for constitutive flow and 1.8‐fold for induced flow. Westbrook et al. (2013) measured both wet and dry mass oleoresin and found dry mass had slightly higher heritability and a near‐perfect additive genetic correlation (0.99) with wet mass. This work demonstrated that lyophilization did not alter the genetic ranking of clones, validating the use of dry mass in quantitative genetic analyses. Our findings confirm that BLUPs calculated from dm are comparable to fm measurements, supporting the validity of both methods and suggesting that breeding for high‐yielding trees can achieve significant gains.

Previous studies reported that constitutive oleoresin flow is heritable and positively correlated with growth in loblolly pine (Squillace & Bengston, 1961; Roberds et al., 2003; Romanelli & Sebbenn, 2004; Westbrook et al., 2013) and in masson pine (Liu et al., 2013; Huanhuan et al., 2018). We also demonstrate constitutive and induced oleoresin flow, and DBH are positively genetically correlated in a new population (ADEPT2) of loblolly pine representative of wild/unimproved individuals from across the species range. Oleoresin defenses are considered energetically costly to produce (Gershenzon, 1994) and tradeoffs between carbon allocation to growth and defense have been hypothesized to exist both within species and across the plant kingdom. Lorio & Sommers (1986) applied the growth‐differentiation balance hypothesis (GDBH) (Loomis, 1932) to southern pines to explain partitioning of photosynthates between radial growth of stems vs oleoresin synthesis and yield. Lorio (1986) also used the GDBH to explain increased southern pine beetle attacks in the spring due to a strong demand of photosynthates for growth‐related processes, primarily the formation of earlywood, whereas in the summer, southern pine beetle attacks decrease due to a shifted investment in defense‐related processes, such as resin canals and increased oleoresin flow. Similarly, Hood & Sala (2015) found that oleoresin flow in ponderosa pine was greatest later in the growing season and under moderate water stress and that while faster‐growing families produced more resin, slower growing trees invested more in resin canal defenses despite producing less resin overall. Westbrook et al. (2015) found resin canal density was negatively genetically correlated with xylem growth but that resin canal number, xylem growth, and oleoresin flow were all positively genetically correlated. Our results with the ADEPT2 population, representing genotypes from across the natural range of loblolly pine, corroborates the evidence from the genotypes in the CCLONES population that constitutive oleoresin flow is positively genetically correlated with growth, and we extend this finding to induced oleoresin flow, supporting that breeding for enhanced oleoresin production will not negatively impact tree growth and will instead improve synergistic traits, simultaneously enhancing wood volume, resistance to insects and pathogens, and biofuel potential.

d‐limonene stood out due to its high broad‐sense heritability (0.70) and potential role in defense. Previous terpene dose–response studies suggest that limonene is one of the most toxic monoterpenes to the most destructive North American bark beetle species, including mountain pine beetle, western pine beetle, spruce beetle, larch beetle, and southern pine beetle (Chiu et al., 2017); however, an alternative hypothesis proposes that successful colonization of host trees is influenced more by the diversity of monoterpenes rather than single‐compound toxicity (Burke & Carroll, 2016). Still, previous studies found limonene concentrations in loblolly pine oleoresin varied across the species range, with higher concentrations reported in the western population than in the eastern source (Coyne et al., 1972; Squillace & Wells, 1981), which may contribute to the increased resistance against insects and fusiform rust (Cronartium quercuum f.sp. fusiforme) observed in western loblolly pine (Schmidtling, 2006). Therefore, given its strong genetic control, enhancing limonene content is a valuable target for breeding programs aimed at enhancing insect resistance.

Mechanistic interpretation and prioritization of candidate pine genes

Breeding and engineering pine trees for elevated terpene content and oleoresin flow can improve resistance against herbivores, insects, and pathogens, while also increasing terpene availability for renewable chemicals and biofuels. Peter (2018) outlined three main strategies to accomplish this: increasing resin canal number, expanding resin canal storage capacity, and enhancing terpene biosynthesis pathways. Increasing the number and size of resin canals can be achieved by manipulating cell fate and differentiation of derivatives from the cambial meristem toward secondary resin canal formation (Peter, 2018), which form constitutively and are induced by insects, fungal pathogens, wounding, reactive oxygen, and plant hormones known to regulate plant defenses (Birchem & Brown, 1979; Franceschi et al., 2005; Zeneli et al., 2006). Increasing terpene accumulation can be achieved by increasing carbon flux to terpenes or altering rate‐limiting steps (Kirby & Keasling, 2009; Peter, 2018). However, the ability to prioritize genes from GWAS or transcriptional studies for functional validation remains a challenge, especially for long‐lived perennial species like loblolly pine with limited genomic resources and genetic engineering tools. Here we use our novel systems‐level MENTOR and RWR‐SP network learning tools to integrate our GWAS candidates with multi‐omic information from Populus. This integration provides a more robust framework for describing candidates than annotation alone by contextualizing them in functional networks and enabling discovery of highly connected genes not identified with GWAS (Kainer et al., 2025; Sullivan et al., 2024). While MENTOR and RWR‐SP provide a powerful framework for mechanistic gene clustering, their use in this study relies on orthologous mapping from loblolly pine to Populus due to the absence of a pine‐specific multiplex network. This inter species transfer introduces potential limitations, as regulatory contexts and network topologies may not be fully conserved. Additionally, multiplex layer composition, such as expression‐derived vs interaction‐derived networks, can bias clustering toward more densely connected data types. However, nearly all of plant biology relies on functional inference through orthology, largely from Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis), an annual, herbaceous dicot. Using Populus as a proxy represents a biologically reasonable compromise that prioritizes regulatory fidelity in a woody perennial species.

Terpene biosynthetic genes supported by GWAS and expression data contribute to natural variation in oleoresin composition

Of the pine genes identified in the GWAS (with 93 Populus orthologs), SNPs in 54 genes were associated with monoterpene traits. Genes in the pink clade were almost exclusively associated with monoterpene traits, and the mechanistic connectivity of this clade was driven by experimental xylem transcriptomic data from Populus (Fig. 4), consistent with roles in stem wood biology.

Terpenes are chemically diverse compounds synthesized through highly conserved cytosolic mevalonic acid and plastid 2C‐methyl‐d‐erythritol‐4‐phosphate (MEP) localized biosynthetic pathways (Chappell, 1995), with plants containing large terpene synthase gene families that produce multiple products from shared substrates (Karunanithi & Zerbe, 2019). Three pine genes (PITA_000080390, PITAhm_000547, and PITA_000000406) annotated as terpene synthases carried multiple significant SNPs identified by three different GWAS models (Table S2; Fig. 5). PITAhm_000547 and PITA_000000406 are annotated as putative (‐)‐α‐pinene synthases and PITA_000080390 as a predicted α‐terpineol synthase. Our gene expression results support a role for PITAhm_000547 and PITA_000080390 in wood terpene biosynthesis. PITAhm_000547 mRNA was more abundant in secondary xylem than in the VCZ both constitutively and after MeJA induction and was also strongly upregulated (4.23‐fold) by MeJA in VCZ tissue during new resin canal formation (Table 6). PITA_000080390 showed the same pattern, being significantly enriched in secondary xylem constitutively, and after MeJA induction, significantly upregulated (6.73‐fold) in the VCZ (Table 6). These results suggest both genes are MeJA‐responsive and contribute to the induced terpene production that accompanies traumatic resin canal development.

The idea that natural variation in terpene synthase genes affects terpene output is also supported across species. A GWAS study of leaf terpene content and composition in Eucalyptus polybractea also identified significant SNPs in several monoterpene synthase genes (Kainer et al., 2019), indicating terpene synthases can be genetic determinants of terpene chemotypes. Likewise, synthetic biology and modeling approaches in cyanobacteria, which also synthesize monoterpenes via the conserved MEP pathway, show that terpene synthase overexpression increases limonene accumulation (Wang et al., 2016), demonstrating that monoterpene synthase expression can be rate limiting for terpene production. Together, these independent lines of evidence reinforce our interpretation that genetic and transcriptional variation in monoterpene synthases can affect terpene accumulation in loblolly pine wood, strongly supporting PITAhm_000547 and PITA_000080390 for functional validation.

Conifers biosynthesize diverse diterpenoids involving large cytochrome P450s gene families (Zhang et al., 2023). Currently, one loblolly pine cytochrome P450 involved in diterpene resin acid biosynthesis has been functionally characterized (Ro et al., 2005). Our GWAS identified additional P450s that are consistent with roles in terpene metabolism and supported by our gene expression analysis. SNPs in two genes with sequence similarity to cytochrome P450s (PITA_000090431; PITA_000074632) in the orange clade were associated with d‐limonene content, and the pink clade contained SNPs in two putative cytochrome P450s (PITA_000010362 and PITA_000088453) annotated in the plant‐specific CYP71 clan (Hamberger & Bak, 2013) (Fig. 5). While pinenes and d‐limonene are not oxygenated, CYP71A and D clade members are involved in biosynthesis of other oxygenated monoterpeniods and diterpenoids (Lanier et al., 2023). PITA_000010362 was significantly more abundant in secondary xylem than in the VCZ under constitutive and MeJA‐induced conditions (Table 6), consistent with enrichment in resin canal‐producing tissues, although it was not significantly induced by MeJA in secondary xylem or the VCZ. By contrast, PITA_000074632 was significantly upregulated (10.48‐fold) by MeJA in the VCZ and in secondary xylem (2.01‐fold) and more abundant in secondary xylem than in the VCZ under constitutive and MeJA‐induced conditions (Table 6). These patterns and similarity to a CYP71‐like protein from Picea sitchensis suggest PITA_000074632 as our strongest candidate for functional characterization in diterpenoid metabolism.

Prenyltransferases condense isopentyl pyrophosphate into substrates for terpene synthases. We identified a significant SNP in a gene (PITA_000006889) encoding a putative geranylgeranyl transferase that was significantly upregulated (3.05‐fold) after MeJA induction in the VCZ. Finally, α‐pinene was significantly associated (using BLINK, FarmCPU, and MLMM) with a SNP in PITA_000090288, a gene of unknown function. The Populus ortholog (Potri.016G135400) encodes a bifunctional inhibitor/lipid‐transfer protein/seed storage 2s albumin‐like protein, and a gene with this same annotation was reported in E. polybractea (Kainer et al., 2019), suggesting that this protein may be involved in terpene transport (Arondell et al., 2000). The convergence of GWAS signals in this study and across species as well as tissue‐specific expression and MeJA‐responsiveness of our GWAS‐identified genes provides support for these loci in shaping terpene composition and oleoresin‐based defenses in loblolly pine.

Pathogen perception and immune signaling genes form a coordinated defense module that initiates oleoresin defense responses

In pine stems, resin canal formation, terpene synthesis and oleoresin flow are induced by insects, fungal pathogens, wounding, reactive oxygen, and plant hormones known to regulate plant defenses (Hepting, 1947; Birchem & Brown, 1979; Franceschi et al., 2005; Zeneli et al., 2006). These responses rely on early recognition of biotic stress, and our GWAS identified several pine genes whose orthologs in other species participate in pathogen perception or immune signaling (Fig. 5). Our gene expression analyses further support some of these candidates, suggesting that variation in immune signaling contributes directly to chemical and anatomical defenses in loblolly pine.

For example, α‐ and β‐pinene were each significantly associated with SNPs in PITA_000019397 and PITA_000069356, respectively, which encode cucumisin‐like serine proteases. Members of this family are involved in plant defense in other species (Figueiredo et al., 2018) (Fig. 5), including plant detection of microbe‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) and activation of pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) (Matsui et al., 2024). PITA_000019397 was significantly more abundant in the secondary xylem than in the VCZ under constitutive and MeJA‐induced conditions (Table 3), consistent with activity in resin canal‐enriched tissues. Independent GWAS studies in pines have repeatedly implicated subtilisin‐like proteases in oleoresin traits, including associations with constitutive oleoresin flow in loblolly pine (Westbrook et al., 2013) and in masson pine (Liu et al., 2020). This convergence across pine studies reinforces the idea that allelic variation in protease‐mediated MAMP perception likely affects terpene‐based defenses.

β‐pinene was also associated with an SNP in PITA_000036189, a gene with sequence similarity to plasma‐membrane RING‐H2‐type E3 ubiquitin ligases. Members of this family are broadly involved in immune response, including resistance to fungal pathogens (Berrocal‐Lobo et al., 2010; Mukoko Bopopi et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2024) and are induced by MAMP (flg22) and pathogen‐associated molecular pattern (chitin) treatment (Maekawa et al., 2012) and influenced by JA signaling (Hondo et al., 2007) (Fig. 5). GWASs in E. polybractea and E. grandis also identified associations between monoterpene traits and RING/E3 ligases (Kainer et al., 2019; Mhoswa et al., 2022), proposing these genes may regulate secretory cavity formation by initiating the formation of schizogenous intercellular spaces, which requires a RING/E3 ligase in liverworts (Ishizaki, 2015). This cross‐species convergence suggests ATL/RING E3‐mediated pathogen responses may be a conserved interface between immune signaling and secretory‐tissue development, and that allelic variation at these loci may affect downstream signaling that regulates terpene accumulation and resin canal formation.

Resin canal number was also significantly associated with an SNP in PITA_000027372, which encodes a transmembrane protein whose orthologs in Arabidopsis function as drug/metabolite exporters involved in secondary cell wall (SCW) deposition and vascular pathogen susceptibility (Ranocha et al., 2010; Denancé et al., 2013). PITA_000027372 is annotated putatively as 5NG4 (v.1.01), which is a characterized auxin‐inducible transporter in loblolly pine (Busov et al., 2004) and was among the most highly upregulated genes in pitch canker‐susceptible vs resistant Pinus radiata genotypes (Carrasco et al., 2017), further linking this gene to hormone‐ and pathogen‐responsive pathways. In our study, PITA_000027372 was significantly more abundant in the stem than in the VCZ both constitutively and after MeJA induction and significantly upregulated (3.63‐fold) in the VCZ after MeJA treatment (Table 6). Together, these observations suggest that PITA_000027372 sits at the interface of hormone signaling, pathogen response, and vascular wall‐associated processes and that natural variation at this locus may contribute to differences in resin canal number and associated defense capacity.

A major class of resistance (R) genes in plants encode coiled‐coil NLR proteins, which are intracellular receptors that primarily detect pathogens and rapidly activate effector‐triggered immunity responses (Jones & Dangl, 2006). In loblolly pine, NLR genes are hypothesized to confer resistance to the most economically significant fungal pathogen in planted pines, Cronartium quercuum f. sp. fusiforme (Lauer & Isik, 2021; Ence et al., 2022). Two GWAS models identified an SNP in a putative NLR gene (PITA_000085944) associated with resin canal density, and we also detected a significant association between constitutive oleoresin flow and a gene with sequence similarity to a leucine‐rich repeat‐receptor‐like kinase (PITAhm_002715), a class of pattern‐recognition receptors central to pathogen perception and downstream immune signaling. Consistent with our findings, Westbrook et al. (2013) found oleoresin flow in loblolly pine was significantly associated with numerous genes encoding cell surface or membrane‐bound proteins, including genes encoding putative NLR proteins. In masson pine, Liu et al. (2020) also found seven NLR genes associated with oleoresin yield, and Kainer et al. (2019) and Mhoswa et al. (2022) found multiple significant associations between monoterpene traits in leaves and NLR genes in E. polybractea and E. grandis, respectively. These associations suggest variation in genes involved in pathogen perception and immune signaling influence not only acute response but also downstream signaling pathways that affect monoterpenes and developmental traits involved in oleoresin production.

Cambial reprogramming genes suggest a shift from tracheid development toward resin canal formation during defense activation

MYB TFs have been previously implicated in terpene biosynthesis and oleoresin‐related traits in conifers. For example, overexpression of PtMYB14 from loblolly pine increases terpene accumulation in spruce seedlings (Bedon et al., 2010), and a slash pine MYB binds the promoter of a 1‐deoxy‐d‐xylulose 5‐phosphate synthase gene (Yi et al., 2022), which catalyzes the first committed step of the MEP pathway. Consistent with this evidence, our GWAS and expression results identified coordinated changes in developmental regulators within the blue MENTOR clade, which contains 7 TFs, 4 of which show sequence similarity to MYB TFs. The RWR‐SP analysis indicated that the mechanistic connectivity within this clade was driven by the Populus‐specific regulatory network (Fig. 4). While direct roles in cambial reprogramming remain unknown, the co‐occurrence of MYB‐like regulators with defense‐activation genes in the same clade is consistent with an integrated module in which immune perception and JA signaling are coupled to shifts in cambial derivative identity (Fig. 5). In conifers, axial resin canal formation and tracheid differentiation originate from fusiform derivatives but diverge transcriptionally depending on developmental or defense cues. MeJA treatment suppresses tracheid formation and promotes traumatic resin canal initiation, indicating a reprogramming of cambial cell fate (Martin et al., 2002; Hudgins et al., 2004; Peter, 2018). This is consistent with known roles of conifer MYB TFs that regulate phenylpropanoid metabolism and wood formation under normal growth conditions (Patzlaff et al., 2003; Bomal et al., 2008) that are transcriptionally reprogrammed upon defense activation to repress genes involved in tracheid cell fate and activate genes promoting resin canal formation and terpene accumulation (Zulak & Bohlmann, 2010; Chano et al., 2017). Our results suggest that natural variation in this regulatory module may contribute to differences in how the cambium alters tracheid‐associated developmental programs and initiates resin canal development during defense activation.

TFs associated with cambial activity and early cell fate decisions

Several TFs associated with oleoresin traits have known roles in meristem activity or boundary formation in other species. For example, d‐limonene content was significantly associated with an SNP in PITA_000012631 which encodes an MYB TF with orthology to Arabidopsis MYB84/RAX3 (AT3G49690), a regulator of axillary meristem formation (Keller et al., 2006; Müller et al., 2006) (Fig. 5). Although the specific developmental role of this locus in pine is unknown, its association with a highly heritable terpene trait suggests allelic variation in meristematic regulators may influence resin canal initiation or the number of fusiform derivatives forming resin canals. Similarly, resin canal density was significantly associated with an SNP in a LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARY (LOB/ASL) domain TF (PITA_000031285), whose orthologs are involved in secondary growth and differentiation of cambium cells and cell proliferation (Rong et al., 2024) (Fig. 5). Their putative function in loblolly pine may similarly involve regulating domains of cambial cell proliferation or setting conditions that favor resin canal formation. We also identified an MYC‐like basic helix loop helix (bHLH) TF (PITA_000054072) associated with resin canal density that was significantly more abundant in the VCZ than in secondary xylem under constitutive conditions and significantly downregulated (2.13‐fold) after MeJA induction in the VCZ (Table 6), consistent with a role in MeJA‐responsive stage cell fate decisions in the VCZ (Fig. 5). The Arabidopsis ortholog (AT3G26744) is a transcriptional activator ICE1 that binds to the master regulator of stomatal differentiation and modulated by JAZ repressors in JA signaling (Li et al., 2025), reinforcing its potential role at the intersection of JA signaling and cambial development.

Shifts in tracheid‐associated SCW programs after MeJA induction

Our GWAS and gene expression results implicate genes putatively involved in (SCW) associated and phenylpropanoid pathways that normally support tracheid development. α‐Pinene was associated with a SNP within gene encoding for an NAC TF (PITA_000078572) that was significantly more abundant in the VCZ than in secondary xylem and significantly downregulated (1.51‐fold) by MeJA in the VCZ (Table 6). Its orthologs in Populus (Potri.006G152700/ PtrNAC151) and Arabidopsis (AT4G29230/NAC075/SND4) are conserved regulators of SCW thickening in xylem (Zhong et al., 2011, 2021), supporting a role for PITA_000078572 in cambial programs that normally promote tracheid SCW formation but are repressed during JA‐induced reprogramming toward resin canal development (Fig. 5). Additionally, α‐pinene was significantly associated with MYB TF (PITA_000090534) whose orthologs in Populus (Potri.009G053900/ PtrMYB021) and Arabidopsis (AT5G12870/ MYB46) function downstream of NAC master regulators to activate xylan and SCW biosynthetic genes (Chen et al., 2019; Nookaraju et al., 2022). MYB46 also influences pathogen susceptibility through transcriptional reprogramming of genes involved in cell wall modifications (Ramírez et al., 2011), highlighting a connection between defense signaling and SCW development.

Additional significant SNPs include an MYB TF (PITA_000087773) associated with constitutive oleoresin flow, whose Arabidopsis ortholog MYB3 (AT1G22640) is a repressor of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Zhou et al., 2017). An SNP in another MYB TF (PITA_000068693) associated with β‐pinene content is orthologous to Arabidopsis MYB15 (AT3G23250), which is induced by effector‐triggered immunity and activates lignification and the antimicrobial coumarin scopoletin biosynthesis during immune responses (Chezem et al., 2017; Hamsa et al., 2024). Similarly, α‐pinene was significantly associated with an SNP in an O‐methyltransferase (OMT, PITA_000040466) whose orthologs in Populus (Potri.001G304800) and Arabidopsis (AT4G34050) encode CAFFEOYL‐CoA O‐METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 (CCoAOMT1), a branch‐point enzyme for both G‐lignin and scopoletin biosynthesis. In loblolly pine, PtMYB4 is an inducer of lignification‐related genes and directly activates CCoAOMT1 (Patzlaff et al., 2003; Yadav et al., 2020). A Blastp of PtMYB4 against the loblolly pine assemblies showed 95% identity to PITAhm_000393 (v.1.01) and 99.25% identity to PITA_05699 (v.2.01), which were both significantly downregulated (PITAhm_000393: 1.53‐fold; PITA_05699 = 1.47‐fold) after MeJA induction in the VCZ (Table 6). These patterns indicate that multiple components of phenylpropanoid and SCW‐associated regulation are transcriptionally modulated during MeJA treatment, and our network analyses further support mechanistic connectivity among these loci. Our RWR‐SP results show that Potri.005G224100 (Populus ortholog of PITA_000068693, MYB) is directly connected in the regulatory layer to Potri.001G304800 (the ortholog of PITA_000040466, CCoAOMT1) and to Potri.007G007900 (the ortholog of PITA_000012631, a putative meristematic regulator). These regulatory connections among Populus orthologs of our GWAS hits combined with the MeJA‐responsive expression of the corresponding pine genes suggest a coherent module in which variation in SCW‐ and phenylpropanoid‐related regulators is linked to both defense signaling and cambial developmental programs (Fig. 5).

Primary wall modification pathways are also implicated in cambial reprogramming

Our gene expression results also point to changes in primary cell wall remodeling. The most strongly MeJA‐downregulated (1858.60‐fold) transcript in the VCZ (Table 6) encodes a xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase (XTH) (2A_I15_VO_L_1_T_124932/133144 (v.1.01)). XTHs are expressed in the developing wood of poplars and are involved in vessel development (Nishikubo et al., 2011). Notably, Westbrook et al. (2015) identified an SNP in an XTH family gene in an independent GWAS of oleoresin flow in the CCLONES population. Together, this suggests that XTHs are MeJA‐responsive and that variation in XTHs may influence oleoresin‐related traits (Fig. 5). We also identified d‐limonene‐associated variation in a pectin carboxylesterase (PCE) family protein (PITA_000000467), whose Arabidopsis ortholog PMR5 (AT5G58600) regulates pectin modification and cell wall acetylation, resulting in enhanced resistance to fungal pathogens (Vogel et al., 2004; Chiniquy et al., 2019). This association suggests that allelic variation affecting primary wall modification may contribute to natural variation in limonene content and links pectin‐associated cell wall properties to chemical defense. Finally, D‐limonene was significantly associated with a gene encoding a DREPP (PITA_000003031), whose Arabidopsis ortholog encodes MICROTUBULE‐DESTABILIZING PROTEIN 25 (MDP25). MDP25 destabilizes microtubules and cleaves actin filaments, regulating numerous aspects of plant development, including GLABRA3‐mediated trichome branching (Xie et al., 2024). PITA_00003031 was significantly more abundant in the VCZ compared with secondary xylem (Table 6), consistent with activity in tissues where resin canal initiation occurs. Additionally, PITA_00003031 was significantly downregulated (2.58‐fold) in the VCZ after MeJA induction (Table 6), indicating that MeJA also alters the expression of cytoskeleton‐associated pathways during defense activation.

Conclusions

Our results support a shared genetic architecture between constitutive and inducible oleoresin defenses, indicating that these complex traits function as coordinated components of a unified defense system rather than independent pathways. We also illustrate a clear positive synergy between growth traits and oleoresin flow in loblolly pine, demonstrating that breeding programs can simultaneously enhance constitutive and inducible oleoresin flow and growth performance for resistance and biofuel potential. Our integrative analyses organize GWAS‐implicated genes into groups with shared regulatory or functional context, including genes involved in terpene biosynthesis, pathogen perception and immune signaling, and JA‐responsive developmental rebalancing toward resin canal formation, highlighting coordinated patterns of variation that influence oleoresin traits. We also provide experimental support for 14 GWAS‐implicated genes that were significantly MeJA‐responsive in the VCZ or secondary xylem as independent evidence that these candidates participate in JA‐associated defense responses in tissues where resin canals initiate and terpene production occurs.

This work moves beyond traditional GWAS approaches that often yield lists of candidate loci by applying a systems biology framework based on multiplex network learning. By mapping loblolly pine genes to orthologs in the well‐annotated Populus multiplex network, MENTOR and RWR‐SP provided enriched biological context for interpreting GWAS hits and revealed how trait‐associated genes cluster into mechanistically coherent modules. This approach enabled systematic prioritization of 236 significant SNPs and their associated candidate genes for our gene expression analyses. Overall, our study establishes a network‐informed roadmap for dissecting complex defense traits in a non‐model tree species and advances efforts toward improving loblolly pine productivity, immunity, and biofuel potential by integrating quantitative genetics, GWAS, multiplex network learning, and gene expression to elucidate the genetic architectures underlying oleoresin defenses and growth and to identify high‐confidence targets for breeding and genetic engineering.
