Use of Menstruation Tracking Apps Among Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Multi-Country Evidence
Authors: Ushakuma Michael Anenga, Jerry Kunle Isogun, Yakaka Mustapha Tatabe, Sesugh Matthew Iorfa, Yusuf Alfa, Juliet Adamezie Nkemdeme, Odigonma Zinobia Ikpeze, Oghenetega Jesse Agbagoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Menstruation-tracking applications have become common digital tools for cycle monitoring, symptom tracking, fertility-related decisions, and reproductive self-awareness. Despite their growing use, evidence on their uptake and real-world use across settings has remained fragmented. This study aimed to systematically review and synthesise evidence on the use of menstruation-tracking apps among women, including uptake, reasons for use, benefits, determinants, concerns about accuracy, and privacy.Methodology:A systematic review and meta-analysis of published studies was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Eligible studies involved adolescent girls or women and reported use of menstruation or fertility-tracking apps. Data were extracted independently. All included studies were synthesised narratively, while a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions was performed for six studies reporting extractable prevalence data. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2. Risk of bias was appraised with design-appropriate tools, and certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE and GRADE-CERQual.Results:Fourteen studies published between 2017 and 2025, involving 3,547 participants, were included, with six contributing to the meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of menstruation-tracking app use was 61% (95% CI: 45%–75%), although substantial heterogeneity (I2= 98.1%) limited the precision of this estimate. Sensitivity analyses showed prevalence estimates of 65% for cross-sectional studies and 43% after excluding high-risk-of-bias studies. Menstruation-tracking apps were primarily used for cycle monitoring, pregnancy planning or avoidance, symptom tracking, and reproductive health information. While users generally perceived these apps as useful, concerns remained about predictive accuracy, privacy, data security, and emotional distress from inaccurate predictions.Conclusion:Menstruation-tracking app use appears common in the studied populations, but available estimates vary widely, and the certainty of evidence is very low. These apps may support self-monitoring and reproductive awareness, but they should be used as adjunctive self-management tools rather than stand-alone clinical tools.

Introduction

Menstrual health is now widely recognised as a public health and human rights issue. The World Health Organization states that it should be understood beyond hygiene alone and should include the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of the menstrual experience, as well as access to accurate information, appropriate products, competent care, and supportive environments free from stigma [1,2].

To address these needs, digital tools for menstrual self-monitoring have expanded rapidly. Menstruation-tracking apps allow women to record cycle dates, bleeding patterns, symptoms, mood changes, and sometimes fertility-related signs such as basal body temperature or cervical secretions. Using these entries, the apps generate predictions for the next menstrual period, ovulation, and fertile window. Recent literature shows that the market includes more than 100 such apps, with major platforms such as Flo, Clue, and Period Tracker accounting for a large share of downloads globally. Women use these apps for cycle monitoring, symptom tracking, trying to conceive, avoiding pregnancy, and general reproductive self-awareness [3-6].

With their increasing popularity, menstrual and fertility tracking apps have attracted growing research attention. Much of the existing literature, however, has focused on app content, app quality, methodological concerns, and qualitative user experiences. Existing reviews are mainly scoping or descriptive reviews; to our knowledge, no prior meta-analysis has quantified the prevalence of menstruation-tracking app use across countries and study populations [3-5]. Primary studies also differ by age group, country, sampling frame, study design, and definition of use, making direct comparison difficult [6-9].

Against this background, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the prevalence of menstruation-tracking app use where quantitative data allowed pooling, describe reasons for use, examine determinants and user experiences, and identify evidence gaps. Because the available literature spans diverse settings and study designs, the pooled estimate was interpreted alongside narrative findings, study-level prevalence ranges, and certainty-of-evidence assessments.

Methods

Study design and protocol registration

This study was conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis of published studies on the use of menstruation tracking apps among women. The review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [10]. The protocol was registered retrospectively on PROSPERO under registration number CRD420261366713. The prospective registration is acknowledged as a methodological limitation because protocol availability after the start of the review may reduce external confidence in pre-specified decisions.

Eligibility criteria

Primary empirical studies were eligible if they involved adolescent girls or women, addressed the use of menstruation or fertility tracking apps, and reported outcomes such as uptake, awareness, reasons for use, user experience, determinants, perceived benefits, barriers, privacy concerns, or accuracy. App-only content analyses without a primary user sample, commentaries, systematic reviews, and supplementary appendices were excluded. Heterogeneous designs were included because the review question covered prevalence, determinants, user experience, accuracy concerns, and privacy issues; quantitative data were pooled only when studies reported extractable numerators and denominators for app use, while qualitative and mixed-methods findings were synthesised narratively.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive and reproducible search strategy was developed and implemented in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Consensus AI, an artificial intelligence-powered research tool, was used only as a supplementary citation-discovery aid and did not replace database searching, manual screening, or reviewer judgement [11]. Any potentially relevant records identified through this route were subjected to the same eligibility criteria as database records. All sources were last searched on 20 March 2026. The search strategy incorporated both individual and combined keywords related to menstruation and digital health technologies, including menstruation, menstrual cycle, period tracking, fertility awareness, ovulation tracking, mobile applications, smartphones, mHealth, digital health, health apps, and women. Boolean operators such as AND and OR were used, the syntax was adapted for each database, and reference lists of included studies were screened manually for additional eligible articles.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers against the predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant reports were then assessed independently by the same reviewers. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers into a structured workbook covering study characteristics, population, sample size, app focus, prevalence data, determinants, benefits, barriers, privacy issues, and user experience. A risk-of-bias assessment was conducted using JBI for cross-sectional studies, CASP for qualitative studies, MMAT for mixed-methods studies, and an adapted appraisal approach for the app-evaluation study.

Data synthesis

All included studies contributed to narrative synthesis, organised by app uptake, reasons for use, determinants, perceived usefulness, accuracy concerns, privacy, and user experience. A random-effects meta-analysis of proportions was performed only for studies reporting extractable prevalence data, defined as a numerator of app users and a denominator for the eligible study population. The pooled prevalence estimate was calculated using an inverse-variance random-effects model on logit-transformed proportions and was back-transformed for interpretation. No zero-event studies were included, and a continuity correction was therefore not required. Heterogeneity was summarized using I2and the prediction interval. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by study design and by excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias. Subgroup meta-analysis or meta-regression was not performed because only six studies contributed to the pooled estimate, making such analyses unstable. All statistical analyses were performed using MetaAnalysisOnline.com, an open-source web platform for conducting meta-analyses using standard inverse variance methods [12].

Reporting bias assessment and certainty of evidence

Reporting bias was assessed visually with funnel plots and statistically with Egger’s test for the prevalence outcome. Because fewer than 10 studies contributed to the meta-analysis, the assessment was considered underpowered and was not regarded as reliable evidence for or against small-study effects. Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE based on study limitations, consistency, precision, directness, and possible reporting bias. Qualitative confidence was assessed using GRADE-CERQual.

Results

Study Selection

The study selection process is summarised in Figure 1. Fourteen studies published between 2017 and 2025 were included for synthesis and contributed data from 3,547 participants. The studies represented 10 country or region categories, including Indonesia, Nigeria, Poland, Jordan, Ireland, the United Kingdom, the United States, multinational samples, and European cross-country samples. Cross-sectional or survey designs were the most common (6 studies), followed by qualitative studies (4), mixed-methods studies (3), and one app-evaluation study with user data. Several studies were conducted among students, app users, or high-income-country samples, which limited generalisability to the wider population of women and girls. Most studies focused on menstrual or period-tracking applications, while a smaller group examined fertility-tracking technologies or app design and governance issues (Table 1).

Meta-analysis of prevalence

Six studies reported extractable prevalence data and were included in the quantitative synthesis (Table 2; Figure 2). Study-level prevalence ranged from 37% in Gambier-Ross et al. to 85% in Nwafor et al., showing wide variation before pooling. Using a random-effects model on logit-transformed proportions, the pooled prevalence of menstruation tracking app use was 61% (95% CI 45% to 75%), with extreme heterogeneity (I2= 98.1%) and a wide prediction interval (11% to 98%). Therefore, the range of study estimates and the sources of heterogeneity are more informative than the pooled central estimate alone. In sensitivity analysis restricted to cross-sectional studies, the pooled prevalence increased to 65% (95% CI 48% to 80%), while it decreased to 43% (95% CI 33% to 53%; I2= 91.9%) after excluding high-risk-of-bias studies.

Reasons for app use and perceived usefulness

In the UK survey by Broad et al., 29.7% of respondents reported that the best thing about using a period tracker was knowing when they were ovulating [7]. In the mixed-methods study by Gambier-Ross et al., the main motivations for fertility-tracking app use were cycle observation (72%), attempts to conceive (34%), informing fertility treatment (12%), and contraception (4%) [13]. In the U.S. survey of menstrual cycle tracking technologies, avoidance of pregnancy was the leading reason for use, reported by 72.8% of respondents, while 73.9% stated that these technologies contributed to their reproductive health knowledge [14]. In the Nigerian undergraduate study, 85.0% used Flo to obtain sexual and reproductive health information, 56.0% used it for combined menstruation, ovulation, and pregnancy information, and 73.0% considered it useful because it provided accurate personalised information, privacy, personal notes, and ease of use [15]. In Indonesia, 78.9% of respondents reported monitoring their cycles, 65.0% used a menstrual tracking application, 49.8% had used one for at least one year, 66.5% identified Flo as the app they used, and 63.9% reported being quite satisfied with app use [16]. These findings were consistent with the work by Epstein et al., which showed that women used tracking tools to remember and predict their periods, better understand their bodies, and support discussions with healthcare providers [17].

Determinants and patterns of use

In the U.S. pilot study by Adnan et al., 39.0% of participants were app users, 24.0% used other tracking methods, and 37.0% did not track their cycles at all [18]. Compared with non-trackers, app users had lower reported ever-use of hormonal contraception (71.8% vs 90.0%, P = 0.001) and lower lifetime smoking prevalence (6.0% vs 17.0%, P = 0.04) [18]. In the study by Gambier-Ross et al., women who used other health apps were significantly more likely to use fertility-tracking apps (P = 0.001), whereas contraceptive users were less likely to use them (P = 0.002), and app use decreased significantly with age (P = 0.001) [13]. The Polish study also showed that app use was common among young women, with 77.6% reporting use of menstrual tracking applications, 68.2% using Flo, and 52.8% learning about such apps from the internet [19]. Qualitative studies added that continued use was influenced by changing reproductive goals, bodily awareness, self-management needs, and the extent to which the app matched users’ cycle patterns and lived experiences [20,21].

Accuracy and emotional consequences

In the survey by Broad et al., 54.9% of respondents reported that menstruation had started earlier than predicted, while 72.1% reported that it had started later than predicted [7]. The qualitative findings from the same study showed that inaccurate predictions were associated with frustration, anxiety, stress, confusion, disappointment, and fear of pregnancy, and in some cases led users to seek advice from healthcare professionals [7]. Gambier-Ross et al. likewise found that inaccuracy, especially among women with irregular cycles, was one of the main reasons apps were considered not useful, and some users described them as upsetting and anxiety-provoking [13]. In the Jordan app-evaluation study, user testing of the top five suggested that the better-rated apps were easy to use and could improve knowledge and awareness, even though the underlying evidence base was often weak [4]. By contrast, the Indonesian student survey found that 86.1% rated app accuracy as sufficient [16], while the Polish study reported that 83.3% believed these apps monitored the menstrual cycle well [19].

Privacy, data governance, and user experience

Broad et al. reported that 83.0% of respondents stated that they had no privacy concerns regarding the data entered into their period tracker, suggesting that concern was not universal [7]. However, Mohan and Jenkins found that users preferred apps with better prediction accuracy and user interfaces, but also wanted ownership over their data, clearer privacy statements, and stronger data protection measures [22]. Levy and Romo-Avilés showed that tracking could empower users by increasing awareness and knowledge, but could also lead to distress, privacy concerns, and cessation because of the work involved [20]. Ford et al. and Lutz et al similarly identified attention to privacy and profit concerns in data-driven app environments [21,23]. In the Irish qualitative study, users reported over-reliance on apps, a desire for improvements, and a tendency to view their own bodies as faulty rather than question app accuracy; they also wanted more inclusive designs, the option to avoid fertility- or pregnancy-focused content, and apps that did not sell user data [24].

Publication Bias

Risk-of-bias appraisal suggested that most included studies were at moderate risk, while four were judged high risk (Figure 4; Supplementary materials). The most frequent limitations were convenience sampling, restricted study populations such as students or current app users, reliance on self-report, and limited information on nonresponders or confounding. For the proportion of menstruation app use, the funnel plot and Egger’s test were not reliable because only six studies contributed to the analysis (Figure 3). These findings were therefore used descriptively and not as definitive evidence of the presence or absence of small-study effects.

The GRADE certainty of evidence was very low because of study limitations, extreme inconsistency, indirectness from selected populations, and imprecision reflected by the wide prediction interval. Qualitative evidence assessed using GRADE-CERQual was also rated very low because of concerns about methodological limitations, coherence across diverse settings, adequacy of data, and relevance to broader populations (Supplementary materials).

Discussion

Menstruation tracking app use appears common in the studied populations, but estimates varied widely. The pooled prevalence was 61%, yet the very high heterogeneity and large shift in sensitivity analyses mean that the pooled estimate should be treated as a broad summary, not a precise estimate for women globally. The variation was likely related to differences in study setting, participant characteristics, age groups, sampling methods, digital access, and the way app use was defined and measured. For example, use ranged from just over one-third in the study by Gambier-Ross et al. to much higher proportions in the Indonesian, Polish, Nigerian, and U.S.-based surveys, reflecting important differences in digital engagement and study populations [13-19].

In this review, menstruation and fertility tracking apps were used mainly for immediate and practical reasons, rather than for passive curiosity alone. The dominant reasons were cycle observation, prediction of menstruation or ovulation, support for conception or pregnancy avoidance, symptom tracking, and access to reproductive health information [3,13,14,20]. This suggested that users largely approached these apps as self-management tools that could help them organize, interpret, and anticipate reproductive events in everyday life.

Perceived usefulness was high across settings, even when users did not appear to regard the apps as exact clinical instruments [7,14,20]. In the mixed survey of menstrual cycle tracking technologies, most participants reported that these technologies contributed to their reproductive health knowledge, and satisfaction was also high [14]. In the qualitative European study, users valued apps for preparing for upcoming periods, improving body awareness, verifying symptoms, and supporting communication with healthcare professionals [20]. Broad et al. also showed that many users valued ovulation-related information and routine tracking despite known imperfections in prediction [7]. Taken together, these findings suggest that usefulness extended beyond date prediction alone and included memory support, preparation, reassurance, and a stronger sense of bodily awareness [7,14,20].

The review also suggested that app uptake and continued use were shaped by reproductive goals, user characteristics, and the broader digital habits of the user [13,18]. Gambier-Ross et al. found that fertility tracking app use was closely tied to reasons such as observing the cycle, trying to conceive, informing fertility treatment, and contraception [13]. Adnan et al. similarly showed that app users formed a distinct group from non-trackers and users of other tracking methods, indicating that menstrual tracking behavior clustered with other health and lifestyle characteristics [18]. These findings imply that app use was not uniform across women, but was influenced by life stage, intention, and how well the app matched the user’s reproductive priorities at a given time [13,18].

A central finding of this review was the tension between perceived usefulness and predictive accuracy [7,25-27]. Broad et al. reported that many users experienced their periods earlier or later than predicted, and that these mismatches were associated with frustration, stress, confusion, and pregnancy concern [7]. This concern was supported by app-evaluation studies. Worsfold et al. showed that top period tracker apps gave conflicting information on period dates, ovulation day, and fertile window [25]. Ali et al. found that many fertility apps relied mainly on menstrual dates alone and concluded that unreliable calendar-based apps remained common on the market [26]. Zwingerman et al. also reported weak scientific grounding and important inaccuracies among commercially available apps [27]. These findings indicate that apps may be useful for logging and planning but should be interpreted cautiously for ovulation prediction, fertility decisions, or contraception, especially among women with irregular cycles.

Another important finding was that the same apps that promoted self-awareness could also produce emotional strain [7,20]. Levy and Romo-Avilés found that app-supported tracking helped users know their bodies better, prepare for menstruation, and validate their experiences, but they also documented distress, privacy concerns, and the effort required to sustain tracking [20]. Broad et al similarly found that when periods did not align with predicted dates, users reported anxiety, disappointment, and, in some cases, the need to seek advice from healthcare professionals [7]. This dual pattern suggests that menstrual tracking apps may increase agency and engagement with reproductive health, while also creating vulnerability when algorithmic predictions conflict with lived bodily experience [7,20].

Privacy, data governance, algorithm transparency, and digital inequity also emerged as important issues [22]. Mohan and Jenkins found that users wanted clearer privacy statements, better interface design, greater control over who could access their data, and stronger data protection measures [22]. These concerns matter because menstrual tracking apps handle intimate and repeated health data, and trust depends on both usability and responsible stewardship of that information [22]. Digital access may also shape who benefits from these tools. Women without smartphones, stable internet access, digital literacy, or language-appropriate apps may be under-represented in the evidence and less likely to benefit from app-based menstrual health support. This limits the global applicability of the findings and is especially relevant for low- and middle-income settings.

From a clinical perspective, app records may help clinicians reconstruct menstrual dates, bleeding patterns, pain, premenstrual symptoms, cycle irregularity, and patient concerns during consultation. However, clinicians should ask how the app defines prediction, whether the cycle is regular, and whether the user is using the app for contraception or conception. App outputs should not replace clinical assessment, pregnancy testing, or validated fertility methods where these are required.

Limitations

The review has several limitations. First, the prevalence meta-analysis included only six studies, and those studies differed markedly in country, age group, sampling frame, recruitment method, and definition of app use. The resulting heterogeneity was extreme, so the pooled prevalence should not be interpreted as a stable global estimate. Second, many studies relied on convenience samples, students, current app users, or high-income-country populations, which limits generalisability. Third, app use was self-reported and was not consistently defined as current use, ever use, frequent use, or use of a specific app. Fourth, qualitative, cross-sectional, mixed-methods, and app-evaluation designs were included to answer a broad review question, but this increased methodological variability. Finally, the funnel plot and Egger’s test were underpowered because fewer than 10 studies contributed to the meta-analysis, so reporting bias could not be assessed reliably.

Conclusion

Menstruation tracking app use appears common in the populations studied, but prevalence estimates vary widely and the certainty of evidence is very low. These apps may support cycle recall, symptom monitoring, reproductive awareness, and communication during consultations. They should, however, be used as adjuncts rather than standalone clinical tools, especially for ovulation prediction, fertility decisions, or contraception. Future studies should use clearer definitions of app use, more representative samples, validated accuracy assessments, transparent reporting of algorithms and data practices, and stronger evaluation of clinical usefulness.
