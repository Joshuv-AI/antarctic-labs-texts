Stem cell therapy: a novel frontier in the treatment of liver fibrosis/cirrhosis
Authors: Yang Liu, Jiacen Li, Wenjie Su, Shuang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver fibrosis and cirrhosis are characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) in the liver, and they represent significant global health concerns due to their increasing prevalence worldwide. Traditional therapeutic approaches, such as liver transplantation, are limited by donor availability and potential complications. Stem cell therapy has emerged as a promising alternative, offering the potential for liver regeneration and repair. This paper reviews the latest advancements in stem cell therapy for liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, drawing from recent clinical trials and laboratory studies. We explore various stem cell types, evaluating their efficacy, safety, and mechanisms of action in liver repair. Our findings suggest that stem cell therapy, with its anti-fibrotic properties, immunomodulatory effects, and ability to promote hepatocyte regeneration, holds significant promise for the future of liver disease treatment.Keywords: Liver fibrosis, Cirrhosis, Stem cell therapy

Introduction

Liver fibrosis and its advanced stage, cirrhosis, have become significant global health concerns due to their increasing prevalence worldwide [1]. Fibrosis is characterized by the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix proteins, such as collagen, in the liver, resulting from chronic damage. This condition can progress to cirrhosis, marked by extensive fibrotic scarring and the emergence of regenerative nodules, disrupting the liver’s typical tissue structure and function [2]. Together, advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis are responsible for approximately two million deaths worldwide each year, representing 3.5% of all annual fatalities. Remarkably, complications arising from liver cirrhosis contribute to half of these deaths [3], underscoring the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies.

The development of fibrosis is associated with repeated liver injuries that trigger an uncontrolled wound-healing response [4]. Continuous damage to hepatocytes, whether from viral hepatitis, alcohol abuse, or metabolic disorders, results in prolonged inflammation and subsequent activation of hepatic stellate cells. These collagen-producing cells proliferate and transform into myofibroblast-like cells under inflammatory stimuli. Their unregulated extracellular matrix secretion leads to the progressive accumulation of scar tissue, ultimately resulting in bridging fibrosis and cirrhosis [5–7]. Current treatments, such as antivirals and anti-inflammatory medications, have limited efficacy in reversing established fibrosis or cirrhosis. Liver transplantation remains the definitive treatment but is constrained by the limited availability of donor organs [8].

Stem cells, renowned for their regenerative potential and paracrine signaling, are emerging as a promising therapeutic avenue. These cells possess the capacity for long-term self-renewal and can differentiate into various cell types, offering prospects for regenerating damaged liver tissue [9]. Stem cells are broadly categorized into embryonic stem cells (ESCs), derived from blastocyst-stage embryos, and adult stem cells found in mature tissues. Notable adult stem cell types with therapeutic promise include mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). Additionally, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), created by reprogramming mature cells, exhibit characteristics similar to ESCs [10, 11]. This review provides an overview of current advances in preclinical and clinical stem cell therapies for liver fibrosis and explores potential challenges and prospects to guide future translational research endeavors.

Pathophysiology of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis

Liver fibrosis, characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, primarily fibrillar collagens, is a hallmark of chronic liver diseases. This condition is a wound-healing response to sustained hepatic injury and inflammation, potentially leading to cirrhosis, an advanced and often irreversible stage of liver scarring [12], as shown in Fig. 1.

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) play a crucial role in hepatic fibrogenesis. In a healthy liver, HSCs remain quiescent, but chronic liver injury prompts their activation into proliferative, fibrogenic cells, significantly contributing to ECM accumulation [13]. The activation process is driven by a complex network of cytokines and signaling pathways involving TGF-β, PDGF, hedgehog ligands, FGF, IL-6, and intracellular pathways like SMAD, MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and Wnt/β-catenin [14, 15].

The immune system and fibroblast axis: the key regulatory network in liver fibrosis

In liver fibrosis, the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) is crucial, but not fundamentally autonomous. It arises from context-dependent signals in the liver microenvironment. The onset and resolution of fibrosis reflect dynamic interactions between immune cells and fibroblast populations, particularly HSCs and sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs). Elucidation of the immune-fibroblast axis is crucial for understanding disease mechanisms and developing effective, mechanism-based therapies.

The dual role of innate immunity in fibrosis

Macrophages and Kupffer cells: key regulators of the fibrotic environment

Kupffer cells and monocyte-derived macrophages present in the liver exhibit remarkable plasticity in coordinating the transformation of fibrosis. In the initial phase of injury, proinflammatory macrophages (e.g., M1) directly activate liver progenitor cells, producing fibrosis mediators such as TGF-β, PDGF, and IL-1β [16]. They also exacerbate damage to hepatocytes (e.g., with FasL) and amplify danger signals through the secretion of DAMP. During healing, macrophages adopt an anti-inflammatory and reparative phenotype (similar to M2), which promotes matrix clearance by increasing MMP-9/MMP-12 and decreasing TIMP, while IL-10 production reduces HSC activation and supports tissue repair [17]. Furthermore, new evidence suggests that macrophages and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) interact by transferring organelles and cargo (e.g., mitochondria) via extracellular vesicles or direct contact, which affects the metabolism and activation status of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) [18].

Neutrophils: early drivers of fibrosis

Neutrophils are rapidly responding cells that exacerbate parenchymal damage and promote HSC activation. These cells secrete myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE) and form extracellular neutrophil traps (NETs). NE specifically degrades the extracellular matrix (ECM) and activates latent TGF-β, promoting a feedback loop that causes fibrosis [19].

Adaptive immunity in the regulation of liver fibrosis

T lymphocytes: spectrum of effects on fibrosis

The cell populations of the adaptive immune system have different effects. Th17 cells amplify inflammatory and fibrotic signals by stimulating hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and HSCs with IL-17 A. CD8⁺ T cells mainly affect hepatocytes through cytotoxicity (Fas/FasL and perforin-granzyme pathways) [20]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), on the other hand, maintain immune balance by producing IL-10 and TGF-β to suppress effector T cells and macrophages. Contact-dependent mechanisms can also directly limit the activation of liver cells prior to proliferation, highlighting immunoregulatory pathways as therapeutic targets [21].

B lymphocytes: new promoters of fibrosis

In addition to producing antibodies, activated B cells can directly influence the behavior of liver progenitor cells and maintain a fibrosis-promoting environment by presenting antigens to CD4⁺ T cells and secreting growth factors such as IL-6, TNF-α, and PDGF [22].

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs): from protectors to promoters of liver fibrosis

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) occupy a strategic position as a critical interface between circulating immune cells and parenchymal/non-parenchymal cells, such as hepatic stem cells (HSCs). Chronic liver injury causes pathological changes known as endothelial cell-mediated angiogenesis LSEC (LSEC), characterized by pore loss and increased adhesion molecules, such as ICAM-1, regulation. This phenotype promotes the recruitment, adhesion, and trapping of leukocytes in sinusoids, causing persistent local inflammation [23]. It is important to note that branched LSECs undergo profound functional changes in paracrine signaling, transitioning from an inactive state determined by anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., nitric oxide) to an activated state that secretes potent fibrogenic factors, including PDGF and TGF-β. This change in secretion directly stimulates the activation and proliferation of adjacent hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), reinforcing the important role of LSECs in the formation of fibrotic tissue [24].

Therapeutic effects: Immunomodulation with mesenchymal stem cells

Recent advances in stem cell therapy, particularly with the use of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), have demonstrated their potential in the treatment of liver diseases. These advances highlight immunomodulation as a primary therapeutic mechanism that goes beyond the simple concept of cell replacement. MSCs exert their regenerative effects primarily through potent paracrine signaling that modulates the immune dialogue in the liver. More specifically, MSCs secrete various factors, including TSG-6, PGE₂, and IL-10, which promote the polarization of proinflammatory M1 macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, interrupting the profibrotic feedback loop and initiating tissue repair [25]. In addition, multipotent progenitor cells modulate T cell dynamics by expressing indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and PGE₂, which suppress Th17 responses while promoting the proliferation of regulatory T cells (Tregs). This reconfiguration of the liver microenvironment indirectly inhibits the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and promotes the resolution of fibrosis [26]. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells can restore the perforated phenotype of sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), improving sinusoidal perfusion and limiting inflammatory cell infiltration, thereby promoting liver regeneration [27]. Taken together, these findings confirm the important role of immunomodulation in mesenchymal stem cell-mediated liver regeneration and provide new insights for more precise therapeutic strategies.

Challenges and future directions in liver fibrosis treatment

Several targeted therapies have been explored for treating liver fibrosis, though with limited translation to clinical use. Anti-fibrotic drugs like pirfenidone and nintedanib have shown efficacy in preclinical models, but human trials reveal modest benefits, with concerns around tolerability and selectivity [28]. Limitations exist in oral bioavailability and optimal dosing. Other small molecules targeting TGF-β, PDGF, or chemokine receptors demonstrate antifibrotic effects preclinically but have not panned out in patients. Issues include lack of specificity, toxicity, and inadequate clinical trial design. Immunomodulatory agents like pentoxifylline have anti-fibrotic effects but variable efficacy in humans. Dietary supplements such as vitamin E demonstrate antifibrotic properties in vitro but no proven clinical benefit. Liver transplantation is an option for end-stage cirrhosis, but shortage of donor organs, high cost, and need for long-term immunosuppression limit its applicability [29].

In contrast, Stem cell therapy, particularly involving mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), presents a promising alternative in the treatment of liver fibrosis. Preclinical studies have revealed that MSCs possess potent anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, and immunomodulatory effects. They not only inhibit the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which is crucial in fibrogenesis, but also stimulate matrix degradation, promote hepatic regeneration, and suppress TGF-β signaling [30, 31]. Early clinical trials indicate the safety and potential efficacy of MSCs in treating liver fibrosis. Current research efforts are dedicated to optimizing cell sources, delivery methods, and combination therapies, aiming to translate these beneficial effects into significant clinical improvements for patients, particularly where conventional treatment options are limited.

Beyond stem cell therapy, significant efforts are directed towards targeting HSC activation, a pivotal event in fibrogenesis. Researchers are exploring a variety of agents, including natural compounds, small molecule inhibitors, and RNA-based therapies, which target molecular pathways involved in HSC activation such as TGF-β, PDGF, and the Endothelin-1 signaling pathways [32]. These strategies aim to maintain HSCs in their quiescent state or revert them from their activated, fibrogenic form.

Another key focus area is the suppression of ECM synthesis and deposition. This involves developing drugs that inhibit the synthesis of collagen and other ECM components or disrupt the signaling pathways leading to ECM production. Agents targeting enzymes like lysyl oxidase (LOX), which cross-link collagen fibers, are under investigation for their potential to reduce ECM stiffness and fibrosis [33].

Equally crucial is the enhancement of the degradation of already deposited collagen. This approach seeks to stimulate the activity of collagen-degrading enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), or to inhibit their natural inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) [34]. Ongoing research is identifying compounds that effectively shift the balance towards ECM degradation and fibrosis resolution.

Targeting pro-fibrogenic signaling mediators is also a promising strategy. This includes developing inhibitors against key cytokines and growth factors that drive fibrogenesis, as well as blocking their receptors or downstream signaling pathways. The use of antagonists or neutralizing antibodies against mediators like TGF-β and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) is currently under investigation [35].

Ultimately, a combination of these approaches may be necessary to successfully halt fibrosis progression or even reverse cirrhosis. This could involve simultaneously targeting multiple steps in the fibrogenic process with optimally timed or staged treatment strategies. The overarching goal is to develop effective antifibrotic therapies that transition liver fibrosis from an inexorably progressive disease to a potentially curable condition.

Stem cell sources for liver treatment

Stem cell therapy has emerged as a promising approach in the treatment of liver diseases, offering new possibilities for restoring hepatic structure and function. Owing to their intrinsic capacities for self-renewal, multilineage differentiation, and paracrine signaling, stem cells represent a cornerstone of regenerative medicine. These cells can differentiate into hepatocyte-like cells, secrete trophic and anti-inflammatory factors, and modulate immune responses, thereby promoting hepatocyte proliferation, reducing fibrosis, and enhancing tissue regeneration.Different types of stem cells—including embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells, hematopoietic stem cells, mesenchymal stem cells, hepatic progenitor cells, hematopoietic stem cell–derived hepatocytes, and hepatocyte-like cells—exert distinct yet complementary mechanisms in liver repair. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), derived from various tissue sources such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and menstrual blood, have demonstrated significant potential in the treatment of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. This potential has been evident in both preclinical animal models and preliminary clinical trials. The therapeutic efficacy of MSCs is believed to arise from several pivotal mechanisms, including immunomodulation, suppression of hepatic stellate cell activation, enhancement of matrix degradation pathways, and the secretion of paracrine factors that promote the proliferation of native liver cells [31, 36].

For example, research has shown that MSCs sourced from the umbilical cord can reduce markers associated with stellate cell activation, such as α-SMA. This suggests their ability to inhibit the progression of fibrosis in mouse models [37, 38]. Furthermore, a phase 1/2a clinical trial conducted in 2023 provided evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of infusing patients with decompensated cirrhosis with MSCs derived from bone marrow. This trial demonstrated improvements in liver function, highlighting the clinical potential of MSCs [39].

It is worth noting that MSCs, depending on their tissue source, may exhibit varied properties. Consequently, current research endeavors are focused on investigating MSCs from diverse sources, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, liver, and menstrual blood, with the aim of identifying the most optimal MSC types for therapeutic interventions in liver diseases.

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are primarily derived from bone marrow and possess the unique ability for self-renewal and differentiation into various blood cell lineages. These lineages include both myeloid cells (such as monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils) and lymphoid cells (including T cells, B cells, and NK cells) [40]. HSCs are characterized by the cell surface marker CD34 and were the first stem cell population to be identified and thoroughly characterized [41].

Numerous preclinical studies involving animals have demonstrated that HSCs, particularly those expressing CD34 + and sourced from either bone marrow or peripheral blood, can be administered intravenously or directly into the liver. Following administration, these cells have been observed to migrate to areas of liver injury [42–44]. Within the liver’s microenvironment, there is evidence suggesting that HSCs may differentiate into cells resembling hepatocytes and cholangiocytes. This differentiation process is believed to occur through mechanisms such as cell fusion or transdifferentiation, possibly influenced by specific liver growth factors [45, 46].

Early-stage clinical trials, which used granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (GCSF) to mobilize autologous CD34 + HSCs from the bone marrow, have produced inconsistent results. While some trials have indicated that the injection of mobilized peripheral blood HSCs can lead to a reduction in ascites and a moderate improvement in liver function [47], others have not observed any significant long-term benefits when compared to conventional medical treatments [48, 49]. Another therapeutic approach that has been explored is the direct transplantation of bone marrow-derived HSCs. The outcomes from this approach have also varied. While some studies have reported improvements in parameters such as the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores and serum albumin levels [50], two comprehensive meta-analyses have concluded that bone marrow HSC therapy does not significantly improve survival rates in patients with advanced liver disease [51, 52].

Hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs)

Hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs) are a specialized subset of facultative stem cells naturally present in the liver. These cells possess the unique ability to differentiate into both hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, the latter being biliary epithelial cells [53]. Under normal conditions, HPCs are localized within the Canals of Hering and remain in a quiescent state. However, during episodes of chronic liver injury, characterized by sustained inflammation and the senescence or loss of hepatocytes, HPCs become activated and undergo proliferation [54].

Evidence from various preclinical studies, particularly those involving rodent models, underscores the significance of HPC activation and subsequent differentiation into hepatocytes. This process is believed to be a crucial innate mechanism facilitating liver regeneration in the context of chronic diseases [55]. It is postulated that, under conditions of chronic injury, endogenous HPCs play a substantial role in hepatocyte turnover. Furthermore, experiments have demonstrated that HPCs, when expanded ex vivo and subsequently transplanted, can effectively rejuvenate and repopulate damaged liver tissues in mice, highlighting their regenerative capabilities [56].

Contemporary research endeavors in this field primarily focus on enhancing the proliferative potential of HPCs, guiding their differentiation specifically toward functional hepatocytes, and improving their overall engraftment and longevity post-transplantation [57]. Some promising strategies under investigation include preconditioning HPCs with growth factors such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), Wnt, and epidermal growth factor (EGF) to enhance proliferation [58]. Genetic engineering techniques, such as transfecting HPCs with genes like HNF4A, CEBPA, and FOXA3, are being explored to promote commitment to the hepatocyte lineage [59]. Additionally, introducing HPCs via biopolymer scaffolds or as 3D spheroids might provide the necessary extracellular matrix and cell-cell interactions to preserve their differentiation capacity [60].

While HPCs present an enticing prospect for autologous cell-based regenerative therapy, the journey from bench to bedside is fraught with challenges. Key milestones ahead include gaining a comprehensive understanding of HPC activation dynamics, refining HPC isolation techniques, ensuring consistent hepatocyte differentiation, and establishing the safety and efficacy of HPC-based interventions. Only after overcoming these challenges can HPCs be truly considered a feasible therapeutic alternative for patients struggling with end-stage liver disease [61].

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have emerged as a groundbreaking autologous cell source. These cells are derived by reprogramming adult somatic cells back into a pluripotent state. This reprogramming is achieved through the forced expression of specific transcription factors, namely Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc [45]. The ability to generate patient-specific pluripotent stem cells through this method holds immense promise for regenerative medicine.

A plethora of studies have adeptly demonstrated the differentiation of human iPSCs into functional hepatocyte-like cells (iHeps) in vitro. This differentiation is orchestrated by manipulating an array of signaling pathways and growth factors [62]. When these iHeps are transplanted into mouse models with liver injury, they exhibit remarkable engraftment capabilities. Furthermore, they assume hepatocyte functions, such as albumin synthesis and drug metabolism [63].

One of the salient advantages of hepatocytes derived from iPSCs is their potential to serve as an inexhaustible autologous cell source for transplantation. This could circumvent the challenges of immune rejection often associated with allogeneic cell transplants [64]. However, a notable limitation is that many current differentiation protocols yield hepatocytes that are developmentally immature when compared to adult primary hepatocytes [65]. Thus, refining methods to generate fully mature and functional iHeps remains a priority.

Several challenges loom large as the scientific community endeavors to translate iPSC technology for clinical applications. These challenges include enhancing the differentiation and maturation of hepatocytes, ensuring consistent hepatic function in vivo, mitigating the risk of tumorigenicity from residual pluripotent cells, and devising cost-effective manufacturing strategies compliant with cGMP standards. When integrated with advanced gene-editing tools, such as CRISPR, iPSCs stand poised to revolutionize our understanding and therapeutic approaches to liver diseases [66, 67].

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs)

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are pluripotent cells isolated from the inner cell mass of blastocyst-stage embryos. Their defining characteristic is the ability to differentiate into all cell types present in the body [68].

Numerous studies have successfully outlined in vitro protocols to derive functional hepatocytes from both mouse and human ESCs [69]. When these ESC-derived hepatocytes are transplanted into rodent models with acute liver injuries, they demonstrate the capability to engraft within the liver parenchyma, contributing to tissue repair and regeneration [70].

Representative preclinical studies evaluating stem cells for liver regeneration

Numerous pivotal studies have emphasized the potential of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), sourced from bone marrow or mobilized from peripheral blood, to home in on injured liver tissue. In mouse models with liver failure induced by agents like carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) or through surgical resection, these HSCs have been observed to differentiate into hepatocyte-like cells. Notably, the transplantation of HSCs or the administration of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) to mobilize endogenous HSCs has demonstrated anti-fibrotic effects. This therapeutic approach has notably improved hepatic function in rat models with liver fibrosis initiated by agents such as dimethylnitrosamine (DMN) or through bile duct ligation [76, 77].

Similarly, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), derived from diverse sources including bone marrow, adipose tissue, and the umbilical cord, have exhibited pronounced hepatoprotective and anti-fibrotic effects. These effects were evident in rodent models of liver injury, induced by agents like CCl4, thioacetamide, alcohol feeding, or specific dietary hepatic toxins [78, 79]. Interestingly, rather than direct engraftment, MSCs seem to exert their influence predominantly through paracrine mechanisms. They secrete hepatotrophic factors, notably vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), in addition to anti-inflammatory cytokines [80].

Furthermore, the transplantation of ex vivo expanded hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs), harvested from rodent livers, has shown promising results. These cells have successfully repopulated the damaged hepatic parenchyma in mouse models subjected to chronic liver injury, underscoring the inherent regenerative prowess of these resident liver stem cells [81].clinical trials in liver regeneration.

In 2011, JK Kim et al. conducted one of the pioneering clinical trials to assess the safety and efficacy of autologous bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cell transplantation in cirrhosis patients. The study involved a single intravenous infusion of mesenchymal stem cells and indicated that the procedure was not only well-tolerated but also led to reduced fibrosis and enhanced liver function [82]. Subsequent trials have expanded on this foundation, exploring mesenchymal stem cells derived from both bone marrow and umbilical cord. Notably, a phase 2 trial led by El Ansary et al. demonstrated that multiple infusions of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells significantly improved liver function, Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores, and survival rates in patients with end-stage liver disease [83].

In parallel, hematopoietic stem cells have also been under clinical investigation for their potential in liver regeneration. A combined phase 1–2 trial led by Mohamadnejad et al. explored the autologous infusion of mobilized peripheral blood hematopoietic stem cells in patients with cirrhosis. The findings revealed enhanced liver function and improved Child-Pugh scores [38]. A more recent study in 2021 by Rostami et al. delved into a combination therapy, integrating hematopoietic stem cells from peripheral blood with mesenchymal stem cells from bone marrow. This synergistic approach was found to bolster liver function and improve survival rates in cirrhosis patients [84].

While the majority of clinical trials have predominantly focused on mesenchymal or hematopoietic stem cells sourced from bone marrow and peripheral blood, there’s a burgeoning interest in harnessing liver stem/progenitor cells. These cells can be derived from fetal liver cell suspensions or expanded ex vivo from adult liver progenitor cells. Preliminary clinical trials utilizing fetal liver-derived progenitor cells for conditions like liver failure and cirrhosis have shown promise in augmenting liver function [85, 86]. Nonetheless, the consistent isolation and expansion of adult liver stem/progenitor cells remain a challenge that needs addressing.

Challenges in stem cell therapy for liver diseases

Stem cell therapy, while promising for the treatment of liver diseases, is accompanied by several challenges that must be addressed for its successful integration into clinical practice:

Cell Source and Isolation: Identifying the most suitable and clinically viable cell source for liver regeneration remains a pivotal challenge. While adult liver stem/progenitor cells offer potential, their isolation and expansion in significant quantities are challenging [87]. Pluripotent stem cells, though capable of extensive proliferation, often exhibit limited differentiation and engraftment capabilities. Mesenchymal and hematopoietic stem cells, though more accessible, do not efficiently differentiate into functional hepatocytes [88]. Recent efforts have shifted towards deriving iPSCs and enhancing their maturation using specific biochemical and mechanical cues [89].

Cell Differentiation and Maturation: Achieving consistent differentiation of stem cells into mature, functional hepatocytes is a significant hurdle. Contemporary techniques employ growth factors, small molecules, and 3D culture systems to guide the differentiation of iPSC-derived hepatic progenitors towards mature hepatocytes, which express hepatic proteins and cytochrome P450 enzymes [90]. However, elevating their metabolic activity to match primary human hepatocytes remains a challenge, limiting their therapeutic potential.

Cell Engraftment and Survival: Post-transplantation, cells delivered to the liver via injection or infusion often face poor engraftment and survival rates. This can be attributed to factors like cell washout from the liver, inflammatory reactions, and cell death. Developing strategies to enhance targeted cell delivery and post-transplantation survival is imperative [91].

Immunogenicity: Allogeneic stem cell therapies present the risk of immunological rejection, potentially rendering liver transplantation unfeasible. Strategies to induce tolerance, whether through immunosuppression, immunomodulation, or stem cell modifications, demand further refinement in clinical settings [92].

Tumorigenicity: The use of pluripotent stem cells is not without risks, notably the potential for teratoma formation. Prolonged expansion of cells might also introduce genetic and epigenetic abnormalities, raising concerns about malignant transformations. This underscores the importance of long-term monitoring of transplant recipients [93].

Optimal Protocols: Establishing the best practices for cell processing, delivery, dosing, timing, and the potential combination with other therapies is crucial. Empirical clinical studies are needed to fine-tune these protocols, ensuring they are tailored to specific liver diseases and their stages [94].

Addressing these challenges is crucial for the successful clinical implementation of stem cell therapies for liver diseases, and ongoing research is essential to refine these approaches and improve patient outcomes.

Future prospects

The field of stem cell research is rapidly evolving, opening up new possibilities for regenerative therapies in liver diseases. A significant focus of this progress is centered around the generation of functional hepatocytes from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Advances in utilizing transcription factors and three-dimensional culture systems are paving the way for the maturation of iPSC-derived hepatic progenitor cells into fully functional hepatocytes [95]. Additionally, there is an intensive exploration into directly reprogramming fibroblasts into hepatocyte-like cells through the controlled expression of lineage-specific transcription factors, aiming to address longstanding challenges in cell sourcing and maturation [96].

Innovative genome editing techniques, such as CRISPR, are allowing for targeted corrections of genetic anomalies in patient-specific iPSCs or their differentiated hepatocytes [97], showing significant promise in addressing liver diseases with a genetic basis.

The effectiveness of stem cell therapy can be enhanced through combination strategies. Co-transplanting mesenchymal stem cells offers both immunomodulatory and trophic support, improving engraftment. Combining cell infusion with drugs, like tyrosine kinase inhibitors that modulate liver fibrosis and regeneration, is actively under evaluation. Three-dimensional bioprinting, enabling the creation of liver tissue constructs with diverse cell types in optimal configurations, holds the potential for further improving engraftment and functionality. There is a growing interest in combining direct in vivo reprogramming of liver fibroblasts with cell transplantation to synergistically promote regeneration [98].

The use of iPSCs marks a new era in personalized medicine. By obtaining skin or blood cells from patients, autologous iPSC lines can be generated, leading to the production of hepatocytes or hepatic progenitors that are genetically matched with the patient, thus avoiding issues of immune rejection. These patient-specific iPSCs also serve as invaluable tools for disease modeling, providing insights and facilitating drug screenings tailored to individual patients. The integration of CRISPR gene editing allows for the correction of specific genetic defects in patient iPSCs, enabling the transplantation of corrected cells [99]. Furthermore, the high-throughput mapping of HLA haplotypes in iPSC lines is driving the establishment of HLA-matched allogeneic cell banks. These developments are revolutionizing stem cell therapy, making it more precise and personalized.

Application of extracellular vesicle-based cell-free therapeutic strategies in liver fibrosis

Cell-free therapeutic approaches based on mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), and the sugars they secrete have become an important area of research in the treatment of liver fibrosis in recent years thanks to advances in regenerative medicine. Extracellular vesicle-based strategies offer significant advantages over traditional cellular therapies, as they eliminate the risks associated with cell transplantation, such as tumor formation, immune rejection, and vascular occlusion, while retaining the anti-fibrotic, immunomodulatory, and regenerative properties of stem cells [100, 101].

Exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) act as an important mediator of cellular communication and exert multiple therapeutic effects in liver fibrosis due to their content of biologically active molecules, including proteins, microRNAs, and lipids. Recent studies have shown that these exosomes can directly inhibit hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) by transmitting anti-fibrotic signals such as miR-181-5p and miR-122, and inhibit excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation by modulating key signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin [102, 103]. Furthermore, exosomes promote the reverse process of fibrosis by transforming the immune environment of the liver through mechanisms such as promoting M2 macrophage polarization and suppressing inflammatory responses [104]. Furthermore, they promote regenerative functions: by transporting angiogenic factors such as VEGF and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), they promote liver parenchymal regeneration [105].

Recent advances in exosome technology have enhanced their therapeutic potential. Important strategies include: (1) Genetic modification of progenitor cells to introduce specific therapeutic exosomes; through genetic modification of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), the exosomes they secrete can be enriched with specific therapeutic molecules (e.g., IL-10) [106]; (2) Direct introduction of drugs into exosomes using physical or chemical methods. For example, methods such as electroporation, ultrasound, or freeze-thaw cycles can be used to incorporate small drug molecules (such as suvafinib) or nucleic acid therapies (such as siRNA) into exosomes [107]. (3) Surface modification methods, such as peptide labeling or antibody conjugation, aim to improve the specificity of exosomes. For example, genetically modified or chemically conjugated target peptides (such as PDGFRβ-binding peptides) labeled on the surface of exosomes can increase their accumulation in hepatic fibroblasts [108]; (4) Developing hybrid* exosome systems that combine multiple sources of vesicles to enhance therapeutic effect. For example, combining exosomes from different sources or combining exosomes with exosome mimics can result in more effective therapeutic carriers [108, 109].

In addition to vesicles, broader MSC secretion, consisting of soluble factors, microvesicles, and exosomes, may provide a synergistic effect with multiple targets. New data suggest that modified exosomes coupled with complementary secretory components regulate fibrosis pathways more effectively than monocomponent EVs, providing justification for multicomponent cell-free therapies [110]. These developments provide a solid foundation for a new generation of liver fibrosis therapies.

Conclusion

In summary, stem cell-based therapies offer a promising avenue for the treatment of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. Both preclinical research and early-phase clinical trials have shown that the transplantation of various stem cell types, including mesenchymal stem cells, hematopoietic stem cells, and hepatic progenitor cells, can result in improved liver function, reduced fibrosis, and the regeneration of damaged tissue in animal models and patients with chronic liver diseases. These benefits are underpinned by mechanisms like immunomodulation, antifibrotic effects, hepatocyte differentiation, and the stimulation of endogenous regeneration.

Nonetheless, there are several challenges that must be addressed before stem cell therapy can become a mainstream and effective treatment. These challenges include issues related to cell sourcing, isolation, expansion, engraftment, and survival, which require advances in bioengineering and cell manufacturing. Combining stem cell therapy with drugs, growth factors, or biomaterials may enhance therapeutic outcomes. Protocols will need to be finely tuned for each specific cause and stage of liver disease through well-designed clinical trials to demonstrate effectiveness.
