Stereotactic radiosurgery for fourth ventricle brain metastases: tumor control outcomes and the need for CSF diversion. Patient series
Authors: Shivani D Rangwala, Jane S Han, Benjamin Strickland, Cheng Yu, Jason C Ye, Gabriel Zada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDStereotactic radiosurgery is a favorable alternative to surgery for intracranial cerebral metastases. Fourth ventricle (V4) metastases are challenging because of the location and surrounding structures, with a high risk for obstructive hydrocephalus and brainstem compression. Here, the authors evaluate the effectiveness in terms of safety, tumor control rates, and permanent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion of primary Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) in treating V4 metastases.OBSERVATIONSEleven of 1060 patients had V4 brain metastases. No patients had pre-GKRS hydrocephalus. The mean tumor volume was 3.3 cm3, treatment dose was 18 Gy to the 50.5% isodose line (average 14 shots), conformity index was 1.4, and volume of brainstem receiving greater than 12 Gy was 0.2 cm3. Eight patients had radiographic and clinical follow-ups. The average time to initial follow-up was 3.1 months. Karnofsy Performance Status did not change from the initial consultation to the post-GKRS follow-up. No patients developed hydrocephalus or required permanent CSF diversion. One patient required transient external ventricular drain placement; however, neurological symptoms resolved with steroid therapy. Seven patients received steroid therapy post-GKRS. Seven patients had radiographic disease control at the 6-month follow-up. Brain metastases are the most common intracranial malignant neoplasm, often presenting with multiple lesions. Metastases to the central nervous system commonly originate from primary neoplasms of the lung, breast, or skin.1, 2 The complexity in the management of intracranial metastases depends on the degree of neurological deficit, the number and location of lesions, and the lesion size. Larger lesions (> 3 cm3) often require surgical intervention, whereas smaller lesions can be amenable to stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) alone. However, even small tumors can cause mass effects and result in devastating consequences if present at critical locations. One such example is fourth ventricle (V4) metastases, which pose clinical challenges due to the restricted dimensions of the posterior fossa and the risk of brainstem compression, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathway obstruction, and hydrocephalus. Only about 2% of intracerebral lesions are intraventricular, and even a smaller proportion of this group arises within the V4.3, 4 In one series of intracranial metastatic tumors, only 0.4% of tumors were in the V4.5 The most common intraventricular metastases include renal, lung, and colon carcinomas, and the lateral ventricle is the most common site of metastases.6, 7 SRS is a preferred treatment option for many intracranial metastases, with favorable outcomes, particularly for tumors of smaller volume.1, 8–12 There is a paucity of literature pertaining to the role of radiosurgery in the treatment of V4 metastases. Given the higher risk of obstructive hydrocephalus and brainstem compression, these lesions often require surgical intervention initially, with possible Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) to the surgical bed postoperatively.3, 13, 14 A few prior studies have addressed the role of conventional GKRS in posterior fossa metastases, but, to our knowledge, there is no study focusing on V4 metastases.1, 15–17 Our study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of GKRS alone in the treatment of V4 metastases and identify any risk factors for the development of hydrocephalus requiring temporary or permanent CSF diversion.

Study Description

Data Collection

A retrospective review of all patients with a V4 metastatic solid tumor was performed. Our institutional SRS database containing 1060 patients was reviewed to identify posterior fossa lesions treated using GKRS between September 1994 and January 2021. Patients prior to March 2007 were excluded, as there was incomplete clinical and radiographic charting in the electronic medical record. Of the GKRS cases at our institution between March 2007 and January 2021, we included tumors with a primary V4 location (defined as arising primarily within the V4) or a secondary V4 location (defined as arising in parenchyma adjacent to the ventricle with > 50% invasion into ventricle).18 We excluded patients who had undergone prior resection of a V4 mass and those who had undergone postoperative radiosurgery to the residual tumor bed. Patients without radiographic or clinical follow-up were excluded.

All patients were evaluated by a radiation oncologist pre- and postoperatively. Pre- and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies were reviewed to determine the initial tumor volume and the extent of local control on follow-up imaging initially, at 6 months, and at 1 year (if available). Clinical data collection included age, gender, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), primary tumor histology, total intracranial metastases, steroid therapy, evidence of hydrocephalus, need for CSF diversion, and symptoms or new deficits at follow-ups. The SRS parameters included tumor volume, margin dose, isodose, maximum dose, conformity index, gradient index, maximum brainstem dose (BDmax), and volume of brainstem receiving more than 12 Gy (V12).

GKRS Planning Technique

GKRS was performed using the model C system until 2008 and the Perfexion system (Elekta AB) subsequently. For GKRS treatment planning, a stereotactic headframe is secured using 4 pins with the patient under conscious sedation. High-resolution MRI of the brain with contrast is then performed with the frame on, and the brain metastases as well as the brainstem are delineated by a radiation oncologist and verified by a neurosurgeon and a medical physicist. A highly conformal plan is then created using a combination of shots with Leksell GammaPlan software (Elekta AB). Due to the lesions’ proximity to the brainstem, a combination of 4-mm and 8-mm shots are generally used in order to create a conformal plan for minimizing the V12. Therescription dose ranged from 14 to 20 Gy (median 17 Gy) to the 50% isodose line (range 50%–60%). Every effort was made to keep the BDmax below 16 Gy. However, in case of overlap between tumor volume and brainstem contour, a small (typically < 0.05 cm3) volume of the brainstem can receive above 16 Gy but below the prescription dose.

Statistical Analyses

SPSS was used for data analysis and basic descriptive statistics of our study population (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.9, IBM Corp.). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified, and a probability value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient Characteristics

Within our database of 1060 posterior fossa metastases, we identified 11 cases that had V4 metastatic tumors. A majority of patients were ≥ 65 years (n = 6, 54.5%) and female (n = 7, 63.5%). On initial evaluation, the median KPS was 80 (range 60–90) and did not change significantly on initial posttreatment visits.

All patients were initially evaluated by a radiation oncologist and were then treated within 5–16 days following the initial consultation (median 11 days). The majority of patients had multiple intracranial lesions (n = 9, 81.8%) rather than a single lesion (n = 2, 18.2%), with most having 2–4 lesions, including the V4 lesion (n = 6, 54.5%). Hydrocephalus Requiring CSF Diversion

Only 8 patients (72.7%) had complete clinical and neuroimaging follow-ups after treatment. The mean time to the initial follow-up was 3.1 months, with some patients seen as early as 6 days after treatment and some seen after 6.5 months. Only 1 patient (12.5%) had documentation of worsening neurological symptoms after GKRS; this patient had worsening nausea, vomiting, and ataxia, requiring hospitalization. No patients had evidence of hydrocephalus on their initial posttreatment MRI. One patient presented 2 days after GKRS with worsening ataxia, nausea, vomiting, and feeding intolerance and required external ventricular drain placement. The opening pressure was 2 mm Hg, and with minimal output and resolution of symptoms with steroid therapy, the drain was discontinued after 2 days. The patient did not require permanent CSF diversion. No patient in our series required ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement or permanent CSF diversion.

Steroid Therapy

The majority of patients (n = 7, 63.6%) receiving GKRS for a V4 lesion required steroid therapy, which was initiated at the follow-up; only one patient was on dexamethasone therapy prior to GKRS, and this dose was continued after treatment. Dexamethasone was prescribed by the medical or radiation oncology team primarily due to patient symptoms. The dosage ranged from 2 mg to 4 mg two or three times daily, at the discretion of the treatment provider. Supplemental Table 1 demonstrates that there was no significant difference between requiring steroid therapy and lesion size, number of lesions, or initial KPS on presentation (p = 0.11, 0.27, 0.57 respectively).

Overall Patient Outcomes

Of the 8 patients with complete follow-up in our series, all had local control of the V4 lesion on the initial follow-up, but only 7 patients (87.5%) had local control at the V4 lesion at the 6-month follow-up. Local control was defined as a stable or a decreased lesion size for this retrospective series. One patient (12.5%) with breast cancer (ER−, PR−, HER2+, IHC 3+) had tumor progression after GKRS and required subsequent surgical debulking and resection of the V4 mass. Of those with follow-ups, 4 patients elected hospice care due to the overall systemic disease burden and did not have long-term follow-up. Only 2 patients had clinical or radiographic follow-ups beyond 6 months, showing local disease control of the V4 lesion (Fig. 2). Four patients died without undergoing posttreatment imaging or clinical follow-ups.

Discussion

Metastases within or near the ventricle are a known barrier to normal CSF flow and can result in hydrocephalus.19 For this reason, tumors within the posterior fossa, and especially within the V4, pose a critical risk of obstructive hydrocephalus or brainstem compression and warrant neurosurgical evaluation. Surgical intervention is indicated in larger tumors, but gross-total resection is not always possible, given the surrounding anatomy, and patients could still need CSF diversion despite resection.3, 20, 21

Observations

In our series, we noted only one patient (12.5%) with neurological complications that resolved on the long-term follow-up. The largest series of V4 tumors by Ferguson et al. describes 55 patients with invasive V4 tumors and their outcomes after surgical intervention.3 Only 5 patients (9%) had metastatic lesions in this series, and gross-total resection was achieved in only 75% of patients. A majority of the patients experienced postoperative complications; specifically, 45% of patients experienced at least one major neurological complication, while 31% experienced a minor neurological complication.3 Given that there are several intricate adjacent structures vulnerable to iatrogenic damage (e.g., cranial nerves, dentate nuclei, cerebellar peduncles, neurovasculature), surgical decompression of V4 tumors can cause various complications. Gait/ataxia, bulbar disorders, and cranial nerve palsies (VI and VII in particular) are common complications after surgical decompression of the V4.3, 22–25 Furthermore, studies have shown an increased incidence of frailty among cancer patients and an increased risk of complications after craniotomy for tumor resection in this population.26–28 Proceeding with surgical intervention in this patient population should be carefully considered.

In the present study, no patient required permanent CSF diversion in this present study, and only one patient (patient I; Table 3) required external ventricular drainage. This patient presented with initial symptoms that correlated with possible acute hydrocephalus and increased peritumoral edema (PTE; ataxia, headaches, nausea, and vomiting); however, his symptoms resolved with steroid therapy and the opening pressure was low. In the literature regarding GKRS in posterior fossa tumors, one patient required permanent CSF diversion after 23 months.1 In the previously mentioned series by Ferguson et al., permanent postoperative CSF diversion was needed in 22% of patients.3 CSF diversion carries the risk of several complications.3 More commonly, there can be shunt malfunctions requiring surgical revisions or other postoperative complications such as infection, which can delay patients in receiving oncological treatment.1 There have also been reports of intratumoral hemorrhage occurring after CSF diversion procedures.29 Rarely, extracranial metastases (e.g., peritoneum) can also occur from tumor dissemination via ventriculoperitoneal shunts, worsening patient prognosis.1, 30

Advancements in radiation therapy offer an alternative to surgical intervention in intracranial disease. SRS is a treatment option for single intracranial metastatic lesions, either with or without whole brain radiation therapy (WBRT), with favorable outcomes.1, 8–12, 31 However, one consideration after radiation therapy is the side effects related to radiation necrosis and PTE, which can cause serious neurological complications, affecting the quality of life.16, 32–34 Steroid therapy remains the mainstay of treatment if complications arise, but some surgeons could prefer surgical intervention for larger lesions or lesions with significant PTE initially.1, 3, 35, 36 One series of 51 large posterior fossa metastases (median lesion size of 2.6 cm) showed a favorable response to GKRS with a statistically significant reduction in the lesion size, reduction in PTE, and reduction in V4 compression.1 While our study focuses only on V4 metastatic lesions, our results support the findings of Muhsen et al.1 The majority of patients undergoing GKRS in our series had adequate disease control at 6 months. GKRS is a safe surgical alternative for invasive V4 metastases, but patients should be monitored closely for symptoms of hydrocephalus or new neurological deficits.

The ultimate outcome for the majority of the patients (n = 7, 63.6%) in our cohort was death or systemic disease progression with transition to hospice. While the KPS was adequate for the majority of our patients at initial consultation, several patients deteriorated due to the overall disease burden after GKRS. This observation potentially highlights V4 involvement as a poor prognostic indicator, with disease progression and patients dying of disease even if the treated V4 metastasis itself could be controlled. While the high rates of systemic progression and death make long-term follow-up for tumor control and GKRS complications difficult, the fact that these patients seldom survive long term could argue toward sparing them from resection and its potential complications or prolonged recovery.

Lessons

We found GKRS to be a safe and effective treatment for invasive V4 metastases, resulting in a stable todecreased lesion size on follow-ups, without the additional risk of permanent CSF diversion. In other instances of brain metastases, GKRS has been shown to be a safe and effective treatment alternative to surgery.16, 37–39 Our results favor GKRS as a safe alternative to aggressive surgical interventions in the V4 and the posterior fossa, particularly in patients who can ultimately progress to hospice or death, as many patients in our series showed good responses, with local control at 6 months. Despite concerns for hydrocephalus or neurological complications due to the tumor location, we only observed a single case that improved with conservative management in our series. Our results suggest that targeted GKRS to the V4 can minimize the side effects of PTE and neurological complications and optimize the quality of life in this patient cohort.

The majority of patients required high-dose steroid therapy, with an improvement in clinical symptoms. Steroid use in the treatment of PTE is a widely accepted practice to minimize clinical symptoms primarily; however, there is no standardized treatment regimen and practices can vary by provider and specialty. There is conflicting evidence on the effect of high-dose steroid therapy on post-GKRS reduction of PTE, but resolution of edema has been shown to improve the quality of life.1, 16, 37 In our series, steroid treatment was commonly initiated by the radiation oncology team based on clinical symptoms, but there was no standardization between providers. We found that patients with greater average tumor volumes, a greater number of lesions, and a worse initial KPS were more likely to require steroid therapy after GKRS, but the results were not statistically significant. Further investigation is needed to assess the role of high-dose steroids on GKRS for V4 metastases and associated PTE. Furthermore, PTE response for intracranial metastases after GKRS depends on primary tumor histology, and primary renal cell carcinoma (RCC) has shown particularly poor PTE control after GKRS.1, 17 Our series had one primary RCC patient who was transitioned to hospice and died 1 month after initial GKRS, without any clinical or radiographic follow-up. Even though this patient (patient D; Table 3) was receiving steroid therapy, it is possible that oral therapy was not adequate and that there was some component of worsening PTE that contributed to clinical decline in this patient. As our results do not offer any additional insights into RCC metastases and their response to GKRS, further investigation is needed.

There are several factors to consider for appropriate patient selection for GKRS. First, prior or concurrent corticosteroid use should be considered. Previous studies have found that corticosteroid use could be associated with reduced radiosensitivity and that it can also be a proxy for disease aggressiveness, given that corticosteroids are usually used due to cerebral edema.40–42 The number and size of metastatic lesions should also be taken into consideration. As discussed previously, while not significant, patients with larger and a greater number of lesions were more likely to need steroid therapy after GKRS. Another factor to consider is the histology of the primary cancer. Along with specific tumor types being associated with a higher likelihood of having a worse PTE response (e.g., RCC), some tumor types do not respond appropriately and can be effectively controlled with GKRS.1, 17 Furthermore, tumor burden, treatment status, and frailty should be considered as well. Patients with larger systemic tumor burdens and/or actively undergoing oncological treatment would be unable to sustain the stress of undergoing an invasive surgical procedure.

This study has several limitations as a single-center retrospective chart review. The availability of records was limited by the electronic medical records available at the time of review, and paper charting was not available for review, which limited our sample size. The data obtained are limited by the accuracy of clinical charting, which was not consistent between provider and specialty. There was no standardized documentation of the justification for steroid therapy initiation, dosage, or duration between providers. There were poor follow-ups, given the nature of this patient population; several patients had significant clinical decline and transition to hospice, which limited accurate follow-up documentation at our institution. However, despite these limitations, we hope that the findings of this retrospective review offer valuable insights into the management of V4 metastases with radiosurgery alone. Areas of further study should include multicenter prospective studies with a larger sample size.
