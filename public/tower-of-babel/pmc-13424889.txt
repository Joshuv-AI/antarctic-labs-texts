Case report

A 70-year-old woman was referred to the department of dermatology for further evaluation of a haematoma-like lesion on the right arm. The patient had undergone mastectomy with simultaneous lymphadenectomy and adjuvant radiotherapy 21 years previously due to mammary carcinoma in the right breast. She had experienced recurrent cellulitis and chronic lymphoedema on her right arm ever since. The haematoma-like lesion had developed on her right arm in the year prior to presentation, slowly increasing in size. Physical examination revealed a livid, red-yellowish swelling on a not-well-circumscribed large area of the right forearm and adjacent upper arm (Figure 1a, b).

Livid, red-yellowish swelling on the forearm and adjacent upper arm, shown from the (a) ventral and (b) dorsal aspects; (c) sonography of the lesion revealed increased, irregular dermal and subcutaneous vessels with high perfusion.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which had previously been requested by the referring gynaecology department, had already been performed prior to the patient’s presentation at our centre. This examination had shown subcutaneous oedema consistent with a haematoma, thus supporting the initial clinical diagnosis. However, given the protracted course of the haematoma-like lesion with progressive rather than regressive changes in the weeks following MRI, the patient was referred to our clinic for further investigation.

In B-mode ultrasound, the lesion appeared as a poorly demarcated, heterogeneous hypoechoic area involving the dermis and subcutis, with partial loss of normal tissue stratification and no clear capsule formation. Multiple irregular anechoic and hypoechoic structures were visible within the lesion. High-resolution sonography revealed increased and irregular dermal and subcutaneous vasculature with marked hyperperfusion (Figure 1c), a finding that would be atypical for a haematoma, which usually lacks internal vascular flow.

Histopathological examination of a deep skin biopsy specimen showed an increase of small, irregular vessels lined by highly pleomorphic, large endothelial cells expressing ERG, CD31, D2-40 and c-Myc. The endothelial cells demonstrated a high proliferation rate using the Ki67-antibody (Figure 2). These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of cutaneous angiosarcoma (cAS).

Following the diagnosis of cAS, the patient underwent computed tomography staging, which revealed no evidence of metastatic disease. Based on multidisciplinary consensus, systemic therapy with paclitaxel was initiated.

Discussion

cAS represents a rare and highly aggressive subtype of soft-tissue sarcoma originating from endothelial cells, and is frequently associated with prior radiation therapy or chronic lymphoedema.1 The specific clinical scenario described in this case – angiosarcoma arising in a patient with postmastectomy lymphoedema – is referred to as Stewart–Treves syndrome.2 This condition carries a poor prognosis due to its rapid progression and high recurrence rate, with a median survival of only 2.5 years.3

cAS exhibits a wide range of clinical presentations and has a low incidence rate, with approximately 3 cases per 1 000 000 person-years reported in the USA in 2020.4 Therefore, the diagnosis of cAS is challenging, and MRI may aid in narrowing the differential diagnosis. However, given that most cases of cAS of the extremities are superficial and may not be detectable by MRI, its diagnostic utility is limited; therefore, it is not recommended for primary diagnosis of cAS.5 The gold standard for the diagnosis of cAS is biopsy with consequent histopathological assessment. However, cAS frequently mimics other dermatological conditions – such as haematoma, haemangioma or other benign and malignant tumours – which may not prompt biopsy if overtly suspicious clinical features are absent. In these cases, sonographic assessment may offer a sensitive, noninvasive diagnostic adjunct to further characterize the lesion. On ultrasound, cAS typically appears as a hypoechoic or heterogeneous subcutaneous lesion with poorly defined margins and marked hypervascularity.6 Such sonographic findings should be followed by a skin biopsy to establish a definitive diagnosis. Early recognition of cAS is critical to guide appropriate management, as therapeutic delays markedly increase disease-specific mortality.

This case highlights the often inconspicuous clinical presentation of cAS and emphasizes the importance of early recognition, which can be facilitated by sonographic ­evaluation.
