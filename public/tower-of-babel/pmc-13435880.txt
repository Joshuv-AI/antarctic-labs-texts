Comparison of objective sleep variables in schizophrenia with comorbid insomnia treated with dual orexin receptor antagonists versus benzodiazepine receptor agonists
Authors: Yusuke Arai, Kazuhiro Suzuki, Akira Tanaka, Yuta Kuraishi, Satoshi Osagawa, Kentaro Saito, Erika Nakashizuka, Toshinori Nakamura, Daimei Sasayama, Shinsuke Washizuka
Published: 2026

Insomnia is prevalent in patients with schizophrenia and represents a clinically important issue associated with symptom exacerbation. 1 Approximately half of the patients with schizophrenia in Japan are prescribed hypnotic medications. 2 Benzodiazepine receptor agonists (BZRAs) are widely used for insomnia; however, their use is associated with adverse effects, such as dependence and cognitive impairment. 3 Dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) have emerged as a novel therapeutic option. Evidence from randomized controlled trials and network meta‐analyses has demonstrated the efficacy and safety of DORAs for insomnia. 4 However, evidence regarding insomnia comorbid with psychiatric disorders remains limited, and data focusing on patients with schizophrenia are scarce. 5 In this study, we aimed to compare objective sleep parameters between patients with schizophrenia and comorbid insomnia receiving DORAs and those receiving BZRAs.

This was a single‐center observational study. We included patients aged 18–65 years who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) criteria for schizophrenia and had been hospitalized for at least 1 year in a chronic psychiatric ward at Kurita Hospital from 2024 to 2025. Eligible participants met the criteria for chronic insomnia according to the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition (ICSD‐3) and had been receiving either a DORA (suvorexant or lemborexant) or a BZRA (including Z‐drugs) at bedtime for at least 4 consecutive weeks. Forty‐five patients were included (DORA group: n = 22; BZRA group: n = 23). Objective sleep parameters were assessed using actigraphy over 7 consecutive days, and mean values were calculated. During the actigraphy period, as‐needed medications were prohibited. Days with <80% wear time were excluded. Sleep–wake states were analyzed using the Cole–Kripke algorithm in ActiLife 6 (ActiGraph). The primary outcomes were sleep latency (SL), total sleep time (TST), wake after sleep onset (WASO), and sleep efficiency (SE). Demographic and pharmacological data were extracted from electronic medical records. During the actigraphy assessment period, clinical evaluations were conducted using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS), Drug‐Induced Extrapyramidal Symptoms Scale, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, and Epworth Sleepiness Scale.

Group comparisons were performed using t‐tests. Multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted for variables identified as significant in the group comparisons, adjusting for age, sex, and psychiatric symptom severity (BPRS). As this was a preliminary study, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied.

Baseline characteristics are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table 1. No significant differences were noted in age, sex, age at onset, years of education, antipsychotic dose, anticholinergic dose, BMI, or psychiatric symptom severity between groups. Comparisons of objective sleep parameters are shown in Table 1. In unadjusted analyses, the DORA group showed significantly shorter SL than the BZRA group (2.4 ± 3.1 min vs. 7.6 ± 11.3 min, p = 0.04). No significant differences were observed in TST, WASO, or SE. In multivariate linear regression analyses (Supporting Information S1: Table S2), the association between DORA use and shorter SL was attenuated and was not statistically significant.

We compared objective sleep parameters between DORA‐ and BZRA‐treated patients with schizophrenia and comorbid insomnia. Although DORAs improve subjective insomnia symptoms in depression, 6 and eszopiclone has shown efficacy in schizophrenia, 7 evidence for DORAs in schizophrenia remains lacking. Thus, our exploratory findings provide preliminary descriptive data on objective sleep in DORA‐treated patients with schizophrenia and comorbid insomnia.

In a phase III trial of lemborexant for insomnia, significant improvements in SL and WASO were observed compared with extended‐release zolpidem at day 29/30. 8 In contrast, we assessed patients in a steady‐state condition after at least 1 month of treatment and found shorter SL in the DORA group in the unadjusted analysis. Differences between studies may be explained by variations in study design, concomitant medications, and sample size, as well as potential pathophysiological differences specific to schizophrenia. However, the observed SL of 2.4 min appears shorter than expected clinically and should be interpreted cautiously. Actigraphy estimates sleep–wake states based on body movement and may misclassify quiet wakefulness as sleep, 9 particularly in chronically hospitalized patients with schizophrenia who may have reduced motor activity. Therefore, the shorter SL observed in the DORA group may partly reflect underestimation of wakefulness and should not be interpreted as evidence of superior sleep‐onset efficacy of DORAs.

Some limitations should be noted when interpreting the present findings. First, this was a small, single‐center observational study, and causal inference is not possible. Second, baseline insomnia severity before the initiation of hypnotic treatment was not assessed using validated instruments, which limits the comparability of the two groups and raises the possibility of confounding by indication. Third, the BZRA group included heterogeneous agents with different pharmacological profiles, including benzodiazepines and Z‐drugs, and some patients received combination therapy.

In conclusion, DORAs showed no clear disadvantage compared with BZRAs in objective sleep outcomes among patients with schizophrenia and comorbid insomnia. Further prospective studies with larger samples and baseline insomnia assessments are needed to clarify their clinical utility.
