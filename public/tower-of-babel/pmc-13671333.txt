Concentrated growth factor in oral bone regeneration: A narrative review
Authors: Qi Yang, Yinghua Fu, Zhixin Zhang, Xiaoping Tang, Jiangling Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionConcentrated growth factor (CGF), a third-generation autologous platelet concentrate, has been increasingly used in oral bone regeneration. This review summarizes its preparation, biological mechanisms, and clinical efficacy, and discusses current limitations and future directions.MethodsWe comprehensively reviewed the literature on CGF preparation, structural features, and bioactive components, and synthesized clinical evidence across various oral bone defect scenarios.ResultsCGF forms a dense three-dimensional fibrin matrix that supports sustained release of growth factors over several days, provides structural support for cell adhesion and proliferation, and modulates the immune microenvironment by promoting macrophage M2 polarization. Through these mechanisms, CGF enhances the coupling of angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Clinically, when used alone or in combination with bone grafts, CGF reduces postoperative complications, accelerates tissue healing, and improves new bone quality in conditions including alveolar ridge preservation, periodontal intraosseous defects, sinus floor elevation, and peri-implant bone defects.ConclusionsCGF is a bioactive autologous material with multi-mechanistic benefits for bone regeneration. However, its clinical translation is constrained by a lack of standardized preparation protocols, inter-individual variability in product quality, and insufficient high-level evidence from large-scale randomized controlled trials. Future research should prioritize protocol standardization and well-designed multicenter trials to establish its definitive role in regenerative dentistry.

Introduction

The reconstruction of oral and maxillofacial bone defects is a common challenge in clinical dentistry. These defects may result from various conditions, including alveolar bone resorption caused by periodontal disease, physiological resorption after tooth extraction, bone cavities following cyst or tumor removal, trauma, infection, and congenital disorders. Insufficient bone volume not only impairs essential functions such as mastication, speech, and facial aesthetics, but may also affect psychological well-being and overall health. Therefore, achieving both structural and functional regeneration of the jawbone is a key goal in oral rehabilitation.

Bone regeneration is not merely a process of space filling. Instead, it is a highly coordinated biological process involving inflammation, angiogenesis, cell recruitment, and extracellular matrix remodeling, requiring the spatially and temporally regulated release of multiple growth factors acting in a coordinated manner [1]. Currently, commonly used grafting materials include autografts, allografts, xenografts, and synthetic substitutes. However, each has inherent limitations. Although autogenous bone is considered the gold standard, it is associated with donor-site morbidity and limited availability. Other substitutes often lack sufficient osteoinductive potential and biological signaling, making it difficult to dynamically regulate the local microenvironment or effectively promote vascularization and new bone formation. As a result, they cannot fully replicate the natural bone healing process [[2], [3], [4]].

In this context, autologous blood-derived products have gained increasing attention in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine due to their safety, lack of immunogenicity, and high content of growth factors. Compared with first-generation platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and second-generation platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), CGF is produced using a variable-speed centrifugation protocol without the need for anticoagulants or exogenous activators, resulting in a denser and more stable three-dimensional fibrin network [5,6]. Studies have shown that CGF contains high concentrations of key growth factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). Its fibrin matrix also enables the sustained release of these bioactive molecules [7]. In addition, CGF is rich in leukocytes and CD34+ cells, which contribute to immunomodulation and multipotent differentiation [8]. These features make CGF more than a simple reservoir of growth factors. It functions as a composite regenerative system that integrates a biological scaffold, a controlled release system, and a cellular source, thereby promoting bone regeneration, enhancing soft tissue healing, and reducing postoperative complications [9].

Although CGF is increasingly used in clinical practice, its mechanisms, efficacy across different indications, and translational challenges merit systematic synthesis. Therefore, this review aims to: (1) summarize the preparation, structural characteristics, and biological components of CGF; (2) elucidate its mechanisms in modulating the regenerative microenvironment, including angiogenesis–osteogenesis coupling and immune regulation; (3) evaluate its clinical applications in various oral bone regeneration procedures; and (4) discuss current limitations and future research directions to support its standardized clinical application.

Methods

Review scope and design

High-quality randomized controlled trials on CGF are currently limited, and existing studies show substantial heterogeneity in experimental design, preparation protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up durations. We therefore conducted a narrative review rather than a systematic quantitative synthesis, covering mechanistic studies, preclinical experiments, and clinical applications. To enhance transparency, we specified the information sources, search strategy, inclusion criteria, and evidence synthesis principles.

Literature search strategy

We searched MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase for literature published between January 2010 and March 2026 on CGF in oral and maxillofacial bone formation. An update was performed in August 2026 to include recently published studies. Search terms included: “concentrated growth factor,” “CGF,” “autologous platelet concentrate,” “APC,” “bone regeneration,” “alveolar ridge preservation,” “socket preservation,” “sinus augmentation,” “sinus floor elevation,” “sinus lift,” “periodontal intrabony defect,” “peri-implant bone defect,” “peri-implantitis,” “jaw bone defect” and “jaw cyst”. A representative PubMed query was: (("CGF"[Title/Abstract] OR “concentrated growth factor*"[Title/Abstract] OR “APC"[Title/Abstract] OR “autologous platelet concentrate*"[Title/Abstract]) AND ("bone regeneration"[Title/Abstract] OR “alveolar ridge preservation"[Title/Abstract] OR “socket preservation"[Title/Abstract] OR “sinus augmentation"[Title/Abstract] OR “sinus floor elevation"[Title/Abstract] OR “sinus lift"[Title/Abstract] OR “periodontal intrabony defect*"[Title/Abstract] OR “peri-implant bone defect*"[Title/Abstract] OR “peri-implantitis"[Title/Abstract] OR “jaw bone defect*"[Title/Abstract] OR “jaw cyst*"[Title/Abstract])). Search strategies were adapted for each database. We also manually searched reference lists of included studies, key guidelines, systematic reviews, and recent narrative reviews to identify additional relevant studies.

Study selection and categorization

We included in vitro studies, animal experiments, clinical studies (case reports, observational studies, cohort studies, and randomized controlled trials), meta-analyses, and systematic reviews related to CGF. Studies unrelated to oral/maxillofacial bone regeneration, duplicate publications, conference abstracts, articles without full text, non-English publications, and studies lacking relevant outcome data were excluded.

Different study types served different inferential purposes in this review. Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews informed clinical efficacy. Cohort and prospective controlled studies assessed prognosis and outcome trends. Case series and case reports provided exploratory evidence for potential applications. In vitro and animal studies primarily elucidated biological mechanisms rather than confirmed clinical efficacy.

Data synthesis

Due to significant heterogeneity in population characteristics, treatment protocols, control groups, follow-up durations, biomarker detection methods, and outcome measures, we did not perform a formal risk-of-bias assessment, GRADE certainty assessment, or quantitative meta-analysis. Effect estimates were interpreted within the context of each original study rather than pooled across studies.

Characteristics of CGF

Preparation of CGF

The preparation of CGF is a centrifugation-based physical process. Venous blood (typically 9 mL) is collected using vacuum tubes without anticoagulants and immediately placed into a dedicated centrifuge (Medifuge). The centrifugation program consists of sequential speed changes: acceleration for 30 s, followed by 2700 rpm for 2 min, 2400 rpm for 4 min, 2700 rpm for 4 min, and 3000 rpm for 3 min, and finally deceleration for 36 s until complete stop [8].

After centrifugation, the blood separates into three layers: a platelet-poor plasma (PPP) layer at the top, a middle CGF layer with a gel-like consistency, and a lower red blood cells (RBCs) layer, as shown in Fig. 1. Depending on clinical needs, CGF can be further processed into different forms. A dense membrane can be used as a barrier in guided bone regeneration, whereas a gel form can be mixed with bone graft materials for implantation. Liquid or loosely structured CGF can be applied for injection or soft tissue filling [10,11].Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the preparation process of CGF. Structure and composition of CGF

Scanning electron microscopy reveals the unique microstructure of CGF. It consists of interwoven fibrin fibers of varying thicknesses, forming a dense, interconnected three-dimensional network [8]. Compared to PRF, CGF typically exhibits coarser fibrin bundles and a denser structure, thereby enhancing mechanical strength and structural stability [5,6]. This porous fibrin scaffold offers several biological advantages. First, it provides a large surface area and spatial guidance for the adhesion, migration, and proliferation of cells (such as osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells). Second, its interconnected pore structure facilitates the diffusion of oxygen and nutrients, as well as the removal of metabolic waste. Most importantly, this dense fibrin matrix protects the embedded growth factors and cells from rapid enzymatic degradation; this property enables the sustained, slow release of bioactive components, thereby mimicking the natural signaling patterns of the physiological wound healing process [6].

CGF is not merely a physical scaffold, but also a repository of bioactive factors and a potential cellular source (Table 1). The variable-speed centrifugation process effectively activates platelets, prompting the release of various growth factors from α-granules [12]. The major growth factors contained in CGF include VEGF, PDGF, TGF-β1, BMPs, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1). VEGF plays a central role in angiogenesis by promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and vascular permeability [13,14]. PDGF is a potent mitogen and chemokine that recruits mesenchymal stem cells and promotes their proliferation, while also participating in angiogenesis [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. TGF-β1 modulates inflammation, promotes extracellular matrix synthesis, and induces osteogenic differentiation [[19], [20], [21]]. BMPs are considered potent osteogenic inducers and can directly induce the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts [22]. IGF-1 promotes cell proliferation and metabolism and exerts synergistic effects with other growth factors. These growth factors do not act independently but rather form a complex signaling network with synergistic and cascading effects, collectively regulating the bone regeneration process. CGF is also rich in leukocytes and CD34+ cells. Leukocytes play a key role in early immune defense and inflammation regulation. In particular, macrophages can be induced by signaling molecules in CGF to polarize toward the M2 phenotype, which promotes tissue repair and regeneration [23]. The CD34+ cell population comprises stem-like cells with multipotent differentiation potential. Researchers have successfully isolated CGF-derived progenitor cells (CPCs) that possess self-renewal capacity and can differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and endothelial cells [24]. Comparison with other platelet concentrates

Although CGF, PRP, and PRF are all autologous blood products designed to enhance tissue regeneration, they differ significantly in their preparation protocols, structural characteristics, cellular composition, and biological functions [25].

PRP, as a first-generation platelet concentrate, is characterized by a high platelet concentration but requires the use of anticoagulants and exogenous activators, which may affect its biological performance. PRF, as a second-generation product, can form a fibrin matrix without additives, overcoming some of these limitations and enabling a more sustained release of growth factors. CGF, commonly classified as a third-generation concentrate, is prepared using a variable-speed centrifugation protocol, forming a denser fibrin network with enhanced mechanical strength and a more sustained release of bioactive molecules. Theoretically, this may offer superior regenerative potential; however, its clinical superiority over PRF has not been consistently confirmed in head-to-head randomized controlled trials. The multifaceted mechanism network of CGF in promoting bone regeneration

As a third-generation autologous platelet concentrate, CGF is not merely a delivery vehicle for growth factors but rather a composite tissue-engineering scaffold with multiple biological activities, as shown in Fig. 2. Its dense three-dimensional fibrin network provides essential mechanical support and spatial guidance for cell growth, while leukocytes and CD34+ cells play crucial roles in immune regulation and tissue reconstruction. Fibrin scaffold

CGF's dense three-dimensional fibrin network stabilizes early blood clots, provides physical support for cell migration and tissue ingrowth, and enables the sustained release of bioactive factors. Unlike the burst-like release of PRP, the fibrin matrix of CGF effectively protects growth factors and sustains their release for over 14 days. This mimics the physiological pattern of sequential growth factor expression during natural healing and demonstrates superior biological effects compared to methods that release large amounts of growth factors all at once [6,26]. This sustained biological stimulation prevents the premature disappearance of signaling and ensures an orderly transition of the repair process from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative and remodeling phases.

Immunomodulatory effects

Traditional regenerative materials often perform poorly in inflammatory environments. However, owing to the presence of leukocytes and bioactive factors, CGF is considered to possess notable immunomodulatory properties. Studies have confirmed that CGF can induce macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory, pro-repair M2 phenotype by activating signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT [23,27]. This shift reduces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), while increasing the release of pro-regenerative factors such as VEGF and TGF-β. Thus, CGF appears to facilitate a shift of the bone defect microenvironment from a chronic inflammatory state toward one that is more favorable for tissue repair and regeneration. This provides a crucial theoretical basis for treating inflammation-related bone defects such as periodontitis and peri-implantitis.

Osteogenic-angiogenic coupling

Angiogenesis is a prerequisite for osteogenesis, delivering essential cells, oxygen, and nutrients for new bone formation. Conversely, newly formed bone provides stable structural support, ensuring the stability and maturation of new vessels. The two processes are highly synergistic in both time and space, jointly driving bone regeneration [28]. CGF efficiently promotes endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and new vessel formation through the sustained release of VEGF, PDGF, and IGF-1 [29,30]. Simultaneously, it powerfully induces osteogenic differentiation via released BMPs and TGF-β1. For instance, BMPs can upregulate VEGF expression, and the endothelial cells within new vessels themselves can secrete osteogenic signaling molecules [31]. CGF simultaneously provides the key signals required to drive these two coupled processes, thereby facilitating rapid new bone formation and maturation on a well-vascularized bed [32,33].

Based on its structural and biological properties, CGF has been widely applied in oral and maxillofacial bone regeneration. It is easy to prepare and flexible in clinical application. Alveolar ridge preservation (Table 3)

Following tooth extraction, the alveolar ridge inevitably undergoes physiological resorption and remodeling, most pronounced within the first 3-6 months, particularly on the buccal aspect. Studies have shown that by 6 months post-extraction, vertical bone height decreases by approximately 11%-22%, and horizontal bone width by approximately 29%-63%, posing aesthetic and functional challenges for subsequent implant-supported restorations [38]. Alveolar ridge preservation aims to implant bone substitute material into the alveolar socket at the time of tooth extraction, thereby establishing a more favorable hard and soft tissue foundation for future implant-supported restoration.

The fibrin network in CGF stabilizes blood clots and delays their resorption; the growth factors released by CGF enhance osteoblast activity; and its immunomodulatory properties help create a stable healing environment. Clinical evidence demonstrates that implanting CGF gel into the extraction socket effectively reduces vertical and horizontal bone resorption at 3 months postoperatively and promotes the formation of a denser, more mature new bone structure [37,39]. Assadi et al. evaluated the efficacy of CGF in anterior tooth site preservation; the results showed that CGF alone reduced early postoperative horizontal alveolar bone resorption and maintained buccal-lingual and mesio-distal bone width. Furthermore, CGF application alleviated patients’ postoperative pain and discomfort and effectively promoted soft tissue healing [34]. Elayah et al. evaluated the efficacy of CGF in the posterior region and found that in the CGF-only group, the height of the buccolingual bone plate, the buccolingual alveolar ridge width, and new bone density were all significantly higher than those in the control group; the periodontal health of adjacent teeth also improved significantly [39]. Similar results were observed when CGF was used in combination with bone substitute materials [40,41]. Compared with bone grafting materials alone, the addition of CGF not only significantly reduced alveolar bone resorption but also yielded higher bone grayscale values, while reducing the need for additional bone grafting during implant placement. Histological analysis revealed a significantly higher percentage of new bone formation and a significantly lower percentage of residual graft material in the CGF-combined groups [35,36]. This suggests that CGF not only provides bioactive signals but may also optimize the healing environment for bone graft materials by improving early-stage blood supply and cell recruitment in the Periodontal intrabony defect (Table 4)

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the periodontal supporting tissues caused by bacteria. It results from the interaction between the host's immune inflammatory defense response and the microorganisms in dental plaque. Periodontal bone defects, particularly vertical bone defects and furcation lesions, are difficult to regenerate due to their complex anatomical morphology, limited blood supply, and persistent inflammatory state [49].

CGF provides an “active scaffold” for periodontal tissue regeneration; its rich array of growth factors synergistically regulates the immune microenvironment and promotes tissue repair. In vitro studies have confirmed that CGF promotes the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner by activating key signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin [33,50]. Furthermore, in a simulated inflammatory environment, CGF can reverse the inhibitory effect of TNF-α on the osteogenic function of human periodontal ligament cells (hPDLCs) and upregulate the expression of osteogenic genes such as RUNX2, OSX, and OCN, demonstrating dual anti-inflammatory and osteogenic potential [51].

Clinical studies have shown that CGF alone can improve periodontal parameters in intraosseous defects [48,52]. A comparative study found that CGF was more effective than PRF in treating intraosseous defects but less effective than autologous bone grafts [46]. Khan et al. compared the efficacy of injectable platelet-rich fibrin (i-PRF) and CGF as single autologous materials in periodontal regeneration of three-walled intraosseous defects. The 6-month follow-up results showed that both materials significantly improved probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and intraosseous defect depth (p < 0.001), while CGF produced superior results in terms of PD reduction, CAL gain, and radiographic bone filling [42]. Lei et al. confirmed through in vitro experiments that both Advanced platelet-rich fibrin(A-PRF) and CGF can continuously release PDGF-AB, VEGF, and TGF-β1 for up to 14 days. Their retrospective analysis of 45 patients with periodontitis and bone defects indicated that both A-PRF and CGF can serve as effective adjunct materials for guided tissue regeneration (GTR), significantly promoting bone regeneration in intraosseous defects, with comparable clinical efficacy [6]. When used in combination with bone grafting materials, CGF demonstrated promising efficacy in periodontal regeneration compared to bone grafting alone [53]. Gan et al. combined CGF with GTR and Bio-Oss bone powder in 78 patients with periodontitis and bone defects; at 6 months postoperatively, the CGF group demonstrated superior outcomes compared to the group treated with Bio-Oss alone in terms of periodontal parameters, new bone quality, incidence of postoperative complications, and soft tissue healing. This indicates that the combination of CGF with minimally invasive bone grafting effectively promotes periodontal tissue regeneration and new bone formation, representing a safe, minimally invasive, and highly effective treatment strategy [43]. Elbadawy et al. combined CGF with bovine xenograft bone and a collagen membrane in 20 patients with chronic periodontitis and intraosseous defects. At 6 months postoperatively, compared with the group treated with bovine xenograft bone plus collagen membrane alone, the CGF combination group showed significantly better outcomes in terms of PD reduction, CAL gain, and reduction in bone defect depth (P ≤ 0.05), however, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of BMD, bleeding on probing (BOP), and plaque index (PI) (P > 0.05), indicating that the combination of CGF with bone grafting materials can effectively promote periodontal tissue regeneration and bone defect repair; the use of CGF in periodontal regenerative therapy is recommended [44]. Similarly, Wu et al. applied CGF combined with synthetic bone powder/autogenous bone powder to the treatment of periodontal bone defects. The results showed that the periodontal index, masticatory efficiency, and osteocalcin levels in the CGF group were all significantly better than those in the control group, suggesting that CGF enhances osteoblast activity [45]. Iao et al. used CGF combined with DBBM to treat superficial subperiosteal defects. After 1 year, CAL improved by an average of 2.48 mm, PD decreased by an average of 2.90 mm, and bone gain averaged 2.46 mm, maintaining a good soft and hard tissue contour [54]. Furthermore, Huidrom et al. combined CGF with GTR and Bio-Oss for the treatment of furcation defects. The results showed that the addition of CGF not only significantly improved clinical periodontal indices but also promoted both vertical and horizontal bone regeneration, establishing it as an effective periodontal regeneration strategy [55]. Maxillary sinus augmentation (Table 5)

Factors such as periodontal disease, maxillary sinus pneumatization, and alveolar bone resorption following tooth loss often result in severe bone deficiency in the maxillary posterior region, posing challenges for implant-supported restoration [62]. Maxillary sinus floor elevation refers to the procedure of elevating the Schneiderian membrane to create space between the mucosa and the inherent sinus floor bone. Bone graft material is then implanted, or endogenous blood clots are used to guide osteogenesis, thereby increasing the available bone volume and providing stable hard tissue support for dental implants [63]. Maxillary sinus floor elevation is classified into two surgical techniques: lateral sinus floor elevation (LSFE) and osteotome sinus floor elevation (OSFE) [64].

Autologous platelet concentrates release various growth factors that effectively promote cell proliferation, early vascularization, and bone remodeling, while reducing postoperative inflammation, swelling, and pain. When used in combination with bone graft materials, they improve bone quality and, in some cases, may reduce the need for autologous bone [65].

Studies have shown that in maxillary sinus elevation procedures via the alveolar crest or lateral window, the use of CGF alone as a graft material yields implant retention rates, sinus floor elevation heights, and marginal bone loss comparable to those achieved with conventional bone graft materials [59,60,66]. Sohn et al. used CGF alone in 61 cases of lateral window maxillary sinus elevation; after an average loading period of 10 months, the implant retention rate reached 98.2%, and both radiographic and histological findings confirmed reliable new bone formation, providing early clinical evidence for the osteogenic potential of CGF when used alone [7]. Through histological analysis, Ghasemirad et al. found that the proportion of new bone in the group treated with CGF alone was significantly higher than in the control group treated with allogeneic bone alone (P < 0.05), and there was less residual graft material (P < 0.05). This suggests that CGF possesses good biocompatibility, osteogenic inductive capacity, and degradability, and has superior bone regenerative capacity compared to allogeneic bone [58]. CGF can also promote early healing of the Schneiderian membrane; when used in combination with bone graft materials, it accelerates graft vascularization, promotes intra-sinus bone regeneration, reduces bone resorption, and increases bone density. A study by Shahood et al. demonstrated that, in OSFE simultaneous implant placement, the combination of Bio-Oss Collagen and CGF outperformed Bio-Oss alone in maintaining the osteogenic space and reducing long-term bone resorption, while bone density was comparable to that of the Bio-Oss group (both significantly superior to the non-bone-grafting group); implant survival rates exceeded 95% in all three groups, and the combination group exhibited the least postoperative pain and the highest patient satisfaction [57]. A retrospective study by Dipalma et al. utilized the CGF-autologous dentin granule mixture for lateral window maxillary sinus floor elevation with simultaneous implant placement. An 84-month follow-up revealed an average increase in bone height of approximately 8.6 mm, with new bone density ranging from 700 to 850 HU and long-term bone stability; all 98 implants survived without complications. Peri-implant bone defects (Table 6)

Peri-implant bone defects typically arise from insufficient bone volume at the time of implant placement or from diseases like peri-implantitis. If bone volume is deficient, bone augmentation procedures (e.g., GBR) can be performed simultaneously with implant placement. Peri-implantitis, a common complication after implant restoration, is characterized by progressive bone resorption around the implant, loss of soft tissue attachment, pocket formation, and can ultimately lead to implant loosening and loss [73]. Lokwani et al. suggested that CGF can significantly improve the quality of new bone around implants and, used alone or with bone grafts, may facilitate vertical bone gain [74]. A randomized controlled trial by Karthik et al. confirmed that CGF applied at implant placement significantly increased peri-implant bone density at 6 months (P < 0.001), though it had no significant effect on crestal bone resorption, suggesting that CGF primarily improves bone quality rather than reducing early marginal bone loss [75].

By rapidly initiating osteogenesis and vascularization, CGF provides a more favorable initial healing microenvironment for implants, potentially enhancing primary stability. Palermo et al. and Atilgan et al. coated implant surfaces with CGF. Scanning electron microscopy revealed a dense fibrin network formed on the implant surface, capable of continuously releasing growth factors like VEGF, TGF-β1, BMP-2, as well as MMP-2 and MMP-9, for up to 28 days. In vitro experiments demonstrated that CGF-coated implants significantly enhanced endothelial cell adhesion and induced osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. This effect upregulated cytokines involved in early osseointegration (TNF-α, RANKL, OPG) and osteogenic genes (RUNX2, OCN), leading to increased mineralized nodule formation and promoting bone remodeling. Clinical trials confirmed that patients receiving CGF-coated implants had significantly lower postoperative VAS scores and achieved higher Implant Stability Quotient values compared to conventional implants [22,76]. These studies indicate that CGF-coated implants, via sustained release of bioactive factors, promote angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation, thereby accelerating osseointegration, reducing postoperative discomfort, and improving peri-implant tissue healing, showing significant clinical potential.

A systematic review by Qadir et al., which included 29 clinical studies on growth factors in implant dentistry, found that both PRF and CGF can enhance implant stability, promote bone regeneration, and accelerate soft tissue healing, although the evidence remains conflicting; standardized protocols have not yet been established [77]. Zohary et al. enrolled 210 patients with periapical lesions undergoing immediate implant placement. After tooth extraction, sockets were filled with CGF, PRF, or left for natural healing before simultaneous implant placement. Both CGF and PRF significantly promoted hard tissue healing and improved soft tissue inflammation, with 100% of implants achieving optimal health at 24 months. CGF was superior to PRF in reducing postoperative pain, improving BOP, and reducing periapical lesion size (P < 0.05), but the two were comparable in plaque control, gingival aesthetics, and crestal bone level [70]. Bajaj et al., in immediate implant placement in the aesthetic anterior zone, used CGF combined with demineralized freeze-dried bone allograft to fill the jump space. This effectively promoted bone regeneration and maintained the peri-implant bone contour, achieving ideal aesthetic outcomes [69].

The core of peri-implantitis treatment lies in infection control and achieving re-osseointegration. Combining CGF with debridement leverages its growth factors and immunomodulatory properties to help control inflammation while promoting bone regeneration in the defect area. Jia et al. evaluated the clinical effect of implantoplasty combined with Bio-Oss Collagen and CGF membrane in treating peri-implantitis. Results showed significant improvement: marginal bone level improved from 4.01 mm preoperatively to 1.51 mm postoperatively, probing depth decreased from 6.54 mm to 3.61 mm, and bleeding on probing decreased from 79.17% to 23.38% (P < 0.001). Implant survival rate was 100%, with an overall treatment success rate of 31.9%. The study indicated that this combined surgical approach effectively controls peri-implantitis, promotes bone regeneration, and maintains implant stability [78]. Repair of jaw bone cavities (Table 7)

Cystic lesions of the jaws can compress the surrounding bone, leading to bone resorption, jaw defects, and even facial deformities. Currently, surgery is the primary treatment method [83]. However, the removal of medium-to large-sized maxillofacial cysts often leaves behind large bone cavities. These cavities have a long healing period and may lead to issues such as bone void formation, secondary infection, or pathological fractures. Even when bone substitute materials are implanted into the bone cavity, poor osteogenic potential and insufficient blood supply at the center of the cavity can still impair bone formation or potentially increase the risk of infection [84]. CGF, as an autologous blood product with excellent mechanical properties and strong biological activity, offers a new solution for the repair of such bone defects.

CGF can promote the healing of bone cavities and the restoration of blood supply. A case report by Franco et al. described a patient who developed bony defects on both the buccal and palatal walls following enucleation of a maxillary anterior cyst. The bone cavity was filled with CGF alone; radiographic and clinical follow-up one year postoperatively confirmed steady bone regeneration and the absence of symptoms in the affected tooth, suggesting that CGF can effectively promote the regeneration of postoperative bone defects following maxillary cyst excision [85]. The combined use of CGF with bone grafting materials can produce synergistic effects, promoting early osteogenesis and improving the density and mechanical properties of new bone. Joshi et al. applied a chitosan-CGF composite in the repair of bone cavities following cystectomy, achieving a higher trabecular bone area and denser bone tissue [79]. Fang et al. combined CGF with Bio-Oss for the repair of maxillary bone defects in 40 cases. The results showed that at 1 and 12 weeks postoperatively, serum bone alkaline phosphatase (BAP) and calcitonin levels in the CGF group were significantly higher than those in the Bio-Oss-only group (P < 0.01), suggesting that CGF promotes early osteogenic activity. CBCT scans at 6 months postoperatively showed that bone density in the CGF group was significantly higher than in the control group (P < 0.001); radiographically, the bone defect had completely healed, whereas the control group still exhibited clearly defined borders, indicating that CGF can effectively improve bone healing quality and regenerative outcomes [81]. Li et al. used CGF in combination with autologous iliac cancellous bone and a collagen-hydroxyapatite composite bone material to repair extensive mandibular defects, finding that this approach promoted early bone formation and reduced the local inflammatory response [80]. Talaat et al. combined autologous bone marrow concentrate with CGF for the repair of bone defects following mandibular lesion excision in 20 patients. A 12-month follow-up revealed that the rate of reduction in bone defect size in the treatment group was significantly higher than that in the natural healing group, and improvements in bone density were significantly better than those in the control group at both 6 and 12 months (P < 0.05), demonstrating that this combined approach can effectively accelerate the regeneration of mandibular bone defects [82].

The efficacy of CGF is not limited to the repair of post-cystectomy bone cavities. In maxillary bone defects caused by fractures, a randomized controlled trial by Malik et al. found that CGF combined with allograft bone achieved bone density improvements comparable to those of CGF combined with autologous bone. This suggests that CGF can effectively enhance the osteogenic potential of allograft bone, providing an effective alternative for patients who cannot undergo or are intolerant to autologous bone grafting, while avoiding the risk of donor site complications [86].

Discussion

This review comprehensively synthesizes the current evidence regarding the biological characteristics and clinical applications of CGF in oral bone regeneration. Unlike traditional biomaterials, which primarily provide structural support, CGF represents a multifunctional autologous system. It integrates a scaffold structure, the delivery of bioactive factors, and cellular components. This integration enables CGF to actively modulate the regenerative microenvironment rather than passively participating in tissue repair. Its dense fibrin network not only provides mechanical stability but also enables spatiotemporal control over growth factor release. Compared to the rapid, transient release observed in PRP, CGF exhibits a more sustained release profile that more closely mimics the physiological wound healing process. Furthermore, the presence of leukocytes and CD34+ cells suggests that CGF may actively participate in immune modulation and tissue remodeling. This distinguishes CGF from traditional graft materials, which typically lack immune-modulatory capabilities. Its ability to simultaneously modulate inflammation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis makes CGF a unique tool in regenerative dentistry.

CGF has shown potential benefits across several clinical scenarios, including alveolar ridge preservation, periodontal intraosseous defects, sinus floor elevation, peri-implant bone defects, and repair of bony cavities caused by cystic jaw lesions, in accelerating soft tissue healing, reducing postoperative discomfort, and enhancing early bone regeneration. However, a critical analysis of the existing evidence reveals significant heterogeneity. Most studies are characterized by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and single-center designs. Although reports of RCTs are increasing, differences in study design, outcome measures, and adjunctive materials make direct comparisons difficult. Importantly, while many studies suggest that CGF improves bone quality and early healing, its superiority over established methods, such as allografts, autografts, or other platelet-rich products like PRF, remains inconclusive. In most studies, CGF has shown comparable rather than superior results, suggesting it plays a supplementary rather than a replacement role.

Despite its vast potential, several key obstacles limit the widespread clinical adoption and standardization of CGF.

First, preparation protocols lack standardization. The preparation of CGF is highly dependent on centrifugation equipment. Although the Medifuge (Silfradent, Italy) is the most commonly used dedicated device for CGF preparation, there are currently a variety of other devices available for producing platelet concentrates; for example, CGF in several published studies was prepared using centrifuges from other brands [43,44,46,47,69], and some studies did not specify the centrifuge model used [6,48,71,80,81]. Centrifuges of different brands and models may vary in rotor radius, angle, and acceleration/deceleration curves, which could result in CGF prepared using different devices being biologically non-equivalent, posing challenges for cross-study comparisons [87]. Furthermore, researchers employ different preparation protocols (such as centrifugation speed, duration, type of blood collection tube, and volume of blood collected) [43,61,69,80]; these differences can, to some extent, affect the fibrin matrix structure, cell content, and growth factor release kinetics of the final product [88]. Studies have indicated that the material (rough or smooth, silicone-coated plastic tubes or glass tubes) and shape (round-bottom or conical-bottom) of blood collection tubes can both influence the structure and composition of CGF [[89], [90], [91]]. Differences in preparation equipment and protocols may be one of the reasons for the conflicting results observed in current studies.

Second, individual patient differences affect CGF quality. As an autologous blood product, the quality of CGF essentially depends on the quality of the donor blood, which varies significantly among individuals. Borsani et al. found substantial variations in growth factor release among donors, suggesting that individualized factors must be considered for clinical application [92]. Donor-related factors, including age, systemic diseases, and medication use, may affect platelet function and count, thereby influencing the concentration and release of growth factors, which in turn affects cell proliferation and clinical efficacy [[93], [94], [95], [96]]. However, there is currently no established framework for patient stratification or individualized application. Furthermore, most studies have not performed quantitative analysis of the components in the prepared CGF, making it impossible to assess individual variations in CGF quality and thereby limiting the generalizability of the reported results.

Third, while CGF is available in various forms, there is a lack of quality control standards. The application of CGF is highly flexible, including clots, lyophilized products, extracts, and membranes [97]. Different forms of CGF exhibit varying fibrin structures, cell contents, and growth factor concentrations; consequently, their efficacy in promoting osteogenesis may differ [98,99]. Ma et al. compared CGF prepared from three different blood collection tubes and concluded that gel phase CGF (GPCGF) had higher growth factor content and superior ability to promote cell growth and migration compared to liquid phase CGF (LPCGF) [100]. However, there are currently no guidelines specifying which form is suitable for which clinical scenario. In vitro studies suggest that the osteogenic effects of CGF are optimal at specific concentrations; however, due to variations in cell types, species origins, and culture systems used across different studies, no unified conclusion has yet been reached [[101], [102], [103], [104]]. In clinical practice, when dealing with bone defects of varying sizes, the dosage of CGF and the mixing ratio of CGF to bone graft materials remain unclear, with only a few studies mentioning specific ratios [36,43,72]. Preliminary histological and clinical studies have confirmed that CGF can be divided into three distinct layers: the upper White Part (WP), the middle Buffy Coat (BC), and the lower Red Part (RP), and that the osteogenic efficacy of these different parts may vary [105,106]. Recently, Wang et al. attempted to implement quality control by extracting the liquid component of CGF and measuring TGF-β1 levels; however, validation of this method remains limited to the laboratory scale [107]. This heterogeneity among the various forms of CGF application may be one of the reasons for the conflicting results across trials.

Fourth, the overall level of existing clinical evidence remains insufficient. Although multiple studies have demonstrated CGF's potential to promote early healing and improve bone quality, existing research generally suffers from small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and predominantly single-center designs. Significant heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of study design (RCTs, retrospective studies, case series), outcome measures (histological analysis, imaging parameters, clinical scores), and follow-up duration, making it extremely difficult to directly compare results across different studies. In particular, regarding bone stability and long-term remodeling effects, there is currently a lack of sufficient long-term follow-up data and histological confirmation. Therefore, future studies, building upon the findings of different research designs, should focus on the following key areas: (1) Establish a standardized CGF preparation and quality control system, including operational guidelines for key variables such as centrifugation parameters, blood collection tube types, and post-preparation processing procedures, and explore quality control methods based on growth factor content testing; (2) Conduct high-quality, large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials with sufficient follow-up periods, using standardized outcome measures, to verify the precise efficacy and safety of CGF in various clinical scenarios of oral bone regeneration; (3) Systematically evaluate differences in osteogenic efficacy among different forms of CGF and between different internal components of CGF to provide a basis for clinical decision-making; (4) Explore stratified treatment strategies based on patient characteristics to identify which patient groups are likely to benefit most from CGF therapy. Evidence should not be considered equivalent, and existing conclusions regarding the efficacy of CGF should be interpreted according to their corresponding levels of evidence.

The following limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. First, as a narrative review, this study did not follow the systematic methodology of a meta-analysis; publication bias and selection bias may have been present in the literature screening process. Second, this review synthesizes results from in vitro experiments, animal studies, and clinical trials. Although preclinical studies provide important mechanistic insights into osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation, caution is warranted when applying these findings to clinical practice. Third, the included studies exhibited high heterogeneity in terms of study design, patient populations, CGF preparation and administration methods, follow-up periods, and outcome measures, which limits the comparability of the results. Therefore, when interpreting the conclusions of this review, readers should distinguish between high-level evidence from randomized controlled trials and exploratory evidence from case series or in vitro studies. There is an urgent need for high-quality, large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials using standardized outcome measures to validate the exact efficacy and safety of CGF in oral bone regeneration.

Conclusion

As a bioactive autologous material, CGF has demonstrated potential to promote healing and regulate the regenerative microenvironment in oral bone regeneration. However, issues such as the lack of standardized preparation, significant individual variability, and insufficient high-quality evidence remain unresolved. the current evidence supports its consideration primarily as an adjunct to established regenerative approaches rather than as a replacement for them. Future efforts should focus on establishing a standardized quality control system, conducting multicenter RCTs, and exploring stratified treatment strategies based on patient characteristics. CGF exerts a synergistic effect by integrating the three key processes of immune regulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis; however, whether it can evolve from a technical innovation into a predictable, standardized therapy remains contingent upon the accumulation of high-quality evidence in the future.
