Exploring the factors affecting career progression in informal faculty mentoring sessions within mentor and mentee relationships: a qualitative study
Authors: Manahil Niazi, Usman Mahboob, Neelofar Shaheen, Shazia Gul, Muhammad Humza Bin Saeed, Amber Kiyani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMentoring plays a pivotal role in mentees’ professional advancement. However, the factors that affect career progression in informal mentoring relationships, especially with respect to faculty members, have not been extensively explored. This study aimed to explore the factors that affect career progression in informal faculty mentorings within mentor and mentee relationships.MethodologyA Qualitative Exploratory Study was designed and conducted from May to October 2023. Faculty members with informal mentoring relationships were recruited through purposive sampling. Seven faculty mentors and eight faculty mentees from various institutions participated in the study. Semi-structured interviews were used to collect data, which were audio-recorded and verbatim transcribed. The transcripts were then analysed using NVivo Software and coded. Braun and Clark’s framework was used for the thematic analysis. The study adhered to the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the qualitative research process.ResultsA total of 76 codes emerged which were classified into six themes: (1) an ideal mentee, (2) an ideal mentor, (3) factors promoting the relationship, (4) the role of gender, (5) factors deteriorating the relationship, (6) overcoming barriers / trust-building strategies. Within each theme, mentor–mentee needs and behaviours were identified, which could lead to positive or deteriorating outcomes.ConclusionFactors affecting career progression in informal faculty mentoring sessions include mentees' positive mindset, internal motivation and clarity of vision, mentors' skills, reputation, and role modelling. Effective communication, trust, respect, and clear goals are also essential. Challenges, such as busy schedules, cross-gender mentoring, and societal biases, affect these relationships. Overcoming these barriers involves sharing experiences, psychosocial support, empathy, active listening, and feedback.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06170-y.Keywords: Informal mentoring, Faculty, Career progression, Factors, Mentor, Mentee

Introduction

Clinicians working in academia have multifaceted roles. In addition to teaching and research, they must manage clinical work while undertaking departmental management tasks [1]. These multiple roles often coincide, leading to task and time management issues [1]. On top of this, the lack of good mentoring for junior clinicians acts as an impediment to career progression [2, 3].

Mentoring is defined as supporting a person's learning and ideas with the guidance and support of an experienced, well-respected, and sympathetic mentor, with or without the need for a formal evaluation [4–6]. It has been linked to increased satisfaction, career success, and faculty retention, thereby reducing the risk of burnout [5]. This makes mentoring an essential part of the career development process of medical professionals [4–6]. According to a study by Chen et al., mentorship was identified as the primary or secondary factor impeding career advancement by 98% of junior faculty members, underscoring the importance of having a mentor for professional growth [3]. It can be used by junior faculty to acquire professional academic skills such as collegial networking, career management, and knowledge of academic medicine [6].

Mentoring has been utilised and advocated as a means of developing faculty, either through informal arrangements established by faculty themselves or through formal programs organised by academic medical centres worldwide [7, 8]. Informal mentoring is a widespread technique that benefits from the strong fit between mentors and mentees [9, 10]. This is done in friendship through mutual respect and appreciation for each other, which usually leads to a long-term relationship [9]. The main objective of informal mentoring is for a mentor and a mentee to establish an organic connection that prioritizes the sharing of pertinent advice, timely recommendations, and other information without a prescribed or planned schedule [6, 10]. Evidence suggests that informal mentoring may be more effective and last longer than formal one due to its spontaneous nature. However, they lack standardization, control, and assessment for improvement [7, 11–13]. Formal mentoring programs, on the other hand, are structured and facilitated, but they are often short-term, with the expectation that they will progress informally over time [9]. Formal faculty mentoring programs have been established globally, but developing countries such as Pakistan which have distinct sociocultural values and practices, have not made a significant contribution to the literature on this subject [7]. Only one Pakistani medical institute, Aga Khan University, has organised a formal mentoring program for its faculty [14]. Thus, the majority of the faculty, particularly those in the field of health sciences, chooses informal mentoring [15].

Although the benefits of mentoring in terms of career success have widely been established, access to mentors and quality of mentorship may not be equitable, optimal, or tailored to mentee needs, leading to variable mentorship experiences [16, 17]. Identifying the elements that contribute to both positive and negative mentorship outcomes is crucial for understanding faculty support, the shortcomings of faculty-to-faculty mentorship interactions, and areas for improvement in faculty-to-faculty mentoring experiences.

Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), developed by Lent et al. in 1994, forms the theoretical framework of this study [18]. Grounded in Bandura's social cognitive theory (1986), which postulates that people are the result of a dynamic interaction between internal subjectivity variables and external environmental ones, along with their earlier and present behaviours, SCCT stipulates three key concepts including self-efficacy and outcome expectations, career interests, and professional goals and actions [19, 20]. This theory explains how individual, behavioural, and environmental factors interact to affect an individual’s career [21]. According to the SCCT, background, contextual affordance, and contextual influences all have an impact on how people choose their careers and areas of interest by affecting their learning experiences [22, 23]. The two categories of contextual variables include overlapping aspects, such as family as well as other social circumstances, which influence a person's academic and professional performance variably at different phases [22]. Mentoring refers to contextual supports that significantly influence mentees' learning experiences, enhancing their self-efficacy in areas like social and occupational domains [24]. SCCT strongly emphasizes the role of social interactions in professional development; with mentoring fundamentally being a social interaction where mentors provide guidance, support, and feedback to the mentees. The focus of SCCT on social learning and observational learning aligns well with the dynamics of a mentor–mentee relationship. Moreover, the three principles of SCCT also support mentoring in the sense that mentoring can significantly influence mentees' self-efficacy by providing them with skills, knowledge, and confidence, shape the expectations of mentees through guidance, experiences, and feedback, and assisting mentees in setting their goals. Frameworks build on a base of known information, provide logical justifications for the links seen, and disclose fresh insights into a phenomenon [25]. This theoretical framework not only provided a literature base for the construction of interview guides, but was also used to interpret the study findings.

The purpose of the present study was to analyse informal mentoring relationships for career advancement, considering the viewpoints of mentors and mentees, to identify the factors that influence career advancement during faculty mentoring sessions.

Methodology

This qualitative enquiry was conducted from May to October 2023, after obtaining approval from the Advanced Studies and Research Board and the Institutional Ethical Review Committee of Khyber Medical University (Ref. No 1–11/IHPER/MHPE/KMU/22–36, dated 15–06-2023). This study employed a basic qualitative approach to the study design [26]. The Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research Checklist (COREQ) has been used in the reporting of this study [23]. The completed checklist is included in the Supplementary information (Annexure A).

Research team

The research team consisted of scholars with backgrounds in health professions education and qualitative research. The principal investigator, a female researcher with significant experience in mentoring dental students, undertook this project as her research thesis in Masters in Health Professions Education (MHPE). The second author, a seasoned qualitative researcher and doctorate supervisor, has extensive expertise in research design and execution. The third author, an experienced qualitative researcher, specialises in mentoring medical students, provided specific field insights. The remaining three researchers brought substantial experience in informal mentoring, adding diverse and practical insights into the execution of the research plan. The research team’s combined expertise ensured a thorough exploration of informal mentoring dynamics, advancing understanding in this field through comprehensive research.

Participant recruitment

Since this study only focused on the factors affecting career advancement in "informal" faculty mentoring sessions, mentors and mentees from the faculty who were actively involved in informal mentoring, were recruited from various medical and dental colleges of Islamabad, Abbottabad, and Peshawar using non-probability, purposive sampling technique. Fifteen participants, seven mentors and eight mentees, were recruited. The first set of participants were junior faculty members as mentees who had senior faculty members as mentors whom they approached for informal suggestions and decisions regarding career progression. Mentees were finally selected if they answered affirmatively to the following questions:

"Can you identify a person or persons who currently perform some aspect of mentoring to you?" and "Have you had a mentor in the past three years?" [2, 27].

The second set of participants were mentors, who were senior faculty members (professors, associate professors, and assistant professors) with junior faculty members as mentees. All faculty members who were either not involved in mentoring, involved in any formal mentoring program, or unwilling to participate in the study were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted between July and August 2023. After a comprehensive analysis of the literature and identification of themes, which served as the foundation for the formulation of open-ended questions, interview guides were developed using the seven steps of the questionnaire development [28]. Initially, 13 open-ended questions based on seven themes were developed for the mentors' interview guide, whereas 15 open-ended questions based on ten themes, were developed for the mentee's guide. Experts in the field of health professions education across the country were recruited based on their advanced academic qualifications, extensive professional experience, significant research contributions, and recognition in the field, to ensure a diverse and representative panel for validation of the interview guides. Subsequently, a pilot test was conducted with two participants, one mentor and one mentee, using the instruments to evaluate the flow of questions, projected interview duration, and participant comprehensibility. The final mentor interview guide (Table 1) contained 11 open-ended questions, while the final mentee’s guide contained 15 questions (Table 2).

A project summary was sent via email or WhatsApp along with a formal invitation letter and informed consent to all the participants, who received the interview guides after agreeing to participate in the study. The interviews were conducted 3–5 days later by MN via telephone calls or face-to-face interviews at their workplaces. Telephone interviews provided a convenient and flexible option for participants whom the investigators could not meet in person due to location and difference in schedules, while face-to-face interviews were held in a private and comfortable space within the participants' workplaces. Efforts were made to protect the privacy of the participants. Each face-to-face interview was recorded using two separate voice recorders; whereas the telephone interviews were recorded using voice recording application after putting the phone on speaker. Every interview lasted between 18–25 min on an average. Majority of the interviews were conducted in English with only a few containing a hint of Urdu language. MN, being proficient in both the languages, then transcribed and translated them during the entire data collection procedure subsequently, along with the conduction of interviews. During the data collection procedure, sample size sufficiency was based on data saturation, pragmatic considerations (literature-based), information-rich data, research design requirements, and sample size guidelines. The point of theoretical saturation of the data was reached after conducting 15 interviews.

Data analysis

The verbatim transcripts of the interviews were analysed using Braun and Clarke's six-step thematic analysis framework following the inductive thematic analysis approach [29]. This study used an inductive thematic analysis approach. The transcripts were carefully reviewed (read and re-read) by two researchers, immersing them in data and familiarising themselves with the content. Notes were made and impressions were jotted down. Using NVivo data management software version 14, line-by-line coding was carried out by the principal investigator (MN) and reviewed by other team members (NS and UM). Throughout the coding process, codes were developed and modified using an open-coding technique. The codes were descriptive, that is, self-explanatory, and in the form of short words or phrases. They were then merged wherever possible; otherwise, a new code was defined. The codes were then organised into subthemes and, ultimately, into broader themes that described the factors affecting the career progression of mentors and mentees. Inductive thematic analysis was used to describe mentors’ and mentees’ descriptions of the factors affecting career progression. These themes were reviewed and refined, and since there were two data sets, one from the mentors and the other from the mentees, congruence was discussed between both sets of themes. They were interpreted in light of the significance of their patterns and their implications relative to the already established literature.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Establishing credibility and reliability is critical for ensuring trustworthiness in qualitative research [30]. Four members of the research team (MN, NS, MHBS and AK) were the faculty members of the institutes under study and as experienced mentors; they had prolonged engagement in the field and had enough time to build rapport with the participants. The authors also kept the reflexive journal side by side, which allowed bracketing the biases and minimizing the subjectivity. This ensured gaining deeper insights and consequently the credibility of the study. Peer debriefing sessions were organized by the principal investigator to minimize the researchers’ biases, confirming the accuracy and enhancing the objectivity of the findings. Researchers also documented comprehensive and thick description of the entire research process to allow the applicability and pertinence of the study's outcomes to different contexts.

Results

Pilot study

The pilot study led to the modification of the sequence of questions in both guides. Moreover, the questions in the mentor’s interview guide were reduced from 13 to 11 due to repetition in the answers, while the questions in the mentee’s guide were retained. The analysis of the pilot interviews was conducted in accordance with the finalised interview guides. Thus, having been conducted with the same methodology and participant criteria, it contributed valuable and comprehensive insights and trends as the main study. Thus, the findings of the pilot study were included in the final data analysis.

The study involved inviting nine faculty mentors and nine faculty mentees to participate. However, only seven mentors and eight mentees accepted the invitation (response rate = 83.3%). Three (20%) of the 15 participants were males, and 12 (80%) were females. In terms of participant designations, two (13.3%) were professors, three (20%) were associate professors, seven (46.6%) were assistant professors, and three (20%) were demonstrators from different institutes. Despite the slightly lower response rate, the robust engagement of the 15 participants was sufficient to capture their diverse and comprehensive perspectives, resulting in reaching the point of theoretical saturation. Two coding techniques "descriptive coding" and "in-vivo coding" were employed which generated 199 codes in the first coding cycle, and 76 codes in the second cycle of coding. Six themes ultimately surfaced from the dataset after these codes were organised into 23 subthemes. The themes and subthemes are summarized in Fig. 1.

Discussion

Mentoring is a crucial component of inter-professional faculty growth and development in academic health facilities where both the mentor and mentee remain in a symbiotic relationship [31]. Our study identified various factors in mentor–mentee relationships that affect the career progression of mentees in informal faculty mentoring sessions.

Revealing the ideal characteristics of mentees, our study confirmed the findings of Sparshadeep et al. that mentees needed to have internal motivation and clarity regarding their vision and goals before approaching a mentor [32]. A contrasting opinion argues that it is the responsibility of mentors to provide clarity of vision to mentees. These findings coincide with social cognitive career theory, which reflects the theoretical framework of this study [18]. All these characteristics align with this theory, that is, "setting of goals" aligns with how much and how well a person wants to do something; "internal motivation" supports the self-efficacy assumption of "Can I do this?", and the assumption of personal goals that "how much do I want to do this" [33].

Our study highlighted the fact that not only mentees but also mentors should be motivated enough to serve the best interests of mentees. Numerous qualities of mentors have been reported in earlier studies including the need to have a professional skills set and the progressive nature of mentors [17, 34] which also aligns with the findings of our study. Furthermore, Hilary et al. reported that mentees considered the mentor's reputation to be a significant factor when selecting a mentor based on their professional abilities and work-life balance [35]. While mentors can serve as role models for mentees, there is a distinct difference between them [36, 37]. This was reinforced by our study participants, who emphasised that mentors should serve as role models. There is an established role of soft skills as essential component for an individual's professional performance as well as relationships with their surroundings [38]. This finding is supported by our study which highlights the substantial physical and emotional efforts put in by mentors, reflecting sincerity in building mentoring relationships. The results of our study not only focused on leadership, advocacy, and good communication, but also on the non-judgmental attitude, friendliness, comfort, compassion, and approachable nature of the mentors, allowing for open and inhibition-free interactions. The significance of these traits in fostering positive mentoring relationships has been mentioned in various studies [5, 23, 32, 35, 39]. Our study aligns with this perspective, as participants consistently emphasised the use of open communication within their mentoring experiences, which also contributed to the establishment of a nurturing and secure environment. Furthermore, this open communication extended to the articulation of mentees' career goals, a practice supported by previous research and integral to the tenets of the SCCT [5, 23, 35]. The literature also supports the value of empathy and active listening in mentoring relationship [40–42]. Additionally, mutual trust, respect, and sincerity have also emerged as vital components of mentoring relationships, conforming to the findings of previous studies [5, 43]. Evidence also suggests that it is the responsibility of the mentee to be respectful of meeting times and prepare for meetings [17, 35]. This is comparable to our study results, where mentors believed that mentees needed to prepare beforehand to keep the discussion precise and extract the maximum benefit from this relationship. Supporting this statement, the majority of the mentees, when asked about their experiences, said that they had an agenda in mind before approaching mentors for these sessions. An important finding of our study in fostering an effective mentoring relationship is the compatibility between mentors and mentees. Lack of professional and personal compatibility adversely affects mentoring outcomes [35, 44].

Additionally, establishing well-defined roles and goals emerged as critical factors for successful mentoring, which is consistent with previous research [35]. Contrary to Hilary et al.'s study, which indicated that external department mentors could support interdepartmental projects [35] our findings identified physical distance for scheduling meetings as a potential challenge. The busy schedules of mentors were a common challenge mentioned in our study, which is consistent with findings in the literature [45]. Mentees reported attending mentoring sessions only on a "need basis”, suggesting that forced mentoring could yield negative outcomes. Mentors also believed that mentoring should only be given to a mentee who asks for it–that is, a mentee with an empty-glass mind-set. Another challenge was societal bias, in which external influences or perceptions from other faculty members could impact the mentoring relationship. Additionally, in conservative societies, such as Pakistan, cross-gender mentoring might introduce unnecessary obstacles. The role of gender in mentoring relationships has been studied in detail with conflicting findings [46]. Some studies have revealed no effect of gender on mentoring functions, whereas others have shown varying effects [47–49]. Our study found a mix of perspectives ranging from no gender difference in mentoring to easier communication within the same gender or minor cultural adaptations. This finding is comparable to that of a study conducted by Daniel et al. [50]. Our findings however also stressed on the importance of setting clear expectations and boundaries from the beginning, addressing challenges such as excessive mentee dependency, staying on track, and issues related to cross-gender mentoring. Experience sharing by mentors to build trust has emerged as an important strategy to overcome barriers in mentoring relationships, which is in line with findings in the literature [51]. In alignment with the assumption of SCCT of "outcome expectations" [18], mentees reported experience sharing as help in decision-making and to put things into perspective. Another strategy mentioned in this regard was to greet mentees with love and give them time. Feedback by mentors plays an important role in terms of the self-efficacy belief component of SCCT by providing mentees with insight into their capabilities to plan and execute steps related to their future [52]. Mubuuke et. al. not only focused on the importance of feedback but also stressed its delivery system within the mentoring relationship [52].

In addition, it has been suggested that a mentor should advise and not dictate a decision [31]. Our findings also align with this, that the mentors did not impose decisions rather offered comprehensive guidance, considering the mentees' circumstances. This approach, as previously described in the literature, facilitated professional decision-making and provided resources, career development, along with networking opportunities [53].

Implications

Iverson et al. have suggested mentoring as a key mechanism to assist early career academics with career progression [54]. Therefore, Understanding the components of the mentor–mentee relationship that contribute to career advancement is essential for optimizing these interactions [55]. The findings of this study highlight several important implications for enhancing career progression through mentoring in academic health facilities. First, there is a clear need for training programs focusing on equipping mentors with the skills and clarifying expectations for mentees. These programs should emphasize on fostering effective communication, empathy, and active listening, which were identified as crucial for building trust and facilitating open dialogue. Additionally, formal mentoring programs should be established and regulated by institutions, ensuring that the mentoring process is well-documented and that the progress of mentees is systematically tracked. However, the benefits of informal mentoring, particularly the mentee's ability to choose their mentor, should be preserved within these formal structures to enhance the positive outcomes of the mentoring relationship. Moreover, creating a more supportive environment for faculty members seeking informal mentoring is also essential, as it can encourage more faculty members to engage in these valuable relationships.

Limitations

Some of the limitations of our investigation include the inclusion of an all-female population as mentees, primarily because the majority of faculty members were females, although they were recruited from separate institutions. Also, although data saturation was achieved with 15 participants, the relatively small sample size and the specific focus on medical and dental faculty in a few cities of Pakistan, limit the generalizability of the findings. However the maximum variation sampling ensured a wide range of perspectives, and a well-documented research process enhances the transferability of the findings to similar settings. Cognitive bias may be considered because of an individual's interpretation of questions. Additionally, the findings of this study are based on participant self-reporting which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias however the semi-structured nature of the interviews, use of open-ended questions and the interviewer's skill in probing responses helped mitigate these potential biases. Finally, some interviews were conducted over the telephone, which, while convenient, might have limited the depth of communication and the ability to observe non-verbal cues, potentially affecting the richness of the data. Despite these limitations, this study adds valuable information to the informal faculty mentoring literature.

Future recommendations

More robust studies involving equal distributions of males and females need to be conducted to better define the factors affecting career progression. Moreover, more qualitative studies on the factors affecting the career progression of mentees in formal mentoring programs for faculty should be conducted after they have been established in the country. Additionally, combining qualitative methods with quantitative approaches, such as surveys or questionnaires, could help validate the findings and provide a more robust analysis of the factors influencing career progression in faculty mentoring. Further qualitative studies highlighting cultural barriers and challenges to informal mentoring can also be conducted. Finally, conducting longitudinal studies that follow mentees over time could provide deeper insights into the long-term impacts of informal mentoring on career progression. This would allow researchers to observe changes and developments in mentees' careers and better understand the enduring effects of mentoring relationships.

Conclusion

Multiple factors within a mentor–mentee relationship affect the career progression of mentees within informal faculty mentoring sessions including mentees' positive mindset, internal motivation, and clarity of vision, and mentor’s professional skills, reputation, and role modelling. Effective communication, mutual trust, respect, and establishment of clear roles and goals have emerged as essential for successful mentoring relationships. Challenges, such as busy schedules, cross-gender mentoring, and societal biases, also affect mentoring relationships. Strategies to overcome these barriers include sharing experiences, providing psychosocial support, showing empathy, active listening, and providing effective feedback.
