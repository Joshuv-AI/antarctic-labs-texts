Parkinson’s disease severity is encoded by non-linear oscillatory interactions across cortical-subcortical networks
Authors: Sahil Chilukuri, Amirreza Alijanpourotaghsara, Vibhash D. Sharma, Yvette Bordelon, Nader Pouratian, Koorosh Mirpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is characterized by motor symptoms linked to dysregulated neural activity within basal ganglia-thalamocortical (BGTC) circuits. While clinical assessments such as the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) effectively measure symptom severity, they are not designed to provide insights into underlying neuropathophysiology. We hypothesize that PD severity is encoded by nonlinear interactions across BGTC neurophysiological biomarkers. We recorded simultaneous preiriolandic electrocorticography and globus pallidus internus (GPi) local field potentials in 47 patients undergoing deep brain stimulation (DBS) implantation. Using 110 spectral features, we compared linear regression against a non-linear ensemble of bagged decision trees to predict preoperative UPDRS scores (off medicine). Linear regression failed to robustly predict severity. Conversely, the non-linear ensemble model successfully predicted motor scores (cross-validation rho = 0.317, p = 0.018). Predictive power emerged from a distributed pattern of biomarkers, including alpha, low-beta, and gamma burst metrics and coherence, rather than a single dominant feature, indicating that PD pathophysiology is encoded by collective network states rather than isolated oscillations. Consequently, accurate, subject-specific severity estimation requires multivariate, non-linear modeling, providing deeper insight into the stable, baseline pathological network states upon which dynamic symptom fluctuations occur.

Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative movement disorder characterized by bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and postural instability1,2. Clinicians commonly use the gold standard Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) to quantify the severity of motor symptoms in people with PD (PwPD)3. Our objective is to complement this clinical assessment by identifying neurophysiological biomarkers within the BGTC circuit that underlie these motor scores, thereby linking clinical severity to its neural mechanisms4–6.

Previous electrophysiological research using local field potentials (LFPs) and electrocorticography (ECoG) has consistently identified abnormal neural oscillations in the basal ganglia-thalamocortical (BGTC) circuits associated with motor deficits in PD7,8. Specifically, beta-band synchronization (13–30 Hz) in subthalamic nucleus (STN) and globus pallidus internus (GPi) correlates with rigidity and bradykinesia, theta-band activity (4–8 Hz) is linked to tremor, and pathological deviations in alpha and gamma-band activity relative to healthy physiological states have been implicated in symptom severity9–13. Recent research has increasingly demonstrated the coupling of behavior and electrophysiology through a broad range of neural biomarkers14.

While there is evidence for correlation between specific electrophysiological biomarkers and symptom severity as measured by UPDRS, it remains unclear whether electrophysiological biomarkers can reliably predict symptom severity15–17. While prior studies have established neurophysiological markers18–22, particularly beta power, for predicting therapeutic outcomes, these single-feature, linear approaches often fail to capture global disease severity across heterogeneous cohorts. Our study advances this framework by demonstrating that symptom severity estimation requires multivariate integration and non-linear modeling.

We hypothesize that beyond the fast dynamics of movement kinematics, the BGTC network also encodes the general, stable severity of the disease. To test this, we utilized a non-linear machine learning approach to determine if disease severity (as indexed by UPDRS) can be decoded from the interactions of multiple electrophysiological features, where simple linear models often fail. To test this hypothesis, we utilize a supervised machine learning approach, specifically an ensemble of bagged decision trees, to model and predict UPDRS scores based solely on simultaneously recorded cortical electrocorticography (ECoG) and GPi local field potential (LFP) data from 47 PD patients undergoing GPi deep brain stimulator (DBS) implantation. Our aim is to identify the most influential biomarkers across multiple frequency bands and cortical-subcortical coherence metrics that contribute significantly to symptom prediction.

Through this approach, we not only demonstrate the predictive power of electrophysiological biomarkers but also assess the relative contribution of different biomarkers to PD symptoms, potentially shedding light on the underlying neural mechanisms and a more direct and potentially causal link between these measures and the clinical manifestation of the disease. Rather than attempting to model the entire complexity of the motor circuit, our objective is to identify a subset of electrophysiological features that collectively predict clinical severity across a heterogeneous patient cohort. Our findings suggest that because PD pathophysiology is heterogeneous, accurate prediction for the individual patient requires a model that integrates the ‘collective’ contributions of multiple spectral features, rather than relying on a single universal biomarker23,24., and provide deeper insight into the neural mechanisms underlying symptom expression.

Methods

The Institutional Review Boards at the University of California, Los Angeles, and UT Southwestern Medical Center approved the study, and all participants gave written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study involved a cohort of 47 patients undergoing GPi DBS implantation for PD, operated on by the same surgeon (NP). Target selection was determined by the multidisciplinary surgical team at each institution based on patient-specific profiles (e.g., cognitive status, mood, severity of dyskinesias). A statistical comparison of demographic variables (age, disease duration) and key electrophysiological metrics (mean beta power) revealed no significant differences between the participants studied across the two centers (p > 0.05), supporting pooling of data.

Leads were implanted in bilateral GPi, except for 5 patients who had unilateral right implantations (Table 1, patient demographic details). We used the full MDS-UPDRS III to determine motor scores on the contralateral side to neural recording, which served as the primary dependent variable in all predictive modeling analyses. UPDRS scores were obtained pre-operatively in the Medication OFF state (approx. 12 h withdrawal) within 3 months of surgery to reflect baseline disease severity. All patients were re-assessed on the day of surgery to ensure disease stability and lack of symptom progression.

Preoperative imaging consisted of a standardized multi-sequence magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) protocol, including T1-weighted (pre- and post-contrast), T2-weighted, and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences as well as a computed tomography (CT) acquired with the stereotactic frame in place. Intraoperative fluoroscopic images and postoperative CT imaging were obtained and used to verify the final location of the implanted DBS leads. GPi LFPs were recorded using the implanted DBS leads (Medtronic 33005, Abbott 6172 or Boston Scientific).

An eight-contact subdural electrocorticography (ECoG) strip with 4 mm platinum-iridium electrodes and 1 cm spacing (AdTech Medical) was inserted subdurally in a posterior direction through a burr hole made for DBS lead insertion25–27. In all cases, the ECoG strip was positioned to span the central sulcus, verified intraoperatively by planning a secondary trajectory that would theoretically traverse the central sulcus. After creating the surgical burr holes, patients were awakened from anesthesia. After establishing wakefulness for at least 30 min, DBS leads were implanted based on microelectrode recordings and kinesthetic confirmation of targeting the motor region of GPi. Cortical electrode localization was performed using the semi-automated technique described previously28,29. Briefly, pre-operative MRI and CT scans were co-registered and reconstructed into 3D surfaces. These 3D models were then spatially aligned to the 2D intraoperative fluoroscopy image by matching common stereotactic landmarks (including frame pins, skull contours, and DBS leads). The 2D coordinates of the ECoG electrodes visible on the fluoroscopy were projected into the 3D space, and their final anatomical locations were determined by calculating the intersection of this projection with the patient’s reconstructed cortical hull, thereby accounting for parallax distortion.

DBS electrode contacts were localized using the Lead-DBS toolbox (v3.0)30. Postoperative CT scans were coregistered to preoperative MRI sequences, including T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), and Fast Gray Matter Acquisition T1 Inversion Recovery (FGATIR) images. The patient’s data was then normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) library, with brain-shift compensation applied to correct for postsurgical deformation. Following automated reconstruction, electrode trajectories were manually refined to ensure precise anatomical placement. The final coordinates for each contact were extracted in both the subject’s native space and MNI space.

Simultaneous LFP and ECoG recordings were obtained using a bandpass filter of 0.1 and 1000 Hz using BCI2000 v3 connected to g.USBamp 2.0 (g.Tec), with 2400 Hz sampling rate or NeuroOmega amplifier with a single scalp reference and 1375 Hz sampling rate. We recorded up to 6 cycles of 30-second rest and movement periods (averaging 3.2 cycles) for each subject. Only rest blocks with at least 15 s of uninterrupted rest were included in the study (see results for details).

Neurophysiological data preprocessing included removing 60 Hz line noise and discarding data segments containing electrical or movement artifacts, as previously defined and described25,29. In brief, artifact removal was conducted through a hybrid approach of automatic detection and visual verification. The automated detection process involved full-wave rectification, taking the first derivative, and applying a five-point median filter to identify segments exceeding five standard deviations of the recorded session. These artifact windows were extended by 2 ms on each side and corrected using linear interpolation. Final preprocessing included a two-way least square FIR bandpass filter (1–300 Hz) and the application of a notch filter to remove 60 Hz line noise and its associated harmonics. Less than 0.5% of data from each subject was excluded due to artifact removal. Power spectral density (PSD) was calculated using Thomson’s multitaper method in 1 s consecutive time windows with no overlap for frequencies of 4 to 110 Hz with ± 2 Hz frequency bandwidth with three tapers31. Each segmented spectrum was normalized to the total power of the signal during each condition (excluding the line-noise and its harmonics). Frequency band averages were calculated at theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–11 Hz), low-beta (12–20 Hz), high-beta (21–35 Hz), and high-gamma (65–110 Hz) using the multitaper PSDs26.

Periods of bursting were defined as any transient increase in band power that exceeded the 75th percentile power for that subject within that specific band and persisted for at least one cycle, as illustrated in Fig. 1C29,32,33. After extracting burst durations for each block of data, the average burst duration, burst amplitude, and burst rate (number of bursts per second) were calculated for each subject. To quantify the degree of co-variability between pairs of cortico-cortical or cortico-subcortical (cortex-GPi) signals, we estimated the magnitude squared coherence using the multitaper method, with time window and frequency smoothing parameters identical to those used in the PSD analysis (with a ± 2 Hz frequency bandwidth and three tapers). Cortico-cortical coherences were calculated for three pairs between the ECoGs (M1-PM, M1-S1 and PM-S1), and cortico-GPi coherences were calculated between the pallidal lead with the highest average beta power and the three cortical ECoGs and averaged across the same five frequency bands defined for the PSD analysis. To ensure connectivity was assessed specifically within the pathological motor network, coherence was calculated using the GPi contact exhibiting the maximum beta power, which served as a functional marker for the motor territory.

Multiple regression analysis

To assess the linear relationship between electrophysiological features and clinical outcomes, we utilized a multiple linear regression framework. The model was implemented using a standard least-squares approach to predict the continuous behavioral score from the spectral and burst metrics. To ensure generalizability and prevent overfitting, regression coefficients were estimated within a strict Leave-One-Out (LOO) cross-validation scheme (see below).

Training and testing ensemble of bagged decision trees

To investigate the complex relationship between electrophysiological biomarkers and UPDRS scores, we developed an ensemble of bagged decision tree regression models. This approach aimed to estimate the strength of the association between biomarkers and behavioral scores, rather than to identify a single deterministic equation. We selected Bagged Decision Trees to effectively model non-linear interactions between features while mitigating the high variance associated with single trees. Furthermore, this method permits the robust calculation of feature importance, facilitating the biological interpretation of the most predictive frequency bands.

In each training iteration, an initial random decision model was established using an ensemble of 100 trees with a minimum number of observations per leaf node. Feature importance was calculated based on the permuted predicted delta errors, allowing us to rank factors according to their contribution to the prediction. We then implemented a recursive feature addition process: factors were systematically added to the model based on their importance rank, and the model error was recalculated using 1 to 100 trees. The optimal model configuration for each iteration was defined as the specific combination of factors and trees that yielded the lowest Out-of-Bag (OOB) error. To assess the statistical significance of the predictions, the model’s performance was rigorously compared against a null distribution generated from 1,000 iterations of shuffled data (see below). The sum of motor scores was used as the dependent variable in our machine-learning model to predict PD motor severity. We employed an ensemble of bagged decision trees regression algorithm, constructed using the MATLAB 2025b Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox.

Bagged tree hyperparameters and replication details

We implemented bagged regression trees using the TreeBagger function in MathWorks MATLAB (Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox, version R2025b). Models were trained with Method="regression”, NumTrees = 100, and minimum leaf size MinLeafSize = 5 observations per terminal node. By default, TreeBagger uses bootstrap sampling with replacement (InBagFraction = 1, SampleWithReplacement=true) and considers a random subset of predictors at each split (NumPredictorsToSample=ceil(p/3) for regression, where p = 110 is the number of predictors). Unless otherwise stated, remaining parameters were left at MATLAB defaults; trees were grown deep by default, consistent with standard bagging practice. We fixed the random number generator for reproducibility rng(42).

We selected the maximum ensemble size based on stabilization of OOB error; OOB MSE decreased rapidly then plateaued, and 100 trees fell in the plateau region.

Cross-validation strategy

To maximize training data efficiency while preventing overfitting and pseudoreplication, we implemented a Leave-One-Out (LOO) cross-validation framework for both multiple regression analysis and bagged decision trees where N equals the subject count. In each iteration i, the model was trained on N-1 subjects and tested on the withheld subject i. The learned projection coefficients were then applied to the test subject.

Statistical evaluation (permutation testing)

Statistical significance of the predictive performance (Pearson correlation coefficient, rho) was determined using non-parametric permutation testing. We generated a null distribution by randomly shuffling the behavioral response variable (Y) 1,000 times, effectively breaking the relationship between brain activity and behavior while preserving the distributional properties of the data. For each permutation, the full LOO cross-validation procedure was re-run. A higher importance value indicates a more influential predictor. Model Performance Metrics:

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) was calculated to assess the average magnitude of the errors between the predicted and actual UPDRS scores. A lower RMSE indicates higher accuracy. Additionally, we computed the Pearson correlation coefficient (rho) to evaluate the strength and direction of the linear relationship between the predicted and actual UPDRS scores. A correlation coefficient close to + 1 indicates a strong positive linear relationship, while a coefficient close to -1 indicates a strong negative linear relationship. The statistical significance of this correlation was determined by calculating the p-value, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Result

We evaluated 47 PwPD (12 women), with an average age of 64.7 years (SD = 6.42) and a mean UPDRS score of 37.69 (SD = 7.19). No significant differences in age or UPDRS scores were observed between the male and female groups (t-test p value > 0.2).

Multiple regression model

We employed a regression model to predict UPDRS scores based on 110 electrophysiological biomarkers, including Burst Rate, Burst Duration, Burst Amplitude, Power, cortico-cortical coherence, and cortico-subcortical coherence. The average values of these biomarkers were calculated across five frequency bands at M1, PM, S1, and subcortical recordings from the Gpi.

A multiple regression analysis yielded a model with a p-value of 0.096, R² of 0.329, and adjusted R² of 0.065. This model demonstrated a correlation coefficient of 0.573 (p < < 0.001) between the predicted and actual UPDRS scores. To assess the robustness of the model, we conducted a LOO cross-validation framework (see methods). The linear regression model achieved a moderate predictive correlation of rho = 0.244 between the predicted and actual clinical scores using LOO method (Fig. 2A). However, based on the permutation null distribution, this result failed to reach statistical significance (p = 0.091, Fig. 2B), suggesting that a simple linear combination of the selected physiological features may not robustly capture the variance in behavioral outcomes. These findings suggest that while the multiple regression model can detect a weak correlation between electrophysiological biomarkers and UPDRS scores, its generalizability is limited. The low adjusted R² values and results from cross-validation analysis underscore the model’s lack of predictive power in this context.

Ensemble of bagged decision trees model

To further explore the complex relationship between electrophysiological biomarkers and UPDRS scores, we developed an ensemble of bagged decision tree regression models, highlighting the model’s predictive power, both when tested on data encountered during training (seen data) and on new, unseen data (cross-validation). The iterative feature selection process demonstrated that model accuracy was dependent on both the number of trees and the quality of the features used. Generally, increasing the ensemble size and incorporating additional high-ranking factors progressively decreased the prediction error. Figure 3A illustrates this trend, showing the reduction in Out-of-Bag Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the top 1, 4, 7, 10, and 13 ranked factors were integrated into the model. In contrast, models constructed using the same number of randomly selected factors (Fig. 3B) showed higher error rates. The optimal model configuration selected for final analysis corresponded to the minimum MSE achieved during this optimization process (indicated by the dotted lines in Fig. 3A and B). While low-complexity models (e.g., single decision trees) occasionally displayed low error rates due to stochastic variance, these results were unstable. The error rates consistently stabilized as the number of trees in the ensemble increased, confirming the necessity of bagging to ensure generalizable, robust predictions.

Model performance

When tested on the seen data, the model exhibited a strong predictive performance. RMSE of 7.278 indicates a relatively low average error between the predicted and actual UPDRS scores compared to an average RMSE of 15.891 for 1000 iterations of shuffled data (permutation test p = 0.001). The correlation coefficient between the predicted and real scores was 0.956 compared to -0.006 in shuffled data, demonstrating a strong linear relationship using an average of 79.1 factors for the prediction. Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient was significant in 99% of the iterations (p < 0.05), compared to 4.9% in shuffled data.

To investigate whether the choice of factors affected the model’s performance, we trained the model using the best factors in 1,000 iterations of the training data and compared its performance to models trained with the same number of random factors. Although the decrement in performance was statistically significant, the decline was not a practically meaningful difference (average rho of best factors = 0.956; average rho of random factors = 0.948, difference = 0.008).

Model cross-validation

To evaluate the model’s generalizability, we performed an LOO cross-validation test (see methods for details). The model yielded a Pearson correlation of rho = 0.317 between predicted and actual clinical scores (Fig. 4A). Permutation testing confirmed that this result was statistically significant (p = 0.0180, Fig. 4B). The null distribution histogram indicates that the observed correlation lies well outside the random chance distribution (red dashed line, Fig. 4B), confirming that the non-linear ensemble method successfully extracted signal from the electrophysiological features to predict clinical state.

Model robustness validation

To further evaluate the model’s robustness and the feature importance, we performed cross-validation, using 20% of the data as an unseen test set in each iteration. The model trained on unshuffled electrophysiological features demonstrated a correlation coefficient (rho) of 0.409 compared to models trained with randomly selected features (rho = 0.295) and models trained and tested with shuffled data (rho = 0.022). Figure 4C presents ROC curves comparing discrimination between shuffled data and models trained with either the best or randomly selected features, yielding AUCs of 0.80 and 0.60, respectively. A significant Pearson correlation (p < 0.05, for each round of cross-validation) was observed in 19.8% of the 1000 iterations when using the best features on unseen data, compared to only 5.8% for randomly shuffled data. This suggests a decrease in predictive accuracy on unseen data, even when using an average of 32.1 features for prediction.

Factor importance

To determine each factor’s contribution to UPDRS prediction, we evaluated the permuted predicted delta errors at every iteration of model building. Figure 5A shows the distribution of average importance for all 110 factors over 1,000 iterations, ranked in descending order. The top 5% of these factors, namely, GPi alpha burst duration, GPi alpha burst rate, M1 × GPi gamma coherence, central gyrus gamma power, and S1 alpha power, stand out with slightly higher importance. Consistent with a distributed encoding of disease severity, the model relied on a broad set of predictors. While no single feature dominated the ranking, the top quartile was consistently populated by power and coherence in the alpha and low-beta bands. Still, the remaining features follow closely in importance, suggesting that the predictive strength of the model emerges from a broad set of electrophysiological parameters rather than just a small handful.

To probe these contributions more deeply, we categorized each factor according to its biomarker (e.g., burst duration, burst rate, power, coherence), anatomic location (PM, M1, S1, Gpi), and frequency band (theta, alpha, low-beta, high‐beta, gamma). We then clustered the factors and calculated the average importance for each biomarker–band or biomarker–channel pair (Fig. 5C, bottom row). A left‐tailed t‐test comparing these averages to a shuffled (null) distribution, again using Bonferroni corrections—demonstrating that many sub-combinations of biomarkers and channels exceeded the null expectation of importance (Fig. 5C, top row).

Overall, we observed a hierarchy of factor contributions to UPDRS motor-score prediction, with burst duration, burst rate, and cortico‐subcortical coherence consistently ranking at or near the top, particularly in the alpha, low‐beta, and gamma frequency ranges. Further, specific bands emerged as more influential in particular channels: for instance, alpha was especially robust in most channels (except M1), low‐beta in PM, theta in GPi, and gamma in M1. Both cortico‐cortical and cortico‐subcortical (cortex-GPi) coherences were also significantly predictive in the low‐beta band.

Figure 5D summarizes these findings, illustrating that while a Friedman test revealed significant differences in factor importance (p < 0.001), the between-group effect sizes were relatively modest. As the boxplots show, the variance within each group often overlaps with that of the others, suggesting that the practical or physiological relevance of any single feature’s advantage may be limited. These analyses reveal that predictive power is not driven by a single dominant biomarker, but rather by a distributed pattern of contributions across the network. Specifically, power and coherence in the alpha, gamma, and low‐beta bands, particularly within M1, PM, and GPi regions, emerge as key collective contributors. This distributed landscape confirms that accurately estimating UPDRS motor scores requires integrating information across multiple spectral and anatomical dimensions, rather than relying on any isolated feature.

Discussion

By leveraging machine learning techniques, specifically an ensemble of bagged decision trees, we demonstrate that electrophysiological data, including peri-rolandic ECoG and GPi LFPs, are strongly associated withPD motor symptoms. While the modest cross-validated correlation (rho = 0.317) indicates this specific model is not yet suitable as a standalone clinical diagnostic tool, it serves as a critical neurophysiological proof-of-concept. The ability of the non-linear model to extract a statistically significant relationship from highly heterogeneous, cross-sectional human data highlights that these biomarkers hold meaningful and quantifiable information about underlying motor symptom severity. The observed difference in performance between the training set and the unseen test set underscores the inherent complexity and variability of PD symptomatology and suggests that additional factors, possibly extending beyond the biomarkers evaluated here (spatially or physiologically), may play a significant role in influencing clinical symptomatology.

These results resonate with a broad literature linking oscillatory activity in the motor network to hallmark PD symptoms such as bradykinesia and rigidity9,34–36, and theta band changes with tremor37–39. However, our study differs from many prior investigations in that we probed a wide array of factors obtained in the resting state, and focused explicitly on prediction of motor-symptom severity, rather than just correlation. While specific electrophysiological signatures, particularly in the beta range, have garnered attention in the literature, our results indicate a broader landscape of features, with high‐ranking factors including not only beta‐band parameters but also alpha, gamma, and various coherence metrics. In fact, although some features (e.g., GPi alpha burst duration, GPi alpha burst rate) emerged in the top 5% of importance, the remaining features followed closely behind. Interestingly, in the recently reported study of adaptive DBS for PD, feature selection ranged from 8–30 Hz, including the alpha band40. Figure 5B maps the inter-correlation and importance gradient of the top predictors. This visualization is critical as it reveals a continuous, distributed contribution of features rather than a steep drop-off after a few dominant markers. This pattern is consistent with the notion that PD severity is encoded by a ‘collective’ network state, where information is redundant and shared across multiple correlated bands, rather than by isolated, independent physiological proxies. Consequently, this justifies the use of ensemble methods capable of leveraging these complex, distributed patterns, as opposed to simple linear models that assume feature independence.

By advancing beyond correlative analysis, our study directly addresses a significant gap in the field. We have shown that these electrophysiological features are not merely coincident with symptom severity but possess genuine predictive power, suggesting a more mechanistically relevant link between oscillatory neural dynamics and the clinical manifestations of PD. This shift in focus from single-feature correlation to multivariate prediction demonstrates a mechanistically relevant link between distributed oscillatory dynamics and the clinical manifestations of PD. While the observed reduction in performance on unseen data underscores the multifaceted nature of the disease, our work establishes a proof-of-concept: the baseline, unmedicated severity of Parkinson’s disease—distinct from momentary symptom fluctuations—is stably encoded across a multifactorial electrophysiological network. Of course, a functional aDBS system requires moment-to-moment parameters of neural activity to trigger stimulation upon demand. However, establishing this baseline severity profile provides a critical prior that can assist in building better individualized models, fine-tuning contextual parameters, and narrowing the exploratory phase of patient-specific programming.

Furthermore, our findings establish that motor severity is reflected non-linearly within the BGTC network. The findings that the non-linear ensemble model successfully estimated symptom severity, while linear regression models failed, suggests that this information is encoded through complex interactions between biomarkers, rather than through isolated linear factors. While individual low-beta features ranked below some alpha and gamma features (appearing in the top ~ 15%), the low-beta band collectively emerged as a robust predictor, particularly in coherence metrics. This result is consistent with established theories implicating excessive beta oscillations in bradykinesia and rigidity9,34–36 but also suggests that predictive power for overall severity is shared across a broader spectral landscape than beta alone. In parallel, alpha oscillations emerged as significant predictors, perhaps reflecting compensatory neural processes or cognitive control mechanisms that modulate movement. Subcortical-cortical coherence in the GPi–M1 axis also emerged as important, reinforcing current models that emphasize the distributed, network‐level generation of pathological PD oscillations. Critically, our rank tests suggest that although some features stand out slightly, the predictive power of most factors is closely clustered—again suggesting a collective, rather than exclusive, role in shaping clinical outcomes.

We acknowledge several limitations in our approach. First, our patient cohort was restricted to individuals undergoing GPi-DBS; thus, these findings may not generalize to those with less advanced disease or those managed without surgery. While UPDRS scores were assessed pre-operatively, they serve as a stable metric of the patient’s general unmedicated disease state. Our model demonstrates that this baseline severity is detectable in intraoperative physiology, distinct from the transient motor fluctuations often studied. The neural signals of the motor network are inherently dynamic; however, by averaging features across multiple resting-state epochs throughout the recording session, we mitigate the influence of acute temporal noise. The fact that these intraoperative resting-state signals significantly correlate with a preoperative clinical exam conducted weeks prior suggests that our non-linear model is capturing the stable, underlying pathological architecture (the trait) of the network, rather than just momentary (state) symptom expression. Additionally, the UPDRS III, while a clinical gold standard, remains subject to inter‐rater variability and offers only a temporal snapshot not aligned precisely with intraoperative recordings. While our model’s RMSE (~ 7 points) exceeds the typical Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) of 3–5 points, it is important to distinguish cross-sectional estimation from longitudinal change detection. Given the inherent inter-rater variability of clinical scoring and the restriction to resting-state data, this error margin serves as a proof-of-concept that electrophysiology captures the gross disease state, though further refinement is needed to achieve the precision required for fine-grained clinical monitoring. Anesthesia and the surgical context may further modulate neural activity in ways that differ from a patient’s typical state. ECoG and LFP recordings sampled only a fraction of the extensive neural circuitry implicated in PD, limiting our spatial resolution, and our analysis was restricted to frequencies between 4 and 110 Hz. While recent evidence suggests that high-frequency oscillations (HFOs, ~ 200–300 Hz) may serve as biomarkers, we excluded them to minimize high-frequency noise and, critically, to limit the dimensionality of the feature space. Given our sample size (N = 47), adding HFO features would have increased the number of predictors, exacerbating the risk of overfitting. Future studies with larger cohorts could benefit from incorporating these high-frequency features.

Despite these caveats, our findings could have clinical implications. Being able to estimate motor symptom severity from intraoperative recordings implies that the signals at that specific location are functionally relevant to the patient’s phenotype. Unlike acute biomarker prediction systems that can be affected by momentary noise, the presented model aims to capture the general state of the disease. Future longitudinal studies integrating postoperative outcomes are required to determine whether the presence of these predictive multivariate signatures correlates with maximum symptom reduction. Nonetheless, identifying the specific features that drive this severity estimation provides a deeper understanding of the stable neurophysiological landscape underlying the disease state, which may eventually inform how we characterize pathological networks.

Recent clinical advancements have introduced adaptive DBS algorithms that continuously adjust stimulation based on real-time, acute biomarker fluctuations. Our study complements this by characterizing the stable, underlying disease burden. By integrating the ‘collective’ contributions of multiple spectral features, we achieve a more holistic estimation of the patient’s baseline pathological state. This does not eliminate the need for monitoring the momentary state of the patient; rather, it provides a stable neurophysiological context to better interpret momentary data. While our model captures the global burden of motor severity (Total UPDRS III), future work must bridge this gap by investigating how dynamic, symptom-specific biomarkers fluctuate atop the non-linear, baseline severity landscape identified here, ultimately enhancing the granularity and individualization of adaptive DBS algorithms.

Although the conceptual understanding of PD as a widespread network disorder is well-established, translating this concept into predictive algorithms remains a challenge. Our computational analysis empirically demonstrates that overall disease severity cannot be adequately captured by linear models of isolated biomarkers like beta activity. The novelty of our findings lies in operationalizing this network theory: we show that moving from single-biomarker linear correlations to multivariate, non-linear decoding is mathematically necessary to characterize the diverse pathological landscape of the disease. Therefore, further research is required to optimize model performance and expand clinical applicability, the evidence presented here supports the potential for machine-learning–based predictive tools to augment current clinical paradigms and ultimately enhance outcomes for individuals living with PD.
