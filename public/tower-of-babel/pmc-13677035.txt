An intron retention-derived secreted Endoglin Isoform as a circulating biomarker in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Hannah R Ortiz, Julia Podgorski, Monique R Archuleta, Atsushi Ishii, Aaron Ramonett, Christopher Cartmell, Nathan A Ellis, Paul R Langlais, Karthikeyan Mythreye, Nadine Hempel, Nam Y Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a lethal malignancy with few effective strategies for early detection or prognostic assessment. Endoglin (CD105) is an endothelial receptor best known for its roles in vascular development and tumor angiogenesis, and its only recognized circulating form arises through proteolytic shedding of the full-length receptor. Here, we report a pancreatic tumor-derived secreted endoglin isoform that is molecularly distinct from all known endoglin variants, enabling the development of an isoform-specific antibody for selective detection. Through integrated transcriptomic, biochemical, and translational analyses, we identified a truncated endoglin isoform generated by intron retention that contains a unique C-terminal peptide absent from canonical endoglin. This isoform was detected in human PDAC cell lines, orthotopic xenografts, primary patient tumors, patient sera, and the KPC genetically engineered mouse model, demonstrating conservation across clinically relevant systems. Tissue microarray analysis revealed that the isoform is prevalent in PDAC and represents a major subset of endoglin-positive tumors, while immunoprecipitation and a newly developed sandwich ELISA confirmed its presence in patient circulation, supporting its potential as a blood-based biomarker. Together, these findings identify a previously unrecognized, tumor-derived secreted endoglin isoform and highlight intron retention as source of circulating protein biomarkers in pancreatic cancer.Keywords: Endoglin, Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Biomarker, Intron retention, Antibody, ELISA

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal human malignancies, with a five-year survival rate of approximately 10% and limited therapeutic advances over recent decades[[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Late-stage diagnosis, rapid disease progression, and marked biological heterogeneity underscore the need for biomarkers that enable earlier detection, prognostic stratification, and longitudinal disease monitoring. Existing circulating biomarkers, including the FDA approved CA19-9, lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity, particularly in early-stage disease and in biologically aggressive subsets of PDAC[[6], [7], [8]].

Endoglin (CD105) is a transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) co-receptor classically regarded as an endothelial-specific protein required for angiogenesis and vascular remodeling[[9], [10], [11], [12]]. In cancer, endoglin has been extensively studied as a selective marker of tumor angiogenesis and vascular target[[13], [14], [15]]. A soluble form of endoglin generated by extracellular proteolytic shedding of the full-length receptor is readily detectable in the circulation of healthy individuals and patients with a wide range of pathological conditions, including cancer, ischemic injury, and inflammatory disorders[16,17], thereby limiting its disease specificity as a circulating biomarker.

Recent studies have revised the endothelial-centric view of endoglin by demonstrating its expression within malignant cells across multiple tumor types, including breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers[[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. However, the molecular diversity of tumor cell-derived endoglin and its potential clinical relevance remain unknown. Here, we identify a previously unrecognized form of endoglin in PDAC arising from intron retention, resulting in a truncated, secreted protein that is structurally distinct from both the canonical full-length transmembrane receptor and the proteolytically shed soluble endoglin commonly found in circulation.

Importantly, this intron retention-derived secreted endoglin variant is not restricted to human patient samples. It is also detected in human PDAC cell lines, orthotopic xenograft tumors, and in genetically engineered mouse model, demonstrating that this variant is conserved across clinically relevant systems. The peptide encoded by the retained intron shows little significant homology to known protein domains, providing a molecular signature distinct from canonical and proteolytically shed endoglin. Leveraging this specificity, we generated an antibody that selectively recognizes this intron retention-derived motif, enabling sensitive and specific detection of the novel endoglin isoform in PDAC cells, patient sera and tumor samples. Together, these findings establish a tractable experimental framework in which the regulation, function, and biomarker potential of this endoglin isoform can be mechanistically interrogated in parallel with human disease.

Collectively, our results expand the importance of endoglin in pancreatic cancer and reveal intron retention-derived secretion as a novel mechanism linking tumor cell RNA dysregulation to circulating biomarker generation. This secreted endoglin variant represents a promising blood-based biomarker for PDAC with potential applications in diagnosis, prognostic stratification, and disease monitoring, while positioning intron retention-generated proteins as an experimentally accessible class of biomarkers in pancreatic cancer.

Methods and materials

Cell lines and culture

Human PDAC cell lines Mia PaCa-2, Capan-1, and Panc1 were purchased from ATCC, while murine KPC-derived PDAC cells, were a generous gift from Dr. David Kashatus (University of Virginia). Cell lines were maintained in DMEM or RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and cultured at 37 C in 5% CO₂. HUVECs were cultured as previously described.

Human tissue specimens and serum

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) human PDAC tissue array slides containing over 80 PDAC patient samples and normal pancreatic tissues were obtained from a commercial source (tissuearray.com). Serum samples were acquired through University of Arizona Cancer Center Biorepository from deidentified 35 PDAC patients and 13 healthy donors under IRB-approved protocols.

Immunoblotting and Immunoprecipitation

Cell and tissue lysates were prepared in lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma). For immunoblotting, 30-50 µg protein per sample was resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Primary antibodies included A8 (full-length endoglin) and the intron retention-specific antibody (Ab-Engir). For immunoprecipitation (IP), lysates or serum samples were diluted (1:25) in lysis buffer, then incubated overnight with Ab-Engir covalently conjugated to agarose beads (Pierce Crosslink Immunoprecipitation Kit) to avoid interference from antibody heavy and light chains, followed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

3′ Rapid amplification of cDNA Ends (3′ RACE)

Total RNA was extracted from Mia PaCa-2 and Capan-1 cells using TRIzol. cDNA synthesis and 3′ RACE were performed using the SMARTer RACE 3′ Kit (Takara Bio) according to manufacturer instructions. Forward primers targeting exons 1 (CGGCACGCTCCCTCTGGCTGTTG) or 5 (GAAGCCAGCCAGGACATGGGCCG) of human endoglin were paired with the universal 3′ RACE primer. PCR products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis, gel-purified, and sequenced to identify intron retention events.

Antibody generation and validation

Engir antibody was generated through a commercial vendor (Genscript). Briefly, a 12-amino acid peptide corresponding partially to the intron retention-derived C-terminal sequence of Engir (WTSGSSPSPRLL) was synthesized and used to immunize rabbits. Polyclonal antibodies were affinity-purified from serum. Specificity was confirmed via IP and immunoblot using COS7 cells transfected with HA-tagged full-length endoglin or HA-Engir constructs.

Immunohistochemistry

FFPE tissue sections and xenograft tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were incubated with A8 or Ab- Engir antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB chromogen development. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, scanned, and analyzed for staining intensity and distribution as percent area using digital image analysis (ImageJ) for each tissue core.

ELISA

The sandwich ELISA was conducted following the ThermoFisher ELISA protocol using the Antibody Pair Buffer Kit. 96-well plates were coated with 2.5 μg/mL of Ab-Engir as the capture antibody and A8 conjugated to HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as the detection antibody. Serum samples were diluted 1:50 in assay buffer, and optical density was measured at 450 nm. Data were normalized to control wells.

Xenograft and genetically engineered mouse models

Five SCID mice were orthotopically implanted with Panc1 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells per mouse). Tumors were harvested after six weeks for IHC analyses upon paraformaldehyde fixation. KPC (KRasG12D/+;Trp53R172H/+;Pdx1-Cre) mice were maintained according to institutional IACUC guidelines. Pancreata were harvested at 20 weeks, sectioned, and processed for H&E and IHC.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the discriminatory performance of biomarker staining between normal and PDAC samples, plotting sensitivity (true positive rate) versus 1−specificity (false positive rate) across the observed range of values. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as a measure of overall discrimination, with 95% confidence intervals estimated using the DeLong method. Sensitivity and specificity were computed across all possible thresholds, and the optimal cutoff was determined using Youden’s J statistic, defined as the threshold maximizing the sum of sensitivity and specificity minus one. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed t tests. For analyses involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison corrections was applied. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Identification of a previously unrecognized endoglin variant in PDAC cell lines

Although endoglin expression has been reported in malignant epithelial cells including PDAC, its relative expression patterns and potential isoform diversity across PDAC cell lines have not been systematically examined. To address this, we performed immunoblot analysis across a panel of human and murine PDAC cell lines to characterize endoglin protein expression. As expected, the canonical full-length endoglin receptor was detected at approximately ∼95 kDa in a subset of cell lines (Fig.1A). In addition, we observed an unexpected immunoreactive band at approximately ∼45 kDa. This lower molecular weight species was distinct from the well-described ∼65 kDa soluble fragment generated by extracellular proteolytic cleavage of endoglin by MMP14[16,17,23,24]. Notably, while full-length endoglin expression was variable, and in some cases, minimally detectable across the PDAC cell line panel, the ∼45 kDa species was present in the majority of lines examined (Fig.1A; red arrow).

To exclude the possibility that the ∼45 kDa band represented a nonspecific antibody artifact or degradation product arising during sample preparation, we performed immunoprecipitation using A8, a polyclonal antibody which has been previously shown to recognizes a large segment of the extracellular domain of endoglin[25], followed by immunoblotting. The ∼45 kDa species was robustly detected following immunoprecipitation but not in HUVECs (Fig.1B), thus likely supporting its identity as endoglin-derived protein that exists in PDAC but not endothelial cells. We next investigated whether this truncated form might arise from proteolytic processing. Panc1 human PDAC cells were treated with a broad-spectrum protease inhibitor cocktail for 6 h prior to protein extraction. Inhibition of proteolysis did not reduce the abundance of the ∼45 kDa band (Fig.1C), indicating that this species is unlikely to represent a proteolytically generated cleavage product. Together, these findings identify a previously unrecognized ∼45 kDa endoglin variant in PDAC cells normally not expressed in endothelial cells that is distinct from both the canonical full-length receptor and the known proteolytically shed fragment.

The 45 kDa endoglin variant arises from intron retention following exon 8

To determine the molecular origin of the 45 kDa endoglin species identified in PDAC cells, we performed 3′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (3′RACE) to characterize alternative endoglin transcripts (Fig.2A). The canonical human endoglin transcript comprises 15 exons encoding a 658 amino acid full-length protein, corresponding to a 2435-nucleotide mRNA (Fig.2A). Total RNA from two human PDAC cell lines expressing the 45 kDa protein (Mia PaCa-2 and Capan-1) was subjected to 3′RACE (Fig.2B). In the first amplification round, a forward primer targeting exon 1 of endoglin was used in conjunction with the universal 3′RACE primer mix (UPM). A second nested PCR employed a forward primer targeting exon 5 together with the short UPM primer. Agarose gel electrophoresis revealed three distinct amplification products of varying sizes (Fig.2B). Sequencing of the three gel-excised bands demonstrated that each transcript corresponded to canonical exon sequences (exon 7, exon 8, or exon 10), followed by intronic read-through into downstream intronic regions rather than normal exon-exon splicing (Fig. 2C).

Sequential PCR was performed using a forward primer in exon 1 paired with the universal primer mix (UPM), followed by a nested reaction using an exon 5 forward primer with UPM to enrich for alternatively processed transcripts. B. Agarose gel shows the 3′ RACE products from Mia PaCa-2 and Capan-1 cells. Primary PCR products generated with the exon 1 primer were subjected to secondary nested PCR with the exon 5 primer. Three prominent bands (red arrows) indicate distinct transcript species. C. Sanger sequencing of gel-purified 3′ RACE products. One band corresponded to a transcript terminating within exon 8, followed by retention of intronic sequence containing an in-frame stop codon, a polyadenylation signal, and a poly(A) tail, consistent with intron retention and premature transcript termination. D. Predicted protein structure of the truncated endoglin isoform. The theoretical translation includes the canonical endoglin sequence through exon 8, followed by a novel intron retention-encoded peptide sequence (highlighted in gray), resulting in a truncated protein lacking the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains.

Notably, the transcript arising from exon 8 (gel band 2) contained an in-frame stop codon (TAG), followed by a putative polyadenylation signal sequence (AATTAAA), and a downstream poly(A) tail (Fig.2C; highlighted in red). The presence of both a termination codon and polyadenylation signal suggests that this exon 8-intron read-through transcript is capable of stable processing and translation rather than being targeted for degradation. The predicted protein product of the exon 8/intron retention transcript has a theoretical molecular weight of 43.58 kDa, closely corresponding to the ∼45 kDa species observed by immunoblotting in PDAC cell lines. Importantly, this 45 kDa product was not detected in endothelial cells, in which only the canonical full-length receptor was observed, supporting a tumor cell-associated origin of this isoform. The exon 8/intron retention transcript encodes the extracellular domain through exon 8, followed by a unique 26 amino acid peptide derived from retained intronic sequence, and terminates prior to the transmembrane domain present in the full-length receptor. Together, these data identify intron retention following exon 8 as the molecular basis of a previously unrecognized and tumor-associated 45 kDa endoglin variant in PDAC cells.

A unique intron-encoded peptide enables isoform-specific antibody generation and in vivo detection

The exon 8/intron retention transcript encodes a 26-amino acid C-terminal peptide that is not present in canonical full-length endoglin. To determine whether this intron-derived sequence shared homology with known protein domains, the 26-amino acid peptide was analyzed using NCBI BLASTp (Fig.3A). Only limited similarity to known proteins was identified. Among them, the top three hits corresponded to human Neuropilin-2 (NRP2), human CLEC9A, and murine NADH dehydrogenase subunit 4 (ND4); however, these alignments demonstrated <50% identity and were noncontiguous, indicating low structural homology (Fig.3A). These findings supported the feasibility of generating an isoform-specific antibody targeting this unique intron-encoded sequence. Accordingly, a polyclonal antibody was generated in rabbit using a 12-amino acid peptide derived from the intron retention sequence as immunogen. The resulting antibody, designated anti-endoglin intron epitope (Ab-Engir), was affinity purified from serum (Fig.3A).

To validate antibody specificity, we cloned an expression construct encoding endoglin through exon 8 followed by the intron-derived 26-amino acid sequence (Engir). This truncated coding sequence was fused to an N-terminal HA epitope tag (Fig.3B). In parallel, HA-tagged full-length endoglin (HA-WT) was generated. The widely used A8 antibody recognizes an extracellular epitope upstream of exon 7 and therefore detects both full-length endoglin and the intron-retained variant (Fig.3B). COS7 cells were transfected with HA-WT, HA-Engir, or empty vector control. Immunoprecipitation (IP) using HA antibody efficiently recovered both HA-WT and HA-Engir at their expected molecular weights, whereas vector control yielded no detectable signal (Fig.3C). In contrast, IP with Ab-Engir selectively recovered HA-Engir but not HA-WT, demonstrating specificity for the intron retention-derived epitope (Fig.3C). Comparison of immunoprecipitates with whole-cell lysates confirmed substantial enrichment, supporting the selectivity and utility of the antibody for detecting the truncated isoform.

To assess whether Ab-Engir recognizes the truncated protein in vivo, Panc1 cells were orthotopically implanted into SCID mice. Six weeks post implantation, xenograft tumors were harvested and serial sections were immunohistochemically stained for endoglin using A8 and Ab-Engir antibodies (Fig.3D). A8 staining demonstrated broader and more uniform endoglin expression, consistent with detection of both full-length and truncated isoforms. In contrast, Ab-Engir staining exhibited a more focal and comparatively less intense pattern, suggesting that the intron retention-derived isoform is expressed in a subset of tumor cells and at lower relative abundance. These findings validate the specificity of Ab-Engir and confirm in vivo expression of the truncated endoglin isoform in PDAC tumors.

Detection of full-length and intron retention-derived endoglin in human PDAC tissues

To evaluate the expression of endoglin isoforms in primary human tumors, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on a PDAC tissue microarray comprising 75 individual patient samples across stages I-IV and 5 normal pancreatic tissues (Fig.4A). Staining with the pan-endoglin antibody (A8), recognizing the extracellular domain of endoglin of both full-length and truncated forms, revealed relatively high and uniformly distributed signal across all tumor stages, whereas normal pancreatic tissues exhibited minimal staining (Fig.4A). Quantitative densitometry analyses demonstrated that approximately 79% of PDAC patients were endoglin-positive using an optimal staining cutoff. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.915 (Fig. 4B,C), representing the first systematic assessment of endoglin prevalence in PDAC patient samples.

In parallel, the intron retention-specific Engir antibody was used to stain the same PDAC tissue microarray. Engir immunoreactivity was detected across all tumor stages, although stage IV tumors generally exhibited reduced staining intensity compared with pan-endoglin (A8) (Fig.4D). Overall, Engir staining was observed in 74.7% (56/75) of PDAC specimens, a frequency comparable to the 78.7% (59/75) positivity detected with pan-endoglin staining (Fig.4F). These similar frequencies indicate that expression of the intron retention-derived isoform is prevalent among endoglin-positive PDAC tumors, suggesting that aberrant intron retention is a common feature of endoglin dysregulation rather than a rare event. Consistent with these findings, ROC analysis demonstrated strong diagnostic performance for Engir immunostaining, with an AUC of 0.888, 74.7% sensitivity, and 100% specificity (Fig.4E). Although the diagnostic performance was modestly lower than that of pan-endoglin (AUC = 0.915), the comparable prevalence and perfect specificity support Engir as a robust and highly selective biomarker for PDAC.

Detection of the intron retention–derived endoglin isoform in PDAC patient serum

Having established Engir expression in PDAC tissues, we next examined whether this intron retention-derived isoform is detectable in patient circulation. Serum samples from 13 normal controls and 35 patients with PDAC were analyzed. We first performed IP using Ab-Engir covalently conjugated to agarose beads, thereby preventing co-elution of antibody heavy and light chains near the ∼45 kDa target protein. Immunoblot analysis revealed minimal or undetectable Engir signal across normal sera. In contrast, roughly half of all PDAC samples demonstrated robust Engir signal (Fig.5A; red asterisks). ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.82, with a sensitivity of approximately 62% and optimal cutoff value that resulted in the designation of Engir-positivity in 17 of 35 PDAC patient samples (Fig.5B). These findings are consistent with tissue microarray data demonstrating that the intron retention-derived isoform represents a major fraction of endoglin-positive cases.

To extend these findings into a scalable detection platform, we developed a sandwich ELISA using Ab-Engir as the capture antibody and HRP-conjugated A8 as the detection antibody (Fig.5C). Normalized ELISA optical density (OD) values demonstrated a right-skewed distribution among PDAC samples, with high outliers reaching approximately 4.0 OD units (Fig. 5D). Although partial overlap was observed between normal and PDAC samples in the 0.6-1.2 range, consistent with heterogeneous and subset-specific secretion, the overall distribution patterns supported discrimination between groups (*p = 0.047). ROC analysis demonstrated an AUC of approximately 0.82, with sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 84.6%.

Taken together, both IP-based detection and ELISA demonstrate that circulating Engir is present in a biologically defined subset of PDAC patients. Given that endoglin expression itself characterizes approximately three-quarters of PDAC tumors, and that Engir represents a subset of these endoglin-positive cases, the observed sensitivity and specificity values are consistent with the expected biology. These findings support further evaluation of Engir as a circulating biomarker reflective of a distinct molecular subset of PDAC.

Intron retention-derived endoglin isoform is expressed in the KPC mouse model

To determine whether Engir expression extends beyond human tumors and xenografts, we examined the presence of endoglin isoforms in the widely used KPC mouse model (KRasG12D/+; Trp53R172H/+; Pdx-Cre), a genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma that recapitulates key features of human disease[26,27]. Immunoblot analysis of a cell line derived directly from KPC tumors, as we have used previously[28], demonstrated the presence of both full-length endoglin and the lower molecular weight Engir isoform (Fig.6A). Silencing endoglin using shRNA resulted in reduction of both the WT and Engir bands, confirming that the truncated isoform originates from the endoglin locus rather than nonspecific cross-reactivity. These findings indicate that intron retention-derived Engir is produced endogenously in KPC tumor cells. We next assessed expression in vivo. Pancreata from 20-week-old KPC mice were sectioned transversely and analyzed by H&E staining and IHC. Wild-type control pancreata exhibited normal histologic architecture without neoplastic lesions, minimal endoglin staining by A8 that was limited to angiogenic vasculature, and no detectable Engir staining (Fig.6B). In contrast, KPC pancreata contained discrete regions of normal tissue interspersed with PanINs and invasive PDAC lesions, as identified by H&E (black arrows) and A8 staining. Engir immunostaining was markedly focal and concentrated within PanINs and PDAC regions, with minimal to absent signal in adjacent normal pancreatic tissue.

Expression of full-length and intron retention-derived Engir in the KPC genetically engineered mouse model of PDAC.

A. Immunoblot analysis of endoglin in KPC-derived mouse PDAC cells. Both full-length and intron retention-derived Engir are detected. Stable knockdown of endoglin was achieved using shRNA at multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 1 and 3, demonstrating reduction of both isoforms and confirming their origin from the endoglin locus. B. Whole pancreata from wild-type control and 20-week-old KPC mice were analyzed by H&E staining and immunohistochemistry for total endoglin (A8) and Engir. Engir staining is focal and enriched within neoplastic lesions, whereas normal pancreatic tissue shows minimal to absent signal, illustrating tumor-specific expression of the intron retention-derived isoform in vivo.

Consistent with human tumor findings, Engir staining was less abundant overall than total endoglin detected by A8 but demonstrated greater spatial restriction to neoplastic compartments. These results establish that intron retention-derived Engir occurs in a GEMM of PDAC and is enriched within neoplastic lesions, supporting conservation of this alternative isoform across species and reinforcing its biological relevance in pancreatic tumorigenesis.

Discussion

This study expands on endoglin biology in PDAC in several fundamental ways. First, it provides the first comprehensive assessment of endoglin prevalence within PDAC tumor cells, rather than focusing primarily on tumor-associated vasculature. Second, it identifies a structurally distinct intron retention-derived endoglin isoform that is molecularly separable from both the canonical full-length receptor and the proteolytically shed soluble form. Third, it demonstrates that this intron-encoded epitope can be leveraged to build a selective detection platform capable of identifying a biologically defined subset of PDAC in tissue and circulation.

Historically, endoglin (CD105) has been viewed as an endothelial marker and vascular therapeutic target[11,14,29,30]. Although tumor cell expression has been reported in several malignancies, its prevalence and isoform diversity in PDAC have not been systematically examined. Our findings demonstrate that endoglin is present in roughly three-quarters of PDAC tumors among 75 patient specimens analyzed, establishing it as a major tumor-cell feature rather than a niche vascular phenomenon. This reframes endoglin from a stromal marker to a tumor-intrinsic molecule with potential biological and clinical relevance in pancreatic cancer.

We further identify an alternative isoform generated through intron retention following exon 8. While intron retention is pervasive in cancer transcriptomes, most retained transcripts are presumed to undergo nonsense-mediated decay or remain biologically silent[[31], [32], [33]]. In contrast, the isoform described here contains an in-frame stop codon and polyadenylation signal, enabling stable processing and translation into a truncated, secreted protein.

The novelty of Engir lies in this intron-encoded peptide. Unlike many alternatively spliced isoforms that retain canonical domains, the retained intronic sequence generates a molecular signature with minimal homology to known proteins. This enabled development of an isoform-specific antibody capable of distinguishing tumor-derived Engir from background soluble endoglin. Although intron retention-derived neoepitopes have been described in other cancers, few have been shown to produce a stable, secreted protein detectable in serum and conserved across species. In PDAC, a disease marked by profound RNA dysregulation, this finding directly links aberrant RNA processing to a tractable circulating biomarker.

Detection of Engir in both human tumors and the genetically engineered KPC model further strengthens its biological relevance. Conservation across species argues against a cell line-restricted artifact and supports intron retention as a reproducible feature of pancreatic tumorigenesis. The focal enrichment of Engir within PanIN and invasive lesions suggests tight coupling to neoplastic transformation rather than generalized tissue injury or inflammation, potentially explaining why subset-restricted expression still yields meaningful discriminatory capacity in circulation.

From a biomarker perspective, Engir represents a biologically stratified signal. Because endoglin expression defines a major but incomplete subset of PDAC, and Engir arises from a fraction of those tumors, achievable sensitivity is inherently bounded by tumor heterogeneity. Interpreted in this context, serum detection rates reflect underlying biology rather than technical limitation. Rather than serving as a universal PDAC marker, Engir may define an endoglin-positive molecular subtype, aligning with precision oncology approaches in biological subsets.

The structural divergence of Engir from proteolytically shed soluble endoglin has practical implications. Conventional assays measuring total soluble endoglin cannot distinguish endothelial shedding from tumor-derived production. Targeting the intron-encoded epitope enables selective detection of a tumor-associated isoform, potentially improving disease specificity. In a clinical landscape where CA19-9 remains an imperfect circulating marker, orthogonal biomarkers reflecting distinct tumor biology are particularly valuable. Engir may ultimately complement existing markers within multi-analyte panels to enhance sensitivity and subtype resolution.

Beyond biomarker development, the existence of a secreted intron retention-derived isoform raises important mechanistic questions. Does Engir retain ligand-binding capacity or modulate TGF-β signaling? Could it function as a dominant-negative regulator or influence tumor-stroma interactions? The absence of the transmembrane domain and presence of a unique C-terminus suggest altered receptor dynamics and potential novel functions. Thus, Engir represents not only a diagnostic tool but also a window into RNA-driven proteomic diversification in PDAC.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Serum cohort sizes were modest, and longitudinal sampling was not available for most patients to evaluate correlations with tumor burden, treatment response, or disease recurrence. In addition, our preliminary analysis of publicly available TCGA pancreatic cancer RNA-sequencing data did not identify evidence of the ENG intron-retention transcript described here. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as conventional bulk RNA-sequencing datasets are not optimized for detecting low-abundance or previously unannotated intron-retention events, which often require greater sequencing depth and specialized splice-aware analytical approaches. Thus, the absence of a detectable signal in TCGA does not exclude the existence or biological relevance of this transcript. Future studies employing deep RNA sequencing and targeted transcriptomic analyses will be important to define the prevalence, regulation, and clinical significance of ENG intron retention in PDAC. Nevertheless, the consistent detection of the Engir protein across PDAC cell lines, orthotopic xenografts, primary human tumors, patient sera, and a genetically engineered mouse model provides strong orthogonal evidence supporting the existence of this intron-retention-derived isoform and establishes a robust foundation for future mechanistic and translational studies.

In summary, we identify a conserved, intron retention-derived endoglin isoform that is structurally unique, tumor-associated, and experimentally targetable. By coupling RNA processing dysregulation to a circulating protein product, these findings introduce a new perspective on PDAC biomarker development and tumor biology, positioning intron retention-derived proteins as an accessible and mechanistically grounded class of biomarkers in pancreatic cancer.
