Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts in eight low- and middle-income countries: a comparison of national surveys
Authors: David Villarreal-Zegarra, Sofía C Malaquias-Obregon, Nikol Mayo-Puchoc, Ali Al-kassab-Córdova, Gloria Yantas-Alcantara, Guillermo Almeida-Huanca, Yscenia Paredes-Gonzales, Jeff Huarcaya-Victoria, G J Melendez-Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is a public health problem that causes around 720.000 deaths worldwide yearly. It is also one of the leading causes of death in the population globally. Therefore, it is relevant to assess the factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts across the life course in eight low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).MethodsA cross-sectional study based on data from 2014 to 2017 derived from the WHO STEPwise approach to surveillance (STEPS) was performed. Algeria, Azerbaijan, Eswatini, Guyana, Kiribati, Malawi, Tuvalu, and Zambia were included. The inclusion criteria were people between 15 and 69 years old with complete data for the variables of interest. A pooled Poisson regression model was performed to analyse the associations of study variables with the outcome variables, suicidal ideation in the last year and lifetime suicide attempts. Study variables included gender, age, marital status, education level, work activity, use of tobacco, use of alcohol, and family history of suicidal behaviour.ResultsA total of 27,786 participants were included. The pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was 5.7% and lifetime suicide attempt was 1.9%. Only 24.8% of people with suicidal ideation sought help for suicidal ideation and only 23.2% sought medical help for their last suicide attempt. Poisons and pesticides were the most frequent methods for the last suicide attempt, followed by medications. In the pooled analysis, having a relative that attempted suicide was significantly associated with suicidal ideation (aPR = 1.07; 95%CI: 1.06–1.09). Also, suicidal ideation was directly associated with lifetime suicide attempts (aPR = 1.21; 95%CI: 1.18–1.22).ConclusionsOur study found a high prevalence of suicidal ideation and lifetime suicide attempts in eight LMICs, making it an important public health concern. Characteristics such as seeking professional support or treatment were associated with lifetime suicidal ideation and suicide attempts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07005-w.Keywords: Suicidal ideation, Suicidal attempt, Algeria, Azerbaijan, Eswatini, Guyana, Kiribati, Malawi, Tuvalu, Zambia

Introduction

Suicide is recognized as a sequential process culminating in a definitive outcome, rather than an isolated incident [1]. This process encompasses a spectrum of suicidal behaviours (SB). First, suicidal ideation (SI) emerges through thoughts about self-inflicted death. Then, detailed plans begin with methods of self-inflicted death. Third, there are several suicide attempts (SA) involving potentially self-injurious behaviour without a fatal outcome. However, in some cases, suicide occurs during the first attempt, highlighting the variability of suicide trajectories and the need to understand different risk patterns [2, 3].

Statistically, suicide is a global health concern with more than 720,000 deaths worldwide every year [4]. An estimation indicates that suicide ranks as the 14 th leading cause of death [5]. Also, it was among the top 10 leading cause of death in Eastern Europe, High-income Asia Pacific, Australasia, Central Europe, and High-income North America in 2016 [6]. For every suicide, likely 20 other people are making a suicide attempt, and many more have serious thoughts of suicide [7]. To prevent this, build an epidemiological profile of SB is needed.

By 2019, suicide was the fourth leading cause of death among 15 to 29-year-olds, and 77% of all suicides occur in low- middle- income countries (LMICs) [8]. However, LMICs are largely excluded of multi-country reports due to non-availability data [9]. Indeed, the Global Burden Disease report showed no increase in suicide deaths in LMICs by 2019, as 21 countries do not represent all regions [10]. LMICs face significant challenges in accurately documenting suicide-related outcomes due to inconsistent data collection methods and underreporting issues [11]. Due to the poverty, these settings have limited resources to prevent and intervene in SB. Thus, people from LMICs struggle with socioeconomic problems, limited mental health services, and welfare support [12].

Factors associated with SI, suicide plans, and SA have been extensively studied in high-income countries (HICs). These factors include sociodemographic characteristics, socioeconomic conditions, mental health disorders, and physical health issues. Sociodemographic factors may include younger age and female sex [13–15]; and the socioeconomic conditions risk factors of SB, could include living in poverty, unemployment, experiencing social, and racial inequalities [16]. Having psychosocial distress risk factors associated with SB may include depression, alcohol disorders [4], and exposure to physical or sexual violence in HICs [17]. Physical factors associated with SB may include chronic conditions, for example, cardiovascular disease, epilepsy, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and low blood pressure in LMICs [18–22]. Health risk behaviours include physical inactivity, early substance use, substance use disorders, and obesity in LMICs [23–25]. Despite this, a review highlighted increasing rates of suicidal ideation and attempts in LMIC, as well as a gap in prevention research, particularly among adolescents and young adults [26].

Given the high burden of suicide in LMICs and the lack of comprehensive data, it is essential to investigate the prevalence and determinants of SI and SA in these regions [10]. Additionally, the prevalence of associated factors changes according to geographical location, educational level, population growth, and age group distribution [6, 17, 27]. However, reports of that for SI, plans, and SA are under-reported or under-seen in LMICs because of the limitations in health systems and national information systems [28]. The aim of the present study was to assess prevalence and associate factors for SI and SA in eight countries of LMICs.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study based on data from 2014 to 2017 obtained from the WHO STEPwise method for surveillance of risk factors for noncommunicable diseases (STEPS), which consists of a national household survey aimed at measuring risk factors that determine the main burden of non-communicable diseases; this follows three steps: a face-to-face interview (items on tobacco use, alcohol consumption, mental health, physical activity, and unhealthy eating), physical measurement (height and weight), and biochemical measurement (blood and urine analysis) [29]. We used the WHO NCD Microdata Repository to extract STEPS survey information from Azerbaijan 2017, Malawi 2017, Zambia 2017, Algeria 2016, Guyana 2016, Kiribati 2015, Tuvalu 2015, and Eswatini 2014 [30].

Setting

As of June 11, 2022, 54 countries and territories in WHO regions had at least one STEPS dataset to request through the portal. The criteria to take into account were the periodicity and income level of these countries according to the World Bank; the former implies using the most current collection data since countries should repeat the survey approximately every 5 years according to WHO recommendation [29], while for the latter criterion, countries with low and middle-income levels were considered since the literature on IS and BS epidemiology evidences greater heterogeneity [11]. Furthermore, in an exclusionary manner, countries that completed the suicide ideation and attempt assessment modules were included; thus, Algeria, Azerbaijan, Eswatini, Guyana, Kiribati, Malawi, Tuvalu, and Zambia were selected. Their surveys were conducted between 2014 and 2017. They belong to Africa (n = 4), Oceania (n = 2), South America (n = 1), and Europe-Asia (n = 1). The most recent one was used for countries with more than one dataset available.

Participants

This study used STEPS data from Algeria, Azerbaijan, Eswatini, Guyana, Kiribati, Malawi, Tuvalu, and Zambia. Stratified cluster sampling was used in three stages. In the first stage, the primary sampling units were proportionally selected among the geographical areas of each country. In the second stage, secondary selection units (households) were selected using a simple random sampling method. In the third stage, tertiary selection units (individuals) were selected through simple random sampling. STEPS calculated the analysis weights by taking the inverse of each participant's probability of selection. These weights were adjusted for differences in the age and sex composition of the sample population compared to the target population.

People between 15 and 69 years old with complete data on lifetime suicide attempts, SI, and covariates of interest were included. Participants with missing data or refusal to answer the main outcomes were excluded. This age group was considered as it was the only one available in the dataset.

Measures

Suicidal Ideation in last year

In our study, suicidal ideation (SI) was conceptualized as the act of thinking about, contemplating, or planning to attempt suicide [31]. SI was assessed by the question"During the last 12 months, have you seriously considered attempting suicide?", with possible responses of"yes","no", or"prefer not to answer". Only positive responses were considered indicative of SI, while negative responses were interpreted as the absence of ideation. Subjects who chose not to answer were excluded from the analysis.

Lifetime suicide attempt

The variable lifetime suicide attempt considers any self-directed non-fatal behaviour with intent to harm and die, even if this behaviour is not evidenced by injury [31]. This was assessed using the single item"have you ever attempted suicide?". Affirmative responses were considered as having the outcome, whereas negative responses as not having the outcome. Participants who refused to answer the question were excluded.

Covariates

The sociodemographic variables evaluated in the study included: gender (female/male); age group (15 to 29, 30 to 44, 45 to 59, and 60 years or older); marital status (single, currently married, separated, divorced, widowed, and cohabiting); educational level (no formal education, less than primary school, end of primary school, end of middle school, end of high school or equivalent, high school, university, postgraduate degree, and Koranic school); and work activity (yes/no). In the case of Algeria, individuals enrolled in Koranic schools were not included due to the small number of participants (n = 1). Other variables were tobacco and alcohol use and family history of suicidal behaviour using dichotomous items. These variables were based on an extensive literature review in which they were associated with SI or SA [14, 32, 33].

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted in Stata 16.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). All analyses considered the STEPS complex sampling design. Absolute and weighted relative frequencies were used to describe the samples. Given that the outcomes were dichotomous and the cross-sectional study design, Poisson regression models were performed to estimate crude (cPR) and adjusted (aPR) prevalence ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Analyses were presented separately for each country and pooled for all countries. Models were adjusted for sex, age range, level education, current smoking, ever drank alcohol, vigorous activity at work, close family member attempted suicide, and SI. Owing to the multiple associations tested and the low prevalence of the outcome, a significance value of 0.01 was chosen.

Associated factors to suicidal ideation and suicide attempt

In the pooled analysis, female participants were slightly more likely to have SI compared to males (aPR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05), and participants aged 45–59 years (aPR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.97–0.98) and 60–69 years (aPR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.98) were slightly less likely to have SI compared to adolescents aged 15–17 years. In addition, active smoking (aPR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) and having a close relative who attempted suicide were associated with a higher likelihood of SI (aPR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.06–1.09). Discussion

Prevalence

The 12-month prevalence of SI in our pooled analysis was 5.7% (range: 1.4%–9.4%), which is comparable to previous cross-sectional studies conducted in 17 countries using population-based samples (range: 3.1%–12.4%) [34] and in studies focused exclusively on LMICs, where the range was 3.5%–11.1% [28]. However, our estimate was higher than that reported in another study involving ten LMICs (2.1%). Similarly, the pooled lifetime prevalence of suicide attempts (1.9%; range: 0.9%–4.8%) aligns with figures reported in countries such as Nigeria (1.0%) [35], South Africa (2.9%) [36], Bhutan (0.7%) [14], Ethiopia (3.7%), India (0.4%), and Nepal (0.7%) [28]. The similarities observed may be explained by the present study conducted in countries facing similar economic challenges. However, it is important to note that SI and SA were assessed based on the self-perception of the respondents rather than within a primary healthcare context. Possibly, the prevalence of psychiatric disorders by each country, access to lethal means, and incidence of non-suicidal self-injuries partially explain the difference in the prevalences of SI and suicide attempt in these countries [37]. In addition, other factors can explain these differences, such as political, social, cultural and economic factors [38]. Such as Malawi and Zambia have been exposed to gender-based, political, and racial violence, and have limited access to health services due to mismanagement of funds [39], and in Eswatini, pro-democracy and anti-police brutality rallies resulted in a harsh crackdown on human rights [39]. It is worth noting that other studies have reported slightly different findings. For example, a meta-analysis conducted among students in predominantly Muslim countries identified a higher one-year prevalence of suicidal ideation compared to our study (13.4%; 95% CI: 11.1%–16.1%) [40].

It was found that the main methods used in the most recent SA were pesticide poisoning and drug overdose (both 18.8%). This partially aligns with findings from countries such as Argentina [41], where only one method was typically used in SA, with drug use being the most common, followed by sharp objects as the second predominant method. However, the pattern differs in cases of completed suicides. In countries like Mexico [42], violent methods (e.g., hanging, firearms, and jumping from heights) are more prevalent, while pesticide use remains more common in rural areas of LMICs.

According to live fife, knowledge of the most commonly use methods of suicide in countries could help to implement suicide prevention strategies to reduce access to the means of suicide (i.e., raising public awareness, engage government sectors in the regulation of pesticides) [43]. In addition, of respondents who reported past 12-month SI, 24.8% has sought for professional help, and among those reported a SA in the last year, 48% sough medical care, results were lower than globally community adults samples (ranging from 34 to 42% and 49% to 55% respectively) [44].

Associated factors

Regarding sociodemographic variables, the risk was found to be nearly identical between men and women for SI and SA (aPR = 1.04 [1.03–1.05] and 1.01 [1.00–1.01], respectively). These findings contrast with previous studies conducted in both LMICs [45] and some HIC [46–49]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the limitations of the current evidence, which is affected by high heterogeneity and wide, imprecise prediction intervals. Moreover, most primary studies examining sex as a risk factor have been conducted in high-income countries [50, 51], potentially introducing bias, given that approximately 75% of suicide attempts occur in LMICs [4], particularly among specific subgroups such as young people.

Our results also showed that the middle-aged and 65 years and older group experienced a higher SI than other groups. Although certain studies corroborate these outcomes [47, 52], they diverge from other cross-sectional studies that have reported elevated rates among young adults [34, 46, 53]. Reasons for the cross-sectional variation in older adult suicide rates between countries could be due to cultural factors, genetic factors, prevalence of mental illness in the elderly (i.e., depression), life expectancy, socioeconomic status [54, 55]. In addition, consistent with other study [56], our findings showed that those with university education have more likely to have SI and SA. Reasons could be to the academic stress, financial pressure, lack of employment, and fear of poor academic performance among university students, and stigma related to mental health [56–58].

Furthermore, the study reported that current smoking and alcohol problems increased the rates of suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts. These results are aligns with other studies [14]. The World Organization Health estimates that both smoking and alcohol use disorders significantly contributes to suicide [4, 59]. Both drinking alcohol and cigarette smoking is associated with a risk of developing behavioral disorders, mental health problems, physical health problems, which could lead to suicide-related behaviors [59–61]. In addition, the family history (having a relative attempted suicide) was reported as risk factor of SI and SA. The event of suicide among the family can have a great impact between them, both in suicide survivors and the relatives of someone who has died by suicide. They often experience stigma linked to the event, loss intimacy, financial crisis, feeling guilty or blame between members, and may receive inadequate social support [62–65].

Although the previous findings were significant, their association was relatively weak. However, the relationship between IS and SA shows a more robust association, with people with IS being 21% more likely to have attempted suicide at some point in their lives. This finding is consistent with the sequential model of suicidal behavior, which suggests a progressive process that begins with suicidal ideation, continues with planning, progresses to self-harm, and culminates in a suicide attempt. It should be noted that suicidal ideation does not necessarily lead to a suicide attempt, as factors such as personality traits, resilience, psychopathological characteristics, and socio-demographic factors influence the outcome [66].

Limitations and strengths

The study findings should be interpreted considering its methodological limitations. First, given the cross-sectional study design, associations can be drawn but causality cannot be determined. Also, some variables could exhibit reverse causality, such as ever drank alcohol and current smoking. Second, females were the predominant sex of the respondents, which could lead to an overrepresentation of them and may not reflect the entire population of the countries under study. Third, the outcome variables were measured through retrospective self-reports, which might arise social desirability bias due to their stigmatizing nature. Although such statistics might be underestimated [67], well-trained pollsters collected information with standardized questionnaires. Further studies assessing SI and suicide attempts with standardized validated tools in the post-pandemic stage are required. Fourth, as this study was based on secondary data analysis, it would be favourable to assess other non-included variables such as mental illnesses, drugs use, family environment, emotional problems, and psychological trauma. Fifth, the data used were collected in different years; this temporal discrepancy makes it difficult to compare between countries, since socioeconomic, political and environmental conditions can vary considerably. Sixth, information on smoking and alcohol consumption impeded to determine if they are the result of a medical diagnosis. In spite of these limitations, this is the first multi-country study assessing SI and lifetime suicide attempt among eight LMICs from different continents. Also, sampling errors were adjusted through sample weights. Seventh, suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviors were assessed over two different periods (within the past year and over the lifetime, respectively). Therefore, the temporal discrepancy in this assessment may introduce bias when interpreting the results. Finally, we excluded missing data from the analysis, which reduced the sample size and could affect the representativeness of the results; for that, findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Implications for public health

LMICs experience poor access to healthcare services, particularly over mental health services provision [68]. Also, most of these countries have high rates of poverty, violence and inequality [69, 70], and poor rates of mental health literacy [71], among other issues that threatens mental health. These problems have led to LMICs accounting for more than 75% of suicides worldwide [4]. Given the specific and unique socio-cultural and environmental factors in LMICs, tailored interventions are required to avert suicide targeting vulnerable groups. It is of paramount importance to recognize these differential determinants and develop comprehensive strategies [72]. Owing to the multidimensional nature of this phenomena, these public health strategies should cover political, financial, social, cultural, science, engineering, educational, religion, and legal areas [73]. Importantly, countries, such as Malawi and Guyana, should decriminalize suicide as it hampers help-seeking and tracking, and stigmatizes suicide [74]. Government suicide prevention programmes prioritising screening, timely reference, access to psychotherapeutic and psychopharmacological interventions, and ensuring an appropriate follow-up are urgently needed. In addition, they should limit access to methods of suicide.

Conclusions

SI and suicide attempts are two major public health concerns in LMICs, but mental healthcare seems to be neglected. Similar characteristics were associated to SI and lifetime suicide attempts in the eight countries under analysis. The strongest association with SI was having a close family member that attempted suicide. Similarly, the strongest association to lifetime suicide attempt was having SI. Improving mental health in LMICs requires multisectoral interventions that are individualised and tailored to the characteristics of each country.
