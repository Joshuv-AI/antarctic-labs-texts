Intestinal dysbiosis associates with silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice via arginine and tryptophan pathways
Authors: Jie Han, Xinyu Zhou, Mingliang Guo, Chongyang Zhang, Chuncheng Liu, Lu Cai, Hongyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a life-threatening interstitial lung disease with a lack of effective therapeutic approaches. Silicosis is a subtype of PF that is specifically caused by the inhalation of crystalline silica particles. In recent years, the gut–lung axis has been shown to be involved in the occurrence and progression of various respiratory diseases. However, the involvement and specific mechanism of action of the gut microbiome in silica-induced PF remain to be elucidated. Therefore, we established a silica-induced PF murine model using an inhalation exposure system, and combined gut metagenomic and untargeted metabolomics data to correlate microbial and metabolic changes with profibrotic cytokine levels.ResultsIn mice exposed to silica dust for 64 days and 128 days, Akkermansia muciniphila and Staphylococcus lentus were significantly enriched, whereas the abundance of Lactobacillus murinus was notably reduced. Relevant network analysis revealed that these gut microbiota changes were highly correlated with metabolic disorders of tryptophan and arginine. Moreover, changes in the gut microbiome composition corresponded with the fluctuations in the levels of profibrotic cytokines, including transforming growth factor-beta, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, fibroblast growth factor, and hydroxyproline.ConclusionWe successfully established a murine model of PF induced by silica inhalation. Our results suggest that Lactobacillus murinus, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Staphylococcus lentus are key microorganisms involved in the development of silica-induced PF, while the arginine and tryptophan metabolic pathways serve as key regulatory pathways in the gut–lung axis contributing to disease development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05023-6.

Background

Silicosis is an interstitial lung disease caused by inhalation of crystalline silica particles, which ranks among the most severe occupational diseases worldwide. Epidemiological estimates indicate that over 10,000 new fatalities are attributed to this disease annually [1]. The basic pathological changes of silicosis include the formation of dust nodules in the lung tissue and diffuse pulmonary fibrosis [2]. Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a severe chronic interstitial lung disease characterized by the aberrant proliferation of fibroblasts and excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), accompanied by inflammatory injury and destruction of the lung tissue structure. The core pathological manifestation of PF is the progressive and irreversible scarring of lung tissue, ultimately leading to diffuse pulmonary interstitial fibrosis, organ dysfunction, interruption of gas exchange, and death due to respiratory failure [3, 4] PF is characterized by high refractoriness and high mortality [5]. However, therapeutic options for PF remain extremely limited. The median survival time of patients with PF is approximately 2–5 years, and the 5–year survival rate is only 20%–40% [6]. These statistics underscore the urgency of conducting in-depth research into its pathogenesis and developing novel therapeutic strategies.

The emerging “gut–lung axis” theory offers a novel perspective on the mechanisms contributing to the development of interstitial lung diseases. Substantial evidence indicates that the gut microbiota are critically involved in respiratory health and disease pathogenesis. Specifically, the gut microbiome has been shown to play pivotal roles in PF, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cystic fibrosis, lung cancer, and respiratory infections [7–12]. Mechanistic studies have shown that pulmonary pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae induce the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway in gut tissue cells, amplifying inflammatory responses [13, 14]. Influenza virus infection can alter the gut microbiota composition to promote the secretion of interleukin (IL)-15 by intestinal epithelial cells, enhance Th17 cell polarization, and thereby triggers intestinal inflammation [15].

Gut microbiota dysbiosis has also been shown to exacerbate lung diseases. Over 50% of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) exhibit a characteristic decline in lung function [16]. In mouse models of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), pulmonary microbial abundance correlates with the IL-17R signaling-mediated fibrotic process [17]. This apparent intricate cross-organ communication establishes a new paradigm for understanding systemic regulation in pneumoconiosis and related interstitial lung diseases.

Advances in multi-omics technologies have enabled gaining a deeper and more detailed molecular understanding of disease pathogenesis [18]. In particular, metabolomics has revealed associations between metabolite alterations and disease states, providing novel insights for exploring underlying disease mechanisms and identifying effective therapeutic targets. Recent studies indicate that gut microbial metabolites, including amino acids, short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and valerate, participate in the development of PF through mechanisms involving ECM accumulation, energy metabolism, epigenetics, and immune regulation [19–21]. Furthermore, metabolic reprogramming and dysregulation of metabolic pathways are recognized features of PF [22].

Despite this recent recognition of the association between the gut microbiome and PF, most related studies have been limited to rapamycin-induced or intratracheal silica-instilled mouse models [23–25], which do not reflect the disease development process of silicosis that arises from the long-term inhalation of silica. Therefore, to better simulate the disease pathogenesis triggered by inhalation exposure, we established a mouse model of PF using an inhalation exposure system for dust challenge. Specifically, we established 64-day and 128-day murine models of silica-induced PF. These models were then subject to metagenomic profiling and serum untargeted metabolomic analysis to dynamically elucidate the roles of the intestinal microbiota and amino acid metabolic profiles in the progression of PF following silica inhalation. This work thus extends the current understanding of the cross-organ regulatory mechanisms underlying the gut–lung axis, which can further provide a theoretical rationale for the development of precision anti-fibrotic interventional strategies targeting the intestinal microbiota.

Materials and methods

Laboratory animals

Thirty male C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were procured from Beijing Speuf Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Certification No.: SCXK (Beijing) 2022-0002). The mice were maintained in a controlled environment with temperatures ranging from 23 °C to 26 °C and relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, under a 12 h light/dark cycle regulated by automated lighting. Prior to experimentation, the animals underwent a one-week acclimation period to stabilize their physiological and behavioral conditions.

Optimization of model establishment conditions

To systematically observe the dynamic progression of pulmonary pathological responses following silica dust exposure and to determine key experimental time points, a preliminary experiment was first conducted to explore the model establishment conditions. In this phase, 50 mice were randomly divided into four silica-exposed groups (10 mice per group) with exposure durations of 7, 14, 28, and 56 days, designated as the PF7, PF14, PF28, and PF56 groups, respectively, along with a concurrent blank control group (10 mice). Control mice were normally housed in the same breeding environment without dust exposure. All exposed groups were treated using a PM 2.5 natural-inhalation exposure system (Shanghai Taw Intelligent Technology, China), where the mice were placed daily in an exposure chamber containing freshly generated 5-µm (99%) SiO₂ dust powder (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 4 h, ensuring whole-body uniform inhalation exposure. The system operating parameters were as follows: dust generator rotation speed 0.2 rpm, SiO₂ exposure concentration 980–1000 mg/m³, airflow rate 17 ± 1 L/min, dust output 21 g/4 h, chamber temperature 22.0 °C, humidity 14.0%, and O₂ and CO₂ levels of 20.4% and 0.04%, respectively.

Pathological progression was systematically evaluated by performing hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Solarbio, China) on lung tissues collected at the above early time points (7, 14, 28, and 56 days).The hydroxyproline (HYP) content in lung tissue was also measured. In addition, inflammatory cytokines [IL-6, transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, fibroblast growth factor (FGF)] were measured in the lung tissues from the 28- and 56-day groups.

Establishment of a PF model using dynamic dust exposure

Based on the preliminary exploration, this study established a well-defined dynamic dust exposure mouse model of PF In the formal experimental phase, 30 mice were randomly divided into three groups (10 mice per group): the blank control group {no treatment (NT) group}, 64-day silica-exposed group (PF64 group), and 128-day silica-exposed group (PF128 group). The dust exposure protocol was consistent with that used in the preliminary experiment, and the housing conditions for the control group remained the same. All subsequent studies were conducted using the PF64 and PF128 models.

Sample collection and processing

For the experimental group, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at designated time points, with death confirmed by the absence of a heartbeat and lack of respiration for at least 2 min. Subsequently, the abdominal and thoracic cavities were dissected to collect the lungs, cecum, and cecal contents. The separated left lung lobe was fixed in 4% tissue fixative, while the right lung lobe was preserved in a cryotube immersed in liquid nitrogen. Intestinal tissues were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline and either stored in cryotubes or directly immersed in tissue fixative; the contents were collected directly into cryopreservation tubes for subsequent use.

For serum preparation, mice were deeply anesthetized with tribromoethanol (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), as confirmed by the loss of corneal reflex, limb relaxation, and absence of a response to toe pinch. Blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and then centrifuged (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 4℃ and 3500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected as the mouse serum sample.

Histopathological observation of lung tissue

The left lung was immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h. After fixation, the tissue was thoroughly rinsed to remove residual fixative, trimmed to an appropriate size and shape, and oriented flat in embedding cassettes. Dehydration was performed through an ascending ethanol series followed by xylene clearing, after which the specimens were infiltrated and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4–5 μm; ribbons were floated onto glass slides, dried on a warming plate to eliminate residual moisture, and then subjected to H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Leica DM3000 LED biological microscope, Leica Microsystems, Germany) to evaluate structural alterations and to assess the extent and pattern of fibrotic changes in the pulmonary parenchyma.

Biochemical assays

Serum and lung tissues were collected according as described above. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (GenMei Biotech, Nanjing, China) were used to quantify the contents of TNF-α, TGF-β, FGF and HYP in lung tissues, as well as the contents of malondialdehyde (MDA) and HYP in serum.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the mouse lung tissues with TransZol Up RNA extraction kit (TransGen Biotech, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the genomic DNA removal reverse transcription kit (Sangon Biotech, China). Primers were designed using the online National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Primer-BLAST tool and synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. The designed primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Using the obtained cDNA strand as the template, RT-qPCR was performed to detect the target genes with SGExcel FastSYBR qPCR Master Mix (Sangon Biotech, China) on a C1000 Touch real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Gapdh served as the internal reference gene and the cDNA from blank control group samples was set as the baseline. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Metagenomic sequencing of mouse cecum contents

Metagenomic DNA extracted from mouse cecal contents was first quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer by assessing the (A260/280 ratio assessed) and inspected for integrity on a 1% agarose gel. Fragmentation to ~ 300 bp was then performed with a Covaris M220 focused-ultrasonicator. Paired-end libraries were subsequently constructed with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq kit, using adapters containing Illumina-compatible primer binding sites. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (2 × 150 bp), with all reagents employed strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC and quality-trimmed using Trimmomatic. Clean reads were assembled, and open reading frames (ORFs)≥100 bp were predicted with Prodigal and translated into amino-acid sequences. Non-redundant representative genes were functionally annotated by BLASTP searches against the NCBI-nr, KEGG, and CAZy databases [26].

Serum non-targeted metabolomics

Mouse serum was extracted with 400 µL of a 4:1 methanol: water solution containing 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine as an internal standard. For every 5–15 samples, an equal-volume pooled quality control (QC) sample was inserted to monitor system stability. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed at Shanghai Maijie Biotechnology Co., Ltd. using a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system coupled with a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 column. Raw data files were processed with Progenesis QI to generate a CSV three-dimensional matrix containing metabolite names, response intensities, and retention times. After removing internal standards and known false-positive peaks, the data were denoised and peaks were merged. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching against the Human Metabolome Database, METLIN, and the Meiji Cloud platform [27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0 software, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. A Two-tailed Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney test was used to compare data between the two groups as appropriate. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to assess the correlations between gut microbiota, serum arginine and tryptophan-related metabolites, and the expression levels of profibrotic factors.

Results

Impact of variable silica exposuretimes on PF development in mice

This study employed a dynamic dust exposure system to establish a murine silicosis-induced PF model. Mice in the experimental group were exposed to high-concentration silica dust via whole‑body natural inhalation (4 h per day; Fig. 1A). Based on preliminary experiments, multiple observation time points were selected: NT, PF7, PF14, PF28 and PF56 groups. Pathological changes in the lung tissue following silica exposure were assessed by H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. S1A-B). H&E results showed that the lung structure of the blank control group remained intact with uniform and orderly alveolar septa. In the PF7 group, the alveolar septa were slightly widened compared with those of the control group. By the PF14 group, inflammatory reactions began to appear, accompanied by widening of the pulmonary interstitium and localized alveolar collapse. In the PF28 and PF56 groups, inflammation persisted, with further progression of interstitial thickening and disorganization of the alveolar structure. Masson staining revealed a gradual expansion of blue-stained areas across the exposure groups. Masson staining revealed a gradual expansion of blue‑stained areas over the exposure period.

The HYP content in lung tissue showed a progressive and significant increase across all time points examined (Fig. S1C, Table S2). In addition, the levels of inflammatory factors (IL‑6, TGF‑β, TNF‑α, FGF) in lung tissue rose progressively at PF28 group and PF56 group with prolonged silica exposure (Fig. S1D-G, Table S2). Furthermore, the mRNA expression of Collagen synthesis‑related genes (Fbn1, α‑SMA, and Col1a2) was measured by RT-qPCR at these later time points, revealing elevated relative expression in the exposed groups compared with controls (Fig. S1H).

These findings from the early time points (7–56 days) demonstrated that the model exhibited progressive inflammation and structural damage to the lung tissue shortly after silica exposure, establishing a dynamic pathological foundation for subsequent fibrosis studies.

As the exposure continued, by day 64, marked alveolar collapse, a significantly widened interstitium, and further aggravated fibrosis were observed in the lung tissue. At day 128, the fibrotic lesions became extensive and notably more severe. Masson staining indicated a time‑dependent increase in collagen fiber deposition (blue‑stained areas) (Fig. 1B-C).

Further assays showed that the levels of inflammatory factors in the lung tissue increased significantly at days 64 and 128 compared with those measured at earlier time points and were markedly higher than those measured at day 56 (Fig. 1D–F, Table S2). HYP levels in the lung tissue and serum, as well as MDA serum levels were also significantly elevated at days 64 and 128 relative to those of the control group, which continued to rise with prolonged exposure (Fig. 1G–I). Similarly, RT‑qPCR analysis of fibrosis‑related genes revealed significant upregulation of the Collagen1α2, Fbn1, and α-SMA genes in the dust‑exposed groups compared with those of the NT group, with higher expression levels detected in the PF128 group than in the PF64 group (Fig. 1J). Additionally, a gradual decline in body weight was observed over the exposure period, indicating systemic physiological effects (Fig. 1K). In summary, this study successfully established a stable silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model through systematic multi-time-point analysis, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent research.

Temporal changes of intestinal flora in silica-induced PF model mice

To investigate the variation trend of gut microbiota during the progression of PF after silica exposure, metagenomic sequencing was performed on the cecal contents of the PF mice. To further explore the differences in microbial communities between various groups, α-diversity and β-diversity analyses were conducted on the samples, including assessments of four key α-diversity indices: the namely Sobs, Chao, Shannon, and Simpson indices (Fig. 2A). At the species classification level, there were statistically significant differences in the Chao and Sobs indicies between the NT group and the PF128 groups, whereas no significant differences were observed in the other indices. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to assess changes in β-diversity, which characterizes the heterogeneity of species composition among communities. PCoA revealed a significant separation between the NT and PF128 groups in the low-dimensional space, whereas no distinct clustering was observed between the NT and PF64 groups (Fig. 2B). These results collectively confirmed that silica exposure reshapes the overall structure of the intestinal microbiota, and this effect is more pronounced under long-term exposure (PF128 group).​

To further clarify the regulatory effect of silica dust exposure on the composition of intestinal flora in mice and more deeply analyze the temporal changes of intestinal flora during PF development, we examined dynamic changes in gut microbiota at the phylum, genus, and species levels. A total of eight phyla were identified at the phylum level (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Among them, three phyla showed significant differences in relation to silica exposure time. Verrucomicrobia and Actinobacteria exhibited a significant trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Proteobacteria abundance was significantly decreased in the PF64 group, whereas its relative abundance significantly increased in the PF128 group, although still remaining at a level lower than that in the NT group (Fig. 2C).

A total of 54 genera were identified via significant enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Pairwise comparisons of differential genera across groups revealed that there were nine distinct genera between the NT and PF64 groups, seven differential genera between the NT and PF128 groups, and five differential genera between the PF64 and PF128 groups. Supplementary Figure S2C–E presents comprehensive details of these pairwise differential genera. Venn diagram analysis of the differential genera (Fig. 2D) indicated that there were no common genera among the three groups. However, three genera, namely Cecivirus, Enteractinococcus, and Helicobacter, were shared between the NT versus PF64 and NT versus PF128 groups. Faecalibaculum was the common differential genus between the NT versus PF64 and PF64 versus PF128 comparisons, whereas Lactobacillus and Staphylococcus were commonly differentiated in the NT versus PF128 and PF64 versus PF128 comparisons. To further determine the temporal changes of the flora, we selected three of these shared genera with relatively high abundance for individual analyses (Fig. 2E). Among them, Lactobacillus presented a cascading decline, Staphylococcus had no significant change in the PF64 group but increased significantly in the PF128 group, whereas the relative abundance of Helicobacter decreased significantly in the PF64 and PF128 groups.

A total of 81 bacterial species were found to be enriched among the samples (Fig. 2F). There were 10 differential species between the NT and PF64 groups, 8 differential species between the NT and PF128 groups, and 6 differential species between the PF64 and PF128 groups. The specific species are shown in Supplementary Figures S2F–H. Venn analysis demonstrated four shared differential species between the NT versus PF64 and NT versus PF128 groups, one common differential species between the NT versus PF64 and PF64 versus PF128 groups, and one shared species in the NT versus PF128 and PF64 versus PF128 comparisons (Supplementary Figure S2I). Since the shared species among the three groups were not analyzed in depth owing to their excessively low relative abundance (Supplementary Table S3), we conducted individual analyses on three differential species with relatively high relative abundance (Fig. 2G). The relative abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila increased significantly in PF64, but decreased in PF128, although still remaining higher than that in the NT group. Lactobacillus murinus showed a cascading gradual decrease with exposure time, whereas Staphylococcus lentus had no significant change between the NT and PF64 groups, but increased significantly in the PF128 group.

To identify specific microbial taxa associated with PF we employed the Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method, which identified 12 species as candidate biomarkers (Fig. 2H–I). Among them, seven were enriched in the NT group, with Lactobacillus murinus, Helicobacter japonicus, Faecalibaculum rodentium, and Prevotella sp MGM1 exerting the most significant effects. The PF64 group contained one potential biomarker, Enteractinococcus helveticum, whereas four candidate biomarkers were identified for the PF128 group, namely Paenalcaligenes hominis, Romboutsia ilealis, Helicobacter_apodemus, and Staphylococcus lentus.

Collectively, these findings indicate that as PF progresses, the gut microbiota composition of dust-exposed mice undergoes progressive restructuring. Critically, we identified characteristic microbial changes across multiple taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, Verrucomicrobia, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria were significantly altered. At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Staphylococcus, and Helicobacter exhibited notable shifts. At the species level, Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus murinus, and Staphylococcus lentus displayed marked changes in abundance. These differentially abundant taxa may play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of PF.

Functional changes of intestinal flora in fibrotic mice

To assess the functional reprogramming of the gut microbiota in the PF mouse model, we performed metagenomic sequencing followed by KEGG pathway analysis of the cecal contents (Fig. 3A). The analysis identified significant enrichment of 46 pathways spanning six major functional modules, with the most pronounced enrichment observed in genetic information processing and metabolism. An in-depth analysis at the pathway level revealed that the global and overview maps and translation pathways occupied a dominant position, followed by the amino acid metabolism pathway. These results indicated that under PF conditions, the intestinal microbial community as a whole exhibited a state of highly active basic metabolism, with its core life activities significantly enhanced, among which amino acid metabolism was particularly prominent.

Further analysis of the differences in KEGG tertiary pathways among the NT, PF64, and PF128 groups revealed the dynamic reconstruction of intestinal flora functions during the progression of PF. Inter-group difference analysis showed that there were 48 significantly different pathways in the NT versus PF64 group, 14 significantly different pathways in the PF64 versus PF128 group, and 43 differential pathways in the NT versus PF128 group (Fig. 3B–D). Venn analysis (Fig. 3E) identified biotin metabolism as the only conserved pathway with significant changes across all three group comparisons. In addition, clear stage-specific characteristics were recognized: the phospholipase D signaling pathway, axon regeneration, and protein export were commonly enriched in the NT versus PF64 and PF64 versus PF128 groups, suggesting that these pathways characterize the early transition features of fibrosis. In contrast, the flavone and flavonol biosynthesis and carotenoid biosynthesis pathways were specifically enriched in the NT versus PF128 and PF64 versus PF128 groups, marking changes occurring in the later stage of PF development.

Notably, 23 differential pathways were found to coexist in the NT versus PF64 and NT versus PF128 comparisons, indicating that the core functional disturbances driven by both the occurrence and progression of PF are widely involved in basic metabolic and inflammatory regulatory pathways, including ABC transporters, Monobactam biosynthesis, Pyrimidine metabolism, MAPK signaling pathway, and Vitamin B6 metabolism. Among them, the significant enrichment of two key amino acid metabolic pathways, Arginine biosynthesis and Tryptophan metabolism, further establishes the core position of amino acid metabolism in PF-related intestinal dysbiosi.

Changes of serum metabolites in PF mice

To further explore the metabolic characteristics of mice with PF, we conducted an untargeted metabolomics analysis on their serum and observed the dynamic changes of metabolites. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a significant separation between the NT group and the PF64/PF128 groups, while no significant separation was observed between the PF64 and PF128 groups (Fig. 4A). Under the screening thresholds of variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and P < 0.05, we identified a total of 484 metabolites between the NT and PF128 groups, among which 86 were differential metabolites; 451 metabolites were shared between the NT and PF64 groups, with 100 being differential metabolites; and 198 metabolites were present between the PF64 and PF128 groups, including 28 differential metabolites (Fig. 4B–D). These results indicated that silica exposure significantly reshapes serum metabolites.

KEGG compound classification analysis revealed that, apart from phospholipids, amino acids were the most highly enriched category of metabolites (Fig. 4E). Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites (Supplementary Figures S3A-B, Fig. 4F) identified eight metabolic pathways shared among the three group comparisons (Supplementary Fig. S3C), including Lysine degradation, Glycerophospholipid metabolism, Tryptophan metabolism, Linoleic acid metabolism, Tyrosine metabolism, alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism, Ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis, and Arachidonic acid metabolism. Among these, Arginine biosynthesis was enriched in both NT versus PF64 and PF64 vs. PF128 comparisons, while Arginine and proline metabolism was enriched in the NT vs. PF128 comparison. Notably, Arginine biosynthesis and Tryptophan metabolism were also enriched in gut microbiota-related pathways.

Focusing on the arginine-related metabolites (Fig. 4G), compared with the NT group, the levels of L-proline and D-citrulline in the PF64 group were significantly increased, and N2-acetylornithine also showed a significant elevation, whereas the change in creatine level was not significant. In the PF128 group, although the level of L-proline decreased compared with that in the PF64 group, it was still significantly higher than that in the NT group; the level of D-citrulline dropped back to a level comparable to that in the NT group; the level of N2-acetylornithine was significantly reduced (resulting in lack of difference from the NT group); and the level of creatine was significantly increased.

Regarding tryptophan-related metabolites (Fig. 4H), compared with the NT group, serotonin showed no significant change in the PF64 group but was significantly decreased in the PF128 group; however, 5-hydroxyindoleacetate was significantly decreased in both the PF64 and PF128 groups. By contrast, Indole-3-carboxylic Acid was increased in the PF64 group and significantly increased in the PF128 group; Kynurenic acid was significantly increased in the PF64 group, and although it decreased from the peak in the PF128 group, it was still significantly higher than the level detected in the NT group.

Collectively, these results indicated that the arginine metabolism and tryptophan metabolism pathways may play critical roles in mediating the interplay between the gut microbiota and lung tissues during the progression of PF. The dynamic changes in metabolites associated with these pathways suggest the complexity of metabolic regulation at different disease stages and their potential links to gut microbial activity.

To explore whether adaptive changes associated with PF occur locally in the intestine, we further examined changes in intestinal barrier integrity and amino acid transport capacity (Fig. 4I–J). The expression levels of key tight junction protein genes in the cecum were significantly downregulated in the PF64 and PF128 groups compared with those in the NT group, with a more pronounced reduction observed in the PF128 group. These findings indicate that silica exposure impairs intestinal barrier function in mice, which is exacerbated during the progression of fibrosis. Concurrently, our results suggest that the intestinal microenvironment for amino acid transport undergoes remodeling during silica-induced PF progression. In comparison with those of the NT group, the expression profiles of multiple amino acid transporters were markedly altered in the PF64 and PF128 groups. These transporters include neutral amino acid transporters (SIT, SLC6A7, SLC1A4, SLC3A2), the basic amino acid transporter SLC7A2, as well as the acidic amino acid transporter SLC1A1.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that the progression of PF is accompanied by the disruption of intestinal barrier structure and remodeling of the intestinal amino acid absorption and an imbalance in intestinal amino acid transport.

Associations of differential bacteria and metabolites with key PF factors

To delve further into the potential regulatory network underlying the observed compositional differences of intestinal flora, dynamic changes in serum metabolites, and the expression of key factors in PF, this study integrated multi-omics data for a systematic correlation analysis. Correlation analysis of the bacterial species with relatively high abundance revealed certain associations among them (Fig. 5A). Moreover, significant intrinsic correlations were found among metabolites related to the tryptophan and arginine metabolic pathways (Fig. 5B). Comprehensive analysis of the relationships among differential flora, differential metabolites, and PF factors (Fig. 5C) further showed that the differentially expressed tryptophan- and arginine-related metabolites in the serum had specific associations with the differential flora at the species level. Specifically, Akkermansia muciniphila was significantly positively correlated with Kynurenic acid and L-proline, but was significantly negatively correlated with 5-hydroxyindoleacetate. Lactobacillus murinus was significantly positively correlated with serotonin and 5-hydroxyindoleacetate, but was significantly negatively correlated with creatine. Staphylococcus lentus showed certain correlations with metabolites, although they were not statistically significant (Fig. 5D).

Notably, correlation analysis among these key metabolites and PF-related factors revealed that creatine was significantly positively correlated with the levels of profibrotic factors (TGF-β, FGF, TNF-α, HYP), whereas serotonin showed a significant negative correlation with PF factors. HYP exhibited a significant positive correlation with L-proline and a significant negative correlation with 5-hydroxyindoleacetate (Fig. 5E). In addition, Lactobacillus murinus abundance was significantly negatively correlated with the expression of these profibrotic factors, whereas Akkermansia muciniphila and Staphylococcus lentus showed positive correlations with TGF-β, FGF, TNF-α, and HYP (Fig. 5F).

Overall, this study preliminarily advances our understanding of potential associations among gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and key PF factors. The findings indicate that specific gut microbiota may influence the levels of tryptophan and arginine metabolites, while certain metabolites (creatine and serotonin) and microbiota (Lactobacillus murinus and Akkermansia muciniphila) show correlations with the expression of pro-fibrotic factors (TGF-β, FGF, TNF-α, HYP). These results provide preliminary evidence for further investigation into the role of the gut-lung axis in PF.

Discussion

PF can be induced by multiple factors, including air pollution, smoking, aging, occupational exposure, microbial infections, and other causes [28]. As a specific subtype of PF, silica-induced PF, or silicosis, is primarily caused by the long-term inhalation of free crystalline silicon dioxide (SiO2) dust [2]. Despite significant differences in etiology, silicosis exhibits the classic pathological features of PF, such as excessive ECM deposition and abnormal fibroblast proliferation [3, 4, 29]. Although studies have shown that the gut microbiota undergoes alterations in mice with PF and that such changes are correlated with disease progression to a certain extent [30], the global in vivo changes in microbiota and metabolism during the development of PF induced by silica dust remain to be further investigated.

In the present study, we successfully established a dynamic inhalation exposure murine model of silica dust to establish pulmonary fibrotic mice at 64 and 128 days post-exposure. The levels of collagen and HYP in the lung tissues of the PF mice were significantly increased compared to those of control mice. ELISA detection further revealed that the levels of profibrotic factors such as TGF-β, FGF, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the PF mouse model. A persistent inflammatory response is a critical driving factor of PF. Damaged alveolar epithelial cells release a variety of cytokines and growth factors such as TGF-β, which in turn promote the transdifferentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. The latter possess strong ECM-producing and contractile capabilities [31]. Meanwhile, activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts can secrete more inflammatory mediators, forming a positive feedback loop that exacerbates the vicious cycle of fibrosis and chronic inflammation [6]. Our model pulmonary fibrotic mice displayed typical pathological features of PF (elevated collagen, increased HYP, upregulated inflammatory factors), consistent with phenotypic traits of silica-induced models reported in previous studies [32–34].

In recent years, the “gut–lung axis” has attracted extensive attention as a potentially crucial pathway regulating inflammation and immunity in distant organs. Alterations in the gut microbiota and its metabolites are associated with fibrotic interstitial lung disease [35]. Consistent with this evidence, our present findings demonstrated that the relative abundance of the phylum Actinobacteria decreased in PF mice during the progression of PF, which is further in line with observations in silicosis patients reported by Zhou et al. [36]. Lactobacillus, probiotic genus belonging to the phylum Firmicutes, is widely present in the gastrointestinal tract and has been reported to exert immunomodulatory effects in chronic respiratory diseases [37]. A reduction in Lactobacillus murinus may lead to the weakening of “probiotic signals” provided to regulatory T cells, thereby affecting immune homeostasis [38, 39]. Luo et al. [40] found that the abundance of Lactobacillus was significantly decreased in a bleomycin-induced mouse model of PF, which was restored after intervention with umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells. These findings are consistent with the results of our study. Akkermansia muciniphila is a mucin-degrading bacterium that exists in the intestinal mucosal layer, which has attracted substantial research attention owing to its immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties [41]. The anti-inflammatory properties of Akkermansia muciniphila stem from its ability to degrade complex mucins into simpler carbohydrates and produce organic acids essential for bacterial growth, which highlights its potential utility in alleviating lung diseases such as asthma, COPD, and respiratory infections [41, 42]. Furthermore, Akkermansia muciniphila was reported to induce the generation of regulatory T cells and promote the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, thereby attenuating airway inflammation and improving lung function [43]. Nevertheless, we found a significant increase in the relative abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila during the progression of silica-induced PF. Our correlation network analysis also indicated that the increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila was significantly correlated with the levels of inflammatory factors in lung tissues. This finding can be explained by previous studies showing that, in a specific intestinal environment, excessive enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila may contribute to the exacerbation of intestinal inflammation caused by epithelial barrier damage in mice [44–46]. Furthermore, the opportunistic pathogen Staphylococcus lentus tends to proliferate substantially during microbial dysbiosis. This bacterium not only exhibits multidrug resistance but also produces enterotoxins; it can also disrupt the integrity of the intestinal epithelium by activating the Th17 inflammatory pathway and forming biofilms [47]. Therefore, in our murine model induced by silica inhalation, the progression of PF was accompanied by altered abundances of three gut bacterial species—Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus murinus, and Staphylococcus lentus—and these changes were strongly correlated with the levels of inflammatory factors in lung tissues.

Our metabolomic analysis revealed the temporal changes of various metabolites during the development of PF, with a particular focus on dysregulation of the arginine and tryptophan metabolic pathways. The levels of arginine metabolites (creatine, proline, putrescine, spermidine, 4-hydroxyproline, and proline-hydroxyproline dipeptide) were also significantly increased. Levels of creatine, proline, and hydroxyproline are also elevated in other pulmonary diseases such as COPD and IPF [48]. Proline and its derivative HYP are important components of collagen, accounting for approximately one-third of its amino acid composition [49], and these metabolites play key roles in important physiological processes such as redox balance, cell proliferation, and differentiation [50]. Arginine can also generate creatine via the methylation of guanidinoacetic acid, and creatine possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [51]. In addition, previous studies demonstrated that arginine and its metabolic pathway play critical roles in bleomycin-induced PF in murine models [52, 53]. Specifically, ornithine, creatine, and hydroxyproline are significantly elevated in patients with silicosis and asbestosis [54]. Metabolic profiling of plasma from a silicosis patient identified proline metabolism as the primary dysregulated pathway, with l-arginine and kynurenine exhibiting a correlation with silicosis severity [55]. Significant differences in serum levels of glutamate, arginine, aspartic acid, and ornithine were also detected between silicosis patients and healthy controls [56].

Tryptophan metabolism also exhibits a significantly activated state in chronic inflammatory lung diseases, such as interstitial lung disease, IPF, and fibrosing alveolitis [57]. L-Tryptophan can enhance the expression of inflammatory markers and exhibits a profibrotic effect, especially under the synergistic action of TGF-β1. In contrast, inhibiting TNF-α signaling can attenuate the inductive effect of L-tryptophan on inflammatory and fibrotic markers [58]. Tryptophan exerts a direct profibrotic effect in bleomycin- and silica-induced PF models, although different metabolites involved in the tryptophan pathway have distinct functions in the fibrotic process. Kynurenine exerts an anti-fibrotic effect by inhibiting fibroblast differentiation and promoting collagen degradation [59], whereas serotonin exacerbates fibrosis by promoting inflammatory responses, protein exudation, and oxidative stress [60]. In this study, we identified a significant correlation between the abundances of arginine/tryptophan metabolites and the relative abundances of specific gut microbiota and PF-related factors. Taken together, our findings confirm the role of arginine metabolism and tryptophan metabolism in silicosis, which may represent a critical link in the involvement of the gut–lung axis in the progression of PF.

By integrating multi-omics data, this study investigated the concomitant changes in gut microbiota and serum metabolites during PF induced by inhaled silica (Fig. 6). Key findings demonstrated that the development of PF is accompanied by significant alterations in the intestinal microbiota composition, including increased abundances of Akkermansia muciniphila and Staphylococcus lentus, as well as a decreased abundance of Lactobacillus murinus. These microbial changes are closely associated with the dysregulation of arginine and tryptophan metabolic pathways. Further analyses revealed a strong correlation between specific microorganisms (and their metabolites) and key profibrotic cytokines, including TGF-β, FGF, and TNF-α. Overall, our findings demonstrate that the development of PF induced by silica inhalation is accompanied by alterations in the gut microbiota, and that amino acid metabolism plays a critical role in the gut–lung axis. In this study, multi-omics data integration allowed us to preliminarily characterize the corresponding changes in gut microbiota and serum metabolites in inhaled silica-induced PF. Yet the inherent complexity of the gut-lung axis and microbiota-metabolite regulatory network has restricted our in-depth exploration of precise causal mechanisms. We focused on the synchronous variations in gut microbiota and serum metabolites, identifying potential correlations between specific microbial taxa and metabolic molecules, but did not validate their causal relationships further. Future research will verify the causal links among key gut microbiota, serum metabolites and PF progression via fecal microbiota transplantation, germ-free animal experiments and metabolite intervention assays.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully established a murine model of PF induced by silica inhalation and conducted a dynamic comparative analysis of the correlation network among gut microbiota, the serum metabolome, and PF-related factors in mice at 64 and 128 days during PF progression. Akkermansia muciniphila, Staphylococcus lentus, and Lactobacillus murinus are key microorganisms involved in the development of PF induced by silica inhalation. The arginine and tryptophan metabolic pathways serve as key regulatory pathways in the gut–lung axis. By leveraging the gut–lung axis and integrating multi-omics approaches, we systematically explored the critical mechanisms underlying PF induced by silica inhalation, thereby providing a novel theoretical foundation for the treatment and prevention of silicosis.
