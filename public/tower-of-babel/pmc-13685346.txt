Prenatal ultrasound diagnosis and follow-up tracking of a case of congenital coronary artery fistula
Authors: Haoliang Huang, Yuxia Liang, Yuhua Yu, Xuebing Zhao, Lijuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital coronary artery fistula (CAF) is a rare congenital cardiovascular malformation characterized by an anomalous connection between a coronary artery and a cardiac chamber or great vessel. Prenatal diagnosis remains challenging.Case summaryA 29-year-old G2P1 pregnant woman was diagnosed with fetal left coronary artery-to-right ventricle fistula via prenatal ultrasound at 21 weeks. Postpartum follow-up showed progressive coronary dilation, leading to surgical repair. A cardiac enhanced CT scan also confirmed the diagnosis of CAF. One-year postoperative ultrasound revealed improved cardiac structure with residual mild complications.ConclusionPrenatal ultrasound is pivotal for early CAF diagnosis, guiding postnatal intervention and improving prognosis.

Introduction

Congenital coronary artery fistula (CAF) is a rare congenital cardiovascular anomaly, defined as an abnormal communication between a coronary artery and a heart chamber or major blood vessel [1]. Large CAFs may cause severe prenatal and postnatal complications, a definitive prenatal diagnosis is of great clinical significance for perinatal management and the selection of appropriate postnatal intervention methods.

Krause first described this malformation in 1866. The incidence is relatively low, accounting for approximately 0.27% to 0.4% of congenital cardiovascular malformations [2, 3]. Treatment options include pharmacotherapy, percutaneous catheter intervention, and surgical procedures. The pathogenesis of CAF remains unclear. Embryologically, it may result from infection or genetic factors leading to the persistent presence of myocardial sinusoidal spaces that fail to regress normally, forming Thebesian-type vessels [4]. CAFs are usually single, though multiple fistulas occur in a minority of cases. Approximately 90% of CAFs originate from a single artery, while bilateral coronary artery involvement accounts for only 2% to 10%. Among cases, 52% to 60% originate from the right coronary artery, and 35% to 40% originate from the left coronary artery. Approximately 20% of CAFs are associated with other intracardiac malformations, including tetralogy of Fallot, aortic atresia, and ventricular septal defect [5]. Theoretically, a fistula can occur between a coronary artery and any adjacent heart chamber or major artery. While most CAFs present with only one fistula, multiple fistulas may occur and be associated with fusiform or cystic coronary artery aneurysms.

Baschat et al. [6] reported that the median gestational age at which normal fetal coronary artery blood flow can be observed is 33+ 6 weeks. In early pregnancy and the second trimester, it is difficult to detect coronary artery abnormalities based on standard blood flow. The visualization of the coronary artery fistula at 21 weeks of gestation in this case confirms that detailed mid-pregnancy ultrasound examination enables the antenatal diagnosis of CAF. Therefore, identifying abnormal blood flow within the heart chambers is key to the prenatal diagnosis of CAF. The identification of an aberrant vessel originating from the aortic root and draining into the right ventricle constitutes the primary diagnostic feature. Due to the elevated flow velocity, the disturbed flow often manifests as a brilliant, mosaic-like color Doppler signal. Spectral Doppler can detect continuous blood flow signals throughout the cardiac cycle or predominantly during diastole. Flow velocities at the fistula outlet are typically elevated, reaching 2 ~ 3 m/s.

Here, we describe a case of prenatal ultrasound diagnosis and subsequent follow-up of a rare congenital coronary artery fistula.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old pregnant woman, gravida 2, para 1 (G2P1), had a unremarkable medical history, with no reported history of drug use, adverse pregnancy outcomes, or genetic diseases. She underwent a grade III prenatal ultrasound examination at 21 weeks of gestation. On May 19, 2023, fetal echocardiography revealed the following measurements: Left atrium (LA) 8 mm, left ventricle (LV) 7 mm, right atrium (RA) 9 mm, right ventricle (RV) 8 mm, interventricular septum (IVS) 1.5 mm, foramen ovale (FO) 4.3 mm, aorta 4.0 mm, and pulmonary artery (PA) 4.9 mm. Doppler studies showed tricuspid valve (TV) E-wave 0.36 m/s and A-wave 0.51 m/s; mitral valve (MV) E-wave 0.45 m/s and A-wave 0.56 m/s; aortic flow velocity 0.87 m/s; and pulmonary flow velocity 0.76 m/s, a fetal cardio-thoracic ratio within normal limits, a left-pointing cardiac apex, cardiac axis, and ratio of the left and right chambers that appeared unremarkable. Normal LV and RV wall motion. However, the left coronary artery was dilated at the root of the aorta, with an inner diameter of 1.4 mm, extending towards the apex (Fig. 1a). Color Doppler ultrasound identified an abnormal blood flow bundle at the aortic root. The left coronary artery exhibited dilation with an inner diameter of 1.6 mm, descending to the anterior ventricular wall at the cardiac apex in a “V” direction, with the fistula entering the right ventricle (Fig. 1b and c). The fistula opening measured approximately 2.3 mm in width, with a maximum peak flow velocity of 3.1 m/s, a pressure gradient of 35 mmHg, and a tortuous course. Pulsed Doppler ultrasound demonstrated left-to-right shunt flow signals during diastole (Fig. 1d); These findings are consistent with a fetal CAF involving a left coronary artery-to-right ventricle shunt. Prenatal consultation was recommended.

A prenatal diagnosis was conducted on the fetus. Amniocentesis was performed for karyotype analysis and chromosomal microarray analysis, both of which yielded unremarkable results. Consequently, the parents elected to continue the pregnancy. On October 5, 2023, two days postpartum, a neonatal color Doppler echocardiogram revealed significant dilation of the left coronary artery, with an inner diameter of 3.2 mm. The artery fistulates into the right ventricle (Fig. 2a). Additional findings included a patent foramen ovale (Fig. 2b), mild mitral regurgitation and a patent ductus arteriosus (Fig. 2c). Spectral Doppler demonstrated biphasic blood flow signals predominantly in the diastolic phase (Fig. 2d).

Follow-up echocardiography on April 30, 2024, showed progressive widening of the left coronary artery, with the main trunk measuring 7.0 mm in diameter. A large lumen was observed merging into the right ventricular apex, with an outlet size of 2.5 mm and a maximum blood flow velocity of 3.5 m/s, PG: 50mmHg. The patent foramen ovale, Patent ductus arteriosus, measured approximately 1 mm in diameter, and mild mitral regurgitation persisted.

A cardiac enhanced CT scan performed May 30, 2024, confirmed the diagnosis of a coronary artery fistula (specifically, a left coronary artery -to- right ventricle fistula) (Fig. 3). Due to the progressive nature of the condition, the patient was hospitalized on July 12, 2024, to undergo surgical correction under general anesthesia. The patient underwent a coronary artery fistula repair, open heart surgery with extracorporeal circulation, mitral valve reconstruction, patent foramen ovale repair and patent ductus arteriosus ligation.

One year following the procedure (August 25, 2025), a repeat cardiac color ultrasound indicated, LA: 30 mm, LV diastolic diameter: 38 mm, LV systolic diameter: 26 mm, RA: 28 mm, RV: 22 mm, IVS: 5 mm, LV ejection fraction: 62%, fractional shortening: 33%, end-diastolic volume: 65 ml, stroke volume: 41 ml, cardiac output: 3.2L/min, main pulmonary artery: 19 mm, AO: 16 mm, TV: 0.6m/s, MV: 1.1m/s, AO: 0.9m/s, PV: 0.8m/s, tissue doppler imaging: septal e’ (E’): 0.08 m/s, septal s’ (S’): 0.05 m/s, a slightly enlarged left atrium and a normal-sized right atrium. No residual shunt was observed at the foramen ovale repair site. Findings included limited tricuspid regurgitation (estimated right ventricular pressure: 15 mmHg) and stable results from the mitral valve reconstruction (regurgitation area:1.95 cm²). While the right ventricle was normal in size, the left ventricle remained enlarged. The interventricular septum was intact, and both the septum and the posterior left ventricular wall displayed coordinated, acceptable motion. The aortic and pulmonary arteries appeared normal. Regarding the coronary arteries, the left coronary artery remained widened, postoperative echocardiography demonstrated a main artery diameter of 6.1 mm and a left anterior descending artery diameter of 8.7 mm, with complete closure of the fistula (no residual shunt). The proximal RCA measured 2.2 mm. No pericardial effusion was detected. The final diagnostic assessment suggests: status post-coronary artery fistula repair, mitral valve reconstruction, and patent foramen ovale repair, with PDA ligation and mild mitral regurgitation.

Discussion

Larger CAFs may cause severe complications requiring intervention during the neonatal period or early infancy. Surgical management involves procedures such as coronary artery incision and repair, ligation of the fistulas, and closure of pericardial fistulas. The primary objective of surgical intervention is to obliterate the fistula bypass without compromising the normal myocardial blood supply. Consequently, prenatal diagnosis is critical for facilitating informed birth decisions, expediting early postnatal diagnosis, and selecting optimal treatment strategies [7].

Although coronary artery fistulas have a small potential for spontaneous resolution, the probability is low. This limited regression potential may stem from continuous blood flow within the relatively thin fistula, which promotes atherosclerotic plaque formation on the inner wall of the vessel [8]. Unlike cases that may undergo spontaneous closure, the patient exhibited progressive coronary artery ectasia, highlighting that regular multimodal surveillance -including echocardiography and coronary artery computed tomography angiography (CTA) -is fundamental for formulating therapeutic strategies. Surgical correction becomes necessary when significant hemodynamic alterations (e.g., risk of high-output heart failure) or anatomical deterioration (e.g., vascular dilation) occur. In this case, surgery was performed at 7 months of age, and one-year postoperative follow-up revealed only mild mitral regurgitation and a tiny patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), confirming the safety and efficacy of early intervention.

However, the sensitivity of fetal ultrasound examination is limited by gestational age, fetal position, and maternal factors. Consequently, CAF requires careful differential diagnosis from the following conditions, it can be differentiated by color and spectral Doppler according to flow timing and origin, : (1) Aortic valve regurgitation: Because certain coronary arteries course along the aortic valve groove, when a coronary artery fistula occurs, the aortic valve groove becomes CAF-associated widening of the groove and increased flow velocity—compounded by the rapid fetal heart rate—may mimic aortic valve regurgitation. In such instances, color and spectral Doppler are essential for differentiation; specifically, aortic valve regurgitation occurs during systole, whereas CAF flow is typically diastolic or continuous; (2) Ventricular septal defect (VSD) : Abnormal flow signals from the coronary arteries entering the heart chambers must be distinguished from those associated with VSDs, particularly the muscular type. Critically, VSD flow is trans-septal, while CAF originates from the atrioventricular wall; (3) Coronary sinus: On two-dimensional echocardiography, the coronary sinus draining into the right atrium is often misidentified as a dilated coronary artery. Color Doppler can distinguish between them, as CAF flow is typically high-velocity and turbulent, whereas coronary sinus return is characterized by lower velocity signals that are frequently difficult to visualize.

The advent of high-resolution ultrasound and evolving prenatal technology has enabled the prenatal diagnosis of coronary artery fistula. In addition to fetal echocardiography, diagnostic modalities also include fetal cardiac MRI and postnatal CTA. However, ultrasound remains the preferred method for prenatal screening and postnatal follow-up due to its noninvasive nature, real-time imaging capabilities, and high repeatability. Despite these tools, a considerable number of cases remain undiagnosed prenatally. In this specific case, the diagnosis was a fetal left coronary artery-right ventricle fistula. Echocardiography revealed abnormal high-speed blood flow bundles within the heart chambers, which was the pathognomonic finding for diagnosis. Typically, the left coronary artery originates abnormally from the aortic root and connects to the right ventricle. This anomaly and associated hemodynamic abnormalities can be characterized through ultrasound, which permits observation of the course of the anomalous blood flow bundle, the flow velocity at the fistula, and consequent changes in cardiac function. Through ultrasound diagnosis, the abnormal heart structure and hemodynamic changes can be visually observed, Antenatal diagnosis can help doctors to determine the presence and severity of the coronary artery fistula, providing detailed anatomical and hemodynamic information, assessing the severity of the lesion, and assisting doctors in formulating reasonable treatment plans, guiding the selection and operation of surgical or interventional treatment. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-case report, the sample is limited in representativeness, and the findings cannot be directly generalized to all patients with CAF. Larger cohort studies are required to validate the long-term prognosis. Second, the absence of intraoperative real-time imaging such as intraoperative echocardiography or angiography and its coregistration with preoperative imaging limits the precise evaluation of dynamic anatomical changes during surgical repair. Third, etiological investigation remains incomplete. Only CMA was performed, without targeted gene panel sequencing for coronary artery anomalies, leaving the underlying genetic etiology unclear. Finally, the follow-up duration of one year is relatively short. This timeframe may be insufficient to capture the full spectrum of coronary remodeling and to predict late complications such as arrhythmias or aneurysm formation. Extended follow-up is warranted to monitor the regression of dilated coronary arteries and to assess the potential risk of long-term adverse events.

In conclusion, Prenatal ultrasound is a valuable tool for the detection of CAF. Although surgical intervention was required in this case, early diagnosis and timely management resulted in favorable short-term outcomes. Familiarity with this anomaly and its characteristic sonographic features facilitates accurate diagnosis and informs management planning. This case supports a proactive surgical approach when progressive coronary dilation is identified during imaging surveillance. It also underscores the importance for clinicians to pay close attention to postoperative long-term structural remodeling and to consider the underlying genetic background of the disease.
