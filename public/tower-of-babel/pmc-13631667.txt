Reprogramming macrophage polarization via CD44-targeted and ROS-responsive apabetalone-loaded micelles for rheumatoid arthritis treatment
Authors: Jing-Feng Huang, Wen Qin, Dong-Mei Xiao, Hai-Bo Mao, Guo-Hua Fu, Jing-Bo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current treatments for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are highly limited by poor joint targeting, systemic toxicity, and an inability to precisely modulate the complex inflammatory microenvironment driven by overactive macrophages and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS). To address these core challenges, this study developed a novel dual-functional nanoplatform: chondroitin sulfate-thioketal-poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (CS-TK-PLGA) micelles designed to encapsulate the anti-inflammatory drug apabetalone (CS/TK/Apa). This system leverages its CS corona for CD44 receptor-mediated uptake by activated macrophages. Subsequently, the ROS-responsive thioketal (TK) linker ensures on-demand drug release triggered by the high oxidative stress unique to the arthritic synovium. Both in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrate that CS/TK/Apa micelles effectively promote M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization, suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways. Crucially, the micellar formulation achieved a 2.2-fold increase in systemic exposure compared to the free drug, translating to significantly improved therapeutic efficacy in alleviating joint inflammation, reducing cartilage degradation, and minimizing bone erosion in RA mouse models. Supported by excellent biocompatibility and low systemic toxicity profiles, this CD44-targeted and ROS-responsive CS/TK/Apa nanoplatform offers a compelling precision strategy for RA therapy.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a common autoimmune disease characterized primarily by chronic, progressive joint inflammation, clinically manifesting as joint swelling, pain, functional impairment, and in severe cases, significant disability. A key pathological hallmark is synovial inflammation accompanied by progressive cartilage and bone destruction, a process in which infiltrating macrophages play a pivotal role (Cutolo et al., 2022). Within the arthritic joint microenvironment—characterized by hypoxia, elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and a dense cytokine milieu—these macrophages are predominantly polarized towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype via HIF-1α activation (Cutolo et al., 2025; Fearon et al., 2016). M1 macrophages aggressively secrete potent pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) that perpetuate and exacerbate joint damage (Cutolo et al., 2022). Consequently, targeting these aggressive cells has emerged as a vital therapeutic strategy. While some approaches focus on depleting M1 macrophages, regulating the microenvironment to induce an M1-to-M2 phenotypic transition holds significant promise, as M2 macrophages actively secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines to promote inflammation resolution (Cutolo et al., 2022). Currently, the clinical management of RA heavily relies on systemic therapies, including NSAIDs, conventional DMARDs, biologic agents, and potent glucocorticoids like dexamethasone (Luta and Mandl, 2026; Zhang et al., 2022). However, the long-term or high-dose systemic administration of these drugs frequently inevitably widespread distribution, leading to substantial adverse effects such as osteoporosis, muscle atrophy, and skin thinning (Palmowski et al., 2026; Tam et al., 2024). Therefore, there is a critical need to develop multifunctional nanocarrier strategies capable of precisely targeting the inflamed joint, thereby remodeling the localized macrophage phenotypes while simultaneously minimizing systemic drug exposure and associated toxicities.

Apabetalone (RVX-208) is a selective inhibitor of the second bromodomain (BD2) across BET proteins (BRD2, BRD3, BRD4), with BRD4 representing a primary functional target. It is currently in advanced clinical development (Phase III) for cardiovascular and metabolic indications, including acute coronary syndrome and chronic kidney disease (Allison, 2021; Nicholls et al., 2021). While preclinical evidence suggests a potential for Apabetalone to modulate inflammatory pathways (Borck et al., 2020; Jahagirdar et al., 2014), the direct therapeutic efficacy of Apabetalone in RA, and specifically its impact on macrophage polarization (M1-to-M2 transition), remains largely unexplored. The clinical translation of Apabetalone faces significant challenges. Its poor aqueous solubility results in limited oral bioavailability, while its hydrophobicity drives non-specific tissue distribution and dose-dependent systemic toxicities. Furthermore, lack of target specificity within the body leads to systemic distribution, which is associated with dose-limiting toxicities observed in clinical trials, such as thrombocytopenia, gastrointestinal disturbances (diarrhea, vomiting), fatigue, anemia, and hyperbilirubinemia (Wang and Sun, 2017). These challenges underscore the need for innovative delivery strategies to enhance drug accumulation at the inflamed joint site and reduce off-target effects, particularly for chronic conditions like RA requiring sustained treatment (Yang et al., 2017).

To overcome the limitations of conventional RA therapies, this project employs nanotechnology to achieve targeted delivery of Apabetalone to the affected joints. Specifically, chondroitin sulfate (CS), a natural glycosaminoglycan and a ligand for the adhesion molecule CD44, is utilized as a targeting moiety. CD44 is known to be overexpressed on activated macrophages and synovial fibroblasts in the RA synovium (Sarraj et al., 2006). To this end, an amphiphilic copolymer, chondroitin sulfate-thioketal-poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (CS-TK-PLGA), was synthesized by conjugating CS to the hydrophobic, biodegradable polymer PLGA via a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive thioketal (TK) linker. This copolymer self-assembles in aqueous media, forming CS-TK-PLGA conjugate micelles capable of efficiently encapsulating the hydrophobic drug Apabetalone, resulting in Apabetalone-loaded CS-TK-PLGA micelles (CS/TK/Apa micelles).

These engineered micelles offer a dual advantage for targeted drug delivery: (1) Active Targeting: The CS shell facilitates specific binding to CD44 receptors, which are abundantly expressed on macrophages within the inflamed RA synovium, thereby promoting selective uptake by these pathogenic cells (Ren et al., 2024; Sarraj et al., 2006). (2) Stimuli-Responsive Drug Release: The TK linker is designed to be selectively cleaved under the elevated intracellular ROS levels characteristic of activated inflammatory cells, such as M1 macrophages, within the RA joint (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). This cleavage triggers rapid micelle disassembly, leading to the localized release of encapsulated Apabetalone at the site of action.

In summary, this study aims to develop and characterize CS/TK/Apa micelles - a novel nanoplatform integrating inflammation-targeting (via CD44) and ROS-responsive drug release capabilities for RA therapy. Given the largely unexplored potential of Apabetalone as a therapeutic agent for RA, particularly concerning its effects on macrophage polarization and the modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment within the joint, we will optimize the preparation process of these micelles and critically evaluate their in vitro and in vivo targeting efficiency, ROS-responsive release kinetics, and therapeutic efficacy in modulating macrophage polarization and suppressing inflammation. This approach holds significant promise for achieving active targeting and precise drug delivery in RA, potentially enhancing the therapeutic index of Apabetalone.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chondroitin sulfate (CS) and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) were purchased from Xi'an ruixi Biological Technology (China). Apabetalone and indocyanine green (ICG) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology (China). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and Nile Red (NR) were purchased from Energy Chemical (China). CCK-8 assay kit (C0041) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Anti-CD44 (30854–1-AP), anti-CD80 (65076–1-Ig), anti-CD206 (98031–1-RR), anti-Arginase-1 (Arg-1, 16001–1-AP) and anti-iNOS (22226–1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech. TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β and IL-10 ELISA kits were purchased from BOSTER Biological Technology (China).

Synthesis and characterization of CS-TK-PLGA

The CS-TK-PLGA conjugate was synthesized using a two-step method (Fig. 1A). In the first step, PLGA (molecular weight 1.5 kDa), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI), and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stirred at room temperature for 1 h to activate the terminal carboxylic acid groups of PLGA. Subsequently, 2,2′-(propane-2,2-diylbis(sulfanediyl))diethanamine (TK linker) dissolved in DMSO was added to the reaction mixture, which was then stirred continuously at room temperature for a further 24 h. The resulting product, TK-modified PLGA (TK-PLGA), was purified by dialysis against deionized water (molecular weight cutoff, 1.0 kDa) for 48 h, followed by lyophilization. In the second step, chondroitin sulfate (CS, molecular weight 8000 Da), O-(7-azabenzotriazol-1-yl)-N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HATU), and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) were dissolved in a DMSO/water mixture (9,1 v/v) and stirred at room temperature for 1 h to activate the carboxylic acid groups of CS. TK-PLGA dissolved in DMSO was then added to this activated CS solution, and the mixture was stirred again at room temperature for 24 h to facilitate conjugation. The final CS-TK-PLGA conjugate product was purified by dialysis against deionized water (molecular weight cutoff, 3.5 kDa) for 48 h and lyophilized. The chemical structure of the synthesized CS-TK-PLGA conjugate was confirmed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR), and its molecular weight distribution was assessed by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). The critical micelle concentration (CMC) of the CS-TK-PLGA conjugates was determined using the pyrene fluorescent probe method (Cai et al., 2025). Briefly, 10 mg of CS-TK-PLGA was accurately weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water to yield a 1 mg/mL stock solution. This stock solution was probe-sonicated for 30 cycles at 100 W (2 s on, 3 s off) to ensure complete dispersion and micelle formation. Serial dilutions of the sonicated stock solution were then prepared in distilled water to obtain concentrations. A specific volume of a pyrene solution in acetone was added to each diluted conjugate solution to achieve a final pyrene concentration of 6 × 10−7 M. After thorough vortex mixing, the samples were incubated in a water bath at room temperature for 30 min to allow pyrene partitioning. The fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of pyrene in each sample were subsequently recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The ratio of the fluorescence intensities at the first (I1 ≈ 374 nm) and third (I3 ≈ 385 nm) vibrational peaks of the pyrene emission spectrum were calculated. The CMC value was determined as the concentration at the inflection point on a plot of the I374/I385 ratio versus the logarithm of the CS-TK-PLGA concentration.

Preparation of CS/TK/Apa micelles

CS/TK/Apa micelles were prepared using the dialysis method (Yoon et al., 2012). Briefly, a predetermined amount of CS-TK-PLGA conjugate was accurately weighed and dissolved in 30 mL of distilled water within a round-bottom flask under magnetic stirring at room temperature. Once fully dissolved, a solution of apabetalone in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (2 mg/mL) was added dropwise slowly to the stirring CS-TK-PLGA solution. The mixture was stirred for an additional 2 h to allow drug loading. The solution was then probe-sonicated (100 W, 2 s on, 3 s off) for 30 cycles to facilitate micelle formation and drug encapsulation. The sonicated solution was transferred into a dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff, MWCO 3500 Da) and dialyzed against a large volume of distilled water for 24 h to remove free (unencapsulated) apabetalone and DMSO. Following dialysis, the solution containing the apabetalone-loaded micelles was probe-sonicated again for 30 cycles (same parameters) and subsequently centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to remove any large aggregates or undissolved material. The supernatant, containing the purified CS/TK/Apa micelles, was collected. Subsequently, the collected clear micellar solution was pre-frozen at −80 °C overnight and then freeze-dried using a lyophilizer for 48 h. The resulting lyophilized CS/TK/Apa micelles appeared as a fluffy, white solid powder.

Characterization of CS/TK/Apa micelles

To prepare1 mg/mL solutions of the CS/TK/Apa micelles, 10 mg of each micelle was accurately weighed and dispersed in 10 mL of distilled water. The resulting dispersion was then subjected to probe sonication for 20 cycles at 400 W, with each cycle consisting of 2 s of sonication followed by a 3-s pause. The particle size distribution and zeta potential were subsequently determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern).

The morphology of the CS/TK/Apa micelles was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi). A micelle solution was diluted to a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL with deionized water, and a small aliquot was applied to carbon film-coated copper grids. Following air-drying, the samples were stained with 10 μL of a 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid solution for 30 s. Excess stain was then removed using filter paper, and the grids were examined via TEM.

The entrapment efficiency (EE%) and drug loading (DL%) of apabetalone within the CS/TK/Apa micelles were determined using a solvent extraction method coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. Briefly, a 200 μL aliquot of the micellar solution was diluted with methanol at a 1:5 (v/v) ratio and vortexed vigorously for 5 min to disrupt the micelles and fully solubilize the drug. The mixture was then centrifuged to remove any insoluble polymer or excipients. The concentration of apabetalone in the clear supernatant was subsequently quantified by HPLC. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a C18 reverse-phase column maintained at 20 °C. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and water (90,10, v/v) delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Apabetalone was detected using a UV detector set at 280 nm. To optimize the formulation, different feeding ratios were evaluated. The feeding ratio is defined as the initial mass percentage of the added drug relative to the carrier material (5%, 10%, and 15%). EE% and DL% were calculated using the following equations:EE%=Amount of apabetalone in micelles/Total amount of apabetalone added×100%DL%=Amount of apabetalone in micelles/Total weight of micelles×100%

Stability of CS/TK/Apa micelles

The stability of the CS/TK/Apa micelles was evaluated by monitoring changes in particle size over time under different storage conditions. Micelle solutions (1 mg/mL) were prepared in three distinct media: deionized water, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and PBS supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The particle size of the micelles in each sample was measured immediately after preparation (day 0) and subsequently at daily intervals for a period of 7 days.

In vitro ROS-responsive release of CS/TK/Apa micelles

The in vitro ROS-responsive release profiles of apabetalone from CS/TK/Apa micelles were evaluated in comparison to non-responsive CS/Apa micelles using a dialysis bag method. Briefly, accurately weighed samples of lyophilized CS/TK/Apa micelles and CS/Apa micelles were each dissolved in 4.0 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) release medium. Each solution was then transferred into a separate dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff, MWCO 3500 Da). The dialysis bags were immersed in 40.0 mL of external PBS release medium (pH 7.4) containing varying concentrations of H2O2 (0, 50, 200 μM) to simulate different levels of ROS. The release experiments were conducted in a thermostated orbital shaker incubator maintained at 37 °C with constant shaking. At predetermined time intervals (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48 h), 1.0 mL aliquots of the external release medium were withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh, pre-warmed (37 °C) PBS release medium containing the corresponding H2O2 concentration to maintain sink conditions. The concentration of apabetalone in the withdrawn samples was quantified by HPLC using the method described in Section 2.4. The cumulative percentage of apabetalone released was calculated and plotted versus time.

Cell viability

The murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 and murine fibroblast cell line L929 were cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for all the subsequent in vitro experiments.

RAW264.7 or L929 cells was seeded at a density of 5× 103 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The medium was removed and the cells were washed twice with PBS before incubating with different concentrations of blank CS/TK micelles for 24 h. Then, 20 μL of CCK-8 solution was added. After 2 h incubation, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated (Liang et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2020). In addition, the cell morphology was captured.

In vitro affinity to RAW264.7 cells

The cellular affinity and internalization efficiency of FITC-conjugated CS/TK/Apa micelles (CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles) were evaluated in RAW264.7 cells. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight. To induce an M1-like inflammatory phenotype, one group was stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL) for 12 h, while an unstimulated control group was cultured in parallel (Wang et al., 2016). Both groups were then incubated with either CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles or CS + CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles for 2, or 6 h. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) to remove unbound micelles. For qualitative assessment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at room temperature, nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min, and internalization was visualized using fluorescence microscopy. For quantitative analysis, cells were seeded into 6-well plates, subjected to identical LPS stimulation and micelle incubation protocols, washed three times with PBS, detached using trypsin, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

CD44-mediated internalization

To investigate the role of CD44 in cellular uptake, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in glass-bottom confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells per dish and incubated overnight. Cells were then stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 12 h to induce an inflammatory phenotype. Following LPS treatment, cells were co-incubated with CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles for 6 h. After incubation, unbound micelles were removed by washing the cells three times with PBS. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. The PFA solution was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with PBS. Non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubating cells in blocking solution (10% FBS in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. The blocking solution was removed, and cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary anti-CD44 antibody. After three PBS washes, cells were incubated with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cells were subsequently washed three times with PBS. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature, followed by a final PBS wash. Samples were mounted using an appropriate antifade mounting medium and stored at 4 °C in the dark until imaging. Internalization of micelles and colocalization with CD44 receptors were visualized and analyzed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Huang et al., 2024).

Immunofluorescence

Following the completion of drug treatments, cells were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. After fixation, the PFA solution was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with PBS to remove residual fixative. Subsequently, cells were permeabilized by incubation with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three additional PBS washes. To minimize non-specific binding, cells were incubated in blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody (iNOS) specific to the target protein. Unbound primary antibody was removed by washing the cells three times with PBS. Cells were then incubated with an appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature protected from light. After three final PBS washes to remove unbound secondary antibody, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Following a brief PBS rinse, samples were mounted using an antifade mounting medium. Fluorescence signals corresponding to the target protein and nuclei were visualized, and protein expression was analyzed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Fisher and Peppas, 2008).

Western blot

Following collection and processing, cell samples were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Total cellular protein was extracted, and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the membrane in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBS for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibody (iNOS, Arg-1 or β-actin), diluted in blocking buffer. Following incubation, the membrane was washed three times for 10 min each with TBS to remove unbound antibody. The membrane was subsequently incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody, diluted in blocking buffer, for 1 h at room temperature. After further washing with TBS, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and imaged. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software to determine relative protein expression levels.

Flow cytometry cell surface staining

Cell surface marker analysis was performed according to standardized procedures. Harvested cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS via centrifugation and supernatant removal. Cells were then resuspended in Flow Cytometry Staining Buffer at a density of 1 × 106 cells per 100 μL aliquot. Primary antibodies were added directly to cell suspensions followed by incubation for 30 min at 4 °C under light-protected conditions. After primary staining, cells underwent one wash with PBS. For indirect detection, fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied to pelleted cells and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C in darkness, followed by an additional PBS wash. Finally, cells were resuspended in 200 μL fresh Flow Cytometry Staining Buffer and immediately subjected to flow cytometric analysis.

ELISA

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 12 h. After that, the cells were treated with CS/TK/Apa micelles for 24 h, Apa and CS/Apa micelles as control. The contents of the cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 were determined using an ELISA kit.

Establishment of RA mice

All animal procedures were performed following the ARRIVE guidelines and the standards set by the US National Institutes of Health for the ethical care and use of laboratory animals. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Huitong Test & Evaluation Technology Group Co., Ltd. (HT-2024-LWFB-0001). Male DBA/1 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Sipeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and randomly assigned to experimental groups. RA was induced via collagen-induced arthritis (CIA). Briefly, an emulsion was prepared on ice by homogenizing 2.0 mg/mL bovine type II collagen (CII) with an equal volume of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA, 2.0 mg/mL). A primary immunization was administered by intradermal injection of 100 μL emulsion (containing 100 μg CII) at the tail base. On day 28 post-immunization, a booster immunization was performed using an emulsion of CII emulsified with incomplete Freund's adjuvant (IFA) at equivalent concentrations (Zhai et al., 2018).

Biodistribution of micelles in RA mice

RA mice were used as the model animals to investigate the in vivo distribution of ICG-conjugated CS/TK/Apa micelles. CS(I)/TK/Apa micelles (1 mg/kg) were intravenously injected into the RA mice. At 6 h and 24 h after administration, the animals were euthanized, and the major organs were collected for ex vivo imaging (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina). Additionally, Nile Red (NR) was used as a model drug to prepare CS/TK/NR micelles, which were also labeled with ICG. RA mice were injected with CS(I)/TK/NR micelles (1 mg/kg) via the tail vein, and the fluorescence signals of ICG and NR at the ankle joint of the model animals were observed and photographed using an in vivo small animal fluorescence imaging system (PerkinElmer IVIS Spectrum).

Pharmacokinetics

Healthy mice were intravenously injected with CS/TK/Apa micelles (Apa, 10 mg/kg), free apabetalone as control. Blood samples were collected at 0.25, 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h post-injection. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to collect supernate for HPLC analysis.

Therapeutic efficacy in vivo

The RA mice were divided into four groups to receive either saline, free apabetalone (10 mg/kg), CS/Apa micelles (Apa, 10 mg/kg), or CS/TK/Apa micelles (Apa, 10 mg/kg). Concurrently, untreated healthy mice served as the control group. Starting on day 28 post-initial immunization, mice received intravenous injections of the assigned formulations on days 42, 44, 46, 48 after the booster immunization (Fig. 6A). Hind paw thickness and arthritis scores were recorded alternately. Arthritis scores for all four paws were assessed on a scale from 0 to 4, with the following criteria: 0 = no signs of erythema or swelling, 1 = erythema and mild swelling, 2 = erythema and mild swelling extending from the ankle to the tarsals, 3 = erythema and moderate swelling extending from the ankle to the metatarsal joints, 4 = erythema and severe swelling encompassing the ankle, foot, and digits or limb ankylosis.

Histological analysis

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and their ankle joints were fixed in 4% PFA solution for 24 h. After decalcification, the joints were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) (Feng et al., 2023). The sections were observed and photographed under an optical microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Immunofluorescence staining

Following deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, ankle joint sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS (1:100) or Arg-1 (1100). Subsequent incubation with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies was performed for 1 h. The sections were then counterstained with DAPI and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Hemolysis test

After collecting 1 mL of blood from healthy mice, it was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the red blood cell pellet was retained and washed three times with physiological saline. Then, a 2% (v/v) red blood cell suspension was prepared. Different concentrations of CS/TK/Apa micelles were added to the suspension, with physiological saline and 1% Triton X-100 serving as negative and positive controls, respectively. All samples were incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate to measure absorbance at 450 nm (Sadat et al., 2024). The hemolysis rate was calculated using the following formula:

Hemolysis rate=Asample–Anegative control/Apositive control−Anegative control×100%

Safety assessment

To assess the safety of CS/TK/Apa micelles, biochemical indicators were measured, including liver function (AST and ALT) and renal function (CREA and UA). Additionally, since rheumatoid arthritis is often accompanied by extra-articular organ damage, the impact of CS/TK/Apa micelles on major organs was further examined through histological analysis of the main organ tissues after treatment.

Statistical analysis

The data were represented by mean ± standard deviation, and the results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8. For analyzing differences between groups, t-tests or one-way ANOVA were employed. A P value of <0.05, <0.01, or < 0.001 indicated significant differences between the groups, with statistical significance.

Results

Characterization of CS-TK-PLGA

The structures of CS-TK-PLGA were verified through 1H NMR spectra (DMSO‑d6), along with a comparison to its precursor materials. As shown in Fig. 1B, the peak at 1.2 ppm was attributed to the methyl proton of formamide on CS (Fig. S2), while the chemical shift at 1.5 and 1.6 ppm belonged to the methyl group on TK and PLGA (Fig. S1 and S3), respectively. The four groups of peaks between 2.6 and 3.3 ppm represented the methylene proton on TK moiety. Moreover, the multiple peaks at around 5.2 and 4.9 ppm were assigned to methylene and methine group of PLGA, respectively. All these suggested the successful attachment of CS to TK-PLGA. In addition, the GPC results demonstrated that CS-TK-PLGA exhibited a higher molecular weight than CS as evidenced by its earlier elution time in GPC chromatography (Fig. 1C). Finally, according to the mass of CS (8000 Da, approximately 17 monomers), the grafting efficiency of TK-PLGA was determined to be 18.5%. The CMC of CS-TK-PLGA, as determined by pyrene solubilization, was found to be 53.8 μg/mL (Fig. 1D).

Characterization of CS/TK/Apa micelles

As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2A, the CS/TK/Apa micelles were successfully constructed via the self-assembly of the amphiphilic CS-TK-PLGA conjugate, which effectively encapsulates apabetalone within its hydrophobic core. To optimize the formulation, we evaluated the impact of varying apabetalone feeding ratios (0% to 15%) on the physicochemical properties of the assembled nanocarriers. DLS measurements indicated that incorporating apabetalone into the hydrophobic core led to a corresponding expansion of the hydrodynamic diameter (Fig. 2B). The particle size increased from 19.5 ± 1.0 nm for the empty vehicles to 34.3 ± 3.7 nm at a 15% drug feeding ratio, while maintaining a monodisperse size distribution with polydispersity index (PDI) values consistently below 0.16 (Fig. 2C). The micelle surface carried a net negative charge driven by the dense sulfate and carboxyl groups of the chondroitin sulfate corona. While the absolute zeta potential slightly decreased as the core expanded - shifting from −25.4 ± 1.4 mV (0%) to −21.4 ± 3.5 mV (15%) - it remained sufficient to provide robust electrostatic repulsion against spontaneous aggregation (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the drug loading capacity exhibited a progressive increase, reaching approximately 10.0 ± 1.1% at the highest feeding ratio (Fig. 2E). Prior to quantifying drug-loading parameters, HPLC-based stability testing was performed to verify that apabetalone remained intact under methanol-assisted sample-disruption conditions (Fig. S4). The retention time and peak area of apabetalone in methanol showed negligible variation over 24 h, without detectable degradant peaks, confirming no drug decomposition during sample pretreatment. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was further adopted to identify the physical state of apabetalone inside micelles (Fig. S5). Free apabetalone powder displayed multiple sharp characteristic crystalline diffraction peaks and blank CS-TK-PLGA micelles only exhibited broad amorphous halo signals. However, in drug-loaded CS/TK/Apa micelles, all sharp crystalline peaks originating from apabetalone disappeared, demonstrating that apabetalone was incorporated in an amorphous, molecularly-dispersed state within the polymer matrix. To balance the requirement for a high therapeutic payload with an acceptable encapsulation efficiency, the formulation prepared at the 15% feeding ratio was selected for all subsequent evaluations.

Morphological mapping via TEM visualized the optimized CS/TK/Apa micelles as distinct, well-dispersed spherical nanostructures (Fig. 2G). The mean diameter calculated from the electron micrographs was somewhat smaller than that determined by DLS, an expected discrepancy resulting from the dehydration and collapse of the hydrophilic polymer corona under vacuum during TEM imaging. Colloidal stability is an essential prerequisite for intravenous application. By tracking the size profiles over a 7-day period across different simulated physiological conditions, the micelles were shown to maintain a constant diameter around 30–35 nm in both deionized water and standard PBS (Fig. 2J). Upon incubation in PBS supplemented with 10% FBS, the nanocarriers remained stable for the initial three days; a subsequent gradual size increase was observed, likely reflecting mild protein corona accumulation upon prolonged serum exposure, which remains highly acceptable for their biological half-life.

The nanocarriers are specifically designed to unravel within the oxidative microenvironment common to active arthritic joints. We mapped the architectural integrity of the micelles under ROS-rich conditions. Initial DLS profiles of the clear micellar dispersion yielded a narrow peak, whereas a 24-h incubation with 200 μM H2O2 prompted macroscopic turbidity. This correlated with a drastic broadening of the size distribution curve and the emergence of massive loose aggregates (>1000 nm), confirming ROS-triggered carrier disassembly (Fig. 2H). Dialysis setups were subsequently used to record the in vitro drug release kinetics (Fig. 2I). In physiological PBS, apabetalone leakage was minimal and sustained, restricting premature off-target release to barely 13.5% after 48 h. The introduction of 50 μM and 200 μM H2O2 provoked an immediate phase of rapid drug discharge. Within the first 8 h, the 200 μM group liberated 68.8% of the loaded apabetalone. Such accelerated release kinetics confirm that the localized oxidative cleavage of the TK linkages efficiently dismantles the micellar core structure. For rheumatoid arthritis models, this highly specific triggering mechanism aims to guarantee that peak drug concentrations are exclusively localized within the hyper-oxidative inflamed synovium, safely minimizing systemic exposure.

These findings indicate that blank CS/TK micelles exhibit excellent biocompatibility and are non-toxic to RAW264.7 and L929 cells, even at higher concentrations. Then, the cellular uptake of the CS/TK/Apa micelles by RAW264.7 cells was investigated. As shown in Fig. 3C and S8, fluorescence intensity results revealed a significantly higher uptake of CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells (② LPS + CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles group) compared to unstimulated cells (① PBS + CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles group) at both 2 h (p < 0.01) and 6 h (p < 0.0001). This observation suggests an enhanced internalization of the micelles by macrophages under inflammatory conditions. Furthermore, to investigate the role of CS in micelle uptake, we included a group pre-incubated with free CS (③ LPS + CS + CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles group). Interestingly, this group exhibited significantly reduced fluorescence intensity at both time points, indicating that the presence of free CS competitively inhibits the uptake of CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles. Flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 3D) corroborated these findings, demonstrating consistent trends in micelle uptake across all experimental groups. Taken together, these results suggest that the CS/TK/Apa micelles exhibit an increased affinity for RAW264.7 cells in an inflammatory microenvironment, likely mediated by the LPS-induced M1-like polarization and subsequent upregulation of CS receptors such as CD44. The competitive inhibition of uptake by free CS further supports the involvement of CS-receptor interactions in the internalization process. This enhanced affinity and internalization efficiency underscore the potential of CS/TK/Apa micelles for targeted drug delivery to activated macrophages in inflammatory settings.

To further elucidate the role of CD44 in the cellular uptake mechanism, we performed a colocalization study of CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles and CD44 receptors using laser scanning confocal microscopy. Quantitative analysis of the confocal images revealed a Pearson's correlation coefficient of 0.77 between the fluorescence signals of the CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles and the CD44 receptors (Fig. 3E and S9). This high Pearson's correlation coefficient indicates a strong degree of colocalization between the micelles and CD44 receptors within the cells (Wu et al., 2006). This finding provides compelling evidence that CD44 receptors play a significant role in the internalization of CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles by LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages. The observed colocalization strongly suggests that the micelles are internalized via a CD44-mediated endocytosis pathway, further supporting the potential of these micelles for targeted drug delivery to cells expressing high levels of CD44 in inflammatory environments.

ROS-responsive release of CS/TK/Apa micelles in RAW264.7 cells

To evaluate the ROS-responsive drug release of CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles within LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, we employed fluorescence imaging to track the release of a model hydrophobic drug, NR. Nile Red is closely known as a solvatochromic dye that exhibits strong fluorescence in hydrophobic microenvironments while its fluorescence is heavily quenched in aqueous environments (Hu et al., 2015).

Following a 2-h co-incubation period, fluorescence imaging revealed a significantly higher intracellular accumulation of CS(F)/TK/NR micelles within LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells compared to PBS-stimulated control cells. This enhanced uptake in LPS-stimulated cells is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that inflammatory stimuli can promote nanocarrier internalization, potentially via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Critically, we observed distinctly intense and widely distributed NR fluorescence signals within LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells after 2 h. This confirms that elevated intracellular ROS levels triggered the degradation of the thioether linkage within the micellar structure, releasing NR, which then rapidly partitioned into the abundant lipophilic compartments of the cell (such as intracellular lipid membranes), emitting strong fluorescence. Conversely, minimal Nile Red fluorescence was detected in PBS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, reflecting both reduced cellular uptake and limited micelle degradation under non-inflammatory conditions (Fig. 3F).

Extending the co-incubation period to 6 h resulted in a further increase in the distribution of CS(F)/TK/NR micelles within LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. This increased accumulation likely reflects continued cellular uptake over time, as observed in other studies investigating nanoparticle internalization kinetics. Importantly, the release of NR was significantly enhanced in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells after 6 h (Fig. 3F), providing further evidence for the ROS-triggered release mechanism. This finding is consistent with the understanding that prolonged exposure to high ROS environments accelerates the degradation of ROS-sensitive nanocarriers and increases the rate of payload release. As before, no significant NR fluorescence was observed in CS(F)/TK/NR micelle-treated PBS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, confirming the stability of the micelle structure and the absence of significant drug release in the absence of ROS. These results demonstrate the effective drug release from CS(F)/TK/Apa micelles in RAW264.7 cells.

Additionally, NR was used as a model drug to prepare CS(I)/TK/NR micelles. RA mice were injected with CS(I)/TK/NR or CS(ICG)/NR micelles via the tail vein, and the fluorescence signals of ICG and NR at the ankle joint of the model animals were observed and photographed using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system. NR is commonly used as a model drug in studies of stimuli-responsive nanomedicines because it remains non-fluorescent in aqueous environments but becomes highly fluorescent in nonpolar or lipid-rich environments such as the intracellular environment. By embedding NR within a nanocarrier, the release of the encapsulated “drug” can be indirectly monitored through fluorescence. Upon the breakdown or responsiveness of the nanocarrier to specific stimuli (e.g., pH changes, redox environments, or enzymatic activity inside cells), NR is released into the intracellular environment, where its fluorescence is triggered (Hu et al., 2015). This change in fluorescence serves as a proxy for the release and dispersion of the actual therapeutic compound. As shown in Fig. 5C, the sites of arthritis in RA model mice treated with CS(I)/TK/NR micelles or CS(ICG)/NR micelles exhibited strong fluorescence signals, as reflected by the obvious green fluorescence of ICG. However, the stronger NR fluorescence signals in the arthritis sites were observed in RA model mice treated with CS(I)/TK/NR micelles compared to those treated with CS(ICG)/NR micelles. The experimental results demonstrated that the micelles could effectively accumulate at sites of arthritis and degrade within the inflammatory regions. Therefore, they could be considered intelligent nanocarriers for the treatment of RA.

The pharmacokinetic profile of Apabetalone was significantly enhanced when encapsulated within CS/TK/Apa micelles compared to the free drug. As shown in Fig. 5D and E, the CS/TK/Apa micelles resulted in a substantially increased systemic exposure, with the AUC0-t value approximately 2.2-fold greater than that of free Apabetalone (8835.4 ± 1798.5 μg/L·h vs. 4060.2 ± 956.7 μg/L·h, *P < 0.05). Although the Cmax values were comparable between the two formulations (1061.4 ± 84.2 μg/L for micelles vs. 1096 ± 132.9 μg/L for free drug), the micellar system demonstrated markedly prolonged circulation, as evidenced by a significantly extended elimination half-life (T1/2). Notably, Apabetalone was detectable in plasma up to 24 h following administration of CS/TK/Apa micelles, whereas it fell below quantifiable levels by 12 h in the free drug group. These results clearly indicate that the CS/TK/Apa micelles effectively prolong the circulation time and improve the pharmacokinetic properties of Apabetalone.

In vivo therapeutic effect of CS/TK/Apa micelles

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CS/TK/Apa micelles in treating RA, mice were divided into six groups: (1) Sham, (2) RA, (3) RA + Blank CS/TK micelles, (4) RA + Free Apa, (5) RA + CS/Apa micelles, and (6) RA + CS/TK/Apa micelles. The therapeutic effects were assessed by measuring paw thickness, joint scores, histopathological changes in ankle joints, and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining was performed to investigate the polarization of synovial macrophages within the arthritic joints.

Paw thickness, a key indicator of RA-related inflammation, was significantly reduced in the CS/TK/Apa micelle treatment group (Group 6) compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 6B). Specifically, the reduction in paw thickness in Group 6 was approximately 1.15-fold greater than that observed in the Blank CS/TK micelle group (Group 3) and 1.11-fold greater than that observed in the free Apa group (Group 4). In contrast, the RA-induced group (Group 2) and the group treated with Blank CS/TK micelles (Group 3) exhibited comparable paw thicknesses (3.73 ± 0.07 mm and 3.71 ± 0.07 mm, respectively), with no significant difference observed between these groups (p > 0.05). This indicates that the Blank CS/TK micelles alone do not possess any therapeutic effect in mitigating RA-induced paw swelling. Histopathological analysis of ankle joints via H&E staining further supported the therapeutic efficacy of CS/TK/Apa micelles (Fig. 6D). Control animals treated with PBS exhibited severe pathological changes, including pronounced cartilage and bone erosion, substantial infiltration of inflammatory cells into the joint space, and extensive pannus formation, obscuring the integrity of the articular cavity. Similarly, treatment with free Apabetalone resulted in severe destruction and extensive loss of cartilage and bone, indicating limited therapeutic benefit under these conditions. In contrast, animals treated with CS/TK/Apa micelles demonstrated a marked improvement in joint pathology. H&E staining revealed near-normal cartilage surfaces with a clearly defined interface, minimal cellular infiltrates, and a significant reduction in pannus formation. The structural integrity of the articular cavity was largely preserved. These findings strongly suggest that CS/TK/Apa micelles effectively mitigate RA-induced joint damage, providing superior protection against cartilage and bone erosion.

To assess the anti-inflammatory efficacy of the treatments, we measured pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in the ankle joints. Saline-treated animals exhibited elevated levels of TNF-α (Fig. 6E), IL-6 (Fig. 6F), and IL-1β (Fig. 6G), indicating persistent inflammation. While treatment with free Apabetalone and CS/Apa micelles resulted in a significant reduction in these cytokines (p < 0.05), the most pronounced anti-inflammatory effect was observed with CS/TK/Apa micelles. This formulation nearly normalized cytokine levels, reducing TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 to levels comparable to those in healthy controls. To further investigate the mechanism of action of CS/TK/Apa micelles, we performed immunofluorescence staining to assess the polarization of synovial macrophages within the arthritic joints. Compared to healthy control mice, inflamed joints exhibited a significant increase in the expression of iNOS, a specific marker for M1-polarized macrophages, indicating a pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype. Notably, treatment with CS/TK/Apa micelles resulted in a marked reduction in iNOS expression and a concomitant increase in the expression of Arg-1, a marker of M2-polarized macrophages. This shift in macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory (M1) to an anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype suggests that CS/TK/Apa micelles exert their therapeutic effect, at least in part, by modulating the immune response within the joint microenvironment.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully designed and characterized a novel nanoplatform, CS/TK/Apa micelles, which integrate targeted drug delivery and ROS-responsive release mechanisms for the treatment of RA (Fig. 8). The primary findings indicate that these micelles possess a CMC of 53.8 μg/mL and a negative zeta potential of −21.4 ± 3.5 mV, ensuring stability and biocompatibility. The micelles exhibited a narrow size distribution and demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, with no significant cytotoxicity to RAW264.7 and L929 cells even at high concentrations. Importantly, the CS/TK/Apa micelles showed enhanced pharmacokinetic properties, with a 2.2-fold increase in systemic exposure of Apabetalone compared to the free drug. The dual functionality of targeting inflammation via CD44 receptors and releasing the drug in response to ROS was validated, confirming the potential of this nanoplatform for efficient RA therapy.Fig. 8Schematic illustration of CS/TK/Apa micelles for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis. CS/TK/Apa micelles release apabetalone in response to intracellular ROS, which leads to the re-polarization M1 macrophages to M2 phenotype in inflamed joints.Fig. 8

Schematic illustration of CS/TK/Apa micelles for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis. CS/TK/Apa micelles release apabetalone in response to intracellular ROS, which leads to the re-polarization M1 macrophages to M2 phenotype in inflamed joints.

Our results corroborate with existing literature that highlights the efficacy of nanocarriers in enhancing drug delivery specificity and pharmacokinetics (Guo et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). Comparable studies have demonstrated the benefits of using nanocarriers for targeted therapy in inflammatory diseases (Li et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2025), but our study uniquely combines CD44 receptor targeting with ROS-responsive drug release. This dual mechanism ensures that drug release is precisely controlled and localized to inflamed tissues, addressing a significant challenge in RA treatment. The enhanced pharmacokinetic profile and targeted release observed in our study align with findings from other research that emphasizes the importance of controlled drug release systems (Baker and Fruk, 2025; Gomerdinger et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2025). However, our results indicate superior targeting and release efficiency, which could be attributed to the specific design of the CS/TK/Apa micelles.

The development of CS/TK/Apa micelles represents a significant advancement in the field of targeted drug delivery for inflammatory diseases. This study introduces a promising approach that combines inflammation-targeting with ROS-responsive release, offering a dual mechanism to enhance therapeutic efficacy and minimize systemic side effects. The findings contribute to the growing body of knowledge on nanocarriers and their potential to revolutionize RA therapy. By improving the pharmacokinetics and ensuring targeted drug delivery, our nanoplatform addresses critical gaps in current RA treatments, potentially leading to better management of inflammation and associated symptoms. Additionally, the successful encapsulation and controlled release of Apabetalone highlights the versatility and potential of the CS/TK/Apa micelles for broader therapeutic uses.

While our study demonstrates the CD44-targeted and ROS-responsive nature of these micelles, it is important to acknowledge the complexity of the RA microenvironment. The arthritic synovium contains substantial extracellular ROS, raising the possibility of premature TK linker cleavage before cellular internalization. However, we anticipate that intracellular drug delivery remains dominant due to a significant ROS concentration gradient—specifically, the intense ‘oxidative burst’ within the endolysosomes of activated macrophages far exceeds extracellular ROS levels. Furthermore, the rapid kinetics of CD44-mediated endocytosis likely outpace extracellular degradation. Even if partial extracellular drug release occurs, the locally confined drug within the joint cavity may still exert anti-inflammatory effects on surrounding synoviocytes. Future studies utilizing advanced real-time intra-articular tracking techniques are warranted to further delineate the precise ratio of intracellular versus extracellular drug release in vivo.

Despite the promising results and the targeted release mechanisms discussed above, this study has several other limitations. Firstly, the in vivo studies were conducted on a limited number of animal models, which may not fully represent the complexity of human RA. Moreover, to maintain in vivo PK/PD consistency, the pharmacokinetic evaluation was strictly conducted in the DBA/1 J mouse model. Its minimal total blood volume (∼1.5 mL) restricted high-frequency early blood sampling due to ethical concerns (the 3Rs principles) and the risk of severe hypovolemia-induced PK distortion. Consequently, our relatively sparse early sampling limited the high-resolution depiction of the initial distribution phase, as the schedule was primarily optimized to capture systemic exposure (AUC) and terminal elimination (T1/2). Additionally, the long-term stability and potential immunogenicity of the micelles were not extensively evaluated, which are critical factors for clinical translation. Another limitation is the relatively narrow scope of the inflammatory markers assessed, which may not capture the full spectrum of inflammatory responses. Lastly, the study did not explore the potential off-target effects of the micelles, which could impact the overall safety profile of the formulation. Future research should focus on addressing the limitations highlighted above. Expanding the in vivo studies to include a broader range of animal models and eventually clinical trials will be essential to validate the efficacy and safety of CS/TK/Apa micelles in humans. Long-term stability studies and comprehensive immunogenicity assessments are also necessary to ensure the clinical viability of the micelles. Additionally, investigating the interaction of the micelles with other inflammatory markers and exploring their potential in other inflammatory diseases could further enhance their therapeutic application. Finally, optimizing the formulation to minimize any potential off-target effects will be crucial for advancing this promising nanoplatform towards clinical use.

Conclusions

The CS/TK/Apa micelles developed in this study demonstrate significant potential as a targeted drug delivery system for rheumatoid arthritis therapy. The dual functionality of CD44 targeting and ROS-responsive release enhances drug bioavailability and ensures targeted delivery to inflamed tissues, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes. The micelles exhibited excellent biocompatibility, stability, and a favorable pharmacokinetic profile, making them a promising candidate for clinical application in inflammatory diseases. Future studies should focus on further validating their efficacy and safety in diverse preclinical models and clinical trials.
