Doubly Robust Estimation of Causal Excursion Effects in Micro-Randomized Trials with Missing Longitudinal Outcomes
Jiaxin Yu

Abstract
Micro-randomized trials (MRTs) are increasingly utilized for optimizing mobile health interventions, with the causal excursion effect (CEE) as a central quantity for evaluating interventions under policies that deviate from the experimental policy. However, MRT often contains missing data due to reasons such as missed self-reports or participants not wearing sensors, which can bias CEE estimation. In this paper, we propose a two-stage, doubly robust estimator for CEE in MRTs when longitudinal outcomes are missing at random, accommodating continuous, binary, and count outcomes. Our two-stage approach allows for both parametric and nonparametric modeling options for two nuisance parameters: the missingness model and the outcome regression. We demonstrate that our estimator is doubly robust, achieving consistency and asymptotic normality if either the missingness or the outcome regression model is correctly specified. Simulation studies further validate the estimator’s desirable finite-sample performance. We apply the method to HeartSteps, an MRT for developing mobile health interventions that promote physical activity.



Keywords:
Causal excursion effect; Double robustness; Micro-randomized trial; Missing at random.

1
Introduction

Mobile health (mHealth) interventions, such as push notifications delivered through smartphones and wearables, are being developed in various domains. Micro-randomized trial (MRT) is an experimental design for developing and optimizing these interventions
(
Klasnja et al.,, 2015
;
Dempsey et al.,, 2015
)
. In an MRT, each individual is repeatedly randomized among treatment options hundreds or thousands of times, resulting in longitudinal data with time-varying treatments. A key quantity of interest in MRT analysis is the causal excursion effect (CEE). The CEE measures whether an intervention is effective and how its effect changes over time or interacts with the individual’s context, which are crucial for optimizing interventions. Semiparametric methods have been proposed for estimating the CEE when the longitudinal outcomes are always observed
(
Boruvka et al.,, 2018
;
Qian et al.,, 2021
;
Cheng et al.,, 2023
)
.

Missing data remains a critical and nearly universal challenge in MRTs, with few principled solutions currently available. Common causes of missingness include missed self-reports and participants not wearing sensors
(
Seewald et al.,, 2019
)
. Current practices for handling missing data in MRTs, such as imputation with zero values
(
Klasnja et al.,, 2019
)
or modal imputation
(
Bell et al.,, 2023
)
, are not principled and can lead to biased estimates.
Shi and Dempsey, (2023)
proposed an inverse probability weighted estimator, but their approach requires correctly specified missingness model and may suffer from large variance
(
Lee et al.,, 2011
)
.

We propose an estimator for the CEE in MRTs where longitudinal outcomes are missing at random. We focus on CEE defined with identify or log link, which covers common longitudinal outcome types including continuous, binary, and count. Our estimator is doubly robust in the sense that it is consistent and asymptotically normal if one of the two nuisance models, a propensity score model for the missingness and an outcome regression model, is correctly specified. Furthermore, the proposed estimator allows the nuisance models to be fitted by data-adaptive or machine learning methods which typically converge at a slower-than-parametric rate. We established the asymptotic properties and validated them via simulations. The method is applied to the HeartSteps MRT
(
Klasnja et al.,, 2015
)
, which aims to promote physical activity of participants.

Being the first doubly robust estimator for CEE with missing outcomes, our method builds upon the extensive literature on semiparametric and doubly robust estimation with missing data
(
Robins et al.,, 1994
;
Scharfstein et al.,, 1999
;
Bang and Robins,, 2005
;
Tsiatis,, 2006
;
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt,, 2015
)
. We also incorporate recent advancements in data-adaptive methods for causal inference
(
Chernozhukov et al.,, 2018
;
Kennedy,, 2022
)
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we define notations and CEE. In Section
3
, we first review methods for estimating CEE in the absence of missing data, and then present the proposed method for settings with missing outcomes. In Section
4
, we establish the asymptotic properties and double robustness of the proposed estimator. Simulation studies are presented in Section
5
. The method is illustrated using the HeartSteps study in Section
6
. We conclude with a discussion in Section
7
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

Consider an MRT that includes observations from
n
individuals, each enrolled for
T
decision points at which treatments are randomized. Variables without subscript
i
represent observations from a generic individual. Let
X_{t}
be the observation from the individual between decision points
t-1
and
t
. Let
A_{t}
represent the treatment assignment at decision point
t
, with 1 indicating treatment and 0 indicating no treatment. Let
I_{t}\in X_{t}
denote the availability indicator, where
A_{t}=0
deterministically if
I_{t}=0
(which can occur, for instance, if the individual is driving for safety reasons). Note that a decision point being unavailable is distinct from variables being missing. The overbar is used to denote a sequence of variables up to a decision point; for example,
\bar{A}_{t}=(A_{1},...,A_{t})
. Information accrued up to time
t
is represented by
H_{t}=(X_{1},A_{1},X_{2},A_{2},...,X_{t-1},A_{t-1},X_{t})=(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{A}_{t-1})
. The randomization probability for
A_{t}
can depend on
H_{t}
and is denoted by
p_{t}(H_{t}):=\text{Pr}(A_{t}=1|H_{t})
, and we sometimes omit
H_{t}
to simply write
p_{t}
.

The proximal outcome following the treatment assignment at time
t
, denoted by
Y_{t,\Delta}
, is a known function of the individual’s data in a subsequent time window of length
\Delta
, where
\Delta\geq 1
is a fixed positive integer; that is,
Y_{t,\Delta}=y(X_{t+1},A_{t+1},...,X_{t+\Delta-1},A_{t+\Delta-1},X_{t+\Delta})
for some known function
y(\cdot)
. A researcher would set
\Delta=1
if they are interested in how the treatment impacts the most immediate outcome, which is the most common practice
(
Klasnja et al.,, 2019
;
Bell et al.,, 2023
)
. A
\Delta
greater than 1 allows one to assess delayed effects. We define these effects rigorously in Section
2.2
.

We consider the setting where
X_{t}
and
A_{t}
are always observed and
Y_{t,\Delta}
can be missing. Let
R_{t,\Delta}
denote whether
Y_{t,\Delta}
is observed (in which case
R_{t,\Delta}=1
) or missing (
R_{t,\Delta}=0
). We assume that the data from different individuals are independent and identically distributed samples from an unknown distribution. Throughout, we represent random variables or vectors with uppercase letters, and their realized values with lowercase letters.

We use
\mathbb{E}
to denote expectation and
\mathbb{P}_{n}
to denote the empirical average over all individuals. For a positive integer
n
, We use
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,2,...,n\}
. We use superscript
⋆
to denote quantities corresponding to the true data generating distribution. We use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the
L_{2}
norm, i.e.
\|f(O)\|=\{\int f(o)^{2}dP(o)\}^{1/2}
for any function
f
of the observed data
O
. For a vector
\alpha
and a vector-valued function
f(\alpha)
,
\partial_{\alpha}f(\alpha):=\partial f(\alpha)/\partial\alpha^{T}
denotes the matrix where the
(i,j)
-th entry is the partial derivative of the
i
-th entry of
f
with respect to the
j
-th entry of
\alpha
.

2.2
Potential Outcomes and Causal Excursion Effect

To define causal effects, we use the potential outcomes framework
(
Rubin,, 1974
;
Robins,, 1986
)
. For an individual, let
X_{t}(\bar{a}_{t-1})
be the observation that would have been observed at decision point
t
if the individual had been assigned a treatment sequence of
\bar{a}_{t-1}
. The potential outcome of
H_{t}
under
\bar{a}_{t-1}
is
H_{t}(\bar{a}_{t-1})=\{X_{1},A_{1},X_{2}(a_{1}),A_{2}(a_{1}),...,A_{t}(\bar{a}_{t-1}),X_{t+1}(\bar{a}_{t})\}
. The potential outcome for the proximal outcome is
Y_{t,\Delta}(\bar{a}_{t+\Delta-1})
.

A causal excursion effect (CEE) is a contrast between the potential outcomes under two “excursions”, i.e., treatment regimes that deviate from the treatment regime in the MRT
(
Boruvka et al.,, 2018
;
Qian et al.,, 2021
)
. Here treatment regimes refer to how the treatment is sequentially assigned at each decision point depending on the history information
(
Murphy,, 2003
)
. Formally, let
{\boldsymbol{p}}
denote the treatment regime in the MRT where for
t\in[T]
the randomization probability of
A_{t}
is
p_{t}(H_{t})
, and let
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
denote some other regime where for
t\in[T]
the randomization probability of
A_{t}
is
\pi_{t}(H_{t})
. We require
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
to be compatible with the availability, i.e.,
\pi_{t}(H_{t})=0
if
I_{t}=0
. Let
S_{t}\subset H_{t}
denote the effect modifiers of interest, chosen by the researcher depending on the scientific question. Given a link function
g(\cdot)
that is either the identity or log function, the CEE of
A_{t}
on
Y_{t,\Delta}
moderated by
S_{t}
is

\displaystyle\text{CEE}_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{\pi}};\Delta}(t;s)

\displaystyle=g[\mathbb{E}_{\bar{A}_{t-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{p}},\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\{Y_{t,\Delta}(\bar{A}_{t-1},1,\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1})\mid S_{t}(\bar{A}_{t-1})=s,I_{t}(\bar{A}_{t-1})=1\}]

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -g[\mathbb{E}_{\bar{A}_{t-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{p}},\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\{Y_{t,\Delta}(\bar{A}_{t-1},0,\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}\mid S_{t}(\bar{A}_{t-1})=s,I_{t}(\bar{A}_{t-1})=1\}].

(2.1)

The right hand side of (
2.1
) is the contrast in potential outcomes under the two excursions:
(\bar{A}_{t-1},1,\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1})
and
(\bar{A}_{t-1},0,\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1})
, where
\bar{A}_{t-1}
(the treatments prior to
A_{t}
) follow the MRT regime
{\boldsymbol{p}}
, and
\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}=(A_{t+1},...,A_{t+\Delta-1})
(the treatments after
A_{t}
but prior to the measurement of
Y_{t,\Delta}
) follow the treatment regime
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. The choice of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
impacts the interpretation of the CEE; for example, setting
\pi_{t}=p_{t}
for all
t
yields the lagged effects considered in
Boruvka et al., (2018)
, and setting
\pi_{t}=0
for all
t
, which means that
\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}=0
, yields the effects considered in
Dempsey et al., (2020)
and
Qian et al., (2021)
. When
\Delta=1
, i.e., when the interest is in the treatment effect on the most immediate outcome
Y_{t,1}
,
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
becomes irrelevant in (
2.1
) and the CEE becomes a contrast between
Y_{t,1}(\bar{A}_{t-1},1)
and
Y_{t,1}(\bar{A}_{t-1},0)
. This is the most common scenario in applications
(
Klasnja et al.,, 2019
;
Qian et al.,, 2022
;
Bell et al.,, 2023
)
.

The CEE (
2.1
) is moderated by the effect modifiers of interest
S_{t}
. A common choice of
S_{t}
is the empty set, where the CEE is a fully marginal effect over all possible effect modifiers. Alternatively, one may include in
S_{t}
past treatments or past covariates/outcomes to assess interactions between treatments at different decision points or effect modification by past covariates/outcomes. The CEE is conditional on
I_{t}(\bar{A}_{t-1})=1
, the individual being available at the decision point. This is necessary for the CEE to be nonparametrically identified, and it also makes the CEE relevant in the mHealth context; after all, safe and ethical interventions should only be sent at times deemed appropriate
a priori
, i.e., when the individual is available, and thus the relevant causal effect should only concern such time points. Conditioning on availability is connected to the notion of feasible dynamic treatment regimes
(
Robins,, 2004
;
Wang et al.,, 2012
)
.

3
A Doubly Robust Estimator under Missing at Random

3.1
Estimator with Fully Observed Outcomes

We consider the estimation of an unknown parameter
\beta
whose true value
\beta^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfies

\displaystyle\text{CEE}_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{\pi}};\Delta}(t;s)=f_{t}(s)^{T}\beta^{\star}\text{ for }t\in[T],

(3.1)

where
f_{t}(\cdot)
is a pre-specified function. For example,
f_{t}(s)^{T}\beta
can include a linear model with effect modifiers
s
and basis functions of
t
. We make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(i)

(Stable unit treatment value assumption [SUTVA]).
The observed data is the same as the potential outcome under the observed treatment assignment, and one’s potential outcomes are not affected by others’ treatment assignments. Specifically,
R_{t,\Delta}=R_{t,\Delta}(\bar{A}_{t+\Delta-1}),Y_{t,\Delta}=Y_{t,\Delta}(\bar{A}_{t+\Delta-1})
, and
X_{t}=X_{t}(\bar{A}_{t-1})
for
t\in[T]
.

(ii)

(Positivity).
There exists
c>0
such that
c<\text{Pr}(A_{t}=1|H_{t},I_{t}=1)<1-c
almost surely for all
t\in[T]
.

(iii)

(Sequential ignorability).
The potential outcomes
\{X_{t+1}(\bar{a}_{t}),A_{t+1}(\bar{a}_{t}),\ldots,X_{T+1}(\bar{a}_{T})\}
are independent of
A_{t}
conditional on
H_{t}
for
t\in[T]
.

In a micro-randomized trial, because the treatment is sequentially randomized with known probabilities bounded away from 0 and 1, Assumptions
1
(ii) and (iii) are satisfied by trial design. Note that Assumption
1
(iii) implies the sequential ignorability concerning
Y_{t,\Delta}
and
R_{t,\Delta}
because they are functions of
(X_{t+1},A_{t+1},...,X_{t+\Delta-1},A_{t+\Delta-1},X_{t+\Delta})
. Assumption
1
(i) may fail to hold if there is peer influence or social interaction between individuals
(
Hudgens and Halloran,, 2008
;
Shi et al.,, 2023
)
. To maintain the focus of this paper, we do not consider such settings here. In the Section A of Supplementary Material, we show that under Assumption
1
, the CEE (
2.1
) is identified as

\displaystyle\text{CEE}_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{\pi}};\Delta}(t;s)

\displaystyle=g[\mathbb{E}_{\bar{A}_{t-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{p}}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{p}}}(W_{t,\Delta}Y_{t,\Delta}\mid A_{t}=1,H_{t})\mid S_{t},I_{t}=1\}]

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -g[\mathbb{E}_{\bar{A}_{t-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{p}}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}\sim{\boldsymbol{p}}}(W_{t,\Delta}Y_{t,\Delta}\mid A_{t}=0,H_{t})\mid S_{t},I_{t}=1\}],

(3.2)

where
W_{t,\Delta}:=\prod_{u=t+1}^{t+\Delta-1}\big\{\frac{\pi_{u}(H_{u})}{p_{u}(H_{u})}\big\}^{A_{u}}\big\{\frac{1-\pi_{u}(H_{u})}{1-p_{u}(H_{u})}\big\}^{1-A_{u}}
can be interpreted as a change of probability from
p_{u}
to
\pi_{u}
for future assignment
\bar{A}_{t+1:t+\Delta-1}
, and we set
W_{t,\Delta}:=1
if
\Delta=1
.

We describe an estimating function for
\beta^{\star}
in (
3.1
) that is unbiased under Assumption
1
when the outcome is always observed. For each
t\in[T]
, let
\mu_{t}(h,a)
be a nuisance function with truth
\mu_{t}^{\star}(h,a):=\mathbb{E}(Y_{t,\Delta}\mid H_{t}=h,A_{t}=a)
. We define the stabilized inverse probability weight
W_{t}:=\big\{\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(S_{t})}{p_{t}(H_{t})}\big\}^{A_{t}}\big\{\frac{1-\tilde{p}_{t}(S_{t})}{1-p_{t}(H_{t})}\big\}^{1-A_{t}}
for
t\in[T]
, where the numerator probability
\tilde{p}_{t}(S_{t})
is a nuisance function that takes value in
(0,1)
and can be estimated, for example, using a logistic regression of
A_{t}
on
S_{t}
. Note that
W_{t}
is distinct from
W_{t,\Delta}
. To describe the estimator with fully observed outcomes, we use shorthand notation
\mu_{at}:=\mu_{t}(H_{t},a)
for
a\in\{0,1\}
,
p_{t}:=p_{t}(H_{t})
, and
\tilde{p}_{t}:=\tilde{p}_{t}(S_{t})
for
t\in[T]
. Let
U_{t}(\beta,\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t}):=I_{t}W_{t}W_{t,\Delta}\epsilon_{t}(A_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t})f_{t}(S_{t})
, where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}:=\begin{cases}Y_{t,\Delta}-(A_{t}+p_{t}-1)f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta-(1-p_{t})\mu_{1t}-p_{t}\mu_{0t}&\text{ if $g$ is identity},\\
e^{-A_{t}f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta}Y_{t,\Delta}-(1-p_{t})e^{-f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta}\mu_{1t}-p_{t}\mu_{0t}&\text{ if $g$ is log}.\end{cases}

A two-stage estimator for
\beta
can be constructed by first obtaining nuisance function estimators
\hat{\mu}_{t}
and
\hat{\tilde{p}}_{t}
, and then solving for
\hat{\beta}
from the estimating equation
\mathbb{P}_{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}U_{t}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\tilde{p}}_{t})=0
. When
g
is identity,
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}={\boldsymbol{p}}
, and
\epsilon_{t}
is replaced by
Y_{t,\Delta}-(A_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t})f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta-\eta_{t}(H_{t})^{T}\alpha
for a pre-specified function
\eta_{t}(H_{t})
(often called control variables),
\hat{\beta}
corresponds to a two-stage version of the weighted and centered least squares method in
Boruvka et al., (2018)
. When
g
is log,
\pi_{t}=0
for all
t
, and
\epsilon_{t}
is replaced by
e^{-A_{t}f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta}Y_{t,\Delta}-e^{\eta_{t}(H_{t})^{T}\alpha}
for pre-specified control variables
\eta_{t}(H_{t})
,
\hat{\beta}
correspond to a two-stage version of the estimator for marginal excursion effect in
Qian et al., (2021)
.
Cheng et al., (2023)
showed that the two-stage estimator
\hat{\beta}
for fully observed outcomes is consistent and asymptotically normal for arbitrary choice of
\hat{\mu}_{t}
and
\hat{\tilde{p}}_{t}
when
\Delta=1
, and the particular form of
\epsilon_{t}
was chosen to improve the efficiency of
\hat{\beta}
over
Boruvka et al., (2018)
and
Qian et al., (2021)
.

3.2
Proposed Estimator For Outcomes Missing At Random

We make additional assumptions when the outcome can be missing.

Assumption 2

(i)

(Positivity for
R_{t,\Delta}
).
There exists
c>0
such that
\text{Pr}(R_{t,\Delta}=1|H_{t},A_{t})

>c
almost surely for all
t\in[T]
.

(ii)

(Missing at random given history information).

Y_{t,\Delta}\perp R_{t,\Delta}\mid H_{t},A_{t}
for all
t\in[T]
.

For each
t\in[T]
, let
e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
be a nuisance function with truth
e_{t}^{\star}(H_{t},A_{t}):=

\text{Pr}(R_{t,\Delta}

=1\mid H_{t},A_{t})
. We propose the estimating function
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{U}_{t}(\beta,e_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})
with

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{t}(\beta,e_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t}):=\frac{R_{t,\Delta}}{e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})}\ U_{t}(\beta,\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})-\frac{R_{t,\Delta}-e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})}{e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})}\ \mathbb{E}\{U_{t}(\beta,\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})\mid H_{t},A_{t}\}.

(3.3)

The first term in (
3.3
) is
U_{t}
weighted by the inverse probability of
Y_{t,\Delta}
being observed. The second term in (
3.3
) is an augmentation term that yields the double robustness (Section
4
) based on the seminal work of
Robins et al., (1994)
.
Algebraic calculation yields

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{t}(\beta,e_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})=I_{t}W_{t}W_{t,\Delta}\Big[\frac{R_{t,\Delta}}{e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})}\{Y_{t,\Delta}-A_{t}\mu_{1t}-(1-A_{t})\mu_{0t}\}

\displaystyle+(A_{t}+p_{t}-1)\{\mu_{1t}-\mu_{0t}-f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta\}\Big](A_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t})f_{t}(S_{t})

(3.4)

if the link function
g
is identity, and

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{t}(\beta,e_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})=I_{t}W_{t}W_{t,\Delta}\Big[\frac{R_{t,\Delta}}{e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})}e^{-A_{t}f_{t}(S_{t})^{T}\beta}\{Y_{t,\Delta}-A_{t}\mu_{1t}-(1-A_{t})\mu_{0t}\}

\displaystyle+(A_{t}+p_{t}-1)\{e^{-f_{t}(S_{t})\beta}\mu_{1t}-\mu_{0t}\}\Big](A_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t})f_{t}(S_{t})

(3.5)

if the link function
g
is
\log
.

We propose a two-stage implementation for
\hat{\beta}
by first estimating the nuisance parameters in
\tilde{U}_{t}(\beta,e_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})
and then solve an estimating equation for
\hat{\beta}
, detailed in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Causal excursion effect estimator
\hat{\beta}
for outcomes missing at random

Given the causal excursion effect model (
3.1
):

Stage 1:
Fit
\text{Pr}(R_{t,\Delta}=1\mid H_{t},A_{t})
,
\mathbb{E}(Y_{t,\Delta}\mid H_{t},A_{t},I_{t}=1)
, and
\text{Pr}(A_{t}=1\mid S_{t},I_{t}=1)
for
t\in[T]
. Denote the fitted models by
\hat{e}_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
,
\hat{\mu}_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
, and
\hat{\tilde{p}}_{t}(S_{t})
, respectively.

Stage 2:
Obtain
\hat{\beta}
by solving
\mathbb{P}_{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{U}_{t}(\beta,\hat{e}_{t},\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\tilde{p}}_{t})=0
with
\tilde{U}_{t}
defined in (
3.4
) if
g
is identity and (
3.5
) if
g
is
\log
.

Remark 3.1

A wide variety of flexible methods can be used in Stage 1 of Algorithm
1
to fit the nuisance parameters, including parametric methods and nonparametric methods such as kernel regression, spline methods with complexity penalties, and ensemble methods
(
Fan and Gijbels,, 1996
;
Ruppert et al.,, 2003
;
van der Laan et al.,, 2011
)
. Depending on the model fitting method in Stage 1, the required conditions for obtaining a consistent and asymptotically normal
\hat{\beta}
in Stage 2 are slightly different. We explain this in detail in Section
4
.

4
Asymptotic Theory

We make the following assumptions for establishing the asymptotic normality of the two-stage estimator in Algorithm
1
. Proof of the theorems is given in Section B of Supplementary Material. For notation simplicity, let
\delta:=(\delta_{1},...,\delta_{T})
with
\delta_{t}:=(e_{t},\mu_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})
denote the collection of all nuisance parameters, and let
\mathcal{T}
denote the space of
\delta
.

Assumption 3
(Convergence of nuisance parameter estimator)

\hat{\delta}
converges in
L_{2}
to some limit
\delta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
as
n\to\infty
.
In other words, for each
t\in[T]
, there exists
\mu^{\prime}_{t}(h_{t},a_{t})
such that

\|\hat{\mu}_{t}-\mu^{\prime}_{t}\|=o_{p}(1)
; there exists
e^{\prime}_{t}(h_{t},a_{t})

such that
\|\hat{e}_{t}-e^{\prime}_{t}\|=o_{p}(1)
; and there exists
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})

such that
\|\hat{\tilde{p}}_{t}-\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{t}\|=o_{p}(1)
.

Assumption 4
(Unique zero)

There exists unique
\beta\in\Theta
such that
\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{U}(\beta,\delta^{\prime})\}=0
.

Assumption 5
(Correctly specifying one nuisance parameter)

For each
t\in[T]
, suppose either
e^{\prime}_{t}=e_{t}^{\star}
or

\mu^{\prime}_{t}=\mu_{t}^{\star}
.

Assumption 6
(Regularity conditions)

(i)

Suppose the parameter space
\Theta
of
\beta
is compact.

(ii)

Suppose the support of
O
is bounded.

(iii)

Suppose
\mathbb{E}{\{\partial_{\beta}U(\beta^{\star},\delta^{\prime})\}}

is invertible.

(iv)

For each
t
, all possible
e_{t}(\cdot)
,

\mu_{t}(\cdot)
, and
\tilde{p}_{t}(\cdot)
consists of a Donsker class.

(v)

For each
t

The required condition

Application

We use data from HeartSteps I (hereafter HeartSteps; data available at
https://github.com/klasnja/HeartStepsV1/tree/main/data_files
), the first study in a series of HeartSteps MRTs aimed at designing mHealth interventions to promote physical activity among sedentary adults
(
Klasnja et al.,, 2019
)
. The study included 37 participants, each randomized up to five times per day over a 42-day period, totaling 210 decision points per participant. At each decision point, participants had a 60% chance of receiving a smartphone-delivered physical activity suggestion. A participant was considered unavailable at a decision point if any of the following conditions were met: they were driving, had manually disabled the intervention, were exercising within 90 seconds of the decision point, or lacked an active internet connection
(
Seewald et al.,, 2019
)
. Out of 7884 total decision points, 6331 (80.3%) were available. Participants selected their preferred randomization times before the start of the study, ensuring at least 90-minute intervals between notifications. Each participant wore a
wristband tracker which continuously recorded their step count. The outcome of interest is the total step count in a subsequent 30-minute window following each decision point, and 9.4% outcomes were missing.

To illustrate the method, we assessed the effect of activity suggestions on the 30-minute total step count. Specifically, we considered three estimands: (a) a fully marginal effect of the suggestion averaged over all decision points; (b) a moderated effect with the effect modifier being the decision point index; (c) a moderated effect with the effect modifier being whether the decision point is on a weekday.

The Stage 1 nuisance functions
e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
and
\mu_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
were fitted using either a generalized linear model or a generalized additive model with penalized splines. We used the following predictors to fit both
e_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
and
\mu_{t}(H_{t},A_{t})
, pooling across
t\in[T]
: the decision point index
t
, lag-1 outcome (the log-transformed step count in the 30-minute window preceding decision point
t
), location (an indicator for being at home or work), the weekday indicator, recent activity level (an indicator of whether the individual took fewer than 56 steps in the 30-minute window preceding decision point
t
), and interactions between treatment and each of these predictors. The numerator probability
\tilde{p}(S_{t})
is set to 0.6, the true randomization probability. The Stage 2 model was specified depending on the estimand. Wald-type 95% confidence interval was obtained based on the asymptotic variance estimators in Theorems
1
and
2
.

We considered three comparator methods: weighted and centered least squares
(
Boruvka et al.,, 2018
, WCLS;)
applied to only complete cases, WCLS after imputing missing outcomes as zero, and WCLS after imputing missing outcomes using individual-specific mean. We used the
wcls

MRTAnalysis

(
Qian et al.,, 2023
)
. Recall that WCLS is appropriate for estimating the CEE had there been no missing outcomes.

The results were qualitatively similar across the five methods (two proposed and three comparators) despite quantitative differences. However, the theoretical advantages of our approach such as the double robustness and the weaker assumption on missing data than its comparators make it appropriate in more settings. In datasets with higher proportions of missing outcomes, our method is expected to offer greater improvements in precision and validity.

7
Discussion

We proposed a two-stage doubly robust estimator for the causal excursion effect in MRTs where the longitudinal outcomes are missing at random. In the first stage, the two nuisance parameters (missingness model and outcome regression) can be fitted either parametrically or nonparametrically. When fitted parametrically, only one of the two nuisance parameter models needs to be correctly specified. When fitted nonparametrically, methods with a slower convergence rate (e.g.,
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
) can be accommodated, which enables a wider range of nonparametric and machine learning nuisance estimators. We established the asymptotic theory and verified the finite sample performance of the estimator through simulations.

One direction for future work is to extend the current methodology to adopt ideas from recent advances in doubly robust estimation, which may further improve the estimator’s performance when both nuisance models are misspecified or when the nuisance parameter estimator converges at a very slow rate. The higher-order influence function idea
(
Díaz et al.,, 2016
)
and restricted empirical maximum likelihood estimation
(
Tan,, 2010
)
are promising future directions. Another interesting extension is to incorporate enhanced propensity score models
(
Cao et al.,, 2009
)
, which balances weights for each individual by adding a scalar parameter in the logistic regression. Lastly, one may incorporate the bias-reduced doubly robust estimation
(
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt,, 2015
)
to reduce the estimator’s sensitivity to model misspecification when both nuisance models are only mildly misspecified.

Another direction for future work is developing methods to handle missing data without relying on the assumption of missing at random (MAR). A key challenge with missing not at random (MNAR) data is the issue of identification—i.e., the parameter of interest may not be uniquely identified from the observed data
(
Yang et al.,, 2019
)
. Shadow variables can be a potential solution
(
Miao et al.,, 2024
)
.

A third direction is to allow for missing covariate or treatment data. This can occur in MRTs due to technical glitches such as app malfunctions or sensor failures.
