Implementing a multimodal antimicrobial stewardship programme in primary care in Kosova—a protocol for implementation and evaluation
Authors: Lul Raka, Shaip Krasniqi, Arianit Jakupi PharmD, Alba Ymerhalili, Nandini Sreenivasan, Ahmad Wesal Zaman, Robert Leo Skov, Gloria Cordoba, Ilir Hoxha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance is a growing global health crisis, with primary care settings being a major contributor due to inappropriate antibiotic prescribing. In Kosova, the overuse of antibiotics for upper respiratory tract infections remains a critical challenge, especially in primary care, driven by limited diagnostic tools, regulatory gaps, and prescribing behaviours. This paper presents the protocol for implementing and evaluating a multimodal antimicrobial stewardship programme in primary healthcare facilities to promote rational antibiotic use and reduce antimicrobial resistance.AimThe primary aim of the intervention is to improve the appropriate use of antibiotics for acute upper respiratory tract infections by implementing an antimicrobial stewardship programme in primary healthcare facilities, and use the knowledge from implementation to implement the programme on a larger scale in other primary care centres or other levels of care.MethodsA mixed-methods implementation research approach will be adopted to examine the impact of the antimicrobial stewardship programme in Kosova, incorporating quantitative and qualitative methods. The multimodal antimicrobial stewardship programme is complemented by research components designed to measure implementation processes and programme performance. These research components are designed in line with multiple conceptual frameworks, such as RE-AIM and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, as the theoretical basis. The study component focusing on the implementation of the antimicrobial stewardship programme in the pilot municipalities will examine barriers to and facilitators of change across all domains of potential influence, i.e., innovation/intervention, participants, inner setting, outer setting, and implementation process. The conjoint analysis examines a subgroup of participants, i.e., prescribers of antibiotics, and their preferences regarding antibiotic prescribing. The cost-effectiveness component contributes to the review of key outcomes of the intervention, i.e., antibiotic use and costs. In contrast, the cost of intervention analysis provides valuable information on inner and outer settings, i.e., the cost implications of implementation relevant to potential scale-up. The Chamber of Doctors of Kosova and its Institutional Review Board formally approved the project under Decision No. 122/24, dated 26.08.2024. All knowledge from the programme implementation will be disseminated through relevant channels and tools.DiscussionBy piloting an antimicrobial stewardship programme aligned with national and international guidelines, key national stakeholders aim to strengthen stewardship practices through training, diagnostics, and continuous quality improvement. This intervention addresses critical gaps between antimicrobial resistance policy commitments and practical implementation in Kosova’s primary care. The mixed-methods research design of study components, grounded in the relevant implementation science frameworks, will generate evidence on barriers, enablers, and economic impact, informing policy updates and scale-up strategies. While focused on Kosova, findings are expected to provide valuable lessons for other low- and middle-income countries facing similar antimicrobial resistance challenges.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant global health threat, leading to increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1, 2]. In 2021, 4.77 million deaths were associated with bacterial AMR worldwide, [3]. The global average loss of life expectancy due to AMR is projected to be 1.8 years globally by 2035 [4]. The drivers of AMR are multifactorial, including the misuse of antibiotics in humans and food-producing animals, limited awareness among the public and healthcare workers about AMR, weak regulatory enforcements, over-the-counter antibiotic sales, the absence of new antibiotics, the lack of evidence-based treatment guidelines, limited diagnostic capacities, and inadequate infection prevention and control (IPC) and sanitation standards [5].

The excessive use of antibiotics remains the primary contributor to the spread of AMR. Between 2000 and 2015, global antibiotic consumption increased by 65% [6, 7]. Countries like Kosova are not an exception to these trends [8, 9]. In 2011, antibiotic use in Kosova was 26.4 Defined Daily Doses (DDDs) per 1,000 inhabitants per day, decreasing to 20.1 DDDs before COVID-19, but the pandemic led to new wave of widespread misuse [10]. Since 2020, the total consumption of antibiotics has been rising again, reaching 27.9 DDDs in 2023, surpassing the levels recorded in 2011 [10]. Azithromycin use in primary care increased sevenfold, while imipenem usage in hospitals surged 12-fold, with evidence of prescribing in primary care [10]. Additionally, the use of "Access" group antibiotics dropped below 60% of the total consumption [10].

The Ministry of Health (MoH) enacted several policy measures to address the country's AMR challenge. The MoH has implemented two national action plans and is finalising the third in collaboration with the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, European Food Safety Authority, and the European Commission, following the holistic “One Health” approach. AMR, patient safety, and IPC have been integrated into the new Law on the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases [11]. Additionally, the Administrative Instruction on AMR, which outlines the roles and responsibilities of each sector, was recently approved by the MoH [12, 13]. AMR, IPC, and Patient Safety have been integrated into university medical curricula, while awareness efforts have included translating the Public Health England “E-bug” programme into Albanian [14]. The MoH incorporated the WHO’s AWaRe classification into the Essential Medicines List in 2019 [15], and in 2024, the MoH approved the official antibiotic use guideline in primary healthcare [16].

Though antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is a key priority in the National Action Plan for Prevention of Infectious Diseases, no programmes have yet implemented AMS at any level of care. In lengthy discussions with critical stakeholders such as the MoH, the National Institute of Public Health of Kosova (NIPHK), International Centre for Antimicrobial Resistance Solutions (ICARS) and representatives from different levels of care, it was decided that primary care and upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) could be a great starting point of action. Primary care is crucial in addressing AMR, as it is the first point of contact for most patients seeking medical care [17]. Existing evidence highlights that establishing and implementing an AMS programme is a fundamental approach to address AMR in primary care [18–22]. In primary healthcare settings, these programmes include leadership and policy commitment, clinical guidelines, prescriber monitoring, healthcare provider education, public awareness initiatives, rapid diagnostic tools, antibiotic prescribing restrictions, community involvement, and digital monitoring systems to enhance antibiotic use and curb AMR [22–25].

The focus on URTIs in primary care is very relevant for Kosova as it faces substantial overuse and excessive prescribing of antibiotics across all age groups. Over-the-counter sale of antibiotics, aggressive pressure from the pharmaceutical industry, and lack of training and knowledge of physicians contribute to over-prescription and overuse, particularly of ceftriaxone. Kosova has had the highest total parenteral ceftriaxone consumption in Europe for years. A 2023 study conducted in six regional primary care centres found that ceftriaxone comprised 24% of all prescribed antibiotics, underscoring its widespread overuse [26]. Clinicians often prescribe antibiotics unnecessarily for viral infections or without proper diagnostic confirmation (e.g., streptococcal pharyngitis). Also, delayed prescribing, appropriate for acute otitis media and uncomplicated sinusitis, is underutilised. New national guidelines recommend rapid streptococcal tests (RSTs) when tonsillitis is suspected, but they are largely unavailable in primary care. A survey at Prishtina’s Main Family Medical Centre (MFMC) found that despite 80% of tonsillopharyngitis cases being viral, 79% of diagnosed patients were prescribed antibiotics [27]. In the communities served by primary care centres, there is insufficient awareness and misinformation about the risks and consequences of antibiotic misuse and AMR [26]. Over half of the respondents used antibiotics without a prescription or medical guidance [26]. The most common reasons were viral infections (44.4%), including the flu and common cold [26].

This paper presents the aims and methods of the implementation and intervention research programme “Addressing Antibiotic Overuse through Continuous Quality Improvement: Developing and Assessing Stewardship Interventions in Primary Care in Kosova.” The AMS programme addresses the need to improve antibiotic overuse and misuse in Kosova, starting with primary care treatment of URTIs.

Methods

AMS intervention description

Aim and summary of the AMS programme

The primary aim of the AMS programme is to improve the appropriate use of antibiotics for acute URTIs through the implementation of an AMS programme in primary healthcare facilities and use this knowledge to scale up the programme in other primary care centres and other levels of care. The AMS programme is structured into five interlinked Work Packages (WPs) that together form an integrated quality-improvement cycle for implementing, evaluating, and scaling antimicrobial stewardship in primary care (Fig. 1). WP1 serves as the preparatory phase, establishing the contextual foundation through a baseline situation analysis and stakeholder engagement. WP2 represents the core intervention component, operationalising the AMS model through education, audit-and-feedback, diagnostic support, and local facilitation. WP3 and WP4 comprise the evaluation components, focusing respectively on the assessment of contextual barriers and enablers and on economic and cost-effectiveness analyses to determine value and feasibility. Finally, WP5 functions as the dissemination and governance component, synthesising learning across the programme, engaging national stakeholders, and supporting policy integration and sustainability. Together, these work packages create a coherent framework that links implementation, evaluation, and learning in a continuous cycle of improvement.Fig. 1Logic of intervention of the AMS programme

Preparatory phase (WP1)

WP1 is a rapid situation analysis for implementation preparation. At the national level, contextual Policy, Economic, Social, Technology, Legal and Environmental (PESTLE) analysis will be utilised to assess the context of implementing AMS and identify barriers and opportunities at a policy-making level that could facilitate the programme implementation at primary health care. The interests of different stakeholders will also be mapped by organising working meetings during the co-development phase to inform the legislation for the AMS and the Technical Working Group in primary care. At the facility level, a SWOT analysis will be conducted based on the Quality, Resilience, and Organisational (QRO) toolkit to gain a contextual understanding of each implementation site.

Main intervention (WP2)

WP2 involves the rollout of the multimodal AMS programme at selected primary care clinics. The AMS programme has structural measures, capacity-building activities, patient information materials, the introduction of new tests, as well as performance tracking processes. The development and implementation of the AMS programme in health facilities will follow guidance from key international resources, including the WHO Practical Toolkit for AMS in low- and middle-income countries, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Outpatient AMS Toolkit, and additional frameworks from British Society for Antimicrobial Chemotherapy (BSAC), European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID), and European Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (ECDC) [23, 24, 28, 29].

Structural efforts include activities at the national level, such as establishing a national AMS technical group to develop the AMS programme and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for primary care. At the facility level, a multidisciplinary team will oversee the implementation of AMS at each MFMC. Each team is responsible for developing a tailored AMS package based on facility-specific analyses.

Capacity-building efforts include a comprehensive training curriculum to be developed and accredited by Kosova’s Medical Chamber. A two-day training will be held at each intervention facility, targeting prescribers, selected nurses, pharmacists, and AMS teams. Training topics will cover the fundamentals of AMS and URTIs, identification of URTIs symptoms, diagnostic practices, and patient communication strategies. Besides the initial training, the project will adhere to regular role-playing activities and peer meetings. This will help strengthen the concepts and address challenges in AMS.

Patient education efforts will support patients who do not receive antibiotics for their healthcare condition and healthcare workers with an antibiotic-free leaflet, adapted from the WHO leaflet and Treat Antibiotics Responsibly: Guidance, Education, Tools (TARGET) [30–32]. A pilot test will be conducted with a small group of healthcare workers and patients to refine the leaflet.

Strep tests will be introduced as part of the programme. The MoH will provide 15,000 tests, which will be distributed as needed. Healthcare professionals will be trained to use the RSTs based on Centre criteria, which will guide use of the strep tests effectively and interpret the results accurately. Patient safety will be monitored through phone calls to ensure patients' safety.

Finally, tracking and reporting the impact of the AMS programme will involve repeated Point Prevalence Surveys (PPS) [33] and clinical audits. The PPS will be conducted by an external team at the health facility and carried out three times: before the intervention, during the high-antibiotic-use season, and finally during the low-antibiotic-use season. PPS will support understanding the effectiveness of changes in overall prescribing practices. Clinical audits will provide detailed data on signs and symptoms linked to antibiotic use.

Evaluation/research components (WP3 and WP4)

WP3 has two study subcomponents. The first component investigates the contextual and behavioural determinants that influence the implementation of AMS in primary care, recognising that successful stewardship requires not only the intervention itself but also the alignment of system-, provider-, and patient-level conditions [34]. This study will examine how structural weaknesses in the health system, resource limitations, diagnostic uncertainty, and prescriber beliefs pose significant challenges to stewardship efforts [34]. The second component will utilise a conjoint analysis to determine the factors influencing antibiotic prescription among prescribers for one or more common URTIs. The study will consider clinical and non-clinical factors such as cough, fever, CRP levels, diagnostic tests, and antibiotic availability. Clinical scenarios generated by orthogonal design will be integrated into a questionnaire to collect data on prescribers and their decision-making regarding antibiotic prescription for URTIs. All prescribers in primary care sites will be invited to participate.

WP4 also has two study subcomponents focused on assessing the program's cost impact due to antibiotic usage in primary care and the cost of implementing the programme. The first study component employs a quasi-experimental design with an interrupted time series (ITS) to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of an AMS programme. The interrupted time-series approach will help us track changes in prescription patterns by collecting data at multiple time points before, during, and after implementation. The cost of the intervention component, on the other hand, will use a bottom-up costing approach to analyse the multifaceted components of AMS by categorising costs into three phases, i.e. design, initiation, and maintenance, as suggested by the conceptual framework proposed by Sohn (2020) [35].

Dissemination component (WP5)

WP5 focuses on the dissemination and sustainability of the intervention. Through WP5, the project aims to maximise reach by integrating a multidimensional approach, including workshops, creation of dissemination materials, information meetings, and both in-country and international results sharing. Informative sessions will be conducted within MFMCs and with national associations to ensure widespread understanding and support for the project. The dissemination part will produce various materials, including audit reports, research outputs from each research component, brochures, and guidelines, distributed to key stakeholders.

Intervention sites

Twelve MFMCs will be selected to participate in the project/intervention. Six centres will be enrolled at the beginning of the project, and the other six in the scale-up phase. Two regional MFMCs and four MFMCs from smaller municipalities will be enrolled in each phase. Sites selected from the beginning and the scale-up of intervention will also serve as intervention and control sites in some study designs, such as conjoint and cost-effectiveness analyses. The country is administratively divided into 38 municipalities and each has one MFMC, so AMS program will cover about a third of all country. Programme beneficiaries/participants

The AMS programme engages three main categories of participants and beneficiaries, i.e. policymakers and health administrators, healthcare professionals, and patients receiving care within primary healthcare facilities. Together, these three groups embody the AMS intervention's multilevel approach, linking policy, practice, and patient experience within a single stewardship framework. Policymakers and health administrators represent the governance level of the health system, including decision-makers from the MoH, municipal health directorates, and primary-care leadership structures. Healthcare professionals, comprising general practitioners, nurses, and pharmacists working within primary healthcare centres, are directly involved in prescribing, diagnostics, patient education, and the application of AMS tools and protocols. Patients and community members are the ultimate beneficiaries of the programme, receiving improved, evidence-based care for URTIs, more transparent communication on antibiotic use, and better protection against AMR.

Implementation and evaluation strategy and theoretical frameworks

Implementing the AMS programme in primary care is guided by a set of complementary conceptual frameworks drawn from quality improvement, health services research, and implementation evaluation. Together, these frameworks provide a structured approach for understanding how the intervention is delivered, how change occurs across system and provider levels, and how contextual factors shape its outcomes.

Quality improvement framework

The design and delivery of WP2, the core intervention component of the AMS programme, is grounded in the principles of Quality Improvement (QI) [36, 37]. QI provides a structured, iterative approach for enhancing clinical practice through continuous measurement, reflection, and local adaptation. The programme applies the Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycle as its central learning mechanism. AMS teams plan changes to prescribing and diagnostic practices, implement them on a small scale, review data from audits and PPSs, and refine actions based on feedback. This cyclical process promotes ownership among frontline professionals and allows rapid correction of ineffective or unsustainable practices. Complementary QI methods, such as audit and feedback, benchmarking, and local facilitation, further strengthen engagement, accountability, and data-driven decision-making.

Theory of change

The Theory of Change underpinning the AMS programme links its five work packages into a continuous improvement and learning cycle that drives behavioural, system, and policy-level change (Fig. 1). WP1 (Situation analysis) establishes the contextual foundation through PESTLE and SWOT assessments that identify barriers, enablers, and readiness for antimicrobial stewardship. Building on this, WP2 (Intervention) delivers the core implementation activities, i.e. capacity building of multidisciplinary AMS teams, patient education, diagnostic strengthening, and regular PPSs and clinical audits. This is expected to enhance prescribers’ capability, motivation, and opportunity for rational antibiotic use. Evidence and learning from implementation are deepened through WP3 (Assessment of barriers and enablers), which applies qualitative inquiry and conjoint analysis to understand the behavioural and contextual determinants influencing practice change, and through WP4 (economic impact), which quantifies cost, cost-effectiveness, and scalability of the AMS intervention. Findings across these components culminate in WP5 (Dissemination), where evidence is synthesised and shared through stakeholder meetings, policy working groups, and publications to inform stewardship policy and institutionalisation. Collectively, this will lead to improved antibiotic prescribing for URTIs, increased policy uptake, and ultimately a reduction of antimicrobial resistance within primary care settings.

Institutionalisation of AMS protocols

Policy integration and local champions

The AMS programme integrates selected strategies that align with the five domains of the RE-AIM framework, which collectively guide both the design and the evaluation of implementation efforts (Table 2). To enhance reach, the programme employs patient-centred education efforts, integrates stewardship messages into routine consultations, and actively engages facility managers and municipal health authorities to ensure broad participation across primary-care sites. Effectiveness is pursued through data-driven quality improvement cycles that strengthen prescribing accuracy and diagnostic stewardship, supported by audit-and-feedback mechanisms and ongoing professional coaching. Adoption is encouraged through leadership endorsement, the formation of local AMS teams, and the inclusion of stewardship responsibilities within routine job structures. Implementation quality is promoted through the use of standardised protocols, fidelity monitoring, and structured feedback loops embedded in the PDSA process. Finally, maintenance is addressed through the institutionalisation of AMS procedures into municipal and national policy frameworks, sustained audit systems, and the creation of local champions who ensure continuity beyond the pilot phase.

Evaluation of the AMS programme similarly draws on the RE-AIM framework to structure measurement of implementation outcomes. Reach will be assessed by the number and representativeness of participating facilities, prescribers, and patients exposed to AMS activities. Effectiveness will be evaluated through changes in antibiotic prescribing patterns, adherence to national guidelines, and cost-effectiveness outcomes. Adoption will be measured as the proportion of sites initiating AMS practices and maintaining active multidisciplinary teams. Implementation will be examined through fidelity to planned activities, feasibility, barriers, and enablers identified through qualitative analysis, and implementation cost.

Consolidated framework for implementation research

While the RE-AIM framework provides a structured lens for assessing implementation outcomes, it does not fully capture the underlying contextual factors that influence them. Therefore, we adopted the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to design the study, looking at barriers and enablers affecting AMS implementation. CFIR was selected over alternative frameworks because of its comprehensiveness and ability to integrate multiple levels of context, including individual, organisational, and system determinants that shape implementation success. The CFIR, updated in 2022, is a comprehensive determinant framework designed to identify and address factors influencing the implementation of innovations across various settings [41–44]. The qualitative study within WP3 will examine barriers and facilitators of change across all domains of potential influence, i.e. innovation/intervention, participants, inner setting, outer setting, and implementation process (Fig. 2) [42]. Physician practice style framework

The conjoint analysis within WP3 is informed by Wennberg’s theory of medical practice variation, which attributes differences in clinical decision-making not solely to patient characteristics or disease burden but to physicians’ individual “practice styles.” [45] Wennberg’s seminal research on small-area variation demonstrated that substantial differences in the use of medical and surgical procedures persist even after accounting for epidemiological and demographic factors, suggesting that much of health-care variation is driven by supply-side factors, including local professional norms, interpretive uncertainty, and individual preferences [45]. These practice styles are understood as relatively stable patterns of clinical reasoning and behaviour that emerge from the interplay of training, experience, peer influence, and local culture. As a result, physicians facing similar clinical scenarios may arrive at markedly different treatment decisions, i.e. whether to prescribe antibiotics for a self-limiting infection, not because of differing patient presentations, but because of variations in diagnostic thresholds, tolerance for uncertainty, or ingrained beliefs about patient expectations [45].

Data collection and analysis across AMS work packages

Data collection within WP2 will include point-prevalence surveys (PPS), clinical audits, AMS team logs, and training and supervision records documenting implementation progress across participating primary-care sites. The analysis will focus on trends in antibiotic use, adherence to AMS protocols, and implementation fidelity, using pre- and post-intervention comparisons to determine the extent and consistency of behaviour change achieved through stewardship activities.

Within WP3, we have two evaluation study components, designed as separate research studies, i.e. a qualitative study examining barriers and enablers to implementation, and a conjoint analysis assessing prescribers’ practice styles in antibiotic prescribing. The qualitative component will employ qualitative data collection methods, including focus group discussions and interviews. Separate focus group discussions involving patients and prescribers will be organised to address concerns about the programme post-implementation. A representative sample of people involved in implementation will be interviewed at the end of the programme to discuss implementation issues. The tools developed for data collection and the information collected will be analysed using the CFIR framework [41–43]. Framework analysis will serve as the main analytical framework [46]. Details on the operationalisation of this research component are available in a recent publication [34].

Conjoint Analysis aims to quantify the relative influence of clinical, contextual, and patient factors on antibiotic prescribing decisions among primary care physicians, using a discrete choice design grounded in Wennberg’s theory of practice variation [45]. The data will be collected via interviews with prescribers in intervention and control sites. Participants will include all doctors involved in antibiotic prescription from 12 selected primary care sites, encompassing general practitioners, family medicine specialists, and residents, recruited through collaboration with the Directors of the MFMCs. This sampling approach is expected to yield participants who meet the minimum requirements established by ISPOR guidelines to ensure robust preference estimation [47]. The conjoint survey will present prescribers with hypothetical clinical scenarios for common upper respiratory tract infection, that systematically vary in diagnostic cues, patient expectations, and contextual attributes. Responses will capture how physicians trade off these factors when deciding to prescribe antibiotics. Data will be analysed using choice modelling techniques (e.g., multinomial or mixed logit models) to estimate the relative importance of each attribute and identify distinct practice-style profiles.

WP4 has two components, i.e. a cost-effectiveness assessment, which evaluates changes in antibiotic use and associated costs before and after implementation, and a cost-of-intervention assessment, which systematically documents and categorises the resources required to design, initiate, and maintain the AMS programme. Data for the cost-effectiveness analysis will be obtained from patient prescription records collected across participating primary healthcare sites. Antibiotic use will be standardised using the Defined Daily Dose (DDD) methodology recommended by the World Health Organisation (WHO), allowing comparison of consumption patterns across sites and time. Total antibiotic use will be expressed as DDDs per 1,000 patient-days, with subgroup analyses by antibiotic class and clinical indication. The financial component will calculate antibiotic costs by multiplying the total DDDs by the average cost per DDD based on public drug prices in Kosova (in euros). Pre- and post-intervention comparisons of DDDs and related costs will quantify changes in antibiotic consumption and expenditure. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) will then be calculated as total intervention costs divided by the change in antibiotic use, providing an estimate of the programme’s efficiency in reducing unnecessary prescriptions.

For the cost of intervention assessment, data on intervention costs will be obtained from financial records and expenditure reports of the institutions involved in AMS implementation. All expenses will be systematically organised into a structured database, categorised by programme phase (design, initiation, and maintenance) and by cost type (central vs site-specific). Each entry will include a cost description, funding source, total amount (in euros), unit cost, and quantity, with verification through supporting documentation such as procurement or payment records. This approach enables detailed tracking of resources used for central activities and site-level implementation. Data will be summarised descriptively and presented in tabular form to allow clear visualisation of cost distribution across phases and sites, providing transparency on the financial requirements of AMS implementation and serving as input for the cost-effectiveness analysis.

For WP2, WP3, and WP4, detailed study protocols, data collection instruments, and informed consent forms will be developed and translated in Albanian. Before data collection, consent forms and participant information sheets will be distributed to respondents. Discussion

Rationale of design

The whole AMS programme, with its research components, is designed to address the supportive policy environment characterised by limited policy implementation. Kosova has been firmly committed to AMR control through National Action Plans and policy updates. However, translating policy into primary care practice has been slow due to resource constraints, limited provider training, and insufficient monitoring systems. This project seeks to bridge that gap by piloting scalable stewardship interventions. Although national guidelines for antibiotic use were recently introduced, there are significant gaps in implementation, i.e., lack of stewardship programmes, limited access to diagnostic tools like rapid strep tests, and poor adherence to prescribing guidelines. Training healthcare workers and introducing point-of-care diagnostics are essential steps toward rational antibiotic use.

This programme aims to document learning from its implementation and use it to embed and execute AMR control policies within the country. This programme and research components are an opportunity to generate evidence for scaling up. By implementing and evaluating this AMS programme in selected clinics, the project will improve local practice and develop practical evidence on enablers, barriers, and costs. This evidence will directly inform policy updates, support national scale-up efforts, and strengthen the broader national AMR strategy.

Strengths and limitations

The project protocol demonstrates several strengths that enhance its robustness and applicability. The AMS programme, i.e., intervention, is aligned with the National Action Plan for Antimicrobial Resistance (NAP-AMR) and international (WHO, ECDC) policies and standards, which set the base for a reliable intervention programme to be assessed. The protocol employs a robust mixed-methods research design, including structured evaluations using the RE-AIM and CFIR, and pre- and post-assessments to monitor changes in antibiotic prescribing patterns. The addition of an economic review further strengthens the analysis. Stakeholder engagement is a key feature, ensuring practical implementation and long-term sustainability. Data collection methods are comprehensive, incorporating procedures such as digital audio recordings, standardised coding, and the data procesing and analysis [33].

The project protocol also faces several limitations. Response bias may affect healthcare workers’ self-reporting, particularly in components like the barriers study or conjoint analysis. Observer bias is another concern, as project staff might unintentionally influence data collection, especially during qualitative assessments of implementation challenges. Inconsistent documentation practices may hinder data collection due to the absence of standardised electronic health records in participating facilities. Finally, the project has limited control over external factors, such as political changes or healthcare policy shifts, which could influence the AMS programme implementation and the validity of the assessments.

Implications for policy, practice and future research

This programme will serve as a central pillar for shaping the implementation of the AMR policy agenda in Kosova’s primary care settings and beyond. The project will directly support the NAP-AMR and related policy frameworks by integrating stewardship principles into frontline healthcare practices. Its success in embedding structured stewardship activities within primary healthcare facilities can catalyse broader adoption across other levels of care, including secondary and tertiary health services.

The AMS programme is poised to substantially impact AMR situation in the country by laying out a structured pathway for implementing AMR countermeasures in primary care. The project is expected to generate considerable momentum for scaling and extending stewardship policies across the broader primary care network by piloting practical interventions such as point-of-care diagnostics, prescriber training, and audit-feedback systems. Moreover, the lessons learned from implementation challenges and successes at the primary care level can inform the design of AMS interventions at higher levels of care, including hospitals, creating opportunities for a unified and sustainable AMR strategy across the health system.

Although the programme is tailored to Kosova, its relevance extends beyond national borders. Countries in the Western Balkans and other low- and middle-income regions facing similar barriers to AMR implementation [9], such as limited diagnostic capacity, inconsistent guideline adherence, and fragmented health governance, can benefit from Kosova’s experience. By documenting practical solutions, enablers, and barriers within real-world primary care settings, this project can offer valuable insights and operational models for other contexts aiming to strengthen their AMR stewardship efforts at the primary care level.

The mixed-methods evaluation approach, including qualitative analysis of barriers and economic evaluation, will contribute to the broader scientific understanding of AMS scalability and sustainability. The findings can guide future implementation research, inform comparative studies across countries, and support the refinement of stewardship frameworks adaptable to diverse health systems globally.

Contribution to the implementation science domain

This programme is designed not only to evaluate the effectiveness of an AMS programme but also to advance the science of implementation. By integrating multiple conceptual and analytical frameworks, namely, RE-AIM, CFIR, and, this protocol and embedded studies in it apply and extend established implementation models within a low- and middle-income country context. The mixed-methods design enables empirical testing of how RE-AIM domains can be operationalised in primary care, while CFIR constructs explain variation in adoption, fidelity, and sustainability. Additionally, incorporating conjoint analysis to capture prescribers’ decision-making patterns introduces a novel behavioural dimension to AMS research. Together, these elements generate methodological and theoretical insights that enhance understanding of how complex interventions can be effectively implemented, adapted, and scaled across diverse healthcare settings.
