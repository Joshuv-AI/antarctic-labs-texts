Testosterone Replacement Therapy is Associated With Increased Risk of Pseudarthrosis After Single-Level Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion: A Propensity Matched Analysis
Authors: William Han, Kyle Stump, Karim Gubara, Prabhat Gottipati, Stephan Aynaszyan, Theresa Pazionis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether preoperative testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) use is associated with increased risks of pseudarthrosis and reoperation following single-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF).MethodsThe TriNetX Research Network was queried to identify adult males aged 18 to 89 years who underwent single-level ACDF. Patients were separated into a TRT exposure group within 1 year before initial ACDF procedure as well as comparator cohort without TRT exposure that was matched 1:1 using propensity score matching based on age, race, and relevant medical comorbidities. Patients were followed longitudinally for 2 years from the index procedure to assess postoperative outcomes. The primary outcomes were pseudarthrosis and reoperation. Risk ratios and time-to-event hazard ratios were calculated along with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 494 matched pairs reached balance across all baseline characteristics. Patients receiving TRT demonstrated a 70% increased risk of pseudarthrosis (RR 1.70, CI 1.20–2.39; HR 1.71 CI 1.19 – 2.46) compared to the non-TRT cohort. TRT use was not associated with an increased risk of reoperation (RR 0.92 CI 0.59–1.43; HR 0.87, CI 0.55-1.38) following ACDF.ConclusionsPreoperative testosterone replacement therapy was associated with increased risk of pseudarthrosis, but not reoperation following ACDF. These findings suggest that TRT may represent an important patient-specific risk marker in patients undergoing ACDF. Given the observational design and potential for residual confounding, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal effect.Keywords: testosterone replacement therapy, hypogonadism, ACDF, cervical fusion, pseudarthrosis, reoperation, complications

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a widely performed procedure for the treatment of cervical radiculopathy and myelopathy, with generally favorable outcomes but well-established risks of complications, including pseudarthrosis and the need for reoperation. 1-4 Pseudarthrosis, defined as failure of osseous fusion, remains a clinically significant complication associated with persistent pain, neurologic dysfunction, and increased likelihood of revision surgery. 3,5 The development of pseudarthrosis is multifactorial, with patient-specific factors such as bone quality playing a critical role; osteopenia and osteoporosis have been consistently identified as predictors of impaired fusion and adverse postoperative outcomes. 3,6 As such, identification of modifiable factors that influence bone healing is essential for optimizing surgical outcomes.

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) is increasingly prescribed for the management of hypogonadism, particularly among aging male populations. Testosterone plays a central role in skeletal homeostasis through stimulation of osteoblastic activity and inhibition of bone resorption, with part of its effect mediated through aromatization to estradiol. 7 Clinical studies have demonstrated that TRT can increase bone mineral density (BMD), particularly at the lumbar spine; however, these effects are modest, site-specific, and inconsistent across populations. 8-10 Importantly, improvements in BMD have not consistently translated into reductions in fracture risk, and some large randomized trials have reported no benefit or even increased fracture incidence with TRT. 11,12

Beyond its effects on bone metabolism, TRT has been associated with systemic risks that may be relevant in the perioperative setting. Large observational studies in orthopedic populations have demonstrated associations between preoperative TRT use and increased rates of postoperative complications, including venous thromboembolism, infection, and revision surgery following total joint arthroplasty. 13,14 Although randomized controlled trials suggest that physiologic-dose TRT does not significantly increase major adverse cardiovascular events, some studies have reported higher rates of atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, and acute kidney injury. 15,16 These findings suggest that TRT may exert complex systemic effects that extend beyond skeletal metabolism and could influence postoperative recovery and complication profiles.

The relationship between TRT and spinal fusion outcomes remains poorly defined. While testosterone may theoretically promote bone healing through anabolic effects on bone remodeling, its modest and delayed impact on BMD, combined with inconsistent effects on fracture risk, raises uncertainty regarding its ability to improve fusion success.8,11 In contrast, targeted osteoanabolic therapies such as teriparatide have demonstrated clear benefits in enhancing fusion rates and reducing pseudarthrosis in spinal surgery, highlighting the importance of mechanism-specific modulation of bone biology.10,17 Furthermore, the association between TRT and increased complication and revision rates in other orthopedic procedures raises concern that TRT may adversely affect fusion outcomes through mechanisms involving altered bone remodeling, systemic inflammation, or thromboembolic risk.

Despite these considerations, no studies to date have directly evaluated the impact of TRT on pseudarthrosis or reoperation following ACDF. This represents a critical gap in the literature, particularly in the context of increasing TRT utilization and the clinical importance of achieving successful arthrodesis. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate whether preoperative testosterone replacement therapy is associated with increased risk of pseudarthrosis and reoperation following ACDF.

Methods

Data Source

Patient data for this retrospective cohort study were sourced from the TriNetX Research Network (Cambridge, Massachusetts), a federated health research platform that compiles de-identified electronic health record data across more than 120 participating healthcare organizations (HCOs) globally. In exchange for contributing patient data, HCOs are granted access to the platform’s suite of research tools at no charge, encompassing capabilities for clinical trial feasibility assessment, structured database querying, and pharmacovigilance.

Ethical Approval

The TriNetX Research Network maintains full compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) Security Rule and holds certification under the ISO 27001:2013 information security standard. Given these protections, the present study was determined to qualify for exemption from institutional review board oversight and the requirement for informed consent. All study procedures and reporting adhere to the guidelines set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) initiative.

Cohort Selection

The study population was drawn from the TriNetX Research Network database using an index surgery window spanning from January 1st, 2010, through May 18th, 2024. Postoperative outcomes were assessed for two years following the index procedure, with data through May 18, 2026, reserved for longitudinal follow-up. This time frame was chosen to allow for a follow-up duration of two years for every patient captured in the dataset. Patient identification, cohort exclusions, matching variables, and clinical endpoints were defined using codes from the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and RxNorm classification systems. The initial query targeted adult male patients between the ages of 18 and 89 years who had undergone single-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) (CPT 22554, 22551). Those with a documented multi-level cervical fusion procedure during the study window were excluded from consideration (CPT 22552). Patients with evidence of testosterone therapy (RxNorm 10379 or 37855) in the twelve months preceding their index procedure constituted the TRT cohort, while those without any record of TRT use during the same preoperative interval served as controls. Prior to matching, among the 29,638 eligible single-level ACDF patients identified over the study period, 497 patients fulfilled criteria for the TRT group, with the remaining 29,141 comprising the control cohort as shown in Figure 1.

Propensity Score Matching

Prior to outcome analysis, both cohorts underwent 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching to reduce the influence of confounding variables. Covariates selected for the matching algorithm included age at the index procedure, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), essential hypertension, nicotine dependence, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, hyperlipidemia, long-term systemic corticosteroid use, chronic kidney disease (CKD), osteoporosis and other coagulopathies. Any patient with missing data for one or more matching variables was removed prior to finalization of the matched cohorts. Adequate balance between groups was confirmed by achieving a standardized difference (std diff) of 0.1 or less across all covariates. Of the 497 initially eligible TRT-exposed patients, three were excluded during propensity score estimation because of missing matching-covariate data. The propensity score matching algorithm selected 494 controls from the 29,141 eligible patients based on similarity across the prespecified covariates to the TRT-exposed cohort.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary outcomes of this study were pseudarthrosis, defined as documentation of ICD-10-CM code M96.0 during the two-year period following the index ACDF, and reoperation at 2 years. Secondary outcomes included acute renal failure, pneumonia, sepsis, and ED utilization within 90 days.

Statistical Analysis

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. For each binary outcome, between-group comparisons were expressed as relative risk (RR) with accompanying 95% confidence intervals (CI). Time-to-event analyses were also performed for pseudarthrosis and reoperation during the two-year postoperative period. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to compare the TRT and control cohorts. All analyses were conducted automatically through the integrated statistical tools within the TriNetX platform. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value below 0.05.

At two years postoperatively, the TRT cohort demonstrated a significantly higher risk of pseudarthrosis than the non-TRT cohort (15.79% versus 9.31%; RR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.20–2.39; P = .002). Time-to-event analysis similarly demonstrated an increased hazard of pseudarthrosis among TRT-exposed patients (HR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.19–2.46; log-rank P = .004). In contrast, neither the cumulative risk of reoperation (7.09% versus 7.69%; RR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.59–1.43; P = .715) nor its hazard differed significantly between cohorts (HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.55–1.38; log-rank P = .561).

No significant differences were observed between cohorts in pneumonia (2.83% vs 3.04%; RR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.46–1.91; P = .851) or emergency department utilization (8.10% vs 10.93%; RR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.50–1.09; P = .129), as shown in Table 3. Acute renal failure and sepsis could not be compared because event counts fell below the TriNetX reporting threshold and were suppressed.

Discussion

Pseudarthrosis is a complication following ACDF which may have devastating impacts in patients including persistent pain, functional limitation and the need for revision surgery. 18,19 Notably, while detection of pseudarthrosis at 1 year post operatively has a favorable outcome with 72% achieving spontaneous fusion, pseudarthrosis detected at 2 years carries a poor prognosis. 20 For these reasons, the identification of modifiable and patient-specific risk factors that may predispose patients to non-union remains of particular interest to spine surgeons. Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) has been increasingly used among the aging male population and has traditionally been associated with improved bone mineral density and skeletal health. 21-23 However, studies have not examined TRT exposure in the context of postoperative ACDF outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that TRT use was associated with a 70% higher risk of pseudarthrosis at 2 years, whereas reoperation risk did not differ significantly between cohorts, suggesting that TRT may be associated with impaired fusion outcomes following ACDF.

The incidence of pseudarthrosis observed in the control cohort was 9.31% at 2 years, which is comparable to the 10.0% rate reported by Tavanaei et al, although direct comparisons are limited by differences in surgical technique, follow-up duration, and definitions of nonunion. 4 The magnitude of risk elevation observed in the present study appears to be similar to several more established risk factors for nonunion, including diabetes mellitus, and other metabolic comorbidities although somewhat smaller than that reported for smoking in many lumbar fusion cohorts. 2,24-27 Notably, these associations in our study persisted despite propensity matching across numerous baseline demographic and medical characteristics known to influence fusion outcomes.

These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence in the literature that suggests that TRT may not confer the expected protective effects on bone healing and instead be associated with fracture risk in a population of patients. A 2024 study by Snyder et al found that treatment with testosterone in middle age men with hypogonadism had an increased risk (hazard ratio 1.43, 95% CI 1.04-1.97) of osteoporotic fracture compared to the placebo group. 11 Our results are also in line with several large scale studies in total joint arthroplasty that demonstrate preoperative TRT use was associated with higher rates of revision surgery. 13,28,29 Most notably, Omurzakov noted periprosthetic fractures (OR 2.7) and revision surgeries (OR 1.5) to be elevated following total knee arthroplasty. 13 In the spine literature, a recent study in 2026 by Sadh et al reported increased odds of reoperation among TRT users following lumbar fusion (OR 2.36) compared with non-users. 30 In contrast, we found no significant difference in reoperation following single-level ACDF, although preoperative TRT exposure was associated with a 70% higher risk of pseudarthrosis. Differences in spinal region, surgical approach, cohort characteristics, and outcome definitions may contribute to these divergent reoperation findings.

From a biological perspective, TRT may potentially impair spinal fusion in several ways. Successful spinal fusion depends not only on bone mineral density but also in coordinated actions related to osteogenic cell recruitment, vascular ingrowth, extracellular matrix deposition and remodeling of newly formed bone. 31 While testosterone may increase bone mineral density through androgen receptor signaling, improvements in bone density does not necessarily equate to enhanced fusion. In fact, TRT has also been associated with erythrocytosis as well as increased blood viscosity which could theoretically impair perfusion and nutrient delivery to the fusion site. 32,33 Additionally, prior studies have indicated that testosterone supplementation may alter levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-alpha, crucial for bone healing and connective tissue remodeling particularly in the fusion process. 34-36 A recent 2026 study by Ng further demonstrates this in spine models as excess of TNF alpha signaling impaired fusion highlighting the importance of balanced inflammatory regulation during arthrodesis. 37 While these mechanisms are not completely understood, they provide plausible biological mechanisms for the increased risk of pseudarthrosis.

This study also demonstrated no significant increase in pneumonia or emergency department utilization within 90 days among TRT users following ACDF. Although the TRAVERSE trial reported associations between TRT use and adverse events including venous thromboembolism, atrial fibrillation, and acute kidney injury, Sadh et al observed no significant increase in 90-day complications following lumbar fusion. 30 Differences in patient populations, baseline complication risk, surgical invasiveness, and statistical power may partially explain discrepancies between arthroplasty and spine surgery 90-day complication rates. 30 Of note, due to the low event count, no formal analysis was able to be conducted in the present study for sepsis and acute renal failure. Interestingly, the substantial increase in pseudarthrosis in the TRT cohort did not translate into a higher rate of reoperation. This discrepancy may suggest that not all cases of pseudarthrosis were clinically symptomatic or severe enough to warrant revision surgery, or that clinicians may exhibit a higher threshold for revision surgery in patients with more complex systemic profiles or active hormone therapies. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether the radiographic non-unions associated with TRT correspond to clinical symptoms requiring surgical intervention.

This observed relationship with TRT use and pseudarthrosis following ACDF warrants for careful consideration by spine surgeons. While we are unable to establish causality and our results should not be interpreted as justification for routine cessation of clinically relevant TRT use, these findings suggest that TRT may represent an underrecognized contributor to pseudarthrosis risk in patients undergoing ACDF. Thus, awareness of TRT use may serve as a potential marker to identify patients at increased risk of pseudarthrosis, allowing surgeons to optimize other modifiable risk factors, including nicotine use and nutritional health, in an effort to reduce fusion failure. However, prospective studies are needed to clarify this relationship before specific perioperative management recommendations can be broadly endorsed.

Our study has several aspects that strengthen the quality of the findings. First, we employed a large multi-institutional database incorporating electronic health records from numerous healthcare organizations, improving the generalizability of our findings across diverse patient populations. Furthermore, we also employed rigorous propensity score matching and achieved balance across numerous demographic and medical characteristics known to influence fusion outcomes, including age, diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, nicotine dependence, and other relevant comorbidities. 2,24-27 Finally, this study represents among the first propensity-matched analyses evaluating the association between TRT use and ACDF outcomes.

This study has several important limitations that must be addressed. Firstly, TriNetX relies on administrative CPT coding, leading to a potential for misclassification of procedures. Secondly, pseudarthrosis was identified using administrative ICD-10 diagnosis codes without independent radiographic or clinical confirmation, which may have resulted in outcome misclassification. Thirdly, although restricting the cohort to single-level ACDF reduced heterogeneity related to surgical approach and number of operative levels, granular operative factors including graft material, use of biologics, interbody device characteristics, instrumentation, and surgical technique could not be incorporated into propensity score matching. Unmeasured differences in these factors may have contributed to residual confounding.

Fourth, confounding by indication remains possible as TRT is a treatment for conditions like hypogonadism. Thus, our observed associations may partially reflect the underlying endocrinopathy or related metabolic abnormalities rather than an independent effect of TRT. The severity of hypogonadism could not be assessed with the available data, therefore, our observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. Similarly, TriNetX does not capture TRT formulation, route of administration, or dosing, so dose-response analysis was not possible. Patients classified within the TRT cohort may consequently have had varying levels of cumulative exposure that were not accounted for in our analysis. Furthermore, information regarding treatment duration, adherence, perioperative discontinuation, and postoperative continuation was unavailable. Therefore, we could not determine actual medication use, potentially resulting in exposure misclassification. This may be particularly relevant when considering whether TRT exposure persisted during the critical postoperative healing phase when the biological impact of TRT may have had greater influence compared to remote preoperative exposure.

Conclusion

In our propensity matched analysis, testosterone replacement therapy was associated with a 70% increase in risk of pseudarthrosis, but not reoperation following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. These findings indicate that TRT may represent an important patient-specific marker associated with impaired fusion. However, the observational design and potential for residual confounding emphasize that these findings demonstrate an association and should not be interpreted as evidence that TRT independently causes adverse fusion outcomes. Despite an incomplete understanding of the biological mechanisms behind this observed trend, alterations in bone remodeling, vascular perfusion and inflammatory signaling may be partially responsible. Given the increase in use of TRT, awareness of this relationship may aid in preoperative counseling, risk stratification, and postoperative surveillance. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify causality and determine whether perioperative management strategies can mitigate this risk.
