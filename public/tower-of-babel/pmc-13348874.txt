An efficient approach to study ANA⁺ B cells in autoimmune diseases integrating flow cytometry with single-cell analysis
Authors: Muriel Velez Arteaga, Shabirul Haque, Manami Watanabe, Yemil Atisha-Fregoso, Sophia Garcia, Cynthia Aranow, Meggan Mackay, Bruce T. Volpe, Betty Diamond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSeveral autoimmune diseases are characterized by the presence of autoantibodies to nuclear antigens. They are present in essentially all patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The study of antinuclear antibodies (ANA) produced by individual B cells can offer critical insights into the structural and functional characteristics of autoantibodies. Conventional techniques of single-cell cloning and sequencing or single-cell (Nojima) cultures have significant limitations. To overcome these limitations, we developed an integrated platform that combines flow cytometric detection of ANA-reactive B cells with an optimized single-B cell culture system.MethodsWe employed a flow cytometry assay using biotinylated nuclear extracts and fluorochrome-tagged streptavidin to detect ANA-reactive B cells, followed by single cell sorting and culture in the presence of feeder cells expressing CD40L and BAFF, IL-2 and IL-21. This integrated approach was designed to enhance clonal resolution while increasing the efficiency of ANA-specific B cell recovery compared to conventional single-cell cloning methods.ResultsWe isolated and expanded ANA + and ANA- B cells of various B cell subsets (naive, CD27 + IgM + , and IgG memory) that were isolated from healthy donors and patients with SLE. The cultures were maintained in standard conditions for 26 days at which time 82% of naïve, 53% of CD27 + IgM + and 64% of IgG⁺ memory B cells produced > 1.0 ug/mL of immunoglobulin. We further demonstrated that 88—95% of ANA + B cells Ig produced ANA-reactivity by HEp-2 staining.ConclusionsThis method establishes a robust platform for isolating and expanding ANA B cells, facilitating the production of monoclonal autoantibodies and analysis of B cell receptor (BCR) gene sequences. This expansion is achieved by culturing single ANA + B cells with CD40L-expressing feeder cells and key cytokines (BAFF, IL-2, and IL-21) which together promote their survival, proliferation, and differentiation into antibody-secreting cells. The approach offers important new potential in understanding the etiology of ANA- reactive IgG in autoimmune disease by enabling the identification of specific autoantigen targets at distinct stages of B cell differentiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01502-8.

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by the production of autoantibodies, most notably antinuclear antibodies (ANA), which contribute to immune complex formation, inflammation, and tissue damage. Among the diverse immune cell populations implicated in SLE pathogenesis, B cells play a central role as the precursors to plasma cells producing pathogenic autoantibodies (Suurmond et al. 2019).

ELISpot assays and single-cell cloning and expression of antibody from individual B cells have provided valuable insights into the tolerance checkpoints in SLE but are limited in their ability to fully characterize ANA + B cells. Only a fraction of B cells in a given population will secrete antibody in an ELISpot assay when the B cells are directly assessed after isolation from blood or cultured with agents that promote differentiation ex vivo to antibody-secreting cells. Single-cell cloning and expression, while informative, is labor intensive and the number of cells that can be analyzed is relatively small (Tiller et al. 2008).

Our novel platform was developed to address these limitations by integrating flow cytometry for the isolation of ANA⁺ B cells (Malkiel et al. 2016) with an optimized single-cell culture system adapted from the Nojima method (Nojima et al. 2011; Su et al. 2016). The combined method allows for the expansion of individual ANA + B cells which in turn enables the analysis of antigenic specificity and heavy and light chain gene sequences. The approach supports the analysis of the antibody repertoire in multiple B cell subsets including naïve, CD27 + IgM +, and memory B cells and greatly facilitates high-resolution analysis of autoreactive B cell repertoires.

In this study, we describe the design and validation of this approach and demonstrate its capacity to identify, expand, and characterize ANA + B cells with high sensitivity and specificity. Our findings provide a foundation for advancing the study of B cell autoreactivity in SLE and other autoimmune diseases.

Materials and methods

Processing of human blood samples

Blood was obtained from healthy donors and patients with SLE that met the criteria of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) from 1997 for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (Hochberg 1997) or SLICC criteria (Petri et al. 2012). Donors and patients were age- and sex-matched. All participants provided written informed consent under a protocol approved by the institutional review board (IRB-07—021). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Cytiva Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cat# 45—001—749, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

ANA staining by flow cytometry

Nuclear extracts were prepared as previously described (Malkiel et al. 2016). HeLa cells were grown to confluence in a T75 flask, and nuclei were isolated using the Nuclei EZ lysis kit (Cat. no. NUC-101, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Cat. no. MT21040CV, Fisher Scientific) and pelleted at 500 g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in 3.0 mL PBS containing one Complete Mini Protease Inhibitor tablet (Cat. no. 11836170001, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), split into two Eppendorf tubes prefilled with 150 µL of 0.5—mm glass beads (Cat. no. SI-BG05, Scientific Industries, Bohemia, NY, USA), and vortexed on a Disruptor Genie (Scientific Industries) for 1 h at 4 °C. The lysate was centrifuged at 21,000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and biotinylated using EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Cat. no. 21335, Fisher Scientific). Biotinylated nuclear extracts were dialyzed against PBS for 24 h at 4 °C using Slide-A-Lyzer Dialysis Cassettes (Cat. no. 87724, Fisher Scientific) and stored at –80 °C (Malkiel et al. 2016).

ANA staining was performed as previously described (Suurmond et al. 2019). PBMCs were washed in staining buffer consisting of 5% sterile fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cat. no. SH30088.03, HyClone, Fisher Scientific, Logan, UT, USA) in HBSS (Cat. no. 14175103, Fisher Scientific) and filtered using a cell strainer (Cat. no. 22—363—548, Fisher Scientific). Cells were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in biotinylated nuclear extract at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL in 1.5% fat-free milk (Cat. no. M0841, Lab Scientific, Livingston, NJ, USA) diluted in filtered HBSS. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, added to the washed PBMCs, gently mixed, and incubated on ice for 30 min. Cells were then washed twice with 5 mL staining buffer by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C.

Conjugated antibody cocktails were prepared in 300 µL staining buffer containing CD19-PECy7 (Cat. no. 302216, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), CD27-PE (Cat. no. 50—103—00, eBioscience, Fisher Scientific), CD38-PE-Texas Red (Cat. no. 61—0389—42, Fisher Scientific), IgA-APC (Cat. no. 130—113—472, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), Streptavidin-APC (Cat. no. 405207, BioLegend), CD1c-BV421 (Cat. no. 331526, BioLegend), IgG-FITC (Cat. no. 555786, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), IgM-BV421 (Cat. no. 562380, BD Biosciences), and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor506 (Cat. no. 65—0866—14, Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated with the antibody cocktail on ice for 30 min in the dark, washed twice, and resuspended in 5% FBS in HBSS. Single cell sorting on a plate was performed using a BD FACSAria™ Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences). Naive B cells were gated as CD19⁺ CD27⁻ CD38⁻IgG⁻CD11c⁻. CD27 + IgM memory B cells were defined as CD19⁺CD27⁺ CD38⁻ IgA⁻IgG. IgG memory B cells were gated as CD19⁺CD27⁺ CD38⁻ IgA⁻IgG⁺. All subsets were further stratified by ANA reactivity (ANA⁻ and ANA⁺) (Suurmond et al. 2019). Representative gating strategy and definition of ANA⁻ and ANA⁺ gates are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Single cell culture of B cell subsets

Single-cell culture was performed as previously described using MS40L-low feeder cells. MS40L feeder cells were originally generated by (Luo et al. 2009). Feeder cells were cultured in IMDM (Cat. no. 12440—053, Invitrogen,Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Cat. no. 15070—063, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 55 µM 2-mercaptoethanol (Cat. no. 21985, Gibco, Waltham, MA USA) (Su et al. 2016). Culture plates used were 96-well plates (Cat. no. 353072, Falcon, Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), tissue culture-treated by vacuum gas plasma, polystyrene, non-pyrogenic. Wells were seeded 24 h prior to sorting and incubated with 3,000 feeder cells at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ in 100 µL B cell medium consisting of RPMI-1640 (Cat. no. 11875—093, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 55 µM 2-mercaptoethanol,1% penicillin–streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Cat. no. 11360—070, Gibco), and 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (Cat. no. 11140—050, Gibco). Once the feeder cells reached 70% confluence the plates were irradiated at 50 grey before cell sorting and 100 µL of 2 × cytokine mix including the following cytokines (all purchased from Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) were added: recombinant human IL-2 (Cat. no. 200—02), IL-4 (Cat. no. 200—04), IL-21 (Cat. no. 200—21), and BAFF (Cat. no. 310—13), at final concentrations of 50 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, and 10 ng/mL, respectively (Watanabe et al. 2019).

ANA- and ANA+ B cells were sorted into wells and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Culture medium was replaced every 3 days by removing 100 µL supernatant and adding 100 µL fresh medium with cytokines. Cultures were maintained for 26 days.

Measurement of immunoglobulin (Ig) by ELISA

ELISA plates (Cat. no. 9618, Corning) were coated with 50 µL/well of either goat anti-human IgG (Cat. no. 2040—01, SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL, USA) diluted 1:100 in PBS, or goat anti-human IgM- unlabeled. (Cat. no. 2020—01, SouthernBiotech) diluted 1:500 in PBS. Plates were incubated overnight at 4 °C, washed, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Cat. no. 001—000—162, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. Culture supernatants were diluted 1:5000. Standards included human IgG (Cat. no. 0150—01, SouthernBiotech) and IgM-L (Cat. no. 0158L-01, SouthernBiotech). Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h.

Detection antibodies anti-human IgG–AP (Cat. no. 2040—04, SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL, USA) and anti-human IgM–AP (Cat. no. 2020—04, SouthernBiotech) were added at 1 µg/mL in 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) in PBS and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were then incubated with substrate buffer containing NaHCO₃, MgCl₂, and one phosphatase substrate tablet (Cat. no. S0942, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at room temperature. Optical density was measured at 405 nm using a Victor3 multilabel counter plate reader (Model 1420, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Immunofluorescence assay (IFA) on HEp-2 cells

ANA reactivity was assessed using HEp-2 cell slides (Cat. no. 32583, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Samples were added at 20 µg/mL, in both IgM and IgG B cell subsets and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Anti human IgG-FITC was used from the kit. IgM detection was optimized for HEp-2 staining in naïve and CD27 + IgM + B cells using anti-human IgM-FITC (Cat. no. 314506, BioLegend) at 1 µg/mL with a 20-min incubation.

Slides were washed three times with PBS, mounted, and visualized using a Zeiss Apotome fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Oberkochen, Germany). Staining patterns were evaluated and scored by observers blinded to the origin of the samples.

ELISA on isolated nuclei

To further evaluate nuclear antigen recognition using a complementary detection assay adapted from (Jitsukawa et al. 1991), culture supernatants previously screened by HEp-2 immunofluorescence were analyzed using an ELISA measuring binding to nuclei. Corning™ 96-Well, High Binding, Flat-Bottom, Half-Area Microplates (Cat. no. 3690, Corning) were coated with 2 µg/mL poly-L-lysine (PLL) (Cat. no. 25988—63—0, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS and incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Plates were washed once with PBS prior to seeding nuclei.

HeLa nuclei were prepared as described above and seeded at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ nuclei/mL and incubated for 1 h at RT with gentle shaking. Nuclei were fixed and permeabilized with cold acetone for 7 min at RT. Wells were air-dried for 2h and washed four times with PBS.

Blocking was performed using 5% FBS in PBS for 1 h at RT. Supernatants were diluted to a final concentration of 10 µg/mL in 0.2% BSA in PBS and incubated for 1 h at RT. Plates were washed four times with PBS 0.05% Tween.

After washing, alkaline phosphatase (AP)-conjugated anti-human IgM (for naïve and IgM memory-derived antibodies) or anti-human IgG (for IgG memory-derived antibodies) (SouthernBiotech) was used at 1 µg/mL in PBS and incubated for 1 h at 37℃. Following washing, binding was detected using phosphatase substrate (Sigma-Aldrich), and optical density at 405 nm using a Victor3 multilabel counter plate reader (PerkinElmer) and the O.D. was measured at 15-min intervals for 90 min.

Specificity and sensitivity of Ig from ANA + B cells by HEp-2 staining

We evaluated ANA reactivity in culture supernatants by HEp-2 staining and assessed ANA pattern to determine if we isolated ANA + B cells with multiple antigenic specificities. Specificity was consistently high across all compartments (82–92%), indicating that the majority of flow-identified ANA⁺ cells demonstrated nuclear reactivity by immunofluorescence. Sensitivity varied by subset, with naïve IgM B cells demonstrating 80% sensitivity and CD27⁺ IgM memory B cells 92% sensitivity, while the IgG memory compartment showed lower sensitivity (68%) (Table 2). The positive predictive value (PPV) of HEp-2 staining relative to flow cytometric identification was 80% for naïve, 86% for CD27 + IgM + and 95% for IgG memory B cells, calculated as True Positive (Hep-2 +) divided by the total number of samples from B cells sorted as ANA +. As seen in Fig. 2, we could detect homogeneous speckled and rim staining in the naive, CD27 + IgM + and IgG memory subset. Discussion

We present a new and efficient method for the identification, isolation, and expansion of individual ANA+ B cells. By integrating flow cytometric detection with a modified Nojima single-cell culture system (Nojima et al. 2011; Watanabe et al. 2019), we achieved robust expansion and immunoglobulin production from individual B cells, including those from both healthy donors and patients with SLE.

Our study addresses a central challenge in autoimmune B cell research: the low frequency of autoreactive B cells in peripheral blood, particularly in healthy donors and in leukopenic individuals with SLE, whether the leukopenia is due to disease activity or immunosuppressive treatments. Conventional techniques such as bulk culture lack single-cell resolution and therefore do not assess the diversity of the B cell repertoire. Single cell level of resolution is essential for understanding B cell selection, tolerance checkpoint, and subset-specific therapeutic targets. In our system, single cell specificity is achieved, high throughput screening is possible, and antibody recovery through single cell cloning and expression is possible when desired (Tiller et al. 2008).

To further assess concordance between flow cytometric ANA identification and HEp-2 reactivity, we examined sensitivity and specificity across B cell subsets. For the naïve and IgM + CD27 + subsets the sensitivity and specificity were over 80%, while the IgG memory compartment showed lower sensitivity (68%). The reduced sensitivity in IgG memory cells may reflect differences in affinity thresholds. It is possible that the biotinylation process masks some antigens but in IgM population this is not seen. Another possibility could be the difference in the Ig distribution and density in the surface in IgG memory. In the flow‑cytometric assay, antigen binding is mediated by membrane‑bound Ig, whose effective avidity depends on surface density. In contrast, HEp‑2 staining evaluates reactivity of the corresponding secreted IgG, which is soluble and can sample epitopes in three dimensions with different valency and flexibility. Importantly, specificity remained highest in the IgG memory subset (92%), supporting the overall robustness of the enrichment strategy despite variability in sensitivity.

HEp-2 staining patterns observed in ANA + culture derived supernatants including homogeneous, rim/peripheral and speckled indicate that our platform captures diverse nuclear antigen specificities (Suurmond and Diamond 2015). These staining patterns are associated with different antigens such as DNA, RNA, Smith, SSA, and SSB. Antibodies against dsDNA are among the most clinically specific for SLE, typically producing homogeneous or rim/peripheral staining patterns on HEp-2 cells; these antibodies are linked to lupus nephritis. Anti-histone and anti-chromatin antibodies also produce homogeneous nuclear staining and are seen in SLE and drug-induced lupus. The speckled pattern, associated with specificities to Ro/SSA, La/SSB, Sm, and RNP antigens Understanding how the reactivity of ANA + B cells differs in different B cell maturation stages could provide valuable insights into the mechanisms governing B cell tolerance and identify potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets to restore immune balance (Dema and Charles 2016).

Single-cell cloning has been a foundational approach in immunology, used to isolate monoclonal antibody-producing cells and track antigen-specific responses. It has been instrumental in characterizing immune memory, infection responses, and autoimmunity. Traditional single-cell cloning is low-throughput, and labor-intensive. Based on the prevalence of ANA+ B cells in the population, conventional techniques would require sorting and culturing approximately 3,030 B cells to obtain 100 ANA⁺ cultures yielding > 1 µg/mL of immunoglobulin. In contrast, our method achieves the same outcome by culturing just 159 flow-sorted ANA+ B cell representing a clear improvement in efficiency. Our method retains the benefits of clonal resolution while significantly enhancing expansion and throughput.

Our goal was to develop and validate a robust platform for the identification, isolation, and expansion of individual ANA⁺ B cells. This approach enables downstream interrogation of antigen-specific reactivity at single-cell resolution. The ability to expand these clones and assess their specificity across defined nuclear antigens provides a framework to dissect differences in antigen targeting, affinity, and autoreactive profiles between health and disease. Future studies leveraging this platform will allow systematic comparison of antigen-specific repertoires and functional properties of ANA⁺ B cells across clinical contexts.
