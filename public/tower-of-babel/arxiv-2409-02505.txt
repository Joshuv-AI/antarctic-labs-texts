Variational Vibrational States of Methanol (12D)
Ayaki Sunaga, Gustavo Avila, Edit Mátyus

Abstract
Full-dimensional (12D) vibrational states of the methanol molecule ( CH 3 ​ OH \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH} ) have been computed using the GENIUSH-Smolyak approach and the potential energy surface from Qu and Bowman (2013). All vibrational energies are converged better than 0.5 cm − 1 \text{cm}^{-1} with respect to the basis and grid size up to the first overtone of the CO stretch, ca. 2000 cm − 1 \text{cm}^{-1} beyond the zero-point vibrational energy. About seventy torsion-vibration states are reported and assigned. The computed vibrational energies agree with the available experimental data within less than a few cm − 1 \text{cm}^{-1} in most cases, which confirms the good accuracy of the potential energy surface. The computations are carried out using curvilinear normal coordinates with the option of path-following coefficients which minimize the coupling of the small- and large-amplitude motions. It is important to ensure tight numerical fulfilment of the C 3 ​ v C_{3\text{v}} (M) molecular symmetry for every geometry and coefficient set used to define the curvilinear normal coordinates along the torsional coordinate to obtain a faithful description of degeneracy in this floppy system. The reported values may provide a computational reference for fundamental spectroscopy, astrochemistry, and for the search of the proton-to-electron mass ratio variation using the methanol molecule.

II.1
Classical and quantum mechanics of molecular rotations and vibrations

To introduce all necessary notation, we start this introduction with the classical Lagrangian of
N
atomic nuclei experiencing the
V
potential energy,

L=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}-V\;.

(1)

For an efficient description of molecular motions, the laboratory-fixed Cartesian coordinates,
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
are replaced
by the
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{NCM}}
translational Cartesian coordinates of the nuclear centre of mass (NCM),
the
\Omega=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})
rotational coordinates, and the corresponding
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
body-fixed Cartesian coordinates,
according to

\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\boldsymbol{O}(\Omega)\boldsymbol{r}_{i}+\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathrm{NCM}}.

(2)

Furthermore, bond distance, angle, and torsion-type internal coordinates,
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{3N-6})
, are defined as a function of
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\ (i=1,\ldots,N)
.
As a short notation for the new coordinates, we introduce

\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\ldots,\zeta_{3N}\}=\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{3N-6},\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},X^{\mathrm{NCM}}_{X},X^{\mathrm{NCM}}_{Y},X^{\mathrm{NCM}}_{Z}\}.

(3)

The Lagrangian for this new set of coordinates is obtained with the chain rule as

L=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{kl=1}^{3N}\dot{\zeta}_{k}g_{kl}\dot{\zeta}_{l}-V,

(4)

where

g_{kl}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}}{\partial\zeta_{k}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{X}_{i}}{\partial\zeta_{l}}\;.

(5)

is the mass-weighted metric tensor.
The elements of
\boldsymbol{g}
can also be written with
\boldsymbol{t}

Ref.
[

g_{kl}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\boldsymbol{t}^{\mathrm{T}}_{ik}\boldsymbol{t}_{il},

(6)

where the vibrational

\boldsymbol{t}_{ik}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}_{i}}{\partial\rho_{k}}\;,\quad k=1,2,\ldots,3N-6\;,

(7)

and the rotational
t

\boldsymbol{t}_{i3N-6+a}=\boldsymbol{e}_{a}\times\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\;,\quad a=1(x),2(y),3(z)

(8)

with the
\boldsymbol{e}_{a}

The

(9)

By defining

(10)

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3N}\sum_{l=1}^{3N}p_{k}G_{kl}p_{l}+V\;.

(11)

\boldsymbol{G}

The quantum Hamiltonian is obtained through the correspondence principle with considering also Podolsky’s note

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{rv,P}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3N-3}\sum_{l=1}^{3N-3}\tilde{g}^{-1/4}\hat{p}_{k}\tilde{g}^{1/2}G_{kl}\hat{p}_{l}\tilde{g}^{-1/4}+V

where

The present work focuses on vibrational computations, so we will continue with the vibration-only part (corresponding to
J=0

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{v,P}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3N-6}\sum_{l=1}^{3N-6}\tilde{g}^{-1/4}\hat{p}_{k}\tilde{g}^{1/2}G_{kl}\hat{p}_{l}\tilde{g}^{-1/4}+V\;.

II.3
Curvilinear normal coordinates

The GF method and the curvilinear (c-normal) coordinates are well known

[
78
,
3
,

,

,

,

,

,
but we briefly reiterate details to introduce the notation necessary for further discussions.

The core idea is to define a linear combination of the primitive internal coordinates (Sec.
II.2
) that reduce coupling both in the kinetic and in the potential energy (within certain approximations) in the neighbourhood of a reference structure.
The relevant procedure is known for small-amplitude vibrations about a well-defined reference structure. So, we define the displacement from the reference structure of the small-amplitude coordinates,

\displaystyle\Delta\xi_{i}=\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\text{ref}}\;,\quad i=1,\ldots,D_{\text{s}}

(39)

where
D_{\text{s}}
is the number of small-amplitude coordinates (assuming to be the first
D_{\text{s}}
coordinates among the internal coordinates).
We aim to define the
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{D_{\text{s}}}
coordinates as a(n invertible) linear transformation of the ‘old’ displacement coordinates,

\displaystyle\Delta\xi_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{D_{\text{s}}}L^{Q}_{ik}Q_{k},

(40)

so that the coupling among the new
Q_{k}
coordinates is small.
The conditions for the smallness of the coupling about the reference structure had been straightforwardly formulated,
e.g.,
Ref.
3
, for the classical kinetic and potential energy (in the harmonic approximation and for the small-amplitude coordinates, labelled with ‘s’),

\displaystyle 2T

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{G}^{\text{s}})^{-1}\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{L}_{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{G}^{\text{s}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}_{Q}\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{E}\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}\;,

(41)

\displaystyle 2V

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{s}}){\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{L}_{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}{\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{s}}}\boldsymbol{L}_{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}\;,

(42)

where we aim to choose
\boldsymbol{L}_{Q}
so that both the
\boldsymbol{E}
(kinetic energy) and the
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
(potential energy) matrices are diagonal. Thereby, the new small-amplitude coordinates are uncoupled (at this harmonic level of description).
This requirement is fulfilled by the diagonalization of the real, non-symmetric ‘GF’ matrix, which implies
\boldsymbol{L}^{\mathrm{T}}_{Q}\neq\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}_{Q}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}_{Q}{\boldsymbol{G}^{\text{s}}}{\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{s}}}\boldsymbol{L}_{Q}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\;.

(43)

So, first, we solve the eigenproblem (by numerical methods)

\textbf{A}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{G}^{\text{s}}}{\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{s}}}\textbf{A}=\mathbf{\Lambda}\;,

(44)

and then, fix the norm of the eigenvectors to fulfill Eqs. (
41
)–(
42
), according to

L^{Q}_{ik}=A_{ik}{N_{k}}\;,\quad\quad N_{k}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\sum_{i,j=1}^{D_{\text{s}}}F^{\text{s}}_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(45)

\lambda_{k}
is the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
diagonal element of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, and it is related to the harmonic frequency
\nu_{k}
through
\lambda_{k}=4\pi^{2}\nu_{k}^{2}
.
The
Q_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,D_{\text{s}})
coordinates defined through Eq. (
40
) with coefficients computed through this GF procedure are called mass-weighted curvilinear normal coordinates.
In computations, it is useful to work with dimensionless c-normal coordinates,
\boldsymbol{q}
, defined as

q_{k}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}Q_{k}\;,

(46)

and therefore, Eq. (
40
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Delta\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D_{\text{s}}}L^{Q}_{ik}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}q_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D_{\text{s}}}L_{ik}q_{k}

(47)

with

\displaystyle L_{ik}=L^{Q}_{ik}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\;.

(48)

This conversion simplifies the computer implementation because
\nu_{k}
does not appear in the integration (Appendix III of Ref.
78
and Appendix E of Ref.
3
).

To compute the linear combination coefficients, Eq. (
48
), for the c-normal coordinates, Eq. (
47

\boldsymbol{G}
matrix, Eqs. (
5
)–(

G^{\text{s}}_{kl}=G_{kl}(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\text{ref}})\;,\quad k,l=1,2,...,D_{\text{s}}\;,

(49)

using the vibrational and rotational
\boldsymbol{t}
-vectors evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\text{ref}}
(
i=1,\ldots,N
),

\boldsymbol{t}_{ik}=\left.\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}_{i}}{\partial\rho_{k}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}=\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{ref}}},\;k=1,2,\ldots,3N-6\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\boldsymbol{t}_{i,3N-6+a}=\boldsymbol{e}_{a}\times\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i},\;a=1(x),2(y),3(z)\;.

Then, the Hessian of the

The selection of the

Regarding the present vibrational computations for CH

At the same time, the coordinate range of the large-amplitude motion relevant for the quantum dynamics is not limited to a small neighbourhood of equilibrium structure(s), LAMs are delocalized over multiple PES wells. Such small differences are, of course, most often irrelevant in practical applications. During the definition of the c-normal coordinates, we had to pay attention to tiny numerical differences, and for this reason, it was necessary to implement the high-precision (restricted) minimum-search algorithm just described. We observed that without this careful numerical procedure, numerical imperfections (noise) in the c-normal coordinate coefficients caused small splittings in degenerate energies. These small splittings were convergence errors for the given coordinate definitions, which were expected to disappear in the infinite basis set (and exact integration) limit, but slowed down the convergence of finite-basis computations.

III
Variational solution of the vibrational Schrödinger equation

We aim for the (quasi-)variational solution of the vibrational Schrödinger equation including the vibrational Hamiltonian, Eq. (
14
),

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{v}}\Psi=E\Psi\;,

(57)

and to compute the ground and all vibrationally excited states up to an energy threshold. This task becomes computationally feasible by defining truncated direct product basis sets and corresponding pruned product integration grids. Efficient basis and grid truncation are possible in conjunction with curvilinear normal coordinates, which minimize the couplings of small-amplitude vibrations.

III.1
Truncation of the direct-product basis

The vibrational wavefunction is straightforwardly expanded as a linear combination of the direct product of one-dimensional basis functions,

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{DP}}_{i}\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{D_{\text{s}}};\tau\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{b_{1}}\ldots\sum_{n_{{D_{\text{s}}}}=0}^{b_{D_{\text{s}}}}\sum_{n_{\tau}=0}^{n_{\tau}}C_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{{D_{\text{s}}}},n_{\tau}}^{i}\prod_{j=1}^{D_{\text{s}}}\psi_{n_{j}}^{(q)}\left(q_{j}\right)\psi_{n_{\tau}}^{(\tau)}(\tau),

where the one-dimensional basis functions are most often constructed from orthogonal polynomials and
b_{j}
is the largest degree of the polynomial in
\psi_{n_{j}}^{(q)}
. For the small-amplitude vibrations, we use harmonic oscillator basis functions, whereas for the
\tau
torsional degree of freedom, Fourier basis function
\psi_{n_{\tau}}^{(\tau)}
are used,

1,\cos(\tau),\sin(\tau),\ldots,\cos\left(n_{\tau}\tau\right),\sin\left(n_{\tau}\tau\right),\quad\tau\in[0,2\pi).

(59)

The direct-product representation increases the size of the basis set too rapidly.
A direct-product basis accounts for the coupling between all SAMs, but actually, the coupling between the SAMs is relatively small (by construction, Sec.
II.3
). We aim to prioritize a higher-order degree of the basis functions, instead of a high-order representation of the SAM couplings (that are small). We have implemented this idea by using a pruned product vibrational basis representation for the SAMs

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{NON-DP}}_{i}\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{D_{\text{s}}};\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{D_{\text{s}}})\leq b}\sum_{n_{\tau}=0}^{n_{\tau}}C_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{{D_{\text{s}}}},n_{\tau}}^{i}\prod_{j=1}^{D_{\text{s}}}\psi_{n_{j}}^{(q)}\left(q_{j}\right)\psi_{n_{\tau}}^{(\tau)}(\tau)

(60)

with the so-called ‘pruning function’
f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{D_{\text{s}}})
,

f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{D_{\text{s}}})=\sum^{D_{\text{s}}}_{i}g_{i}(n_{i})\geq 0\;.

(61)

Here
g_{i}(n_{i})
is a function to tune the weight of the number of basis functions included in the basis set for the coordinate
i
.
In the present study, the simplest possible function (identity) is used,

g_{i}(n_{i})=n_{i}.

(62)

By choosing more complicated
g_{i}
functions, we could give more ‘weight’ to certain degrees of freedom in the basis representation,
e.g.,
to include more functions for the low-energy SAMs.

The effect of fine-tuning
g_{i}(n_{i})
can improve the convergence of the energies and wave functions with respect to the total basis set size.
In previous work on HCOOH
[
10
]
, the lowest harmonic wave number among the SAMs (632
\text{cm}^{-1}
) was smaller than the wave number of the torsional fundamental vibration (673
\text{cm}^{-1}
), so in that case, it was essential to increase the basis set size for this low-energy SAM
[
10
]
.
In methanol, the lowest-energy SAMs vibration wave number (1066
\text{cm}^{-1}
, Table
2
) is two orders of magnitude larger than that of the LAM (9.1
\text{cm}^{-1}
).
Furthermore, the basis sets with
n_{i}=1
dominantly contribute to the lowest 100 states computed in the present work (Sec.
IV.3.2
). Hence, the simplest pruning scheme, Eq. (
62
), is expected to perform well in the present study. A more elaborate pruning scheme would be necessary to compute higher-excited states. It is an interesting idea to possibly combine the Smolyak scheme with basis sets contraction
[
61
,
63
,
60
,
62
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the combination of basis contraction and the Smolyak scheme was never reported, but it is ongoing work in our research group for a molecular complex.

[

\boldsymbol{s}
-vector formalism
[

may be more efficient instead of using
\boldsymbol{t}
-vectors, since the

\boldsymbol{s}
-vectors without matrix inversion.

III.3
Sigmoid functions and curvilinear coordinate ranges

The dimensionless normal coordinates can take values in the
q\in(-\infty,\infty)
interval, and correspondingly, the integration grid points are in this interval. If we used these values in Eq. (
47
), we may obtain
\boldsymbol{\xi}
‘primitive’ internal coordinate values outside the coordinate range of bond distances, bond angles.
To ensure that the internal coordinate values are in their mathematically correct interval,
i.e.,
bond distance,
r\in[0,\infty)
; bond angle,
\theta\in[0,\pi]
; torsional coordinate,
\tau\in[0,2\pi)
; we used the ‘mapping functions’ defined in Ref.
9
.
The parameters in the mapping functions are tuned so that small variations in the precise choice of the mapping function itself do not significantly affect the vibrational energies.
In our largest computations (
b=8
, Sec.
IV.3.2
), the largest (smallest) grid points corresponding to the dimensionless normal coordinates are about
\pm 4.5
. From the conversion through the
L
transformation matrix, the coordinate range in the internal coordinate,
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}
, is about
\pm 0.81
in bohr and radian (for distances and angles) from the reference coordinate value,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\text{ref}}
.
In this computation, the mapping functions are necessary for some degrees of freedom, and they will be essential for larger basis and grid sizes.

Numerical results and discussion

First of all, we note that the expected accuracy of the vibrational band origins is limited to a few cm

The manuscript and Supporting Information extensively discuss the tight convergence of degenerate pairs, which is relevant for planned rovibrational computations and direct comparison with high-resolution microwave and infrared spectra of rotational intervals.

IV.3.2
Comparison with experiment

Tables
6
and
7
list all computed vibrational energies up to 2182
\text{cm}^{-1}
beyond the ZPVE using
b=8
in the GENIUSH-Smolyak program and the PES13 of CH
3
OH.
Based on the analysis shown in the Sec. S3 of Supporting Information, we decided to use the following basis and grid parameters in the largest computation,
n_{\tau}=33
and
M_{\tau}=54
for the LAM basis and grid size; and

b=8
and
H=22
for the SAM basis truncation and grid pruning parameters, respectively.
The convergence of the vibrational energies was estimated by comparing the
b=7
and
b=8
results. All vibrational energies differ by less than 0.5
\text{cm}^{-1}
(indicating an excellent convergence) up to 2000
\text{cm}^{-1}
beyond the zero-point vibrational energy. These states can be assigned to the ground or the vibrational fundamental,
n_{i}=0,1\ (i=1,\ldots,11)
, for each SAM degree of freedom. At the same time, states with up to the
n_{\tau}=5
th state (4th overtone) of the (low-energy and floppy) torsional mode are observed.
The current
b=7
and
b=8
SAM basis truncation parameters (and other basis and grid details specified earlier) are excellent for the complete vibrational description of this energy range (up to 2000
\text{cm}^{-1}
).

Beyond 2000
\text{cm}^{-1}
, we observe overtones and combination bands of the SAMs, and correspondingly a larger, up to 1.5
\text{cm}^{-1}
, deviation of the
b=7
and 8 vibrational energy values is found. For a sub-1
\text{cm}^{-1}
convergence, larger
b
values would be required to confirm the current
b=8
values or to improve upon them.

The doubly-degenerate pairs of states, dictated by the
C_{3\text{v}}(\text{M})
molecular symmetry group, are obtained numerically. With the numerical symmetry-adaptation approach during the curvilinear normal coordinate definition (to eliminate numerical noise from the GF calculations), it was possible to converge (with the present basis sizes) the degeneracies better than 0.01
\text{cm}^{-1}
with the exception of three pairs of states explicitly listed in the footnotes of the tables.

We also note that the high-energy vibrational fundamentals corresponding to the
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3},
and
\nu_{9}
normal modes (Table
2
), do not appear in the energy range of our computation. For them, it would be necessary to compute more than 100 vibrational states.

Our results are in good agreement with the experiments, and the deviation is only a few
\text{cm}^{-1}
for most of the states.
The energetic ordering in a dense region (12th-16th states) does not agree with experiment, but the computed energies are close to the experimental values. We find that the no. 43, 46-47, 48 computed energies differ
by more than a few
\text{cm}^{-1}
from the experimental values (Tables 4 and 7 of Ref.
109
, and Table I of Ref.
108
).
Interestingly, for these states (no. 43, 46-47, 48),
our computation agrees better with the older experiment by Serrallach
et al.
in 1974
[
108
]
than with the more recent experiment in 2003
[
109
]
. When the experimental assignments differ from ours, we indicate the experimental assignments in the footnote.

To the best of our knowledge, computed vibrational states in the 1500–2000
\text{cm}^{-1}
range are first reported in the present work. The torsional excited states are observed in this region.

Regarding the strongly mixed states, #94–99, the earlier vibrational perturbation theory
[
30
]
and MULTIMODE reaction path
[
29
]
computations use different PESs from ours (PES13) and report values closer to the experiment.
Regarding our variational vibrational computation, we think that the vibrational energies are converged (within a few
\text{cm}^{-1}
for these energy levels, Figure S1 of Supporting Information) with respect to the vibrational basis set size, and the integration grid is also adequately chosen. Therefore, the larger deviation from the experiment (10-23
\text{cm}^{-1}
, #94–99 in Table
7
) most likely indicates imperfections of the PES for these levels. Most importantly, the basis sets in the electronic structure computation, CCSD(T)-F12b/aug-cc-pVDZ, or the fitting error should be considered in future revisions.

V
Summary, conclusion, and outlook

Full-dimensional (12D) vibrational computations are performed for the methanol molecule using the GENIUSH-Smolyak computer program and the full-dimensional
ab initio
potential energy surface developed by Qu and Bowman
[
33
]
.
All vibrational states converged better than 0.5
\text{cm}^{-1}
up to 2000
\text{cm}^{-1}
beyond the zero-point vibrational energy (ZPVE).
The ZPVE is estimated to be converged better than 0.05
\text{cm}^{-1}
.
Automated computation of curvilinear normal coordinates for the small-amplitude vibrations are implemented in the GENIUSH program (in Fortran) in order to minimize the coupling of the small amplitude vibrations necessary for using efficient basis and grid truncation techniques.
During the numerical implementation and computation of the coordinate coefficients, special attention is devoted to suppressing the numerical noise. This ensures strict symmetry adaptation for good convergence of symmetry features according to the
C_{3\text{v}}(\text{M})
molecular symmetry group in finite basis representations of this floppy molecule.

The computed and experimental torsional vibrational band origins (where the experimental value is available) are in excellent agreement, within 5
\text{cm}^{-1}
. Most of the non-torsional vibrational band origins are also in good agreement.
Since the vibrational computation is well converged, this result indicates the good quality of the PES, while some small imperfections, especially at higher energies are identified.
Further development of the PES for the higher-energy range and extensive comparison (perhaps triggering re-assignments) of the experiments would be welcome in the future.

As to further computations and developments on the quantum dynamics side, we plan to compute rovibrational states, by adapting an efficient body-fixed frame to be able to use the vibrational eigenstates computed in this work as a good basis for the rovibrational computation. This development will open the route towards a direct (line-by-line) comparison with high-resolution experiments and the development of an
ab initio
quantum dynamics database for methanol.
In addition, further applications are planned using the path-following GF method and the GENIUSH-Smolyak approach for systems with large amplitude motion(s).

S3
Convergence of the vibrational energies with respect to the basis size

The basis and grid sizes have been extensively tested.
Regarding the
\tau
torsional degree of freedom, the 1D-torsional computations (Table
S4
) provided guidance. At least
n_{\tau}=33
torsional basis functions were necessary to converge the lowest 100 torsional states up to ca. 2500
\text{cm}^{-1}
beyond the ZPVE.
The corresponding number of integration points was determined (Table
S5
) to be

M_{\tau}=54
for a 0.01
\text{cm}^{-1}
convergence.

Regarding the SAMs, we have carried out computations with increasingly large
b
values, (Eqs. 41 and 42 of the main text),
b=5,6,7,
and 8.
A systematic convergence is observed by increasing the basis set size, and the differences between
\tilde{\nu}_{b=8}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{b=7}
are less than 1
\text{cm}^{-1}
for the most states (Figure
S1
).

The difference between ave-
L(\tau)
and ave-
L
in the 12D computation using several basis set sizes is shown in Figure
S2
. It is observed that by increasing the basis size, the different coordinate choices have a smaller role. The difference of the vibrational energies decreases with an increasing basis size, and in the infinite basis limit, all (correct) coordinate definitions are, of course, expected to give the same energies.
For
b=7
, the largest absolute deviation of the ave-
L
and ave-
L(\tau)
energies was 0.01
\text{cm}^{-1}
.

The ZPVE obtained using ave-
L(\tau)
is
larger
by a few
10^{-3}

\text{cm}^{-1}
than the ZPVE obtained with ave-
L
(same basis size,
b=5
and 6). This ZPVE difference is tiny, but we add that we would have expected the other way round: a lower value from ave-
L(\tau)
, which is expected to provide a better coordinate representation (corresponding to the numerical scheme used to construct the coordinate definition), so this deviation is probably due to numerical noise. Regarding 1D model computations (Table 3 in the main text), the relaxed path model, sym-
\xi(\tau)
, performed better.
Regarding ave-
L(\tau)
, it would be necessary to rule out any (remaining) numerical noise, but the convergence level with
b=7
is already sufficient for our purposes. In the large-scale production computations, we will use the sym-
\xi(\tau)
and ave-
L
combination of the coordinate definition.
