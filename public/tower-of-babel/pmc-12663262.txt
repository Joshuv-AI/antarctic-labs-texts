Impaired use of function words in European French-speaking children with developmental language disorder
Authors: Marie-Thérèse Le Normand, Hung Thai-Van
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Function word (FW) is a reliable marker of Developmental Language Disorder (DLD), yet their profile in European French (EF) preschoolers remains poorly understood. This corpus study compared FW use in 73 monolingual EF-speaking children with DLD (39–84 months, M = 54, SD = 10.93) and 76 typically developing peers (24–36 months, M = 30, SD = 5.97) matched on mean length of utterance in words (M = 2.5). Six FW categories—articles, prepositions, demonstrative pronouns, subject pronouns, and modal and auxiliary verbs—were analysed using chi-square tests and log-linear regression. Performance was comparable for articles and prepositions, but children with DLD showed impairments of FW use related to verbal contexts—reduced pronoun use, skewed FW distribution, and frequent omission or agreement errors with modal and auxiliary verbs. These patterns indicate processing limitations beyond developmental variability, implicating working memory, procedural learning, and sensitivity to input regularities. The findings refine cross-linguistic models of DLD and highlight FW assessment as a sensitive diagnostic tool for EF-speaking children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26862-9.

Introduction

Developmental Language Disorder (DLD), previously known as Specific Language Impairment (SLI), is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects an individual’s ability to acquire and use language. This occurs despite having normal intelligence and no apparent neurological, sensory, or social-emotional deficits. DLD is considered a major public health concern according to the two major diagnostic systems, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems1,2. It has been shown to be predictive of persistent impairment in social skills throughout life3,4 and has been described as an identifiable risk factor for behavioural problems as well as academic failure and illiteracy5. Given the high prevalence of DLD (7.6% in the general population6–8 and 12.6% in preschoolers aged 5–6 years9) and the long-term negative social and economic consequences of its late diagnosis, identifying early clinical markers of the disorder should improve management strategies and outcomes.

Several theories have been proposed to explain the aetiology of DLD, integrating biological, cognitive, linguistic, and environmental factors. Neurophysiological studies, using for instance electroencephalogram and functional magnetic resonance imaging, have shown that children with DLD exhibit reduced neural responses to statistical regularities in auditory input, indicating inefficiencies in auditory processing networks10–12. Neurobehavioural evidence suggests that local structure-building mechanisms emerge during the second and third years of life and culminate in the acquisition of an abstract grammatical system by approximately age three13. One influential line of research highlights deficits in statistical learning—the ability to detect and internalise distributional patterns over time—as a key contributor to language acquisition challenges in DLD14–17. Specifically, children with DLD may have difficulty processing linguistic entropy, i.e. the inherent variability and unpredictability in natural language input, which may reduce the salience of linguistic features and hinder the extraction of grammatical rules18,19. Furthermore, domain-general mechanisms, such as limitations in working memory20,21 and deficits in attentional control22,23, may exacerbate these difficulties by constraining the capacity to retain and manipulate linguistic information during real-time processing. Cross-linguistic research has consistently demonstrated that, although the specific grammatical errors observed in children with DLD vary across languages, these children often face difficulties related to the perceptual and temporal processing demands of low-salience morphemes and syntactic cues. According to Leonard24,25, children with DLD exhibit domain-general processing limitations—such as reduced speed of processing and working memory capacity—that interfere with the efficient mapping of linguistic input to representations and output, thereby contributing to persistent grammatical difficulties across languages. These findings suggest that the productive use of function words (FWs) is impaired in DLD and that this impairment may arise from an interplay between domain-general cognitive limitations and language-specific processing constraints, rather than from a selective disruption of syntactic competence per se. One perspective, which is adopted herein, is that DLD could be attributed to limited processing capacity stemming from an interrelation between a weak working memory that may hinder the temporary storage and manipulation of morphosyntactic information and a deficit in procedural learning, which may hinder the acquisition and automatisation of rule-governed grammatical structures. Procedural learning, according to the Procedural Deficit Hypothesis26, refers to the acquisition and execution of rule-governed skills and sequences, supported primarily by frontal/basal ganglia circuits. While procedural learning is often studied in the context of motor skills, it also encompasses cognitive domains such as rule-based sequence processing, which may underlie aspects of grammar acquisition. This is distinct from implicit learning, which refers more broadly to learning without conscious awareness across various systems, and from statistical learning, which concerns the extraction of distributional regularities from input. Although these three constructs are related and can interact, we focus here on procedural learning because of its proposed role in supporting the processing of sequential, combinatorial rules that characterise grammar.

Cross-linguistic research has significantly advanced our understanding of the impairment in FW categories in DLD, particularly regarding the debate over their universality versus language-specificity. Universalist theories attribute DLD to underlying cognitive or neurodevelopmental constraints that operate independently of language typology, predicting consistent grammatical impairment across languages. Evidence supporting this view includes the presence of gender and number agreement errors in both nominal and verbal contexts across diverse linguistic systems, pointing to broader impairments in morphosyntactic processing27,28. In contrast, language-specific theories argue that the manifestation of DLD is shaped by the structural properties of individual languages. According to this view, morphological markers such as gender, number, person agreement vary in difficulty depending on system complexity. For example, the opaque and rich inflectional morphology in French may impose greater processing demands on children with DLD than languages with more transparent morphology, leading to more pronounced impairment in FW use. Overall, cross-linguistic research highlights that FWs vary in both prominence and complexity across languages, shaping how grammatical impairments manifest in children with DLD. For instance, articles are a common FW in languages such as Italian, French, Spanish, and Swedish, and they often pose challenges for children with DLD29–33. Subject pronouns, which are particularly salient in Spanish and French, have also been shown to present persistent difficulties in these populations25,34–36 Notably, unlike Spanish and other pro-drop Romance languages, French requires overt subject pronouns due to its less distinctive verbal inflectional system. Verb tense marking is another well-documented area of weakness in children with DLD acquiring Germanic languages, such as Dutch, German, Swedish, and English37–39 In contrast, European French (EF)-speaking children with DLD demonstrate relatively high accuracy in present tense verb inflections—especially in first-, second-, and third-person singular forms—comparable to that of younger, Mean Length of Utterance in words (MLU-w)-matched typically developing peers33,40. EF-speaking children with DLD may omit subject pronouns, resulting in telegraphic speech such as veux gâteau (want cookie) rather than the grammatically complete je/tu/il/elle veux/t un gâteau (I/you/he/she want a cookie). In languages with rich case systems, such as Hungarian and Finnish, case-marking errors are common among children with DLD41,42. Similarly, in morphologically rich languages like Hebrew and Hungarian, children with DLD tend to substitute grammatical morphemes rather than omit them entirely. One grammatical pattern that appears consistent across typologically diverse languages is the difficulty children with DLD have in producing pronominal forms associated with verb morphology, as opposed to nominal forms. This pattern has been documented in English, Italian, Spanish, Hebrew, French, and Cantonese38.

Within this cognitive and cross-linguistic context, FWs are strong candidates for investigating grammatical impairment in EF-speaking children with DLD for several compelling reasons. First, FWs are generally processed with minimal cognitive effort. Unlike lexical words—such as nouns, verbs, and adjectives—which require deliberate lexical retrieval, FWs are typically accessed through automatised linguistic routines. This makes them particularly sensitive to subtle cognitive or neurological disruptions. Second, FWs are among the most frequent and stable components in natural language. Their high frequency and consistent use across a wide range of contexts mean that even slight deviations—such as omissions, substitutions, or misplacements—can serve as sensitive markers of underlying grammatical impairments. Third, FWs play a crucial role in sentence structure and syntactic organisation, providing the scaffolding necessary for grammatical coherence.

This study employs corpus analysis to examine how children with and without DLD use function words (FWs). We examined six FW categories: articles, prepositions, demonstrative pronouns, subject pronouns, modal and auxiliary verbs. In addition to overall frequency, we analysed how 24 FWs are used in nominal and verbal contexts, and assess the accuracy of modal and auxiliary verb production. The goal was to determine whether FW use can serve as a reliable marker for distinguishing children with and without DLD. We hypothesised that children with DLD would experience greater difficulty with FWs, particularly in verbal contexts, compared with nominal ones. This prediction stems from the fact that the EF language has complex inflectional morphology, marked for grammatical features such as person, gender, and number—features that may impose a heavier cognitive load on children with DLD. Such demands could affect language processing at multiple stages, from auditory perception to speech production. This research also aims to shed light on potential underlying mechanisms like limited working memory, procedural and statistical learning, and to contribute to the broader cross-linguistic research on FW use in children with DLD.

Results

Chi-square analysis of function word use in DLD and non-DLD groups

To investigate group differences between DLD and Non-DLD children in their use of four FWs categories—articles, prepositions, demonstrative pronouns, and subject pronouns—separate chi-square tests were conducted. No significant differences were observed for articles, χ2(1, N = 3401) = 2.55, p = 0.11, or prepositions, χ2(1, N = 1416) = 1.59, p = 0.21. In contrast, significant differences emerged for demonstrative pronouns, χ2(1, N = 1634) = 10.20, p < 0.001, and subject pronouns, χ2(1, N = 2668) = 13.70, p < 0.001, with the non-DLD group exhibiting higher use in both categories. Log-linear regression analysis of function word use across categories in DLD and non-DLD children

To investigate group differences between DLD and Non-DLD children in their use of FWs across categories, log-linear regression analyses were performed. Four categories were examined in detail: articles (six types), prepositions (seven types), demonstrative pronouns (two types), and personal pronouns (six types). Across all cases, the models demonstrated an excellent fit to the data (McFadden’s pseudo-R2 = 1.00, ps < 0.001), indicating that they provided an accurate account of the observed distribution of FW use across categories.

A significant interaction emerged between article type and group, χ2(5) = 32.18, p < 0.001, indicating that the two groups differed in their use of articles. Children with DLD produced the indefinite article un (β = − 0.55, p < 0.001) and the elided form l’ (β = − 0.37, p = 0.007) significantly more frequently than their Non-DLD peers. These differences remained significant following Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.012). No significant group differences were found for the other articles (le, les, une). A significant main effect of preposition type was observed, χ2(6) = 410.28, p < 0.001, indicating differences in frequency of use among the prepositions. In contrast, neither the main effect of group nor the interaction between group and preposition type reached significance, χ2(1) = 2.55, p = 0.110; χ2(6) = 8.95, p = 0.177, even without Bonferroni correction. Thus, although overall use differed across prepositions (relative to the reference preposition à), these differences did not vary between the DLD and Non-DLD groups. Both groups used prepositions in a comparable manner: all six prepositions—dans, de, pour, avec, par, and sur—were produced significantly less frequently than the reference à (all ps < 0.001), a pattern consistent across groups. Figure 2b presents these results.

A significant interaction between demonstrative pronoun type and group was observed (β = 0.77, p < 0.001), indicating that the DLD and Non-DLD groups differed in their use of these pronouns. This interaction, which was the primary focus of analysis, remained highly significant following Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.025). Children with DLD produced the pronoun c’ more frequently (M = 566.00, SE = 23.79), whereas the Non-DLD group showed greater use of ça (M = 306.00, SE = 17.49). This pattern may suggest that ça is a less accessible or more challenging form for children with DLD. Figure 2c presents the estimated marginal means for each pronoun type.

A significant interaction was also found for personal pronouns, χ2(5) = 65.46, p < 0.001, indicating that the two groups differed in their frequency of pronoun use. The DLD group relied heavily on the first-person singular pronoun je (M = 259.00, SE = 16.09), which served as the reference category in the model, whereas the Non-DLD group employed a broader range of pronouns more frequently. Interaction terms for all other pronouns (tu, il, elle, on, ils) were significant at p < 0.001. Rate ratios for these interactions ranged from 1.83 (on) to 2.39 (il), indicating that the Non-DLD group used these pronouns at substantially higher rates than the DLD group. All interaction effects remained significant following Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.01). Figure 2d illustrates this pattern, highlighting the more restricted pronoun repertoire in the DLD group.

Frequency analysis of function word use in children vs. adult European French speakers

To assess whether the use of high- and low-frequency FWs in children with and without DLD is comparable to that of adult EF-speakers, frequency counts obtained from the present corpus analysis, and normalised per million words, were compared to those of the Lexique243, a reference adult EF corpus, also normalised per million words (Adults: Lexique2 counts: 140,000 words; Children without DLD: 20,470 words; Children with DLD: 30,013 words;

According to the Lexique2, the five most frequently used FWs per million words in adults were je, de, la, le, l’. In children without DLD, the five most frequently used FWs per million words were ce/c’, la, il, le, dans. Finally, in children with DLD, the five most frequently used FWs per million were ce/c’, la, le, il, je.

Regarding the high-frequency articles such as la, le, and un (e.g., la occurred 14,946 times in Lexique2), while both children with and without DLD also used these articles at high-frequency, those with DLD often showed a lower frequency of use than children without DLD. For instance, la appeared 30,737 times per million words in the speech of children without DLD but only 18,661 times per million words in the speech of children with DLD. Similar patterns were observed for other articles (le, les, une), indicating a general underuse of articles by children with DLD.

Conversely, the high-frequency prepositions such as de, à, and dans extensively used by adults (de: 25,221 occurrences), were less frequently used by both children with and without DLD; however, their frequency of use remained higher in children without DLD. For example, à appeared 10,262 times per million words in the speech of children without DLD versus 8,129 times per million words for children with DLD. Notably, the low-frequency FWs such as sur and par (sur: 2,520 occurrences in Lexique2), also less frequently used by children with and without DLD, showed a slight increase in use among children with DLD compared to those without DLD (e.g., sur: 933 with DLD vs. 684 without DLD).

A clear contrast between adults and children was observed in the use of demonstrative pronouns. The rather low-frequency pronoun ça in the adult corpus (8,934 occurrences) was more frequently used by children without DLD (14,952 per million words), but not by those with DLD (4,197 per million words). This discrepancy in frequency pattern is even more pronounced for ce/c’, which was used at high frequency by children without DLD (31,074 per million words) and by those with DLD (18,855 per million words), despite its low frequency in the adult corpus (5,219 occurrences).

Finally, subject pronouns showed some of the most robust group differences. For example, il, a highly frequent FW, appeared 29,741 times per million words in the speech of children without DLD and 12,128 times per million words among children with DLD for only 13,223 occurrences in adult speech. The first-person pronoun je was slightly more balanced (8,841 without DLD vs. 8,628 with DLD), possibly reflecting its early emergence and high communicative salience. Log-linear regression analysis of group, response accuracy, and interaction effects on modal and auxiliary verbs

The two models were fitted with group (DLD vs. Non-DLD), response accuracy (correct vs. incorrect), and their interaction as predictors with Bonferroni adjusted p-values setting the significance level at α = 0.010 (5 tests) for modal verbs and at α = ≈ 0.007 (7 tests) for Auxiliary verb. For modal verbs, there was a significant main effect of group (β = 0.52, SE = 0.08, z = 6.35, p < 0.001), with children without DLD demonstrating higher accuracy, and a significant main effect of response accuracy (β = − 1.79, SE = 0.17, z = − 10.36, p < 0.001). The group × accuracy interaction was also significant (β = 0.49, SE = 0.20, z = 2.38, p = 0.017), indicating that the accuracy gap between correct and incorrect responses was larger for children with DLD. For auxiliary verbs, the model likewise showed a significant main effect of group (β = 0.61, SE = 0.14, z = 4.21, p < 0.001) and of response accuracy (β = 0.51, SE = 0.15, z = 3.45, p = 0.007), as well as a significant group × accuracy interaction (β = − 0.41, SE = 0.19, z = − 2.17, p = 0.030). The interaction effect was stronger for modal verbs (p = 0.017) than for auxiliary verbs (p = 0.030), suggesting that children with DLD experience greater difficulty in producing modal verbs accurately. For modal verbs, errors were defined as omissions, whereas for auxiliary verbs, errors included both omissions and agreement errors. Log-linear regression analysis of past participle use for auxiliary verbs

A log-linear regression analysis examined past participle use for auxiliary verbs as a function of verb regularity (regular vs. irregular) and group (Non-DLD vs. DLD). Discussion

This study employs corpus analysis to examine the use of FWs in children with and without developmental language disorder (DLD). We analyse six FW categories—articles, prepositions, demonstrative pronouns, subject pronouns, and modal and auxiliary verbs—In addition, we examine the use of 24 individual FWs across nominal and verbal contexts and assess the accuracy of modal and auxiliary verb production. All EF-speaking children in the study, with and without DLD were matched on MLU-w, a key metric in cross-linguistic DLD research. The goal is to determine whether FW use can serve as a reliable marker for distinguishing children with and without DLD. We hypothesise that children with DLD will face more challenges with FWs, especially those associated with verbal contexts, compared to nominal ones. This prediction is based on the significant morphophonological complexity of EF, a Romance language rich with inflectional morphology. The intricate nature of French grammar presents several hurdles. Auxiliaries are marked for person, gender, and number; subject pronouns are obligatory. These pronouns are frequently contracted or phonologically merged with auxiliaries (e.g., j’ai from je ai, ‘I have’), which reduces their perceptual salience. Furthermore, French requires subject–verb agreement, a rule often signaled by subtle morphological cues (e.g., il est vs. ils sont, ‘he/it is’ vs. ‘they are’). Adding to this complexity, phonological processes like elision and liaison can blur the boundaries of FWs, and agreement markers for gender and number on articles and subject pronouns introduce additional layers of difficulty.

The most significant finding of this study is the clear contrast between FW categories: prepositions and articles did not reliably differentiate the groups, but all other categories did. The frequency of articles and prepositions in the spontaneous speech of children with and without DLD, as observed herein is consistent with data from the WordBank database44, an open-access repository of vocabulary development. This corpus includes data from 92,771 children aged 12 to 30 months across 42 languages, including 1,824 from EF-speaking children. According to caregiver reports, many of these children accurately produce articles and prepositions within this age range, mirroring the production of children with and without DLD in the present study. Moreover, the comparison of speech production between DLD and non-DLD children with that of the adult reference corpus Lexique2 reinforces the high-frequency use of the articles le and la across both groups of children. Conversely, the preposition dans was frequently produced only by children without DLD. These three FWs are structurally predictable in French, occurring in fixed syntactic position. These findings support the claim made by Ambridge45 regarding the influence of input regularity and perceptual salience in shaping early grammar. Consistent exposure to frequently occurring, positionally stable elements—such as articles and prepositions—provides young children with reliable distributional cues for segmenting speech and identifying early FWs. The fact that articles precede nouns and prepositions typically precede their complements in French makes these items particularly salient and accessible for early learners. Even before fully mastering their grammatical functions, children often produce such items as “filler syllables”, which serve as transitional forms46–48. Between the ages of 2 and 4, children begin to use articles and prepositions more accurately within structurally appropriate contexts49–51. By age 3, definite and indefinite articles are produced in approximately 80% of obligatory contexts. Furthermore, once children reach a MLU-w of 2.00, article use becomes nearly systematic, corresponding with a marked decline in omission errors. These developmental patterns underscore the importance of frequency, input structure, and cognitive salience in early grammar. The differential use of dans by children with and without DLD suggests that limitations in processing the preposition dans or input sensitivity may constrain access to less salient or context-dependent FWs. Regularly positioned and frequent words like articles and prepositions provide consistent statistical cues helping infants and young children segment speech and identify FWs. Early grammar is learned through exposure and pattern recognition, not just innate mechanisms45.

Another key finding is the reduced use of subject and demonstrative pronouns in EF-speaking children with DLD. This pattern suggests a potential delay or disruption in the acquisistion of these early pronouns, indicating that the difficulties observed are not simply the result of typical developmental variability but reflect underlying processing limitations. Both pronoun types are phonologically bound to rich inflectional morphology (person, gender, number), and their production requires not only morphosyntactic knowledge but also efficient phonological encoding and retrieval—processes often impaired in children with DLD. The data indicate that EF-speaking children with DLD have difficulty representing and storing verb-related information in the mental lexicon37,52–54, consistent with a bottom-up selective deficit. Unlike top-down learning, which moves from explicit to implicit knowledge, bottom-up learning progresses from implicit to explicit representation Limitations in this processing pathway may be linked to deficits in statistical learning55 particularly in handling linguistic entropy, which reduces the predictability of upcoming words and increases cognitive load during both comprehension and production.

Another important finding is that, although the non-DLD group produced more FWs overall, the DLD group showed a higher relative use of FWs, particularly those associated with nouns, whereas non-DLD children produced more FWs linked to verbs. This pattern suggests that, in EF —similar to English and other Germanic languages —FW categories related to verbs are particularly vulnerable in children with DLD. By contrast, studies in languages such as Icelandic and Turkish have reported more pronounced impairments in nouns-related FWs56,57, while research in Romance languages like Italian and Spanish has identified deficits across both verbal and nominal domains. These cross-linguistic differences indicate that the domains most affected by DLD are shaped by the morphological richness and structural complexity of the target language.

Although most research on FW use in children with DLD has centered on English58, cross-linguistic evidence shows substantial variation in whether nominal and/or verbal morphology is more impaired. In morphologically rich languages—including Hebrew, Turkish, Italian, Spanish, Polish, Cypriot Greek, Serbo-Croatian, Hungarian and French—the severity and distribution of DLD-related impairments often differ from patterns observed in morphologically simpler languages38. For instance, in French, children with DLD may struggle with the obligatory use of subject pronouns and gender agreement, reflecting challenges tied to the language’s inflectional complexity.

The present findings reinforce that FW categories involving verbal morphology—particularly modals and auxiliaries—pose a significant and persistent challenge for children with DLD. Accuracy comparisons revealed a marked disparity between groups, with the DLD group showing higher rates of both omission and agreement errors. These difficulties appear closely tied to the morphological features that are most problematic for this population, including person, number, and gender marking in subject–verb agreement. Log-linear regression analyses further confirmed the reduced use of third-person singular in children with DLD, consistently showing lower rate than their typically developing peers across both modal and auxiliary verbs. A notable asymmetry emerged in modal verb production: children with DLD had disproportionately greater difficulty using the pronoun elle compared to il, pointing to a specific vulnerability in gender marking within this syntactic context. For auxiliary verbs, although on, elle, and the elided form l’ were generally more difficult than il for both groups, there was no statistically significant interaction after strict Bonferroni correction. This reflects broader grammatical limitations rather than a unique deficit tied to these forms. Nevertheless, the consistently lower rate for phonologically reduced forms like l’ across both groups—and particularly within the DLD group—supports the view that processing subtle morphophonological cues is especially demanding. Reduced use with l’ are not random; rather, they reflect a deeper difficulty with fine-grained morphophonological sensitivity53,59,60, a skill that is critical for achieving language fluency.

Regarding the past participle use dataset, both verb regularity and group status independently influence their use. Consistent with previous research61, irregular verbs were produced less than regular verbs, likely due to their lower morphological predictability and greater memory demands. Participants with DLD showed reduced use overall compared to their non-DLD peers, suggesting a general morphological processing weakness rather than a selective deficit for irregular forms. The absence of a significant interaction between verb type and group indicates that the irregular-verb disadvantage was comparable across groups. These results suggest that while DLD is associated with broad morphological challenges, the cognitive load imposed by irregular forms is similarly taxing for both groups probably due to the young age of the participants. These patterns are likely rooted in more foundational cognitive limitations, such as a constrained phonological working memory41,62,63 or deficits in processing rapid, sequential auditory information64. Such limitations could disrupt the rapid integration of semantic and syntactic information from auditory input to speech production. This interpretation is strongly supported by the clinical poor performance of all children with DLD in this study on phonological working memory and rhythmic processing tasks. This finding is also consistent with prior research that has established a clear link between phonological working memory and syntactic development, even in very young children as 19, 25, and 31 months65. Similarly, studies by Delage and Frauenfelder66 have shown a positive correlation between working memory performance and the ability to comprehend and produce complex syntactic structures in typically developing children, further underscoring the critical role of working memory in syntactic proficiency.

This corpus analysis consistently shows that during the preschool years, children with DLD exhibit difficulties with verb morphology, a pattern that is not specific to French but is observed across typologically diverse languages such as Hebrew, German, and Spanish29,67–69. In contrast, children without DLD use auxiliaries at significantly higher rates, demonstrating a greater ease and automaticity in their use of modal and auxiliary verbs, particularly with third-person singular forms. These differences underscore the importance of a detailed examination of verbal morphology, especially within syntactic contexts involving gender distinctions or phonological elision. Conducting such fine-grained analyses is critical not only for identifying clinically meaningful markers of DLD in French but also for advancing our understanding of the disorder in other morphologically rich languages.

In summary, these findings are consistent with cognitive models of DLD that point to limited processing capacity as a key factor. Children with DLD have limitations in working memory41,63, as well as deficits in procedural learning, specifically in the rule-based sequence learning processes proposed to support grammar. While procedural, implicit, and statistical learning55 are sometimes used interchangeably in the literature, this theoretical framing allows us to connect the observed grammatical difficulties to a specific learning and memory system, while acknowledging that other forms of learning (e.g., implicit, statistical) may also contribute.

Deficits in procedural learning may hinder their ability to acquire and apply rule-based morphological processes. This is especially true when auxiliaries require the integration of multiple grammatical features, like person, gender, and number. Furthermore, impairments in statistical learning may reduce a child’s sensitivity to distributional patterns in language. This is particularly relevant for low-frequency or phonologically reduced forms like l’, which rely on implicitly tracking contextual probabilities. Ultimately, the difficulties children with DLD have with verbal morphology may be a result of broader language processing challenges. These challenges are often rooted in lower-level speech perception deficits, which make it difficult for them to segment the continuous flow of the auditory signal into discrete phonological units.

By analysing how children with DLD perform across different linguistic systems, it becomes possible to distinguish between universal deficits—those that appear consistently across languages—and language-specific manifestations shaped by the grammatical architecture of each language. Crucially, language-specific theories should not be seen as contradicting universalist accounts; rather, they provide a complementary perspective. Integrating cross-linguistic data enriches our understanding of DLD by demonstrating how the disorder is modulated by language-specific features. The present corpus data contribute to a growing body of evidence that supports a more nuanced view of the disorder—one that accounts for both shared cognitive-linguistic deficits and variability arising from the grammatical and morphological properties of individual languages. From a clinical standpoint, the results highlight FWs related to verbal morphology as valid linguistic markers of early grammatical difficulties in EF children with DLD. These findings reinforce the importance of developing diagnostic tools and intervention strategies that are sensitive to the structural characteristics of each language, thereby improving the precision and effectiveness of support for children with DLD.

Conclusions

This corpus study highlights the importance of fine-grained analyses in understanding the deficit of FW use in verbal contexts among EF-speaking children with DLD. The 24 FWs investigated, spanning six categories, exhibit diverse morphological features that tap into different cognitive process. The stronger effects observed in verbal morphology are consistent with cognitive models of DLD that emphasise constraints in working memory, procedural learning, and statistical learning. The results also support the view that DLD involves both universal cognitive-linguistic limitations and language-specific vulnerabilities shaped by grammatical complexity. By integrating these perspectives, the study not only refines theoretical models of language acquisition and impairment but also informs clinical approaches across typologically diverse languages.

Methods

Participants

Seventy-three EF-speaking DLD children (52 boys and 21 girls) aged 39–84 months (mean = 54 months, SD = 10.93) were recruited at the Hôpital de la Pitié Salpétrière (Paris, France). All children were previously independently diagnosed with DLD by a multidisciplinary team composed of speech-language pathologists, neuropsychologists, and paediatricians who used the same standardised assessment tests. To be included, children had to be a native monolingual EF speaker, have no hearing loss, no neurological disorder, and no motor problems in the oral, buccal, or facial area, have a non-verbal intellectual quotient equal or higher than 8570 attesting for the absence of intellectual disability. A score of -1.25 standard deviations (SD) below the mean or lower was used for all reception tests and expression tests, and for cognitive tasks, as recommended by CATALISE71,72. Receptive abilities were assessed using picture-pointing tasks involving words for vocabulary and sentences for morphosyntax. Expressive abilities were evaluated using a word repetition task for phonology, a picture-naming task for vocabulary, and a sentence completion task for morphosyntax. Cognitive abilities were tested using phonetic auditory perception, phonological working memory, and rhythmic tasks. All these subtests were administered using the same standardised French language examination scale for children aged 4 to 8 years, Nouvelles Epreuves pour l’Examen du Langage (N-EEL)73.

Seventy-six EF-speaking non DLD children (EF-non-DLD; 38 boys and 38 girls), aged 24–36 months (mean age = 30 months, SD = 5.97), were recruited from homes and nurseries in Paris, France. Inclusion criteria included being a native monolingual EF speaker, passing an audiometry screening test, and achieving a score within the normal range on an age-appropriate nonverbal cognitive test74. The socio-economic level (SES) of the participants’ family was evaluated using a standardised SES classification system75, which considered family income, parental education, and occupation. Half of the children came from families with a high socio-economic level, while the other half were from families with a low socio-economic level.

The corpus study

This corpus study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB Number 00000096) of the French National Institute of Health. This study was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations. All parents or legal guardians of the children involved in the study gave informed consent for their participation. Procedure

Corpus design provides a high degree of ecological validity77,78, complementing standardised assessments by offering fundamental insights into a child’s strengths and weaknesses in language skills. All words spoken by the children were transcribed by a trained research assistant using the CHAT format79–81, with another assistant reviewing the transcripts. Agreement between the two sets of transcripts was 95%. Utterances were defined as word combinations not separated by a pause or a change in intonation. Single words, routine expressions from songs, stories, or games, imitations of previous adult utterances, yes/no responses, and partially intelligible utterances were excluded from the analysis.

The corpus consisted of 14,053 spontaneous utterances, including 6,769 from EF-speaking children without DLD and 7,284 from EF-speaking children with DLD. All utterances were annotated for grammatical morphology, including agreement features specific to the EF language (gender, number, person, elision). Separate chi-square tests were initially performed for each function word (FW) category using Jamovi (version 1.6; jamovi.org) with a significance level set at α = 0.05. These tests were followed by a series of log-linear regressions to analyse the effects of different function word (FW) use across category, group (DLD vs Non-DLD) and their interaction on articles, prepositions, demonstrative and subject pronouns, modal and auxiliary verbs This approach directly tested for a group × category interaction. Additional analyses examined the use of these FW use in nominal and verbal contexts, as well as the accuracy of modal and auxiliary verb production. This is crucial for determining whether that EF-speaking children with DLD demonstrate a reduced frequency and accuracy of FWs in verbal morphology. To control for multiple comparisons in the family of chi-square tests, a Bonferroni correction was applied, adjusting the significance threshold.

The frequency of FW use across groups and a reference corpus (Lexique243) was also compared using Chi-square tests. To calculate FW frequencies per million words for each FW used by children with DLD (total words = 30,013) and children without DLD (total words = 20,470), the following formula was applied: frequency per million = (raw count/total words) × 1,000,000.

To examine group differences in the accuracy of modal and auxiliary verb use, logistic regression models were also performed. This method allowed for a more precise testing of group differences (DLD vs. non-DLD), while controlling for response accuracy (correct vs. incorrect) and for third person singular subject pronoun use (il, elle, on, l’).
