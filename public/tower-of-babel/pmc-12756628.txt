Protocol to isolate quiescent hepatic stellate cells in sufficient yield while maintaining cytokine responsiveness from aged female mice
Authors: Takao Seki, Sachiko Komazawa-Sakon, Yuichi Tsuchiya, Kyoko Fukuhara, Yasuhiro Nakano, Kenichi Ikejima, Katsuhide Okunishi, Hiroyasu Nakano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIsolation of primary hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) in sufficient quantities is technically challenging. We present a protocol for isolating quiescent HSCs from mice using Nycodenz density-gradient centrifugation. The procedure includes cervical dislocation, vena cava cannulation, liver perfusion, enzymatic digestion, and preparation for density separation. We then describe centrifugation, enrichment, washing, counting, and culture preparation. This protocol provides fresh HSCs suitable for diverse experimental applications.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Seki et al.1Subject areas: Cell biology, Cell culture, Cell isolation, Cell separation/fractionation, Model organisms, Stem cells

Before you begin

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the major storage site for vitamin A and play a central role in maintaining vitamin A homeostasis under physiological conditions.2,3 Following liver injury, however, HSCs become activated and transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts, which produce large amounts of extracellular matrix and thereby drive liver fibrosis. To elucidate the mechanisms by which cytokines and mechanical cues activate HSCs, it is essential to obtain sufficient numbers of cells in their quiescent state.

In practice, reproducibly isolating adequate quantities of fresh quiescent HSCs from murine livers is technically challenging. Consequently, immortalized human or mouse HSC lines are often used to study cytokine responsiveness and signaling pathways.4 However, there are many discrepancies between primary quiescent HSCs and immortalized lines, underscoring the need for robust methods to isolate fresh HSCs for physiologically relevant analyses.

Innovation

This protocol provides a reproducible and relatively straightforward method for isolating quiescent hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) from murine livers. It builds on established procedures that combine pronase/collagenase digestion with Nycodenz density-gradient centrifugation, while incorporating several key optimizations. These include the use of aged female mice, cervical dislocation, vena cava cannulation, liver perfusion, enzymatic digestion, and refined preparation for density-gradient separation. Together, these improvements markedly enhance cell yield while reliably preserving the responsiveness of isolated HSCs to profibrotic cytokines. This refined protocol thus offers a valuable platform for fibrosis research, narrowing the gap between in vitro studies using immortalized HSC lines and analyses that more accurately reflect the physiological behavior of primary HSCs.

Mouse preparation and housing

Timing: 7–14 days

1.Obtain female C57BL/6 mice aged ≧24 weeks (average body weight: 25-30 g).

Obtain female C57BL/6 mice aged ≧24 weeks (average body weight: 25-30 g).

Note: A typical yield is 0.5 × 106 to 1.0 × 106 viable HSCs per mouse, with a purity of 80–90%.

CRITICAL: Use only aged female mice. Aged male mice exhibit markedly reduced HSC isolation efficiency and purity due to lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, which interferes with density-gradient separation and compromises HSC yield (data not shown). Retired breeder females are a cost-effective choice for this protocol, as they meet the age requirement (≥24 weeks) and are typically available at a lower cost than younger, purpose-bred mice aged 8–12 weeks.

Step-by-step method details

This protocol builds on established methods for hepatocyte and HSC isolation but incorporates critical optimizations to improve yield, purity, and reproducibility. Key improvements include: (1) a rapid transition from anesthesia to perfusion to minimize needle displacement, (2) cost-effective filtration using commercial mesh materials, (3) optimized gentle processing to prevent premature HSC activation, and (4) specific adjustments for aged female mice to enhance vitamin A–dependent density separation.

CRITICAL: The perfusion and isolation steps (Steps 2-13) are adapted from established HSC isolation methods,5,6 with modifications in the enzymatic digestion process (Steps 12-13) designed to preserve both structural integrity and functional responsiveness. Parameters such as perfusion flow rate, enzyme concentrations, and timing have been optimized specifically to retain cytokine responsiveness.1,7

Expected outcomes

This protocol enables the isolation of HSCs that retain both morphological and functional features critical for fibrosis research.1,7 Freshly isolated cells displayed a characteristic star-shaped morphology with multiple lipid droplets visible under phase-contrast microscopy (Figure 5A). Gentle mechanical processing is essential: mild agitation preserved the quiescent phenotype, whereas excessive stress induced premature activation, marked by upregulation of α-SMA expression and an increased proportion of α-SMA-positive cells (Figures 5A and 5B).

Activation status-specific cytokine responsiveness was also observed. Quiescent HSCs responded robustly to TGF-β and FGF18, thereby inducing profibrotic genes (Col1a2, Acta2) and the proliferation marker Ccnd1, respectively (Figure 6A). In contrast, 5-day-cultured HSCs strongly elevated basal Col1a2 and Acta2 expression, but diminished TGF-β responsiveness. Notably, 5-day-cultured HSCs exhibited enhanced FGF18 responsiveness, based on the induction levels of Spp1 (freshly prepared HSCs vs. 5-day-cultured HSCs) (Figures 6A and 6B).

Freshly isolated HSCs maintained a quiescent phenotype for 24–48 h in culture before spontaneous activation begins.1 During this period, they expressed desmin with minimal α-SMA, making them suitable for experiments to test TGF-β responsiveness. For studies focusing on the quiescent state, assays should therefore be performed within 48 h of isolation.

Overall, this protocol provides access to HSCs with activation status-dependent responsiveness to TGF-β (quiescent) and FGF18 (activated), offering a valuable platform for investigating fibrosis progression, intercellular communication among HSC populations, and potential therapeutic interventions targeting stage-specific signaling pathways.

Limitations

Despite the protocol’s utility, several limitations should be noted. First, the protocol is optimized for relatively aged female mice (≧24 weeks old) but is not suitable for aged male mice or young mice. Attempts to isolate HSCs from aged males were unsuccessful, likely because lipid accumulation in hepatocytes interferes with Nycodenz gradient separation (data not shown). In addition, isolation from 8-12-week-old mice (average body weight: 20 g) yielded only about 2.0 × 105 cells per animal (data not shown). Retired breeder females are readily available and useful for isolating HSCs from wild-type mice, but obtaining gene-deficient mice at the required age is time-consuming and logistically challenging.

Second, because the isolated HSCs are derived from aged animals, we cannot exclude the possibility that aging itself affects cytokine responsiveness, even when cells are freshly prepared. Ideally, gene expression profiles should be compared between young and aged mice; however, the yield of HSCs from young mice is markedly lower, rendering such experiments laborious, although the comparisons remain feasible.

Third, the number of HSCs obtained from one mouse is approximately 5.0 × 105 - 1.0 × 106. This yield is sufficient for qPCR but not for biochemical analyses such as Western blotting. Increasing mechanical agitation might improve yield, but as shown in Figure 6A, excessive disruption leads to premature HSC activation, marked by α-SMA expression. To overcome this limitation, larger animals such as rats—originally the species for which the Nycodenz protocol was developed—could be used.8 Alternatively, induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells may be differentiated into HSC-like cells. Several groups have reported encouraging results using this approach,9,10 but it remains unclear whether iPS-derived HSCs faithfully replicate the gene expression profiles of primary HSCs.

Fourth, many laboratories employ immortalized murine or human HSC lines. Our in-house immortalized HSCs retain responsiveness to FGF18 and possibly other cytokines but show no response to TGF-β (Figure 6C). The widely used human HSC line LX-24 exhibits only weak responsiveness to TGF-β and no response to FGF18 (Figure 6D). While immortalized lines are easy to maintain and suitable for repeated experiments, results obtained with these cells must be interpreted with caution.
