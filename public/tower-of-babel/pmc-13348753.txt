Primary care among newborns at a Canadian urban center: an observational study
Authors: Sloane Jaye Freeman, Rosane Nisenbaum, Thivia Jegathesan, Kavin Qiu, Michael D Sgro
Published: 2026

Background

Canada is currently facing a primary healthcare crisis. Challenges such as physician shortages and long wait times contribute to the lack of primary care among Canadian children [1, 2]. These issues are especially important for young children who require a primary care provider (PCP) to monitor growth, address physical and mental health needs, and to provide preventative healthcare, including immunizations.

The 2023 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth reported on 69,522 children and youth and found that 8.1% did not have a regular healthcare provider [2]. This number varied by province with a range between 4.1% in Ontario and 22.3% in Prince Edward Island [2]. The COVID-19 pandemic placed additional burdens on an already taxed primary healthcare system with one in five family physicians planning to close their practices within the next five years [3]. Research has shown that Canadian children who live in areas with fewer primary care providers were more likely to have emergency department visits [1]. Young children aged 2–9 years were the age group most likely to use the emergency department for minor medical conditions when they experienced barriers to primary care [4–6]. Accessible primary care has also been shown to associate with fewer emergency department visits and hospitalizations among children in the US [7].

Additionally, research has shown that socioeconomic status is an important predictor of healthcare access among children [8]. A report from Manitoba’s Centre for Health Policy and Evaluation demonstrated that children from low-income neighbourhoods had lower rates of continuity of provider care and immunizations compared to those from high-income neighbourhoods, suggesting barriers to primary care [9].

Newborns are a vulnerable population who require medical visits within the first days of life to monitor weight, assess for hyperbilirubinemia, and screen for congenital problems. Newborns also require regular visits to ensure adequate physical and cognitive development. The Canadian Paediatric Society and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend that newborn discharge plans should include identification of a PCP and assessment within 72 h after discharge [10, 11]. Newborns who had their first medical visit within 72 h from hospital discharge (which is typically the case for newborns with primary care) had a 15% reduction in hospital readmission rates compared to those without early primary care visits [12]. US studies have shown that almost half of neonates presenting to urban tertiary care emergency departments were for non-acute problems, which could have been managed in a primary care setting [13, 14]. There is limited literature on primary care access for Canadian children, and even less is known about the prevalence of primary care access for newborns in Canada.

Given the primary care crisis affecting Canadian children and the lack of data on primary care access among newborns we sought to estimate the prevalence of newborns without primary care, and the association between lack of primary care and sociodemographic factors at a large Canadian urban hospital.

Methods

Setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study from June 2023 to May 2024, at a large urban academic hospital in Toronto, Ontario, affiliated with the University of Toronto, with a birth rate of 2,557 births per year [15]. The hospital has a Level 2 academic neonatal intensive care unit and a large outpatient pediatric program serving a diverse urban population with high social risk factors including families experiencing homelessness, substance use disorders, and newcomers [16–18]. This study received approval from the Unity Health Toronto Research Ethics Board (Reference No. 25–053).

Participants

Caregivers of newborns (defined as parents or guardians) were approached by a research assistant on the post-partum ward and given the Primary Care Access for Newborns Survey after obtaining informed consent. Inclusion criteria were parents of newborns up to one week of age, who had delivered by either vaginal delivery or Cesarean section. We excluded caregivers of newborns who self-identified as tourists or visitors, and those of newborns who were admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for > 48 h, as hospital admission may delay the arrangement of primary care after discharge.

Survey

The Primary Care Access for Newborns survey was comprised of 15 open- and closed-ended questions and was administered by a trained research assistant in person on the postpartum ward using a paper copy of the survey. The survey asked whether or not caregivers had a PCP arranged for their newborn, type of PCP (including family physicians, pediatricians, or midwifery), as well as caregivers’ sociodemographic characteristics. The survey was not validated or pilot-tested before use in the study. However, questions relating to sociodemographic characteristics were adapted from the REACH School Network sociodemographic data collection tool, which has been published and used in similar study populations by our institution [19–21]. Questions pertaining to race or ethnicity were based on standardized closed-ended geographically derived categories that have shown good agreement with widely used open-ended ethnicity questions [22]. Among caregivers who reported that they did not have a PCP for their newborns, we collected data on the reasons for not having primary care access.

Data collection and analysis

All survey data was transcribed by the research assistant from paper to Qualtrics [24]. For caregivers who did not feel comfortable reading and responding in English, the research assistant read the survey questions to the hospital’s virtual interpretation service, Voyce [23], and participant answers were interpreted by Voyce and transcribed by the research assistant. Mothers’ and neonates’ Ontario Health Insurance Plan numbers were also collected from medical records for future data linkage with administrative databases at the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES). ICES is an independent research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act permits it to collect and analyze healthcare and sociodemographic data for health system evaluation and development without individual patient consent [25]. Because of twins in the sample, the prevalence of newborns without a PCP with 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated using generalized estimating with a logit link. For determining the association between lack of a PCP among newborns and caregivers’ sociodemographic characteristics, we considered only one child per caregiver. A logistic regression model estimated unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (OR)s and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and statistical significance was defined if p-values were < 0.05.

Results

We approached a total of 836 caregivers and 657 (78.6% response rate) provided consent and completed the survey. However, one participant was excluded because they were missing data on whether or not they had primary care for their newborn, resulting in a final sample size of 656 caregivers. Because of three pairs of twin births, the total number of newborns was 659. Of these, 202 did not have access to a PCP, resulting in a prevalence of 30.6% (95% CI 27.2% − 34.3%).

Among the 201 caregivers asked about the reason for not having primary care for their newborns (one had twins), 116 (57.7%) indicated they could not find a PCP, the PCP contacted was not accepting new patients, or they were on a waitlist. Other reasons included: caregivers not yet attempting to find a PCP (28 or 13.9%), actively searching for a PCP (16 or 8.0%), being told to wait to find a PCP (16 or 8.0%), other reasons (15 or 7.5%), having a caregiver or sibling with a PCP, who had not yet confirmed whether they would accept the newborn (8 or 4%), or the PCP having retired or closed their practice (2 or 1.0%). Among 455 caregivers who reported having primary care for their newborns, 268 (58.9%) identified a family physician, 125 (27.5%) a pediatrician, 34 (7.5%) a midwife, 19 (4.2%) identified more than one provider, 6 (1.3%) indicated a nurse or another type of provider, and 3 (0.7%) did not provide information. Discussion

This survey of 656 caregivers identified 30.6% newborns without a PCP before hospital discharge. The majority of families reported either that they could not find a PCP, the PCP was not accepting new patients, or they were on a waitlist. Sociodemographic factors of caregivers, including not having a PCP for themselves, not owning a home, and being born outside of Canada, were associated with lack of access to primary care for newborns.

The proportion of newborns without primary care in our study was greater than that reported for children residing in Ontario and for all of Canada, according to the 2023 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth [2, 26]. Reasons for not having a regular healthcare provider on the Canadian-wide survey were simliar to the reasons reported among our cohort, including not being able to find a PCP, and being on a waitlist, indicating that PCP supply and availability are important factors contributing to lack of access [2]. Ontario data has also shown that newborns residing in counties with the lowest supply of PCPs had a higher proportion of newborns without a follow-up primary care visit after discharge from birth, compared to those residing in areas with a high supply of PCPs [1]. A systematic literature review from Australia examining barriers to primary care access for newborns of migrant families, demonstrated that an appropriate physician supply was among the most significant barriers [27]. Similarly, low provider supply contributed to limited access to effective primary care among adults in Canada and the US [28, 29].

Caregivers who lacked a PCP for themselves were more likley to have newborns without a PCP. Given the primary care crisis among Canadian adults [26], it is not surprising that those without primary care for themselves would also face barriers securing primary care for their newborns. Similar to our findings, the 2023 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth reported that a higher proportion of children living in lower income households (10.7%) lacked a regular healthcare provider compared to those living in higher income households (6.4%) [2]. However, in contrast to our finding that parental educational attainment was not associated with lack of primary care, the 2023 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth reported that a higher proportion of parents with lower educational attainment (high school diploma or equivalent or lower) had children without a regular healthcare provider (12.4%) compared to parents with higher educational attainment (Bachelor’s degree or higher) (7.6%) [2]. The high percentage of non-Canadian-born caregivers in our sample may help explain why parental educational attainment was not associated with lack of primary care among newborns. Our findings showed that 68.5% of caregivers identified as being born outside of Canada, and 70.2% of these caregivers reported being in the country ≤ 10 years. Immigrants to Canada often arrive with high educational attainment due to the self-selection process for immigration [30, 31]. However, educational attainment outside of Canada among immigrants does not necessarily translate to better healthcare access, due to persistent barriers including language and cultural differences, and socioeconomic challenges [32]. Similar to the 2015-16 Canadian Community Health Survey, and the 2023 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth which found that new immigrants were less likely to have regular healthcare compared to established immigrants [2, 33], we found that the odds of not having a PCP for newborns was 2.4 times higher for recent immigrants (≤ 10 years) compared to established immigrants (> 10 years) (Table 2). Recent immigrants may lack language proficiency and socioeonomic stability compared to established imimgrants, rendering them less likely to find a PCP for their newborn.

This study had some limitations. First, our study was conducted at a single urban centre and may not be generalizable to other regions with differing socio-economic contexts. For example, the proportion of children without a regular healthcare provider varies across provinces and neighbourhoods [2]. Second, data was obtained through caregiver report and is therefore subject to recall and social desirabiltiy bias [34]. Third, we had missing data. For example 26% of paritipants were missing household-income data which may be related to social desireabiltiy bias and may therefore limit the generalizability of our findings [34]. Additionally, the survey was not pilot-tested and portions of it were not validated.

Conclusion

This study found that nearly one third of newborns at a large Canadian urban centre did not have a PCP. Sociodemographic circumstances of caregivers were associated with lack of access to primary care. Given the primary care crisis in Canada, it is essential to understand the lack of access to primary care among newborns; an understudied yet vulnerable population. Our next steps will be to link survey data to administrative provincial datasets at ICES to evaluate the association between lack of access to primary care and emergency department visits and hospital admissions within the first year of life.
