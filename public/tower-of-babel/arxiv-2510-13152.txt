The Dependency of Bar Formation Timescale on Disk Mass Fraction, Toomre Q Q , and Scale Height
Bin-Hui Chen, Juntai Shen

Abstract
Bars are one of the most prominent galactic structures. The classical swing-amplification theory can qualitatively describe the spontaneous bar instability of stellar disks. Still, it cannot quantify the bar formation process or explain why some disk galaxies do not have a bar. Recent studies found that the bar formation timescale depends exponentially on the disk mass fraction of the host galaxy (dubbed as “Fujii relation”), but they only explored a limited parameter space, where the physical effects of Toomre Q Q (local disk stability parameter) and disk scale height of the host galaxies are not fully explored. In this work, we check the robustness of the Fujii relation in a higher-dimensional parameter space of disk mass fraction, Toomre Q Q , and scale height. We find that the Fujii relation holds for disk galaxies with physically reasonable Toomre Q Q and scale height. Furthermore, the bar formation timescale also approximately linearly depends on both Toomre Q Q and scale height, with a more prolonged bar formation in a hotter or thicker disk. We propose an empirical relation to combine the dependency of the bar formation timescale on the three parameters. Based on the empirical relation and recent observations, we estimate that the bar formation timescale in pure stellar disks ranges from 0.20 − 0.06 + 0.09 ​ Gyr 0.20_{-0.06}^{+0.09}\ \mathrm{Gyr} to 12.20 − 2.80 + 3.37 ​ Gyr 12.20_{-2.80}^{+3.37}\ \mathrm{Gyr} or even significantly beyond the Hubble timescale in some extreme cases.

I
Introduction

As one of the most prominent galactic structures, bar exists in about 2/3 of disk galaxies
(
P. B. Eskridge et al. 2000
;
K. Menéndez-Delmestre et al. 2007
;
F. D. Barazza et al. 2008
;
K. Sheth et al. 2012
;
B. D. Simmons et al. 2014
;
P. Erwin 2018
;
Y. H. Lee et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. These barred disk galaxies constitute a main branch of the Hubble Sequence
(
E. P. Hubble 1926
;
G. de Vaucouleurs 1959
)
. Thus, thoroughly understanding the formation and evolution mechanisms of bar structure is a crucial step in unraveling the origin of the Hubble Sequence, which is one of the most important problems in modern astronomy. As one of the main contributors to the host galaxy’s central potential, bar structure has an essential impact on its host galaxy
(
F. Hohl 1971
;
D. Lynden-Bell & A. J. Kalnajs 1972
;
F. Combes et al. 1990
;
E. Athanassoula 1992
;
J. Kormendy & J. Kennicutt 2004
;
E. Athanassoula 2005
;
I. Minchev & B. Famaey 2010
;
M. Portail et al. 2015
;
Z. Li et al. 2016
;
Z. Li et al. 2017
;
F. Fragkoudi et al. 2019
;
D. A. Gadotti et al. 2020
;
A. Saintonge & B. Catinella 2022
;
J. Neumann et al. 2024
;
B.-H. Chen et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, therefore, thoroughly understanding the formation and evolution mechanisms of bars is also invaluable in studying galactic physics.

The prevailing theoretical framework for bar formation is the swing-amplification theory (see
J. Binney 2020
for a comprehensive introduction). By solving the linearized equations for a razor-thin stellar disk,
A. Toomre 1981
extended the Lin–Shu density wave theory
(
C. C. Lin & F. H. Shu 1964
)
beyond the tightly wound approximation. In this picture, bars form via a bar instability: transient spiral arms, seeded by noise spectrum, are amplified into trailing spirals through swing-amplification driven by the disk’s differential rotation. These arms are subsequently inverted—likely via reflection at the Lindblad resonances or refraction near the galactic center
(
J. Binney & S. Tremaine 2008
;
J. A. Sellwood & K. L. Masters 2022
)
—to enter the next swing-amplification, thus establishing a feedback loop. The resulting amplification resembles the resonance in a radio feedback circuit, ultimately leading to the formation of a central bar as the amplified spirals collapse under self-gravity. Alternative mechanisms, such as the groove mode
(
J. A. Sellwood & D. N. C. Lin 1989
;
J. A. Sellwood & F. D. Kahn 1991
)
, have also been proposed. However, the origin of the initial spiral arms is generally attributed to the local dynamical instabilities of the stellar disk, often characterized by Toomre’s
Q
parameter
(
A. Toomre 1964
)
:

Q(R)=\dfrac{\sigma_{R}(R)\kappa(R)}{3.36G\Sigma(R)}.

(1)

Bar formation is possible in disks with
1.0\lesssim Q\lesssim 2.0
. During the swing-amplification phase, the bar strength,

A_{2}\equiv\left|\dfrac{\sum_{j}m_{j}\exp(2i\phi_{j})}{\sum_{j}m_{j}}\right|,

(2)

exhibits exponential growth with time, similar to the evolution of an unstable mode
(
J. Binney 2020
;
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2023
)
.

However, the swing-amplification theory can only qualitatively describe the bar formation process in an ideal situation. It cannot quantify the bar formation process in real disk galaxies or explain why there are some unbarred disk galaxies
(
D. Zakharova et al. 2023
;
J. A. Sellwood & R. G. Carlberg 2023
)
such as the M33
(
J. A. Sellwood et al. 2019
)
. Therefore, quantitative studies of bar formation mechanisms beyond the scope of swing-amplification theory frequently employ numerical simulations, which have shed more light on the bar formation mechanism. For example, many
N
-body simulations have verified a bar can form through the bar instability even in a disk with finite thickness
(
F. Combes & R. H. Sanders 1981
;
G. Efstathiou et al. 1982
;
F. Combes et al. 1990
;
N. Raha et al. 1991
;
J. Shen et al. 2010
, e.g.,)
;
E. Athanassoula 2002
found that an initially dominant dark matter (DM) halo strongly delays the onset of the bar instability.

Recently,
M. S. Fujii et al. 2018
found that in
N
-body simulations, the bar formation is somewhat inevitable for disk galaxies in an isolated environment, where the bar formation timescale and age of a disk galaxy determine the galaxy’s superficial barred or unbarred appearance. By defining the bar formation timescale
t_{\mathrm{bar}}
as the first time when
A_{\mathrm{2,max}}\equiv\max\{A_{2}\text{ among radial bins}\}
exceeds 0.2, they found in their models that
t_{\mathrm{bar}}
exponentially depends on the disk mass fraction

f_{\mathrm{disk}}\equiv\dfrac{V_{\mathrm{c,disk}}^{2}}{V_{\mathrm{c,tot}}^{2}}\bigg|_{R=2.2R_{\mathrm{d}}}

(3)

(
R_{\mathrm{d}}
is the scale length of the disk), in the form

t_{\mathrm{bar}}/\mathrm{Gyr}=(0.146\pm 0.079)\exp\left[(1.38\pm 0.17)/f_{\mathrm{disk}}\right].

(4)

Later,
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2023
proposed a more physical definition of the bar formation timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
as

A_{2}(t)=A_{2}(0)\cdot\displaystyle{\exp(\frac{t}{\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}})},

(5)

employing
A_{2}
’s exponential growth nature
(
J. Binney 2020
)
.
10
verified the exponential relation between
t_{\mathrm{bar}}
and the
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
. They named it the “Fujii relation”.
10
further proposed an alternative form of the Fujii relation that can match their “high mass” models:

\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}=\tau_{\mathrm{H}}\cdot\exp\left[-13.0\left(f_{\text{disk }}-0.328\right)\right],

(6)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{H}}
is the Hubble timescale. Besides the Fujii relation, they found that the bar formation timescale also depends on the DM halo mass or gas fraction: A greater halo mass, associated with a higher halo concentration in their models, or a greater gas fraction can delay the bar formation. The physical effect of gas fraction on bar formation is further explored later in
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2024
;
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2025
.

20
and
10
revealed that the bar formation timescale in disk galaxies depends on the disk mass fraction. However, their studies only explored a limited parameter space with few values of Toomre
Q
and scale height
h_{z}
.
Q
and
h_{z}
are also important for bar formation: Theoretically,
h_{z}
will affect the applicability of the razor-thin disk assumption in the swing-amplification theory; Toomre
Q
determines the formation rates of transient spiral arms, which are necessary for the bar instability. Besides, studies both in simulations and observations showed that a hotter disk (with a greater Toomre
Q
) will delay the bar formation
(
E. Athanassoula & J. A. Sellwood 1986
;
K. Sheth et al. 2012
;
T. Worrakitpoonpon 2025
)
. Thus, whether the Fujii relation holds in greater parameter space and how
Q
and
h_{z}
affect bar formation timescale still require further exploration.

In this study, we construct several
N
-body models of disk galaxies with different physically reasonable
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
,
Q
, and
h_{z}
(in this paper, we always refer to
Q|_{R=2.2R_{\mathrm{d}}}
and the exponential scale height). We check the robustness of the Fujii relation in these models and further explore the dependency of the bar formation timescale on
Q
and
h_{z}
. We find that the Fujii relation holds in the explored parameter space at fixed
Q
and
h_{z}
, and the bar formation timescale also linearly depends on
Q
and
h_{z}
. Combining these dependencies, we propose an empirical relation between
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
and
(f_{\mathrm{disk}},\ Q,\ h_{z})
. With this empirical relation and recent observations, we estimate the range of the bar formation timescale in pure stellar disks.

Model Setup

We set up the models using the
AGAMA
package
(
E. Vasiliev 2019
)
. Each model contains a DM halo and a stellar disk. The DM halo has an Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile
(
J. F. Navarro et al. 1996
)

\rho_{\mathrm{NFW}}(r)=\rho_{0}(\frac{r}{a})^{-1}(1+\frac{r}{a})^{-2}\times\exp{[-(\frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{c}}})^{2}]},

(7)

where
\rho_{0}
is a normalization constant determined by the total mass of the DM halo. The stellar disk has a quasi-isothermal distribution function
(
E. Vasiliev 2019
)

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{J})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\tilde{\Sigma}\Omega}{2\pi^{2}\kappa^{2}}\times\dfrac{\kappa}{\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\kappa J_{R}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{2}}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\times\dfrac{\nu}{\tilde{\sigma}_{z}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\nu J_{z}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{z}^{2}}\right)\times\left.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }J_{\phi}\geq 0,\\
\exp\left(\dfrac{2\Omega J_{\phi}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{2}}\right)&\text{if }J_{\phi}<0,\end{array}\right.\right.

where
\kappa
/
\nu/\Omega
is the epicycle/vertical/circular frequency,
J_{R},\ J_{\varphi},\ \ J_{z}
are the actions, and

\begin{split}\tilde{\Sigma}(R)&=\Sigma_{0}\exp{(-R/R_{\mathrm{d}})},\\
\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{2}(R)&=\sigma_{R,0}^{2}\exp{(-2R/R_{\sigma_{R}})},\\
\tilde{\sigma}_{z}^{2}(R)&=2h_{z}^{2}\nu^{2}(R).\end{split}

(9)

More details of the model setup are available in
E. Vasiliev 2019
and
T. Tepper-Garcia et al. 2021
.

Because
AGAMA
uses an iteration strategy to make the model get into equilibrium, the parameters of the created
N
-body model may slightly deviate from the specified parameters
(
E. Vasiliev 2019
)
. Thus, to get a model with the required parameters, we check the actual parameters of each created
N
-body model and modify its specified parameters until the model’s actual parameters reach the required values. The steps are as follows:

1.

Construct a fiducial model and measure its
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
,
Q
and
h_{z}
.

2.

Based on the required values of
(f_{\mathrm{disk}},\ Q,\ h_{z})
and the gotten values, we fine-tune the following three specified parameters: first modify the disk mass to match the required
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
, then modify
\sigma_{R,0}
to match the required
Q
and finally modify the disk scale height to match the required
h_{z}
.

3.

Construct the fine-tuned model, and measure its
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
,
Q
and
h_{z}
.

4.

Repeat the above two steps until the
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
,
Q
, and
h_{z}
of the created model converge to the desired values within some tolerances.

Using a semi-automated program implementing the above steps, we construct 693
N
-body models with
f_{\mathrm{disk}}=
0.30, 0.35, …, 0.80 (11 values);
Q=
0.8, 1.0, …, 2.0 (7 values);
h_{z}/\mathrm{kpc}=
0.2, 0.3, …, 1.0 (9 values). The convergent tolerances are
\epsilon(f_{\mathrm{disk}})=0.025
,
\epsilon(Q)=0.1
, and
\epsilon(h_{z})=0.05\ \mathrm{kpc}
. Table
1
lists other parameters of these models. The disk scale length is fixed, so
h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}}
ranges from
6.7\%
to
33.3\%
in the models. To facilitate the exploration of the great parameter space, we adopt a slightly low particle resolution in both the disk and halo, which may introduce collisional relaxation/heating
(
J. A. Sellwood 2013
;
A. D. Ludlow et al. 2021
;
M. J. Wilkinson et al. 2023
)
. In the Appendix
A
, we verify that our main results on bar formation timescales are robust when the particle resolution is doubled.

Note that the initial condition of the models includes no bulges. Though bulges are also one of the most prominent structures in disk galaxies, most bulges emerge from the formed bar
(
J. Shen & X.-W. Zheng 2020
)
, so they might not exist in the precursor disks. Other spheroidal classical bulges are dynamically similar to a compact DM halo, which reduces
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
by increasing the central mass concentration of the non-disk ingredient. However, as the mass of classical bulge in Milky Way–like disk galaxies is
\lesssim 10\%
of the disk
(
J. Shen et al. 2010
)
, its impact on bar formation is not significant, so we neglect it to simplify the models.

We use
GADGET4
code
(
V. Springel et al. 2021
)
to evolve the created

-body models. Each model is evolved for an adaptive time ranging from
8\ \mathrm{Gyr}
to

III.2
Empirical relation for
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
,
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
,
Q
and
h_{z}

The above results show that
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
depends exponentially on
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
and approximately linearly on
Q
and
h_{z}
. We find that these trends are unaffected if we replace the exponential bar formation timescale in Equation
5
with
t_{\mathrm{bar}}
as the first time when
A_{2}
first exceeds some threshold value, as done by
20
. The
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
varies slightly if we increase the softening length by a factor of 2, and by a factor of 7 it systematically increases about
20\%
as expected
(
J. A. Sellwood & E. Athanassoula 1986
)
. Still, the dependencies of
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
on
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
and
Q\times h_{z}
persist. Thus, these dependencies are physical results rather than numerical artifacts.

These dependencies can be combined as an empirical relation as follows:

\dfrac{\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}}{[T]}=AQ\dfrac{h_{z}}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}\exp(\dfrac{f_{\mathrm{disk}}}{B}),

(10)

where
[T]=\mathrm{1.0\ Gyr}
is the simulation’s unit of time and
R_{\mathrm{d}}=3.0\ \mathrm{kpc}
in all runs. We include
[T]
and use the dimensionless relative thickness
h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}}
rather than
h_{z}
, aiming to make the free parameters
A
and
B
dimensionless so that the above formula has a more general applicability. Using
scipy.optimize.curve_fit
again, we fit the distribution of
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
in the sample with the above formula and get

\dfrac{\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}}{1216\ \mathrm{Gyr}}=Q\dfrac{h_{z}}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}\exp(-\dfrac{f_{\mathrm{disk}}}{0.110}),

(11)

where the fitting results are
A=1216\pm 158
and
B=-0.110\pm 0.004
. Neglecting the fitting errors, we can equivalently write the above equation as

\tau_{\text{bar }}/\mathrm{Gyr}=Q\frac{h_{z}}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}\exp(-\frac{f_{\text{disk }}}{0.11}+7.1)
.

4
, we compare the measured
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}

. The left two panels show the distribution of
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
against

can quantitatively predict the variation of
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
in the three-dimensional parameter space of

III.3
Implication of the empirical relation

We provide a somewhat speculative explanation attempting to understand the above empirical relation.

The spontaneously formed bars originate from amplified spirals in initially unperturbed stellar disks, through the swing-amplification feedback loop of shearing sheets
(
J. Binney 2020
)
or a succession of groove instabilities
(
J. A. Sellwood & R. G. Carlberg 2019
)
.
10
found that the initial growth of the bar strength is in a nearly exponential fashion, similar to an unstable mode
(
J. Binney 2020
)
. Our models show similar initial exponential growth. Thus, we consider a bar grows in the form of Equation
5
, assuming that the bar strength grows by
g
during a characteristic timescale
\Delta t

1
1

1

For example, the period of one swing-amplification loop or the timescale of a single groove mode. In the discussion later,
\Delta t
can actually be defined as an infinitesimally short interval with no connection to any physical timescale. However, binding it to a physical timescale during bar formation is more natural.
at time
t
, namely

g=\dfrac{A_{2}(t+\Delta t)-A_{2}(t)}{A_{2}(t)}.

(12)

Substituting Equation
5
into it, we get

g=\exp(\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}})-1.

(13)

The spiral patterns rotate at a rate comparable to the local radial/epicycle frequency
\kappa

(
A. Toomre 1981
)
during bar formation. Thus we can estimate
\Delta t\sim 1/\kappa
, which is of the order of
0.01\ \mathrm{Gyr}
in the bar region where
\kappa\gtrsim 90\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,kpc^{-1}}
(extrapolated inward from the values for the Galactic outer disk reported by
J. R. D. Lépine et al. 2008
). This timescale is much shorter than the bar formation timescales we found, so we can approximate the above equation with its linear expansion

g=\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}}.

(14)

Thus

\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}=\dfrac{\Delta t}{g},

(15)

where
\Delta t
and
g
on the right-hand side may vary with time, but their ratio always equals
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
.

A. Toomre 1981
found that the logarithm of “maximum full-swing amplification factor” is tightly correlated with

X=\frac{\kappa^{2}R}{2\pi G\Sigma m}

(16)

(Equation 6.77 of
J. Binney & S. Tremaine 2008
), where for a Mestel disk (
V_{c}=\mathrm{constant}
)
X=1/f_{\mathrm{disk}}
for an
m=2
mode. As shown in Figure 7 of
A. Toomre 1981
, for a razor-thin disk model
(
T. A. Zang 1976
)
, the logarithm of the maximal growth factor is a sharply decreasing function of
X
when
X\gtrsim 1.25
(corresponding to
f_{\mathrm{disk}}\lesssim 0.8
). Inspired by their results, we speculate that in our models we may approximate
\ln g
as

\ln g\simeq\dfrac{C}{X}=Cf_{\mathrm{disk}},

(17)

for some positive constant
C
. The above form is no more than an ansatz, but it can help us understand how the bar formation timescale correlates with
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
.

The classical swing-amplification theory indicates that the characteristic timescale of a spiral pattern should also depend on
Q
and
h_{z}
. (1) A disk with a lower
Q
has a greater wavelength range of unstable density wave modes. Thus, a lower
Q
corresponds to a lower
\Delta t
. Notably, the classical theory forbids the spiral instability in a disk with
Q>1
, which is inconsistent with the results of
N
-body simulations in the literature. This reflects the necessity of modification on the classical Toomre
Q
criterion (e.g.,
M. Behrendt et al. 2015
;
K. George & R. Schönrich 2025
). However, a thorough discussion of this question is beyond the scope of this work. (2) Finite
h_{z}
breaks the razor-thin assumption of the classical theory, which changes the dynamical timescale (Equation 2.40 of
J. Binney & S. Tremaine 2008
) as

t_{\mathrm{dyn}}\simeq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho}}\propto\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi R^{2}h_{z}}{M}}.

(18)

This reflects that a thicker disk should have a longer
\Delta t
. Therefore,
\Delta t
should be some increasing function of both
Q
and
h_{z}
:

\Delta t=f\left(Q,\ \dfrac{h_{z}}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}\right).

(19)

Substituting Equations
17
and
19
into Equation
15
, the bar formation timescale should have a form as

\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}=f\left(Q,\ \dfrac{h_{z}}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}\right)\exp(-Cf_{\mathrm{disk}}),

(20)

which is consistent with Equation
11
. Since theorists are still struggling with an analytic theory of bar formation, we cannot rigorously derive the functional form of
f(Q,\ {h_{z}}/{R_{\mathrm{d}}})
. Our numerical experiments seem to prefer the linear form of
Q
and
h_{z}
,

\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}\propto Q\times\dfrac{h_{z}}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}\exp{(-Cf_{\mathrm{disk}})}.

(21)

In summary, the empirical relation may stem from two aspects: the growth rate depends exponentially on
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
when
f_{\mathrm{disk}}\lesssim 0.8
(consistent with
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2023
;
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2024
), and the disk’s hotness and thickness secondarily, but still significantly, increase the timescale of bar growth. In the presence of gas, the analysis for stellar disks may need to be modified
(
10
)
, as there are more complex physics
(
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2024
;
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2025
)
. We also refer interested readers to the detailed discussion in
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2025
about the JT66 shearing sheet approach
(
W. H. Julian & A. Toomre 1966
;
J. Binney 2020
)
.

III.4
Constraining
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}
in pure stellar disks

Equation
11
may help us to constrain the bar formation timescale of pure stellar disks, with presupposed ranges of
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
,
Q
, and
h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}}
: (1)
S. H. Price et al. 2021
presented a systematic analysis of 41 massive, large star-forming galaxies at cosmological redshift
0.67\lesssim z\lesssim 2.45
. Their disk mass fractions range from
0.12
to
0.67
and concentrate around
0.45
(see Figure 6 of
10
). We employ their reported range of
f_{\mathrm{disk}}
. (2) There are few direct observations on the
Q
of external galaxies, and we assume
Q
of disk galaxies ranges from
0.8
to
2.0
, covering values from unstable to stable regimes. (3) Some recent observations show that the scale height of disk galaxies in the Universe ranges approximately from
0.1\ \mathrm{kpc}
to
1\ \mathrm{kpc}

(
J. Lian & L. Luo 2024
;
N. Ranaivoharimina et al. 2024
;
T. Tsukui et al. 2025
)
, with the relative thickness
h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}}
ranging from
1/5
to
1/11
which is independent of redshift when
0.1<z<3.0

(
T. Tsukui et al. 2025
)
. With these ranges and Equation
11
, we estimate the range of the bar formation timescale in pure stellar disks, shown as gray shadows in Figure
4
. Among this range, some characteristic values are as follows: the fastest bar formation in the Universe has
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}=0.20_{-0.06}^{+0.09}\ \mathrm{Gyr}
for
(f_{\mathrm{disk}},\ Q,\ h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}})=(0.67,\ 0.8,\ 1/11)
, a milder bar formation has
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}=4.04_{-1.00}^{+1.24}\ \mathrm{Gyr}
for
(f_{\mathrm{disk}},\ Q,\ h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}})=(0.42,\ 1.2,\ 1/8)
, and a slow bar formation has a timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{bar}}=12.20_{-2.80}^{+3.37}\ \mathrm{Gyr}
closing to a Hubble timescale for
(f_{\mathrm{disk}},\ Q,\ h_{z}/R_{\mathrm{d}})=(0.35,\ 1.2,\ 1/5)
, consistent with
20
and
10
.

Note that in real disk galaxies, the presence of gas can further delay bar formation, with the degree of delay depending on the gas mass fraction (
10
;
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2024
;
J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2025
). Therefore, the preceding estimates should be regarded as the lower limit on the bar formation timescale in real disk galaxies.

IV
Summary

In this paper, we extend the Fujii relation of bar formation timescale in disk galaxies to a three-dimensional parameter space of
(f_{\mathrm{disk}},\ Q,\ h_{z})
, where the bar formation timescale also depends approximately linearly on
Q
and
h_{z}
. We proposed an empirical relation (Equation
11
) to characterize these dependencies, and found that such a relation can be motivated by the classical theory. With the recent observations, we estimate that the bar formation timescale for pure stellar disks in the Universe is about
0.20_{-0.06}^{+0.09}\ \mathrm{Gyr}
for the coldest and most massive disks,
4.04_{-1.00}^{+1.24}\ \mathrm{Gyr}
for disks with milder parameters, and close to or even exceeding a Hubble timescale for extremely light/hot/thick disks.
