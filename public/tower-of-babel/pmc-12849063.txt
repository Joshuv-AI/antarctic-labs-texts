Carbon mitigation effect of service trade innovation: quasi-experimental evidence from China
Authors: Yantuan Yu, Yutong Cai, Xuhui Huang, Zhenhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper examines the environmental impact of service trade innovation in the context of China’s dual-carbon goals. Leveraging the staggered difference-in-differences combined with double/debiased machine learning strategy, we identify the causal effect of the Service Trade Innovation and Development Pilot Policy on urban carbon emissions. Results show that the policy reduced emissions by an average of 8.9%. The carbon mitigation effect is more pronounced in coastal cities, those with more developed service sectors, and non-Two Control Zones. The examination of the fundamental mechanisms identifies four primary channels: the intensified enforcement of low-carbon policies, progress in green innovation, the expansion of regional market integration, and the improvement of urban trade networks. Spatial spillover analysis indicates significant carbon reductions within 0-100 km of pilot cities, but a rebound effect in the 100–500 km range, possibly due to resource agglomeration. These results underscore the environmental benefits associated with reforms in service trade and emphasize the necessity for regionally coordinated approaches to promote spatial equity in the implementation of low-carbon transition initiatives.JEL classificationO14; Q56; Q58; R11Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-025-00384-3.

Introduction

Achieving an equilibrium between sustained economic development and effective reduction of carbon emissions has emerged as a critical challenge for countries worldwide, particularly in the context of increasingly stringent global climate policies [30, 46]. Trade in services, as a fundamental component of the contemporary economy, is commonly recognized for its intrinsic “green potential”, attributed to its relatively low energy consumption, limited environmental externalities, and greater value-added production [32]. The accelerated expansion of emerging formats, including digital services and knowledge-intensive services, has significantly enhanced the indirect contribution of service trade in advancing industrial upgrading, supporting energy transition, and fostering environmental governance. These developments suggest that service trade is playing an increasingly pivotal role in facilitating green and low-carbon transitions. Despite its increasing importance, there is still a notable absence of systematic and rigorous empirical research examining whether institutional reforms in service trade can achieve substantial carbon mitigation effects.

The research on environmental economics has long focused on the connection between trade and environmental performance. Existing studies have shown how trade liberalization, particularly within the realms of aggregate or manufacturing trade, on pollution emissions. This influence operates through mechanisms including comparative advantage, the reallocation of production factors, and technological upgrading [1, 10, 11]. For instance, Cherniwchan [10] finds that trade liberalization significantly reduced air pollutants by increasing intermediate goods imports and inducing technological upgrading. On the contrary, using city- and firm-level data from China between 1998 and 2007, Zhang [58] shows that the environmental impacts of trade openness vary across different regions, providing evidence of “pollution haven” effects specifically in cities located in the western region.

In contrast, the environmental impacts associated with the trade of services have been comparatively less investigated. Previous studies generally emphasize the environmentally friendly nature of service trade, highlighting its lower resource intensity and pollution levels. Several empirical studies suggest that the growth of service trade has the potential to mitigate local environmental pollution [18] and increase carbon efficiency [17, 59]. However, more recent studies reveal structural and nonlinear effects between service trade and pollution emissions [51, 61]. Although the significance of service trade within global value chains and its potential role as a policy instrument for green transformation have been increasingly recognized, current research has not yet elucidated the institutional mechanisms through which reforms in service trade impact carbon emissions. Moreover, little is known about how these effects vary across space or whether regional spillovers amplify or attenuate the environmental effects.

This paper investigates the environmental effects of China’s Service Trade Innovation and Development Pilot Policy (STIDPP). This policy seeks to modernize the service trade system through the enhancement of regulatory frameworks, the expansion of market access, the promotion of digital service innovation, and the reinforcement of policy support for both service exports and imports. These institutional changes enable a more unrestricted movement of service-related inputs across different regions and provide a natural quasi-experimental design to examine how reforms in service trade affect urban carbon emissions. Based on a panel dataset of China’s 285 cities from 2006 to 2022, we empirically estimate the effect of STIDPP on urban carbon emissions using the staggered difference-in-differences (DID) and double/debiased machine learning (DDML) strategy. To assess the scope and intensity of policy spillover effects across different spatial intervals, we further develop a spatial DID model incorporating distance-based spatial weight matrices to capture potential spatial externalities.

Three major contributions are made in this study. First, it treats the STIDPP as an exogenous policy intervention and adopts a staggered DID design combined with a DDML approach to rigorously identify its causal effect on urban carbon emissions. This shifts the trade and environment literature from a focus on manufacturing trade to service trade and, within services, from aggregate openness to an institutional reform centered on innovation. Second, this paper systematically uncovers how institutional reforms in service trade impact carbon emissions by defining and testing four channels: strengthening low-carbon policy enforcement, promoting green innovation, enhancing regional market integration, and improving urban trade networks, thereby clarifying how liberalization in the service sector aligns with climate mitigation objectives. Third, this paper develops a spatial DID to trace distance-varying spillovers, distinguishing proximity-based and network-mediated diffusion from agglomeration-driven siphoning, which yields a structured assessment of externalities and informs regionally coordinated reform design. These contributions underscore the need to incorporate spatial interdependencies when designing and implementing service-trade liberalization with environmental implications.

Hypotheses

Direct effect

The service sector is typically less resource-intensive and has limited direct environmental impact [32]. Building on this sectoral feature, the STIDPP promotes digital trade, cross-border e-commerce, and service outsourcing, fostering new business models that accelerate structural upgrading from pollution-intensive industries toward service-oriented activities. The policy also encourages the servitization of manufacturing, improving resource allocation efficiency and lowering process-related emissions. In addition, by improving the urban business environment, the STIDPP helps attract high-quality foreign direct investment in clean energy and environmental services [35] and mobilizes domestic capital toward green sectors via green credit and sustainable finance instruments. By facilitating collaboration in services trade, it reduces the cost of green innovation and enables knowledge transfer in green technologies. Finally, green certification schemes, environmental monitoring systems, and big-data-driven regulatory innovations raise firms’ incentives to adopt low-carbon practices and shift consumer preferences toward environmentally friendly services. Given that, we propose:

Hypothesis 1

The STIDPP contributes to urban carbon mitigation.

Indirect effects

Low-carbon Policy Intensity. As a core dimension of environmental governance capacity, low-carbon policy intensity captures regulatory stringency and enforcement and is typically negatively correlated with emissions [28, 52]. The STIDPP can strengthen this intensity through a sequence of institutional reforms: (i) improving inter-departmental coordination to curb policy fragmentation and enhance implementation; (ii) aligning domestic rules with international low-carbon standards via openness and cooperation, thereby increasing effective stringency; (iii) easing factor mobility and providing resource support (talent, data, finance) to strengthen on-the-ground execution; and (iv) upgrading data-driven supervision to target high-carbon links and ensure compliance. In parallel, by dovetailing with low-carbon policy instruments, such as emission trading schemes, environmental tax reform, and clean-technology subsidies, the STIDPP operationalizes a dual track of institutional constraints and market incentives that steers cities toward lower emissions. Based on this analytical framework, we propose:

Hypothesis 2a

The STIDPP reduces carbon emissions by strengthening low-carbon policy intensity.

Green Innovation. Green innovation is a central driver of sustainable decarbonization [21, 56]. The STIDPP promotes green technology development through a synergy of fiscal incentives, knowledge spillovers, and regulatory pressures. First, firms in pilot cities gain access to fiscal subsidies and tax incentives, lowering R&D costs and raising innovation incentives [60]. Second, pilot regions attract advanced technologies and talent, facilitating technology exchange and accelerating the reallocation of innovation factors. Third, under the policy’s supportive environmental regulatory framework, firms are incentivized to adopt cleaner production processes and materials, improving efficiency and reducing emissions [36]. Given that, we propose:

Hypothesis 2b

The STIDPP reduces carbon emissions by enhancing green innovation.

Market Integration. Market integration reflects the degree of cross-regional openness and economic connectivity and is positively associated with carbon efficiency [26]. By establishing regional service-trade frameworks and reducing administrative fragmentation, the STIDPP facilitates cross-city cooperation. On the production side, deeper integration encourages interregional specialization, helping to eliminate redundant and high-carbon capacity [44]. Along supply chains, more unified service markets enhance technological spillovers, improving marginal abatement efficiency. Given that, we propose:

Hypothesis 2c

The STIDPP mitigates carbon emissions by promoting regional market integration.

Urban Trade Networks. Trade networks capture a city’s centrality and capacity to mobilize resources across space. The urban trade network is a key channel for local influence to spread globally, highlighting the deep interdependence among cities in the interconnected world [42]. Through initiatives such as digital trade platforms and regional service alliances, the STIDPP strengthens pilot cities’ network spillover effects. Internally, attracting high-end service firms turns pilot cities into hubs for clean production technologies, lowering carbon intensity via industrial upgrading. Externally, pilot cities act as knowledge-sharing nodes, diffusing green management practices and low-carbon business models to neighboring areas. Given that, we propose:

Hypothesis 2d

The STIDPP reduces carbon emissions by strengthening urban trade networks.

Spatial spillover effects

New Economic Geography emphasizes the interplay of agglomeration and diffusion in shaping spatial outcomes [31]. The STIDPP may transform pilot cities into hubs of activity and innovation, attracting talent, capital, and technology from surrounding regions. Such clustering supports local modernization and innovation and, via knowledge sharing and supply-chain linkages, can spread green technologies and management practices to adjacent cities, generating positive spatial spillovers that reduce emissions in adjacent areas.

As distances increase, the spatial effects can become mixed. Under the siphoning effect hypothesis, resource-rich and policy-privileged cities may attract high-quality factors away from neighboring non-pilot areas, weakening their environmental investment capacity and potentially raising emissions [39]. Moreover, industrial transfer theory suggests the STIDPP could accelerate the relocation of pollution-intensive, low-value-added activities from pilot to non-pilot cities. Where recipient cities lack adequate regulation or enforcement, such relocations may worsen local pollution and create negative spillovers [15, 57]. Conversely, if managed effectively, industrial transfers can also carry cleaner technologies and management practices, yielding positive spillovers. The overall sign, therefore, depends on geographic proximity and absorptive/regulatory capacity. Given that, we propose:

Hypothesis 3

The STIDPP has significant spatial spillover effects on urban carbon emissions, which may be positive or negative depending on geographic proximity and absorptive capacity.

For balanced panel data, when units receive treatment at different times, the distribution of the sample across relative treatment periods becomes unbalanced. There may be very few observations in relatively early or late relative periods. Consequently, researchers typically undertake preprocessing procedures on the data. In practice, there are two main methods: data binning and data trimming. Data binning involves setting an event window [-K, L], where the relative period of observations earlier than the event window (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l \leqslant - K$$\end{document}) is reset to period -K, and the relative period of observations later than the event window (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l \geqslant L$$\end{document}) is reset to period L. Data trimming also involves setting an event window, but differs in that samples outside the event window are deleted rather than binned. However, Sun & Abraham [45] demonstrate that consistency estimates of the binned event study approach will occur only when the relative periods binned together have an identical average treatment effect. Furthermore, an advantage of data trimming is that it does not require the imposition of supplementary assumptions regarding homogeneous treatment effects, as is necessary when employing data binning methods. Given that, we adopt the method of trimming data to conduct event analysis.

Spatial DID

Following Halleck Vega & Elhorst [24], we further use a spatial DID method to assess whether the STIDPP generates spillover effects on carbon emissions in non-treated neighboring cities. Specifically, a spatial weighting matrix is constructed utilizing the inverse of geographic (traffic) distance (calculated based on the latitude and longitude of various cities), which is then interacted with the treatment indicator to produce a spatially lagged policy variable. This term is incorporated into the regression framework to capture potential spillover effects of the STIDPP across adjacent cities.

By explicitly modelling the spatial diffusion of policy impacts, the SLX framework allows us to test whether policy benefits extend beyond treated units and whether untreated cities within a certain geographic range experience indirect carbon emission reduction. This methodology also serves to alleviate issues related to spatial autocorrelation and omitted variable bias, which could potentially distort the estimated treatment effect. Data and variables

Variable definitions

Dependent and key independent variables. We use the logarithm of city-level CO2 emissions (in tons) as the outcome variable. The original carbon information is collected from the European Union’s Emissions Database for Global Atmospheric Research (EDGAR). The key independent variable is a binary indicator for the implementation of the STIDPP. The policy was launched in 2016 as part of China’s broader strategy to enhance the international competitiveness of its service sector and address long-standing trade deficits in services. In line with a “pilot-first, gradual expansion” mode, the State Council designated 15 regions in the initial batch of pilots, followed by expansions in 2018 and 2020, ultimately covering 28 cities.1 The pilot program aims to foster institutional reform, promote high-quality development in service trade, and create demonstration zones for openness and innovation. Accordingly, the treatment indicator takes the value of 1 for city i from the year it was included in the STIDPP onward, and 0 otherwise. The assignment of pilot status is based on official policy documents issued by the State Council and the Ministry of Commerce. Cities not included in any batch of the STIDPP serve as the control group. A detailed map of policy adoption across cities is presented in Appendix Figure A1.

Mechanism Variables. Following the theoretical framework outlined earlier, four mechanism variables are considered. Low-carbon policy intensity (LCP) is measured using a composite index based on three dimensions, including carbon emissions, policy framework, and technological support. Prior research has shown that low-carbon policies significantly contribute to emission reductions and play a vital role in urban carbon mitigation [60]. Green innovation (GI) is calculated as the number of green patents per 10,000 persons per year, reflecting the regional intensity of green innovation activities.2 Market integration (MAR) is measured by the city-level market integration index.3 Urban trade network (UTN) is proxied by the number of subsidiaries of listed companies (in an individual city) established in other cities, indicating a city’s position in the national trade network and its capacity for intercity economic linkages.4

Control Variables. Several city characteristics are controlled to release the potential bias caused by omitted variables, including scale, technique, and composition effects. Population scale (lnPOP) is proxied by the logarithm of the registered population, capturing the scale effect of urbanization, which prior studies have linked to increased environmental pressure [37, 40]. Technology capacity (TECH) is proxied by the share of scientific and technological expenditure in the general public budget, as technological innovation has been shown to significantly affect environmental pollution [49]. Industrial structure (SIND) is measured by the share of the secondary industry in regional GDP, since a higher industrial proportion is generally associated with more pollution-intensive activities [25, 50]. In addition, we further control environmental regulation (ER), which is proxied by the centralized sewage treatment rate, a widely used indicator of local regulatory effectiveness [6, 29].

Baseline results

Table 2 reports the baseline regression results. Columns (1) and (2) present the effects of the STIDPP on carbon emissions without and with control variables, respectively. Across both specifications, the independent variable of interest is significantly negative at the 1% level. Having accounted for city and year FE as well as a comprehensive set of covariates, the results show that the STIDPP leads to an average reduction of approximately 8.9% in total carbon emissions. This finding serves as empirical validation for Hypothesis 1 and is consistent with previous studies suggesting that service trade development contributes to improving carbon efficiency [17].

To address potential sample selection bias in the STIDPP implementation, we further employ a Propensity Score Matching (PSM)-DID approach. Specifically, two matching strategies are adopted: caliper matching and 1:4 nearest neighbor matching. Based on the matched samples, the estimated results are shown in columns (3) and (4) of Table 2. The interested coefficients are − 0.069 (p < 0.01) for caliper matching and − 0.055 (p < 0.01) for nearest neighbor matching. These results confirm the robustness of the policy’s carbon mitigation effect.

Parallel trend test

Based on Eq. (2), we first use the two-way fixed effects (TWFE) estimator to test the parallel trend assumption. To further validate the findings, we apply a set of recently developed heterogeneity robust estimators tailored to staggered treatment settings with heterogeneous treatment effects [5, 7, 13, 16, 20, 23, 45, 48]. These methodologies enable more robust causal identification when accounting for dynamic adaptation and treatment effect heterogeneity.

The estimated results of testing the parallel trend assumption are illustrated in Fig. 2. For all specifications, pre-treatment coefficients are statistically insignificant, which supports the parallel trends assumption. On the contrary, post-treatment estimates show a consistent and statistically significant decline in emissions, with effects persisting for several years after implementation. These results reinforce the conclusion that the STIDPP generates sustained carbon mitigation effects.

Addressing endogeneity

To mitigate concerns over potential endogeneity between policy assignment and carbon emissions, we construct a set of historical and geographical instrumental variables (IVs) and employ the control function approach proposed by Wooldridge [47].

First, considering that service trade development relies heavily on digital infrastructure [19], we use the minimum distance to the “Eight Vertical and Eight Horizontal” optical fiber backbone network as an IV. Cities closer to this network were more likely to develop early digital infrastructure, which is plausibly exogenous to current emission levels. Second, following Blonigen and Wilson [3], we use the distance to the nearest seaport to capture geographic access to trade routes, which influences service trade potential but is unlikely to correlate with contemporary emission trends. Third, we include a dummy variable for treaty port status, reflecting long-term institutional openness and exposure to international commerce. Fourth, we use the logarithm of trade volume in 1911 to proxy for historical commercial development. Both variables capture deeply rooted trade capacity and are predetermined relative to current carbon outcomes.

To improve the relevance of the instrumental variables and to address baseline heterogeneity in trade capacity, each instrument is interacted with the lagged proportion of import-export relative to GDP. These interaction terms strengthen the first-stage explanatory power by capturing pre-policy variation in institutional readiness. Following Wooldridge [47], the first-stage regression estimates the treatment indicator using the interaction terms, and the residuals (CF1 to CF4) are then included in the outcome equation as control functions.

Table 3 reports the results. The coefficients on the policy variable remain significantly negative across specifications, confirming the emission-reducing effect of the STIDPP. The control function terms are statistically insignificant, suggesting that endogeneity bias does not drive our main findings. These results reinforce the credibility of the causal interpretation.

Robustness tests

Alternative measures of variables of interest and estimation methods

To account for the staggered implementation and varying intensities of the STIDPP, we re-estimate the model using alternative policy variables and estimation strategies.

First, the dependent variable is alternatively measured by the city-level process-related CO2 emissions (tons, logarithm form) [4], and the estimated results are shown in column (1) of Table 4. Evidently, the coefficient of DID remains significantly negative, indicating our baseline results are robust to alternative measures of the dependent variable.

Second, we construct two alternative treatment indicators, DID1it and DID2it, to better capture the temporal dynamics of policy rollout. The variable DID1it is assigned a value of 0.5 in the year when the STIDPP is adopted, and this value is set to 1in the subsequent years, reflecting the gradual nature of policy implementation [14]. The variable DID2it is designed to capture the specific month of policy enactment, taking values of 2/12, 6/12, or 8/12 during the implementation year for pilot cities categorized into the first, second, and third batches, respectively, and 1 thereafter. As shown in columns (2) and (3) of Table 4, both coefficients remain significantly negative, indicating our baseline results are robust to alternative policy definitions.

Third, we employ the Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood (PPML) estimator to address potential issues of heteroskedasticity [43]. Column (4) of Table 4 confirms a persistently negative and significant treatment effect under the PPML specification.

Finally, we adopt a DDML method to address potential biases from high-dimensional confounders and model misspecification [12]. Columns (5) and (6) report estimates based on LASSO and ridge regression, respectively. The estimated effects remain significantly negative, and their magnitudes are comparable to the baseline, corroborating the robustness of the research conclusions in this paper.

Controlling for concurrent policies

To isolate the impact of the STIDPP from other contemporaneous policy impacts, we introduce dummy variables for four major policies: The Carbon Emission Trading System (CETS), the Low-Carbon City Pilot (LCCP), the Free Trade Zone Initiative (FTZI), and the Green Finance Reform Initiative (GFRI). Previous studies suggest that each of these policies can contribute to carbon mitigation [22, 38, 41, 55]. In Table 5, columns (1) to (4) progressively incorporate each policy indicator variable within the baseline regression framework. Across all model specifications, the coefficients consistently exhibit statistically significant negativity, validating that the observed carbon mitigation effect is not confounded by these contemporaneous policy shocks.

We conduct a series of additional robustness checks, including Bacon decomposition, placebo tests, adjustments for time trends, lagged effects, and alternative time windows, all of which consistently support the credibility of our core results (see Appendix Figure A2, Figure A3, and Table A2 for details).

Further analysis

Heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneity across geography. We evaluate the policy effects across coastal and inland regions, and the results are summarized in columns (1) and (2) of Table 6. Empirical findings indicate that coastal cities experience more pronounced benefits from STIDPP with respect to the reduction of carbon emissions. Several mechanisms may explain this disparity. Coastal cities typically possess more developed service sectors, greater foreign investment inflows, and higher levels of market integration [2, 9, 33]. The policy thereby promotes the expedited implementation of green technologies and the acceleration of industrial transformation by capitalizing on these geographical advantages. Moreover, coastal areas benefit from more advanced infrastructure and institutional frameworks, which enhance the spillover and synergistic effects associated with innovation in the service trade sector. In contrast, inland cities frequently continue to rely on conventional manufacturing industries, exhibit less robust innovation ecosystems, and encounter infrastructural constraints related to transportation and digital connectivity. These constraints limit their ability to fully harness the benefits of STIDPP, leading to a relatively weaker policy impact on emission reduction.

Heterogeneity across the service sector development level. Regional abilities to capitalize on service trade for simultaneous economic and environmental benefits are fundamentally dependent on the service sector’s advancement level. A more developed service sector generally promotes increased volumes of both service imports and exports, which may subsequently impact carbon emissions. This study investigates the regional differences in the impact of STIDPP on carbon emissions, utilizing the value-added proportion of tertiary industries within GDP as a measure of service sector development. Cities exhibiting a service sector development level exceeding the average are categorized as high-development cities.

Columns (3) and (4) of Table 6 demonstrate a significantly enhanced carbon abatement efficacy of the STIDPP in cities exhibiting advanced service sector development. This is likely due to these cities’ greater regulatory capacity, optimized industrial structures, and higher service trade competitiveness, which enable them to more effectively implement carbon mitigation policies. In contrast, cities with less developed service sectors may face challenges such as lower innovation capacity and inefficient service industries, limiting the policy’s carbon reduction potential [27].

Heterogeneity in environmental regulation intensity. We further examine heterogeneity based on environmental regulation stringency by distinguishing between cities within and outside the Two Control Zones (TCZs) – areas designated for acid rain and sulfur dioxide control. Column (5) of Table 6 presents results for cities within the TCZs, while column (6) shows outcomes for non-designated areas. The findings suggest that the STIDPP significantly curbs carbon emissions in both types of regions, but the absolute coefficient is notably larger for non-TCZs.

The difference can be attributed to the initial regulatory conditions. In TCZs, the existence of stringent environmental controls provides an institutional foundation that complements the policy’s objectives. However, the marginal impact of the policy may be constrained due to already binding regulations and relatively saturated policy space. In contrast, in non-TCZ regions, where regulatory frameworks have traditionally been less stringent, the STIDPP functions with greater autonomy by promoting service-oriented openness, facilitating the diffusion of technology, and enhancing resource allocation through market mechanisms. Consequently, the policy demonstrates more pronounced incremental impacts on carbon reduction within these regions characterized by less stringent regulatory frameworks.

Mechanism analysis

Mechanism analysis proceeds through sequential estimation of Eq. (4) with four mechanism variables capturing low-carbon policy intensity, green innovation, market integration, and urban trade network.

Column (1) of Table 7 illustrates the role of low-carbon policy intensity. The estimated coefficient is significantly positive, indicating that the STIDPP contributes to the increased rigor and implementation of local carbon governance. This is consistent with the concept that institutional reforms have the potential to enhance environmental governance by augmenting regulatory capabilities, improving implementation criteria, and reinforcing the enforcement of policies [54]. The STIDPP facilitates enhanced policy-driven emission reductions by increasing the efficacy of local emission control strategies, validating Hypothesis 2a.

Column (2) of Table 7 shows the effect of the policy on green innovation. The positive and significant coefficient suggests that the STIDPP fosters innovation in environmentally friendly technologies. This is consistent with prior research indicating that institutional transparency and enhanced business conditions can promote green R&D by lowering innovation obstacles and fostering the aggregation of innovation resources [36]. Consequently, the policy promotes the adoption and dissemination of environmentally sustainable technologies, providing support for Hypothesis 2b.

Column (3) of Table 7 presents the market integration channel. The result indicates that the STIDPP significantly improves cross-regional market integration. As noted by Jiang & Ma [29], deeper integration reduces institutional fragmentation and facilitates the reallocation of resources from inefficient, high-emission sectors toward more productive, service-oriented activities. By reducing trade barriers and increasing the mobility of production factors, the policy facilitates economies of scale and the diffusion of knowledge, which together contribute to enhancing carbon efficiency across different regions, verifying Hypothesis 2c.

Column (4) of Table 7 outlines the effect of STIDDP on the urban trade network. The significantly positive coefficient indicates that STIDPP strengthens inter-city connectivity by facilitating stronger trade linkages. This process enables the development of low-carbon supply chains and promotes the diffusion of environmentally sustainable practices beyond municipal borders. Previous research has demonstrated that trade network centrality plays a crucial role in influencing regional emission patterns by affecting industrial structure, the dissemination of knowledge, and firm behavior [8]. These findings support Hypothesis 2d, suggesting that enhanced trade network effects represent a key channel through which the STIDPP mitigates carbon emissions.

Spatial spillover effects

Figure 3 presents the coefficient estimates for the spatially lagged policy variable across discrete distance bands, along with their 95% confidence intervals. Within (0, 100] km, the results reveal a statistically significant decline in emissions in nearby cities, suggesting positive spatial spillovers from the STIDPP. This result likely reflects the dissemination of green technologies, industrial advancement, and enhanced inter-city cooperation originating from pilot cities, which, owing to their geographic proximity, promote the adoption of low-carbon practices in neighboring cities [53].

Within (100, 500] km, the coefficient becomes positive but insignificant, suggesting that the initial spillover effect weakens with distance and may even reverse direction. One potential explanation is a moderate resource reallocation effect, in which pilot cities draw mobile production factors – such as skilled labor and clean investments – from neighboring areas. This dynamic may weaken the low-carbon transition capacities of more remote cities without entirely offsetting their emissions.

Interestingly, within (500, 700] km, the coefficient turns significantly negative, suggesting a second wave of positive spillovers at intermediate distances. This is consistent with network-mediated diffusion, operating through market integration and urban trade networks, where standardized platforms and cross-city contracts enable the adoption of codified low-carbon practices without close geographic proximity. Where institutional alignment and absorptive capacity are stronger, these network effects outweigh residual reallocation, yielding the observed reduction.

As the distance exceeds 700 km, the estimated coefficients decrease in magnitude and cease to be statistically significant, indicating that the spatial externalities of the STIDPP attenuate over greater distances. This attenuation is likely attributable to heightened geographical barriers, diminished economic linkages, and lower institutional homogeneity among more remote regions. Overall, the evidence confirms Hypothesis 3, demonstrating that while the STIDPP yields direct carbon reduction benefits within treated cities, it also produces non-monotonic spatial spillovers. These findings highlight how crucial it is to take geography into account when assessing service trade reforms and their broader environmental effects.

Conclusions

Drawing on a panel dataset covering 285 Chinese cities between 2006 and 2022, this paper evaluates the STIDPP’s carbon mitigation effect. Employing a staggered DID strategy combined with the DDML method, we provide robust causal evidence on the policy’s carbon mitigation effect. Our findings reveal three key insights. First, the STIDPP significantly reduces carbon emissions in pilot cities, with an average decline of 8.9%, and this effect remains robust across a series of alternative specifications. Second, the emission reductions operate through four primary channels: strengthening low-carbon policy enforcement, promoting green innovation, enhancing regional market integration, and improving urban trade networks. Third, we identify complex nonmonotonic spatial spillovers: significant emission reductions within 0-100 km of treated cities; weakly positive effects at 100–500 km, consistent with resource reallocation; renewed significant reductions at 500–700 km, attributable to network-mediated diffusion via market integration and urban trade networks; and diminishing effects beyond 700 km.

These findings yield three actionable policy implications. First, deepen institutional reforms in service trade with an explicit environmental orientation. Policymakers should embed carbon mitigation objectives into the rule-making, supervision, and support systems of service trade. This includes establishing green evaluation criteria for service trade activities, offering fiscal and tax incentives for low-carbon services, and expanding access to green finance for service-sector enterprises. Given the observed heterogeneity across regions, targeted support mechanisms should be tailored to local conditions. Cities with weaker service sector foundations or less stringent environmental regulations may require additional institutional capacity building to effectively implement low-carbon service trade policies and prevent further regional divergence.

Second, strengthen the enabling environment for low-carbon transformation by aligning public and market instruments. Local governments should intensify low-carbon regulation in the service sector and support enterprise-led green innovation. Our findings on the policy’s transmission mechanisms suggest that complementary policy tools can amplify these effects. Mechanisms such as dedicated R&D subsidies, performance-based procurement, and industry-university-research alliances can accelerate clean technology diffusion. Additionally, measures that promote market integration, such as harmonizing cross-city certification standards, removing administrative barriers to service mobility, and supporting digital trade infrastructure, can foster regional coordination and reduce system-level emissions.

Third, adopt a differentiated spatial strategy to mitigate negative spillovers and promote coordinated regional decarbonization. The observed nonmonotonic spatial spillover patterns underscore the importance of distance-sensitive policy design. In areas adjacent to pilot zones, efforts should focus on enhancing knowledge diffusion through formalized intercity collaborative platforms. In regions at intermediate distances, where resource reallocation effects may emerge, horizontal compensation mechanisms (e.g., fiscal transfers or carbon quota adjustments) could balance efficiency and equity. For more distant areas, investment in connectivity infrastructure – both digital and institutional – can facilitate the transmission of low-carbon practices, including support for enclave-based low-carbon service parks paired with national policy guidance.

Although this paper conducts a quantitative analysis of the carbon emission reduction effect and its mechanism of action of the STIDPP, the main research limitation lies in the failure to explore the policy’s economic and cost effects. In particular, whether the policy suppresses the marginal abatement cost of carbon and the specific mechanisms through which this is achieved are key directions worthy of in-depth study in the future. In addition, we have not systematically examined the environmental effects of the STIDPP from the perspective of micro-enterprises, which is also one of the key directions worthy of in-depth study in the future.
