Transfer learning with multiomics integration and deep neural networks reveals drug resistance mechanisms in cancer
Authors: Semih Alpsoy, Osman Ugur Sezerman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug resistance remains one of the primary challenges in effective cancer therapy. In this study, we employed a deep neural network (DNN)-based transfer learning (TL) approach to predict drug response and uncover drug resistance mechanisms. We integrated gene expression, somatic mutation, and copy number aberration (CNA) data with drug response profiles using multi-omics integration (MI). We used the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) data for training and incorporated drugs with same pathways into the training models. We then evaluated drug response predictions on independent in-vivo PDX Encyclopedia (PDX) and ex-vivo the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets. In addition, we conducted pathway enrichment analyses to elucidate the mechanisms underlying drug resistance for paclitaxel, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), gemcitabine, and cetuximab. We also applied Fisher’s exact test (FET) to assess potential associations between drug resistance and the presence of mutations or CNAs. Our pan-drug models outperformed other methods based on the area under the precision-recall curve (AUCPR). Our pathway enrichment analyses revealed LDHB-mediated pyruvate metabolism and FYN-mediated focal adhesion might have pivotal roles in paclitaxel resistance, while PINK1-mediated mitophagy might be critical in 5-FU resistance. In addition to transcriptional activation, FET suggested that CNAs in LDHB and PINK1 may also be associated with resistance to paclitaxel and 5-FU, respectively. Furthermore, enrichment results for paclitaxel and cetuximab indicated shared resistance mechanisms between the two drugs. Importantly, our findings are consistent with prior experimental studies, providing literature-based validation of our results. Overall, our DNN-based TL approach achieved strong predictive performance across PDX & TCGA datasets and enrichment analyses provided valuable biological insights into drug resistance mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23435-8.Keywords: Multi-omics integration, Transfer learning, Deep neural networks, Drug response prediction, Drug resistance in cancerSubject terms: Systems biology, Oncology, Cancer, Data integration, Data mining, Machine learning

Introduction

Cancer is a complex and heterogeneous disease characterized by uncontrolled cell growth and division1. Its development is driven by several hallmarks, which include sustaining proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, resisting programmed cell death, enabling replicative immortality, promoting angiogenesis, facilitating invasion and metastasis, deregulating cellular energetics, and avoiding immune destruction2. Various treatment approaches such as surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and hormone therapy are individualized according to the type and stage of cancer, underlying molecular and genetic alterations, and the overall health status of patients3,4. However, the success of treatments can be limited due to the development of several drug resistance mechanisms. Increased drug efflux, drug inactivation and detoxification, changes in drug targets, enhanced DNA repair, apoptotic evasion, immune evasion, activation of alternative signaling pathways, changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME), epigenetic changes, metabolic reprogramming, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) are examples of such diverse and complex resistance mechanisms5,6.

In addition to resistance mechanisms, tumor heterogeneity is another factor that might limit success in treatment. It poses significant challenges due to the varying responses to therapies and adaptive changes that drive resistance mechanisms7. There are two types of heterogeneity: inter-tumor and intra-tumor heterogeneity. Inter-tumor heterogeneity encompasses the varying genetic, molecular, and phenotypic changes in tumors across different individuals. Even variations observed in different tumor sites within the same individual are considered inter-tumor heterogeneity8. On the other hand, intra-tumor heterogeneity includes genetic, molecular, and phenotypic differences in different regions within a single tumor. The accumulation of distinct mutations, CNAs, or epigenetic changes in sub-clones within the tumor due to clonal evolution, differences in the TME, and treatment pressures can lead to variability in tumor progression and treatment responses8. Therefore, the variability in individual responses to treatments including cases where patients initially respond but later become non-responsive can be attributed to the combined effects of various drug resistance mechanisms and tumor heterogeneity9.

The existence of diverse and complex resistance mechanisms as well as tumor heterogeneity necessitates the need for generalizable and robust computational models10. MI strategies with a TL approach have great potential to be applied for this purpose11. MI enables a comprehensive and holistic understanding of drug resistance mechanisms in cancer by simultaneously examining multiple data layers12. When combined with MI, TL offers the development of large, diverse, generalizable, and robust training models to better understand inter-tumor heterogeneity and resistance mechanisms13. Knowledge of heterogeneity and general resistance mechanisms gained from such trained models can be transferred to external datasets for validation by using TL even if they have a small sample size or are subject to distribution shifts14. TL approaches leveraging the advantages of DNNs have gained significant popularity in recent years toward this goal15.

In this study, a TL approach utilizing DNNs with MI was applied to build pan-drug models, i.e., models trained across multiple drugs within the same pathway class rather than restricted to a single drug, to predict the response of various drugs in PDX & TCGA datasets16,17. Multi-omics datasets derived from hundreds of human cancer cell lines and response profiles of various drugs targeting mitosis, cytoskeleton, DNA replication, and EGFR signaling pathways in GDSC were separately trained for this purpose18. Drug response prediction for five drugs using only the PDX datasets and eight drugs using only the TCGA datasets was conducted successfully and demonstrated strong performance, surpassing that of similar methods applied to the same datasets13 Moreover, pathway enrichment analyses provided insights into drug resistance mechanisms following robust drug response predictions using four different strategies for paclitaxel, 5-FU, gemcitabine, and cetuximab, which are shared between PDX & TCGA datasets. FET further revealed that somatic mutations and CNAs can contribute to drug resistance in addition to transcriptional changes. These findings demonstrated the effectiveness of the TL approach with DNNs and MI in predicting drug responses and uncovering key drug resistance mechanisms, thereby paving the way for advancements in personalized and precision medicine approaches to cancer chemotherapy.

Preprocessing steps for gene expression, somatic mutation, CNA, and drug response datasets

All preprocessing steps described in the Multi-Omics Late Integration (MOLI) pipeline were followed for the GDSC, PDX, and TCGA datasets13 The PDX expression dataset was converted from Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) to Transcripts Per Million (TPM), and log2-transformation was performed. The TCGA expression dataset was log2-transformed after TPM normalization. The GDSC gene expression dataset was Robust Multi-array Average (RMA)-normalized and log2-transformed, similar to the PDX & TCGA datasets. This approach ensured that all expression datasets were comparable. In addition, pairwise homogenization was performed between the datasets by removing batch effects using the sva R package21. For mutation datasets, genes with non-synonymous mutations affecting protein structure were binarized, assigning a value of 1 to genes carrying the mutations and 0 to those without them. For CNA datasets, the copy number of each gene was divided by the ploidy of the copy-neutral state in the sample after processing the genome segmentation files in TCGA, then log2-transformation was performed. In this way, the TCGA dataset was comparable with the gene-level estimates of total copy number data in GDSC & PDX. Insertions and deletions were binarized by assigning a value of 1 to insertions or deletions and 0 to copy-neutral regions. The GDSC drug response dataset was used in its binarized form, as described in Iorio et al.19. In contrast, the PDX & TCGA drug response datasets, which were reported based on RECIST criteria, were categorized by grouping partial and complete responses as sensitive, and progressive and stable diseases as resistant22.

No imputation strategy was applied during preprocessing for the PDX & TCGA datasets as missing data did not pose a significant issue in the multi-omics layers. The primary criterion for sample selection was the availability of drug response data. For PDX, samples with drug response annotations were identified based on the supplementary material of Gao et al.16, while for TCGA, sample selection was based on the drug response annotations provided by Ding et al.20. Only samples with available multi-omics data were included in the analysis. If any omics layer was missing, that sample was excluded. This approach was also applied to the GDSC dataset as not all cell lines with drug response measurements have complete multi-omics profiles. Sensitive and resistant labels from Iorio et al.19 were used directly, and only the cell lines with available multi-omics data and drug response information were included in the final datasets.

Finding and unionizing differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

DEGs between sensitive and resistant samples were identified using microarray gene expression dataset and binary response profiles of each drug within the four pathways (mitosis, cytoskeleton, DNA replication, and EGFR signaling pathways) in GDSC. The limma R package was used for this purpose23. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were selected as DEGs. Moreover, the DEGs identified for at least one drug within the same pathway were combined into a unionized feature set for each corresponding pathway (Fig. 2).

Drug response prediction using a TL approach with DNNs and MI

TL was implemented using the GDSC cell line dataset as the source domain due to its large sample size and comprehensive pharmacogenomic profiling across multiple cancer types. In addition, the availability of multi-omics data across a large number of cell line samples and drug response information for multiple drugs targeting the same pathway enabled the construction of pan-drug datasets in the source domain. The PDX & TCGA datasets were selected as target domains due to their limited pharmacological profiling and smaller sample sizes compared to GDSC. Moreover, drug overlap and the inclusion of biologically relevant cancer types between the source and target datasets provided a consistent framework for assessing TL performance.

Feed-forward subnetworks were independently applied to gene expression, somatic mutation, and CNA datasets using a similar architecture and the same hyperparameter ranges of the MOLI TL workflow to enable a fair performance comparison13 To optimize model performance and ensure generalizability, a random search was conducted over a predefined hyperparameter space for 50 iterations, accompanied by 5-fold cross-validation to prevent overfitting. The search space included variations in mini-batch size to balance gradient noise and computational efficiency with different input layer sizes to accommodate network capacity according to feature dimensionality. A diverse set of learning rates was employed to enable both rapid convergence and fine-grained weight updates. The number of training epochs was varied to account for different convergence behaviors across datasets. Moreover, multiple dropout rates were evaluated to prevent overfitting, particularly in data-scarce settings. Weight decay values were also explored to regularize model complexity and enhance generalization. The hyperparameter ranges used in the model training phase are summarized below.

mini batch size = [8, 16, 32, 64].

input layer size= [1024, 512, 256, 128, 64, 32, 16].

learning rate = [0.5, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, 0.005, 0.0005, 0.0001, 0.00005, 0.00001].

epoch = [10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100].

dropout rate = [0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8].

weight decay = [0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001].

This study introduces several key differences compared to the original MOLI framework. While MOLI utilizes all genes with more than 5% variation as input features and integrates all three omics datasets (gene expression, somatic mutation, and CNA) into a single representation for drug response prediction, it does not include a fine-tuning step. In contrast, this study uses a smaller set of DEGs as features, which reduces model complexity, potentially improves predictive performance, and yields more biologically meaningful results. Furthermore, MOLI applies TL only in the context of EGFR-targeting drugs by training on GDSC data and predicting responses of erlotinib and cetuximab in PDX dataset. In contrast, this study expands the TL framework by incorporating drugs that target four distinct pathways (Mitosis, cytoskeleton, DNA replication, and EGFR signaling) from the GDSC dataset, and predicting drug responses in both PDX & TCGA datasets. Rather than combining all omics data into a single integrated representation as in MOLI, three separate integration strategies were employed: expression–mutation (EM), expression–CNA (EC), and expression–mutation–CNA (EMC). Combining mutation data with gene expression (EM) allows the model to account for driver alterations that may not be reflected at the transcriptional level. Similarly, integrating CNAs with expression data (EC) helps capture gene dosage effects that influence expression without changing sequence information. The full integration of expression, mutation, and CNA data (EMC) enables the model to leverage both regulatory and structural genomic information. Moreover, unlike the original MOLI workflow, which combines binary cross-entropy loss with triplet loss, this study uses only binary cross-entropy loss for classification. Finally, although MOLI reports the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as the primary evaluation metric despite substantial class imbalances in drug response data, this study adopts AUCPR as the main evaluation metric. AUCPR offers a more realistic assessment of model performance under imbalanced class distributions13.

For drugs that belong to the same pathway, the identified DEGs were unionized as gene features to reduce feature size, allowing the training phase to be carried out with less noise and more meaningful features. TL was utilized not only to predict drug responses of five drugs in PDX and eight drugs in TCGA, but also of the four drugs shared between the two datasets (Paclitaxel, 5-FU, gemcitabine, and cetuximab). The total sample sizes and proportions of sensitive samples in GDSC, PDX, and TCGA for these drugs are visualized in Fig. 1C–E. Once the feature representations for all three omics layers were obtained, they were concatenated into a single representation. Only the genes common to all datasets were included during integration. In these representations, columns corresponded to genes, and rows to cell line samples for drugs within the same pathway (Fig. 3).

Workflow for constructing EM, EC, and EMC multi-omics representations using the GDSC source dataset. Gene expression, somatic mutation, and CNA data from GDSC were first preprocessed, and only the genes common to all three omics layers were retained. DEGs identified in a unionized manner across drugs targeting the same pathway were then used to filter each omics dataset. Three separate autoencoders for each omics type were trained independently with hyperparameters optimized via random search. The resulting low-dimensional representations were concatenated to generate EM, EC, and EMC multi-omics representations. Drugs sharing the same pathway were used for representation learning with the number of drugs denoted by N. These N drug-specific samples were combined row-wise to form each representation matrix. In these matrices, gene expression features are continuous, whereas somatic mutation, CNA, and drug response features are binary.

During the construction of multi-omics representations, hyperparameters for the gene expression, somatic mutation, and CNA auto-encoder layers (AutoencoderE for gene expression, AutoencoderM for somatic mutation, and AutoencoderC for CNA) were selected using random search within predefined ranges. Each omics-specific auto-encoder consisted of a linear layer followed by batch normalization, a ReLU activation function to introduce non-linearity and improve computational efficiency, and dropout for regularization. The model used dropout, weight decay, and batch normalization as regularization techniques; however, early stopping was not applied. The values for these regularization parameters were chosen from the same hyperparameter ranges outlined above.

After constructing the multi-omics representations, a classifier was built as the final subnetwork. The classification layer constructed a linear layer with an input size equal to the sum of the hidden layer sizes from the multi-omics representations. This was followed by dropout and a sigmoid activation function, which was applied to enable binary drug response prediction. The Adagrad optimizer, similar to that in MOLI, was applied for model training. Fine-tuning was exclusively performed in the classification layer by freezing the expression, mutation, and CNA auto-encoders. 50 iterations with random search and the same hyperparameter ranges were used in fine-tuning as those employed during the training phase. Model performance was evaluated using AUCPR although AUC was also reported (Fig. 4). The model architecture is also illustrated in Fig. 5, where three separate auto-encoders (AutoencoderE, AutoencoderM, and AutoencoderC) independently transform their respective input features into low-dimensional representations. These representations are then concatenated and passed to a final classifier layer, which outputs drug response predictions in the PDX & TCGA datasets.

Pathways with a large number of annotated drugs in the GDSC dataset, specifically mitosis, cytoskeleton, DNA replication, and EGFR signaling inhibitors, were focused on to enable TL. Among the test datasets, only paclitaxel, 5-FU, gemcitabine, and cetuximab were shared between PDX & TCGA with sufficient sample sizes for robust evaluation. Therefore, these four drugs were selected for systematic assessment of model performance and resistance mechanisms using four complementary evaluation strategies.

This four-strategy approach effectively addressed potential biases arising from including tested drugs and overlapping cancer types in the training datasets.

Elucidating drug resistance mechanisms via pathway enrichment analysis

The pathfindR R package was used to investigate the resistance mechanisms of paclitaxel, 5-FU, gemcitabine, and cetuximab24. The models with the highest predictive performance among the EM, EC, and EMC configurations on the TCGA datasets were selected to identify clinically relevant enriched terms through enrichment analysis. For this purpose, only the gene features derived from these best-performing models were utilized. The corresponding p-values and |log₂FC| values of the gene features identified in GDSC were used as input for pathfindR. Enrichment analysis was conducted only under the first and second strategies. A comparison of the enrichment results was then performed to identify both unique and common terms. The common terms were considered indicative of general resistance mechanisms, while the unique terms were examined for their potential association with drug-specific resistance mechanisms.

Association of mutations and CNAs with drug resistance using FET

FET was applied to the GDSC samples to evaluate the associations of mutation and CNA statuses independently with drug resistance status. Odds ratios (ORs) and p-values were calculated to assess associations. Mutations or CNAs with OR > 1 and p-value < 0.05 were considered potentially associated with drug resistance. However, it was not performed on the PDX & TCGA datasets due to their low sample sizes and high class imbalances. The test was limited to the four drugs used in the enrichment analysis.

Results

Predictive performances of pan-drug models

Drug response predictions yielded strong performance for the five drugs in PDX and eight drugs in TCGA, not only across all samples but also specifically for cohort-level samples (Supplementary Table S1). Table 1 presents the best performing models for these drugs. Predictions were significantly superior to random predictions despite the presence of high class imbalances in drug response profiles. In general, the EC and EMC models achieved the highest performance across both datasets, but EMC models showed greater dominance in TCGA.

In PDX, the EC and EM models yielded the highest AUC and AUCPR scores (1.0) for cetuximab in the Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC) cohort and 5-FU in the Colorectal Cancer (CRC) cohort, respectively. However, a reliable assessment of predictive power could not be made for these cases as each included only one sensitive sample. The EM model was the most predictive for erlotinib in the NSCLC cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.93 and an AUCPR of 0.81. For gemcitabine in the Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cohort, the EMC model achieved the highest performance, with an AUC of 0.74 and an AUCPR of 0.67. The EC model trained with mitotic inhibitors and the EMC model trained with cytoskeleton inhibitors predicted AUC scores close to 0.90 for all paclitaxel samples with AUCPR scores slightly above 0.40. Similarly, the EC model trained with mitotic inhibitors and the EMC model trained with cytoskeleton inhibitors achieved AUC scores approaching 0.90 for paclitaxel in the Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA) cohort. However, the cytoskeleton-trained model outperformed the mitotic-trained model, achieving an AUCPR of 0.72 compared to 0.67. For all the five drugs, the AUC and AUCPR scores obtained from response predictions were significantly higher than those of random predictions (Table 1).

Association of mutation and CNA statuses with drug resistance

FET did not identify any significant association between mutation status and resistance for the gene features used in the models for paclitaxel and cetuximab. However, significant associations were observed for 5-FU with TJP1 (OR = 6.83, p-value = 0.02) and CNTRL (OR = 6.41, p-value = 0.03), and for gemcitabine with NUP210 (OR = ∞, p-value = 0.04) and COL3A1 (OR = ∞, p-value = 0.04) (Supplementary Table S9).

FET identified significant associations between CNA status and resistance for gene features used in the models for paclitaxel, 5-FU, and gemcitabine. Among paclitaxel-unique genes, LDHB showed a potentially significant association (OR = 2.87, p-value = 0.05). In addition, several genes within enriched pathways demonstrated significant associations including PLAU (OR = 5.87, p-value = 0.007), TCF7L2 (OR = 4.11, p-value = 0.02), and DKK1 (OR = 3.51, p-value = 0.04). For 5-FU, more than 500 genes exhibited significant associations including several 5-FU-unique genes such as PINK1 (OR = 2.34, p-value = 0.003), PREX1 (OR = 2.68, p-value = 5.7 × 10⁻⁵), TNFSF13B (OR = 2.13, p-value = 0.002), and MYO1F (OR = 2.72, p-value = 0.0005). Significant associations were also identified for gemcitabine, with over 100 genes showing CNA-related resistance links (Supplementary Table S9).

Discussion

Our enrichment analyses revealed that LDHB-mediated “Pyruvate metabolism” was uniquely active for paclitaxel. LDHB was upregulated exclusively for paclitaxel among all mitotic, cytoskeleton, DNA replication, and EGFR signaling inhibitors, and “Pyruvate metabolism” was uniquely enriched for paclitaxel. Furthermore, FET for LDHB indicated a possible association between paclitaxel resistance and CNA status. These findings suggest that transcriptional upregulation and CNAs in LDHB may be associated with paclitaxel resistance. Supporting this, previous studies reported that resistant cancer cells may upregulate LDHB through metabolic reprogramming by converting lactate to pyruvate and utilizing it via oxidative phosphorylation under hypoxic conditions34,35. In addition, LDHB upregulation was linked to paclitaxel resistance in oral squamous cell carcinoma36. Moreover, LDHB knockdown in Merkel cell carcinoma cell lines was shown to increase paclitaxel-induced apoptosis, which was reversed upon LDHB reintroduction37.

Paclitaxel-resistant cell lines are likely in a drug-tolerant persister (DTP) state, which is associated with the development of drug-resistant phenotypes38. In DTP, cancer cells prioritize survival over proliferation in a state of reversible quiescence in response to drug stress, and Wnt signaling pathway is commonly reported to be active39. In this state, stress response and compensatory pathways are activated, epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming occurs, stem-like properties are exhibited, and pathway activities are either partially active with only a few genes mediating them or completely suppressed39. In our paclitaxel enrichment results, most terms were suppressed or partially active with upregulation of several genes across many terms. Although many survival-related pathways were suppressed, TCF7L2-mediated stemness-associated compensatory Wnt and Hippo signaling pathways maintained survival31,32. FYN-mediated “Focal adhesion” might also contribute to apoptotic evasion and promote survival40. Beyond its role in promoting survival, FYN upregulation was an important finding since its activation was reported as a general characteristic of the DTP phenotype41. In addition, LDHB-mediated “Pyruvate metabolism” and ODC1-mediated “Glutathione metabolism” indicated metabolic reprogramming, while the EZH2-mediated “MicroRNAs in cancer” pointed to epigenetic reprogramming42.

In our enrichment results for paclitaxel, terms related to cellular adhesion and cell-cell interaction such as “ECM-receptor interaction” and “Tight junction” were suppressed. In contrast, FYN-mediated “Focal adhesion” and TCF7L2- and FYN-mediated “Adherens junction” were partially active. In addition, survival-related signaling pathways such as p53 and PI3K/Akt were suppressed. These results imply anoikis resistance, in which FYN upregulation may represent a paclitaxel-specific survival mechanism. FYN was identified as a DEG exclusively for paclitaxel among 21 mitotic and cytoskeletal inhibitors. Our finding that FYN was the only upregulated gene in the most significant term, “Focal adhesion”, may indicate that paclitaxel-resistant cell lines utilize FYN-driven adhesion signaling to evade apoptosis. Supporting this, Chou et al.43 showed that FYN was upregulated in paclitaxel-resistant basal-like triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Similarly, Egeland et al.44 reported FYN upregulation in resistant TNBC patients and in rapidly progressing metastatic lesions. However, FLNB and MYL9, two other paclitaxel-specific DEGs we found in “Focal adhesion” term, were downregulated. These genes are known to be involved in actin cytoskeleton remodeling and contraction45,46. Their downregulation may help cells survive mitotic arrest by disrupting cytoskeletal tension. FYN upregulation and MYL9 downregulation were also observed in “Adherens junction” and “Axon guidance” terms.

Our enrichment results showed that TCF7L2 upregulation may also contribute to paclitaxel resistance, potentially acting in concert with FYN. TCF7L2 was found to be upregulated not only in “Adherens junction”, but also exclusively in the Wnt and Hippo signaling pathways, as well as in various cancer-related terms. Unlike FYN, however, FET indicated that CNAs in TCF7L2 may also play a role in paclitaxel resistance in addition to its transcriptional upregulation. Mechanistically, FYN can activate the Wnt signaling pathway both directly by phosphorylating β-catenin or indirectly by reducing β-catenin degradation through interaction with GSK3β in the β-catenin destruction complex47–49. Thus, TCF7L2 upregulation might be triggered by FYN activation.

FYN may be a critical player in paclitaxel resistance not only by upregulating TCF7L2 and by being activated through phosphorylation by CXCR4, as well50. CXCR4, which was co-upregulated with FYN in “Axon guidance”, was also co-upregulated with TCF7L2 in “Pathways in cancer”. CXCR4 is known to be upregulated in hypoxic niches, where it may promote chemotactic migration to CXCL12-rich adaptive areas in TME, enabling cells evade paclitaxel exposure51. CXCR4 was also upregulated in “Regulation of actin cytoskeleton”. This indicates that alterations in the actin cytoskeleton may increase invasiveness, motility, metastasis, and EMT in paclitaxel-resistant cell lines, supporting resistance through apoptotic evasion52. In particular, CXCR4 had the highest logFC among all genes upregulated by paclitaxel in this study. These findings suggest that CXCR4, FYN, and TCF7L2 might act together in an interconnected mechanism contributing to paclitaxel resistance.

Similar to the enrichment results for paclitaxel, CXCR4, FYN, and TCF7L2 were mostly upregulated in the same terms in the enrichment results for cetuximab. This suggests that both drugs may share similar resistance mechanisms. Clinical trial results showing improved treatment response with the combination of paclitaxel and cetuximab in metastatic or recurrent HNSC support the hypothesis that these genes may play a pivotal role in resistance and that co-targeting them could enhance treatment success in combination strategies53,54. In our paclitaxel results, FYN was a unique DEG among mitotic and cytoskeleton inhibitors, while it was upregulated for cetuximab but did not meet the DEG threshold by logFC. However, FYN was a DEG for other EGFR inhibitors such as gefitinib, afatinib, and lapatinib, suggesting a potential broader role in resistance to EGFR-targeted therapies. This aligns with previous studies reporting that FYN can be activated by EGFR pathway mutations and may play a role in resistance and cancer progression49,55. Moreover, while CNA status of TCF7L2 was significantly associated with paclitaxel resistance, no such association was observed for cetuximab, indicating that TCF7L2 may contribute to cetuximab resistance mainly through transcriptional upregulation. Among the genes upregulated for cetuximab, CXCR4 had the highest logFC, consistent with its expression pattern in paclitaxel.

Our enrichment results for cetuximab indicate that cetuximab-resistant cell lines may be in a DTP state analogous to that observed in paclitaxel-resistant cell lines. In the enrichment results, most terms were either partially active or suppressed, with a higher number of downregulated genes. Key survival-related pathways such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK, NF-κB, Wnt, and Hippo signaling were all partially active, indicating limited survival signaling. In addition, PIK3CG-mediated inositol phosphate metabolism, along with ZEB1-, E2F2-, and VIM- mediated “MicroRNAs in cancer” term pointed to both metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming. Most importantly, FYN upregulation, a general characteristic of the DTP phenotype, was also observed41.

Our enrichment results for cetuximab also revealed key findings about its resistance mechanisms. The suppression of “EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance” suggested that canonical EGFR-dependent mechanisms may not be the main drivers of resistance, and that resistant cells may survive by bypassing EGFR56. In support of this, our results indicated that PIK3CG-mediated “Inositol phosphate metabolism” may activate the PI3K/Akt pathway in an EGFR-independent manner through metabolic reprogramming, thereby promoting cell survival and resistance with immune suppression57,58. Moreover, the upregulation of VIM and ZEB1 and the downregulation of CDH1 across multiple enriched terms implied that EMT might play a role in cetuximab resistance59. Cetuximab might also contribute to resistance through immune suppression by blocking the EGFR signaling pathway60. Since EGFR inhibition may suppress the MAPK pathway, our findings align with this as we found only partial activation of MAP4K1-mediated “MAPK signaling pathway” with our enrichment results61. Several immune-related terms including “B-cell signaling pathways” and “T-cell signaling pathway” were partially active, while key components of the AP-1 TF complex (JUN, FOS, and FOSL1), associated with immune responses through the MAPK pathway, were downregulated within these terms62. However, LCK, a Src kinase, was upregulated in immune-related terms, either alone or in combination with FYN, another Src kinase. Both FYN and LCK are important in T-cell response63. This pattern suggests that, rather than triggering a full immune response, cetuximab-resistant cell lines might be in a partial immune-like signaling state by selectively activating Src kinases to support survival and cytoskeletal adaptation64.

Our enrichment results for 5-FU showed an opposite trend compared to paclitaxel and cetuximab, with many terms being active and containing many upregulated genes. In addition, some cancer stem cell (CSC) markers such as ALDH1A1, ALDH1A3, SOX9, KLF5, and ITGB1 were upregulated in several enriched terms65–69. These findings suggest that 5-FU-resistant cell lines likely exhibit a stem-like phenotype associated with strong drug resistance70,71.

Enrichment analyses for 5-FU revealed that genes uniquely associated with 5-FU among the unionized gene features of 13 DNA replication inhibitors were represented in the enriched terms. Among these, FET analysis showed a significant association between CNA status and resistance for PINK1, PREX1, TNFSF13B, and MYO1F, suggesting that both CNAs and transcriptional regulation of these genes might contribute to 5-FU-specific resistance. In our enrichment results, PINK1 was upregulated, while the other three genes were downregulated. The association between PINK1 upregulation and 5-FU resistance has been reported in previous studies. Liu et al.72 demonstrated that low expression of the lncRNA ABALON led to PINK1 upregulation, which promoted mitophagy and contributed to 5-FU resistance. Similarly, Yamashita et al.73 found that PINK1 upregulation was linked to mitophagy, 5-FU resistance, and poor prognosis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. However, to our knowledge, no prior studies have shown that CNAs in PINK1 may also contribute to 5-FU resistance. Therefore, this study is the first to suggest that both transcriptional upregulation and CNAs in PINK1 might be together associated with 5-FU resistance.

The relatively weak results observed for gemcitabine in the enrichment analysis can be attributed to the limited number of DEGs identified for this drug. Specifically, only one DEG was detected following differential expression analysis, which was insufficient to conduct pathway enrichment. Despite this limitation, the prediction performance for gemcitabine in both the PDX & TCGA datasets consistently exceeded that of a random classifier. This is likely due to the contribution of additional informative genes included in the unionized feature space derived from other DNA replication inhibitors. These shared DEGs provided a meaningful signal to the model, compensating for the paucity of gemcitabine-specific features and enabling above-baseline performance. This suggests that gemcitabine may exhibit cancer-type-specific activity and involve distinct resistance mechanisms. Another plausible explanation is that resistance to gemcitabine may be more strongly influenced by epigenetic or post-transcriptional regulation, as well as other genomic alterations, rather than transcriptional activity alone74–76. Therefore, conclusions regarding gemcitabine resistance mechanisms should be interpreted with caution. Future studies focusing on specific cancer types in which gemcitabine is clinically effective are necessary to gain deeper insights into its resistance mechanisms.

Although the GDSC dataset included a large number of cell line samples, the number of drug response–annotated samples in the PDX & TCGA datasets was unfortunately limited. In fact, the imbalance in drug response classes posed an additional challenge. When we further restricted the analysis to cohort-specific samples rather than using all available data, the sample size decreased even more. Nevertheless, our TL approach was able to generate strong predictions even with these small datasets. In particular, obtaining more meaningful and reliable predictions would require larger sample sizes, particularly from cohorts in which the drug is known to be effective. This would ultimately enable more robust and generalizable predictions.

In this study, we integrated gene expression, somatic mutation, CNA, and response data from GDSC, PDX, and TCGA using a DNN based TL approach. We combined drugs targeting mitotic, cytoskeleton, DNA replication, and EGFR signaling pathways in the GDSC dataset during the training phase, and subsequently performed drug response predictions using independent PDX & TCGA test datasets. Our predictions outperformed MOLI on these two test datasets in terms of AUCPR for all the six drugs MOLI predicted. In particular, our models outperformed MOLI for erlotinib and cetuximab, where MOLI applied TL to predict responses in the PDX dataset, highlighting the effectiveness of training drugs within the same pathway using the TL approach. However, a larger number of samples is required to further enhance predictive power77. Integration of additional omics data such as metabolomics and proteomics could further enhance drug response prediction78. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that integrating all omics layers does not necessarily improve predictive performance. Integrating different omics data can lead to more complex models, and the presence of sparse data may hinder model learning; therefore, using all omics data together does not necessarily guarantee more powerful predictions79. Furthermore, the structural similarity and target profiles of drugs were not considered in this study. Incorporating such drug-based features into the model training phase could lead to the development of more powerful and generalizable models80.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that using a DNN-based TL approach with MI and drug pathway information enables strong predictive performance. Our approach not only improved predictive performance on in-vivo PDX and ex-vivo TCGA datasets, but also uncovered key mechanisms of resistance to paclitaxel, 5-FU, gemcitabine, and cetuximab. In particular, LDHB-mediated pyruvate metabolism emerged as a potential driver of paclitaxel resistance, whereas PINK1-mediated mitophagy may have a pivotal role in 5-FU resistance. In addition, we showed that integrating all omics layers does not necessarily yield the most powerful or generalizable models. We also revealed that utilizing DEG information of drugs within the same pathway can enhance predictive performance by prioritizing a reduced set of biologically meaningful features. These findings underscore the value of integrating omics-driven insights with pathway-informed modeling to enhance drug response prediction, elucidate mechanisms of drug resistance, and support personalized treatment strategies in precision oncology.
