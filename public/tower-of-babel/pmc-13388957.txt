4D metallic metamaterials for bone implants via biodegradation
Authors: Yu Qin, Zehao Jing, Youhao Wang, Bo Peng, Zhinan Mao, Chongbin Wei, Hongtao Yang, Peng Wen, Yufeng Zheng, Weishi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract4D scaffolds offer transformative potential for bone implants. Yet their application to metallic materials is constrained by the scarcity of suitable alloys and the requirement for harsh external stimuli to trigger shape change. Here, we introduce 4D metallic metamaterials driven by controlled biodegradation that combine biodegradable constraints with biometals of higher corrosion potential. We show that upon electrochemical degradation of the constraint, the metamaterials recover their original geometry—via stretching, bending, or expansion—generating programmable recovery forces tuned through structural design parameters. We demonstrate that when turned into scaffolds for bone implants, the 4D metallic metamaterials are cytocompatible and promote bone regeneration through the synergistic effects of bioactivity and mechanical stimulation in vivo. This strategy establishes a paradigm in 4D shape transformation of metal via metamaterial design, enabling bioactive, self-recovering implants with broad applicability across biomedical engineering.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Mechanical properties, Implants, Targeted bone remodelling

Introduction

3D-printed implants have long been at the forefront of research in orthopedics1–3. Over the last decade, this innovative technology has evolved beyond three dimensions. The emerging concept of 4D implants adds the dimension of time, transforming static scaffolds into dynamic structures capable of self-assembly and shape-shifting over time4,5. Generally, such 4D shape transformation behavior has predominantly been associated with polymer materials that are equipped with suitable stimuli-sensitive switches. Those switches can controllably respond to external stimuli, such as heat6, electromagnetic fields7, and humidity8,9, to realize predictable dynamic behaviors. Nonetheless, the development of 4D metallic implants remains limited. Existing 4D metallic systems generally include shape memory alloys10–12 and metallic composites13,14. These systems rely on complex alloy design balancing biocompatibility and 4D transformation capability, or stringent external stimuli hindered by tissues, which together limit their applicability as bone implants.

Biodegradable metals are expected to gradually degrade in vivo via electrochemical corrosion15,16, thus supporting as temporary scaffolds for bone healing17,18 or serving as a temporary stent19,20. Consequently, biodegradable metals inherently exhibit temporal change in vivo without necessitating stringent external stimuli. Nevertheless, pitting corrosion and localized degradation of single biodegradable metal led to unpredictable distortion and cannot be defined as a 4D transformation21,22. Mechanical metamaterials provide a pathway to transcend the inherent limitations of monolithic materials by exploiting precisely engineered microstructures and geometries23. Through structure design, they can be tailored to exhibit unprecedented mechanical properties and functionalities. Notably, while conventional metamaterials focus on achieving static properties such as a negative Poisson’s ratio or enhanced energy absorption, extending this concept to the time dimension is difficult24.

In this work, we propose 4D metallic structures by integrating metamaterial design with biodegradable metals. We further demonstrate its biocompatibility with cells involved in osteogenesis and angiogenesis, as well as its successful application as an implantable bone reconstruction scaffold.

Results

Establishment of 4D metallic metamaterials

The design of 4D metallic metamaterials (4DMM) comprises two key components: an elastically deformable biometal body and a biodegradable metal constraint. The deformable body is precisely designed to operate entirely within its elastic range and to match the constraint geometrically. This allows the assembly of the constraint with the biometal to produce pre-deformed 4DMM. Shape recovery is triggered in simulated body fluid when the corrosion potential of the constraint is lower than that of the deformable body, leading to gradual degradation of the constraint and recovery of the original shape, as shown in Fig. 1a.

The 4DMM enables diverse deformation modes through structural modifications. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, we fabricated a Ti scaffold incorporating elastically deformable helical elements and rigid frame units, and paired it with an Mg constraint of slightly larger diameter and length. Assembly yielded a pre-bent one-dimensional Ti@Mg 4D unit, which recovered from a bent to a planar two-dimensional (Fig. S1 and Movie S1) or three-dimensional configuration (Fig. 1d and Movie S2) after 285h of immersion in simulated body fluid (Hank’s solution). Arranging the helices radially produced an expandable stent structure (Fig. 1e), in which the entire biometal body deformed elastically. After precisely coupling with the Mg constraint, a Ti@Mg 4D stent was established. In vitro immersion for 24 h allowed the stent to expand from 79% of its original diameter back to its full size (Fig. 1f, g and Movie S3). When the biometal helices were arranged axially, the structure enabled either compression or extension recovery (Fig. 1h). In this configuration, a cylindrical Zn@Mg 4D scaffold was constructed. Immersion in Hank’s solution helps its length to recover from 89 to 100% (Fig. 1i), after which this kind of 4DMM was used for detailed in vitro and in vivo investigations.

Mechanical and degradation properties of 4DMM

For stretching-type 4DMM, the biodegradable spring constraint is fabricated with a variable pitch from top to bottom (P1 to Pn) to achieve controlled deformation. The exterior scaffold, made of biometal, adopts a double-helix structure with a fixed pitch (P1), and its total length (L) is given by the size of pitch and number of cycles (Nc) (Fig. 2a). The interior spring and exterior scaffold are manufactured separately via laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and subsequently assembled by threading the biodegradable spring into the scaffold (Fig. S2). To ensure a precise fit, both components are initially aligned to the same pitch (P₁). As the spring is advanced further into the scaffold (Fig. 2b and Movie S4), the variable pitch (Pn) of the constrant gradually deviates from the constant pitch (P₁) of the exterior structure, enabling progressive mechanical engagement with the exterior scaffold. The simulated stress during this process is within the yield strength of base materials (Fig. S2c). Therefore, the entire deformation process is elastic. Meanwhile, this progressive engagement avoids stress concentrations and uniformly applies pre-deformation along the length of the scaffold, thereby ensuring controlled shape recovery upon subsequent degradation. As shown in Fig. 2c, the assembled 4D scaffolds can be pre-deformed in either compression (Pn < P₁, Zn@Mg) or tension (Pn > P₁, Zn@MgT) states. Furthermore, by selecting different metal combinations (Fig. 2d), the corrosion rate can be tuned, thereby controlling the recovery time of the 4DMM. Zn@Mg scaffolds gradually regained their original shape in Hank’s solution during the degradation of Mg constraints. The mass loss proceeded at a relatively steady rate (Fig. S3), compared with a speedy increase in recovery length (Fig. 1i). When Mg was loosely positioned within the Zn scaffold (P₁ = P₂), forming a non-pre-deformed Zn@Mgo structure, Mg degradation slowed substantially. Consequently, Zn@Mg exhibited a greater mass loss rate than either Zn@Mgo or the standalone Zn scaffold. Galvanic electrochemical degradation between Zn and Mg was characterized using the Scanning vibrating electrode technique (SVET) (Fig. 2e). The current flow from Mg to Zn among 24 h indicates that Mg degradation was constantly accelerated while Zn was cathodically protected. Unlike single biodegradable metals, whose corrosion potential typically decreases during degradation25–27, the potential difference in Zn@Mg remained stable over 24 h, consistent with the observed steady mass loss (Fig. 2f).

Cell compatibility of 4D scaffolds

Metal scaffolds are primarily utilized for implantable devices such as bone implants and vascular stents. To evaluate the biocompatibility of 4DMM, we conducted cell viability assays of mouse-derived preosteoblasts (MC3T3-E1) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) using Zn, Zn@Mgo, and 4DMM Zn@Mg. Scaffold extracts were employed to assess cellular responses independent of the mechanical effects of 4D recovery. As shown in Fig. 3a, inductively coupled plasma spectrometer (ICP) analysis of the non-diluted extracts showed that Zn@Mg had the highest concentration of Mg ions and was approximately twice that of Zn@Mgo. Zn ion concentrations were similar in Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo, as the primary degradation occurred in the Mg component of both samples. CCK8 tests (Fig. 3b) demonstrate that the proliferation rate of Zn@Mg at non-diluted was lower than that of Zn@Mg₀ after 3 and 5 days of culturing and showed certain toxicity as the cell proliferation rate continuously decreased. However, with twofold diluted extracts, the proliferation rate of Zn@Mg exceeded that of Zn@Mg₀ at days 3–5. At fourfold dilution, no significant difference was observed between the two groups. Thus, fluorescence staining of live/dead and DAPI/FITC tests (Fig. 3c) was performed using a twofold concentration. The cell morphology results matched the CCK8 outcomes, with no significant difference in cell viability and spreading between Zn@Mgo and Zn@Mg, but both were superior to pure Zn. To assess the impact on osteogenic differentiation, MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured for 14 days with 50% concentration. ALP staining and activity results in Fig. 3d indicated a modest increase in ALP expression for Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo compared to Zn. Additionally, ARS staining in Fig. 3e revealed that Zn@Mg exhibited larger and more mineralized nodules than Zn@Mgo and Zn, indicating higher calcium deposition efficiency by MC3T3-E1 cells.

The cell viability test with HUVECs was further assessed using 100% concentration extracts. Fluorescent staining showed good cell viability across all groups after 24-hour culturing, with Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo exhibiting better spindle-shaped cell morphology (Fig. 3f). Proliferation tests (Fig. 3g) revealed no significant differences among the three groups, suggesting that HUVECs proliferation is relatively insensitive to the concentrations of Mg and Zn ions. Cell migration assays indicated that Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo significantly enhanced HUVECs migration compared to Zn (Fig. 3h). While Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo exhibited rare differences between each other (Fig. 3i). In short, Mg ions were the primary contributing factor to cell viability, both Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo significantly promoted cell proliferation compared with Zn. While twofold diluted extracts of Zn@Mg, relative to Zn@Mgo, further enhanced calcium deposition in MC3T3-E1 osteoblast-like cells without significantly altering MC3T3-E1 or HUVEC proliferation and migration.

Coupled bioactive and mechanical effects of 4DMM on in vivo bone regeneration

At 6 weeks post-implantation in rabbit femoral condyle bone defects of three scaffolds, lateral X-ray imaging revealed markedly increased bone density surrounding Zn@Mg, as indicated by red arrows in Fig. 4a. Micro-CT reconstruction confirmed preservation of scaffold architecture (Fig. 4b). In contrast, Zn scaffolds underwent severe localized degradation due to the absence of anodic protection from Mg, resulting in structural failure. Zn@Mgo scaffolds, lacking mechanical recovery capability, exhibited compressed deformation in vivo. Quantitative micro-CT analysis showed superior bone integration and osteogenesis for Zn@Mg compared with both Zn@Mgo and Zn scaffolds, at the implant–bone interface and within the scaffold pores (Fig. 4c–e). Specifically, BV/TV values for Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo exceeded those of Zn scaffolds, demonstrating the osteogenic contribution of Mg. Moreover, in the interfacial green ROI, BV/TV for Zn@Mg was significantly higher than for Zn@Mgo, while no significant difference was observed in the orange ROI, indicating that mechanical recovery of 4DMM enhanced interfacial bone formation. Histological evaluation with Masson–Goldner trichrome staining (Fig. 4f) confirmed that Zn@Mg induced the most robust bone regeneration at 6 weeks, with a pronounced increase in trabecular bone density. High-magnification images showed near-complete Mg degradation in Zn@Mg, accompanied by only sparse residual hydrogen gas, whereas Zn@Mgo retained both undegraded Mg and larger hydrogen pockets (Fig. S8). Meanwhile, sufficient bone regeneration indicates mechanical stability throughout the load-bearing period of Zn@Mg, accommodating the subsequent mechanical reconstruction associated with ourterior Zn degradation in the later stage.

Proteomic analysis was subsequently performed on bone tissues surrounding the implanted scaffolds (Fig. S9). Compared with Zn@Mgo and Zn (Fig. 4g), Zn@Mgo implantation reduced NF-κB-related protein expression while increasing the expression of MAPK pathway–associated proteins such as CACNB3. Further comparison between Zn@Mg and Zn@Mgo (Fig. 4h) revealed that pre-compressed Zn@Mg upregulated ECM–receptor interaction genes such as IBSP and increased the expression of Wnt pathway–related proteins. Heatmap analysis (Fig. 4i) confirmed activation of these signaling pathways, degradation of the internal Mg constraint released Mg²⁺ and other products that attenuated inflammation via NF-κB pathway suppression and promoted osteogenesis through MAPK pathway activation. Additionally, Zn@Mg enhanced ECM–receptor interaction gene expression and activated Wnt signaling pathways associated with mechanical stimulation-driven osteogenesis. Besides, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Fig. 4j) indicated upregulation of biological functions such as oxygen carrier activity and positive regulation of cell population proliferation, which help to mechanically stimulate bone regeneration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Zn@Mg 4DMM not only possess excellent biocompatibility and bioactivity derived from the base materials but also deliver mechanical stimulation that enhances bone regeneration (Fig. 4k), offering significant advantages for orthopedic implant applications.

Discussion

4D shape transformation holds considerable promise for biomedical engineering, yet research remains largely confined to diagnostic tools28,29 and wearable devices30,31, typically classified by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as Class I or II medical devices. Translation to Class III implantable devices presents greater challenges32,33, primarily due to the difficulty of delivering effective, long-term stimuli in vivo. Common triggers such as light, electric fields, and magnetic fields are attenuated by tissue, while others—pH, temperature, and humidity—are relatively stable within the body, demanding complex materials development to exploit them effectively (Fig. 5a). Biodegradable metals, which degrade in physiological fluids while releasing bioactive ions, represent an attractive alternative. Their degradation inherently provides a time-dependent stimulus, but uncontrolled degradation and unpredictable mechanical responses have so far prevented their integration into 4D strategies.

In this work, we introduce 4DMM with programmed pre-deformation, capable of recovering its original shape through controlled corrosion in physiological fluids. Unlike conventional 4D printed systems that rely on stimuli-responsive materials, the time-dependent behavior in 4DMM arises from the integration of three design elements, architected pre-deformation encoded in the metamaterial geometry, elastic energy storage within the deformable biometal body, and the deliberate pairing of metals with different degradation rate (DR) (Fig. 5b). Thus, 4DMM offers material flexibility far beyond that of SMAs and allows the selection of metals with optimal biocompatibility, harnessing both the mechanical strength of the metal material and the intrinsic bioactivity of degradable metals. Moreover, 4DMM provides mechanical stimuli comparable to similar SMA structures while achieving recovery times orders of magnitude longer compared with other 4D materials (Fig. 5c)34–38. Still, the shape transformation in 4DMM is irreversible due to the permanent degradation of the metal constraint. This characteristic is aligned with the requirements of temporary bone implants, where a one-time geometric change is sufficient to enhance mechanical integration and support bone regeneration. Notably, we observed upregulation of ECM–receptor interaction pathways in Zn@Mg compared with Zn@Mgo. Given that the provisional ECM formed during bone healing serves as a medium for cell adhesion and transmits mechanical signals, this finding suggests that the recovery force generated during degradation may synergize with the natural healing process to promote interfacial bone formation39.

Beyond bone regeneration, the modular 4DMM design offers additional functionalities in translating 4D shape transformation to the metal materials40. The degradation timeline can be tuned by material selection, and the platform could be extended to vascular stents, tendon-bone interface regeneration, drug delivery, and so on. Future work may also leverage multi-metal additive manufacturing to automate assembly and achieve multi-stage transformations. Meanwhile, the current 4DMM precision of shape recovery is achieved through macroscopic design and material selection; finer control via alloy optimization and further investigation under varied implantation conditions remain important next steps before clinical applications.

Methods

Ethics

All animal experimental protocols were conducted by the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board on Biomedical Ethics in the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Project Number: LA20222326).

Materials and additive manufacturing

We fabricated the Ti (Ti6Al4V), Zn (Zn-1Mg), and Mg (WE43) scaffolds using a laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) system (BLT S210, China) with an argon shielding chamber and a focused laser spot diameter of 70 µm. The Ti powder had a powder size distribution of 24–59 μm, the Zn powder ranged from 10–39 μm, and the Mg powder ranged from 15–53 μm. For Ti, a laser power of 280 W, scanning speed of 1200 mm/s, and layer thickness of 30 μm were used. For Zn, the parameters were laser power of 60 W, scanning speed of 400 mm/s, and layer thickness of 30 μm. For Mg, a laser power of 80 W, a scanning speed of 800 mm/s, and a layer thickness of 30 μm were applied. Typically, the geometry was designed using SolidWorks software (SolidWorks 2021, USA) and converted into a standard triangulation language (STL) file and transferred to the LPBF machine. After additive manufacturing, scaffolds were ultrasonically cleaned for 15 min in acetone, ethyl alcohol, and deionized water. Zn and Mg were chemically polished with acid solution composed of 5% HCl, 5% HNO3, and 90% C2H5OH for 120 s and 30 s, respectively. Mg constraints were passivated with high temperature oxidation to reduce the degradation rate and avoid violent pitting corrosion22. Afterward, the biodegradable constraints were manually integrated with the deformable biometal structures, forming the 4DMM. Specifically, the deformable bodies were designed with either helical, radial, or axial configurations to accommodate bending, expansion, or stretching deformation modes. While the constraints were tailored with the deformable bodies to enable controlled mechanical engagement, Precise geometric matching between the two components ensured uniform pre-deformation during assembly and predictable shape recovery upon constraint degradation.

In vitro biodegradation and mechanical tests

To qualitatively demonstrate shape recovery, the 4DMM was fully immersed in Hank’s solution at room temperature and recorded with a camera until recovery was complete. During this period, 10 vol% of fresh Hank’s solution was replenished every 6 h. In vitro quantitative biodegradation tests were performed on 4DMMs in Hank’s solution (at 37 °C, pH 7.4) according to ASTM-G31-72. The exposure ratio was set as 20 mL/cm2. Five duplicates were used at each observation to test weight loss with an analytical balance (Sartorius, Germany) and lengths or diameters with a micrometer screw. After degradation, the samples were cleaned using distilled water, and the corrosion surface was observed using SEM (Zeiss, Germany). The element composition was further examined using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. X-ray diffraction was applied (XRD, Rigku D/max-2550, Japan) with CuKα radiation at 40 kV and 150 mA, using a continuous scan mode at 4°/min over a range of 2θ = 10–90°. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (NAP-XPS, Specs, Germany) was also utilized to analyze the chemical composition of corrosion products. The C 1 s peak was calibrated by using the hydrocarbon contamination C 1 s peak at 284.5 eV. The scanning vibrating electrode technique (SVET) system (Applicable Electronics Inc., USA) was used to detect the dynamic current density of cross sections of 4D samples in Hank’s solution. Measurements were performed to measure the potential and current distribution with a 100 μm step size.

Recovery force was conducted on an all-electric dynamic test instrument (Instron E3000). A PMMA container and a PMMA gasket at the compression head location were utilized to avoid contact between samples and other metal materials. A recirculation system of Hank’s solution was heated to 37 °C in a water bath to simulate dynamic degradation. The compression head was carefully lowered into contact with the sample surface with pre-stress above 0 and less than 0.1 N. The mechanical test program was set with zero displacement, and 72 h of recovery force tests were then performed.

The finite element modeling (FEM) of Zn@Mg assembly was conducted on a 32-core and 64-thread CPU (Intel Xeon Gold 6226 R Processor) using Abaqus/Explicit software (Abaqus 6.14, Dassault Corp, French). The Mg constraint was rotated into the Zn scaffold in the simulation in the same manner as in the actual operation. Mg and Zn materials were set as homogeneous with a Poisson ratio of 0.3. Young’s modulus of Mg and Zn were set as 40 GPa and 80 GPa, respectively. The logarithmic elastic strain (LE) and von-Mise stress were used to analyze the interaction between two components.

Cell viability tests

MC3T3-E1 (SCSP-5218, NCACC, China) and HUVECs (CRL-1730, ATCC, USA) were used to evaluate the impact of materials on cell biocompatibility. The MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in MEM-α (12561056, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. HUVECs were cultured with DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Extracts of the scaffolds were prepared following the standardization method (ISO 10993-12). The scaffolds were first sterilized with UV light for 30 min on each side and then incubated in a medium with a volume-to-area ratio of 1.25 mL/cm² at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the supernatants were collected and stored at 4 °C. The concentrations of Zn and Mg ions in the alloys were measured using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, iCAP6300) analysis.

Cell proliferation was studied using 100 µL cell suspension. Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL for each well and allowed to attach for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with extracts of the materials, diluted from non- to fourfold (100–25% concentration), while culture medium and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide served as negative and positive controls, respectively. After incubating for 1, 3, and 5 days (MC3T3-E1) or 1 and 2 days (HUVECs), cell viability and proliferation were assessed using a cell viability kit (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan).

For fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Japan) observation, cells were stained following a Live/Dead staining procedure (Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit for Animal Live & Dead Cells (Calcein, AM EthD-I)). For confocal (CLSM, Leica TCS SP5, Germany) visualization, 1 mg/mL DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, China) and 1.0% v/v FITC–phalloidin (P5282, Sigma, Germany) were used to stain the nuclei and actin, respectively.

For alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and ARS (Alizarin Red S) staining test, after 14 days of culture in twofold diluted extraction, MC3T3-E1 cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. The BCIP/NBT ALP color development kit (CA1620, Solarbio, China) was used to stain ALP activity. For Alizarin Red S staining, Alizarin red solution (C0148, Beyotime, China) was used to stain calcium nodules for 10 min to analyze calcification characteristics. Image acquisition was subsequently performed, followed by quantitative analysis via ImageJ (NIH, Maryland, USA).

To measure the cell migration, cells were seeded in a six-well plate and cultured with normal medium for 48–72 h to reach confluency. A 10-μL lance tip was used to scratch along the line on the back side of the plate, with the tip kept vertical and not tilted. Then the damaged cells were washed with a PBS solution three times. Serum-free medium containing the corresponding extracts was added and cultured for 0 and 24 h, and then photographed. The cell-covered area was calculated using ImageJ software.

Application to orthopedic defects

In total, 18 adult New Zealand white rabbits (male, 25 weeks, 3.0 ± 0.3 kg) were purchased commercially (JMY-TZ-002, JinMu Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and randomly assigned into three groups (n = 6 per group) and subjected to surgery to create bone defects. The surgical procedures were performed with the rabbits under general anesthesia using pentobarbital sodium (30 mg kg−1, i.p.). Briefly, the right femurs of rabbits were exposed, and defects of 6 mm in length were created. 4DMM samples were implanted inside afterwards. Rabbits were sacrificed after 6 weeks, and their femurs were collected and fixed in 10% neutral formalin buffer for 24 h at room temperature. Then, radiographic analysis was performed using X-ray and micro-CT (Siemens, Germany). CT scanning rate was 6°/min with a resolution of 9 μm. The source voltage was 80 kV, and the beam current was 80 μA using filtered Bremsstrahlung radiation. The 3D micro-CT images were reconstructed using Mimics software (Mimics Medical 21.0, Materialise, Belgium). The peripheral 2-mm region around and the intra-porous space within the implant were selected as the regions of interest (ROIs). In the ROIs, the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) were calculated with Mimics software, where TV is the whole volume of green plus orange ROIs. Trabecular separations and trabecular thickness were calculated using a skeletal biology plug-in for ImageJ (BoneJ).

Histological sections were prepared with the axial direction. The specimens were fixed in 10% formalin for another 13 days and dehydrated in serial ethanol concentrations (40%, 75%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) for three days. The specimens were then embedded in methyl methacrylate (MMA) and sectioned with an EXAKT power saw with a diamond blade (EXAKT Apparatebau, Germany). This system was then used to prepare ground sections of 40–50 μm, which were then stained with Masson–Goldner trichrome. A NanoZoomer digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) was used to photograph the stained sections.

The new bone tissues of three specimens from each group were carefully separated right after sacrifice and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. After thorough grinding, the phenol extraction method was applied for protein extraction. The proteomics sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd., China. After the significance analysis, differentially expressed genes were selected for volcano and heatmap plots according to certain criteria (P value < 0.05 and fold change >2). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses by OECloud Tools. A schematic of related pathways was drawn with 3ds Max software (3ds Max 2020, USA).

Statistical analysis

The numerical data were analyzed using Origin 2019b (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). A two-tailed test was used to determine significant differences between the two groups. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n indicates number of independent samples). Besides, Ontologies included GO were selected based on enrichment scores, with a Fisher test P value of <0.05.
