General and Pediatric Dentists' Knowledge and Clinical Management of Molar-incisor Hypomineralization in Dubai: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: AlAnood AlAnezi, Manal Al Halabi, Iyad Hussein, Anas Salami, Eman Alnuaimi, Mawlood Kowash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMolar-incisor hypomineralization (MIH) poses notable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges for dental professionals. A comprehensive understanding of MIH among general dental practitioners (GDPs) and pediatric dentists (PDs) is critical to enhancing clinical outcomes in affected children.AimThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the knowledge, clinical management approaches, and self-reported confidence in treating MIH among GDPs and PDs practicing in Dubai.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey was conducted among licensed GDPs and PDs in Dubai. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research. A total of 126 participants completed a validated questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 84%. The questionnaire assessed participants' demographics, knowledge of MIH epidemiology, etiology, diagnosis, and clinical management.ResultsOf the respondents, 74.5% (n = 76) were female, with the majority aged 25–34 years (44.4%, n = 56). Approximately 31% (n = 42) had less than 5 years of clinical experience. PDs demonstrated significantly higher mean scores than GDPs across all domains: theoretical knowledge (86.46 vs 67.34), clinical management (70.63 vs 55.16), and overall MIH knowledge (77.66 vs 60.57), indicating greater competence and confidence in managing MIH cases.ConclusionWhile theoretical awareness of MIH was generally adequate among dental practitioners in Dubai, clinical confidence and management skills were notably stronger among PDs. These findings underscore the need for targeted MIH education and training, particularly within undergraduate programs and continuing professional development initiatives, to bridge the gap between knowledge and practice.How to cite this articleAlAnezi A, Al-Halabi M, Hussein I, et al. Introduction

Molar-incisor hypomineralization (MIH) is a developmental enamel defect affecting one or more first permanent molars (FPMs), frequently accompanied by affected permanent incisors.1 The condition is characterized by demarcated opacities, posteruptive enamel breakdown, and increased sensitivity, often leading to restorative challenges and compromised oral health-related quality of life in children.2,3 The global prevalence of MIH varies widely, ranging from 2.4% to 40.2%, depending on geographic location, diagnostic criteria, and population studied.4

Despite its clinical significance, MIH remains underdiagnosed and inconsistently managed, particularly in general dental practice settings.5 The etiology of MIH is multifactorial, involving systemic factors during the first 3 years of life, such as childhood illnesses, environmental toxins, and genetic predisposition.6,7 Early recognition and appropriate intervention are crucial to prevent progression and minimize the need for extensive restorative procedures.8

Pediatric dentists (PDs), due to their specialized training, are generally more familiar with MIH and its management protocols compared to general dental practitioners (GDPs).9 However, disparities in knowledge, diagnostic confidence, and treatment approaches between these groups may affect the quality of care provided to affected children.10 Understanding these differences is essential for developing targeted educational strategies and improving clinical outcomes.

This study aims to assess and compare the level of knowledge, clinical management practices, and self-reported confidence in treating MIH among GDPs and PDs in Dubai. By identifying gaps in understanding and practice, the findings may inform curriculum development and continuing professional education to enhance MIH-related care.

Materials and Methods

This was a prospective cross‑sectional study. Data were collected prospectively using a questionnaire distributed to licensed general dental practitioners and pediatric dentists during the study period. This cross-sectional study targeted licensed GDPs and PDs practicing in Dubai across government, private, and academic/teaching dental clinics and hospitals. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross-sectional studies. The study was conducted over a period of 9 months (January 2024–September 2024), during which data collection and statistical analysis were completed.11

Based on the most recent statistics in the United Arab Emirates,12 the total number of registered dentists was 6,800. According to the Dubai Health Authority Medical Registry, the number of GDPs and PDs was 4,420 and 207, respectively, totaling 4,627 eligible participants. Assuming a 50% prevalence of the factor of interest, a sample size of 116 was calculated to achieve a 9% absolute precision and 95% confidence level.

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire from a previously validated survey, with permission granted for its use.2 The instrument comprised three main sections and was designed to be completed in under 10 minutes. The first section gathered sociodemographic data, including age, years of practice, work sector, and country of dental qualification. The second section included 11 items assessing perceived knowledge of MIH. The third section presented three clinical scenarios involving MIH, requiring participants to select the most appropriate treatment option.

The survey was distributed exclusively via WhatsApp, targeting professional groups such as the Emirates Pediatric Dentistry Club and the British Dentists in the UAE. Additionally, QR codes linked to the questionnaire were displayed at national dental conferences and professional gatherings in Dubai. An estimated 150 participants were approached, based on confirmed dissemination through these channels. Due to the informal nature of distribution, the exact number of GDPs and PDs who received the link could not be verified; however, the estimate reflects a conservative and documented outreach.

Participants were instructed to review three clinical images and respond to questions regarding MIH knowledge, clinical presentation, and management strategies. The clinical scenarios included (1) a severe MIH case with posteruptive breakdown, for which a preformed metal crown was the optimal treatment; (2) a partially erupted molar with moderate MIH, best managed with glass ionomer cement (GIC); and (3) a moderately hypomineralized incisor in a 9-year-old child, ideally treated with microabrasion.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice (GCP), and UAE regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee (MBRU IRB-2023-226).

Data were analyzed using SPSS for Windows, version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical and ordinal variables. Cross-tabulations were employed to assess associations between categorical variables. Given the nature of the data. Two types of knowledge were evaluated. Perceived knowledge: participants' self-reported confidence and awareness (e.g., responses to “how confident do you feel diagnosing MIH?”). Assessed knowledge: responses to objective questions on MIH etiology, diagnosis, and treatment.

Molar-incisor hypomineralization knowledge scores were calculated from responses to theoretical and clinical management questions. Theoretical knowledge was scored out of 12 points, and clinical management out of 15 points. Scores for the clinical scenarios were based on the appropriateness of selected treatments.

Nonparametric tests, including chi-square, were used for categorical data analysis. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare ordinal data between GDPs and PDs. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pediatric dentists consistently scored higher than GDPs across all MIH-related domains: theoretical knowledge: PDs (86.46) vs GDPs (67.34); clinical management: PDs (70.63) vs GDPs (55.16); and overall, MIH knowledge score: PDs (77.66) vs GDPs (60.57).

No statistically significant differences were found between male and female participants in MIH theoretical knowledge, clinical management, or overall knowledge scores. Both genders demonstrated comparable levels of understanding and confidence in managing MIH.

Analysis across age groups revealed no significant differences in MIH-related knowledge or clinical management scores. Although participants aged 35–44 years had the highest theoretical scores (mean = 80.08), and those under 25 years scored highest in clinical management (mean = 68.33), these variations were not statistically significant.

No significant differences were observed in MIH knowledge or management scores based on the country where participants obtained their dental degrees. Graduates from non-Arab universities had slightly higher theoretical scores (mean = 82.78), while UAE graduates scored higher in clinical management (mean = 64.81), but these differences were not statistically significant.

A statistically significant difference was found in theoretical MIH knowledge scores across experience levels. Participants with 11–15 years of experience had the highest theoretical scores (mean = 82.54), while those with less than 5 years scored lowest (mean = 71.43) (p < 0.01). However, no significant differences were observed in clinical management or overall MIH knowledge scores across experience groups.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study assessed the knowledge, self-reported confidence, and clinical management practices related to MIH among GDPs and PDs in Dubai. The findings revealed that PDs demonstrated significantly higher levels of theoretical knowledge, clinical confidence, and appropriate management strategies for MIH compared to GDPs.

To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study conducted in Dubai to explore MIH-related knowledge and practices among both GDPs and PDs. As such, it provides valuable insights into the current state of MIH awareness and highlights the need for targeted educational interventions to bridge the gap between knowledge and clinical practice.

Our study utilized a validated questionnaire originally developed by Alanzi et al.2 to assess MIH-related knowledge and practices among Kuwaiti dentists, ensuring methodological consistency and comparability. The study achieved a robust response rate of 84%, reflecting strong engagement from the dental community in Dubai. This high participation rate underscores the relevance of MIH as a clinical concern and the willingness of practitioners to contribute to research aimed at improving pediatric oral health.

The findings revealed significant differences between the two groups, with PDs consistently demonstrating superior theoretical understanding, diagnostic confidence, and clinical decision-making in managing MIH cases. These results align with previous studies conducted in Kuwait, Spain, Australia, Chile, Hong Kong, and Saudi Arabia, which similarly reported higher MIH competence among PDs.10,13–15

While overall awareness of MIH was high (92.1%), the depth of understanding varied. PDs were more familiar with MIH's clinical features and diagnostic criteria, likely due to their specialized training and frequent exposure to pediatric patients. GDPs, on the other hand, showed lower confidence and knowledge, often citing insufficient training and limited clinical exposure as barriers to effective diagnosis and management.2,16 This gap underscores the need for targeted educational interventions and curriculum enhancements to better prepare GDPs for managing MIH.

Treatment preferences also differed significantly between the groups. PDs favored SSCs for severe MIH cases and minimally invasive options such as ICON® resin infiltration and microabrasion for mild cases. GDPs were more inclined toward composite restorations and showed greater uncertainty in treatment selection, particularly for mild MIH. These findings are consistent with studies by Serna-Muñoz et al., Delgado et al., and Dian et al., which highlighted PDs' preference for long-term and esthetic solutions, while GDPs often opted for conventional restorative approaches.10,13,17

Challenges in MIH management were also evident. Achieving effective local anesthesia was reported as a major difficulty, especially among PDs, due to the hypersensitivity of MIH-affected teeth. This issue has been widely documented in the literature and is known to impact both practitioner confidence and patient cooperation.3,14,16 Additionally, many GDPs reported insufficient training in MIH management, reinforcing the need for continuing education and clinical workshops focused on pediatric dental conditions.

Regarding sources of MIH knowledge, PDs primarily relied on continuing education programs and dental journals, while GDPs reported more varied sources, including the internet and books. This difference reflects PDs' stronger engagement with structured learning and their recognition of MIH as a routine clinical concern. Studies by Silva et al. and Gómez-Clavel et al. support this trend, noting that PDs are more proactive in seeking evidence-based resources.9,18

A significant proportion of respondents expressed a need for comprehensive training in all aspects of MIH management. While some prioritized treatment-specific education, fewer participants emphasized diagnosis and etiology. PDs were generally more confident and knowledgeable, whereas GDPs showed a greater demand for further training. These findings echo previous research indicating that MIH is underrepresented in undergraduate curricula, particularly for general dentistry students.9,17

Analysis of demographic variables revealed no significant differences in MIH knowledge or management based on gender, age, or country of graduation. This aligns with findings from studies conducted in Portugal and Egypt, where demographic factors such as age and gender did not significantly influence MIH knowledge levels among dental professionals.13,19

However, years of clinical experience showed a positive correlation with theoretical knowledge, suggesting that practical exposure contributes to improved familiarity with MIH. In the Egyptian study, PDs and those with postgraduate qualifications demonstrated significantly higher knowledge scores compared to GDPs, indicating that clinical exposure and advanced education enhance understanding of MIH.19

This reinforces the importance of continuous professional development and clinical mentorship in enhancing diagnostic and treatment capabilities. The European Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (EAPD) emphasizes ongoing education and updated clinical guidelines to improve MIH management outcomes.20 Moreover, mentorship has been identified as a key component of professional development, offering personalized guidance, knowledge transfer, and emotional support that collectively foster clinical competence and confidence.21,22

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its cross-sectional design. The use of an electronic, self-administered questionnaire may introduce response and recall bias, and self-reported data may not accurately reflect actual clinical practices. The nonprobability sampling and voluntary participation limit the generalizability of findings to the broader dental community.

The study was conducted exclusively in Dubai, which may restrict the applicability of the results to other regions. Additionally, some survey questions lacked specificity—such as the type of tooth or severity of MIH—potentially leading to variability in interpretation. Lastly, while the study assessed educational preferences, it did not directly measure participants' clinical competence or adherence to MIH management guidelines.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the knowledge, confidence, and clinical management of MIH among GDPs and PDs in Dubai. PDs demonstrated significantly higher awareness and clinical competence, likely due to their specialized training and frequent exposure to pediatric cases.

Despite high overall awareness, GDPs showed notable gaps in diagnostic confidence and treatment decision-making. Challenges such as achieving effective anesthesia and selecting appropriate restorative materials were more common among GDPs, highlighting the need for targeted education.

To improve MIH-related outcomes, it is recommended that undergraduate curricula and continuing professional development programs incorporate focused training on MIH diagnosis and management. Emphasis should be placed on evidence-based treatment strategies and practical case-based learning. Future research should expand to other regions and include objective assessments of clinical competence to validate and build upon these findings.
