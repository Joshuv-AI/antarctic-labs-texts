Dual-Curved Cold-Plate Integration for Enhanced Liquid-Based Thermal Management of Cylindrical Li-Ion Batteries
Authors: Temesgen Abera Takiso, Jianwu Yu, Zhihao Zhang, Yongzheng Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid-cold-plate-based battery thermal management systems (BTMSs) are widely adopted in new-energy vehicles (NEVs) due to their high heat-transfer efficiency and cost-effectiveness; however, their application to cylindrical batteries is limited by curvature-induced compatibility issues. To address this challenge, a novel dual-curved thermally conductive cold plate (TCCP) integrated with a liquid-based BTMS is proposed, providing an efficient and compact heat-transfer interface for cylindrical battery modules. The thermal behavior of the battery system is comprehensively evaluated under a high discharge rate of 5 C, with particular focus on the effects of the discharge rate, coolant flow speed (CFS), and TCCP structural parameters (including channel diameter, height, and contact angle). The results show that the single-channel dual-curved TCCP maintains the peak temperature and temperature variation below 310.39 and 4.40 K, respectively, at a CFS of 0.09 m/s. Furthermore, an optimized dual-channel TCCP-based BTMS is developed and evaluated against the single-channel design, demonstrating reductions in maximum temperature and temperature variation by 0.17 K and 7.50%, respectively, while simultaneously lowering CFS and weight ratio by 44.40 and 6.67%. Overall, the proposed dual-channel TCCP significantly enhances the thermal management performance and energy efficiency, offering a promising pathway for extending the cycle life and mileage of NEVs.

Introduction

The rapid global expansion of NEVs offers a promising solution to mitigate environmental damage and global warming caused by fossil-fuel-based energy use. LIBs have emerged as the primary power source for NEVs compared to other types of rechargeable batteries, owing to their advantages, including high energy density, rapid charging capability, long cycle life, and a lower self-discharge rate. − The ongoing advancement of LIBs continues to improve their application scope and efficiency, making them the cornerstone of new-energy solutions. The key components of the LIB are the anode, cathode, electrolyte, and the separator. Lithium ions travel from the anode via the electrolyte to the cathode, producing electrical energy during the discharge. In contrast, the process switches or reverses during charging, with lithium ions returning to the anode. LIBs come in various geometric models including cylindrical, pouch, and prismatic types. Each model is designed to meet specific requirements and applications based on factors such as safety, cost, and energy density. Cylindrical cell models offer greater structural stability, enhanced durability, and comparatively simpler manufacturing processes than their prismatic and pouch counterparts. ,

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations, with performance, safety, and lifespan critically dependent on maintaining an optimal operating temperature (OOT), typically 15–35 °C, with temperature gradients below 5 °C. ,, During charge and discharge, heat from Joule heating, entropic reactions, side reactions, and mixing can raise cell temperature, risking efficiency loss, accelerated degradation, and safety hazards, including thermal runaway. , Efficient battery thermal management systems (BTMS) are therefore essential to ensure reliability, extend cycle life, and enable safe operation, particularly for NEVs and renewable energy storage. Common BTMS technologies include liquid-based, ,, air-based, − PCM-based, − heat pipe-based, − and hybrid configurations. − Each presents trade-offs: air-based systems are simple, lightweight, and low-cost but limited in heat dissipation; PCM-based systems store heat efficiently but suffer from low thermal conductivity; and heat pipe systems offer passive transfer but incur high cost and design complexity. Effective BTMS design must balance thermal performance, energy efficiency, weight, space, and cost.

Among existing technologies, liquid-based BTMSs have emerged as the most effective and widely adopted solution due to their high heat-transfer coefficient, large contact area, and strong capability to manage high thermal loads. Various coolants, including water, glycol, oils, nanofluids, acetone, and liquid metals, have been used to achieve the desired thermal performance. Compared with air- and PCM-based systems, liquid-based BTMSs offer superior heat dissipation and maintain battery stability under high charge/discharge rates and elevated temperatures. Consequently, it has become the mainstream thermal management strategy for large-scale power batteries in NEVs and is widely implemented by leading manufacturers such as Tesla, BYD, BMW, and Hyundai. ,,,

Ongoing research has advanced liquid-based BTMS, which are generally classified into direct contact cooling (DCC) and indirect contact cooling (ICC) systems. DCC allows direct coolant–battery interaction and offers high TMP, but it poses risks such as leakage and coolant contamination. In contrast, ICC employs an intermediate heat-dissipating component, typically a cold plate, preventing direct contact and enabling the use of a wider range of coolants. Owing to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and reliability, ICC is widely adopted in practical applications. However, inadequate cold-plate design may introduce additional thermal resistance and cause temperature nonuniformity. Hence, optimizing cooling structure, compactness, and contact area is essential for improving ICC performance. For instance, Li et al. demonstrated that a liquid-cooled plate with diamond-shaped channels significantly enhanced heat dissipation and reduced power consumption, achieving an effective balance between cooling performance and system efficiency.

Cold-plate-based BTMS for rectangular batteries, such as prismatic and pouch cells, are well-studied and widely implemented. For instance, Hwang et al. employed ANSYS software to investigate the TMP of prismatic LIBs cooled by double cold plates, analyzing the influence of cooling channel designs under various discharge rates. Their results confirmed that optimizing the cold-plate structure significantly enhances BTMS efficiency. Similarly, Mubashir et al. introduced a novel cold-plate design with uniform circular fins for prismatic batteries, demonstrating improved thermal performance by achieving a maximum temperature of 29.87 °C and a temperature difference of 3.52 °C under a 3 C discharge rate. In another study, Wang et al. proposed a butterfly-shaped channel cold plate for a 50 Ah prismatic battery module, successfully maintaining peak temperature and temperature variation below 30.86 and 4.96 °C, respectively, at a 2 C discharge rate with a 5 g/s mass flow rate. Collectively, these studies highlight that optimizing cold-plate geometry and structural parameters can substantially improve heat-transfer efficiency, promote uniform temperature distribution, and minimize both temperature rise and pressure drop in battery modules.

Conversely, the design and application of cold-plate-based systems for cylindrical batteries remain limited due to compatibility challenges associated with their curved surfaces, which hinder efficient thermal contact. This often results in insufficient heat exchange between the cells and cooling plates. To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored specialized cold-plate designs. For example, Wang et al. developed a modular cold-plate-based liquid-cooling system for cylindrical battery modules, employing both numerical simulations and experimental investigations. Their study examined the effects of coolant flow rate and cooling configurations (series versus parallel) on the thermal management performance of the battery module under a 3 C discharge condition. The results revealed that, compared to the series configuration, the parallel cooling mode improved temperature uniformity, whereas increasing the coolant flow rate yielded only marginal reductions in the peak cell temperature. Likewise, Zhao et al. evaluated the performance of serpentine channel cold plates for cylindrical battery packs. By shortening the flow path and enlarging the contact surface area between the channels and battery cells, the optimized design significantly improved the temperature uniformity, as confirmed by numerical simulations.

Chen et al. proposed an innovative cold-plate configuration based on topology optimization to enhance the thermal management efficiency of BTMS for cylindrical battery modules. Three cold-plate designs with varying inlet and outlet configurations were developed to investigate the effects of coolant flow rate, cold-plate thickness, and the number of cold plates. Under a discharge rate of 3C and an inlet flow rate of 2.5 × 10–6 m3/s, the peak temperature and temperature variation across the battery module were maintained at 30.90 and 4.87 °C, respectively. The results further demonstrated that the TMP of the liquid-based cooling system improved significantly with increases in both cold-plate thickness and number. Similarly, You et al. and Zhao et al. examined the TMP of cold-plate-based cooling strategies for cylindrical lithium-ion batteries under various cooling configurations and operational conditions. Their findings indicated that optimizing the cold-plate structure ensures safe operating temperatures, while higher inlet flow rates markedly enhance TMP and extend battery cycle life. However, although many studies have confirmed that increasing inlet flow rate improves cold-plate performance, excessively high flow rates can negatively impact the overall efficiency of the BTMS. ,,

The aforementioned studies have significantly advanced the development of cold-plate-based BTMSs for cylindrical lithium-ion batteries. Nevertheless, limited attention has been devoted to cold-plate configurations that simultaneously incorporate dual-curved geometries and multichannel coolant pathways specifically designed to conform to cylindrical cell surfaces. Existing cold-plate designs often face challenges associated with limited thermal contact area, nonuniform heat dissipation, and increased cooling requirements under high-rate operating conditions. Although curved cold plates can improve battery-to-cold-plate contact and enhance heat-transfer and multichannel configurations can improve coolant distribution and cooling effectiveness, the combined application of these two design concepts remains largely unexplored. ,

To bridge this research gap, this study proposes a novel dual-curved, dual-channel thermally conductive cold plate (TCCP) for a liquid-cooled BTMS. The novelty of the proposed design lies in the integration of (i) a dual-curved structure that increases the effective thermal contact area between the cold plate and cylindrical battery cells and (ii) a dual-channel flow configuration that enhances coolant distribution while reducing the coolant flow rate required for effective thermal regulation. This integrated design aims to improve heat dissipation, temperature uniformity, and thermal management performance while maintaining a compact and lightweight configuration suitable for new-energy vehicle applications. To evaluate the proposed approach, a lightweight TCCP is first developed and integrated into a liquid-cooled BTMS. Subsequently, numerical simulations are conducted to investigate the effects of the discharge rate, coolant flow speed (CFS), and key structural parameters, including TCCP diameter, height, and thermal contact angle, on the TMP, energy consumption, and system weight. Furthermore, the thermal and hydraulic performance of the proposed dual-channel TCCP configuration is systematically assessed and compared to that of the single-channel TCCP as well as established BTMS solutions documented in the literature. The findings of this study provide new insights into the design and optimization of multichannel cold-plate-based BTMSs for cylindrical LIBs and offer practical guidance for enhancing battery performance, safety, and driving range in NEVs.

Numerical Simulation

This study employed CFD software, ANSYS Workbench Fluent, to study the comprehensive thermal behavior of lithium-ion batteries through 3D numerical computations based on the finite-volume method (FVM). ANSYS Fluent offers two battery modeling approaches: the single-potential empirical battery model (EBM) and the dual-potential multi-scale multi-domain (MSMD) model. , The EBM is computationally efficient but limited to solving a single potential equation, which restricts its ability to capture detailed electrochemical reactions and degradation phenomena, particularly in batteries with complex geometries. In contrast, the MSMD model employs a physics-based, multi-scale framework that couples electrochemical, thermal, and electrical domains, thereby enabling more accurate predictions of battery behavior and performance under realistic operating conditions.

In this study, a dual-potential MSMD model is employed, treating the battery as a continuous medium with directionally dependent (orthotropic) properties such as electrical and thermal conductivity. The Newman P2D electrochemical thermal model is implemented to capture the coupled electrochemical and thermal behavior of the cell. In the simulation, the NCR18650BD cylindrical battery is modeled with positive and negative electrodes, current collectors, a separator, and a central rolled mandrel. To minimize computational expense, the computational domain was confined to a single cell, positioned symmetrically between four TCCPs, as depicted in Figure c. Table presents the thermal and physical properties of the materials used in this study. The Reynolds number corresponding to the CFSs investigated in this study (0.01–0.20 m/s) was calculated to range from approximately 200 to 600. These values lie well within the laminar-flow regime for internal channel flows. Therefore, the coolant flow within the TCCP channels was considered as laminar throughout the scenario, and no turbulence model was applied. This modeling approach is consistent with previously established cooling configurations operating under low-Reynolds-number. A transient model is employed to capture the unsteady heat generation behavior of the battery. A pressure-based solver is selected to compute battery parameters and evaluate their influence on the system’s TMP. To achieve accurate pressure-velocity coupling, the SIMPLE algorithm is applied. Spatial discretization is performed using least-squares cell-based for gradients, second-order for pressure, and second-order upwind schemes for momentum and energy. All governing equations are solved iteratively, with under-relaxation factors of 0.7 for momentum, 0.3 for pressure, and 1.0 for energy. A second-order implicit scheme is used for the transient formulation to ensure numerical stability and reliable convergence. The simulation is executed with a time-step size of 1 s, a maximum of 20 iterations per time step, and convergence criteria of 10–5 for continuity and 10–6 for velocity and energy equations.

Boundary Conditions and Modeling Assumptions

In the current study, the fluid inlet was specified as a velocity-inlet boundary condition, while the channel outlet was defined as a pressure outlet with a gauge pressure of 0 Pa. The inlet coolant temperature and ambient temperature were both set to 298.15 K. To reduce computational complexity while maintaining the essential heat-transfer characteristics of the BTMS, the interface between the cylindrical battery cell and the TCCP was modeled as a thermally coupled wall. Ideal thermal contact was assumed; therefore, thermal contact resistance at the battery–TCCP interface was neglected. Moreover, radiative heat transfer was not considered in the present study. These assumptions are widely adopted in numerical studies of liquid-based BTMSs and are considered reasonable for systems operating within the temperature range investigated in this work, where heat transfer is mainly governed by conduction and convection. The interfaces between the TCCP and the coolant were also defined as thermally coupled walls to enable conjugate heat transfer between the solid and fluid domains. A no-slip boundary condition was imposed at all fluid–solid interfaces, consistent with conventional liquid-cooling applications. The external surface of the battery cell was subjected to natural convection with a heat-transfer coefficient of 5 W/m2K. All remaining walls of the cooling system were assumed to be adiabatic.

Grid Independence Evaluation

A grid independence study was conducted to ensure the reliability of the numerical simulations and to identify an appropriate mesh resolution. The evaluation was performed using a TCCP configuration with a contact angle of 60°, a height of 65 mm, and a diameter of 1.7 mm, under boundary conditions of an inlet temperature of 298.15 K, a discharge rate of 2 C, and a coolant flow velocity of 0.01 m/s. The maximum battery temperature was selected as the evaluation metric. The computational domain was discretized using a hybrid mesh of tetrahedral and hexahedral elements, with local edge refinement applied in both fluid and solid regions to enhance accuracy near critical interfaces, as shown in Figure a.

(a) Computational grid model structure. (b) Mesh number independence test.

Six mesh sizes ranging from 8,85,028 885,028 to 2 3,44,476 2,344,476 elements were examined. As illustrated in Figure b, increasing the grid size from 8,85,028 885,028 to 12,21,276 1,221,276 elements reduced the maximum battery temperature from 308.720 to 308.396 K, corresponding to a relative deviation of 0.105%. When the grid size exceeded 13,11,373 1,311,373 elements, the relative deviation fell below 0.0302%, indicating mesh-independent results. Consequently, a mesh with 13,11,373 1,311,373 elements was selected as an optimal compromise between the computational cost and accuracy. The minimum orthogonal quality and maximum skewness of the selected mesh were 0.3066 and 0.7464, respectively, confirming an adequate mesh quality for reliable simulations.

Results and Discussion

This study presents a dual-curved, dual-channeled TCCP integrated with a liquid-based BTMS for cylindrical LIBs, targeting enhanced TMP under a 5 C discharge rate. This section evaluates the influence of the discharge C-rate, CFS, and TCCP structural parameters on the thermal regulation of the BTMS. The cooling performances of single-channel and dual-channel TCCP configurations are comparatively analyzed. The overall system performance is evaluated in terms of the maximum temperature, temperature difference, and pressure drop.

Impacts of Discharge C-Rates

In this section, the influence of discharge C-rates on the battery temperature and the TMP of the proposed cooling system is investigated by simulating four cases ranging from 2 to 5 C in 1 C increments, as illustrated in Figure . The simulations were conducted at six different CFS, ranging from 0.01 to 0.2 m/s, using a TCCP with a diameter (d) of 1.7 mm. The height (h) and thermal contact angle (α) of the TCCP were set to 65 mm and 52°, respectively.

Effect of various discharge C-rates on the battery temperatures: (a) Maximum temperature. (b) Temperature variation.

Figure a,b shows the battery peak temperature and temperature variation, respectively, at various discharge rates. As the discharge rate increases, the peak temperature increases significantly. At a CFS of 0.2 m/s, the battery peak temperature under a 5 C discharge is 9.75 K higher than that under a 2 C discharge. This behavior is primarily attributed to a substantial increase in internal heat generation and the resulting rise in battery temperature, which is mostly associated with the irreversible heat generation rate and leads to a notably higher thermal load at higher discharge rates. Consequently, the heat generation rate exceeds the cooling system’s dissipation rate, causing thermal accumulation to challenge the cooling system.

Figure b further shows that the temperature variation across the battery increases with higher discharge rates. At a CFS of 0.2 m/s, the temperature variation rises by 2.57 K when the discharge rate increases from 2 to 5 C, indicating a deterioration in thermal uniformity and an increase in the thermal gradients within the battery cell. These effects can be attributed to the nonuniform distribution of generated heat and poor heat conduction within the battery under high-rate operating conditions, thereby making heat accumulation more pronounced and resulting in higher peak temperatures and greater temperature variation within the battery. These results show that higher discharge rates degrade the battery temperature and uniformity, underscoring the need for effective BTMS under high-power conditions to ensure safe, reliable operation within recommended temperature limits and suppress thermal gradients.

However, increasing the CFS effectively mitigated this thermal effect. For instance, when a CFS of 0.01 m/s is used, increasing the discharge rate from 2 to 5 C results in a 16.6 % increase in peak battery temperature (from 307.82 to 358.92 K). In contrast, at a CFS of 0.2 m/s, the same increase in discharge rate causes only a 3.25 % rise in peak temperature (from 299.94 to 309.69 K). Thus, this demonstrates that higher CFS substantially enhances the heat removal capability of the TCCP-based BTMS. The improved thermal performance at higher CFSs can be attributed to the increased convective heat transfer between the coolant and TCCP. Overall, higher discharge rates increase battery heat generation; however, appropriate coolant flow management can significantly mitigate these effects. Thus, balancing operating conditions with the cooling capacity is essential for optimal thermal conditions, safety, and battery longevity.

Effects of the Coolant Flow Speed (CFS)

As shown in Figure a in Section , during a discharge rate of 5 C, the battery temperature increases significantly, accompanied by a highly nonuniform thermal distribution within the cell. Such thermal nonuniformity can adversely affect overall battery performance, including efficiency, lifespan, and safety. Therefore, the subsequent sections present additional simulations conducted at the same high discharge rate (5 C) under varying operational scenarios to further investigate these effects and assess the potential mitigation strategies.

This section examines the impact of CFS on the performance of a liquid-based cooling system integrated with dual-curved TCCPs. The simulations were conducted at nine different CFS values to quantify the trade-offs between thermal regulation, energy efficiency, and operational feasibility. In these simulations, other TCCP parameters, including diameter, contact surface angle, and height, were set to 2 mm, 60°, and 65 mm, respectively. Consequently, the peak temperature and temperature variation decrease by 7.74 and 93.6%, respectively, when the CFS increases from 0.01 to 0.2 m/s.

However, the pressure drop at 0.2 m/s is more than double that at 0.09 m/s, which significantly affects cooling efficiency. Excessively increased CFS are not cost-effective, as they require high-power consumption to cool the battery system.

Hence, to design an efficient TMS, the optimal CFS should be determined on the basis of a balanced consideration of energy consumption, TMP, and the overall efficiency of the BTMS. The results demonstrate that the proposed dual-curved TCCP-based cooling system effectively regulates battery operating temperatures while maintaining low coolant flow rates, making it an energy efficient solution. Accordingly, a CFS of 0.09 m/s was adopted for further simulations in this study.

Conclusions

A dual-curved, adaptable TCCP integrated with a liquid-based BTMS is proposed for cylindrical LIBs to maintain thermal conditions within safe limits and enhance the battery TMP in NEVs. The Newman P2D model is used to analyze the thermal behavior of a module of 18,650 cells under a 5 C discharge. A compact dual-curved TCCP is designed to improve heat dissipation, and the effects of the discharge rate, CFS, and TCCP structural parameters, including diameter, height, and contact angle, on BTMS performance are systematically evaluated. Finally, a dual-curved, dual-channel TCCP is developed, and its cooling efficiency is compared to that of the single-channel design.

The major conclusions are presented as follows:(1)The dual-curved adaptable TCCP structure coupled to the liquid-based BTMS demonstrated effective thermal management by maintaining cell temperatures within the optimum operational range. The study results indicate that the battery’s peak temperature and temperature variation can be controlled below 310.39 and 4.4 K, respectively, at a CFS of 0.09 m/s.(2)An increase in the CFS value significantly improved the TMP of the battery system. However, increases beyond the optimal range yield diminishing benefits and a proportional increase in power consumption, thereby reducing practical viability. The findings revealed that when the CFS exceeds 0.08 m/s, both the peak temperature and temperature nonuniformity can be maintained within safe operating thresholds. However, increasing the CFS to 0.2 m/s introduces a trade-off between an enhanced cooling performance and a substantially higher pressure-drop penalty.(3)Evaluation of the TCCP structural parameters indicates that a moderate reduction in TCCP height decreases temperature variation and system weight, albeit with a slight increase in maximum temperature, whereas excessive reduction in TCCP height diminishing the thermal regulation capability and causes the peak temperature to exceed acceptable limits. Increasing the TCCP diameter significantly improves TMP. Specifically, increasing the diameter from 1.7 to 3 mm leads to a notable reduction in peak temperature, temperature difference, and pressure drop of 1, 66.67, and 68.05%, respectively. A larger surface contact angle further lowers both the peak temperature and temperature difference, but increases system weight. Based on these trade-offs, the optimal TCCP configuration in this study comprises a height of 65 mm, a diameter of 2 mm, and a contact angle of 60°.(4)Compared with the single-channel TCCP-based liquid BTMS, the dual-channel TCCP configuration exhibits a superior TMP. The optimized dual-curved, dual-channel TCCP maintains a maximum temperature of 310.22 K and a temperature difference of 4.07 K at a low CFS of 0.05 m/s, which is within safe operating limits for LIBs. The improved thermal performance is attributed to the enhanced heat-transfer capability of the dual-channel TCCP, driven by more-uniform coolant flow distribution and an increased heat exchange area, while lowering the pressure drop.
