Molecular-Scale Study of the Interfacial Effects of Kinetic Inhibitors on Hydrate Growth and Crystal Stability
Authors: Huiyong Liang, Peizhuo Mo, Haihong Chen, Rui Qin, Shi Shen, Xin Lv, Xingbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kinetic hydrate inhibitors (KHIs) have shown significant potential in preventing hydrate blockages and ensuring flow assurance by delaying hydrate nucleation and aggregation. However, the molecular mechanisms by which KHIs influence the early-stage growth and stability of hydrate fluids under oil and gas flow conditions remain insufficiently understood, limiting the optimization and development of more efficient formulations. In this paper, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to systematically investigate the regulatory effects of two representative KHIspolyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and polyvinylcaprolactam (PVCap)on gas hydrate prevention; and the interaction mechanism between the molecules was elucidated by combining molecular dynamics simulations with Density Functional Theory (DFT). Key parameters, including the number of cage structures, the water molecule ordering parameters, and the potential energy, were also analyzed to assess the inhibitors’ impacts on gas hydrate growth and fluid stability. The results reveal a triple synergistic mechanism involving site-blocking, mass transfer interference, and hydrogen-bond disruption, which provides a theoretical basis for the design and optimization of high-efficiency KHIs in the traditional fossil fuel transportation, particularly in oil and gas flowlines.

Introduction

With the development of offshore oil resources, the exploitation of deepwater oil and gas resources has gradually become an important direction for the development of the global oil and gas industry. , There are numerous flow risks in deepwater oil and gas pipelines, among which solid-phase sediments such as gas hydrate and paraffin constitute the main flow obstacles in the development and transmission process of deepwater oil and gas fields, and the accumulation of these substances not only affects the transmission efficiency, but also may lead to poor flow in the pipeline, or even clogging. Since deepwater oil and gas extraction operations are usually located in a low-temperature and high-pressure deep-sea environment, this happens to provide ideal external conditions for the rapid formation of hydrates. Once hydrates are generated in the pipeline, they will rapidly agglomerate, adhere, settle, clog and a series of other complex processes. If they are not detected and cleaned in time, they can cause safety problems such as restricted flow paths, clogged pipelines, , and damaged equipment, as well as huge economic losses.

To address the above problems, two hydrate control methods, thermodynamic control and kinetic control, are commonly used in deepwater oil and gas extraction operations. Thermodynamic control breaks the thermodynamic equilibrium of hydrates by changing the environmental conditions required for hydrate formation, such as temperature and pressure. Common methods include heating, pressure reduction, and thermodynamic inhibitor injection. However, these types of controls are costly to use, especially with thermodynamic inhibitors, which require the injection of large amounts of inhibitors to ensure their effective functioning. In contrast, kinetic control has been widely noticed and used due to its advantages such as low cost and effectiveness. Different from thermodynamic methods, kinetic methods can effectively inhibit hydrate formation mainly by adsorbing on the surface of hydrate, occupying the nucleation sites of hydrates, and hindering the formation of an ordered hydrogen bonding network structure of water molecules. A frequently used method is the injection of kinetic inhibitors, such as polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and polyvinylcaprolactam (PVCap), which are susceptible to failure due to subcooling. The central goal of both thermodynamic and kinetic control is to inhibit hydrate nucleation and growth.

Zhang et al. investigated the synergistic inhibition mechanism of PVCap and leucine (Leu) by molecular dynamics simulations, and the results showed that the 1 wt % PVCap +2 wt % Leu system could completely inhibit the nucleation of methane hydrate for a longer duration. Wei et al. used molecular dynamics simulations to study the effect of three modified polymers, polyacrylamide (PAM), hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM), and poly­(acrylic acid) (PAA), on the nucleation of methane hydrate, and the results showed that these polymers inhibit methane hydrate nucleation by occupying the active sites on the surface of the hydrate, and then adsorbing methane molecules in the solution and forming hydrogen bonds with the water molecules. Li et al. used molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the synergistic mechanisms of kinetic inhibitors (KHI) and thermodynamic inhibitors (THI), and the results showed that the whole synergistic process of KHI and THI mainly consists of three phases: gas adsorption, water displacement disruption, and spatial site resistance, where the first phase usually dominates the whole inhibition process and weakens the driving force of hydrate formation. Castillo-Borja et al. used molecular dynamics simulations to study the effect of a total of six different types of gas hydrate inhibitors (methanol, ethylene glycol, two nonprotonic ionic liquids, EMIM-Cl and EMIM-NO3, and two protonated ionic liquids, EAF and TMACl) on methane hydrate growth. The results show that TMACl is the best choice to inhibit the growth of methane hydrate under the same conditions. Meng et al. constructed a dissipation coefficient fitting equation based on the rise-fall-dissipation theory and combined it with the density functional theory (DFT) to calculate the transition state and Gibbs free energy barriers for the nucleation process of methane hydrate. The 512-cage structure is identified as the key nucleation unit, and the dissipation coefficient increases with temperature and decreases with pressure; the DFT calculations show that the energy barriers between the transition state and the products are 4–5 times higher than those between the reactants and the products, and the barriers are mainly distributed in the boundary region, where kinetic and electrostatic energies play a dominant role; and the 512-cage structure has a thermodynamic advantage in terms of the Gibbs free-energy deviation of more than 16%. Lu et al. used MD simulations to study the role of thermodynamic inhibitors such as ethylene glycol and methanol in methane hydrate nucleation and combined them with DFT calculations to analyze the intermolecular interactions and the critical core size changes. The results showed that the hydrophobic groups of ethylene glycol and methanol can disrupt the hydrate cage structure through solvation, and also inhibit hydrate nucleation by increasing the critical nucleus size. Liao et al. combined experiments with MD simulations to study the effect of the composite system of thermodynamic inhibitors (NaCl, CaCl2, MEG) and kinetic inhibitor (PVP) on methane hydrate formation, and analyzed the mean square displacement (MSD), diffusion coefficient, and RDF. The thermodynamic inhibitor reduces the spatial site resistance of the kinetic inhibitor by restricting the motion of water molecules and enhances the effect on methane molecules. It is determined that the composite system of 1% PVP and 5% CaCl2 has a more significant inhibition effect, and it is confirmed that the composite inhibitor can inhibit the nucleation by modulating the critical core size. Dalla et al. presented a systematic new method to assess one-dimensional transient multiphase flow simulations for hydrate management, enabling an accurate determination of Cool-Down Time and capturing the evolution of the driving force for hydrate formation.

In the experimental aspect, Liao et al. synthesized the copolymer SYZ-2 from N-vinyl-ε-caprolactam and N,N-dimethylacrylamide, and tested the effect of SYZ-2 and its NaCl system on the nucleation process of methane hydrate by using the constant rate cooling method. Tong et al. established a method to evaluate the synergistic inhibition of hydrate inhibitors and paraffin inhibitors, and systematically carried out compatibility experiments of hydrate inhibitors and paraffin inhibitors. The results showed that the combination of 0.2 wt % PI and 1 wt % PVCap had the best synergistic effect in reducing hydrate nucleation. The combination of 0.2 wt % PIvb and 0.2 wt % dodecyl dimethyl benzyl ammonium bromide (DDBAB) had the best synergistic effect in decreasing the adhesion of hydrate and the strength of hydrate agglomeration. Liu et al. compared the inhibition effects of the conventional inhibitor KHI (PVP) and the autonomously synthesized inhibitor KHI (HY-501) through kinetic and rheological experiments, and proposed the concept of “critical time” with the experimental results. The three critical time points are the critical time point for the hydrate nucleation, the critical time point for the rapid hydrate growth phase, and the critical time point for the viscosity to reach the threshold value. Semenov et al. studied the effect of methanol concentration (0–50 wt %) on the nucleation and growth of methane hydrate, and the results show that methanol can promote the growth of methane hydrate in the concentration range of 0–40 wt %, and the maximum growth rate is reached at 10 wt %. At high concentrations (≥50 wt %), methanol can effectively inhibit the nucleation and growth of methane hydrate. Zare et al. developed an intelligent model based on gene expression programming (GEP) to predict the induction time for methane hydrate formation in the presence of Luvicap 55W. Daraboina et al. experimentally evaluated the performance of three KHIs under saline (NaCl) and oil-containing conditions using a standard rocking cell (RC-5) apparatus. They found the inhibition strength ranked as Luvicap Bio < Inhibex 505 < Luvicap 55W. Additionally, the hydrate decomposition temperature was slightly reduced in the presence of crude oil.

The research progress and existing issues reveal that previous work primarily focused on the macroscopic effects of kinetic inhibitors on the hydrate formation stage. However, the intrinsic mechanisms by which these inhibitors control hydrate development at the microscopic level have not yet been systematically explained. Therefore, this study first selected the key active ingredients in Luvicap-55Wpolyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and polyvinylcaprolactam (PVCap)as the primary subjects for simulation research. By integrating molecular dynamics simulations with density functional theory, the kinetic processes of inhibitor participation in hydrate growth were thoroughly investigated. By analyzing the formation mechanism of the cage-type structure in hydrates, the characteristics of the molecular dynamics and the interaction energy changes between different molecules, we reveal the inhibition mechanism of the inhibitor on the hydrate growth process, and elucidate the dynamic evolution law of the microscopic structure of hydrates and the interaction mechanism between molecules, so as to lay a theoretical foundation for the design and development of kinetic inhibitors.

Methods

Model Building

Based on the previously reported research findings, ,,, we chose to construct a unit (with approximately 3000 molecules) as the computational model for this study to balance the computational cost of the full atomic OPLS-AA force field with the demand for long-term (250 ns) simulation, while also considering comparability with existing research data. Prior to the hydrate growth simulation tests, a 3 × 3 × 6 SI-type methane hydrate cell (Figure a) with dimensions of 36 Å × 36 Å × 72 Å was first constructed based on the Bernal-Fowler ice rule in conjunction with X-ray diffraction analyses to obtain the initial coordinates of hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Subsequently, the inhibitor-containing monomers PVP and PVCap were introduced into the initial methane hydrate growth systems, designated as PMH and PPMH (Figure a and b), respectively. Both systems contain a hydrate phase and a mixture liquid phase, where the hydrate phase consists of 3 × 3 × 3 SI-type methane hydrate cells with a size of 36 Å × 36 Å × 36 Å, which serve as the crystal species for hydrate formation. The mixture liquid phase, on the other hand, consists of 216 methane molecules, 1296 water molecules, and inhibitor monomers with dimensions of 36 Å × 36 Å × 36 Å.

(a-c) Initial configurations of two kinetic inhibitor–containing hydrates.

Simulation Conditions and Parameter Settings

In this study, all calculations were done in the LAMMPS software package. The water molecules were described using the TIP4P/Ice model and the methane molecules were described using the OPLS-UA joint atom model, a particle model that has been successfully applied to study the structural and mechanical stability of hydrates. The PVCap and PVP monomer molecules were described by the OPLS-AA all-atom model, and all the force field parameters were obtained from the LigParGen database, which are shown in Table . The simulations were numerically solved by the Velocity-Verlet algorithm with a simulation step of 1 fs. The nonbonding interactions were calculated by the Lennard-Jones potential function calculated as shown in eqs –, and the truncation distance for short-range interactions was set to 12 Å. The SHAKE algorithm was used to constrain the bond lengths and bond angles of the water molecules in the system to ensure that the geometries of the water molecules and the inhibitor molecules were kept stable during the simulation. The cross-interaction potential parameters between different atoms were calculated using the Lorentz–Berthelot combination rule. Remote electrostatic interactions were handled by the smooth particle lattice PPPM algorithm with a computational accuracy of 1 × 10–6 kcal/mol and periodic boundary conditions applied in all directions. The specific simulation steps were as follows: first, the initial hydrate model containing the inhibitor monomer was subjected to energy minimization using the conjugate gradient (CG) method to obtain optimal conformation. The energy convergence limit was set to 1 × 10–4 eV and the force convergence limit was 1 × 10–4 eV/Å. Subsequently, a relaxation equilibrium of 2 ns was performed in an NVT system at a temperature of 275 K. Finally, 250 ns of hydrate growth simulations were performed in an NPT system at a temperature of 275 K and a pressure of 50 MPa. Results and Discussion

Analysis of Hydrate Growth

Figure presents the evolution of microscopic structures during hydrate growth with and without inhibitors (PVP and PVCap). It is evident that gas hydrates form regular cage-like structures and expand quickly at 250 K and 50 MPa. The liquid phase region continuously shrinks, revealing a growth pattern where hydrates spread from both sides toward the central area. This visual effect of natural gas hydrate growth toward the center arises from the periodic boundary conditions applied in all directions within the simulation setup. Subsequently, comparison revealed that in the inhibitor-free system (MH), a large amount of natural gas hydrate formed within a short period, whereas hydrate growth in systems containing kinetic inhibitors (PMH and PPMH) was markedly slower due to the inhibitory effect. It can be observed that by t = 25 ns, the hydrate fronts on both sides of the MH system begin to connect, indicating that hydrate growth has entered its final stage. By t = 80 ns, all liquid water molecules and methane molecules have been consumed, forming a complete and symmetrical hydrate-cage structure. In contrast, the growth of hydrates in PMH (Figure b with PVP inhibitor) and PPMH (Figure c with PVCap inhibitor) systems took 250 ns, representing a 2.125-fold increase in growth time. Furthermore, observation of the final conformation revealed that some liquid water molecules and methane molecules in the PMH and PPMH systems remained uninvolved in the hydrate growth process. This suggests that PVP and PVCap molecules occupy nucleation sites during growth and considerably inhibit the growth of hydrates, preventing the formation of complete hydrate cage structures in localized regions. Notably, the encapsulation of PVP and PVCap molecules within the hydrate did not induce an “exclusion” effect on hydrate formation. This phenomenon may be related to the interactions between PVP and PVCap molecules and adjacent H2O molecules, as well as the cage-like structure of the hydrates.

Microscopic evolution of hydrate growth. (a) MH, (b) PMH, and (c) PPMH.

Evolution of Methane Bubble Aggregation

During the early stages of hydrate growth, we observed a noticeable aggregation of methane bubbles. To investigate the evolution of bubble aggregation during natural gas hydrates growth and the inhibition mechanism of kinetic inhibitors, the Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) software was used to visualize and track the distribution of methane molecules throughout the simulation. Figure illustrates the dynamic evolution of methane gas bubble aggregation in three models. The comparison clearly shows pronounced methane gas bubble aggregation in the PMH and PPMH systems, whereas no such aggregation occurs in the MH system.

Dynamic evolution of methane bubble aggregates. (a) MH, (b) PMH, and (c) PPMH.

In addition, the presence of inhibitors causes the dissolved gas to gradually aggregate from its initial dispersed state and participate in the hydrate growth as shown in Figure b and c. At t = 0 ns, methane gas molecules are uniformly dispersed in the system; Between 0 and 15 ns, methane gas molecules begin to locally aggregate, forming larger methane gas bubble and creating a distinct gas–liquid interface (red and blue dashed box). From 15 to 250 ns, the methane gas bubble gradually shrinks over time, and eventually redissolves into the liquid phase. However, in the MH system depicted in Figure a, the methane gas molecules in the liquid phase are rapidly consumed (within t < 25 ns) and trapped in the cages of hydrates, which hinders the aggregation of dissolved gas. This behavior implies that PVP and PVCap molecules could cause dissolved gas to aggregate, which lowers the liquid phase’s dissolved gas concentration and inhibits the growth of hydrates.

Notably, PVP and PVCap molecules are always located at the gas–liquid interface rather than on the surface of the hydrates, regardless of whether gas aggregation or redissolution are occurring. Previous experimental and simulation studies have suggested that KHIs inhibit hydrate growth by adsorbing onto the surface of hydrate agglomerates and disrupting the hydrogen bond network structure of adjacent water molecules. However, the observed phenomena in this study cannot be entirely explained by this inhibitory mechanism. To address this discrepancy, researchers have proposed a novel mechanism: KHIs strongly adsorb onto methane bubble surfaces through interactions involving both hydrophobic bonds and hydrogen bonds. This adsorption simultaneously promotes bubble aggregation and reduces gas redissolution while lowering methane concentration in the liquid phase, thereby inhibiting the gas hydrate growth process.

Careful examination of Figure reveals that during the aggregation and dissolution of methane bubbles, inhibitor molecules also adsorb onto the inner surface of hydrate crystals (t = 15 ns) and reside at the gas–solid–liquid interface. Therefore, we conclude that the inhibition mechanism of inhibitors cannot be explained by a single mechanism but rather results from the coordinated action of multiple mechanisms. Specifically, inhibitor molecules not only form steric hindrance effects at the gas–solid–liquid interface through adsorption, directly inhibiting gas hydrate growth. They also indirectly suppress hydrate formation by inducing bubble aggregation, reducing secondary gas dissolution, and lowering gas concentration in the liquid phasethereby diminishing the driving force for hydrate growth.

To further validate the reliability, this study employed a rocking cell experimental apparatus to systematically evaluate the inhibition performance of selected kinetic inhibitors under the most severe operating conditions (see Supporting Information Figures S1 and S2). Concurrently, by integrating experimental observations with molecular dynamics simulation data, we conducted an in-depth comparative analysis of the formation conditions and evolution patterns of the “bubble aggregation” behavior induced by kinetic inhibitors. Figure compares the hydrate growth behavior in pure water and Luvicap-55W systems. It can be clearly observed that significant bubble aggregation occurs on or around the hydrate surface, whereas no similar behavior is observed in the pure water system. This suggests that kinetic inhibitors may alter gas–liquid interface properties or local mass-transfer conditions, thereby significantly affecting the hydrate growth mode and kinetics. Notably, once formed, these bubbles do not rupture or dissolve immediately; instead, they maintain a relatively stable size over an extended period and remain adhered to the hydrate-water interface. This behavior aligns closely with the bubble aggregation observed in the simulation studies, indicating that inhibitors not only suppress hydrate growth but may also stabilize bubble adhesion by modifying interfacial properties. The mutual validation between these cross-scale experimental and simulation results further confirms the reliability and accuracy of the simulation findings and provides important insight into the mechanism of inhibitor action.

Growth behavior of hydrates in (a) pure water and (b) Luvicap-55W systems.

Analysis of Hydrate Cage Formation

To investigate the structural stability of hydrate fluids in the presence of inhibitors, the number of hydrate cages formed during hydrate growth was identified and quantified using the Hydrate Cage recognition algorithm. Figure illustrates the dynamic evolution of cage-like structures in the system throughout the hydrate growth process. A clear nonlinear growth trend in the number of cages over time can be observed. This process can be further divided into two distinct stages: a rapid growth stage and a slow growth stage.

(a) Changes in the number of complete cage structures during natural gas hydrates growth; (b) Growth rate of cage structures during the first 40 ns.

During the rapid stage (0–40 ns), the number of hydrate cages increases significantly, indicating a burst stage of hydrate formation driven by thermodynamic forces. In contrast, during the slow stage (t = 50–250 ns), the number of cage-like structures exhibited a gradual increase, ultimately approaching a dynamic equilibrium. This phenomenon indicates that as the hydrate growth progresses, the dissolved methane gas concentration in the liquid phase gradually decreases, weakening the growth driving force. Consequently, the growth rate of cage-like structures slows significantly, also signifying that the gas hydrate growth process has entered its final growth stage. Figure b displays the growth rates of cage structures during the first 40 ns with and without the presence of the inhibitor. Comparison reveals that during the rapid stage, the 512 and 51262 cage structures in the MH system (without inhibitor) exhibit higher growth rates, which are 1.22/1.25 times, and 1.26/1.40 times higher than that of the PMH (with PVP inhibitor) and PPMH (with PVCap inhibitor) systems, respectively. This suggests that the inhibitor strongly inhibits the development of the main cage structures during the rapid stage. Notably, in the slow growth stage, the growth rate of cage structures exhibits significant differences, specifically. The amount of cage structures within the MH system (without inhibitor) is approximately 102 for 512 and 322 for 51262, representing 1.06 and 1.14 times the amounts of cage structures in the PMH (with PVP inhibitor) and PPMH (with PVCap inhibitor) systems, respectively. This behavior indirectly demonstrates that the inhibition mechanism of PVP or PVCap entails occupying the nucleation sites of gas hydrates, which controls the creation of the main cage structures (512 and 51262) and prevents the expansion of hydrates.

Potential Energy and F3 Parameter Analysis

To precisely determine the phase state of water molecules within a specific region, this section introduces the water molecular order parameter F3 and potential energy analysis. F3 is a parameter composed of three-body (three water molecules) configurations, characterizing the deviation of tetrahedra and regular tetrahedra formed by the central oxygen atom and other oxygen atoms within a 3.5 Å radius. 4 F3=1ni(ni−1)2∑j=1ni−1∑k=j+1ni(|cos⁡θjik|cos⁡θjik+cos2(109.47))2 where θ represents the angle between the oxygen atom of the central water molecule and any two other oxygen atoms i and j within a 3.5 Å radius, while 109.47° corresponds to the ideal tetrahedral angle. The F3 parameter exhibits significant differences between solid water (such as gas hydrates and ice) and liquid water. Specifically, the F3 parameter values for liquid water and solid water (ice and gas hydrates) are approximately 0.1 and 0.01, respectively.

Figure illustrates the evolution of the water molecule order parameter F3 and potential energy during the growth of hydrates in the MH, PMH, and PPMH systems. The figure reveals that during the rapid growth stage (0–40 ns), both the F3 order parameter and potential energy exhibit a significant decreasing trend with increasing simulation time across all three systems. This indicates that liquid water molecules within the system are rapidly consumed under thermodynamic driving forces to form an ordered hydrogen-bond network structure, undergoing a significant phase transition from liquid water to solid hydrate. Since hydrate growth is an exothermic process, the system releases substantial energy to the surroundings during the rapid growth stage, leading to a marked fall in potential energy. Additionally, a comparison of the three systems shows notable variations in the drop of the F3 order parameter for water molecules: the MH system has a decline of about 80%, while the PMH and PPMH systems experience a decline of about 70%. This phenomenon indicates that inhibitors can suppress the phase transition process of water molecules, preventing liquid water molecules from forming an organized hydrogen-bond network structure. The mechanism of action may be related to the interaction between inhibitor molecules and water molecules. Additionally, in all three systems, the F3 order parameter and the potential energy of water molecules stop changing significantly during the slow development stage. The F3 order parameters stabilize at a certain level, indicating that the cage structure has entered a relatively stable energy state after completing its primary growth process, and the liquid water molecules no longer undergo phase transitions.

Analysis of Intermolecular Interactions

To further investigate and quantify the weak interactions between water molecules, hydrate cages, and inhibitor molecules, this subsection employs density functional theory (DFT) calculations to select representative molecular clusters from MH, PMH, and PPMH systems for IGMH (Independent Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partition) analysis. This approach reveals the influence of inhibitor molecules on the structural stability of hydrates from the perspective of intermolecular forces. The DFT calculations employed here represent a conventional and reliable method for estimating the binding energies (BE) of various hydrogen bonds and noncovalent systems.

Figure presents the isosurface maps of the selected clusters derived from the IGMH analysis. The color of the ellipsoids between molecules indicates the type of interaction: green corresponds to van der Waals forces, while blue denotes hydrogen bonding. The size of these ellipsoids (isosurfaces) reflects the relative strength of the intermolecular interactions. As illustrated, both van der Waals and hydrogen bonding interactions are present among the water molecules within the cluster. However, the predominance of blue ellipsoids suggests that hydrogen bonding is the dominant intermolecular force stabilizing the cluster structure.

IGMH-based isosurface maps of the molecular clusters.

To more intuitively reflect the interactions between kinetic inhibitor molecules and adjacent water molecules, hydrogen bonds between PVP/PVCap and adjacent water molecules were extracted separately. Figure illustrates the interaction relationship between PVP/PVCap and water molecules. The figure reveals the existence of multiple isosurfaces between the PVP/PVCap and adjacent water molecules, indicating that PVP/PVCap molecules can form multiple hydrogen bonds with their neighbors. As a result, a stable cage structure cannot form in the immediate area because the adjacent water molecules are unable to create an ordered, stable hydrogen bond network. Consequently, hydrate formation is inhibited.

Hydrogen bonding between (a) PVP and surrounding water molecules and (b) PVCap and surrounding water molecules.

It is worth noting that free water molecules tend to form multiple stable hydrogen bonds with the five-membered lactam ring (the hydrophilic group) in PVP and the seven-membered lactam ring (the hydrophilic group) in PVCap, whereas the vinyl group (the hydrophobic group) hardly forms stable hydrogen bonds. These distinct groups endow PVP and PVCap molecules with strong adsorption capabilities, which play a crucial role in inducing bubble aggregation. On one hand, the hydrophilic groups immobilize surrounding liquid water molecules, restricting their migration to the hydrate lattice interface and preventing them from combining with the water molecules in the seeding region to form an ordered hydrogen-bond network. Meanwhile, these “bound” water molecules cannot serve as effective building blocks for hydrate growth, significantly reducing the availability of “growth material” and delaying nucleation and lattice expansion. On the other hand, the local water density near hydrophobic groups is significantly reduced, creating a microenvironment resembling a “dehydration effect”. This nonuniform distribution of water molecules promotes the aggregation of small bubbles around the hydrophobic ends, which may further coalesce into a stable gas bubble region.

The binding energies (EH) of different types of hydrogen bonds within the molecular clusters extracted from the three systemsMH, PMH, and PPMHwere analyzed. Twelve representative dimers were selected from these molecular clusters for further analysis. Figure illustrates the distribution of hydrogen bond strengths within the MH, PMH, and PPMH molecular clusters. As shown in the figure, the hydrogen bonds in methane hydrates are evenly distributed, with only small variations in bond energy. This indicates the formation of a highly ordered and energy-balanced hydrogen-bonding network within the methane hydrates. Such a uniformly distributed network endows the hydrates with excellent structural stability, enabling uniform stress transfer, effective energy dissipation, and maintenance of the topological integrity of the internal cage structure.

However, compared with MH system (without inhibitor), the average hydrogen bond strengths in PVP/PVCap-containing hydrates are significantly reduced, at −4.39974 kcal/mol and −5.70636 kcal/mol, respectively. The reason for this significant difference in bond energy lies in the presence of PVP/PVCap molecules, which occupy the nucleation sites originally intended for hydrate cages, disrupting the ordered distribution of water molecules in the local region and forming hydrogen bonds with adjacent water molecules. In addition, the hydrogen bonding network formed by inhibitor molecules in the interface region exhibits significant heterogeneity in spatial distribution, which weakens the intermolecular interactions at the gas–liquid interface and effectively reduces the gas–liquid surface tension of the system. This is supported by the experimental surface tension test results (see Supporting Information), which show a considerable decrease in surface tension value following the addition of the inhibitor. The decrease in surface tension helps lower the energy barrier that methane molecules dissolved in the liquid phase must overcome to cross the bubble–water interface, resulting in a decrease in the resistance for gas molecules to migrate into the bubble from the liquid phase. The decrease in surface tension enhances bubble stability, indirectly inhibiting the nucleation and growth of hydrates.

To summarize the aforementioned findings, Figure illustrates the spatial relationship and temporal evolution of the proposed triple synergistic mechanism. As depicted, the overall inhibition process is driven by three interconnected pathways: First, the inhibitor molecules rapidly adsorb at the interface, creating a strong steric hindrance effect (site-blocking). Second, this interfacial accumulation induces the localized aggregation of dissolved methane into nanobubbles, which significantly impedes continuous gas mass transfer. Finally, the inhibitors form competitive hydrogen bonds with adjacent water molecules, disrupting the local network and destabilizing the nascent hydrate cages. Together, these coordinated microscopic actions effectively delay the macroscopic nucleation and crystal growth of gas hydrates.

Schematic diagram of the triple synergistic mechanism of KHIs.

It should be noted that this study relies on a simplified pure water–methane-inhibitor model to isolate fundamental mechanisms. In actual oil and gas flowlines, the presence of complex componentssuch as waxy light/medium crude oil and formation saltscan significantly alter interfacial dynamics. Consequently, the direct applicability of the present conclusions to field conditions carries certain limitations. Future research will expand to multicomponent systems containing these complex constituents to systematically investigate the inhibitory behavior under realistic working conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, the effects of PVP and PVCap on the methane hydrate prevention and fluid stability in oil and gas flowlines were investigated using molecular dynamics simulations and DFT calculations. The results indicate that PVP and PVCap significantly inhibit hydrate growth and reduce the fluid stability of hydrates through a triple synergistic mechanism involving site-blocking, mass transfer interference, and hydrogen-bond disruption.(1)Through adsorption, the inhibitors (PVP/PVCap) create steric hindrance effects at the gas–solid–liquid interface, which directly prevents the growth of gas hydrates by preventing the formation of 512 and 51262 cage structures.(2)The inhibitor (PVP/PVCap) promotes bubble aggregation and reduces secondary gas dissolution through hydrophilic/hydrophobic groups, thereby significantly lowering the dispersion of gas in the liquid phase. This process impedes effective gas diffusion during mass transfer, reducing the supersaturation required for hydrate nucleation and growth.(3)The inhibitor (PVP/PVCap) prevents local water molecules from forming ordered hydrogen bond networks and modulating the strength differences of surrounding hydrogen bonds. This reduces the energy barrier for dissolved methane molecules to cross the bubble–water interface, thereby weakening the stability of gas hydrate cage structures. This provides a theoretical basis for optimizing highly efficient kinetic inhibitors.
