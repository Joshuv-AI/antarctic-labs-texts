Comparative effectiveness and recurrence rates of endoscopic sclerotherapy, ligation, and combined therapy for grades I-III internal hemorrhoids
Authors: Wanliang Wang, Yong Yang, Lanlan Hou, Shuimei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the comparative effectiveness, safety, and recurrence rates of endoscopic sclerotherapy (ES), endoscopic rubber band ligation (RBL), and combined ligation - sclerotherapy (CT) for grades I - III internal hemorrhoids.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent endoscopic minimally-invasive treatment for internal hemorrhoids at Wuhu First People’s Hospital from January 2020 to December 2024. Patients were divided into ES, RBL, and CT groups. Safety was evaluated by recording post-procedural pain and adverse events. Efficacy was assessed within 3 months post-treatment, and recurrence was defined as symptom reappearance > 3 months post-treatment. Patient satisfaction was also recorded.ResultsA total of 253 patients were included (168 males, 85 females; mean age 46.2 ± 12.8 years), with 107 in the ES group, 122 in the RBL group, and 24 in the CT group. Hemorrhoid grade distribution was 68 cases (26.9%) of Grade I, 112 cases (44.2%) of Grade II, and 73 cases (28.9%) of Grade III. In terms of efficacy, for all grades combined, the efficacy rate was 90.6% (95/107) for ES, 92.6% (113/122) for RBL, and 95.8% (23/24) for CT. CT showed the highest efficacy, especially for grade III hemorrhoids (95.0% [19/20]). Regarding safety, ES had a lower proportion of severe pain (6.5% [7/107]). Adverse events had low rates (bleeding: 4.2–6.6%; fever: 2.8–4.2%) with no significant inter-group differences. Patient satisfaction rates were 84.1% (90/107) for ES, 82.0% (100/122) for RBL, and 83.3% (20/24) for CT, with no substantial variation. For recurrence rates, CT had the lowest rate (4.3% [1/23]), followed by RBL (9.7% [11/113]) and ES (11.6% [11/95]), particularly evident in higher-grade hemorrhoids.ConclusionEndoscopic treatments, especially CT, are effective and relatively safe for grades I - III internal hemorrhoids. Introduction

Internal hemorrhoids, a prevalent anorectal disorder, affect a significant proportion of the global population, with recent epidemiological surveys in China indicating that hemorrhoids account for 98.09% of all anorectal diseases among adults aged ≥ 18 years, with internal hemorrhoids being the most common subtype [1, 2]. Characterized by symptoms such as rectal bleeding, prolapse, swelling, pain, pruritus, and defecation difficulties, internal hemorrhoids significantly impair patients’ quality of life and daily functionality [3, 4]. Traditional management strategies for internal hemorrhoids have historically relied on pharmacotherapy and surgical resection. However, with advancements in endoscopic technology, minimally invasive endoscopic interventions have emerged as promising alternatives, offering the advantages of reduced invasiveness, shorter recovery times, and improved safety profiles compared to traditional surgery [5].

The pathophysiology of internal hemorrhoids is rooted in the descensus of the anal cushion—a vascular and connective tissue complex responsible for maintaining anal continence—and subsequent disruption of its supporting structures, leading to venous dilation, tissue prolapse, and inflammation [1]. Grading of internal hemorrhoids (I to III based on Goligher classification) is critical for treatment selection: Grade I is characterized by mucosal hyperemia without prolapse, Grade II involves prolapse reducible spontaneously, and Grade III requires manual reduction [6, 7]. While conservative treatments (e.g., dietary modifications, topical medications) are often initially employed, endoscopic interventions are increasingly preferred for patients with persistent symptoms or advanced grades, as they effectively address hemorrhoidal symptoms while preserving anal function [8].

Endoscopic techniques for internal hemorrhoids primarily include endoscopic sclerotherapy (ES) and endoscopic rubber band ligation (RBL), with a growing interest in combined therapy (CT) (i.e., sequential RBL and ES). ES involves the submucosal injection of sclerosing agents (e.g., polidocanol) to induce fibrotic obliteration of hemorrhoidal vessels, with a recent systematic review confirming its efficacy and safety for grades I-III hemorrhoids [5, 9]. While RBL uses elastic bands to ligate the base of hemorrhoids, causing ischemic necrosis and subsequent mucosal retraction [10, 11]. Combined therapy aims to synergize the hemostatic effects of sclerotherapy with the mechanical reduction of prolapse via ligation, potentially optimizing outcomes for complex cases [12]. This concept of combining modalities is further supported by newer techniques like sclerobanding, which has shown promise even in high-risk patients requiring antithrombotic therapy [13].Despite their widespread adoption, the comparative effectiveness and long-term safety of these endoscopic modalities remain incompletely understood, particularly in the context of different hemorrhoidal grades. Existing evidence from single-center studies has reported high initial success rates for both ES and RBL, but discrepancies exist regarding recurrence rates and complication profiles [12, 14]. ES is often associated with less postprocedural pain but higher rates of delayed bleeding in some series, while RBL demonstrates superior short-term hemostasis but may cause more acute discomfort due to tissue strangulation [15, 16]. The role of combined therapy in reducing recurrence rates for advanced grades (Grade III) remains controversial, with limited data from randomized controlled trials.

As endoscopic treatment of internal hemorrhoids is still in the development stage in China, its long-term efficacy and safety need further study. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis of the cases of grade I to III internal hemorrhoids treated endoscopically in our hospital in recent years, aiming to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of endoscopic ligation and sclerosant injection in the treatment of internal hemorrhoids, and to provide a theoretical basis for the establishment of a standardized endoscopic treatment system for internal hemorrhoids.

Materials and methods

Study population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent endoscopic minimally invasive treatment for internal hemorrhoids at the Endoscopy Center of Wuhu First People’s Hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. All procedures were performed by three senior endoscopists, each possessing over 8 years of therapeutic endoscopy experience and more than 5 years of specialized training in endoscopic hemorrhoid management. Hemorrhoid grading followed the Goligher classification [17], which, despite its known limitations in subjectivity and focus on anatomical prolapse rather than patient-reported symptoms [18], remains the most widely adopted system in clinical practice and previous research, facilitating comparative analysis. Diagnostic criteria were also aligned with the Japanese Guidelines for the Treatment of Anal Disorders (Hemorrhoids) [19]. Eligibility criteria included age ≥ 18 years, Grade I–III internal hemorrhoids with symptoms (e.g., bleeding, prolapse), and receipt of endoscopic sclerotherapy, rubber band ligation, or combined therapy. Exclusion criteria comprised Grade I–III hemorrhoids with incarceration, thrombosis, ulceration, or infection; incomplete clinical records; and follow-up < 3 months.The study was approved by the Wuhu First People’s Hospital’s Institutional Review Board. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies (Supplementary Table 1).

Endoscopic techniques

Endoscopic sclerotherapy (ES)

Full colorectal examination was performed using a Fujifilm EC-L590ZW colonoscope. After retroflex visualization of the lower rectum to assess hemorrhoid grade and morphology, a transparent cap was attached, and a 23G disposable injection needle (Boston Scientific M00518301) was used to inject a 1% polidocanol (Shaanxi Tianyu) and 1% methylene blue (Jizhou Pharmaceutical). Methylene blue was added as a visual tracer to ensure precise submucosal placement and dispersion, reducing deep injection risk, and for its ancillary sclerosing and analgesic effects [20, 21]. Injections were made at 1–6 sites per hemorrhoid base. Optimal dosage per site was determined by post-injection effluent: blue-tinged or light red blood indicated adequate sclerosant delivery, while bright red blood prompted supplemental injection until light red efflux was observed. Maximum volume per site was 1.5 mL at a delivery rate of 0.1 mL/s.

Endoscopic rubber band ligation (RBL)

Following colorectal inspection, a Fujifilm EG-600WR gastroscope fitted with a seven-ring ligator (Boston Scientific M00542251) was used. Negative pressure was set to 300 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa) to ensure effective tissue aspiration (“full red sign”). Using retroflex imaging, ligation was performed in a vertical tandem manner: first on the oral-side mucosa 2–3 cm above the dentate line, followed by ligation of the hemorrhoid base at 2–3 points directly below the initial ligations in the same longitudinal axis.

Combined Ligation-Sclerotherapy (CT)

After completing vertical tandem ligation, the ligator handle was removed while maintaining the transparent cap. Residual hemorrhoids with visible red signs underwent supplemental sclerosant injection (0.5 mL/site at 2–3 points on the base) using the polidocanol-methylene blue mixture, administered under forward visualization.

Outcome assessments

Safety evaluation

Post-procedural pain was recorded within 24 h using a visual analogue scale (VAS), categorized as mild (1–3), moderate (4–6), or severe (7–10), with documentation of analgesic use. Adverse events included post-procedural bleeding (> 20 mL rectal blood within 1 week), fever, dysuria, anal tenesmus, stenosis, and thrombotic external hemorrhoids.

Efficacy evaluation

Assessed within 3 months post-treatment, efficacy was defined as:

Cure: Complete resolution of bleeding and prolapse.

Marked improvement: Significant reduction in symptom frequency or severity.

Failure: Unchanged or worsened symptoms.

Effective rate was calculated as (cure + marked improvement)/total cases × 100%.

Recurrence evaluation

Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of pre-procedural symptoms (bleeding, prolapse, pain) > 3 months post-treatment, requiring repeat endoscopic therapy or surgery after failed conservative management. Recurrence rate was calculated as recurrent cases/effective cases × 100%.

Patient satisfaction

Patients were classified as “satisfied” (endorsed efficacy and willingness to recommend) or “unsatisfied.”

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and compared via t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) with nonparametric tests. Categorical data were expressed as counts (%) and compared via chi-squared test. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, with P < 0.05 denoting statistical difference.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the cohort

Of 285 initially screened cases, 32 were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 253 patients: 107 treated with endoscopic sclerotherapy (ES), 122 with endoscopic rubber band ligation (RBL), and 24 with combined ligation-sclerotherapy (CT).

A total of 253 patients (168 males, 85 females; mean age 46.2 ± 12.8 years) were included, with 107 in the endoscopic sclerotherapy (ES) group, 122 in the endoscopic rubber band ligation (RBL) group, and 24 in the combined therapy (CT) group. Efficacy evaluation

The efficacy of the three endoscopic minimally - invasive treatment methods for different grades of internal hemorrhoids was evaluated, as shown in Table 2. For Grade I internal hemorrhoids, the efficacy rate of endoscopic sclerotherapy was 94.4% (34/36), that of endoscopic rubber band ligation was 96.3% (26/27), and the combined ligation - sclerotherapy achieved 100% (3/3). In Grade II internal hemorrhoids, the efficacy rates were 91.8% (45/49) for endoscopic sclerotherapy, 93.5% (58/62) for endoscopic rubber band ligation, and 100% (1/1) for combined ligation - sclerotherapy. For Grade III internal hemorrhoids, the efficacy rates were 80.0% (16/20) for endoscopic sclerotherapy, 87.9% (29/33) for endoscopic rubber band ligation, and 95.0% (19/20) for combined ligation - sclerotherapy. The high efficacy rates observed in the combined therapy group for Grade I (n = 3) and Grade II (n = 1) hemorrhoids warrant cautious consideration due to the very limited sample sizes in these subgroups. Overall, for all grades combined, the efficacy rate of endoscopic sclerotherapy was 90.6% (95/105), that of endoscopic rubber band ligation was 92.6% (113/122), and that of combined ligation - sclerotherapy was 95.8% (23/24). Safety and satisfaction evaluation

The safety and patient satisfaction following the three endoscopic treatment methods for internal hemorrhoids were evaluated, as presented in Table 3.Post-operative pain varied by treatment: endoscopic sclerotherapy had 74.8% mild, 18.7% moderate, and 6.5% severe pain; endoscopic rubber band ligation had 61.5% mild, 24.6% moderate, and 13.9% severe pain; combined ligation-sclerotherapy had 75.0% mild, 16.7% moderate, and 8.3% severe pain. A statistically significant difference in pain distribution was observed among the groups (p = 0.006). Adverse events included bleeding (4.7–6.6%), fever (2.8–4.2%), dysuria (0–2.5%), and anal tenesmus (9.3–12.5%) across groups, with no significant differences detected in the incidence of these complications (all p > 0.05). Patient satisfaction rates were 84.1% for sclerotherapy, 82.0% for ligation, and 83.3% for combined therapy, and no significant differences were found among the groups (p = 0.927).

In terms of recurrence rates for different grades of internal hemorrhoids, for Grade I internal hemorrhoids, the recurrence rate of endoscopic sclerotherapy was 5.9% (2/34), that of endoscopic rubber band ligation was 3.8% (1/26), and the combined ligation - sclerotherapy had a recurrence rate of 0% (0/3). The small sample sizes in certain subgroups, particularly for combined therapy in Grade I and II hemorrhoids, may limit the robustness of comparative interpretations across treatment groups.

Disscussion

Internal hemorrhoids are a widespread anorectal condition that significantly impacts a large segment of the global population. In China, their prevalence within the spectrum of anorectal diseases among adults aged 18 years and above is notably high [22]. The pathophysiology of internal hemorrhoids involves the descent of the anal cushion, a vascular and connective tissue complex crucial for maintaining anal continence, and the subsequent disruption of its supporting structures, leading to a cascade of events such as venous dilation, tissue prolapse, and inflammation [23]. This understanding forms the basis for the development and evaluation of various treatment modalities.

Our study focused on comparing the effectiveness, safety, and recurrence rates of endoscopic sclerotherapy (ES), endoscopic rubber band ligation (RBL), and combined ligation - sclerotherapy (CT) for grades I - III internal hemorrhoids. The endoscopic approach was selected based on its technical advantages over transanal anoscopy, particularly its superior optical capabilities that ensure precise therapeutic targeting—a critical requirement for achieving optimal outcomes in minimally invasive interventions [24]. Additionally, the endoscopic platform provides the significant clinical benefit of enabling concurrent diagnostic evaluation of the entire colon, thereby facilitating the identification of potential concurrent pathology that might otherwise remain undetected with a purely transanal approach [25, 26]. From an efficacy perspective, all three endoscopic techniques demonstrated notable success across different hemorrhoid grades. For grade I internal hemorrhoids, while ES and RBL achieved high efficacy rates, CT yielded a success rate of 100% in our cohort. This might be attributed to the dual - action mechanism of CT, which combines the mechanical reduction of prolapse through ligation and the obliteration of hemorrhoidal vessels via sclerotherapy [27]. In grade II hemorrhoids, RBL and CT again showed excellent results, with ES having a slightly lower but still respectable efficacy rate. The differences in efficacy among these methods for grade II hemorrhoids could potentially be related to the varying degrees of tissue and vascular involvement at this stage. For grade III hemorrhoids, which are more advanced and complex, CT was associated with the highest efficacy rate among the three interventions. This is likely because the combined approach of CT addresses both the mechanical and vascular aspects of the more severely prolapsed and dilated hemorrhoidal tissue more comprehensively than ES or RBL alone. Overall, CT was associated with the highest efficacy rate (95.8%), followed by RBL (92.6%) and ES (90.6%). These findings suggest that CT may provide a therapeutic benefit, especially in more advanced cases; however, these results originate from a single-center study and their generalizability to broader clinical practice remains to be established.

Safety is a paramount consideration in any treatment modality. In our study, ES was associated with a relatively lower proportion of severe pain, which can be explained by its minimally invasive nature. The submucosal injection of sclerosing agents in ES causes fibrotic obliteration of hemorrhoidal vessels without the acute tissue strangulation that occurs in RBL. RBL, conversely, had a higher proportion of severe pain, likely due to the immediate constriction of the hemorrhoidal base by the rubber bands, leading to ischemic necrosis. CT was observed to have a favorable pain profile, with a high proportion of mild pain. This could be because the initial ligation in CT is followed by a more targeted sclerotherapy, which has the potential to mitigate some of the pain - inducing effects of a more extensive sclerotherapy alone [28]. Regarding adverse events, although bleeding, fever, dysuria, and anal tenesmus were observed in all groups, the rates were relatively low and no significant differences were found among the groups. These findings suggest that these endoscopic methods are relatively safe in our clinical context, but it should be noted that each has its own unique safety - related characteristics that clinicians should be aware of.

The recurrence rates observed in our study provided further insights into the long-term outcomes of the three treatments. For grade I hemorrhoids, the recurrence rates were low for all methods, with CT achieving a recurrence rate of 0%. As the grade of hemorrhoids increased, the differences in recurrence rates among the methods became more pronounced. In grade II, ES and RBL had higher recurrence rates compared to CT. For grade III, ES had the highest recurrence rate, followed by RBL, with CT associated with a significantly lower rate. Overall, CT was associated with the lowest recurrence rate (4.3%), followed by RBL (9.7%) and ES (11.6%). The lower recurrence rate of CT may be attributed to its ability to more effectively address the underlying pathophysiology of internal hemorrhoids. By combining ligation, which reduces the prolapsed tissue, and sclerotherapy, which targets the vascular component, CT could potentially create a more durable correction of the hemorrhoidal condition, thereby possibly reducing the likelihood of recurrence [29].

Our study offers evidence that may support for the use of endoscopic treatments in the management of internal hemorrhoids. CT, in particular, appears as a potentially effective option, especially for patients with grade III hemorrhoids. Based on our findings, clinicians could consider the grade of the hemorrhoids, along with other patient - specific factors, when choosing an endoscopic treatment method. For patients with milder grades (I and II), ES and RBL may be viable options, whereas CT could be considered for more severe cases to potentially improve outcomes.

Nevertheless, our study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The most critical limitation is the substantial selection bias arising from the retrospective, single-center design. As a retrospective analysis, the study is inherently susceptible to selection bias, which was further compounded by differential treatment allocation based on disease severity. The non-randomized assignment of treatments introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding factors that may not have been fully adjusted for in the analysis, thus possibly distorting the observed treatment effects. Moreover, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the single-institution origin and the relatively limited sample size, which may not adequately represent broader patient populations. Another notable constraint is the short duration of follow-up, which impedes the assessment of long-term treatment efficacy and safety and particularly limits the evaluation of late recurrence rates, such as among Grade III hemorrhoids. Furthermore, due to the low incidence of certain complications, we were unable to perform detailed analyses of specific adverse events, including acute profuse bleeding following rubber band ligation and tenesmus pain associated with the application of multiple bands (> 3) in Grade III cases. To overcome these limitations, future studies should prioritize large-scale, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trials with prolonged follow-up periods. Such research would yield more reliable evidence and facilitate a comprehensive understanding of optimal endoscopic treatment strategies for internal hemorrhoids, ultimately improving patient care.

Conclusion

In summary, our study contributes to the existing body of knowledge on the endoscopic treatment of internal hemorrhoids. The results suggest the effectiveness and relative safety of these techniques, with CT showing potential advantages in terms of efficacy and recurrence prevention in our study cohort. However, further research is needed to validate these findings and fully optimize the use of these endoscopic methods in clinical practice.
