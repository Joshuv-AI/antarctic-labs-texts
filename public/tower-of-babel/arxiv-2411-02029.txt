GDP nowcasting with large-scale inter-industry payment data in real time – A network approach
Anastasia Mantziou, Kerstin Hötte, Mihai Cucuringu, Gesine Reinert

Abstract
Real-time economic information is essential for policy-making but difficult to obtain. We introduce a granular nowcasting method for macro- and industry-level GDP using a network approach and data on real-time monthly inter-industry payments in the UK. To this purpose we devise a model which we call an extended generalised network autoregressive (GNAR-ex) model, tailored for networks with time-varying edge weights and nodal time series, that exploits the notion of neighbouring nodes and neighbouring edges. The performance of the model is illustrated on a range of synthetic data experiments. We implement the GNAR-ex model on the payments network including time series information of GDP and payment amounts. To obtain robustness against statistical revisions, we optimise the model over 9 quarterly releases of GDP data from the UK Office for National Statistics. Our GNAR-ex model can outperform baseline autoregressive benchmark models, leading to a reduced forecasting error. This work helps to obtain timely GDP estimates at the aggregate and industry level derived from alternative data sources compared to existing, mostly survey-based, methods. Thus, this paper contributes both, a novel model for networks with nodal time series and time-varying edge weights, and the first network-based approach for GDP nowcasting based on payments data.



Keywords: Nowcasting, networks, payment data, supply chains, vector-autoregressive model

1
Introduction

Real-time economic information is essential for policymaking in a rapidly changing and interconnected world, faced

with
local and global shocks, technological change, and increasing environmental and social pressure. Yet, such information is difficult to obtain.

In particular,
GDP is a major indicator used in decision-making, but its compilation is complex and draws on hundreds of data sources, which leads to long publication delays. Autoregressive estimates fail to capture unanticipated events, leading to hefty revisions when the flow of incoming information reveals an increasingly detailed picture of the state of the economy
(
OSR, 2023
;
Barrand et al., 2021
)
.

Economic nowcasting can fill the data gap, by using alternative, observable real-time information to predict current economic outcomes. It has attracted much high-level policy interest, particularly in the aftermath of COVID-19 and recent energy price shocks
(
Woloszko, 2020
;
ONS, 2023a
)
. Examples of nowcasting include methods relying on aggregate real-time information extracted from Economic News
(
Barbaglia et al., 2023
)
, Google Data
(
Ferrara and Simoni, 2023
;
Woloszko, 2020
)
, seismographic patterns caused by industrial activities
(
Tiozzo Pezzoli and Tosetti, 2022
)
, payments data
(
Galbraith and Tkacz, 2018
;
Esteves et al., 2009
;
Carlsen and Storgaard, 2010
;
Das, 2021
;
Aprigliano et al., 2019
;
Duarte et al., 2017
)
,
or utilising various economic indicators
(
Koop et al., 2024
;
Proietti and Giovannelli, 2021
)
.

Most of these approaches rely on aggregate data and use mixed frequency sampling or factor models to extract statistical signals from various time series that are characterised by high collinearity and asynchronous updating and availability
(
Bańbura et al., 2013
;
Bok et al., 2018
)
.
Such aggregate methodology hides the mechanisms and channels of how observed aggregate phenomena drive economic fluctuations.
Understanding these channels is relevant for targeted policies aimed at preventing and mitigating shocks, that propagate through an interconnected economy, and at responding to their effects at the micro and macro level on time
(
Acemoglu et al., 2012
;
CEA, 2023
)
.

Supply chains are a major propagation mechanism of economic dynamics and disruptions
(
Acemoglu et al., 2012
)
, as powerfully illustrated by
recent supply chain disruptions associated with COVID-19, the war in Ukraine, and natural hazards.
This observation suggests pursuing ideas from network analysis for GDP nowcasting.

Most network autoregressive models however
focus
on modelling time series which are observed on the nodes of a fixed network;

here is a brief survey.

Zhu et al., 2017
introduce a network autoregressive model tailored for networks with nodal time series, which includes a network effect parameter that explains the impact of neighbouring nodes
for each node
in the network.
Knight et al., 2020
generalise the model by
Zhu et al., 2017
by using the notion of higher-stages of node neighbours which also allows to capture the impact of
time series
between nodes that are not direct neighbours in the network.

Martin et al., 2024

introduce a network autoregressive model for panels of multivariate time series represented through a multiplex network, assuming that the network is weighted with fixed weights, and
has a stochastic block model

(SBM) structure.

While these three models
assume Gaussian noise,

Armillotta and Fokianos, 2024
propose a network autoregressive model for count time series observed on the nodes of a fixed network
with

Poisson
distributed
responses.

In contrast, the payment data between industry groups are more naturally represented as edge weights in a network. For time series on the edges of a network,
in
Mantziou et al., 2023
it is shown that a network approach with nodes representing industries and weighted edges representing transactions between industries captures subtle dependencies in the data which a standard vector autoregressive (VAR) model would not include.

However time series on nodes, which could for example model GDP, are not included in the model by
Mantziou et al., 2023
.

In this paper, we introduce a theoretically informed network-based approach to GDP nowcasting at the industry and macroeconomic level, by using novel
experimental
real-time payment data that captures supply chain dynamics among UK industries
(
ONS, 2023d
;
Hötte and Naddeo, 2024
)
, in combination with quarterly GDP estimates provided by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS)
.
We contribute a granular model to nowcast macro- and industry-level GDP
(
ONS, 2023b
)

which we coin

GNAR-ex
,

extending current

network autoregressive models
(
Knight et al., 2020
;
Mantziou et al., 2023
)
. Nodes in the network represent industries, and edges are payment flows between them.

On
the
(fixed)
network
with industry groups as nodes
, we observe two types of time series: time series of GDP on the nodes (industries) and time series of payment flows
between industries, represented as

time-varying weights on the edges of the network. The model is a restricted VAR model
(
Lütkepohl, 2005
)
, with GDP being estimated as a function of lagged GDP and lagged payment flows according to the network structure through the notions of neighbouring nodes and neighbouring edges.

Revisions are a major issue in economic nowcasting; GDP estimates for a given time stamp differ across data releases
(
Hepenstrick and Marcellino, 2019
)
.
For the ONS data, initial estimates are revised in subsequent quarterly releases, until after around 36 months a final GDP figure is achieved.

We combine 9
subsequent

ONS
releases of GDP data to obtain

a nowcasting method
that is robust

across
revisions.

Here we stress that the results are based on experimental data.

Here is a summary of our
contributions.

1.

We introduce a novel model, the GNAR-ex model, that combines time series information observed both on the nodes
and the edges of a fixed network, using information about the network structure through combining the notions of
neighbouring nodes and neighbouring edges in the same network autoregressive model. We demonstrate the estimation
performance and predictive power of the GNAR-ex model in synthetic data experiments. Moreover, we address model
uncertainty by model averaging (MA) and explore the accuracy of the MA forecasts in synthetic data experiments.

2.

We apply the GNAR-ex model on the real data to provide nowcasts of GDP at the industry and macroeconomic level.
The model parameters are estimated via least-squares, using data from a given release, and the model predicts GDP
of the next period as published in the subsequent ONS release; this nowcast is compared to the official first ONS
estimate of GDP for that quarter. We calculate the relative error and benchmark the predictive performance against
an ARIMA model which does not include network information.

3.

To address multicollinearity, we apply network sparsification methods informed by economic intuition and time series
correlations. We observe that network sparsification significantly improves the predictive power.

4.

Investigating the performance release-by-release, we show that the GNAR-ex model outperforms the best-fitting
ARIMA benchmark, but the optimal model configuration varies by the number of lags and network neighbours being
included. For real-life nowcasting, a more practical approach is required. To obtain a uniform and well-performing
model, we apply MA and show that the GNAR-ex model is competitive with an ARIMA(0,1,0), which was suggested
as one of the best performing models using the R function
auto.arima
.

5.

To the best of our knowledge, our approach delivers the first statistical network time series model applied to economic
nowcasting of GDP. Economists have shown that the topology of supply chain networks can be decisive for explaining
aggregate fluctuations, with large industrial hubs and well-connected industries being key drivers of GDP volatility

(
Acemoglu et al., 2012
)
. Such models often reduce the network impact to a static vector of influence at the node
level, describing the impact of individual sectors on macroeconomic outcomes. Instead, the GNAR-ex model explicitly
includes fluctuations on the network edges in a structural model that combines node- and edge-level information. To
highlight the novelty of our approach, network models have rarely been used in economic nowcasting, albeit there
is related work;
Macqueen et al., 2023
use network information embodied in input-output tables to infer statistical
relationships between GDP components, to incorporate timely information when making predictions. Our approach
is different to
Macqueen et al., 2023
since our novel data set for payments data dictates the network structure and
allow inferences for network parameters explicitly.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: The next section (Sec.
2
) explains the payment and GDP data. Sec.
3
introduces the model and Sec.
4
demonstrates its performance in synthetic data experiments. In Sec.
5
, we show our main empirical results; Sec.
6

provides a discussion of the approach.
Appendix
A
contains additional results from synthetic data experiments and the real data application.

2
Data

We combine two publicly available monthly time series data: (1) payment flows between industries, and (2) industry-level gross value added (GVA) data.
On the node (industry) level, we observe time series of month-to-month GDP growth rates
G_{i,t}
for each industry
i
at time
t
, while on the edges we observe time series of month-to-month payment growth rates
P_{ij,t}
between industries
i,j
at time
t
.

Our forecasting goal is

the cross-industrial aggregate
growth rate
of GVA,
which we view
as a proxy of UK GDP.

2.1
The payment data

The
payment
data was published by the ONS in December 2023, as an experimental dataset with ongoing development.
It is a data set which is an monthly industry-level aggregate of

business-to-business (B2B) financial transactions made through the Bacs Payment System, which is one of the major routes of how businesses execute regular payments in the UK. Businesses were mapped to SIC codes and payments were aggregated by industry at a monthly basis,
yielding 38 industry groups
. In principle, such data can be extracted in real-time from the payment infrastructure of the Bacs Payment System, making it a promising source of real-time economic indicators. Broadly speaking, the payments reflect trade in intermediates between industries.
ONS, 2023c
;
Hötte and Naddeo, 2024
provide a more detailed description, validation and conceptual discussion of the data.

We represent this
time series of inter-industrial payment data
as a network time series
on a simple directed network with
K
industry groups as nodes, and

the flows of payments between a pair of

industry groups

i,j\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}}1,\ldots,K}}{}

as edge weights
;
self-loops are excluded.

While the edge weights change monthly, the set
s
of nodes
and

edges are
assumed to be fixed. Thus, we construct a static
directed
network with nodes representing the industries
and edges
between two industries
encoding that payments between the two industries, made across time, could be of interest.
This network is constructed before the data are inspected.

As the above formulation indicates, not all payments between industries may be of interest. Moreover, in a given month there may be some industry pairs for which no payments are observed, but a corresponding edge in the static network is still present.
Details can be found in Section
5
.

2.2
Gross value added

The Gross Value Added (GVA) data is obtained from the
ONS, 2023b
. It represents a series of quarterly data releases of monthly industry-level GVA index data,
which is
consistent with quarterly national accounts and distinguishes 99 distinct industries, classified by Classification of Products by Activity (CPA) codes. To harmonise the aggregation with the 38 industries available in the payment data, we transformed the index data (2019=100) into levels of

GVA
using industry-level GVA data from the 2019 Supply-and-Use Tables (SUT)
(
ONS, 2023e
)
to obtain appropriate industry-weights before aggregating multiple CPAs into broader industrial categories.

One major issue in nowcasting is the continuous updating of early estimates
(
Bańbura et al., 2013
)
, when new data becomes available. Hence, the first estimates of published GVA close to the publication date may be revised in future releases. Further, methods for data collection and compilation are subject to minor changes, mostly arising from methodological improvements.
As a consequence, the monthly level of the GVA index of industry
i

at a
given
t
may differ across data releases. The effect of incoming data affects the most recent GVA estimates, while methodological revisions affect the whole time series, which starts in 1997.

The analysis in this paper is based on nine different industry-level GVA data releases, being published on a quarterly basis between December 2021 and December 2023. This was the maximum number of available releases when downloading the data in May 2024. We excluded the March 2024 release, as our approach assumes to use only data that had been available in December 2023, which also coincides with the availability of payment data.
We use the March 2024 release only to evaluate the forecasts using the December 2023 release.

In abuse of notation, for easier interpretability we abbreviate “GVA as proxy for GDP” by “GDP”.
Similarly, instead of “industry groups” we use “industry”.

3
The model

The model is anchored in the setting

of a

fixed network with
K
nodes and
M
edges,

and time series being

observed both on the nodes and on the edges.

Figure
1
shows a toy example

to illustrate the setting.

Figure 1
:
A toy example of a network with nodes representing industries and edges representing
potential
transactions. Time series on the nodes represent time series of GDP growth rates and time series on the edges represent time series of transaction values growth rates.

The GNAR-ex model is a time series model which starts with a given static, directed, unweighted network on
K
nodes, without self-loops or multiple edges.

As in
Knight et al., 2020
and
Mantziou et al., 2023
,
the set of nodes is denoted by
\mathcal{V}

and
\mathcal{E}
denotes the set of
edges
; we assume that
|\mathcal{E}|=M
. The set
\mathcal{N}_{node}^{1}(i)
of
1-stage neighbouring nodes
for node
i
is the set of all nodes for which a directed edge from or to node
i
exists, defined as

\mathcal{N}_{node}^{1}(i)=\{j\in\mathcal{V}/\{i\}:\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\text{ or }\{j,i\}\in\mathcal{E}\}

For
r\geq 2
, we
denote the set of
r
-stage neighbouring nodes of node
i

as the set

\mathcal{N}_{node}^{r}(i)=\mathcal{N}^{1}_{node}\{\mathcal{N}_{node}^{r-1}(i)\}/[\{\cup_{q=1}^{r-1}\mathcal{N}_{node}^{q}(i)\}\cup\{i\}].

For an edge
\{i,j\}
the set
\mathcal{N}_{edge}^{1}(\{i,j\})
of
1-stage neighbouring edges

is the set of all edges which are incident to at least one of the nodes
i,j
.
Formally,

\mathcal{N}_{edge}^{1}(\{i,j\})=\{\{k,l\}\in\delta^{+}(i)\cup\delta^{-}(i)\cup\delta^{+}(j)\cup\delta^{-}(j):\{k,l\}\neq\{i,j\}\}

with
\delta^{+}(\cdot),\delta^{-}(\cdot)
denoting the sets of outgoing and incoming edges of a node, respectively.
For
r\geq 2
we similarly denote the set of
r
-stage neighbouring edges of the edge
\{i,j\}

as the set

\mathcal{N}_{edge}^{r}(\{i,j\})=\mathcal{N}^{1}_{edge}\{\mathcal{N}_{edge}^{r-1}(\{i,j\})\}/[\{\cup_{q=1}^{r-1}\mathcal{N}_{edge}^{q}(\{i,j\})\}\cup\{\{i,j\}\}].

Let
L
denote the maximum number of past time stamps,
and let
R_{l}
denote
the maximum stage of neighbouring nodes
and neighbouring edges for each lag
l=1,\ldots,L
.

For
\boldsymbol{G_{t}}=(G_{1,t},\ldots,G_{K,t})

denoting the time series on the
K
nodes at time
t
,

and
\boldsymbol{P_{t}}=(P_{1,t},\ldots,P_{M,t})

denoting the time series on the
M
edges at time
t
,

assuming a labelling
q:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,M\}

on the set of edges

(with an ordering
on the edge set), our new
GNAR-ex
model is

\displaystyle G_{i,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left[\alpha_{l}G_{i,t-l}+\sum_{r=1}^{{R_{l}}}\frac{\beta_{l,r}}{|\mathcal{N}_{node}^{r}(i)|}\sum_{j\in N_{node}^{r}(i)}G_{j,t-l}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\gamma_{l}}{|E_{i}|}\sum_{kp\in E_{i}}\left\{P_{kp,t-l}+\sum_{r=1}^{{R_{l}}}\frac{\delta_{l,r}}{|\mathcal{N}_{edge}^{r}(\{k,p\})|}\sum_{mn\in N^{r}_{edge}(\{k,p\})}P_{mn,t-l}\right\}\right]+u_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle P_{ij,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left[\alpha_{l}P_{ij,t-l}+\sum_{r=1}^{{R_{l}}}\frac{\beta_{l,r}}{|N_{edge}^{r}(\{i,j\})|}\sum_{mn\in N_{edge}^{r}(\{i,j\})}P_{mn,t-l}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\gamma_{l}}{2}{(G_{i,t-l}+G_{j,t-l})}+\sum_{r=1}^{{R_{l}}}\frac{\delta_{l,r}}{|N^{r}_{node}(i)\cup N^{r}_{node}(j)/[\{i\},\{j\}]|}\sum_{k\in N^{r}_{node}(i)\cup N^{r}_{node}(j)/[\{i\},\{j\}]}G_{k,t-l}\right]+u_{ij}^{t},

with
E_{i}=\{\{i,j\}\cup\{j,i\}:j\in N_{node}^{1}(i)\}

being
the set of edges which are incident to node
i
,
\alpha_{l},\gamma_{l}
being standard autoregressive parameters and
\beta_{l,r},\delta_{l,r}
being network effect parameters, for
l=1,\ldots,L
and
r=1,\ldots,R_{l}
.

The error terms
u_{i}^{t}
’s and
u_{ij}^{t}
’s are
assumed to be
i.i.d. centred normal with
unknown
variance
\sigma^{2}
.
This setting gives a restricted Gaussian vector autoregressive (VAR) model for the multivariate time series comprising of
a time series on the nodes and a time series on the edges of the static network.

It inherits the theoretical guarantees for VAR models, such as asymptotic unbiasedness and asymptotic efficiency of least-squares estimates,
see for example
Lütkepohl, 2005
.

We compare the GNAR-x model to an ARIMA model,
fitted individually to each
node time series, ignoring the network structure.

The ARIMA model has three components: an autoregressive component
which indicates the size
,
P
,
of lags on past values, the integrated component,
with parameter
D

which represents the
order of
differencing of raw observations, and the moving average
component,
with parameter
Q
, capturing the dependency on lagged residuals.
The general form of a Gaussian ARIMA(
P,D,Q
) model is

G_{i,t}^{{}^{\prime}}=c+\sum_{p=1}^{P}\phi_{p}G_{i,t-p}^{{}^{\prime}}+\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}\epsilon_{t-q}+\epsilon_{t}

where
G_{i,t}^{{}^{\prime}}
is the
D
-differenced time series
for node

i
, with
i=1,\ldots,K
, and the
\epsilon_{t}
’s are independent centred normal with variance
\sigma^{2}
. For example, an ARIMA(0,1,0) model has the form

G_{i,t}=c+G_{i,t-1}+\epsilon_{t},

translating into assuming that the increments (the growth rates) are i.i.d. normal.
As a second comparison, we fit a vector autoregressive model using the Lasso-type VAR estimation process (HLAG) approach by
Nicholson et al., 2020
in synthetic data experiments. Due to the superior performance of ARIMA compared to VAR (HLAG) on the synthetic data, in our real data application in Section
5
for comparisons to GNAR-ex we only consider the ARIMA model.

The unrestricted VAR model
for the multivariate time series data set comprising of
M+K
time series

\displaystyle Y_{n,t}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}P_{n,t},&\text{if}\ n=1,\ldots,M\\
G_{n,t},&\text{if}\ n=M+1,\ldots,M+K\end{cases}

has the form

\displaystyle Y_{n,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{n}+\alpha_{n_{1,1}}Y_{1,t-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n_{M+K,1}}Y_{M+K,t-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n_{1,L}}Y_{1,t-L}+\ldots+\alpha_{n_{M+K,L}}Y_{M+K,t-L}+u_{n}^{t}.

Simulation results for GNAR-ex

To validate the model and
test its prediction performance, we simulate nodal and edge time series

for networks with different topologies
and implement the GNAR-ex model on the simulated time series.
As in
Mantziou et al., 2023
, we consider three
commonly used random
network
models,

namely an Erdős-Rényi (ER), a Stochastic Block Model (SBM) and a geometric random graph, specifically a Random Dot Product graph (RDP).
The directed networks considered in our experiments have 20 nodes with
parameters set to achieve
a density of approximately 0.4.
We simulate nodal and edge time series using the GNAR-ex model under the parameter specifications presented in Table
1
.
To describe each model considered in our experiments, the notation GNAR-ex(
L
,[
R_{1},\ldots,R_{L}
]) denotes the GNAR-ex model with maximum lag
L
and maximum stages of neighbours
R_{l}
, for lags
l=1,\ldots,L
.
We fit the GNAR-ex model on the simulated time series to make inferences for the model parameters. To evaluate the estimation performance of the model, we obtain the RMSE of the estimated coefficients with respect to their true values
, and calculate the proportion of times that the true values lie within the 95% confidence interval. For each simulation regime, we repeat the experiment 50 times for 50 different seeded simulated data sets. In Tables
2
,
3
and
4
we present the results for each simulation regime.

We observe that under all three simulation regimes and different network structures, we obtain accurate estimates of the true size of the model parameters, as indicated by the small RMSE values and high coverage close to nominal level of 95%.
In particular,
the estimation performance for the autoregressive parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is better than that for the network autoregressive parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}
, as indicated by the smaller RMSE compared to the RMSE, across all simulation regimes. This result suggests that making inferences for the network parameters is more challenging; nonetheless, we obtain a relatively small RMSE with respect to the true size of both the autoregressive and network effect parameters.
The estimation performance is
similar across the different network models considered; however, we observe that for the ER networks in the simulation regime with the highest complexity (simulation regime 3), the coverage of 95% confidence intervals is slightly smaller for the autoregressive parameters at lag 2 than for the network effect parameters.

We further explore the predictive performance of the GNAR-ex model and compare it to two baseline models, namely an ARIMA model fitted individually to each time series using the
auto.arima
function that allows model selection with BIC/AIC criteria, and a vector autoregressive model using

5
Nowcasting UK GDP

In this section, we describe how
we obtain a
network from the data as well as the
nowcasting task.
We propose
a
sparsification of the network because
high correlations between GDP and payments time series can
affect the precision of the parameter estimates.

5.1
Network sparsification

In the first step, we remove industries (nodes) from the network with respect to (1) knowledge about issues with the payment data and (2) economic intuition, namely no expected relevance for GDP fluctuations. The industries removed from the network at this first step are the following:

•

O84 Public administration,

•

G45 Wholesale vehicles,

•

G46 Wholesale except vehicles,

•

G47 Retail except vehicles,

•

Q86 Human health,

•

Q87-88 Residential care.

The reasoning is as follows.

Early exploration of the payment data set indicated that

the Public administration sector suffers from inconsistencies between payment data and GDP accounting data with regard to recording of economic transactions.

Further, the classification
in the payment data
is conceptually different from the practices
often
used in official statistics
(
Hötte and Naddeo, 2024
)
.
The retail
and
wholesale industries are intermediary sectors that “destroy” the direct link between the buying and selling industry (however, they should be 2-stage neighbours).
Health- and care-related spendings are excluded for conceptual reasons, as they are expected to be unrelated to GDP fluctuations.

The second step for sparsifying the network involves removal of payments (edges) that are highly correlated to GDP time series. Specifically, we remove edges corresponding to payments time series that have cross-industry Pearson’s correlation to GDP nodal time series above 0.4 or below -0.4, thus keeping
only
time series with weak correlation
in the sense of

(
Evans, 1996
)
.
The resulting network has 32 nodes and 231 edges.

5.2
Approach

To examine the nowcasting
accuracy of our model across the different data releases,
we use each
of the 9
GDP data releases as a training sample to fit our model, nowcast the next (monthly) time stamp,
and evaluate the nowcasting accuracy with data in subsequent releases. Each GDP data release provides industry GDP data with a lag of 6 months. For example, the data release of December 2021 includes industry GDP values up to June 2021. In our setup, we use this data as training sample to fit our model and nowcast
the next time stamp,
July 2021. To evaluate the nowcasting
accuracy, we compare our
nowcast to the July 2021 industry GDP values released in the subsequent data release of March 2022. We repeat the same procedure for the 9
ONS
data releases
since December 2021
.

For the time series of growth rates, we apply a seasonal-trend decomposition (STL) using the R function
stl
and fit the GNAR-ex model on the residuals.
We evaluate the forecasting accuracy on the original scale of the GDP value by obtaining the total GDP (sum of industry-level GDP values) and using the relative error
{|\text{forecast-actual}|}/{\text{actual}}
as a measure of nowcasting
performance.

For comparison, we fit an ARIMA model on the raw GDP time series for each industry and allow the parameter selection to be performed through the AIC criterion using the R function
auto.arima
. We also fit an ARIMA(0,1,0) model as this was identified as
the best fitting model when using
auto.arima
.

6
Conclusions

This paper introduces a novel network approach for GDP nowcasting using payments data. The GNAR-ex model, as we call it, is a network autoregressive model tailored for networks with nodal time series and time-varying edge weights, that allows inferences of network effect parameters utilising the inherent network structure of the payments data.

Using the GNAR-ex model for GDP nowcasting often yields more accurate results than models which do not include network information.

From an economics viewpoint there are
more economic industry- and macroeconomic variables which are commonly controlled for in economic nowcasting models and could be added in our GNAR-ex model application. Examining the value of adding further economic control variables and refining the nowcasts is work in progress.

While in this paper we applied the GNAR-ex model to GDP nowcasting,
this model can also be implemented on similar data sets which can be represented using a network with time series on the nodes and edges.

There are a number of theoretical improvements possible. One could assume different parameters for the Gaussian errors for the nodal and the edge time series. It could also be interesting to consider non-Gaussian errors for some time series. Moreover, the current model assumes stationarity; there may be change points in the time series, such as around Covid-19 restrictions. Identifying change points is another area for future work.

Yet another potential avenue to investigate pertains to building a factor model for the multivariate time series, which could further enhance the downstream forecasting task.

The empirical work in this article relies on an experimental version of the payment data and quarterly published GVA data, as were publicly available when conducting the research. Improvements of the payment data by coverage and granularity are underway, and coupled with higher GVA frequency data this will provide
a valuable basis for future work on GDP nowcasting based on ONS data.
