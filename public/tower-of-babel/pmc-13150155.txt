Medical and Non-Surgical Strategies for Mild Traumatic Brain Injury
Authors: Min Soo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Historically, mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) was often overlooked. However, growing clinical consensus emphasizes that active intervention is essential to manage physiological impairments and facilitate functional recovery. As secondary impact syndrome poses fatal risks during the acute phase, transitioning from passive rest to multimodal, active management is critical. This paper reviews current medical and non-surgical strategies for mTBI, shifting the paradigm from simple observation to proactive, evidence-based interventions. The review categorizes non-surgical interventions into 4 primary domains. First, lifestyle modification serves as the foundation, prioritizing 24–48 hours of rest, hydration, and minimized cognitive load from screens and light. Second, manual and physical therapies are employed; osteopathic manipulative medicine addresses musculoskeletal dysfunction, while hyperbaric oxygen therapy and blood flow restriction training support neurovascular repair and physical conditioning. Third, pharmacotherapy and supplementation focus on mitigating oxidative stress via antioxidants like MLC901 and enhancing cognitive function through medications such as methylphenidate or galantamine. Finally, active rehabilitation incorporates early intermediate-level aerobic exercise and vestibular rehabilitation to normalize cerebral blood flow and resolve balance deficits. The management of mTBI has evolved into a multimodal approach. By integrating these specialized strategies, clinicians can effectively mitigate long-term sequelae and promote rapid functional restitution.

INTRODUCTION

Historically, the symptoms following a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) were often overlooked or considered minor. However, there is a growing clinical consensus that active intervention is essential to manage the various physiological impairments and symptoms that arise from the injury. While strong evidence for a definitive medical “cure” remains limited, current strategies focus on symptomatic relief and functional recovery. During the acute phase, patients often experience significant limitations in cognitive, physical, and social activities. Proper recovery is critical because a secondary impact before the initial injury has healed can lead to “secondary impact syndrome,” characterized by rapid brain swelling and potentially fatal risks.

LIFESTYLE MODIFICATION: THE FOUNDATION OF CARE

The most fundamental management for mTBI involves a period of rest and hydration for 24 to 48 hours.11) During this time, lifestyle modification is reported to be crucial for recovering symptoms and restoring impaired neuronal connectivity and function. It is highly beneficial to minimize background noise and distractions to reduce cognitive load. Additionally, reducing the use of screens such as monitors and smartphones is helpful, and wearing sunglasses or hats for several days or weeks can effectively manage light sensitivity by avoiding bright light.10)

OSTEOPATHIC MANIPULATIVE MEDICINE (OMM)

As a proactive measure, OMM is implemented to address issues within the musculoskeletal system. The acceleration and deceleration forces involved in a head injury often cause musculoskeletal dysfunction and pain, which can subsequently limit the recovery of other symptoms. To alleviate this pain, techniques such as suboccipital release, counterstrain, muscle energy, and myofascial release are widely used and have been reported to provide immediate improvement in Visual Analog Scale scores.2) While long-term improvement still lacks sufficient evidence and requires further research, osteopathic manipulative treatment (OMT) is also being studied for its potential to resolve vestibular dysfunction symptoms such as dizziness and gaze fixation disorders.3) Research conducted by Yao et al.17) used the SCAT2 tool l, a standardized clinical assessment for evaluating concussion symptoms and cognitive deficits, to demonstrate improvements in physical, sensory, cognitive, and emotional symptoms following OMT, though individual clinical judgment is still necessary due to certain study limitations.15)

PHARMACOTHERAPY AND ANTIOXIDANT SUPPLEMENTS

Following an mTBI, the brain’s attempt to repair damaged cells and neurons often disrupts the balance between reactive oxygen species and antioxidants, leading to oxidative stress. This process results in a decrease in the body’s antioxidant levels, making the supplementation of antioxidants a helpful recovery strategy.4) Specifically, the natural supplement MLC901 (NeuroAiD II) is reported to be beneficial as it induces cell proliferation to repair damaged nerves and improve cognitive function while reducing neurodegeneration.6) Long-term use of MLC901 for over 6 months has also shown efficacy in improving complex attention and executive functioning.14) When cognitive decline is prominent, medications such as methylphenidate and galantamine can be used to improve cognitive performance and depressive symptoms.518) Methylphenidate works by blocking the reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine in the frontal lobe to enhance information processing speed and executive function, while galantamine inhibits the breakdown of acetylcholine to specifically improve episodic memory (TABLE 1).

PHYSICAL THERAPIES

Modern protocols have shifted from passive rest to active recovery strategies.

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT)

HBOT involves breathing high-concentration oxygen in a pressurized chamber to facilitate oxygen transport through plasma, which improves oxygen delivery to areas with microvascular damage. This process activates mitochondria to repair damaged neurons and stimulates vascular endothelial growth factor to recover the neurovascular network, showing positive effects on memory, information processing, and depression. However, some reports indicate that excessive oxygen can lead to oxidative stress symptoms, such as increased fatigue.13)

Blood flow restriction (BFR) and cooling

For athletes, BFR training involves performing moderate-intensity interval exercise at approximately 65% of the predicted maximal heart rate while applying venous flow limitation devices to the limbs. In practice, this method can be applied to TBI patients who struggle with high-intensity physical exertion due to post-concussive symptoms; for instance, using BFR during low-load resistance training or simple gait exercises can promote muscle protein synthesis without causing excessive intracranial pressure or cognitive fatigue. Furthermore, this approach is effective in the early stages of recovery to counteract muscle atrophy from prolonged bed rest, maintaining physical conditioning even when heavy lifting is contraindicated.8) This technique causes the body to perceive the activity as high-intensity exercise due to the accumulation of metabolites in the muscles, which triggers the release of growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor-1.7) Furthermore, combining this training with body cooling helps reduce cellular stress and supports a stable recovery process that maintains athletic motivation and physical conditioning.16)

AEROBIC EXERCISE AND VESTIBULAR REHABILITATION (VRT)

Recent studies suggest that early treatment using intermediate-level aerobic exercise aids in the recovery of activity intensity levels. This approach facilitates a faster recovery by normalizing cerebral blood flow through improved autonomic nervous system regulation, enhancing neuronal connectivity via brain-derived neurotrophic factor production, and providing anti-inflammatory benefits.9) Additionally, since impairment of the vestibulospinal tract can cause dizziness, balance issues, and gaze fixation difficulties, VRT therapies such as Brandt-Daroff exercises and gait stabilization training are highly recommended.112)

CONCLUSION

The paradigm for mTBI management has evolved from simple rest and hydration to an active, multimodal approach. By integrating lifestyle modifications, specialized manual therapies, targeted pharmacotherapy, and early active rehabilitation, clinicians can more effectively mitigate long-term sequelae and promote faster functional recovery for patients (TABLE 2).
