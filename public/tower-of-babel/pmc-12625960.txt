Introduction

The human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine helps protect against cancers caused by HPV infection. HPV can cause a number of cancers, notably causing over 95% of cervical cancer.1 In the country of Georgia, cervical cancer is the third leading cause of female cancer death.2 The HPV vaccine was first piloted in the country in 2017, then added to the national vaccination schedule for girls aged 9–13 in 2019. There is also a ‘catch up’ programme which extends the eligibility criteria to the age of 18. Achieving adequate HPV vaccination coverage may allow cervical cancer in Georgia to be virtually eliminated over time.3 However, while data on HPV vaccination in the country is limited, sources concur that coverage remains low and that it fell over the course of the COVID-19 pandemic. The WHO and UNICEF estimate that globally at least 3.5 million children missed the first dose of the HPV vaccine as a result of the pandemic,4 and the problem in Georgia is particularly stark, with joint WHO/UNICEF estimates for coverage of the first dose of the HPV vaccine among 10–14 year old girls in Georgia falling from 38% for 2019 to 14% for 2021,5 a 63% decrease in coverage. This compares unfavourably to other countries: there were decreases of 41%, 24% and 22% in England, Switzerland and Greece respectively, while other countries such as Israel and Australia saw negligible impact on the coverage of HPV vaccination as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic.6

Behavioural science has been extensively employed to develop and implement strategies to promote vaccine uptake globally. One such strategy is the use of social norms, which entails communicating to individuals the high vaccination rates in their community to encourage conformity.7 Another effective approach is the use of financial incentives, such as gift cards and lotteries, to incentivise vaccine uptake.8 Behavioural science has also been leveraged to address vaccine hesitancy by identifying and addressing the underlying beliefs and concerns that prevent people from getting vaccinated. For example, informative and emotionally resonant messaging has been shown to be effective in addressing concerns about vaccine safety.7 Additionally, behavioural science has facilitated the development of interventions that aim to reduce barriers to vaccination, such as waiting times, to make the vaccination process more convenient and accessible. These strategies have demonstrated effectiveness in increasing vaccine uptake and continue to play an integral role in global vaccination efforts.9

Some of the most promising evidence in high-income countries exists for behaviourally informed interventions (sometimes known as a ‘nudge’), which are informed by insights from behavioural science to influence people’s decisions and actions more effectively. For instance, those seeking to encourage vaccine uptake may offer incentives to parents and healthcare workers, or make information more salient or use trusted messengers to deliver information.10 There is growing evidence from the USA and other high-income countries that nudges, in particular those using short message service (SMS), can be a successful and cost-efficient method of encouraging vaccination.11,14 Framing indicating that a vaccine has been ‘reserved’ for the individual (or someone in their care) has been found to be particularly effective in some instances.15,17 Where knowledge of the vaccine schedule is limited, reminder and recall messages can be effective at bridging the information gap and encouraging patients and/or their caregivers to take up the offer of immunisation.18 Evidence from low- and middle-income countries, although with significant heterogeneity, also suggests that SMSRs can contribute to achieving high and timely childhood immunisation coverage.19 A Cochrane review of patient reminder and recall interventions to improve vaccination rates included a meta-analysis on 55 studies with 138 000 participants which estimated that such interventions increase uptake by an average of 28% in relative terms.20 However, evidence outside of the USA (and English-speaking countries in general) has remained scarce. Given this, a large-scale randomised control trial (RCT) was carried out as part of efforts by the UNICEF Georgia Country Office to increase HPV vaccination rates in Georgia. UNICEF partnered with the Behavioural Insights Team (BIT) to explore behavioural barriers to vaccination in the country, to develop solutions and to test their effectiveness in increasing vaccination uptake. This study also provides valuable evidence on the effectiveness of nudges in Georgia, where they have not been previously trialled.

Randomised control trial

This RCT included all 55 176 Georgian girls aged 10–12 who were eligible to receive their first dose of the HPV vaccine and had at least one caregiver contact number associated with them in the National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC)’s public health database. The trial tested the impact of four behaviourally-informed SMS reminders (SMSRs) sent to their caregivers, against a control in which no SMSR was sent. The four versions of the SMSR were chosen as they build on promising evidence from research in this area and address the fact that caregivers did not previously receive reminders that their daughter was due for HPV vaccination, a specific barrier identified during earlier exploratory work. The reminders were informed by the recent behavioural science literature and emulated other SMS and non-SMS based nudges to encourage vaccination in the USA and other high-income countries,21,23 within a context (non-English speaking, former-Soviet country) where such evidence is limited. Recent evidence suggests a high acceptability among caregivers for mobile-phone reminder interventions to improve routine childhood immunisation coverage and timeliness.24 25

Details on intervention: SMSRs

The four versions of the SMSR, to be tested against a control (no SMSR), were as follows: (1) a short simple reminder message with no additional information, (2) a reminder message with a link to the section of the NCDC’s website that provides information about HPV vaccination, (3) a reminder message with the NCDC link and ‘reserved for her’ framing and (4) a reminder message with the NCDC link and additional safety information about the HPV vaccine. These SMSRs were translated into Georgian by the UNICEF Georgia country office, with specific care to maintain the integrity of the insights from behavioural science being employed. The Georgian-language versions are shown in Table T12 in the Technical Appendix. The intervention was then implemented through the national SMSR system for vaccination, supported by the Information Technology Agency (ITA) in Georgia. This involved identifying the population of girls eligible to receive the HPV vaccine (those aged 10–12 who have not yet received the first dose of the HPV vaccine) with the mobile phone number of at least one caregiver in the national e-health system database. The target population was then randomised into five arms, in which the girls’ caregivers would receive either no reminder (control) or one of the four versions of the SMSR (treatment). The NCDC and ITA sent the SMSRs using their national SMS system, according to group allocation, on 15 September 2022. The overall reported delivery rate of the SMSRs was 99.5% (99.4% or 99.5% for all treatment arms), see Table T9 in the Technical Appendix for more detail. Recipients of the SMSRs saw the sender ID as ‘NCDC.ge’ (‘ge’ being the 2-letter ISO country code for Georgia), indicating that the SMS was sent from an official source. No follow-up SMSRs were sent during the trial period.

UNICEF received ethical approval for the trial from the Institutional Review Board of the Georgian National Centre for Disease Control and Public Health (NCDC), with reference number ‘IRB # 2022–057’. The trial was self-assessed as having a low ethical risk using the Behavioural Insights Team’s ethics assessment framework. The trial was pre-registered with ClinicalTrials.Gov on 13 September 2022, with the ID NCT05536674.

Methods

Research aims and hypothesis

SMSRs were hypothesised to increase the uptake of HPV vaccination. The primary outcome measure was HPV vaccination status (first dose) at trial end. Academic literature on similar interventions in other settings indicates that the ‘reserved for (her)’ framing such as that used in version 3 of the SMSRs may be the most effective in terms of increased uptake of the HPV vaccine.15 16 However, given the lack of evidence from similar settings, no hypothesis was pre-specified as to which version would be most effective in increasing vaccination rates in this specific study.

Power calculations

Based on previously published meta-analysis,20 our power calculations indicated that for all comparisons (any treatment arm vs control), the anticipated effect size was a 28% relative increase in uptake of the first dose of the HPV vaccine, between the treatment and control conditions. For instance, assuming a base rate of vaccination of 3% during the trial period, a relative increase of 28% would amount to a 0.84 pp increase in the take-up of the first dose of the HPV vaccine, to a rate of 3.84%. Given standard assumptions (shown in Table T11 in the Technical Appendix), the planned minimum detectable effect size (with correction for multiple comparisons) was anticipated to be lower than the anticipated effect of the intervention if the total sample was at least n=46 250. As described below in ‘Participant selection and eligibility’, the final sample was larger than this (55 176).

Participant selection and eligibility

Participants in the trial were eligible girls (eg, those aged 10–12 who had not yet received any doses of the HPV vaccine, with at least one caregiver’s mobile phone number in the e-health system). The total national population of girls aged 10–12 within the NCDC database was 89 821 in August 2022. Of these, 69 717 had not received the first dose of the HPV vaccine (meaning a vaccination coverage of 22.4% for this cohort). Caregiver contact numbers were available for 55 176 of these 69 717 girls, representing the eligible population of interest. The participant flow diagram is shown in figure 1.

Given the large scale of the sample (the sample of 55 176 girls contained over 61% of the nationwide population of girls aged 10–12 in Georgia), it was not possible to involve participants or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of the research. As the intervention itself does not affect the treatment being provided to patients, not obtaining explicit consent for sending the SMSRs was considered justifiable.

Randomisation and balance checks

The eligible population was randomised at the individual (girl) level, into five arms (four of n=11 035, and one of n=11 036) using randomisation functionality in Stata 16.1. The caregiver(s) of each girl were sent one of the four versions of the SMSR (or no SMSR) according to the girl’s allocation. Each girl could have up to four caregivers associated with them within the NCDC/ITA database (mean n=1.8), with each caregiver having up to six telephone numbers (mean n=1.1), meaning that each girl could have her caregiver(s) be sent and receive her assigned SMSR multiple times.

Randomisation was not stratified on any variable; however, balance checks were performed post-randomisation to test that each of the five trial arms was balanced on the key covariates available within the data: age, region and number of caregivers. The trial arms were similar with respect to each of these covariates. This also applied (ex post) to the number of SMSRs a girl’s caregiver(s) were sent as part of the trial, with an even distribution of the number of reminders sent across treatment arms. Full details and balance checks confirming the consistent distribution of participants by region, age, number of parents, number of SMSRs sent and primary caregiver type are provided in the Technical Appendix (see Tables T4, T5, T6, T7 and T8 in the Technical Appendix, Section A3).

Spillover

Randomisation was carried out at the girl, rather than caregiver, level. This meant there was a chance of contamination across trial arms, given the trial covered a very large sample of caregivers of girls aged 10–12 (with the sample of 55 176 girls containing over 61% of the total population of girls aged 10–12 in Georgia). This individual (girl) level randomisation did not take account of siblings within the sample, meaning that two or more eligible girls who were sisters could have been randomised into different trial arms, and the same caregiver(s) being sent different versions of the reminder. If one sister was included in the control group, but another sister(s) included in one of the treatment groups, their common caregiver would have been sent an SMSR, with no specific reference as to which daughter the SMSR was referring to. Our subsequent analysis found that approximately 17% of girls in the control arm (1871) had at least one caregiver who was sent an SMSR. This constitutes a relatively significant instance of spillover, meaning that our estimates of the treatment effects from the trial are likely to be conservative. This is considered further in the ‘Discussion’ section below. A competing but less likely cause of spillovers is that two caregivers who know each other will receive different messages, or one caregiver of the two will receive no SMSR.

Statistical analysis

The logistical regression model used in our analyses is described in the Technical Appendix, Section A5. The Stata code used for data cleaning, analysis and generation of figures is available for replication purposes (see Appendices A8–A12 in the Technical Appendix).

Data collection

NCDC/ITA sent the SMSRs using their national SMS system, according to group allocation. SMSRs were sent on 15 September 2022, between 11 am and 1 pm local time. Data collection occurred 62 days later, on 16 November 2022, with the final dataset available in online supplemental file 2. The primary outcome was a girl’s HPV vaccination status at this date. Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research due to the large scale and nature of the intervention, which was implemented at the national level using existing public health systems.

Results

2032 girls across the full eligible sample received the HPV vaccine during the 62-day trial period, representing an aggregate vaccination rate of 3.76%. Fewer girls in the control group received the vaccine than those in the treatment arms (255 girls out of 10 828 eligible for analysis in the control group, a rate of 2.4%, compared with a mean rate of 4.1% (1777 girls out of 43 200) across the four treatment groups (range 3.9%–4.7%). The group whose caregivers were sent version 3 of the SMSR had the highest rate of HPV vaccination (4.7%). This SMSR used framing indicating that the vaccine was ‘reserved’ for the girl, and its relative success is a reproduction of findings in other contexts.

Intention-to-treat analysis: the effect of sending a behaviourally informed SMSR on vaccination

An intention-to-treat (ITT) logistic regression analysis was used to examine the impact of treatment conditions on likelihood of being vaccinated during the 62-day trial period. ITT analysis involves analysing participants based on their initial randomisation to the treatment and control group(s), regardless of whether they received the assigned treatment/control or not. In practice, this means that all girls who were assigned to a given treatment group are included in the analysis as such, regardless of whether their caregiver(s) did actually receive the assigned version of the SMSR (or were sent no SMSR, or a version they should not have been sent according to the trial design). The trial was analysed as ITT (that is, analysis on SMSRs sent, rather than delivered or read), as not all SMSRs sent would have been delivered to or read by caregivers. The logistic regression controlled for the following factors: age, number of caregivers, cumulative number of SMSRs sent, region within Georgia and the ‘type’ of primary caregiver (eg, mother, father, guardian, etc).

The ITT analysis showed that each of the SMSRs was more effective than the control condition in encouraging HPV vaccination during the trial period (p<0.01). Figure 2 shows the treatment effect for each version of the SMSR, by including the realised vaccination rate for the control group and constructed counterfactual vaccination of what would have happened if the control group had received that treatment (figure 2 does not display the actual rates of vaccination for each treatment arm, but instead for each bar represents the vaccination rate for the control group plus the treatment effect for each version of the SMSR as estimated by the ITT regression analysis). Girls whose caregiver(s) were sent version 3 (the SMS with ‘reserved for her’ framing) had 65% greater odds of receiving the vaccine relative to the control group (OR=1.65; 95% CI 1.38 to 1.97). Among girls whose caregivers received version 1 (Short SMS), version 2 (Short SMS+NCDC link) and version 4 (Safety information+NCDC link), the odds of receiving the HPV vaccine during the trial were respectively 42% (OR=1.42; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.70), 34% (OR=1.34; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.61) and 35% (OR=1.35; 95% CI 1.13 to 1.62) higher than the control arm. Treatment-on-treated analysis: the effect of receiving a behaviourally-informed SMSR on vaccination

While no sensitivity analysis was pre-specified, on discovering the extent of treatment spillover into the control arm (as described in ‘Methods’, and considered further in ‘Discussion’), we wished to check the robustness of the results. To do so, the analysis was repeated using a ‘Treatment-on-Treated’ (ToT) approach, analysing only the participants who received and completed the assigned treatment (or control) condition. This approach excluded girls who, for example, through some error in SMS dissemination, did not receive the treatment (or control) condition assigned to them during the randomisation process. For the ToT logistic regression analysis, the following were excluded: (a) 1871 girls in the control group who had at least one caregiver that was sent an SMSR, due to the fact they shared a caregiver with another girl who was assigned to one of the four treatment groups; and (b) 130 girls in the treatment groups whose caregiver(s) were sent SMSR(s) that were not delivered (according to the ITA’s tracking systems). The exclusion of these girls does not cause the groups to become unbalanced across any characteristic.

For the ToT analysis, although excluding this cohort under (a) above does (1) reduce the size of the control group relative to the other groups within the analysis and (2) imply that girls in the ToT control group are potentially less likely to have sisters than the girls in the treatment arms, the sample is still sufficiently powered, and the results (and their interpretation) are consistent with the findings from the ITT analysis, as seen in figure 3. As can be seen from a comparison of figure 2 (ITT) and figure 3 (ToT), the relative effectiveness and rank of the SMSRs under each analytical approach are almost identical (with only some minor changes in percentages), that is, the results are consistent across the two analyses.

Exploratory results: age, numbers of SMSRs sent, regional differences and temporal trends

While the primary aim of the trial was to investigate the impact of sending SMSRs on the subsequent uptake of the HPV vaccine among unvaccinated girls cared for by the recipients of the SMSR, our results show that there are other factors that are strongly correlated with HPV vaccine uptake during the trial period. These factors include a girl’s age, the number of SMSRs sent to their caregiver(s), the region within the country and time elapsed within the 62-days trial period. Findings related to the first two of these factors are presented below. The analyses of these factors were not pre-specified; however, they were found to have had a statistically significant impact on the likelihood of receiving the vaccine.

Age

The results of the logistic regression (Table T1 in the Technical online supplemental appendix file) show that age also had a statistically significant impact on the likelihood of girls receiving the HPV vaccine during the 62-day trial period. The age variable produced an odds ratio of 0.71 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.76), indicating that for each year older a girl within the sample is, she was 29% less likely to have received the vaccine. This age effect is not driven by the different versions of the SMSRs having more/less impact on caregivers depending on the age of the girls in their care. When the primary analysis is run separately for each age cohort (10 year-olds, 11 year-olds, 12 year-olds), the relative effectiveness of the SMSRs does not change across cohorts (see online supplemental figure S1 and table T2 respectively in the Technical Appendix for HPV vaccination rate and regression coefficients disaggregated by trial arm and by age).

Number of SMSRs sent per girl

Another factor which had a statistically significant impact on the likelihood of a girl receiving the HPV vaccine during the trial period is the cumulative number of SMSRs sent to all caregivers associated with the girl within the NCDC/ITA database. Although the mean number of caregivers associated with a girl was 1.8, this ranged from one for some girls to a maximum of four for others. In addition, within the database, each caregiver could have up to six telephone contact numbers associated with them. The maximum number of SMSRs sent to the caregivers of any individual girl was 10, and the mean number 1.9. As randomisation was carried out at the girl level, messages sent to the caregiver(s) of a girl were all identical (unless those caregiver(s) had eligible girls in different treatment arms in their care, with such ‘spillover’ addressed in the Discussion section).

Controlling for all other factors, for each additional SMSR a girl’s caregiver(s) were sent, the girl was 10% more likely to have received the HPV vaccine within the trial period (OR=1.10 (SE=0.02; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.14)). See online supplemental figure S2 and table T3 in the Technical Appendix for both vaccination rate and the number of girls disaggregated by the number of SMSRs sent per girl, and Table T2 in the Technical Appendix for the ITT regression results controlling for numbers of SMSRs sent. This result controls for the number of caregivers associated with a given girl within the NCDC data, and the raw number of caregivers itself is not found to have a statistically significant effect on the likelihood of vaccination (OR 1.09 (SE 0.07; 95% CI 0.96 to 1.24)).

Discussion

Primary analysis

The results outlined above show a positive and statistically significant impact for each of the four versions of the SMSR, relative to the control condition where no SMSR was sent. While each of the four versions of the SMS achieved a statistically significant increase in vaccination relative to the control group, version 3 of the SMSR (with ‘reserved for her’ framing) was the most effective in encouraging uptake of the HPV vaccine. This corroborates what is found in the literature in other contexts.15 16 If all caregivers in the trial had received this most effective message, an estimated additional 488 girls would have been vaccinated, in addition to the 2032 that received the HPV vaccine during the trial. In short, cumulatively, the four versions of our SMSR intervention were associated with a 75% relative increase in vaccination rate compared with the control (no SMS), at a marginal cost of just $0.15 (0.40 GEL (Georgian Lari) at the time of the trial) per additional vaccination observed. This result was obtained by targeting only one of the barriers to vaccination (a lack of reminders for caregivers) identified in the previous exploratory phase of the research, and with a sample of girls whose caregivers had not yet brought them to receive the HPV vaccine. It is probable that these caregivers were more hesitant towards the HPV vaccine than the average caregiver in Georgia.

Exploratory analysis

Age

Exploratory analysis showed that older girls within the sample were significantly less likely to have received the HPV vaccine. This may in part be explained by the fact that girls who were already aged 12 by the time the sample was selected had already been eligible to receive the free HPV vaccine for at least 2 years. Therefore, on average, they may be more likely to have caregivers who were hesitant towards the vaccine compared with the 10-year-old girls in the sample (who may have been more likely to have not received a vaccine simply due to a lack of opportunity or planning on behalf of their caregivers during their shorter time in the window of eligibility).

Number of SMSRs sent per girl

For each additional SMSR sent, girls were 10% more likely to have received the HPV vaccine during the trial period. There are two potential explanations for this effect. First, it is possible that the impact of the treatment—a girl’s caregiver(s) being sent SMSR(s)—on the likelihood of the girl receiving the vaccine simply grows (in cumulative terms and by an average of 10% per SMS) with the number of SMSR sent. If so, this highlights the importance of keeping accurate caregiver contact details within the e-health system, and of contacting as many caregivers as possible with such reminders or informational messages. However, another explanation that must be considered is that caregivers/families who register a greater number of contact numbers with the NCDC (via family doctors, etc.) are inherently more likely to bring their daughter(s) to be vaccinated on receiving an SMS—that is, they may have a greater latent desire for their daughter to be vaccinated and/or a greater trust in the health system generally (but had not yet organised HPV vaccination for the girl(s) in their care). To better understand this consideration, future research could make use of qualitative data on caregivers’ attitudes towards vaccination and/or data on each girl’s uptake of other vaccines and/or on her and her family’s engagement with the public health system generally.

Trends in vaccination during the trial period

There was a distinctively high number of vaccinations among the group that received the ‘reserved for her’ SMS (orange graph line) in the weeks immediately following the intervention (figure 4). It is possible that this ‘reserved for her’ framing prompted a more immediate call to action from recipient caregivers, relative to those who received versions 2 and 4 of the SMSRs.

Challenges in implementation: spillover effects

Our analysis found that approximately 17% of girls in the control arm (1871) had at least one caregiver who was sent an SMSR, which meant that some of the control group were directly exposed to the treatment. While any spillover is undesirable from a research perspective, the partial exposure of the control group to various treatment conditions would lead to an underestimation of the effects of the intervention (rather than an overestimation) within the ITT analysis, and likely a higher number of girls receiving a potentially life-saving vaccine.

Randomisation at the level of the caregiver, although not possible to implement given data availability and linkages when the trial took place, would have eliminated this spillover effect and would have ensured that two sisters who have both been included in such a trial receive the same treatment or non-treatment. A further consideration when planning to implement such an intervention in similar contexts is the requirement for a centralised or single digital database containing the requisite data on vaccination status, demographics and contact details. While this was possible due to the availability of this data via the NCDC and ITA, it may not be possible in all low- or middle-income countries, where necessary elements (such as digitalisation of health records) may be lacking.

Implications and conclusion

The results of this trial represent a novel contribution to the literature, demonstrating the application of an emerging tool (SMSRs informed by behavioural science) to a previously untested context. Working directly with partners in the NCDC and ITA, over 55 000 girls aged 10–12 were included in the trial, representing the entire population of eligible unvaccinated girls of this age, allowing for five well-powered trial arms testing four versions of the SMSR. While the absolute rate of vaccination remained modest across all arms, ultimately the 65% effect size (observed by comparing the vaccination rate of the control group with that of the ‘reserved for her’ treatment group) compares favourably to the 28% anticipated effect size suggested in the academic literature.26 This is a notable result for what was in essence a recall campaign, for girls that were already eligible for the HPV vaccine and whose caregivers were simply receiving a reminder of this (ie, rather than a scheduled notification on becoming eligible for vaccination). Therefore, it is likely that the population of girls included within our trial is those with caregivers who, either through complacency, a lack of understanding or hesitancy, had not taken their daughters to receive the HPV vaccine before the trial.

If the entire sample of 55 176 girls in the trial had been sent the ‘reserved for her’ SMSR (version 3), this would have resulted in approximately 2520 girls receiving the HPV vaccine during the 62-day period, meaning a further 488 girls receiving the vaccine in just 62 days. Comparing a scenario in which all girls in the sample had caregivers who were sent the ‘reserved for her framing’ SMS (version 3) against a scenario where no SMSRs were sent, the difference would have been an additional 1250 girls receiving the vaccine (ie, 2565 girls if all received version 3; vs 1315 girls if no SMSRs were sent). The total cost of sending 97 057 SMSRs across all treatment arms was approximately US$146, a marginal cost of less than US$0.01 per SMSR sent. Given that the cost of sending SMSRs to each treatment arm was US$36.50, and that version 3 of the SMS achieved 250 vaccinations in excess of the control group, there is an estimated marginal cost of less than US$0.15 for each additional vaccination attributable to version 3 of the SMSR. Note that these calculations of marginal cost assume the prior existence of an e-health system for sending such SMSRs, as is the case in Georgia. Establishing such a system would add significant implementation costs if this trial was to be replicated in contexts where this would be required. Where such systems enabling SMSRs are not in place, alternative interventions targeting a lack of reminders for caregivers might include visits from community health workers, postal reminders, or public health campaigns via radio or television.

These results show great promise for achieving considerable real-world impact at a low cost, and the NCDC and Ministry of Health in Georgia have decided to (1) use the most effective version of the SMS (‘reserved for her’ framing) with all future cohorts of girls as they become newly eligible for vaccination and (2) scale the use of this version of the SMS to their national catch-up campaign for unvaccinated 13–18 year olds, potentially leading to thousands of HPV vaccinations that may not have occurred otherwise. Furthermore, these national health authorities have decided to expand the use of this ‘reserved for her’ framing to their 2023 public health reminder campaigns for routine immunisation, screening for breast cancer, cervical cancer, colorectal cancer and screening for hepatitis C. Ultimately, the SMSR intervention evaluated in this trial directly addresses only one of the many barriers to vaccination (a lack of reminders for caregivers). While the SMSRs have demonstrated promising potential, it will also be necessary for public health authorities and policymakers to implement—and evaluate—interventions and policies that target other barriers to children receiving this and other potentially life-saving vaccines.
