Personalized neoadjuvant treatment regimen selection in locally advanced rectal cancer based on regimen-specific response modeling
Authors: Xiangyu Liu, Yuanling Tang, Song Zhang, Haiyang Bian, Hanlin Shu, Leen Liao, Xiaolin Pang, Qianting Lv, Jia Chen, Peirong Ding, Ping Liu, Yu Shen, Ziqiang Wang, Shouping Zhu, Jie Tian, Zhenyu Liu, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoadjuvant therapy is standard for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), yet regimen selection remains population-based, risking over- or undertreatment. We developed and validated a deep learning framework that provides a generalizable paradigm for data-driven treatment regimen selection by estimating patient-specific probabilities of pathological complete response (pCR) across multiple therapeutic options. In a multicenter cohort, a hard-gated mixture-of-experts model integrating pretreatment multiparametric MRI and clinical variables generated regimen-specific pCR probabilities to support clinician-led treatment decision-making. The model achieved strong predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.827 and 0.790 in the validation and prospective test cohorts. In the combined validation and test cohorts, 53.16% of patients were recommended treatment escalation, with an observed pCR rate of 11.11% and a model-estimated pCR probability of 30.95% under the model-supported regimen. Meanwhile, 5.91% of patients were identified for de-intensification while maintaining a high estimated likelihood of response. This framework provides probabilistic support for multidisciplinary optimization of neoadjuvant treatment intensity in LARC. The prospective cohort was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400085797; June 18, 2024).Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT), which consists of preoperative radiotherapy combined with concurrent chemotherapy, followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, has long been considered the standard of care for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC)1,2. While this approach improves local control, it provides limited systemic eradication of micrometastatic disease3,4. Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT, a treatment strategy that delivers all systemic chemotherapy before surgery, either before or after chemoradiotherapy) has emerged as a promising regimen for LARC, demonstrating significant improvements in pathological complete response (pCR) and disease-free survival (DFS) compared to standard nCRT5,6. However, for patients with low- or intermediate-risk rectal cancer, the potential for overtreatment and unnecessary toxicity associated with nCRT and TNT is a cause for concern7,8. In parallel, recent evidence suggests that neoadjuvant chemotherapy (nCT) alone provides comparable oncologic outcomes to nCRT for carefully selected low-risk patients, with reduced toxicity9–11. The growing diversity of therapeutic options, combined with substantial variability in treatment sensitivity between patients, highlights the need for decision-support tools that can tailor treatment intensity to individual patient profiles. This would optimize responses while minimizing toxicity12.

In current practice, the choice of neoadjuvant treatment regimen for LARC is usually based on clinical or radiological factors, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serving as a central tool. MRI enables precise tumor staging and identification of high-risk features, such as circumferential resection margin (CRM) involvement and extramural venous invasion (EMVI). While useful for risk stratification, existing clinical and imaging indicators are insufficient to reflect treatment sensitivity and inform individual regimen selection13. pCR represents a highly favorable tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy14,15. Patients who achieve pCR may be considered for a watch-and-wait approach and generally have improved long-term outcomes, making it a clinically relevant endpoint for guiding treatment regimen selection. An optimal strategy would maintain the likelihood of pCR while minimizing treatment intensity, escalating therapy only when necessary to increase the probability of pCR. This highlights the critical need for accurate, noninvasive prediction of pCR prior to therapy initiation, which remains a major clinical challenge.

With the continuous advancements of artificial intelligence, MRI analysis based on radiomics16–19 and deep learning20,21 has made encouraging progress in assessing the response to neoadjuvant therapy. However, most existing studies have not clearly specified the neoadjuvant treatment regimen when developing response prediction models, or focused exclusively on standard nCRT. TNT, as a currently recommended approach, and nCT, as a promising emerging strategy, have rarely been specifically addressed in existing response prediction models. In addition to differences in treatment intensity, inter-patient variability in treatment sensitivity contributes to highly heterogeneous responses within each neoadjuvant regimen6,22,23. Consequently, current models offer limited clinical utility for guiding regimen selection across contemporary treatment options.

In this study, we aimed to develop a pretreatment multiparametric MRI-based deep learning framework that predicts pCR probabilities under three neoadjuvant regimens (TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT) and validated its performance in a prospective cohort. The model incorporates prior knowledge regarding regimen intensity and uses a hard-gated mixture-of-experts (MoE) design to generate individualized counterfactual estimates from MRI and clinical features. These regimen-specific pCR probabilities enable patient-level therapy recommendations, favoring lower-intensity therapy when sufficient and escalation when needed to optimize response. Results

Patient clinical characteristics

As shown in Table 1, a total of 760 eligible patients were included in the retrospective cohort and randomly assigned into the training (n = 618) and validation (n = 142) cohorts. Among these patients, 258, 414, and 88 received TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT, respectively, with corresponding pCR rates of 30.23%, 19.08%, and 20.45%. The prospective test cohort comprised 95 patients, of whom 52, 24, and 19 received TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT, respectively, with pCR rates of 28.85%, 8.33%, and 10.53%. Among all 855 patients, there were 557 males (65.15%) and 298 females (34.85%). 489 patients (57.19%) were aged 60 years or younger at diagnosis. In both the validation and prospective test cohorts, several baseline clinical characteristics—including age, sex, tumor distance from the anal verge, and lateral lymph node metastasis (LLNM) status—did not differ significantly across treatment groups. Treatment compliance was generally satisfactory across the three treatment strategies. Radiotherapy completion rates were high in the TNT and standard nCRT groups (91.2% vs 92.3%). In the TNT group, 81% of patients completed 6 CAPOX cycles. In the nCT group, more than 90% of patients completed 4 CAPOX cycles.

Neoadjuvant therapy-response prediction performance

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 2a, b, the proposed hard-gated MoE-based response model (UNet-MoE-Cli) performed best overall among all the methods compared (including a Product-of-Experts (PoE)-based therapy selection strategy24), achieving an AUC of 0.827 and 0.790 in the validation and prospective test cohorts, respectively. Clinical-only models constructed using conventional high-risk clinical variables demonstrated limited predictive ability (Supplementary Table S1). Among several machine learning algorithms evaluated, LightGBM yielded the highest AUC in both cohorts (0.584 and 0.639) and was therefore selected as the clinical baseline. Overall, the modest performance across all algorithms underscores the restricted discriminative value of clinical factors alone.

Compared with ROI-only encoding networks such as ResNet-2D and ResNet-3D, the multi-task UNet encoder, which performed both segmentation and feature extraction, demonstrated more stable and superior performance across various datasets (AUC = 0.730 and 0.650). This indicated the benefit of structural priors and spatial context learning in MRI representation. Furthermore, the hard-gated UNet-MoE framework, which incorporated clinical priors and therapy-specific expert routing, showed a further performance improvement compared to the basic UNet model (AUC: 0.755 vs. 0.730 on validation; 0.702 vs. 0.650 on test). After finally incorporating clinical characteristics, UNet-MoE-Cli demonstrated the best performance across almost all metrics, confirming its robustness and generalization. Its high specificity and NPV indicated strong reliability in identifying non-pCR patients, providing a basis for further therapy decision support. Furthermore, to evaluate robustness to ROI boundary variations, 10% of training cases were randomly selected for 5-pixel expansion, and another 10% for 5-pixel contraction of the segmented ROIs, and the model was retrained while the validation and prospective test sets remained unchanged. The resulting UNet-MoE-Cli model exhibited only modest changes in predictive performance across both cohorts, suggesting that the framework primarily captures broader intratumoral and mesorectal response patterns rather than relying on highly precise contour delineation (Supplementary Table S2).

The pCR prediction performance of UNet-MoE-Cli for patients receiving different neoadjuvant therapies is summarized in Fig. 2c–e. The model achieved AUCs of 0.804, 0.820, and 0.748 in patients receiving TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT, respectively, indicating consistent discriminative performance across treatment regimens. The prognostic relevance of the model-derived risk score for DFS was further assessed using K-M analysis, with a median follow-up period of 40 months (Fig. 2f, g). A significant separation between risk groups was observed in both the training and validation cohorts, with Log-rank p-values of 0.0158 and 0.0297, respectively. To account for differences in preoperative treatment strategies (TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT), univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were further conducted in the combined training and validation cohorts (Supplementary Table S3). After adjustment for clinical variables and treatment regimen, the model-derived risk score remained independently associated with DFS. The treatment regimen also showed a significant association with survival outcomes. These findings indicate that the observed prognostic relationship was not solely attributable to baseline clinical characteristics or treatment allocation.

Clinical benefit of model-informed neoadjuvant regimen selection

Patient distribution under the hierarchical recommendation rule, together with the corresponding observed pCR rates and model-predicted pCR proportions, is summarized in Table 3 and Supplementary Table S4. In the validation cohort (n = 142), 50 patients (35.21%) were categorized by the model as maintaining their current regimen, 83 patients (58.45%) as candidates for treatment escalation (nCT to nCRT, nCT to TNT, nCRT to TNT), and 9 patients (6.34%) as candidates for de-intensification (TNT to nCRT, TNT to nCT, nCRT to nCT). In the prospective test cohort (n = 95), 47 patients (49.47%) were categorized as maintenance, 43 patients (45.26%) as escalation, and 5 patients (5.26%) as de-intensification. Patients in the model-supported maintenance group exhibited higher observed pCR rates than the overall cohort (validation: 30.00% vs. 23.24%; test: 21.28% vs. 20.00%), consistent with preferential retention of effective regimens. Patients in the model-supported escalation group had significantly worse observed outcomes under their original regimens, with lower pCR rates than those in the combined maintenance and de-intensification groups (validation: 12.05% vs. 38.98%, p < 0.001; test: 9.30% vs. 28.85%, p = 0.021). This finding suggested that the model could prospectively identify patients at risk of inadequate tumor response who may benefit from treatment intensification.

Counterfactual evaluation of the model-supported strategies demonstrated differences in estimated pCR probabilities across alternative treatment branches (Table 4). Among patients categorized as candidates for therapy escalation in the combined validation and test cohorts, the observed pCR rate under their actual regimens was 11.11%, whereas the model-estimated probability of pCR under the higher-intensity regimen was 30.95% (McNemar p < 0.001). This finding suggests that, within the model framework, a subset of patients classified as non-responders under their original therapy showed higher model-estimated response probabilities under the intensified regimen branch. Conversely, patients categorized for de-intensification exhibited high observed pCR (92.86%), and the model-estimated probability under the lower-intensity regimen remained comparably high (100%), indicating that response probability was preserved within the model projections. For patients assigned to maintenance, model-estimated probabilities remained relatively stable (25.77–27.84%), supporting internal consistency of the decision process. Based on model-estimated pCR probabilities across different therapy branches, 39 patients were reclassified by the model from estimated non-pCR under the original therapy branches to estimated pCR under the model-supported escalation strategy branches, whereas 12 patients maintained high estimated response probabilities while being categorized for de-intensification (Fig. 3a, b).

At the individual level, model-based reassignment to alternative treatment branches was associated with higher estimated pCR probabilities compared with the corresponding model-estimated probabilities under the original treatment branch (Fig. 3c, d). Mean predicted probabilities increased from 0.3319 to 0.5816 in the validation cohort and from 0.2570 to 0.4318 in the test cohort (both p < 0.0001). The magnitude of probability change was most pronounced in the escalation subgroup (validation: 0.1345 to 0.5845; test: 0.0289 to 0.4282; both p < 0.0001). In addition, the results of DCA confirmed the clinical applicability of the model for personalized therapy across implemented regimen subgroups and its potential to support individualized regimen selection based on model-predicted pCR probabilities (Fig. 3e–j).

Sensitivity analysis using IPTW

To assess robustness against non-random treatment allocation, we conducted an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis. After reweighting based on baseline clinical covariates and center information, predictive performance remained stable. The AUCs of UNet-MoE-Cli under the weighted setting were comparable to the primary analysis in both validation and test cohorts (Supplementary Table S5 and Fig. S1), with preserved discrimination across regimen subgroups. DeLong tests showed no significant differences between the IPTW-adjusted and original AUCs in the validation cohort (p = 0.1661) or the prospective test cohort (p = 0.7961), nor within the TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT subgroups (p = 0.1282, 0.7605, and 0.9468, respectively).

Importantly, the hierarchical recommendation patterns and directional differences in observed pCR rates were also maintained (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). Patients categorized for escalation continued to demonstrate lower observed pCR rates under their original regimens, whereas maintenance and de-intensification groups retained relatively higher response rates. In the combined validation and test cohorts, model-estimated pCR probabilities under intensified regimens in the escalation group remained substantially higher than the observed rates under actual treatment. These findings support the stability of the proposed framework under adjusted treatment assignment.

Model interpretability

Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) visualizations demonstrated consistent and clinically interpretable MRI response signatures across different treatment groups, distinguishing responders from non-responders and supporting the biological plausibility of the model’s decision-making (Fig. 4). Patients who achieved pCR, whether receiving the original therapy or following the model-supported therapy, all showed focal, convergent basal activation patterns on MRI. For patients who received nCT or standard nCRT but did not respond well to therapy, and who could achieve pCR after the model recommended an escalation therapy, the heatmap features were partially convergent, but expanded activation regions. In addition, despite advanced staging, a subset of clinically high-risk TNT patients exhibited focal heatmaps, leading the model to recommend therapy de-intensification to achieve pCR, suggesting a potentially favorable biology. By contrast, patients who consistently failed to achieve a pCR showed diffuse, ring-like distributions on MRI scans.

Discussion

This study developed a deep learning-based framework that leverages multicenter pretreatment MRI and clinical characteristics to predict pCR in patients with LARC, serving as a mediator for constructing a generalizable paradigm for neoadjuvant regimen selection. This framework was primarily validated for precise treatment choice among TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT, consistently demonstrating high predictive performance with AUC values approaching or exceeding 0.80. By integrating both treatment intensity and individual sensitivity, our model provides regimen-specific predictions and supports personalized decision-making in neoadjuvant therapy. Importantly, the model is intended as a probabilistic decision-support tool rather than a prescriptive system. In real-world multidisciplinary practice, the regimen-specific probability profiles could be reviewed during tumor board discussions as structured comparisons across alternative strategies, facilitating transparent consideration of potential treatment escalation or de-intensification. Final decisions remain guided by multidisciplinary expertise and additional patient-specific factors not explicitly modeled, including comorbidities, functional status, treatment tolerance, and individual preferences. To our knowledge, this represents the first study to establish such a predictive framework that translates LARC pCR estimates into individualized regimen selection across multiple neoadjuvant regimens.

The RAPIDO and PRODIGE-23 have shown that intensifying treatment through TNT leads to superior disease control (disease-related treatment failure: 23.4% vs. 30.7%; 3-year DFS: 76.0% vs. 69.0%) and higher pCR rates (28.4% vs. 14.3%; 27.8% vs. 12.1%) compared to standard nCRT in patients with LARC6,23. However, these benefits are accompanied by increased toxicity (grade 3 or higher adverse events: 47.6% vs. 24.7%; 46.9% vs. 35.7%). Meanwhile, the results of the PROSPECT trial suggested that selected low- and intermediate-risk patients could achieve comparable outcomes to standard nCRT with nCT while avoiding the potential toxicities of pelvic irradiation (5-year DFS: 80.8% vs. 78.6%; pCR: 21.9% vs. 24.0%)9. While treatment intensity clearly affects pCR, individual variability in treatment sensitivity further contributes to differences in response. Currently, no reliable clinical indicators can accurately predict this sensitivity or distinguish individual responses across different neoadjuvant strategies13. Consistently, in our study, pCR prediction based solely on clinical characteristics achieved only moderate performance in the validation and test cohorts. By contrast, the deep learning model proposed here provided accurate pCR predictions and could estimate patient-specific pCR probabilities under different treatment regimens, thereby enabling individualized therapy recommendations.

A key innovation of this study is the inclusion of patients treated with all three guideline-endorsed neoadjuvant strategies, nCT, standard nCRT, and TNT. This comprehensive design allowed the model to estimate pCR probabilities for each therapy at the individual level, distinguishing how a patient may respond differently to these alternative treatment regimens. To capture the complexity of multi-therapy scenarios, we developed a hard-gated MoE framework in which each treatment regimen was represented by a dedicated expert subnetwork. This design allowed the model to learn therapy-specific response patterns within separate feature subspaces. Furthermore, we introduced a clinically informed therapy-perturbation mechanism during training, which deliberately routed samples across different experts according to prior clinical knowledge. This encouraged each expert to better capture the theoretical therapy-response distribution and mitigate bias arising from real-world, non-randomized therapy assignment. In addition, while deterministic hard gating enhances interpretability and enforces regimen-specific feature learning, it may limit the explicit representation of uncertainty in borderline cases where predicted response probabilities across regimens are similar. In contrast, soft gating mechanisms allow weighted contributions from multiple experts and may better capture ambiguity in such scenarios. However, soft gating may reduce regimen-specific disentanglement and complicate clinical interpretability. In the present study, we prioritized structural interpretability and clear regimen attribution to facilitate clinical translation. Future work could explore hybrid or uncertainty-aware gating strategies to balance interpretability and probabilistic flexibility in real-world deployment.

Nevertheless, the clinically informed priors embedded in the therapy-perturbation strategy may not universally apply across all biological subtypes, treatment protocols, or evolving therapeutic paradigms. For example, the assumption that patients achieving pCR under nCT would also respond to intensified regimens may not hold in cases with differential chemotherapy sensitivity, radiotherapy resistance, or regimen-specific toxicity constraints. Institutional variations in treatment protocols, drug combinations, and radiation techniques may further influence the validity of these priors. Importantly, the proposed MoE framework is inherently extensible. As new neoadjuvant regimens or treatment sequences emerge, additional expert subnetworks can be incorporated to model regimen-specific response patterns, and the therapy-perturbation routing rules can be recalibrated to reflect updated clinical evidence or revised therapeutic hierarchies. Recalibration may involve retraining the model on contemporary multicenter datasets or fine-tuning individual experts while preserving shared encoder representations. Furthermore, as molecular stratification becomes increasingly integrated into treatment selection, the gating mechanism could incorporate biomarker features to enable biologically informed routing. In this way, the overall architecture remains stable while adapting to evolving standards of care and maintaining external validity.

Within this framework, patients categorized by the model as candidates for escalation exhibited low observed pCR rates under their received regimens (validation: 12.05%; test: 9.30%), indicating limited response under the existing treatment intensity. Conversely, those categorized for de-intensification demonstrated high observed baseline pCR rates (validation: 88.89%; test: 100.00%), suggesting that a subset of patients may maintain favorable response probabilities even under lower-intensity strategies within the model projections. Although true counterfactual outcomes cannot be directly observed, the pCR probabilities estimated by the well-trained expert subnetworks across different therapy branches provide a structured framework for comparing relative response likelihoods under alternative treatment scenarios. These model-derived probability shifts should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of clinical benefit. Under a hypothetical model-guided allocation scenario, the aggregated estimated pCR probability across the combined validation and test cohorts reached 33.76%, compared with the observed rate of 21.94%. This difference reflects the redistribution of estimated response probabilities across treatment branches rather than demonstrated therapeutic improvement. Importantly, the projected redistribution was not driven solely by universal treatment escalation, but by individualized reassignment of treatment intensity, whereby some patients were categorized for escalation and others for de-intensification. Notably, a subset of patients remained unlikely to achieve pCR even under intensified TNT within the model projections, underscoring that treatment decisions in such cases should continue to be guided by clinical judgment, tumor biology, and individualized therapeutic objectives. For patients with initially unresectable tumors or those unsuitable for sphincter preservation, intensified neoadjuvant strategies may still be considered for downstaging based on multidisciplinary evaluation rather than solely on model outputs.

These findings highlight the importance of carefully interpreting pCR-based treatment redistribution within the broader clinical context. Although pCR is clinically meaningful, particularly in the context of organ preservation, it remains an imperfect surrogate for long-term outcomes such as DFS and overall survival. Long-term prognosis is influenced by multiple factors beyond pathological response, including surgical quality and postoperative management, and therefore regimen optimization based on predicted pCR probabilities should not be interpreted as direct evidence of survival benefit. Rather, the present framework is intended to support individualized selection of the initial neoadjuvant strategy within the preoperative setting. Importantly, the model-derived pCR probability was independently associated with DFS in multivariable Cox analyses, suggesting potential prognostic relevance. In addition, prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of deep learning-based prediction of distant metastasis risk and chemotherapy benefit in locally advanced rectal cancer25,26. These complementary prognostic models provide a foundation for future integration with regimen-specific response prediction, potentially enabling a more comprehensive decision-support framework that spans both initial treatment selection and long-term outcome stratification.

In terms of MRI characterization, this study used the ROIs of the primary tumor and mesorectum, adopting a multi-task constraint of segmentation and classification to improve characterization accuracy. In previous studies, Jayaprakasam et al. developed a radiomic model based solely on the mesorectum, achieving a reasonable predictive performance for treatment response27. Kaval et al. found that combining radiomic features from the primary tumor and mesorectum yielded superior predictive performance for pCR, compared with models using tumor features alone28. These findings suggested that image features from the mesorectum may provide complementary information reflecting the tumor microenvironment and therapy response29. In addition, Jin et al.’s research showed that integrating interrelated segmentation and classification tasks into a unified model could improve the predictive performance of rectal cancer therapy response21. The results of the comparative experiment involving ROI-only characterization in this multicenter prospective study further validated this view.

The heatmap-derived activation patterns further provided biologically and clinically interpretable insight into the model’s individualized therapy recommendations. In patients with pCR, the heatmap exhibited a focal-convergent pattern, which may reflect more homogeneous intratumoral architecture and imaging characteristics. In contrast, non-pCR patients displayed diffuse and ring-like patterns reflecting higher intratumoral heterogeneity and complex infiltrative patterns that required the model to extract information from broader regions. Notably, patients who did not achieve pCR under actual treatment but were predicted by the model to achieve pCR under a model-supported therapy exhibited intermediate, partially convergent attention patterns. These patterns suggested that actual treatment may be insufficient for this subgroup, highlighting a potential benefit of therapy escalation based on model-guided recommendations. In Fig. 4b, the patient with ID 0019360733 presented with several high-risk clinical characteristics, including positive EMVI and CRM, for which standard nCRT was recommended clinically; however, the patient did not achieve pCR. The corresponding heatmap exhibited a relatively non-convergent activation pattern, and the model recommended an update to TNT, under which the patient was estimated to have a higher likelihood of achieving pCR. In contrast, some patients with even higher clinical stage displayed more focal, convergent heatmap patterns, and the model indicated that standard nCRT might also achieve pCR in these cases (e.g., Fig. 4a, ID 0013874527). The aforementioned interpretability analysis revealed that deep learning-based models have the potential to capture intrinsic tumor heterogeneity that may not be identifiable through traditional clinical or radiological assessments. In this context, the high-dimensional imaging features may provide complementary information related to tumor behavior and potential treatment response. Together, the expert network-specific pCR probabilities and the corresponding heatmap signatures provide complementary information that may assist clinicians in making individualized neoadjuvant therapy decisions. In addition, although these activation patterns should not be directly interpreted as radiotherapy target volumes, future studies integrating model-derived response-associated regions with spatial radiation dose distribution may help explore whether such imaging signatures could inform radiotherapy planning strategies.

There were some limitations to this study. First, treatment allocation was not randomized, and all regimens were determined through real-world multidisciplinary decision-making. Therefore, the therapy-specific pCR probabilities generated by the model were derived from observational data rather than randomized comparisons. The counterfactual analyses comparing actual and model-identified regimens are hypothesis-generating and do not establish causal treatment effects. Moreover, interpretation of the counterfactual reassignment framework relies on the assumption that the model-derived regimen-specific probabilities reasonably approximate response likelihood under alternative treatment strategies. However, the predicted responses under alternative regimens were not directly observed, and therefore these estimates should not be interpreted as ground truth. In real-world practice, additional factors that were not explicitly modeled—such as treatment tolerability, completion rates, dose modifications, and other unmeasured clinical confounders—may influence therapeutic outcomes and could alter the realized response probabilities.

Although we implemented a therapy-specific MoE framework to mitigate bias arising from non-randomized therapy assignment, residual confounding may persist. In addition, IPTW-based sensitivity analyses demonstrated stable predictive performance and preserved recommendation patterns after adjustment for baseline covariates and center variation, further supporting the robustness of the framework under observational data. Prospective randomized controlled trials will be required to determine whether model-supported treatment selection can translate into improved patient outcomes. Such trials should incorporate time-to-event endpoints, including DFS, time to relapse, and progression-free survival, in order to rigorously evaluate whether model-guided regimen allocation confers durable clinical benefit beyond improvements in pCR rates alone.

Second, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown striking efficacy in molecularly selected LARC patients (e.g., dMMR/MSI-H), and this evidence is supported by prospective data and guidelines. However, MSI-H tumors account for only a small fraction of rectal cancers (approximately 2–5%)30, and immunotherapy-based strategies were therefore not included in the modeling framework. Accordingly, the present study focused on the three neoadjuvant regimens most commonly used in routine practice. Future work should incorporate molecular stratification and immunotherapy-based strategies. Third, despite utilizing prospective multicenter data, the inclusion of patients receiving nCT was limited to a single center, due to the more recent and selective implementation of this therapy approach in clinical practice. Fourth, the framework did not incorporate dynamic treatment response information from the early stages of therapy, which may enable more adaptive and granular treatment optimization. Finally, our study did not incorporate multimodal data sources such as biopsy whole slide images, genomics, or structured clinical text. Previous studies have demonstrated that multimodal fusion may further enhance predictive performance18,19,24. Future work should expand the sample size, incorporate multimodal inputs, and evaluate the framework in prospective interventional settings.

In conclusion, we developed a deep learning framework that integrates pretreatment MRI with clinical characteristics to assist in LARC regimen selection by estimating the responses of various neoadjuvant therapies, validated using multicenter prospective data. The model effectively captured differential pretreatment sensitivities to TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT, supporting clinician-guided therapy de-intensification or escalation strategies based on estimated response probabilities, pending prospective interventional or randomized validation. This approach underscores the potential of AI-driven imaging biomarkers to enhance precision management in rectal cancer.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (No. 2024-766), Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (No. SL-B2023-029-02), The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No. 2019ZSLYEC-169), and Yunnan Cancer Hospital (No. KYLX2025-095). We retrospectively collected baseline pretreatment multiparametric MRI acquired prior to initiation of any neoadjuvant therapy, along with corresponding clinical data, from patients with LARC treated between June 2015 and May 2022 at three centers: West China Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, and The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. An independent prospective test cohort was subsequently enrolled between July 2024 and January 2025 from three centers, including West China Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, and Yunnan Cancer Hospital. The prospective, non-interventional cohort was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400085797, registration date: June 18, 2024, URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/hvshowprojectEN.html?id=254550&v=1.0). The requirement for individual patient consent in the retrospective cohort was waived, and all participants in the prospective cohort signed informed consent forms before enrollment. Eligible patients had pathologically confirmed adenocarcinoma, MRI-confirmed T3-4 or lymph node-positive disease, received TNT, standard nCRT, or nCT, and underwent TME with complete pathological assessment. In addition, all patients had available pretreatment multiparametric MRI, including T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps. Patients with concurrent malignancy, poor-quality MRI, or prior pelvic radiotherapy were excluded. The MRI acquisition parameters are shown in Supplementary Table S8.

In both the retrospective and prospective cohorts, treatment strategies (TNT, standard nCRT, or nCT) were determined according to contemporary clinical guidelines and patient-specific clinical factors, including baseline clinical staging, MRI-defined risk features, patient performance status, and physician clinical judgment, with multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions conducted when necessary at each participating center. For the retrospective cohort, all treatment decisions had been completed prior to model development, and the AI model was applied only for post hoc analysis. In the test cohort, patient data were collected prospectively, and the AI model did not influence real-world therapeutic decision-making. Treating physicians were blinded to the AI model outputs, and treatment selection remained entirely independent of model recommendations. Specifically, in the standard nCRT group, patients received long-course radiotherapy (50–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions) with concurrent capecitabine6. In the TNT group, long-course radiotherapy was combined with CAPOX-based chemotherapy administered during the neoadjuvant phase (typically 4–6 cycles)31, whereas patients in the nCT group received 4 cycles of CAPOX chemotherapy alone32.

Data preprocessing

To ensure robust and standardized inputs, tumor and mesorectum ROIs were first automatically segmented on T2WI using our previously validated model33 and subsequently refined by an experienced radiation oncologist. To evaluate intra-observer reproducibility, 50 randomly selected cases underwent a second round of manual refinement by the same experienced radiation oncologist after an interval. The second refinement was performed independently and blinded to the first annotation. Agreement between the two refinements was high (mean Dice = 0.980 ± 0.018; mean Jaccard = 0.961 ± 0.034), indicating that manual adjustments of the auto-segmented ROIs were consistent and reliable.

For each patient, 10 central slices with the largest tumor burden from T2WI and ADC maps were extracted, intensity-normalized, and stacked into a 128 × 128 × 20 multi-channel input. Clinical characteristics (cT, cN, EMVI, CRM, LLNM, tumor location) were encoded using one-hot encoding. Neoadjuvant regimens were encoded as TNT = [1,0,0], standard nCRT = [0,1,0], and nCT = [0,0,1].

Hard-gated MoE-based response model construction

We developed a regimen-specific hard-gated MoE model to predict pCR probabilities under TNT, standard nCRT, and nCT. A joint nnUNet34 backbone was employed for simultaneous ROI segmentation and multi-scale feature extraction, yielding a unified latent representation of MRI. Both image- and clinical-derived features were projected to 64-dimensional embeddings via modality-specific multi-layer perceptions (MLPs), concatenated, and fed into the MoE module. The total loss included cross-entropy for response prediction, Dice loss for segmentation, and a regularization term. During validation and testing, perturbation was disabled, and only the expert corresponding to the actual therapy was used.

Response model-based therapy decision support

To facilitate potential clinical application, we implemented a model-driven regimen recommendation strategy using the trained image-clinical encoder and the three regimen-specific expert subnetworks. For each patient in the training cohort, the encoder output was forwarded to the nCT, standard nCRT, and TNT experts to generate regimen-specific predicted probabilities of pCR. Optimal decision thresholds for each regimen were derived within their respective therapy subgroups using the Youden index. A hierarchical selection rule was then applied to balance preservation of predicted response during de-intensification and maximization of predicted response during escalation: nCT was selected when the predicted pCR probability exceeded the nCT-specific threshold; otherwise, standard nCRT was selected if its threshold was met. Patients not meeting either criterion were assigned to the TNT branch or managed according to clinician judgment (Fig. 1). This framework enabled flexible allocation across the full treatment spectrum, allowing direct reassignment between nCT, standard nCRT, and TNT branches, rather than restricting decisions to single-step escalation or de-intensification.

To quantify the potential decision-level utility of the framework, we generated patient-specific counterfactual response estimates by applying all three experts to each individual, independent of the regimen they originally received. For each patient, we obtained three quantities: (i) the observed pCR outcome under the originally administered clinical regimen, (ii) the predicted pCR probability generated by the expert corresponding to that original regimen (representing the model-estimated response probability under the received treatment and used to evaluate predictive performance), and (iii) the predicted pCR probability generated by the expert corresponding to the model-selected regimen derived from the hierarchical rule (binary pCR classifications under each branch were defined using the regimen-specific thresholds: ThresholdTNT, ThresholdnCRT, and ThresholdnCT). These paired estimates enabled calculation of the change in model-estimated response probability between originally assigned and model-selected regimens and facilitated statistical assessment of model-guided treatment stratification.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome measure was pCR, which was defined as ypT0N0 according to the guidelines of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)1. Two experienced pathologists evaluated the surgical resection specimen. If there was a disagreement, a third pathologist with over 10 years of experience was consulted for review. To minimize assessment bias, all pathologists were blinded to patients’ treatment allocation and all preoperative imaging findings.

The performance of the response prediction model was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis with the Log-rank test was performed to assess DFS, with patients stratified into low- and high-pCR probability groups according to the median model-predicted probability derived from the training cohort. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was further conducted to quantify the potential clinical benefit of the model in treatment decision-making. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test, McNemar test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python.
