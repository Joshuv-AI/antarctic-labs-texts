Frailty and outcomes after extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a binational registry-based cohort study
Authors: Ryan Ruiyang Ling, Ashwin Subramaniam, David Pilcher, Kollengode Ramanathan, Krishnaswamy Sundararajan, Kiran Shekar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty predicts adverse outcomes in critical illness, but its prognostic value in patients receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) remains unclear.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study across 116 intensive care units (ICUs), we analysed data from the Australia and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Adult Patient Database on adults (≥ 16 years) who received ECMO between 2018 and 2024. Frailty was assessed using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), and we defined patients as being frail if they had a CFS of ≥ 5. The primary outcome was one-year mortality, which we assessed using g-computation. We further analysed the association between frailty and death among several subgroups, including age, sex, and primary ICU admission diagnostic group (cardiac medical, cardiac surgical, cardiac arrest, respiratory, transplant, and others). Secondary outcomes included in-hospital and ICU mortality, lengths of hospital and ICU stay, delirium, and pressure injuries.ResultsWe included 2,084 patients (51.3 years [IQR: 38.9–61.3], 64.2% male). Frailty was present in 254 (12.2%) patients. The adjusted one-year mortality was 41.9% (760/1830) in patients without frailty and 42.1% (113/254) in patients with frailty (adjusted risk difference: +0.27%, 95%-CI: -6.65% to +7.19%). These results were consistent across sensitivity and subgroup analyses, particularly amongst principal diagnostic groups (cardiac medical: -7.87%, cardiac surgical: + 3.70%, cardiac arrest: -1.66%, respiratory: + 10.02%, transplant: + 2.19%, others: -0.58%).ConclusionsFrailty was not associated with short or long-term mortality among patients who received ECMO. This may reflect patterns in patient eligibility for ECMO. More studies are required to investigate this relationship and validate these findings.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05801-0.Keywords: Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, Frailty, Survival

Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has been increasingly used as a rescue therapy in patients with refractory cardiorespiratory failure, particularly in the last few years following the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, [1–6] and with more recent studies in patients with cardiogenic shock and cardiac arrest [7, 8]. Yet, patient selection remains controversial.

A key consideration in selecting patients to receive ECMO is that they should have the potential to achieve a functional recovery following their acute illness. Some institutions use age as a surrogate marker of functional reserve, and as a component of eligibility (ranging between 60–70 years in most randomised clinical trials) for ECMO [5, 6, 9–12]. More recent studies of the Extracorporeal Life Support Organisation (ELSO) registry have shown that increasing chronological age is associated with poorer outcomes in patients receiving ECMO [13–15]. Nonetheless, there is emerging evidence that biological age, as opposed to chronological age, is more predictive of outcomes in patients with and without critical illness [16, 17]. One way to estimate biological age is the presence and extent of frailty, a multidimensional syndrome characterised by reduced physical, physiological and functional reserves [18]. Outside of ECMO, frailty has been shown to be associated with mortality, longer lengths of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU), and poorer functional outcomes in the longer term [19–24]. However, such data on patients receiving ECMO remain unknown. Thus far, only two single-centre studies suggest conflicting data on premorbid functional status and outcomes [25, 26]. Nonetheless, certain regional and national guidelines, and RCTs have listed frailty as a clinical modifier, relative contraindication, or exclusion criteria from ECMO [27–29], while others loosely reference functional status as a consideration [5, 9–11]. Given this, we aimed to examine whether frailty (defined as a Clinical Frailty Scale ≥ 5) is associated with mortality and poorer recovery after ECMO in a large binational cohort, based on the Australia and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database (APD).

Methods

Study design and participants

In this retrospective, binational, registry-based cohort study, we pooled data from 116 ICUs across Australia and New Zealand who recorded the presence of one or more patient who received ECMO. We included all unique encounters of patients aged ≥ 16 years with a documented frailty status, admitted to an ICU receiving their first run of ECMO. The study inclusion period is as follows: 1 st January 2018 through 30th June 2024 for patients admitted to an ICU in Australia, and 1 st Jan 2018 through 30th September 2024 for patients admitted to an ICU in New Zealand. We excluded patients who did not receive ECMO, patients who received ECMO but were not admitted to ICU, admissions for palliative care or organ donation purposes, and patients who did not have their frailty status or ECMO indicator documented in the registry. For patients who were readmitted to ICU within the same hospital stay after their first run of ECMO, and patients who were readmitted to ICU during a future hospitalisation, we only included data related to their first ICU admission for ECMO. This study was approved by the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (Reference 549/25) and the ANZICS Centre for Outcome and Resource Evaluation Management Committee, and was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [30].

Data sources, definition, and collection

Data were extracted from the ANZICS-APD, a binational clinical quality registry dataset that collects deidentified information on all admissions to 98% of adult ICUs in Australia, and 67% of ICUs in New Zealand [31]. Data collectors receive regular training and quality assurance reviews and data are collected using a standardised data dictionary [31]. In addition, regular automated data checks further ensure the validity of recorded data. We extracted data on age, sex, comorbidities (as defined by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation [APACHE] II and III/IV scoring systems), ICU admission source, admission diagnosis, illness severity scores (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA], Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation [APACHE] II and III/IV), predicted risk of death estimated using Australian and New Zealand Risk of Death [ANZROD], treatment limitations at admission to ICU, frailty status (see below), ICU organ supports provided at any time during ICU (mechanical ventilation, non-invasive ventilation, vasopressors, ECMO, renal replacement therapy), ICU and hospital mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay, and discharge destinations (home, another acute care hospital, rehabilitation facility, chronic care facility/nursing home, or mental health facility).

Frailty

In the ANZICS-APD, frailty is measured using a modified version of the Canadian Study of Health and Aging Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), which categorises patients as not frail (1 = very fit; 2 = well; 3 = managing well; 4 = vulnerable) or frail (5 = mild; 6 = moderate; 7 = severe; 8 = very severe; 9 = terminally ill) [32]. The CFS has been shown to predict functional status within and outside ICU settings, correlates well with other frailty scales, and has relatively low inter-rater variability [33–35]. Based on corroborative history and a retrospective chart review, local clinicians in each ICU assessed the patient’s level of function in the two months preceding ICU admission, and assigned a corresponding CFS. These data were collected in a standardised manner using patient progress notes and medical history. In the ANZICS-APD, the CFS is modified to eight categories without a CFS of 9 (terminally ill) [33].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was one-year mortality. Secondary outcomes were analysed descriptively and included discharge destination at hospital discharge, and hospital-level outcomes (ICU and hospital mortality, length of ICU and hospital stays, and ICU complications [delirium and pressure injury]). The ANZICS-APD collects data on outcomes surrounding the patient’s admission (mortality, length of stay, complications, and discharge destination). We linked the ANZICS-APD to the National Death Indexes of each country and derived the death date of each patient, if present. We then estimated their overall survival time, and censored time-to-event data on 1 st July 2024 for Australia and 1 st October 2024 for New Zealand, or at their death date, whichever was earlier. Mortality at each follow-up timepoint was then derived based on whether each patient was alive or not at the point of follow-up in our analysis.

Statistical analysis

We summarised baseline characteristics and demographic data using appropriate descriptive statistics and compared between groups using absolute standardised differences (ASD), taking an ASD of > 0.1 as an indicator of imbalance between groups. As in previous studies of the ANZICS-APD [22], we created a separate category “unknown” for patients in which the data field was not recorded or not known (see Table 1 for missingness of each demographic variable).

The index time for all analyses was ICU admission. The exact timing of ECMO initiation was not available in the registry and hence, we treated ECMO as a binary exposure within each admission. We used mixed-effects logistic regression, guided by a directed acyclic graph (Supplementary methods) to estimate the association between frailty (defined as a CFS of ≥ 5) and one-year mortality using a logistic regression. Based on the directed acyclic graph (Supplementary methods), we adjusted for the following covariates: sex, age, and chronic comorbidities (respiratory, cardiovascular, liver, and renal disease, metastatic cancer, immunosuppression, and diabetes mellitus), and acute illness severity using the SOFA score. Thereafter, we used g-computation to estimate the marginal effect of frailty on one-year mortality and reported the results of our analyses using adjusted risk differences (RDs) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (95%-CIs). Briefly, g-computation estimates causal effects by predicting the average population outcome under different theoretical scenarios (where everyone has frailty, and no one has frailty) and taking the difference in the outcomes. We then compared survival trajectories between patients with and without frailty by generating adjusted Kaplan–Meier curves (see Supplementary Methods).

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings. These included alternative definitions of frailty (different CFS thresholds for grouping, and categorical vs. continuous coding), a different basket of confounders (including acute illness severity and biochemical results in the first 24 h of ICU admission), varying outcomes (replacing one-year mortality with hospital mortality, and landmark analyses in patients who survived beyond 48 h of ICU admission, and to ICU discharge to account for potential immortal time bias), multiple imputation for missing data regarding frailty (in approximately 23% of the population who were excluded from the primary analysis). In addition to this, we noted plateaus in the survival curves after three months of follow up, which may have suggested that the proportional hazards assumption may have been violated. As such, we conducted two additional post-hoc sensitivity analyses to investigate and explain this phenomenon: we estimated the time-varying hazards ratios for frailty to relax the proportional hazards assumption, and used Weibull-mixture cure models to allow for long-term survivors who do not experience the outcome of interest (death). Details of these analyses are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

We then conducted several pre-defined subgroup analyses by introducing a multiplicative interaction term between the variable of interest and frailty, including age (< 65 and ≥ 65 years), and principal diagnostic group (cardiac arrest, cardiac other, respiratory, solid organ transplant, or others; more details in Supplementary Methods). We conducted the data analysis using R 4.4.1 (further details in Supplementary Methods) and IBM SPSS Version 30 (Armonk, NY, USA) and used a p-value of < 0.05 to indicate statistical significance.

Results

During the study period, there were 3,860 patients receiving ECMO in 91 ICUs within the ANZICS-APD. After applying our inclusion and exclusion criteria, we included 2,084 patients in our study (Fig. 1). Table 1 details the baseline demographics and characteristics of patients with and without frailty, and Table S1 compares the characteristics of patients with a missing frailty status with our overall study population. In general, patients with frailty were older, more frequently female, and admitted from home. Those with frailty were also more likely to be admitted from the operating theatre than patients without frailty. Patients with frailty also more commonly had chronic comorbidities, but had comparable illness severity scores (SOFA, APACHE II and APACHE III/IV scores) or lower predicted risk of death (ANZROD) on admission to ICU, compared to patients without frailty. There were no substantial differences in treatment limitations and initiation of life organ support therapies as well.

At one year follow up, the adjusted mortality for patients without frailty was 41.9% (95%-CI: 38.9% to 44.8%) and 42.1% (95%-CI: 35.4% to 48.9%) in patients with frailty (adjusted RD: 0.27%, 95%-CI: −6.65% to 7.19%, Figs. 2 and 3, see Table S2 for full multivariable logistic regression model). This was consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses modelling frailty in various ways (Figs. S1, S2), replacing one-year mortality with hospital mortality (RD: −0.06%, 95%-CI: −6.62% to + 6.50%; Fig. S3), or when using multiple imputation to account for missing frailty status (RD: −0.02%, 95%-CI: −9.49% to + 9.45%), or relaxing the proportional hazards assumption (Fig. S4). Modelling survival using a parametric cure model revealed similar results: frailty was not associated with a lower odds of survival (adjusted odds ratio: 0.89, 95%-CI: 0.68 to 1.16). Discussion

In this multinational registry-based cohort study of 2,084 patients admitted to the ICU receiving ECMO, frailty was not associated with mortality in the short- or long-term. These results were consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses assessing the exposure (frailty), the outcome (mortality), and potential confounders (missingness, new basket of covariates, subgroup analyses).

The findings from this study are important for several reasons. They contrast previous analyses in patients with critical illness [19–22, 24], and prior smaller cohorts in patients receiving ECMO [25]. Outside of patients receiving ECMO, frailty has generally been shown to be associated with mortality and poorer outcomes in patients in the ICU. These findings challenge the assumption that premorbid frailty necessarily predicts worse outcomes in ECMO. In addition, the results are based on a comprehensive result with consistent results in multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses. They are also based on a large multicentre database of more than 2,000 patients receiving ECMO, spanning all hospital types and regions in Australia and New Zealand. In addition, the coverage of the ANZICS-APD is much wider than ECMO-specific registries such as the Extracorporeal Life Support Organisation (ELSO) or the EXCEL registry. Furthermore, the primary outcome of longer-term mortality at up to one year is an important outcome beyond hospital discharge to relevant stakeholders [36], and is often under-reported in studies investigating patients receiving ECMO. We also used a validated, clinically relevant tool to screen for frailty, which allowed for comprehensive overall assessment and robust analyses. Given the novelty of these findings, they require further examination.

We hypothesise several potential reasons for our findings. First, that frailty being not associated with death may imply that frailty acts upstream in the decision to initiate ECMO, rather than downstream in determining prognosis after ECMO is initiated. Of note, only 12.2% of patients had frailty, and 1.6% of patients with frailty had treatment limitations; these are lower than in other reports of the ANZICS-APD [19, 20, 22]. This may be relevant in clinical practice and future clinical trial design. The EURO-SHOCK study excluded patients with a CFS of ≥ 5 from receiving ECMO [28], and other trials loosely reference conditions that suggest frailty as a potential exclusion criteria [5, 9–11]. In addition, some regional and national guidelines list frailty as a contraindication or clinical modifier for ECMO candidacy [27, 29]. Our study may provide a basis for further studies to examine how frailty impacts decisions about access to ECMO, and how ECMO candidacy is standardised to maximise equity and parity in administration. Equally possible is the presence of robust selection criteria: some patients with frailty still receive ECMO, but their outcomes are comparable to patients without frailty; this may also suggest very appropriate use of ECMO in the context of Australia and New Zealand. In our study, we found that the predicted mortality rate (based on ANZROD) was in fact lower in patients with frailty, and those with frailty had similar disease severity scores compared to those without frailty. This might suggest that patients with frailty for whom ECMO is provided may have milder forms of frailty than all-comer ICU patients, hence the contrast with other studies of frailty in critical care. A final, but unlikely theoretical possibility that we cannot exclude is that there are fewer patients with frailty who ever reach a physiological derangement at which ECMO might otherwise be considered for an equivalent patient without frailty. Of note, more than 60% of patients with frailty had mild frailty, and given this, there is poorer precision of the estimates at higher levels of frailty. Yet, we lack a counterfactual population, who were eligible for or referred for consideration of ECMO but did not receive it. We also do not have detailed data regarding acute illness severity prior to ECMO initiation. Though our study did not reflect this, it is nonetheless possible that patients with severe frailty do not share a similar association with mortality compared to patients with milder or no frailty. More importantly, frailty only reflects one aspect of ECMO candidacy. Being a high-resource and high-risk intervention, ECMO candidacy should be considered holistically, incorporating patient, disease, and institutional factors [37]. Given this, these hypotheses remain speculative, and should be interpreted cautiously. Further studies focussing on eligibility criteria for ECMO selection and the counterfactual of patients who are frail but do not receive ECMO are required to better unpack the findings of this study.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, although certain important data regarding ECMO are reported to the ANZICS-APD, these are only reported by several ICUs (and hence not available for all patients), and poorly recorded. These include the specific indication for ECMO, mode of ECMO, time of ECMO initiation (prior to or after ICU admission, in-hours vs. after hours, and weekdays vs. weekends), ECMO settings, duration of ECMO, and baseline characteristics and acute illness severity prior to and during ECMO. Given that the timing of ECMO was unclear, there was a risk of immortal time bias, which we attempted to mitigate using landmark analyses. In addition, other potentially important covariates such as functional autonomy and nutritional status were not well captured in the ANZICS-APD. Separately, despite consistent results with multiple imputation, it is unclear what the impact of missingness was on our results. Second, despite adjustment of potential confounders, there may still be a risk of unmeasured residual confounding in the observational design of this study. Third, despite multiple sensitivity analyses which confirmed similar findings, it is possible missing or inaccurate data may have affected our results. Fourth, the ANZICS-APD does not presently record patient-reported outcome measures and functional outcomes at present, although these data are recorded in ECMO-specific registries in Australia and New Zealand [36]. Fifth, we only included patients who received ECMO i.e. they were deemed to be “fit” enough for ECMO despite their frailty. Without a counterfactual population, we cannot exclude the possibility that ECMO may mediate the association between frailty and mortality. More importantly, we cannot exclude the possibility that frailty may have an impact on outcomes, for example in a scenario where all patients (regardless of frailty and functional status) were candidates for ECMO. Sixth, although the CFS has not been formally validated in patients ≤ 65 years, it has demonstrated to be associated with outcomes in patients of younger age groups in ICU cohorts. Finally, these data are limited to ICUs in Australia and New Zealand, where workflows, patient candidacy, and management may differ from other parts of the world. Thus, the findings may not be generalisable more broadly.

In conclusion, this study found that frailty was not associated with mortality in patients receiving ECMO. Nonetheless, this may potentially reflect selection practices and patient candidacy and is also limited by its observational nature and missing data with regards to ECMO. Given that frailty has been incorporated into ECMO trial exclusion criteria and guideline recommendations, our results may form the basis for further studies to examine how frailty impacts decisions about access to ECMO, and how ECMO candidacy is standardised to maximise equity and parity in administration. Further work, including prospective international studies, is needed to validate these findings and clarify whether frailty should influence ECMO candidacy.
