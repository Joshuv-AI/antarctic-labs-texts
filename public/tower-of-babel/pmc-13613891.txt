MXenes at the Immune Interface: A Perspective on Influence of Physicochemical Design in Immunomodulation
Authors: Alireza Rafieerad, Leena Regi Saleth, Sanjiv Dhingra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MXenes are a recently discovered family of two-dimensional (2D) transition metal-based nanomaterials, known for their compositional diversity, exceptional tunability, structural versatility, and surface functionality. Beyond their well-established application in energy storage, MXenes have attracted growing interest for their promising role in regenerative biomedicine, particularly in antimicrobial and immune engineering applications. Recent studies, including contributions from our group, demonstrate that MXenes exhibit unique interactions with immune cells and associated immunosuppressive functions. Notably, MXene nanosheets and derived quantum dots can be tailored to modulate key immunosuppressive mechanisms, including the enhancement of regulatory T-cells (Tregs), reduction of proinflammatory cytokines, macrophages modulation and autophagy induction. These findings position MXenes as potential next-generation platform for immunomodulatory therapy, either as standalone agents or in combination with existing immunosuppressants. In this perspective, we systematically examine how physiochemical parameters including size, dimension, morphology, surface chemistry, and functionalization govern their interactions with innate and adaptive system. We highlight recent advances in MXene-mediated immunomodulation, paving a roadmap for further investigations, by identifying key knowledge gaps and translational challenges. We further outline future research directions necessary to bridge the divide between experimental studies, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven optimization/predictive approaches, and clinical applications of MXene-based immunotherapies.

Introduction and Background of the Field

Since the discovery of the first 2D composition of transition-metal carbides and/or nitride nanosheets, known as ‘MXenes’, they have emerged as a promising functional-group-terminated family of carbon-based nanomaterials with broad applications ranging from energy storage/transfer, electronics, optoelectronics, catalysis, sensors, filtration, wearable devices, and, more recently, in regenerative nanomedicine. − Over time, MXenes have expanded substantially through the synthesis of new nanosheets and quantum dots compositions, as well as their derived heterostructures. Parallel theoretical and experimental developments of novel MXene-based designs have taken place by improving their synthesis and post-processing methods, alongside precise tailoring of surface terminations and functional properties for targeted applications. In this context, the multidisciplinary field of MXenes has witnessed the emergence of several review articles that comprehensively summarize their biomedical applications, including antimicrobial and cancer therapy, biosensing, tissue engineering, regenerative medicine, and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted development. Despite this remarkable progress in the aforementioned applications, the understanding of the precise bioactivity mechanisms and immunological properties of MXenes are still in their infancy. This is partly because existing publications present application-focused overviews on MXene bioactivity, integrating relevant biological principles, that collectively determine therapeutic performance within specific biomedical applications. Consequently, immune-regulation, reprogramming, and suppression are typically presented as one among the many biomedical applications rather than as the central systems-level mechanism, through which MXene physicochemical properties drive distinct biological outcomes across diverse bio-environments and pathological conditions. Continued universal research is therefore essential to advance MXenes towards real-world applications by optimizing surface stability, enhancing biocompatibility, and preserving their physiochemical characteristics and bioactivity, while enabling application-specific customization beyond lab-scale demonstrations. Ultimately, the goal is to achieve robust consistency, reproducibility, and cost-effective MXene-based products as building blocks for future nano-biotechnologies.

In this context, one of the important aspects for designing and modifying immunosuppressive MXenes is the necessity of remaining functionally inert toward non-activated immune cells, yet regulating activated immune cells during inflammatory conditions, such as allograft vasculopathy and autoimmune diseases. This dual interaction is crucial for suppressing local and systemic inflammation and preventing post-transplantation organ rejection. MXenes with distinct compositions and forms exhibit differing capacities to control inflammation, including inducing the production of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and shifting macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, while regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis. The interaction of MXenes with immune system has been reported for different forms including titanium-, tantalum-, niobium, molybdenum, and vanadium-carbide in the form of nanosheets, quantum dots, flakes, and mixed-dimensional composites. While these studies differ in dosage, exposure time, and biological model, all these studies collectively conclude that physicochemical properties of MXenes critically determine the mechanisms of immunosuppression and efficacy. For instance, titanium carbide MXene (Ti3C2), with its negatively charged, hydrophilic surface and bioactive terminations, can trigger multiple pathways in immune cells. In anticancer applications, Ti3C2-based MXene systems can generate ROS under photothermal activation, enhancing oxidative stress and promoting tumor ablation. , In contrast, He et al. demonstrated that surface-engineered Ti3C2T x -based MXenzyme could effectively treat inflammatory bowel disease by promoting the repolarization of M2 macrophages and boosting ROS-scavenging mechanisms. Similarly, other transition metal-based MXenes, such as molybdenum carbide MXene (Mo2C), can reduce oxidative stress through ROS-scavenging activity, highlighting the context-dependent dual role of MXenes in regulating ROS homeostasis. Consequently, current efforts are expanding from established titanium carbide-based immunomodulatory MXenes toward emerging transition metal-based MXenes, using rational design strategies that improve biocompatibility and immune performance while preserving their core physiochemical properties.

Building on these considerations, this perspective advances the field by proposing a MXene-centred immunosuppression paradigm that unifies the previously fragmented mechanisms into a coherent, system-level roadmap for future discovery and development. In contrast to prior reviews that predominantly catalogue MXene-based biomedical applications, our perspective defines immune-regulation as a central mechanism through which the physicochemical properties of MXenes are translated into context-dependent biological outcomes. Within this framework, immunomodulation serves as the systems-level interface that defines biological and therapeutic identity of MXenes across diverse pathological settings. Specifically, we integrate five interdependent levels such as physiochemical design, immunometabolic regulation, dynamic biomolecular corona formation, spatiotemporal microenvironmental interaction, and data-driven optimization, into a unified framework that governs MXene-mediated immunological responses. We underscore the latest advancement in bioactive MXenes focussing on their intrinsic or modified properties for anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive applications. We also highlight how these bioactive MXenes are developed to influence innate and adaptive immune responses by modulating cell signaling pathways for inflammation resolution, treatment of immune system related disorders, and preventing the allograft rejection.

We further outline how to select appropriate size, architecture, porosity, and other influential immunoregulatory design parameters for MXene-based biomaterials and provide strategies for rational directions for future studies to advance this technology. Given the pivotal role of MXenes’ physicochemical/bioactive characteristics in controlling inflammation, we also discuss surface-modification and termination strategies. Lastly, we highlight their biocompatibility with immune cells and discuss the potential cytotoxicity and adverse effects on biosystems, especially at higher doses, integrate the reported MXene-immune interactions, to provide a clearer framework for developing robust and efficient immunomodulatory strategies, and precise nano-immunotherapies.

Preparation, Properties, and Potential of MXenes for Immunosuppression Applications

MXene nanosheets are synthesized by converting their 3D MAX phase (M n+1AX n , “A” refers to an element from group 13 or 14 of the periodic table) to 2D flakes with a general formula of M n+1X n T x (n = 1-4) through top-down approaches or bottom-up methods. ,,, The common top-down methods include acid etching with/without fluorine-containing solutions, milder etchants such as hydrochloric acid with lithium, sodium, or potassium fluorides, bifluoride salts (e.g., ammonium bifluoride), fluorine-containing molten salts, and fluorine-free methods including alkaline hydrothermal, electrochemical, halogen, and Lewis salt etching. From the materials chemistry perspective, top-down method derived MXenes, obtained as accordion-like multilayers or delaminated flakes (ranging from few 100 nm to a few μm), offer a large surface area of transition-metal carbide/nitride and oxides, enriched with multiple bioactive surface terminations (T x ). MXene nanosheets and derived quantum dots possess intrinsic hydrophilic surfaces, negatively charged over a broad range of pH (approximately 7–11), optically transparent, and can be further surface modified to offer fluorescent properties at specific excitation/emission wavelengths or tailored to adjust their surface charge toward less negative or even positive values. Their rich terminal functional groups and diverse chemical bonds, enable dispersion in aqueous media without additional surfactants, advantageous for in vivo immuno-engineering applications.

As stated earlier, MXene biomaterials have been reported to possess high compatibility with various human and mammal cells/tissues at controlled doses (usually ≤50–100 μg mL–1). Their immunological behaviour and biocompatibility largely depend on their chemical composition, surface properties, fabrication method, flake size, lateral thickness, number of layers, surface defects and particle topography. In particular, surface sharpness, interlayer spacing, and intensity/size of the formed metal-oxide particles are the key parameters of MXenes’ biological responses. Reasonably, MXene nanosheets prepared with fewer surface defects likely possess higher resistance to decomposition/degradation for long-term biocompatibility, while higher surface enrichments with bioactive terminations can reduce toxicity risks at higher doses and modulate immune cell interaction, cellular uptake, cell localization, biodistribution and clearance. Additionally, rational surface modification or post-functionalization strategies should minimize added chemical complexities and preserve the core MXene structure and properties while tuning their biological performance.

In our studies, we observed comparable immunocompatibility and cytocompatibility of titanium and tantalum carbide MXene nanosheets and quantum dots. , Multilayered accordion-like titanium MXene flakes showed safe working doses ≤50 μg mL–1, although sharp edges and agglomeration at higher doses may cause minor cellular damage via uptake or physical contact. The multilayer MXenes generally exhibit higher electrical conductivity while delaminated, single-layer, or few-layer sheets possess a higher negative surface charge due to their higher surface exposure of functional groups. However, the quantum dots further differ due to their higher active surface area and colloidal dispersibility. The quantum dots offer cellular uptake, and resistance to oxidative degradation, enabling higher biocompatibility (≤100 μg mL–1) and lower effective doses (≤20 μg mL–1). Notably, the crystalline phase and morphology of metal oxides, formed by hydrolysis during hydrothermal process to obtain quantum dots markedly influence biocompatibility and immunosuppressive properties.

In addition to the intrinsic physiochemical properties of MXenes, the formation of a protein corona upon exposure to biological fluids is another key factor that determines their immunomodulatory properties. Nanomaterial protein corona serves as a critical determinant of immunomodulatory properties, because the layer of adsorbed biomolecules largely controls how the immune system recognizes and responds to the nanomaterial. The composition of this corona promotes uptake by immune cells, or establishes a more “stealth” interface that dampens immune engagement, thereby altering the downstream signaling pathways and clearance routes. , The organization of this corona is strongly influenced by the physiochemical characteristics of MXenes, including surface chemistry, charge, morphology and terminal functional properties, which collectively controls the extent and specificity of protein adsorption. Protein coronas formation and the associated conformational changes critically affects the colloidal stability, cytotoxicity and bioactivity of MXenes in biological environments. Thus, the interplay between the surface properties of MXenes and protein corona formation jointly defines their immunomodulatory functions. Studies examining Ti3C2T x MXenes synthesised via two commonly adapted etching methods and incubated in human plasma, have reported distinct coronas, enriched to varying extents with immunoglobulins, complement components and coagulation factors, as revealed by proteomic analysis. Surface modifications, such as PEGylation, further reshape the corona, thereby influencing colloidal stability, cellular uptake routes, and the balance between ROS generation and scavenging. In addition, comparative studies of different transition metal-based MXenes including single- and multi-layered titanium carbide, single-layered molybdenum titanium carbide and multi-layered vanadium carbide MXenes, demonstrated that the configuration and concentration of MXenes affects protein corona formation. These variations subsequently impacted the conformational, thermal and colloidal stability of the MXenes under study. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that protein corona formation is a critical interfacial phenomenon that governs protein adsorption, immune recognition, cellular internalization, colloidal stability, biodistribution, and clearance, ultimately shaping the immunomodulatory behavior and biocompatibility of MXenes.

The experimental variables discussed above are highly sensitive and even small differences in the synthesis, surface modification, post-functionalization, purification, handling, and storage conditions can significantly affect the biological/immunological properties, cellular uptake, and oxidation state of the end-products. Beyond synthesis and characterization variables, sterilization methods, dispersion protocols, and environmental conditions of the experiments may also influence the immunomodulatory performance of MXenes and contribute to inconsistencies across different studies. In addition, sterilization approaches can alter surface chemistry, composition, and oxidation state, and therefore should be carefully considered for material standardization and successful translation into real-world therapeutic applications. Immunosuppressive Role of MXene Nanosheets and Quantum Dots

The first report on the direct immunosuppressive properties of titanium carbide MXene (Ti3C2T x ) quantum dots came from our group. In this study we synthesized Ti3C2T x quantum dots with an average particle size of around 3–5 nm with crystal lattice d-spacing of ∼0.22 nm corresponding to the crystalline facet (0110). The rationale for selecting 0D MXene quantum dots of these dimensions over 2D MXene nanosheets or conventional low-dimensional biomaterials lies in their enhanced physicochemical properties, higher active surface area, compatibility with biological systems and improved water dispersibility. The immunomodulatory properties of these quantum dots were tested in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. It was reported that these nanomaterials can suppress the immune cells by reducing the activation of CD4+IFN-γ+ T-lymphocytes, and by promoting the expansion of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ regulatory T-cells. The quantum dots-treated immune cells showed a significantly lower production of proinflammatory TNF-α and IFN-γ cytokines alongside an increasing trend of IL-10 production compared to the control cells. Our data further showed that these quantum dots can spontaneously enter the antigen-presenting cells and suppress allo-immune responses. Another important property of MXene nanomaterials that can be beneficial for bio-medical applications is the autofluorescence nature. − In a follow proof-of-concept study, we reported the autofluorescence of MXene quantum dots at different wavelengths (detectable in GFP green and Cy5 red fluorescent filters). Together, these immunomodulatory properties and autofluorescence nature of MXenes are largely dependent on their final composition (Ti3C2T x /surface Ti-oxides), therefore synthesis processes and surface modifications that can define compositions of end products play important role in defining the biomedical properties of MXenes.

Following our initial MXene-based immunosuppression studies with quantum dots, next we investigated whether the immunomodulatory effects of Ti3C2T x arise from its physical structure or surface chemistry. The Ti3C2T x nanosheets and quantum dots differ significantly in their composition, where nanosheets display mixed TiO2 phases, while quantum dots are rich in stable TiO2 anatase (in certain cases also TiO2 rutile), which may affect their immunomodulatory behaviour. Therefore, in a follow-up study we examined the immunomodulatory role of Ti3C2T x nanosheets in an in vivo rodent model of allograft vasculopathy and using in vitro co-culture systems. We observed downregulation in the expression of genes associated with alloantigen presentation, as a result there was reduced activation of allogeneic T-cells. Our bulk RNA-sequencing data further confirmed suppression of pathways related to immune response, allograft rejection, that particularly involved interferon signaling and cell-cycle regulation. These findings confirm that both titanium carbide quantum dots and nanosheets have the ability to suppress inflammatory and allo immune responses, but through different mechanisms. Therefore, size and shape of the nanomaterial play an important role in defining its interaction with immune cells.

Beyond transplant allograft vasculopathy, MXene-mediated immunomodulation has also been reported in other cardiovascular applications. Preclinical studies demonstrate that MXene-based conductive hydrogels and nanozymes can mitigate post-infarct oxidative stress and inflammation, enhance electrical coupling across fibrotic scars, and promote cardio-myogenesis and neovascularization, thereby improving myocardial repair and ventricular function after myocardial infarction. Lee at al. developed an adhesive, electroconductive MXene-incorporated gelatin hydrogel as a new paintable cardiac patch for repairing infarcted heart through tissue regeneration. The designed composite hydrogel stably adhered to beating epicardium sites, induced M2 macrophage polarization, attenuated fibrosis by enhanced anti-inflammatory tissue response, improved the cardiac functions and alleviated pathological remodeling of the infarcted area in vivo. Similarly, Ren et al. developed an injectable Ti3C2T x MXene-incorporated conductive hydrogel with potent antioxidant activity that scavenged ROS and restored cardiac function. A decellularized ECM- based cardiac patch with Ti3C2T x MXenes further extended antibacterial activity and cardiomyocyte biocompatibility while promoting neovascularization in the infracted myocardium. Beyond myocardial repair, MXene-based nanozymes targeted to atherosclerotic plaques and inflamed endothelium were found to downregulate NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling pathway, reduce oxidative stress and promote M2 macrophage polarization, thereby improving the immune-endothelial crosstalk and vascular oxidative microenvironment. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MXene-mediated immunomodulation has translational promise across a spectrum of cardiovascular conditions, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, cardiac fibrosis, vascular inflammation, and engineered cardiac repair. By integrating immunomodulation with electrical conductivity, structural support, and targeted antioxidant therapy, MXene-based biomaterials represent a promising platform for next-generation cardiovascular immunotherapy.

Moving beyond titanium-based MXenes, recent investigations into other transition metal carbides have revealed size- and composition-dependent immunomodulatory behaviors that are reshaping our understanding of nanoscale immune interactions. Comparative studies among MXenes with varying elemental compositions, lateral dimensions, and morphologies ranging from few-layer nanosheets to quantum dots now provide a framework for correlating physicochemical traits with immune response profiles. For example, a pilot study by a different group examined the immunosuppressive and antiviral roles of mono-/few-layer MXene nanosheets, including titanium-, niobium-, tantalum-, and molybdenum-carbide variants (flake sizes ∼150–800 nm) prepared via hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching and tetramethylammonium hydroxide delamination. The study demonstrated that specific MXenes exhibit selective antiviral activity depending on viral genotype, while also showing high biocompatibility and the ability to modulate immune responses. MXenes were found to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine release and regulate immune cell activity, highlighting their dual role as antiviral and immunomodulatory agents. Building on these findings, subsequent work explored Ti3C2T x MXene quantum dots (MQDs), characterized by a reduced lateral size below 10 nm. This transition from nanosheets to ultrasmall structures resulted in distinct physicochemical properties, including increased surface area, enhanced colloidal stability, and the emergence of quantum confinement effects such as photoluminescence. Consequently, MQDs demonstrated strong potential for bioimaging and theranostic applications due to their low toxicity, biodegradability, and tunable optical behavior influenced by size and surface chemistry. Further studies systematically evaluated how differences in MXene composition and lateral size influence their biological interactions, particularly cellular uptake and detection across immune cell populations. Using computational approaches alongside flow cytometry analyses, the results demonstrated that lateral size plays a critical role in modulating MXene binding and internalization by immune cells. These variations directly affect detectability, as reflected in measurable signal intensity. Notably, these size-dependent effects were evident both for individual MXenes and when administered as mixed systems at varying doses, underscoring the importance of size control in predicting MXene behavior in biological environments.

Building on the known anti-inflammatory activity of vanadium-based compounds, vanadium carbide MXenes have been developed and assessed for their immunoregulatory effects. V4C3T x nanosheets, with lateral sizes ranging from 0.3 to 1 μm, demonstrated inherent immunosuppressive activity by interfering with T cell–dendritic cell signaling, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and dampening antigen presentation, all while exhibiting excellent biocompatibility. These findings position V4C3T x as a promising platform for modulating excessive immune responses, offering potential applications in the treatment of inflammatory disorders and autoimmune diseases. Single- and few-layered Mo2C nanosheets were synthesized via a modified wet chemical etching and intercalation method, using hydrochloric acid and lithium fluoride followed by hydrothermal treatment to selectively remove the Ga layer from the Mo2Ga2C MAX precursor. This safer, HF-free approach produced multilayered Mo2C with an accordion-like structure, which was further delaminated into few-layer nanosheets through intercalation and ultrasonic treatment. The nanosheets displayed abundant surface terminations and defect-rich structures, including lattice distortions and vacancies, contributing to high surface reactivity and negative surface charge. These features translated into multifunctional biological activity, enabling strong catalytic and antioxidant effects, particularly ROS scavenging. Mo2C nanosheets exhibited anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and pro-angiogenic behavior, promoting M2 macrophage polarization, cell proliferation, migration, and collagen deposition, thereby accelerating diabetic wound healing. These studies illustrate a broader principle that MXenes’ immunomodulatory effects are shaped not only by lateral size, but also by composition, surface chemistry, and structural cues. Titanium, tantalum, vanadium, and molybdenum MXenes demonstrate that careful design of physicochemical properties can achieve a balance of biocompatibility and targeted immunomodulation, paving the way for next-generation applications from anti-inflammatory therapies to hybrid nano-immunotherapies.

Despite substantial progress in MXene research, there has been significant debate on the long-term biocompatibility of MXenes based products, creating the need to design bio-friendly MXene-based compositions. Beyond the shift in size from larger MXene nanosheets to ultrasmall quantum dots, their composition emerges as a key determinant of their biocompatibility and immunomodulatory potential. Hence, in our follow-up studies, we designed and synthesized Ti3C2T x MXene quantum dots (MQDs) with enhanced biocompatibility and promising anti-inflammatory effects, including the suppression of pro-inflammatory Th1 polarization in naïve CD4+ T cells, positioning them as attractive candidates for immunoengineering applications. It is known that high intensity of TiO2 in the chemical composition of Ti3C2T x sheets or quantum dots, especially at higher doses (e.g., 50–100 μg mL–1), can activate apoptosis and cell death through the activation of downstream molecular cascades, compromising cell viability. Similarly, synthesis routes involving hydrofluoric acid (HF) often imposes excessive surface defects on the structures of MXene sheets and quantum dots, hindering their long-term dispersibility and biocompatibility, which are crucial both for immunosuppression. Similar dose-dependent effects can be observed across different MXene compositions, underscoring the need for engineered delivery strategies that preserve immunosuppressive efficacy within a safe therapeutic window in vivo, in contrast to the direct cellular contact effects typically observed in vitro. This distinction arises because in vitro toxicity reflects direct, high-concentration exposure in culture, whereas in vivo tolerable doses for Ti3C2T x depend critically on the administration route, biodistribution, and clearance kinetics. Accordingly, systemic tolerability of MXenes is governed both by the route of administration and the formulation strategy. Recent comprehensive toxicity studies in rats have demonstrated that dermal, oral, and intraperitoneal administration of Ti3C2T x can be well tolerated without significant organ damage, inflammatory activation, or oxidative stress, provided that exposure remains below critical thresholds. Likewise, studies on bile-acid–modified MXenes have identified gavage-based administration as an alternative to intravenous bolus injection for immunosuppression and post-transplant applications, reducing peak systemic nanotoxicity while preserving immunomodulatory effects. In parallel, tailoring surface functional groups and encapsulating MXenes within scaffolds or hydrogels have been shown to enhance bioactivity and biocompatibility. Optimizing etching and delamination conditions to generate −O/–OH-rich and low-fluorine surface terminations markedly reduces ROS generation and apoptosis, yielding biocompatible Ti3C2T x formulations with endothelial cells and red blood cells. Characterizing protein corona formation on MXenes at varying concentrations may further clarify the dose-dependent biocompatibility profiles. In addition, encapsulating Ti3C2T x with biomaterial carriers enables sustained, local release while limiting systemic exposure, as demonstrated in MXene-based hydrogel , and patch systems for cardiac and wound healing applications. Such localized delivery strategies maintain the immunomodulatory activity of MXenes while improving their overall biocompatibility in vivo. Looking forward, the development of non-titanium MXene and MXene–polymer hybrids can widen the therapeutic index by combining MXene-mediated immunosuppression with improved biodegradability and reduced long-term toxicity. ,

To address one of the challenges associated with potential cytotoxicity of Ti3C2T x MXene at higher doses, we reported the first fabrication of tantalum carbide MXene quantum dots with stable phases of surface tantalum oxides (TaO2/Ta2O5, akin to TiO2 in Ti3C2T x ) without using HF. Tantalum’s inherent inertness and biocompatibility have already been leveraged in clinical settings, such as orthopedic implants and bone repair. Here, we used hydrochloric acid and sodium fluoride as the etchant to yield MXenes enriched with oxygen, hydrogen, chlorine, and fluorine-based surface terminations. These MQDs demonstrate enhanced colloidal stability, improved biocompatibility, and potent immunosuppressive effects at low doses. Functionally, we found that tantalum MQDs are internalized by endothelial and antigen-presenting cells, where they suppress Th1 responses, reduce inflammatory cytokine production, and promote immune tolerance, as demonstrated in allograft transplantation models. In contrast, titanium-based MQDs preferentially enhance Treg responses. Using global proteomics and spatial transcriptomics based approaches we further demonstrated that Ti3C2T x MQDs (∼10 nm, 0.15–75 μg mL–1) can regulate COVID-19 associated inflammation and also affect immune-cancer cell interactions. , These studies highlight that lateral size, from extended MXene nanosheets to ultrasmall quantum dots, critically shapes both physicochemical properties and biological functionality. Larger nanosheets are particularly effective for antiviral activity and immune modulation, likely due to enhanced surface interactions that enable viral inhibition and regulation of immune cell responses. In contrast, MQDs exploit their ultrasmall dimensions and quantum confinement effects for imaging, diagnostic, and theranostic applications. By integrating these factors and strategically combining with compositional and surface engineering, provides a powerful framework for designing MXenes with improved biocompatibility and finely tuned antiviral and immunomodulatory functions. By integrating size, composition, and structural design, next-generation MXene platforms can be strategically optimized to simultaneously combat viral infections, modulate immune responses, and support advanced biomedical applications.

Extending beyond antiviral and immunosuppressive applications, the immunomodulatory properties of MXenes are increasingly being exploited in regenerative medicine, where lateral size, surface chemistry, and physicochemical features critically dictate biological outcomes. In particular, Ti3C2T x nanosheets exemplify how controlled material properties can precisely shape immune-mediated tissue repair. For instance, leveraging mild photothermal stimulation to enhance immunosuppressive effects of MXenes enabled control over macrophage polarization towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This regulated immunomodulation enhanced IL-10 secretion and supports osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells, highlighting a MXene-based photothermal strategy to remotely regulate the immune microenvironment for bone regeneration. Similarly, in skeletal muscle repair, the antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties of Ti3C2T x MXenes promote the transition of M1 to M2 macrophages, mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation. This environment fosters myoblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and muscle fiber regeneration in vivo, demonstrating that MXene size, bioactive surface functionalities, and physical cues such as photothermal or photoelectromagnetic stimulation orchestrate complex tissue repair pathways. Beyond nanosheets, MXene-based composites such as Ti3C2T x /β-tricalcium phosphate (“T-MXene”) further extend these capabilities to promote mandibular bone regeneration by modulating the immune microenvironment in bone defects. These composites were found to guide T-cell differentiation and activate N-glycosylation pathways, increasing IL-4 and promoting anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative responses. By upregulating Th2-associated genes and engaging the PI3K-Akt pathway, these materials enhance osteogenesis, mitochondrial metabolism, and tissue repair, both in vitro and in mandibular defect models. Furthermore, Wan et al. reported the application of MXenes-based composite hydrogel with gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) to promote macrophage polarization by leveraging the immunomodulatory functions of heat-shock protein (HSPs), together with the intrinsic photothermal and immunomodulatory properties of MXenes, and the enhanced biocompatibility provided by GelMA. Their study suggested that photothermal activation-mediated HSPs expression contributes to the regulation of macrophage polarization. The synergistic integration of ROS-scavenging activity to alleviate oxidative stress, photothermal properties to enhance antibacterial efficacy, and immunomodulatory regulation of macrophage polarization enables MXene-based composites to serve as multifunctional therapeutic platforms for chronic inflammatory diseases. ,, Zhu et al. reported the antibacterial and immunomodulatory properties of an injectable, biodegradable MXene-composite hydrogel for the treatment of diabetic bone defects. This composite exhibited multifunctional therapeutic activity, with evidence suggesting that its photothermal property contributed to macrophage polarization, thereby enhancing osteogenic potential for bone regeneration. Collectively, these studies underscore that MXenes function as tunable immunomodulatory platforms, where size, composition, surface chemistry, and applied physical stimuli synergistically guide immune responses, inflammation control, and tissue repair. Harnessing these interconnected properties offers a perspective framework for engineering next-generation MXene-based regenerative biomaterials with tunable multifunctionality, bridging antiviral, immunosuppressive, and tissue regenerative applications in a unified design strategy.

Emerging evidence highlights a broader functional potential for MXenes, revealing their ability to regulate autophagy-mediated immune suppression during tissue repair and to potentiate tumor immunotherapy through piezoelectric effects. In skeletal muscle repair, bioactive MXene hydrogels have been shown to promote autophagy in myoblasts, reduce oxidative stress, and shift macrophages toward the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype. These multifunctional biocompatible hydrogels facilitate angiogenesis, enhance myogenic differentiation, and improve muscle innervation, ultimately accelerating fiber formation, restoring tissue architecture, and recovering neuromuscular function. Similarly, MXene-based piezoelectric hydrogels have been designed to trigger tumor cell apoptosis and stimulate antitumor immunity. By integrating MXenes into poly­(vinyl alcohol)/polyvinylidene fluoride scaffolds loaded with biomimetic cancer cell membranes, these systems enhance electrical signaling under ultrasound stimulation, inducing endoplasmic stress and danger-associated molecular patterns. This, in turn, promotes dendritic cell maturation, cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and reversal of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Such strategies illustrate the versatility of MXenes, demonstrating that their physicochemical properties, including conductivity, piezoelectricity, and surface chemistry, can be harnessed to modulate immune responses in both regenerative medicine and cancer therapy.

Key Considerations for MXene-Based Immunomodulation

Our insights, combined with assessments of the broader literature, emphasize key considerations in the evolving transformation of MXenes from electronic materials to biomedical platforms, with particular focus on their immunomodulatory applications. This progression has revealed how lateral dimensions, thickness, surface terminations, edge structures, and interlayer spacing dictate its application. It is evident that their therapeutic potential is closely tied to their physicochemical properties, which, in combination with composition, structural features, and physical cues, govern immune cell interactions, biocompatibility, and overall functional performance across applications including immunosuppression, tissue regeneration, and cancer therapy. We speculate that transitioning from large nanosheets to ultrasmall quantum dots offers precise control over size and surface chemistry fine-tunes cellular uptake, antiviral and anti-inflammatory activity, and tissue-regenerative outcomes. Edge sharpness, interlayer spacing, and surface terminations further modulate dispersibility, wettability, and long-term biocompatibility, emphasizing their relevance for translational applications. Our assessment also underscores the importance of integrating MXenes’ physical properties including conductivity, photothermal activity, and piezoelectricity with chemical and structural design to direct macrophage polarization, enhance regenerative ability, and stimulate antitumor immune responses. This multifactorial versatility underscores how MXenes can evolve from electronic conductors to precisely engineered biomedical platforms for targeted therapeutic modulation. Building on our meta-analytic assessment of MXene biomedical applications over the past ten years, we highlight the need for a more integrated research approach that combines experimental, computational, and translational perspectives. Our analysis reinforces that systematic evaluation of MXenes’ size, composition, surface chemistry, and structural features is critical for predicting biological outcomes and optimizing immunomodulatory performance.

Moreover, recent reports have highlighted the importance of computational, AI-assisted and data-driven approaches in facilitating MXenes synthesis and optimizing their physiochemical properties. Computational methods, such as machine-learning (ML) algorithms can handle large, complex datasets and reveal patterns that are difficult to capture with conventional methods. In MXene research, these methods are applied to interpret surface chemistry, establish relationship between synthesis conditions and material properties, and support the rational design of MXenes with tailored characteristics. , ML also improves prediction of key properties such as layer thickness, dynamic surface changes, and scalable synthesis conditions by learning from experimental and computational datasets. − In addition, these approaches help elucidate MXene–polymer interactions in composite systems, enabling optimization of mechanical, thermal, and electrical performance across energy storage, catalysis, sensing, and materials science. In this context, ML-based algorithms have been integrated with density functional theory (DFT) calculations and high-throughput screening to predict MXene composition, stability, electronic structure, catalytic activity, and other physicochemical features, including work functions, formation energy, synthesis outcomes and candidate materials. These computational frameworks have substantially accelerated MXene discovery and optimization across diverse technological applications. However, these efforts remain confined to synthesis and physicochemical optimization in energy storage-based applications rather than directly predicting and modeling complex immune interactions and responses, which are crucial for therapeutic applications, large-scale production and long-term safety evaluation. Nevertheless, these advances highlight the current capabilities and the potential of integrating AI-, ML-, and DFT-based approaches, and suggest that these methods could eventually be extended to predict and optimize the immunomodulatory properties of MXenes. The convergence of ML, AI-based design, and single-cell and spatial omics may reveal previously inaccessible structure–activity relationships relevant to MXene-based immune engineering. Emerging approaches that integrate mathematical, computational and AI-based predictive frameworks can also offer the potential to rationally design MXenes with tailored size, morphology, composition, and surface chemistry, maximizing immunomodulatory efficacy while minimizing toxicity. Looking ahead, this opportunity lies in combining multidimensional biological and materials-property datasets into robust predictive frameworks that can guide experimental validation of optimal MXene formulations in in vitro and in vivo studies before clinical translation. In this context, systematic studies evaluating long-term biocompatibility, dose-dependent effects, and bio-clearance will remain essential. By integrating these principles with physical cues, MXenes hold promise as multifunctional platforms capable of precise immune modulation, regenerative therapy, and cancer immunotherapy, reflecting a convergence of material engineering, biological insight, and computational design in realizing their biomedical potential.

Current Translational Status, Clinical Considerations and Outstanding Challenges

Despite increasing evidence demonstrating the immunomodulatory properties of MXenes, their development toward clinical applications remains at an early preclinical stage. Current evidence supporting MXene-enabled immunoregulation, that has been discussed here and available, have been primarily generated through in vitro studies and small-animal disease models, , like zebrafish, mice and rats, where MXenes have demonstrated potential in regulating inflammatory signaling, macrophage polarization, oxidative stress responses, tissue regeneration, autophagy, antimicrobial immunity, and tumor-associated immune responses. Collectively, these findings indicate that MXenes can influence immune responses in a composition- and structure-dependent manner at cellular and tissue levels. However, the biological mechanisms underlying MXene-mediated immunomodulation and their relevance to human physiology remain incompletely understood, and no MXene-based immunotherapeutic platform has yet received clinical approval. , A major barrier to clinical translation is the gap between laboratory-scale demonstrations and clinically relevant validation, as current evidence relies largely on conventional two-dimensional cell cultures and small-animal models that only partially capture human immune complexity, tissue-specific microenvironments, and chronic inflammatory processes. Systematic studies using large-animal models and human-relevant systems remain limited, restricting the ability to predict interactions between MXenes and human physiology, immune responses, and disease-associated microenvironments. Greater use of primary human immune cells, organoids, organ-on-chip platforms large-animal models, and long-term in vivo studies will be necessary to improve the clinical relevance of preclinical findings. In addition, long-term data on biosafety, biodistribution, biodegradation, and clearance remain insufficient, underscoring the need for comprehensive pharmacokinetic and toxicological assessments under clinically relevant conditions. ,,

Beyond these translational challenges and considerations, several fundamental questions persist regarding the relationships between MXene material properties and biological responses. MXenes exhibit substantial compositional and structural diversity, including variations in transition metal chemistry, surface terminations, oxidation states, defect densities, and lateral dimensions, which are strongly influenced by synthesis and post-synthetic processing. The lack of a systematic understanding of how MXene composition and structural characteristics influence immune responses is a major challenge. These physicochemical characteristics affect protein adsorption, nano–bio interface formation, cellular uptake, and immune cell activation. However, the contribution of individual physicochemical properties to specific immunological outcomes has not been systematically established. Consequently, differences among studies reporting either pro-inflammatory or immunosuppressive effects may arise from variations in MXene composition, surface chemistry, and biological context rather than intrinsic differences in MXene-mediated immune regulation. ,,, Another unresolved issue is the limited understanding of quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSARs) for MXene-mediated immunomodulation. Many studies modify multiple physicochemical parameters simultaneously and evaluate only a limited set of immune responses, making it difficult to associate specific material features, including composition, size, surface charge, or functional groups, with biological outcomes such as macrophage polarization, T-cell activation, and cytokine production or immune tolerance. As a result, reproducibility remains a significant challenge. Variations in MAX phase precursors, etching chemistry, delamination procedures, surface functionalization, storage conditions, oxidation behavior, and characterization methods can substantially alter MXene properties and their biological activity. In addition, differences in cell models, animal models, dosing strategies, and assay protocols introduce additional variability, making it difficult to compare findings between studies or establish reproducible safety and efficacy profiles. Inconsistent reporting of MXene physicochemical characteristics and experimental conditions further limits cross-study comparison and the development of predictive design principles. Therefore, standardized protocols for synthesis, characterization, and biological evaluation protocols will be essential for improving reproducibility and enabling meaningful cross-study comparisons.

These issues are further accompanied by outstanding practical challenges associated with manufacturing and regulatory translation. Large-scale production of MXenes with consistent physicochemical properties remains challenging due to variations in precursor materials, synthesis conditions, etching strategies, delamination efficiency, surface functionalization, and storage stability. These factors can influence MXene composition, oxidation state, surface chemistry, and biological activity, creating challenges for batch-to-batch reproducibility and quality control. Establishing scalable, standardized synthesis protocols and characterization criteria will therefore be essential for ensuring consistent material performance and reliable biological assessment. Regulatory translation will further require manufacturing processes compatible with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, including defined specifications for material composition, purity, stability, sterility, and functional properties. In addition, regulatory frameworks will require standardized evaluation of immunogenicity, toxicity, pharmacokinetics, and long-term safety profiles. ,, Overall, addressing these challenges through coordinated efforts in material characterization, scalable manufacturing, advanced biological models, and regulatory science will be necessary to determine the feasibility of translating MXene-based immunomodulatory platforms from experimental systems toward clinical applications. Systematic investigation of well-characterized MXene libraries under standardized experimental conditions, rigorous physicochemical characterization, integration of human-relevant experimental models, scalable manufacturing strategies, and harmonized regulatory frameworks, will produce reproducible design principles, improve mechanistic understanding of MXene–immune interactions, and accelerate their clinical translation.

Conclusion and Outlook

In summary, MXenes have rapidly emerged as promising immunomodulatory materials with expanding applications across nanomedicine. Continued progress in synthesis strategies and material design has enabled the development of diverse MXene platforms with improved functionality and therapeutic relevance. However, key challenges remain in ensuring reproducibility and quality control of these biomaterials, in both their preparation and biological performance. Comprehensive studies addressing long-term biocompatibility and the interactions of MXenes with diverse immune cell populations will be pivotal for advancing their translational promise. We believe future research should prioritize systematic and mechanistic studies to better understand MXene interactions at molecular and cellular levels, supported by multi-omics approaches and advanced analytical tools. Concurrently, integrating artificial intelligence and computational modeling will accelerate optimization of MXene properties and enhance their translational potential. The roadmap ahead demands interdisciplinary expertise and universal investigation by bringing different aspects of nanotechnology and basic science research together with ethical practices and a collective foresight on future commercial impact on global healthcare systems.

Taking all these discussions into consideration, through this perspective, we emphasize the importance of detailed mechanistic investigations into how bioactive MXenes regulate genes, proteins, lipids, and other biomolecular pathways using multi-omics approaches, enabling a deeper understanding of their dose- and physicochemical effects. Exploring new immunosuppressive mechanisms and carefully evaluating long-term biological impacts, including potential phenotypic, genotypic, and metabolomic alterations, will be essential for safe clinical translation. In parallel, robust optimization of physicochemical properties of MXenes and corresponding immunomodulatory outputs using advanced techniques will be critical to maximize their therapeutic potential in preclinical applications. As of July 2026, there have not been any particularly reported human clinical trials and publicly available dataset or finalized governmental regulatory evaluations for any MXene nano and quantum-scale materials. Important progress has been obtained in improving nano toxicity, but limitations around long-term biodegradation and clearance, and potential adverse health impacts remain unexplored. We anticipate that this perspective will draw focused attention from the scientific community to the emerging field of immunomodulatory MXene-based materials and stimulate further in-depth investigations to advance their physicochemical understanding in this rapidly evolving area.
