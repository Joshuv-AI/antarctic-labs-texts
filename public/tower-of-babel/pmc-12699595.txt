Surgical and oncological outcomes of right hemicolectomy with complete mesocolic excision in the treatment of right colon cancer: study protocol for a single-centre retrospective cohort study
Authors: Caterina Maria Nava, Hugo Najberg, Michel Adamina, Alexis Litchinko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionComplete mesocolic excision (CME) with central vascular ligation (CVL) has been proposed as an oncologically superior surgical technique for right-sided colon cancer, mirroring the impact of total mesorectal excision (TME) in rectal cancer. While promising results have been reported, evidence from routine clinical practice, particularly within Swiss healthcare settings, remains limited. This study aims to evaluate the surgical and oncological outcomes of CME compared with standard right hemicolectomy (non-CME) over a 10-year period.Methods and analysisThis is a retrospective cohort study of adult patients who underwent right hemicolectomy, either CME or non-CME, for histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma of the right colon or right third of the transverse colon at a tertiary centre, between 2010 and 2020. Patients with concomitant malignancies or distant metastases will be excluded. The primary outcome is 3-year and 5-year disease-free survival. Secondary outcomes include number of harvested lymph nodes, 3-year and 5-year overall survival, and 30-day morbidity.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the relevant Ethics Committee (CER-VD 2025-00503). All patients provided general consent for secondary use of anonymised data. Results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal and presented at national and international conferences.

Introduction

Right-sided colon cancer accounts for a significant proportion of colorectal malignancies, which remain among the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 2 In Switzerland, colorectal cancer remains a major burden. Between 2013 and 2017, colon cancer ranked third most common cancer in men and second in women.3 Each year, about 2500 men and 2000 women were diagnosed. Colon cancer is also a leading cause of cancer-related death, ranking third in mortality for both sexes, causing approximately 920 deaths annually in men and 750 in women in Switzerland. While incidence rates have remained stable among women, they have shown a slight decline in men over the last decade. Mortality rates have decreased over the past 30 years, by 45% in men and 41% in women, reflecting improvements in screening and treatment. As of 2021, an estimated 25 600 individuals in Switzerland were living with a diagnosis of colon cancer from the past 10 years, highlighting the growing population of survivors. The 5-year relative survival rate has improved only marginally over time, reaching 67% in men and 68% in women between 2013 and 2017. compared with other European countries, Switzerland ranks among the top third for survival outcomes, though absolute differences remain modest.3

Beyond its frequency, right-sided colon cancer is considered a clinically and biologically distinct entity from left-sided colon cancer, with important implications for prognosis and treatment planning.4 Tumours located on the right side of the colon originate embryologically from the midgut and are more commonly diagnosed in older patients and women, often at a more advanced stage and more poorly differentiated, which contributes to a generally poorer prognosis compared with left-sided tumours.57 On a molecular level, right-sided and left-sided colon cancers follow distinct molecular pathways. Tumours on the right side of the colon are more often associated with features such as mucinous histology, microsatellite instability and certain genetic mutations, such as in the BRAF gene.8 9 In contrast, left-sided tumours more commonly show alterations in genes like KRAS and TP53.10 These molecular differences partly explain variations in treatment response.11

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment for colon cancer. However, the optimal extent of mesocolic excision, particularly in right-sided cases, continues to be debated.12 13 In recent years, complete mesocolic excision (CME) with central vascular ligation (CVL) has been introduced as a potentially superior oncological approach, mirroring the paradigm shift observed in rectal cancer management following the implementation of total mesorectal excision (TME).14 15 First described by Hohenberger et al, CME involves sharp dissection along the embryological planes, preservation of the mesocolic fascia and central ligation of the ileocolic and right colic vessels at their origin from the superior mesenteric artery. For tumours of the transverse colon, the middle colic artery is similarly transected at its root.16 Hohenberger’s original CME series reported a reduction in 5-year local recurrence from 6.5% to 3.6%, and an increase in cancer-related 5-year survival from 82.1% to 89.1%.4

Pathologically, CME specimens have been shown to be oncologically superior. Compared with standard colectomy, CME results in a greater distance from tumour to vascular tie (131 mm vs 90 mm, p<0.0001), longer bowel resections (314 mm vs 206 mm, p<0.0001), wider mesenteric resection areas (19 657 mm² vs 11 829 mm², p<0.0001) and higher lymph node yields (30 vs 18, p<0.0001).17 Several retrospective studies using data from the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results programme,1820 other cancer registries21 and systematic reviews18 have shown that a higher lymph node yield is associated with better survival, especially for right-sided lesions. These differences are likely to contribute to improved staging accuracy and disease-free survival (DFS). Several retrospective series and meta-analyses have supported these findings, reporting improved lymph node harvest, better specimen quality and potentially superior long-term outcomes.2225

Despite its potential advantages, CME is technically more demanding than standard right hemicolectomy. It requires central vascular dissection near the superior mesenteric vessels, which increases the risk of intraoperative complications, particularly vascular injuries.26 27 Furthermore, evidence for its widespread adoption remains limited, with much of the current literature based on single-centre experiences, non-randomised studies, meta-analyses12 24 28 29 and little data available from the Swiss healthcare context.12 30

Two randomised controlled trials are currently investigating the role of CME in right colon cancer. The RELARC trial (China) and the CoME-in trial (Italy) have both reported encouraging short-term outcomes, including higher lymph node yields and acceptable complication rates.31 32 However, final data on long-term oncological outcomes are still pending. Notably, the RELARC trial did report a higher rate of vascular injury in the CME group (3% vs 1%), highlighting the importance of technical risks.31

CME can be performed via open or laparoscopic approaches. The equivalence of laparotomy and laparoscopy in terms of resected lymph nodes was shown in four large, multicentre studies: Clinical Outcomes of Surgical Therapy, Conventional versus Laparoscopic-Assisted Surgery in Colorectal Cancer (CLASSIC), Colon Cancer Laparoscopic or Open Resection I (COLOR I) and the Australasian Randomised Clinic Study Comparing Laparoscopic and Conventional Open Surgical Treatments for Colon Cancer (ALCCaS).3336 A recent meta-analysis comparing open versus laparoscopic CME with CVL in right-sided and left-sided tumours showed that patients from the laparoscopic group had a shorter hospital stay and a shorter recovery time to regain gastrointestinal function.37 In summary, the current data suggest that the laparoscopic approach offers the same quality of resected specimens as the open approach in CME with CVL for colon cancer while maintaining all of the short-term benefits of a minimally invasive approach.

Although interest in CME is growing, national data in Switzerland are lacking, particularly regarding long-term outcomes in real-world settings. This study aims to address this knowledge gap by conducting a retrospective cohort analysis of all patients undergoing right hemicolectomy with CME compared with non-CME, at a tertiary hospital in Switzerland over a 10-year period. We hypothesise that CME will outperform non-CME on: (H1) DFS rate at 3 (H1a) and 5 (H1b) years; (H2) total lymph nodes harvested and (H3) overall survival (OS) (ie, including cancer relapse) at 3 (H3a) and 5 (H3b) years. We also expect that (H4) CME has a non-inferior 30-day morbidity to non-CME.

The project has received approval from the appropriate regional ethics committee and is expected to contribute important data on the surgical and oncological outcomes of CME in the literature and guide current clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study status

Data collection is scheduled to begin at the end of November 2025. Preliminary results are expected by the end of January 2026, with final study completion anticipated by June 2026.

Study design

This is a single-centre retrospective cohort study comparing surgical and oncological outcomes of right hemicolectomy with CME and CVL versus standard right hemicolectomy (non-CME). The study will include all eligible patients treated at Fribourg Cantonal Hospital, a tertiary referral centre in Switzerland, over a 10-year period from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2020. This timeframe allows for a 5-year follow-up. No interventions will be introduced as part of the study. Data will be collected exclusively through retrospective review of institutional electronic medical records. This protocol has received ethical approval from the Regional Human Research Ethics Committee of the Canton of Vaud (CER-VD 2025-00503). Data collection will begin only after this protocol is accepted for publication.

Study population

Potential participants will be identified using hospital administrative records, filtered by surgical procedure titles including ‘Hemicolectomy’ and ‘Right Hemicolectomy’ and ‘CME’. Eligibility will be confirmed through detailed chart review according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The cohort will be described according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for observational cohort studies.38

Classification into ‘CME’ or ‘non-CME’ groups will be based on this explicit terminology used in the operative reports. To maximise objectivity, we will have two co-authors independently classify each case as CME or non-CME. Discrepancies will be resolved by the senior co-author.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

(H1) DFS at (H1a) 3 and (H1b) 5 years, defined as the time from surgery to either documented recurrence or death from any cause, whichever occurs first.

Secondary outcomes

(H2) Total number of lymph nodes harvested from the surgical specimen (per histopathology report).

(H3) OS at (H3a) 3 and (H3b) 5 years: defined as time from surgery to death from any cause.

(H4) 30-day morbidity. Morbidity was defined as any postoperative surgical complications. Postoperative complications were graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification, ranging from 1 to 5.39

Data collection

Relevant patient demographics (age, sex, BMI), relevant comorbidities, tumour characteristics (location, staging, histology), treatment variables (surgical approach, chemotherapy), surgical specimen variables (length of resected bowel, mesocolic area (cm2), mesocolic integrity score when reported), hospital stay (length, complications, re-operation, death) and follow-up data will be extracted from the institutional electronic database. All data will be de-identified and handled in compliance with data protection standards. A secure, password-protected Excel database will be created, with change-tracking enabled to ensure full auditability. A separate linkage file containing the correspondence between neutral study identifiers and patient chart numbers will be stored securely in a locked drawer in the surgery department office, accessible only to authorised study personnel.

Record retention

In compliance with Swiss data protection regulations, electronic records and key files will be stored for 10 years. The patient code file will be held on an encrypted USB device locked in the department office and permanently erased after the retention period. A paper backup will be securely stored in the department’s document archive and destroyed after 10 years.

Sample size

We expect approximately 200 patients to have undergone CME within our inclusion timeframe. This estimate accounts for institutional trends: approximately one right hemicolectomy with CME per month from 2010 to 2014, and two per month between 2015–2020. To determine the required size of the non-CME cohort for 80% power, we performed a priori power analysis using the R package pwr.40 For a one-sided two-proportion test (H1, H3) and a one-sided t-test (H2, H4) with unequal group sizes (200 CME vs non-CME) and a p value threshold of 0.05, a medium effect size (Cohen’s h and Cohen’s d=0.5) requires 28 non-CME patients.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be performed using R V.4.2.1 default functions if not stated otherwise.

Positive control

To ensure that the inclusion of both right colon tumours and proximal third transverse tumours does not affect our hypotheses’ testing, we will test if both types differ in their total number of harvested lymph nodes in both the CME and non-CME groups, as indexed by Cohen’s ds below 0.4. In the case of a higher Cohen’s d, the patients that drive the difference the most will be excluded until reaching this threshold. If this threshold cannot be respected through data exclusion, only the largest group will be kept for the hypotheses testing.

Hypothesis testing

One-sided test of two proportions will be used between the CME and non-CME groups on the DFS (H1) and OS (H3) at both 3 (a) and 5 (b) years. To correct for two dependent tests within each hypothesis, the p value threshold will be lowered to 0.025 according to the Šidák correction.

A one-sided Welch t-test will be used between the CME and non-CME groups on the total number of harvested lymph nodes (H2) and morbidity at 30-day (H4). For the 30-day morbidity, as we expect non-inferiority, the t-test will assess whether the difference between groups is greater than −1 rather than 0.

In addition, population characteristics and Kaplan-Meier curves will be drawn for the DFS and OS to allow a descriptive interpretation of the data.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Canton of Vaud, Switzerland (CER-VD 2025-00503). As a retrospective and observational study, it involves no additional procedures or patient interventions. All included participants provided general informed consent for the secondary use of anonymised clinical data, in accordance with institutional policy and national regulations.

Patient confidentiality will be strictly maintained. Data will be extracted from the hospital’s secure electronic medical record system and de-identified using neutral numerical codes. The linkage key connecting these codes to patient identities will be stored separately in a locked, access-restricted location within the surgical department. As this study does not involve direct contact with patients or prospective data collection, it poses no physical, psychological or financial risk to participants.

No personal identifiers (such as medical record numbers) will be collected. The main potential risk (a breach of data confidentiality) will be mitigated by assigning unique study codes and storing the code-breaking information securely and separately from the main dataset. Only authorised members of the study team will have access to any sensitive material.

This study complies fully with the Swiss Human Research Act, the Ordinance on Human Research and the General Data Protection Regulation.

The results of this study will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed medical journal and presented at national and international surgical conferences. No individual-level data will be shared publicly. A summary of findings will be shared with the hospital’s colorectal surgery department and oncology department to support future clinical practice.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the development, design, conduct or dissemination plans of this retrospective study. The study is based on routine clinical data collected during standard hospital care. As such, no active patient participation or consultation was undertaken.
