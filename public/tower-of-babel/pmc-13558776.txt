Inflammatory neuropathy in mouse and primate models of colorectal cancer
Authors: Caitlyn M Gaffney, Angela M Casaril, Iqbal Mahmud, Bo Wei, Yanyan Jiang, Nathan T Fiore, Jialu Liu, Karen M Valadez, Elizabeth A Kolb, Fisher R Cherry, Lei Shi, Kenneth Dunner, Philip L Lorenzi, Steven R Reiken, Peter M Grace, Rajasekaran Mahalingam, Theresa A Guise, Carolyn L Hodo, Andrew J Shepherd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer survivors are at increased risk of developing neurological issues, particularly peripheral neuropathy and chronic pain. Although pre-existing neuropathy is a risk factor for chronic pain, tumor-induced neuropathy has not been firmly established in pre-clinical models. Consistent with clinical observations, we show that mice with colorectal cancer develop peripheral neuropathy, which was associated with subtle locomotor deficits, without overt hypersensitivity. We detected widespread differences in pro-inflammatory cytokines and lipid metabolites in peripheral nerves from tumor-bearing mice. Macrophage accumulation, myelin decompaction and ryanodine receptor oxidation were associated with dysfunctional calcium homeostasis and reduced spike amplitude in sensory neurons. Similar alterations in plasma inflammatory mediators and lipid metabolites were associated with neuropathy and macrophage accumulation in peripheral nerves of rhesus macaques with colorectal cancer. These findings suggest colorectal cancer is causally linked to a subacute form of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy across species, which may represent an under-reported, yet important risk factor for neurological dysfunction in colorectal cancer survivors.Subject terms: Neurodegeneration, Somatic system, Neuroimmunology, Chronic pain

Introduction

There will be an estimated 158,000 diagnoses of colorectal cancer (CRC) in the US in 20261. Advances in screening and treatment have improved CRC survival, with 5-year survival for localized disease above 90% and more than 1.4 million total CRC survivors in the US in 20252. This success inevitably prompts greater focus on the long-term complications of cancer and its treatment, especially neuropathy and pain. Overall pain prevalence in cancer survivors is estimated at 45–60%3,4, with survivors frequently reporting widespread, persistent pain for months to years after completing treatment. The major precipitating factors in this pain are invasive surgery and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy5. In addition to a lack of effective pharmacotherapies for neuropathic pain in cancer survivors6, there is also a dearth of understanding surrounding the factors that increase the risk of developing chronic pain. However, prior injury to the somatosensory nervous system is one such risk factor7. This injury has many potential causes, including surgery, trauma, metabolic dysfunction and - crucially - cancer itself.

Cancer-related or “paraneoplastic” neuropathy is clinically defined by neurological symptoms due to autoantibody production against neuronal antigens and is a well-established, albeit rare sequela of lymphoma, lung, and breast cancer8,9. However, the extent to which other neoplasms drive neuropathy is largely unknown, and the widespread underdiagnosis of many forms of neuropathy10 raises the likelihood that paraneoplastic neuropathy is similarly under-reported. Additionally, two prior studies of CRC patients found somewhat unexpected sensory deficits and reduced cutaneous nerve fiber density before beginning chemotherapy11,12, hinting that neuropathy may be a more common and therefore under-diagnosed complication of CRC. Determining the mechanistic basis of this neuropathy is an important step toward accurately assessing the risk of neurological complications and other adverse events due to CRC and its treatment.

Our previous work showed reduced cutaneous nerve fiber density and sensory neuron mitochondrial dysfunction in a mouse model of CRC, both hallmarks of peripheral neuropathy13. We also detected increased pro-inflammatory cytokines in serum, but the behavioral effects of this neuropathy and the implications for peripheral nerve inflammation are not fully understood. We now show that an immunocompetent, orthotopic model of high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) CRC exhibits neuropathy which manifests as inflammation and lipid dysregulation in the plasma and peripheral nerves, demyelination, and disrupted calcium homeostasis in sensory neurons. We also recapitulate these neuroinflammatory, neurodegenerative and demyelinating effects in rhesus macaques predisposed to development of MSI-H CRC. Because pre-existing neuropathy driven by CRC could represent a major risk factor for subsequent neurological issues, these findings may have previously unrecognized implications for quality-of-life in cancer survivorship.

Results

MC38 tumor mice show no clear changes in tactile and thermal sensitivity, alongside motor coordination deficits

Orthotopic injection of MC38-Luc-RFP cells enables monitoring of tumor burden alongside sensorimotor behavior testing (Fig. 1a). Tumor burden (measured by luciferase signal) did not significantly differ between males and females (Fig. 1b, c), and significant loss of PGP9.5-immunoreactive intraepidermal nerve fibers (IENFs) was seen in hindpaws of MC38 mice versus vehicle controls (Fig. 1d, e). This is consistent with our prior observations13 and, importantly, occurs despite there being no detectable overlap of hindpaw and colon innervation (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We also detected reduced epidermal nerve fiber density in ear skin, which is innervated by the trigeminal ganglia, further indicating that non-GI overlapping dermatomes are similarly affected (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 1b). MC38 tumor growth was associated with modest decreases in von Frey hindpaw withdrawal threshold (Fig. 1h), with a significant main effect of time post-injection and an interaction between injection type x time. Tumor-bearing males showed significant decreases in paw withdrawal threshold between baseline/week 2 and week 4 (0.76 ± 0.08 g at baseline, 0.75 ± 0.04 g at week 2, 0.5 ± 0.05 g at week 4). There was no significant correlation between tumor burden and paw withdrawal threshold among MC38 mice, though the effect in males was statistically borderline (p = 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 1c). We also saw comparable performance between vehicle controls and MC38 mice in the burrowing assay, a pain-suppressed behavioral measure14 (Supplementary Fig. 1d). The opioid receptor antagonist naltrexone was used to unmask any potential latent pain sensitization in MC38 mice15. There was a significant three-way interaction between naltrexone, timepoint and tumor status (Supplementary Fig. 1e), but no significant effect of naltrexone on paw withdrawal thresholds of MC38 mice at any post-injection timepoint.

Neuropathic and inflammatory pain states commonly feature cold and heat hypersensitivity, respectively16,17. Using a 15–40 °C thermal gradient ring18 (Fig. 1i), we did not detect any shift in temperature preference or aversion post-MC38 injection (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 1f). Peripheral neuropathy is also a risk factor for gait abnormalities and impaired balance19. Comparing gait patterns of vehicle and MC38 mice, we saw that hind paw print intensity and single stance time did not significantly differ (Fig. 1k, l). However, tumor growth affected overall run speed, stand time, overall step cycle time, and swing speed, particularly in weeks 1 and 4 post-injection (Fig. 1m–p). Stand index, mean print intensity at max contact, body speed, and stride length were also impacted by tumor growth (Supplementary Fig 2a–d). Despite main effects of time, swing, print dimensions and toe spread metrics did not significantly differ between control and MC38 mice (Supplementary Fig. 2e–l). There were similar main effects of time and interactions with sex (Supplementary Fig. 3a–r), but the only statistically significant sex differences were observed between vehicle-injected controls in run speed and step cycle time (p = 0.0096 and 0.045, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 3a, b).

To establish the extent to which gait disruptions were due to inflammation, we treated a cohort of MC38 tumor mice with the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) carprofen (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Multiple gait changes in MC38 mice were attenuated in MC38 + carprofen mice. While no significant main effect of carprofen was seen in stand/swing time and swing speed (Supplementary Fig. 4c); other measures showed a main effect of carprofen treatment (print/stride length, single stance time, run/body speed), time (stand index, mean/max intensity, body speed variation and print width), or both (duty cycle, dual stance; Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). A significant time × carprofen interaction was found for print area and maximum contact area (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Collectively, this suggests that NSAID-sensitive inflammation contributes to gait disturbances in tumor-bearing mice, particularly in the early stages of tumor growth.

Because gait analysis measures normal, voluntary locomotor function, we also subjected mice to the more demanding beam walk test, which measures traversal time of three increasingly challenging beams (Fig. 1q). Three weeks post-injection, MC38 mice took significantly more time to cross all three beam difficulties compared to their pre-injection baselines (Fig. 1r–t, Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Video Files 1-6). Although mice with the greatest performance decrement tended to have MC38 tumors, the average number of errors (slips, inversions, falls) did not significantly increase post-MC38 injection (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Consistently, the worsened narrow flat beam walk performance at week 3 was significantly attenuated in carprofen-treated MC38 mice (Supplementary Fig. 5c), although carprofen did not protect against loss of IENF density (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e). Collectively, this suggests that MC38 mice exhibit histological signs of peripheral neuropathy, without overt or latent hypersensitivity to tactile or thermal stimuli, but with subtly impaired motor coordination, which may be partly attributed to an inflammatory response to the tumor in its earlier stages.

Hypercoagulability and dyslipidemia in MC38 mice

We next determined whether MC38 mice develop biomarkers of hyperglycemia, hypercoagulability, or renal/hepatic failure, since they are all known complications of cancer and potential causes of neuropathy20. We observed no significant difference in blood glucose, and there was a non-significant tendency toward reduced bleeding time and blood loss in MC38 mice, which was accompanied by significantly increased fibrinogen and a non-significant trend toward reduced fibrinogen time (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Other coagulation measures (prothrombin time, antithrombin III, activated partial thromboplastin time) did not significantly differ (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

With the exception of aspartate transaminase, markers of hepatic function (alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, alanine transaminase; Supplementary Fig. 6c) and renal function (phosphorus, sodium, potassium, creatinine, albumin, total protein; Supplementary Fig. 6d) were not significantly altered by MC38 tumor growth. This suggests that, although liver and kidney failure can cause neuropathy, neither are likely causes of MC38-related neuropathy. Finally, lactate dehydrogenase (a marker of CRC burden), but not creatine kinase (a marker of muscle degeneration), showed a non-significant trend toward elevation in MC38 mice (Supplementary Fig. 6e)21,22.

Because increased gut permeability is associated with inflammation23, we tested whether CRC was causing overt damage to the GI tract. Oral dosing of vehicle and MC38 mice with a fluorescent dextran conjugate did not lead to any increase in fluorescence in the blood of MC38 mice relative to controls, nor could we detect lipocalin-2 elevation in feces, despite its increased levels in plasma (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). Both observations are consistent with GI integrity being broadly intact at this disease stage. Additionally, a luminol test did not detect occult fecal bleeding (Supplementary Fig. 7d), also suggesting that frank anemia is also unlikely to underlie MC38-driven neuropathy. Histologically, there was some evidence of increased cellularity in colonic submucosa (Supplementary Fig. 7e–h), but no obvious loss of integrity or perforation. This suggests that MC38-driven neuropathy is associated with symptoms of GI inflammation, without evidence to support overt loss of barrier integrity.

CRC and MC38 tumor growth elevate serum pro-inflammatory cytokines13, an effect which has been linked to dyslipidemia in several chronic diseases24–26. We next surveyed plasma lipid metabolites to establish whether dyslipidemia could underlie the neuropathy caused by MC38 tumor growth (Fig. 2a). Of the 371 distinct lipid species detected in MC38 mouse plasma, 13 were significantly upregulated and 101 were significantly downregulated versus controls (Fig. 2b). MC38 mouse plasma also showed widespread depletion of triglycerides (TGs; Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Long-chain fatty acids (FAs; >18 carbons) and unsaturated FAs were also enriched in MC38 mouse plasma, while comparatively shorter chain lengths (13-18 carbons) and unsaturated FAs were depleted (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 8b). Numerous families of “inflammatory lipids” (sphingolipids, sphingomyelins, ceramides, hexosylceramides, cholesterol esters) were enriched in MC38 mouse plasma, along with organelle-specific lipid ontologies (“mitochondrion,” “endoplasmic reticulum,” “endosome/lysosome;” Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 8c), however, an ELISA specific for the pro-inflammatory lipid sphingosine-1-phosphate (a known pro-nociceptive lipid mediator27) did not detect differences in MC38 mice (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Around two-thirds of phosphatidylcholine (PC) species detected were enriched in MC38 mouse plasma, whereas two-thirds of phosphatidylethanolamines (PE) were reduced. As expected, enrichment and reduction in PC and PE were reflected in the levels of their products: lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LPE; Fig. 2g–i). Finally, phosphatidylinositol (PI) species were both enriched and reduced in tumor-bearing mice, with no clear distinction in chain length or saturation index (Fig. 2j). Broadly similar changes in plasma lipid enrichment and depletion were replicated in an independent cohort of vehicle and MC38 tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 8e–p). Importantly, these disruptions to lipid composition were largely prevented by treatment with carprofen (Supplementary Fig. 9a–j), specifically implicating MC38 tumor-driven inflammation in the disruption of circulating lipids.

Disruptions to neuronal lipid composition consistent with neuropathy in tumor-bearing mice

The profound dyslipidemia in MC38 mouse plasma is consistent with prior reports of malignancy-associated dyslipidemia in mice and humans26,28. We next ran a non-targeted survey of the lipid composition of the sciatic nerve, which is known to be disrupted by plasma dyslipidemia in demyelinating diseases29 (Fig. 3a). Consistent with the results in plasma, we observed 250 enriched and 50 reduced lipid species in sciatic nerves from MC38 mice, along with significant overall reduction of TGs (Fig. 3b–d). The enrichment of longer-chain FAs and depletion of medium-length FAs seen in plasma was mirrored in sciatic nerves, as was enrichment of several lipid ontologies such as mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum-related lipid species (Fig. 3e, f). Inflammatory lipid classes including sphingolipids, sphingomyelin and ceramides were also significantly enriched (Fig. 3g, h). As with plasma lipids, an additional experimental run reproduced these changes in nerve lipid composition (Supplementary Fig. 10a–g).

These substantial changes in peripheral nervous system lipids prompted us to re-evaluate pre-existing bulk sequencing datasets of sensory ganglia from MC38 tumor-bearing mice at the same timepoint. Consistent with the lipidomic findings, there was significant differential expression of genes related to lipid metabolism and Schwann cell function (e.g., Bdnf, Car3, Fmo2, Lrg1, Lpl, Mag, Myrf, Pitpnb, Plin4, Pltp, Pmp22, Sirt1, Smpd2, Sox11, Vcan; Supplementary Fig. 11a)30,31. Collectively, this suggests that MC38 tumor growth either directly or indirectly distorts lipid metabolism in ways that may lead to neuronal/Schwann cell dysfunction.

Evidence of Schwann cell injury and inflammation in MC38 mouse nerves

Because we detected lipidomic and transcriptional changes suggestive of Schwann cell dysfunction, we analyzed MC38 mouse sciatic nerves for histological and ultrastructural signs of inflammation or injury (Fig. 4a). Immunoreactivity for S100B—which is secreted by activated Schwann cells in neurodegenerative conditions32—was elevated in sciatic and intercostal nerves from MC38 mice and attenuated by carprofen (Fig. 4b–d and Supplementary Fig. 11b–g). This led us to examine nerve ultrastructure using transmission electron microscopy. Vehicle controls showed typical electron-dense, compact myelin around larger caliber axons (Fig. 4e, f). However, a significant increase in myelin decompaction (“onion bulb” formations) was seen in MC38 mouse nerves (Fig. 4g–i). This was associated with decreased axon cross-sectional area and reduced inner-to-outer diameter ratio of myelinated axons (“G-ratio”; Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 11i). This MC38-related decrease in G-ratio was seen across myelinated axons of all calibers (Supplementary Fig. 11j). Although the average number of unmyelinated axons per Remak bundle did not significantly differ (Fig. 4k-m), unmyelinated axons in MC38 mice had a rightward shift in their distribution of cross-sectional area, indicative of axonal swelling (Fig. 4n and Supplementary Fig. 11k). Furthermore, bulk RNA-sequencing of cultured Schwann cells exposed to MC38 serum showed robust upregulation of transcripts associated with activation of the interferon response (Gbp6, Isg15, Bst2)33–35, dedifferentiation/proliferation (Meis1)36 and metabolic stress (Cox7a1; Supplementary Fig. 11z)37 when compared to Schwann cells exposed to serum from healthy mice. Crucially, similar myelin decompaction, reduced G-ratio and unmyelinated axon swelling were also detected in intercostal nerves originating from upper thoracic ganglia (Supplementary Fig. 12a–i), consistent with widespread nerve damage occurring beyond the sciatic nerve alone.

Consistent with the ultrastructural and inflammatory changes in the nerves of MC38 mice, histological staining of sciatic nerves revealed some evidence of epineurial edema, although individual nerve fascicles and the surrounding perineurium remained intact (Supplementary Fig. 12j). Accordingly, we did not detect any increase in sodium fluorescein extravasation into the endoneurium of MC38 mouse nerves, suggesting that tumor growth does not cause overt loss of blood-nerve barrier integrity (Supplementary Fig. 12k, l). We previously showed that mRNA for several cytokines and chemokines is increased in sensory ganglia from MC38 mice13. Consistently, several cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and adhesion molecules were substantially upregulated (mean ≥1.3-fold) in sciatic nerves from MC38 mice versus controls (Fig. 4o). Collectively, this suggests MC38 tumor growth is associated with demyelination, small fiber damage and plasma extravasation in the epineurium and upregulation of inflammatory mediators that may facilitate leukocyte infiltration.

Deposition of IgG and macrophage accumulation in MC38 mouse nerves and sensory ganglia

Because development of autoantibodies against neuronal antigens is a well-described phenomenon in paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes and preclinical models of peripheral nerve injury8,38, we next investigated the accumulation of IgG in sciatic nerves and lumbar sensory ganglia from MC38 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 5a). Although we did not detect a significant increase in IgG deposition in MC38 sciatic nerves, NF200 expression was significantly increased (Fig. 5b–e). In contrast, NF200 expression was not significantly increased in DRGs of MC38 mice, but we did detect increased IgG immunoreactivity (Fig. 5f–i and Supplementary Fig. 10a–d). Importantly, IgG immunoreactivity was inducible by surgical nerve injury in wild-type mice, and negligible in MC38 tumor-bearing muMt mice, which develop tumors at a similar rate, but lack mature B-cells39 (Fig. 5j, k and Supplementary Fig. 13e, f), indicating that the increased IgG immunoreactivity seen in wild-type mice is specific and consistent with the response to other forms of nerve damage.

Since macrophages accumulate in sensory ganglia and peripheral nerves in several neuropathies40,41, and because of the complex interplay between IgG immune complexes and macrophage activation, we next stained for macrophages (Fig. 6a). CD68 staining density was elevated in sciatic nerve epineurium and peri/endoneurium of wild-type MC38 mice (Fig. 6b–d). This was mirrored in the meninges and parenchyma of lumbar DRG (Fig. 6e–g). There was a non-significant tendency of carprofen to reduce CD68 staining density in DRG and sciatic nerves from MC38 mice (Supplementary Fig. 14a–c). Both sciatic nerve (Fig. 6h–j) and DRG (Fig. 6k–m) also showed a significant reduction in CD206 staining density, suggesting a net reduction in an anti-inflammatory, pro-repair macrophage phenotype.

Chemogenetic deletion of macrophages worsens MC38-induced neuropathy

To understand the contribution of these macrophages to the progression of neuropathy, we injected MC38 cells into Macrophage Fas-Induced Apoptosis (“MaFIA”) mice42, which enables chemogenetic ablation of peripheral macrophages (Fig. 7a). Three days of treatment significantly reduced CD68 staining density in DRG and sciatic nerve (Fig. 7b–e), without significant effects on tumor burden or spinal cord microglia (Supplementary Fig. 15a–d). There was a tendency of macrophage ablation to reduce sciatic nerve S100B immunoreactivity in MC38 mice, but it did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 15e, f). In the beam walk task, macrophage-ablated MaFIA mice with MC38 tumors performed significantly worse than their macrophage-replete counterparts and macrophage-ablated, non-tumor bearing controls (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 15i).

To better understand the functional changes in macrophages being induced by tumor growth, we analyzed the transcriptome of cultured bone marrow-derived macrophages following their exposure to serum collected from tumor-bearing mice. Exposure to serum from MC38 mice led to robust expression of a pro-inflammatory, M1-like phenotype, with upregulation of chemokines (e.g., Cxcl10, Ccl2, Ccl3, Ccl7), interferon-stimulated genes (Ifit1/2/3, Isg15, Isg20, Oasl1) and downregulation of transcripts associated with M2-like polarization (Rora, Bmpr1a, Arg2)43,44 and cholesterol biosynthesis (Hsd17b7, Idi1; Fig. 7g, h)45,46. Crucially, depletion of lipids from tumor-bearing serum prevented many of these transcriptional alterations in BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 15h–k). In addition to the expected downregulation of transcripts related to lipid sensing, synthesis, and elongation (e.g., Acaca, Elovl1; Supplementary Fig. 15j), lipid depletion counteracted expression of genes related to pro-inflammatory signaling and cell stress responses, and counteracted MC38-related shifts away from RNA processing, translation and oxidative phosphorylation (e.g; Tlr9, Parp1, Eif1, Eif4a2, Cox5a, Atp5e; Supplementary Fig. 15k). Collectively, these data suggest that consistent with observations in tissues, circulating factors in MC38 mouse serum instate a pro-inflammatory program in macrophages, a process which depends at least partially on tumor-induced changes to circulating lipid composition.

Ryanodine receptor oxidation is associated with DRG neuron dysfunction in MC38 mice

In addition to dyslipidemia, inflammation and cytokine release can induce ryanodine receptor (RyR)-induced calcium dysregulation47. We detected a significant upregulation of Ryr1 transcript in MC38 mouse DRG in our previously published dataset13 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Consistent with inflammation-associated oxidative stress, we detected increased RyR oxidation and reduced calstabin binding in MC38 mouse lumbar DRG, both indicators of dysfunctional or “leaky” RyR (Fig. 8a–c). These deficits were associated with elevated serum TGF-β1 (Supplementary Fig. 16a), as has been described for skeletal muscle RyR dysfunction secondary to bone metastasis47. RyR oxidation and dissociation from calstabin could be prevented by chronic dosing of MC38 mice with carprofen (Supplementary Fig. 16b, c), consistent with oxidative stress being driven by inflammation.

To determine whether RyR oxidation affected sensory neuron function, we carried out live-cell Ca2+ imaging of dissociated DRG neurons. Exposing DRG neurons to the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum (ER) Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) pump inhibitor thapsigargin in Ca2+-free external solution enables assessment of ER Ca2+ content48. Additionally, the rate of intracellular Ca2+ increase upon restoration of extracellular Ca2+ is influenced by Ca2+ stores (Fig. 8d). Consistent with RyR oxidation causing leak of ER Ca2+, thapsigargin mobilized significantly less Ca2+ from MC38 mouse lumbar DRGs (Fig. 8e, f), and the slope of Ca2+ influx upon bath re-addition of Ca2+ was significantly slower (Fig. 8g, h). Similar deficits in thapsigargin-evoked Ca2+ were detected in neurons isolated from cervical and upper thoracic ganglia (Supplementary Fig. 16d–g). Consistent with the observed effects of carprofen on RyR oxidation, carprofen restored thapsigargin-induced Ca2+ flux in MC38 mouse neurons (Supplementary Fig. 16h). Finally, in vitro treatment with S107, which stabilizes the RyR-calstabin interaction49, also augmented the thapsigargin response (Fig. 8i, j). Collectively, these data indicate that MC38-driven inflammation is responsible for RyR oxidation and defective calcium homeostasis in sensory neurons.

We next used multi-electrode arrays to determine whether dysregulated ER Ca2+ homeostasis affected sensory neuron activity in MC38 mice (Fig. 9a). At 48 h ex vivo, DRG neurons from vehicle and MC38-injected mice showed similar levels of neuronal density (Fig. 9b). Thirty-seven hours after plating, vehicle and MC38 mouse neurons showed a similar number of active electrodes (>1 spike/min; Fig. 9c) but there was substantially less spontaneous activity and fewer active electrodes within the first ~9 h ex vivo (Fig. 9d–g and Supplementary Fig. 17a–c). At three hours ex vivo, spike amplitude was reduced in MC38 mouse neurons, with significantly fewer spikes at or above 10 µV in amplitude (Fig. 9h–j and Supplementary Fig. 17d). We then electrically evoked neuronal activity to assess the degree of adaptation/desensitization. Although there was a trend to reduced desensitization with repeated electrical stimulation of thoracolumbar and upper thoracic DRG neurons from MC38 mice, there was a main effect of MC38 tumor growth only when data from all neuronal levels were combined (Fig. 9k, l and Supplementary Fig. 17e). There was a trend to greater activity at lower voltages in a “ramp” stimulation paradigm, but this was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 17f). These data suggest that tumor growth causes electrical deficits in sensory neurons, some of which are acutely reversible once in culture (i.e., reduced spontaneous activity), and others that are more persistent (i.e., reduced desensitization). Such deficits in spike amplitude, frequency and homeostatic plasticity could potentially underlie the sensory/proprioceptive deficits associated with MC38 tumor growth.

Inflammation and nerve degeneration in nerves of rhesus macaques with CRC

To test the generalizability of our findings using the MC38 mouse model, we analyzed plasma lipids and cytokines, along with sciatic nerves of rhesus macaques with a diagnosis of CRC at necropsy, compared with those that did not receive a cancer diagnosis (Fig. 10a). Lipidomic analysis of plasma revealed profound differences in overall lipid composition, with numerous enriched and depleted species (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). We observed a pattern of widespread triglyceride depletion (Fig. 10b) and enrichment of sphingomyelins, cholesterol esters and ceramides (Fig. 10c), Acetylcarnitines (Fig. 10d), and PC/LPC and PE/LPE species (Fig. 10e and Supplementary Fig. 18c), broadly consistent with the lipid dysregulation observed in MC38 mice.

Analysis of plasma cytokine content revealed substantial, but variable elevations in cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, many of which we previously detected in MC38 mouse samples (e.g., BAFF, CCL2, EGF, FGF, GDF-15, GM-CSF, IFNγ)13 and/or have been associated with the development of CRC in humans (CRP50, CXCL1151, HGF52, Resistin53, Pentraxin-354, VEGF55). Importantly, one sample was collected from a subject harboring the MSH6-mis mutation, but without a CRC diagnosis. This sample more closely resembled healthy control samples from wild-type subjects, suggesting the inflammatory profile seen in CRC monkey plasma is more likely due to tumor growth than the mutation per se (Fig. 10f).

Sciatic nerve biopsies from non-cancer macaques showed few signs of inflammation or nerve damage (Fig. 10g). However, cellular infiltrates and axonal degeneration were readily detectable in biopsies from subjects with CRC (Fig. 10h and Supplementary Fig. 18d). This was reflected in a significant difference in the frequency and degree of inflammatory cell infiltrate (Fig. 10i) as well as degeneration (Fig. 10j). Accordingly, Büngner bands (regenerating nerve tracts formed by Schwann cells following axonal degeneration) were detected in 1/7 control samples, but 11/15 CRC samples (Fig. 10k). Sporadic Iba1 immunoreactivity was seen in control nerve sections (a marker of macrophages and other cells of histiocytic origin; Fig. 10l). There was an increased histiocytic infiltrate in nerve biopsies from macaques with CRC, (Fig. 10m), indicating that several of the key neurological abnormalities detected in MC38 tumor-bearing mice are recapitulated in spontaneous non-human primate cases of CRC. Collectively, these data suggest a working model wherein mice and non-human primates with CRC develop inflammation and dyslipidemia, which lead to the development of a form of inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, associated with impaired neuronal Ca2+ homeostasis (Fig. 10n).

Discussion

Increasing cancer survivorship inevitably places greater emphasis on long-term complications due to the lingering effects of cancer and its treatment. The widespread and debilitating neurologic complications in cancer survivors lack effective therapies, necessitating an improved understanding of somatosensory nervous system damage due to cancer. For example, paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes are regarded as an infrequent consequence of cancer-driven autoimmunity. Diagnoses of “true” paraneoplastic neuropathy in CRC are rare56, but prior studies have uncovered deficits in epidermal nerve fiber density and sensory acuity in small cohorts of CRC patients11,12. Because these were unanticipated findings, these studies were not designed to address causality. For example, type 2 diabetes and alcohol use disorder are potential confounds which cause neuropathy and elevate the risk of developing CRC57,58. Therefore, the objective of this study was to characterize CRC-driven neuropathy in preclinical models to address causality, and to identify potential causative factors. Our results using an orthotopic, immunocompetent mouse model and spontaneous CRC in rhesus macaques suggest there is indeed a causal link between CRC and development of sub-clinical neuropathy.

Consistent with our prior observations in male mice13, intraepidermal nerve fiber density was also reduced in females. This nerve fiber loss occurred despite there being no evidence of shared sensory nerve input to the hindpaw and GI tract, and the nerve fiber loss was also present in dermatomes that do not overlap with innervation of the GI tract, suggesting a systemic, rather than direct injury effect. However, there was only weak evidence, in males, of any increase in tactile sensitivity. This sensory neuropathy without overt hypersensitivity parallels non-painful neuropathy in CRC patients, where bump detection threshold in a pegboard task was impaired12. Interestingly, this study also identified warming and cooling detection deficits, which we did not detect in mice in the thermal gradient ring test. Other reports have associated elevated levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 with non-painful neuropathy, which we saw in sciatic nerves from tumor-bearing mice and was shown to be downregulated in patients with painful neuropathy59. In fact, the most common first manifestation of paraneoplastic and other neuropathies is sensory loss60, which may contribute to its underdiagnosis. Similarly, studies in diabetic neuropathy suggest that the loss of intra-epidermal nerve fibers is often detected in patients with non-painful neuropathy61.

The lack of robust, sustained hypersensitivity in MC38 tumor-bearing mice following naltrexone suggests that MC38 mice do not experience substantial latent pain sensitization. This contrasts with other forms of nerve injury, such as cisplatin-induced neuropathy, which shows rapid and robust naltrexone-induced reinstatement of pain in the remission phase15.

Because the mechanisms underlying different forms of neuropathy vary widely, the persistence of CRC neuropathy and the degree to which it transitions to painful neuropathy remain to be established. However, we did uncover sensorimotor disturbances in the gait analysis and beam walk tasks. The NSAID-induced attenuation of gait changes in the early phases of tumor growth suggests that inflammatory responses to the tumor are partially responsible for the early changes to gait patterns, but later-stage disruptions, more consistent with emergence of nerve damage indicators, persisted. Our prior study in this model did not detect significant loss of sensory acuity in plantar skin13, perhaps suggesting these locomotor deficits are driven by proprioceptor and/or motor neuron deficits, rather than overt sensory loss. The observed reduction in spike amplitude and spontaneous activity in acutely isolated sensory neurons hints at the reduced fidelity of action potential propagation. Interestingly, a prior report using a motor coordination task in CRC rats saw similar motor coordination deficits following chemotherapy62, which could provide a basis for the increased fall risk seen in cancer survivors, a risk that is independent of age or medication use63. It is also tempting to speculate that the reversal of neuronal activity changes after 9–12 h in culture means that these neurons are able to recover from the damage inflicted by CRC.

In search of factors underlying these neuropathy symptoms, we showed that circulating metabolic and hemostasis markers in MC38 mice remained largely normal, with the notable exception of fibrinogen. An increased clotting propensity is broadly consistent with CRC patient data64 and our prior transcriptomic analysis of sensory ganglia from MC38 tumor mice13. While the underlying mechanism (if any) that links hypercoagulability to CRC-induced neuropathy remains to be determined, it is conceivable that coagulation impairs endoneurial blood flow, causing a form of ischemic neuropathy20.

Neurodegenerative diseases such as multiple sclerosis are accompanied by dysregulation of plasma lipids29, and the same is true for plasma, tumor, and adipose tissue lipids in CRC and other malignancies26,28,65. Many cancers promote FA synthesis, elongation and desaturation to support their growth66. Accordingly, PC/LPC and PE were elevated MC38 mouse and CRC macaque plasma. We also detected enrichment of cholesterol esters, which has been reported in lung cancer65, and depletion of short-chain FAs and enrichment of long-chain FAs67, as well as an increase in PUFAs, alongside depletion of monounsaturated/unsaturated FAs68. The normalization of many of these disruptions to lipid composition by carprofen strongly suggests that tumor-driven inflammation underlies this dyslipidemia.

Paralleling the dyslipidemia in MC38 mouse plasma, we also detected enrichment of hexosylceramide, sphingomyelin, and LPC, alongside triglyceride depletion in sciatic nerves. Importantly, these disruptions mirror lipid dysregulation reported in other models of nerve injury and neurodegeneration69,70. Interestingly, PI species which were enriched in tumor-bearing mice can be anti-inflammatory71, hinting at the potentially complex interplay between tumor growth, inflammation, metabolic derangement and nerve injury responses. Nevertheless, attenuation of many of the pro-inflammatory transcriptional changes in cultured macrophages by lipid depletion of serum hints at the contribution of these changes to the inflammatory neuroimmune microenvironment in peripheral nerves. Considering the RyR dysfunction in the ER of MC38 mouse neurons, it is possible that cancer-induced ER stress72 either initiates or sustains this dyslipidemia, since activation of the unfolded protein response is known to lead to many of these derangements in lipid metabolism26.

Macrophage accumulation and increased NF200 expression are hallmarks of inflammatory neuropathy in other disease models17,73. Interestingly, neuropathy has been linked to inflammatory bowel disease74. Since chronic inflammatory conditions such as ulcerative colitis substantially increase CRC risk75, it is conceivable that many CRC patients could develop neuropathy years prior to a CRC diagnosis. Macrophages can adopt protective as well as pathological roles in peripheral neuropathies76, which led us to assess the phenotype and function of macrophages in this context. Despite the profound shift toward a pro-inflammatory, M1-like state, the worsening of symptoms following their ablation is consistent with a model where this or another subset of macrophages are a net constraint on neuronal dysfunction, similar to that reported in diabetic neuropathy77.

In addition to leukocyte infiltration and dysregulation of lipid metabolites, Schwann cell secretion of S100B and myelin decompaction are common symptoms in other neuropathies, e.g., following cisplatin chemotherapy78. The dysregulation of myelin across all calibers of myelinated axons suggests motor axons as well as sensory Aβ and Aδ fibers responsible for transducing innocuous touch and proprioceptive stimuli could all be impacted. Furthermore, prior studies suggest that swelling of unmyelinated axons occurs in response to injury in neuropathy79,80. It is possible all these signs of dysfunction are secondary to pro-inflammatory cytokine production or circulating dyslipidemia81. In either case, dysfunctional myelination likely reduces action potential velocity and amplitude82, leading to the observed motor coordination deficits.

Traditionally, paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes are typified by autoimmunity against the nervous system, caused by tumor-driven production of ‘onconeural’ antibodies83. Consequently, signs and symptoms of paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes resemble other autoimmune neurologic conditions, such as complex regional pain syndrome, multiple sclerosis and Guillain-Barré syndrome84. Paraneoplastic neuropathy is most commonly associated with lung cancer and lymphoma, but we detected IgG deposition in the sensory ganglia of MC38 tumor-bearing mice that is consistent with antibody production against neuronal antigens seen in other, more prototypical paraneoplastic neuropathies85. This may suggest a model where IgG deposition underlies at least some of the CRC-related inflammation and neuropathy, though further work is needed to understand the antigen(s) involved and the relative contribution of autoantibodies to CRC-driven nerve injury.

It is also plausible that the degree of inflammation is influenced by the mutational burden and therefore immunogenicity of different CRC cases. Notably, both MC38 cells and the rhesus macaques used in this study are models of mismatch repair-deficient/microsatellite instability-high CRC. This is due to mutations in Msh3 and Pold1 in MC38 cells, and MLH1 germline mutation or hypermethylation in rhesus macaques86,87. This leaves open the question of whether mismatch repair-proficient/microsatellite stable CRC causes neuropathy via similar mechanisms, though our prior observations using CT26 tumors in mice indicate that peripheral nerve damage, neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction develop in that context13.

Malik et al.56 report that symptomatic neuropathy is rare in CRC, but our findings add to the weight of evidence that the true incidence of neuropathy in CRC may be under-reported. The authors of this study also speculated that the paraneoplastic neuropathy most closely resembled chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP). Indeed, macrophage infiltration of nerves, phagocytosis of myelin and autoantibody deposition are all diagnostic of CIDP88. Corticosteroids are often effective in CIDP, although more preclinical research needs to be done, especially when weighing the risks versus benefits of potential immunosuppression in patients being treated for CRC.

There are several limitations of our study which should be acknowledged. The bidirectional links between inflammation and lipid composition make attributing causality exclusively to cytokines or other peptide factors versus lipid species incredibly challenging. A plethora of pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators are themselves lipids or lipid metabolites89, which complicates interpretation. The detection of autoantibody deposition in the peripheral nervous system is indicative of damage, but without a comprehensive survey of antigen specificity, it will be difficult to establish whether these antibodies are truly “onconeural” or secondary to neuronal injury, as others have described38. More targeted ablation of pro- versus anti-inflammatory macrophages may yield additional insights into the contribution of macrophage phenotype to disease progression. A more extensive survey of the heterogeneous sensory nerve subtypes and their degree of susceptibility to neuropathy would also be informative. Finally, the growing appreciation of the links between CRC, gut dysbiosis, and lipid metabolite profile90,91 as well as their relationship to neuropathy92 should also be addressed. Finally, a lack of matched skin/tumor biopsies or behavioral data in CRC macaques limits our ability to assess the extent of the mechanistic overlap across mice and primates. Clearly, thorough exploration of the relationship between mutational burden, neoantigen expression, and neuropathy is warranted in both species.

Together, our findings suggest a multi-hit model where tumor-driven inflammation causes dyslipidemia and metabolic stress, with the resulting injury triggering macrophage infiltration and autoantibody production, which is associated with Schwann cell and sensory neuron dysfunction, manifesting in the observed sensorimotor deficits. Our data also raise the prospect that CRC-driven neuropathy represents an underappreciated risk factor for development of neurologic issues in CRC survivors. It is possible that subacute neuropathy in CRC could interact with chemotherapy-induced neurotoxicity or immune-related toxicities associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors93, though our understanding of the links between immunotherapies and chronic neuropathic pain is still in its infancy and further work is required. Nevertheless, pre-existing neuropathy is an established risk factor for development of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy7. The latent neuropathy associated with CRC could therefore represent a ‘priming’ event94 in some individuals, particularly in the susceptible long nerves of the hands and feet95. If this prior insult impaired the ability of already-damaged axons to recover from neurotoxic chemotherapy, it may increase the risk of developing more persistent neurosensory symptoms. Future studies should address any potential relationship between CRC-induced neuropathy and the development and resolution of neuropathy in response to subsequent insults, with the goal of understanding and mitigating these debilitating symptoms for cancer survivors.

Methods

Animals

All experiments and procedures involving the use of live mice were approved by MD Anderson Cancer Center Animal Care and Use Committee (Houston, TX, USA, protocol number 00001967-RN02), in accordance with the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All mice (Mus musculus) used in this study entered into an experiment between 8 and 12 weeks of age. Male and female C57BL/6J (Strain #: 000664), C57BL/6-Tg(Csf1r-EGFP-NGFR/FKBP1A/TNFRSF6)2Bck/J (Macrophage Fas-Induced Apoptosis (MaFIA); strain #: 005070), and B6.129S2-Ighmtm1Cgn/J (muMt; strain #: 002288) were sourced from Jackson Laboratories. MaFIA and muMt mouse lines are both on the C57BL/6J background. Other than keeping the male/female ratio in experimental groups approximately equal, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups and housed in the MD Anderson Cancer Center’s Research Animal Support Facility. Mice were housed up to five per individually-ventilated cage on a 12:12 light:dark cycle, switching at on at 06:00 and off at 18:00. Ambient temperature was maintained between 18 and 23 °C and 40–60% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to food and water. AP20187 was administered to MaFIA mice to deplete peripheral macrophages as has been reported17. Three daily injections of AP20187 (2 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle (PBS + 10% v/v PEG-400 + 1.7% v/v Tween-80) were delivered at the experimental timepoints indicated.

A closed breeding colony of Indian-origin rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) bred at the MD Anderson Cancer Center Michale E. Keeling Center for Comparative Medicine and Research since 1989 spontaneously develops CRC with microsatellite instability and mismatch repair deficiency. The colony is specific pathogen free for Herpes B, Simian retroviruses and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. A subset of these animals (approximately 5%87) harbor a pathogenic germline mutation in MLH1 (c.1029 C < G, p.Tyr343Ter)86,87. Animals in the colony that died or were euthanized for meeting clinical endpoints were submitted for complete diagnostic necropsy including gross examination and histopathology. Retrospective review of pathology records identified subjects diagnosed with CRC at necropsy and controls with no CRC from which archived formalin fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sciatic nerve was available. For histology, 18 cases of CRC (n = 14 females, 4 males, average age 21.3 ± 2.9 years) were compared against nine non-CRC controls (n = 8 females, 1 male, average age 16.7 ± 5.8 years). Archived frozen plasma was available from five healthy control animals without history of gastrointestinal disease (n = 4 females, 1 male, average age 20.3 ± 5.9 years) and nine with CRC confirmed at necropsy (n = 5 females, 4 males, average age 21.3 ± 7.6 years). This sex distribution is broadly representative of the colony as a whole (~80% female). Exclusion criteria were age <5 years (pre-sexual maturity), diagnosis of other systemic inflammatory conditions (e.g., sepsis, rheumatoid arthritis), seropositivity for Trypanosoma cruzi, and autolysis of tissues at necropsy.

Cell culture

The MC38 colon adenocarcinoma cell line was obtained from Alstem Cell Advancements (MC38-Luc-RFP; RRID: CVCL_B7NQ). Cells were maintained in 10 cm TC dishes at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in high glucose DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM glutamine, 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids, 10 mM HEPES, and 1x penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were used within 10–18 passages.

Orthotopic MC38 Model and Bioluminescent imaging

Orthotopic MC38 tumors were implanted via transanal rectal injection13. Briefly, 2 × 105 MC38 cells in 50 µl growth medium were transanally injected into the rectal submucosa under isoflurane anesthesia. Approximately 95% of injections resulted in successful engraftment. Mice without detectable bioluminescent signal two weeks post-injection were excluded from analysis. Unless otherwise stated, experiments were carried out on mice three weeks after vehicle/MC38 cell injection, since this was when statistically significant peripheral neuropathy first becomes detectable13. Tumor growth was monitored weekly for up to 4 weeks by bioluminescent imaging using an IVIS Lumina XR (PerkinElmer)13. Briefly, mice were injected with D-luciferin (Goldbio; 150 mg/kg, i.p. in PBS) and imaged 10 min post-injection using auto-exposure settings. Luminescence was quantified within a circular ROI overlying the colorectal region and expressed as average radiance (p/s/cm2/sr) using Living Image 4.5.2 software (PerkinElmer).

Peripheral nerve tracing and DRG whole mount imaging

Hindpaw and colorectal innervation were traced using Wheat Germ Agglutinin, Alexa Fluor 488 and 594 conjugates (Cat.# W11261, Cat.#W11262), adapting a previously described approach96. Ten microliters total of WGA 594 (1 mg/ml) was injected bilaterally into the colon rectally and 5 µl of WGA 488 (1 mg/ml) was injected into each paw. Bilateral DRGs were collected 7 days after injection from 2 mice. DRGs were collected into 4% PFA for post-fixation overnight. After 24 h, tissues were transferred into 30% sucrose. The following day tissues were mounted using FluorSave and allowed to dry at 4 °C for 2 days. Images were captured using a Nikon Ti2 confocal microscope at ×10 magnification.

Behavioral assays

Mice were habituated to all behavior testing equipment 24 hours prior to beginning testing. Testing was split during the light cycle, and carried out during the same time of day, either between 07:00 h and 11:00 h, or between 13:00 h and 16:00 h.

Von Frey

Hindpaw mechanical sensitivity was determined using von Frey filaments in the “up-down” method13,97. Fifty percent paw withdrawal thresholds were calculated from the series of withdrawal responses. Naltrexone hydrochloride (Millipore Sigma) was dissolved in sterile PBS and injected subcutaneously into the nape of the neck at 3 mg/kg.

Thermal gradient ring

To detect any potential changes in temperature preference/sensitivity, mice were tested using a Thermal Gradient Ring device (Ugo Basile) attached to a PC running ANY-maze 7.65 tracking software (Stoelting)18. Each mouse was allowed to freely explore the circular arena of the device, the floor of which was set to a temperature gradient, ranging from 15 to 40 °C. Mice were placed into the device at an innocuous temperature zone (approximately 23–27 °C). After 15 min exploration, a 15-min recording period began, where the dwell time in each pair of twelve temperature zones (each with a plane angle of 15°/ 0.26 rad) was recorded using the device’s top-down camera. For quantification, these 12 temperature zones were binned into four quartiles: Q1 (zones ranging from 15 to 19 °C), Q2 (19 to 25 °C), Q3 (25 to 32 °C) and Q4 (32 to 40 °C).

Burrowing assay

Burrowing activity is a pain-depressed behavior in mice that can be reversed by known analgesics, as we previously described14. Briefly, 30 g of new bedding (¼” corn cob bedding) and 5 g of home cage bedding was placed in a 50 mm (diameter) × 125 mm (length), close-ended tube made from translucent red acrylic. Mice were placed in a cage individually and the bedding displaced from this tube after 15 min was calculated.

Catwalk gait analysis

Footfall patterns and gait were analyzed using the Catwalk XT device and version 10.2 software (Noldus)16. Gait indices were averaged across both hindpaws for four compliant runs (duration between 0.5 s and 5 s, maximum variation in run speed ≤60%) for each mouse at each timepoint and are depicted as percent change relative to baseline.

Beam Walk

Mice were trained on three consecutive days to cross a 12 mm-wide rectangular beam (“wide flat”), followed by a 6 mm-wide rectangular beam (“narrow flat”) and a 6 mm-diameter cylindrical beam (“round”)98. All three beams are 85 cm in length and placed at an approximate 15° incline. The time taken to cross, and the number of errors (paw slips, inversions) were determined by investigators blinded to treatment. Mice that fell from the beam or failed to complete the task within 60 s were assigned the cutoff time of 60 s.

Blood glucose

Blood glucose was measured using a Precision Xtra glucometer (Abbott) to measure 4 h fasting blood glucose from the lateral tail vein, as described previously99.

Carprofen treatment

Carprofen was added to standard reverse osmosis drinking water at a concentration of 0.067 mg/ml and maintained as the sole source of drinking water, from the day of MC38 injection until sacrifice four weeks later, with the bottle switched out biweekly. This solution was shown to be stable for at least 7 days, and the dosing regimen was shown to achieve carprofen levels comparable to conventional bolus dosing after 12 h of access to carprofen drinking water, without evidence of toxicity100,101.

Tail bleed assay

A tail bleed experiment was performed to test the coagulation ability in tumor bearing mice102. Briefly, tail veins of isoflurane-anesthetized mice were transected and placed in tubes containing warm saline to allow the bleeding time to be visualized and recorded. The weight of the tubes was recorded before and after bleeding to calculate the volume of blood lost.

Coagulation and blood chemistry panels

Mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose and blood was collected via cardiac puncture. For coagulation measures, blood was mixed at a 9:1 blood: 3.2% (w/v) sodium citrate ratio. Plasma was then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 15 min and immediately snap frozen. The resulting plasma samples were analyzed using an STA Satellite automated coagulation analyzer (Stago). For blood chemistry panel analysis, blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h. The blood was then centrifuged for 10 min at 1300 × g at 4 °C. Resultant serum samples were then analyzed on a Roche Cobas Integra 400 Plus clinical chemistry analyzer.

Gut permeability assay

Mice were fasted overnight and dosed p.o. with 0.15 mL of 80 mg/mL FITC dextran (4 kDa). After 4 h blood was collected via cardiac puncture. Samples were then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. Plasma was then diluted in 1x PBS (1:10) and transferred to a black, opaque-bottom 96-well plate. Fluorescence was read on a BioTek Synergy HTX plate reader at ex: 485/em: 530 nm.

Fecal occult blood test

Fecal matter and blood sample positive control samples analyzed in a luminescent assay for detection of occult bleeding103. Briefly, samples were homogenized with 1 mL of 1X RBC Lysis Buffer (eBioscience) and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 2 min at room temperature. A 6% (w/v) solution of Luminol Blend for Forensic Detection of Blood (Innovating Science™ IS5040) was prepared in Milli-Q water. Using an opaque 96-well white plate, 5 μL of each sample were plated in triplicate. To each well, 100 μL of luminol solution was added before luminescence was read in a Bio-Tek Synergy plate reader. Luminescence values were background subtracted and expressed as fold-increase vs. negative control (luminol only).

Blood/fecal lipocalin-2 ELISA

Blood and fecal samples were collected from mice 1- and 4-weeks post MC38 injection. Samples were stored and diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Lipocalin-2 ELISA assay was performed with no modifications from the manufacturers protocol (Cat.# ELM-Lipocalin2-1).

Histology

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane until reflex activity was lost, followed by thoracotomy and transcardial perfusion with 1x PBS followed by 10% neutral buffered formalin. Sciatic nerve and colon were processed in an automated processor with an overnight routine protocol (dehydration to 100% ethanol, followed by xylene and paraffin) to produce FFPE blocks. After sectioning FFPE blocks at 5 µm, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin in a HistoCore SPECTRA ST (Leica Biosystems). Slides were deparaffinized through xylene and ethanol before incubating in hematoxylin for 22 min, followed by washes and eosin for 1 min 45 s, dehydration through an ethanol series and xylene before mounting. Stained slides were digitized in an Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems) at ×20 magnification. FFPE sections of rhesus sciatic nerves were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Images were semi-quantitatively scored for inflammatory cell infiltrates and axonal degeneration by a board-certified veterinary pathologist who was blinded to group identity.

Mouse serum cytokine array

Mouse serum (0.15 ml) was added to Mouse Cytokine XL Proteome Profiler arrays (R&D Systems ARY028) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Chemiluminescent signal intensities were quantified using an Amersham ImageQuant 800 blot scanner and ImageQuant TL toolbox v8.2.0 software (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences) as described previously104.

Chronic constriction injury

Chronic Constriction Injury (CCI) was performed on isoflurane-anesthetized mice by making a 10 mm incision in the skin overlying the left biceps femoris. The biceps femoris was carefully separated to expose the sciatic nerve beneath. The sciatic nerve was lifted away from surrounding muscles using sterile plastic pipette tips (200 µL) and 3, 7–0 chromic gut suture (Ethicon 1745 G) was loosely tied around all three branches of the sciatic nerve about 1 mm away from each other, proximal to the trifurcation of the nerve. A single nylon suture was used to close the overlying muscle and wound clips were applied to close the skin incision.

Immunohistochemistry and fluorescence microscopy

Dorsal root ganglia, sciatic and intercostal nerves, spinal cord, 3 mm hindpaw plantar punch biopsies and ear skin were collected following isoflurane anesthesia (until reflex activity was lost), followed by thoracotomy and transcardial perfusion with 1x PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffer (PB), pH 7.413. Minimum 2-3 biological replicates were measured from at least two different cohorts of mice for all tissues. Plantar tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight before being transferred into 15% sucrose and 10% EDTA in PBS for 48 h for decalcification of bones105. Sciatic nerves and DRG were incubated in 15% sucrose in PBS without EDTA. After further overnight incubation in 30% sucrose, 40 µm lateral plantar, 20 µm sciatic, and 10 µm DRG sections were collected onto microscope slides using a Leica CM3050S cryostat. Immunohistochemistry was then performed13. After three 5-minute washes with 0.1 M PB, slides were blocked and permeabilized with blocking buffer (10% goat serum, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PB) for 1 h at 4 °C. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer (rabbit anti-PGP9.5/Uchl1, 1:250, Abcam ab108986, clone EPR4118; rat anti-mouse CD68, 1:500, Bio-Rad/AbD Serotec MCA1957GA, clone FA-11, lot 158250; rabbit anti-S100B, 1:100, Abcam ab227914, clone CL2720; rabbit anti-CD206, 1:500, Cell Signaling 24595, clone E6T5J, lot 5; rabbit polyclonal anti-Iba1, 1:500, Fujifilm/Wako 019-19741). Negative controls were included for each antibody wherein primary antibodies were omitted. The following day, samples were washed 3 times in blocking buffer and then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (goat anti-rat IgG-Alexa Fluor 488, Thermo Fisher A-11008; goat anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 594, Thermo Fisher A-11012; goat anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 647, Thermo Fisher A-21245) at 1:500 in blocking buffer and 1 µg/ml DAPI for 4 h, protected from light at 4 °C.

IgG staining followed the same procedure, but used donkey serum in the blocking buffer in place of goat serum, blocked tissues overnight with blocking buffer and stained with a polyclonal goat anti-mouse F(ab’)2 fragment (1:100; Jackson ImmunoResearch 115-005-072, lot 143001) and rabbit polyclonal anti-mouse NF200 (1:100; Sigma N4142, lot 508946), which were detected using 1:500 donkey anti-goat IgG-Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher A-11055) and 1:500 donkey anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher A-31572), respectively. Following secondary incubation, slides were washed 3 times in 0.1 M PB, mounted with Fluorsave, coverslipped, sealed with nail polish and stored at −20 °C. Images were captured using a Nikon Ti2 confocal microscope at ×20 magnification. Images are a composite of 10 focal planes in a 40 µm z-stack at 2 µm increments. 3–5 technical replicates were randomly acquired from each biological replicate (n = 4–8 for PGP9.5, n = 3–7 for S100B, n = 3–7 for NF200/IgG, n = 3 for muMt, n = 3–10 for CD68, n = 3–4 for CD206. Following image acquisition, density of staining including IENFs was determined using Nikon NIS-Elements Imaging Software13. Signal density was calculated by the application of ROIs guided by DAPI staining to identify borders between cutaneous/neuronal/meningeal structures.

Immunohistochemistry of rhesus sciatic nerves for Iba1 was conducted on 4 µm sections of FFPE tissue adhered to positively charged slides. Slides were immersed in 95˚C Target Retrieval Solution (Dako) for 20 min, followed by a gradual cooling step. Slides were then immersed and rinsed in wash buffer. The next steps were followed sequentially by a rinse with wash buffer and performed on the Fisher Autostainer: block slides for endogenous peroxidase activity using dual enzyme block (Dako), Background Sniper (Biocare), Fc Receptor Block (Innovex), primary antibody (rabbit anti-Iba1, 1:300, Abcam ab178680, clone EPR6136(2)) for 30 min, secondary polymer (Envison+, DAKO), 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate for 10 min (DAKO) and counter stained for 5 min with automated hematoxylin (DAKO). Slides were then dehydrated through alcohol gradients and xylene before being coverslipped.

Blood-nerve barrier permeability was assessed following intravenous injection of sodium fluorescein (20 mg/kg)106. One hour after injection, sciatic nerves were collected and 5 µm transverse sections were imaged on the Olympus System Microscope Model BX53.

Transmission electron microscopy

Sciatic and intercostal nerve samples were fixed using 3% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.3). Samples were then washed in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer and treated with 0.1% Millipore-filtered cacodylate buffered tannic acid, post-fixed with 1% buffered osmium and stained en bloc with 0.1% Millipore-filtered uranyl acetate. Samples were dehydrated through an ethanol series and infiltrated and embedded in LX-112 medium. Samples were then polymerized in a 60 °C oven for approximately three days. Ultrathin sections were cut using a Leica Ultracut microtome and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate in a Leica EM Stainer. Stained samples were examined in a JEM 1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL USA) using an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Digital images were obtained using an AMT imaging system (Advanced Microscopy Techniques). Three to five randomly chosen image fields were acquired from each biological replicate (n = 7–8 biological replicates for sciatic nerve, n = 4–5 for intercostal nerve). Morphometric analysis of cross-sectional diameter/area was conducted using NIH ImageJ 1.54 h.

Schwann cell culture and serum exposure

ScienCell Mouse Schwann Cells (MSC #M1700-57) were maintained in T75 flasks at 37 °C and 5% CO2, according to manufacturer’s recommendations. Following 24 h in Schwann Cell Medium (SCM, Cat. #1701) in a 6-well plate, FBS was replaced with 25% serum from vehicle control or MC38 mice (collected at 3 weeks post-tumor injection). Cell pellets were isolated for bulk sequencing 6 h later and snap frozen as described in the Methods for bone marrow-deried macrophage bulk RNA sequencing.

Lipidomics

Plasma and sciatic nerve samples for lipidomic analysis were snap-frozen on dry ice. Tissues from two mice were pooled together for each technical replicate. After isoflurane anesthesia (until reflex activity was lost) and thoracotomy, mouse blood was collected via cardiac puncture, collected in Precellys tubes and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Rhesus macaque blood was collected into tubes containing K2EDTA and plasma was processed according to a circulating cell-free DNA isolation protocol, which entails an initial 10 min centrifugation at ~1400 × g followed by 10 min at ~16,000 × g at 4 °C. Macaque plasma was frozen at −80 °C until analysis. Twenty microliters of plasma was used for untargeted lipidomics analysis.

Plasma samples were extracted by adding 200 µl of extraction buffer (ice-cold ethanol containing 1% 10 mM butylated hydroxytoluene, and 2% Avanti SPLASH® LIPIDOMIX® Mass Spec Standard). Samples were vortexed for 10 min, placed on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were then collected for LC-MS analysis. Nerves were pulverized and then homogenized in ethanol, vortexed for 5 min, placed on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were evaporated to dryness under nitrogen and 200 µL of extraction buffer was added to reconstitute. Mobile phase A (MPA) was 40:60 acetonitrile: water with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. Mobile phase B (MPB) was 90:9:1 isopropanol: acetonitrile: water with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. The chromatographic method included a Thermo Fisher Scientific Accucore C30 column (2.6 µm, 150 × 2.1 mm) maintained at 40 °C, a mobile phase flow rate of 0.200 ml/min, an autosampler tray chilling at 8 °C, and a gradient elution program as follows: 0–3 min, 30% MPB; 3–13 min, 30–43% MPB; 13.1–33 min, 50–70% MPB; 33–48 min, 70–99% MPB; 48–55 min, 99% MPB; 55.1–60 min, 30% MPB. The injection volume was 10 µL. A Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer with heated electrospray ionization source was operated in data dependent acquisition mode, in both positive and negative ionization modes, with scan ranges of 150–1500 m/z. An Orbitrap resolution of 240,000 (FWHM) was used for MS1 acquisition and spray voltages of 3600 and −2900 V were used for positive and negative ionization modes, respectively. Vaporizer and ion transfer tube temperatures were set at 275 °C and 300 °C, respectively. The sheath, auxiliary and sweep gas pressures were 35, 10, and 0 (arbitrary units), respectively. For MS2 and MS3 fragmentation a hybridized HCD/CID approach was used. Data were analyzed using Thermo Scientific LipidSearch software (version 5.1) and R scripts written in-house. The peak area (raw relative abundance) of each metabolite was normalized by dividing by the tissue weight of the respective sample followed by z-score normalization.

Serum lipid depletion

Cleanascite™ Lipid Removal Reagent and Clarification (Cat. #: X2555-10) was used to deplete lipids from MC38 and vehicle mouse serum. Depletion was validated using total cholesterol (Cat. #: EEA026) and triglycerides (Cat. #: EEA028) ELISAs according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Cleanascite (or PBS for lipid-intact control samples) was added to serum at a 1:2 ratio, mixed thoroughly and placed on a shaker for 10 min at 200 rpm at room temperature. Samples were then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at room temperature. Serum supernatants were then applied to cell cultures for 6 h, prior to isolation for bulk RNA sequencing as described in the “Materials and methods”.

DRG neuron culture

DRG neurons were isolated from adult mice following isoflurane anesthesia until reflex activity was lost, followed by decapitation107. Ganglia from cervical and upper thoracic levels (~C6-T8) were cultured separately from thoracolumbar (T9-L6/S1) levels. Isolated ganglia were enzymatically digested for 30 min in 1.8 U/ml Liberase DH before plating onto laminin-coated glass coverslips or multi-electrode array plates. To remove excessive debris for multi-electrode array plating, cell suspensions were centrifuged through a 12.5% and 28% Percoll density gradient before resuspension in culture media, as previously described13. Cells were cultured in a 1:1 ratio of TNB media: protein-lipid complex, and DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h before use in calcium imaging experiments, and for 1 h before use in multi-electrode array recordings.

Calcium imaging

Calcium imaging of mouse DRG neurons was carried out as previously described13,108, with minor modifications. Dissociated neurons were plated onto laminin-coated 12 mm circular glass coverslips. At 16–24 h in vitro, individual coverslips were incubated with 2 µM Fura-2 AM (Invitrogen) at room temperature for 20 min. Fura-2 dye was dissolved in HEPES-buffered HBSS (“HH buffer”), containing (in mM): 140 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1.3 CaCl2, 0.4 MgSO4, 0.5 MgCl2, 0.4 KH2PO4, 0.6 NaHPO4, 3 NaHCO3, 10 glucose, 10 HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH and 310 mOsm with sucrose. HH buffer containing only nominal calcium was created by omitting CaCl2. Coverslips were then mounted into an MS-512SP recording chamber (ALA Scientific) on the stage of an inverted Nikon Ti2 microscope. Coverslips were continuously superfused with HH buffer at a flow rate of 1–2 mL/min. Fura-2 fluorescence was alternately excited at 340 and 380 nm (2 nm band-pass filter, 100 ms exposure) at 1 Hz using a Lambda LS Xenon light source and controller (Sutter Instruments). Fluorescence emitted at 510 nm was captured using a 10x/NA 0.5 objective and sCMOS pco.edge camera. Nikon NIS Elements 5.30.05 software was used to calculate the F340:F380 ratio for each neuron. After switching to HH buffer without CaCl2, 2 µM thapsigargin was superfused for 5 min before returning to Ca2+-containing buffer, followed by a terminal pulse of 50 mM KCl (“K50”) to identify viable neurons. The threshold for a Ca2+ response during KCl application was defined by a peak F340:F380 ratio >0.1 over baseline. In select experiments, DRG neurons were incubated with 10 µM S107 from plating until recording 16–24 h later.

Multi-electrode array

Dissociated cervico-thoracic and thoracolumbar DRG neurons were plated in 10 µl droplets (~104 neurons) onto three poly-L-ornithine/laminin-coated wells of a 24-well CytoView MEA plate13. After 1 h to allow cell attachment, each well was filled with 0.75 ml of culture media and the plate placed into a Maestro Edge MEA device (Axion BioSystems) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Real-time field potential recordings were acquired for a period of 15 min every 2 hs (12.5 kHz sampling rate, 200–3000 Hz band-pass per electrode across 16 electrodes per well). Spike number and firing rate were calculated using AxIS Navigator 3.12.7 software and Neural Metrics Tool 4.1.5 (Axion BioSystems). Evoked activity was delivered at 24 h in vitro via four of the sixteen electrodes in each well using ten stimulations at 10-s intervals. Biphasic stimulation was delivered at 0.3 and −0.3 V (each 0.75 ms; 1.5 ms total), followed by stimulation artifact elimination (2 ms discharge at 1 µA, soft switch at 1 ms; 3 ms total). Alternatively, a ramp protocol of evoked activity was delivered at 24 h in vitro via four of the sixteen electrodes in each well using ten stimulations at 10-second intervals. Biphasic stimulation was delivered at ±3, 10, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210, 240, 270, and 300 mV, with a 3-s interval between each stimulation (each phase of the stimulation = 0.75 ms; 1.5 ms total), followed by stimulation artifact elimination (2 ms discharge at 1 µA, soft switch at 1 ms; 3 ms total). In all recordings, spikes were defined as voltages >5.5x the standard deviation of the noise for each individual electrode. Coincident artifacts (0.24 ms window) and spikes <2 ms post-stimulation were excluded from analysis using Axion Neural Metrics Tool. Spike waveforms were exported from raw traces using Axion Data Export Tool.

Immunoprecipitation and western blotting

RyR was immunoprecipitated from DRG lysates with 2 µg anti-RyR (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-376507) in 0.5 mL of modified RIPA buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl,1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 1 mM Na3VO4, and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Cell Signaling 5871S)] for 4 h at 4 °C. Immune complexes were incubated with Protein G Sepharose® 4 Fast Flow (GE Healthcare 17-0618-01) overnight at 4 °C, and the beads were washed three times with RIPA buffer. The immunoprecipitates were size-fractionated on 4–20% SDS-PAGE gels (for RyR) and 15% (for Calstabin) and transferred onto PVDF membranes for 2.5 h at 200 mA. Immunoblots were developed using mouse anti-RyR (clone F-1, 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-376507) and mouse anti-FKBP12/Calstabin (clone H-5, 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-133067). To determine RyR oxidation, the carbonyl groups in the protein side chains were derivatized to 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone (DNP-hydrazone) by reaction with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine. DNP signal was determined using a specific mouse anti-DNP antibody (clone 9H8.1, 1:1000, Millipore MAB2223). All immunoblots were developed using the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher 34577) or IRDye-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat anti-mouse IgG-IRDye 680LT, Li-Cor 926-68020, 1:10,000) and detected using a Li-Cor Odyssey system and Li-Cor Acquisition Software 2.4. The bait protein RyR acted as an internal control. Relative band intensity was quantified using NIH ImageJ. Uncropped, unprocessed scans of western blots are included in the Source Data file.

Cell exposure to mouse serum and bulk RNA sequencing

Mouse Bone Marrow Derived Macrophages (mBMDMs; iXCells Biotechnologies 10MU-030) were maintained in T75 flasks according to manufacturer’s recommendations. Following 24 h in Macrophage Culture Medium (MD0097) in a 6-well plate, FBS was replaced with 25% serum from vehicle control or MC38 mice (collected at 3 weeks post-tumor injection). Cell pellets were isolated for bulk sequencing 6 h later and snap frozen. Cell pellets were shipped on dry ice and the bulk RNA sequencing (mRNA library preparation) was performed by Singulomics (Bronx, NY). After ensuring the samples met or exceeded the proper quality control criteria (Agilent 2100 RNA integrity number for all samples >7), Raw reads in FASTQ format were analyzed using the FastQC tool for quality control109. The STAR package was used for the reference genome mapping with the mouse genome (mm10)110. The gene counts were estimated from uniquely mapped reads using the feature Counts program from the Subread tool111. Further, the gene counts were normalized and differentially expressed genes (adjusted p-value < 0.05, >0.5 Log2 fold-change) between the control and MC38 mouse samples were calculated using the DEseq2 program112. The pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Enrichr tool with default parameters.

Cytokine quantification

Cytokine content of mouse sciatic nerve lysates was determined semi-quantitatively using a Proteome Profiler Mouse XL Cytokine Array (R&D Systems ARY028) according to the manufacturer’s instructions13 in conjunction with an ImageQuant 800 western blot imaging system and ImageQuant TL 11 analysis software. For plasma TGF-β1, blood was collected from mice via cardiac puncture. Platelet-free plasma was isolated and TGF-β1 concentrations were determined using a commercially available Quantikine mouse TGF-β1 ELISA (R&D Systems DY-1679) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytokine content of rhesus macaque plasma was analyzed using the human-specific version of the Cytokine XL Proteome Profiler Array kit (R&D Systems ARY022B). Only those factors where humans and macaques share ≥90% amino acid sequence identity (92/105 factors) were analyzed.

Statistics

Von Frey and Catwalk data were compared between groups and across time using two-or three-way, repeated measures ANOVA with Šidák’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Beam walk data were analyzed using aligned rank transformed one-way ANOVA or paired T-tests where appropriate, all other behavioral tests used unpaired T-tests. Mixed-effects analyses were used in place of two-way ANOVA when datapoints were missing. Immunohistochemistry- and Western blot densitometry, ELISAs, hematological assays, spike amplitudes and endpoint fluorescence were compared using unpaired Student’s T-test. Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze calcium imaging and rhesus macaque histology data. Gaussian best-fit distributions and all statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad).
