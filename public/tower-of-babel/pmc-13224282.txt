Spontaneous spinal subdural hematoma: Comparative analysis of clinical features, time course, and outcomes with epidural hematoma
Authors: Yuma Hiratsuka, Yasufumi Ohtake, Makoto Senoo, Hirohiko Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Spontaneous spinal subdural hematoma is rarer and less characterized than epidural hematoma, and comparative data on clinical features and management are limited.Methods: We retrospectively reviewed cases of spontaneous spinal subdural and epidural hematoma diagnosed between 1995 and 2023 at two tertiary hospitals. Neurological status was graded using the American Spinal Injury Association impairment scale for both groups.Results: Five subdural and 45 epidural cases were included. Headache occurred in 60% of subdural cases and in none of the epidural cases. Admission blood pressure was lower in the subdural group; time to admission was longer in the subdural group, as was time to diagnosis. Hematoma length was similar (median four vertebral levels) for both populations, but spinal canal occupancy rates were lower in the subdural group. Notably, conservative management was used in 80% of subdural cases versus 46.7% of epidural cases. Interestingly, neurological recovery was comparably favorable in both groups.Conclusion: Subdural and epidural spinal hematomas show distinct presentations and time courses. Headache, delayed presentation, and lower spinal canal occupancy ratios were characteristic of subdural hematomas. Notably, conservative management was appropriate for 80% of spontaneous spinal subdural hematoma but just 46.7% of spontaneous spinal epidural hematoma patients with mild deficits.

INTRODUCTION

Spontaneous spinal subdural hematoma (SSSH) and spontaneous spinal epidural hematoma (SSEH) are rare neurological emergencies requiring prompt diagnosis and conservative versus surgical treatment.[4,9] SSEH, defined as blood collection in the epidural space without trauma or iatrogenic cause,[2] has been relatively well studied with an estimated incidence of 0.1/100,000/year.[8] Alternatively, SSSH, characterized by blood accumulation in the subdural space, accounts for approximately 4.1% of all spinal hematomas[3] and remains poorly characterized due to its rarity. This study aimed to clarify the clinical features, time course, and outcomes of SSSH versus SSEH.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We identified 5 SSSH and 45 SSEH patients radiologically through a systematic review of medical records, 1995–2023; those with a history of significant trauma or iatrogenic causes were excluded. The study was approved by the institutional review board. Neurological status was assessed using the American Spinal Injury Association impairment scale (AIS) at admission and final follow-up. Pain characteristics were evaluated based on location. All patients underwent spinal MRI, with recording of hematoma location, extent, and spinal canal occupancy ratios (maximum hematoma area divided by spinal canal area [Figure 1]). Treatment was classified as conservative or surgical. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with an interquartile range. Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test were used for comparisons using JMP version 18 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA); P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Pain was the predominant symptom in both groups; headache was the most common pain location in SSSH but was absent in SSEH patients. However, SSEH patients most frequently reported neck pain. Other findings included neurogenic bladder in 40% of SSSH and 42.2% of SSEH patients, and only SSEH patients exhibited spinal shock (8.9%). Symptom progression was markedly slower in SSSH patients with a median time from onset to admission of 24 h in SSSH versus 2 h in SSEH patients. The median time to diagnosis averaged 101 versus 4 h, respectively (SSSH vs. SSEH). Admission blood pressure was lower in the SSSH group. SSSH hematomas involved the thoracic (40%), cervicothoracic, thoracolumbar, and cervical regions (20% each), while SSEH predominantly involved the cervical spine. An average of 4 vertebral levels exhibited hematomas in both groups, while the spinal canal occupancy ratio was lower in SSSH vs. SSEH patients. On admission, 80% of SSSH patients were AIS D or E versus 44.4% in SSEH. Surgery was performed in 1/5 SSSH and 24/45 SSEH patients. At final follow-up, all SSSH patients achieved AIS D or E, while SSEH patients achieved AIS E [Tables 2 and 3]. One SSEH patient died due to an acute myocardial infarction, unrelated to the hematoma.

DISCUSSION

This study identified three distinguishing features of SSSH relative to SSEH: a higher incidence of headache, more gradual symptom onset, and lower spinal canal occupancy ratio. Headache occurred in 60% of SSSH patients and was absent in SSEH. The higher prevalence compared to previously reported rates (18%) may reflect referral bias.[1] The slower progression in SSSH likely reflects anatomical differences between the two spaces. The epidural space is compartmentalized by meningovertebral ligaments and contains fatty tissue and venous plexus, which tend to confine hematoma spread and increase compression. In contrast, the subdural space allows greater cranio-caudal spread while maintaining lower occupancy ratios. The lower occupancy ratio in SSSH (36.5% vs. 51.6%) likely contributes to milder initial deficits and a longer diagnostic window, which can extend from a few hours to 3 weeks.[6]Conservative management was employed in 80% of SSSH patients, contrasting with a 72% surgical rate reported previously.[1] Most conservatively managed patients achieved favorable outcomes.[5,7] The optimal treatment strategy should be based on neurological severity, rate of progression, and hematoma characteristics. CONCLUSION

SSSH is distinguished from SSEH by headache as a presenting symptom, more gradual onset, and less extensive spinal canal compression. Notably, more SSSH patients (80%) could be managed conservatively versus SSEH patients (46.7%).
