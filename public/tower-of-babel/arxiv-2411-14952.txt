1.
Introduction

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero. If
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, then
there are many vanishing results known on the Lie algebra cohomology. For example, by Whitehead’s first and second lemma we have

H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},M)=H^{2}(\mathfrak{g},M)=0
for every finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
. It is natural, to study the cohomology also for

perfect
Lie algebras, i.e., for Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]=\mathfrak{g}
. It turns out that the Whitehead lemmas
are no longer true for perfect Lie algebras. So the study of the first and second cohomology groups is much
more interesting in this case.

The most important
\mathfrak{g}
-modules for the coefficients of the cohomology of
\mathfrak{g}
are the trivial module and the adjoint module.
We are interested here in particular in the adjoint module. One reason is a conjecture by Pirashvili
[
11
]
, stating that
a perfect complex Lie algebra is semisimple if and only if the adjoint cohomology vanishes, i.e., if it satisfies

H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})=0
for all
n\geq 0
. One direction is easy to see, namely that the adjoint cohomology vanishes for
complex semisimple (and hence perfect) Lie algebras. However the converse direction is still open. Many authors have constructed
perfect non-semisimple Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying

H^{0}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})=H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})=H^{2}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})=0,

see
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. But so far, nobody found a perfect non-semisimple Lie algebra with vanishing adjoint cohomology,
or could prove that such a Lie algebra does not exist.

On the other hand, the adjoint cohomology of perfect Lie algebras is interesting in general, and not so much is known about it.
We began a study in
[
7
]
, which we will continue here. There are some explicit results in the literature for
particular families, namely for the perfect non-semisimple Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{m}
, where
V_{m}
is the
irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
of dimension
m+1
with
m\geq 1
.
Here the cohomology groups
H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
have been determined for
k=0,1,2
, see
[
12
,
13
]
and the references therein.

In this paper we will extend these results, and compute the adjoint cohomology in various other cases.
In particular, for odd
m=2n-1
, we show in Proposition
3.20
that

H^{3}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{3}\rfloor},\;H^{4}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})\cong\mathbb{C}.

Here we use a long exact sequence in cohomology and compute certain spaces determined by the
multiplicities
N(j,k,n)
of
V_{jk-2n}
in the exterior product
\Lambda^{j}(V_{j+k-1})
for
j\leq 4
.
The numbers
N(j,k,n)
are given by the coefficient of
q^{n}
in the Gaussian
q
-polynomial
(1-q)\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{j+k}{k}_{q}
.
We have
N(j,k,n)=p(j,k,n)-p(j,k,n-1)
, where
p(j,k,n)
is the number of partitions of
n
into at most
k
parts
with largest part at most
j
. We are able to compute some of these multiplicities, so that we can
apply the Hochschild-Serre formula to obtain a result on the adjoint cohomology of
\mathfrak{g}
.
For
m=1,2,3,5,7
we can even compute the total adjoint cohomology of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{m}
.

Finally, we give a classification of complex perfect Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
of dimension
n\leq 9
and
explicitly compute the cohomology spaces
H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
with
k=0,1,2
for all Lie algebras from the classification list.

2.
Perfect Lie algebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra. Although many results also hold for arbitrary fields of characteristic zero,
it is sufficient for our main results to consider only complex Lie algebras.
We denote by
{\rm Der}(\mathfrak{g})
the Lie algebra of derivations of
\mathfrak{g}
, and by
{\rm ad}(\mathfrak{g})
the ideal of inner derivations in
{\rm Der}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Furthermore, we denote by
Z(\mathfrak{g})
the center of
\mathfrak{g}
, by
\rad(\mathfrak{g})
the solvable radical
of
\mathfrak{g}
, and by
\mathop{\rm nil}(\mathfrak{g})
the nilradical of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is called
perfect
, if it satisfies
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]=\mathfrak{g}
.

Note that a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is perfect if and only if
H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathbb{C})=0
for the trivial
\mathfrak{g}
-module
\mathbb{C}
.
It is known that the solvable radical of a perfect Lie algebra is nilpotent, see for example Lemma
2.4
in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a perfect Lie algebra. Then we have
\rad(\mathfrak{g})=\mathop{\rm nil}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Of course every semisimple Lie algebra is perfect, but the converse need not be true. We can use the Levi decomposition
of a Lie algebra to decide whether or not it is perfect. The following result is well known.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes\rad(\mathfrak{g})
be a Levi decomposition of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with solvable radical
\mathfrak{r}=\rad(\mathfrak{g})
. Consider

V=\mathfrak{r}/[\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{r}]
as an
\mathfrak{s}
-module. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is perfect if and only if
V
does not contain the trivial

1
-dimensional
\mathfrak{s}
-module.

Using this result we can construct perfect non-semisimple Lie algebras as follows.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{s}
be a semisimple Lie algebra and
V
be a representation of
\mathfrak{s}
not
containing the
1
-dimensional trivial representation, and define a bracket
on
\mathfrak{s}\times V
by

[(X,v),(Y,u)]:=([X,Y],Xu-Yv).

This turns the vector space
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\dotplus V
into a perfect Lie algebra, the semidirect
product
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
.

Note that
Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
for these Lie algebras, and that
\rad(\mathfrak{g})=V
is abelian. Consider the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
with basis
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
and Lie brackets given by

[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{3},\,[e_{1},e_{3}]=-2e_{1},\,[e_{2},e_{3}]=2e_{2}.

Let
V_{m}
be the irreducible representation of dimension
m+1
with basis
(e_{4},\ldots,e_{m+4})
.

Example 2.5
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{m}
with
m\geq 1
is a non-semisimple
perfect Lie algebra of dimension
m+4
, with basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m+4})
.

It is clear that
\mathfrak{g}
has a nonzero solvable radical, so that it is not semisimple. Also,
it has a trivial center. However, note that a perfect Lie algebra may have a non-trivial center in general.
For
m=1
the Lie brackets for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{1}
are given by

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{2}]

\displaystyle=e_{3},

\displaystyle[e_{2},e_{3}]

\displaystyle=2e_{2},

\displaystyle[e_{3},e_{5}]

\displaystyle=-e_{5}.

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{3}]

\displaystyle=-2e_{1},

\displaystyle[e_{2},e_{4}]

\displaystyle=e_{5},

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{5}]

\displaystyle=e_{4},

\displaystyle[e_{3},e_{4}]

\displaystyle=e_{4},

3.
Adjoint Lie algebra cohomology

In this section we want to obtain results on the adjoint cohomology spaces
H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
with
k\geq 0
for
the perfect Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{m}
with
m\geq 1
. For
k\leq 1
this is well known.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{m}
. Then we have, for all
m\geq 1
,

\displaystyle H^{0}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

For
k=0
we have
H^{0}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})=Z(\mathfrak{g})
for all Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
. Hence the claim follows from
Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
for

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\ltimes V_{m}
. The result for
k=1
is a special case of a general result, see Proposition

3.3
in
[
7
]
. Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
, where
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple and
V
is an
\mathfrak{s}
-module. Then we have

H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})\cong H^{1}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(V,V).

In particular, if
V
is simple, we have
\dim H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})=1
.
∎

We recall the following from
[
7
]
. Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
, where
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple and
V
is an
\mathfrak{s}
-module.
Consider
V
as an abelian Lie algebra. Then we have a short exact sequence of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules

0\rightarrow V\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}/V\rightarrow 0.

This is also a short exact sequence of
V
-modules by restriction to
V\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
. Here
V
and
\mathfrak{g}/V
are trivial
modules. This yields a long exact sequence in cohomology, because the functor of
\mathfrak{s}
-invariants is exact on finite-dimensional
modules.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
, where
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple and
V
is an
\mathfrak{s}
-module. Then we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\to H^{0}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{0}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{0}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\to H^{1}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{1}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{1}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\to H^{2}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{2}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{2}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\to H^{3}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{3}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}\to H^{3}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\to\cdots

The sequence continues until
k>\dim V
, in which case we have

H^{k}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}=H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}=H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}=0.

For each
k\geq 0
the following holds, see
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
, where
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple and
V
is an
\mathfrak{s}
-module. Then we have

\displaystyle H^{k}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(\Lambda^{k}(V),V),

\displaystyle H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(\Lambda^{k}(V),\mathfrak{s}).

The Hochschild-Serre formula from
[
10
]
yields the following result, see
[
7
]
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
with
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
,
V=V_{m}
and
k\geq 0
. Then we have

\displaystyle H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{i+j=k}H^{i}(\mathfrak{s},\mathbb{C})\otimes H^{j}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}\oplus H^{k-3}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}.

In particular we have

\displaystyle H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle\cong H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}\text{ for }k=0,1,2.

The idea for computing the cohomology spaces
H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
is as follows. First we determine
the spaces
H^{k}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}
and
H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}
in Lemma
3.3
by computing the multiplicities
of the irreducible
\mathfrak{s}
-modules
V
and
\mathfrak{s}
in the exterior product
\Lambda^{k}(V)
. Then we try to obtain
information on the spaces
H^{k}(V,\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{s}}
from the long exact sequence in cohomology in Lemma
3.2
.
In the last step we use Proposition
3.4
to conclude a result on
H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
.

Let
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Because of
\Lambda^{1}(V)\cong V
we have

\displaystyle H^{1}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(V,V)\cong\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle H^{1}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(V,\mathfrak{s})\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{C},\text{ if }V\cong V_{2}=\mathfrak{s}\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

by Schur’s Lemma. For
\Lambda^{2}(V)
we have the following result.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
V_{m}
denote the simple
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module of dimension
m+1
. Then the decomposition of the

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-modules
\Lambda^{2}(V_{2n-1})
and
\Lambda^{2}(V_{2n})
into simple modules is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}(V_{2n-1})

\displaystyle\cong V_{0}\oplus V_{4}\oplus V_{8}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{4n-4},

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}(V_{2n})

\displaystyle\cong V_{2}\oplus V_{6}\oplus V_{10}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{4n-2}.

Proof.

Let
V_{1}
denote the natural
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module. Then we have
V_{m}\cong{\rm Sym}^{m}(V_{1})
, and
by
[
8
]
, Exercises
11.35
and
11.31
,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}({\rm Sym}^{m}(V_{1}))

\displaystyle\cong{\rm Sym}^{2}({\rm Sym}^{m-1}(V_{1})

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}{\rm Sym}^{2(m-1)-4k}(V_{1}).

For
m=2n-1
and
m=2n
we obtain the claim.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V
with
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
V=V_{m}
. Then we have

\displaystyle H^{2}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{C},\text{ if }m\equiv 2\bmod 4\\
0,\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

\displaystyle H^{2}(V,\mathfrak{g}/V)^{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{C},\text{ if }m\equiv 0\bmod 2\\
0,\text{ if }m\equiv 1\bmod 2\end{cases}

Proof.

If
m=2n-1
is odd, then

H^{2}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(V_{0}\oplus V_{4}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{4n-4},V_{2n-1})=0.

For
m=2n
we have

H^{2}(V,V)^{\mathfrak{s}}\cong\Hom_{\mathfrak{s}}(V_{2}\oplus V_{6}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{4n-2},V_{2n}),

which is zero for even
n
, and one-dimensional for odd
n
. The second claim follows similarly.
∎

In general, there is a formula for the multiplicities of a simple
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module
V_{\ell}
within

{\rm Sym}^{m}(V_{n})
. It was discovered by Cayley in a letter to Sylvester around
1855
, but only
proved in
1878
by Sylvester. We use the reference
[
9
]
, where this formula is
given in Theorem
3.1
of Section
3
, and is called
explicit plethysm
.
Note that the determinant factor there equals one in our case. Furthermore, we have
V_{k}\cong{\rm Sym}(V_{1})^{k}
,
and
{\rm Sym}^{j}(V_{k})\simeq\Lambda^{j}(V_{j+k-1})
. Recall that the Gaussian polynomial

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{j+k}{k}_{q}
is defined by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{j+k}{k}_{q}=\frac{(1-q^{j+k})(1-q^{j+k-1})\cdots(1-q^{j+1})}{(1-q)(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{k})}.

Theorem 3.7
.

There is an isomorphism of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-modules

\Lambda^{j}(V_{j+k-1})\simeq\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\lfloor\frac{jk}{2}\rfloor}(V_{jk-2n})^{\oplus N(j,k,n)},

where
N(j,k,n)
is the coefficient of
q^{n}
in the Gaussian polynomial
(1-q)\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{j+k}{k}_{q}
.
We have

N(j,k,n)=p(j,k,n)-p(j,k,n-1),

where
p(k,j,n)
is the number of partitions of
n
into at most
k
parts, where the largest part is at most
j
.

For
j=3
we obtain the following explicit result.

Proposition 3.8
.

The multiplicity of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module
V_{2}
in

\Lambda^{3}(V_{k+2})\simeq\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\lfloor\frac{3k}{2}\rfloor}(V_{3k-2n})^{\oplus N(3,k,n)}

is zero, if
k
odd. For all even
k\geq 2
we obtain

N\left(3,k,\frac{3k-2}{2}\right)=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $k\equiv 2\bmod 4$}\\
0,\text{ if $k\equiv 0\bmod 4$}\end{cases}

Proof.

The multiplicity is zero, in case there is no nonnegative integer
n
with
3k-2n=2
. If
k
is odd,
then there is no such
n
.
Hence let
k
be even. Assume first that
k=4r
. Then, by Theorem
1.3
, Formula
(1.8)

in
[
9
]
, and since
N(j,k,n)=N(k,j,n)
, we have

\displaystyle N\left(3,k,\frac{3k-2}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=N(4r,3,6r-1)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{6r-1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{6r-2}{3}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{2r-2}{2}\right\rfloor-1

\displaystyle=(3r-1)-(2r-1)-(r-1)-1

\displaystyle=0.

A similar computation for
k=4r+2
gives

\displaystyle N\left(3,k,\frac{3k-2}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=N(4r+2,3,6r+2)

\displaystyle=(3r+1)-2r-(r-1)-1

\displaystyle=1.

∎

.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\ltimes V

for

We also study the case

in Theorem

in the exterior product
\Lambda^{4}(V_{k+3})
for all

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
\ell,k\geq 1
. Then the multiplicity of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module
V_{2\ell}
in
\Lambda^{4}(V_{k+3})
is given by

N(4,k,2k-\ell)=c_{2k-\ell}-c_{2k-2\ell+1},

where the

are the coefficients in the power series

C(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x^{2})(1-x^{3})(1-x^{4})}=\sum_{i\geq 0}c_{i}x^{i}.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.7

\Lambda^{4}(V_{k+3})\simeq\bigoplus_{n=0}^{2k}(V_{4k-2n})^{\oplus N(4,k,n)}.

For

, i.e., for

we obtain the summand
(V_{2\ell})^{\oplus N(4,k,2k-\ell)}
. We have

N(4,k,2k-\ell)=p(4,k,2k-\ell)-p(4,k,2k-\ell-1).

Note that we have the identity

p(j,k,n)=p(j,k-1,n)+p(j-1,k,n-k),

see Equation
(3.2.6)
in the proof of Theorem
3.1
in
[
1
]
. This yields

p(4,k,2k-\ell-1)=p(4,k-1,2k-\ell-1)+p(3,k,k-\ell-1).

The number
p(4,k,2k-\ell)
counts the partitions into maximal
k
parts of size at most
4
.
Here
p(4,k-1,2k-\ell-1)
counts those partitions among them, whose smallest part is equal to one. Hence

p(4,k,2k-\ell)=p(4,k-1,2k-\ell-1)+P(2k-\ell),

where
P(2k-\ell)
is the number of partitions of
k-\ell-1
with parts in
\{2,3,4\}
, so not having a part
equal to one. Such a partition automatically cannot have more than
k
parts. Together we obtain

N(4,k,2k-\ell)=P(2k-\ell)-p(3,k,k-\ell-1).

The coefficients
c_{m}
in
C(x)
equal
P(m)
, because the parts are in
\{2,3,4\}
. This yields
P(2k-\ell)=c_{2k-\ell}
.
It remains to show that
p(3,k,k-\ell-1)=c_{2k-2\ell+1}
. It is clear that
p(3,k,k-\ell-1)
is the coefficient
d_{m}
in

D(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-x^{2})(1-x^{3})}=\sum_{m\geq 0}d_{m}x^{m}

for
m=k-\ell-1
. Because

\frac{C(x)-C(-x)}{2}=\frac{x^{3}}{(1-x^{2})(1-x^{4})(1-x^{6})}

is the series with only odd coefficients
c_{2m+1}
, we see that

\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{2m+3}x^{m}=\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-x^{2})(1-x^{3})}=\sum_{m\geq 0}d_{m}x^{m}.

In particular,
d_{m}=c_{2m+3}
. For
m=k-\ell-1
this gives

p(3,k,k-\ell-1)=d_{k-\ell-1}=c_{2(k-\ell-1)+3}=c_{2k-2\ell+1}.

∎

For
\ell=1
we have
c_{2k-\ell}-c_{2k-2\ell+1}=0
. This yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.14
.

The adjoint
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-module
V_{2}
does not occur in
\Lambda^{4}(V_{k+3})
for all

N(4,k,2k-1)=p(4,k,2k-1)-p(4,k,2k-2)=0.

This result is remarkable. It is also surprising that these partition numbers for
2k-1
and
2k-2
are equal.
