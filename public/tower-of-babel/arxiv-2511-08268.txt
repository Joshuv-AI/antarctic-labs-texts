Exact-factorization framework for electron-nuclear dynamics in electromagnetic fields
Vladimir U. Nazarov, E. K. U. Gross

Abstract
The Exact Factorization (EF) theory aims at the separation of the nuclear and electronic degrees of freedom in the many-body (MB) quantum mechanical problem. Being formally equivalent to the solution of the MB Schrödinger equation, EF sets up a strategy for the construction of efficient approximations in the theory of the correlated electronic-nuclear motion. Here we extend the EF formalism to incorporate the case of a system under the action of an electromagnetic field. An important interplay between the physical magnetic and the Berry-curvature fields is revealed and discussed within the fully non-adiabatic theory. In particular, it is a known property of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation that, for a neutral atom in a uniform magnetic field, the latter is compensated by the Berry-curvature field in the nuclear equation of motion ( Yin and Mead 1992 ). From an intuitive argument that the atom must not be deflected by the Lorentz force from a straight line trajectory, it has been conjectured that the same compensation should occur within the EF theory as well. We give a rigorous proof of this property.



I
Introduction: THE CONCEPT OF EXACT FORCES ON THE NUCLEI

Consider a system of
N_{e}
electrons and
N_{n}
nuclei. In the absence of external fields its propagation is described by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\hat{H}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})\Psi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t),

(1)

where
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}=({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{2},\dots{\boldsymbol{r}}_{N_{e}})
and
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}=({\boldsymbol{R}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{R}}_{2},\dots{\boldsymbol{R}}_{N_{n}})
represent the sets of electronic and nuclear position vectors. The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
contains the kinetic energies of electrons and nuclei and the mutual Coulomb interactions between all particles of the system:

\hat{H}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})\!=\!\hat{T}_{n}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})\!+\!\hat{H}_{nn}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})\!+\!\hat{T}_{e}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}})\!+\!\hat{H}_{ee}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}})\!+\!\hat{H}_{en}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})

(2)

with

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{n}=\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}\nabla_{I}^{2}}{2M_{I}},

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{e}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N_{e}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}\nabla_{i}^{2}}{2m},

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{nn}=\sum\limits_{I>J=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{e^{2}Z_{I}Z_{J}}{|{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{J}|},

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{ee}=\sum\limits_{i>j=1}^{N_{e}}\frac{e^{2}}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{r}}_{j}|},

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{ne}=-\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\frac{e^{2}Z_{I}}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}|}.

(7)

Here
M_{I}
and
eZ_{I}
denote the mass and charge of the
I
-th nucleus,
m
and
-e
being the mass and charge of electron. Let us now focus on one single nucleus with index
K
and ask what the force on this particular nucleus is. The answer is clear: Following the steps first formulated by Ehrenfest in the early days of quantum mechanics
Ehrenfest 1927
, we determine the momentum expectation value of this particular nucleus from the full electron-nuclear wave function
\Psi(t)
, i.e. the solution of Eq. (
1
):

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{K}(t)=\langle\Psi(t)|-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}|\Psi(t)\rangle_{{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}}.

(8)

Next we evaluate the time derivative of the momentum expectation value (
8
) yielding

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{P}}_{K}(t)}{dt}=\langle\Psi(t)|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{K}|\Psi(t)\rangle_{{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}}.

(9)

The resulting force operator
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{K}
obviously consists of the sum of all bare Coulomb forces exerted on the
K
-th nucleus by the electrons and by all other nuclei, i.e.

\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{K}=-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}(\hat{H}_{ne}+\hat{H}_{nn}).

(10)

While mathematically doubtlessly correct, Eq. (
10
) is not a very useful way of writing the force on a given nucleus because it still involves the electronic degrees of freedom explicitly. Moreover, Eq. (
10
) obscures the fact that, for most systems, the low-lying modes of the nuclear subsystem are collective vibrations implying that the forces should be of harmonic nature. Usually, when approximately evaluating the force on a given nucleus, we take the derivative of a Born-Oppenheimer (BO) surface. In this way, the electronic degrees of freedom are already taken care of but we make an approximation, the BO approximation. This raises the question, can one define an exact force on the
K
-th nucleus with the electronic degrees of freedom integrated out, but without making the BO or any other approximation? This is indeed possible with an approach known as the exact factorization
Abedi et al. 2010
;
Abedi et al. 2012
(EF) where the solution of Eq. (
1
) is written in the form of a product

\Psi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t).

(11)

The physical meaning of the two factors becomes clear when looking at the absolute square of Eq. (
11
). The left-hand side,
|\Psi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)|^{2}
,
is the joint probability density of finding the nuclei at
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}
and the electrons at
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}
(at time t).
Knowing that any joint probability can be written as a product of a marginal probability and a conditional probability,
|\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)|^{2}
is the (marginal) probability density of finding the nuclei at positions
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}
, while
|\Psi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|^{2}
is the conditional probability density of finding the electrons at
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}
, given the nuclei are at
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}
. To be interpretable as a conditional probability, the electronic factor must satisfy the partial normalization condition

\int d^{3N_{e}}r|\Psi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|^{2}=1,\text{ for each }{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}.

(12)

The equations of motion for the two wave functions
\chi
and
\Phi
can be deduced either from
the McLachlan variational principle
McLachlan 1964
, or by direct insertion of (
11
) into the Schrödinger equation (
1
) and making use of Eq. (
12
).
\chi
and
\Phi
are unique up to within a phase factor
\exp[i\theta({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)]
, where
\theta({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
is real: Clearly the transformation

\begin{split}&\tilde{\chi}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\exp[i\theta({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)]\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t),\\
&\tilde{\Phi}_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)=\exp[-i\theta({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)]\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t),\end{split}

(13)

leaves the full wave function (
11
) unchanged, while not affecting the partial normalization (
12
). This freedom corresponds to a gauge transformation of the equations of motion
Abedi et al. 2012
. The equation of motion for
\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
is a standard
N_{n}
-body Schrödinger equation which contains a time-dependent scalar potential given by

\begin{split}&\epsilon({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\langle\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|\hat{H}^{BO}-i\hbar\partial_{t}|\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}+\\
&\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\left\{\langle\hbar\nabla_{I}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}(t)|\hbar\nabla_{I}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}(t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}-{\boldsymbol{A}}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)^{2}\right\}/2M_{I},\end{split}

(14)

as well as a vector potential that has the form of a Berry connection:

{\boldsymbol{A}}_{K}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\langle\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}|\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}.

(15)

The EF allows one to deduce an alternative representation of the exact force on nucleus
K
: One first inserts Eq. (
11
) into Eq. (
8
), yielding:

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{K}(t)=\langle\chi(t)|-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}+{\boldsymbol{A}}_{K}(t)|\chi(t)\rangle_{{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}}.

(16)

This representation of the exact momentum expectation value is then used to evaluate the rate of change of the momentum
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{K}(t)
:

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{P}}_{K}(t)}{dt}=\langle\chi(t)|\hat{\mathbfcal{F}}_{K}|\chi(t)\rangle_{{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}}.

(17)

This equation allows one to identify the force operator as follows
Li et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbfcal{F}}_{K}=\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{K}+\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{K},

(18)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{K}=\hat{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{K}+\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{K}\times\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{K}

(19)

with the electric-like and magnetic-like forces

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{K}=\partial_{t}\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{K}-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}\epsilon,

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{K}=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}\times\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{K}.

(21)

and the velocity operator

\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{K}=(-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}+\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{K})/M_{K}.

(22)

Finally, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) is an internuclear Lorentz-like force,
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{K}
, which acts on the
K
-th nucleus but involves the velocities of only the other nuclei:

\hat{D}_{G_{K}}=\sum\limits_{J\neq K,G^{\prime}_{J}}\left(\partial_{G^{\prime}_{J}}A_{G_{K}}-\partial_{G_{K}}A_{G^{\prime}_{J}}\right)\hat{v}_{G^{\prime}_{J}}

(23)

with
G_{I}\in(X_{I},Y_{I},Z_{I})
. There is no classical analogue of (
23
). We emphasize that Eqs. (
18
-
23
) are the
exact
forces on the nuclei. Neither have we made a classical approximation
Agostini et al. 2014
, nor did we invoke the adiabatic approximation. A very interesting aspect is the appearance of the Lorentz-like forces in (
19
) and (
23
). These forces have to be distinguished from their BO counterparts. The latter are associated with the adiabatic approximation

\Psi^{adiab}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\chi^{adiab}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}^{BO}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}),

(24)

where a single BO state is multiplied with a nuclear wave packet
\chi^{adiab}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
.
If the latter is determined by plugging Eq. (
24
)
in the McLachlan variational principle, the variationally best nuclear wave function
\chi^{adiab}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
satisfies a Schrödinger equation which also contains a Berry-connection-type vector potential. The latter is associated with a single BO state:

{\boldsymbol{A}}_{K}^{adiab}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\langle\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}^{BO}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{K}}|\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}^{BO}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}.

(25)

The vector potential (
25
) can be very different from the exact vector potential (
15
). This is known from the fact that the geometric phases associated with (
15
) and (
25
) are generally different
Min et al. 2014
;
Requist et al. 2016
;
Requist et al. 2017
. In other words, the adiabatic approximation (
25
) is not necessarily a good approximation for the exact vector potential (
15
).

In this article we will investigate the role of the vector potential (
15
) in the presence of an external electromagnetic field. A particularly interesting situation appears when the Berry
curvature, i.e. the magnetic-field-like object associated with the vector potential (
15
) competes with a genuine external magnetic field. The purpose of this article is two-fold: First we generalize the EF formalism to include systems exposed to an arbitrary time-dependent external electromagnetic field. This is a valid goal in its own right, as it extends the power of the EF formalism to the realm of magnetic phenomena. Second, the combined effect of the external and the Berry-curvature magnetic fields is shown to be of great consequence:
We prove that, for an eigenstate of a neutral atom, the two fields compensate each other exactly in the nuclear Schrödinger equation,
ensuring the atom’s free motion in the uniform magnetic field.
The corresponding property has been known before to hold in BO dynamics
Yin and Mead 1992
;
Peters et al. 2022
and, on the basis of intuitive arguments, it had been anticipated to hold in the exact theory as well
Qin et al. 2012
.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
we present the EF formalism extended to include arbitrary electromagnetic fields.
Section
III
is devoted to the proof of the cancellation between the physical and the Berry-curvature magnetic fields in the case of a single neutral atom.
In Sec.
IV
, we illustrate the theory using a simple analytically solvable model of the Harmonium atom.
In Sec.
V
we consider similarities and distinctions arising in the case of molecules. Conclusions are collected in Sec.
VI
.
Appendix
A
contains details of the derivation of the equations of motion.
Appendix
B
addresses specifics of the BO approximation.
In Appendix
C
, by an explicit counterexample, we show that the exact compensation is a property of eigenstates rather than of arbitrary wave-packets.

II
Exact factorization formalism in the presence of external electromagnetic fields

We are dealing with an interacting system of
N_{n}
nuclei and
N_{e}
electrons exposed to an electromagnetic field and described by the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{n}(t)+\hat{H}_{nn}+\hat{H}_{e}(t)+\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ne},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n}(t)=\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{1}{2M_{I}}\left[-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}-\frac{Z_{I}e}{c}{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I},t)\right]^{2},

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{e}(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left[-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}+\frac{e}{c}{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i},t)\right]^{2}.

(28)

In Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
),
{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}},t)
is the vector potential associated with an arbitrary external electromagnetic field (generally time-dependent), and
c
is the velocity of light (We choose the Weyl gauge (
\phi({\boldsymbol{r}},t)=0
)).
Writing the total wave-function in the factorized form as in Eq. (
11
), the equations of motion for the two factors
\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
and
\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)

read as follows (a detailed derivation can be found in Appendix
A
)

\begin{split}i\hbar\partial_{t}\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)&\!=\!\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\!\frac{1}{2M_{I}}\!\left[-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\!-\!\frac{Z_{I}e}{c}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{tot}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\right]^{2}\!\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\\
&+\epsilon({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t),\end{split}

(29)

\begin{split}&i\hbar\partial_{t}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\!=\!\left[\hat{H}^{BO}(t)-\epsilon({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\!\right]\!\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\\
&+\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{1}{2M_{I}}\left[i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}+\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\right]^{2}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\\
&+\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{1}{M_{I}}\left[\frac{i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)}{\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)}+\frac{Z_{I}e}{c}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{tot}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I},t)\right]\\
&\cdot\left[i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}+\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\right]\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t).\end{split}

(30)

In Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
),

\hat{H}^{BO}(t)=\hat{H}_{e}(t)+\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ne}+\hat{H}_{nn},

(31)

the total vector potential
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{tot}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
consists of the external
{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I},t)
and the Berry-connection
\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
parts

{\boldsymbol{A}}^{tot}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)={\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I},t)-\frac{c}{Z_{I}e}\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t),

(32)

where
\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
is defined as

\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\langle\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|-i\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}|\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},

(33)

and

\epsilon({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\!=\!\langle\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|\hat{H}_{BO}(t)\!-\!i\hbar\partial_{t}|\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}\!+\!Q({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t),

(34)

where
Q({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
is given by

\begin{split}&Q({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)=\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{1}{2M_{I}}\times\\
&\left[\langle\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|\hbar\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}\!-\!\mathbfcal{A}_{I}^{2}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\right].\end{split}

(35)

We note that: (I)
{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}},t)=\nabla\Theta({\boldsymbol{r}},t)

in the absence of an external magnetic field, when Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
) reduce, obviously, to their non-magnetic analogues

Abedi et al. 2010
;
(II) If the electromagnetic field is present, it enters the nuclear equation of motion (
29
) through
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{tot}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
of Eq. (
32
),
the latter comprised of both the external and the Berry-connection vector potentials;
(III) In the electronic equation of motion (
30
),
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{tot}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
and
\mathbfcal{A}_{I}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)

II.1
Nuclear density and current density

The power of EF lies largely in its providing a way to evaluate averages of some of the operators related to nuclei only, such as nuclear density and current density, with the use of the nuclear wave-function
\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)
only. Since, so far, this property has been known to hold for systems without external field, below we prove it anew with the inclusion of the latter.

The density operator of the
I
-the nucleus is

\hat{N}_{I}({\boldsymbol{R}})=\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}}).

(36)

Therefore, the average density reads

N_{I}({\boldsymbol{R}})=\langle\Psi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})|\Psi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}}.

(37)

Making the substitution (
11
), integrating over electron coordinates
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}
, and using the condition (
12
), we have

N_{I}({\boldsymbol{R}})=\langle\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)|\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})|\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\rangle_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}.

(38)

Furthermore, the operator of the current-density of the
I
-th nucleus is

\begin{split}\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}_{I}({\boldsymbol{R}})&=-\frac{i\hbar}{2M_{I}}\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})+\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\right]\\
&-\frac{Z_{I}e}{M_{I}c}{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I},t)\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}}).\end{split}

(39)

Therefore,

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{J}_{I}({\boldsymbol{R}})&=-\frac{i\hbar}{2M_{I}}\langle\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})+\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}|\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle\\
&-\frac{Z_{I}e}{M_{I}c}\langle\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)|{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I},t)\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})|\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle_{{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}}{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}},\end{split}

(40)

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{J}_{I}({\boldsymbol{R}})&=\frac{i\hbar}{2M_{I}}\langle\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)]+\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}}\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)]|\delta({\boldsymbol{R}}_{I}-{\boldsymbol{R}})|\chi({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}},t)\Phi_{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}},t)\rangle\\

IV.1
Harmonium atom in magnetic field

The eigenvalue problem for the separated relative Hamiltonian of the two-body problem in uniform magnetic field reads
Herold et al. 1981

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{rel}\phi_{\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}})&=\left[\frac{\mathbf{K}^{2}}{2M_{c}}+\frac{e}{cM_{c}}(\mathbf{K}\times{\boldsymbol{B}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\right.\\
&\left.+\frac{e}{2c}\left(\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{M}\right){\boldsymbol{B}}\cdot({\boldsymbol{r}}\times\hat{\mathbf{p}})+\frac{e^{2}}{8\mu c^{2}}({\boldsymbol{B}}\times{\boldsymbol{r}})^{2}+V({\boldsymbol{r}})\right]\phi_{\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=E_{\mathbf{K}}\phi_{\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}}),\end{split}

(78)

where
\mu=Mm/M_{c}
is the reduced mass.
In the case of the harmonic inter-particle potential

V({\boldsymbol{r}})=\frac{1}{2}\mu\omega_{0}^{2}{\boldsymbol{r}}^{2}

(79)

this problem admits a solution
Herold et al. 1981

\phi_{\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=\exp\left(i\frac{M-m}{2\hbar M_{c}}\alpha\mathbf{K}_{\perp}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}\right)\phi_{0\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}}-\alpha{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}),

(80)

where

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}=-c\mathbf{K}\times{\boldsymbol{B}}/(eB^{2}),

(81)

\displaystyle\alpha=\left(1+\frac{Mmc^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}}{e^{2}B^{2}}\right)^{-1},

(82)

and
\phi_{0\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})
is the solution of the harmonic oscillator problem

\begin{split}&\left\{\frac{K_{z}^{2}}{2M_{c}}+\frac{1}{2}\mu\omega_{0}^{2}z^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x^{\prime}}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{y^{\prime}}^{2}}\right)\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{ie\hbar}{2c}\left(\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{M}\right)B(x^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\prime}}-y^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}})+\left(\frac{e^{2}}{8\mu c^{2}}B^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu\omega_{0}^{2}\right)({x^{\prime}}^{2}+{y^{\prime}}^{2})+\frac{K_{\perp}^{2}}{2M_{c}}(1-\alpha)\right\}\phi_{0\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})=E_{\mathbf{K}}\phi_{0\mathbf{K}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}).\end{split}

(83)

Although the analytical eigenfunctions of the problem (
83
) are known, we do not need them explicitly.

V
Implications for molecular systems

Let us now turn to a neutral system with more than one nucleus in an external uniform magnetic field. The separation of the c.m. motion is equally possible in this case
Avron et al. 1978
. Applying the formalism of EF, we can try to develop a method similar to that of Sec.
III
.
Obviously, we will encounter a situation fundamentally different from that of the case of one nucleus : the
N_{n}
-body nuclear wave-function obeys the Schrödinger equation with non-trivial, and usually very complicated,
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{tot}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})
and
\epsilon({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}})
.

Nevertheless, we can proceed as follows.
Considering that, within the EF formalism, the division of particles of a system into two classes is arbitrary, we can single out one nucleus and ascribe it to the first class, while all the other particles, nuclei and electrons, are referred to the second class.
Then, with the same analysis as in Sec.
III
, we come to the conclusion that
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{tot}({\boldsymbol{R}})
and
\epsilon({\boldsymbol{R}})
, where
{\boldsymbol{R}}
is the coordinate of the selected particle, are constant. The latter suggests this particle’s free motion.

This conclusion may seem paradoxical, considering that it applies to any selected particle in the system. The resolution of the paradox lies in the fact that we have integrated out coordinates of all the particles other than the selected one, and the remaining
\chi({\boldsymbol{R}})
obeys, indeed, the free-particle equation of motion.
\chi({\boldsymbol{R}})
can be useful to construct expectation values of some single-particle operators, e.g., this particle’s density and current-density, but it is, of course, useless to describe the relative motion of particles in the system. To do the latter, the corresponding
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}^{\prime},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}})
is needed, where by
{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}^{\prime}
we have denoted coordinates of all the nuclei other than the selected one. If both
\chi({\boldsymbol{R}})
and
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{R}}({\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{R}}^{\prime},{\underset{\text{\raisebox{3.0pt}{=}}}{r}})
are found, then any observable can be evaluated, with the result independent on the way of the division of the system into the two classes.

Instead of one arbitrary nucleus, we could separate the c.m. coordinate
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n,cm}
of the whole nuclear subsystem, while integrating out the other
N-1
particles. Similarly to the above, this would result in the free motion of
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n,cm}
, which constitutes an exact generalization of the corresponding result of
Peters et al. 2022
, the latter obtained within the BO approximation.

VI
Conclusions

We have included an external magnetic field in the framework of the Exact Factorization formalism. The resulting equations of motion, those for nuclei and electrons, contain both the physical and the Berry-connection vector potentials.
We have shown, that nuclei move under the action of the sum of the two vector potentials, while for electrons, the two potentials enter the equation of motion in a more complicated, asymmetric form.

As an important particular case, we have applied the EF formalism to an eigenstate of a neutral atom with the moving center of mass.
We have proven that the nucleus of the atom moves as a free particle.
The latter arises as a result of the exact compensation, in the nuclear equation of motion, between the physical and the Berry-connection vector potentials.
We have illustrated the general theory with the analytical solution for the Harmonium atom in magnetic field, where the quantities relevant to EF formalism admit explicit evaluation and visualization.

Our results being based on the exact theory rather than relying on specific approximations, they provide insights in the Exact Factorization method as a whole.
