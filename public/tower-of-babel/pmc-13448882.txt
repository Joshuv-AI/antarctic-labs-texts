What Is a Clinically Meaningful Change in Diabetes Distress? Findings for Diabetes Care and Research From the SFDT1 Cohort
Authors: Dulce Canha, Jean‐Pierre Riveline, Fatima Kazanlieva, Gloria A. Aguayo, Charline Bour, Bruno Vergès, Pierre‐Yves Benhamou, Michaël Joubert, Fawaz Alzaid, Emmanuel Cosson, Guy Fagherazzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsDiabetes distress (DD) is common and evolves heterogeneously over time. We aimed to estimate minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for the Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) scale and its sub‐dimensions and to identify predictors of worsening over 1 year in people with type 1 diabetes (PwT1D).Materials and MethodsMCIDs for PAID total and sub‐dimension scores were derived using the standard error of measurement approach. One‐year DD worsening, stability and improvement were defined using the ±1 MCID threshold. We analysed changes in DD using data from the SFDT1 cohort. Logistic regression models identified predictors of worsening for PAID total and sub‐dimensions, adjusting for baseline DD, age, sex and social vulnerability.ResultsWe analysed data from 2457 adults with type 1 diabetes (51.6% female, 41.3 years old (SD 14.0), 23.7 years diabetes duration (SD 14.0)). The MCID for PAID total was 5.0 points, with sub‐scale MCIDs ranging from 8.5 (emotional distress) to 14.3 points (management distress). Over 1 year, 42% experienced clinically meaningful worsening of total DD, 30% improved and 28% remained stable. Worsening of total PAID was associated with social vulnerability, HbA1c increase and treatment burden. Distress trajectories varied across sub‐dimensions, with emotional distress and burnout most frequently worsening (34% each); initiation of automated insulin delivery was solely associated with lower odds of worsening on the burnout dimension.ConclusionsThis study provides MCID values for the PAID scale and its sub‐dimensions. The high temporal variability observed suggests a need for regular monitoring and clinically informed assessment of DD in PwT1D.

Introduction

People living with type 1 diabetes (PwT1D) are faced with intensive self‐management and treatment routines, financial pressures, fear of hypoglycaemia, controlling chronic complications and diabetes stigma. A central psychological consequence of this lifelong burden is diabetes distress (DD), a continuous emotional response to the demands and challenges of living with diabetes [1, 2]. DD is associated with poorer self‐management, higher HbA1c, increased risk of diabetes‐related complications and reduced quality of life [3]. It is therefore a relevant and common clinical problem, with prevalence rates varying from 22% [4] to 77% [5] in T1D.

The most commonly used and recommended person‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) to assess DD are the Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) [6] scale and the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS) [7]. Specific T1D measures include the T1‐DDS [8] and Type 1‐Diabetes Distress Assessment Scale (T1‐DDAS) [5]. Developed in 1995, PAID is the earliest and most widely used diabetes‐specific measure of distress [9], having played a foundational role in distinguishing diabetes‐specific emotional burden from general depressive symptoms. In addition to its emotional focus, PAID demonstrates psychometric validity across sex, cultures and types of diabetes, enabling direct comparisons across these populations [10, 11].

Although these tools are well‐validated, a key issue is determining how much change in a score reflects clinically meaningful improvement or deterioration. For this purpose, the concept of minimal clinically important difference (MCID) has been proposed [12]. Recent studies demonstrated the feasibility of distribution‐based approaches to estimate MCIDs for DD scales, including the DDS, T1‐DDS and T1‐DDAS [5, 13, 14]. Using PAID ≥ 40 [15] alone can misrepresent meaningful change because small shifts around the cut‐off can minimise magnitudes of change (e.g., reduction in PAID score from 41 to 39 vs. 60 to 45) [12]. Despite PAID being the most frequently used measure of DD, it has, until recently, lacked validated sub‐dimensions and established MCID thresholds, unlike the DDS. Although sub‐dimensions for PAID have recently been proposed [16], MCID estimates for PAID remain unavailable, representing a gap in both clinical interpretation and longitudinal research.

We had three objectives: (1) to determine the MCID for total PAID scores and sub‐dimension scores, (2) to apply the derived thresholds to characterise DD progression over 1 year and (3) to explore which factors are associated with clinically meaningful increases in PAID scores.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This work is based on participants from the Study of the Francophone Society of Type 1 Diabetes (SFDT1 cohort, NCT04657783) [17]. This cohort includes data on monthly electronic PROMs (ePROMs) and face‐to‐face interviews, physical examinations, clinical assessments, blood samples and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) measures. The study was approved by the ethics committee CPP Ouest V‐RENNES (no. ID‐RCB: 2019A01681‐56) in December 2019.

The current analysis is longitudinal and includes adults with T1D enrolled between December 2020 and May 2025. In addition to the inclusion criteria for this cohort study [17], the present study includes participants with DD data assessed using the PAID questionnaire, which was self‐reported remotely at 3 months after inclusion (ePROM M3) and 1 year later (ePROM M15). Based on data availability, two populations were defined: a population including participants who completed the PAID questionnaire at least once and a longitudinal subpopulation including participants with PAID data available at both M3 (longitudinal baseline) and M15 (1‐year follow‐up). Throughout this paper, ‘baseline’ refers to the inclusion visit for individual and clinical variables and to PROMs collected between M1 and M3. All baseline characteristics were assumed to remain stable between the inclusion visit and M3.

Measures

Diabetes Distress Assessment

The PAID scale consists of 20 items concerning common diabetes‐related issues (e.g., ‘feeling alone with your diabetes’, ‘coping with complications of diabetes’) that are scored on a Likert scale from 0 (not a problem) to 4 (serious problem) [6]. A total score between 0 and 100 is generated, with higher scores indicating higher DD. A score of 40 or above indicates clinically high distress [15]. In our recent work [16], we further validated six PAID sub‐dimensions, also expressed on a 0–100 scale and examined in this study: emotional distress, complications‐related distress, social distress, eating distress, management distress and diabetes burnout.

Individual Variables

Individual variables included at baseline age, sex, higher education (defined as 2 or more years after high school), social vulnerability (measured using EPICES [Evaluation de la Précarité et des Inégalités de santé dans les Centres d'Examens de Santé], a validated French questionnaire to assess the level of precariousness at the individual level, with a cut‐off of ≥ 30.17 indicating vulnerability) [18], alcohol consumption and smoking status (categorised as never, former or current smoker).

Clinical Variables

Clinical measures included at baseline diabetes duration, CGM use, insulin treatment (use of multiple daily injections (MDI), an insulin pump, automated insulin delivery (AID) system or none following beta cell function replacement), BMI (kg/m2), last glycated haemoglobin test (HbA1c (%, mmol/mol)), systolic blood pressure (mmHg), heart rate (bpm), total cholesterol (mmol/L), high‐density lipoprotein (mmol/L), low‐density lipoprotein (mmol/L) and triglycerides (mmol/L). Insulin treatment and HbA1c were also self‐reported at 12 months post‐inclusion (ePRO 12 M).

For those using CGM, baseline CGM‐derived data included time below range (TBR, below 70 mg/dL [3.9 mmol/L]), time in range (TIR, between 70 and 180 mg/dL [3.9 and 10.0 mmol/L]), time above range (TAR, 180 mg/dL [10.0 mmol/L]), glycaemic risk index (GRI, evaluates the risk of acute hypoglycaemic and hyperglycaemic events from four CGM variables [19]) and coefficient of variation (%). The CGM data collection covered a 2‐week period and was required to capture at least 70% of the data and the sum of TBR, TAR and TIR had to be between 98% and 102% to ensure data consistency [20].

Prior history of acute diabetes complications was recorded at baseline during the inclusion visit and incident severe hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis events were self‐reported at 1‐year follow‐up (ePRO 12 M). Long‐term complications, recorded at baseline, included cardiovascular disease (CVD), retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy. CVD was defined as a history of stable angina, acute coronary syndrome, coronary artery bypass graft, stroke, transient ischaemic attack or hospitalisation for heart failure. Retinopathy was diagnosed based on participant interviews, retinography, fundus examination and/or medical reports. Nephropathy was identified based on an albumin/creatinine ratio > 3 mg/mmol or an eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 [21]. Neuropathy was assessed through physical examination using the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument, with a diagnosis threshold > 2 [22].

Data From Other PROMs

Diabetes‐dependent quality of life was measured remotely (ePROM M1) using the Audit of Diabetes‐Dependent Quality of Life (ADDQoL), ranging from −9 (worst) to +3 [23]. Treatment burden was evaluated using the Treatment Burden Questionnaire (TBQ, ePROM M2), with a total score ranging from 0 to 150, where ≥ 59 indicates individuals at risk of becoming overwhelmed [24].

Other PROMs were collected at baseline during face‐to‐face interviews. These included hypoglycaemia awareness status, fear of hypoglycaemia and eating disorders. The first was assessed using the Gold Score (< 3 reflects awareness, > 3 impaired and = 3 undetermined) [25]. The second was measured using the short form of the Hypoglycaemia Fear Survey (HFS‐II), which provides separate behavioural and affective scores ranging from 0 to 16 [26]. Eating disorders were screened using the SCOFF questionnaire, with a global score of 1–5, where a cut‐off ≥ 2 suggests a likely eating disorder [27]. A summary of the PROMs timeline is provided in Figure S1.

Missing Data

Data was missing for some measures (Table S1). CGM‐derived metrics were excluded from imputation as their missingness was linked to centre‐level differences in data collection, violating the missing‐at‐random assumption. For the remaining variables, assuming a missing‐at‐random mechanism, we applied multiple imputation by chained equations with the ‘mice’ R package (version 3.18.0) [28].

Following an intermediate approach, imputation was conducted on the full population (N = 2457) and the longitudinal sample (N = 1225) was obtained by subsetting each imputed dataset to participants with PAID data available at both M3 and M15 [29]. Because repeated measures (HbA1c, insulin treatment, severe hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis events) were collected at aligned time points, these were represented as separate variables in wide format within the imputation model [30].

A total of 38 imputed datasets were generated, based on the maximum proportion of missing data. The number of iterations was 20 and the number of predictors was 25 [31]. We used an imputation model with the best predictors of missing data, by including relevant confounders and excluding highly correlated variables. We evaluated convergence with means, variances and visual representations. Summary statistics and regression models were calculated separately within each imputed dataset and parameter estimates and confidence intervals were pooled using Rubin's rules [32].

Data Analysis

Participants with outlier values in the total MCID change score of continuous PAID, defined as absolute z‐scores greater than 3 [33], were excluded before all longitudinal analyses.

We used means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous and normally distributed variables and medians (IQR) otherwise. Categorical variables were reported as counts (%). Comparative analyses between populations were based on absolute standardised mean differences (ASMD). We interpreted ASMD values > 0.2 as a potentially meaningful imbalance between the full dataset and the longitudinal subset, with values between 0.1 and 0.2 flagged as non‐trivial differences [34].

Following previous work, we used the standard error of measurement (SEM) as a distribution‐based indicator of the MCID for the PAID total score and its sub‐dimensions. The SEM‐based approach identifies a magnitude of change beyond measurement error or ‘noise’, thus representing a clinically meaningful intra‐individual shift [35]. Specifically, 1 SEM has been shown to approximate the anchor‐based MCID across disease‐specific health‐related quality of life instruments in chronic conditions and diabetes PROMs [36, 37]. Accordingly, we employed the 1 SEM formula [1×SD×√1−α], where SD denotes the baseline standard deviation and α represents Cronbach's alpha. The latter reflects internal consistency, computed as [kk−11−∑i=1kσi2σT2], where k is the number of items, σi2 the variance of each individual item and σT2 the variance of the total (sub)scale. We conducted sensitivity analyses stratified by sex and age, with uncertainty estimates and between‐group differences assessed using non‐parametric bootstrap resampling.

To examine stability and change in DD over time, we considered the prevalence of participants who experienced clinically meaningful worsening (≥ + 1 MCID), improvement (≤ −1 MCID) or stability (within ±1 MCID) over 1‐year follow‐up. We then employed logistic regression models to identify predictors of DD worsening over one year. Worsening was defined separately for the PAID total score and for each PAID sub‐dimension as a ≥ 1 MCID increase, with each outcome modelled independently. Models focused on baseline individual, clinical and psychosocial characteristics, as well as changes in insulin treatment, HbA1c levels and incident acute events, with predictors selected a priori based on clinical relevance. As we examined specific risk factors (hypothesis‐driven approach), confounders (respective baseline PAID score, age, sex and social vulnerability) were adjusted for each risk factor‐outcome relationship separately, rather than combining all predictors in a single multivariable model [38, 39]. For categorical variables with multiple levels, pairwise models were used to identify the most informative combinations.

As a sensitivity analysis, we identified predictors of clinically meaningful improvement (≤ −1 MCID), using the same modelling approach and the same set of confounders. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.2.

Results

Study Population

A total of 2457 PwT1D diabetes completed the PAID questionnaire at least once (Table 1). The mean (± SD) age was 41.4 ± 14.0 years, with a diabetes duration of 23.8 ± 14.0 years; 1276 (51.9%) participants were female. At baseline, the mean PAID total score was 40.6 ± 21.8, with 1223 (49.8%) reporting high levels of DD (PAID ≥ 40). Among sub‐dimensions, complications‐related distress was the primary concern (50.6 ± 24.8), while social distress was the least (27.1 ± 25.7).

MCID Estimates and Predictors of Clinically Meaningful Worsening

The calculated SEM‐based MCID for the total PAID score was 5.0 points (Table 2). MCIDs for the PAID sub‐scales ranged from 8.5 (emotional distress) to 14.3 (management distress) points. All scales showed high internal consistency (α > 0.70) [40], validating the reliability of the PAID questionnaire and the previously derived PAID sub‐dimensions [16]. Sensitivity analyses using bootstrap resampling indicated no sex‐ or age‐related differences in MCID estimates (Table S3).

Although overall mean PAID scores showed little change over 1 year, applying MCID thresholds revealed significant heterogeneity: 41.9% of participants experienced clinically meaningful worsening (≥ + 1 MCID), 30.4% improved (≤ −1 MCID) and 27.8% remained stable (Figure 1a). In contrast, using the ≥ 40 conventional cut‐off alone, only 19.8% changed category (11.0% worsened; 8.7% improved), while 80.2% remained either above or below the threshold. Worsening was most frequent for emotional distress and burnout, while stability was highest for eating‐related distress (Figure 1b).

In logistic regression models adjusted for corresponding baseline PAID score, age, sex and social vulnerability, some factors were associated with clinically meaningful worsening of DD over 1‐year (Figure 2a). Social vulnerability increased the risk of worsening across several sub‐dimensions, with the strongest risk observed for emotional distress (OR [95% CI] = 1.56 [1.16, 2.14], p < 0.01) and burnout (1.56 [1.12, 2.17], p < 0.01). Older age was negatively associated with increasing emotional distress (per 10‐year increase: 0.90 [0.82, 0.98], p < 0.05). Current smoking (vs. past smoking) increased the odds of worsening complications‐related (1.69 [1.05, 2.72], p < 0.05) and emotional distress (1.64 [1.08, 2.50], p < 0.05). Interestingly, former smoking (vs. never smoking) was associated with a lower risk of worsening complications‐related distress (0.67 [0.48, 0.94], p < 0.05).

Among clinical factors, increasing HbA1c over 1‐year was associated with higher total PAID score (per 1% increase: 1.20 [1.04, 1.38], p < 0.05) and management‐related distress (1.22 [1.02, 1.46], p < 0.05). Higher BMI was specifically associated with worsening burnout (per 5‐unit increase: 1.15 [1.02, 1.29], p < 0.05). Regarding insulin treatment, initiating AID (vs. continuing pump) was associated with reduced odds of worsening burnout (0.63 [0.40, 0.97], p < 0.05). Pump continuation (vs. MDI continuation) was associated with an increased risk of worsening eating‐related distress (1.69 [1.06, 2.10], p < 0.05).

Baseline PROMs were strong predictors of meaningful worsening (Figure 2b). High treatment burden consistently increased the risk of worsening across multiple DD dimensions, with the strongest association observed for eating distress (1.97 [1.39, 2.80], p < 0.001). Symptoms of anxiety or depression were associated with higher odds of worsening eating distress (1.54 [1.08, 2.20], p < 0.05). Impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia was linked to increased odds of management‐related distress (1.66 [1.15, 2.40], p < 0.01). Only affective fear of hypoglycaemia was associated with worsening complications‐related distress (per 3‐unit increase: 1.18 [1.03, 1.35], p < 0.05). Higher diabetes‐dependent QoL was strongly and consistently associated with reduced risk of DD worsening across all dimensions, including the total PAID score (0.85 [0.78, 0.93], p < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses examining clinically meaningful improvement over 1‐year are given in Figure S3. Importantly, determinants of DD improvement were not simply the inverse of those observed for worsening. For example, diabetes duration (per 10‐year increase: 1.25 [1.02, 1.53], p < 0.01) and TBR (per 5% increase: 1.05 [1.02, 1.09], p < 0.05) were significantly associated, respectively, with higher odds of eating‐ and social‐related DD improvement, baseline clinical factors that were not significant in the corresponding worsening models.

Discussion

Main Findings

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of DD evolution in PwT1D. We combined SEM‐based MCID thresholds with longitudinal analysis to characterise clinically meaningful changes and to identify determinants of worsening distress over 1 year. Although PAID scores remained stable at the population level, MCID‐based classification revealed that more than 40% of participants experienced clinically meaningful worsening in total DD.

Clinically Meaningful Change in Diabetes Distress

The MCID estimates derived for the PAID total and sub‐dimensions were consistent with prior methodological work and reflected the high dependence of SEM on the number of items in each subscore. The total PAID MCID of 5.0 points is highly comparable to the recently derived MCID for the T1‐DDAS Core scale of 0.27 [5], as well as with the T2‐DDAS Core scale of 0.25 [41] (around 5% of the scale range). Both are derived from US national samples, supporting cross‐scale and cross‐national consistency within Western contexts. Although psychometric work suggests scale properties (including SEM‐based MCID) are relatively stable across diverse diabetes samples [10, 11], region‐specific PAID thresholds and MCIDs are still understudied.

While the mean population scores remained stable, nearly 42% of SFDT1 participants experienced a clinically meaningful worsening of overall DD. Notably, only 11% transitioned to scores ≥ 40, demonstrating that reliance on cut‐offs alone would have underestimated clinically meaningful within‐category worsening.

We found that emotional distress and burnout were among the most frequent areas of worsening (34% each), dimensions that are closely linked to the chronic cognitive and emotional demands of diabetes management. In contrast, stability was most frequently observed for eating‐, social‐ and management‐related distress (58%–65%), the areas of least concern at baseline. These findings reinforce the separation between emotional burden and task‐specific subdimensions.

Clinically Meaningful Worsening Across Diabetes Distress Dimensions

Across logistic regression models, social vulnerability emerged as one of the most consistent and strongest predictors of distress worsening. This aligns with a growing body of literature emphasising the role of social determinants of health in psychological outcomes in diabetes [42]. Older age appeared preventive specifically for emotional distress, potentially reflecting greater coping resources, experience or (e)health literacy [43]. Smoking (vs. past smoking) was associated with worsening in complications‐ and emotional‐related subdomains. However, the lower risk observed among past smokers (vs. never smokers) for complications‐related distress may reflect smoking cessation and health behaviour changes triggered by disease‐related concerns. This aligns with evidence that new chronic disease diagnoses can act as ‘teachable moments’ for quitting smoking and improving health outcomes [44], including psychological ones. Interestingly, current smoking (vs. never smoking) was associated with higher odds of improving eating‐related distress in sensitivity analysis, with no other relationship found. Although this finding is isolated and should be interpreted with caution, the eating‐related PAID subdimension captures feelings of food deprivation and preoccupation with eating, which may be alleviated by nicotine's appetite suppressing effects [45].

Importantly, female sex was not associated with distress worsening, even though females consistently report higher cross‐sectional DD levels [4, 16], indicating stable sex differences rather than different change trajectories.

Higher baseline BMI was specifically associated with worsening burnout. Studies have shown that higher BMI in PwT1D is linked to sustained fatigue and greater diabetes‐related stigma that can affect self‐esteem and efforts to comply with management [46, 47], which aligns closely with the construct of burnout.

Worsening glycaemic control was associated with clinically meaningful worsening in management‐related distress and total DD. Increases in HbA1c may primarily raise concerns about treatment demands, while also contributing to the broader emotional burden captured by the total score. In sensitivity analysis, we did not find a mirror effect for DD improvement, suggesting that higher glycaemic values drive worsening distress, but lowering them does not automatically translate into distress relief.

Some clinical predictors appeared only in improvement models, with no mirror effect in worsening models. Longer diabetes duration was associated with higher odds of improving eating‐related distress, possibly resulting from healthier diet routines found in PwT1D with longer disease duration [48]. Higher TBR at baseline was associated with improvement in social distress, which may reflect more room for stabilisation over follow‐up reducing hypoglycaemia‐related social and leisure restrictions. Higher total cholesterol, a marker of cardiovascular risk [49], was associated with lower odds of improving management‐related distress, which might reflect greater treatment complexity in this group. The exclusive appearance of these factors in improvement models suggests they may be more relevant in decreasing distress than preventing its deterioration.

Technology‐related factors were particularly informative. AID initiation was associated with lower odds of worsening burnout; however, in supplementary analysis, it was not associated with improvement in this sub‐dimension, suggesting a potential buffering effect rather than a reduction effect. At the same time, AID initiation was associated with improving eating‐related distress, consistent with previous cross‐sectional findings [16]. These asymmetric and domain‐specific associations indicate that technologies may alleviate specific experiential burdens but do not fully address the emotional and psychological challenges faced by PwT1D.

Treatment burden, social vulnerability, eating disorders, anxiety or depression, impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia and affective fear of hypoglycaemia were all associated with clinically meaningful worsening in total DD and/or specific distress sub‐dimensions. This pattern supports that baseline psychosocial vulnerability is a central factor in susceptibility to DD [50] and its evolution.

In supplementary analysis, higher ADDQoL and lower treatment burden were bidirectional predictors, associating with both lower odds of worsening and higher odds of improving across several distress dimensions. This mirror pattern may indicate these factors work as both protective targets against worsening and therapeutic targets for reducing DD. In contrast, the remaining psychosocial risk factors appeared solely in worsening models. This asymmetry supports an amplification dynamic, consistent with recent network analysis in PwT1D [51] showing that constructs such as loneliness, worrying, burnout, treatment burden and the perceived impact of diabetes on daily life cluster together into distinct but interacting syndromes, where burden multiplies burden. However, the absence of these factors did not symmetrically predict improvement, implying that improvement is a distinct process, driven by different psychosocial determinants, such as increasing diabetes acceptance [52].

Insights for Clinical Practice and Research

Our findings show that clinically meaningful worsening of DD is common, heterogeneous and often invisible when relying on mean score changes at the population level, conventional thresholds alone or with single cross‐sectional assessments. Recent work has emphasised the importance of moving beyond mean changes in DD to responder‐based or MCID‐informed outcomes [12, 14]. Indeed, 42% of individuals experienced meaningful worsening, supporting the use of MCID‐based responder approaches in both clinical practice and research. Screening for a ≥ 5‐point increase in total PAID score, even when absolute values remain below 40, may help identify individuals at risk. Patients meeting both criteria should be prioritised for monitoring.

Our results further demonstrate that drivers of worsening differ across PAID sub‐dimensions, showing the importance of assessing distress domains separately rather than relying solely on total scores. Moreover, the asymmetric pattern of drivers of DD worsening and improvement suggests that clinical strategies to prevent deterioration and promote improvement may involve distinct mechanisms. These findings support a more personalised approach to distress monitoring, tailored to patients' baseline DD profiles and specific vulnerabilities.

Across domains, treatment burden was a consistent predictor of worsening, highlighting it as a central and potentially modifiable target for intervention. Actively assessing and reducing treatment burden through regimen simplification, education and shared decision‐making may help prevent DD progression.

Diabetes technologies should be implemented as part of a structured care transition, integrating education, expectation‐setting and psychosocial support. Structured education accompanying technology use, particularly CGM, has been shown to improve PROMs in PwT1D, supporting that the psychological benefits of technology depend in part on the surrounding care model rather than the device alone [53]. In contrast, AID systems have reported inconsistent improvements in DD [54], stressing the need for study designs that separate device effects from implementation and support effects. This view is consistent with recent work calling for a multiverse and qualitative assessment of psychosocial responses to diabetes technology and for biopsychosocial, person‐centred outpatient care models in which structured psychosocial support is integrated [55, 56]. Future studies should also examine the impact of AID system type, duration of use and their interaction on DD.

The recent EASD guidelines on DD emphasise routine assessment, use of validated instruments and individualised care, while acknowledging the limited evidence base for interventions targeting DD over time, particularly in adults with T1D [54]. Our findings extend current guidance by defining thresholds for clinically meaningful changes in DD and by identifying domain‐specific and psychosocial risk profiles. We view DD worsening as a distinct, influential and potentially preventable outcome and provide concrete targets for future studies and routine assessment.

Strengths and Limitations

We used a SEM‐based approach to derive MCIDs for the PAID total score and its sub‐dimensions. SEM‐based thresholds are widely used in the literature, which facilitates comparison with prior work and interpretability across studies. Specifically, the 1 SEM formula has been previously applied to derive MCIDs for other DD scales across both type 1 and type 2 diabetes [5, 13, 14, 41], supporting cross‐scale comparability of DD evolution measurement. All PAID scales demonstrated high internal consistency, reinforcing their validity and reliability. MCID estimates were stable across sex and age groups, suggesting that a single set of PAID thresholds can be applied broadly without introducing systematic bias.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to conduct a broad exploratory analysis specifically focused on predictors of clinically meaningful worsening of DD and to implement it across PAID sub‐dimensions.

Some limitations must be acknowledged. MCIDs derived using distribution‐based methods depend on the characteristics of the sample studied. Absolute thresholds may not be directly transferable to populations with different PAID distributions. However, our large and diverse sample (> 2000 PwT1D) from a broad range of centres across various regions of France and different healthcare settings supports the robustness and generalisability of these distribution‐based thresholds. As robust anchors were unavailable in our dataset, anchor‐based validation was not possible. Future studies should validate the derived 1‐year MCID thresholds by anchoring PAID change scores to patient‐perceived meaningful improvement (e.g., through a global rating of change item) [37].

Importantly, this was an observational study. Exposures such as AID initiation were self‐ or clinician‐selected. Consequently, observed associations should not be interpreted as causal determinants. Moreover, predictors were primarily assessed at enrolment, 3 months before baseline DD assessment and a limited number of longitudinal clinical factors were considered. Even though this may have affected our findings, we assumed the impact to be minimal given the short interval to M3 and the expected stability of most of the clinical factors considered.

When interpreting the asymmetry between worsening and improvement models, regression to the mean and differences in sample composition between subgroups cannot be fully excluded, although adjustment for baseline PAID scores, age, sex and social vulnerability helps mitigate these concerns. Finally, DD was self‐reported at two time points 1‐year apart, which may miss short‐term fluctuations. Future prospective and interventional studies are needed to test whether targeting the identified factors can prevent clinically meaningful worsening of DD.

Conclusion

Using a rigorous approach, we compute and apply, for the first time, MCID values for the PAID scale and its sub‐dimensions. Our findings enable a more personalised interpretation of longitudinal change and provide an appropriate metric for evaluating the efficacy of interventions. We show that clinically meaningful DD worsening is common and heterogeneous, requiring regular monitoring and clinically informed assessment.
