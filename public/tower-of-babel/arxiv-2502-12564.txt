Sample Efficient Omniprediction and Downstream Swap Regret for Non-Linear Losses
Jiuyao Lu, Aaron Roth, Mirah Shi

Abstract
We define “decision swap regret” which generalizes both prediction for downstream swap regret and omniprediction, and give algorithms for obtaining it for arbitrary multi-dimensional Lipschitz loss functions in online adversarial settings. We also give sample complexity bounds in the batch setting via an online-to-batch reduction. When applied to omniprediction, our algorithm gives the first polynomial sample-complexity bounds for Lipschitz loss functions—prior bounds either applied only to linear loss (or binary outcomes) or scaled exponentially with the error parameter even under the assumption that the loss functions were convex. When applied to prediction for downstream regret, we give the first algorithm capable of guaranteeing swap regret bounds for all downstream agents with non-linear loss functions over a multi-dimensional outcome space: prior work applied only to linear loss functions, modeling risk neutral agents. Our general bounds scale exponentially with the dimension of the outcome space, but we give improved regret and sample complexity bounds for specific families of multidimensional functions of economic interest: constant elasticity of substitution (CES), Cobb-Douglas, and Leontief utility functions.



1
Introduction

Predictions are useful insofar as they can be used to inform downstream
action
— but different agents have different objectives. In a dynamically changing environment, each agent — if sufficiently sophisticated — could run their
own
learning algorithm, attempting to learn the right actions to play to minimize their loss function. But if a single service could broadcast predictions that were simultaneously useful to
all
agents, no matter what their loss functions were, this would be more compelling. This idea has been recently been pursued in two closely related literatures:

1.

Omniprediction:
Omniprediction, introduced by
Gopalan et al. (2022)
, aims to produce a predictor for a label space
\mathcal{Y}
that can be efficiently post-processed to optimize a class of downstream loss functions in a way that is competitive with some benchmark class of functions
\mathcal{C}
containing hypotheses
c:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The omniprediction literature has for the most part (with a few important exceptions which we discuss in Section
1.1
) focused on one dimensional binary labels in the batch setting when examples are drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution
Gopalan et al. (2022)
;
Gopalan et al. (2023)
;
Gopalan et al. (2024a)
;
Globus-Harris et al. (2023)
;
Hu et al. (2024)
. The fact that labels are paired with informative features is crucial to make the problem interesting in this setting. An important extension beyond the binary label setting is given by
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
who study omniprediction for real valued labels
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]
, and show how to obtain real valued omnipredictors for
convex
loss functions, albeit with sample complexity bounds that scale exponentially with the error parameter.

2.

Prediction for Downstream Regret:
A related line of work
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
;
Noarov et al. (2023)
;
Roth and Shi (2024)
;
Hu and Wu (2024)
has focused on making sequential predictions of adversarially chosen outcomes from some space
\mathcal{Y}
so that downstream agents, who may have arbitrary discrete action spaces, can optimize their own loss. Several papers from this literature
Noarov et al. (2023)
;
Roth and Shi (2024)
have gone beyond the binary label setting and have focused on arbitrary loss functions that for each action are
linear
in the state
y\in[0,1]^{d}
to be predicted. This is useful for modeling risk neutral agents, who wish to maximize their
expected utility
over the label outcome, as expectations are linear. The goal is to guarantee that all downstream decision makers have diminishing regret in the worst case. Motivated by decision makers interacting in competitive environments,
Noarov et al. (2023)
;
Roth and Shi (2024)
;
Hu and Wu (2024)
give results to guarantee downstream decision makers diminishing
swap
regret at near optimal rates
(agents with no swap regret guarantees cannot be exploited in the same ways that agents with standard no regret guarantees can be
(
Braverman et al., 2018
;
Deng et al., 2019
;
Rubinstein and Zhao, 2024
;
Arunachaleswaran et al., 2024
)
).
Because calibration is known to be unobtainable in online adversarial settings at
O(\sqrt{T})
rates
(
Qiao and Valiant, 2021
;
Dagan et al., 2024
)
, this literature has from the beginning used techniques (like “U-calibration”
(
Kleinberg et al., 2023
)
and extensions of decision calibration
Zhao et al. (2021)
;
Noarov et al. (2023)
;
Roth and Shi (2024)
) that sidestep these lower bounds while being strong enough to guarantee downstream decision makers no (swap) regret. In contrast to the omniprediction literature, this literature has focused on settings in which there are no features.

In this paper we make use of techniques from each literature to give new results in the other. In particular, we use and extend the approach of
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
of approximately representing non-linear loss functions over a higher dimensional basis in which they can be represented linearly. Using this basis we make online adversarial predictions that are useful to downstream decision makers with arbitrary action spaces and Lipschitz loss functions in multi-dimensional real-valued action spaces, and that guarantee no swap regret with respect to rich classes of context-dependent benchmarks. Modeling agents as optimizing a non-linear loss is a common way of modeling risk aversion in strategic settings, and so this extends the prediction-for-downstream-action literature beyond prediction for risk neutral agents. Rather than using calibration in a batch setting as
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
do, we obtain our results using algorithms for guaranteeing an extension of decision calibration that guarantees swap regret in an online adversarial setting
Noarov et al. (2023)
. This gives regret bounds, that when translated back into sample complexity bounds via an online to batch reduction in the style of
Gupta et al. (2022)
, gives exponentially improved bounds compared to
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
.
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
give sample complexity bounds for 1-dimensional convex Lipschitz loss functions that scale exponentially with the desired error parameter, whereas we give
the first omniprediction sample complexity bounds for Lipschitz losses that scale polynomially, rather than exponentially in the error parameter
— without requiring convexity. We also give sample complexity bounds for arbitrary
d
-dimensional Lipschitz loss functions which scale exponentially in
d
, but give improved bounds for specific families of loss functions of economic interest: constant elasticity of substitution (CES), Cobb-Douglas, and Leontief utility functions. For CES loss functions, our sample complexity and regret bounds scale polynomially with both
d
and the error parameter. For Cobb-Douglas, we give
pseudo-polynomial
bounds, scaling exponentially with the
log
of the inverse error parameter. For Leontief loss functions, our bounds remain exponential in the dimension, but have only a polynomial dependence on the error parameter.

1.1
Additional Related Work

Omniprediction was introduced by
Gopalan et al. (2022)
.
The main technique that has emerged from this literature is to produce predictions that are
multi-calibrated

(
Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018
)
with respect to some benchmark class of policies
Gopalan et al. (2022)
, or that satisfy a related set of calibration conditions (e.g. calibration and multi-accuracy)
Gopalan et al. (2023)
. In particular,
Gopalan et al. (2023)
show that omniprediction can be obtained by jointly promising
hypothesis outcome indistinguishability (OI)
and
decision OI
. Decision OI is a generalization of decision calibration, first introduced in
Zhao et al. (2021)
, and informally requires that the predicted loss of optimizing for a given loss function matches the realized loss, in aggregate over many examples, conditional on the action chosen. Recently several papers have studied omniprediction in the online adversarial setting and have directly optimized for variants of decision OI (rather than calibration, which implies it) to circumvent lower bounds for calibration in the online adversarial setting.
Garg et al. (2024)
;
Dwork et al. (2024)
;
Okoroafor et al. (2025)
These papers (like almost all of the omniprediction literature with the exception of
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
) continue to restrict attention to binary label spaces
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
. Decision IO does
not
guarantee downstream decision makers no swap regret. In our results, we use a different generalization of decision calibration, first given by
Noarov et al. (2023)
and used by
Roth and Shi (2024)
, which informally guarantees that conditional on the action taken by a downstream decision maker, the predicted loss is equal to the realized loss
simultaneously for every action
(not just for the action selected by the decision maker). This strengthening of decision calibration is sufficient to give swap regret guarantees. The notion of decision swap regret that we give generalizes both swap regret and omniprediction, but is distinct from (and weaker than) the notion of “swap omniprediction” which was studied in batch settings by
Gopalan et al. (2024a)
. Swap omniprediction implies calibration, and hence is subject to the same lower bounds that calibration is in online settings
(
Garg et al., 2024
)
.

1.2
Organization

In Section
2
, we define our notion of decision swap regret. Section
3
develops machinery to obtain low decision swap regret for any agent with a linear loss function. In Section
4
, we construct basis functions that give uniform approximations to
d
-dimensional Lipchitz losses, as well as better constructions for several structured special cases of interest. Together, the results established in Sections
3
and
4
lead to our results for non-linear losses, which we present in Section
5
. Here we also show how to obtain a predictor for the offline batch setting via an online-to-batch reduction, and derive guarantees for offline omniprediction as an application of our results.

2
Model and Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the feature space and
\mathcal{Y}
denote the outcome/label space. Throughout, we consider
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{d}
(more generally, we can consider any convex, bounded
d
-dimensional space with a constant degradation in our bounds). We let
\mathcal{P}
denote the prediction space. We model agents as having an arbitrary action space
\mathcal{A}
and a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
taking as input an action and an outcome. We let
\mathcal{L}
denote a family of loss functions.

We take the role of an online forecaster producing predictions that will be consumed by agents with loss functions belonging to a family
\mathcal{L}
. We consider the following repeated interaction between a forecaster, agents, and an adversary. In every round
t\in[T]
:
(1) The adversary selects a feature vector
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
and a distribution over outcomes
Y_{t}\in\Delta\mathcal{Y}
;
(2) The forecaster produces a distribution over predictions
\pi_{t}\in\Delta\mathcal{P}
, from which a prediction
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
is sampled;
(3) Any agent equipped with a loss
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
chooses an action
a_{t}
as a function of the prediction
p_{t}
;
(4) The adversary reveals an outcome
y_{t}\sim Y_{t}
, and the agent (with loss function
\ell
) suffers loss
\ell(a_{t},y_{t})
.

2.1
Decision Swap Regret

We measure our performance using a notion of regret, which we call
decision swap regret
. Informally, decision swap regret compares the loss of actions chosen based on our predictions against the loss of actions suggested by a collection of benchmark policies. A strength of our definition comes from its ability to condition the choice of benchmark policy on the
action
the decision maker chooses. The definition implies that in hindsight, simultaneously for each action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, on the subsequence of rounds on which the decision maker chose action
a
, they could not have done any better by acting according to any policy
c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}
instead. As we will see this definition generalizes both omniprediction (which does not condition the benchmark policy on the action) and swap regret (which does condition on the action, but uses a benchmark class of constant policies).

Definition 1

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Decision Swap Regret)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. For a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}\in\mathcal{Y}
, we say that a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}\in\mathcal{P}
has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
decision swap regret if for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, there is some action selection rule
k^{\ell}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{A}
such that choosing actions
a_{t}=k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))
achieves:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t})\right)\leq\varepsilon

for any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

In other words, decision swap regret measures the worst-case regret incurred by any agent with loss
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
who follows our predictions,
when they could have instead taken actions suggested by
c_{a}(x_{t})
rather than playing action
a
for some set of benchmark policies
\{c_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

Remark 1
.

Our definition of decision swap regret is not to be confused with the notion of swap omniprediction, defined in
Gopalan et al. (2024a)
(see also
Globus-Harris et al. (2023)
for a related characterization of multicalibration in terms of a contextual swap regret condition.) Swap omniprediction allows both the loss function and the benchmark policy to be indexed by the forecaster’s prediction. In contrast, in decision swap regret, the loss function is fixed, and the benchmark policy is indexed by the decision maker’s best response action (which is a coarsening of the forecaster’s prediction). Swap omniprediction is equivalent to multicalibration
(
Gopalan et al., 2024a
)
, whereas we explicitely use the fact that decision swap regret can be obtained without calibration, which is what allows us to obtain our substantial sample complexity improvements compared to
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
.

We will give regret guarantees when agents
best respond
to our predictions—that is, choose the action that would minimize loss if our prediction was accurate. Decision swap regret guarantees that trusting our predictions and acting accordingly dominates playing any policy in
\mathcal{C}
, even informed by the best response action to the prediction made by the forecaster, and is akin to a truthfulness condition in mechanism design.

Definition 2

(Best Response)
.

Fix a loss
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
and a prediction
p\in\mathcal{Y}
. The best response to
p
according to
\ell
is the action
\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p)=\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ell(a,p)
.

2.2
Examples of Decision Swap Regret

Omniprediction:

Omniprediction
corresponds the special case of decision swap regret in which the benchmarking policies are action-independent—i.e.
c_{a}=c
for for some
c\in\mathcal{C}
, independently of
a
. We can thus view omniprediction as the “external regret” version of decision regret. Omniprediction is often studied in the batch setting, although
Garg et al. (2024)
recently introduced omniprediction in the online setting. Online omniprediction is strictly more general than batch omniprediction—we derive our bounds in the online setting, and then reduce to the batch setting in Section
5
.

Definition 3

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omniprediction regret)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. We say that a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
omniprediction regret with respect to a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}
if for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, there is some action selection rule
k^{\ell}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{A}
such that choosing actions
a_{t}=k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))
achieves:

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(c(x_{t}),y_{t})\right)\leq\varepsilon

Definition 4

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omnipredictor)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a join distribution over pairs
(x,y)
. We say that a predictor
p:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}
is a
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omnipredictor if for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, there is some action selection rule
k^{\ell}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{A}
such that:

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p(x)),y)-\ell(c(x),y)\right]\leq\varepsilon

Swap Regret:

The traditional notion of
swap regret
is studied in a context free setting and is the special case of decision swap regret in which
\mathcal{C}=|\mathcal{A}|
is the set of constant policies that play a single action independently of the context.

Definition 5

(
(\mathcal{L},\varepsilon)
-Swap Regret)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions. A sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}\in\mathcal{Y}
has
(\mathcal{L},\varepsilon)
-swap regret with respect to outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}
if for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and swap function
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
, choosing actions
a_{t}=\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))
achieves:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(\phi(a_{t}),y_{t})\right)\leq\varepsilon

Remark 2
.

Our notion of swap regret is equivalent to “Calibration Decision Loss” as defined by
Hu and Wu (2024)
. They consider the family of loss functions defined as expectations over a 1 dimensional boolean outcome, or equivalently linear over a 1-dimensional outcome.

3
Decision Swap Regret for Linear Losses

We first restrict our attention to the task of obtaining low decision swap regret for losses
\ell
that are linear and Lipschitz functions of the outcome. More precisely,
\ell
is linear if for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we can write
\ell(a,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{\ell}_{i}(a)\cdot y_{i}
, where
\{r_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i\in[d]}
is a set of coefficient functions. Furthermore,
\ell
is
L
-Lipschitz (in the
L_{\infty}
norm) if for any
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
|\ell(a,y)-\ell(a,y^{\prime})|\leq L\|y-y^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
.

Our starting point is a general recipe for omniprediction. Specifically, as shown by
Gopalan et al. (2023)
, for a family
\mathcal{L}
consisting of linear losses,
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})
-Omniprediction is implied by:

1.

Calibration: predictions are unbiased conditional on the value of the prediction itself, i.e.

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[p_{t}=p](p_{t}-y_{t})\right|=0
for predictions
p\in\mathcal{P}

2.

Multiaccuracy: predictions are unbiased conditional on a class of “tests” derived from
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.
\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{\ell,c}(x_{t})(p_{t}-y_{t})\right|=0
for “tests”
h_{\ell,c}
that depend on losses
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and policies
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

The omniprediction literature has focused on 1-dimensional outcomes because calibration is expensive in high dimensions; in
d
dimensions, the number of (discretized) predictions is exponential in
d
, and so the sample complexity of producing non-trivial calibrated forecasts using existing algorithms scales exponentially in
d
. The downstream regret literature, meanwhile, has tackled the high-dimensional setting using the key insight that when predictions are used to inform downstream actions, it suffices to enforce a coarsening of calibration:
decision calibration

(
Zhao et al., 2021
;
Noarov et al., 2023
)
. (A more recent literature
Garg et al. (2024)
;
Dwork et al. (2024)
;
Okoroafor et al. (2025)
has used a related observation to get polynomial improvements in omniprediction sample complexity in the 1 dimensional outcome setting.) Decision calibration asks that the forecaster’s predictions be unbiased conditional not on the predictions themselves, but rather on the downstream actions induced by best responding to the predictions. Unlike calibration, decision calibration can be obtained at rates polynomial in
d
with runtime polynomial in
d
. Alongside decision calibration, we give a notion of
decision cross calibration
and show that decision swap regret bounds follow from bounds on decision calibration and decision cross calibration.

We define decision calibration and decision cross calibration below. These differ slightly from the notion of decision calibration originally given by
Zhao et al. (2021)
and from the subsequent definition of decision-OI
Gopalan et al. (2023)
— those definitions would suffice to give omniprediction bounds, but not decision
swap
regret bounds. The versions we use here were first used in
Noarov et al. (2023)
.

Definition 6

(
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-Decision Calibration)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
. Let
\beta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-decision calibrated with respect to a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}
if, for every
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
:

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t})=a](p_{t}-y_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta(T^{\ell}(a))

where
T^{\ell}(a)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t})=a]
.

Informally speaking, decision calibration is enough to guarantee an decision maker that their estimated reward for playing any action is unbiased conditionally on their decision — which is enough to guarantee that they will have no
swap
regret. However to be able to promise that we can compare their performance to that of other
action policies
which map context to action, we will also want the following decision
cross-calibration
condition to hold.

Definition 7

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-Decision Cross Calibration)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-decision cross calibrated with respect to a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}\in\mathcal{Y}
if for for every
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
,
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
,
c\in\mathcal{C}
:

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t})=a,c(x_{t})=b](p_{t}-y_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha(T^{\ell}(a,b))

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\beta,\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are concave functions. This will be the case in all the bounds we give; in general, this condition holds for any sublinear error bound
T^{c}
for
c<1
.

We now show that these two ingredients imply no decision swap regret for linear losses. At a high level, we can minimize decision swap regret by ensuring that our predictions allow every agent to accurately assess their losses (on average) conditionally on their chosen action—for both their selected actions and the actions suggested by benchmark policies. Decision calibration ensures the former, and decision cross calibration ensures the latter. Since agents select the optimal actions given our predictions, their actions are competitive against any choice of benchmark policies.

Theorem 1

(Decision Swap Regret for Linear Losses)
.

Consider an outcome space
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
that are linear and
L
-Lipschitz in the second argument. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. If the sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-decision calibrated and
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-decision cross calibrated, then it has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-decision swap regret, where

\varepsilon\leq\frac{L|\mathcal{A}|\beta(T/|\mathcal{A}|)+L|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\alpha\left(T/|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\right)}{T}

when agents choose actions
a_{t}=\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t})
.

For any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, by definition of best response, we have that for any choice of benchmark policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(a_{t},p_{t})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),p_{t})

Thus, it suffices to bound the difference in loss under our predictions
p_{t}
and the outcomes
y_{t}
for both our sequence of chosen actions and the sequence of actions recommended by any choice of benchmark policies. We show this in the next two lemmas, using decision calibration and decision cross calibration, respectively.

Lemma 1
.

If
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-decision calibrated, then for any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
:

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(a_{t},p_{t})-\ell(a_{t},y_{t}))\right|\leq|\mathcal{A}|\beta(T/|\mathcal{A}|)

Proof.

Using the linearity of
\ell
, we can write:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(a_{t},p_{t})-\ell(a_{t},y_{t}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a](\ell(a,p_{t})-\ell(a,y_{t}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\ell\left(a,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a]p_{t}\right)-\ell\left(a,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a]y_{t}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a](p_{t}-y_{t})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\beta(T^{\ell}(a))

where the first inequality follows by
L
-Lipschitzness of
\ell
, the second inequality follows from
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-decision calibration. By concavity of
\beta
and the fact that
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}T^{\ell}(a)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a]=T
, this expression is at most:

L|\mathcal{A}|\beta(T/|\mathcal{A}|)

∎

Lemma 2
.

If
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-cross calibrated, then for any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and any selection of benchmark policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),p_{t})-\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t}))\right|\leq L|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{T}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}\right)

Proof.

Using linearity of
\ell
, we can compute:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),p_{t})-\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a,c_{a}(x_{t})=b](\ell(b,p_{t})-\ell(b,y_{t}))\right|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigg(\ell\left(b,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a,c_{a}(x_{t})=b]p_{t}\right)

\displaystyle-\ell\left(b,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a,c_{a}(x_{t})=b]y_{t}\right)\Bigg)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a,c_{a}(x_{t})=b](p_{t}-y_{t})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\alpha(T^{\ell}(a,b))

where the first inequality follows from
L
-Lipschitzness of
\ell
, and the second inequality follows from
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-decision cross calibration. By concavity of
\alpha
and the fact that
\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}T^{\ell}(a,b)=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[a_{t}=a,c_{a}(x_{t})=b]=T
, this expression is at most:

\displaystyle L|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{T}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}\right)

∎

We can now complete the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

Fix any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
. Applying Lemmas
1
and
2
, and the fact that
a_{t}=\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t})
, we can conclude that the decision swap regret to any selection of benchmark policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
is bounded by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(a_{t},p_{t})-\ell(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t}),p_{t}))+\frac{L|\mathcal{A}|\beta(T/|\mathcal{A}|)+L|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{T}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}\right)}{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L|\mathcal{A}|\beta(T/|\mathcal{A}|)+L|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{T}{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}\right)}{T}

∎

Definition 8

(
(n,\lambda)

.

Fix a family of functions

\mathbb{R}\}

We can also refer to an

Definition 9

We observe that the Lipschitz constant

Lemma 3

Suppose

We have that:

\left(O((1/\delta)^{\ln d}),Ae^{Rd},\delta\right)
-approximate basis for the family
\mathcal{F}^{d}_{\Omega_{\text{exp},g}}
, for

Proof.

Recall that for any

for some
A\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We can thus approximate
f
using a finite Taylor expansion of
\omega
. In particular, let
\hat{f}
be the approximation to
f
given by the
k^{th}
degree Taylor expansion of
\omega
at
a=0

\displaystyle\hat{f}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\omega_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}\frac{\omega^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}\frac{\omega^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{n}\in[d]}r_{i_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{n}}\cdot g(y_{i_{i}})\cdots g(y_{i_{n}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{n}\in[d]}\frac{\omega^{(n)}(0)}{n!}r_{i_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{n}}\cdot g(y_{i_{i}})\cdots g(y_{i_{n}})

Therefore, the set of functions
\mathcal{S}=\cup_{n=1}^{k}\{g(y_{i_{1}})\cdots g(y_{i_{n}})\}_{i_{1},...,i_{n}\in[d]}
approximately spans
\Omega_{\text{exp},g}
and thus
\mathcal{F}^{d}_{\Omega_{\text{exp},g}}
. By Lemma
4
, the approximation error is:

\displaystyle|f(y)-\hat{f}(y)|=\left|\omega\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})\right)-\omega_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})\right)\right|=\left|\frac{\omega^{(k+1)}(z)}{(k+1)!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})\right)^{k+1}\right|

for some value
z
between
0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})
. Now, using the assumption that
|\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}g(y_{i})|\leq C

|\omega^{(k+1)}(z)|=Ae^{z}\leq Ae^{C}

\displaystyle|f(y)-\hat{f}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ae^{C}}{(k+1)!}C^{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ae^{C}}{\sqrt{2\pi(k+1)}\left(\frac{k+1}{e}\right)^{k+1}}C^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{Ae^{C}}{\sqrt{2\pi(k+1)}\left(\frac{k+1}{Ce}\right)^{k+1}}

where in the second step we use Stirling’s inequality:
n!\geq\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}
. It remains to verify that

is large enough so that the approximation error is at most
\delta

|f(y)-\hat{f}(y)|\leq\delta
. Observe that we have the following:

\displaystyle\frac{Ae^{C}}{\sqrt{2\pi(k+1)}\left(\frac{k+1}{Ce}\right)^{k+1}}\leq\delta

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi(k+1)}\left(\frac{k+1}{Ce}\right)^{k+1}\geq\frac{Ae^{C}}{\delta}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\ln(k+1)+\ln(2\pi))+(k+1)(\ln(k+1)-\ln(Ce))\geq\ln A+C+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

Now, the LHS is bounded below by
(k+1)(\ln(k+1)-\ln(Ce))\geq\Omega(k\ln k)
. Thus taking

suffices to bound the approximation error by
\delta

|\mathcal{S}|=d+d^{2}+...+d^{k}=O(d^{k})=O\left(d^{\ln(1/\delta)}\right)=O\left((1/\delta)^{\ln d}\right)
.

By Lemma
3

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k}\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{n}\in[d]}\left|\frac{\omega^{(n)}(0)}{n!}r_{i_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{n}}\right|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{k}A\cdot\frac{(Rd)^{n}}{n!}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A\cdot\frac{(Rd)^{n}}{n!}=Ae^{Rd}

Here, we first use the assumption that each
|r_{i_{j}}|\leq R
and the fact that

for all

Leontief production functions

Next we consider
\mathcal{F}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
, the family of Leontief production functions
that are
L

[0,1]^{d}
— i.e., for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
, there exists
a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}\geq 1/L
such that we can write:

f(y)=-\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\{y_{j}/a_{j}\}

Proposition 5
.

For any
\delta>0
, there exists a
\left(O\left(\frac{d^{4}c^{d}\ln^{3d+1}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{(6d+8)/(d+2)}}\right),O\Big(\frac{Ld^{5/2}c^{d}\ln^{2d+1/2}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{(5d+6)/(d+2)}}\Big),3L\delta\right)
-approximate basis for
\mathcal{F}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
, where

The remainder of the section is dedicated to proving Proposition
5
.

We directly extend techniques developed by
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
for 1-dimensional convex 1-Lipschitz functions to the
d
-dimensional setting. Our approach closely follows that of
Gopalan et al. (2024b)
and consists of constructing bases for several intermediate function classes. First, we consider a discretization of the function class. We then represent any discretized function using point indicator functions, which we show can be represented using MReLU functions (a multivariate extension of ReLU functions). Then, we construct a basis for MReLU functions using indicator functions of hyperrectangles (a multivariate extension of interval indicators). Finally, we construct a basis for hyperrectangles using a low-rank approximate factorization of the identity matrix.

In fact, this approach gives us a general template to represent any non-linear Lipschitz multivariate function. For Leontief functions in particular, we will be able to find an upper bound to the sum of the coefficients in the representation that is better than, as we will see, the naive bound of
(1/\delta)^{d}
. We proceed with the argument below; missing proofs can be found in Appendix
B
.

For ease of notation in the proof, we will rewrite every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
as the analogous
utility
function (the negative loss function). First, observe that we can write a Leontief utility function in the following form:

f=L\cdot\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\left\{\frac{1}{a_{j}L}\cdot y_{j}\right\}

where the labels
y_{1},\cdots,y_{d}
and their coefficients
\frac{1}{a_{1}L},\cdots,\frac{1}{a_{d}L}
are all in
[0,1]
.

For notational convenience, we assume
\delta=1/m
where
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Our first step is to discretize and scale both the labels and their coefficients by multiplying them by
m
and then rounding to integers.
Let
\operatorname{Round}(x)
represent the closest integer to
x
in
\{1,\cdots,m-1\}
. For each
j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
, let
b_{j}=\operatorname{Round}(\frac{1}{a_{j}L\delta})\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}
,
z_{j}=\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{j}}{\delta})\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}
. Define
g:\{1,\cdots,m-1\}^{d}\to\{1,\cdots,(m-1)^{2}\}
as:

\displaystyle g(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\{b_{j}z_{j}\}

And denote the family of

as
\mathcal{G}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}=\{g(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d}):b_{1},\cdots,b_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}\}
, which can be viewed as a scaled and discretized version of Leontief utility functions. We show that
g
is close to the original Leontief utility function

Lemma 5
.

For any
y_{1},\cdots,y_{d}\in[0,1]
and Leontief utility function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
, the corresponding
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
constructed as described above satisfies:

\left|f(y_{1},\cdots,y_{d})-L\delta^{2}g\left(\operatorname{Round}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{\delta}\right),\cdots,\operatorname{Round}\left(\frac{y_{d}}{\delta}\right)\right)\right|\leq 2L\delta

Proof.

We note that for any
j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{a_{j}L\delta}\cdot\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}-b_{j}\cdot\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{j}}{\delta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{a_{j}L\delta}\cdot\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}-b_{j}\cdot\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}\right|+\left|b_{j}\cdot\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}-b_{j}\cdot\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{j}}{\delta})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{a_{j}L\delta}-b_{j}\right|\cdot\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}+b_{j}\cdot\left|\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}-\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{j}}{\delta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1\cdot m+m\cdot 1

\displaystyle=2m

Due to the 1-Lipschitzness of the
\min

\displaystyle|f(y_{1},\cdots,y_{d})-L\delta^{2}g(\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{1}}{\delta}),\cdots,\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{d}}{\delta}))|

\displaystyle=\left|L\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\{\frac{1}{a_{j}L}\cdot y_{j}\}-L\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\left\{\delta b_{j}\cdot\delta\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{j}}{\delta})\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=L\delta^{2}\left|\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\{\frac{1}{a_{j}L\delta}\cdot\frac{y_{j}}{\delta}\}-\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\left\{b_{j}\cdot\operatorname{Round}(\frac{y_{j}}{\delta})\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq L\delta^{2}\cdot 2m

\displaystyle=2L\delta

We will construct a

m
-approximate basis for
\mathcal{G}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}

-approximate basis for the Leontief utility functions

As a starting point, any
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}
can by straightforwardly represented by point indicator functions:

\displaystyle g(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}}g(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})\mathbbm{1}[z_{1}=i_{1},\cdots,z_{d}=i_{d}]

Now we represent point indicator functions using MReLU functions. For any thresholds
i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the function
\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}:\{1,\cdots,m-1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}
is defined as:

\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})=\max\{z_{1}-i_{1},\cdots,z_{d}-i_{d},0\}

MReLU functions are 1-Lipschitz due to the 1-Lipschitzness of the
\max
function. When

, this reduces to the well known
\operatorname{ReLU}
function,
\operatorname{ReLU}_{i}(z)=\max\{z-i,0\}
.

Any indicator function
\mathbbm{1}[z_{1}=i_{1},\cdots,z_{d}=i_{d}]
is a linear combination of MReLU functions whose thresholds are adjacent to
(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\cdots,z_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}
,
i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}[z_{1}=i_{1},\cdots,z_{d}=i_{d}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{d+\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{d}}\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}+\sigma_{1},\cdots,i_{d}+\sigma_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{1+\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{d}}\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}-\sigma_{1},\cdots,i_{d}-\sigma_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})

For example, when
d=1
, Lemma
6
gives us:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}[z=i]=\operatorname{ReLU}_{i+1}(z)-2\operatorname{ReLU}_{i}(z)+\operatorname{ReLU}_{i-1}(z)

And when
d=2

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}[z_{1}=i_{1},z_{2}=i_{2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}+1,i_{2}+1}(z_{1},z_{2})-\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}+1,i_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})-\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},i_{2}+1}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}-1,i_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})+\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},i_{2}-1}(z_{1},z_{2})-\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}-1,i_{2}-1}(z_{1},z_{2})

6

\displaystyle g(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}}g(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})\Bigg[\sum_{\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{d+\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{d}}\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}+\sigma_{1},\cdots,i_{d}+\sigma_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})

\displaystyle\hskip 130.0002pt+\sum_{\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{1+\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{d}}\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}-\sigma_{1},\cdots,i_{d}-\sigma_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})\Bigg]

Rearranging terms, we derive a representation for

based on
\operatorname{MReLU}

Corollary 2

For any
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{Leon}}^{d}

\displaystyle g(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})=\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{0,\cdots,m\}}c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}^{g}\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})

\displaystyle c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}^{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}:\\

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}:\\

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\in\{0,1\}:\\
i_{1}+\sigma_{1},\cdots,i_{d}+\sigma_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m-1\}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{1+\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{d}}\min\{b_{1}(i_{1}+\sigma_{1}),\cdots,b_{d}(i_{d}+\sigma_{d})\}

Lemma 7
.

For
c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}^{g}
as defined above, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{0,\cdots,m\}}\left|c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}^{g}\right|\leq 12^{d+1}m^{3}

Thus, we have a basis consisting of MReLU functions. However, this basis has size
(m+1)^{d}
, which we can already obtain for all Lipschitz functions.
With some more work, we can find a smaller basis for MReLU functions. To do this, we first turn to representing differences between adjacent MReLU functions — i.e. MReLU functions with thresholds that differ by 1 in one coordinate.

Let the interval function
\mathbb{I}_{a,b}(z)=\mathbbm{1}[z\in[a,b]]
be the indicator for the interval
[a,b]
in one dimension. Observe that for
d=1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ReLU}_{i}(z)-\operatorname{ReLU}_{i+1}(z)=\mathbb{I}_{i+1,m}(z)

As an extension to higher dimensions, we define
hyperrectangle functions
, i.e. indicator functions for hyperrectangles, to be products of interval functions over a multi-dimensional space. For instance,
\mathbb{I}_{1,m}(z_{1})\mathbb{I}_{1,m}(z_{1}-z_{2})\mathbb{I}_{1,m}(z_{1}-z_{3})
corresponds to a hyperrectangle in the space of
(z_{1},z_{1}-z_{2},z_{1}-z_{3})
. The following lemma states the difference between adjacent MReLU functions over higher dimensions as a hyperrectangle function.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\cdots,z_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
,
i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{0,\cdots,m\}
. For
d\geq 2
, we have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})-\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}+1,i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})=\mathbb{I}_{i_{1}+1,m}(z_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{d}\mathbb{I}_{i_{1}-i_{j}+1,m}(z_{1}-z_{j})

This can be equivalently stated as:

\displaystyle\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})-\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}+1,i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})=\mathbb{I}_{i_{1}+1,m}(z_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{d}\mathbb{I}_{i_{1}-i_{j}+1+m,2m}(z_{1}-z_{j}+m)

[1,\cdots,2m]
for convenience.

Ultimately, this will allow us to discretize our basis of MReLU functions, while filling in the remaining gaps using hyperrectangle functions. More specifically, consider a set of discrete MReLU functions:

\mathcal{R}_{s}=\{\operatorname{MReLU}_{i_{1}s,\cdots,i_{d}s}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d}):i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\in\{1,\cdots,m/s\}\}

(m/s)^{d}

and
hyperrectangle functions. Later, we will determine

Our task thus reduces to constructing an approximate basis for

\{1,\cdots,2m\}^{d}

\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{I}_{a_{j},b_{j}}(w_{j}):a_{j},b_{j},w_{j}\in\{1,\cdots,2m\}\right\}

\prod_{j=1}^{d}[a_{j}2^{h_{j}},(a_{j}+1)2^{h_{j}}-1]

We refer to indicator functions for dyadic hyperrectangles as dyadic hyperrectangle functions. Notice that every interval on
\{1,\cdots,2m\}

2\log_{2}m

Lemma 9
.

Every hyperrectangle on

c

Decision Swap Regret for Non-Linear Losses

In this section we present our main result—an algorithm making predictions that guarantee low decision swap regret for losses that admit approximate basis representations. The bulk of the work in proving Theorem
4
has already been completed; what remains is to combine results established in previous sections. First, we rely on a basis
\mathcal{S}
to (approximately) represent a loss
\ell
by a higher-dimensional linear loss
\hat{\ell}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
. Because
\hat{\ell}
is linear in the higher-dimensional representation
s(y)
, we can use the
Decision-Swap
algorithm to make predictions of
s(y)
. Then, best responding according to
\hat{\ell}
guarantees vanishing decision swap regret under
\hat{\ell}
. But,
\hat{\ell}
pointwise approximates
\ell
, and so for both the actual loss suffered and the loss achieved by the benchmark policy, we can switch
\ell
and
\hat{\ell}
without much change in loss, implying vanishing decision swap regret under
\ell
.
Now, recall that the guarantees of
Decision-Swap
depend on the size of the loss family and thus only apply to finite families of losses. We will thus use a net-based argument: rather than running
Decision-Swap
on
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
, which might have infinite size, we run
Decision-Swap
on a
\gamma
-
cover
of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
, which has finite size and uniformly approximates any
\hat{\ell}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}
up to
\gamma
.

Definition 10

(
\gamma
-Cover)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
. We say that
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
is a
\gamma
-cover of
\mathcal{L}
if for every
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists
\ell_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
such that for every
a\in\mathcal{A},y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
|\ell(a,y)-\ell_{\gamma}(a,y)|\leq\gamma
.

We discretize linear losses by “snapping” their coefficients to the closest discrete point.

Lemma 12

(Discretization of Linear Losses)
.

Consider
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of losses
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
that are linear and
\lambda
-Lipschitz (in the
L_{\infty}
norm) in the second argument. Then there exists a family of losses
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
that is a
(d\gamma)
-cover of
\mathcal{L}
with
|\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}|\leq(2\lambda/\gamma)^{d|\mathcal{A}|}
.

Proof.

Recall that for any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we can write
\ell(a,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{\ell}_{i}(a)\cdot y_{i}
for some collection of coefficients
\{r_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i\in[d]}
. Moreover, by
\lambda
-Lipschitzness of
\ell
and the fact that the range is bounded between
[0,1]^{d}
, we have that
|r_{i}^{\ell}(a)|\leq\lambda
for all
i\in[d]
. So, for any
\ell
, we can construct a discretized loss
\ell_{\gamma}(a,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a)\cdot y_{i}
, where each
r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a)
is obtained via rounding each coefficient
r^{\ell}_{i}(a)
to the nearest multiple of
\gamma
, e.g. the nearest value in
\{-\left\lfloor{\lambda/\gamma}\right\rfloor\gamma,...,-\gamma,0,\gamma,...,\left\lfloor{\lambda/\gamma}\right\rfloor\gamma\}
. Thus since
|r^{\ell}_{i}(a)-r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a)|\leq\gamma
for all
i\in[d],a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have that:

\displaystyle|\ell(a,y)-\ell_{\gamma}(a,y)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{\ell}_{i}(a)\cdot y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a)\cdot y_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}(r^{\ell}_{i}(a)-r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a))\cdot y_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|r^{\ell}_{i}(a)-r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a)\right|\cdot y_{i}

\displaystyle\leq d\gamma

where in the last inequality we use the fact that each
y_{i}\in[0,1]
. Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}=\{\ell_{\gamma}\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}
is a
(d\gamma)
-cover of
\mathcal{L}
. Finally, since each
r^{\ell}_{\gamma,i}(a)
take one of at most
2\lambda/\gamma
values, we have that
|\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}|\leq(2\lambda/\gamma)^{d|\mathcal{A}|}
.
∎

The exponential dependence on
|\mathcal{A}|
and
d
will not be prohibitive—remember that
Decision-Swap
gives error bounds that depend only logarithmically on the size of the loss family. Next we state the result.

5.1
Continuous Action Spaces

The decision swap regret bounds we have derived depend on
|\mathcal{A}|
, the number of actions. This is acceptable in games that have finite action spaces (as often studied in the “downstream regret” literature) — but the omniprediction literature often takes
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Y}
— i.e. actions correspond to (continuous) labe predictions. Here we extend our results to continuous but bounded action spaces
\mathcal{A}=[0,1]^{m}
(this is without loss, since taking
\mathcal{A}
to be any bounded space
[-C,C]^{m}
will only introduce a constant factor to our analyses). Our results for finite action spaces translate to continuous action spaces as long as losses are additionally Lipschitz in the actions, and agents choose actions from a
\theta
-net of
\mathcal{A}
. This has implications for decision tasks where the action space is the outcome space (e.g. omniprediction). For these tasks, if we assume losses to be Lipschitz in the outcomes, then it is generally reasonable to assume that they are also Lipschitz in the actions (most loss functions considered in machine learning are Lipschitz in both the prediction and the label — e.g. squared loss satisfies this assumption).

Definition 11

(
\theta
-Net)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an arbitrary continuous space. We say that
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}\subset\mathcal{S}
is a
\theta
-net of
\mathcal{S}
if for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists
\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
such that
\|s-\tilde{s}\|_{\infty}\leq\theta
. Observe that when
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{m}
, we can always obtain a
\theta
-net of
\mathcal{S}
of size
|\mathcal{S}_{\theta}|\leq 1/\theta^{m}
by discretizing each coordinate into multiples of
\theta
.

The idea is straightforward. Given a family of losses
\mathcal{L}=\{\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]\}
, we can construct a corresponding family of losses
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\{\ell|_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}}:\mathcal{A}_{\theta}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}
, where
\ell|_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}}
is the restriction of
\ell
to the a
\theta
-net
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
of
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
is finite, we can invoke our previous result for finite loss families (Theorem
4
) on
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
, when agents choose actions from
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
. We can think of agents as “snapping” their actions to a nearby action in
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
. There is just one complication: our previous result only lets us compare our performance to benchmark policies that suggest actions in
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
. However, we show that benchmark policies that instead suggest actions in
\mathcal{A}
are not much more powerful when losses are Lipschitz in the actions.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[0,1]^{m}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
be a
\theta
-net of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of losses
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}

(\mathcal{L}_{\theta},\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\varepsilon^{\prime})

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}+\theta

Proof.

Fix any

and any assignment of benchmark policies

\displaystyle\ell(\tilde{c}_{\tilde{a}_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\ell(c_{\tilde{a}}(x_{t}),y_{t})\leq\|\tilde{c}_{\tilde{a}_{t}}(x_{t})-c_{\tilde{a}_{t}}(x_{t})\|_{\infty}\leq\theta

\tilde{c}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}

\theta

\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell(\tilde{a}_{t},y_{t})-\ell(c_{\tilde{a}_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t}))\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell|_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}}(\tilde{a}_{t},y_{t})-\ell|_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}}(\tilde{c}_{\tilde{a}_{t}}(x_{t}),y_{t}))+\theta\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}+\theta

which proves the theorem.
∎

and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
p\sim\pi(x)\end{subarray}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p)}(x),y)\right]\leq\varepsilon+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+4\delta

when the action selection rule is
k^{\ell}(p)=\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p)
for a nearby
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
.

Proof.

Fix a loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.
We note that

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D},p\sim\pi(x)}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p)}(x),y)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x)),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]

is the distributional analogue of the empirical decision swap regret,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})\right)

which is bounded by Theorem
4
.

We will show that the distance between the empirical decision swap regret and the distributional version is bounded by bounding the following terms:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

Lemma 13
.

For any
\kappa>0
, with probability at least
1-\kappa/2
, for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+2\delta

Proof.

We first prove that, with probability at least
1-\kappa/2
, for any
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

We define the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left(\left\{\left(x_{s},y_{s},p_{s}\right)\right\}_{s=1}^{t}\right)
. For a fixed
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
, consider the following sequence adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,

W_{t}=W_{t-1}+\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right].

Since
W_{t-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\E\left[\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

we have
\E[W_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=W_{t-1}
, so
\{W_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is a martingale.

We also have
|W_{t}-W_{t-1}|\leq 1
, so by applying Azuma-Hoeffding’s inequality (Lemma
20
), we get that with probability
1-\frac{\kappa}{2|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}|}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(2|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}|/\kappa\right)}{T}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(2(2\lambda/\gamma)^{n|\mathcal{A}|}/\kappa\right)}{T}}

For our setting of
\gamma=\frac{1}{2n\sqrt{T}}
, this expression is at most:

O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

Taking the union bound over every
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
, we get that with probability
1-\kappa/2
, for any
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
:

\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

Since
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
is the
(n\gamma)
-cover of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is an
\delta
-approximate basis for
\mathcal{L}
, we get that when the above inequality holds, for any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq(n\gamma+\delta)+(n\gamma+\delta)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+2\delta

where we use the triangle inequality.

∎

Lemma 14
.

For any
\kappa>0
, with probability at least
1-\kappa/2
, for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+2\delta

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
13
. We first prove that, with probability at least
1-\kappa/2
, for any
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

For a fixed
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
and assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, consider the following sequence adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,

S_{t}=S_{t-1}+\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]

Since
S_{t-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\E\left[\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

we have
\E[S_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=S_{t-1}
, so
\{S_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is a martingale.

We also have
|S_{t}-S_{t-1}|\leq 1
, so by applying Azuma-Hoeffding’s inequality (Lemma
20
), we get that with probability
1-\frac{\kappa}{2|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}||\mathcal{C}|^{|\mathcal{A}|}}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(2|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}||\mathcal{C}|^{|\mathcal{A}|}/\kappa\right)}{T}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(2(2\lambda/\gamma)^{n|\mathcal{A}|}|\mathcal{C}|^{|\mathcal{A}|}/\kappa\right)}{T}}

For our setting of
\gamma=\frac{1}{2n\sqrt{T}}
, this expression is at most:

O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

Taking the union bound over every
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
and every assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, we get that with probability
1-\kappa/2
, for any
\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

Since
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\gamma}
is the
(n\gamma)
-cover of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is an
\delta
-approximate basis for
\mathcal{L}
, we get that when the above inequality holds, for any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}(c_{\mathrm{BR}^{\hat{\ell}_{\gamma}}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq(n\gamma+\delta)+(n\gamma+\delta)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+2\delta

where we use the triangle inequality.

∎

We then combine Lemma
13
, and Lemma
14
with the triangle inequality to derive

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x)),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})\right)

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t})),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x)),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))}(x_{t}),y_{t})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x))}(x),y)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+4\delta

This finishes the proof of Theorem

.
∎

5.3
Offline Omniprediction

With the extension to continuous action spaces and the online-to-batch reduction, we obtain sample complexity bounds for offline omniprediction, as long as there exists a reasonably sized basis for the losses
\mathcal{L}
and randomized predictors are allowed.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[0,1]^{d}
be the action space and
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{d}
be the outcome space. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
. Suppose
\mathcal{S}
is a
(n,\lambda,\delta)
-approximate basis for
\mathcal{L}
, where
n
and
\lambda
scales polynomially with
1/\delta
. For any
\varepsilon
, there exists an algorithm that returns an
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omnipredictor with probability
1-e^{-O(poly(1/\varepsilon))}
. The time and sample complexity of the algorithm scales polynomially with
1/\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
be a
\theta
-net of
\mathcal{A}
for
\theta=\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}n}{T}\right)^{1/(3d+2)}
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\{\ell|_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}}:\mathcal{A}_{\theta}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}
be the restriction of the loss functions to
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\{\tilde{c}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}_{\theta}\}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
be the collection of policies with “snapped” outputs. According to Corollary
4
, running Algorithm
1
with inputs
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
,
\mathcal{S}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
produces predictions
\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{T}\in[0,1]^{n}
that has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon^{\prime})
-decision swap regret for:

\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq O\left(\frac{\lambda^{2/(3d+2)}n^{1/(3d+2)}\sqrt{\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T)}}{T^{1/(3d+2)}}\right)+2\delta

By Theorem
6
, we can feed i.i.d. examples to this algorithm and obtain a randomized predictor
\pi
such that, for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
p\sim\pi(x)\end{subarray}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p)}(x),y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}|\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+4\delta

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\lambda^{2/(3d+2)}n^{1/(3d+2)}\sqrt{\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T)}}{T^{1/(3d+2)}}\right)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n\left(\frac{T}{\lambda^{2}n}\right)^{d/(3d+2)}\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}{T}}\right)+6\delta

\displaystyle=\underbrace{O\left(\frac{\lambda^{2/(3d+2)}n^{1/(3d+2)}\sqrt{\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T)}}{T^{1/(3d+2)}}\right)}_{:=\tau_{1}}+\underbrace{O\left(\frac{n^{(d+1)/(3d+2)}}{\lambda^{d/(3d+2)}T^{(d+1)/(3d+2)}}\sqrt{\ln(n\lambda|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}\right)}_{:=\tau_{2}}+6\delta

where
T
is the number of i.i.d. examples,
n
and
\lambda
may depend on
\delta
for different loss families.

For any
\varepsilon
, we can choose
(\delta,\kappa,T)
such that
\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+6\delta
, is at most
\varepsilon
. We first present the steps for constructing
(\delta,\kappa,T)
, and then provide their explicit expressions for 1-dimensional convex 1-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{1}_{\text{cvx}}}
and
d
-dimensional
L
-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}_{L}}
in the proof of Corollary
5
and
6
.

We can first set
\delta=\varepsilon/18
. Since
n
and
\lambda
scales polynomially with
1/\delta
,
\tau_{1}
scales polynomially with
1/\varepsilon
and
1/T
now. We can select
T
such that
T
scales polynomially with
1/\varepsilon
and
\tau_{1}
is at most
\varepsilon/3
. Then we can select
\kappa
such that
\ln(1/\kappa)
scales polynomially with
1/\varepsilon
and
\tau_{2}
is at most
\varepsilon/3
. As a result,
\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+6\delta\leq\varepsilon
, the predictor
\pi
is an
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omnipredictor.

The time complexity is the product of the sample complexity and the per round time complexity of Algorithm
1
, which scales polynomially with
\delta
,
|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}|
,
|\mathcal{C}|
, as indicated by Theorem
11
from
Noarov et al. (2023)
. Therefore, the time complexity also scales polynomially with
1/\varepsilon
.
∎

Here we give sample complexity bounds for key loss families of interest: 1-dimensional convex 1-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{1}_{\text{cvx}}}
and
L
-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}_{L}}
in
d
dimensions.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[0,1]
be the action space and
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]
be the outcome space. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Consider the family of convex 1-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{1}_{\text{cvx}}}
. For any
\varepsilon
, there exists an algorithm that returns an
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{1}_{\text{cvx}}},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omnipredictor with probability
1-e^{-O(1/\varepsilon^{2})}
. The sample complexity of the algorithm is
O\left(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)\ln^{5/2}(|\mathcal{C}|/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{17/3}}\right)
.

Proof.

Since we have a
\left(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{2/3}},O(1),\delta\right)
-approximate basis for 1-dimensional convex 1-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{1}_{\text{cvx}}}
, the predictor
\pi
described in the proof of Theorem
6
satisfies:

\displaystyle\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
p\sim\pi(x)\end{subarray}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p)}(x),y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{2/3}}\right)^{1/5}\sqrt{\ln(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{2/3}}|\mathcal{C}|T)}}{T^{1/5}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{2/3}}\right)^{2/5}\sqrt{\ln(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)}{\delta^{2/3}}|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}}{T^{2/5}}\right)+6\delta

By setting
T=O\left(\frac{\ln^{4/3}(1/\delta)\ln^{5/2}(|\mathcal{C}|/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{17/3}}\right)
,
\kappa=e^{-O(1/\varepsilon^{2})}
and
\delta=\varepsilon/18
, we can verify that this expression is at most
\varepsilon
in this case.
∎

Corollary 6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[0,1]^{d}
be the action space and
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{d}
be the outcome space. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Consider the family of
L
-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}_{L}}
. For any
\varepsilon
, there exists an algorithm that returns an
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}_{L}},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Omnipredictor with probability
1-e^{-O(1/\varepsilon^{2d})}
. The sample complexity of the algorithm is
O\left(\frac{L^{d}d^{3d/2+1}\ln^{3d/2+1}(L|\mathcal{C}|/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4d+2}}\right)
.

Proof.

Since we have a
\left((L/\delta)^{d},1,\delta\right)
-approximate basis for
L
-Lipschitz losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}_{L}}
in
d
dimensions, the predictor
\pi
described in the proof of Theorem
6
satisfies:

\displaystyle\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
p\sim\pi(x)\end{subarray}}\left[\ell(k^{\ell}(p),y)-\ell(c_{k^{\ell}(p)}(x),y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{(L/\delta)^{d/(3d+2)}\sqrt{\ln((L/\delta)^{d}|\mathcal{C}|T)}}{T^{1/(3d+2)}}\right)+O\left(\frac{(L/\delta)^{d(d+1)/(3d+2)}}{T^{(d+1)/(3d+2)}}\sqrt{\ln((L/\delta)^{d}|\mathcal{C}|T/\kappa)}\right)+6\delta

By setting

Appendix A
Contextual Swap Regret

Here we elucidate the connection between our definition of decision swap regret and the following definition of
contextual swap regret
appearing in the literature (e.g.
Garg et al. (2024)
;
Lin and Chen (2024)
).

Definition 12

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Contextual Swap Regret)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. A sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-contextual swap regret with respect to outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}
if for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, any policy
c\in\mathcal{C}
, and any swap function
\phi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
, choosing actions
a_{t}=\mathrm{BR}^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))
achieves:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(\phi(a_{t},c(x_{t})),y_{t})\right)\leq\varepsilon

Like decision swap regret, contextual swap regret is a strengthening of both omniprediction and swap regret, since benchmarks can depend on the actions taken and the suggested actions of a fixed policy. We show that making predictions that achieve low decision swap regret (over a slightly richer class) is only more general than making predictions that achieve low contextual swap regret.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\Phi
be the collection of all swap functions
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}=\{\phi\circ c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}\}_{\phi\in\Phi,c\in\mathcal{C}}
. If a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}_{\Phi},\varepsilon)
decision swap regret with respect to outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}
, then it has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
contextual swap regret with respect to the same outcomes.
This implies that when
\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}=\mathcal{C}
, i.e., the collection of policies
\mathcal{C}
is closed under any swap function, decision swap regret is stronger than contextual swap regret.

Proof.

Fix any
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
. By
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}_{\Phi},\varepsilon)
decision swap regret, we have that for any assignment of
\{(\phi\circ c)_{a}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, or written equivalently,
\{\phi_{a}\circ c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(\phi_{a_{t}}(c_{a_{t}}(x_{t})),y_{t})\right)\leq\varepsilon

In particular, this implies that for any assignment
\{\phi_{a}\circ c\in\mathcal{C}_{\Phi}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
where
c_{a}=c
for some
c\in\mathcal{C}
, we have that:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\ell(a_{t},y_{t})-\ell(\phi_{a_{t}}(c(x_{t})),y_{t})\right)\leq\varepsilon

Since the output of
\phi_{a_{t}}(c(x_{t}))
depends both on
a_{t}
and
c(x_{t})
, this expression is exactly the contextual swap regret as measured by
\ell
, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Making Predictions for Naive Decision Makers under Smooth Best Response

In this section we show how to make more interpretable predictions that are better suited to simple/naive decision makers. Our techniques produce predictions of the coefficients of basis functions in a high dimensional space, and to translate these into actions, a downstream decision maker must first produce an approximate representation of their loss function in this higher dimensional space, and then use it to best respond. We show how to produce predictions in the original label space so that a downstream decision maker can act straightforwardly using a smoothed version of best response — i.e. that chooses a distribution over actions that is weighted towards higher utility actions, but is not a point-mass on the single best response action.

For concreteness, we instantiate “smooth best response” to take the form of quantal response, which is a common model of bounded rationality studied in the behavioral economics literature
(
Luce, 1959
;
McFadden, 1976
;
McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995
;
Anderson et al., 2002
;
Goeree et al., 2002
)
. Quantal response is motivated by the hypothesis that agents do not behave perfectly rationally and thus do not always perfectly best respond; instead, their choices are
noisy
. This noise is captured by modeling agents who randomly select their actions according to some distribution. The quantal response function chooses actions with probability inversely proportional to the exponential loss. We note that the results we present below hold in general for any response function that is Lipschitz in the predictions and an approximate best response (the bounds we give depend on both the Lipschitz constant and the approximation factor), and our restriction to quantal response is purely for concreteness.

Definition 13

(Smooth Best Response)
.

The smooth best response to a prediction
p
according to a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
is the distribution
q^{\ell}(p)\in\Delta\mathcal{A}
where the weight placed on action
a
is:

q^{\ell}(a,p)\propto\exp(-\eta\cdot\ell(a,p))

given some smoothness parameter
\eta>0
. Observe that as
\eta\to\infty
, the smooth best response converges to an exact best response.

Next we give analogues of some definitions to accommodate for randomized actions. First we extend the definition of decision swap regret, which we state as an expectation over the random action.

Definition 14

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-Decision Swap Regret)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. For a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}\in\mathcal{Y}
, we say
p_{1},...,p_{T}\in\mathcal{P}
has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
decision swap regret if for any loss function
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, there is a randomized action selection rule
k^{\ell}:\mathcal{P}\to\Delta\mathcal{A}
such that choosing actions
q_{t}=k^{\ell}(p_{t}(x_{t}))
achieves:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{a\sim q_{t}}\left[\ell(a,y_{t})-\ell(c_{a}(x_{t}),y_{t})\right]\leq\varepsilon

for any assignment of policies
\{c_{a}\in\mathcal{C}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

Next, we give the smooth analogues of decision calibration and decision cross calibration. At first blush, it might seem that extending decision calibration and decision cross calibration to distributions over actions would involve conditioning on the entire smooth response distribution. This would be unfortunate, as achieving such a notion would require regret bounds scaling exponentially with the cardinality of the action space. However, it turns out that conditioning on the probability of playing each action
marginally
will be enough.

Definition 15

(
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-Smooth Decision Calibration)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
. Let
\beta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-smooth decision calibrated with respect to a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}
if, for every
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
:

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{\ell}(a,p_{t})(p_{t}-y_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta(T^{\ell}_{q}(a))

where
q^{\ell}(a,p_{t})
is the weight placed on action
a
under the smooth best response distribution
q^{\ell}(p_{t})
, and
T^{\ell}_{q}(a)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{\ell}(a,p_{t})
.

We similarly extend decision cross calibration:

Definition 16

(
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-Smooth Decision Cross Calibration)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a family of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
and
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that a sequence of predictions
p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-smooth decision cross calibrated with respect to a sequence of outcomes
y_{1},...,y_{T}\in\mathcal{Y}
if for every
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
,
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
,
c\in\mathcal{C}
:

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{\ell}(a,p_{t})\mathbbm{1}[c(x_{t})=b](p_{t}-y_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha(T^{\ell}_{q}(a,b))

where
q^{\ell}(a,p_{t})
is the weight placed on action
a
under the distribution
q^{\ell}(p_{t})
, and
T^{\ell}_{q}(a,b)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{\ell}(a,p_{t})\mathbbm{1}[c(x_{t})=b]
.

With these extended definitions in hand, we can now state decision swap regret guarantees for linear losses when agents smoothly best respond.

Theorem 8

(Decision Swap Regret for Linear Losses Under Smooth Best Response)
.

Consider an outcome space
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be any collection of loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
that are linear and
L
-Lipschitz in the second argument. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of policies
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}

p_{1},...,p_{T}
is
(\mathcal{L},\beta)
-smooth decision calibrated and
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-smooth decision cross calibrated, then it has
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-decision swap regret where

\varepsilon\leq\frac{L|\mathcal{A}|\beta(T/|\mathcal{A}|)+L|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\alpha\left(T/|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\right)}{T}+\frac{\ln|\mathcal{A}|+1}{\eta}

when agents play their smooth best response

with smoothness parameter
\eta

As stated, smooth best response only increases an agent’s regret: observe that as
\eta
goes to
\infty

\frac{\ln|\mathcal{A}|+1}{\eta}
goes to 0, and we recover the bound on decision swap regret under exact best response (Theorem
1
). However, to give decision swap regret guarantees later on, we will need a form of agent response that, unlike the exact best response, is Lipschitz in our predictions—which smooth best response satisfies.

The argument follows a similar structure as the argument when agents exactly best respond (Theorem
1
): we show that smooth decision calibration ensures that our self-assessed expected losses for
smooth
responses are (on average) accurate, while smooth decision cross calibration ensures that our self-assessed expected losses for the actions recommended by the benchmarks are (on average) accurate. Now that agents smoothly, rather than exactly, best respond, it is no longer the case that their actions are optimal given our predictions. However, we show that the smooth best response is
approximately

Lemma 16
.

Expanding out the expectation and using linearity of

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E_{a\sim q^{\ell}(p_{t})}[\ell(a,p_{t})-\ell(a,y_{t})]\right|

where the first inequality follows by

C.1

Now we show how to make predictions of the outcome

\hat{p}_{t}
of the higher dimensional basis representation

\hat{p}_{t}
as our prediction; instead, we will broadcast a value
p_{t}
in the

p_{t}\in s^{-1}(\hat{p}_{t})
(the inverse needs not to be unique; if there are multiple elements in the pre-image, we can choose on arbitrarily). Thus, we can interpret
p_{t}
as a prediction of the outcome
y_{t}

Now, agents with losses that have an exact basis representation (e.g.
L_{p}

\mathcal{F}^{d}_{p}

\mathcal{F}^{d}_{\Omega_{\beta,g}}

according to their loss, and so can obtain the same decision swap regret guarantees of Theorem
4

On the other hand, agents with losses that have only an approximate basis, must now smoothly best respond; this is to bypass discontinuities of the best response function when swapping between agents’ actual losses and their approximate representations. Below we give decision swap regret guarantees for agents who smoothly best respond to predictions

Lemma 19
.

For any two loss functions
\ell:\mathcal{A}\times[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]
and
\hat{\ell}:\mathcal{A}\times[0,1]^{n}\to[0,1]

|q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))-q^{\ell}(a,y)|\leq e^{2\eta\delta}-1
.

Proof.

Without loss, assume that
\ell(a,y)\leq\hat{\ell}(a,s(y))
. We compute:

\displaystyle\frac{q^{\ell}(a,y)}{q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\exp(-\eta\cdot\ell(a,y))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(-\eta\cdot\ell(a^{\prime},y))}}{\frac{\exp(-\eta\cdot\hat{\ell}(a,s(y)))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(-\eta\cdot\hat{\ell}(a^{\prime},s(y)))}}

\displaystyle=\exp(-\eta\cdot(\ell(a,y)-\hat{\ell}(a,s(y))))\cdot\frac{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(-\eta\cdot\hat{\ell}(a^{\prime},s(y)))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(-\eta\cdot\ell(a^{\prime},y))}

\displaystyle=\exp(\eta\delta)\cdot\exp(\eta\delta)

\displaystyle=\exp(2\eta\delta)

Rearranging the expression, we have that
q^{\ell}(a,y)\leq q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))\exp(2\eta\delta)

q^{\ell}(a,y)-q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))\leq q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))\exp(2\eta\delta)-q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))=q^{\hat{\ell}}(a,s(y))(\exp(2\eta\delta)-1)\leq\exp(2\eta\delta)-1
