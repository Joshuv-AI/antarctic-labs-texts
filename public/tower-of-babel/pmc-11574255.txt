Reporting practices of anesthetic and analgesic use in rodent orthopedic research
Authors: Yijun Pan, Shari Cohen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines, along with the updated ARRIVE 2.0, provide a crucial framework for transparent reporting in animal research. These guidelines enhance the quality and reproducibility of studies while supporting animal welfare. However, concerns persist regarding the reporting practices of biomedical researchers in scientific articles related to anesthesia and analgesia despite the endorsement of ARRIVE guidelines, as has been demonstrated in previous descriptive analyses. The current study systematically reviews articles published from 2016 to 2023 that involve rodent orthopedic surgeries. These procedures were selected as our focus given that anesthetics and analgesics are essential for such high impact procedures. We highlight significant reporting gaps, noting that anesthetic and analgesic details were missing in 29.4% and 74.8% of the articles, respectively. Our findings reveal no correlation between reporting completeness and journal impact factors, indicating that this issue generalized across journals. Furthermore, among articles that did report analgesia use, we observed a low prevalence of multimodal analgesia. Overall, this study underscores the urgent need to develop effective strategies to improve reporting of anesthesia and analgesia in animal studies to enhance ethical standards, animal welfare, and the robustness and reproducibility of research.

Introduction

Laboratory animal welfare stands at the forefront of ethical considerations in scientific research, demanding meticulous attention and adherence to established guidelines and best methodologies. Among these, the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines emerge as a beacon for conscientious research practices1,2. The ARRIVE guidelines and the updated version ARRIVE 2.0 are a checklist of recommendations for the full and transparent reporting of research involving animals—maximizing the quality and reliability of published research, and enabling others to better scrutinize, evaluate and reproduce it. It also provides a structured framework to identify and support welfare issues concerning laboratory animals (mice, rats, rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, rabbits, birds, mini-pigs, non-human primates, and fish).

The ARRIVE guidelines recommend reporting species-appropriate details of the animals used, including species, strain, sex, age and weight (where relevant), which are critical factors that can influence most experimental results. For surgical procedures, the ARRIVE guidelines recommend the researchers not only describe the surgical procedures, but also provide details on anesthetic and analgesic used1,2. By disclosing this information, researchers can demonstrate careful consideration of animal ethics review, research design, highlight useful interventions to improve laboratory animal welfare and contribute to research data robustness as well as reproducibility. While the surgical procedures are usually well described, unfortunately, the completeness of reporting on anesthesia and pain management protocols is concerning. According to a review conducted by Carbone et al.3, the completeness of reporting on anesthetics and analgesics use was variable for the majority of 400 articles for a range of animal research fields (250 accepted for publication pre-2011, and 150 in 2014–15). Of these, 62 articles did not mention the use of anesthetics or analgesics, and only 98 articles mentioned post-surgical analgesics.

An updated review for articles published after 2016 is not yet available to determine if reporting practices have improved. To address this knowledge gap, articles involving orthopedic procedures (including spinal laminectomies) published between 2016 and 2023 were reviewed to analyze the reporting of anesthetics and analgesics use. Orthopedic surgeries were specifically targeted because they are high impact procedures, often involving a potentially painful prolonged recovery period, have a high risk to animal welfare, and are well-suited for a multimodal analgesia regimen. Given rodents being the most popular laboratory animals, the current study focuses on rats and mice. In addition, using the available literature searched, this study also explored the patterns of anesthetic and analgesic use in laboratory rats and mice undergoing orthopedic surgical procedures.

Methods

Article search and selection

PubMed® was employed for article searching using the search terms including orthopedic, surgery, analgesia, analgesic, rodent, mouse, and rat. Variation in English spellings were automatically considered by the database. The date of publications was limited to between the1st of January 2016 and the 1st of December 2023, as a comprehensive review evaluating the completeness of information on anesthesia and analgesia in animal studies published before 2016 was conducted by Carbone et al.3

The inclusion/exclusion criteria have been summarized in Table 1. Articles that were not primary research, without abstract/full text, and not in English were automatically excluded using the database search engine. Retracted articles were removed. Studies investigating effects of novel compounds on nociception, nociceptive pathways, those evaluating novel drug delivery device/formulation of analgesics or similar articles excluded. These articles were excluded (recorded as irrelevant in the Supplementary Materials S1) as routine analgesics are contraindicated, and therefore are beyond the scope of this review. The methodology sections of all articles were reviewed by Y.P., a senior postdoctoral biomedical scientist. Article selection was reviewed by S.C., an Australian and New Zealand College of Veterinary Scientists’ fellow in veterinary animal welfare, ethics and law. Data synthesis and retrieval

Data extracted from selected articles were tabulated using Microsoft Excel. The unit of analysis for the current review was reported as analgesic and anesthetic protocol use per article.

Anesthetic and analgesic description completeness score (AADCS): The recommendations for the detailed description of anesthetics and analgesics do not differ between either version of the ARRIVE guidelines. As per the ARRIVE guidelines, the drug name, dose, volume, concentration, site of administration, route of administration, and frequency of administration should be included when describing anesthetic and analgesic use in surgical procedures4. A simple scoring system with a total of 8-points was designed and applied to semi-quantify the completeness of information on anesthetic and analgesic regimen. The scoring matrix has been provided in Table 2. It must be noted that the appropriateness of the use of anethetics and analgesics in the selected articles was not evaluated, which can be an area for future work from a professional perspective. The scoring system was consistently applied in this study to allow for semi-quantification to enable statistical analysis (e.g. correlation analysis) of the reviewed literature. Journal Impact Factor (JIF) and Journal Citation Indicator (JCI): A previous review in this field by Carbone et al.3 scored their selected articles (published between 2010 and 2015) using 2013 Impact Factor for each journal. To improve accuracy in the subsequent analysis, we adapted a Nearest-Neighbor method5, by associating JIF and JCI of the year before publication to the selected article; for example, articles published in 2023 will be associated with the 2022 JIF and JIC for subsequent analysis. The Nearest-Neighbor method is appropriate given that Clarivate Analytics calculates and releases JIF and JIC annually, relying on data from the previous year. The JIF and JIC for each article were retrieved from Clarivate Analytics (London, United Kingdom).

Geographical location: The geographical location of an article was allocated based on the disclosure of the animal ethics committee (AEC) location within the article. There were three article that did not disclose the AEC information. These articles were allocated to the country where the corresponding author was located.

Perioperative pain management: We specifically recorded if pre-operative local anesthetics, pre-, intra-, and post-operative analgesics were used (and the regimen if available) in the selected articles in Microsoft Excel. We defined multimodal analgesic approach in the same way as King et al.6, and ≥ 2 analgesics and/or local anesthetics used for peri-operative pain management was considered to be a multimodal approach to analgesia.

Data analysis

Raw data, (e.g. number of articles including a full description of anesthetic regimen) was reported in Tables and Figures where appropriate. Percentage data, (e.g. the percentage of articles providing a complete description of analgesic regimen) was calculated using Microsoft Excel. The figures were prepared using Microsoft Word and Prism GraphPad (Boston, MA). Spearman’s correlation was employed to investigate the relationship between AADCS, JIF and JIC. Statistical correlations were calculated between these metrics to elucidate any interdependencies, with a particular focus on linear correlations, for the entire dataset. The country-specific correlations were also studied, where articles published by researchers in China, USA, and Japan were grouped for sub-analysis, as these countries accounted for most of the studies. The analysis was performed using Python 3.9.13 (Wilmington, DE).

Results

Identification of relevant articles

The search retrieved a total of 998 articles, of which one abstract-only, three retracted publications, two systematic reviews, and seven video protocols were removed. The method/methodology section of 985 articles were screened to determine relevance to the review, with 143 articles selected for detailed review to extract information on anesthetic and analgesic use, species of animal (mouse/rat, strain, sex), geographical location of research, and surgical procedure from all selected articles. Anesthetic and analgesic use characteristics

The pharmacological agents used for anesthesia and analgesia, along with their dosing regimen (Column B, D, E), for each type of orthopedic surgery/model (Column M) are presented in Supplementary Materials S2, S4. It must be noted that we have not evaluated the appropriateness of these dosing regimen, and therefore the readers should carefully perform independent evaluation when referring this table to design their anesthesia and analgesia protocol. A large portion of the articles (29.4%) did not provide any information about anesthetic use; however, many often referred to the literature which was unable to be corroborated. For example, some articles have cited multiple previous studies where anesthetic protocol was inconsistent, which make it difficult for interpretation. The most commonly used anesthetics were isoflurane (30.1%), pentobarbital (17.5%), ketamine/xylazine (9.8%), and chloral hydrate (4.2%) (Table 4). The majority of the articles did not report the use of pre-operative analgesia (90.2%), intra-operative analgesia (88.8%) and/or post-operative analgesia (78.3%) (Table 4). The most used analgesic was buprenorphine, and its sustained-release form (SRB). Occasional use of NSIADs (meloxicam, carprofen, flunixin) and anilides (paracetamol) was noted. The correlation between completeness of description and journal impact and citations.

The AADCS, JIF and JCI for each selected article are available in Supplementary Materials S2. The correlation between AADCS, JIF and JCI were investigated to reveal if articles with more complete description of anesthetic and analgesic use were more likely to be published in journals of higher impact and citations (Fig. 3). No correlation was identified between AADCS and JIF (r = − 0.1) or JCI (r = 0.025). A moderate-high correlation between JIF and JCI was noted (r = 0.83), in agreement with previously published results7. However, when geographical location was stratified for sub-analysis, the correlation patterns were not uniform between countries. Notably, Japan exhibited two distinct correlation patterns different to other countries, with a weak correlation (r = 0.55) between AADCS and JIF, and a moderate correlation (r = 0.88) between AADCS and JCI observed. However, the number of studies from Japan (n = 14) was much lower than China (n = 63) and USA (n = 42), which may potentially introduce bias. Discussion

The current study systematically reviewed articles published between 2016 and 2023 involving rodent (mouse and rat) orthopedic surgeries that would require anesthetics and analgesics. It was noted that 29.4% and 74.8% of the articles did not report any use of anesthetics and analgesics, respectively. Many articles did not report details of anesthetic and analgesic use nor their doses or frequencies; others referred to surgical procedures in the wider literature. The low reporting of anesthetic or analgesic regimens aligns with previous reviews6,8. Interestingly, a higher percentage of articles reported complete regimens for anesthesia (25.0–57.9%) than for analgesia (3.7–25%). This may be because researchers are more inclined to mention that surgical procedures were performed on anesthetized animals, while there are less prompts for them to mention analgesia. Analgesic regimens, route of administration, dosing frequency and duration, can often be missed from reporting which is of a potential concern. It must be emphasized that the anesthesia/analgesia method used in animal experiments would have undergone IACUC review, and therefore even if an article does not report what was used it does not mean a method of analgesia/anesthesia was not in place. The current study only addresses the reporting behavior of biomedical scientists, rather than confirming if researchers have used any pharmacological agents or if their use was appropriate. However, at the very least for the purposes of transparency, reproducibility and adherence to ARRIVE guidelines this information should either way be clearly stated.

Similar to what was observed by Carbone et al.8, AADCS (a score for completeness of anesthetic and analgesic regimen description, and animal details) was not typically found to be correlated with JIF and JCI. However, the exception was for Japanese studies where a moderate correlation was observed. The nearest neighbor method was employed to extract JIF and JCI data from Clarivate, while Carbone et al. used 2013 Journal impact for articles published for all years. The method used in this paper considers the annual change in these indices, and therefore may potentially enhance the sensitivity of correlation analysis. Future studies could consider stratifying the data by geographical location or journal publishers, as specific animal ethics policies may differ9,10.

The lack of correlation between AADCS and JIF/JCI suggests that the adherence of ARRIVE guidelines does not appear to affect acceptance of articles by highly ranked journals, despite being in-use for more than a decade. This also aligns with a report by Baker et al.11 indicating that the ARRIVE guidelines have not yet resulted in an enhancement of reporting quality of post-surgical analgesics. The latest ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 were published in 2020, which contain updates to promote greater uplift of transparency and reproducibility in research using animals. It would be interesting for future studies to assess the impact of the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0, and systematically explore potential factors that may be barriers or facilitators for adherence to these guidelines. While many journals require authors to adhere to the ARRIVE guidelines, a different approach may be required by Editors to ensure these requirements have been met prior to accepting articles for publication. Authors should therefore always include descriptions of surgical procedures, anesthetics, and/or analgesics regimens to better facilitate research reproducibility in either the methods or supplementary sections. Non-adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines may not only potentially affect the quality, transparency and reproducibility of the study, but also pose potential ethical and welfare concerns.

As for pharmacological agents use pattern, the current study identified that isoflurane was the most used anesthetic, followed by pentobarbital and ketamine/xylazine. Buprenorphine was found to be commonly used for peri-operative analgesia. This pattern is comparable to that reported by King et al.6 for craniotomy and was most strongly demonstrated in the data from our research in 2019. The use of multimodal analgesia was extremely low (1.4%) for rodent orthopedic surgeries when indirectly compared to the reported use in rodent craniotomy surgeries for 2009 and 2019 (1.6% and 5.7%, respectively). The low levels of reported multimodal analgesia use in biomedical research could be attributed to a different understanding and perspectives of pain management between researchers and clinical veterinarians12. Availability of multiple analgesics, dosing frequencies, and associated costs might also contribute to the reduced use of multimodal analgesia for laboratory animals. All three potential factors can potentially be partially addressed in the early planning phases of experimental design, in funding requests, use of long-acting drugs13, and/or newer drug formulation or drug delivery approach. More importantly, while guidelines for rodent pain management are available (e.g. veterinary textbooks)14,15, they often target veterinarian readers and are not always easy to access16 or interpreted by non-veterinary researchers17. Although many institutions provide their own guidance for researchers, a lack of understanding of the concepts underlying these guidelines may hinder their effective implementation, especially for multimodal analgesia. Engaging animal ethics committees, appropriately qualified veterinarians, and biomedical researchers in the development of institutional guidelines on analgesic and anesthetic could be helpful to promote increased uptake of appropriate multimodal analgesia and application of improved anesthetic techniques to improve welfare and research outcomes18.

It is worth noting that we retrieved a large proportion of studies investigating pain management effect of novel chemicals and drug delivery technologies, where the use of routine analgesics is withheld may be contraindication. Regardless, the intentional withholding of analgesics should be discussed and reported, as it is the important information for future researchers to consider both from a scientific and welfare perspective. A strong justification to deviate from adhering to the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 or veterinary clinical practices, should be supported by experimental data rather than an assumption. For example, if there are concerns that the use of analgesia is prohibited due to an undesirable impact on research outcomes, this must be supported by robust experimental evidence. The evidence should show the use of pain relief is a genuine confounder of importance in research and clinical outcomes rather than merely a difference in results. Given the presence of pain can be its own experimental confounder and of considerable ethical concern, it is critical to determine the difference between the two scenarios.

This study is not without limitations. It focuses solely on the reporting of anesthetic and analgesic use, and the patterns illustrated are based only on studies that reported this information. This may introduce bias, as articles that did not report the use of anesthetics or analgesics may still have utilized them, which our analysis cannot capture. Therefore, the anesthesia and analgesia use pattern from our analysis should be interpreted with care. Moreover, our study did not assess the adequacy of the regimens listed nor did it assess if authors provided sufficient description of experimental protocols to allow full reproducibility of their research. These aspects were out of scope for the current review but are also important topics to be addressed in future studies. This will very likely require extensive full text review and therefore will be labor intensive. Natural language processing technology may be considered for this type of systematic review.

In conclusion, scientific journals, researchers, and animal ethics committees should be encouraged to endorse the implementation of the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 requirements of using and disclosing analgesic and anesthetic regimens for better research, data reproducibility and animal welfare. Adhering to these guidelines would help alleviate ethical concerns and better support the social license of research with animals. More studies should find ways to apply better veterinary practices in analgesia and anesthesia (including multimodal methods) as well as investigate options to optimize their use in laboratory rodent surgeries.
