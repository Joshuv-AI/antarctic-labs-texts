Mobile phone-based plasmodium parasites stage detection from Giemsa stained blood smear by convolutional neural networks
Authors: Hilal Bedir, Mükremin Özkan Arslan, Muhammet Mustafa Akıner, Murat Öztürk, Zihni Onur Uygun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasmodium vivax is a malaria parasite with a broad geographic distribution worldwide. The unique biological characteristics of P. vivax, such as early gametocytogenesis and its latent hypnozoite stage, make it more difficult to control compared to P. falciparum. Malaria remains a significant global health concern, particularly in regions with limited diagnostic infrastructure. This study aims to develop a computer-assisted method for characterizing and classifying malaria parasites using a machine learning approach based on light microscopic images of peripheral blood smears. One of the major challenges in malaria diagnostics is the inadequacy of current detection methods. To address this, the study introduces a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based pipeline for the automated detection and staging of malaria infections from Giemsa-stained blood smear images. The dataset used in this study was annotated into four classes: Ring Form, Trophozoite, Schizont, and Uninfected Red Blood Cells (RBCs), encompassing diverse staining qualities and morphological variations. The dataset was divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. The CNN achieved an overall classification accuracy of 92.4%, with precision, recall, and F1-scores exceeding 0.90 across all classes. Statistical metrics, including mean accuracy (92.4% ± 2.1%), precision (93.1% ± 1.8%), and recall (92.8% ± 1.9%), demonstrated the robustness of the model. Class-specific analysis revealed that the Schizont stage achieved the highest classification accuracy (94.7%), while the Ring Form stage showed slightly lower performance (91.2%), likely due to inherent morphological overlaps with early Trophozoite forms. Visualizations, including confusion matrices and class probability distribution overlays, provided detailed insights into the model’s decision-making processes. The pipeline was further evaluated using cross-validation techniques, showing high reliability across various dataset splits. This approach offers scalability and adaptability, with the potential for deployment in real-world diagnostic workflows, particularly in resource-constrained settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-025-08535-8.Keywords: Parasite, Plasmodium vivax, Malaria, Convolutional neural networks, Xception, MobileNet

Introduction

Malaria is one of the most devastating infectious diseases, caused by obligate intracellular protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium and transmitted to humans through the bite of female Anopheles mosquitoes (Madhu et al. 2023; Boit and Patil 2024). Six Plasmodium species have been identified as the causative agents of malaria: Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium malariae, Plasmodium ovale curtisi, Plasmodium ovale wallikeri, and Plasmodium knowlesi (Lalremruata et al. 2017; Ansari et al. 2016). The complex life cycle of Plasmodium parasites encompasses a sexual phase (sporogony) within mosquitoes and an asexual phase (schizogony) in humans, both of which are pivotal for sustaining the infection (Fig. 1) (Kariuki and Williams 2020). P. falciparum and P. vivax are the most prevalent species that infect humans (Naing et al. 2014). P. vivax demonstrates the unique ability to remain dormant in human liver cells as hypnozoites, which can reactivate and cause recurring infections. This biological feature, combined with its ability to invade Duffy-positive red blood cells, contributes to its epidemiological patterns. While P. vivax has a broad geographical distribution, its presence in sub-Saharan Africa is limited due to the high prevalence of Duffy-negative populations in this region (Howick et al. 2019).

Life cycle of Plasmodium vivax. Malaria infection begins when an infected female Anopheles mosquito bites a person, injecting Plasmodium parasites in the form of sporozoites into the bloodstream. The sporozoites quickly travel to the liver, where they multiply asexually in liver cells over a period of 8 to 18 days without causing any symptoms. This structure, called a schizont, contains multiple nuclei, each surrounded by cytoplasm and organized into individual units known as merozoites. A single sporozoite can produce between 10,000 and 30,000 merozoites. In P. vivax and Plasmodium ovale, some schizonts enter a dormant phase known as hypnozoites. These hypnozoites can remain inactive in liver cells for months or even years, eventually causing relapses characteristic of tertian malaria. Subsequently, thousands of newly formed merozoites are released into the bloodstream and invade red blood cells (RBCs). The parasites are transported within the RBCs as they circulate. After a 48-h cycle, the infected RBCs (PRBCs) rupture, releasing new merozoites and perpetuating the blood stage of the cycle, thus advancing the disease. Some infected RBCs exit the cycle of asexual reproduction. Instead of replicating, the merozoites in these cells develop into sexual forms of the parasite, called gametocytes, which circulate in the bloodstream. Malaria remains a severe global health challenge: the World Malaria Report 2024 records ≈ 263 million clinical cases and 597,000 deaths for 2023, figures that have risen slightly since the COVID-19 plateau years (World malaria report 2024). While Plasmodium falciparum is the leading cause of malaria-related mortality, P. vivax accounts for 3.5% of global malaria cases and is the most common malaria parasite causing clinical disease outside Africa, particularly in South and Southeast Asia, the Western Pacific, and Latin America (Kochar et al. 2005; Guerra et al. 2010). Growing evidence suggests that P. vivax malaria is less benign than previously assumed. Furthermore, it is the most widely distributed species of human malaria (Guerra et al. 2010). The relatively lower mortality rate of P. vivax is often attributed to its lower parasitemia levels compared to P. falciparum; however, severe complications such as anemia and respiratory distress are increasingly recognized, especially in vulnerable populations (Kevin Baird 2013).

The main strategy for malaria control is early and accurate diagnosis followed by effective treatment, which is essential for efficient disease management and malaria surveillance (Berzosa et al. 2018). The WHO recommends the use of RDTs (Rapid Diagnostic Tests) and microscopy for routine case management (WHO 2023). The microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained blood smears remains the gold standard for diagnosing malaria, providing both parasite detection and density estimation (Ramos-Briceño et al. 2025). However, this method requires trained personnel and significant manual effort. In contrast, RDTs offer a simpler and quicker alternative, relying on the detection of specific antigens such as HRP-2 and pLDH. These tests have improved access to malaria diagnosis in resource-limited settings (WHO 2017). Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) offer high sensitivity in detecting low-density malaria infections (below 1 parasite/µL); however, their high cost and complexity have limited their application primarily to research settings. Therefore, the use of NAATs is recommended for research purposes only (Miguel et al. 2013).

The recent leap in AI-based diagnostic methodologies has significantly improved both the sensitivity and precision of detecting diseases such as malaria in clinical samples. The basis for this advancement is the integration of deep-learning architectures most notably convolutional neural networks (CNNs), as used in this study, which excel at recognizing and classifying image features. These algorithms are trained on extensive, annotated datasets, allowing them to learn subtle morphological distinctions that might otherwise require years of specialized training to discern (Tan and Liang 2023). Consequently, even medical practitioners or researchers who are not experts in microscopy can harness these tools to analyze blood smears with a level of accuracy that is comparable to seasoned specialists. A pivotal aspect of effective AI-driven malaria diagnostics lies in robust image preprocessing strategies. Techniques such as Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) and Gaussian filtering enhance image quality by augmenting local contrast and mitigating high-frequency noise, respectively (Zuiderveld 1994; Ryan and Walochnik 2025; Gonzalez 2019). This preprocessing not only makes parasitic inclusions more conspicuous but also reduces the variability introduced by diverse staining protocols and inconsistent imaging conditions factors that often hinder consistent diagnostic results across different laboratories and geographies. By systematically refining the input data, these preprocessing methods help CNN models focus on salient features, such as the size, shape, and pigmentation of parasitic inclusions, thus improving classification accuracy and overall model robustness. Moreover, the deployment of AI-based systems aligns particularly well with the public health priorities outlined by global health agencies, as these technologies can be adapted to mobile or low-cost devices. In regions where resource constraints limit the availability of trained microscopists, portable AI-assisted tools offer a practical route to more timely and accurate case identification. This capability supports not only faster clinical decision-making enabling the prompt initiation of antimalarial treatments but also more efficient disease surveillance. The ability to systematically record, store, and analyze large volumes of image data facilitates real-time epidemiological monitoring, which is critical for informing targeted control measures. As a result, AI-fueled diagnostics transcend conventional laboratory boundaries, bringing reliable malaria detection to under-served communities that are most vulnerable to morbidity and mortality associated with delayed or inaccurate diagnoses. In conclusion, the union of advanced deep learning models with carefully combined with image preprocessing techniques has the potential to revolutionize malaria diagnostics by minimizing human-dependent errors and optimizing workflow efficiency. Through rigorous training and validation on diverse datasets, these models promise a level of reproducibility previously unattainable by manual methods alone. As the field continues to evolve, further incorporation of cutting-edge approaches ranging from transfer learning to domain adaptation will likely enhance the adaptability of AI-based diagnostic systems, ensuring their sustained effectiveness across multiple settings and parasitic species.

Methods

A dataset of Giemsa-stained blood smear images was curated for the detection and classification of malaria infection stages, including Ring Form, Trophozoite, Schizont, and Uninfected RBC. Images were captured at 100X magnification using a light microscope. The dataset was preprocessed and split into training, validation, and test sets in the following proportions: 70%, 15%, and 15%, respectively. Stratified splitting ensured equal representation of classes in each subset. All images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels to match the input requirements of the convolutional neural network (CNN). Prior to model training, a series of preprocessing steps were applied to the images to enhance the quality and extract features critical for classification. Gaussian filter (Gonzalez 2019) (5 × 5) was applied to reduce noise and smoothen the images. Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) (Zuiderveld 1994) was employed to enhance contrast in regions with uneven staining, using a clip limit of 2.0 and an 8 × 8 tile grid size. Binary thresholding (Gonzalez 2019) segmented foreground (cells) from the background, providing a clean binary representation. Opening and closing operations were performed using a 3 × 3 kernel to eliminate noise and refine the boundaries of segmented regions. All pixel values were normalized (Ryan and Walochnik 2025) to a range of [0, 1] to ensure uniformity across the dataset. A custom CNN was designed for the classification of malaria infection stages (LeCun et al. 1998; Howard et al. 2017; Chollet 2016). The architecture consisted of the following layers;

The training process used Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba 2014) an initial learning rate of 0.0001. Categorical cross-entropy was utilized to handle multi-class classification. Data augmentation (Shorten and Khoshgoftaar 2019), including random rotations, flips, and zooming, was applied to improve generalization. Model performance was monitored using a separate validation set, and early stopping was employed to mitigate overfitting. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets using stratified splitting to maintain class distributions (Kumar et al. 2024). The train-test split ratio was set to 80–20, with the test set further divided into validation and test subsets of equal proportions. This ensured that the training process and evaluation were conducted on mutually exclusive data. The class distributions across splits were visualized using bar charts to verify integrity and consistency. The trained models were evaluated using the test set based on the following metrics. Accuracy is the proportion of correctly classified samples. Precision and Recall are class-wise evaluations of predictive performance. F1-Score is a harmonic mean of precision and recall to balance these metrics (Powers 2020). Statistical metrics are worst, best, mean, standard deviation, median, and mode accuracy were calculated across different splits and models. The model’s prediction results were visualized using bar charts representing probabilities for each class. This complemented the classification output and facilitated better interpretability of model confidence levels.

The preprocessing pipeline implemented in this study was crucial for enhancing image quality and preparing the data for robust classification of malaria infection stages. Preprocessing addressed several challenges inherent in blood smear imaging, such as noise, variable staining, and artifacts, ensuring that the input data was clean and standardized for the convolutional neural network (CNN). Noise in blood smear images, introduced by the staining process or inconsistencies in microscopy, can obscure key features such as chromatin and cytoplasmic structures. Gaussian blurring was applied as the initial step in the preprocessing pipeline to mitigate high-frequency noise while preserving critical edges. Results showed that blurring effectively smoothed background variations without significantly altering cellular boundaries. For instance, uneven staining artifacts in the Schizont stage were reduced, ensuring that the CNN focused on biologically relevant features like merozoite clusters. Giemsa-stained images exhibit uneven contrast due to variations in staining intensity and slide thickness. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) was employed to enhance local contrast, particularly in underexposed regions, without amplifying noise. The application of CLAHE markedly improved the visibility of key features, such as hemozoin pigments and nuclear material, which are critical for distinguishing between Ring Form, Trophozoite, and Schizont stages. CLAHE enhancement was particularly beneficial for images with faint staining, as it rendered subtle features more discernible. Quantitatively, contrast enhancement led to a 12% improvement in classification accuracy during preliminary testing compared to models trained on raw images. Binary thresholding was applied to segment foreground regions (cells) from the background. This step was essential for isolating individual cells and reducing irrelevant background information. Results indicated that thresholding effectively delineated cells in most images, particularly for Uninfected RBCs, which typically have well-defined boundaries. However, thresholding posed challenges for stages like Trophozoite, where chromatin material extends beyond the typical boundaries of the erythrocyte. These limitations were mitigated by subsequent morphological operations. Morphological opening and closing operations were applied to refine the segmented images, eliminating small artifacts and filling gaps in cellular boundaries. Watershed segmentation separates overlapping cells and highlights their boundaries (Fig. 3). These operations were particularly effective for addressing residual noise after thresholding and ensuring that irregularly shaped features, such as amoeboid structures in the Trophozoite stage, were accurately represented. The results showed that morphological processing enhanced the overall clarity of segmented regions, which translated into more consistent feature extraction by the CNN. For instance, the inclusion of these operations led to a 9% increase in F1-scores for Schizont detection. The model architecture implemented in this study was designed to classify malaria infection stages with high accuracy and interpretability. Conclusion

The simple application of deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), in analyzing Giemsa-stained blood smears for malaria detection and infection staging demonstrates a significant advancement in computational pathology and diagnostic automation. This study integrates an end-to-end pipeline encompassing dataset curation, pre-processing, training, testing, and visualization, showcasing the potential of machine learning in enhancing the speed and accuracy of malaria diagnostics. The CNN model achieved robust classification performance across all infection stages, including Ring Form, Trophozoite, and Schizont stages, as well as uninfected red blood cells (RBCs). The precision, recall, and F1-scores consistently exceeded acceptable thresholds, ensuring the model’s reliability in real-world diagnostic scenarios. This not only enhances user trust in the model’s decisions but also provides a layer of clinical validation by correlating visual activations with known pathological markers, such as chromatin patterns and hemozoin pigment distribution. This study underscores the transformative potential of artificial intelligence in modern healthcare by demonstrating its application in malaria diagnosis and infection staging. In summary, the Xception-centred pipeline presented here achieves 92% accuracy on four clinically relevant P. vivax stages while executing inference in under 30 ms on a mobile-class GPU. Two factors limit immediate deployment. First, the dataset—although balanced—contains only 1600 images sourced from a single slide preparation protocol, curtailing morphological diversity. Second, ring-form versus early trophozoite overlap still accounts for 68% of residual misclassifications. To address these gaps we are currently curating a 10,000-image, multi-center repository and exploring domain-adaptation techniques such as style-transfer augmentation and self-supervised pre-training. Future studies will also investigate Vision Transformers for improved global-context modelling and integrate an uncertainty-aware post-processor to flag borderline cases for manual confirmation. Collectively, these steps aim to translate the model from proof-of-concept to a deployable decision-support tool in resource-limited clinics.
