The Bacteria-Derived dsRNA Was Used for Spray-Induced Gene Silencing for Rice False Smut Control
Authors: Yujie Zhang, Mengyao Wu, Han Zhang, Yu Li, Yufu Wang, Fanzhu Meng, Wei Zhao, Shun He, Weixiao Yin, Chao-Xi Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFalse smut caused by Ustilaginoidea virens is one of the most destructive diseases in rice. The disease is primarily controlled with fungicides, leading to the development of fungicide resistance. Although spray-induced gene silencing (SIGS) has been utilized for disease management, it has not been applied to control rice false smut. In this study, we introduce a novel approach involving the in vivo synthesis and exogenous application of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to manage rice false smut disease. The UvCYP51, UvBI-1, and UvbZIP11 genes were selected as target genes and highly efficient fragments for gene silencing were identified through screening of silencing transformants. Although direct dsRNA uptake by U. virens was not observed, in vivo synthesis and application of dsRNA to rice effectively reduced the expression of target genes. Treatment with dsRNA targeting the genes resulted in a decrease in smut balls, providing a promising disease management strategy against rice false smut.Keywords: Ustilaginoidea virens, rice false smut, double-stranded RNA, in vivo, spray-induced gene silencing, RNA interference, disease management

Introduction

RNA interference (RNAi) is a biological process that inhibits gene expression and translation by targeting mRNA (mRNA) with small RNA molecules (sRNA).1,2 When double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) from either internal or external sources enter the cell, they are broken down into small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) measuring 21–24 nucleotides in length by the ribonuclease Dicer or Dicer-like proteins. Additionally, endogenous hairpin RNAs (hpRNAs) can also be cleaved into siRNAs. These siRNAs then bind to Argonaute (AGO) proteins to form the core of RNA-Induced Silencing Complex (RISC), which induces the degradation of complementary mRNAs or inhibits translation, ultimately leading to gene silencing.1,3,4

RNAi serves as a natural defense mechanism that was found in a wide range of eukaryotic organisms, such as fungi, animals, and plants.2 In plants, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are produced to specifically target and degrade viral RNA, ultimately preventing virus replication.5 These siRNAs can act as mobile silencing signals that move between host plants and parasitic organisms,6,7 offering a new approach to managing plant diseases.8 Host-induced gene silencing (HIGS) has been utilized to target fungal glucanosyltransferase gene mRNAs, resulting in transgenic plants exhibiting resistance to Blumeria graminis.9 Similarly, HIGS targeting fungal cytochrome P450 lanosterol C-14α-demethylase (CYP51) genes confers plant resistance against Fusarium species.10 Nevertheless, challenges with HIGS include the absence of standardized transformation protocols and public concerns regarding genetically modified crops.

The spray-induced gene silencing (SIGS) has recently been demonstrated as an effective method for disease control.11 Barley treated with in vitro synthesized dsRNA targeting three fungal ergosterol biosynthesis genes (CYP51A, CYP51B, and CYP51C) exhibited efficient inhibition of the necrotrophic fungus Fusarium graminearum.12 Importantly, these dsRNAs were able to translocate through the vascular system of plant, reaching distal regions and still inhibiting fungal growth even in unsprayed areas.12 Spraying dsRNAs targeting specific genes on host plants proved effective in protecting crops from gray mold disease for up to 8 days.13 Additionally, the application of Myo5-dsRNA has shown promise in reducing phenamacril resistance and pathogenicity of Fusarium asiaticum, with varying silencing efficiencies observed for different gene fragments.14 Different eukaryotic microbe species display varying efficiencies in dsRNA uptake, offering valuable insights for SIGS in disease control.15

Although dsRNA synthesis in vitro is relatively fast and effective, the high cost of this process is a significant limiting factor. Bacteria were genetically modified to produce specific dsRNA fragments in high quantities, which were then fed to Caenorhabditis elegans to induce RNAi phenotypes.5,16 The HT115 strain, exhibiting the optimal expression efficiency, was identified from a range of strains, providing a basis for further research. An efficient and cost-effective method for dsRNA expression and extraction has been established using Trizol and 75% alcohol extraction.17 Although this method has been used in insect control, its application in controlling of fungal diseases has been less common.

Rice as one of the most crucial food crops globally, requires effective management of disease. One of the destructive fungal diseases affecting rice worldwide is false smut, caused by Ustilaginoidea virens. In addition to causing yield losses, this fungus also produces mycotoxins that pose a threat to both human and animal health.18 Although SIGS has been successfully used to manage rice blast and sheath blight,15,19 its application in combating rice false smut remains unexplored. Research on the molecular mechanisms underlying pathogenicity of U. virens has identified potential target genes for SIGS.20 Bax inhibitor-1 (BI-1) is an evolutionarily conserved protein and can suppress cell death induced by proapoptotic protein Bax. Our previous study found that UvBI-1 is a negative regulator of mycelial growth and is essential for pathogenicity of U. virens.21 Furthermore, the cytochrome P450 lanosterol C-14α-demethylase (CYP51) serves as the target for demethylation inhibitors (DMIs) fungicides, with the mutation from tyrosine to histidine at position 137 (Y137H) being a key factor in U. virens fungicide resistance.22−24 The CYP51 genes were used as target genes in F. graminearum and fungal growth was inhibited after the application of dsRNA.12 Additionally, although the data have not yet been published, our previous study showed that the bZIP transcription factor UvbZIP11, which is homologue of M. oryzae MoAP1,25 contributed to the pathogenicity of U. virens in our previous study. Therefore, UvBI-1, UvCYP51, and UvbZIP11 were selected as target genes.

In this study, we identified the most efficient silencing fragments for each of the three target genes. The highly efficient fragments were then assembled into a tandem array and subsequently inserted into the vector for dsRNA expression, and HT115 was utilized to produce the dsRNA. After treatment with dsRNA, there was a significant reduction in the expression of the target genes and the number of smut balls were significantly reduced, demonstrating the potential of SIGS for controlling of rice false smut. Our finding provides valuable insights and a theoretical foundation for the management of other diseases.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Culture Conditions

The U. virens wild-type strain JS60–2 was used for transformation. To determine the growth, plugs of 3 mm diameter were cultured on PSA (200 g/L of potato, 20 g/L of sucrose, and 15 g/L of agar) or PDA (200 g/L of potato, 20 g/L of d-glucose, and 15 g/L of agar) plates at 27 °C for 14 days. The morphology of the colonies was observed and photographed. The size of the colonies was measured using the crossover method, with the agar diameter subtracted when measuring the colony diameter. The resistance to fungicide was assayed after incubation at 27 °C for 14 days on PSA plates with or without 0.1 μg/mL tebuconazole. The inhibition rate was calculated using the formula: inhibition rate = (average of strain colony diameters on PSA – average of strain colony diameters on PSA with chemical added)/average of strain colony diameters on PSA × 100%. All experiments were repeated three times with three replicates each time. Mycelia were harvested from 7-day-old cultures in liquid PSB (PSA without agar) for genomic DNA and total RNA extractions.

Gene Expression Analysis

Total RNAs were extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), the cDNA was synthesized with Hifair II first Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, China). The reaction mixtures were diluted 10 times and used as templates for quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.). Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with the β-tubulin gene fragment in U. virens serving as a reference as previous studies mentioned.26,27 The primers are listed in Table S1. The Cts (threshold cycle) of these primers are approximately 26, and there is no clear upward trend in the amplification curve, and the inflection point is clear.

Fungal Transformations and Transformants Verification

The pSilent-1 vector was used for the construction of silencing vectors,28 by inserting complementary sequences were inserted on either side of the intron. These vectors, containing the corresponding positive and negative fragments flanking the intron, were introduced into U. virens protoplasts through PEG-mediated transformation.29 Transformants that can grow on PSA supplemented with 180 μg/mL of hygromycin B were selected. Genomic DNA was extracted from mycelium using the cetyltrimethylammonium-based (CTAB) method and served as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) verification.

dsRNA Synthesis In Vitro and Observation of Fungal Uptake

The T7 promoter sequence was fused into the 5′ and 3′ ends of fragments through PCR with primers listed in Table S1. The fragments containing T7 promoters were used for in vitro dsRNA synthesis. The dsRNA was synthesized using the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit (Vazyme, China) following the instructions. For the assessment of fungal uptake of dsRNA, GFP-dsRNA was labeled using the Fluorescein RNA Labeling Mix (Merck, USA) containing Fluorescein-12-UTP following the instructions. A mixture of 5 μL each of 150 ng/μL GFP-dsRNA and a suspension of 1 × 106 conidia/mL of U. virens or B. cinerea was thoroughly combined and cultured on PSA medium directly applied to the surface of microscope slides, followed by incubation at 27 °C for 24 or 48 h. Prior to fluorescence observation, the germinated conidia or mycelium were treated with micrococcal nuclease (New England Biolabs, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min to degrade surface dsRNA. The fluorescence signal was visualized using a confocal microscope (Leica DM6B, Germany).

U. virens Treatment with Synthesized dsRNA In Vitro

The plugs were inserted into the rice leaf medium and shaken at 160 rpm at 27 °C to stimulate conidia formation.30 After 5–6 days, 10 mL of culture fluid was transferred into 50 mL of PSB and left to culture for an additional 2 days. Subsequently, 500 μL of culture fluid was collected into a tube and in vitro synthesized dsRNA was added at a final concentration of 20 ng/μL. The mixture was then incubated for 24 or 48 h at 27 °C with continuous shaking. Total RNA was extracted to detect the expression of the target gene.

dsRNA Synthesis In Vivo and Purification

The L4440 vector is a valuable tool for in vivo dsRNA production.31 The GFP and CYP-bZIP-BI fragments were amplified and inserted into the L4440 vector using the One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). These recombinant vectors were then transformed into E. coli HT115 (DE3) competent cells. The dsRNA production and purification procedures were carried out as previously described.31 The 1 mL of bacterial liquid was added into 100 mL of LB medium (20 μg/mL ampicillin and 10 μg/mL tetracycline) and cultured at 37 °C until OD600 reached to 0.4–0.5. The inducer agent isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG) was added at a final concentration of 0.4 mM, and the bacteria were cultured for an additional 6 h. The bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 2 min at 10 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded, and 4 mL of phosphate-buffered saline buffer (Servicebio, China) containing 75% ethanol was added, followed by ultrasonication. After a 5 min incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was removed. Next, 1 mL of 150 mM NaCl solution was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 1 h. Subsequent centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min was performed, and the supernatant was collected into an RNase-free tube.

Rice Treatment with dsRNA and Inoculation with U. virens

A susceptible rice cultivar (Wanxian-98) was used for inoculation. The dsRNA was diluted to a concentration of 100 ng/μL, and 2 mL of this solution was injected into the upper part of the rice panicle. After 12 h, the approximately 5 mL of conidia suspensions with a concentration of 5 × 106/mL were injected into the same location for inoculation following the previously described method.32 The rice plants were then kept in a greenhouse at 27 °C with 90% to 100% relative humidity (RH) for 7 days before being transferred to an environment of 27 °C with 80% RH until the appearance of smut balls. Symptoms were observed and the number of smut balls was counted 21 days after inoculation. The experiments were independently repeated three times, each time with a minimum of 8 panicles per inoculation.

Results

The Diagram of the Gene Fragments Used for RNAi Constructions

In consideration of their contribution to virulence, UvCYP51, UvBI-1, and UvbZIP11 were selected as target genes for SIGS. Upon application of exogenous dsRNA, it was noted that distinct gene fragments displayed varying levels of silencing effectiveness.33 Therefore, the silencing efficiency of these different fragments was initially assessed through the RNAi method. Specifically, four fragments from UvCYP51, four from UvbZIP11, and two from UvBI-1 were selected and individually inserted into the pSilent-1 vector, resulting in the generation of dsRNA sequences with hairpin structures (Figure 1A,B).

The PtrpC and TtrpC indicate the promoter and terminator of the trpC gene in Aspergillus nidulans, respectively. Screening of Efficient RNAi Fragments

RNAi constructions were introduced into the wild-type U. virens strain JS60-2 to evaluate the silencing efficiencies of target gene fragments. Transformants were identified through PCR validation with specific primers (Figure S1). Three positive transformants were selected for each fragment. The transformants were then assessed for the silencing efficiency of each gene fragment by measuring the expression levels of target genes. Two transformants per fragment were selected for RNA extraction and expression analysis using qRT-PCR. Among the transformants, UvCYP51-3-RNAi, UvbZIP11-4-RNAi, and UvBI-1-2-RNAi transformants showed the lowest expression levels of their respective genes (Figure 2), indicating efficient silencing. These findings suggest that these gene fragments in the transformants are suitable for subsequent dsRNA synthesis.

Expression detection of the target genes in silenced transformants. Total RNAs were extracted from the transformants and the expression of the target genes was determined in the silenced transformants by qRT-PCR after reverse transcription. “*” indicates p < 0.05, “**” indicates p < 0.01 and “**” indicates p < 0.001. The Student’s t test (unpaired, two-sided) was conducted to compare the results with those obtained from the wild-type JS60-2 strain. The results were consistent across three independent experiments.

Phenotypic Determination of Silencing Transformants

CYP51 is the target protein of DMI fungicides, and deletion of FgCYP51A in F. graminearum results in increased sensitivity to tebuconazole.34 The knockout mutants of UvBI-1 exhibit increased mycelial growth,21 while the mutants of UvbZIP11 show decreased growth. To determine whether the transformants with the highest silencing efficiency also displayed the corresponding phenotypes, the resistance of UvCYP51-3-RNAi transformants to tebuconazole and the growth of UvbZIP11-4-RNAi and UvBI-1-2-RNAi transformants were assessed. The colony diameters of wild-type strain JS60-2, silencing transformants UvCYP51-3-RNAi-1 and UvCYP51-3-RNAi-2 were measured on PSA medium with or without 0.1 μg/mL of tebuconazole, and the inhibition rate of mycelial growth was calculated. The results indicated that the silencing transformants UvCYP51–3-RNAi-1 and UvCYP51-3-RNAi-2 showed higher inhibition rates compared to the wild-type, suggesting increased sensitivity to tebuconazole (Figure 3A,B). Additionally, colony diameters of wild-type strain JS60–2, two UvbZIP11–4-RNAi transformants, and two UvBI-1-2-RNAi transformants were measured on PSA and PDA. The growth of UvbZIP11-4-RNAi transformants was significantly reduced compared to wild-type JS60-2, while the growth of UvBI-1-2-RNAi transformants was significantly increased (Figure 3C–F), consistent with previous studies.

Identification of Exogenous dsRNA Uptake by U. virens

The uptake of dsRNA by fungi plays a critical role in the efficacy of SIGS for controlling plant diseases.15 To investigate the ability of U. virens to take up dsRNA, conidia were incubated with fluorescein-labeled green fluorescent protein (GFP) dsRNA synthesized in vitro on PSA coated on a glass slide. After 24 or 48 h, micrococcal nuclease was used for a 30 min treatment to eliminate any labeled dsRNAs. Subsequently, the fluorescence was examined using a confocal fluorescence microscope. No fluorescence signal was detected in U. virens, indicating its inability to uptake environmental dsRNA (Figure 4A). In contrast, B. cinerea showed fluorescence signals, suggesting efficient RNA uptake. Despite the lack of dsRNA uptake by U. virens, an investigation was conducted to assess whether the application of dsRNA could interfere with the expression of target genes. Highly efficient sequences targeting specific genes were selected as templates for dsRNA synthesis. Using the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit, CYP51-bZIP11-BI-1-dsRNA was synthesized in vitro (Figure 4B). The CYP-bZIP-BI dsRNA was then incubated with a suspension of conidia and mycelium for 48 h. Following this incubation, RNAs were extracted, reverse transcribed, and used for qRT-PCR analysis. Similar to the GFP-dsRNA treatment, the CYP51-bZIP11-BI-1-dsRNA treatment did not lead to a decrease in the expression of the respective target genes in U. virens (Figure 4C). These findings suggest that exogenous dsRNA cannot be taken up by U. virens, and therefore does not reduce the expression of target genes.

Determination of exogenous dsRNA uptake by U. virens. (A) Fluorescence microscopy of the uptake of exogenous dsRNA by U. virens. Fluorescein-labeled GFP-dsRNA was incubated with conidia of B. cinerea or U. virens for 24 or 48 h, respectively. Subsequently, the samples were treated with micrococcal nuclease (MNase) for 30 min prior to examination using confocal microscopy laser scanner. (B) Synthesis dsRNA of CYP51-bZIP11-BI-1 in vitro. Highly efficient silencing fragments of the target genes were selected as templates for the synthesizing dsRNA. The synthesis was conducted in vitro with the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit. (C) The expression levels of target genes in U. virens. Mycelium and conidia suspensions were incubated with CYP-bZIP-BI dsRNA for either 24 or 48 h. Subsequently, total RNA was extracted, and the expression levels of UvCYP51, UvbZIP11, and UvBI-1 were assessed through qRT-PCR.

Exogenous Application of dsRNA Decreases Smut Ball Disease

Although U. virens cannot directly take up dsRNA, siRNAs can successfully migrate into U. virens.35 External dsRNA can be translocated into plant cells and cleaved into sRNAs by plant Dicer or Dicer-like proteins, which can then be taken up by fungi. Previous studies have shown that sprayed dsRNA can translocate and induce gene silencing in distal (unsprayed) rice leaves.19 This finding led us to investigate the use of dsRNA to treat rice, aiming to validate whether externally applied dsRNA targeting U. virens genes can reduce disease incidence. Due to the high cost of synthesizing dsRNA in vitro, three tandem fragments were inserted into the L4440 vector, which was subsequently transformed into E. coli HT115 (DE3) strain to produce dsRNA. The dsRNA was purified using a previously described method31 (Figure S2). To evaluate the efficacy of dsRNA in reducing U. virens pathogenicity, the purified HT115-CYP-bZIP-BI-dsRNA was diluted to 100 ng/μL and infiltrated into rice panicles at the late booting stage (3 to 5 days before heading). After a 12-h dsRNA treatment, the rice panicles were inoculated with a suspension of U. virens conidia and mycelium. Total RNA was extracted from the panicles 48 h after inoculation, and the expression of the target genes in U. virens was analyzed. The expression levels of UvCYP51, UvbZIP11, and UvBI-1 were significantly reduced following treatment with HT115-CYP-bZIP-BI-dsRNA compared to treatment with HT115-GFP-dsRNA (Figure 5A). Furthermore, disease symptoms were assessed 21 days after inoculation, revealing a 50% reduction in smut ball formation in the HT115-CYP-bZIP-BI-dsRNA treatment group compared to the control groups treated with ddH2O and HT115-GFP-dsRNA (Figure 5B,C). These results indicate that the exogenous application of dsRNA to rice can effectively silence target genes in U. virens, leading to a decrease in its pathogenicity.

Discussion

SIGS technology, an innovative disease control method based on RNAi, has shown significant potential in managing diseases that affect fruit and vegetable crops.11 As research progresses, the potential application of this technology in managing grain crop diseases is becoming more evident. Rice, a globally important food crop, faces a significant threat to both yield and grain quality due to the frequent occurrence of diseases. The application of SIGS technology has proven to effectively silence target genes in Rhizoctonia solani, reducing the incidence of rice sheath blight.15 This establishes a scientific basis for utilizing SIGS technology in controlling rice diseases. However, there have been no reported studies on using SIGS technology to control rice false smut disease. Transgenic rice has been developed for resistance to rice false smut by targeting pathogenicity-related genes of U. virens using HIGS.35,36 Moreover, siRNA has been observed to migrate into conidia and mycelium,35 demonstrating the potential effectiveness of SIGS technology in controlling rice false smut disease. This provides a crucial theoretical and experimental foundation for further development and application of this technology.

The advantages of HIGS technology its environmentally friendly and efficiency. By inducing gene silencing in pests or pathogens within the infected transgenic crops, the reliance on chemical pesticides can be significantly reduced, thereby minimizing environmental pollution. However, HIGS transgenic crops also present certain hidden risks, such as gene drift and contamination. Consequently, the industrialization of genetically modified crops based on HIGS faces challenges due to national regulatory bottlenecks.37,38 Additionally, the process of developing a genetically modified crop and obtaining approval can take over a decade. The SIGS method resembles conventional pesticide application, wherein dsRNAs are synthesized to target specific genes and function through feeding by pests or infection by pathogens. However, because dsRNA molecules are easily to denaturation or degradation in the environment, it is essential to develop specialized formulations to enhance the stability of the active ingredient. While the production of dsRNA is a cost-effective process, commercial RNA pesticides have been successfully developed.39 Therefore, advancing the development of RNA pesticides could provide a safer and more targeted approach to combating crop pathogens.

The effectiveness of RNAi depends on the specific region of the gene. When different RNAi fragments of the Myo5 gene were tested, varying levels of resistance to phenamacril were observed.14 Similarly, four distinct RNAi fragments targeting the β2-tubulin gene in Fusarium asiaticum showed differing silencing capabilities. The most efficient RNAi fragment was selected to produce dsRNA, which was then applied to plants to enhance resistance against multiple pathogens.40 These results highlight the importance of considering different target regions within a gene for optimal silencing efficiency. Furthermore, the selection of target genes is crucial for SIGS.41 In this study, UvCYP51, UvbZIP11, and UvBI-1 were identified as target genes for SIGS. By targeting various regions within these genes, the fragments for effective gene silencing were identified through the construction of specific silencing vectors and detection expression levels of the target genes in transformants. A previous study has found that the fragment located at the 3′ end of the Myo5 gene in F. asiaticum exhibited the highest efficiency.14 It was observed that fragments near the 3′ end have significant silencing effects on all three genes, suggesting that the 3′ end of these genes may possess higher silencing efficiency.

A previous study found that the effectiveness of SIGS technology in plant disease control is dependent on the fungal RNA uptake efficiency.15 However, no uptake of exogenous dsRNA by U. virens was observed in this study, and treatment with dsRNA did not lead to changes in the expression levels of the target genes, indicating the lack of dsRNA uptake ability in U. virens. In the context of HIGS, host plant Dicer or Dicer-like proteins cleave dsRNA to produce siRNA, which is then transferred into fungi to silence target genes.9,10 Two chitin synthase genes in U. virens were selected as target genes for engineering false smut resistance in rice through HIGS. It was observed that siRNA successfully entered the fungus.35 One of the mechanisms of SIGS involves the uptake of sprayed dsRNA by the plant, which is subsequently cleaved to generate siRNA in the plant. This siRNA is then released and enters the fungi, leading to a mechanism similar to that of HIGS.11 The dsRNA can either directly enter the fungal cell and induce gene silencing or enter the host plant to indirectly silence fungal genes. Therefore, although the ability of fungi to uptake exogenous dsRNAs is crucial for SIGS, the uptake of sprayed dsRNA by plants also plays a functional role in disease control using SIGS technology. In this study, highly efficient silencing fragments were tandem and the corresponding dsRNA was synthesized using the E. coli strain HT115. After treatment with dsRNA, the expression of target genes in U. virens was significantly reduced, leading to a significant decrease in the number of smut balls. These findings highlight the effectiveness and feasibility of SIGS technology in controlling rice smut disease.

The occurrence of fungicide resistance strains is frequently observed due to strong survival pressure. In this study, nonlethal genes were targeted using SIGS technology to enhance long-term effectiveness of disease management. By inhibiting specific genes related to physiological functions of U. virens to reduce its damage in rice, rather than completely eliminating the fungus, this approach can improve the sustainability of disease management. This target gene selection strategy can impede the adaptation and resistance development of the fungus, leading to stable and long-term disease control without accelerating resistance evolution.

The stability of dsRNA plays a crucial role in the effectiveness of disease control. In its natural form, naked dsRNAs are vulnerable to damage from environmental factors such as ultraviolet light, limiting efficacy and longevity when applied in the field.42 Utilizing nanocarriers can improve the stability of dsRNA in SIGS for crop protection.43 A system known as spray-induced and nanocarrier-delivered gene silencing (SI-NDGS) has been developed to identify potentially lethal genes in the white-backed planthopper (WBPH) and can be utilized for WBPH control.44 Encapsulating of dsRNA in artificial nanovesicles (AVs) has been shown to prolong action of dsRNA for 10 days or more, providing an effective and longer-lasting protection against B. cinerea.45 Various nanoparticle carriers have been evaluated in against Rhizoctonia solani.46 The self-assembled multicomponent nanobioprotectant exhibits advantages in disease management.47 The cost synthesizing dsRNA is a significant challenge that needs to be addressed in SIGS application. In this study, the bacteria were employed for dsRNA synthesis, which could help reduce costs. Future research should focus on optimizing the extraction and purification of dsRNA from recombinant bacterial strains, as well as developing cost-effective dsRNA synthesis technologies. Additionally, exploring the formulations or nanocarriers to enhance RNA stability, uptake efficiency, and prolong protection periods is recommended.

The study focused on selecting fragments with high silencing efficiency as templates for dsRNA synthesis to control disease. Although dsRNA cannot be directly taken up, SIGS technology has been utilized for rice smut disease control through host plant-mediated generation of siRNA. Bacteria were selected for in vivo dsRNA synthesis to lower costs. The results of this research have important implications for the advancement of SIGS as a prominent disease control approach. The study presents a new strategy for managing rice false smut disease, with meaningful theoretical and practical implications.
