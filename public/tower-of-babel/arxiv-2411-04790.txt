Quantum State Preparation with Optimal T-Count
David Gosset, Robin Kothari, Kewen Wu

Abstract
How many T T gates are needed to approximate an arbitrary n n -qubit quantum state to within error ε \varepsilon ? Improving prior work of Low, Kliuchnikov, and Schaeffer, we show that the optimal asymptotic scaling is Θ ⁡ ( 2 n ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) \Theta\left(\sqrt{2^{n}\log(1/\varepsilon)}+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right) if we allow ancilla qubits. We also show that this is the optimal T T -count for implementing an arbitrary diagonal n n -qubit unitary to within error ε \varepsilon . We describe applications in which a tensor product of many single-qubit unitaries can be synthesized in parallel for the price of one.

Discussion.

In this work we have characterized the worst-case
{T}
-count of quantum states and diagonal unitaries. A challenging open question is to understand the optimal
{T}
-count for general
n
-qubit unitaries.
We can prove an
\widetilde{\Omega}(2^{n})

{T}
-count lower bound (see
Theorem

4.3
for details).
However there is a glaringly large gap between this lower bound and the best known upper bound
\widetilde{O}\left(2^{1.5n}\right)

[
33
,
39
]
. This upper bound is obtained in
[
33
]
by reducing the unitary synthesis task to that of preparing
2^{n}

n
-qubit states, each of which uses
\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{2^{n}}\right)

{T}
gates;
[
39
]
also shows how to synthesize a unitary efficiently with
O\left(\sqrt{2^{n}}\right)
calls to a
2n

O(2^{n})

{T}
gates (see
Lemma

2.1
).

While our focus is on
T
-count and our construction gives the asymptotic optimal bound, the number of Clifford gates in our state preparation circuit is
O(2^{n}\log(1/\varepsilon))
, which can be verified given the explicit description of our synthesis procedure. This bound is close to the optimal
\Omega(2^{n}/n)
lower bound. In particular, if the goal is to minimize total gate count, we can replace
Lemma

2.1
and
Remark

2.2
with the construction that achieves the
O(2^{n}/n)
gate count for Boolean functions; then our final circuit will have
O(2^{n}/n)
gates, albeit a similar amount of
T
gates.
It remains an open problem whether it is possible to achieve
O(\sqrt{2^{n}})

T
-count and
O(2^{n}/n)
total gate count at the same time.

We emphasize that our savings on the
T
-count is made possible with the use of ancillary qubits, which is also necessary by a simple counting argument.
By tuning the construction for
Lemma

2.1
with different number of ancillas and
T
-count, we believe our construction achieves near optimal tradeoffs between ancillas and
T
-count (with some logarithmic slack in
n
and
1/\varepsilon
). The best achievable tradeoff between these two metrics is still unknown.

A similarly interesting question is to study the
T
-depth in place of
T
-count. The most depth-consuming part in our construction is the synthesis of (Boolean) diagonal unitaries, which, in light of
Remark

2.2
, should be
\mathsf{poly}(n)
. The optimal
T
-depth is still open and we expect that the lower bound side will be significantly more challenging, if given unbounded ancillas.

3.1
Approximating a state to constant error:
Lemma

3.2

In this section we describe how to approximate a state to constant error (
Lemma

3.2
) and in the next section we discuss the finer approximation (
Lemma

3.4
).

To get the coarse approximation of
Lemma

3.2
, we take our target state and apply a random diagonal unitary with
\pm 1
entries followed by a binary Fourier transform (i.e., the Hadamard matrix). This does a reasonable job of flattening the amplitudes of the state, by which we mean that most of the amplitudes are now of similar magnitude. We then apply a diagonal unitary to make all the phases
+1
, which gives a state that has constant overlap with the uniform superposition
|+^{n}\rangle
. To implement this strategy we use the standard Khintchine inequality.

Fact 3.6

(Khintchine inequality
[
22
,
50
]
)
.

Let
N\geq 1
be an integer and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{N}
be arbitrary complex numbers satisfying
\sum_{j\in[N]}|\beta_{j}|^{2}=1
.
Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\E_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{N}}\bigg[\bigg|\sum_{j\in[N]}\beta_{j}\cdot x_{j}\bigg|\bigg]\leq 1.

(18)

Using
Fact

3.6
, we show how to flatten the amplitudes of a quantum state.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be an arbitrary
n
-qubit state. Let
F=H^{\otimes n}
or more generally, let
F
be any
n
-qubit unitary such that
|F_{ij}|=1/\sqrt{2^{n}}
for
i,j\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then

\E_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{\{0,1\}^{n}}}\left[\left\|F~\mathsf{diag}(x)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{1}\right]\geq\frac{\sqrt{2^{n}}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(19)

Proof.

Write
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\beta_{j}|j\rangle
with
\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\beta_{j}|^{2}=1
. Then

\displaystyle\E_{x}\left[\left\|F~\mathsf{diag}(x)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\biggl[\biggl\|\sum_{i,j}F_{ij}x_{j}\beta_{j}|i\rangle\biggr\|_{1}\biggr]

(20)

\displaystyle=\E_{x}\biggl[\sum_{i}\biggl|\sum_{j}F_{ij}x_{j}\beta_{j}\biggr|\biggr]=\sum_{i}\E_{x}\biggl[\biggl|\sum_{j}F_{ij}x_{j}\beta_{j}\biggr|\biggr],

(21)

where the last equality used the linearity of expectation. By assumption,

F_{ij}=f_{ij}/\sqrt{2^{n}}
, where
f_{ij}
is a complex number of unit modulus.
Then
f_{ij}\beta_{j}
satisfies the assumption of
Fact

3.6
since
\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|f_{ij}\beta_{j}|^{2}=1
, and hence

\displaystyle\E_{x}\left[\left\|F~\mathsf{diag}(x)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{i}\E_{x}\biggl[\biggl|\sum_{j}f_{ij}x_{j}\beta_{j}\biggr|\biggr]\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}=\frac{\sqrt{2^{n}}}{\sqrt{2}}

(22)

as claimed.
∎

After flattening, the state becomes essentially a uniform superposition with different phases.
Moreover, in the case of interest where
|\psi\rangle
is assumed to have real amplitudes, these phases are simply
\pm 1
.
Then we can correct the Boolean phases and reverse the flattening procedure to obtain an approximation of the original state
|\psi\rangle
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.2
.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be an
n
-qubit state with real amplitudes. By
Lemma

3.7
, there exists a Boolean phase oracle

such that

\||{\widetilde{\psi}}\rangle\|_{1}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2^{n}}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(23)

Since
|\psi\rangle
has real amplitudes, so does
|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle
.
Let
B_{1}
be the Boolean phase oracle such that
\langle x|B_{1}|x\rangle=\mathrm{sign}(\langle x|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle)
for all

.

Letting

, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi\middle|\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|B_{2}{H}^{\otimes n}B_{1}{H}^{\otimes n}|0\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\psi}|B_{1}{H}^{\otimes n}|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\cdot\||\widetilde{\psi}\rangle\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

(24)

as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.8

In this section we prove

Adaptive Clifford+
{T}
Circuits.

Consider a quantum computation
\mathcal{A}
with an
n
-qubit input register as well as an
a
-qubit ancilla register that is initialized in the all-zero state.
The computation proceeds via a sequence of Clifford gates,
{T}
gates, and single-qubit measurements in the computational basis.
These operations may act on any of the
n+a
qubits. Moreover, each Clifford or
{T}
gate may be classically controlled on the outcomes of measurements that have occurred previously.

Let
r\in\{0,1\}^{*}
be a binary string that describes the measurement outcomes in order obtained during the course of the computation.
23
23

23

For maximum generality, we do not assume that the number of single-qubit measurements is bounded. If we knew there were exactly
m
measurements, then
r
would be an
m
-bit string.
For each
r
, there is a Kraus operator
K_{r}
which describes the corresponding sequence of
{T}
gates, Clifford gates, and single-qubit projectors
|r_{i}\rangle\langle r_{i}|
for
i=1,2,\ldots
. The adaptive Clifford+
{T}
circuit implements a quantum channel which maps the
n
-qubit input state
\rho
to an
(n+a)
-qubit output state:

\mathcal{A}(\rho)=\sum_{r}K_{r}\left(\rho\otimes|0^{a}\rangle\langle 0^{a}|\right)K_{r}^{\dagger}.

(49)

For example, in the simplest case where there are no measurements and the circuit simply applies an
(n+a)
-qubit Clifford+
{T}
unitary
U

\mathcal{A}(\rho)=U\left(\rho\otimes|0^{a}\rangle\langle 0^{a}|\right)U^{\dagger}
. As another example, if the circuit applies a unitary
U
and then measures the first qubit, then we have
\mathcal{A}(\rho)=\sum_{r\in\{0,1\}}(|r\rangle\langle r|\otimes I)U\left(\rho\otimes|0^{a}\rangle\langle 0^{a}|\right)U^{\dagger}(|r\rangle\langle r|\otimes I)
.

We can view the output state
\mathcal{A}(\rho)
as a probabilistic mixture of the states

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}(\rho)\equiv\frac{K_{r}\left(\rho\otimes|0^{a}\rangle\langle 0^{a}|\right)K_{r}^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{r}^{\dagger}K_{r}\left(\rho\otimes|0^{a}\rangle\langle 0^{a}|\right))},

(50)

where
\sigma_{r}(\rho)
occurs with probability
p_{r}(\rho)\equiv\mathrm{Tr}(K_{r}^{\dagger}K_{r}\left(\rho\otimes|0^{a}\rangle\langle 0^{a}|\right))
.

T-Count.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{r}
be the number of
{T}
gates used by the circuit, conditioned on measurement outcome
r
.
The maximal number of
{T}
gates used by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{r\in\{0,1\}^{*}}\mathcal{T}_{r}.

(51)

We also consider the expected
{T}
-count of
\mathcal{A}
starting with the all-zeros input state:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{0}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{r}p_{r}(|0^{n}\rangle\langle 0^{n}|)\mathcal{T}_{r}.

(52)

Finally, we define the expected
{T}
-count of
\mathcal{A}
for the worst-case input state as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{\rho}\sum_{r}p_{r}(\rho)\mathcal{T}_{r},

(53)

where
\rho
is any
n
-qubit mixed state and
\sum_{r}p_{r}(\rho)\mathcal{T}_{r}
is the expected number of
{T}
gates used in
\mathcal{A}(\rho)
. For example, if the circuit is unitary and has no measurements, then all three of these measures equal the
{T}
-count of the unitary.

Note that clearly we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{0}(I\otimes\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{T}^{0}(\mathcal{A})\leq\mathcal{T}(I\otimes\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})\leq\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{max}}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{max}}(I\otimes\mathcal{A}).

(54)

Here we used the fact that, if
\mathcal{A}
is an adaptive Clifford+
{T}
circuit with an
n
-qubit input register, then so is
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=I\otimes\mathcal{A}
where the first register can have any number of qubits

and the second register is
n
qubits (in particular,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is just
\mathcal{A}
applied to the second register).

In the following we will prove lower bounds on the expected
{T}
-count
\mathcal{T}^{0}(\mathcal{A})
(for state preparation) and
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
(for unitary synthesis). These in turn imply lower bounds on the maximal
{T}
-count due to

Equation

54

As an intermediate step in the proof, it will be convenient to work with a model of Clifford circuits with Pauli postselection, defined as follows.

An operator

n

|\psi\rangle

Fact 4.14
.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\left|\iota_{U}\right\rangle\left\langle\iota_{U}\right|-\left|\iota_{V}\right\rangle\left\langle\iota_{V}\right|\right\|_{1}

Proof.

By

68

\displaystyle\left\||\iota_{U}\rangle-e^{i\theta}|\iota_{V}\rangle\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\biggl\|{2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|x\rangle\otimes\left(\left(U-e^{i\theta}V\right)|x\rangle\right)}\biggr\|^{2}

(75)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left\|\left(U-e^{i\theta}V\right)|x\rangle\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(U-e^{i\theta}V\right)^{\dagger}{U-e^{i\theta}V}\right)

Appendix A
Canonical form of Clifford circuits with magic states and Pauli postselection

To describe our generalization of
Lemma

4.8
, we need to recall the definition of stabilizer group.

Definition A.1

(Stabilizer group)
.

For a state
|\tau\rangle
, its stabilizer group
\mathsf{Stab}(|\tau\rangle)
is the set of Pauli operators
P
satisfying
P|\tau\rangle=|\tau\rangle
.
For a set
\Lambda
of states, we define its stabilizer group as
\mathsf{Stab}(\Lambda)=\bigcap_{|\tau\rangle\in\Lambda}\mathsf{Stab}(|\tau\rangle)
.
Note that the stabilizer group is a set of commutative Hermitian Pauli operators and does not contain
-I
.
We also remark that the size of a stabilizer group is an integer power of two.

Now we present the formal statement for the canonical form as
Theorem

A.2
.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Clifford circuit with
m
Pauli postselections,
n
input qubits, and
a
ancillas.
Let
\left\{|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
and
\left\{|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
be two sets of
n
-qubit states indexed by
\mathcal{S}
.
Assume

|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle|0^{t+a}\rangle=\mathcal{C}\left(|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle|{T}\rangle^{\otimes t}|0^{a}\rangle\right)

(81)

holds for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{S}
,
where we recall the circuit expression in
Definition

4.4
.
Then there exists an
(n+t)
-qubit Clifford unitary
C
and matrices
M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{c}
such that

|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle|0^{t}\rangle\propto CM_{c}\cdots M_{2}M_{1}\left(|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle|{T}\rangle^{\otimes t}\right)

(82)

holds for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{S}
,
where each
M_{j}
is
I+P_{j}
for some
(n+t)
-qubit Hermitian Pauli
P_{j}
and

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=t-\log\left(\left|\mathsf{Stab}\left(\left\{|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}\right)\right|\right)+\log\left(\left|\mathsf{Stab}\left(\left\{|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}\right)\right|\right).

(83)

Intuitively,
Theorem

A.2
says that the number of ancillary qubits and the number of Pauli postselections are upper bounded by the number of
{T}
gates.

•

If
\mathcal{S}
is a singleton set and
|\phi\rangle=|0^{n}\rangle
, then
\mathsf{Stab}(|\phi\rangle)=2^{n}
and
\mathsf{Stab}(|\psi\rangle)\leq 2^{n}
.
Thus
c\leq t
and
Theorem

A.2
specializes as
Lemma

4.8
, which justifies the above intuition for state preparation task, as also proved in
[
6
]
.

•

If
\left\{|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
ranges over all
n
-qubit states with
|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle=V|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle
for an
n
-qubit unitary
V
, then
\left|\mathsf{Stab}\left(\left\{|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}\right)\right|=\left|\mathsf{Stab}\left(\left\{|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}\right)\right|=1
.
Thus
c=t
and
Theorem

A.2
justifies the above intuition for unitary synthesis of
V
.

For intermediate tasks like implementing the first
K
columns of a unitary (aka partial unitary synthesis),
Theorem

A.2
can also be used, by setting
\left\{|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
to be the first
K

\left\{|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
.

The proof of

A.2
follows the analysis in
[

, Section A.7]
.
We start with the following convenient fact.

.

Let

be two anticommuting Hermitian Pauli operators.
Then
-iPQ
is Hermitian Pauli and

is Clifford.

Proof.

Consider the Clifford that conjugates
P
to
{X}\otimes I
and
Q
to
{Z}\otimes I
.
Then the result follows.
∎

In light of
Equation

60
and
Equation

, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r+1}
be
K
-qubit Clifford unitaries and
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
be
K
-qubit Hermitian Pauli operators.
Define
M_{j}=I+P_{j}
for each

.

\left\{|\rho_{\lambda}\rangle\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{E}}

Observe that

\displaystyle C_{r+1}M_{r}C_{r}\cdots M_{1}C_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{r+1}M_{r}C_{r}\cdots M_{2}C_{2}C_{1}\left(C_{1}^{\dagger}M_{1}C_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{r+1}M_{r}C_{r}\cdots M_{2}C_{2}C_{1}A_{1}

\displaystyle=\cdots=

(86)

\displaystyle=C_{r+1}\cdots C_{2}C_{1}\cdot A_{r}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}

(define

\displaystyle=CA_{r}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}.

(define

Since each

\displaystyle=Q_{j}A_{j-1}\cdots A_{1}R|\rho_{\lambda}\rangle

Appendix B
Improved analysis of Low-Kliuchnikov-Schaeffer state preparation

We observe that the proof of
Theorem

1.2
works equally well for diagonals of single-qubit unitaries.
With
Fact

2.4
, we can also decompose arbitrary single-qubit unitary into diagonal unitaries for which
Theorem

1.2
directly applies.
These are presented as the following
Lemma

B.1
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
n\geq 0
be an integer and
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{2^{n}}
be arbitrary single-qubit unitaries.
Define
(n+1)
-qubit unitary
U=\mathsf{diag}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{2^{n}})
.
Then
U
can be implemented up to error
\varepsilon
by a Clifford+
{T}
circuit using

O\left(\sqrt{2^{n}\log(1/\varepsilon)}+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)

{T}
gates and ancillary qubits.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof
Theorem

1.2
once we make sure that the determinant of each
U_{j}
is
1
.
To achieve this, let
D=\mathsf{diag}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2^{n}},\beta_{2^{n}})
be an
(n+1)
-qubit diagonal unitary such that the determinant of
U_{j}^{\prime}
is
1
for all
j
, where
U_{j}^{\prime}=\mathsf{diag}(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})U_{j}
.
Note that
D^{\dagger}
can be constructed using
Theorem

1.2
, and then
DU=\mathsf{diag}(U_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,U_{2^{n}}^{\prime})
can be handled in the same way as in
Theorem

1.2
.
Combining both gives
D^{\dagger}(DU)=U
.

An alternative proof is to use
Fact

2.4
, which represents each
U_{j}
as
A_{j}{H}B_{j}{H}C_{j}
for single-qubit diagonal unitaries
A_{j},B_{j},C_{j}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\mathsf{diag}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{2^{n}})\mathsf{diag}({H},\ldots,{H})

(109)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathsf{diag}(B_{1},\ldots,B_{2^{n}})

(110)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathsf{diag}({H},\ldots,{H})\mathsf{diag}(C_{1},\ldots,C_{2^{n}}),

(111)

where
\mathsf{diag}({H},\ldots,{H})
is simply a Hadamard gate on the
(n+1)
-th qubit and, moreover,
\mathsf{diag}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{2^{n}})
(resp.,
\mathsf{diag}(B_{1},\ldots,B_{2^{n}}),\mathsf{diag}(C_{1},\ldots,C_{2^{n}})
) can be implemented by
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

As a direct application of
Lemma

B.1
, we improve the state synthesis bound in
[
33
]
.
Note that
Corollary

B.2
is only off by the extra
n
in front of

, which does not dominate the overall complexity unless

is extremely small.
Given this, this construction may be of practical interest.

Corollary B.2
.

Any
n
-qubit state can be prepared up to error
\varepsilon
by a Clifford+
{T}
circuit starting with the all-zeros state using

O\left(\sqrt{2^{n}\log(1/\varepsilon)}+n\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)\text{ ${T}$ gates}

(112)

and

O\left(\sqrt{2^{n}\log(1/\varepsilon)}+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)\text{ ancillary qubits.}

(113)

Proof.

The state synthesis algorithm in
[
33
]
uses Grover-Rudolph algorithm
[
21
]
:
The first qubit is rotated to have the correct marginal. Then conditioned on the first qubit, the second qubit is rotated to have the correct marginal. This process is iterated until the
n
th qubit is rotated conditioned on the first
n-1
qubits.

Let
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
.
The
k
-th operation above is a rotation on the
(k+1)
-th qubit, controlled by the first
k
qubits.
Therefore, it is a diagonal of single-qubit unitaries, for which we apply
Lemma

B.1
.
Let
\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon/2^{n-k}
.
Then by
Lemma

B.1
, the
k
-th operation above can be
\varepsilon_{k}
-approximately implemented with

\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{2^{k}\log(1/\varepsilon_{k})}+\log(1/\varepsilon_{k})\right)

\displaystyle=O\biggl(\sqrt{2^{k}\left(n-k+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)}+n-k+\log(1/\varepsilon)\biggr)

(114)

T gates and ancillas.

Through all
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, the maximal number of ancillas is

\displaystyle O\biggl(\max_{k}\sqrt{2^{k}\left(n-k+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)}+n-k+\log(1/\varepsilon)\biggr)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sqrt{2^{k}\log(1/\varepsilon)}+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right).

(115)

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}2^{k-n}\leq\varepsilon,

(116)

and the total number of
{T}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}O\biggl(\sqrt{2^{k}\left(n-k+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)}+n-k+\log(1/\varepsilon)\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}O\biggl(\sqrt{2^{k}(n-k)}+\sqrt{2^{k}\log(1/\varepsilon)}+\log(1/\varepsilon)\biggr)
