Flow Index Trajectories During Spontaneous Breathing Trials Are Associated With Extubation Failure: A Latent Class Trajectory Modeling Study
Authors: Jun Shen, Jun Liu, Chang Liu, Xiliang Fu, Meng Xu, Fuhui Yan, Shaokang Yang, Yadan Luo, Sen Lu, Jingchao Luo, Xiaobo Huang, Yunming Li, Chun Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtubation failure may occur despite a successful spontaneous breathing trial (SBT), suggesting that conventional SBT criteria may not fully capture respiratory reserve. The Flow index (FI), derived from inspiratory flow time waveform morphology, reflects inspiratory effort during pressure support ventilation. We investigated whether FI trajectories during SBT were associated with extubation failure. In this retrospective cohort study, FI values during the final 10 min of SBT were summarized within six prespecified 2 min windows using patient level medians. Latent class trajectory modeling was used to identify FI patterns, followed by clinically and statistically justified consolidation into three trajectory groups. Multivariable logistic regression evaluated the association between FI trajectory and extubation failure. Mean based trajectories and machine learning analyses were performed as exploratory supplementary analyses. Among 432 screened patients, 123 were included, and 27 (22.0%) experienced extubation failure. The consolidated groups were Traj1, stable/moderate reserve (n = 89; failures, 12.4%); Traj2, depleted/low effort (n = 18; failures, 50.0%); and Traj3, escalating/high effort (n = 16; failures, 43.8%). Compared with Traj1, Traj2 (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 8.23; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.69–39.98; p = 0.009) and Traj3 (adjusted OR, 16.06; 95% CI, 3.02–85.40; p = 0.001) were associated with higher odds of extubation failure after adjustment for age, sex, SOFA score, and dynamic compliance. Median based FI trajectories during SBT identified distinct patterns associated with extubation failure and may provide complementary post SBT risk stratification information. Prospective external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is the cornerstone of respiratory support for critically ill patients in the ICU. However, prolonged MV and delayed weaning are associated not only with increased mortality, but also with a higher incidence of complications and significantly longer hospital stays [1, 2, 3]. Early and successful liberation from MV is therefore a key milestone in the recovery of ICU patients [4, 5]. Accurately identifying high risk patients before extubation and optimizing the timing of weaning remain major challenges in clinical practice.

SBT is the standard step for assessing a patient's ability to tolerate liberation from MV. By transiently reducing ventilatory support to simulate the post extubation load, SBT is used to evaluate the recovery of spontaneous breathing capacity [6, 7]. However, prior studies have shown that even among patients who successfully pass SBT, 10%–20% subsequently develop respiratory failure and require reintubation [8, 9]. This not only prolongs hospital stay and increases healthcare costs, but is also associated with worse outcomes. These observations suggest that SBT “pass/fail” criteria alone are insufficient to capture a patient's ability to sustain adequate ventilation after extubation. Conventional SBT assessment primarily relies on respiratory rate (RR), tidal volume (Vt), and rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI), which mainly describe the breathing pattern rather than the underlying inspiratory effort. As a result, they may fail to capture key physiological determinants of extubation failure.

Inspiratory effort reflects the combined effect of respiratory drive and respiratory muscle function. Appropriate inspiratory effort is essential to maintain effective ventilation, whereas excessive or insufficient effort indicates excessive load or poor reserve, respectively both important risk signals for extubation failure [10, 11]. In clinical practice, Vt, RR, and RSBI are often used as surrogate markers of inspiratory effort [12], but they may not accurately reflect true respiratory muscle load [13]. Parameters such as airway occlusion pressure (P0.1) and occlusion derived pressure swings (ΔPocc) are physiologically closer to respiratory drive [14, 15], yet their clinical use is limited by technical complexity, intermittent measurement, and limited availability. Esophageal pressure monitoring, regarded as the “gold standard” for quantifying intrathoracic pressure swings and work of breathing, is invasive, technically demanding, and difficult to apply continuously in routine ICU practice [16, 17]. Diaphragm electrical activity monitoring and ultrasound based assessment offer alternative approaches, but are similarly constrained by cost, equipment availability, and operator dependence [18, 19]. Overall, there is still no noninvasive, bedside, continuously available tool that can quantitatively characterize inspiratory effort during SBT.

In recent years, extracting time series “physiologic fingerprints” from ventilator waveforms has attracted growing interest [20]. Studies have shown that, under PSV, the morphology of the inspiratory flow time curve is highly sensitive to patient ventilator interaction [21]. Albani et al. [22] first proposed and validated the concept of the Flow index, which mathematically quantifies the concavity of the inspiratory flow decay segment to indirectly represent inspiratory effort. They demonstrated that the Flow index correlates closely with esophageal pressure derived indices such as the pressure time product (PTP) and outperforms traditional indices such as RSBI in reflecting inspiratory load. Subsequently, Miao et al. externally validated the Flow index in an independent cohort of patients with brain injury, confirming its stable correlation with esophageal pressure parameters and its diagnostic performance in screening abnormal inspiratory effort [23]. However, most existing studies have used cross sectional or single time point designs, evaluating the Flow index at a fixed support level [23, 24]. This approach does not fully align with the inherently dynamic, stress test nature of SBT. Extubation failure is rarely driven by a single instantaneous abnormal value; more often, it reflects an inability to sustain stable work of breathing once support is withdrawn. Compared with a single measurement, tracking the dynamic trajectory of the Flow index throughout SBT whether it stabilizes, continuously rises, continuously falls, or fluctuates abnormally may better capture respiratory muscle endurance and reserve, and thereby improve prediction of extubation failure.

Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that dynamic FI trajectories during SBT would identify clinically meaningful patterns associated with extubation failure. Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to derive FI trajectory groups from SBT time series data using latent class trajectory modeling and to evaluate their association with extubation failure. To reduce the influence of within window random variation and unequal numbers of valid respiratory cycles across patients, the primary trajectory analysis summarized FI values using the median within six prespecified time windows during the final 10 min of SBT, with mean based trajectory modeling retained as a sensitivity analysis. In response to the limited sample size and number of events, machine learning analyses, LASSO screening, calibration, decision curve analysis, and SHAP interpretation were treated as exploratory supplementary analyses rather than as the basis for a definitive clinical prediction model.

Study Design and Methods

Study Population

Data for this study were obtained from the Electronic Health Record Database of Critical Care Medicine at Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital (EDSPPH). We first identified 432 adult patients who received invasive MV in the ICU between May 2023 and May 2025. According to predefined eligibility criteria, 123 patients were finally included; all had been judged by the attending intensivist to have passed SBT and to meet standard criteria for liberation from MV.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duration of MV ≥ 48 h; (2) weaning process consistent with the AARC clinical practice guideline for SBT [25]; and (3) ventilator mode PSV during SBT, with a pressure support level of 5–8 cmH2O and PEEP of 5 cmH2O. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) missing ventilator waveform data or unknown extubation outcome; (3) presence of CNS disease or traumatic injury involving the thoracic cage or diaphragm; and (4) > 20% missing information in the electronic medical record. According to our institutional protocol (Table S1), patients who successfully passed the SBT underwent an additional assessment of mental status and cough effectiveness before extubation. The overall study flow is shown in Figure 1. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital (approval No. 2024‐543).

Exposure

The exposure variable was the temporal trajectory of the Flow index (FI). Under PSV, each inspiratory cycle generates a flow time waveform. As previously described, the concavity of the decelerating inspiratory flow limb can be quantified to obtain FI; the detailed formula is provided in Figure S1. Under PSV, a higher FI indicates a more pronounced concavity in the deceleration phase of inspiratory flow, suggesting greater inspiratory effort. In this study, FI values during the final 10 min of SBT before extubation were grouped into six prespecified 2 min windows corresponding to 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min. Because the number of valid FI measurements differed between patients and between windows according to respiratory rate and waveform quality, and because non fitted or manually disturbed waveforms were excluded during FI calculation, the primary analysis summarized FI within each window using the median value for each patient. Mean FI values within the same windows were used in a sensitivity analysis.

Latent Class Trajectory Model

Latent class trajectory modeling was performed using the complete included cohort. Candidate models with 1–6 classes were fitted to the six window FI time series [26]. Model selection considered log likelihood, Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), average posterior probability, class size, and clinical interpretability. BIC was used as the primary statistical criterion. In the median‐based primary analysis, the 5 class solution had the lowest BIC and was therefore selected as the initial data driven solution (Figure S2 and Table S2). Because three of the original classes showed clinically similar stable or gradually declining FI patterns, we consolidated the 5 class solution into three clinically interpretable trajectories. This consolidation was supported by trajectory morphology, event rates, and slope equivalence testing using two one sided tests with a prespecified equivalence margin of ±0.05 FI units per min (Table S3). The consolidated groups were labeled Traj1 (stable/moderate reserve), Traj2 (depleted/low effort), and Traj3 (escalating/high effort). All primary association analyses used these three trajectory groups, with Traj1 as the reference group. The mean based trajectory solution was analyzed as a sensitivity analysis and did not redefine the primary trajectory groups.

Clinical Outcome

The primary clinical outcome was extubation failure (Outcome = 1). In accordance with the joint guidelines on tracheal intubation and extubation from the French Society of Anaesthesia and Intensive Care Medicine and the French speaking Intensive Care Society [27], extubation failure was defined as the occurrence of unplanned noninvasive ventilation or the need for reintubation within 48 h after extubation. All other patients were classified as having successful extubation (Outcome = 0). At our center, “planned NIV” refers to noninvasive ventilation that is explicitly prescribed or documented in the medical orders/progress notes before extubation as a prophylactic strategy; “unplanned NIV” refers to rescue NIV initiated after extubation when predefined criteria for clinical deterioration are met.

Feature Selection

LASSO logistic regression was performed as an exploratory candidate predictor screening analysis rather than as the basis for the primary multivariable model [28]. The prespecified candidate predictors were FI trajectory group, age, sex, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, SOFA score, dynamic compliance, and reason for ICU admission. Continuous variables were standardized, and categorical variables were encoded as indicator variables with Traj1 as the reference category. Ten fold cross validation was used to select the penalty parameter, and both lambda.min and the more parsimonious lambda.1se criteria were reported (Figure S3). The LASSO results were used only to describe exploratory variable screening behavior and were not used to redefine the primary logistic regression model.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to the three consolidated FI trajectory groups. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used one‐way ANOVA, the Kruskal Wallis test, the chi‐square test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The primary association between FI trajectory and extubation failure was evaluated in the full cohort using logistic regression. Three models were specified: a crude model including trajectory group only; Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; and Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score, and dynamic compliance. This limited adjustment set was chosen a priori to preserve model parsimony given the sample size and number of extubation failure events. Effect estimates were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prediction Model Development

Exploratory machine learning analyses were conducted to assess whether adding FI trajectory to routinely available clinical variables improved discrimination across different modeling frameworks. Five algorithms were evaluated: logistic regression model (LRM), elastic net logistic regression (GLMNet), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and naive Bayes. The primary exploratory predictor set included FI trajectory group, age, sex, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, SOFA score, dynamic compliance, and reason for ICU admission. For comparison, otherwise identical models excluding FI trajectory were also fitted. Model performance was assessed using 10 repeats of 10 fold stratified cross validation; no independent external validation cohort was used.

Model Evaluation

Exploratory model performance was summarized using cross validated AUROC distributions (Table S4). Receiver operating characteristic curves comparing models with and without FI trajectory were generated from patient‐level mean out of fold predicted probabilities across repeated cross validation (Figure S4). Calibration curves and decision curve analyses were generated using the same cross validated predictions to explore model behavior and potential clinical utility (Figure S5). For model interpretability, an exploratory SHAP analysis was performed for the random forest model including FI trajectory. Because the local analysis environment did not use a package specific TreeSHAP implementation, SHAP values were estimated using a reproducible model agnostic grouped feature permutation approach and were used only for interpretation, not for variable selection or primary inference (Figure S6).

Latent class trajectory modeling was performed in R version 4.5.3 using the flexmix package. Data processing, primary logistic regression, figure and table generation, and exploratory LASSO, machine learning, calibration, decision curve, and SHAP analyses were performed in Python version 3.12.13 using pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit learn, and matplotlib.

Results

Flow Index Trajectory Identification and Consolidation

Among 432 screened patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation, 123 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Overall, 27 patients (22.0%) experienced extubation failure. For the median based primary analysis, latent class trajectory models with 1–6 classes were fitted to the six window FI time series. The 5 class solution had the lowest BIC and was selected as the initial data driven solution (Figure S2 and Table S2).

The original 5 class solution was subsequently consolidated into three clinically interpretable trajectory groups. Original classes 1, 2, and 5 showed moderate FI values with gradual decline or stabilization and were consolidated as Traj1 (stable/moderate reserve). Original class 3 showed low FI values with progressive depletion and was retained as Traj2 (depleted/low effort). Original class 4 showed a progressively rising FI pattern and was retained as Traj3 (escalating/high effort). The consolidation was supported by trajectory morphology, event rates, and slope equivalence testing among the stable/moderate original classes (Table S3).

The final consolidated trajectory groups were Traj1 (n = 89), Traj2 (n = 18), and Traj3 (n = 16). Traj1 showed a relatively stable, moderate reserve pattern; Traj2 showed a low and progressively depleted FI pattern; and Traj3 showed increasing FI over time, consistent with escalating inspiratory effort (Figure 2). All subsequent primary analyses used these three trajectory groups, with Traj1 as the reference group.

Exploratory LASSO Screening

LASSO logistic regression was performed as an exploratory supplementary analysis to examine candidate predictor screening behavior (Figure S3). At lambda.min, eight features were retained: Traj3 versus Traj1, Traj2 versus Traj1, dynamic compliance, SOFA score, age, respiratory rate, heart rate, and male sex. Using the more parsimonious lambda.1se criterion, five features were retained: dynamic compliance, Traj3 versus Traj1, SOFA score, Traj2 versus Traj1, and age. These results supported the relevance of FI trajectory and respiratory mechanics but were not used to redefine the primary multivariable logistic regression model.

In Model 2, which was adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score, and dynamic compliance, the association remained statistically significant. Compared with Traj1, Traj2 was associated with higher odds of extubation failure (adjusted OR, 8.23; 95% CI, 1.69–39.98; p = 0.009), and Traj3 showed an even stronger association (adjusted OR, 16.06; 95% CI, 3.02–85.40; p = 0.001). The wide confidence intervals reflect the modest sample size and the limited number of events within trajectory subgroups.

Exploratory Machine‐Learning Analyses

Exploratory machine‐learning analyses were performed to assess whether adding FI trajectory improved discrimination across different modeling frameworks. Using patient level mean out of fold predicted probabilities from repeated 10 fold cross validation, models including FI trajectory consistently showed higher AUROC values than corresponding models without FI trajectory (Figure S4). The AUROC increased from 0.758 to 0.812 for LRM, from 0.768 to 0.821 for GLMNet, from 0.721 to 0.770 for RF, from 0.713 to 0.747 for SVM, and from 0.765 to 0.836 for naive Bayes. Cross validated AUROC distributions for the five models are summarized in Table S4.

Exploratory Model Calibration, Decision Curve Analysis, and Interpretability

Calibration and decision curve analyses were also performed using the same cross validated predictions (Figure S5). Calibration varied across algorithms, and calibration plots suggested possible underprediction in parts of the predicted risk range, reinforcing that these exploratory models should not be used for absolute risk estimation without external validation. Models including FI trajectory generally showed comparable or improved calibration and higher mean net benefit over clinically relevant threshold probabilities compared with models excluding FI trajectory. In the exploratory SHAP analysis of the random forest model including FI trajectory, dynamic compliance had the largest mean absolute SHAP value, followed by FI trajectory, SOFA score, age, and respiratory rate (Figure S6). These exploratory analyses were used to support interpretation and assess potential incremental information from FI trajectory, not to establish a definitive clinical prediction model. In the mean based sensitivity analysis, the overall trajectory interpretation was broadly consistent, with higher failure risk concentrated in the depleted/low effort and escalating/high effort patterns (Figure S7 and Table S5).

Discussion

This study evaluated dynamic FI trajectories during SBT among patients who had already met conventional SBT success criteria. The main findings were as follows. First, the median based FI time series identified heterogeneous trajectory patterns that could be consolidated into three clinically interpretable groups. Second, the depleted/low effort and escalating/high effort trajectories had substantially higher extubation failure rates than the stable/moderate reserve trajectory. Third, these associations persisted after adjustment for age, sex, SOFA score, and dynamic compliance. Finally, exploratory machine learning analyses suggested that adding FI trajectory to clinical variables numerically improved model discrimination and provided interpretable model level information, although these analyses were supplementary and hypothesis generating rather than definitive prediction model development.

The Flow index has previously been proposed as a bedside, noninvasive surrogate of inspiratory effort. Albani et al. introduced the concept and, in a prospective study, demonstrated a strong correlation with esophageal pressure time product and other reference measures of inspiratory effort [22]. Miao et al. later confirmed its diagnostic performance in an independent cohort of patients with brain injury [23]. However, these prior studies were largely cross sectional, emphasizing FI values at a single level of support or a single time point. This approach may not fully capture the dynamic nature of SBT as a respiratory stress test. By treating FI as a time series throughout the final phase of SBT, our trajectory based approach preserves temporal information and aligns with clinical constructs of respiratory endurance and reserve.

The clinical interpretation of the three trajectory groups is physiologically plausible. Traj1 showed a stable or gradually declining moderate FI pattern and had the lowest failure rate, suggesting relatively preserved respiratory reserve during SBT. Traj2 showed low FI values with progressive depletion and had the highest failure rate, which may reflect insufficient inspiratory effort, respiratory muscle fatigue, or inability to sustain adequate ventilation after support is withdrawn. Traj3 showed increasing FI over time and also had a high failure rate, consistent with escalating inspiratory effort and possible load capacity mismatch. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that both excessive and insufficient inspiratory effort can be associated with adverse outcomes [29, 30]. Importantly, our results suggest that the direction and stability of FI over time may be more informative than a single isolated FI value.

The trajectory consolidation strategy was intended to balance statistical fit and clinical interpretability. Although the median based 5 class solution provided the best BIC, several classes shared similar stable or gradually declining morphology and were clinically difficult to distinguish. Consolidation into three groups was supported by trajectory shape, outcome patterns, and slope‐equivalence testing. This approach reduced over fragmentation in a cohort with 123 patients and 27 events while retaining clinically meaningful high risk patterns. The mean based sensitivity analysis showed broadly similar clinical interpretation, supporting the robustness of the main trajectory concept.

The multivariable association analysis supports the potential prognostic relevance of FI trajectory. Compared with Traj1, both Traj2 and Traj3 were associated with higher odds of extubation failure after adjustment for age, sex, SOFA score, and dynamic compliance. This finding suggests that FI trajectory may provide information beyond selected clinical severity and respiratory mechanics variables. However, the confidence intervals were wide, particularly for the smaller Traj2 and Traj3 groups, and the estimates should therefore be interpreted as exploratory evidence of association rather than precise risk quantification or proof of causality.

The supplementary machine learning analyses should be interpreted in the same context. Across five modeling frameworks, adding FI trajectory to prespecified clinical variables yielded numerically higher AUROC values than otherwise identical models without FI trajectory. Calibration and decision curve analyses provided additional exploratory information on model behavior, and SHAP analysis of the random forest model identified dynamic compliance and FI trajectory as the two leading contributors to model output. These findings support the potential incremental information carried by FI trajectory, but they do not establish a deployable bedside prediction tool. SHAP values explain model predictions and should not be interpreted as causal evidence or as proof that modifying FI trajectory would change clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, median based FI trajectories during SBT identified three clinically distinct dynamic patterns. Compared with the stable/moderate reserve trajectory, the depleted/low effort and escalating/high effort trajectories were associated with higher odds of extubation failure. Exploratory analyses suggested that FI trajectory may add predictive information to routinely available clinical variables, but these findings require prospective external validation before clinical implementation. Future studies should evaluate whether trajectory‐informed monitoring can improve post‐SBT risk stratification across different SBT protocols, ventilator platforms, and patient populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single center retrospective analysis with a modest sample size and only 27 extubation failure events. Effect estimates, especially within the smaller trajectory groups, therefore had wide confidence intervals and require external validation. Second, the final three group classification was derived by consolidating the BIC selected 5‐class median based solution according to statistical and clinical criteria. Although this strategy improved interpretability and was supported by sensitivity analyses, the optimal number and boundaries of trajectory groups may vary across cohorts, SBT protocols, waveform sampling strategies, and ventilator platforms. Third, FI computation depends on high quality ventilator waveform acquisition and may be affected by device specific signal processing, waveform artifacts, suctioning, and other bedside interventions. Fourth, residual confounding may remain because factors such as sedation depth, secretion burden, airway protection, fluid balance, rehabilitation status, and clinician decision making could not be fully captured. Finally, the LASSO, machine learning, calibration, decision curve, and SHAP analyses were exploratory and internally cross validated only; they should not be interpreted as an externally validated clinical prediction model.
