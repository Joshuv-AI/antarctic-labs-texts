Fredholm Approach to Nonlinear Propagator Models
Eduardo Abi Jaber, Alessandro Bondi, Nathan De Carvalho, Eyal Neuman, Sturmius Tuschmann

Abstract
We formulate and solve an optimal trading problem with alpha signals, where transactions induce a nonlinear transient price impact described by a general propagator model, including power-law decay. Using a variational approach, we demonstrate that the optimal trading strategy satisfies a nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation with both forward and backward coefficients. We prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution under a monotonicity condition reflecting the nonlinearity of the price impact. Moreover, we derive an existence result for the optimal strategy beyond this condition when the underlying probability space is countable. In addition, we introduce a novel iterative scheme and establish its convergence to the optimal trading strategy. Finally, we provide a numerical implementation of the scheme that illustrates its convergence, stability, and the effects of concavity on optimal execution strategies under exponential and power-law decay.

1
Introduction

Price impact refers to the empirical observation that executing a large order adversely affects the price of a risky asset in a persistent manner, leading to less favorable execution prices. Consequently, an agent seeking to liquidate a large order, known as a
metaorder
, must split it into smaller parts, referred to as
child orders
, which are typically executed over a period of hours or days. A fundamental question in this context concerns the impact of a metaorder as a function of its size. A large body of empirical and theoretical work suggests that this relationship is concave and in fact, well approximated by a square-root law, see e.g.
[
13
,
15
,
25
,
30
,
32
,
34
]
and Chapter 12.3 of
[
9
]
for a comprehensive introduction. The square-root price impact law states that the peak impact
D^{\textrm{peak}}
for a metaorder of volume
X
, executed over a time horizon
T
, is given by

D^{\textrm{peak}}=Y\sigma_{T}\left(\frac{X}{V_{T}}\right)^{\delta},\quad\textrm{for }X\ll V_{T},

(1.1)

where
Y
is a constant of order 1,
V_{T}
is the total traded volume in the market, and
\sigma_{T}
is the asset’s contemporaneous volatility, both measured over time
T
. The scaling exponent
\delta
is estimated to be in the range
[0.4,0.7]
, and is often taken as
0.5
as an approximation.
The square-root law has been shown to be a good approximation in various markets (such as equities, foreign exchange, options, and even cryptocurrencies) and is general enough to include different types of market liquidity, broad classes of execution strategies, and various trading frequencies (
[
7
,
12
,
33
,
34
]
).

While the square-root law provides a simple connection between the metaorder size and its impact on the mid-price, price impact evolves dynamically, and the reaction of the mid-price to the metaorder is mostly transient.
Propagator models are a central tool for mathematically describing this decay phenomenon. They express price moves in terms of the influence of past trades and therefore capture the decay of price impact after each trade (
[
9
,
18
]
).
The price distortion
D_{t}
is quantified by

D^{X}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)dX_{s},\quad t\geq 0,

(1.2)

where
X_{t}
describes the amount of shares of the asset executed by time
t
and
G(t,s)
is a positive semi-definite Volterra kernel, often referred to as a
propagator
.

Obizhaeva and Wang [29]
and
Gârleanu and Pedersen [17]
, along with follow-up papers, assume that the price distortion
D_{t}
decays exponentially over time, that is,
G(t,s)=\kappa e^{-\rho(t-s)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>s\}}
for some positive constants
\kappa
and
\rho
.
On the other hand,
Bouchaud et al. [9]
(see Chapter 13.2.1 and references therein) report on empirical observations that the propagator
G
decays as a power-law of the lag, i.e.,

G(t,s)\approx(t-s)^{-\beta}\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>s\}},\quad t,s\geq 0,

(1.3)

with
0<\beta<1
, and these results are also supported by theoretical arguments.

While the power-law behavior of price impact decay is strongly supported by market data, results on corresponding optimal liquidation strategies have been scarce. Power-law or other general propagators that do not exhibit exponential decay introduce non-Markovianity in the corresponding optimal trading problem, thereby rendering standard stochastic control tools, such as dynamic programming or FBSDEs, inapplicable. Recent progress has been made in the context of optimal execution with the linear propagator model, see
[
19
]
for the case without signals and
[
1
,
2
,
4
]
for the case with stochastic signals. However, incorporating both the nonlinearity arising from the square-root law in (
1.1
) and the power-law decay in (
1.3
) remains a long-standing open problem.

As a first step in merging these models,
Alfonsi et al. [5]
introduced a nonlinear propagator model, motivated by the local shape density of the limit order book and its reaction to market orders. In their model, the price impact
I_{t}
of a metaorder
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is given by

I^{X}_{t}=h(D^{X}_{t}),\quad t\geq 0,

(1.4)

where
h
is an increasing, nonnegative, and concave function, and
D^{X}
is defined in (
1.2
). Note that the case
h(x)=x
corresponds to the linear propagator model, while the case
h(x)=\text{sign}(x)|x|^{\delta}
, with
\delta\in(0,1)
, is inspired by (
1.1
) (see
[
8
,
11
,
21
]
).
Alfonsi et al. [5]
then solved the associated execution problem, which involves minimizing the expected execution costs,

C(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}h(D^{X}_{t})dX_{t}\right],

(1.5)

in the special case where
G(\cdot)
is an exponentially decaying function, i.e., the Markovian case, obtaining closed-form, deterministic optimal strategies.

Hey et al. [21]
generalized the framework of
[
5
]
by incorporating a predictive trading signal (also referred to as
alpha
) into the price process. From a modeling perspective, this means that the unaffected price process is no longer a martingale. The agent therefore faces a tradeoff between exploiting the signal to make an immediate profit and managing price impact costs. The objective functional then changes to maximizing the expected revenue functional:

R(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\alpha_{t}-I_{t}^{X}\right)dX_{t}\right],

(1.6)

where the signal
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 0}
captures the drift term information of the unaffected asset price. In addition, inventory risk and a penalty for terminal inventory can be incorporated into (
1.6
). The optimal execution strategy with respect to (
1.6
) is expected to be adapted to the signal’s filtration.
Hey et al. [21]
worked within the Markovian setup and derived a first-order condition that characterizes an implicit connection between price distortion and the signal. A more tractable relation between impact and alpha was then established in the case where the function
h(x)
in (
1.4
) follows a power-law, and the optimal price impact was derived in closed form.
The model in
[
21
]
can be regarded as a generalization of the exponentially decaying linear propagator model studied in
[
24
,
26
]
, for which closed-form solutions were derived.
For additional work on cost misspecification in the setting of
Hey et al. [21]
, we refer to
[
20
]
, and a similar problem for the general linear propagator model was studied in
[
28
]
.

The main goal of this work is to study the optimal execution problem in which the price impact is a nonlinear (concave) function of the distortion as in (
1.4
), and the price distortion decays as in the general propagator model (
1.2
). The special case where
G
is given by (
1.3
) will be of major importance, as it is as close as possible to empirical studies. From a mathematical perspective, this will generalize the work in
[
1
,
4
]
to nonlinear price impact models, and generalize the Markovian nonlinear model in
Hey et al. [21]
to the non-Markovian case. We provide an outline of our main results below.

Existence, uniqueness, and first-order conditions.

To characterize the optimal strategy for the aforementioned nonlinear, non-Markovian optimal execution problem, we use a variational approach to derive the first-order condition, which takes the form of a nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation with both forward and backward terms. Under sublinear growth conditions on
h
in (
1.4
) and a monotonicity condition (see (
2.15
)), we prove that there exists a unique solution to this Fredholm equation, which is the maximizer of the gain functional (see Theorem
2.6
).
We then provide two examples where the above conditions are satisfied, one of which is the well-known case of an exponential propagator studied in
[
21
]
. Theorem
2.6
can be regarded as a generalization of Theorem 4.2 in
[
21
]
to the non-Markovian setting of general propagators. Indeed, in Proposition
2.8
, we show that the monotonicity condition (
2.15
) reduces to the conditions assumed in
[
21
]
.

Since the monotonicity condition is quite challenging to verify, we extend the results of Theorem
2.6
by relaxing it. In Theorem
2.10
, we prove an existence result for the solution to the execution problem by replacing the monotonicity condition with an assumption of a countable underlying probability space.
Moreover, we prove that this solution must satisfy the stochastic Fredholm equation. These assumptions allow us to include a large class of propagators in our models, including power-law decay. Our proof presents a novel approach for obtaining the sequential weak lower semi-continuity of the negative of the gain functional (
2.10
), which, together with the proof of coercivity, yields the existence of a solution to the Fredholm equation.

Numerical scheme and convergence results.

We formulate an iterative scheme to solve the nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation with both forward and backward terms, which was derived from the first-order condition, in order to determine the optimal trading strategy. In Theorem
2.16
, we prove the convergence of the scheme and derive a bound on its convergence rate. Our scheme is designed to handle a broad class of kernels and stochastic signals. Numerical methods for nonlinear propagator models remain scarce, to the best of our knowledge, the only existing approach is
[
10
]
, which employs neural networks to approximate the solution, but has been implemented only for the exponential kernel. In contrast, our approach guarantees convergence with explicit bounds on the convergence rate, and is also implemented for power-law propagators.

Moreover, we illustrate the convergence of the numerical scheme for both deterministic and stochastic signals and for general kernels. In the case of an exponential kernel and vanishing instantaneous impact, we use the explicit impact solution provided by
Hey et al. [21]
as a benchmark (see Section
3
). Additionally, we provide a numerical implementation that showcases the stability of the numerical scheme when approximating the power-law kernel by sums of exponentials.

Organization of the paper.

In Section
2
, we introduce the nonlinear propagator model. We state the main existence theorems, present a proposition on well-posedness in the single-exponential case, and prove two stability results. Additionally, we outline the iterative numerical scheme designed to determine the optimal trading strategy.
Section
3
focuses on numerical results. We implement our numerical scheme for various scenarios and discuss its numerical convergence.
Sections
4
–
9
contain the proofs of our main results. Finally, in the appendix additional numerical results are provided.

2
Problem formulation and main theoretical results

Fix a finite time horizon
T>0
and a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
satisfying the usual conditions. We denote by
\mathrm{d}t
the Lebesgue measure on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}\left([0,T]\right)
, and we denote by
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}
the product measure on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}\left([0,T]\right)\otimes\mathcal{F}
. We introduce the standard Banach spaces

\mathcal{L}^{p}:=\left\{f:[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}\;\text{prog.\penalty\ measurable},\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|f_{t}|^{p}\mathrm{d}t\right]<\infty\right\},\quad p\geq 1.

For
p=2
, we equip
\mathcal{L}^{2}
with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f_{t}g_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right],\quad f,g\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

which makes it a Hilbert space with the associated norm
\|f\|:=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}
. For
u\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
and
t\in[0,T]
, we denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{t}u
the conditional expectation of
u
with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

We consider an agent who wishes to liquidate a given amount of assets
X_{0}>0
at an admissible trading rate
u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. The agent’s inventory
X^{u}
is therefore given by

X^{u}_{t}:=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.1)

Notice in particular that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{u}_{t}|^{2}\right]<\infty.

We fix a process
S=(S_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
with terminal random variable
S_{T}\in L^{2}(\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},\mathbb{P}\right),\mathbb{R})
representing the fundamental price of the risky asset. The execution price
S^{u}=(S^{u}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is therefore given by,

S^{u}_{t}=S_{t}+\frac{\gamma}{2}u_{t}+h(Z^{u}_{t}),\quad t\in[0,T],

(2.2)

where
\gamma>0
is a constant,
h\colon\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function satisfying suitable regularity conditions
to be specified
and
Z^{u}=(Z^{u}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the process in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
given by

Z^{u}_{t}:=g_{t}+\left(\mathbf{G}u\right)_{t},\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.3)

Here
g\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
is an input stochastic process independent of
u
and
\mathbf{G}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\mapsto\mathcal{L}^{2}
is the linear operator induced by a Volterra kernel
G\colon[0,T]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The terms
\frac{\gamma}{2}u
and
Z^{u}-g
in (
2.2
) are, respectively, the instantaneous and transient price impacts of the trading strategy
u
on the unaffected price process
S
. Financially,
\gamma
captures slippage costs, i.e., bid-ask spreads. Furthermore,
h
is the impact function applying a potentially nonlinear effect to the price distortion
\mathbf{G}u
caused by the trading strategy
u
. When
h\equiv\text{Id}
, our setting reduces to the one in
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
, where linear transient price impact is investigated. We discuss the financial interpretation of
g
below (
2.12
).

Next we present the class of admissible price impact kernels, which are also known as propagators.

Definition 2.1
.

A Volterra kernel
G\colon[0,T]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
admissible
if

C_{G}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}|G(t,s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(2.4)

Any admissible kernel
G
induces a unique linear and bounded integral operator
\mathbf{G}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\mapsto\mathcal{L}^{2}
defined by

\left(\mathbf{G}u\right)_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)u_{s}\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T],\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

such that, by Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality,

\|\mathbf{G}u\|^{2}\leq TC_{G}\|u\|^{2},\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}.

(2.5)

As a consequence of Fubini’s theorem and the tower property of the conditional expectation, the unique linear and bounded adjoint operator
\mathbf{G}^{\ast}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\to\mathcal{L}^{2}
of
\mathbf{G}
is explicitly given by

\left(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}u\right)_{t}:=\int_{t}^{T}G(s,t){\mathbb{E}}_{t}[u_{s}]\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T],\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(2.6)

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{t}
denotes conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.
The boundedness of
\mathbf{G}^{\ast}
is ensured by conditional Jensen’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem such that

\|\mathbf{G}^{\ast}u\|^{2}\leq TC_{G}\|u\|^{2},\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(2.7)

see for example
[
14
, Chapter 6, Section 2]
.

We introduce the following revenue-risk functional of the agent,

\mathcal{J}(u):=\mathbb{E}\bigg[-\int_{0}^{T}S_{t}^{u}u_{t}\mathrm{d}t+X^{u}_{T}S_{T}-\frac{\phi}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(X^{u}_{t})^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\varrho}{2}(X^{u}_{T})^{2}\bigg],\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(2.8)

where
\phi
and
\varrho
are nonnegative constants representing the penalty factor on the risk aversion term and on the terminal inventory, respectively. In order for
\mathcal{J}
in (
2.8
) to be well-defined we require that
S^{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
for every
u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. This is satisfied by enforcing the a sublinear growth condition on
h
(see see Definition
2.3
).
Our objective is to maximize the performance functional
\mathcal{J}
, that is, to find an optimal trading rate
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
such that

\mathcal{J}(\hat{u})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}}\mathcal{J}(u).

(2.9)

Inserting the definitions of the final inventory
X_{T}^{u}
from (
2.1
) and the effective price
S^{u}
from (
2.2
) into
\mathcal{J}
from (
2.8
), while using the tower property of the conditional expectation, allows us to rewrite the performance functional as follows

\mathcal{J}(u)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\alpha_{t}-I_{t}^{u}\right)u_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\phi}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(X^{u}_{t})^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\varrho}{2}(X^{u}_{T})^{2}\bigg]+X_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{T}\right],\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}.

(2.10)

This expression of
\mathcal{J}
features the standard tradeoff between the exploitation of the asset’s alpha-signal,

\alpha_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{t}[S_{T}-S_{t}],\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(2.11)

and the price impact,

I_{t}^{u}:=\frac{\gamma}{2}u_{t}+h\left(Z^{u}_{t}\right),0\leq t\leq T,

(2.12)

see for example
[
3
, Lemma 5.3]
,
[
21
, Equation (3.2)]
.

Financial interpretation.

The stochastic process
\alpha
in (
2.11
) captures the drift of the fundamental price
S
, and it is interpreted as a short-term trading signal (see
[
24
]
). The process
g
in (
2.3
) provides additional flexibility to the model, as it can serve as a proxy for the aggregated price impact generated by the order flow of other market participants trading the same asset.
It is shown in Theorem 2.13 of
[
27
]
, using game-theoretical arguments, that for
h\equiv\text{Id}
the aggregated price impact of other players serves as an additional signal competing with
\alpha
. In the case of a concave
h
the resulting effective signal is given by
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha-h(g+\mathbf{G}u)
.

Remark 2.2

(Flexibility of the model specification)
.

Our model formulation includes some well-known examples for nonlinear price impact models. In particular, it accommodates the model introduced in
[
5
]
in the special case where

G(t,s):=\xi e^{-x(t-s)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>s\}},\quad\xi,\;x>0,

(2.13)

as well as the case of nonlinear permanent price impact by setting
G
to a constant. Our framework also includes the cases where
G
is a sum of multiple decaying exponentials as in
[
21
]
, or has a power-law decay as illustrated in Section
3
.
Finally, taking the exponential kernel (
2.13
) with
\xi=x
, and taking
\gamma\to 0
and
x\to\infty
yields a nonlinear instantaneous price impact such that (
2.12
) has the form

I_{t}^{u}=h\left(g_{t}+u_{t}\right),

which is reminiscent of the impact models in
[
22
,
23
]
.

2.1
Main results on existence of the optimal strategy

Since the performance functional (
2.10
) is nonlinear and not necessarily convex, one of the main fundamental questions addressed in this section deals with existence and characterization of the optimal strategy. We start by introducing the set of admissible impact functions.

Definition 2.3
.

An impact function
h\colon\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
is said to be admissible if it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

h
is differentiable with bounded derivative
h^{\prime}
,

(ii)

h
grows strictly sublinearly, i.e., there exists
0<\zeta<1
such that

|h(x)|=\mathcal{O}\left(1+|x|^{\zeta}\right),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Given an admissible impact function
h
, using Definition
2.3
(i), we can define an operator
\mathbf{A}\colon\mathcal{L}^{2}\mapsto\mathcal{L}^{2}
such that, for every
u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\!\!\mathbf{A}(u)_{t}

\displaystyle:=h(Z^{u}_{t})+(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}(h^{\prime}(Z^{u})u))_{t}

(2.14)

\displaystyle=h\left(g_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)u_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right)+\int_{t}^{T}G(s,t){\mathbb{E}}_{t}\bigg[h^{\prime}\left({g_{s}+}\int_{0}^{s}G(s,r)u_{r}\mathrm{d}r\right)u_{s}\bigg]\mathrm{d}s,

where
Z^{u}
is defined in (
2.3
). The following monotonicity condition will play a crucial role in the proof of the existence of a maximizer to (
2.10
),

\langle u-v,\mathbf{A}(u)-\mathbf{A}(v)\rangle\geq 0,\quad u,\,v\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(2.15)

as it ensures the strong concavity of the functional
\mathcal{J}
, see Lemma
4.3
.

Remark 2.4

(Assumptions on the impact function
h
)
.

We briefly discuss assumptions (i) and (ii) in Definition
2.3
. (i) allows for a rigorous Gâteaux differentiation of the performance functional
\mathcal{J}
in (
2.8
) and ensures that the nonlinear operator
\mathbf{A}
in (
2.14
) is well-defined. (ii) ensures that the performance functional is well-defined in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
and also guarantees the connectivity of the performance functional, which is needed in order to obtain existence of an optimal trading strategy.

Remark 2.5

(Linear transient impact)
.

Note that the case of linear transient price impact, i.e.,
h(x)=x
, (
2.15
) is equivalent to requiring the linear integral operator
\mathbf{G}
in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
to be positive semi-definite in the following sense, that is,

\langle u,\mathbf{G}u\rangle\geq 0,\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}\iff\langle u,\left(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\right)u\rangle\geq 0,\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(2.16)

where the equivalence is a consequence of Fubini’s Theorem. Indeed, from (
2.3
) and (
2.14
) it follows that,

\langle u-v,\mathbf{A}(u)-\mathbf{A}(v)\rangle=\langle u-v,\left(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\right)\left(u-v\right)\rangle.

Such assumption on
\mathbf{G}
is standard to ensure the well-posedness of linear price impact problems and has already appeared in
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
19
]
.

Moreover, we introduce the admissible kernel

H_{\phi,\varrho}(t,s):=\left(\phi(T-t)+\varrho\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>s\}},\quad s,t\in[0,T],

(2.17)

which captures both the running and terminal inventory soft-penalization terms in the performance functional (
2.8
). In particular, the corresponding operator
\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}
in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
is positive semi-definite in the sense of (
2.16
) as shown in Lemma
4.3
.

Our first main result characterizes the optimal strategy in terms of the first-order condition (FOC) in the Gâteaux derivative sense.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
h
be an admissible function as in Definition
2.3
. Assume that the operator
\mathbf{A}
in (
2.14
) satisfies the monotonicity condition (
2.15
). Then, there exists a unique admissible optimal control
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
satisfying (
2.9
). In particular, the optimal control
\hat{u}
is the unique
\mathcal{L}^{2}-
valued solution to the nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation

\gamma\hat{u}_{t}+\left(\mathbf{A}(\hat{u})\right)_{t}+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}\hat{u}\right)_{t}+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{*}\hat{u}\right)_{t}=\alpha_{t}-X_{0}\left(\phi(T-t)+\varrho\right),\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-a.e.,

(2.18)

where
\alpha
and
H_{\phi,\varrho}
are defined in (
2.11
) and (
2.17
), respectively.

The proof of Theorem
2.6
is given in Section
4
.

Remark 2.7
.

Assuming the monotonicity condition (
2.15
), Theorem
2.6
shows that computing the optimal control reduces to solving the nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation (
2.18
). In general, such equation does not admit explicit solutions, except for the following two limit cases.

(i)

The linear impact case
h(x)=x
. Here, (
2.18
) reduces to a linear stochastic Fredholm equation which has recently been explicitly solved in
[
2
]
in terms of operator resolvents.

(ii)

The case where the slippage costs
\gamma\downarrow 0
, the coefficients
\phi=\varrho=0
, the process
g\equiv 0
and the kernel
G
is the exponential (see (
2.13
)). Then (
2.18
) reduces to

h\left(Z_{t}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}G(s,t)h^{\prime}(Z_{s})\mathrm{d}X_{s}\bigg]=\alpha_{t},\quad t\in[0,T].

We recall that from (
2.3
) we get
Z_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)\mathrm{d}X_{s}
. Then from Proposition
2.8
below it follows that
\mathbf{A}
is monotone.
In this setting, one would not expect
X
to be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. In fact, for suitable increasing odd impact functions
h\in C^{2}
that are concave in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, explicit solutions for the volume impact

are derived in
[

, Corollary 4.4]
with the presence of bulk trades at
t=0
and
t=T
, corresponding to Dirac measure at the extremities of the trading horizon.

Given an admissible impact function
h
, an admissible kernel
G
and a process
g\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
, it turns out that verifying that the operator
\mathbf{A}
is indeed monotone in the sense of (

) can be very challenging. The following proposition, which will be proved in Section

, provides sufficient conditions on

such that

is monotone.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
h
be differentiable with bounded derivative, and consider the exponential Volterra kernel

G(t,s)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{t\geq s\}}ae^{-b(t-s)},\quad t,s\in[0,T],\quad a>0,\;b\geq 0.

(2.19)

Assume that the following hold:

(i)

h
is nondecreasing on
\mathbb{R}
,

(ii)

g\equiv 0
,

(iii)

x\mapsto xh^{\prime}(x)
is nondecreasing on
\mathbb{R}
.

Then
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the monotonicity property (
2.15
).

Remark 2.9
.

If
h
is twice differentiable and
h^{\prime}>0
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus A
, for some discrete subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, then condition (iii) is equivalent to

-x\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(x)}{h^{\prime}(x)}\leq 1,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus A.

(2.20)

Note that (
2.20
) coincides with the sufficient condition on concavity given in Section 4.1 of
[

]
.

Since the monotonicity condition (
2.15
) is quite difficult to verify, we would like to bypass it and still derive the existence of an optimal strategy to the execution problem (
2.9
). In the following theorem, which is proved in Section

, we establish such result when considering a countable underlying probability space
\Omega

Theorem 2.10
.

Assume that
(\Omega,2^{\Omega},\mathbb{P})
is a countable probability space, where
2^{\Omega}
denotes the power set of
\Omega
. Assume that
h:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a globally Lipschitz continuous function of sublinear growth as in Definition
2.3
(ii).
Then, there exists a global maximizer
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
for the performance functional
\mathcal{J}
from (
2.10
). If, in addition,
h
satisfies Definition
2.3
(i), then
\hat{u}
is a solution to the nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation (
2.18
).

Finally, in order to achieve uniqueness of the solution beyond the monotonicity condition (
2.15
), we introduce the space

\mathcal{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega,L^{2}([0,T])\right):=\left\{f:[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}\;\text{prog.\penalty\ measurable},\;\esssup_{\omega\in\Omega}\left\lVert f(\omega)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}<\infty\right\},

and state the following theorem, which is proved in Section
9
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
2.10
are satisfied, and that

\alpha,\,g\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega,L^{2}([0,T])).

(2.21)

Moreover, let
h\colon\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be differentiable, with bounded and Lipschitz continuous derivative, and assume that
G
is positive semi-definite and satisfies

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{t}^{T}|G(s,t)|^{2}ds<\infty.

Then, if the slippage parameter
\gamma>0
is sufficiently large, there exists a unique optimal trading strategy
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
for
\mathcal{J}
satisfying (
2.9
). In particular,
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega,L^{2}([0,T])\right)
is the unique solution of the nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation (

) in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

Remark 2.12
.

The countability assumption on the underlying probability space in Theorem
2.10
essentially means that the existence of an optimal trading strategy continues to hold for real-world tactical discrete-time trading activities, where states of the world are well captured within such framework. The additional assumption in Theorem
2.11
of a sufficiently large slippage parameter with respect to the the trading horizon and other impact model parameters ensures the regularization of the Fredholm equation (
2.18
) (see Section
9
for additional details).

2.3
An iterative numerical scheme

In this subsection, we propose an iterative scheme which leverages the explicit solutions of linear stochastic Fredholm equations in order to derive a solution to the non-linear Fredholm equation (
2.18
). Recall that by Theorem
2.6
, when the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-operator
\mathbf{A}
is monotone in the sense of (
2.15
), there exists a unique solution to (
2.18
) which is the optimal trading strategy with respect to (
2.9
). To execute our scheme, we subtract from the operator
\mathbf{A}
the linear price impact,

\big(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(u)\big)_{t}:=\left(\mathbf{A}(u)\right)_{t}-\left(\mathbf{G}u\right)_{t}-\left(\mathbf{G}^{*}u\right)_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(2.24)

so that (
2.18
)
becomes for for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\gamma u_{t}+\left(\left(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}\right)u\right)_{t}+\left(\left(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}\right)^{*}u\right)_{t}=\alpha_{t}-\big(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(u)\big)_{t}-X_{0}\left(\phi(T-t)+\varrho\right).

(2.25)

The iterative scheme is defined as follows:

(i)

Initialization:

u^{[0]}_{t}:=0,\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.26)

(ii)

Update:
for
n\geq 1
, having
u^{[n-1]}
, we derive
u^{[n]}
using (
2.25
) but fixing the nonlinear term to
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(u^{[n-1]})
, that is we solve the linear Fredholm equation,

\gamma u^{[n]}+\left(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}\right)u^{[n]}+\left(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}\right)^{*}u^{[n]}=Y[n-1],

(2.27)

where we define the source-term
Y
by

Y_{t}[n-1]:=\alpha_{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(u^{[n-1]})_{t}-X_{0}\left(\phi(T-t)+\varrho\right),\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.28)

In the following proposition we establish that the iterative scheme (
2.26
)-(
2.28
) converges to the solution of (
2.18
) under suitable conditions. The proof is postponed to Section
7
.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
h
be differentiable with bounded and Lipschitz continuous derivative with a Lipschitz constant
L>0
, and let the operator
\mathbf{G}
induced by the admissible kernel
G
be positive semi-definite. Denote by
(u^{[n]})_{n\geq 0}
the iterations of the scheme (
2.26
)-(
2.28
). Suppose that there exists a solution
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
of (
2.18
) such that

M_{\gamma}(\hat{u}):=\esssup_{\omega\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{T}|\hat{u}_{t}(\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t<\infty

(2.29)

and

\widetilde{C}:=2\sqrt{TC_{G}}\Big(1+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{L}{2}\sqrt{C_{G}M_{\gamma}(\hat{u})}\Big)<\gamma,

(2.30)

where
C_{G}
is the constant defined by (
2.4
). Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{[n]}=\hat{u}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{L}^{2}$.}

Moreover, the convergence rate is bounded by,

\left\lVert u^{[n]}-\hat{u}\right\rVert\leq\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{C}}{\gamma}\bigg)^{n}\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.31)

As a by-product of Proposition
2.16
, there exists at most one solution of (
2.18
) that satisfies (
2.29
) and (
2.30
). Indeed, if two such solutions exist, then the same sequence
(u^{[n]})_{n\geq 0}
converges to both solutions, and by uniqueness of the limit, they must be equal.
In the following section, we illustrate the performance of our numerical scheme for various propagators and market scenarios.

3
Numerical illustrations

In this section, we first explain how to implement the numerical scheme (

)–(
2.27
) in practice, leveraging the Nyström approximation of linear operators and Least-Squares Monte Carlo (LSMC) to solve at each step the resulting linear stochastic Fredholm equation in (
2.27
) and how to quantify the numerical error of the scheme. Then, we specify an impact function that satisfies our assumptions, along with signal processes, to illustrate our theoretical findings, including:

(i)

the convergence of the numerical scheme to an explicit asymptotic solution when the impact function is concave and when
\gamma

(ii)

the stability result from Proposition
2.13
when approximating the fractional kernel by a sum of exponential decays;

(iii)

the impact of concavity and the comparison between exponential and power-law decay on optimal trading in presence of a “buy” signal.

3.1
The iterative scheme in practice

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and consider the scheme (
2.26
)–(
2.27
). Given
u^{[n-1]}
,
u^{[n]}
solves the linear stochastic Fredholm equation (
2.27
) and is obtained by using the explicit operator formula in
[
2
, Proposition 5.1]
in terms of
Y[n-1]
from (
2.28
) and its conditional expectations
\left({\mathbb{E}}_{t}Y_{s}[n-1]\right)_{0\leq t<s\leq T}
. The only step left is estimating numerically such quantities at each step of the scheme. By the linearity of the conditional expectation, this amounts to estimating for
0<i<j\leq N-1

\displaystyle E_{i,j}[n-1]:=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}\left[\widetilde{Y}_{t_{j}}[n-1]\right],

(3.1)

\displaystyle\;\widetilde{Y}_{t_{j}}[n-1]=

\displaystyle\;\alpha_{t_{j}}-h\left(g_{t_{j}}+\left(\mathbf{G}u^{[n-1]}\right)_{t_{j}}\right)-\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\left(h^{\prime}\left(g+\mathbf{G}u^{[n-1]}\right)u^{[n-1]}\right)_{t_{j}}

\displaystyle+\left(\mathbf{G}u^{[n-1]}\right)_{t_{j}}+\left(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}u^{[n-1]}\right)_{t_{j}},

over a discrete time grid
\left\{0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{N}=T\right\},\;N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, where all the operators are discretized with the Nyström scheme in the same way as in
[
3
, Section 3.2]
.

Least Square Monte Carlo (LSMC).
For this, we use LSMC techniques in Markovian settings (e.g. with an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck drift and
G
given by an exponential, or sum of two exponentials) in the same spirit as
[
3
, Section 3.3]
.

Indeed, assume we have
P\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
(Markovian) regression variables observed at date
t_{i},\;i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}

\mathbb{X}_{t_{i}}:=\left(X_{t_{i}}^{p}\right)_{p\in\{1,\cdots,P\}}

to expand into an orthonormal polynomial basis of maximum degree
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
of the form

\mathcal{B}^{d}(\mathbb{X}_{t_{i}}):=\left\{\Pi_{p=1}^{P}L_{l_{p}}\left(X_{t_{i}}^{p}\right)\;\Big|\;\left(l_{p}\right)_{p\in\{1,\cdots,P\}}\in\mathbb{N}^{P},\;\sum_{p=1}^{P}l_{p}\leq d\right\},

(3.2)

where
L_{l_{p}},\;p\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the polynomial of degree
l_{p}
of the basis. Consequently, there are in total

\binom{P+d}{d}

features in the expanded basis (
3.2
), given that we distribute at most

d

degrees among the

P
features, including the possibility of assigning none (see the stars and bars theorem). Finally, the conditional expectations (
3.1
) are estimated by

E_{i,j}[n-1]\approx\langle l_{i,j}^{d}[n-1],\mathcal{B}^{d}(\mathbb{X}_{t_{i}})\rangle,

(3.3)

where
\left(l_{i,j}^{d}[n-1]\right)_{0<i<j\leq N-1}
are obtained by minimizing the Ridge-regularized least squares of the dependent variables
\widetilde{Y}_{t_{j}}[n-1]
against the explanatory variables
\mathcal{B}^{d}(\mathbb{X}_{t_{i}})
over
M
sample trajectories of the regression variables.

The impact of the quality of estimation of the conditional expectations in (
3.3
) on the PnL functional (
2.8
) as well as the overall convergence of the scheme will be discussed numerically in Section
3.3
and in Appendix
A
.

Profit and Loss.
From (
2.10
) we extract the Profit and Loss (PnL) defined by

PnL(u):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\alpha_{t}-I_{t}^{u}\right)u_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right],\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}.

Numerically, PnL is approximated over
M\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
discrete sample trajectories on the uniform time grid with step
\Delta:=\frac{T}{N-1}>0
by

PnL^{N,M}(u):=\frac{\Delta}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left(\alpha_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m})-I_{t_{i}}^{u}(\omega_{m})\right)u_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m}),\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(3.4)

where
I_{t_{i}}^{u}
is approximated again by the Nyström scheme.

Error metric when solving the nonlinear Fredholm equation (
2.18
).
After
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
iterations of the scheme (
2.26
)–(
2.27
), we define the following error metric applied to
u^{[n]}
:

\displaystyle E\left(u^{[n]}\right):={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\bigg|

\displaystyle\gamma u^{[n]}_{t}(\omega)+\left(\mathbf{A}\left(u^{[n]}\right)\right)_{t}(\omega)+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}u^{[n]}\right)_{t}(\omega)+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{\ast}u^{[n]}\right)_{t}(\omega)

\displaystyle+X_{0}(\phi(T-t)+\varrho)-\alpha_{t}(\omega)\bigg|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\bigg].

As in (
3.4
),
E(u^{[n]})
is approximated numerically by

E^{N,M}\left(u^{[n]}\right):=\frac{\Delta}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}E^{N}\left(u^{[n]}\left(\omega_{m}\right)\right),

(3.5)

with

\displaystyle E^{N}\left(u^{[n]}\left(\omega_{m}\right)\right):=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigg|

\displaystyle\gamma u^{[n]}_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m})+h\left(g_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m})+\left(\mathbf{G}u^{[n]}\right)_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m})\right)

\displaystyle+\left[\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{\ast}u^{[n]}\right)_{t_{i}}+\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\left(h^{\prime}\left(g+\mathbf{G}u^{[n]}\right)u^{[n]}\right)_{t_{i}}\right](\omega_{m})

\displaystyle+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}u^{[n]}\right)_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m})-\alpha_{t_{i}}(\omega_{m})+X_{0}(\phi(T-t_{i})+\varrho)\bigg|^{2}.

(3.6)

Here, all the operators are estimated by the Nÿstrom scheme and the conditional expectations by Least-Square Monte Carlo (LSMC) with a Ridge regularization, similarly to (
3.3
).

J_{t}^{*}=\text{sign}\left(\alpha_{t}+\tau\tilde{I}_{t}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+c}\left|\alpha_{t}+\tau\tilde{I}_{t}\right|\right)^{1/c},\quad t\in(0,T).

Now, also considering that
\alpha_{T}=0
, the boundary conditions on the impact function
I^{*}
read
I_{0}^{*}=0
,
and
I_{T}^{*}=\alpha_{T}=0

which translate for
J^{*}
into
J_{0}^{*}=0
and

J_{T}^{*}=0.

Thus, the optimal traded volume, which satisfies

Q_{t}^{*}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}J_{s}^{*}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J_{s}^{*}}{\tau}\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in(0,T],\quad Q_{0}=0,

is explicitly given by

Q_{t}^{*}=J^{*}_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}\text{sign}\left(\alpha_{s}+\tau\tilde{I}_{s}\right)\bigg(\frac{|\alpha_{s}+\tau\tilde{I}_{s}|}{1+c}\bigg)^{1/c}\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in(0,T],

(3.13)

with the bulk trades

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{0}=Q_{0+}-Q_{0}=\text{sign}\left(\alpha_{0}+\tau\tilde{I}_{0}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+c}\left|\alpha_{0}+\tau\tilde{I}_{0}\right|\right)^{1/c},

(3.14)

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{T}=Q_{T}-Q_{T-}=J_{T}-J_{T-}=-\text{sign}\left(\tilde{I}_{T}\right)\left|\frac{\tau\tilde{I}_{T}}{1+c}\right|^{1/c}.

Such bulk trades ensure that the boundary conditions are satisfied.

Proof of Theorem
2.6

The main idea of the proof is to use general results of convex optimization in infinite dimensional spaces. We recall that, given a positive constant
\beta>0
, a map
\mathcal{T}\colon\mathcal{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\beta-
strongly concave
if the following inequality is satisfied for every
u,\,v\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
:

\mathcal{T}(\theta u+(1-\theta)v)\geq\theta\mathcal{T}(u)+(1-\theta)\mathcal{T}(v)+\frac{\beta\theta(1-\theta)}{2}\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert^{2},\quad\theta\in(0,1).

(4.1)

Supposing that
\mathcal{J}
in (
2.8
) is concave and Gâteaux differentiable in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, we can study the existence of optimal strategies
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
in the sense of (
2.9
) by solving the equation

\nabla\mathcal{J}(u)=0,\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(4.2)

where
\nabla\mathcal{J}(u)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
is the Gâteaux differential of
\mathcal{J}
, defined by

\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}(u),h\rangle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{J}(u+\epsilon h)-\mathcal{J}(u)}{\epsilon},\quad h\in\mathcal{L}^{2}.

More precisely, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that the performance functional
\mathcal{J}
in (
2.8
) is well-defined and Gâteaux differentiable in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

(i)

If
\mathcal{J}
is concave in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, then the set of optimal strategies
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
satisfying (
2.9
) coincide with the set of solutions of (
4.2
). In addition, if the functional
-\mathcal{J}
is coercive, i.e.,
\lim_{\left\lVert u\right\rVert\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(u)=-\infty
, then there exists at least one optimal strategy that solves (
2.9
). If additionally
\mathcal{J}
is strictly concave, then such an optimal strategy is unique.

(ii)

In particular, if
\mathcal{J}
is strongly concave in the sense of (
4.1
), then there exists a unique optimal strategy
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
satisfying (
2.9
), which is also the unique solution of (
4.2
).

Proof.

The proof of the theorem statements are consequences of well-known results of convex analysis in Hilbert spaces that can be found in
[
6
]
(see also
[
16
]
). We first notice that
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
satisfies (
2.9
) if and only if

-\mathcal{J}(\hat{u})=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}}(-\mathcal{J}(u)),

(4.3)

and that
-\mathcal{J}
is proper because, by the hypothesis of well-posedness of
\mathcal{J}
,
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{L}^{2})\subset\mathbb{R}
. Since
\mathcal{J}
is supposed to be Gâteaux differentiable and concave in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, the fact that the set of optimal strategies
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
according to (
4.3
) (or (
2.9
)) coincides with the set of solutions to (
4.2
) is ensured by
[
6
, Proposition 17.4]
. Moreover, by
[
6
, Proposition 17.39]
, the opposite performance functional
-\mathcal{J}
is lower semi-continuous in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Consequently, if we further require
-\mathcal{J}
to be coercive, an application of
[
6
, Proposition 11.14]
yields the existence of at least one optimal strategy
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
satisfying (
4.3
), hence (
2.9
), too. Such an optimal strategy
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
is unique when
-\mathcal{J}
is strictly convex by
[
6
, Corollary 11.8]
. This completes the proof of
(i)
.

As for the assertion in
(ii)
, it follows by the same arguments as before combined with
[
6
, Corollary 11.16]
. The theorem is now fully proved.
∎

Given an admissible kernel
G
and an admissible impact function
h
, we can readily observe that assuming either assumption (i) or assumption (ii) ensures that
\mathcal{J}
is well-defined in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

Therefore, it remains to prove that
\mathcal{J}
is

1.

strongly concave,

2.

Gâteaux differentiable, and to compute its derivative.

This is the objective of the next two subsections.

4.1
\mathcal{J}
is Gâteaux differentiable

The next lemma shows that, under suitable requirements on the impact function
h
, recall also Remark
2.4
, the performance functional
\mathcal{J}
is Gâteaux differentiable in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
. In the sequel, we denote by
\mathbf{1}
the linear integral operator in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
defined by

(\mathbf{1}v)_{t}:=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s\leq t\}}v_{s}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T],\quad v\in\mathcal{L}^{2}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that the impact function
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies assumption (i) from Definition
2.3
. Then the functional
\mathcal{J}
in (
2.10
) is well-defined and Gâteaux differentiable in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, with Gâteaux differential given by

\nabla\mathcal{J}(u)=\alpha-X_{0}\left(\phi(T-\cdot)+\varrho\right)-\gamma u-\mathbf{A}(u)-\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}(u)\right)_{\cdot}-\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{*}(u)\right)_{\cdot},\quad u\in\mathcal{L}^{2},

(4.4)

where
\mathbf{A}
and
H_{\phi,\varrho}
are respectively defined in (
2.14
) and (
2.17
).

Proof.

Note that, for
u\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
, the definition of
\mathcal{J}
in (
2.10
) can be rewritten as follows

\mathcal{J}(u)=\langle\alpha,u\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}-\langle h(Z^{u}),u\rangle-\frac{\phi}{2}\left\lVert X^{u}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{\varrho}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{u}_{T}|^{2}\right]+X_{0}{\mathbb{E}}S_{T}=:\sum_{i=1}^{6}\mathcal{J}_{i}(u).

(4.5)

We now proceed to Gâteaux differentiate all the addends in (
4.5
). Evidently,
\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}_{1}(u),f\rangle=\langle\alpha,f\rangle
,
\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}_{2}(u),f\rangle=\langle-\gamma u,f\rangle
and
\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}_{6}(u),f\rangle=0
for every
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}.
As for
\mathcal{J}_{3}
, we let
\epsilon\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
and compute
the incremental ratio in a direction
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
, namely

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{J}_{3}(u+\epsilon f)-\mathcal{J}_{3}(u)}{\epsilon}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{h(Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t})-h(Z^{u}_{t})}{\epsilon}(u_{t}+\epsilon f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}h(Z^{u}_{t})f_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{h(Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t})-h(Z^{u}_{t})}{\epsilon}u_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\left(h(Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t})-h(Z^{u}_{t})\right)}f_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]-\langle h(Z^{u}),f\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=:\mathbf{I}^{u,f}(\epsilon)+\mathbf{II}^{u,f}(\epsilon)-\langle h(Z^{u}),f\rangle.

(4.6)

We then study the limits of
\mathbf{I}^{u,f}(\epsilon)
and
\mathbf{II}^{u,f}(\epsilon)
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Notice that, from the definition in (
2.3
),
Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t}=Z^{u}_{t}+\epsilon\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t},\,t\in[0,T]
, so that, by Lipschitz continuity of
h
with constant
\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, we immediately get

\left|h(Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t})-h(Z^{u}_{t})\right||f_{t}|\leq\epsilon\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}|f_{t}|\left|\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t}\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0,\quad\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}-\text{a.e}.

Since
f,\;\mathbf{G}f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}\subset\mathcal{L}^{1}
, the dominated convergence theorem entails that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbf{II}^{u,f}(\epsilon)=0
. Regarding
\mathbf{I}^{u,f}(\epsilon)
, by the tower property of the conditional expectation and Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}^{u,f}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{h(Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t})-h(Z^{u}_{t})}{Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t}-Z^{u}_{t}}\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t}\neq 0\right\}}u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\,-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}h^{\prime}(Z^{u}_{t})\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t}u_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]=-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\left(h^{\prime}(Z^{u})u\right)\right)_{t}f_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\left(h^{\prime}(Z^{u})u\right)\right)_{t}f_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]=-\langle\mathbf{G}^{\ast}\left(h^{\prime}(Z^{u})u\right),f\rangle.

The passage to the limit under the integral sign in the previous step is again justified by the dominated convergence theorem, which can be applied since
\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-
a.e.,

\frac{\left|h(Z^{u+\epsilon f}_{t})-h(Z^{u}_{t})\right|}{\epsilon}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t}\neq 0\right\}}|u_{t}|\leq\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left|\left(\mathbf{G}f\right)_{t}\right||u_{t}|\in\mathcal{L}^{1}.

Therefore, thanks to the previous computations, we take the
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}
in (
4.6
) to infer that

\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}_{3}(u),f\rangle=-\langle\mathbf{G}^{\ast}(h^{\prime}(Z^{u})u)+h(Z^{u}),f\big\rangle.

Recall the definition of
X^{u}
in (
2.1
), which in particular gives for any
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
,
X_{t}^{u+\epsilon f}=X_{t}^{u}-\epsilon\int_{0}^{t}f_{s}\mathrm{d}s,\;t\in[0,T],\;\mathbb{P}-
a.s. Then, expanding the squares, taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, and applying Fubini readily yields

\big\langle\nabla\left(\mathcal{J}_{4}(u)+\mathcal{J}_{5}(u)\right),f\rangle=-\langle\phi\mathbf{1}^{\ast}X^{u}+\varrho{\mathbb{E}}_{\cdot}\left[X_{T}^{u}\right],f\big\rangle.

Notice that, by Fubini’s theorem, for any
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\phi\left(\mathbf{1}^{\ast}X^{u}\right)_{t}+\varrho{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\left[X_{T}^{u}\right]

\displaystyle=\phi X_{0}(T-t)+{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s>t\}}u_{r}\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}s+\varrho X_{0}+{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\int_{0}^{T}\varrho u_{r}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=X_{0}\left(\phi(T-t)+\varrho\right)+{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{r}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s>t\}}\mathrm{d}s\right)u_{r}\mathrm{d}r+{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\int_{0}^{T}\varrho u_{r}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=X_{0}\left(\phi(T-t)+\varrho\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s>t\}}\mathrm{d}s\right)u_{r}\mathrm{d}r

It is clear that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{I}_{n}=0
, hence we only have to focus on
\mathbf{II}_{n}
. By hypothesis, the space
\Omega
is countable and the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is defined on the
\sigma
-algebra
2^{\Omega}
. Therefore, for every
\bar{\omega}\in\Omega
such that
\mathbb{P}(\{\bar{\omega}\})>0
, from the weak convergence of

to

in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
we infer that, while using the tower property of the conditional expectation, Fubini’s Theorem and the fact that

and

are adapted,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\{\bar{\omega}\})}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bar{\omega}\}}(\omega)\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}^{n}(\bar{\omega})\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\{\bar{\omega}\})}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bar{\omega}\}}\psi(t)](\omega)u_{t}^{n}(\bar{\omega})\bigg]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\{\bar{\omega}\})}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bar{\omega}\}}\psi(t)](\omega)u_{t}^{n}(\bar{\omega})\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\{\bar{\omega}\})}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bar{\omega}\}}(\omega)\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}(\omega)\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t\bigg]=\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}(\bar{\omega})\psi(t)\mathrm{d}t,\quad\psi\in L^{2}.

Note that, in the previous step, we consider a progressively measurable version of the process
(t,\omega)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bar{\omega}\}}](\omega),
which exists by, e.g. the optional projection theorem, hence
\mathbb{E}_{.}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bar{\omega}\}}\phi(.)](\omega)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
is a proper test function. This demonstrates that
u^{n}(\omega,\cdot)\rightharpoonup u(\omega,\cdot)
in
L^{2}
for
\mathbb{P}-
a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
. Moreover, the restriction of
\mathbf{G}
to the separable Hilbert space

is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. Indeed, denoting by

an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}
, by the monotone convergence theorem and Parseval’s identity

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert\mathbf{G}e_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left(\mathbf{G}e_{n}\right)(t)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)e_{n}(s)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\langle G(t,\cdot),e_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq TC_{G}<\infty.

As a result, the restriction of
\mathbf{G}
to
L^{2}
is a compact operator, which in particular maps weakly convergent sequences into strongly convergent ones. Combining the two previous observations with the Lipschitz continuity of
h
we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lVert h(g_{\cdot}(\omega)+\mathbf{G}u_{\cdot}^{n}(\omega))-h(g_{\cdot}(\omega)+\mathbf{G}u_{\cdot}(\omega))\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=0,\quad\text{for $\mathbb{P}-$a.e. $\omega\in\Omega$}.

(6.5)

By strict sublinearity of

, fix

(6.9)

If

which proves the desired sequential weak continuity of

and concludes the proof.

C_{G}^{\ast}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{t}^{T}\left|G(s,t)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Let
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable with bounded derivative
h^{\prime}
, and assume that the following bound holds:

\gamma>2\sqrt{T}\max\left\{\widetilde{C}_{H,G},\;\sqrt{C_{H}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left(\sqrt{C_{G}}+\sqrt{TC_{G}^{\ast}}\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)\right\},

(9.1)

with

\widetilde{C}_{H,G}:=\sqrt{C_{H}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{G}},

(9.2)

where the constants
C_{H},\,C_{G}
associated with the admissible kernels
H_{\phi,\varrho},\;G
, respectively, are defined in (
2.4
).
Then a solution
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
of (
2.18
) satisfies
\mathbb{P}-
a.s. the a priori estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\widetilde{C}_{\gamma}}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{G}^{\ast}}\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)\Bigg),

(9.3)

where

\tilde{\alpha}_{v}:=\alpha_{v}-X_{0}\left(\phi(T-v)+\varrho\right),\quad v\in[0,T],

(9.4)

and

\widetilde{C}_{\gamma}:=\gamma-\sqrt{T}\left(2\sqrt{C_{H}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left(\sqrt{C_{G}}+\sqrt{TC_{G}^{\ast}}\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)\right).

(9.5)

Proof.

Fix a solution
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
to the nonlinear Fredholm equation (
2.18
). Note that (
2.18
) re-writes as

\gamma\hat{u}_{v}=\tilde{\alpha}_{v}-\left(\mathbf{A}(\hat{u})\right)_{v}-\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}\hat{u}\right)_{v}-\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{*}\hat{u}\right)_{v}\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-a.e.,

where
\tilde{\alpha}
is given by (
9.4
), so that applying the absolute value together with the triangular inequality yields

\gamma\left|\hat{u}_{v}\right|\leq\left|\tilde{\alpha}_{v}\right|+\left|\left(\mathbf{A}(\hat{u})\right)_{v}\right|+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{*}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-a.e.

(9.6)

By boundedness of
h^{\prime}
,
h
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}
satisfying

\left|h\left(g+\mathbf{G}u\right)\right|\leq\left|h\left(g\right)\right|+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left|\mathbf{G}u\right|,

(9.7)

such that using the definition of
\mathbf{A}
from (
2.14
), we readily get

\left|\left(\mathbf{A}(\hat{u})\right)_{v}\right|\leq\left|h\left(g_{v}\right)\right|+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left(\left(\mathbf{G}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}+\left(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}\right)\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-a.e.

(9.8)

Now, combining (
9.6
) and (
9.8
) yields

\displaystyle\gamma\left|\hat{u}_{v}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\alpha}_{v}\right|+\left|h\left(g_{v}\right)\right|+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left(\left(\mathbf{G}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}+\left(\mathbf{G}^{\ast}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}+\left(\mathbf{H}_{\phi,\varrho}^{*}|\hat{u}|\right)_{v}\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-a.e.

(9.9)

Fix
t\in[0,T]
and define

m_{v}^{(t)}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{v>t\}}\mathbb{E}_{t}{[|\hat{u}_{v}|]},\quad v\in[0,T].

Then, taking the conditional expectation
\mathbbm{1}_{\{v>t\}}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]
on the last estimate (
9.9
) gives, thanks to the tower property,

\displaystyle\gamma m_{v}^{(t)}\leq\;

\displaystyle Y_{v}^{(t)}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{v>t\}}\left(\left(\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\mathbf{G}^{(t)}+\mathbf{H}^{(t)}_{\phi,\rho}\right)m^{(t)}\right)_{v}

\displaystyle+\left(\left(\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left(\mathbf{G}^{(t)}\right)^{\ast}+\left(\mathbf{H}^{(t)}_{\phi,\rho}\right)^{\ast}\right)m^{(t)}\right)_{v},\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-a.e.

(9.10)

Here
\mathbf{G}^{(t)}
(resp.
\mathbf{H}^{(t)}_{\phi,\rho}
) is the linear integral
\mathcal{L}^{2}-
operator associated with the kernel

G^{(t)}(v,s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{s>t\}}G(v,s)\quad\text{(resp.\penalty\ $H^{(t)}_{\phi,\rho}(v,s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{s>t\}}H_{\phi,\rho}(v,s)$)},

for
s,v\in[0,T]
, and
Y^{(t)}=(Y^{(t)}_{v})_{v\geq 0}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
is the auxiliary process defined by

\displaystyle Y^{(t)}_{v}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{v>t\}}\Big(

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\tilde{\alpha}_{v}|]+\mathbb{E}_{t}[|h(g_{v})|]+\int_{0}^{t}H_{\phi,\rho}(v,s)|\hat{u}_{s}|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}G(v,s)|\hat{u}_{s}|\mathrm{d}s\Big),\quad v\in[0,T].

(9.11)

Taking the
L^{2}([0,T])-
norm in the previous estimate (
9.10
), similarly to (
2.5
) and (
2.6
),

\gamma\lVert{m^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert{Y^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}}+2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\lVert{m^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}},\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s.},

where
\widetilde{C}_{H,G}
is given by (
9.2
). Considering also the joint measurability in
[0,T]\times\Omega
of the processes
(t,\omega)\mapsto\left\lVert m^{(t)}(\omega)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
and
(t,\omega)\mapsto\left\lVert Y^{(t)}(\omega)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
, it follows from assumption (
9.1
) that

\lVert{m^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}\lVert{Y^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T],\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s.}

(9.12)

Furthermore, from (
9.11
) we compute, also employing conditional Jensen’s and Jensen’s inequalities

\lVert{Y^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\quad\left(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)-\text{a.e.}

(9.13)

In particular, injecting the estimate (
9.13
) into (
9.12
), together with the triangular inequality yields

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{m^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}\Bigg(\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+T\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\Bigg).

(9.14)

Furthermore, by the estimate (
9.7
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, from (
2.14
) we obtain
\mathbb{P}
almost surely the following estimate:

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\hat{u})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\sqrt{TC_{G}}\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{G}^{\ast}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{m^{(t)}}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(9.15)

Starting from (
2.18
), taking the
L^{2}
-norm, injecting (
9.15
) and then (
9.14
) yields successively

\displaystyle\gamma\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\hat{u})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+2\sqrt{TC_{H}}\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\sqrt{T}\left(2\sqrt{C_{H}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{G}}\right)\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{G}^{\ast}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{m^{(t)}}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{G}^{\ast}}\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg(\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\left\lVert\tilde{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\left\lVert h(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{T}\bigg(2\sqrt{C_{H}}+\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\Big(\sqrt{C_{G}}+\sqrt{TC_{G}^{\ast}}\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{T}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{C}_{H,G}\Big)\bigg)\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

which holds
\mathbb{P}-
a.s. By (
9.1
) and (
9.5
), this inequality gives the desired a priori estimate (
9.3
), completing the proof.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
9.1
, the following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 9.2
.

Under the hypotheses of Lemma

, if

then any solution of (
2.18
) belongs to the space
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;L^{2}([0,T]))
.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

2.10
ensures the existence of an optimal trading strategy
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
that satisfies the nonlinear stochastic Fredholm equation (
2.18
).
For every
\gamma

) in Lemma

, and an application of Corollary

under assumption (
