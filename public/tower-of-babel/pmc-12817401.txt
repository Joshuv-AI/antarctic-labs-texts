Dual-targeted bacterial outer membrane vesicles enhance glioblastoma immunotherapy by regulating tumor microenvironment and inducing IFN-γ-mediated ferroptosis
Authors: Chen Chen, Xiaojing Zhu, Zefeng Song, Yutong Xing, Wenrui Jin, Fan Li, Pir Tariq Shah, Siqi Song, Haoran Geng, Kai Xu, Hong Wang, Zhenyong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) immunotherapy is limited by the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the tumor immune resistance. Here, we develop an engineered bacterial outer membrane vesicle (OMV) through safe and straightforward genetic modification of Escherichia coli, termed OMV-C-C, enabling co-expression of cell-penetrating peptides (CPP) and chlorotoxin (CLT) on its surface. The OMV-C-C efficiently crosses BBB and specifically targets tumor cells both in vitro and in vivo. A single intravenous administration of OMV-C-C significantly inhibit GBM growth by enhancing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in orthotopic brain tumor models. Mechanistically, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) released by CD8+ T cells induces ferroptosis-specific lipid peroxidation in tumor cells through inhibiting cystine-glutamate exchanger (system Xc−) and downregulating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a pathway demonstrated to be extremely activated in human GBM samples. Furthermore, IFN-γ facilitates erastin- and RSL-3-induced ferroptosis of tumor cells, particularly in temozolomide (TMZ)-resistant cells. Additionally, OMV-C-C@RSL-3 synergistically suppresses GBM growth in vivo. Thus, biosynthetically engineered OMV-C-C integrates intrinsic immunomodulatory activity with ferroptosis enhancement to strengthen glioblastoma immunotherapies, offering a versatile platform to overcome limitations in brain tumor immunotherapy. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and invariably lethal primary brain tumor, characterized by aggressive invasion, profound heterogeneity, and lack of effective therapies [1–3]. Despite maximal safe surgical resection supplemented with temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy being the most effective clinical treatment available, patient prognosis remains dismal, with a median survival of 15 months and a 5-year survival rate of less than 5% [4, 5]. GBM treatment remains a major challenge in neuro-oncology. Immunotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy that harnesses the host immune system for tumor elimination [6–8]. However, its efficacy in GBM therapy is severely constrained by multiple factors. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) presents a formidable obstacle, restricting the delivery of immune modulators to the tumor site [9–11]. The highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) actively inhibits immune cell infiltration and function, creating an immune resistance niche for tumor progression [12–15]. Furthermore, GBM exhibits intrinsic resistance to immunogenic cell death pathways, leading to serious disease recurrence [16, 17]. Addressing these barriers is essential to unlocking the full potential of immunotherapy for GBM.

Bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have recently emerged as a versatile nanoscale platform for drug delivery and immune modulation, offering distinct advantages over other nanomaterials commonly used in GBM treatment, including mammalian-derived extracellular vesicles, organic nanoparticles, and inorganic nanoparticles [18–21]. Compared to these nanomaterials, OMVs exhibit superior immunogenicity and simple genetic engineering modifications for therapeutic enhancement. However, the major limitation of OMV-based immunotherapy for GBM lies in its inefficient BBB penetration and nonspecific tumor-targeting capability. As a short peptide, cell-penetrating peptides (CPP) can be modified to enhance the ability of nanoparticles to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) both in vitro and in vivo, as well as improve their targeting efficiency [11, 22]. Moreover, OMVs have exerted potent anti-tumor immune effects by activating CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells in melanoma and breast cancer, leading to tumor clearance via secretion of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [23]. Despite these promising findings, the precise mechanisms underlying OMVs-mediated tumor suppression remains poorly understood.

Ferroptosis is a novel form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation of membrane, tightly controlled by glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [24, 25]. To escape this lethal process, tumor cells often upregulate GPX4, increasing their tolerance to oxidative stress and suppressing ferroptotic signaling. Many types of tumors exhibit intrinsic resistance to ferroptosis, severely limiting its application in treatment, which is also closely associated with therapy-refractory phenotypes such as TMZ resistance [26–28]. These challenges highlight an urgent demand for innovative approaches that can sensitize GBM cells to ferroptosis-based therapies.

Here, we developed an OMVs-based platform, OMV-C-C, for GBM immunotherapy (Fig. 1). CPP and chlorotoxin (CLT) were co-expressed on the surface of OMV-C-C through simple genetic engineering modifications. The CPP peptide of OMV-C-C facilitated efficient BBB crossing. While the CLT, a tumor-targeting peptide derived from scorpion venom, enabled specific targeting of OMV-C-C to GBM. We found that OMV-C-C elicited a significant anti-tumor immune response by modulating the GBM tumor microenvironment, specially enhancing robust CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and activation. We further demonstrated that IFN-γ, released by CD8⁺ T cell following OMV-C-C treatment, sensitized GBM cells to ferroptosis in an orthotopic GBM model. Moreover, bioinformatics analysis identified GPX4, a key ferroptosis regulator, as a critical driver of drug resistance in GBM. Combining OMV-C-C with ferroptosis inducers, RSL-3, significantly potentiated GBM cell death, particularly in TMZ-resistant tumors. By seamlessly integrating targeted immune activation with ferroptosis induction, this study established an efficient therapeutic strategy to overcome key TMZ-resistance in GBM. Collectively, these findings offer a promising approach to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in GBM.

Results

Preparation and characterization of dual-functional OMV-C-C

To develop dual-functional OMV capable of BBB penetration and GBM targeting, we genetically engineered non-pathogenic E. coli BL21 (DE3) to co-display cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) and tumor-targeting peptide-chlorotoxin (CLT) on their outer membrane surface. To achieve this, two separate plasmids were constructed to encode CPP and CLT. Their sequences were fused to the AIDAc transmembrane domain and tagged with Myc and HA respectively, which ensure stable co-expression of CPP and CLT on the outer membrane (Fig. 2a). After E. coli BL21 (DE3) was transformed with both plasmids, dual-functional OMV, termed OMV-C-C, was successfully biosynthesized and purified (Fig. 2b). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization showed that OMV-C-C maintained a spherical shape with an average diameter of 21 ± 7 nm, comparable to wild-type OMVs (OMV-WT) at 20 ± 6 nm (Fig. 2c, d). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed hydration diameters of 74 ± 1 nm for OMV-C-C and 66 ± 0 nm for OMV-WT (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Table 1). Zeta potential measurements indicated a slight increase of surface charge in OMV-C-C (−29 ± 1 mV) compared to OMV-WT (−36 ± 1 mV), likely due to the additional surface peptide modifications (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Table 1). To confirm successful peptide expression, flow cytometry analysis using Alexa fluor 594-labeled Myc-tag and FITC-labeled HA-tag determined that 99% of engineered E. coli (E. coli-C-C) displayed the dual peptides on their outer membrane (Fig. 2g). Western blot analysis further confirmed the presence of Myc-tagged CPP and HA-tagged CLT in both E. coli-C-C and OMV-C-C, while these proteins were absent in wide-type controls (Fig. 2h). Moreover, immunogold labeling directly showed surface localization of CPP and CLT on OMV-C-C (Fig. 2i), further validating the success of our engineering strategy. Additionally, SDS-PAGE analysis revealed no significant differences in the overall protein profiles between OMV-WT and OMV-C-C, indicating that the engineering process did not substantially alter OMV composition (Fig. 2j).

To assess the biosafety of OMV-C-C, mice were subjected to varying dosages and administration frequencies via tail vein injection (Supplementary Fig. 1a). No mortality was observed in OMV-C-C-treated mice across different dosages and administration frequencies compared to PBS-treated controls, demonstrating a favorable safety profile (Supplementary Fig. 1b). A significant increase in spleen size suggested strong immune activation (Supplementary Fig. 1c), yet body weight remained stable, further supporting the biocompatibility of OMV-C-C (Supplementary Fig. 1 d). Moreover, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining revealed no significant pathological damage in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Supplementary Fig. 1e). ELISA analysis of serum cytokines levels 15 days post-OMV-C-C injection showed a significantly increase in INF-γ, suggesting prolonged immune activation, while IL-6 and TNF-α levels remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 1f-h). Liver function markers including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) maintained within normal ranges, proving no detectable hepatotoxicity caused by these treatments (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j). Additionally, the sucrose preference test (SPT) results showed that the percentage of sucrose intake did not change in the OMV-C-C-treated group compared to the untreated group, indicating that there was no loss of pleasure symptoms (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The open-field test (OFT) results demonstrated that OMV-C-C treatment did not alter the total distance traveled and the time exploring the center area in comparation with the untreated group (Supplementary Fig. 2b-d). The Y-maze test results showed that spontaneous alternating behavior of mice entering three different arms consecutively was not different between OMV-C-C-treated group and untreated group (Supplementary Fig. 2e). These results suggested that OMV-C-C treatment also did not affect the behaviors of mice, including movement, memory, and emotion. In summary, we successfully engineered OMV-C-C of co-expressed CPP and CLT, with high biocompatibility and an acceptable safety profile, which demonstrated OMV-C-C as a promising candidate for GBM immunotherapy.

To determine whether CPP and CLT retained their functionality within the complex membrane environment of OMVs, we assessed the ability of OMV-C-C to cross the BBB and target tumor cells, separately. In an in vitro BBB model, OMV-C-C-ICG exhibited significantly higher transport efficiency (approximately 2-fold) compared to free ICG and OMV-WT-ICG, (Fig. 3a, b), with difference becoming more pronounced over time (4 h vs. 2 h) (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, pretreatment with methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) or tannic acid, both transcytosis-inhibiting drugs, significantly reduced OMV-C-C transport efficiency, indicating that CPP-mediated transcytosis plays a crucial role in enhanced BBB penetration (Fig. 3b). We then confirmed the ability of OMV-C-C to cross the BBB in vivo. We tracked OMV-C-C-ICG fluorescence intensity in mice. Signal peaked in the brain 2 h post-injection and gradually declined, with detectable fluorescence persisting up to 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 3a). In vivo and ex vivo fluorescence images of brain showed that OMV-C-C-ICG efficiently crossed BBB in mice, significantly outperforming free-ICG and OMV-WT-ICG (Fig. 3c, d). Additionally, ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs showed that while both OMV-WT and OMV-C-C mainly accumulated in the liver and kidneys-a common phenomenon of nanomaterials-OMV-C-C exhibited lower off-target organ accumulation than OMV-WT (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). To further assess the GBM-targeting efficiency of OMV-C-C via surface CLT, we treated GL261 mouse glioma cell and HMC3 human microglial cell with Dil-labeled OMV-WT and OMV-C-C. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that 86.1% of GL261 cells were positive for OMV-C-C-Dil fluorescence, compared to only 36.6% of HMC3 cells, demonstrating specifical target to glioma cells (Fig. 3e, f). In vivo validation using OMV-C-C-ICG showed a strong overlap between the bioluminescence signal from the tumor region and the ICG fluorescence, indicating selective accumulation of OMV-C-C at tumor sites (Fig. 3g, h). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of excised brains further confirmed these findings, showing OMV-C-C localization exclusively within tumor tissues, as supported by statistical analysis (Fig. 3i, g).

Given the immunostimulatory nature of OMVs, we next investigated whether it could induce immune responses in mouse primary bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Flow cytometry analysis showed a significant increase in CD80 and CD86 expression of BMDCs treated with OMV-C-C, confirming its role in promoting BMDCs maturation (Fig. 3k, l). Furthermore, ELISA analysis of cytokines levels in BMDCs culture supernatants revealed a marked increase in TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ secretion, further supporting the immunostimulatory effects of OMV-C-C (Fig. 3m). Thus, these results demonstrated that OMV-C-C possesses dual functionality: efficiently crossing the BBB and targeting GBM cells via the surface-expressed CPP and CLT, while also promoting dendritic cell maturation.

OMV-C-C inhibits GBM growth and prolongs survival in mice

To evaluate the anti-GBM effects of OMV-C-C in relation to its ability to cross the BBB and target tumors, we established an orthotopic GBM mouse model. Ten days post-tumor implantation, mice were administrated by a single intravenous injection with OMV-C-C, OMV-WT, or PBS, respectively (Fig. 4a). OMV-C-C treatment significantly reduced tumor burden compared to OMV-WT or PBS-treatment, as evidenced by bioluminescence imaging and quantitative analysis of bioluminescence intensity (Fig. 4b, c). By day 28, all PBS-treated mice had succumbed to tumor progression, while OMV-WT-treated mice also began to exhibit mortality. In contrast, all OMV-C-C-treated mice remained alive, indicating a productive survival benefit (Fig. 4b, c). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed the median survival time in OMV-C-C-treated mice was 35 days, which was significantly longer than those treated with PBS (22.5 days) or OMV-WT (25.5 days) (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, body weight monitoring curve showed that from day 18 onward, the PBS group exhibited a decline compared to the OMV-C-C group, though the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4e). H&E staining revealed that while OMV-WT partially inhibited tumor growth, OMV-C-C treatment exhibited a significantly stronger tumor-suppressive effect than it, further supporting its therapeutic potential (Fig. 4f). These results demonstrate that OMV-C-C exerts potent anti-tumor activity and significantly prolongs survival in a GBM mouse model, underscoring its strong potential as a promising therapeutic for GBM.

OMV-C-C-activated CD8⁺ T cell regulates tumor ferroptosis in GBM mouse model via IFN-γ

To determine whether the anti-tumor effects of OMV-C-C are associated with alternations in the tumor microenvironment (TME), we analyzed immune cell infiltration and functional status using cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF). A panel of 21 monoclonal antibodies was used to identify immune cell lineages and functional molecules in the orthotopic tumors. Unsupervised clustering analysis automatically categorized tumor-infiltrating cells into 30 clusters according to their expression profiles (Fig. 5a-b, and Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, OMV-C-C treatment led to a significant expansion of the CD8⁺ T cell population (Cluster 2) in the TME compared to PBS- or OMV-WT-treated groups (Fig. 5c). In contrast, the proportion of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages (Cluster 7, Cluster 29) was markedly reduced in the OMV-C-C-treated tumors (Fig. 5d). The percentage of other immune cell populations remained largely unchanged across all treatment groups (Fig. 5c-e). These results suggest that the anti-tumor efficacy of OMV-C-C is primarily driven by its ability to reshape the TME, enhancing CD8⁺ T cells infiltration while reducing M2 macrophages-mediated immunosuppression, thereby fostering a more robust anti-tumor immune response.

Previous studies have shown that CD8⁺ T cells regulate ferroptosis in tumor cells via IFN-γ during immunotherapy [29, 30]. Consistent with this, fifteen days after OMV-C-C administration, IFN-γ was the only cytokine exhibited a significant increase (Supplementary Fig. 1 h). To investigate whether OMV-C-C-mediated immune responses eradicate GBM cells through IFN-γ-induced ferroptosis, we assessed IFN-γ levels within the tumor sites. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that OMV-C-C treatment significantly increased IFN-γ production compared to PBS treatment (Fig. 5f). Moreover, IFN-γ was co-localized with CD8⁺ T cell in tumor tissues, indicating that CD8⁺ T cells were the primary source of IFN-γ following OMV-C-C treatment (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis showed a significant increase of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS), a hallmark of ferroptosis, in CD45− tumor cells from OMV-C-C-treated group compared to PBS-treated group. However, IFN-γ-induced lipid ROS accumulation was not occurred in CD45+ immune cells (Fig. 5g, supplementary Fig. 5a-c). Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay showed that OMV-C-C treatment also increased the lipid peroxidation in tumor tissues compared to untreated group (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Additionally, OMV-C-C-mediated tumor suppression was abrogated after pretreatment with anti-IFN-γ antibody, whereas pre-treatment with an anti-IgG antibody did not exhibit any suppression effect (Fig. 5h, supplementary Fig. 6). These results suggest that the anti-tumor effects mechanism of OMV-C-C are that IFN-γ, released by infiltrating CD8+ T cells, regulate ferroptosis in GBM.

To elucidate the role and molecular mechanism of IFN-γ in GBM ferroptosis at the cellular level, we conducted a series of validation experiments in GBM cells. Cytotoxicity analysis demonstrated that IFN-γ induced a concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect in U87-MG and GL261 cells (Fig. 6a-b). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase of lipid ROS levels following IFN-γ-treated cells in both cell lines compared to the control groups (Fig. 6c-d). Moreover, IFN-γ treatment significantly elevated the proportion of Annexin Ⅴ+/PI+ double-positive cells, indicating the enhancement in ferroptotic cell death (Fig. 6e-f). Mechanistically, IFN-γ treatment promoted the phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) and up-regulating interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF-1), while downregulating solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and solute carrier family member 2 (SLC3A2) (Fig. 6g-h). Notably, IFN-γ induced greater degree of ferroptosis in GL261 cells compared to U87-MG cells (Fig. 6a-f), suggesting intrinsic differential ferroptosis vulnerability between these GBM cell lines.

Ferroptosis pathway suppression is a hallmark of human GBM and strongly correlates with drug resistance

To determine whether ferroptosis suppression is a characteristic of human GBM, we analyzed transcriptomic data from GBM patient cohorts in the TCGA database, integrated with normal brain tissue data from the GTEx database. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis revealed significant alterations in gene expression (Fig. 7a). GSEA analysis revealed significant positive enrichment of ferroptosis-related pathways in GBM samples, including IFN-γ and ferroptosis pathway, indicating upregulation of genes in these pathways (Fig. 7b). Similarly, GSVA analysis showed higher activity of ferroptosis-related pathways in GBM populations, such as IFN-γ pathway and reactive oxygen species pathway (Fig. 7c). Moreover, the pathway driven the suppression of ferroptosis through enhancing antioxidant defenses were enriched, like PI3K-AKT pathway (Fig. 7c). These results suggested that ferroptosis-related suppression pathways are more active in GBM compared to normal samples. Additionally, GSEA and GSVA analysis also showed the enrichment of drug resistance-related pathways in GBM, including EGFR pathway, p53 pathway, Notch pathway (Fig. 7b-c). To further investigate the expression levels of specific genes within these pathways and their contributions to ferroptosis suppression and drug resistance, subsequent analyses of pathway-related genes were performed. Venn diagram analysis identified 28 overlapping genes involved in all three categories: DEGs, ferroptosis-related genes, and drug resistance-associated genes (Fig. 7d). The distinct expression patterns of these 28 genes were also shown in a heatmap, including TP53, SLC3A2, GPX4, ACSL4, and SLC7A11 (Fig. 7e). These findings indicate a strong correlation between ferroptosis suppression and tumor drug resistance.

To pinpoint key regulators of ferroptosis and drug resistance, we applied machine learning-based feature selection methods, including LASSO, Random Forest, and XGBoost, to analyze the 28 overlapping genes. LASSO coefficient profiles and cross-validation error plots identified candidate genes (Supplementary Fig. 7a-b). Random Forest and XGBoost determined prioritized key features based on error minimization and feature importance (Supplementary Fig. 7c-e). A Venn diagram of genes selected by all three methods revealed four critical ferroptosis-regulated genes: GPX4, SLC3A2, SLC7A11, and ATG7 (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Furthermore, survival analysis demonstrated that only high GPX4 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in GBM patients, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.42 (95% CI: 1.00–2.01.00.01, p = 0.047) (Fig. 7f). To experimentally validate our bioinformatics findings, we performed histopathological analyses on human GBM and normal brain tissues. H&E staining images confirmed morphological differences, with normal brain tissue exhibiting uniform cellular architecture and GBM samples showing characteristic tumor heterogeneity (Fig. 7g). Immunohistochemical staining images demonstrated significantly elevated GPX4 expression in GBM samples compared to normal brain tissues, further supporting ferroptosis suppression in GBM patients (Fig. 7h). Collectively, these findings underscore that ferroptosis pathway suppression, a key hallmark of GBM, may be closely correlated with drug resistance.

IFN-γ sensitizes TMZ-resistant GBM cells to ferroptosis

To determine whether ferroptosis induction could overcome temozolomide (TMZ) resistance in GBM, we first assessed TMZ susceptibility in GL261 and U87-MG cells. It was showed that GL261 cells exhibited much higher degree of TMZ resistance compared to U87-MG cells (Fig. 8a). Treatment with ferroptosis inducers RSL-3 and erastin triggered a concentration-dependent cell death in both cell lines, with GL261 cells displaying greater sensitivity, particularly to RSL-3 (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Pre-treatment with Ferrostatin-1 (Fer1), a ferroptosis inhibitor, effectively blocked cell death in both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Notably, co-treatment with ferroptosis inducers and IFN-γ synergistically enhanced cell death of GL261 and U87-MG cells compared to RSL-3 or erastin alone treatment, as determined by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 8b, c). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the Annexin Ⅴ+/PI+ double positive population in both cell lines following combined treatment, compared to treatment with RSL-3 or erastin, respectively (Fig. 8d, e and Supplementary Fig. 8b). Furthermore, an enhanced percentage of lipid peroxidation positive cell was observed by flow cytometry in the co-treatment group, further indicating the augmentation of ferroptosis by IFN-γ (Fig. 8f, g and supplementary Fig. 8c). In addition, western blot analysis revealed that RSL-3 significantly reduced GPX4 expression, this inhibiting effect was further potentiated by IFN-γ co-treatment (Fig. 8h). These results suggest that TMZ-resistant cells exhibit increased sensitivity to ferroptosis, and IFN-γ enhances ferroptotic cell death following combination with ferroptosis inducers (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Building on the ability of OMV-C-C to enhance IFN-γ production at the tumor site, we encapsulated RSL-3 into OMV-C-C to generate OMV-C-C@RSL-3 nanoparticles, aiming to maximize the synergistic therapeutic effects of IFN-γ and ferroptosis inducers in vivo. Characterization analysis confirmed that OMV-C-C@RSL-3 maintained comparable hydration diameters, zeta potential, and morphology to OMV-C-C as demonstrated by DLS and TEM measurements (Supplementary Fig. 10a-c). The loading efficiency of RSL-3 was 91.8 ± 0.6%, and it could be slowly released from OMV-C-C@RSL-3 (Supplementary Fig. 10d). The concentration-time curve of the blood plasma showed that the pharmacokinetics of the RSL-3 were modified by OMV-C-C@RSL-3 nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 11). The corresponding pharmacokinetic parameters were shown in Supplementary Table 3. The terminal elimination half-life (t1/2) values of free RSL-3 and OMV-C-C@RSL-3 were 14.10 ± 7.68 and 105.16 ± 17.25 h, respectively. The AUC (0–24 h) values were 1.77 ± 0.02 µg·h·mL − 1 for free RSL-3 and 1.51 ± 0.11 µg·h·mL − 1 for OMV-C-C@RSL-3. Their clearance, CLz ((L/h)/kg), were 286.16 ± 113 ((L/h)/kg) for free RSL-3 and 80.24 ± 21.42 ((L/h)/kg) for OMV-C-C@RSL-3. The mean residence time (MRT) values were 11.27 ± 0.95 and 10.51 ± 0.34 h, separately. These results suggested that the pharmacokinetic profiles of RSL-3 were improved using OMV-C-C@RSL-3 nanoparticles. To evaluate the therapeutic efficiency of OMV-C-C@RSL-3, TMZ-resistant GL261-Luc tumor-bearing mice were divided into four treatment groups: PBS, TMZ, TMZ + RSL-3, and OMV-C-C@RSL-3 (Supplementary Fig. 10e). Bioluminescence imaging demonstrated a substantial reduction of tumor burden in OMV-C-C@RSL-3-treated mice compared to other groups (Fig. 8i). Notably, OMV-C-C@RSL-3 exhibited significant tumor suppression in TMZ-resistant GBM, exceeding the efficacy of TMZ alone and TMZ combined with RSL-3 (Fig. 8j). Importantly, OMV-C-C@RSL-3 treatment was well tolerated, as evidenced by stable body weight throughout the study (Fig. 8k). Furthermore, survival curve analysis further revealed a prominent extension in survival time in the OMV-C-C@RSL-3 treatment group compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 8l). These results collectively demonstrate that OMV-C-C@RSL-3 effectively overcomes the TMZ-resistance of GBM by inducing ferroptosis in vivo, offering a potent therapeutic strategy against TMZ-resistant GBM.

Discussion

In this study, we developed OMV-C-C, a genetically engineered nano-platform, for GBM immunotherapy. Our findings demonstrate that OMV-C-C effectively crosses the BBB and preferentially accumulates in tumor tissues, leading to potent anti-tumor immune responses. Mechanically, anti-tumor effects of OMV-C-C in an orthotopic GBM model rely on CD8+ T cell-released IFN-γ, which in turn induces ferroptosis in tumor cells. Moreover, our data reveals significant suppression of ferroptosis pathways in human GBM, which contributes to drug resistance. Importantly, the OMV-C-C@RSL-3 nanoparticles, combining ferroptosis induction with immunotherapy, offers a robust therapeutic strategy for overcoming TMZ-resistance in GBM.

The major novelty of our study lies in the co-expression of cell-penetrating peptides and tumor-targeting peptides on the surface of OMVs by genetic engineering, enabling efficient BBB penetration and specifical tumor cells targeting. This differs from previous approaches that relied on indirect delivery by immune cell-mediated transport or chemical surface modifications [31, 32]. Conventional chemical modification methods have limited attachment efficiency and may disrupt CPP function due to the vigorous reaction [33, 34]. In contrast, our genetic approach ensures a higher expression efficiency while naturally preserving peptides functionality. The insertion of the lactose operon in plasmid allowed us to control the expression of CPP and CLT on the surface of OMV through isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) induction (Fig. 2a). Moreover, CPP itself may facilitate GBM targeting by binding to heparan sulfate on tumor cell surfaces, working synergistically with CLT [35]. This approach provides an effective strategy for optimizing signal peptides and enhancing their expression through vector modifications to achieve precise control over peptide abundance.

Crucially, we found a previously unrecognized mechanism that the anti-tumor effect of OMVs is dependent on IFN-γ-induced ferroptosis in tumor cells. IFN-γ is known to exert anti-tumor effects by regulating adaptive immune responses, including, but not limited to, the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, the polarization of Th1 immune responses, and the modulation of regulatory T cells [36, 37]. Our study shows OMV-C-C effectively crosses the BBB and induces robust CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation, leading to enhanced IFN-γ production in tumor tissues (Fig. 5c, f). While multiple immune cell subsets, including NK cells, CD4⁺ T cells, NKT cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, etc. can secrete IFN-γ, we observed no significant differences in NK cell proportions between OMV-WT and OMV-C-C- treatments, despite an overall increase compared to PBS treatment (Fig. 5c). This suggests that CD8⁺ T cells play an important role in OMV-C-C-mediated immunotherapy. Moreover, OMVs have been shown to exert antitumor effects in peripheral solid tumors, such as breast cancer and melanoma, primarily through IFN-γ released by activated CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells [23]. However, the underlying anti-tumor mechanism remains unknown. Our findings reveal that IFN-γ significantly elevates lipid ROS levels in GBM cells, thereby promoting ferroptosis (Fig. 6). The application of recombinant IFN-γ neutralizing antibody markedly inhibited the anti-tumor effect of OMV-C-C (Fig. 5h), suggesting the important role of IFN-γ-driven ferroptosis in OMV-C-C-mediated anti-GBM immunotherapy. Thus, future studies should explore strategies to further enhance immune responses by inducing other immune-active agents.

Our results underscore the power of combining immunostimulants with inducers of immunogenic cell death, such as ferroptosis, to enhance tumor immunotherapy. This cell death type could kill tumor cells and exert a synergistic immunostimulatory effect through released contents from dying cells [38]. Given the ferroptosis-sensitizing effect observed with OMV-C-C, we designed a composite nanotherapeutic platform, OMV-C-C@RSL-3, to maximize ferroptosis induction. In vitro, IFN-γ synergizes with RSL-3 to enhance ferroptotic cell death in GBM cells (Fig. 8b-g). In vivo, OMV-C-C@RSL-3 treatment significantly reduces GBM burden (Fig. 8i-l). These findings suggest that OMV-C-C can serve as an adjuvant therapy for GBM treatment, particularly for overcoming chemotherapy resistance. Future studies should further investigate the intrinsic mechanisms of nanomaterial-mediated tumor suppression and explore additional synergistic drug combinations to potentiate therapeutic efficacy.
