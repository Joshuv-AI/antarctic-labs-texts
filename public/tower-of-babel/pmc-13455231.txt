Optimizing shielding protocols in tube current modulation chest computed tomography: a phantom study
Authors: Qingting Sun, Yihan Fan, Pengfei Zhu, Jing Wang, Baohui Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing public radiation protection awareness has heightened clinical focus on shielding. This study examines the relationship between shielding material placement, the overranging (OR), and scout image visibility in chest tube current modulation (TCM) computed tomography (CT). It analyzes changes in overall radiation dose within the patient imaging area and image quality of tissues at the scanning boundary near the shield to provide evidence for selecting optimal clinical shielding protocols.Materials and methodsThis study utilized an anthropomorphic phantom to perform chest CT scans under TCM on a GE Revolution CT scanner. Lead shielding was placed intra- and extra-OR, categorized as scout-visible or invisible, to assess its impact on imaging area dose and changes in boundary organ image quality versus an unshielded control.ResultsIntra-OR shields increased the radiation dose in the imaging area, with volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) rising by up to 22.9% (range: 0.43%-22.9%) depending on the collimation-pitch combination, and caused degradation of image quality at the scan boundary (SNR and CNR reductions approximately twice those of the extra-OR shields group, with 50% of image scores ≤ 2, indicating limited diagnostic value). In contrast, extra-OR shields produced minimal changes: CTDIvol increased by less than 3% in all cases, SNR/CNR reductions were below 10%, and all images scored ≥ 3 (meeting diagnostic requirements). Furthermore, scout-visible lead shielding was associated with increased radiation dose and reduced image quality. Conversely, scout-invisible lead shielding had a negligible effect on radiation dose but led to a more substantial degradation in image quality relative to the visible configuration.ConclusionsBased on this phantom study, to optimize radiation protection and image quality in clinical chest TCM CT examinations, it is suggested that the shielding device be placed in the extra-OR region. However, as these findings derive from phantom experiments, further validation in clinical settings is warranted before routine application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02474-1.

Introduction

The widespread use of computed tomography (CT) has led to a steady increase in global examinations, with approximately 260 million performed annually [1]. Although CT accounts for only 17% of all radiological procedures, it contributes about 50% of the total medical radiation burden [2]. The radiation dose associated with CT scans for patients has attracted widespread public attention.

To reduce CT radiation dose, common strategies include optimizing scan parameters (e.g., lowering tube voltage and current without compromising image quality) [3, 4], using advanced technologies such as tube current modulation (TCM) which automatically adjusts tube current based on tissue attenuation differences at different Z-axis positions in the scout images to balance radiation dose and image quality [5–7], and applying contact shielding [8]. Contact shielding includes in-plane bismuth shields placed within the imaging volume and out-of-plane lead shields wrapped outside the imaging volume. Out-of-plane shields reduce scattered radiation by placing shielding material outside the planned scan area, thereby decreasing unnecessary exposure to healthy tissues [8, 9]. For instance, in pediatric chest CT, out-of-plane shields have been demonstrated to reduce radiation dose outside the scanning range from 19.1% to 4.3% [10]. However, in recent years, several international professional organizations have provided clear guidance on the use of contact shielding in CT examinations. The American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM), in its position statement, recommends against the routine use of patient shielding in CT, citing potential interference with TCM systems that may inadvertently increase patient dose, as well as the possible introduction of artifacts that degrade image quality [11]. Guidelines published by the British Institute of Radiology (BIR) indicate that the use of shielding in CT lacks a dosimetric basis and may have adverse effects [12]. In addition, the relevant European consensus also emphasizes that under the action of TCM systems, the protective effect of shielding may be canceled or even counterproductive [13]. While these guidelines collectively highlight the potential risks of CT shielding, they do not offer strategies to address the continued demand for shielding from some clinicians or patients. While several studies have reported on localized dose reduction using shielding materials, most have focused exclusively on dose variations within the shielded area [10, 14]. A systematic evaluation of how out-of-plane shields affect radiation dose within the primary imaging region remains lacking. Larjava et al. [15] found that when shielding material is visible on the scout image, the tube current per slice near the shielded end increases significantly, with a maximum cumulative mA increase of up to 217%. Kennedy et al. [16] further noted that the dose optimization effect of lead shielding is highly dependent on its relative position to the imaged region. However, these studies did not address the critical variable of whether the shielding material is visible on the scout image. Conventional out-of-plane shielding is usually visible in the scout image [8, 15], but is the interference to the TCM system different if the shielding material is placed outside the scout image range (scout- invisible shields). To our knowledge, there are no studies that systematically evaluate the effect of “scout- invisible shields” on radiation dose and border tissue image quality in the CT scan range.

Helical CT has an inherent, unavoidable phenomenon called overranging (OR), where additional gantry rotations are required for complete data reconstruction. This causes the actual scan range to extend beyond the planned imaging volume, increasing radiation dose [17, 18]. Studies show that OR significantly raises the dose to organs adjacent to the scan area. Therefore, this study developed a shielding strategy based on OR [19, 20].

Based on the OR range, shielding materials were placed either within (intra-OR) or outside (extra-OR) the OR region, and further classified as scout-visible or scout-invisible. This study aimed to evaluate how these shielding strategies affect total radiation dose within the scan range and image quality at the scan boundary in chest CT under TCM, providing a basis for clinical optimization.

Materials and methods

Study protocol

The experiment was conducted on the 256-row CT scanner (Revolution CT, GE Healthcare, USA) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University. Experimental materials included an adult anthropomorphic phantom (PBU-60, Kyoto Kagaku Co., Ltd., Japan), composed of tissue-equivalent materials and sized to represent a small adult (approximately 165 cm, 50 kg), containing structures such as the lungs, heart, liver, and vertebral bones, and widely used in CT dose optimization studies; and lead shielding (Scanflex Medical AB, model 60 × 70 cm NL, 0.5 mm Pb equivalent), with a lateral width sufficient to completely encircle the phantom’s cross-section (approximately 60 cm), thereby providing consistent attenuation. The scanning parameters were set as follows: a planned imaging range from the apex of the lung to the bottom of the diaphragm (295 mm), fixed tube voltage of 120 kV, TCM technique with a tube current range of 210–505 mA, slice thickness of 5 mm, and rotation time of 0.5 s. A total of eight combinations of pitch and collimation width were used: for a collimation of 40 mm, the corresponding pitches were 0.516/0.984/1.375/1.531; for a collimation of 80 mm, the corresponding pitches were 0.508/0.992/1.375/1.531. For each combination, the corresponding OR length was calculated (Sect. Calculation of OR).

All collimation-pitch combinations were tested under six shielding strategies. Based on the OR length, the placement of shielding material was classified into two categories: (1) intra-OR shields: the leading edge of the shield was positioned 1 cm outside the planned scan boundary; (2) extra-OR shields: the leading edge of the shield was positioned 1 cm outside the end of the OR region. The 1 cm distance was measured on the phantom surface using a ruler (precision: 1 mm) and independently verified by two researchers, with a positioning error of ± 1 mm. Based on whether the shield was visible on the scout image, the strategies were further classified as: (1) scout-visible shields: the scout image scan range includes partial shielding materials; (2) scout-invisible shields: the scout scan range was reduced to exclude the shielding material from the scout field of view. The specific groups were as follows: (1)AV: Unshielded control group (scout image length is consistent with the scout-visible shields groups); (2)AI: Unshielded control group (scout image length is consistent with the scout-invisible shields groups); (3)BV: intra-OR shields + scout-visible shields; (4)BI: intra-OR shields + scout-invisible shields; (5)CV: extra-OR shields + scout-visible shields; (6)CI: extra-OR shields + scout-invisible shields. Both control groups (AV/AI) had no shield, but their scout scan lengths matched those of the corresponding experimental groups to exclude the effect of scout length. Shielding was placed before scout exposure and remained unmoved during the procedure. All shields continuously covered the phantom from the starting position toward the distal end, maintaining the prescribed gap to the planned imaged region. The phantom was not moved after placement.

The phantom was placed on the scanner table with its lateral line at the midaxillary level. Shielding was applied per protocol, ensuring all materials remained outside the planned imaging volume, scanning was then initiated.

Data processing and statistical analysis

All measurements were repeated twice, and the mean values (CTDIvol, SNR, CNR) were used for analysis. The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated to assess repeatability. Only two repeated measurements were performed due to practical constraints, which is a limitation of this study. The percentage changes in CTDIvol, SNR, and CNR for each experimental group relative to the corresponding blank control group were calculated separately. As this was an exploratory phantom study aimed at observing trends in radiation dose and image quality rather than establishing definitive statistical conclusions, only descriptive statistics (percentage changes, CV) are reported.

Result

CV and repeatability

In the two repeated experiments, the CV of CTDIvol was less than 2% in all cases, and the CV of SNR and CNR for each organ was less than 5%. These CV values suggest acceptable measurement consistency, although the assessment was limited to only two repeats. The mean values of the two measurements were therefore used for subsequent analysis.

OR under various beam collimation and pitch configurations

The calculated OR lengths were consistent across the three different planned scan lengths (16 cm, 26 cm, and 36 cm). The OR, which is influenced by the selection of beam collimation and pitch, and the corresponding OR calculated using the AAPM method are presented in Fig. 2. As the collimation width increased from 40 mm to 80 mm, the OR showed a corresponding increase. Furthermore, raising the pitch from its minimum values (0.516 for 40 mm collimation and 0.508 for 80 mm collimation) to 1.531 resulted in a significant increase in OR. However, at a pitch of 0.984/0.992, the OR decreased, representing the minimal OR under each respective collimation width.

Discussion

With growing public awareness of radiation protection, the clinical use of shielding materials has gained increasing emphasis. However, most research has focused on dose reduction within the shielded area. For example, Yu L et al. [10] found that positioning lead aprons 1, 5, and 10 cm below the scan range reduced radiation outside the scan area by 19.1%, 10.1%, and 4.3%, respectively. Nevertheless, several international organizations now advise against routine patient shielding in CT, as it can interfere with TCM, paradoxically increase patient dose, and introduce artifacts that degrade image quality. However, these guidelines lack detailed recommendations for optimizing shield placement to minimize risk in cases where some patients or clinicians continue to request shielding. The present study was therefore designed to address this demand. Using OR as a reference, we systematically evaluated the effects of shield placement (intra-OR vs. extra-OR) and its visibility on the scout view on total radiation dose within the chest CT scan range and on image quality at the boundary tissues under TCM mode, aiming to provide specific and feasible risk minimization strategies for patients who request shielding. This study evaluated the impact of different shielding approaches on radiation dose and image quality. Specifically, the intra-OR shields group demonstrated more significant increases in radiation dose and greater declines in image quality for organs at the scanning boundary compared to the extra-OR shields group. As shown in Table 2, the BV group (intra-OR + scout-visible) exhibited a maximum CTDIvol increase of 22.90%, whereas the CV group (extra-OR + scout-visible) showed CTDIvol increases of less than 3% in all cases. Meanwhile, the percentage decreases in SNR and CNR for each target organ were greater in the BV group than in the CV group. This difference was directly confirmed by the mA curves: the intra-OR shields group showed a sharp increase in mA near the shielded end, whereas the mA curve of the extra-OR shields group almost overlapped with that of the control group. This finding is consistent with the report by Larjava et al. [15], which demonstrated that when applying shielding, the tube current per slice increased markedly at slices near the shielded end, with the maximum cumulative mA increase reaching 217%, correspondingly elevating the final radiation output. The underlying mechanism is that the TCM system automatically adjusts the tube current based on differences in tissue attenuation along the z-axis on the scout view [5, 7]. When scout-visible intra-OR shields are applied, the shielding material appears as a high-density structure on the scout image, and the TCM system misinterprets it as highly attenuating patient tissue. Consequently, the system compensates by increasing the tube current to maintain image quality, ultimately leading to an elevated radiation dose within the scan range. However, this compensatory increase in tube current does not fully offset the X-ray attenuation caused by the shielding material and may instead introduce streak artifacts and beam-hardening artifacts. The subjective scoring results of this study further support this explanation: 50% of the scan protocol images in the BV group received a score of only 2, characterized by partially unclear anatomical structures, blurred margins, or mild artifacts. In contrast, all images in the control group (AV) received scores above 3, indicating they all met diagnostic requirements. By comparison, the scores in the CV group were close to those of the control group, with no noticeable degradation in diagnostic quality. These findings indicate that intra-OR shields not only increase radiation dose but also substantially impair the clinical diagnostic utility of the boundary region. Previously, a phantom study reported that compared to unshielded scans, placing shielding in proximity to the patient’s scanning region resulted in higher radiation dose to the patient when investigating fetal dose using a Siemens Somatom Definition Flash CT scanner [22]. This is consistent with our findings, which show that the use of shielding materials leads to an increase in the radiation dose in the scanned area. This occurs because lead shielding may introduce streak artifacts or reduce visibility [13], thereby compromising diagnostic imaging accuracy. To maintain image quality, the TCM system automatically increases the mA, resulting in increased radiation output. Relevant studies have demonstrated that the use of bismuth shielding in CT scanning leads to a slight decrease in the CNR [23]. This finding deviates from our results, which showed a marked decline in image quality. We hypothesize that this discrepancy may arise from the different shielding materials employed. Kennedy et al. [16] have previously established that the degree of dose optimization achieved by lead shielding depends critically on its position relative to the imaging area. The maximum reduction in dose to the shielded region occurs when the superior edge of the shielding is aligned with the inferior boundary of the imaging volume. Another study [24] demonstrated that employing conventional lead aprons to provide consistent attenuation around the patient can reduce dose to the shielded area by up to 99%. However, although the percentage reduction in dose outside the scanning area due to shielding may be substantial, the scattered radiation outside this area is relatively minimal compared to the radiation dose within the scanning region, as indicated by previously published research [25]. Therefore, we focus on the changes in dose and image quality within the scanned area by implementing scout-visible shields. The increase in radiation dose and the decrease in image quality in the intra-OR shields group were significantly lower than those in the extra-OR shields group because the shielding material of the intra-OR shields group was closer to the scanning boundary, which interfered more with the image quality of the terminal tissue, resulting in a larger dose output after TCM. Therefore, we believe that for patients requiring shielding, placing the shield outside the OR region is more appropriate; however, the specific feasibility still requires clinical validation.

Furthermore, this study also investigated the effects of scout-visible versus scout-invisible shields on TCM chest CT. The results showed that with intra-OR shields, the percentage increase in CTDIvol was below 2% in all cases for the scout-invisible shields group, whereas the scout-visible shields group exhibited a considerably larger percentage increase. With extra-OR shields, both scout-visible and scout-invisible shield groups showed small percentage increases in CTDIvol, with the former below 2% and the latter below 1% in all cases. Meanwhile, with intra-OR shields, the percentage decreases in SNR and CNR for the scout-invisible shields group were markedly different from those of the scout-visible group, whereas with extra-OR shields, the differences in the percentage decreases in SNR and CNR between the two groups were small. Previous studies have demonstrated that when the CT scanner’s TCM is based on the scout image, scout-visible shields located outside the planned scan range may substantially increase patient dose [15]. Our results are consistent with this conclusion. This seemingly contradictory phenomenon can be explained by the TCM compensation mechanism: under scout-visible shields, the TCM system detects the high-attenuation region and actively increases the mA [3, 5, 7], leading to a marked rise in radiation dose; however, this increased dose also improves image quality (although still below that of the control group) [23]. Under scout-invisible shields, the TCM system does not detect the high attenuation, so the mA remains almost unchanged; nevertheless, the shielding material is still present during the actual scan, and the resulting X-ray attenuation causes a substantial degradation in image quality without dose compensation. The subjective scoring results provide strong evidence for this mechanism. Under intra-OR shields, 75% of the protocols in the scout-invisible shielding group (BI) received a score of 2 (limited diagnostic value), whereas only 50% of the protocols in the scout-visible shields group (BV) scored 2, with the remainder scoring 3. This indicates that, despite the higher dose in the BV group, the clinical acceptability of image quality was better in the BV group than in the BI group. However, from the perspective of the ALARA principle, intra-OR shields should still be avoided. Under intra-OR shield conditions, the two shielding strategies have comparable effects on radiation dose and image quality, and either may be flexibly selected depending on the clinical scenario.

The findings of this study are highly consistent with the core recommendations of international guidelines, such as those from the AAPM, which advise against routine use of shielding in CT because shielding may interfere with TCM, increase patient dose, and degrade image quality. However, in clinical practice where some patients or clinicians strongly request shielding, optimized strategies balancing image quality and radiation dose are needed; the present study provides concrete guidance: if shielding is required, it should be placed extra-OR, with its scout visibility flexibly chosen according to clinical needs. It is worth mentioning that OR is an inherent phenomenon in helical CT scanning. Currently, there is an international debate regarding its precise definition: (1) Acquisition length minus the planned scan length [26]; (2) Acquisition length minus the reconstructed image length [27]. The reconstructed image length equals the planned scan length plus the slice thickness. This study adopts definition (1). Figure 2 shows that the length of the OR increases significantly with the increase of pitch (except 0.984/0.992). At the same time, with the increase of collimation width (from 40 mm to 80 mm), the length of the OR also increases accordingly. This is consistent with the conclusion of Tzedakis et al. [19]. It should be noted that the specific variation in OR pitches of 0.984/0.992 is not an isolated or incidental finding. In a 2024 study, researchers [28] also reported a marked reduction in OR length under the combination of a 40 mm collimation and a pitch of 0.984, achieving a reduction of up to 35%. The study explicitly attributed this dose optimization effect to an algorithmic approach to mitigating helical OR. In this study, groups were defined based on the OR region. In the intra-OR group, shielding was placed 1 cm outside the planned scan boundary; in the extra-OR group, 1 cm outside the OR region. The 1 cm distance was chosen because: (1) the smallest unilateral OR length among the eight combinations was 1.47 cm, so 1 cm clearly distinguished intra- and extra-OR placement. (2) At least half an extra rotation is needed at both ends of the scan for complete data reconstruction, requiring adequate distance between shielding and scan boundaries [27]. (3) A 1 cm distance facilitated experimental operation and uniformity of placement. Placement was verified on the phantom surface using a ruler (1 mm precision) by two researchers, with a tolerance of ± 1 mm. Even with this potential error, intra- and extra-OR placement remained clearly distinguishable.

This study has several limitations: experiments were performed on only one CT scanner (GE Revolution), whereas TCM algorithms vary across different manufacturers and models; a tissue-equivalent phantom was used instead of real patients, precluding the simulation of complex factors such as respiratory motion and anatomical variations, thus requiring clinical validation; the study was limited to chest CT and may not apply to regions with different attenuation characteristics, such as the abdomen or pelvis. Future studies should use a wider or unrestricted mA range, increase repeat measurements, include multiple CT scanners and real patient data, adopt SSDE for more accurate dose estimation, and extend the investigation to other anatomical sites.

Conclusion

International guidelines recommend against the routine use of patient shielding in CT because shielding may interfere with TCM, increase patient dose, and degrade image quality. However, in some situations, patients or clinicians may still request shielding for reassurance. If shielding is unavoidable, it is recommended to place the shielding material outside the OR region to minimize its adverse effects on radiation dose and image quality. On this basis, the visibility of the shield on the scout can be flexibly selected according to the specific clinical scenario and diagnostic priorities. The present study does not advocate routine use of shielding but rather provides evidence for optimization strategies when its use is unavoidable. It should be emphasized that this study was based on a phantom experiment, and the conclusions require validation with clinical patient data to confirm their generalizability and safety; caution is warranted when translating these findings into clinical practice.
