Assessment of the biomechanical performance of digitally manufactured space maintainers: a finite element analysis
Authors: Meltem Karahan, Bahar Basak Kızıltan Elıacık, Berk Yuzbasıoglu, Beyza Korkmaz, Elifnur Guzelce Sultanoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of band and loop space maintainers, fabricated using digital technologies, and the luting cements used for their fixation, on mesio-distal tooth movement using the Finite Element Analysis method. Additionally, the biomechanical performance of the materials was analyzed by comparing the maximum Von Mises stress distribution. Methodology: A total of fifteen finite element models were created using combinations of five different band and loop materials (BruxZir, Conventional, Trilor, polyetheretherketone and carbon fiber-reinforced polyetheretherketone) and three types of luting cements: conventional glass ionomer cement, resin-modified glass ionomer cement, and dual-cure resin cement. A vertical force of 70 Newton was applied in the occluso-gingival direction at the band-loop junction in each model. Stress distribution and potential failure zones were analyzed using the finite element analysis method. Results: Among the tested materials, BruxZir exhibited the least amount of displacement and generated the lowest stress on the tooth. It was followed by Conventional, Trilor, and carbon fiber-reinforced polyetheretherketone and polyetheretherketone. Polyetheretherketone showed the highest level of displacement. When comparing luting cements, the resin-modified glass ionomer, which has the lowest elastic modulus, caused the greatest mesial movement. This was followed by the self-adhesive resin cement and the conventional glass ionomer cement. Conclusions: The integration of digital technologies in pediatric dentistry enables the clinical use of space maintainers fabricated from various advanced materials. However, further clinical studies are necessary to assess the long-term success and biocompatibility of these digital materials.Keywords: FEM, 3 Dimensional diagnosis and treatment planning, Pediatric dentistry, AppliancesSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Materials science, Medical research

Background

Primary teeth serve as natural space maintainers and play a critical role in guiding the proper alignment and eruption of permanent teeth within the dental arch1. However, the premature loss of primary teeth due to extraction, trauma, or caries may compromise the eruption space required for permanent teeth2. This condition may lead to crowding and the development of various malocclusions, which can affect occlusion both aesthetically and functionally. Moreover, space loss may result in the rotation or mesial drift of adjacent teeth, potentially causing impaction of the permanent teeth. If a space maintainer is not planned following premature loss of primary teeth, these complications may necessitate more invasive interventions in adulthood, such as orthodontic treatment or tooth extraction3.

The band and loop space maintainer is the most commonly used appliance for unilateral tooth loss. Additionally, it can be employed in cases of bilateral primary molar loss when the permanent incisors have not yet erupted or developed4,5. Traditionally, these appliances are fabricated from stainless steel and typically require impression-taking, model preparation, and a labor-intensive manual workflow involving both the dentist and the dental technician. In addition to custom-fabricated appliances, prefabricated and chair-side fabricated space maintainers—delivered in a single visit without the need for impressions—are also available in clinical practice, although they may present challenges in adaptation accuracy and long-term durability6–8. However, they are also associated with poor esthetics, potential to cause metal allergies, and an increased risk of failure due to factors such as cement loss, solder joint failure, or soft tissue impingement9–13. Failure rates of space maintainers have been reported to range between 10 and 63%. Although cement loss is most commonly cited as the primary cause of failure, other reported reasons include solder or band breakage, soft tissue lesions, impaction of the first permanent molars, appliance displacement, occlusal interferences, and demineralization of the abutment teeth9,10,13,14.

In recent years, various studies have explored the use of digital technology as an alternative to conventional band and loop space maintainers, aiming to overcome their associated disadvantages4,15–18. The integration of digital technologies into pediatric dentistry has demonstrated remarkable progress in contemporary clinical practice. One of the key advantages of these innovations is the enhancement of patient compliance and acceptance of treatment among children. Space maintainers fabricated using CAD/CAM or 3D printing technologies with modern and biocompatible materials are referred to as 'Digital Space Maintainers’ or ‘Digitainers’. It is believed that the challenges and limitations associated with conventional fabrication methods can be effectively addressed through the use of these technologies19. Beyond esthetic and workflow-related advantages, digital fabrication may also influence the mechanical performance of space maintainers. Enhanced precision in marginal fit, uniform material density, and the elimination of solder joints or manual bending steps can potentially improve stress distribution, minimize weak points, and enhance long-term durability20. Digitainers, fabricated from biocompatible materials, hold the potential to overcome the challenges associated with conventional manufacturing methods. The advancement of digital workflows enables the production of personalized orthodontic appliances by eliminating labor-intensive manual processes, thereby facilitating faster, more reliable, and longer-lasting outcomes. Reduced costs, shorter treatment times, and improved patient compliance are notable benefits4,16,19. Moreover, the demand for metal-free materials is steadily increasing due to aesthetic concerns and the potential complications associated with metal allergies21. Considering the opportunities offered by the digital era, the clinical adoption of these technologies is rapidly expanding19.

Recent advances in computer-aided design and manufacturing technologies have facilitated the production of digitally fabricated space maintainers, resulting in enhanced precision, improved adaptation, and greater material reliability compared to conventional techniques. Nevertheless, biomechanical data remains insufficient to explain how digitally manufactured band and loop space maintainers, when used with different luting agents, affect stress distribution within the surrounding dental structures tissues. Although digital fabrication techniques hold promise for improving clinical performance,there is still no biomechanical evidence—particularly finite element analysis studies—evaluating how digitally manufactured band and loop space maintainers and different luting cements affect stress distribution and tooth displacement. Therefore, investigating these aspects is essential to determine whether the anticipated mechanical advantages of digital fabrication translate into measurable differences in performance. The present study aimed to assess the effects of digitally fabricated band and loop space maintainers, together with different luting cements, on mesio-distal tooth movement and to analyze their biomechanical performance by comparing maximum Von Mises stress distribution using the finite element analysis method. The null hypothesis is that neither the type of luting cement nor the use of digital fabrication affects mesio-distal tooth movement or maximum Von Mises stress distribution.

Methods

Data acquisition and segmentation

Mandibular bone and tooth models were obtained from CBCT data of a pediatric patient (8 years, male). Imaging parameters were: voxel size 0.2 mm, slice thickness 0.33 mm, 90 kVp, 5 mA, field of view 80 × 80 mm. The resulting DICOM (.dcm) files were imported into 3D Slicer (version 5.2) software. Using reported Hounsfield Unit thresholds (enamel: > 2500 HU, dentin: 1250–2500 HU, bone: > 500 HU), the data were segmented to obtain three-dimensional anatomical models. Surface smoothing (iterations: 30, relaxation: 0.6) and hole filling were applied while preserving anatomical accuracy (max deviation ≤ 20 μm). Models were exported in STL format.

Modeling of cortical bone, trabecular bone, teeth, and periodontal ligament

The STL models were imported into ALTAIR HyperMesh (version 2022) software. A 2.0 mm inward offset was applied to the mandibular bone to generate the cortical bone model, using the internal surface to define trabecular bone. The outer surfaces of the teeth were offset outward by 0.20 mm to create uniform-thickness periodontal ligament (PDL) models22. Models were checked for intersections and non-manifold edges and finalized in Blender (version 3.6, Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The coordinate system was standardized (occlusal plane aligned to XY, mid-sagittal plane to YZ).

Modeling of the band and loop space maintainer and cement layer

The band and loop space maintainer used in the study was modeled in accordance with the mandibular bone and second primary molar using Blender software. The band was designed with a thickness of 0.6 mm, the loop with a thickness of 1.5 mm, and a loop length of 7 mm20,23. Loop–band junctions were filleted (radius: 0.5 mm) to reduce artificial stress concentrations.

A cement layer 0.060 mm (60 µm) thick was created by offsetting the crown surface of the abutment tooth outward. A conformal mesh alignment (node-to-node) between tooth–cement–band interfaces was ensured in HyperMesh to allow accurate stress transfer.

Meshing and mesh-ındependence

Mathematical models were generated by discretizing the geometry into finite elements using ALTAIR HyperMesh. Surface meshes consisted of triangular elements (target size: 0.05–0.25 mm), and solid meshes were created using 10-node quadratic tetrahedral elements (TET10). Local mesh refinement (0.05 mm) was applied at the cement margins, loop–band junction, and PDL regions.

Mesh-independence testing was performed using three different mesh densities (~ 1.1 million, ~ 1.5 million, and ~ 2.0 million elements). Peak Von Mises stress and mesio-distal displacement varied by less than 5% between the two most refined meshes, and the intermediate mesh (~ 1.5 million elements) was adopted for all analyses.

Boundary conditions and loading

A vertical load of 70 N was applied at the band–loop junction to simulate the occlusal force transmitted to the space maintainer during mastication37. This location represents the primary site of stress transfer, where the loop engages with the opposing dentition. The selection of this load magnitude was informed by published data indicating that bite forces in children aged 6–12 years generally range from 50 to 100 N during routine mastication, with maximum axial forces reaching up to 150 N. Accordingly, a load of 70 N was chosen to reflect a physiologically relevant vertical masticatory force for pediatric patients38–43. The force was distributed across surrounding nodes to prevent stress singularities at the loading areas (Fig. 2). The superior surface of the mandibular bone was fully constrained in all degrees of freedom (Ux = Uy = Uz = 0) to prevent rigid body motion. No symmetry boundary conditions were applied; the full model was analyzed. To prevent rigid body motion and ensure computational stability, the superior surface of the mandibular model was constrained. This boundary condition was chosen to realistically simulate the functional stabilization provided by the surrounding musculature and the temporomandibular joint during mastication. Such an approach is consistent with protocols commonly employed in previously published finite element analysis (FEA) studies, thereby supporting the validity and comparability of the present findings22,24.

Solver settings and post-processing

All finite element analyses were performed in ALTAIR OptiStruct (version 2022, Altair Engineering Inc., Troy, MI, USA) using a linear static solver. The convergence criterion was set to a relative tolerance of 0.001, with a maximum of 50 solver iterations per analysis.

Primary outcome variables were:

Maximum Von Mises stress in the band, loop, cement layer, tooth, and periodontal ligament (PDL).

Mesio-distal displacement of the abutment tooth, measured at a defined occlusal reference point on the crown.

For consistent visual comparison across all models, identical color scales and stress range limits were applied to post-processed stress maps. All displacement results were reported in millimeters [mm] and stress values in megapascals [MPa].

In this study, the maximum Von Mises stress values were utilized as a comparative metric to assess the overall distribution of mechanical stress within the cement layer and its supporting structures. While it is acknowledged that dental cements typically exhibit brittle behavior, the Von Mises criterion was purposefully selected. This decision allowed for the capture of the combined effects of multi-axial stresses, thereby offering a more comprehensive and clinically relevant understanding of stress concentration patterns that may arise during functional loading.

Quantitative model ınformation

The constructed analysis model consists of a total of 405,373 nodes and 1,496,984 elements.

Contact definitions

Contact definitions were as follows: tooth–cement and cement–band were bonded; PDL–root and PDL–bone were bonded; loop–tooth was defined as frictionless with hard contact in the normal direction to prevent penetration. These definitions reflect clinical fixation and allow realistic relative motion where appropriate.

Among the tested materials, BruxZir consistently demonstrated the lowest displacement values across all measurement points, regardless of cement type. For example, at the mesiobuccal cusp, displacement ranged from 2.544 mm (Conventional GIC) to 2.607 mm (Resin Modified GIC), while distal root apex displacement remained stable at approximately 7.68–7.70 mm.

Conventional stainless steel exhibited slightly higher cusp displacement values than BruxZir (mesiobuccal cusp: 2.550–2.614 mm), but the apical displacement patterns were nearly identical.

Trilor showed moderately higher displacement values, particularly at the central cusp (3.483–3.559 mm) and mesiobuccal cusp (2.922–3.000 mm), with minimal changes in root apex displacements compared to metal-based materials.

CFR-PEEK presented further increases in cusp displacement (mesiobuccal cusp: 2.962–3.054 mm), while distal root apex displacement slightly decreased compared to Trilor (7.44–7.47 mm).

PEEK recorded the highest displacement values among all materials, with central cusp displacement reaching 4.367–4.452 mm and mesiobuccal cusp displacement between 3.769–3.856 mm. Interestingly, PEEK demonstrated the lowest distal root apex displacement in the study (7.15–7.18 mm), indicating a shift in deformation localization toward the crown (Table 3).

Effect of luting cement

Across all materials, changing the luting cement type produced smaller variations in displacement compared to the effect of material choice. Resin Modified GIC generally resulted in slightly higher cusp displacement values than Conventional GIC and Self-Adhesive Resin Cement, particularly in low-modulus materials such as PEEK and CFR-PEEK (Table 3). Discussion

The present study examined the effects of digitally fabricated band and loop space maintainers and their luting cements on tooth movement and stress distribution, demonstrating that material properties are decisive factors in biomechanical behavior. Based on the findings of the finite element analysis, the null hypothesis that neither the type of luting cement nor the use of digital fabrication would affect mesio-distal tooth movement or maximum Von Mises stress distribution was rejected. These results indicate that material selection plays a critical role in the biomechanical performance of space maintainers. From a clinical perspective, these findings provide evidence-based guidance for selecting space maintainer materials and luting cements that minimize mesial tipping forces, thereby potentially reducing the risk of space loss during the mixed dentition period.

In recent years, the advent of 3D printing technology has led to a significant transformation in dentistry. This technology enables the fabrication of complex three-dimensional objects using digital models, offering full customization that enhances patient comfort, treatment compliance, and overall performance. Furthermore, it enables the production of space maintainers with patient-specific anatomical precision, integrating complex features that enhance clinical functionality and treatment efficiency44. Studies comparing conventional and 3D-printed band and loop space maintainers have shown that 3D-printed versions perform better, provide higher patient satisfaction, reduce fabrication time, and improve treatment compliance4,17,45. When compared to preformed (chair-side) space maintainers—which can be applied in a single clinical session without impression taking—digitally fabricated appliances may require an additional laboratory stage. However, they offer superior marginal fit, elimination of solder joints, and use of metal-free biocompatible materials. This may translate into fewer failure rates related to cement loss or fracture, improved esthetics, and better acceptance among pediatric patients. Nevertheless, in urgent cases where immediate space maintenance is required, preformed appliances may still have an advantage in terms of treatment speed6–8. In the present study, four different materials -BruxZir, a zirconia-based material commonly used in various fields of dentistry; Trilor, a resin-based material reinforced with fiberglass; CFR-PEEK; and PEEK were investigated. Each material was assessed with three different luting cements, and mesio-distal displacement values at multiple reference points (Table 3) allowed a detailed comparison of biomechanical performance.

One of the most important parameters analyzed in finite element analysis is the equivalent stress, commonly referred to as Von Mises stress. This metric evaluates the combined stress acting on a material from all directions. In finite element analysis, complex loading conditions at specific points and regions of stress concentration within the model are most accurately represented by the equivalent (Von Mises) stress37. Therefore, performing finite element analysis is essential before the clinical application of any material. As a computer-assisted method, this analysis allows for the evaluation of tooth movement along the Y-axis. Our results (Table 2) demonstrated that materials with a higher elastic modulus generally restricted tooth movement more effectively, indicating better resistance to mesial tipping.

In a case report involving the fabrication and clinical application of a zirconia space maintainer using digital technologies, it was noted that intraoral digital impression techniques enabled fast and efficient data acquisition, which could be beneficial in managing the behavior of young pediatric patients. The report also highlighted that a tooth-colored space maintainer with enhanced mechanical and biological properties could be produced from zirconia without operator-dependent errors. Although monolithic zirconia restorations are known for their high survival rates and low risk of complications, the report emphasized the potential risk of fracture in zirconia-based space maintainers, recommending bulkier designs to minimize this risk18. This is consistent with our findings, where BruxZir combined with conventional GIC showed the lowest overall mesial displacement across all measurement points (Table 3).

Trilor is a metal-free, biocompatible, and elastic fiber-reinforced composite material used in CAD/CAM systems, and is 3 to 5 times lighter than zirconia46. A case report has documented the use of Trilor in a fully digital workflow to fabricate a space maintainer specifically designed to be safe for patients with special healthcare needs, such as those requiring regular magnetic resonance imaging of the head due to conditions like epilepsy or vascular disorders. In this case, the Nance-type space maintainer was planned and fabricated entirely metal-free, and was reported to be aesthetic, easy to clean, lightweight, comfortable, and particularly safe for such patient groups47. The results of this study indicate that conventionally fabricated space maintainers caused less mesial tooth movement compared to those made from Trilor. However, given its metal-free composition, aesthetic appearance, lightweight structure, and rapid fabrication through digital technologies, Trilor may still be considered a viable alternative.

Other materials proposed as alternatives to conventional options include metal-free, next-generation polymers such as polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and carbon fiber-reinforced polyetheretherketone (CFR-PEEK)48. PEEK exhibits high biocompatibility, favorable mechanical properties, excellent thermal resistance, low plaque accumulation, and strong bonding strength, and demonstrates high performance in personalized 3D fabrication49. PEEK matrices can significantly enhance their mechanical properties, including elastic modulus, flexural strength, and microhardness50. Due to its high mechanical performance and low density, PEEK is commonly reinforced with carbon fibers (CF)51. CF added to PEEK in varying lengths, thicknesses, and weight ratios can produce CFR-PEEK variants with elastic modulus values comparable to those of human bone52,53. As a result, it has been suggested that these materials, with their high strength and appropriate elastic modulus, may be suitable for intraoral use48. Several researchers who have fabricated space maintainers using PEEK have concluded that it is a highly suitable material for space maintainer fabrication49,54. Moreover, space maintainers fabricated from PEEK using CAD/CAM technology have been reported to exhibit superior mucosal adaptation compared to conventional appliances, along with high effectiveness in maintaining space55. In the present study, the space maintainer fabricated from PEEK material was found to cause excessive mesial tooth movement, indicating that it may not be a suitable material for space maintainer fabrication. In contrast, CFR-PEEK is presumed to perform more successfully than PEEK due to differences in elastic modulus.

In the evaluation of luting cements used for the fixation of space maintainers, resin-modified glass ionomer cement is generally considered the most ideal option, followed by self-adhesive resin cement and conventional glass ionomer cement. However, several studies have reported that self-adhesive resin cement demonstrates greater resistance to decementation compared to both resin-modified and conventional glass ionomer cements, and also exhibits superior performance in terms of shear peel bond strength and overall bond strength56,57. Accordingly, further research is warranted to evaluate not only the materials used in the fabrication of space maintainers but also the various luting cements employed for their retention. While the present finite element analysis revealed differences in stress distribution among the cement types tested, it is important to recognize that these numerical variations may not directly translate to clinical performance. Intraoral factors such as moisture, pH fluctuations, temperature changes, and variations in occlusal load direction can significantly influence the in vivo behavior of each cement. For example, although the model indicated higher stress concentrations with self-adhesive resin cement, its superior bonding properties and reduced technique sensitivity may compensate for this limitation in a clinical context. Conversely, resin-modified glass ionomer cements provide advantages such as fluoride release and enhanced biocompatibility, which could contribute to their long-term stability. Therefore, interpretations of stress distribution findings should be integrated with clinical considerations—including bond strength, retention, and biocompatibility—to achieve a more holistic understanding of the overall performance of luting cements in space maintainer applications.

This study utilized a finite element model derived from a single pediatric mandible, providing methodological consistency but inherently limiting the generalizability of the findings. The analysis was conducted under static and linear elastic assumptions, with materials modeled as isotropic to ensure computational stability. While this approach aligns with protocols established in previous dental FEA research, it does not fully capture the anisotropic and viscoelastic properties of dental tissues and restorative materials. Furthermore, dynamic oral factors such as cyclic loading, temperature fluctuations, saliva presence, and cement degradation were not simulated, nor was experimental or clinical validation performed. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as indicative of relative biomechanical trends rather than as definitive clinical outcomes. Future research employing patient-specific models and dynamically validated methodologies is recommended to enhance clinical relevance.
