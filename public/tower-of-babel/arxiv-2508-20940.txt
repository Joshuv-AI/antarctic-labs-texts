On the non-existence of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric
Giuseppe Del Prete, Antonio Roccolano, Ferdinando Zullo

Abstract
. We study perfect codes in the sum-rank metric, a generalization of both the Hamming and rank metrics relevant in multishot network coding and space-time coding. A perfect code attains equality in the sphere-packing bound, corresponding to a partition of the ambient space into disjoint metric balls. While perfect codes in the Hamming and rank metrics are completely classified, the existence of nontrivial perfect codes in the sum-rank metric remains largely open. In this paper, we investigate linear perfect codes in the sum-rank metric. We analyze the geometry of balls and derive bounds on their volumes, showing how the sphere-packing bound applies. For two-block spaces, we determine explicit parameter constraints for the existence of perfect codes. For multiple-block spaces, we establish non-existence results for various ranges of minimum distance, divisibility conditions, and code dimensions. We further provide computational evidence based on congruence conditions imposed by the volume of metric balls.

1.
Introduction

Consider a finite metric space
(X,d)
.
An
s
-code

\mathcal{C}
in
X
is a subset of
X
with at least two elements such that for every
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
with
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
, we have
d(c_{1},c_{2})\geq s
.

For
c\in X
and an integer
r\geq 0
, we define the ball of radius
r
centered at
c
as

B_{r}(c)=\{a\in X:d(a,c)\leq r\}.

Since
d
is a metric, one immediately observes that the balls of radius

k=\Big\lfloor\tfrac{s-1}{2}\Big\rfloor

centered at the elements of an
s
-code are pairwise disjoint, and their union is contained in
X
. This yields the bound

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}|B_{k}(c)|\leq|X|.

If, in addition, the metric
d
is translation-invariant, then all balls of radius
k
have the same size, so

(1)

|\mathcal{C}|\,|B_{k}(c)|\leq|X|,\qquad\text{for some }c\in\mathcal{C}.

When
d
is the Hamming metric and
X=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is a finite field of order
q
, the bound (
1
) is known as the
sphere-packing bound
(or
Hamming bound
), and codes achieving equality in (
1
) are called
perfect codes
.

By analogy, we will refer to (
1
) as the
sphere-packing bound
in the general setting, and to codes attaining equality as
perfect codes
.

Examples of linear perfect codes in the Hamming metric are:

•

trivial codes (i.e.,
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and the repetition code);

•

Hamming codes;

•

the binary and ternary Golay codes of length
23
and
11
, respectively.

After a series of works (see
[
11
,
3
,
2
,
28
,
27
,
26
,
23
,
25
]
),
Tietäväinen
[
24
]
completely classified the linear perfect codes in the Hamming metric as precisely those listed above (up to equivalence).

Another well-studied case is the rank metric.
Taking
X=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
with
d
the rank metric, Loidreau
[
13
]
proved that the only linear perfect codes in this setting are the trivial ones.
More recently, the situation in which
X
is a restricted matrix space (i.e., when
X
is the space of symmetric, Hermitian, or alternating matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) has also been studied; see
[
18
,
17
,
1
]
.

This paper is dedicated to the study of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric.
Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field, and consider a vector partitioned into several blocks, where each block is a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The
sum-rank weight
of such a vector is defined as the sum of the ranks of all its blocks. Similarly, the
sum-rank distance
between two vectors is defined as the sum of the ranks of the differences between corresponding blocks.

The sum-rank metric generalizes both the Hamming and the rank metrics. If each block consists of a single element, it coincides with the Hamming metric; if there is only one block, it reduces to the rank metric. This metric is particularly useful in coding theory, as it captures both block-wise and rank-based error structures. Applications include
multishot network coding
, where errors can affect multiple transmissions, and
space-time coding
in wireless communications, where blocks correspond to different antennas or time slots. For further details, we refer to
[
7
,
15
,
14
]
.

Covering properties have been widely studied in both the rank-metric and the sum-rank metric (see
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
29
]
), and a detailed study of the geometry of balls in the sum-rank metric has been carried out in
[
22
,
20
,
7
,
19
]
.

The first study of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric was conducted by Martinez-Peñas in
[
16
]
, where he observed that perfect codes in the Hamming metric can also be viewed as perfect codes in the sum-rank metric. He left as an open question the existence of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric that do not arise from the Hamming metric. To the best of our knowledge, no other perfect codes are known, though a construction of
quasi-perfect sum-rank metric codes
was recently provided by Chen in
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we focus on detecting parameter sets for which linear perfect codes in the sum-rank metric do not exist.
After recalling some preliminaries and notation for sum-rank metric codes in Section
2
, in Section
3
we study spheres and balls in the sum-rank metric and describe how the sphere-packing bound appears in this context. We also derive some bounds on the volumes of spheres and balls.

In Section
4
, we study perfect codes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\,n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\,n\times n}
. Following the approach of Loidreau in
[

]
, which combines the bounds we have on the volumes of the balls with the Singleton-like bound, we prove that perfect codes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
can exist only for specific parameters.

Section

\underbrace{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\ldots\oplus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times n}}_{t\text{ times}}

•

the minimum distance is

or

•

the minimum distance is
5
or
6

);

•

•

•

In Section

requires that the volume of a ball of radius

2.
Sum-rank metric codes

We fix now the notation that we will use for the whole paper. For us
q
is a prime power and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is the finite field with
q
elements. We will often consider the degree
m
extension field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
Let
t
be a positive integer. From now on

\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{t}),\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{t})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{t}
will always be ordered tuples with
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\ldots\geq n_{t}\geq 2
and
m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq\ldots\geq m_{t}\geq 2
.
We use the following compact notations for the direct sum of vector spaces of matrices

\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}.

In this section we recall the basic notions of sum-rank-metric codes seen as elements in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, that will be useful for the rest of the paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X\coloneqq(X_{1},\dots,X_{t})\in\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.
The
sum-rank weight
of
X
is the quantity

\ww_{\mathrm{srk}}(X)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\rk(X_{i}).

With these definition in mind, we can endow the space
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with a distance function, called the
sum-rank distance
,

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}:\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\times\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

defined by

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}(X,Y)\coloneqq\ww_{\mathrm{srk}}(X-Y).

In particular, if
t=1
the sum-rank distance corresponds to the rank distance, and if all the
n_{i}
’s or all the
m_{i}
’s are equal to one, then the sum-rank distance coincides with the Hamming distance.

We can now give the definition of sum-rank metric codes.

Definition 2.2
.

A
sum-rank metric code

\mathcal{C}
is an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear subspace of
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
endowed with the sum-rank distance.
The
minimum sum-rank distance
of a sum-rank code
\mathcal{C}
is defined as usual via

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}(\mathcal{C})\coloneqq\min\{\ww_{\mathrm{srk}}(X)\,:\,X\in\mathcal{C},X\neq\mathbf{0}\}.

The parameters of a sum-rank metric code are related via a Singleton-like bound, as proved in
[
7
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Singleton-like bound)

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a sum-rank metric code in
\Mat(\textbf{m},\mathbf{n},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with
n_{i}\leq m_{i}
for any
i
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}(\mathcal{C})=d
.
Let
j
and
\delta
be the unique integers satisfying
d-1=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}n_{i}+\delta
and
0\leq\delta\leq n_{j}-1
. Then

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{\sum_{i=j}^{t}n_{i}m_{i}-m_{j}\delta}.

The sum-rank metric codes whose parameters satisfy the equality in the above bound are called
Maximum Sum-Rank Distance
(or shortly
MSRD
) codes.

The interested reader is referred to
[
7
,
8
,
12
,
9
]
for a more details about sum-rank metric codes and their connections.

3.
Spheres, balls and sphere-packing bound in the sum-rank metric

In the following, we assume that the integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{t},m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}
are such that

n_{i}\leq m_{i},\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}.

This is motivated by the fact that in the following sections we will deal with tha case in which all the
n_{i}
’s and the
m_{i}
’s are equal.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
l
be a non-negative integer. For every vector
X\in\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, the
sphere
centered at
X
and of radius
l
in the sum-rank metric is defined as the following set:

S(X,l)=\{Y\in\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\,|\,\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}(X,Y)=l\}.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
r
be a non-negative integer. For every vector
X\in\Pi
, the
ball
centered at
X
and of radius
r
in the sum-rank metric is defined as the following set:

B(X,r)=\{Y\in\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\,|\,\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}(X,Y)\leq r\}.

Note that the sphere centered at any vector
X
in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
and of radius
r
corresponds to the union of all spheres of radius between
0
and
r
centered in
X
:

(2)

B(X,r)=\bigcup_{l=0}^{r}S(X,l).

Let us denote
\mathbf{V}(B(X,r))=|B(X,r)|
, and we will refer to the cardinality of a ball as its
volume
. Clearly, the size of the spheres and balls in the sum-rank metric are independent of the chosen center. Therefore, the quantity
\mathbf{V}(B(X,r))
represents the volume of any ball with a given center and radius
r
.

Thus, it is legitimate to consider the volume of a ball of radius
r
in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
as the volume of the ball centered at the origin. To refer to this quantity, we will use the notation
\mathbf{V}_{r}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))
, emphasizing that the ball lies in the space
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.

The same considerations apply to spheres. For this reason, the spherical surface centered at the origin and of radius
l
is given by the set:

S_{l}=\{Y\in\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\,|\,w_{sr}(Y)=l\},

so that we can define its volume as

\mathbf{V}(S_{l})=|\{Y\in\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\,|\,w_{sr}(Y)=l\}|.

Recalling (
2
), we obtain:

(3)

\mathbf{V}_{r}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=\sum_{l=0}^{r}\mathbf{V}(S_{l}).

By counting the number of the elements in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
having having a fixed sum-rank weight, in
[
7
, Section III]
the authors proved the following (see also
[
22
, Section 4]
).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
l
and
r
be two non-negative integers. Then

\mathbf{V}(S_{l})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=l\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n_{i}}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{m_{i}}-q^{j}),

and

\mathbf{V}_{r}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=\sum_{l=0}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=l\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n_{i}}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{m_{i}}-q^{j}).

The above formula is not very practical and difficult to compute, especially when studying covering properties; see also
[
20
]
for a dynamic program to compute it.
We explicitly provide it for a very special cases in the following proposition, which is useful for the next results and is also expository for showing how this formula is intricate.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=n
. In
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
the following hold

(4)

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=1+t\,\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1},

and

(5)

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=1+t\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}+\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{4}}{(q-1)^{2}}+\frac{t(q^{n}-1)^{2}(q^{n-1}-1)^{2}q}{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.3
, we derive that

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=\sum_{l=0}^{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=l\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j}).

Clearly,
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=0
implies that all the
k_{i}
’s are zero and
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=1
implies that all but one of the
k_{i}
’s are zero and the remaining one is one.
Using this, one can immediately observe that
\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=1+t\,\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}
.

Again, using Proposition
3.3
, we derive that

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=\sum_{l=0}^{2}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=l\end{subarray}}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j}).

Clearly, we have that

(6)

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=1+t\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=2\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j}).

Note that we have that

k_{1}+\cdots+k_{t}=2

if and only if one of the following cases hold

(1)

there exists
s\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that
k_{s}=2
,
k_{j}=0
for every
j\neq s
;

(2)

there exist
s,u\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that
k_{s}=k_{u}=1
,
k_{j}=0
for every
j\neq s,u
.

Case (1)

Suppose that there exists
s\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that
k_{s}=2
,
k_{j}=0
for every
j\neq s
.
Note that

\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j})=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{2}_{q}(q^{n}-1)(q^{n}-q),

as
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_j}_{q}=1
for any
j\neq s
.

Case (2)

Assume that there exist
s,u\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that
k_{s}=k_{u}=1
,
k_{j}=0
for every
j\neq s,u
.
Thus, we have that

\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j})=\left(\frac{(q^{n}-1)}{q-1}\times(q^{n}-1)\right)^{2}=\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{4}}{(q-1)^{2}}.

For the Case (1) there are exactly
t
choices for
k_{s}
, so that

(7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\{0,1\}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=2\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j})=t\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{2}_{q}(q^{n}-1)(q^{n}-q)=qt\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}(q^{n-1}-1)^{2}}{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}.

For Case (2), there are
\binom{t}{2}=\frac{t(t-1)}{2}
choices for

and

, therefore we get

(8)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\{0,2\}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=2\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{n}-q^{j})=\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{4}}{(q-1)^{2}}.

By summing (

) and (

) together with (

), we obtain the value of
\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))
.

Later, we will see some bounds that will allow us to prove some non-existence results for perfect codes in the sum-rank metric.
In this context the sphere-like bound reads as follows, see
[
7
, Theorem III.6]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

(Sphere-packing bound)

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a sum-rank code in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, and let
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{srk}}(\mathcal{C})=d
be its minimum distance. Then

|\mathcal{C}|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{|\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})|}{\mathbf{V}_{r}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{q^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}n_{i}}}{\sum_{l=0}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=l\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n_{i}}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{m_{i}}-q^{j})}\right\rfloor,

where
r=\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor
.

Codes for which equality holds in the previous bound are called
perfect codes
.
We know that such codes exist when
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=1
, or
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=1
, that is, in the Hamming metric. For
t=1
, i.e., when considering the rank metric, it is known that there are no non-trivial perfect codes, as proved by Loidreau in
[
13
]
. As Martinez-Peñas
pointed out in
[
16
]
, it seems very challenging to find examples of perfect codes that do not originate from the Hamming metric.
As far as we know, at the moment only examples of quasi-perfect codes have been found by Chen in
[
10
]
.

Our aim is to exclude the existence of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric for several values of the parameters, by following the approach of Loidreau in
[
13
]
, which combines bounds on the volumes of the balls and the Singleton-like bound.

In the rank metric, the following bounds have been derived.

Proposition 3.6
.

(see
[
13
, Proposition 1]
)
Let
\mathbf{n}=n
and
\mathbf{m}=m
, and let
k
be a positive integer such that
k\leq\min\{n,m\}
. Then:

q^{(n+m-2)k-k^{2}}\leq\mathbf{V}(S_{k})\leq q^{(n+m+1)k-k^{2}},

q^{(n+m-2)k-k^{2}}\leq\mathbf{V}_{r}(\mathrm{Mat}(n,m,{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\leq q^{(n+m+1)k-k^{2}+1}.

The lower bounds on the volume of spheres and balls in the rank metric given in Proposition
3.6
can also be extended to the sum-rank metric. In this case, we assume that
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=m
, so that
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
is the direct sum of
t
matrix spaces of size

, that is,

\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\mathrm{Mat}(n,m,{\mathbb{F}}_{q})\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathrm{Mat}(n,m,{\mathbb{F}}_{q}).

The following inequalities on the volumes of balls and spheres in the sum-rank metric can also be derived from those in
[
22
,
20
,
19
]
, and note that the bounds we derive are even less precise. For our purposes, we need lower and upper bounds that approximate the volumes of balls and spheres as
q
-power expressions as closely as possible. Therefore, for the sake of completeness, by using a similar argument to
[

]
we prove the following bounds and present them in the form we have established.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
k
be a positive integer,
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=m
. In
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
the following inequalities hold

\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\geq\mathbf{V}(S_{k})\geq q^{(m+n-\frac{k}{t}-2)k-\frac{t}{4}}.

Proof.

In this case, the volume of a sphere can be written as follows

\mathbf{V}(S_{k})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{m}-q^{j}),

and therefore, by Proposition
3.6
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{m}-q^{j})\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}q^{(n+m-2)\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}}\geq

\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\left\{q^{(n+m-2)\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}}\right\}=q^{(n+m-2)k}\,\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\left\{q^{-\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}}\right\}=

=q^{(n+m-2)k}\,q^{-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}\right\}}.

The integer
k
can be written as

k=k_{*}t+h,

with
0\leq h<t
; hence we can consider the tuple
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})
, with
k_{i_{1}}=\ldots=k_{i_{h}}=k_{*}+1
and
k_{i_{h+1}}=\ldots=k_{i_{t}}=k_{*}
, and observe that

k=h(k_{*}+1)+(t-h)k_{*}=hk_{*}+h+tk_{*}-hk_{*}=tk_{*}+h.

One can check that this tuple minimizes the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}
with the restriction that
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k
.
Therefore,

(9)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}\right\}=h(k_{*}+1)^{2}+(t-h)k_{*}^{2}=h+2hk_{*}+tk_{*}^{2}.

Since
k_{*}=\frac{k-h}{t}
, (
9
) becomes

h+2h\frac{(k-h)}{t}+t\frac{(k-h)^{2}}{t^{2}}=

=\frac{t^{2}h+2ht(k-h)+t(k-h)^{2}}{t^{2}}=h+\frac{2hk-2h^{2}}{t}+\frac{k^{2}+h^{2}-2hk}{t}=\frac{k^{2}-h^{2}}{t}+h.

Hence,

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}\right\}=\frac{k^{2}-h^{2}}{t}+h.

Also, we have that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\leq t-1\end{subarray}}\left\{h-\frac{h^{2}}{t}\right\}\leq\frac{t}{4}.

Therefore,

q^{(n+m-2)k}\,q^{-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}\right\}}=q^{(n+m-2)k}\,q^{-\left(\frac{k^{2}-h^{2}}{t}+h\right)}\geq

\geq q^{(n+m-2)k}\,q^{-\frac{k^{2}}{t}-\frac{t}{4}}=q^{(m+n-\frac{k}{t}-2)k-\frac{t}{4}}.

The assertion then follows from the fact that
\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\geq\mathbf{V}(S_{k})
.

∎

The integers involved in the
t
-tuple of partitions
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})
, with
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k
, where
k
denotes the radius of the sphere considered in the sum-rank metric, are known to satisfy

k_{i}\leq\min\{n,m\}

for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
. Setting
n=\min\{n,m\}
, it is possible to define the set of all such ordered partitions with respect to the above constraint:

\tau_{k,t,n}=\left\{k=[k_{1},\ldots,k_{t}]\mid\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}=k,\quad k_{i}\leq n\right\},

where the notation
k=[k_{1},\ldots,k_{t}]
indicates the integers
k_{i}
involved in the partition.

The cardinality of this set corresponds to the number of ways the radius
k
can be decomposed into a
t
-tuple in which each component is at most
n
. For this, one has the bound:

(10)

|\tau_{k,t,n}|\leq\binom{k+t-1}{t-1},

see
[
21
]
for further details. These considerations, together with Proposition
3.6
, allow us to derive an upper bound for the volume of spheres and balls in the sum-rank metric.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
k
be a positive integer,
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=m
. In
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
the following inequalities hold

\mathbf{V}(S_{k})\leq\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\leq k(k+1)\binom{k+t-1}{t-1}q^{(n+m+1-\frac{k}{t})k+\frac{4-t}{4}}.

Proof.

By Propositions
3.6
and
3.7
we have that

\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=l\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{k_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(q^{m}-q^{j})\leq

\leq(k+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\\
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{t}=k\end{subarray}}q^{(n+m+1)k-\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}+1}=

=(k+1)\sum_{k\in\tau_{k,t,n}}q^{(n+m+1)k-\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}+1}.

By making use of (
10
) we derive that

\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\leq k(k+1)\binom{k+t-1}{t-1}q^{(n+m+1)k+1}\max\{q^{-\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}}\}=

=k(k+1)\binom{k+t-1}{t-1}q^{(n+m+1)k+1}q^{-\min\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}^{2}\}}\leq

\leq k(k+1)\binom{k+t-1}{t-1}q^{(n+m+1)k+1-\frac{k^{2}}{t}-\frac{t}{4}}=

=k(k+1)\binom{k+t-1}{t-1}q^{(n+m+1-\frac{k}{t})k+\frac{4-t}{4}}.

∎

We underline that the above bounds are not tight and improvements to them have been provided in
[
22
,
20
,
19
]
.

4.
Perfect codes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}

As previously stated, Loidreau demonstrated in
[
13
]
that when
t=1
(i.e., when the sum-rank metric corresponds to the rank metric), non-trivial perfect codes do not exist. Consequently, the first open case in the sum-rank metric pertains to the situation where
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
is the direct sum of two square matrix spaces. This section focuses on studying this particular case, in which we manage to rule out a large set of parameters concerning their existence.
We divide the analysis into two main cases according to the large or small values of the minimum distance.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
t=2
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
. If
q\geq 5
,
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor\geq 6
then there do not exist perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a perfect code in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.
If
d
is odd, then
k=\frac{d-1}{2}
and
d=2k+1
.
The Singleton-like bound (cfr. Theorem
2.3
) reads as follows

(11)

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{2n^{2}-2kn}

If
d
is even, then
k=\frac{d-2}{2}
and
d=2k+2
. The Singleton-like bound now reads as

(12)

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{2n^{2}-(2k+1)n},

implying that in both the cases the inequality
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{2n^{2}-2kn}
holds and so we will use it to consider both the cases simultaneously.
Since
\C
is perfect, we have

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=\lvert\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\rvert=q^{2n^{2}},

and by (
11
) we have

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\leq q^{2n^{2}-2kn}\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})),

that is
q^{2kn}\leq\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))
.
Proposition
3.8
, with
n=m
and
t=2
, implies that

\mathbf{V}_{k}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\leq k(k+1)\binom{k+2-1}{2-1}q^{(2n+1-\frac{k}{2})k+\frac{4-2}{4}}=k(k+1)\binom{k+1}{1}q^{(2n+1-\frac{k}{2})k+\frac{1}{2}}

from which it follows

q^{2kn}\leq k(k+1)^{2}q^{(2n+1-\frac{k}{2})k+\frac{1}{2}}

i.e.

(13)

q^{\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}}\leq k(k+1)^{2}.

By taking the base
q
logarithm in the above inequality we get

(14)

\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}\leq\lg_{q}(k)+2\lg_{q}(k+1)=\frac{\ln(k)+2\ln(k+1)}{\ln(q)}

Applying the following well-known inequalities

\ln(1+x)\leq x\ \ \forall x>-1,

\ln(x)\leq x-1\ \ \forall x>0,

we obtain

\ln(k)+2\ln(k+1)\leq k-1+2k=3k-1

and so (
14
) becomes

\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}\leq\frac{3k-1}{\ln(q)}

i.e.

k^{2}\ln(q)-2k\ln(q)-\ln(q)-6k+2\leq 0\implies k^{2}\ln(q)-(2\ln(q)+6)k-\ln(q)+2\leq 0,

that is

(15)

f(k)\leq 0,

where

f(k)=k^{2}\ln(q)-(2\ln(q)+6)k-\ln(q)+2.

Since
q\geq 5
we also have that
\ln(q)>\frac{34}{23}
and

f(6)=36\ln(q)-12\ln(q)-36-\ln(q)+2=23\ln(q)-34>0\iff\ln(q)>\frac{34}{23},

implying that
f(6)>0
.
Observe that

f^{\prime}(k)=2k\ln(q)-2\ln(q)-6\geq 0\iff k\geq\frac{2\ln(q)+6}{2\ln(q)}=1+\frac{3}{\ln(q)}.

Since
q\geq 5
we have
1+\frac{3}{\ln(q)}<6
, from which it follows that

1+\frac{3}{\ln(q)}<6<k.

Therefore, for
k\geq 6
the function
f(k)
increases. Finally, by taking the limit as
k\to\infty
we obtain

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}f(k)=+\infty

We then conclude that

f(k)>0\ \ \forall k\geq 6

Therefore, we have a contradiction to (
15
).
∎

We now consider the case where the minimum distance is at most
12
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
t=2
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
. If
q>e^{3}
and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor\leq 5
, then there do not exist perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a perfect code in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.
We can again assume that
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{2n^{2}-2kn}
(from the Singleton-like bound) and
q^{\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}}\leq k(k+1)^{2}
(from (
13
)).
The latter inequality implies that

q^{\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}}\leq k(k+1)^{2}\leq 180.

By considering the base
q
logarithm in the above inequality, we obtain

\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}\leq\lg_{q}(180)

i.e.

0\geq\frac{k^{2}}{2}-k-\frac{1}{2}-\lg_{q}(180)\geq\frac{k^{2}}{2}-5-\frac{1}{2}-\lg_{q}(180)=\frac{k^{2}}{2}-\frac{11}{2}-\frac{\ln(180)}{\ln(q)}.

This condition does not hold for
\ln(q)>3
since

(k^{2}-11)\ln(q)>2\ln(180)\iff\ln(q)>\frac{2\ln(180)}{k^{2}-11}

and

\frac{2\ln(180)}{k^{2}-11}<\frac{11}{k^{2}-11}<3<\ln(q),

a contradiction to the assumption that
q\geq e^{3}
.

∎

Therefore, as a consequence, we restrict the existence of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric in the following cases.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
t=2
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
.
If a perfect code with minimum distance
d
exists, then one of the following hold:

•

5\leq q<e^{3}
and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor\leq 5
;

•

q\in\{2,3,4\}
.

We are able to exclude the case in which
q=2
, and
q=3
for large values of the minimum distance.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
t=2
,
q\in\{2,3\}
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
.
If one of the following conditions hold
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor\geq 5
,

•

q=2
,
n\geq 4
and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor\neq 2
;

•

q=3
and
n\geq 3
,

then there do not exist perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a perfect code in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.

Case 1
:
q=2
.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, from (
13
) we have that

q^{\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}}\leq k(k+1)^{2},

and for
q=2
it reads as

2^{\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}}\leq k(k+1)^{2}.

Simple computations show that the above inequality yields a contradiction when
k\geq 6
.
For the cases
2\leq k\leq 5
, see the Appendix
8
.

Case 2
:
q=3
.

Again from (
13
) we have that

3^{\frac{k^{2}-2k-1}{2}}\leq k(k+1)^{2},

which yields a contradiction when
k\geq 5
. For
2\leq k\leq 4
, see Appendix
9
.
∎

5.
Larger number of blocks

In this section we derive some non-existence results for perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
when
t>2
and
n>1
.
Clearly, if
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=0
(i.e. when
d=1
or
2
), then we have that a perfect code with minimum distance
d
needs to be the entire space
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Therefore, we start with the case where
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=1
and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=2
, where we are going to use the explicit value of the
\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))
and
\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))
from Proposition
3.4
.
Then we will move some general results, where we prove some non-existence result on perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
when the required minimum distance is large enough with respect to the number of blocks and finally we prove some non-existence results by finding some congruences involving
q
,
t
and the minimum distance.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
t
,
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\underbrace{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\ldots\oplus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times n}}_{t\text{ times}}
.
If
q>t+1
, there do not exist perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
having minimum distance
d
with
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=1
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a perfect code
\C
in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d
.
By Proposition
3.4
, we have that

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=1+t\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}.

Since
\C
is a perfect code, we have

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=\lvert\underbrace{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\ldots\oplus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times n}}_{t\text{ times}}\rvert=q^{tn^{2}}.

By Singleton-like bound, we have

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{tn^{2}-n(d-1)},

implying that

\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leq q^{tn^{2}-2n}.

By plugging together the above inequalities, we obtain

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=q^{tn^{2}}\leq\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))q^{tn^{2}-2n},

i.e.

q^{2n}\leq\mathbf{V}_{1}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=1+t\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1},

from which we derive

q^{2n}(q-1)\leq(q-1)+t(q^{n}-1)^{2},

that is

q^{2n+1}+2tq^{n}\leq(t+1)q^{2n}+q+t-1.

Now, since

q+t-1<2tq^{n}

we have that

q^{2n+1}+2tq^{n}<(t+1)q^{2n}+2tq^{n},

which gives a contradiction since
q>t+1
.
∎

Now, we consider the case when
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=2
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
t
,
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
.
If one of the following conditions:

•

q
is odd, neither
t\not\equiv 1\pmod{q}
nor
t\not\equiv 2\pmod{q}
;

•

q=2
,
4\nmid t-2
and
4\nmid t-1
;

then there do not exist perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d\in\{5,6\}
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists a perfect code
\C
in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d\in\{5,6\}
.
Since
\C
is perfect we have that

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=q^{tn^{2}}\implies\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=q^{tn^{2}-\dim(\mathcal{C})}.

Since
\C\neq\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, we have that
q
divides
\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))
, and so
\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\equiv 0\pmod{q}
.
From (
5
) we have that

V_{2}=1+t\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}+\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{4}}{(q-1)^{2}}+\frac{t(q^{n}-1)^{2}(q^{n-1}-1)^{2}q}{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}.

Let analyze the congruence modulo
q
of each summand:

(1)

We have that

\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}=\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}(q^{n}-1)=(q^{n-1}+\cdots+q+1)(q^{n}-1)\equiv-1\pmod{q};

implying that

\frac{t(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{q-1}\equiv t(-1)=-t\pmod{q}.

(2)

Observe that

\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{4}}{(q-1)^{2}}=\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}(q^{n}-1)(q^{n}-1)\equiv(-1)\cdot(-1)=1\pmod{q},

that is

\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{4}}{(q-1)^{2}}\equiv 1\cdot\frac{t(t-1)}{2}=\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\pmod{q}.

3

Finally, we have

\frac{t(q^{n}-1)^{2}(q^{n-1}-1)^{2}q}{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}\equiv 0\pmod{q},

as it is a multiple of
q
since
q^{2}-1
divides either
q^{n-1}-1
or
q^{n}-1
and
q-1
divides both of them.

Combining the previous items, we have that

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\equiv 0\pmod{q}\iff 1-t+\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{q},

i.e.

(16)

(t-1)\frac{t-1}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{q}.

If
q
is odd then
2
is invertible in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
and we obtain

(t-1)(t-2)\equiv 0\pmod{q}.

Since
q
is a power of a prime and
t-1
and
t-2
are coprime, we have that either
t\equiv 1\pmod{q}
or
t\equiv 2\pmod{q}
.

If
q=2
, then (
16
) reads as follows

1-t+\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

Suppose that
t=2r
, for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then we have that

1-2r+r(2r-1)\equiv 0\pmod{2},

i.e.
r\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, that is
r
is odd and
4\mid t-2
.
Similarly, if
t=2r+1
, for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then

r\equiv 0\pmod{2}\implies 4|t-1.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

In particular, if
q
is odd then there does not exist perfect code in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d\in\{5,6\}
when
t\in\{3,\cdots,q\}
.

Another condition that needs to be satisfied by perfect codes in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d\in\{5,6\}
regards their dimension.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
t
,
n_{1}=\ldots=n_{t}=n
and
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{t}=n
be positive integers and
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\underbrace{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\oplus\ldots\oplus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times n}}_{t\text{ times}}
.
If there exists a perfect code
\C
in
\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with minimum distance
d\in\{5,6\}
, then
n
divides
\dim(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is perfect and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=2
, we have that

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=q^{tn^{2}},

and since
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=q^{dim(\mathcal{C})}
, we have

(17)

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\Mat(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},{\mathbb{F}}_{q}))=q^{tn^{2}-\dim(\mathcal{C})}.

From (
5
), we know that

Conclusions

In this work, we studied the existence of perfect codes in the sum-rank metric. Building on the classical sphere-packing framework and combining it with the Singleton-like bound, we derived several non-existence results for different parameter regimes. In particular, we extended Loidreau’s arguments from the rank metric to the sum-rank setting, showing that large families of parameters cannot admit perfect codes. For the case of two square blocks, we excluded existence both for sufficiently large and sufficiently small minimum distances, depending on the field size. We then generalized our analysis to a larger number of blocks, where explicit computations of the volumes of small-radius balls allowed us to rule out perfect codes for certain congruence conditions and divisibility constraints. Finally, computational results provided further evidence of the scarcity of perfect codes in this metric, confirming the absence of solutions for a wide range of values of
t
,
k
and
q
.

Overall, our results strongly suggest that perfect codes in the sum-rank metric are extremely rare, and that the only natural families arise from the Hamming setting. This aligns with the intuition that the rigid combinatorial structure required by perfect codes is incompatible with the complexity of the sum-rank geometry. An interesting direction for future research is to investigate the existence and characterization of quasi-perfect codes in this metric, following the recent constructions of Chen, and to explore whether weaker notions of covering optimality might yield richer families of codes with practical applications in network coding and distributed storage.

, then

\mathbf{V}_{k}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=q^{2n^{2}}\leq q^{2n^{2}-2nk}

i.e.

(23)

q^{2kn}\leq\mathbf{V}_{k}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q})).

We split the proof according to the values of
k\in\{1,3,4,5\}
.

Case 1:

k=1
.

Using (5) we have that

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=1+2\cdot(2^{n}-1)^{2}.

Since the code is perfect then

|\C|\mathbf{V}_{1}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=2^{2n^{2}},

i.e.

|\C|=\frac{2^{2n^{2}}}{\mathbf{V}_{1}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))}=\frac{2^{2n^{2}}}{1+2\cdot(2^{n}-1)^{2}},

which turns out to be not an integer, a contradiction.

Case 2:

k=3
.

From (5) we have that

\mathbf{V}_{2}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=1+2\cdot(2^{n}-1)^{2}+2^{2}\cdot\frac{(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}}{3}+(2^{n}-1)^{4}.

From

(24)

(2^{a}-1)^{2}<(2^{a})^{2}=2^{2a}\ \ \ \forall a>-1,

it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{2}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\cdot 2^{2n}+\frac{4}{3}\cdot 2^{2n}\cdot 2^{2n-2}+2^{4}n\leq 1+2^{2n+1}+2\cdot 2^{4n-2}+2^{4}n

\displaystyle=1+2^{2n+1}+2^{4n-1}+2^{4n}\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}.

Using this inequality, together with (
23
) and (
20
), we can derive the following conditions

2^{6n}\leq\mathbf{V}_{3}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=\mathbf{V}_{2}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))+\mathbf{V}(S_{3})\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+\textbf{V}(S_{3}),

and so

\displaystyle 2^{6n}

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+\textbf{V}(S_{3})=1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+2^{4}\cdot\frac{(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n-2}-1)^{2}}{(2^{3}-1)(2^{2}-1)}+

\displaystyle+2^{2}\cdot\frac{(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}}{2^{2}-1}\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+2^{6n-6}+\frac{4}{3}2^{6n-2}\leq

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+2^{6n-2}\left(1+\frac{4}{3}\right)=1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2},

i.e.

2^{6n}-1\leq 3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2},

which yields a contradiction when
n\geq 2
.

Case 3:

k=4
.

Equation (
23
) becomes

2^{8n}\leq\textbf{V}_{4}=\textbf{V}_{3}+\textbf{V}(S_{4}).

Using equation (
21
), we obtain the following condition

\displaystyle 2^{8n}\leq\mathbf{V}_{3}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))+\mathbf{V}(S_{4})\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2}+\mathbf{V}(S_{4}).

Combining (
21
) and (
24
), we obtain an upper bound on
\mathbf{V}(S_{4})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(S_{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{128}{315}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n-2}-1)^{2}(2^{n-3}-1)^{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{16}{21}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n-2}-1)^{2}+\frac{16}{3}(2^{n}-1)^{4}(2^{n-1}-1)^{4}\leq

\displaystyle\leq 2^{8n-12}+2^{8n-6}+\frac{16}{3}2^{8n-4}\leq 2^{8n-4}\left(1+1+\frac{16}{3}\right)=\frac{22}{3}2^{8n-4}.

Therefore,

2^{8n}-1\leq 3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2}+\textbf{V}(S_{4})\leq 3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2}+\frac{22}{3}2^{8n-4}

This condition does not hold for
n\geq 2
.

Case 4:

k=5
.

(
23
) reads as

2^{10n}\leq\mathbf{V}_{5}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=\mathbf{V}_{4}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))+\mathbf{V}(S_{5})

\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2}+\mathbf{V}(S_{4})\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2}+\frac{22}{3}2^{8n-4}+\mathbf{V}(S_{5}).

Using (
22
) and (
24
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(S_{5})

\displaystyle=\frac{2048}{9765}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n-2}-1)^{2}(2^{n-3}-1)^{2}(2^{n-4}-1)^{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{128}{315}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n-2}-1)^{2}(2^{n-3}-1)^{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{32}{63}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n}-1)^{2}(2^{n-1}-1)^{2}(2^{n-2}-1)^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\leq 2^{10n-20}+2^{20n-12}+2^{10n-8}\leq 3\cdot 2^{10n-8}.

Hence, we obtain the following condition

2^{10n}-1\leq 3\cdot 2^{4n}+\frac{7}{3}2^{6n-2}+\frac{22}{3}2^{8n-4}+3\cdot 2^{10n-8}.

This condition fails for
n\geq 1
.
∎

9.
The case
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n\times n}

As before, we can derive the following.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
q=3
,
t=2
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
,
m_{1}=m_{2}=n
be integers and Mat(
n
,
m
,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
)=
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n\times n}
. Then

(25)

\textbf{V}_{2}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})=1+(3^{n}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(3^{n}-1)^{4}+\frac{3}{8}(3^{n}-1)^{2}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}

(26)

\textbf{V}(S_{3})=\frac{54}{416}(3^{n}-1)^{2}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}(3^{n-2}-1)^{2}+\frac{3}{16}(3^{n}-1)^{4}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle\textbf{V}(S_{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{1458}{33280}(3^{n}-1)^{2}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}(3^{n-2}-1)^{2}(3^{n-3}-1)^{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{27}{416}(3^{n}-1)^{4}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}(3^{n-2}-1)^{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{81}{16}(3^{n}-1)^{4}(3^{n-1}-1)^{4}

As for the case
q=2
, we use the above proposition and the Singleton-like bound for completing the proof of Proposition
4.4
for the case
q=3
.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
q=3
,
t=2
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
,
m_{1}=m_{2}=n
be integers and Mat(
n
,
m
,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
)=
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n\times n}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n\times n}
. If
n\geq 3
and
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor\in\{2,3,4\}
, then there do not exist perfect codes in Mat(
n
,
m
,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) with minimum distance
d
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a perfect code in Mat(
n
,
m
,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) with minimum distance
d
, then

(28)

q^{2kn}\leq\textbf{V}_{k}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})

where
k=\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor
.

Case 1:

k=1
.

By using (5) we have that

\mathbf{V}_{1}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=1+2\frac{(3^{n}-1)^{2}}{2}=1+(3^{n}-1)^{2}=3^{2n}-2\cdot 3^{n}+2=3^{n}(3^{n}-2)+2.

Since the code is perfect then

|\C|\mathbf{V}_{1}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))=3^{2n^{2}},

i.e.

|\C|=\frac{2^{2n^{2}}}{\mathbf{V}_{1}(\text{Mat}(\textbf{n},\textbf{m},\mathbb{F}_{q}))}=\frac{3^{2n^{2}}}{3^{n}(3^{n}-2)+2},

which turns out to be not an integer, as the denominator is not a multiple of three, a contradiction.

Case 2:

k=2
.

(
28
) reads as

3^{4n}-1\leq(3^{n}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(3^{n}-1)^{4}+\frac{3}{8}(3^{n}-1)^{2}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}

this condition fails for
n>0
.

Case 3:

k=3
.

From

(29)

(3^{a}-1)^{2}<(3^{a})^{2}=3^{2a}\ \ \forall a>-\frac{\lg(2)}{\lg(3)}

it follows that

\textbf{V}_{2}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})\leq 1+3^{2n}+\frac{1}{4}3^{4n}+\frac{3}{8}3^{4n-2}\leq 1+3^{4n}\left(1+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{3}{8}\right)=1+\frac{13}{8}3^{4n}

and

\textbf{V}(S_{3})=\frac{54}{416}(3^{n}-1)^{2}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}(3^{n-2}-1)^{2}+\frac{3}{16}(3^{n}-1)^{4}(3^{n-1}-1)^{2}\leq 2\cdot 3^{6n-2}.

Using (
28
), we obtain the following condition

3^{6n}\leq\textbf{V}_{3}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})=\textbf{V}_{2}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})+\textbf{V}(S_{3})\leq 1+\frac{13}{8}3^{4n}+2\cdot 3^{6n-2},

i.e.

3^{6n}-1\leq\frac{13}{8}3^{4n}+2\cdot 3^{6n-2},

which yields a contradiction when
n\geq 1
.

Case 4:

k=4
.

Combining (
29
) and (
27
), we obtain an upper bound on
\textbf{V}(S_{4})
:

\textbf{V}(S_{4})\leq 3^{8n-8}+3^{8n-6}+\frac{81}{16}3^{8n-4}\leq\frac{113}{16}\cdot 3^{8n-4},

(
28
) becomes

3^{8n}\leq\textbf{V}_{4}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})=\textbf{V}_{3}(\text{Mat({n},{m},$\mathbb{F}_{q}$)})+\textbf{V}(S_{4})\leq 1+\frac{13}{8}3^{4n}+2\cdot 3^{6n-2}+\frac{113}{16}\cdot 3^{8n-4}.

This condition fails for
n\geq 3
.
∎
