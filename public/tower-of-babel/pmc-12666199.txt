Neutrophil extracellular traps–STC1 positive feedback loop promotes immune evasion and metastasis in bladder cancer
Authors: Tingting Cai, Tao Feng, Wei Zhang, Qintao Ge, Liangju Peng, Yue Wang, Jindong Xie, Xinpei Deng, Wenkai Zhu, Shengming Jin, Jin Wang, Dingwei Ye, Yiping Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment strategy for bladder cancer (BLCA), but primary resistance still occurs in most patients. Recent evidence suggests that neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) play a key role in cancer therapy resistance, but their specific role in BLCA remains unclear.MethodsAn integrated analysis of multiple clinical cohorts receiving ICIs was performed to explore the link between NETs and patient outcomes. Immunofluorescence staining, in vitro co-culture system, scanning electron microscopy, and mouse lung metastasis model were used to evaluate the biological effects of NETs. Proteomics and single-cell transcriptomics analysis were further applied to elucidate the molecular mechanisms.ResultsClinically, NETs abnormally accumulated in BLCA and promoted tumor metastasis and immunotherapy resistance in mice. Mechanistically, NETs upregulated the anti-phagocytic checkpoint STC1 in tumor cells through the TLR2-MAPK-FosL1 axis. Notably, STC1 inhibited antigen presentation by sequestering calreticulin, while its secreted form enhances NET formation, forming a self-reinforcing feedback loop. In addition, secreted STC1 impaired differentiation of CD14+ precursors into mature dendritic cells, further exacerbating immune suppression.ConclusionThis study revealed the critical role of the NETs-STC1 feedback loop in BLCA immunoresistance. Targeting this axis could simultaneously enhance efficacy and safety of immunotherapy, providing a novel translational strategy for overcoming resistance in clinical settings.Keywords: Bladder Cancer, Neutrophil, Immunotherapy, Immune related adverse event - irAE

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Patients with bladder cancer (BLCA) have limited overall benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) released by tumor-associated neutrophils are thought to contribute to tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance, but key inhibitory pathways and targetable targets in BLCA remain unclear.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study confirmed that abnormal NETs accumulation in BLCA was significantly associated with metastatic burden, low response to ICIs, and poor survival. We further identified a “NETs-TLR2-MAPK-FosL1-STC1” axis: NETs activated MAPK-FosL1 via TLR2, which upregulated STC1 on tumor cells. STC1 captured calreticulin and restricted its membrane translocation, impairing phagocytosis and antigen presentation, thereby suppressing CD8+ T-cell immunity. Furthermore, we found that secreted STC1 reversely promoted NETs formation and reshaped the phenotype of antigen-presenting cells precursor, forming a NETs-STC1 immunosuppressive positive feedback loop.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings offer new insights for clinical practice: first, targeting NETs or STC1 to interrupt the immunosuppressive circuit; second, incorporating serum myeloperoxidase-DNA, tumor citH3, and STC1 levels into patient stratification and efficacy or toxicity monitoring; and third, providing a basis for designing prospective clinical trials combining ICIs with NETs inhibitors, potentially improving precision medicine and risk management in immunotherapy.

Background

Muscle-invasive bladder cancer accounts for approximately 25% of new bladder cancer (BLCA) cases. Due to its strong invasiveness and rapid progression, once distant metastasis occurs in patients, the 5-year survival rate plummets to 5–15%, making it one of the most challenging malignant tumors in clinical treatment.1,3 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have markedly transformed treatment strategies for solid tumors in recent years, including advanced BLCA. However, the clinical benefit rate of ICIs monotherapy is still limited, and only less than 25% of patients with BLCA can achieve long-term and lasting immune responses.4 5 Meanwhile, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) caused by ICIs treatment are frequent, which seriously affects patients’ treatment compliance and long-term efficacy.6 7 For example, the KEYNOTE-045 trial in 2023 indicated that the objective remission rate for anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) monotherapy for BLCA was merely 21.1%, with about 16.5% of patients experiencing grade 3 or above severe irAEs.8 Current biomarkers, such as programmed death-ligand 1 expression and tumor mutational load, exhibit insufficient predictive accuracy for treatment response.9 This predicament underscores the urgent need to explore novel biomarkers and combination therapy strategies.

More and more evidence has shown that the limited response of ICIs is largely influenced by the immunosuppressive state of tumor microenvironment (TME).10 BLCA shows a high degree of immune heterogeneity in the TME, and the mechanisms by which tumor cells evade immune recognition and attack are complex and diverse,11 such as antigen presentation dysfunction,12 increased secretion of immunosuppressive factors,13 and reduced infiltration of effector T cells.14 At the same time, many insights have been put on the role of non-lymphocytes in tumor immune escape,15,17 especially the role of neutrophils in immune regulation has gradually been revealed.18

Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) are pivotal components of the TME, promoting tumor progression through various mechanisms.19 Clinical studies suggest that an elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is associated with immunotherapy resistance and poorer clinical outcomes in BLCA.20 Recent studies have further pointed out that TANs play a pivotal role in facilitating tumor metastasis via the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).21 NETs are extracellular network structures comprised of DNA backbones and granule proteins, which are usually involved in the host’s innate immune defense against invading pathogens.22 However, various studies have pointed out that NETs abnormally accumulated in the TME driven by various factors.23 Abnormal aggregation of NETs promoted epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of tumor cells or surrounded tumor cells to play a “barrier protection role”, thereby escaping the attack of the immune system.24

Although studies have demonstrated the influence of NETs on facilitating tumor progression and metastasis in some cases, the specific mechanism of NETs in BLCA immune escape and ICIs resistance has not been studied in depth. In particular, as an important component of the innate immune system, whether NETs could serve as an immunosuppressive factor in TME and further affect the efficacy of immunotherapy is still unclear.

This study aims to combine data from multiple immunotherapy patient cohorts, analyze clinical samples and preclinical experimental models, systematically clarify the specific mechanism of action of NETs in BLCA metastasis and immunotherapy resistance, and further explore combined treatment strategies targeting NETs, in order to improve the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy in patients with BLCA.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

Clinical samples were enrolled from September 2022 to March 2025 at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) and Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC). Peripheral blood samples were obtained from patients with BLCA or healthy donors (HDs). Tissue samples, including tumor and adjacent normal tissues, were collected from patients with BLCA treated with transurethral resection of bladder tumor or radical cystectomy. All enrolled patients in the study were newly diagnosed, with no prior treatment with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, or other antitumor therapies before surgery.

Cell culture

The human BLCA cell lines UMUC-3 (CVCL_1783), 5637 (CVCL_0126) and T24 (CVCL_0554), murine BLCA cell line MB49 (CVCL_7076) and HEK293T (CVCL_0063) cells were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (HyClone) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cell lines were incubated at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. All cell lines were tested for Mycoplasma contamination and confirmed to be free of contamination.

Stc1 knockout and Stc1 knockdown cell lines construction

To construct an Stc1 knockout MB49 cell line, CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing was used. A single guide RNA sequence targeting the mouse STC1 gene was designed and co-transfected with Cas9 protein into MB49 cells. After transfection, the knockout effect of STC1 was verified by western blot, quantitative PCR (qPCR) and ELISA. Multiple monoclonal cell lines (sgSTC1#1/#2/#3) successfully knocked down were selected and mixed for subsequent experiments. To achieve knockdown of Stc1 in the 5,637 cell line, lentiviral vectors carrying specific shRNAs against Stc1 (shSTC1#1/#2/#3) and negative control shRNA (shNC) were constructed. These lentiviral particles were co-added to 5,637 cells with 2 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich), and an antibiotic screen was performed after 48 hours of infection to obtain cell lines stably expressing shRNA. Subsequently, STC1 knockdown efficiency in the 5,637 cell line was tested by western blot, qPCR and ELISA. The sequences of the above guide sequence and shRNAs were listed in online supplemental table S1.

Isolation of neutrophils and NETs formation assay

Peripheral blood samples (15 mL) were collected from HDs. Neutrophils were isolated using the Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (LZS11131, TBD Science) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, whole blood was layered onto density gradient separation medium and centrifuged at 600×g for 35 min. The neutrophil-enriched layer was carefully aspirated, washed two times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (350×g, 10 min). Purified neutrophils were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium, then incubated for 1 hour at 37°C under 5% CO₂.

Freshly isolated neutrophils (1×10⁷ cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and stimulated with 500 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; P1585, Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 hours at 37°C/5% CO₂. Post-stimulation, detached cells and NETs were mechanically dislodged using cell scrapers, followed by three washes with ice-cold RPMI-1640. The supernatant was centrifuged (350×g, 10 min) to remove debris. The above cultured supernatant was called NETs-containing conditioned media (NETs-CM). NETs-CM concentration was quantified using Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Q32851, Invitrogen) on a Qubit V.4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen). Aliquots of NETs-CM (1,000 ng DNA/mL) were stored at −80°C and diluted 10-fold for subsequent experiments. DNase I (150 U/mL; LS006322, Worthington) was used to digest NETs in some conditions.

CD14+ monocyte isolation and co-culture system

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells from HDs were separated by Human Lymphocyte Separation Medium (DKW-KLSH-0100; Dakewei). Human CD14+ monocytes were isolated using CD14 MicroBeads (130-118-906, Miltenyi Biotec) with >95% purity confirmed by flow-cytometry analysis (FACS). For co-culture experiments, a Transwell co-culture system was employed. STC1 knockdown (shSTC1#3) and control (shNC) 5637 cell lines were seeded in the lower chamber of a 24-well Transwell plate with 0.4 µm pore size (3,412, Corning) at a density of 2×10⁵ cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, freshly isolated CD14+ monocytes (1×10⁶ cells/well) were added to the upper chamber. Cells were co-cultured at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator for 48 hours.

Scanning electron microscopy visualization of NETs

Neutrophils were stimulated with tumor cells CM with unstimulated neutrophils serving as negative controls and PMA-stimulated neutrophils as positive controls. NETs-containing samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at 4°C for 24 hours to preserve extracellular DNA-protein complexes. Gradual ethanol dehydration series minimized structural collapse of delicate NET filaments. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was executed on APREO 2 SEM (Thermo Fisher). NETs-positive areas were defined as web-like structures >5 µm in diameter with intersecting DNA fibers.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using Radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer (89,901, Thermo Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (78,440, Thermo Fisher). Protein concentrations were determined via Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (A55860, Thermo Fisher). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle agitation: citrullinated histone H3 (1:1,000; ab281584, Abcam); STC1 (1:1,000; sc-293435, Santa Cruz); calreticulin (CRT) (1:3,000; 27 298–1-AP, Proteintech); FosL1 (1:1,000; 5281, CST); MEK1/2 (1:1,000; 9122, CST); p-MEK1/2 (1:1,000; 9154, CST); ERK1/2 (1:1,000; 4695, CST); p-ERK1/2 (1:1,000; 5726, CST); ATP1A1 (1:5,000; 55 187–1-AP, Proteintech); GAPDH (1:10,000; 60 004–1-Ig, Proteintech); Histone H3 (1:5,000; 17168–1-AP, Proteintech); α-tubulin (1:5,000; Proteintech); Flag (1:1,000; 2368S, CST). Then secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 hours at room temperature.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR

To verify the binding of FosL1 to the STC1 promoter region, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR was used for analysis. ChIP Assay Kit (17–10086, Millipore) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, UMUC-3 cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min to crosslink proteins and DNA, and then glycine was added to terminate the crosslinking reaction. Cells were collected after washing with PBS and sonicated in lysis buffer to shear chromatin to fragments of approximately 200–500 bp. The resulting chromatin solution was incubated with FosL1-specific antibodies (5281, CST) or an equal amount of isotype control IgG overnight, and then protein A/G magnetic beads were added to capture the immune complexes. After washing, crosslinking was reversed by heating and proteinase K treatment, and DNA was extracted. The extracted DNA was used as a template for qPCR using specific primers for the STC1 promoter region to evaluate the enrichment of FosL1 in this region. Primer sequences were listed in online supplemental table S1.

Co-immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells and UMUC-3 cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 30 min on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 30 min at 4°C, and supernatants were collected for reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments. Primary antibodies against STC1 (2 µg; sc-293435; Santa Cruz), CRT (2 µg; 27298–1-AP, Proteintech) and were incubated with 500 µg total protein lysates overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. Input controls (10% total lysate) and IP eluates were separated on 10% gels. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes and probed with reciprocal antibodies, CRT (1:3,000; 27298–1-AP, Proteintech) for STC1 pulldowns, STC1 (1:1,000; Santa Cruz) for CRT pulldown.

Immunofluorescence staining

For NETs detection in tumor tissues, paraffin-embedded tumor sections (5 µm) from patients with BLCA were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in Tris/EDTA buffer. After permeabilization (0.2% Triton X-100) and blocking (5% BSA), sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against citrullinated histone H3 (citH3; 1:500), myeloperoxidase (MPO; 1:1,000). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000; A11029, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000; A21429, Invitrogen) were applied for 2 hours, followed by DAPI counterstaining. NETs-positive areas (citH3+/MPO+/DAPI+ co-localization) were imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope and quantified with ImageJ (V.1.53). For each image, a linear region of interest was drawn across representative tumor areas, and the fluorescence intensity profile of citH3 was extracted using the “Plot Profile” function. The resulting curve shows fluorescence intensity (arbitrary units, AU) along distance (pixels). The area under the curve (AUC) was then calculated to represent the total fluorescence signal (AU pixels) for each sample. For the STC1-CRT co-localization assay, UMUC-3 cells cultured in confocal dishes were stained with Mito-Tracker Deep Red (100 nM, 30 min; C1034, Beyotime). After fixation (4% paraformaldehyde) and permeabilization (0.2% Triton X-100), cells were blocked with 1% BSA and incubated with antibody STC1 (1:100, sc-293435, Santa Cruz) and CRT (1:100, 27298–1-AP, Proteintech) antibodies at 4°C overnight. Alexa Fluor 488/555-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000, Invitrogen) and DAPI (1 µg/mL) were sequentially applied. Co-localization analysis was performed using Olympus FV3000 confocal system with FIJI Coloc2 plugin (Pearson’s coefficient >0.5 defined positive interaction).

Quantitative spatial proximity analysis

Quantitative spatial proximity analysis of immunofluorescence (IF) scan results of mouse lung metastatic tissues was conducted using HALO software (V.4.0, Indica Labs). The HALO Highplex FL analysis module was used. The distribution of citH3+ cells within a certain range relative to CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells was calculated to reflect whether there was a spatial exclusion relationship between NETs and T cells. After a given radius, the number of corresponding cell types in different segments will be counted.

Lung metastasis model and treatments

6-week-old C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with 1×105 MB49-Luc cells in 200 µL PBS via tail vein. Bioluminescent imaging (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer) was performed weekly for 4 weeks to quantify metastatic progression. Body weight was recorded twice weekly as a health indicator. To regulate the level of NETs in mice, lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 µg/mouse; L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) was injected into the mice via tail vein 24 hours before cell inoculation, and then DNase I (300 U/mouse; LS006322, Worthington) was injected intraperitoneally in some groups to degrade NETs. Antibodies were injected intraperitoneally three times a week using InVivoMAb anti-mouse PD-1 (200 µg/mouse; BE0146, Bio X Cell) and its IgG control. In certain groups for the in vivo depletion, mice were treated with InVivoMAb anti-CD8α, anti-CSF1R and anti-NK1.1, as described previously.25 Briefly, mice were intraperitoneally treated with anti-CSF1R antibody (800 µg/mouse; BE0213, Bio X Cell) or anti-CD8α (200 µg/mouse; BE0061, Bio X Cell) or anti-NK1.1 (200 µg/mouse; BE0036, Bio X Cell) or their matched IgG control 3 days pre-inoculation, and subsequently given 400 µg every 3 days throughout tumor development.

Single-cell RNA transcriptome sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (GSE180045) related to immune-related adverse events (irAEs) was downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).26 Two groups were categorized based on the occurrence of irAEs: one group consisted of individuals who received ICIs treatment without irAEs (A1, A2, A3), while the other group included individuals who received ICIs treatment and experienced irAEs (B1, B2, B3, MCE1, MCE2, MCL1, MCL3). For the included 10x Genomics single-cell data, we used the R package Seurat (V.4.4.1) for data preprocessing. Initially, the DoubletFinder package was applied to identify and remove doublets. Low-quality cells were filtered based on the following criteria: detected genes in each cell <200 or >6,000, and mitochondrial gene content >20%. At the gene level, low-expression genes expressed in fewer than five cells were excluded. The cell cycle status was evaluated using the CellCycleScoring() function, and the effects of the cell cycle were regressed out via the regress.out parameter in the ScaleData() function. Following normalization and data scaling, batch effect correction was performed using the R package Harmony, and the top 3,000 variable genes were selected. Subsequently, principal component analysis was conducted based on the top 2,000 highly variable genes, and the first 15 principal components were chosen to construct the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding. Cell clustering was performed using the FindClusters() function with a clustering resolution set to 0.5. Cell type annotation was based on classical marker genes reported in the literature markers,26 combined with clustering characteristics for manual identification. The final visualization analysis was achieved through the omicverse Python module.

MCP-counter deconvolution and pathway enrichment analysis

The 27Microenvironment Cell Populations (MCP) counter method facilitates absolute abundance analysis of immune cell populations in the context of transcriptomic data. Myeloid cell data from MCP-counter was used to conduct a deconvolution analysis on the extracted CD14+ cells, with the aim of elucidating the differential cell abundance between groups. Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithms were performed based on given pathways to investigate different immune patterns across groups.28 Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) analyses were performed based on Gene Ontology (GO) database for comprehensive biological pathways exploration.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (V.10.0), R (V.4.2.4), and Python (V.3.9). Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared via two-tailed Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (≥3 groups). Non-normally distributed data were analyzed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test. Factorial designs were assessed via two-way ANOVA. Survival curves were generated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and compared via log-rank test. Correlation analyses used Pearson’s coefficient (linear) or Spearman’s rank correlation (non-linear). Categorical variables were evaluated by χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. All experiments included ≥3 independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean±SD (parametric) or median with IQR (non-parametric), adhering to STRIVE guidelines. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p<0.05, with exact p values reported for critical comparisons. ns: not significant; *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001.

Results

Elevated NETs levels positively correlated with tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance in BLCA

To investigate the role of NETs in tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance in BLCA, we quantified citH3 and MPO in tumor tissues in two independent cohorts. Overlapping regions of citH3 and MPO were considered NETs-positive areas.29 IF signals revealed pronounced co-localization of citH3 and MPO in immunotherapy non-responders versus responders in the FUSCC cohort (figure 1A,B). Line-scan analyses showed higher citH3 signal along the scan distance in non-responders, with a significantly greater area under the fluorescence-intensity curve (AUC) (figure 1C,D). CitH3 is the most specific protein marker for NETs.23 An immunohistochemistry study (FUSCC cohort, n=27) showed that tumor citH3-positive area (figure 1E) was higher in non-responders. Additionally, western blot analysis also supported higher citH3 protein expression in non-responders to immunotherapy (online supplemental figure S1G). Serum MPO-DNA complex level is considered a systemic biomarker of NET burden.30 We measured the MPO-DNA in both cohorts. Consistently, elevated serum MPO-DNA was observed in non-responders (figure 1F,J). In further subgroup analyses, we found that higher NET burden, represented by citH3 positivity or serum MPO-DNA, was associated with lack of benefit from ICIs (figure 1G,I), tumor metastasis (figure 1H,I; online supplemental figure S1A–F) and shorter progression-free survival (figure 1K).

Multivariable logistic regression adjusting for T stage, age, and gender confirmed circulating MPO–DNA as an independent predictor of metastasis (OR 10.293, 95% CI 4.021 to 29.591; p<0.001) and of reduced ICIs benefit (OR 3.583, 95% CI 0.994 to 15.421; p=0.0429), whereas other covariates were not significant (figure 1L,M). The clinical characteristics of the cohorts were presented in online supplemental figure S1H and table S2.

Collectively, these data suggest that NETs positively correlated with metastatic progression and predicted poorer responsiveness to immunotherapy in BLCA. NETs may not be merely a biomarker but could be an active driver of immune resistance and metastatic progression.

NETs fueled experimental metastasis in a partially immune-dependent manner and fostered immunosuppressive TME formation

To confirm the role of NETs in driving metastasis and immune resistance in BLCA, an LPS-induced high-NETs lung metastasis model was established in C57BL/6 and nude mice (figure 2A). Serum MPO-DNA complexes were monitored as a validation of NETs induction (online supplemental figure S2A). In vivo bioluminescence imaging demonstrated that NETs promoted metastasis in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice but not in immunodeficient nude mice, and whereas DNase I only consistently attenuated metastasis in C57BL/6 mice, with a blunted effect in nude (figure 2B,C; online supplemental figure S2B,C). Histopathological analysis further confirmed that the area of lung metastases in C57BL/6 mice was significantly larger in the LPS group (online supplemental figure S2D,E). Furthermore, to determine whether NETs acted by interfering antitumor immunity and to exclude background effects unrelated to immune deficiency in nude mice, we introduced antibody-mediated depletion groups (IgG, anti-CD8, anti-NK1.1) in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. The results showed that DNase I could reduce metastatic radiance in IgG and anti-NK1.1 groups, but not when CD8+ T cells were depleted (figure 2D,E; online supplemental figure S2G,H). These results indicated that the pro-metastatic influence of NETs may be achieved by interfering with antitumor T-cell immunity. To define this further, we profiled the core antitumor CD8+ T-cell compartment in lung lesions by flow cytometry. LPS increased neutrophil infiltration and reduced both the infiltration and functional competence of CD8+ T cells (figure 2F–H; online supplemental figure S2G). The positive ratio of CD8+ functional markers T cell factor 1 (TCF1) (stem-like T cells), granzyme B (cytotoxicity), and CD107a (degranulation) was significantly decreased (figure 2G,H). Spatial analysis was conducted to determine the compartmental distribution of T cells. LPS reduced intratumoral CD4+/CD8+ densities, whereas DNase I shifted cells back toward the tumor parenchyma (figure 2I,J; online supplemental figure S2K,L). Distance-to-centroid scatter plots showed that, in the LPS group, CD4+/CD8+ T cells were farther from the tumor centroid and DNase I shortened these distances (figure 2K). Together, these results pointed out that NETs fueled experimental metastasis in an immune-dependent manner and fostered immunosuppressive TME formation.

Combination of anti-PD-1 and NETs inhibition (DNase I) enhanced immunotherapy efficacy and reduced immune-related inflammation in mice

Given these findings, we tested whether NETs inhibition could improve immunotherapy efficacy in BLCA. We designed four treatment groups in LPS-induced high NETs lung metastasis model: isotype control, DNase I monotherapy group, anti-PD-1 monotherapy group, and combination therapy group (anti-PD-1 + DNase I) (figure 3A). A substantial and statistically significant decrease in metastatic burden was observed in the combination group, as shown by in vivo bioluminescence imaging, compared with anti-PD-1 monotherapy (figure 3B,C). Histopathological quantification further demonstrated that the combined treatment group exhibited a reduction in lung metastatic foci compared with the anti-PD-1 group (figure 3D,E; online supplemental figure S3A).

Additionally, to better understand the TME landscape in different treatment groups, we analyzed these key immune cell types by FACS (gating in online supplemental figure S3B). The infiltration level of CD8+ T cells was markedly increased in the combination (anti-PD-1+DNase I) group (figure 3F), with significant upregulation of functional subset markers granzyme B, TCF1, and CD107a (figure 3G). Meanwhile, major antigen-presenting cells (APCs) components, macrophages and dendritic cells, were increased in the combination group in parallel (figure 3F). Additionally, the combination group also exhibited the lowest levels of immunosuppressive cytokines interleukin (IL)-4 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) (online supplemental figure S3C). Together, these results indicated that NETs inhibition could improve APCs infiltration, further enhance the infiltration and function of CD8+ T cells in vivo.

Since irAEs limit immunotherapy in the clinic,31,33 we evaluated safety-related readouts in our model. Evidence of adverse events of immunotherapy was observed in our mouse model as well. An increased concentration of alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase (ALT/AST), serum creatinine (CREA) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) after anti-PD-1 treatment was detected (online supplemental table S3). Histopathological scores32 34 in liver and kidney tissues of mice showed that anti-PD-1 induced mild-to-moderate acute inflammatory damage (online supplemental figure S3G) while the combination group exhibited none to mild organ damages. In addition, more weight loss (online supplemental figure S3D) and elevated levels of the inflammation-associated cytokines IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α were observed in the anti-PD-1 group compared with the combination group (figure 3H,I).

The potential role of neutrophils and NETs has emerged as a significant focus owing to their strong association with systematic inflammation.33 35 To further explore this potential mechanism, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from patients who developed irAEs during immunotherapy (online supplemental figure S3E,F). Cell populations, including neutrophils, were identified referring to signature markers (online supplemental figure S3E). We found that neutrophil clusters from irAEs displayed higher NET-related transcripts (PADI4, CYBB, NCF1, NCF2)36,38 (online supplemental figure S3F), hinting at enhanced NET-program priming in the irAEs context. Collectively, NETs inhibition enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 and reduced systemic inflammatory toxicity, supporting NETs as a promising target to improve both efficacy and tolerability of immunotherapy.

NETs reshaped the proteomic landscape and upregulated the phagocytosis checkpoint STC1 in bladder cancer cells

Then we came to further explore how NETs mediate immune evasion at the molecular level. We established an in vitro co-culture system using NETs-CM (figure 4A; neutrophil purity verification in online supplemental figure S4A). Confocal microscopy and FACS analyses revealed that tumor cells could significantly uptake NETs (figure 4B), suggesting a direct interaction between NETs and tumor cells. Notably, after co-culture with NETs-CM for 24 hours, we observed robust proteomic changes in tumor cells, including increased expression of STC1, MMP9, and VEGFA, which were associated with immune evasion and metastasis39 40 (figure 4C). GSEA analysis also revealed that immune response signaling was significantly downregulated in the NETs-CM co-culture group (online supplemental figure S4B). These findings suggest that NETs induced tumor cells to an immune escape phenotype.

STC1 was identified as a key downstream effector of NETs-mediated immune evasion

Among all the molecules that showed significant changes after NETs stimulation, we noticed the STC1, which is highly associated with immune escape and immunotherapy resistance.41 STC1 has been identified as a novel pan-cancer phagocytosis checkpoint that is aberrantly upregulated in multiple cancers and associated with poor prognosis.42 43 Then we performed pseudo-time analysis of bulk data in an external cohort to observe the sequential relationship between the rise of STC1 and the formation of NETs (figure 4D). Consistent with our proteomics data, it was found that the rise of STC1 was tightly followed by the upregulation of NETs-related genes (figure 4E). Therefore, we hypothesized that STC1 may act as a key downstream effector in the process of NETs-mediated immune escape. The western blot (figure 4F), qPCR (online supplemental figure 4C) and ELISA (online supplemental figure S4D) were conducted to confirm the upregulated flow of STC1 after NETs-CM treatment. Moreover, NETs upregulated STC1 in a dose-dependent manner (figure 4G), further confirming the promoting effect of NETs on STC1 expression. Next, we explored the changes in STC1 expression and its clinical significance in the FUSCC cohort. ELISA assays revealed that serum STC1 levels in the serum of immunotherapy responder were significantly lower than those in non-responder (figure 4H). Meanwhile, STC1 expression gradually increased with tumor progression, reaching its peak at the metastatic stage, representing the same trend with NETs (online supplemental figure S4E–G). Furthermore, we analyzed the association between STC1 expression and markers of NETs formation, and a significantly positive correlation was observed (figure 4I). Notably, patients with higher STC1 tended to benefit less from immunotherapy (figure 4J) and had a poorer survival prognosis (online supplemental figure S4H). In vivo, knockout of STC1 in MB49 cells significantly reduced lung metastases in C57BL/6 mice (figure 4K,L; online supplemental figure S4I–N). These multilevel evidence presented collectively indicates that STC1 was the crucial downstream effector in NETs-mediated immune evasion and metastatic progression of BLCA.

STC1 disrupted antigen presentation by capturing CRT in mitochondria

Building on the observation that STC1 acted downstream of NETs, we first verified whether its immunosuppressive effect is mediated through interfering with APCs. We depleted APCs with anti-CSF1R in C57BL/6 mice in vivo. Anti-CSF1R treatment significantly depleted macrophages (online supplemental figure S5A). It was found that, in the IgG group, STC1 knockout lowered metastatic burden (figure 5A,B, online supplemental figure S5B–D) and increased CD8+ T-cell frequency and effector function (interferon-γ, granzyme B) (figure 5C,D, online supplemental figure S5E). Strikingly, these STC1-dependent differences were lost on APC depletion. Specifically, anti-CSF1R eliminated the STC1-associated pro-metastatic effect and abrogated STC1-mediated suppression of CD8+ T-cell infiltration and function (figure 5A–D; online supplemental figure S5B–E). These data indicate that the inhibitory effect of STC1 on T-cell immunity is APCs dependent.

Antigen presentation function of APCs is strictly regulated by the dynamic balance between anti-phagocytic signals and pro-phagocytic signals on the tumor cell membrane.44 Among the latter, CRT is a classical pro-phagocytic signaling molecule.40 45 46 We measured the surface CRT exposure and found that NETs selectively reduced surface CRT on tumor cells (figure 5E; online supplemental figure S5F), an effect not reproduced by LPS, PMA, poly(dA:dT), or poly(I:C) (figure 5F; online supplemental figure S5H–J). Importantly, siSTC1 reversed the NETs-induced loss of surface CRT, while total CRT levels remained largely unchanged (figure 5E; online supplemental figure S5G). These data point to impaired CRT translocation rather than diminished protein abundance.

We next tested whether STC1 directly intercepts CRT. Co-IP demonstrated an interaction between STC1 and CRT in NETs-treated UMUC-3 cells (figure 5G; online supplemental figure S5M) and HEK293T (online supplemental figure S5K,L). Confocal imaging revealed increased intracellular co-localization of STC1 and CRT in the mitochondrial region in vitro (figure 5H,I), and co-localization was also observed in metastatic muscle-invasive bladder cancer (mMIBC) patients tissues (figure 5J). Consistently, dual-IF in vivo showed higher STC1–CRT co-localization in the LPS group (NETs-enriched lesions) than LPS+DNase I group (online supplemental figure S5N). Together, these findings support a model in which NETs induced STC1, which sequestered CRT intracellularly in the mitochondrial region, thereby limiting its display on the tumor surface.

Functionally, we assessed whether this axis would alter phagocytic clearance. In the macrophage-tumor co-culture system, STC1 knockout significantly enhanced phagocytosis (figure 5K,L). FACS confirmed a significantly higher proportion of PKH26+CD14+ double-positive events within the tumor:macrophage ratio (figure 5M–N; gates/isotype controls in online supplemental figure S5O,P). In summary, these results delineate a NETs-STC1-CRT pathway that reduced tumor immunogenicity by limiting surface CRT, attenuating macrophage phagocytosis and antigen presentation, and ultimately suppressing CD8+ T-cell immunity.

NETs transcriptionally upregulate STC1 via the TLR2-MAPK-FosL1 axis

Having established that NETs upregulated STC1 expression in BLCA cells, we further investigated the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying NETs-induced STC1 upregulation. First, we performed an integrative analysis combining transcription factors (TFs) upregulated by NETs-CM in our proteomics data with multiple TF prediction databases. This analysis identified FosL1 as the only overlapping candidate regulator of STC1 (figure 6A, online supplemental figure S6A). In The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) BLCA cohort, FosL1 expression exhibited a positive correlation with STC1 expression (figure 6B). Besides, after the silence of FosL1, STC1 was significantly reduced, even in the presence of NETs-CM stimulation (figure 6C). Furthermore, the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net) predicted the presence of an FosL1 binding site within the STC1 promoter region (figure 6D), and a distinct ChIP sequencing enrichment peak of FosL1 in this region could be observed in multiple BLCA cell lines (figure 6E). On these predictions, we performed ChIP-qPCR in UMUC-3 cells, which directly demonstrated that FosL1 specifically bound to the STC1 promoter region (figure 6F,G). Moreover, the enrichment of STC1 promoter fragments in the FosL1-IP group was significantly higher than that in the IgG control group (figure 6G). A dual-luciferase reporter assay also confirmed that FosL1 overexpression significantly enhanced the transcriptional activity of the STC1 promoter (figure 6H). Previous studies have demonstrated that FosL1 was regulated by the MAPK pathway.47,49 Our observations consistently demonstrated that NETs stimulation significantly activated MAPK-FosL1, resulting in the upregulation of STC1 (figure 6I).

To investigate how tumor cells internalize NETs and trigger STC1 transcriptional activation, we surveyed classic damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP)-sensing pathways—including cGAS-STING, TLRs (TLR2, TLR4, TLR9), NLRP3, CCDC25, and RAGE.2450,53 qPCR screening revealed selective induction after NETs treatment in UMUC-3 and T24 cells (figure 6J). The messenger RNA of TLR2 and NLRP3 significantly increased, while cGAS-STING, CCDC25, and RAGE and other TLRs were not notably increased or activated (figure 6J; online supplemental figure S6B). Functionally, TLR2 knockdown (siTLR2) could attenuate NETs-induced MAPK activation, lowered FosL1, and decreased STC1, while NLRP3 knockdown (siNLRP3) produced no effect (figure 6K). Indeed, TLR2 knockout markedly impaired NETs uptake in UMUC-3 cells (figure 6L,M). Together, these data delineate a receptor-to-promoter pathway in which NETs engaged TLR2 to activate MAPK–FosL1, thereby driving STC1 upregulation in BLCA cells.

Secreted STC1 augments NETs formation to establish a self-reinforcing NETs-STC1 loop in the TME

Given that STC1 is a secreted protein, its functional role within the stromal compartment outside tumor cells warrants further investigation. ELISA results revealed that STC1 secreted at significantly elevated levels into the TME (figure 4H; online supplemental figure S4D). To investigate whether high extracellular levels of STC1 promoted the formation of NETs, we first established STC1 knockdown cell lines and collected their CM to stimulate neutrophils in vitro (online supplemental figure S7A). IF staining showed a marked increase in NETs structures in neutrophils treated with high STC1 CM (online supplemental figure S7B,C). In contrast, NETs formation was less evident in neutrophils exposed to low STC1 CM or negative control medium (online supplemental figure S7B,C). Neutrophils exposed to CM from STC1 knockout cells CM further confirmed this (figure 7A,B).

In vivo, NETs structures were rarely detectable in lung metastases derived from sgSTC1 tumors, whereas abundant NETs were observed in sgNC lesions (figure 7F). To test whether STC1 alone is sufficient to trigger NETs, we titrated recombinant human STC1 (rhSTC1) on neutrophils. The results showed that rhSTC1 induced NETs in a dose-dependent manner, with 300 ng/mL triggering robust web-like NETs structure (figure 7E and online supplemental figure S7D). To exclude the possibility that sgSTC1 altered other tumor-derived NETs inducers, we profiled sgNC and sgSTC tumor cells by RNA-seq. The results showed no significant changes in the expression of established known NETosis-inducing factors from literatures, including PGE2 (Ptges), PGE2 (Ptgs2), MIF (Mif), TGF-β1 (Tgfb1), TNF-α (Tnf), IL-1β (Il1b), GM-CSF (Csf2), G-CSF (Csf3), Cathepsin L (Ctsl), cathepsin B (Ctsb), and HMGB1 (Hmgb1)29 30 38 (online supplemental figure SE,F). These demonstrated that secreted STC1 is sufficient to trigger NETs, establishing a self-reinforcing NETs–STC1 loop in the TME. Such a feedback loop between NETs and STC1 could amplify immunosuppression and metastasis and may explain why immune evasion was so difficult to reverse once established.

Secreted STC1 impedes APCs maturation through transcriptional reprogramming

In the above results, we elucidated the mechanism by which intracellular STC1 inhibits antigen presentation by capturing CRT. On this basis, we further explored whether secreted STC1 would have additional effects on APCs cells. Here we used STC1 knockdown tumor cells or control co-cultured with CD14+ monocytes in vitro (online supplemental figure S7G,H). The co-cultured CD14+ cells underwent RNA-seq, and cellular components were analyzed using the MCP-counter algorithm for deconvolution. ssGSEA analysis indicated enhanced antigen-presenting capacity in the low STC1 group (figure 7G). Furthermore, under low STC1 condition, CD14+ cells were more likely to differentiate into dendritic cells, accompanied by a decreased monocyte and neutrophil ratio (figure 7H). Differential expression analysis revealed that several key immunosuppressive factors, such as IL1RN, CSF1, and S100A9, were significantly downregulated in CD14+ cells under low STC1 condition. In contrast, immune activation-associated chemokines, including CXCL9, CXCL10, and the antigen-presentation-associated molecule HLA-DOA, were significantly upregulated (online supplemental figure S7I). These expression trends were validated through qPCR experiments (online supplemental figure S7J) and pseudotime analysis (online supplemental figure S7K). These upregulated genes under low STC1 condition were significantly enriched in biological pathways related to immune activation and leukocyte chemotaxis (figure 7I). In contrast, those upregulated genes under high STC1 condition were enriched in granulocyte chemotaxis, which may increase neutrophil chemotaxis and infiltration (online supplemental figure S7L). This finding further supported the negative role of STC1 in regulating the maturation of APCs infiltration and antigen-presenting functions. In summary, secreted STC1 not only enhanced NETs formation but also reprogrammed CD14+ APCs precursors, further impairing antitumor immunity in the TME.

The graphic abstract (figure 7J) summarized the main points of our study. NETs accumulation in BLCA triggered immune evasion through STC1. Mechanistically, NETs increased the overall flux of STC1 through TLR2-MAPK-FosL1 pathway, which suppressed antigen presentation by captured CRT. Secreted STC1 further enhanced NETs formation, establishing a self-amplifying immunosuppressive loop. Additionally, secreted STC1 reprogrammed APCs precursors, further dampening antitumor immunity.

In conclusion, our study identifies the NETs–STC1 positive feedback loop as a central mechanism driving immune evasion in BLCA, providing new insights into potential therapeutic strategies for restoring antitumor immunity and improving immunotherapy outcomes.

Discussion

Despite significant advances in immunotherapy for BLCA, high rates of therapeutic resistance and the limited proportion of patients who respond remain major clinical challenges.5 8 54 In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the immunosuppressive mechanisms of innate immune components within the TME, particularly TANs and their release of NETs.22 Initially regarded as a host defense mechanism against pathogens, NETs have been shown to exert pro-metastatic and pro-thrombotic effects in various solid tumors.55,57 Notably, multiple factors within the TME—including inflammatory cytokines, DAMPs, and tumor-derived chemokines30 —have been implicated in the pathological accumulation of NETs. However, whether NETs contribute to immune escape and progression in BLCA remains poorly defined, and most existing studies have concentrated on their physical barrier function or their role in EMT, with limited insight into their immunoregulatory crosstalk with tumor cells.

In this study, focusing on how NETs mediate immune resistance in BLCA, we established a multilevel logical framework from clinical observation to mechanistic validation. First, two real-world BLCA immunotherapy cohorts were collected and demonstrated that NET levels were significantly elevated in immunotherapy non-responder patients with BLCA. In vivo, we found that NETs could promote metastasis in an immune-dependent manner and targeting NETs could increase the efficacy of ICIs and reduce associated side effects. At the molecular level, NETs activated the TLR2-MAPK–FosL1 axis to transcriptionally upregulate phagocytic checkpoint STC1, promoting immune evasion in BLCA. In addition, STC1 not only inhibited the phagocytic process by blocking CRT-mediated antigen presentation, but its secreted form could also reversely promote the abnormal accumulation of NETs, forming a self-reinforcing loop to amplify the immunosuppressive effect, and hinder the development of APCs precursors to aggravate the immunosuppressive TME. This mechanistic chain could provide us a theoretical basis for the optimization of combined treatment strategies.

NETs, as an important effector product of the innate immune response of neutrophils, have long been regarded as a key mechanism for defending against infection.22 29 However, increasing evidence shows that persistent neutrophil activation and NETs-related chronic inflammation in cancer play a role in promoting tumor growth.30 For example, NETs can act as a physical protective barrier to hinder T cell-mediated cytotoxicity58 or promote EMT in tumor cells.24 51 Surprisingly, our study further expanded this understanding from the perspective of immune regulation function, revealing the mechanism of NETs in promoting immune escape by affecting the innate immune anti-phagocytic pathway of tumor cells. This is not only another immunosuppressive mechanism besides adaptive immunity16 59 (such as T-cell exhaustion and immune checkpoint inhibition), but also suggests that there is a key molecular crosstalk between innate immune cells and tumor-intrinsic immune escape strategies.

Our study innovatively pointed out that STC1 functioned as the key effector of NETs, mediating their pro-immune escape effects in BLCA. STC1, a newly identified innate immune checkpoint molecule which has been has been validated in more than 20 types of cancer. A known, effective antitumor immunity relies on proper antigen presentation, which requires a dynamic balance between pro-phagocytic signals, like CRT, and anti-phagocytic checkpoints, such as STC1, CD47 on tumor cells.54 57 When CRT translocates to the membrane, it acts as an “eat me” signal, allowing APCs to recognize and phagocytose tumor cells. However, this system can be hijacked by tumor cells through upregulation of anti-phagocytic molecules that block CRT-mediated recognition. In our study, the novel role of STC1 in inhibiting antigen presentation by capturing CRT was reconfirmed, further emphasizing the key role of “imbalance between phagocytic and anti-phagocytic signals” in BLCA disease progression and immunotherapy response. Moreover, the functional role of secreted STC1 has rarely been addressed in previous studies. Our findings unexpectedly revealed that secreted STC1 further promoted NETs accumulation, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing immune escape loop. This immune escape loop may, at least in part, account for the refractory nature and complexity of immunotherapy resistance observed in BLCA. This also highlights that single-agent therapies are often insufficient to reverse the established immune-resistant microenvironment, further underscoring the clinical value of developing combinatorial treatment strategies.

Consistent with clinical situations, our research observed following anti-PD-1 treatment, characterized by elevated serum biochemical markers (ALT, AST, CREA, and BUN) and acute inflammatory histological signs in the livers and kidneys. These findings further underscore the clinical challenges posed by irAEs, which jeopardize patient safety and limit the duration and continuity of immune checkpoint blockade therapy.60 In this context, we identified a potential mechanistic link between the formation of NETs and irAEs.35 Neutrophils from patients with irAEs showed higher expression of NET-associated transcripts. Given that NETs are associated with autoimmunity,29 endothelial injury,61 and cytokine amplification,62 their upregulation may serve as a central mediator linking excessive innate immune activation to tissue-specific toxicity. More importantly, combined inhibition of NETs can alleviate organ damage and inflammation caused by anti-PD-1 therapy without compromising its antitumor efficacy. These findings underscore the previously underestimated contribution of NETs to ICIs-induced inflammatory toxicity. Targeting NETs may offer dual benefits—enhancing the therapeutic index of immune checkpoint inhibitors by mitigating irAEs while maintaining or even enhancing their antitumor efficacy. Future research should explore the integration of NET-targeting strategies into clinical immunotherapy regimens, particularly for patients at high risk of irAEs. However, several limitations temper this inference. First, transcript abundance in neutrophils is not a direct clue of NETosis. Second, our inflammatory cytokine results (circulating IL-6, TNF-α) are consistent with—but not specific for—NETs activity. Third, cohort size, sampling windows, and treatment heterogeneity may introduce residual confounding, such as intercurrent infections, antibiotics usage, or occult tissue injury, that we could not fully control.

Despite the strengths of targeting NETs, several noteworthy limitations remain. First, targeting NETs may raise safety concerns, particularly regarding the risk of increased susceptibility to infection due to impaired innate immune defense. As NETs are essential for antimicrobial protection, broad inhibition may disrupt physiological immune surveillance. Future strategies should aim to selectively modulate tumor-associated NETs without compromising systemic immunity. Second, the fundamental mechanisms underlying the excessive NETs accumulation in BLCA remain unclear. While our findings implicate NETs in immune evasion, it is unclear whether their accumulation results primarily from enhanced formation, impaired degradation, or both. The role of other components in the TME—such as cytokines, DAMPs, and tumor cell-derived secretions—in sustaining NET formation also warrants further investigation. A systematic solution to these issues will enhance our understanding of the origin of abnormal NET activation and facilitate the transformation and application of targeting NETs-STC1 circuit in clinical settings in a safer and more controllable manner.

Conclusion

Our study uncovered a critical immunosuppressive feedback loop driven by NETs and STC1 in BLCA, which impaired antigen presentation, promoted tumor metastasis, and led to resistance to immunotherapy. Mechanistically, NETs upregulated phagocytosis checkpoint STC1 through the TLR2-MAPK–FosL1 axis, while STC1, in turn, enhanced the formation of NETs, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing immunosuppressive cycle. Disrupting this loop by targeting NETs or STC1 significantly restored antigen presentation capacity, thus improving immunotherapy efficacy. Notably, NETs also mediate irAEs and targeting NETs can alleviate inflammatory toxicity while preserving the antitumor response, providing a dual benefit for optimizing immunotherapy. These findings not only highlighted the critical role of the NETs-STC1 circuit in immune resistance and toxicity but also provided a theoretical basis for combined targeting strategies aimed at enhancing the safety and efficacy of immunotherapy in BLCA.
