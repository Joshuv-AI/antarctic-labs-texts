Smartphone-Based Automated Quantification of Bulbar Conjunctival Hyperemia Using Vessel Density Mapping
Authors: Giovanni Rubegni, Mario Fruschelli, Martina Mugnai, Tommaso Padula, Francesco Cappellani, Niccolò Castellino, Alessandro Avitabile, Alessio Luschi, Karl Mercieca, Gian Marco Tosi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop a smartphone application (Conjunctiva VD) that objectively quantifies bulbar conjunctival hyperemia by estimating the conjunctival vessel density (VD) within a user-defined region of interest (ROI).MethodsA total of 100 conjunctival images from 2 public datasets were analyzed. An external imaging technician selected images meeting predefined quality criteria and delineated an inferior bulbar conjunctival ROI within the app. After ROI selection, the application automatically resized each ROI to 2100 × 1800 pixels and performed on-device vessel segmentation using a fixed computer-vision pipeline to compute the VD (%). Three expert ophthalmologists independently graded hyperemia on the technician-defined ROI using the scale (0–4) and provided a subjective percentage estimate of the VD. The inter-rater agreement (the quadratic-weighted κ for Efron grading and the intraclass correlation coefficient for the subjective VD) and associations between the VDs and the expert assessments (Spearman's ρ) were evaluated.ResultsThe inter-rater agreement was high for the Efron grading (quadratic-weighted κ = 0.854–0.885), with a similarly high reliability for the subjective VD estimation (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.910; 95% confidence interval, 0.877–0.935). The application-derived VD correlated strongly with Efron grades across graders (Spearman's ρ = 0.780–0.807; all P < 0.001) and with the expert-estimated VD (ρ = 0.814–0.843; all P < 0.001).ConclusionsA lightweight smartphone application can provide an objective VD metric that shows a strong association with an expert clinical assessment of conjunctival hyperemia.Translational RelevanceA portable, widely available smartphone-based tool for objective hyperemia quantification may lower barriers to point-of-care use and enable scalable applications in outpatient triage, longitudinal monitoring, and teleophthalmology.

Introduction

Conjunctival hyperemia is a frequent clinical sign observed across a broad range of ocular disorders and represents a visible manifestation of vascular dilation within the conjunctival (and sometimes episcleral) circulation. It is often associated with ocular surface inflammation such as allergic conjunctivitis and corneal or conjunctival infections, as well as intraocular or deeper inflammatory conditions including uveitis and scleritis.1,2 In both settings, the extent of hyperemia often reflects the intensity of the underlying inflammatory or irritative process, making it clinically meaningful for the diagnosis, stratification of disease severity, monitoring of therapeutic response, and communication with patients.3 In addition, conjunctival hyperemia may be induced or exacerbated by topical medications, including some antiglaucoma agents, where redness is a relevant tolerability outcome in routine care and clinical trials.4–6 Despite its clinical importance, the assessment of bulbar conjunctival hyperemia is still commonly based on semiquantitative grading scales. Several systems coexist, including the Validated Bulbar Redness Scale, the Institute for Eye Research scale, the Efron scale, and the McMonnies/Chapman–Davies scale, among others.7–10 These scales typically use ordinal categories (e.g., 0–4 or 0–5) supported by reference images. Although practical, this approach is inherently subjective and susceptible to intra- and interobserver variability related to grader experience, viewing conditions, and illumination.11 Moreover, the discrete nature of ordinal scoring limits sensitivity to subtle changes, and incompatibilities among scales can hinder comparisons across studies or centers.12,13 To overcome these limitations, investigators have explored quantitative approaches based on digital imaging, including colorimetric features and vascular-related metrics such as percent pixel coverage within a conjunctival region of interest (ROI).14–18 Because hyperemia is fundamentally linked to vascular dilation and increased vessel prominence, quantifying the conjunctival vasculature provides a rational basis for objective assessment.19,20 In particular, bulbar conjunctival vessel density (VD), defined as the fraction of a standardized bulbar conjunctival region occupied by segmented vascular structures, offers an intuitive continuous metric that can be reported globally and spatially (e.g., by quadrant), enabling both the grading and the mapping of redness distribution.21–24

Although objective measures of hyperemia are feasible, their translation into routine practice is still limited by practical deployment barriers. Many published workflows require dedicated acquisition setups, specialized software environments, or nontrivial processing pipelines that are not readily available at the point of care.25–28 In contrast, smartphone-based solutions could lower these barriers by enabling a rapid, quantitative assessment using a widely available device, potentially supporting outpatient triage, longitudinal monitoring, decentralized clinical studies, and teleophthalmology. A portable tool that produces an interpretable continuous metric and a visual quality-control output could improve standardization while remaining feasible in everyday clinical settings.

In this study, we describe and evaluate a smartphone application (Conjunctiva VD) designed to automatically estimate the bulbar conjunctival VD from conjunctival images after user-defined ROI selection. We report the image-processing pipeline (ROI selection, vessel enhancement, and segmentation) implemented on the device and evaluate the relationship between the application-derived VD and an expert clinical assessment of hyperemia severity.

Methods

Datasets

Images analyzed in this study were selected from two publicly available conjunctival image datasets. The first dataset was the Eye Conjunctiva Segmentation Dataset (Mendeley Data), which includes 547 high-resolution color images (3000 × 4000 pixels) acquired using two smartphones (OnePlus 9R, 48 MP, f/1.7; OnePlus 9 Pro, 48 MP, f/1.8) under ambient room lighting.29 The second dataset was the Image Dataset on Eye Diseases Classification (Uveitis, Conjunctivitis, Cataract, Eyelid Drooping, and Normal) with Symptoms and SMOTE Validation (Mendeley Data).30 The dataset provides JPEG (.jpg) color images collected from online sources (via a Google image search using disease-specific keywords), with duplicates removed and a subsequent quality review as described by the dataset contributors. The dataset includes five diagnostic categories (normal, conjunctivitis, uveitis, cataract, and eyelid drooping) and reports a balanced distribution after augmentation/oversampling (649 images per class).

Ethics and Data Use

This work represents a secondary analysis of deidentified images obtained from publicly available datasets. No new participant recruitment was performed and no additional clinical data were collected. Dataset use complied with the datasets’ terms of use and licensing conditions.

Image Selection and ROI Definition

Image selection and ROI definition were performed by an external professional imaging technician (AL) who was blinded to the dataset origin, image labels, and study objectives. All conjunctival images available in the two datasets were screened (dataset 1: 358 conjunctival images; dataset 2: 687 conjunctival images). Augmented/SMOTE-derived images were excluded. When multiple images from the same patient were available, only one image per patient was retained; therefore, duplicate and near-duplicate images were excluded. From the remaining eligible images, the technician selected a total of 100 images meeting predefined quality criteria (adequate exposure, sharp focus, minimal glare, and correct field of view), with 50 images sampled from the Eye Conjunctiva Segmentation Dataset and 50 images sampled from the Eye Diseases Classification dataset.29,30 For each selected image, the technician manually delineated the ROI, defined as the exposed inferior bulbar conjunctiva, excluding eyelid margins, the cornea/limbus, and other nonrelevant structures. ROI delineation was performed within the iOS application using a polygon-based selection tool (sequential point placement), and the ROI was finalized using the ‘Close polygon’ function before analysis (Fig. 1A).

Because the images were collected from heterogeneous online sources, the acquisition parameters (e.g., camera type, illumination, and native resolution) were not standardized and the image size varied across samples. Therefore, after ROI selection the application automatically standardized each ROI during preprocessing by resizing it to a fixed resolution of 2100 × 1800 pixels (width × height) before VD computation. The resulting ROIs were then used as input in the clinical and software evaluations.

Clinical Grading

Each selected image was independently assessed by three experienced ophthalmologists (MF, FC, NC) with expertise in ocular surface evaluation. Grading was performed on the technician-defined conjunctival ROI to ensure that all evaluators assessed the same anatomical region across the images. Conjunctival hyperemia severity was graded using the Efron Grading Scale (0–4) based on standardized reference images. In addition, the same examiners provided an independent subjective estimate of the conjunctival VD for each image, defined as the perceived percentage of the ROI occupied by visible conjunctival vessels and expressed on a continuous scale ranging from 0% to 100%. This percentage-based estimate was not intended as a standardized clinical grading scale, but rather as an exploratory expert-derived continuous comparator expressed in the same unit as the application output. The instruction was conceptually based on previous work by Fukushima et al.,31–33 in which the severity of ocular redness was related to the software-calculated VD percentage; the correspondence between conjunctival hyperemia and the VD percentages reported in those studies was used as a reference framework for the graders’ subjective percentage estimates. Grading sessions were performed independently and without discussion among examiners. Examiners were masked to the dataset origin, image labels, app-derived VD values, and app-generated vessel masks.

Image Analysis Software and VD Computation

The smartphone application (Conjunctiva VD) was implemented and tested on an iPhone 17 (iOS version 26.1; Apple, Cupertino, CA); all analyses reported in this study were executed on this device using Conjunctiva VD (version 1.0). The app was developed using Swift/SwiftUI for the user interface and an on-device image-processing engine implemented with OpenCV through an Objective-C++ bridge. The application accepts JPG/JPEG images and performs all computations locally on the device (no cloud processing).

After ROI delineation, the analysis followed a deterministic parameter-fixed pipeline. The ROI image was resized to a fixed resolution of 2100 × 1800 pixels (width × height) using area-based interpolation to reduce scale-related variability. The algorithm extracted the green channel from the original BGR image to leverage favorable vessel-to-background contrast, and local contrast was enhanced using contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (clipLimit = 2.0; tileGridSize = 8 × 8). The enhanced image was intensity inverted and segmented using Gaussian adaptive thresholding to account for nonuniform illumination (blockSize = 15; C = −2), producing a binary vessel mask. The mask underwent morphological opening using a 1 × 1 structuring element (current implementation).

The VD was calculated as the proportion of segmented vessel pixels within the ROI and expressed as a percentage: VD(%)=(Nvessel/Ntotal)×100where Nvessel is the number of nonzero pixels in the final vessel mask and Ntotal is the total number of pixels in the ROI. For each image, the software returned the VD value together with an overlay of the vessel mask for visual verification.

For each analyzed image, the app computed the VD (%) and generated a binary vessel mask, displaying the resized ROI side by side with the corresponding binary vessel map for visual quality control (Fig. 1).

Because the processing pipeline is fully deterministic (with fixed parameters and no stochastic components), repeated analyses of the same input (an identical ROI image) yield identical VD values; accordingly, a single run per image was sufficient and no averaging across repeated runs was required.

ROI Reproducibility Assessment

To quantify variability introduced by manual ROI delineation, we evaluated reference-guided intra- and interoperator ROI redelineation on a representative subset of conjunctival images. ROI selection was performed within the iOS application using the polygon-based selection tool.

For the reference-guided intraoperator assessment, the same operator retraced the conjunctival ROI on the same images after a 3-day interval. During the second session, the previously defined ROI remained visible as a visual reference, simulating a follow-up scenario in which a clinician aims to reassess the same conjunctival region over time. For the reference-guided interoperator assessment, a second operator independently delineated the ROI while likewise using the original ROI as a visual reference.

For each delineation, the polygon ROI was exported and converted into a binary ROI mask (ROI = white; background = black) for quantitative analysis. To ensure pixel-wise comparability, ROI masks were generated at the same fixed resolution of 2100 × 1800 pixels (width × height), ensuring identical pixel sampling across paired masks.

To evaluate the impact of reference-guided ROI redelineation on the quantitative output of the application, the VD was recalculated for each delineation using the same automated analysis pipeline. The mean VD values obtained from the original ROI, the reference-guided intraoperator redelineation, and the reference-guided interoperator redelineation were subsequently compared.

Vessel Segmentation Performance Assessment

To evaluate the pixel-wise performance of the vessel segmentation algorithm implemented in Conjunctiva VD, application-generated vessel masks were compared with manually generated reference vessel masks on a representative subset of conjunctival ROIs.

The manual reference vessel masks were generated by an experienced ophthalmic image annotator who was blinded to the dataset source, image labels, and all application-derived outputs. Visible conjunctival vessel areas were manually delineated on each ROI using Fiji/ImageJ, generating binary reference masks (vessels = white; background = black). The application-generated vessel masks were exported from the app for the corresponding ROIs using the same binary representation (vessels = white; background = black). Thus, both the manual and the application-generated masks represented the same segmentation target (vessel area). To ensure pixel-wise comparability, both the manual and the application-derived masks were generated at the same fixed resolution of 2100 × 1800 pixels (width × height), consistent with the application preprocessing pipeline, thereby ensuring identical pixel sampling across the paired masks (Fig. 2).

Example of the vessel-segmentation evaluation workflow. (A) Bulbar conjunctival ROI. (B) Expert manual vessel tracing on the ROI. (C) Application output: binary vessel segmentation mask. (D) Expert annotator binary vessel mask.

Binary segmentation performance was evaluated on a pixel-wise basis considering vessel pixels as the positive class. For each image, true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives were computed, from which the Dice coefficient, intersection over union (IoU), precision, sensitivity (recall), and specificity were calculated. Metrics were computed independently for each image and summarized across the dataset (Supplementary Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 28; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables (the application-derived VD and the subjective VD) were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and, where appropriate interquartile range (IQR).

Because the Efron scale is an ordinal variable (0–4), the intergrader agreement for the Efron grading was assessed using a quadratic weighted κ. The inter-rater reliability for subjective VD estimates (continuous %) was quantified using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Associations between application-derived VDs and expert assessments were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ) with two-sided significance testing. Spearman correlation was used to quantify the association between the application-derived VDs and (i) each grader's Efron score and (ii) each grader's subjective VD estimate. Bootstrap 95% CIs were reported for correlation coefficients.

Bias and calibration were assessed only for the continuous VD measures by comparing the application-derived VDs with the mean subjective VDs across graders using Bland–Altman analysis and linear regression (calibration). Dataset-stratified analyses (dataset 1 vs. dataset 2) were performed to evaluate the consistency of associations across sources.

The relationships between the application-derived VDs the and expert assessments were visualized using scatter plots (Fig. 2), including an identity line (y = x) for comparisons with the subjective VD. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Vessel segmentation quality and ROI selection reproducibility were evaluated using standard pixel-wise metrics (Dice coefficient, IoU, precision, and recall, as applicable). The ROI and segmentation agreement analyses were implemented in Python using OpenCV and NumPy; summary statistics for these metrics were reported as medians and IQRs.

The application-derived VDs showed a mean ± standard deviation of 6.97 ± 5.08% (IQR, 3.00%–9.25%). The inter-rater agreement for the ordinal Efron scale was high, with a quadratic weighted κ of 0.885 (grader 1 vs. grader 2), 0.871 (grader 1 vs. grader 3), and 0.854 (grader 2 vs. grader 3). The inter-rater reliability was also high for subjective VD estimates (ICC = 0.910; 95% CI, 0.877–0.935).

Reference-Guided ROI Redelineation

The pixel-wise agreement of reference-guided ROI redelineation was evaluated on a representative subset of 20 conjunctival images (60 ROI masks), comparing the repeated delineations by operator 1 (session 1 vs. session 2) and independent delineations by operator 2, both performed using the original ROI as a visual reference. The intraoperator ROI overlap was high, with a median with Dice coefficient of 0.945 (IQR, 0.931–0.954) and a median IoU of 0.896 (IQR, 0.870–0.911). The interoperator ROI overlap remained high, with a median Dice coefficient of 0.946 (IQR, 0.920–0.953) and a median IoU of 0.898 (IQR, 0.852–0.909), demonstrating a high consistency of the reference-guided ROI redelineation under the evaluated conditions.

The effect of the reference-guided ROI redelineation on the resulting VD measurements was minimal. The mean VD was 8.42% for the original ROI, 8.29% following the reference-guided intraoperator redelineation, and 8.63% following the reference-guided interoperator redelineation.

Vessel Segmentation Performance

The pixel-wise agreement between the application-generated vessel masks and manually generated reference vessel masks was evaluated on a representative subset of 20 conjunctival ROIs. The application demonstrated moderate agreement with the manual reference, achieving a median Dice coefficient of 0.464 (IQR, 0.382–0.498) and a median IoU of 0.302 (IQR, 0.236–0.332). The median precision was 0.820 (IQR, 0.705–0.852), and the median sensitivity (recall) was 0.329 (IQR, 0.254–0.358).

Comparison With Efron Grading

The VD values generated by the proposed application (application-derived VDs) were computed for all 100 conjunctival ROIs and compared with the Efron hyperemia grades assigned by each of the three expert ophthalmologists. The application-derived VDs showed consistently strong associations with the Efron grades across all three raters. Specifically, the application-derived VDs showed strong monotonic associations with the Efron grades for grader 1 (Spearman's ρ = 0.797; 95% CI, 0.699–0.858; P < 0.001), grader 2 (ρ = 0.807; 95% CI, 0.723–0.863; P < 0.001), and grader 3 (ρ = 0.780; 95% CI, 0.689–0.843; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Comparison With the Subjective VDs

The VD values generated by the proposed application (application-derived VDs) were computed for all 100 conjunctival ROIs and compared with the subjective VD estimates provided independently by each of the three expert ophthalmologists. Strong monotonic associations were observed for grader 1 (Spearman's ρ = 0.818; 95% CI, 0.725–0.875; P < 0.001), grader 2 (ρ = 0.843; 95% CI, 0.764–0.893; P < 0.001), and grader 3 (ρ = 0.814; 95% CI, 0.733–0.866; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

To assess bias and calibration, the application-derived VDs were compared with the mean expert-estimated VDs across the three graders. A Bland–Altman analysis (difference: application-derived VD − subjective VD) showed a mean difference of +0.33% with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −4.57% to +5.23%, indicating a small average positive bias. For calibration, the subjective VD was modeled as a function of the application-derived VD using linear regression (subjective VD = α + β· application-derived VD), yielding a β of 0.841 and an α of 0.775, suggesting an approximately linear relationship that deviates from perfect identity.

Dataset-Stratified Analyses

When the analyses were stratified by dataset/source (dataset 1, n = 50; dataset 2, n = 50), the app-derived VDs remained strongly associated with the expert assessments in both datasets. For Efron grading, the application-derived VDs showed strong monotonic associations across graders in dataset 1 (grader 1, Spearman's ρ = 0.801; 95% CI, 0.642–0.881; grader 2, ρ = 0.885; 95% CI, 0.818–0.927; grader 3, ρ = 0.845; 95% CI, 0.745–0.904; all P < 0.001) and in dataset 2 (grader 1, ρ = 0.788; 95% CI, 0.641–0.876; grader 2, ρ = 0.729; 95% CI, 0.554–0.830; grader 3, ρ = 0.776; 95% CI, 0.617–0.879; all P < 0.001).

Similarly, the application-derived VDs were strongly associated with the expert-estimated VDs in both datasets (dataset 1, ρ = 0.848 [95% CI, 0.733–0.908], 0.889 [95% CI, 0.809–0.930], and 0.880 [95% CI, 0.781–0.932] for graders 1–3, respectively; dataset 2, ρ = 0.783 [95% CI, 0.619–0.870], 0.789 [95% CI, 0.640–0.879], and 0.782 [95% CI, 0.629–0.863]; all P < 0.001).

The intergrader agreement for the ordinal Efron scale remained high within each dataset based on quadratic weighted κ (dataset 1, κw = 0.884 [grader 1 vs. 2], 0.871 [grader 1 vs. 3], 0.876 [grader 2 vs. 3]; dataset 2, κw = 0.884 [grader 1 vs. 2], 0.868 [grader 1 vs. 3], 0.822 [grader 2 vs. 3]). The inter-rater reliability for the subjective VD estimates was also high in both datasets (dataset 1 ICC = 0.911; dataset 2 ICC = 0.907).

Discussion

In this study, we tested an iOS smartphone application (Conjunctiva VD) designed to objectively quantify conjunctival hyperemia by estimating conjunctival VDs within a user-defined conjunctival ROI. Across 100 images selected from two public datasets, the application-derived VDs showed consistently strong monotonic associations with the expert assessments, including Efron hyperemia grading (Spearman's ρ = 0.780–0.807) and grader-estimated VDs (ρ = 0.814–0.843; all P < 0.001), with a consistent performance across both datasets. The inter-rater agreement among the three ophthalmologists was high for Efron grading, with quadratic weighted κ values ranging from 0.854 to 0.885, and the inter-rater reliability was also high for subjective VD estimations (ICC = 0.910), supporting the consistency of the clinical reference against which the app was evaluated.

The vessel segmentation analyses demonstrated moderate-to-good agreement between the app-derived vessel masks and the manual annotations. This result should be interpreted in light of both the intrinsic difficulty of manually annotating conjunctival vessels and the intended purpose of the algorithm. The conjunctival VD was designed primarily to generate an aggregate VD metric rather than to provide an anatomically exhaustive reconstruction of every individual vessel. Therefore, even when the app-derived mask does not perfectly match the full manual vessel tracing, it may still capture the global vascular burden within the ROI by detecting a representative proportion of the overall vascular pattern. This issue is particularly relevant in cases of marked conjunctival hyperemia, where vessels may be densely crowded, overlapping, located at different apparent depths, and frequently intersecting, making precise manual annotation extremely challenging. In such settings, the ability to quantify the overall vascular burden of the region may be more clinically relevant than perfect vessel-to-vessel correspondence. This may explain why the app-derived VDs remained strongly associated with expert clinical grading despite imperfect pixel-level correspondence between the vessel masks.

A key motivation for this work was that, despite the growing evidence supporting objective hyperemia quantification, practical deployment in routine settings remains limited.34 Many published approaches rely on nontrivial software workflows, dedicated imaging setups, or device-specific pipelines that can hinder adoption at the point of care.25–28 By implementing the analysis directly on a smartphone, our aim was to reduce these barriers and provide a portable tool that can be used quickly in outpatient settings and potentially extended to remote monitoring scenarios.

In line with the literature, our findings confirm that vessel-based quantitative metrics provide a clinically meaningful and continuous surrogate of conjunctival hyperemia. Wong et al.22 reported VD correlations with a mean Efron grading of 0.83 in an internal test set and 0.80 in an external dataset, comparable with inter-rater agreement. Yoneda et al.23 reported excellent short-interval repeatability for percent vessel coverage (r = 0.998) and a strong association with the averaged clinical grading (r = 0.953). Deep-learning approaches have also shown clinically meaningful performances, including an ensemble model whose output correlated with vessel-area occupancy (ρ = 0.737) and an automated segmentation/feature pipeline reporting a Spearman's ρ of 0.701 against the reference Efron grades.18,33 Overall, these reports support the validity of vessel-based objective metrics for hyperemia assessment. Consistent with this evidence, our smartphone application achieved effect sizes that are comparable with those reported in prior segmentation- and learning-based pipelines. Specifically, the associations observed in our study between the app-derived VDs and the Efron grading (ρ = 0.780–0.807) were similar to those reported by Wong et al.22 (0.80–0.83) and higher than those reported by Curti et al.18 (ρ = 0.701) and Masumoto et al.33 (ρ = 0.737), but lower than the correlation reported by Yoneda et al.23 between the percent vessel coverage and the averaged clinical grading (r = 0.953).

However, the present study differs from these reports in both acquisition setting and intended use. Most previous studies evaluating vessel-based hyperemia metrics relied on slit-lamp photographs, dedicated imaging setups, desktop-based processing, and/or more complex algorithmic pipelines, including deep-learning–based methods trained on annotated datasets. These approaches provide important evidence that VD and vessel-coverage metrics are clinically meaningful, but they may be less easily deployable in routine outpatient settings, decentralized care, or resource-limited environments. By contrast, Conjunctiva VD was intentionally designed as a lightweight and portable workflow, capable of running directly on a smartphone and providing an immediate numerical VD value together with a visual quality-control output. Another distinctive feature is operator-controlled ROI selection: because conjunctival vascularization is spatially heterogeneous and conjunctival exposure may vary across acquisitions, allowing the examiner to select the same conjunctival sector may facilitate more consistent repeated assessments under reference-guided conditions. The high consistency of the reference-guided ROI redelineation observed in this study, together with the negligible effect on the resulting VD measurements, supports the feasibility of this strategy for the repeated assessment of the same conjunctival sector. Finally, our algorithm was evaluated on a heterogeneous set of public images, including images obtained under variable acquisition conditions and, in many cases, with commercial cameras. This represents a more challenging dataset than those used in many controlled imaging studies, because illumination, focus, magnification, compression, and vessel visibility are less standardized. However, we believe that this heterogeneity also better reflects the type of variability that a pragmatic portable tool may encounter outside highly controlled imaging environments. From this perspective, the strong associations observed between the app-derived VDs and the expert clinical assessments suggest that a simple on-device VD approach can still capture clinically relevant hyperemia information, even under nonideal image-acquisition conditions. The main novelty of our approach is, therefore, not the concept of VD itself, but its operationalization as a simple, interpretable, and potentially widely deployable workflow. Nevertheless, the present study should be considered an initial evaluation rather than a definitive clinical validation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the ROIs were manually delineated; although ROI definition was performed by an external imaging technician masked to the dataset origin, labels, and study objectives, a residual risk of selection bias cannot be fully excluded. However, we intentionally retained clinician-controlled ROI selection because it reflects a pragmatic clinical workflow: conjunctival exposure is not always identical across acquisitions, and local factors (e.g., variable lid position, shadows, reflections, or concurrent ocular surface findings) may obscure portions of the conjunctiva, making a fully standardized whole-field analysis impractical in some cases. Reassuringly, reference-guided ROI redelineation showed excellent consistency in selecting the same conjunctival area both with the same operator over time and between different operators. However, because the original ROI remained visible during repeat delineation, these findings should be interpreted as reference-guided consistencies rather than independent intra- or interoperator reproducibility. Future longitudinal studies should evaluate independent ROI selection across separate imaging sessions. Future work should aim to complement (rather than replace) manual selection by incorporating automated ROI detection and objective quality-control metrics (e.g., glare and blur detection), while preserving the option for clinician-directed regional assessment.

Second, the vessel segmentation agreement with manual the annotations was moderate to good. We believe that this result primarily reflects differences in annotation style: the expert annotator tended to trace the most prominent vessels, and smaller, low-contrast, or blurred microvessels were often not labeled. This divergence is accentuated in markedly hyperemic images, where vessel crowding and diffuse redness make the consistent manual delineation of fine vasculature challenging. Importantly, despite imperfect pixel-level correspondence between vessel masks, Conjunctiva VD remained strongly associated with expert clinical grading, supporting the clinical relevance of the derived VD metric. Future work will focus on improving vessel-mask specificity and robustness to artifacts (e.g., reflections and residual boundary responses) and on validating segmentation performance under standardized acquisition conditions and across a wider range of devices.

Third, although subjective VD estimation was conceptually grounded in previous quantitative work,31–33 it remains a nonstandard, expert-derived measure rather than a validated clinical grading scale. Future studies may determine whether human percentage-based estimation of conjunctival VD represents a clinically valid and reproducible measure of conjunctival hyperemia.

Fourth, one dataset included images collected from heterogeneous online sources with variable acquisition conditions and incomplete metadata; despite resolution standardization, variability in illumination, pigmentation, optical magnification, spatial resolution, blur, compression artifacts, and vessel visibility may still influence the vessel segmentation and the VD estimates. The present datasets did not include calibration targets or standardized magnification; therefore, we could not determine the true lateral resolution of the images or directly assess small vessel-diameter changes. The app-derived VD values should be interpreted as apparent image-based vessel-occupancy metrics rather than as direct measurements of absolute vessel diameter or micrometer-scale changes in vascular caliber. Importantly, standardized high-resolution images would be expected to benefit a vessel-based algorithm by improving vessel visibility and reducing sampling noise; accordingly, we anticipate equal or potentially improved performance when applying the method to images acquired under standardized clinical conditions using a slit-lamp biomicroscope coupled with a high-resolution camera or images acquired directly with smartphone cameras under real-world conditions.

Fifth, to maximize consistency during development, the application was implemented and tested on iOS (iPhone 17); although the underlying image-processing approach is platform agnostic, future studies will evaluate performance, usability, and runtime across a broader range of smartphones, camera modules, and operating systems. Finally, we tested against Efron grading and expert-estimated VDs; broader generalizability across other hyperemia scales and standardized acquisition protocols should be explored in future prospective studies.

Conclusions

We present and test a smartphone application for objective conjunctival hyperemia quantification based on conjunctival VDs. By demonstrating a strong association between the application-derived VDs and expert clinical assessments in a selected set of publicly available conjunctival images, this portable, lightweight workflow represents a promising approach that may facilitate the wider adoption of quantitative hyperemia assessment at the point of care. If these measurement properties are confirmed prospectively, Conjunctiva VD could be used as a rapid adjunct to slit-lamp examination to generate a continuous, objective VD value from a clinician-selected conjunctival sector, supporting baseline documentation and repeated assessments. This app could also improve standardization in multicenter settings by providing a numeric endpoint for monitoring ocular surface inflammation and treatment tolerability. In the longer term, once validated on images captured directly with smartphone cameras under standardized acquisition guidance, the same workflow could be extended to remote follow-up and patient-submitted images, with clinician review focused on quantitative changes over time. Importantly, the authors plan to release the application publicly, with the goal of enabling the broader dissemination of objective hyperemia assessment beyond specialized centers.
