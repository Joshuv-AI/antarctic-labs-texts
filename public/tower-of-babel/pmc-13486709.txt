Investigation of vorticity and secondary flow strength near the protected bridge pier in a sharp bend
Authors: Mohammad Moghanloo, Mohammad Vaghefi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One notable feature of the flow dynamics along bend-shaped pathways is the presence of secondary flows, which introduce a degree of complexity to the flow behavior in this region. This study aimed to investigate the flow characteristics within a bend-shaped channel that features a central angle of 180º, incorporating an oblong pier with a collar. To collect velocity data within an equilibrium bed, a three-dimensional Vectrino velocimeter was employed. The findings indicated that the peak tangential velocity was observed in proximity to the convex bank and at the water surface level, suggesting an escalation in the pressure gradient in that area. During the examination of the collar with a thickness of 12 mm, the highest vorticity was observed in the counterclockwise direction at the 84º section of the bend. Furthermore, the peak strength of the secondary flow was observed during the examination of the collar with a thickness of 3 mm at the 70º section. It is noteworthy that increasing collar thickness from 0.06b to 0.24b reduced the peak secondary flow strength by approximately 30%, indicating a significant weakening of curvature and pier-induced secondary circulation. In both experiments, the secondary flow strength experienced a decline between 70 to 80º, followed by an increase thereafter. These findings provide practical guidance for optimizing collar design to mitigate flow-induced risks around bridge piers in sharp bends.

Introduction

The majority of rivers in their natural state exhibit a meandering course, with only a limited number demonstrating a completely linear trajectory.This meandering geometry creates complex flow dynamics that include both a primary longitudinal component and secondary flow components. The secondary flows strongly influence sediment transport, river morphology, water quality, velocity distribution, momentum, and boundary shear stress1–3. In bends, the centrifugal force raises the water surface on the outer bank and lowers it on the inner bank, producing a velocity pattern that differs markedly from that of straight channels.

The presence of a bridge pier adds further complexity, leading to scour around the pier and eventual structural degradation.

Over the past several decades, extensive research has been conducted on the flow patterns within bend-shaped channels. Notably, Shukry4 conducted a thorough investigation into the flow behavior surrounding bends. The findings of this research indicated the presence of helical flow patterns in straight paths as well as meandering rivers. Bathurst et al.5 examined the patterns of secondary flows and the distribution of boundary shear stress within a river bend in a field study. It was noted that, in addition to the primary eddies, smaller-radius eddies exhibiting an opposing directional flow were also present in proximity to the outer bank. Lien et al.6 investigated and simulated the flow patterns observed in bend-shaped channels using a two-dimensional depth-averaged model. In this model, an analysis of the dispersion stresses revealed that these stresses significantly influenced the lateral displacement of momentum from the convex to the concave bank in both gentle and sharp bend conditions. In the research conducted by Moghanloo et al.7 examined the parameters of turbulent kinetic energy, Reynolds stress, and streamlines along the 180° sharp bend of a channel. The findings revealed that an increase in collar thickness correlated with a heightened inclination of the streamlines to the convex bank upstream of the pier. Wei et al.8 investigated the development of self-aeration and air diffusion in high-velocity open-channel flows, with emphasis on the evolution of turbulent flow structures and energy redistribution under supercritical conditions. Their results provided detailed insight into the spatial complexity of highly turbulent velocity fields. Dehghan et al.9 examined how collar width affects flow around an oblong pier with a round nose in a 180° sharp bend. The collar produced upstream return vortices that significantly reduced the downstream-current strength at the pier’s upstream face. Doubling the collar width increased upstream vorticity and the magnitude of secondary flows upstream of the pier by approximately 0.93 and 0.68 times, respectively. Chavan et al.10experimentally examined turbulence behavior around a circular bridge pier in the non-uniform sand bed channel under seepage and non-seepage conditions. A key finding of their study was that the highest values of streamwise velocity and Reynolds shear stress were concentrated near the pier boundary in the upstream region and close to the channel bed downstream of the pier. Hu and Yu11, explored the effects of Reynolds number on secondary flows in a channel featuring a 180º sharp bend, with a ratio of R/B = 3 and a width of 0.4 m. The findings indicated that at the 90º position of the sharp bend, in comparison to the gentle bend, three distinct rotating flows with varying centers were generated near the convex bank, concave bank, and the channel bed. An increase in the Reynolds number from 23,000 to 37,000 resulted in a 50% reduction in the strength and size of the secondary flows near the outer bank; conversely, these parameters exhibited a 20% increase near the inner bank. Vaghefi et al.12 examined how factors including the quantity, length, and installation angle of submerged vanes affected local scouring around a bridge pier situated in a 180º open channel bend. The results of their tests demonstrated that the most significant reduction in scouring was achieved with three submerged vanes positioned at a 15º angle relative to the centerline of the bend, having a length that is 1.5 times greater than the pier diameter.

In recent years, researchers such as Moghanloo et al.13 investigated how collar level and thickness influence scouring around a circular pier in a laboratory flume with a 180° sharp bend. The study identified that the optimal collar installation height for reducing scour was 0.2 times the pier width beneath the initial bed level when a 3-mm thick collar was used. Keshavarz et al.14conducted laboratory experiments in a 180° sharp bend and showed that the use of collars around bridge piers can effectively reduce local scour, with the degree of reduction strongly dependent on pier shape and position within the bend. Baranwal and Das15, conducted a comprehensive review of bridge-pier scouring, compiling over 80 experimental and field datasets and evaluating more than 60 empirical equations developed across the past eight decades for both clear-water and live-bed conditions. Their analysis emphasized the critical influence of hydraulic and bed-roughness parameters on equilibrium scour depth and identified the most reliable predictive equations for different scouring regime. Shahhosseini et al.16 combined three-dimensional numerical simulations using FLOW-3D with laboratory experiments to investigate the hydrodynamic role of collars in mitigating local scour around piles under uniform flow conditions. Their results showed that reducing overall scour dimensions by about 14%, while decreasing scour-pit width by about 21% upstream and 40% along the pile sides, primarily through weakening near-bed pressure gradients and erosive forces. Sha et al.17 provided an integrated synthesis of fluid–sediment and foundation–sediment interaction mechanisms governing local scour, offering a unified perspective on how hydrodynamic processes and sediment response jointly control scour development. One of the key contributions of their study is the systematic evaluation of existing scour countermeasures in terms of their interaction with flow structures and sediment dynamics, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and applicability ranges. Pasha et al.18 reviewed collar-based countermeasures for mitigating local scour around bridge piers, focusing on collar geometry, installation level, and configuration. Their synthesis of experimental and numerical studies showed that optimized collars weaken horseshoe vortices and reduce scour depth, while also highlighting practical challenges such as installation accuracy, debris accumulation, and long-term performance under complex hydraulic conditions. Ali et al.19 experimentally investigated the temporal evolution of clear-water scour around a circular pier equipped with collars installed at different angular positions. Their results showed that increasing the collar diameter to 3–3.5 times the pier diameter substantially enhanced scour mitigation, reducing upstream scour depth by up to 85% compared to the unprotected case. Kumari et al.20 performed an advanced numerical study on the flow field and morphological evolution around tandem bridge piers. The findings showed that the interaction between the wake generated by the upstream pier and the downstream pier substantially alters the local flow structure, leading to a marked decrease in scour development depending on the pier arrangement. Previous experimental studies have demonstrated that collar geometry can significantly influence the flow field around bridge piers in curved channels. For example, Moghanloo et al.7 examined the effect of collar thickness on general flow patterns, streamline behavior, and turbulence-related parameters around an oblong pier in a 180° sharp bend, while Dehghan et al.9 investigated the influence of collar width ratio on velocity distribution and secondary flow structures in a bend. Although these studies provided valuable insights into how collar dimensions modify the overall flow behavior, they primarily focused on flow patterns or turbulence metrics. However, a quantitative, three-dimensional characterization of vorticity components and secondary flow strength, and their spatial evolution in response to increasing collar thickness in sharp bends, remains limited. Addressing this gap is essential for a more mechanistic understanding of how collar thickness alters curvature- and pier-induced secondary circulation and near-bed flow dynamics in strongly curved channels.

Upon reviewing the pertinent research and recognizing the significance of this matter, it is evident that a comprehensive understanding of flow characteristics is crucial. This knowledge can significantly assist the engineering team in devising designs and solutions to mitigate the scouring phenomenon, thereby enhancing the rivers morphology. Consequently, this study employed a laboratory flume with a 180º sharp bend, incorporating an oblong bridge pier constructed from PVC, positioned at the 90º section of the bend, to investigate the 3D flow components, vorticity and the strength of the secondary flows. Additionally, an investigation was conducted to assess the impact of increasing the collar thickness with a width four times that of the plexiglass pier on the aforementioned parameters. In this case, the collar was positioned 2 cm above the original bed level. In the present study, two important flow parameters in a 180-degree sharp bend with an oblong pier integrated with a collar have been investigated. The present study focuses on examining the influence of collar thickness on flow characteristics near an oblong pier in a sharp bend with an equilibrium bed.

Materials and methods

The investigation of fluid dynamics in natural environments presents substantial challenges due to the multitude of variables and the presence of uncontrollable conditions. Consequently, the experiments were conducted in the advanced hydraulic laboratory at Persian Gulf University, located in Bushehr, with a focus on principal and quantifiable parameters. The experiments were conducted in a rectangular open-channel flume with a width of B = 1.0 m. The flume consisted of a 6.5 m straight upstream reach, followed by a 180° sharp bend with a centerline radius corresponding to R/B = 2, which aligns with the classification of sharp bends as defined by Leschziner and Rodi21 and a 5.1 m straight downstream pathway. The bridge pier was installed at the midpoint of the channel width and located at the bend apex (θ = 90º). A schematic representation of the channel is provided in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Laboratory channel featuring a 180º sharp bend.

Laboratory channel featuring a 180º sharp bend.

An oblong pier with a length of 0.20 m and a width of 0.05 m was used as bridge pier. The pier was protected by a collar with dimensions of 0.35 m × 0.20 m and two thickness specifications of 3 mm and 12 mm. The collar was mounted horizontally around the pier at an elevation of 0.4 times the pier width above the original bed level. As illustrated in Fig. 2, an overview of the pier equipped with the collar and the associated flow separation around the pier is presented. In Fig. 2b, the different flow regions formed around the pier are illustrated. At the upper flow levels and in the upstream region, an up-flow predominates, leading to the formation of a return zone. A flow separation zone was observed around the pier, which, as illustrated in the figure, exhibits a skewness toward the inner bank. Also, downstream of the bridge pier and near the pier, surface vortices are observed. The oblong (rounded rectangular) pier geometry was selected because it is commonly used in engineering practice and is known to generate asymmetric flow separation and complex secondary circulation in curved channels. The selection of collar thicknesses and installation level was based on prior experimental evidence. In particular, Moghanloo et al.7 showed that collar thickness significantly affects flow–scour interaction, and that the hydraulic response differs markedly between minimum and maximum collar thicknesses. Based on these findings, the present study deliberately considers the lowest and highest collar thicknesses to capture the full range of hydrodynamic response in terms of secondary flow and vorticity. The collar installation level was set 2 cm above the original bed level to ensure early interaction between the incoming flow and the collar and to enable reliable velocity measurements in the vicinity of, and beneath, the collar during the flow-pattern experiments.

Flow conditions were calibrated to achieve a rate of 0.07 cubic meters per second, employing an ultrasonic flow meter accurate to within ± 1 mm/s, an approximately 18 cm level in the upstream trajectory, as per the research findings of Oliveto and Hager22. The flow parameters across both experimental setups were observed to be under near-threshold clear-water conditions, with the mean velocity maintained (U = Q/Bh) at approximately 0.98 of the critical velocity (U/Uc ≈ 0.98). The corresponding Froude number was therefore Fr ≈ 0.30, indicating near-critical subcritical flow conditions. Based on the flow depth and mean velocity, the flow regime was fully turbulent. The experiments consisted of two consecutive stages. In the first stage, a movable-bed experiment was conducted to allow the development of an equilibrium scour bed around the pier. To determine the relative equilibrium time for conducting the experiments, a 34-hour equilibrium time test was performed using an oblong pier installed at the 90° position of the bend, without a collar and without altering the bed level. During the equilibrium time test, the temporal evolution of the maximum scour depth was continuously monitored. Measurements were continued until no noticeable change in the maximum scour depth was observed over three consecutive 4-hour intervals. Equilibrium time tests for this flow–pier configuration have previously been conducted and reported by Vaghefi et al.23, indicating that approximately 95% of the maximum scour depth develops during the initial stage of the experiment. Accordingly, a duration of 15 h was selected as the relative equilibrium time for all experiments.

After completing the scour experiment for each test and recording the bed topography during this equilibrium time, the flume was drained and the bed morphology was fixed using concrete adhesive and fiberglass. In the second stage, flow-pattern measurements were performed over the fixed equilibrium bed to prevent sediment motion during Acoustic Doppler Velocimeter (ADV) measurements and to isolate the hydrodynamic characteristics of the flow. Velocity data acquisition was carried out over approximately 20 working days.

The sediment material used as the movable bed was characterized through a sieve analysis prior to the experiments. Figure 3 presents the grain-size distribution curve of the sediment. The median grain size was d50 = 0.0015 m with a geometric standard deviation of σg = 1.14, indicating a nearly uniform sediment. Grain-size distribution of the bed material used in the experiments.

Velocity measurements were obtained using an ADV operating at a sampling frequency of 25 Hz for a duration of 60 s at each measurement point. Figure 4 shows two probes utilized in this experiment. To ensure data quality, the raw ADV signals were post-processed following standard procedures. Velocity samples were screened based on a minimum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 15 dB and a correlation coefficient threshold of 70%. Data points not meeting these criteria were discarded. A phase-space despiking algorithm was applied to remove spurious spikes from the remaining time series. Short gaps resulting from data rejection were filled using linear interpolation, while records with excessive data loss were excluded from further analysis. The device captures 3000 data points at each location and in various directions; the average of these readings was designated as the flow velocity, utilizing third-party Vecterino and Explorer V software programs. These procedures are consistent with established best practices for ADV data processing and have been widely adopted in recent experimental studies. Measurement uncertainty and experimental repeatability were considered when interpreting the results. The accuracy of the ADV measurements, together with the applied signal quality filtering criteria (SNR and correlation thresholds), indicates that the observed differences between the two test cases exceed the expected measurement uncertainty. In addition, repeated measurements at selected locations showed consistent velocity statistics, confirming the repeatability and robustness of the reported trends. The authors also acknowledge that the experiments are repeatable, and repeated scour tests resulted in nearly identical bed topography.

The collection of three-dimensional flow velocity data was carried out using a non-uniform measurement grid along the bend. The in-plane grid resolution was locally refined in the vicinity of the pier to accurately capture the complex flow structures and strong velocity gradients in this region. Velocity measurements were conducted at five elevations above the initial bed level and at least two elevations below it within the scour holes. The positive elevations were located at 1, 4, 8, 12, and 17 cm above the initial bed, while the negative elevations were measured about 2 and 4 cm under the initial bed level to adequately resolve the velocity field inside the scour holes. The initial bed level, defined as the zero-reference level, corresponds to the bed condition prior to the onset of the scour experiments. Result and discussion

The hydrodynamic behavior of bend-shaped rivers has a sophisticated flow structure, despite the installation of a protected bridge pier within the flow pathway. This study demonstrated that the placement of an oblong bridge pier, fortified with a collar and situated at the 90º position of the bend which, due to the narrow cross-sectional area of the flow led to an increase in flow velocity in this region. This increased velocity subsequently elevated the potential for sediment transport downstream. Moreover, as indicated in the research conducted by Moghanloo et al.7, analysis of streamlines within the transverse sections revealed the formation of several rotational flow cells in this area. Such phenomena created adverse conditions for the pier situated within the open channel bend. It is therefore imperative to grasp the characteristics of the flow—specifically, the flow velocity in various directions, vorticity, the strength of secondary flows, and the deviation of streamlines relative to the horizontal axis, which were addressed in this study. Gaining insight into these factors will significantly contribute to preventing scour around piers and enhancing the morphological features of the river.

The secondary flow represents a critical element in creating bed scouring. The interaction between the secondary flows and longitudinal flow forms a helical flow structure that facilitates sediment transport by disrupting the direct flow of water and altering its directional movement across the channel width. To assess the strength of the secondary flow, various factors must be considered, including vorticity, which will be the subject of further investigation. Vorticity, or velocity curl, refers to the rotation of a fluid element within the water. The methodology for calculating the vorticity of each fluid element within the velocity grid (as depicted in Fig. 9) was derived from Eq. 124. In this relation, ωx is the value of rotation about the x-axis. Also, the v and w axes of the velocity components are in the direction of the y and z axes, respectively. Based on this definition, vorticity becomes zero in straight channels and in flows without rotational motion. Physically, vorticity represents the local rotational behavior of a fluid element and corresponds to the curl of the velocity field in the transverse plane. Figure 10a and b depict the variations in vorticity and the strength of the secondary flow throughout the entire bend, respectively. As indicated in Fig. 10a, there was a noted increase in vorticity at the beginning of the bend across both tests. It is observed that at the 60º section of the bend, the vorticity values from both experiments converge, showing similar magnitudes; however, this value decreases as one moves toward the place of the protected pier. As the flows approached the pier location, a downward trend in vorticity was observed in test TC = 0.06b, with the opposite occurring when the collar thickness was increased. In test TC = 0.24b, the maximum vorticity was noted in the counterclockwise direction at the 84º section of the bend. The accompanying figure illustrates that the vorticity decreased in tandem with a 10% variance in both tests downstream of the pier. Analyzing the vorticity profile associated with test TC = 0.24b indicated a notable increase in transverse vortices along the latter half of the bend, attributable to the augmented collar thickness. Figure 10b depicts the strength of the secondary flow in both tests. The figure displays that the variations in secondary flow strength within the first half of the bend exhibited an up-flow trend. The highest strength of the secondary flow observed during the testing of 3 mm thickness of the collar (TC = 0.06b) was recorded at the 70º section. This parameter experienced a reduction of approximately 30% as the thickness of the collar increased. The reduction in secondary-flow strength and vorticity with increasing collar thickness has clear implications for scour and sedimentation processes. Secondary circulation and pier-induced down-flow control near-bed shear stress and sediment mobilization around the pier. A reduction of about 30% in secondary-flow strength for the thicker collar indicates weaker cross-sectional circulation and reduced down-flow intensity, which directly limits the flow ability to initiate and sustain scour. In both experimental tests, there was a noted decrease in the strength of the secondary flow within the angular range of 70 to 80º, after which an increase was observed. Furthermore, in the latter half of the bend, there was a significant decline in the strength of the secondary flow. The different trends and peak locations of vorticity and secondary-flow strength arise because these parameters represent distinct physical aspects of the three-dimensional flow. Vorticity quantifies local rotational motion associated with velocity gradients and shear layers, and therefore it tends to peak near regions of intense local shear and vortex formation around the pier. In contrast, secondary-flow strength represents the strength of cross-sectional circulation and is governed by curvature-induced pressure gradients and large-scale momentum redistribution within the bend. As a result, secondary-flow strength reaches its maximum where the secondary circulation cell is most coherent and developed, which does not necessarily coincide with the location of maximum vorticity. Although both parameters originate from the same velocity field, their extrema differ because high local vorticity does not always correspond to maximum energetic secondary circulation. Furthermore, the observed redistribution of radial velocity away from the pier and toward the inner bank suggests a flow field that is less favorable for sediment removal near the pier base and more conducive to sediment deposition. These hydrodynamic changes explain why a thicker collar is expected to be more effective in controlling bed deformation by suppressing the flow structures responsible for scour, even though direct scour measurements were not included in the present study.

Conclusions

This study examined the influence of collar thickness on three-dimensional flow characteristics around a protected oblong bridge pier located in a 180° sharp bend, with all measurements conducted over a stabilized equilibrium bed. The results demonstrated that collar thickness plays a significant role in modifying the secondary flow structure, vorticity distribution and deviations of streamlines at various flow levels. The maximum secondary flow intensity occurred near the 70° section of the bend for the thinner collar (TC = 0.06b), while increasing the collar thickness to TC = 0.24b resulted in an approximate 30% reduction in peak secondary flow strength. The reduction in secondary-flow strength with increasing collar thickness can be attributed to the physical blockage and redistribution of near-bed momentum caused by the collar. Thicker collars extend further into the flow, effectively limiting the tangential and transverse velocity components close to the bed, which are the primary contributors to secondary-flow strength. Downstream of the pier, tangential velocity progressively shifted from the inner toward the outer bank, with both its minimum and maximum values tending toward the free surface. This behavior can be attributed to the downstream recovery of the flow and the weakening of pier-induced vortical structures, leading to a redistribution of momentum toward the outer bank and upper flow layers. Increasing collar thickness enhanced radial velocity toward the convex bank. In contrast, collar thickness had a negligible effect on upstream vertical velocity, and vertical velocities above the collar surface remained minimal. Future studies should build upon the present flow-based analysis by coupling detailed velocity measurements with direct observations of bed deformation, and by exploring intermediate collar thicknesses and alternative collar elevations. Such extensions would enable a more comprehensive assessment of the implications of collar-induced flow modification for scour and sedimentation processes in sharp channel bends.
