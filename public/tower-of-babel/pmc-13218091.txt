Machine learning-based prediction of a high-risk kidney function trajectory class after acute kidney injury
Authors: Chien­-Liang Liu, You-Lin Tain, Chih-­Chien Lin, Yueh-Lung Peng, Chien-Ning Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMonitoring kidney function after acute kidney injury (AKI) hospitalisation is essential for identifying patients at risk of rapid progression. This study developed a machine learning (ML) model to predict membership in a high-risk longitudinal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) trajectory associated with subsequent kidney replacement therapy (KRT) initiation.MethodsWe conducted a cohort study of 88 632 adults with ≥2 post-discharge eGFR measurements between 2010 and 2017. Joint latent class mixed models were used to identify dynamic eGFR trajectories from 665 499 measurements. Patient-level data splitting (80/20) was strictly enforced. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and random forest models were trained using clinical features within 3 months after discharge to predict membership in the highest-risk eGFR trajectory class.ResultsThree eGFR trajectories were identified: Steady Low (high-risk phenotype), Slow Decline and Recovery. The Steady Low group showed the strongest association with KRT. The 10-variable XGBoost model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUROC 0.974, sensitivity 0.913, specificity 0.919). The eGFR slope from discharge to 3 months was the most influential predictor of high-risk progression.DiscussionAlthough the study was limited to a single healthcare system, this ML framework identified patients at risk of rapid kidney function decline using routinely available clinical data. The model supports early risk stratification after AKI and may enable more timely kidney-protective interventions. External validation, calibration assessment and benchmarking against simpler eGFR-based rules remain important next steps.ConclusionA 10-variable XGBoost model accurately identifies patients with deteriorative eGFR trajectories (Steady Low) early after discharge, providing a practical framework for personalised post-AKI kidney care.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is increasing globally1 and is associated with high mortality, kidney replacement therapy (KRT) and cardiovascular complications.2 3 Survivors of AKI remain at elevated risk of long-term kidney function decline, even when short-term recovery appears complete.4 However, reliable approaches to identify patients at highest risk of rapid post-discharge deterioration remain limited.

Since the 22nd Acute Kidney Injury Quality Initiative recommendations in 2018,5 post-AKI care has focused on serial monitoring of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to enable timely intervention and reduce the risks of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and KRT initiation.6 Yet best practices for post-AKI follow-up remain poorly standardised. Prior evidence suggests that 30.5% of patients who survive 90 days after AKI do not recover by 150 days, and these individuals have a 4.13-fold higher risk of kidney disease progression and a 1.26-fold higher risk of death than those who recover.7 These results underscore the importance of characterising longitudinal kidney function trajectories, which may enable actionable strategies such as haemodynamic optimisation, avoidance of nephrotoxins and timely nephrology referral.

Although machine learning (ML) models using routinely collected data often outperform conventional regression in predicting AKI onset,811 most existing approaches rely on static, single time-point kidney function measures, such as baseline or discharge eGFR, and therefore do not capture dynamic recovery trajectories after hospitalisation. Unlike conventional prediction models that target a single endpoint at a fixed time point, trajectory-based prediction aims to identify patients likely to follow an unfavourable longitudinal pattern of kidney function over time. This approach may better capture heterogeneous post-AKI recovery and identify patients whose risk is not fully reflected by a single cross-sectional kidney outcome.12 This study therefore aimed to (1) identify high-risk longitudinal eGFR phenotypes after AKI using latent class mixed models, (2) develop and validate an ML model for early risk prediction at the 3-month post-discharge landmark and (3) provide a transparent risk-scoring framework to support clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study population, data sources, and ethics

Data were obtained from AKI Recovery Evaluation Study (AKIRES), an adult hospitalised cohort drawn from multiple Chang Gung Memorial hospitals (CGMHs) in Taiwan. AKIRES was defined between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2017 using a de-identified electronic health records (EHR) database.9 CGMHs are distributed across Taiwan and accounted for approximately 10% of Taiwan’s National Health Insurance programme health service delivery in 2018.13 The analysis included 88 632 patients who survived 3 months after the index hospitalisation and had at least two eGFR measurements for trajectory modelling. AKI was defined according to KDIGO criteria based on two SCr measurements obtained at different time points (online supplemental Table S1).

Outcome definition for eGFR trajectories

The primary outcome was membership in the worst eGFR trajectory class, defined as a model-derived high-risk phenotype. eGFR trajectories were modelled using Growth Mixture Models (GMM) and Joint Latent Class Mixed Models (JLCMM),14 15 incorporating repeated eGFR measurements collected from 3 months after hospital discharge (the landmark date) until the occurrence of long-term KRT, death, loss to follow-up or the end of the study period (31 December 2017), whichever came first (figure 1 and online supplemental Figure S1). Long-term KRT was defined as the requirement for any dialysis modality for at least 3 months or receipt of a kidney transplant after the 3-month landmark.

Conceptual framework for predicting deteriorative eGFR trajectory.

Both GMM and JLCMM were unsupervised approaches used to identify distinct, mutually exclusive latent classes that represent underlying kidney function phenotypes. This approach enables identification of clinically meaningful but otherwise unrecognised patterns, such as persistently low function vs progressive decliners, to be identified from longitudinal data rather than cross-sectional assessment alone. In addition, the JLCMM jointly models eGFR trajectories with the risk of KRT initiation, allowing robust characterisation of heterogeneous post-AKI recovery patterns while smoothing clinical measurement noise.12

Model selection compared linear and quadratic GMM and JLCMM specifications with three and four latent classes. The optimal model was selected on the basis of statistical and clinical criteria, including a lower Bayesian Information Criterion, high mean posterior class membership probabilities (>0.8) and clinical interpretability. Solutions that generated extremely high eGFR trajectories, suggestive of low creatinine states or measurement artefact rather than meaningful phenotype, were considered less clinically interpretable. The final model produced trajectories consistent with real-world patterns of recovery, stability and persistent impairment.

To confirm that identified high-risk phenotype reflected clinically meaningful kidney deterioration, Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the association between trajectory class and KRT risk. A higher HR for KRT initiation among identified eGFR trajectories was interpreted as evidence of worsening kidney function during follow-up.

ML model development and interpretation

The cohort was randomly split in a 4:1 ratio into derivation and validation sets (online supplemental Figure S2). To prevent data leakage, all longitudinal data from a given patient were assigned exclusively to one dataset. Clinical features were extracted from routinely collected EHRs and spanned six domains: demographics, diagnoses, medications, laboratory results, healthcare utilisation and surrogate time-varying features derived within 3 months after hospital discharge (online supplemental Table S2).

A 3-month landmark design was used: predictors were defined using information available up to 3 months after discharge, and follow-up for KRT initiation began after the landmark to ensure that predictors strictly preceded outcomes and to minimise reverse causation (figure 1 and online supplemental Table S2). A total of 55 candidate features were pre-specified. Continuous features with more than 80% missing data were excluded (baseline total cholesterol, glucose, erythrocyte sedimentation rate and serum total protein), leaving 51 features for outcome prediction. Missing values in continuous variables were imputed using sex-stratified medians, whereas missingness in categorical variables was retained and handled natively by the algorithm.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and random forest (RF) algorithms were applied to identify clinical features associated with the primary outcome. These ensemble learning techniques were chosen for their complementary operational mechanisms: XGBoost used a sequential boosting approach to correct errors from previous models, ideal for capturing complex, non-linear medical data,16 while RF employs a bagging approach to aggregate independent decision trees, enhancing robustness and reducing overfitting.17

To ensure robust performance and mitigate over-optimism, all models were trained using fivefold nested cross-validation. Model discriminative ability was primarily assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The final classification threshold was determined by maximising the Youden index as a pragmatic way to summarise model performance at a single operating point that balanced sensitivity and specificity during internal evaluation; alternative thresholds may be more appropriate in practice, depending on the relative consequences of false negatives and false positives.

Model interpretation and scoring function

To enhance clinical utility and interpretability, the recursive feature elimination (RFE) technique with fivefold cross-validation was employed to identify the number of features required for accurate risk classification.17 Models containing 5, 10, 15 and all candidate features were compared with determine the best balance between parsimony and performance. The primary prediction model was the 10-variable XGBoost classifier. To support bedside interpretability, we additionally expressed the same selected predictors in a simplified logistic regression-based scoring framework. This score was intended as an explanatory, implementation-oriented tool rather than as a calibration layer for XGBoost or as a replacement for the primary model.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics are summarised as mean (SD) for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Analyses were conducted in R (version 3·5; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Weighted Cox regression incorporating posterior class membership probabilities was used to evaluate the association between trajectory class and kidney disease progression. Trajectory modelling was performed using jointlcmm (JLCMM) and hlme (Hierarchical Latent Class Mixed Models for GMM); time-to-event analyses used survival package (Kaplan–Meier curve) and coxphw (Cox proportional hazard model). Additional packages were used for prediction modelling and supporting analyses. Two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Reporting followed TRIPOD guidance for the development and validation of multivariable prediction models.

eGFR trajectories and risk of KRT

During follow-up, KRT was initiated in 1987 patients. In total, 665 499 eGFR measurements were available to model the longitudinal kidney function from 3 months after hospital discharge until KRT initiation, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up.

eGFR values used in the trajectory analyses were not constrained to a prespecified physiologic range (eg, 0 to 120 mL/min/1.73 m²) to preserve generalisability and to reflect the full spectrum of kidney function patterns observed in routine real-world EHR data. eGFR values above the conventional normal range (eg, 150, or even >200 mL/min/1.73 m²) may reflect hyperfiltration or low creatinine states (eg, early diabetes, pregnancy, or reduced muscle mass) rather than “super-normal” kidney function. Accordingly, trajectories shown in the figures (figure 2, online supplemental Figure S3 to S5) should be interpreted as longitudinal patterns derived from clinical measurements, with caution against over-interpreting isolated high eGFR values as indicating better kidney function.

(A) Three long-term eGFR trajectories over the study period and (B) Event-free probability was lowest in the Steady Low eGFR trajectory (latent class 4).

Three- and four-class configurations were compared for both GMM (LCMM) and JLCMM. Although the three-class linear JLCMM was more parsimonious (online supplemental Table S4), the four-class linear JLCMM was selected because it provided more clinically informative stratification of time-to-KRT risk and generated trajectories that more closely reflected real-world post-AKI eGFR patterns (online supplemental Figure S3 to S5). At model convergence, each patient was assigned to the latent class with the highest posterior probabilities of membership.

Under the selected four-class linear JLCMM, one class had negligible membership; therefore, results are reported for the three non-degenerate classes (figure 2A): Steady Low eGFR trajectory (Latent class 4; deteriorative trajectory), Slow Decline trajectory (Latent class 3), and transition/recovery characterised by an early decline followed by partial improvement (Latent class 2; Recovery trajectory). KRT risk differed markedly across trajectory classes (figure 2B). Using Latent class 2 (Recovery trajectory) as the reference, Latent class 4 (Steady Low trajectory) was associated with substantially higher risk of KRT initiation (HR 9.841; SE 0.409), whereas Latent class 3 showed lower risk (HR 0.037; SE 0.540) (figure 2B).

Model performance and explainability

In the derivation cohort, the performance difference between XGBoost and RF using all candidate features was small (AUC 0.9728±0.0025 vs 0.969±0.0006; online supplemental Table S5). XGBoost was selected because of its slightly higher AUC and more balanced sensitivity and specificity.

Sensitivity analyses evaluated three data-handling strategies using the XGBoost (online supplemental Table S6). Performance with native missing-value handling, without imputation, was comparable to that of the primary strategy, suggesting that overall discrimination was not materially influenced by the imputation approach.

The final model was a 10-variable XGBoost classifier selected through RFE (online supplemental Figure S6) and online supplemental Table S7). All primary discrimination metrics reported in table 2 refer to this model. The eGFR slope from discharge to 3 months was the most influential predictor, with a more negative slope associated with higher predicted risk (online supplemental Figure S7).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first ML-based risk stratification scores developed to predict membership in high-risk longitudinal post-AKI eGFR trajectory classes. The final 10-variable model stratified risk for subsequent kidney function deterioration and adverse outcomes among hospital survivors, independent of the initial AKI stage or type. The eGFR slope from discharge to 3 months emerged as a key predictor, offering stronger prognostic information than single time-point measures.18 19 The simplified LR-based score was developed secondarily to provide a transparent bedside representation of the selected predictors. These findings support an actionable post-AKI surveillance strategy at the 3-month landmark to prioritise kidney-protection interventions for patients at highest risk.

The ‘Steady Low’ trajectory represents a persistently high-risk post-AKI phenotype and likely includes many patients with pre-existing CKD and incomplete recovery; thus, the model primarily identifies sustained vulnerability to progression and KRT rather than de novo kidney injury. The simplified 10-variable score can be applied at the 3-month post-discharge visit to trigger targeted kidney protection bundles, including early nephrology referral, closer eGFR monitoring and medication optimisation (avoidance of nephrotoxins and dose adjustment of kidney-cleared drugs), patient education on sick-day rules20 and structured follow-up to track long-term kidney function. Early identification therefore supports a proactive, trajectory-based approach for patients at risk of rapid deterioration.

AI applications in kidney disease have largely focused on predicting AKI onset across heterogeneous settings, such as intensive care, emergency and outpatient settings,21 with fewer tools designed to support post-AKI management, including prediction of AKI non-recovery,22 re-hospitalisation23 or progressive kidney disease.24 This gap is clinically important because AKI-related morbidity is not confined to critically ill populations, and subsequent new-onset chronic kidney disease25 and KRT remain global health concerns.2 26 Accordingly, an early predictor of kidney function deterioration after AKI is needed to guide post-discharge care.

Nephrology guidelines emphasise the evaluation of renal function decline using serial eGFR changes rather than a single measurement.27 Prior studies have shown that eGFR trajectories can help predict kidney disease progression and mortality,28 29 yet their application after AKI remains limited. In this study, a joint modelling framework based on JLCMM was used to define clinically meaningful eGFR trajectory phenotypes, with a higher posterior probability of membership in the worst trajectory class serving as the prediction target. This differs from conventional endpoint prediction because it focuses on identifying patients likely to follow an unfavourable longitudinal recovery pattern over time rather than merely estimating the probability of a single event at a fixed horizon.

AKI outcomes are influenced by baseline kidney function, recovery and the timing of recovery.29 30 In this longitudinal EHR cohort from a multi-institutional healthcare network in Taiwan (88 632 hospitalised individuals), repeated eGFR measurements from before hospitalisation through follow-up were analysed to estimate membership in the worst longitudinal eGFR trajectory class rather than to predict ESKD as a single endpoint. More than 600 000 eGFR values collected over up to 8 years identified high-risk trajectory phenotypes associated with KRT; a persistently low eGFR trajectory beginning at the 3-month post-discharge landmark showed the strongest association with chronic dialysis initiation, supporting guideline-concordant reassessment at approximately 3 months after hospitalisation.31 This trajectory-based framing is clinically relevant because it captures heterogeneous recovery patterns over time rather than relying solely on a single cross-sectional kidney function value.

The XGBoost and RF algorithms demonstrated similarly high discrimination for distinguishing the worst eGFR trajectory from the other trajectories, although feature rankings differed. RFE selected a parsimonious 10-variable model from the 51 candidate features. Beyond baseline kidney function, the eGFR slope from discharge to 3 months provided substantial predictive value, indicating that early post-discharge decline may better capture long-term deterioration risk than single time-point function.7 32

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is one of the largest cohort studies to characterise high-risk deteriorative eGFR trajectories associated with subsequent KRT after inpatient care. More than 7 years of follow-up enabled analysis of a large number of eGFR measurements, supporting robust assessment of long-term kidney function variability and its relationship with advanced kidney outcomes. Although KDIGO guidelines recommend reassessing kidney function approximately 3 months after AKI,31 recovery and subsequent progression may be under-recognised without systematic follow-up or electronic reminders. The proposed ML framework uses routinely available clinical features and may be suitable for future integration into clinical decision support to identify high-risk patients at the 3-month post-discharge landmark and support timely, targeted interventions after external validation.

The present study has several limitations. First, patients with more frequent eGFR measurements may have had higher healthcare utilisation because of comorbidities, increasing the probability of detecting a deteriorative eGFR trajectory. This concern was addressed by using a JLCMM framework, which integrates longitudinal dynamic markers with time-to-event outcomes to handle irregularly timed data. Although JLCMM is robust for modelling linear trends,15 future research should evaluate approaches that can capture more complex non-linear dynamics. Second, several baseline laboratory variables had substantial missingness. Because laboratory testing in routine EHR practice is clinician-driven rather than protocolised, missingness may have been informative, whereas more frequent testing may identify patients with greater clinical complexity or renal concern. Although sensitivity analyses suggested that overall discrimination was similar across missing-data handling strategies, the imputation approach cannot fully eliminate potential bias arising from informative missingness. Third, high eGFR values in the study cohort should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect hyperfiltration or low muscle mass rather than superior renal health. To preserve the generalisability and reflect the true heterogeneity of the post-AKI population, we did not apply additional exclusions based on care settings (for example, intensive care) or specific clinical subgroups. Fourth, although the model was derived from a large multicentre cohort with high predictive performance (AUC 0.97), the absence of external validation in geographically or temporally distinct cohorts remains a critical limitation. Such high performance can sometimes indicate over-optimism or overfitting to regional clinical practice patterns. External validation is therefore required before implementation in diverse clinical settings. Although the proposed score showed strong discrimination, formal calibration assessment was not performed; therefore, the score should be interpreted primarily as a discrimination-oriented risk stratification tool, and the score-to-probability mapping should be interpreted cautiously until it has been tested in external populations and adjusted if needed. Future work should also evaluate model performance across multiple decision thresholds and benchmark the multivariable approach against simpler eGFR-based rules.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that longitudinal eGFR trajectories after hospitalisation were strongly associated with progression to ESKD, independent of the initial AKI stage or type. Using a large cohort with up to 8 years of follow-up, we developed and internally validated an ML model that predicts membership in a high-risk deteriorative eGFR trajectory class at 3 months after discharge with excellent discrimination. The trajectory-based risk assessment may improve early identification of high-risk survivors and support targeted interventions to reduce adverse kidney outcomes.
