Quantitative estimates for the relative isoperimetric problem and its gradient flow outside convex bodies in the plane
Elena Mäder-Baumdicker, Robin Neumayer, Jiewon Park, Melanie Rupflin

Abstract
. We prove three related quantitative results for the relative isoperimetric problem outside a convex body Ω \Omega in the plane: (1) Łojasiewicz estimates and quantitative rigidity for critical points, (2) rates of convergence for the gradient flow, and (3) quantitative stability for minimizers. These results come with explicit constants and optimal exponents/rates, and hold whenever a simple two-dimensional auxiliary variational problem for circular arcs outside of Ω \Omega is nondegenerate. The proofs are inter-related, and in particular, for the first time in the context of isoperimetric problems, a flow approach is used to prove quantitative stability for minimizers.



1.
Introduction

Two central themes in the study of geometric variational problems are stability and dynamics: how does the energy grow near a minimizer or critical point, and how rapidly does the associated gradient flow converge to equilibrium? It is well known that both properties are closely linked to nondegeneracy, and when the second variation at any critical point is nondegenerate modulo symmetries, one expects a quadratic stability estimate for minimizers and exponential convergence to equilibrium of the gradient flow.
Verifying nondegeneracy, however, is in practice often delicate or intractable, especially in geometric settings where the space of competitors is constrained by curvature, topology, or boundary behavior.

This paper investigates these themes in the context of the exterior isoperimetric problem in the plane. Given a convex body
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e. a compact, convex set with nonempty interior, with
C^{2}
boundary
\Sigma
and a prescribed area
\eta>0
, consider the isoperimetric problem

(1.1)

{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)=\inf\left\{P(E;\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega)\ :\ E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega,\ \ |E|=\eta\right\}.

Here
P(E;\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega)
is the relative perimeter, which is equal to
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial E\setminus\Sigma)
when
E
has
C^{1}
boundary; see Section
2
.

An important aspect of this problem is that we can reduce the question of the nondegeneracy of the second variation of critical points for the infinite-dimensional problem (
1.1
) to the nondegeneracy of the Hessian for critical points of an explicit two-dimensional variational problem over circular arcs; see
1.1
below.
For any convex body
\Omega
and area constraint
\eta>0
for which this two-dimensional nondegeneracy condition holds, we establish a suite of sharp results predicted by the second variation theory: (1) quantitative rigidity and Łojasiewicz estimates, (2) exponential convergence of the associated gradient flow, and (3) quadratic stability for minimizers.

We prove these three main results in an interconnected manner. In particular, to address quantitative stability—that is, the question of whether a set almost achieving the infimum in (
1.1
) must be quantitatively close to a minimizer—we evolve the boundary of a set by the free boundary area-preserving curve shortening flow and then integrate out a Łojasiewicz-type estimate along the trajectory.
Flow-based approaches to proving quantitative stability have been developed in recent years in the contexts of maps from

The quantitative results established in this paper for the aforementioned minimizers and critical points are closely connected to our third main focus: the asymptotic behavior of the associated gradient flow, i.e. the free boundary area-preserving curve shortening flow, or freeAPSCF, introduced by the first author in

In our main results below, we assume either that

Assumption 1.1

Given

Our first main result is the following quantitative rigidity and Łojasiewicz estimate for critical points.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a convex body with
C^{2}

\Sigma=\partial\Omega

\eta>0
and assume that either
\Sigma
is a circle or the pair

\bar{L}>0

For any

with length

and turning angle

\|\gamma-c^{*}\|_{C^{1}(ds_{\gamma})}\leq C_{0}\big[\|\kappa_{\gamma}-\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(ds_{\gamma})}+|\alpha_{1}(\gamma)-{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}|+|\alpha_{2}(\gamma)-{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}|\big]

and

(1.7)

|L(\gamma)-L(c^{*})|\leq C_{1}\big[\|\kappa_{\gamma}-\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(ds_{\gamma})}+|\alpha_{1}(\gamma)-{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}|+|\alpha_{2}(\gamma)-{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}|\big]^{2}

where
\alpha_{1,2}\in[0,\pi]
denote the intersection angles at which
\gamma
intersects
\Sigma
.

We carry out the proof of this theorem using explicit geometric constructions, which in particular avoid any use of compactness arguments. As such, the constants
C_{0,1}
appearing in the above theorem are computable in terms of basic geometric and analytic quantities associated to problem.

\Theta_{\Sigma}(r)\leq 2\pi-2\arctan(1/(r{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}))\,.

We can hence get a slightly improved upper bound for

In particular,
{\frac{1}{2}}r_{c^{*}}^{2}\phi\leq\eta,

and so by (

{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)\leq 2\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(1-\pi^{-1}\arctan\Big(\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2\eta}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}}^{-1}\Big)\right)^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\,.

We fix the universal constant

(2.5)

\bar{\mathfrak{c}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{{4}}{25 {\pi}}$}}\arcsin^{2}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{4\pi}$}}\right)\approx.0415.

In
Theorem

1.4
, we assume
\eta<{\bar{\mathfrak{c}}}{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}^{-2}

Lemma 2.1

Suppose
\eta<{\bar{\mathfrak{c}}}{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}^{-2}

There is an explicitly computable constant

{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)+\delta<\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{4}{5 \kappamaxSi}$}}\arcsin\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\eta}{(\IP(\eta) +\delta)^2}$}}\right)\,.

Proof.

The estimate (

. Since the inequality (

Remark 2.2
.

The proof of

actually goes through for any

small enough such that (

Remark 2.3
.

A basic geometric argument shows that
{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}^{-1}\leq d_{\Sigma}/2
, where

(2.7)

\displaystyle d_{\Sigma}:=\min\{|x-x^{\prime}|:x,x^{\prime}\in\Sigma,\tau_{\Sigma}(x)=-\tau_{\Sigma}(x^{\prime})\}

is the

of

, that is, the minimal distance of two parallel lines touching

. Hence the right-hand side of (

\frac{2}{5}\arcsin(1/(2\pi))d_{\Sigma}

{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)+\delta\leq d_{\Sigma}/2
whenever
(

) holds for a given
(\eta,\delta)
.

The isoperimetric profile is differentiable outside a countable set and is left- and right-differentiable everywhere. A classical argument shows that the left-and right-derivatives of
{I_{\Omega}}
at
\eta
are bounded above by the (constant) curvature
\kappa
of any minimizer of
{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)
, and at differentiability points,
{I_{\Omega}}^{\prime}(\eta)=\kappa
.
So,
{I_{\Omega}}
is absolutely continuous on compact subsets of

and thus by the fundamental theorem of calculus, for any

(2.8)

\Big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2\eta_2}$}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})\leq{I_{\Omega}}(\eta_{2})-{I_{\Omega}}(\eta_{1})\leq\Big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{\eta_1}$}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}).

2.2.
Notation and basic properties of oriented curves

For a regular oriented
C^{2}
curve
\gamma

from
x_{1}(\gamma)
to
x_{2}(\gamma)

we let
\tau_{\gamma}=\frac{\gamma^{\prime}}{|\gamma^{\prime}|}

be the unit tangent to the curve, and let
\nu_{\gamma}=J\tau_{\gamma}
, where

J
denotes a counterclockwise rotation by
+\pi/2
. We let
L(\gamma)
be the length of
\gamma
and
s_{\gamma}
the arclength parameter.
We denote by
\kappa_{\gamma}=\frac{\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime},\nu_{\gamma}\rangle}{|\gamma^{\prime}|^{2}}
the signed curvature with respect to
\nu_{\gamma}=J\tau_{\gamma}
.
We also set

\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{L(\gamma)}\int_{\gamma}\kappa_{\gamma}\,ds_{\gamma}
and denote by

\phi_{\text{turn}}(\gamma):=\int_{\gamma}\kappa_{\gamma}\,ds_{\gamma}

the total turning angle of
\gamma
.
We recall that
\kappa_{\gamma},\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma},\phi_{\text{turn}}(\gamma),\tau_{\gamma},
and
\nu_{\gamma}
are of course independent of the choice of parametrization, but that they flip a sign when the orientation is reversed.

In the following we use the convention that
\Sigma
is parametrized with positive orientation (i.e. counterclockwise). With this convention
\nu_{\Sigma}(p)
is the inner unit normal and
\kappa_{\Sigma}
is nonnegative.
Throughout the paper we consider curves
\gamma\in\mathcal{B}
for

(2.9)

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}:=&\Big\{\gamma:\gamma\text{ is an oriented $H^{2}$ curve for which }\gamma\cap\Omega=\{x_{1}(\gamma),x_{2}(\gamma)\}\Big\}\,.\end{split}

Definition 2.4
.

Given a curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{B}
, we concatenate
\gamma

with the (unique) oriented sub-arc
\sigma_{\gamma}
of
\Sigma
for which the oriented immersed closed curve
\gamma+\sigma_{\gamma}
is contractible in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\rm int}(\Omega)
and define the relative area
A_{\Sigma}(\gamma)
enclosed by
\gamma
as

(2.10)

A_{\Sigma}(\gamma):=A(\gamma+\sigma_{\gamma}):=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int\gamma\cdot\nu_{\gamma}\,ds_{\gamma}+\int\sigma_{\gamma}\cdot\nu_{\sigma_{\gamma}}\,ds_{\sigma_{\gamma}}\bigg).

An explicit way to construct
\sigma_{\gamma}
is by taking the projection
\tilde{\sigma}_{\gamma}=\pi_{\Sigma}\circ\gamma
of
\gamma
onto
\Sigma
, and defining
\sigma_{\gamma}
as the (unique) oriented arc of
\Sigma
that is homotopic to
-\tilde{\sigma}_{\gamma}
(with fixed endpoints).
We note that
\sigma_{\gamma}
can traverse
\Sigma
multiple times, may have the same or opposite orientation as the full curve
\Sigma
, and can even just be a point.
If
\gamma
is embedded and oriented so that

\gamma+\sigma_{\gamma}
has positive orientation, then
A_{\Sigma}(\gamma)
coincides with the area of the region bounded by
\gamma
and
\Sigma
.

Remark 2.5
.

As
\sigma_{\gamma}
is determined uniquely by the above topological condition, this construction in particular ensures that
\sigma_{\gamma_{t}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{t})
vary continuously along any continuous family of curves
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{B}
, and that

t\mapsto A_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{t})
is differentiable whenever
t\mapsto\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{B}
is differentiable with

(2.11)

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{d}{d t}$}}A_{\Sigma}\left(\gamma_{t}\right)=-\int_{\gamma}X_{t}\cdot\nu_{\gamma_{t}}ds_{\gamma_{t}}=-\int_{\gamma_{t}}X_{t}\cdot J\tau_{\gamma_{t}}\,ds_{\gamma_{t}}\,\qquad\text{ where }X_{t}:=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{d}{d t}$}}\gamma_{t}\,.

We note that the variation of
\sigma_{\gamma_{t}}
does not appear in the above formula as
\partial_{t}\sigma_{\gamma_{t}}
is tangential.

Since
a change in orientation of
\gamma
results in a change of sign of

A_{\Sigma}(\gamma)
,
we define for each given
\eta>0
the set of admissible curves for the relative isoperimetric problem as

\mathcal{B}_{\eta}:=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{B}:|A_{\Sigma}(\gamma)|=\eta\}.

It is immediate from the definition of
A_{\Sigma}
that

L(\gamma)\geq{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)
whenever
\gamma\in\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
is embedded and a short argument, which we include in Appendix
A
, ensures that
we can always bound

(2.12)

L(\gamma)\geq I_{\Omega}(\eta)\qquad\text{ for every }\gamma\in\mathcal{B}_{\eta}\text{ and every }\eta>0

and hence in particular
L(\gamma)\geq(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{\frac{1}{2}}
by (
2.1
).

We denote by
\alpha_{i}(\gamma)\in[0,\pi]
the angles at which a given curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{B}
intersect
\Sigma
,
characterized by

(2.13)

\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{1})=R_{\alpha_{1}}\tau_{\gamma}(x_{1})\text{ and }\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{2})=R_{-\alpha_{2}}\tau_{\gamma}(x_{2})\,.

Here and in the following

R_{\psi}
denotes the rotation by angle
\psi
in positive, i.e. counter-clockwise direction.

In what follows will often consider curves
\gamma\in\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
whose length
L(\gamma)
and turning angle
|\phi_{\text{turn}}(\gamma)|
are bounded by given numbers
\bar{L}
and
\bar{\phi}
and whose intersection angles
\alpha_{i}
with
\Sigma
defined in (
2.13
) satisfy

for some
\bar{\beta}<{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}
.
As
\Sigma
is convex, this ensures that
|\phi_{\text{turn}}(\gamma)|\geq\pi-2\bar{\beta}

and hence, recalling (

), that

(2.14)

\frac{\pi-2\bar{\beta}}{\bar{L}}\leq|\bar{\kappa}|\leq\frac{\bar{\phi}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\eta^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

In the following we also use that the parametrisation by arclength of any curve
\gamma:[0,L(\gamma)]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
can be expressed as

(2.15)

{\gamma}(p)=\gamma(0)+\int_{0}^{p}(\cos{\theta}_{\gamma}(q),\sin{\theta}_{\gamma}(q))\,dq\qquad\text{ for }\qquad\theta_{\gamma}(q)=\theta_{0}+\int_{0}^{p}\kappa_{\gamma}(s)\,ds_{\gamma}\,,

where

at the starting point and the

Proof of Lemma
3.3

As the claim is invariant under rescaling, translation and rotation we can assume without loss of generality that

While the parametrization of

to prove that

Our choice of
\varepsilon_{1}

\bar{r}

To prove (

)
we can hence apply Lemma

for this choice of

and

If

In the case where

The reparametrised curve

|\tilde{\gamma}_{1}(p)-{\gamma}_{1}(p)|\leq|L-L_{1}|,\qquad|\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{\prime}(p)-{\gamma}_{1}^{\prime}(p)|\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{L}+\bar{\kappa}\right)|L-L_{1}|\leq\tfrac{(1+2\pi)}{L}|L-L_{1}|\leq\tfrac{\eta^{1/2}(1+2\pi)}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}|L-L_{1}|,

This allows us to prove the following key ingredient for the proof of Theorem

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Sigma
and
\gamma
be as in Lemma

.
Then there exists a circular arc
\tilde{c}_{\gamma}\in\mathrm{Circ}_{\eta}
so that

(3.9)

\|\gamma-\tilde{c}_{\gamma}\|_{C^{1}(ds_{\gamma})}\leq C_{3}\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)\qquad\text{ for }\qquad\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma):=\|\kappa_{\gamma}-\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(ds_{\gamma})}

|\alpha_{i}(\gamma)-\alpha_{i}(\tilde{c}_{\gamma})|\leq C_{4}\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma),\qquad i=1,2

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

It suffices to consider curves for which
\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)
is less than a fixed constant
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{2}(\Sigma,\eta,\bar{L},\bar{\phi})>0
,
as the claims trivially hold for
\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)\geq\varepsilon_{2}
and any
\tilde{c}_{\gamma}\in\mathrm{Circ}_{\eta}
by choosing the constants depending on
\varepsilon_{2}.

As before, we write for short
L=L(\gamma)
and let
\bar{r}=\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma}^{-1}
be the radius of the circular arc
c_{\gamma}
of
Lemma

3.1
. We also recall that by
Lemma

3.1
,
\gamma
is embedded and
|\phi_{\text{turn}}(\gamma)|<2\pi
, and that combining this latter fact with (
2.14
) guarantees that
(\frac{\eta}{2\pi})^{1/2}\leq\bar{r}\leq\frac{6\bar{L}}{5\pi}
.
Furthermore, since
\Sigma
is embedded, there exists a constant

so that

\angle(\tau_{\Sigma}(x),\tau_{\Sigma}(\tilde{x}))\leq C|x-\tilde{x}|
for all
x,\tilde{x}\in\Sigma
.

Lemma
3.1
hence in particular ensures that the angle between the tangent to
\Sigma
at the endpoints
x_{2}(\gamma)
of
\gamma
and
x_{2}(c_{\gamma})
is bounded by
C\|\gamma-c_{\gamma}\|_{C^{0}}\leq C\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)
. Our construction furthermore ensures that

\tau_{\gamma}(x_{1}(\gamma))=\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(L)
and that the angle between this vector and
\tau_{c_{\gamma}}(x_{2}(c_{\gamma}))=\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(L_{1})
is given by
\frac{1}{\bar{r}}|L(\gamma)-L(c_{\gamma})|\leq(\frac{\eta}{2\pi})^{-1/2}2\left|\gamma(L)-{\gamma}_{1}(L)\right|\leq C\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)
.
Combined we hence obtain that

|\alpha_{2}(\gamma)-\alpha_{2}(c_{\gamma})|\leq C\|\gamma-c_{\gamma}\|_{C^{1}}\leq C\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)
,
C=C(\eta,\Sigma)
. For
\varepsilon_{2}
small enough this in particular ensures that the second intersection angle

\alpha_{2}(c_{\gamma})
is bounded away uniformly from
0
and
\pi
, say so that
\alpha_{i}(c_{\gamma})\in[\pi/4,3\pi/4]
for

, while this estimate is trivially true for

.

Since
\gamma
is embedded, we can bound

(3.11)

|A_{\Sigma}(c_{\gamma})-\eta|=|A_{\Sigma}(c_{\gamma})-A_{\Sigma}(\gamma)|\leq 2\pi(\bar{r}+\|c_{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{C^{0}(ds_{\gamma})})\|c_{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{C^{0}(ds_{\gamma})}\leq C\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma),

for an explicitly computable constant

, where in the final inequality we use
\bar{r}\leq\frac{6\bar{L}}{5\pi}
.
After reducing
\varepsilon_{2}
if necessary, we assume
C\varepsilon_{2}\leq\eta/4
for this constant, and thus (
3.11
) guarantees that

|A_{\Sigma}(c_{\gamma})-\eta|\leq\eta/4
.

We now let
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be the center of the circle that contains
c_{\gamma}
and consider the (continuous) family of circular arcs

in
\mathcal{B}
parametrized on

with center

. The uniform a priori bounds on the angles

, and the area of

ensure that the map

is a well-defined

map into

at least on an interval of the form

for a number

(compare Remark

The first variation of the area along families of circular arcs

with fixed center is given by
\partial_{r}A_{\Sigma}(c_{r}(z))=L(c_{r}(z))
; see (

) below.
In particular,

\partial_{r}A_{\Sigma}(c_{r}(z))\geq I_{\Omega}({\frac{1}{2}}\eta)\geq\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{\frac{1}{2}}

for all

A_{\Sigma}(c_{r}(z))\geq{\frac{1}{2}}\eta
.

So, after reducing
\varepsilon_{2}
if necessary, we deduce that there is a (unique)

for which

and

(3.12)

|\hat{r}-\bar{r}|\leq\frac{|A_{\Sigma}(c_{\gamma})-\eta|}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq C\varepsilon_{\kappa}(\gamma)

Thus
\tilde{c}_{\gamma}=c_{\hat{r}}(z)\in\mathrm{Circ}_{\eta}
. It is simple to check that

\|\tilde{c}_{\gamma}-c_{\gamma}\|_{C^{1}([0,L])}\leq C|r-\hat{r}|
for an explicit

. Combining this with (

) and the bound (

The second claim follows from the first. As noted above,
|\angle(\tau_{\Sigma}(x),\tau_{\Sigma}(\tilde{x}))|\leq C|x-\tilde{x}|
for all

. So, again using that

for unit vectors

, we have

|\alpha_{i}(\tilde{c}_{\gamma})-\alpha_{i}(\gamma)|\leq C|x_{i}(\tilde{c}_{\gamma})-x_{i}(\gamma)|+2\arcsin(\frac{1}{2}|\tau_{\tilde{c}_{\gamma}}(x_{i}(\tilde{c}_{\gamma}))-\tau_{\gamma}(x_{i}(\gamma))|)\leq C\|\tilde{c}_{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{C^{1}(ds_{\gamma})}
, which combined with (

) completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Analysis of circular arcs

We let
\mathrm{Circ}
be the subset of
\mathcal{B}
that is made up of circular arcs which intersect
\Sigma
transversally and let
\mathrm{Circ}_{\eta}:=\mathrm{Circ}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
, compare (

). Given

, we denote by

and

the center and radius of the circle that contains

.

We first note that for any

there exist neighborhoods
U_{c}
of

,
U_{1,2}
of the endpoints

,
x_{2}(c)
of

and

so that for every

and any

there is a unique circular arc

with radius

and center

whose endpoints

are in

As

is assumed to be

Remark 3.5
.

For any
r_{0}>0
and
\beta_{0}<\pi/2
there exist numbers
d_{0,1}=d_{0,1}(r_{0},\beta_{0},\Sigma)>0
so that the following holds true. If the radius and intersection angles of

are so that

and
|\alpha_{i}(c)-\pi/2|\leq\beta_{0}
,

, then the above family
(r,z)\mapsto c_{r}(z)
is well defined on
(r_{c}-d_{0},r_{c}+d_{0})\times B_{d_{1}}(z_{c})
, and the maps
(r,z)\mapsto x_{i}(c_{r}(z))
and
(r,z)\mapsto\theta_{i}(c_{r}(z))
which assign to each such pair the endpoints and the angles between
x_{i}-z
and
e_{1}
are given by
C^{2}
maps whose norms are bounded by a constant that only depends on

,
\beta_{0}
and the
C^{2}
norm of
\Sigma
.

In the following it suffices to consider circular arcs with positive orientation and it will be convenient to parametrize these arcs
c_{r}(z)
with constant speed over
[0,1]
, i.e. as

(3.13)

p\mapsto z+r(\cos,\sin)(\theta(p)),\quad\theta(p):=\theta_{1}+p(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}),p\in[0,1],

which then provides a way of viewing
(r,z)\mapsto c_{r}(z)
as a
C^{2}
function from

(r_{c}-d_{0},r_{c}+d_{0})\times B_{d_{1}}(z_{c})
to
C^{2}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
with uniformly bounded norms. We can furthermore use that the dependence of the intersection angles
\alpha_{i}(c_{r}(x))
on
r
and
x
is controlled in
C^{1}
since
\Sigma
is
C^{2}
.

For families of such circular arcs we now show the following useful lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
c_{\varepsilon}=c_{r_{\varepsilon}}(z_{\varepsilon})
be a differentiable family of circular arcs in
\mathrm{Circ}
which have positive orientation.
Then the variation of the enclosed area is given by

(3.14)

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}A_{\Sigma}(c_{\varepsilon})=\partial_{\varepsilon}r\cdot L+\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,-J(x_{2}-x_{1}))\rangle.

Furthermore, the variation of the endpoints along general variations
c_{\varepsilon}
can be expressed as

(3.15)

\partial_{\varepsilon}x_{1}=\mu_{1}\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{1})\quad\text{ and }\quad\partial_{\varepsilon}x_{2}=-\mu_{2}\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{2})\quad\text{ for }\quad\mu_{i}=\frac{-\partial_{\varepsilon}r+\langle\nu_{c}(x_{i}),\partial_{\varepsilon}z\rangle}{\sin(\alpha_{i})}.

For area preserving variations (
3.15
) this formula reduces to

(3.16)

\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{\sin(\alpha_{i})}\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,Y_{i}(c)\rangle\quad\text{ for }\quad Y_{i}(c):=\nu_{c}(x_{i})-L^{-1}J(x_{2}-x_{1}),\quad i=1,2

where
\nu_{c}
is the inward unit normal of
c
, which in turn ensures that the variation of the length along area preserving variations is given by

(3.17)

\partial_{\varepsilon}L(c_{\varepsilon})=-\sum_{i}\cot(\alpha_{i})\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,Y_{i}(c)\rangle.

In the above lemma and its proof we use the convention that all geometric quantities, such as the length
L
, the endpoints
x_{i}
, the intersection angles
\alpha_{i}
are evaluated for the corresponding circular arc
c=c_{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 3.7
.

From (
3.14
), we see that
along families of circular arcs with fixed center, the first variation of the area is given by

(3.18)

\partial_{r}A_{\Sigma}(c_{r}(z))=L(c_{r}(z)),

and is in particular bounded below by
(2\pi\eta)^{1/2}>0
whenever
c_{r}(z)\in\mathrm{Circ}_{\eta}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

We parametrize the circular arcs
c_{\varepsilon}
as in (
3.13
) and use that the orientation of
c_{\varepsilon}
ensures that
\nu_{c_{\varepsilon}}
is the inner normal to
c_{\varepsilon}
to write
\nu_{\varepsilon}(p)=-(\cos,\sin)(\theta_{\varepsilon}(p))
. As
J\nu_{\varepsilon}(p)=-\tau_{\varepsilon}(p)=(\sin,-\cos)(\theta_{\varepsilon}(p))
, this allows us to express the generating vector field

X=\partial_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}
as

(3.19)

X=\partial_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{\varepsilon}z_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{\varepsilon}r_{\varepsilon}\nu_{c}-r\partial_{\varepsilon}\theta_{\varepsilon}J\nu_{c}.

As
|c^{\prime}|=L
and as
\frac{L}{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}}=r
, the formula
(
2.11
) for the variation of the area hence yields that

(3.20)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}A_{\Sigma}(c_{r_{\varepsilon}}(z_{\varepsilon}))=-\int_{c}X\cdot\nu_{c}\,ds_{c}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varepsilon}r\cdot L+L\cdot\Big\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,\int_{0}^{1}(\cos,\sin)(\theta_{1}+p(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}))dp\Big\rangle=I+II.

For term
II
, we integrate to find

(3.21)

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=\tfrac{L}{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}}\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,(\sin,-\cos)(\theta_{2})-(\sin,-\cos)(\theta_{1})\rangle=\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,rJ\nu_{c}(x_{2})-rJ\nu_{c}(x_{1})\rangle=\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z_{\varepsilon},-J(x_{2}-z)+J(x_{1}-z)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z_{\varepsilon},-J(x_{2}-x_{1})\rangle

which together with (
3.20
) establishes the first claim (
3.14
) of the lemma.

Next, we note that since the endpoints
x_{1},x_{2}
are contained in
\Sigma
, their variation can be written as
\partial_{\varepsilon}x_{i}=\pm\mu_{i}\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{i})
for some
\mu_{i}=\mu_{i}(\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where here and in the following
\pm
is to be understood as

for

and as
-
for
i=2
.
To determine
\mu_{i}
we can use that

\partial_{\varepsilon}r_{\varepsilon}={\tfrac{1}{2}}r^{-1}\partial_{\varepsilon}|x_{i}-z|^{2}=\langle r^{-1}(x_{i}-z),\pm\mu_{i}\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{i})-\partial_{\varepsilon}z\rangle=-\mu_{i}\langle\nu_{c}(x_{i}),\pm\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{i})\rangle+\langle\nu_{c}(x_{i}),\partial_{\varepsilon}z\rangle

since
\nu_{c}(x_{i})=-\frac{x_{i}-z}{r}
is the inner unit normal. We can now use that
\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{i})=R_{\pm\alpha_{i}}\tau_{c}(x_{i})
, compare
(
2.13
), to write

\langle\nu_{c}(x_{i}),\pm\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{i})\rangle=\langle J\tau_{c}(x_{i}),\pm R_{\pm\alpha_{i}}\tau_{c}(x_{i})\rangle=\cos(\pi/2-\alpha_{i})=\sin(\alpha_{i}).

Thus

\partial_{\varepsilon}r_{\varepsilon}=-\mu_{i}\sin(\alpha_{i})+\langle\nu_{c}(x_{i}),\partial_{\varepsilon}z\rangle.

This establishes the formula (
3.15
) for the variation of the endpoints.

Now, by (
3.14
),
area preserving variations are characterized by
\partial_{\varepsilon}r=L^{-1}\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,J(x_{2}-x_{1})\rangle.
Making this substitution in (
3.15
) directly yields the expression (
3.16
) for
\mu_{i}
in the case of area preserving variations.
Inserting the resulting expression for
\partial_{\varepsilon}x_{i}
into the formula
(

) for the first variation of the length yields the claimed expression in (

) of

(3.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}L(c_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}x_{2},\tau_{c}(x_{2})\rangle-\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}x_{1},\tau_{c}(x_{1})\rangle=-\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\langle\tau_{\Sigma}(x_{i}),\tau_{c}(x_{i})\rangle=-\sum_{i}\cot(\alpha_{i})\langle\partial_{\varepsilon}z,Y_{i}(c)\rangle.

∎

We note that (

) in particular ensures that

is a

manifold that can be parametrized locally using the center.
To be more precise,
given a local

of a neighborhood of a given

so that the corresponding arc

encloses the required area

is

.

This allows us to view the restriction of the length functional to this

of

whose first variation is described by (

).
As

is a submanifold of
\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
, and since the critical points of the length functional (with prescribed enclosed area) are always circular arcs which intersect
\Sigma
transversally (and indeed orthogonally), it is trivially true that any critical point
\gamma\in C_{\eta}^{*}
of our original problem is also a critical point of this restricted functional. Conversely, as the vectors

,

,
appearing in the formula (

) of

are trivially linearly independent, we can immediately deduce that

vanishes if and only if

\cot(\alpha_{i})=0
for both

, i.e. if and only if
both intersection angles are
\alpha_{i}={\tfrac{\pi}{2}}
. We also note that
|Y_{i}(c)|\leq 2
and hence that
|d{\mathcal{L}_{\eta}}(\partial_{\varepsilon}z)|\leq 2(|\cot(\alpha_{1})|+|\cot(\alpha_{2})|)|\partial_{\varepsilon}z|
.

We can hence use that
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
and
L
are related by the following.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
\eta>0

we have
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{\eta}=\{c\in\mathrm{Circ}_{\eta}:d\mathcal{L}_{\eta}(c)=0\}

\varepsilon>0
and

so that
for any

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}{\mathcal{L}_{\eta}}(z^{*})

\displaystyle=-\cot^{\prime}(\pi/2)(\partial_{z_{1}}\alpha_{1})(z^{*})\langle e_{1},Y_{1}(z^{*})+Y_{2}(z^{*})\rangle=(\partial_{z_{1}}\alpha_{1})(z^{*})\langle e_{1},\nu_{c^{*}}(x_{1}^{*})+\nu_{c^{*}}(x_{2}^{*})-2L^{-1}J(x_{2}^{*}-x_{1}^{*})\rangle

4.
Exponential convergence of the gradient flow

In this section, we recall some background on the area preserving curve shortening flow with Neumann free boundary conditions, including results of the first author that will be crucial to apply the flow to prove
Theorem

1.4
in the next section. Then, we prove
Theorem

1.3
.

4.1.
Background on the flow

Parabolic regularity theory implies that a solution of (
1.9
) with initial regularity
C^{2+\alpha}
satisfies

\gamma\in C^{2+\alpha,1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\left([a_{1},a_{2}]\times[0,T_{max}),{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\cap C^{\infty}\left([a_{1},a_{2}]\times(0,T_{max}),{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\ \ \ \text{ for }0<\alpha<1

where
C^{2+\alpha,1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}
denotes the usual parabolic Hölder spaces and
T_{max}>0
is the maximal time of existence.
We will use the notation
\gamma_{t}:=\gamma(\cdot,t)
. This flow was constructed as (formal)
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the length with the constraint that the enclosed area is constant. It satisfies in particular

(4.1)

\frac{d}{dt}L(\gamma_{t})=-\|\kappa_{\gamma_{t}}-\bar{\kappa}_{\gamma_{t}}\|_{L^{2}(ds_{\gamma_{t}})}^{2}\,.

Due to the flow’s non-local nature and the free boundary condition, preserved properties are rare to find. In
[
23
]
it was shown that convexity of
\gamma_{t}
is preserved under the flow. However, embeddedness and the property of being contained in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega
is not preserved in general. Thus, for general initial data, understanding how to close up the curve to define the enclosed area is
subtle
, as doing this naively might produces a family of closed curves whose enclosed area jumps by
\pm|\Omega|
. An example to illustrate this behavior can be found in Figure
1
.

In this paper, we only deal with flows that stay outside of
\Omega
, and therefore we avoid this subtlety. Instead, Definition
2.4

In the proof, we will need to compare the

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

and

\displaystyle\left|L(\gamma_{t})\cdot\|\partial_{t}\tilde{\gamma}_{t}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}-\|\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}\|_{L^{2}(ds_{\gamma_{t}})}^{2}\right|\leq\ 4L(\gamma_{t})\,\bigg(\int|\kappa||\partial_{t}\gamma|\,ds_{\gamma_{t}}\bigg)^{2}.

Proof.

We closely follow
[
33
]
.
We write
\gamma_{t}(p)=\gamma(p,t)
and use the notation
\gamma^{\prime}(p,t)
to mean
\partial_{p}\gamma(p,t)

For

consider the strictly increasing function
p\mapsto\varphi(p,t):={L(\gamma_{t})}^{-1}\int_{a_{1}}^{p}|\gamma^{\prime}(q,t)|\,dq
and let
\psi(\cdot,t):[0,1]\to[a_{1},a_{2}]

For the normal speed, the change of variable

\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{t}\gamma(\psi(p,t),t)|^{2}\,dp=L(\gamma_{t})^{-1}\|\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}\|_{L^{2}(ds_{\gamma_{t}})}^{2}

norm of the tangential speed, which by the same change of variable is

(4.4)

I:=\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{t}\psi(p,t)|^{2}\,|\gamma^{\prime}(\psi(p,t)|^{2}\,dp=L(\gamma_{t})^{-1}\int_{a_{1}}^{a_{2}}\big|\partial_{t}\psi\big|_{(\varphi(q,t),t)}\big|^{2}|\gamma^{\prime}(q,t)|^{3}\,dq\,.

Toward this aim, we differentiate the identity
q=\psi(\varphi(q,t),t)
with respect to

and

to find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi\big|_{(\varphi(p,t),t)}=-\partial_{p}\psi\big|_{(\varphi(p,t),t)}\partial_{t}\varphi(p,t)=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{L(\gamma_t)}{|\gamma'(p,t)|}$}}\partial_{t}\varphi(p,t).

Now, from the Frenet equation

and the fact that
\partial_{t}\gamma
is orthogonal to

, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{a_{1}}^{p}|\gamma^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\int_{a_{1}}^{p}\langle{\partial_{t}\gamma^{\prime}},\tau\rangle=\int_{a_{1}}^{p}\partial_{p}(\langle\partial_{t}\gamma,\tau\rangle)-\langle\partial_{t}\gamma,\partial_{p}\tau\rangle=-\int_{a_{1}}^{p}\langle\partial_{t}\gamma,\kappa\nu\rangle|\gamma^{\prime}|.

Therefore,

\partial_{t}\varphi(p,t)=-L(\gamma_{t})^{-1}({\partial_{t}L(\gamma_{t})}\,\varphi(p,t)+\int_{a_{1}}^{p}\langle\partial_{t}\gamma,\kappa\nu\rangle|\gamma^{\prime}_{t}|).

\displaystyle I=L(\gamma_{t})^{-1}\int_{a_{1}}^{a_{2}}\bigg({\partial_{t}L(\gamma_{t})}\,\varphi(p,t)+\int_{a_{1}}^{q}\langle\partial_{t}\gamma,\kappa\nu\rangle|\gamma^{\prime}|\bigg)^{2}ds_{\gamma_{t}}(q)\leq 2\,(\partial_{t}L(\gamma_{t}))^{2}+2\bigg(\int|\partial_{t}\gamma||\kappa|ds_{\gamma_{t}}\bigg)^{2}.

Lemma 5.7
.

Fix
\eta>0
.
There exist positive constants
C_{2}=C_{2}({\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)},\eta)
and
\delta_{2}=\delta_{2}({\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)},\eta)

\Sigma
on a connected, positive

-measure set,

\displaystyle|E\Delta F|^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq C_{2}\,\delta_{\eta}(E)\quad\text{ and }\quad P(F;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)\leq P(E;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)+C_{2}\,\delta_{\eta}(E)\,.

Proof.

Step 1:

Recall that a set of finite perimeter
G
is said to be indecomposable if
|G_{1}|\,|G_{2}|=0
for any disjoint sets
G_{1},G_{2}
such that
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
and
P(G)=P(G_{1})+P(G_{2})
. By
[
1
, Theorem 1]
,

admits a unique decomposition as the union of at most countably many pairwise disjoint indecomposable sets
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that
|E_{i}|>0
and
P(E)=\sum_{i\in I}P(E_{i})
. It follows from
[
1
, Proposition 3]
and Federer’s and De Giorgi’s theorems
[

, Theorem 16.2, Theorem 15.9]
that
P(E;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})=\sum_{i\in I}P(E_{i};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})
as well. Therefore, letting
\eta_{i}=|E_{i}|
and reindexing so the

are non-increasing,

5.6
implies that

(5.20)

\displaystyle\delta_{\eta}(E)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in I}{I_{\Omega}}(\eta_{i})-{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)\geq c_{0}\sum_{i\geq 2}\eta_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq c_{0}\Big(\sum_{i\geq 2}\eta_{i}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=c_{0}(\eta-\eta_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Here
c_{0}=c_{0}({\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)},\eta)
is the constant from
Lemma

5.6
and the final inequality follows from concavity. Therefore, the indecomposable set
E_{1}
satisfies

(5.21)

\delta_{\eta}(E)\geq c_{0}(\eta-\eta_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}=c_{0}|E\Delta E_{1}|^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad P(E_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\leq P(E;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\,.

We choose
\delta_{2}\leq c_{0}\eta^{1/2}/2
, so that the first estimate in (
5.21
) guarantees that
\eta_{1}\geq\eta/2.

We claim that
P(E_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)<P(E_{1})
, which in turn guarantees that
\partial E_{1}
intersects
\Sigma
on a set of positive
\mathcal{H}^{1}
measure and that

is indecomposable.
To see this,
take a ball of area

at positive distance from

and

. By the first bound in (

), this ball has perimeter at most

for

. So, using the second bound in (

), the union

Here, the second inequality holds thanks to the upper bound (
2.4
) for the isoperimetric profile, provided we choose
\delta_{2}\leq\frac{1}{C}\,2\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{\frac{1}{2}}\{1-(1-\pi^{-1}\arctan(\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2\eta}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}}^{-1})^{{\frac{1}{2}}}\}
. On the other hand,
P(\tilde{E})\geq 2\pi^{1/2}\eta^{1/2}
by the isoperimetric inequality. Thus
P(\tilde{E};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)<P(\tilde{E})
, which by definition of

proves the claim.

Step 2:

Next we ”fill in the holes” of
E_{1}\cup\Omega
. More specifically, since

is indecomposable,

[
1
, Corollary 1]
says that the essential boundary
\partial^{M}(E_{1}\cup\Omega)
admits a unique decomposition into at most countably many rectifiable Jordan curves

and
\{C^{-}_{j}\}_{j\in J}
with
\text{int}(C^{-}_{j})\subset\text{int}(C^{+})
such that

E_{1}\cup\Omega=\text{int}(C^{+})\setminus\bigcup_{j\in J}\text{int}(C^{-}_{j}),\qquad P(E_{1}\cup\Omega)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(C^{+})+\sum_{j\in J}\mathcal{H}^{1}(C^{-}_{j}).

Let
G=\text{int}(C^{+})
and

F_{1}=G\setminus\Omega
.
Notice that by construction,

,

is a single rectifiable curve in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega}
with endpoints on
\Sigma

is a connected set with positive
\mathcal{H}^{1}

Letting

\hat{C}^{-}_{j}=C^{-}_{j}\setminus\Sigma

P(E_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\hat{C}^{+})+\sum_{j\in J}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\hat{C}^{-}_{j})

P(F_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})\leq P(E_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})\,.

To bound

, so that

. So, as

\begin{split}(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}|E_{1}\Delta F_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2}}&\leq P(E_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})-P(F_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})\\
&\leq P(E_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})-{I_{\Omega}}(\eta_{1})\leq\delta_{\eta}(E)+({I_{\Omega}}(\eta)-{I_{\Omega}}(\eta_{1})).\end{split}

So, recalling from above that

, the local Lipschitz estimate (

Combining this with (

Step 3:

If

we complete the proof by
taking

.
Otherwise, as in step 1, take a ball of area

, and thus of perimeter at most

. Since

is bounded, we may translate this ball from infinity along some ray so that it is disjoint from
{\Omega}\cup F_{1}
and its boundary intersects

. Then, by a slight deformation of

gives a set satisfying the conclusions of the lemma.
∎

5.3.
Reduction to a set bounded by a convex curve

The next step toward
Proposition

5.2
is to replace a set of the type obtained in

5.7
by a relatively convex set with acute contact angle. First we prove the following elementary geometric lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

Fix
\bar{L}>0.
There exists
C=C(\bar{L})>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a rectifiable curve with
L(\gamma)\leq\bar{L}
and let
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a parametrization of the linear segment joining
\gamma(0)
and
\gamma(1)

(5.25)

d_{H}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})^{2}\leq C(L(\gamma)-L(\tilde{\gamma})).

Proof.

It suffices to bound the distance between any point
z\in\gamma
to its projection
\hat{z}
onto the line through the endpoints
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
of
\gamma
. Pythagoras, applied to the triangles
\Delta(x_{0},z,\hat{z})
and
\Delta(x_{1},z,\hat{z})
, immediately gives the required bound of

(5.26)

\displaystyle 2|z-\hat{z}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|z-x_{0}|^{2}-|\hat{z}-x_{0}|^{2}+|z-x_{1}|^{2}-|\hat{z}-x_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\bar{L}\cdot(|z-x_{0}|+|z-x_{1}|-[|\hat{z}-x_{0}|+|\hat{z}-x_{1}|])\leq 2\bar{L}\cdot(L(\gamma)-L(\tilde{\gamma})).

∎

Lemma 5.9

Fix

\displaystyle P(E;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)\leq{I_{\Omega}}(\eta)+\delta_{3}.

\displaystyle P(F;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega)

(5.30)

\gamma

\Sigma
.
The set
\tilde{\Sigma}

\gamma

\delta_{3}\leq\delta_{0}

\Sigma

Let
j_{0}<j_{1}
be the endpoints of the interval

\tilde{\Sigma}
is the trace of
\sigma
restricted to the interval
J
, and let

x_{0}=\sigma(j_{0})
and
x_{1}=\sigma(j_{1})
.
Assume
\gamma
is oriented such that

\underline{a}<\overline{a}

\displaystyle\underline{a}

\displaystyle=\sup\{p\in[0,1]:\gamma(p)\in n_{x_{0}}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{p\in[0,1]:\gamma(p)\in n_{x_{1}}\}.

\gamma(\underline{a})\in n_{x_{0}}
and
\gamma(\overline{a})\in n_{x_{1}}

\hat{\gamma}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega
by letting

on

and

The convexity of

Moreover, we claim that

d_{H}(\hat{\gamma},\gamma)^{2}\leq 3\bar{L}\,(L(\gamma)-L(\hat{\gamma}))\,.

5.32

\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\Omega

{\kappa_{max}(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq{I_{\Omega}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq P(G;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega})\leq 2({I_{\Omega}}(\eta)+\delta_{3})\leq 4(\pi\eta)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\delta_{3}.

and
|A_{\Sigma}(\gamma)-A_{\Sigma}(\gamma_{\epsilon})|<\epsilon
, and moreover

(2)

the image of
\gamma_{\epsilon}
is the union of finitely many piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{2}
curves
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\cdots\gamma_{k}
where
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
is embedded and
\gamma_{i}
are closed and embedded for
i=1,\cdots,k
, where for
i,j\in\{0,\cdots,k\}
,
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
do not intersect except possibly meeting transversally at their endpoints.

As
\gamma
is of class
H^{2}
, and hence
C^{1}
,
such a
\gamma_{\epsilon}
can be easily constructed to be in fact even piecewise linear;
taking a fine enough subdivision
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{i}=i/k<\cdots<t_{k}=L(\gamma)
of
[0,L(\gamma)]
for a large integer
k
and replacing
\tilde{\gamma}|_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}
with the line segment connecting
\tilde{\gamma}(t_{i})
and
\tilde{\gamma}(t_{i+1})
, we can get a piecewise linear curve which satisfies (
1
). Then, up to slightly perturbing the vertices of the piecewise linear
\gamma_{\epsilon}
to avoid any overlapping segments,
\gamma_{\epsilon}
can be taken to satisfy (
2
). Denote by
E_{0}
the region bounded by
\gamma_{0}
and
\Sigma
, and
E_{k}
the region bounded by
\gamma_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,k
. By the usual isoperimetric inequality for closed curves we have
L(\gamma_{i})^{2}\geq 4\pi|E_{i}|
, and
L(\gamma_{0})\geq I_{\Omega}(|E_{0}|)
. Therefore,

L(\gamma_{\epsilon})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}L(\gamma_{i})\geq I_{\Omega}(|E_{0}|)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{4\pi|E_{i}|}.

On the other hand, note that
|E_{0}|+|E_{1}|+\cdots+|E_{k}|\geq\eta-\epsilon
since the
E_{i}
’s are counted with a sign in the algebraic area of
\gamma_{\epsilon}
. By (
2.1
) we have that
\sqrt{4\pi a}\geq I_{\Omega}(a)
for any
a>0
. We can now use that the isoperimetric profile is sub-additive in the sense that
I_{\Omega}(a)+I_{\Omega}(b)\geq I_{\Omega}(a+b)
for any
a,b>0
; see Lemma
5.6
for a more general and quantitative version of this statement. Because
I_{\Omega}(a)
is nondecreasing in
a
, combining all of the above we have that

L(\gamma_{\epsilon})\geq I_{\Omega}(|E_{0}|)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{4\pi|E_{i}|}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}I_{\Omega}(|E_{i}|)\geq I_{\Omega}(|E_{0}|+\cdots+|E_{k}|)\geq I_{\Omega}(\eta-\epsilon).

Taking
\epsilon\to 0
finishes the proof.
∎
