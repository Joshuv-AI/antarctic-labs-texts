On Classifying HyperKähler Kummer 8-orbifolds
B.S. Acharya

Abstract




Abstract:

HyperKähler spaces, including manifolds, orbifolds and conical singularities play an important role in superstring/
M
-theory and gauge theories as well as in differential and algebraic geometry. In this paper we provide hundreds of new examples of compact hyperKähler orbifolds of Kummer type
T^{8}/G
, where
T^{8}
is the maximal torus of the compact Lie group
E_{8}
and
G
a finite group of isometries whose holonomies form a subgroup of the Weyl group of
E_{8}
. We show that, out of all of these examples, the only orbifolds whose singularities have a known holomorphic symplectic resolution lead to manifolds diffeomorphic to the two currently known examples of compact hyperKähler 8-manifolds. We also demonstrate that these methods can, when combined with theorems of Joyce, be extended to construct potentially new manifolds of
\operatorname{SU}(4)
- and
\operatorname{Spin}(7)
- holonomy. All of these examples give rise to new vacua of string/
M
-theory in two/three dimensions.

1
Introduction

Einstein’s equations with zero cosmological constant are equivalent to the existence of a metric
g(M)
on the spacetime manifold
M
whose Ricci tensor vanishes,
\operatorname{Ric}(g(M))=0
. Such manifolds are called Ricci flat. They have played a ubiquitous role in both physics and mathematics for more than a century. In particular, in superstring/
M
-theory they are natural candidates as models for the extra dimensions of space and thus compact Ricci flat manifolds have been the subject of fairly intense study over the past four decades. At present the only known examples of compact, simply connected Ricci flat manifolds are those with special holonomy groups:
\operatorname{Sp}(k),\operatorname{SU}(m),G_{2},\operatorname{Spin}(7)
, corresponding to hyperKähler, Calabi-Yau and exceptional holonomy respectively. Hence, special holonomy Ricci flat manifolds have played a particularly prominent role. Physically, the Ricci flat special holonomy groups are precisely those which preserve supersymmetry, which was the original motivation for studying Calabi-Yau manifolds (
\operatorname{SU}(m)
-holonomy) in string theory
[
9
]
. Presently, it is not known, apart from in real dimension four, if there are finitely or infinitely many diffeomorphism types of Ricci flat special holonomy manifolds in each dimension. This question is part of the motivation for this work, which can be viewed as a search for new hyperKähler manifolds.

From a physical point of view, theories with larger supersymmetry groups are more tightly constrained than those without. From the special holonomy point of view, it is hyperKähler manifolds which give rise to the largest supersymmetry groups and these will be the main subject of this paper. Part of the motivation is that,
at present, there are very few known examples of compact hyperKähler manifolds. The current list, which we will state in the next section, comprises only two examples in each real dimension
4n

[
1
]
and an additional two exceptional examples in real dimension 12 and 20
[
23
,
24
]
. There have been no new examples found for three decades and it is not known if the current list is exhaustive or not.
Whilst Yau’s proof of the Calabi conjecture applies to hyperKähler manifolds, finding the explicit holomorphic symplectic Kähler manifolds to which it applies is the challenge.

The exceptional groups
G_{2}
and
\operatorname{Spin}(7)
are also challenging Ricci flat special holonomy groups as there is no analogue of the Calabi conjecture and the best available theorems are those of Joyce
[
20
,
21
,
19
,
22
]
which are based on demonstrating that if one is sufficiently close to a special holonomy metric, then under suitable circumstances one can perturb to a genuine Ricci flat metric. Joyce applied these results to go on to construct the first compact examples of
G_{2}
and
\operatorname{Spin}(7)
holonomy manifolds by a generalised Kummer construction, where one attempts to resolve the singularities in a finite quotient,
T^{7}/G
or
T^{8}/G
, by removing small neighbourhoods of the singular set and gluing in suitably small model manifolds and Ricci flat metrics and perturbing.
One of the key aspects of Joyce’s work is that one has to carefully choose the finite group actions on
T^{n}
in order that the singular sets are suitably mild enough, typically consisting of codimension four
ADE
singularities which ideally do not intersect each other. This is achieved by carefully choosing the rotations
and
translations in
G
since, without translations, all components of the singular set of
T^{n}/G
intersect at the origin whereas with them, certain elements of the group act freely on the fixed locus of other elements.

The idea behind this paper is to try to construct compact hyperKähler manifolds using a similar strategy. To this end we consider the Weyl group
W(E_{8})
of
E_{8}
acting on
T^{8}\cong T(E_{8})
, the maximal torus of
E_{8}
. First we classify all of the subgroups of
W(E_{8})
preserving a hyperKähler structure on
T^{8}
. This leads to 139 model hyperKähler orbifolds
T^{8}/H
, with the elements of
H
acting as rotations in
\operatorname{O}(8)\times\{\mathbb{1}\}\subset\operatorname{O}(8)\ltimes\mathcal{T}(T^{8})\cong\mathcal{E}_{8}
where
\mathcal{E}_{8}
is the Euclidean group i.e. the translations are trivial.
We then go on to show that, no matter which translations are included compatible with the
H
-action, it is impossible to generate singular sets which have crepant resolutions, apart from those diffeomorphic to the previously known examples.
This proves that no new examples of compact hyperKähler 8-manifolds can be constructed with this method.

In order for the hyperKähler orbifolds we consider to admit a desingularisation to a compact hyperkähler manifold, the singularities occuring in the singular set must admit a
symplectic resolution
. The classification of such singularities is not complete but it is thought that few, if any, more examples will be added to the current known list, see
[
3
,
4
]
for details. Generically, hyperKähler orbifolds will have some singularities that are known not to admit symplectic resolutions.
We will call such singularites
non-resolvable symplectic (NRS) singularities
. They may also contain singularities for which a symplectic resolution is not known to exist, and these will be called
undecided symplectic (US) singularities
. These are described in detail in
[
3
,
4
]
and examples will appear throughout this paper.

The maximal torus of
E_{8}
was chosen because it has a huge symmetry group given by
W(E_{8})
and so it can provide many potentially interesting orbifolds through quotients by finite subgroups.
The subgroups of
W(E_{8})
that preserve a hyperKähler structure can be classified and will be described in section
3
.
It turns out there are three classes of such subgroups, which we call
G_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
). For every subgroup
H
of
G_{1},G_{2}
or
G_{3}
one can consider including all possible translations compatible with the
H
action on
T^{8}
giving a group
H^{\prime}
isomorphic to
H
but with a different action
1
1

1

In the language of crystallographic groups, there can be more than one space group for a given point group
H
.
on
T^{8}
. For any such hyperKähler orbifold,
T^{8}/H^{\prime}
, there are three possibilities:
(i):

T^{8}/H^{\prime}
admits a symplectic resolution to a compact hyperKähler manifold;
(ii):

T^{8}/H^{\prime}
has at least one NRS singularity and hence can not be resolved to a compact hyperKähler manifold;
(iii):

T^{8}/H^{\prime}
has at least one US-singularity and no NRS-singularities and we currently are unable to show that it can or cannot admit a symplectic resolution.

Our main results are given in Tables
1
,
2
and
3
. Each line in the table is a subgroup
H
. This gives 139 compact hyperKähler 8-orbifolds, of which one is Type
(i)
, 138 are Type
(ii)
and none are Type
(iii)
. We further show that every choice of
H^{\prime}
for the Type
(i)
case leads to a Type
(ii)
orbifold.
For the 138 Type
(ii)
examples we are able to show that for every choice of
H^{\prime}
we can never remove all of the NRS-singularities and, hence the orbifolds will remain Type
(ii)
.

Although we were unable to produce new hyperKähler manifolds, this construction does provide many new compact hyperKähler orbifolds which may still be of interest. Furthermore, the methods in this paper can certainly be applied to study higher dimensional hyperKähler orbifolds as well as other holonomy groups. In fact we show that one can construct new Calabi-Yau manifolds using this approach and provide a pair of examples at the end of the paper.

In section
2
, we review some basic properties of compact hyperKähler manifolds as well as details about symplectic singularities and their resolutions. In section
3
, we describe the classification of all the subgroups of
W(E_{8})
preserving a hyperKähler structure and then explicitly represent these subgroups as matrix groups acting on
\mathbb{R}^{8}
. We then provide the tables detailing all 139 of the model hyperKähler orbifolds. In section
4
, we consider the singular sets of these orbifolds for all choices of translations and describe the method by which we can determine if an orbifold is non-resolvable.

2
HyperKähler Geometry

2.1
Compact hyperKähler manifolds

The following can found in, for example,
[
18
]
. Compact hyperKähler manifolds can be viewed from the Riemannian point of view or from the complex geometric point of view, where they are often known as irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds. From the former viewpoint, they are
4n
-dimensional compact Riemannian manifolds
(M,g)
with holonomy group
\operatorname{Hol}(g)=\operatorname{Sp}(n)
. From the latter viewpoint, they are compact Kähler manifolds
X
that are simply connected and admit a
(2,0)
-form that is closed and non-degenerate on all of
X
, they then necessarily have even complex dimension
2n
. To pass from the former to the latter viewpoint is straightforward: a hyperKähler manifold is equipped with three integrable complex structures
I,J,K
that are Kähler with respect to the hyperKähler metric
g
, call the Kähler forms
\omega_{I,J,K}
. One can then take, for instance, the Kähler manifold
(M,I)
which admits a holomorphic 2-form that is closed and non-degenerate on all of
M
given by
\Omega=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
. Furthermore,
(M,I)
is simply-connected by virtue of being a compact Riemannian manifold with holonomy
\operatorname{Sp}(n)
and also
(M,I)
cannot be written as the product of lower-dimensional complex manifolds else
g
would not have irreducible holonomy. To go from the latter to the former viewpoint is slightly more involved and the details can be found in
[
18
,
1
]
.

As mentioned in the introduction, in contrast to, say, Calabi-Yau manifolds, examples of compact hyperKähler manifolds are very few and far between and no new examples have been found for nearly 30 years. In real dimension 4 the only example is a
K3
surface. The full known list for real dimension greater than 4 is the following,

(i)

The
Hilbert scheme of
n
points

\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X)
where
X
is a
K3
surface
[
1
]
. This manifold has real dimension
4n
and
b_{2}(\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X))=23
for
n>1
.

(ii)

The
generalised Kummer variety

\operatorname{K}_{n}(X)
, where
X
is a real 4-dimensional torus
[
1
]
. This has real dimension
4n
and
b_{2}(\operatorname{K}_{n}(X))=7
for
n>1
.

(iii)

A real dimension 20 example due to O’Grady
[
23
]
. It has
b_{2}=24

[
12
]
.

(iv)

A real dimesnion 12 example due to O’Grady
[
24
]
. It has
b_{2}=8
.

We will elaborate slightly on each of these examples. The Hilbert scheme
\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X)
can be understood as a crepant resolution of the symmetric product
X^{n}/S_{n}
. For
n=2
this resolution can be seen explicitly as a blow-up of the
A_{1}
-singularities lying along the diagonal surface in
X^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. When
X
is a
K3
surface or a torus
T^{4}
it is true that
\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X)
is holomorphic symplectic however only when
X
is a
K3
surface is
\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X)
also irreducible
[
1
]
. When
X
is
T^{4}
,
\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X)
has a finite cover isomorphic to
K_{n-1}(X)\times X
. We can see
K_{n-1}(X)
as the kernel of the map,

\operatorname{Hilb}^{n}(X)\overset{\pi}{\rightarrow}X^{n}/S_{n}\overset{\Sigma}{\rightarrow}X

(2.1)

where
\Sigma
is the map which takes the sum of the
n
points. Note that
K_{1}(T^{4})
is a
K3
surface obtained from the Kummer construction, hence the name ‘generalised kummer variety’. The examples due to O’Grady were obtained as crepant resolutions of the singular moduli space of certain sheaves on
K3
surfaces.

2.2
Symplectic resolutions

Let
G\subset\operatorname{Sp}(n)
be a finite subgroup and
V
be a symplectic representation of
G
. Then
V/G
is a
symplectic singularity
and admits a 2-form that is closed and non-degenerate on all of the smooth part of
V/G
. A desingularisation
\widetilde{V/G}\rightarrow V/G
is called a
symplectic resolution
if the pull back of the 2-form on
V/G
is closed and non-degenerate on all of
\widetilde{V/G}
. The current list of singularities
V/G
known to admit a symplectic resolution is the following
[
2
]
:

(i)

G=S_{n+1}
acting on
V=W\oplus W\cong\mathbb{C}^{2n}
where
W=\left\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\mid\sum x_{i}=0\right\}
and
S_{n+1}
acts by permuting
x_{1}
,…,
x_{n}
.

(ii)

G=H\wr S_{n}
(where
\wr
denotes the wreath product) acting on
V=\mathbb{C}^{2n}
in the following way,

\frac{\mathbb{C}^{2n}}{G}=\operatorname{Sym}^{n}\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}/H\right)

(2.2)

where
H\subset\operatorname{SU}(2)
is a finite subgroup acting in the fundamental representation and
S_{n}
permutes the factors.

(iii)

G=\mathbb{T}
, the binary tetrahedral group, acting in the following way: take the standard irreducible 2-dimensional representation on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
given by
\mathbb{T}\subset\operatorname{SU}(2)
, i.e. with generators given by,

g_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1+i&1+i\\
-1+i&-1-i\end{pmatrix},\quad g_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1+i&-1-i\\
1-i&-1-i\end{pmatrix}

(2.3)

and take the tensor product with one of the two non-trivial 1-dimensional representations (e.g. where
g_{1}
is given by multiplication by
\operatorname{exp}(2\pi i/3)
), then take the direct sum of this representation with its dual representation. In other words, take the generators to be,

\hat{g}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\,g_{1}&\\
&e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\,g_{1}^{*}\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{g}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\,g_{2}&\\
&e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\,g_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}

(2.4)

(iv)

G=Q_{8}\circ D_{4}
, the central product of the quaternion group with the dihedral group of order 8, acting in the tensor product representation of the irreducible 2-dimensional representation of
Q_{8}
given by,

I=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix},\quad K=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}

(2.5)

with that of
D_{4}
given by,

r=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad f=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(2.6)

For example, one can take the matrices,

I\otimes r,\quad I\otimes f,\quad J\otimes r,\quad K\otimes r

(2.7)

as generators.

This is not yet a complete classification. In
[
2

the authors state that they suspect there are few (if any) other examples remaining to be discovered, see there for more discussion on this point. In the more recent works
[

3
Quotients of the Maximal Torus of
E_{8}

3.1
Classification of subgroups of
W(E_{8})
preserving a hyperKähler structure

In this section we describe a classification of the subgroups of the Weyl group of
E_{8}
that preserve a hyperKähler structure on
T^{8}
, the maximal torus of
E_{8}
.

We make use of the fact that
\operatorname{Spin}(8)
has three distinct
8
-dimenionsal representations
\pi_{i}:\operatorname{Spin}(8)\to\operatorname{SO}(V_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
so that the subgroups that preserve an
\operatorname{Sp}(2)
-structure of one orientation in
V_{1}
correspond to the subgroups that are the identity on some
3
-plane in
V_{2}
while the subgroups that preserve an
\operatorname{Sp}(2)
-structure of the other orientation in
V_{1}
correspond to the subgroups that are the identity on some
3
-plane in
V_{3}
.

The root system of
E_{8}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{8}={\mathbb{O}}
is usually described as

\Phi_{0}=\{\,{}\pm e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq 8\,\}\cup\{\,\tfrac{1}{2}(\epsilon_{1}\,e_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{8}\,e_{8})\,|\,\epsilon_{i}=\pm 1,\ \epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{8}=1\,\}.

Note that
|\Phi_{0}|=240
and that every
u\in\Phi_{0}
satisfies
|u|^{2}=2
.

For our purposes, though, it will be much more convenient
to invoke the ring of integral octonions
{\mathbb{D}}\subset{\mathbb{O}}

of Dickson and Coxeter (see appendix
A.1
), for then we can simply let

\Phi={\mathbb{D}}^{\times}=\{\ x\in{\mathbb{D}}\ |\ |x|^{2}=x\bar{x}=1\ \}.

\Phi
is also a root system of type
E_{8}
.
Since
|xy|=|x|\,|y|
for
x,y\in{\mathbb{O}}
, it follows that
\Phi

is a multiplicative subset of
{\mathbb{D}}
, one that includes the multiplicative unit
1
.
However, it must be emphasized that, because
{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}

is not associative,
\Phi
does not form a group under multiplication.

The Weyl group
W(E_{8})
of
E_{8}
is generated by the reflections
\rho_{u}

for
u\in\Phi
, which have the formula

\rho_{u}=-L_{u}\circ R_{u}\circ c=L_{u}\circ R_{u}\circ\rho_{1}=\rho_{1}\circ L_{\bar{u}}\circ R_{\bar{u}}

where
c=-\rho_{1}
is octonionic conjugation
c:{\mathbb{O}}\to{\mathbb{O}}
. (Note that
since
\rho_{1}\in W(E_{8})
and
-I_{8}\in W(D_{8})\subset W(E_{8})
, we have
c\in W
.)
In particular, since
{\rho_{1}}^{2}=1
, it follows that

\rho_{u}\circ\rho_{v}=L_{u}\circ R_{u}\circ L_{\bar{v}}\circ R_{\bar{v}}\,,

a formula that will be important below.

The order of
W(E_{8})
is known to be
696,729,600=2^{14}\cdot 3^{5}\cdot 5^{2}\cdot 7
. Hence, the order of
W^{+}=W(E_{8})\cap\operatorname{SO}(8)
is
348,364,800=2^{13}\cdot 3^{5}\cdot 5^{2}\cdot 7
.
W^{+}
is the group of linear transformations that are a product of an
even
number of the 120 distinct reflections
\{\,\rho_{u}\ |\ u\in\Phi\,\}
. The center of
W^{+}
is
\{\pm I_{8}\}
, and the
quotient
W^{+}/\{\pm I_{8}\}\subset\operatorname{SO}(8)/\{\pm I_{8}\}=\operatorname{PSO}(8)
is a simple group of order
174,182,400=2^{12}\cdot 3^{5}\cdot 5^{2}\cdot 7
.

Our goal is to find a way to locate subgroups of
W^{+}
that preserve a hyperKähler structure on
\mathbb{O}
. We will do this by exploiting
triality in
\operatorname{SO}(8)
(see appendix
A.2
to fix notation
and the basic properties of triality).

Let
\tilde{W}^{+}={\pi_{1}}^{-1}(W^{+})\subset\operatorname{Spin}(8)

denote the ‘binary even Weyl group’ of
E_{8}
. Since
W^{+}\subset\operatorname{SO}(8)

contains the center
\{\pm I_{8}\}
of
\operatorname{SO}(8)
, it follows that
\tilde{W}^{+}

contains the center of
\operatorname{Spin}(8)
. In particular,
\tilde{W}^{+}

contains
(I_{8},-I_{8},-I_{8})
. Because of the identity

\rho_{u}{\circ}\rho_{v}=L_{u}{\circ}R_{u}{\circ}L_{\bar{v}}{\circ}R_{\bar{v}}
,
it follows that
{\pi_{1}}^{-1}(\rho_{u}\circ\rho_{v})\in\tilde{W}^{+}

consists of the two elements

(\rho_{u}{\circ}\rho_{v},\pm L_{\bar{u}}L_{v},\pm R_{\bar{u}}R_{v})

for any
u,v\in\Phi
.
Hence, the set

\Gamma=\{\,(\rho_{u}{\circ}\rho_{v},L_{\bar{u}}L_{v},R_{\bar{u}}R_{v})\,|\,u,v\in\Phi\,\}\subset\operatorname{Spin}(8).

generates
\tilde{W}^{+}
.

It follows that
\pi_{2}(\tilde{W}^{+})\subset\operatorname{SO}(8)

is generated by
\{\,L_{u}L_{v}\,|\,u,v\in\Phi={\mathbb{D}}^{\times}\,\}

and preserves
\Phi={\mathbb{D}}^{\times}
. Hence it lies in
W^{+}

(which is the subgroup of
\operatorname{SO}(8)
that preserves
\Phi
).
Since
\pi_{2}(\tilde{W}^{+})
and
W^{+}
have the same cardinality,
it follows that
\pi_{2}(\tilde{W}^{+})=W^{+}
.
Similarly,
\pi_{3}(\tilde{W}^{+})=W^{+}
.

However, if
\Gamma\subset W^{+}
is a proper subgroup of
W^{+}
,
then it need not be the case that

\Gamma_{2}=\pi_{2}\bigl(\pi_{1}^{-1}(\Gamma)\bigr)

and
\Gamma_{3}=\pi_{3}\bigl(\pi_{1}^{-1}(\Gamma)\bigr)

are isomorphic to
\Gamma
.

Example 1
.

For example, suppose that
\Gamma
is the subgroup of
W^{+}

that fixes an element
u\in\Phi={\mathbb{D}}^{\times}
.
Since
W^{+}
acts transitively on
\Phi
, we may as well take
u=1
.
Now,
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{SO}(1^{\perp})
consists of symmetries
of the root system
1^{\perp}\cap\Phi
, which is known to be
isomorphic to the root system of
E_{7}
and is generated by
the
63
reflections
\rho_{v}
for
v\in 1^{\perp}\cap\Phi
,
i.e., the Weyl group of
E_{7}
, which is known to be the

E_{7}
.
Consequently,
\Gamma
is the group generated by
\rho_{v}{\circ}\rho_{w}

for
v,w\in 1^{\perp}\cap\Phi
.

It follows that
\Gamma_{2}
is the group generated by
L_{v}L_{w}

for
v,w\in 1^{\perp}\cap\Phi
. Via the identification
\Phi={\mathbb{D}}^{\times}
,
it is easy to show that
\Gamma_{2}
acts transitively on
\Phi
.
Moreover, since
-I_{8}\not\in\Gamma
,
it follows that
\pi_{2}:\pi_{1}^{-1}(\Gamma)\to\Gamma_{2}
is injective
and hence an isomorphism. Moreover,
\Gamma_{2}
acts preserving
a
\operatorname{Spin}(1^{\perp})
-structure, i.e., a
\operatorname{Spin}(7)
-structure, on
{\mathbb{O}}

(in addition to preserving the lattice
{\mathbb{D}}\subset{\mathbb{O}}
).

3.1.1
Subgroups preserving special structures

Generalizing the above example, we are going to be interested in
subgroups
\Gamma
of the even Weyl group that act as the identity on
a subspace of
{\mathbb{O}}
of dimension
1
(respectively,
2
, or
3
),
because these are the conditions that ensure that the corresponding group

\Gamma_{2}
or
\Gamma_{3}
acts preserving the lattice
{\mathbb{D}}

and a
\operatorname{Spin}(7)
-structure (respectively, a
\operatorname{SU}(4)
-structure
or a
\operatorname{Sp}(2)
-structure, since
\operatorname{Spin}(6)=\operatorname{SU}(4)
and
\operatorname{Spin}(5)=\operatorname{Sp}(2)
).

Suppose that a subgroup
\Gamma\subset W^{+}

acts as the identity on a subspace
S\subset{\mathbb{O}}
.
Fix a base
B\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}
for the root system
and let
F=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{8}\}\subset{\mathbb{D}}^{\times}

be the corresponding set of fundamental weights,
which span the extreme edges of the corresponding
fundamental Weyl chamber. Let
s\in S
be
a ‘generic’ element. After conjugation in
W^{+}
, we can
assume that
s
lies in the fundamental Weyl chamber.
Writing
s=s_{1}\,f_{1}+\cdots+s_{8}\,f_{8}
with
s_{i}\geq 0
,
we know that any element of
W^{+}
that fixes
s
must
fix every
f_{i}
where
s_{i}>0
. Thus,
\Gamma
must fix
each such
f_{i}
. The result is that, if
\Gamma
fixes
the elements of a subspace
S
, then it fixes the elements
of a (possibly larger) subspace
\bar{S}
that is spanned by
a set of fundamental weights.

Consequently, in classifying the subgroups of
W^{+}

that leave the elements of some subspace
S\subset{\mathbb{O}}
fixed,
it suffices to consider only the subspaces
S

that have a basis drawn from
\Phi={\mathbb{D}}^{\times}
.

In this case,
S\cap\Phi
is a root system
of rank equal to

,

have

,
\mathrm{A}_{2}
,
and
\mathrm{A}_{3}

•

Rank 2:

•

Since

acts transitively on
\Phi

are conjugate to

that preserves

.

For

and

Since the Weyl group of
\mathrm{D}_{6}
acts transitively on the roots,

\mathrm{A}_{1}\times\mathrm{A}_{1}\times\mathrm{A}_{1}
,

For
\mathrm{A}_{2}

Remark 1
.

Using our set of examples, the probability of finding a symplectically resolvable orbifold is less than 1%. It would be interesting to know how reasonable a measure this is for the probability of finding resolvable examples in the set of all compact hyperKähler 8-orbifolds.

For

denote left multiplication by
p

p

By Dickson’s Theorem, the subalgebra of

generated by any two elements is associative. Thus

(L_{p})^{2}=L_{p^{2}}\,,\qquad(R_{p})^{2}=R_{p^{2}}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_{p}\circ R_{p}=R_{p}\circ L_{p}\,,

although
L_{p}L_{q}\not=L_{pq}
, etc. for general

Define conjugation

to be the linear map
satisfying
c(1)=1
while

when

is purely imaginary.
Then
p\,\bar{p}=\bar{p}\,p=|p|^{2}
for all

(where
\bar{p}:=c(p)

which implies that, for all

,
we have
\langle u,v\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}(u\,\bar{v}+v\,\bar{u})
.

Another useful fact is the following:
When

is reflection in the hyperplane
perpendicular to

\rho_{u}(v)=v-2\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{|u|^{2}}\,u=v-\frac{(u\,\bar{v}+v\,\bar{u})u}{|u|^{2}}=-\frac{u\,\bar{v}\,u}{|u|^{2}},

since
(v\,\bar{u})u=|u|^{2}v
. When
|u|^{2}=1
,
since

, we have the identities

\rho_{u}\circ\rho_{1}=L_{u}\circ R_{u}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{1}\circ\rho_{u}=L_{\bar{u}}\circ R_{\bar{u}}\,,

and

, which will be useful below.

For any pair

, we have the identities
\langle x,yz\rangle=\langle x\,\bar{z},y\rangle=\langle\bar{y}\,x,z\rangle

which implies that
\mathrm{Re}(xy)=\mathrm{Re}(yx)
.

Moreover, even though the octonions are not associative, we have

so that the expression
\mathrm{Re}(xyz)
is unambiguous. (However, note

is not equal to
\mathrm{Re}(xzy)

Two special facts are noteworthy: First there is L. E. Dickson’s theorem that any subalgebra of
{\mathbb{O}}
generated by two elements is associative. Second, there is Moufang’s identity:

.

the maps

\operatorname{Spin}(8)

Setting

there is are automorphisms

[

]
and described by Brown in
[

. We then study the singular sets of the arising orbifolds to determine whether or not they admit symplectic resolutions. Firstly, we review the basics on space groups (we found the notes
[

useful) and outline the algorithm (following

B.1
Determining all space groups given a point group

Preliminaries on space groups:

Take
N\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote an affine transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
(N,t)
, acting as
(N,t)\cdot x=Nx+t
. Products and inverses are thus given by,

(N,t)(N^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(NN^{\prime},t+Nt^{\prime}),\qquad(N,t)^{-1}=(N^{-1},-N^{-1}t)

(B.1)

Define the
Euclidean group

\mathcal{E}_{n}
as the group of all affine mappings
(N,t)
such that
N\in\operatorname{O}(n)
. There is an obvious group homomorphism,

\displaystyle\pi:\;\;\mathcal{E}_{n}

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{O}(n)

\displaystyle(N,t)

\displaystyle\mapsto N

A subgroup
G\subset\mathcal{E}_{n}
is called a
space group
if it contains a maximal lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a subgroup (maximal meaning the basis of the lattice spans
\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In other words, the kernel
T:=\{(\mathbb{1},t)\in G\}
of the mapping
\pi|_{G}:G\to\operatorname{O}(n)
defines a maximal lattice,

L_{T}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}t_{i}\mid c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\;(\mathbb{1},t)\in G\right\}

(B.2)

which we will call the
translation lattice
. The image
\pi(G)
is called the
point group
of
G
and is isomorphic to
G/T
. Note that, importantly,
G
usually does not contain a subgroup isomorphic to its point group. This occurs when, for example,
G
contains an element
(N,t)
with
N\neq\mathbb{1}
such that
t
is not in the translation lattice.

Since
T
is the kernel of
\pi
, it is a normal subgroup of
G
and therefore the point group is a subgroup of the automorphism group
\pi(G)\subset\operatorname{Aut}(L_{T})
. As a consequence,
\pi(G)
is always finite since
\operatorname{Aut}(L_{T})
is. A matrix
N\in\operatorname{O}(n)
is an automorphism of
L_{T}
if
N
and
N^{-1}
send a basis of
L_{T}
to another basis of
L_{T}
, this can be summarised as,

\operatorname{Aut}(L_{T})=\left\{N\in\operatorname{O}(n)\mid NM_{gen}=M_{gen}C,\;\;C\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\right\}

(B.3)

where
M_{gen}
is the matrix whose columns form a basis of
L_{T}
. Note that the matrices
C
form an integral representation of
\operatorname{Aut}(L)
.

Since the point group acts on and preserves the lattice
L_{T}
, it is natural to write the space group and its point group with respect to the lattice basis
\{t_{i}\}
rather than the standard basis
\{e_{i}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In fact, this is necessary to facilitate implementing the algorithm we will describe later on a computer. Transforming to the lattice basis amounts to conjugating the whole space group by
M_{gen}
,

(N,t)\mapsto(N^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\left(M_{gen}^{-1}\;N\;M_{gen},\;M_{gen}^{-1}\;t\right),\quad\forall(N,t)\in G

(B.4)

This has the following consequences:

•

The translation lattice now becomes
\mathbb{Z}^{n}

•

The point group matrices
N^{\prime}
are no longer orthogonal and now lie in
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
. Instead, they preserve the intersection form
A=M_{gen}^{T}M_{gen}
, i.e.
(N^{\prime})^{T}AN^{\prime}=A
. This is how we retain the knowledge of our original lattice.

Defining a space group given a point group:

Let
P
be a subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
preserving the intersection form
A
of some lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\{N_{1},...,N_{p}\}
be a choice of generators of
P
. A set of vectors
\tau=\{t_{a},t_{b},...\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one vector for each element

, is called a
vector system
for
P
if and only if,

t_{ab}=t_{a}+a\;t_{b}\mod\mathbb{Z}^{n},\quad\forall a,b\in P

(B.5)

If we are given an abstract finite presentation of
P

\langle N_{1},...,N_{p}\mid R_{1},...,R_{r}\rangle

(B.6)

where each
R_{i}
is a word in the
N_{i}
that equals
\mathbb{1}
, then a vector system is completely determined modulo
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by the set of vectors
\{t_{N_{1}},t_{N_{2}},...,t_{N_{p}}\}
such that
t_{R_{i}}=\vec{0}\mod\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(where
t_{R_{i}}
is evaluated using the rule (
B.5
)).

A space group (in the lattice basis) is then defined by a point group
P\subset\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
preserving the intersection form
A
of some lattice and a vector system
\tau
. The generators of the space group are given by
(N_{i},t_{N_{i}})
. We consider two space groups
G
and
G^{\prime}
to be the same if they are
affinely equivalent
, meaning

G^{\prime}=(X,\eta)^{-1}\;G\;(X,\eta)

(B.7)

for some
(X,\eta)\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
. It is true that there are only finitely many space groups, up to affine equivalence, in each dimension and that two space groups are abstractly isomorphic if and only if they are affinely equivalent
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

The problem is then: given a point group, determine a representative of each affine equivalence class of space groups with that point group.

To this end, it is useful to define two vector systems
\tau^{\prime}=\{t^{\prime}_{a}\}
and
\tau=\{t_{a}\}
for a point group
P
to be

if there exists an

X\in N_{\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})}(P)=\{X\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\mid X^{-1}PX=P\}

(B.8)

and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

t_{a}^{\prime}=X\;t_{X^{-1}\,a\,X}+(\mathbb{1}-a)\eta

(B.9)

(this formula arises from conjugating
(a,t_{a})
by
(X,\eta)
). Let
\mathcal{V}_{P}
be a set of representatives of the classes of equivalent vector systems for
P
. Zassenhaus
[
26
]
showed that each
n
-dimensional space group with point group
P
is affinely equivalent to one and only one of the space groups built from
P
and some
\tau\in\mathcal{V}_{P}
. This means that each affine equivalence class has a unique distinguished representative given by a space group built from
P
and some
\tau\in\mathcal{V}_{P}
.

Thus, to determine a representative of all affine equivalence classes of space groups with a given point group
P
, we need only determine
\mathcal{V}_{P}
.

Brief outline of the algorithm:

We first define two vector systems to be
strongly equivalent
if they satisfy
(
B.9
)
with
X=\mathbb{1}
. This amounts to a shift of the origin. We denote the set of representatives of strongly equivalent vector systems by
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{P}

The algorithm to determine
\mathcal{V}_{P}
(the set of representatives of classes of equivalent vector systems) for a given point group
P
goes as follows (see
[
8
]
for details):

1.

Determine a finite presentation of
P
given by generators
N_{1},...,N_{p}
and relations
R_{1},...,R_{r}
and determine all the vector systems of
P
(i.e. all
\tau=\{t_{N_{i}}\}
such that
t_{R_{i}}=\vec{0}\mod\mathbb{Z}^{n}
).

2.

Find representatives of the classes of strongly equivalent vector systems.

3.

Compute the normaliser of
P
in
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})

N_{\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})}(P)=\{X\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\mid X^{-1}PX=P\}

and reduce the list of representatives of classes of strongly equivalent vector systems to a list of representatives of classes of equivalent vector systems.

For solving steps 1 and 2, observe that we can write
t_{R_{i}}
in terms of the
t_{N_{i}}
as,

t_{R_{i}}=\sum_{a=1}^{p}B(i,j)t_{N_{j}},\quad j=1,...,r

(B.10)

where each coefficient
B(i,j)
is an
n\times n
matrix. These coefficient matrices can be efficiently computed using GAP. One can combine these
pr
distinct matrices into a single
pn\times rn
matrix
B
and form the
nk\times 1
vector
\hat{t}
by concatenating all the
t_{N_{i}}
so that
t_{R_{i}}=\vec{0}
for
i=1,...,r
is equivalent to the matrix equation
B\;\hat{t}=\vec{0}\mod\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Then diagonalise
B
over the integers to obtain
D=PBQ
for
P,Q\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
. As explained in
[
8
]
, one can then simply read off the representatives of the strong equivalence classes: if
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},...,d_{q},0,0,...,0)
then the representatives of the strong equivalence classes are the
d_{1}\cdot d_{2}\cdot...\cdot d_{q}
vectors,

Q\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}/d_{1}\\
t_{2}/d_{2}\\
\vdots\\
t_{q}/d_{q}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad t_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}

(B.11)

If one is only interested in computing the possible singular sets that arise from quotienting
T^{8}
by these space groups then one can stop here. Indeed we had to do this for some examples as computing the normaliser of some point groups proved to take too long to justify (we will indicate which examples in the next subsection). However for all other examples we proceeded with step 3 for completeness. One can compute the normaliser in GAP using the function
NormalizerInGLnZ
. One can then compute the action of each generator of the normaliser using similar methods to the computation of
B
. Each generator acts as some permutation on the set
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{P}
. One must find the orbits under the group generated by these permutations and choose a representative of each orbit and the algorithm is complete.

For studying the singular sets of the orbifolds, we found the GAP crystallography package CRYST
[
15
]
to be an invaluable tool, in particular the function
WyckoffPositions
for computing the fixpoints of a group action modulo a lattice.

The HyperKähler Structure Preserved by the Groups
G_{i}

Here we provide for each group
G_{i}
a basis change matrix
B^{(i)}
, such that the matrices,

T^{\prime(i)}_{j}=(B^{(i)})^{-1}\;T^{(i)}_{j}B^{(i)},\quad j=1,2,3,4

(C.1)

preserve the standard hyperKähler structure on
\mathbb{C}^{4}
(where we choose complex coordinates
z^{i}=x^{2i-1}+ix^{2i}
in terms of the coordinates
x^{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{8}

\omega_{1}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4}dz^{i}\wedge d\bar{z}^{i},\quad\omega_{2}+i\omega_{3}=dz^{1}\wedge dz^{2}+dz^{3}\wedge dz^{4}

(C.2)

Note that after this basis change, the lattice preserved by the matrices has generator matrix,

M_{\text{gen}}^{\prime}=(B^{(i)})^{-1}\,M_{\text{gen}}

(C.3)
