Insights into the Mechanism of Installation of 5-Carboxymethylaminomethyl Uridine Hypermodification by tRNA-Modifying Enzymes MnmE and MnmG
Authors: Praneeth Bommisetti, Vahe Bandarian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolutionarily conserved bacterial proteins MnmE and MnmG (and their homologues in Eukarya) install a 5-carboxymethylaminomethyl (cmnm5) or a 5-taurinomethyl (τm5) group onto wobble uridines of several tRNA species. The Escherichia coli MnmE binds guanosine-5′-triphosphate (GTP) and methylenetetrahydrofolate (CH2THF), while MnmG binds flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and a reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH). Together with glycine, MnmEG catalyzes the installation of cmnm5 in a reaction that also requires hydrolysis of GTP. In this letter, we investigated key steps of the MnmEG reaction using a combination of biochemical techniques. We show multiple lines of evidence supporting flavin-iminium FADH[N5=CH2]+ as a central intermediate in the MnmEG reaction. Using a synthetic FADH[N5=CD2]+ analogue, the intermediacy of the FAD in the transfer of the methylene group from CH2THF to the C5 position of U34 was unambiguously demonstrated. Further, MnmEG reactions containing the deuterated flavin-iminium intermediate and alternate nucleophiles such as taurine and ammonia also led to the formation of the anticipated U34-modified tRNAs, showing FAD[N5=CH2]+ as the universal intermediate for all MnmEG homologues. Additionally, an RNA-protein complex stable to urea-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was identified. Studies involving a series of nuclease (RNase T1) and protease (trypsin) digestions along with reverse transcription experiments suggest that the complex may be noncovalent. While the conserved MnmG cysteine C47 and C277 mutant variants were shown to reduce FAD, they were unable to promote the modified tRNA formation. Overall, this study provides critical insights into the biochemical mechanism underlying tRNA modification by the MnmEG.

Introduction

Posttranscriptional modifications of tRNA are crucial in expanding the functional diversity of tRNA molecules and ensuring accurate decoding of genetic information during translation.1−3 Among the many tRNA modifications, those occurring at the wobble position (usually position 34) of the tRNA anticodon play a pivotal role in maintaining translational fidelity.4 Of the diverse array of tRNA modifications that occur at the wobble position, 5-methyluridine (xm5U)-derived modifications are required for the accurate translation of codons ending in A or G in the mixed codon family boxes.5 The first step in the biosynthesis of xm5U modifications in bacteria is the installation of a 5-carboxymethylaminomethyl (cmnm5) group onto C5 of the uridine by the enzymes MnmE and MnmG6−10 (Figure 1A). The cmnm5 group may be further converted to 5-methylaminomethyl (mnm5) by the bifunctional enzyme MnmC in bacteria12−15 (Figure 1A). MnmC(o) domain catalyzes the cleavage of the carboxymethyl of cmnm to generate 5-aminomethyl (nm5).15 Alternatively, MnmEG is also suggested to install nm5U in vitro and in vivo using ammonia as the substrate, depending on the growth conditions6,15 (Figure 1A). Methylation by the MnmC(m) domain leads to the formation of mnm5U.14,15 Additional C5-independent modifications, such as methylation at the 2′-hydroxyl group of the sugar16 or the insertion of sulfur/selenium at C2 of uridine,17−20 lead to an array of xm5(s/se)2U and xm5(s/se)2Um hypermodifications on tRNAArg, tRNAGln, tRNAGlu, tRNAGly, tRNALeu, and tRNALys (Figure 1A).11

The bacterial enzymes MnmE (or TrmE) and MnmG (or GidA) that catalyze the formation of cmnm5U have garnered considerable attention over the years due to their unique architecture and the complex array of components required for the reaction.6,7,21 MnmE is a GTPase that binds to multiple tetrahydrofolate (THF) derivatives,6,22−24 while MnmG is an oxidoreductase that binds FAD and NADH.6,25−27 In vitro, MnmE and MnmG are proposed to form an α4β2 complex that utilizes FAD, NADH, and K+ as cofactors, N5–N10-methylenetetrahydrofolate (CH2THF), glycine (Gly), and tRNA as the substrates, to catalyze the guanosine-5′-triphosphate (GTP) hydrolysis-dependent modification of tRNA.6,7,21,23 Notably, these proteins are conserved across prokaryotes and eukaryotes. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, MSS1 and MTO1 (homologues of MnmE and MnmG) are localized to the mitochondria, where they install cmnm5U34 on mitochondrial (mt) tRNAs for Lys, Glu, and Gln28 (Figure S2A). By contrast to their counterparts in other organisms, the human homologues hGTPBP3 and hMTO1 install a taurinomethyluridine (τm5U34) on mt tRNAs specific for Lys, Glu, Gln, and Leu29−31 (Figure S2B). Notably, in these enzymes, taurine is used as one of the substrates instead of glycine.29

Understanding the mechanistic details of the MnmEG reaction in vitro has remained challenging for over a decade due to the complexity of substrates and cofactors involved. Recently, our in vitro reconstitution efforts employing isotopically enriched THF derivatives demonstrated unambiguously CH2THF as the one-carbon donor of the methylene group attached to the C5 of the uridine in cmnm5U34.7 Moreover, NADH (or DTT) was shown to play a role in reducing MnmEG-bound FAD to FADH2, indicating that a reduced flavin cofactor is likely the starting point of the reaction.7 Based on these studies, along with its common ancestry with a FAD/folate-dependent tRNA methyltransferase (TrmFO),32,33 we have proposed a working mechanism that utilizes the FAD cofactor in covalent catalysis mediating the methylene group transfer from CH2THF to the base (Figure 1B–D).

In this mechanism, FADH2 was proposed to form a covalent adduct with the methylene group on CH2THF to form an iminium intermediate, which in turn is transferred to the uridine base and undergoes addition with the nucleophilic substrate (glycine) to the cmnm moiety (Figure 1B). In analogy to TrmFO, a Cys residue from the proteins has been proposed to play a key role in activating the uridine for methylene transfer34,35 (Figure 1C). Nevertheless, an alternate mechanism for activating uridine for nucleophilic attack that utilizes a lone pair on N1 on the base is also plausible36−38 (Figure 1D). A glycine moiety is then proposed to be appended to the methyleneU in MnmEG, whereas a hydride transfer to the methyleneU completes the methylation in the TrmFO system.39 The precise nature of the chemical steps driven by GTP binding and hydrolysis remains unknown.

This study investigated several key steps in the reaction catalyzed by MnmEG. The results provide indirect evidence for the existence of an iminium intermediate and direct evidence for the involvement of a flavin cofactor in the transfer of the methylene group from CH2THF to C5 of the uridine in a covalent manner. The formation of modified tRNA with alternate substrates, such as taurine, was also demonstrated here, which has implications for the mechanism of the human MnmEG homologues. Further, we identified a potential catalytically relevant protein-RNA complex that forms under the reaction conditions. Characterization of this protein-RNA complex, which forms without GTP, reveals that it is potentially a noncovalent association between MnmEG and RNA, with the region encompassing position 34 of the tRNA being protected. The possible roles of two evolutionarily conserved MnmG cysteines were also probed. The implications of these results in the context of postulated mechanisms are discussed.

Materials and Methods

Cloning of the Proteins Used in the Study

All of the enzymes required for recombinant DNA preparation and transformation were procured from New England Biolabs (NEB). The preparation of pET29a vectors pAY756 and pAY760 for producing E. coli proteins MnmE and MnmG has been described previously.7 To generate vectors containing mnmG C47A, C277A, and C47A/C277A variants, a site-directed mutagenesis (SDM) protocol was employed by using pAY760 as the template. Briefly, primers for SDM were designed using NEBaseChanger (https://nebasechanger.neb.com/) for use with Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase in PCR amplification. The primers used for PCR amplification are listed in Table S1, which was carried out using recommended annealing temperatures (64 °C for C47A and 62 °C for C277A) by NEBaseChanger for 25 cycles. The reaction mixtures were then treated with DpnI restriction endonuclease, T4 polynucleotide kinase, and T4 DNA ligase, following the manufacturer’s instructions. An aliquot of the ligation reaction was transformed into E. coli DH5α electrocompetent cells (NEB) and inoculated onto Lennox broth (LB) agar plates containing kanamycin (34 μg/mL). Several colonies from these overnight plates were cultured in 5 mL of LB broth containing 34 μg/mL kanamycin. Plasmid stocks were obtained with a GeneJET plasmid extraction kit (ThermoFisher) and sequenced by the University of Utah core facilities to confirm the presence of the desired mutation.

Expression of MnmE, MnmG, and Its Variants

The expression of MnmE and MnmG proteins was previously described in detail.7 The cysteine variants of MnmG used in this study were expressed similarly to the wild type (WT) MnmG. The plasmids for the respective proteins were transformed into E. coli BL21-DE3 phage T1-resistant electrocompetent cells (NEB), and colonies were obtained on LB plates maintained with kanamycin (34 μg/mL). A single colony was used to generate a primary culture of approximately 100 mL in LB medium for large-scale expression. Approximately 10 mL of the primary culture was added to eight 2.8 L Fernbach flasks containing 1 L of LB media and maintained with kanamycin. The cells were grown at 37 °C and 185 rpm to A600 of 0.4, at which point, Isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 100 μM) was added to induce the protein overexpression. Riboflavin (∼20 μg/mL) was added to the cell cultures to ensure that sufficient flavin was present to aid in the overexpression of MnmG variants. The induced cells were grown overnight (∼12–16 h) at 37 °C and 185 rpm. Cells were harvested by ultracentrifugation, frozen using liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Purification of MnmE, MnmG, MnmG C47A, MnmG C277A, and MnmG C47A C277A Proteins

The anaerobic purification of MnmE, MnmG, and its variants followed our previously described methods.7 The protein purifications were conducted in a Coy glovebox chamber (∼97% N2, ∼3% H2) under anoxic conditions. All of the buffers were stirred overnight in the glovebox to remove oxygen. Likewise, the plastics used for purification were stored in the glovebox to minimize the plastic-bound oxygen. The cells expressing MnmE or MnmG and its variants obtained from 8 L of culture (described above) were resuspended in ∼100 mL of buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0 and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The suspended cells were placed on ice and lysed by using a sonicator inside the glovebox. The resulting lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 36,000 × g for 20 min in airtight Oakridge tubes. The clarified lysate was transferred to the glovebox and loaded onto a ∼40 mL Q-Sepharose FF column connected to an AKTA FPLC (Amersham Biosciences) and pre-equilibrated with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0.

After the column was washed with the same buffer, the protein of interest was eluted using a linear gradient of 1 M KCl in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 buffer. Fractions containing the protein were identified using SDS-PAGE analysis and combined, and solid ammonium sulfate was added to a final concentration of 1 M. A small amount of protein that precipitated during this process was removed by centrifugation. The protein solution was loaded onto a ∼40 mL butyl Sepharose FF column (hydrophobic) washed with the loading buffer 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 M (NH4)2SO4, and eluted using a linear gradient of a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 without ammonium sulfate. The protein of interest was identified using denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis, pooled, and concentrated using a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) (Amicon) filter to a final volume of ∼18 mL. The proteins were desalted into a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 and 100 mM KCl using a manually poured Bio-Rad BioGel P6 DG (100–200 mesh wet) gravity desalting column inside the glovebox (void volume ∼17 mL). The desalted protein was further concentrated to ∼5 mL by using a 30 kDa MWCO filter and flash frozen in aliquots. Quantification was performed by using the Bradford assay with BSA as a standard. A representative SDS-PAGE analysis is shown in Figure S3A for the MnmE, MnmG, and its variants used in the study.

Preparation of E. coli tRNA Lys by In Vitro Runoff Transcription

The tRNA Lys from E. coli was prepared in vitro using the HiScribe T7 Quick High-Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences of template and primer DNAs are listed in Table S2. Briefly, 1 μg of gel-purified template DNA (Integrated DNA Technologies) was incubated with 1 μg of primer (HSC cores, University of Utah) and necessary reagents for in vitro transcription overnight at 37 °C. The reaction mixtures were then treated with 2 U of DNase I for 15 min at 37 °C. Following this, 250 μL of Qiazol (Qiagen) and 50 μL of chloroform (Fisher Scientific) were added per 50 μL of the reaction mixture. The mixtures were vortexed and centrifuged at 15,000 × g to extract the RNA into the aqueous layer. The top layer was removed, mixed with ∼2.5 volumes of ethanol, and incubated at −20 °C overnight to precipitate the RNA. The RNA pellet was obtained by centrifuging at 15,000 × g for 2 min and removing ethanol. The pellet obtained was dried under vacuum using a Savant spin concentrator, resuspended in 20 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, and 1 mM EDTA buffer, filtered using a Corning 0.45 μm centrifugal spin filter, and further quantified using a Nanodrop. Typically, 50 μL reaction mixtures with a 1 μg template-primer combination yielded 300–400 μg of RNA. A representative urea-PAGE (6%) analysis of the in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys is shown in Figure S3B.

Preparation and Characterization of FADH[N5=CD2]+

Synthetic coenzyme intermediate FADH[N5=CD2]+ was prepared using perdeuterated chloromethyl methyl sulfide (see below), and FADH2 following a previously reported method but with a few modifications.40 Briefly, a 1 mL solution of 0.5 mM FAD in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 was reduced by stirring with 10-fold excess sodium dithionite (5 mM) in the glovebox for 1 h. After the solution turned partially yellow or colorless, a 100-fold excess of ClCD2-S-CD3 (50 mM) was added and stirred at room temperature inside the glovebox for 2 h. After this, the excess ClCD2-S-CD3 was removed from the reaction mixture by extraction with an equal volume of dichloromethane three times by using a centrifuge to separate the layers. The reaction mixture was then characterized using HPLC-HRMS analysis (see the additional methods in Supporting Information) and UV–vis spectrophotometry (Agilent 8453). Aliquots were either directly used in the assays or flash frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after removing from the glovebox and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Preparation of Perdeuterated Chloromethyl Methyl Sulfide

Chloromethyl methyl sulfide was prepared following the previously reported procedures (Figure S4A).40,41 Briefly, a solution of ∼7.2 mL (100 mmol) of thionyl chloride (Sigma-Aldrich) in 10 mL of degassed dichloromethane (DCM) was refluxed under a nitrogen atmosphere for 15 min. To this, a solution of ∼6.7 mL of perdeuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6, 95 mmol) in 10 mL of DCM was slowly added under the nitrogen atmosphere at 60 °C. After the addition, the reaction mixture was further stirred at 60 °C for 15 min and then stirred at room temperature overnight under the nitrogen atmosphere. The DCM was removed by a rotary evaporator, followed by vacuum distillation of the resultant solution to give a slight, yellow-colored liquid compound. The authenticity of the product was verified by 13C NMR (Figure S4B) and compared to the previously reported values.40,41

In Vitro Reconstitution of the MnmEG Reaction

All reactions were conducted within a Coy anaerobic chamber under an atmosphere of ∼97% N2 and ∼3% H2. To minimize the presence of oxygen in the reactions, we stirred the reaction solutions, such as buffers (1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.0), salt solutions (500 mM MgCl2 and 2 M KCl), and RNase-free water, inside the glovebox for at least 24 h. RNase-free water was prepared by treating Millipore water with diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC, ∼1 mL/1 L) and autoclaving it to hydrolyze any remaining DEPC. Reagent stock solutions, including CH2THF (Schircks Laboratories), FAD, NADH, GTP, and glycine, were prepared in the glovebox in 50–100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0. Ammonium acetate and taurine stock solutions were prepared anaerobically in water. When necessary, the concentrations of these stock solutions were determined by using an Agilent 8453 UV–visible spectrophotometer inside the glovebox. The extinction coefficients for accurate concentration calculations were 25,000 M–1 cm–1 at 294 nm for CH2THF, 11,300 M–1 cm–1 at 450 nm for FAD, and 6220 M–1 cm–1 at 340 nm for NADH. The stock solutions were divided into small aliquots for immediate use, or the aliquots in sealed Eppendorf tubes were flash frozen outside the glovebox and stored at −80 °C until needed. Aliquots of tRNA substrates were prepared aerobically to minimize the contamination of RNases and stored at −80 °C. The added volume of RNA to the reaction mixtures constituted <5% of the total reaction volume to minimize dissolved oxygen.

In the reconstitution experiments, MnmE and MnmG were first preincubated at concentrations of 40–50 μM in a solution containing 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 to 150 mM KCl, and 5% glycerol for 30 min. The typical assay mixture consisted of 50 mM Tris-HCl, 5–10 mM MgCl2, 100–150 mM KCl, 0.5 mM CH2THF, 0.5 mM FAD, 0.5 mM NADH, 2 mM glycine, 2 mM GTP, 15–20 μg of in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys and 3 to 5% glycerol per 50 μL of the reaction mixture. The in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys was mixed with 0.05 vol of Murine (RNase inhibitor, NEB) prior to addition to the reaction mixtures. For reactions with total tRNA, a typical reaction mixture contained 0.2 mM unlabeled CH2THF, 0.2 mM FAD, 0.2 mM NADH, and 80–100 μg of total tRNA purified from ΔmnmEE. coli per 100 μL of the reaction mixture. The concentration was arbitrary in reactions containing FADH[N5=CD2]+, but the added FADH[N5=CD2]+ stock constituted up to 20% of the reaction volume. Additionally, in reactions containing FADH[N5=CD2]+, the reagents FAD, NADH, and CH2THF were omitted. The CD2THF used was prepared using our previously reported method,7 and the added CD2THF constituted up to 20% of the reaction volume. Taurine or ammonium acetate was included at a concentration of 20 mM without glycine. The reactions were initiated by adding the preincubated MnmEG to a final 2–4 μM concentration. For reactions with iodoacetamide(IA)-treated MnmEG, IA was added to a final concentration of 10 mM to the MnmEG preincubation mixture, and then MnmEG was added to the reaction assays to reach a final concentration of 4 μM. All reaction mixtures were incubated overnight (12–14 h) at 37 °C inside the glovebox.

RNase T1 Digestion and HPLC-HRMS Analysis of RNA Fragments

The reaction mixtures (50 μL) containing in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys were denatured by mixing them with 250 μL of Qiazol (Qiagen) and 50 μL of chloroform (Fisher Scientific). The RNA was then extracted into the aqueous layer by centrifugation at 15,000g for 2 min. The top aqueous layer was mixed with 3 volumes of ethanol and incubated at −20 °C overnight. The RNA was then pelleted by centrifugation at 15,000g for 2 min, and the ethanol was removed. The pellet was dried under vacuum in a Savant spin concentrator and dissolved in 60–70 μL of buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA and then incubated with 1 μL of RNase T1 (1000 U/μL, Thermo Scientific) at 37 °C for 60 min. The digestion reactions were filtered using Corning 0.45 μm centrifugal spin filters and subjected to HPLC-HRMS analysis using a Vanquish UHPLC (Thermo Fisher) instrument connected to a Q Exactive (Thermo Fisher) mass spectrometer. For details on the extraction and analysis of ΔmnmEE. coli total tRNA incubated with the enzymes, refer to the additional methods in the Supporting Information.

The RNase T1-digested tRNA Lys was injected onto a Hypersil Gold C18 column [particle size: 1.9 μM, dimensions: 2.1 mm (D) × 150 mm (L), Thermo Fisher] that was pre-equilibrated with 97.5% of buffer A containing 0.2 M hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) and 0.085 M triethylamine (TEA) in Optima water (Thermo Fisher), and 2.5% of buffer B containing 0.1 M HFIP and 0.042 M TEA in Optima methanol (Thermo Fisher). The flow rate was set at 0.2 mL/min. Separation was performed using a gradient of buffer B over 21 min (0–3.1 min: 2.5% B, 3.1–16.1 min: 2.5–35% B, 16.1–24.0 min: 100% B). Negative ion mode MS acquisition was carried out within a scan range of m/z 400–2100 at a resolution of 70,000. The mass spectrometer was operated with a sheath gas flow rate of 35, an auxiliary gas flow rate of 12, a source voltage of 4 kV, and a capillary temperature of 320 °C. Data analysis was performed by using Thermo Fisher Xcalibur, and the data were plotted by using Origin Graph.

Gel Extraction of the RNA-Protein Complex

The reaction conditions for probing the protein-RNA complex are listed in additional methods in the Supporting Information. Reaction samples intended for gel extraction were quenched with 2× urea loading buffer (Invitrogen) and analyzed using urea-PAGE (3.5–4%) at 120 V until the upper band dye xylene cyanol eluted the gel. Protein-RNA complex bands were detected by UV-shadowing at 302 nm after ethidium bromide staining (Figure S5). Gel pieces corresponding to these bands were excised, crushed, and placed in 1.5 mL LoBind Eppendorf tubes. The macromolecules in the gel pieces were eluted into solution by incubating them overnight at 4 °C on a rotator mixer in 540 μL of gel extraction buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA), 60 μL of 2× trypsin buffer (NEB) and 500 ng of trypsin ultra (NEB). In samples in which gel proteolysis was not required, trypsin and its digestion buffer were omitted, and gel pieces were incubated in 600 μL of gel extraction buffer. After overnight extraction, the tubes were centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was further filtered using a 0.45 μm Corning centrifugal spin filter to remove the remaining gel pieces.

The flowthrough was mixed with 2.7 volumes of cold ethanol and incubated at −20 °C overnight. The potential RNA pellet was collected by centrifugation and dried under a vacuum using a Savant speed-vac concentrator. The resulting white residue was resuspended in 50–60 μL of 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA buffer, and passed through a 0.45 μm Corning centrifugal spin filter to remove undissolved materials. The RNaseT1 digestion was carried out by adding 1 μL (∼1000 U, Thermo Fisher) and analyzed using the HPLC-HRMS method described earlier for the tRNA Lys fragments. In a few analyses where the complex was extracted without trypsin during the gel extraction, the proteolysis with 500 ng of trypsin was carried out after RNase T1 digestion of the extracted complex. For samples intended for reverse transcription (RT) experiments, the potential RNA pellet obtained after ethanol precipitation was dissolved in 0.05 mL of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and subjected to RT experiments as described later.

Results

FAD as a Methylene Transfer Agent

Evidence for Existence of an Iminium Intermediate:The first half of our working model for MnmEG suggests that the FAD bound to MnmG is reduced to FADH2, which further reacts with CH2THF bound to MnmE to form a FADH[N5=CH2]+ iminium species (Figure 1B). The reducing equivalents for the reduction of the flavin in MnmG are derived from NADH, though DTT can also be substituted in vitro.7 Moreover, MnmG can also reduce exogenously supplied FAD to FADH2 with NADH, even in the absence of MnmE (Figure S6A). We note that excess NADH could reduce FAD to FADH2 under anaerobic conditions42,43; however, the MnmG assays were carried out with equimolar concentrations of FAD and NADH. In the absence of MnmG, with equimolar NADH, the reduction of FAD is slow and no significant FADH2 forms in the 60 min time frame of the experiment (Figure S6B). Therefore, a reduced state of the flavin cofactor, FADH2, bound to MnmG, is likely the starting point of the reaction. Thus, experiments were carried out to probe the existence of a flavin-iminium species.

As an initial attempt, MnmE and MnmG were incubated with excess CH2THF, FAD, and NADH, followed by the addition of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) to quench the reaction and precipitate the proteins. The resulting supernatant was analyzed using HPLC-HRMS. We could not detect FADH[N5=CH2]+ directly, perhaps due to the acidic quench conditions destroying the intermediate.44 To stabilize the intermediate and simplify the analysis, the reaction mixtures were treated with either NaCNBH3 or NaCNBD3 to reduce FADH[N5=CH2]+ to the corresponding N5-methyl FADH (scheme in Figure 2A). The resulting mixtures were filtered through 10 kDa MWCO filters and subjected to HPLC-HRMS analysis. The extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) at m/z of 802.19 for N5-methyl FADH in the sample containing MnmEG, CH2THF, FAD, and NADH, and quenched with NaCNBH3 shows a peak at a retention time of 9.5 min (Figure S7). Similarly, peaks were observed in EIC chromatograms corresponding to 13C- or D-containing N5-methyl FADH when 13CH2THF, CD2THF, or NaCNBD3 were substituted for CH2THF or NaCNBH3 in the above experiments (Figure S7).

The MS spectra of the species eluting in Figure S7 are shown in Figure 2B. With CH2THF, FAD, NADH, and NaCNBH3 as the reagents, the N5-methyl FADH shows an m/z peak 802.19 consistent with its expected mass (* in spectrum a, Figure 2B). The mass spectrum of the N5-methyl FADH shifts by +1 Da with 13CH2THF, reflecting the incorporation of one 13C atom from the THF donor (* in spectrum b, Figure 2B). The inclusion of CD2THF led to an increase of +2 Da in m/z of N5-methyl FADH, showing the incorporation of two deuteriums from the THF donor (* in spectrum c, Figure 2B). Finally, substituting NaCNBH3 with NaCNBD3 for reduction resulted in a +1 Da shift in m/z of the corresponding species, consistent with one deuterium being incorporated from the reducing agent (* in spectrum d, Figure 2B). In control experiments, omitting NADH or NaCNBH3, EIC peaks consistent with the mass of N5-methyl FADH could not be observed (Figure S7). We also observed a background in these assays, corresponding to a stable N5-alkyl FADH derivative with m/z 1 Da less than N5-methyl FADH. This species is sensitive to input isotope labels (represented by # in Figure 2B). The identity of this peak was not explored further. Instead, we utilized a direct approach described below to show the intermediacy of the FADH[N5=CH2]+.

MnmEG Can Use Synthetic FADH[N5=CD2]+

To directly probe the intermediacy of the iminium species, a doubly deuterated isotopologue of FADH[N5=CH2]+, FADH[N5=CD2]+, was prepared and incubated with MnmEG. The synthesis of the FADH[N5=CD2]+ followed established synthetic protocols,40 starting from FADH2 obtained by reduction of FAD, followed by alkylation with perdeuterated chloromethyl methylsulfide (see Figure S8A for the synthetic scheme and Materials and Methods section for details). The doubly deuterated flavin-iminium species were analyzed by HPLC-HRMS and UV–vis spectrophotometry. The UV- and MS-detected HPLC traces shown in Figure S8B reveal two species, one corresponding to the FADH[N5=CD2]+ eluting at ∼8.3 min and another corresponding to FAD at ∼8.6 min. The major species, FADH[N5=CD2]+, exhibits an m/z of 802.19 (top panel, Figure S8C), which is within 0.5 ppm of the theoretical value expected for doubly deuterated FADH[N5=CD2]+. The minor species exhibits an m/z of 786.16, in line with the expected mass of the FAD (bottom panel, Figure S8C). The UV–vis spectrum of this sample (Figure S8D) exhibits an absorption maximum at ∼365–370 nm, consistent with the previous reports.40 The synthetic FADH[N5=CD2]+ sample was used without further purification to remove the minor FAD species.

In the first set of experiments, MnmEG was incubated with synthetic deuterated iminium intermediate, GTP, Gly, and total tRNA obtained from a ΔmnmEE. coli strain. Since the assays included the putative one-carbon intermediate, FAD, NADH, and CH2THF were omitted. A parallel reaction containing MnmEG, GTP, Gly, FAD, NADH, CH2THF, and total RNA from the ΔmnmE strain was also conducted. Control experiments were also carried out that omitted the synthetic intermediate or MnmEG. After incubation, the tRNA from the reaction mixtures was extracted, digested to nucleosides, and analyzed by HPLC-HRMS for the presence of the cmnm5s2U-modified base, as reported previously.7 As shown in the EIC traces in Figure 3A, the fully modified cmnm5s2U nucleoside was clearly observed to elute at ∼5 min when CH2THF was the methylene source. The MS spectrum of the species observed in the chromatogram shows a peak at m/z of 348.08, which is identical to that expected for the cmnm5s2U nucleoside (Figure 3B). In the presence of FADH[N5=CD2]+ as the methylene source, the peak shifts to 350.09 (Figure 3B) with a similar retention time (Figure 3A) for cmnm5s2U-d2. This peak was absent in samples where either FADH[N5=CD2]+ or MnmEG were absent (Figure 3A). The peak at m/z 348.19 was present in all samples and was insensitive to isotope labels (Figure 3B). Therefore, it was assumed as a background peak and not explored further.

While the experiments described earlier provide evidence for the appearance of cmnm modification in total tRNA, they do not provide any evidence that the modification occurs in the wobble position of the cognate tRNAs. To establish the modification site unambiguously, the experiments were repeated with in vitro transcribed RNA corresponding to tRNAUUULys from E. coli. Assays were carried out as described previously, except that MnmEG was incubated with in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys, GTP, Gly, and either FADH[N5=CD2]+ or FAD/NADH/CH2THF as the methylene source. Control experiments were also carried out with either FAD/NADH/CD2THF as the methylene source or MnmEG excluded. After overnight incubation, the tRNA was extracted from the reaction mixtures, and ethanol precipitated. The tRNA was resuspended into solution and treated with RNaseT1, followed by the HPLC-HRMS analysis of the fragments generated (Figure S9A).

The RNaseT1 digestion of in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys generates an oligonucleotide fragment, ACUUUUAAUCAAUUGp, where the underlined uridine is the site of hypermodification by the MnmEG. The TIC and mass spectra shown in Figure S9B,C are representative HPLC-HRMS analyses of the RNaseT1 digested tRNA Lys incubated with or without MnmEG. The unmodified ACUUUUAAUCAAUUGp elutes toward the end of the run (14.5–15.0 min) and is well separated from the rest of the fragments (Figure S9B, top panel). The MS corresponding to the peak eluting at 14.8 min exhibits a mass spectrum consistent with the presence of multiple charge states as expected from a multiply charged oligonucleotide (Figure S9C, top panel). In samples containing MnmEG, while the fragment with the modified base ACU[cmnm5U]UUAAUCAAUUGp was not well separated from the unmodified one (Figure S9B, bottom panel), the apparent differences in their mass spectra allow them to be readily identified (Figure S9C, compare top and bottom panels). The mass spectra of the unmodified and modified anticodon fragments in the absence and presence of enzymes are shown in Figure 3C, respectively. The envelope at a m/z of 792.42 is that of the unmodified anticodon fragment in the z = −6 charge state (Figure 3C).

In the reactions containing CH2THF or CD2THF as a methylene source, or when the THF donor was substituted with the synthetic iminium compound, a new envelope emerges between m/z of 806 and 809 corresponding to the z = −6 charge state of the modified product (Figure 3C). When the reaction is carried out with CH2THF, the new envelope has a monoisotopic peak at m/z of 806.93, which is within 5 ppm of that expected for the ACU[cmnm5U]UUAAUCAAUUGp (Figure 3D). However, with CD2THF or FADH[N5=CD2]+ as the methylene donors, the monoisotopic mass of the new envelope shifts by 0.333 in the z = −6 charge state and displays the expected mass for ACU[cmnm5U-d2]UUAAUCAAUUGp (Figure 3D). This difference in m/z of the monoisotopic product peaks of the z = −6 charge state envelope is consistent with an overall 2.008 Da increase in the mass of the product, which aligns with the incorporation of two deuteriums originating from the flavin-iminium intermediate or CD2THF. Therefore, the data suggest that the doubly deuterated methylene group from the synthetic flavin-iminium intermediate was transferred to C5 of the uridine in the modification. The previously presented data (Figure 3C,D) and the total tRNA data (Figure 3A,B) unambiguously establish the intermediacy of the iminium species in the reaction catalyzed by MnmEG.

Reaction with Alternate Substrates

Human homologues of MnmEG are known to use taurine, generating τm5U on mt tRNAs.29,30,45 One early in vitro reconstitution of the MnmEG reaction revealed ammonia as an alternate substrate, forming 5-aminomethyl (nm5) uridine on native E. coli tRNAs.6 In this context, the promiscuity of the E. coli MnmEG was tested by using taurine or ammonia as substrates instead of glycine to see if FADH[N5=CD2]+ could support the formation of τm5U- or nm5U-modified tRNA. Assays were carried out as described previously, where MnmEG was incubated with in vitro tRNA Lys, FAD, NADH, CH2THF, GTP, and Tau/NH4OAC. Parallel reactions were also carried out with FADH[N5=CD2]+ as the methylene source instead of the FAD/NADH/CH2THF.

After incubation with taurine or ammonia, the tRNA was extracted from the reaction mixtures, digested with RNase T1, and analyzed via HPLC-HRMS. MS-detected HPLC TIC elution profiles for tRNA modified with taurine and ammonia are shown in Figure S10. Figure 4A,B shows only the region of the MS spectrum that spans the mass envelopes for the modified and unmodified tRNA fragments observed with taurine and ammonia in the z = −6 charge state. As shown in Figure 4A,B, taurine and ammonia led to the formation of τm5U- and nm5U-modified tRNA Lys. The yields with Tau/NH4OAc were low (∼5–10% conversion at 20 mM Tau/NH4OAc) compared to cmnm5U-tRNA Lys (∼50–53% conversion at 4 mM Gly). The monoisotopic mass observed for nm5U- or τm5U-modified fragments of tRNA are within 5 ppm of the theoretical. Furthermore, in all cases, when a labeled analogue FADH[N5=CD2]+ was used, a shift of 0.33 Da was observed in the z = −6 charge state of the fragment of interest, reflecting an overall mass change of +2 Da (insets, Figure 4A,B). This observation is consistent with the incorporation of two deuteriums that originate from FADH[N5=CD2]+ onto the modified tRNA. These data prove mechanistic commonalities among the bacterial and human homologue systems.

Probing for the Existence of a Covalent Protein-RNA Complex

The results above implicate FADH[N5=CH2]+ as a key intermediate in the formation of cmnm5 (or τm5)-modified base by MnmEG and their homologues. Next, we investigated how the methylene moiety might be transferred to the base. Several uridine-modifying enzymes utilize a cysteine side chain to activate the C5 of the uridine for a nucleophilic attack35,46,47 (Figure 1C, e.g., BsTrmFO, TrmA—SAM-dependent methyl transferase). A Cys thiolate side chain from either MnmE or MnmG has been proposed to be involved in the nucleophilic attack at C6 of uridine to activate position C5 for a nucleophilic attack on the flavin-iminium species.34 However, an alternative model whereby the N1 assists the transfer of methylene without forming an enzyme-RNA covalent complex is also viable and has been reported in the literature for ThyX36,37 (Figure 1D). The formation of a stable complex between MnmEG and tRNA in our reactions was probed by first preactivating the MnmEG complex by incubating with excess CH2THF, FAD, and NADH, followed by removing the excess reagents and addition of in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys, GTP, and Gly in combinations. Aliquots from each reaction were removed at 2 and 30 min, analyzed by PAGE using 3.5–6% urea-TBE gel to resolve nucleic acids, and stained with ethidium bromide or SYBR Green II to visualize nucleic acids.

The PAGE gel (6%) stained with SYBR Green II shows no apparent bands in the resolving area when preactivated MnmEG was incubated with tRNA and GTP (Figure S11). The band corresponding to the input tRNA was visible compared with that of the migration of input tRNA Lys. However, we observed high molecular weight (HMW) complexes, as evidenced by the accumulation of RNA stain-sensitive species in the loading well (Figure S11). These species were absent when either tRNA or MnmEG was omitted, and GTP did not appear to be required for the band to form. Next, a similar set of reaction samples was subjected to 3.5–4% PAGE (7 M Urea—TBE) analysis to resolve the higher molecular weight species. Under these conditions, as shown in Figure 5A, the higher molecular weight species were dislodged from the loading well and observed within 2 min of incubation time. A band for input RNA can be seen compared to the starting input tRNA Lys (lane SR, Figure 5A). As with data shown in Figure S11, GTP was not required to form the high molecular weight species. A small amount of signal observed in the loading well in the absence of tRNA may correspond to nonspecific staining of SYBR Green II (RNA stain) toward the proteins. Further, the presence of excess CH2THF, FAD, and NADH in reaction mixtures did not affect the formation of this complex (Figure S12).

To probe the nature of this potential protein-RNA complex further, similar reaction samples as described earlier were prepared and analyzed using 3.5% TBE-PAGE but with applied voltage for an extended time to resolve the protein-RNA complex better (Figure 5B). Under these conditions, the putative protein-RNA complex migrates slower than a 1000 nt ssRNA strand (Figure 5B). The complex diminished when both GTP and Gly were present, suggesting that it was a catalytically competent intermediate (Figure 5B). A band cannot be observed for the input tRNA Lys since it elutes from the gel during the run. Treatment of preactivated and desalted MnmEG with 10 mM iodoacetamide before adding to tRNA also led to the formation of a distinct protein-RNA complex (Figure S13A). Iodoacetamide irreversibly alkylates the cysteine side chains, potentially eliminating their roles in the reaction.

Moreover, all of these protein-RNA complexes that respond to SYBR Green II and are located on a urea-TBE gel also respond to Coomassie blue staining, which is specific to the proteins (Figure S13B). The presence of input tRNA Lys was confirmed by extracting the complex from the gel and HPLC-HRMS analysis after RNase T1 digestion (Figures S14 and S15). In additional control experiments, size exclusion chromatography (SEC) confirmed that MnmEG forms a high molecular weight species resistant to 7 M urea (Figure S16), consistent with a stable complex in urea-PAGE analyses (Figure 5). These results collectively suggest that the complex has protein and nucleic acid components and that its formation may not require Cys residues.

RT Experiments To Probe the Mode of Complexation

The ability of this apparent protein-RNA complex to survive the harsh conditions employed in the urea-PAGE experiments was surprising. To obtain evidence that the protein(s) interaction is specific to the anticodon loop of tRNA Lys, we next probed the location of the interaction between the protein(s) and RNA by RT experiments. The RNA recovered from gel bands in the presence or absence of trypsin was subjected to RT experiments using the Protoscript II reverse transcriptase. The primers used for RT experiments were 5′-FAM-labeled (fluorescein), which provides a convenient means of locating the newly formed cDNA product using fluorescence detection in the urea-PAGE analysis of the RT reactions. Alternatively, the gels were analyzed by using ethidium bromide to visualize total nucleic acids.

During RT, a blockage in the path of the RT enzyme in processing RNA causes it to dissociate prematurely to generate abortive cDNA molecules, commonly termed RT stops. A working model to explain the potential cause of RT stops that may be observed in our samples is depicted in Figure 6A. If a protein is attached to position 34 of the RNA, an RT stop of ∼41–42 nt is expected. As shown in Figure 6B (*, lane 1), the RNA recovered from the gel in the absence of trypsin shows an RT stop that leads to abortive cDNA formation. Including trypsin in gel extraction would still leave a peptide fragment on the RNA if it was covalently attached. Surprisingly, the RT stops were not observed in the RNA sample recovered from the gel in the presence of trypsin (lane 2, Figure 6B). As expected, the starting tRNA Lys produces exclusively full-length cDNA (lane 3, Figure 6B), which was absent in no RT control (lane 4, Figure 6B). Staining with ethidium bromide reveals the ladder and shows that one of the RT stops was between 50 and 35 nt (Figure 6B, bottom gel). A similar profile was observed in additional replicate experiments shown in Figure S17, and the migration of the abortive cDNA stop between 50 and 35 nt matches the migration of a 41 nt DNA standard.

These results suggest that the RNA in the gel-extracted complex likely contains a blockage at the site of the position of modification (nucleotide 34), which was removed in the presence of trypsin. In both the postulated mechanisms shown in Figure 1C,D, a FADH–CH2–RNA covalent intermediate is proposed that could be a source of an RT stop. However, in one case, the protein covalently attached to RNA via the Cys residue could be an additional cause of an RT stop (Figure 1C). The absence of any stops in RT experiments of a trypsin-digested sample may support the hypothesis presented in Figure 1D, where the nitrogen on the base mediates the nucleophilic attack of C5 of uridine on the methylene group on FADH[N5=CH2]+ to give rise to a FADH–CH2–RNA intermediate that degrades when removed from the protein environment. Nevertheless, we were unable to find any MS evidence for the potential ACU[FADH–CH2–U]UUAAUCAAUUGp fragment or the potential peptide cross-linked ACU[XnCys-{FAD-CH2}U]UUAAUCAAUUGp in the analyses presented in Figures S14 and S15.

Potential Role of MnmG Cysteines in the Catalysis

The potential role of MnmG cysteines C47 and C277 in the reaction has been explored previously by in vivo complementation analysis of ΔmnmGE. coli,34 which shows that these two residues are necessary for producing the cmnm5s2U in vivo. However, there was no biochemical characterization of these variants. Further, the C47 and C277 residues were proposed as potential candidates for Cys-mediated covalent activation of the uridine, as shown in Figure 1C.34,35 Multiple sequence alignments show that C47 and C277 are highly conserved across prokaryotes and eukaryotes (Figure S18). To explore the potential covalent enzyme-RNA complex formation in the MnmEG reaction, we assessed the C47 and C277 site-directed MnmG variants in our experiments.

MnmG variants where either C47 or C277 was mutated to A, and a double mutant containing both substitutions, were constructed, expressed in E. coli, and purified under anaerobic conditions. In addition to plasmid sequencing to verify gene mutations, the modification(s) in the proteins were also verified by trypsin digestion and HPLC-HRMS. In each case, a tryptic digest fragment spanning the mutation sites could be identified and shown to have the expected Δm/z corresponding to the Cys → Ala substitutions (Figures S19 and 20). As with WT MnmG,7 the purified MnmG C47A and C277A single variants were also bright yellow, consistent with bound flavin. The UV–vis spectrum of the purified single-mutant variants after TCA precipitation aligns with the spectrum of a flavin cofactor (Figure S21A,B).

Likewise, the flavin cofactor bound to the single-mutant variants was determined as FAD by comparing it to a commercial standard in HPLC-HRMS analysis (Figure S22A). However, during purification, the MnmG C47A/C277A variant exhibited a significant loss of flavin cofactor. The UV–vis spectrum of the double mutation variant does not display any features corresponding to a flavin cofactor (Figure S21C), which was also reflected in the HPLC-HRMS analysis (Figure S22). The bound FAD stoichiometry was determined to be ∼1:4, ∼1:2.5, and ∼1:25 for MnmG C47A, C277A, and C47A/C277A variants, respectively, as compared to ∼1:5.5 observed for the WT MnmG using HPLC-HRMS analysis. Therefore, while the mutations of C47 and C277 individually did not lead to an appreciable loss of flavin in the purified protein, the C47A and C277A double mutant did not retain the flavin well.

MnmG alone can catalyze the reduction of FAD to FADH2 with reducing equivalents derived from NADH (Figure S6). Earlier in the article, we have shown that in the time frame of reduction assays, FAD remained oxidized when the MnmG was not present (Figure S6). Consistent with this, ∼3 μM of all the three MnmG variants (C47A, C277A, and C47A/C277A) reduce exogenously supplied FAD to FADH2 (∼60 μM) in 30 min when incubated with equimolar amounts of NADH (∼60 μM, Figure S21D). Therefore, mutation of the conserved residues does not impair the reduction of FAD, which is a key early step in the conversion of U to cmnm5U. When WT MnmG was replaced with the cysteine variants in the assays containing MnmEG along with CH2THF/FAD/NADH or FADH[N5=CD2]+ and GTP/Gly/tRNA, no cmnm5 formation was observed (Figure S23A–C). Control experiments with the WT MnmG conducted in parallel showed the formation of cmnm5U-modified tRNA. Interestingly, including 10 mM IA in MnmE and MnmG (WT), preincubation prior to addition to reaction assays also did not lead to the formation of the cmnm5U-modified tRNA (Figure S23D).

Next, as proposed in the literature, we investigated C47 and C277 for their potential roles in forming covalent enzyme-RNA complexes. In these experiments, WT MnmG was replaced with its variants, and MnmEG was first preincubated with excess of CH2THF, FAD, and NADH followed by tRNA addition to probe for the RNA-protein complex for which RT experiment indicated an interaction at position 34 on tRNA (Figures 5A,B and 6B). The RNA-protein complex forming in the absence of GTP, identified earlier in the article, was observed with all the MnmG variants (C47A, C277A, C47A/C277A) employed in the reactions (Figure S24). Moreover, we have already shown that preincubation of WT MnmEG with 10 mM IA prior to addition to the reaction mixtures also led to the formation of a protein-RNA complex, although its migration pattern differs from that of the IA-untreated protein-RNA complex (Figure S13). These results make it unlikely that C47 and C277 form a covalent protein-RNA complex.

Discussion

In bacterial tRNAs, several wobble uridines are altered to xm5U derivatives like mnm5s2U and cmnm5s2U (Figure 1A), which recognize codons ending with A or G in mixed codon family boxes.5 The first step in the xm5U bacterial pathway involves the modification of U to cmnm5U by the MnmE-MnmG complex.6,7,9,10,21 These proteins are conserved in eukaryotes, and mutations in the corresponding enzymes/RNA that lead to the absence of a related modification in human mt tRNAs can lead to neurological disorders such as MELAS and MERRF.48−50 Despite recognizing bacterial genes involved in cmnm5U biosynthesis over two decades ago9,10 and the initial in vitro reconstitution over a decade ago,6 understanding the molecular mechanism of the MnmEG reaction has proven challenging.

The complexity of MnmEG (and their homologues) arises from the intricate interplay between MnmE, which binds CH2THF and GTP, and MnmG, which binds FAD and NADH, and their collective actions driven by GTP hydrolysis5,6,21,24−26 (Figure 7A,B). Additionally, the sensitivity of the reaction components to oxygen necessitates anaerobic conditions during in vitro studies. In our earlier studies, we established an anaerobic in vitro system to show CH2THF as the substrate and to examine the involvement of reducing equivalents in the MnmEG reaction.7 Drawing on the findings and the shared common ancestry of MnmG with TrmFO,32 a FAD/folate-dependent methyltransferase, we proposed a mechanism for the MnmEG reaction (Figure 1B–D). This mechanism involves the use of FADH2 to transfer the methylene group from CH2THF onto C5 of U34 on the tRNA in a covalent manner (Figure 1B–D). A similar methylene transfer process was observed in BsTrmFO, which is responsible for installing a C5-methyl on U54 of several tRNAs in Gram-positive bacteria.33,35,40,51

When we probed for the existence of FADH[N5=CH2]+ iminium, a key postulated intermediate in the reaction, we could not detect its presence under the conditions used, although such a species was reported as stable in the TrmFO system.33 Nevertheless, we identified small amounts of reduced N5-methyl FADH when the reducing agent NaCNBH3 was used to quench the reaction mixtures. The observation was supported using isotopically labeled CH2THF (D2 or 13C) derivatives and NaCNBD3. Additionally, we demonstrated that introducing synthetically prepared FADH[N5=CD2]+ in MnmEG reactions with native total tRNA or in vitro transcribed tRNA Lys leads to the formation of cmnm5(s2)U modification with two deuteriums, consistent with FADH[N5=CH2]+ as a viable intermediate in the MnmEG reaction.

Small amounts of MnmEG activity with either taurine or ammonia were shown previously in two independent studies.6,45 Further, under taurine depleting conditions, glycine was shown to be incorporated into mt tRNAs as cmnm5U34 in vivo in human cell lines through the reaction catalyzed by human homologues MTO1 and GTPBP3.45 In our in vitro experiments, substituting glycine with taurine or ammonia in MnmEG reactions with a synthetic flavin-iminium intermediate showed FADH[N5=CH2]+ as the carbon source for τm5U or nm5U installation. Since human homologues of MnmEG are taurine-utilizing enzymes,29 this observation supports the intermediacy of the FADH[N5=CH2]+ in this class of enzymes from all kingdoms of life (Figure 7B,C).

The use of synthetic flavin-iminium FADH[N5=CH2]+ was first demonstrated with TrmFO and ThyX in their respective reactions.40 In addition to TrmFO and MnmEG, ThyX found in human pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis is another well-characterized protein that carries out FAD/folate-dependent methyl transfer onto 2′-deoxyuridine-5′-monophosphate (dUMP) to generate 2′-deoxythymidine-5′-monophosphate (dTMP).52,53 ThyX was also shown to work with a synthetic flavin-carbinolamine (FADH[N5–CH2OH]) in carrying out the methylation of dUMP, presumably because of its equilibrium with flavin-iminium.54 While bound FADH[N5=CH2]+ is implicated in MnmEG, its theoretical equilibrium with a potential FADH[N5–CH2OH] intermediate at the active site cannot be excluded based on the precedent observed in ThyX.54 Although MnmEG uses flavin for methylene transfer, the transfer is coupled to an amino acid attack to install the hypermodification (Figure 7C). Thus, subtle differences may exist between these systems (MnmG, TrmFO, and ThyX) that utilize a flavin cofactor to mediate methylene group transfer.

Following the formation of FADH[N5=CH2]+, the next key step in the MnmEG reaction involves transferring the methylene group from FADH[N5=CH2]+ to the C5 of the U34 on tRNA. The most commonly postulated mechanism utilizes a Cys thiolate side chain on either of the proteins to mediate the transfer, which could lead to the formation of a covalent enzyme-RNA complex35,46 (Figure 1C and Hypothesis II, Figure 7C). In TrmFO, two conserved cysteines—C53 and C226—are shown as necessary for catalysis, later identified as the residue involved in the covalent activation of uridine for the methylene group transfer.35 Nonetheless, an alternative mechanism that relies on N1 on the uridine base to mediate the transfer would lead to a noncovalent protein-RNA complex (Figure 1D and Hypothesis I, Figure 7C) and has been reported for ThyX.36−38 The noncovalent activation of uridine by N1 nitrogen necessitates polarization of a deprotonated N3 by positively charged residues at the active site of ThyX.37,38 Further, a dUMP-CH2–flavin (derivative) intermediate was trapped in the ThyX reaction that is not covalently attached to the protein.36 Nevertheless, both postulated mechanisms for MnmEG utilize a flavin-uridine adduct where the flavin cofactor is covalently linked to the C5 of the U34 via a methylene group (Figure 7C), similar to those observed for TrmFO and ThyX.35,36

When probed for the presence of a protein-RNA complex in our assays, we observed a supershifted band that migrates slower than a 1000 nt ssRNA under the low-resolution urea-PAGE gels (3.5–4%). The complex was observed in the absence of GTP/Gly and responded to RNA and protein stains. The nature of the protein-RNA complex identified was probed through elution into solution and a series of protease or nuclease digestions, followed by HPLC-HRMS analysis and RT experiments. HPLC-HRMS analyses unambiguously established the presence of tRNA Lys in the complex. However, we could not find MS evidence for either ACU[FADH–CH2–U]UUAAUCAAUUGp fragment or the potential peptide cross-linked ACU[XnCys-{FAD-CH2–U}]UUAAUCAAUUGp supporting either hypothesis (I or II, Figure 7C). RT experiments indicated that an unidentified entity blocked the U34 region on the RNA, which was not present when trypsin was included in the gel extraction of the protein-RNA complex. Moreover, a protein-RNA complex was observed when the enzymes were pretreated with IA, although distinct from the initially identified protein-RNA complex. These observations suggest that the complex involves relatively strong noncovalent interactions or is covalent but sensitive and could not withstand the workup required for the HPLC-HRMS experiments.

The presence of the RT stops near the modification site strongly supports the notion that complex formation occurs near the modification site. MnmG has been identified as the primary tRNA-binding partner in the reaction,34 and two MnmG dimer forms (I and II) have been reported for Aquifex aeolicus MnmG homologue with flavin cofactor bound.34 In both dimer forms analyzed, the MnmG cysteines C47 and C277 are located at ∼ 5 and 15 Å away, respectively, from the FAD (E. coli numbering, Figure S25)34 and were proposed as potential candidates for the covalent activation uridine.34,35 In our assays, the MnmG C47A, C277A, and double mutant variants could reduce exogenously supplied FAD, although they were all catalytically inactive in promoting the cmnm5U formation. Moreover, the HMW complex that is responsive to RT experiments, identified in urea-PAGE analyses, could also be seen with single- and double-mutant variants. Another conserved cysteine, C242, is also within 15 Å from the FAD-binding site34 (Figures S18 and S25), but its role remains to be established.

An alternative paradigm for activating the base takes advantage of the N1 on the uridine base, although such a mechanism may require uridine polarization by positively charged residues at the active site.36−38 A few positively charged residues (R275, K283, and K286, E. coli numbering) important for tRNA-binding are found close to the FAD-binding site in both reported MnmG dimer forms (Figure S25). R275 is conserved across all MnmG homologues (Figure S18), and positions 283 and 286 are conserved as positively charged residues (K or R, Figure S18). A triple R275A/K283A/R286A AaMnmG homologue mutant was shown to weakly bind the in vitro transcribed tRNA substrate,34 and each of the individual mutants displayed significantly reduced in vivo activities.34 These three residues are part of a mobile segment observed at ∼245–295 region,27 where C277 is also located (E. coli numbering) and are potential candidates for uridine polarization. Our data do not explicitly address this possibility, and it remains a plausible mode for the MnmEG.

It is important to note that both proteins MnmE and MnmG are required for the reaction, and how tRNA may be accommodated at the combined active site is unclear. The binding and hydrolysis of GTP by MnmE are thought to drive the conformational changes required for the reaction.8,21,24,57,58 A low-resolution small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) study proposes a hetero α4β2 catalytic complex between four MnmE and two MnmG molecules driving the cmnm modification per two tRNA molecules.21 However, the precise chemical nature of the steps involved in pre-, during, or post-GTP hydrolysis remains unknown.

Conclusions

Overall, the MnmE/MnmG complex represents a unique enzyme system in which a flavin cofactor, FAD, in MnmG mediates the transfer of a methylene group from CH2THF on MnmE to the uridine, and GTP turnover by MnmE drives the enzymatic reaction (Figure 7). The intermediacy of the flavin-iminium intermediate is likely conserved among MnmEG homologues, including humans (Figure 7). Likewise, hydrolysis of GTP, which is thought to drive the conformational changes and communication between the subunits, is also maintained by prokaryote and eukaryote proteins.31,45 Advanced structural investigations (e.g., cryoelectron microscopy) of the protein-tRNA complex prior to and during GTP hydrolysis in combination with their respective cofactors would shed light on the complete mechanism of the reaction. The results reported in this study provide a biochemical architecture for the structural basis of the MnmEG reaction.
