Evaluating Telugu Speech Perception Measures for Hearing-Aid Benefit: Rationale for Developing Telugu Speech-in-Noise Tests
Authors: S. B. Rathna Kumar, Mendem Bapuji, P. Phani Krishna, Bandla Lenin Babu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:We examined how Telugu conventional and high-frequency word lists captured aided speech-perception benefit under conventional amplification and nonlinear frequency compression in older adults with high-frequency hearing loss.Methods:Thirteen elderly patients with acquired bilateral symmetrical sloping HFHL with postlingual onset served as participants. Speech perception was indexed through SIS (%) obtained with CWLs and HFWLs under quiet listening and +5 dB SNR conditions.Results:The patients obtained comparable SIS (%) (P > 0.05) for CWLs in the conventional and NLFC strategies. However, they received significantly lower SIS (%) (P < 0.05) in the conventional strategy for HFWLs than for CWLs. Likewise, they obtained comparable SIS (%) (P > 0.05) for CWLs and HFWLs in the NLFC strategy. However, they received significantly greater SIS (%) (P < 0.05) with the NLFC compared to the conventional strategy. Across hearing-aid strategies and word-list types, noise consistently reduced SIS (%) relative to quiet listening.Conclusions:The CWLs overestimated patients’ speech perception abilities and their performance with the conventional strategy. Conversely, the HFWLs identified genuine speech perception challenges, in addition to recognizing some of the benefits of the NLFC strategy. However, tests explicitly designed to evaluate speech-in-noise perception abilities, along with cognitive function assessments, need to be employed to provide evidence-based hearing validation.

INTRODUCTION

Speech is a broadband signal typically distributed over a frequency range of 300–6000 Hz.[1] Hence, one of the prime purposes of speech perception assessment should be to quantify an individual’s ability to perceive speech across a wide frequency range. The audiogram captures not only the severity of hearing loss but also how that loss is distributed across frequencies. When thresholds are comparably elevated across the audiometric range, the loss is commonly described as flat-frequency hearing loss (FFHL). Greater threshold elevation at either the lower or higher end of the frequency range is described as “low-frequency hearing loss (LFHL) or high-frequency hearing loss (HFHL)”, respectively.[2] Speech-perception materials should therefore be selected with the listener’s audiometric configuration in mind so that actual speech-perception challenges can be identified accurately.

Speech perception tests have been developed in some Indian languages to measure the speech identification score (SIS) as a percentage (%).[345] By tradition, they are called phonemically balanced or conventional speech perception tests. They consist of word lists, each constituting both voiced and voiceless consonants of the concerned language, with some predetermined number of occurrences. Hereafter, they are referred to as conventional word lists (CWLs). While CWLs are operational for describing speech perception abilities in relation to hearing loss, their applicability has been validated, especially for assessing speech perception in individuals with FFHL.[2] They are constrained in determining the genuine communication challenges individuals with hearing loss experience across configurations other than FFHL. Therefore, specific speech-perception difficulties individuals face due to comparatively greater hearing loss at low frequencies (e.g., LFHL) or high (e.g., HFHL) frequencies might remain hidden when CWLs are used alone.

HFHL is one of the most challenging hearing-loss configurations encountered by audiologists. One of the primary challenges individuals with HFHL encounter is difficulty perceiving speech sounds that contain spectral energy, particularly in high-frequency regions.[6] Hence, performing a speech perception assessment using speech stimuli with predominantly high-frequency spectral energy would be the most effective way to address the genuine challenges in speech perception experienced by individuals with HFHL. Speech perception tests used to measure SIS (%) in individuals with HFHL include word lists with a greater proportion of voiceless consonants. This is because, in contrast to voiced consonants, voiceless consonants have significant spectral energy located at higher frequency speech regions.[7] Hereafter, they are referred to as high-frequency word lists (HFWLs). Concerning English, Gardner[8] developed two HFWLs with 25 monosyllabic words in each list, with 100% voiceless consonants, while Pascoe[9] developed one HFWL with 50 monosyllabic words, in which 63% are voiceless consonants. Attempts have been made to establish HFWLs in some Indian languages.[101112]

Telugu, a major Dravidian language, is spoken widely in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. In Telugu, two word lists, each containing 25 compound words, were developed to measure the speech recognition threshold.[13] In addition, four CWLs with 25 disyllabic words in each list[14] and two HFWLs with 25 disyllabic words in each list[15] for measuring SIS (%) were developed. Among them, HFWLs were found to be more suitable speech stimuli than CWLs for representing the genuine communication difficulties individuals encounter due to HFHL. An investigation of aided speech-perception outcomes in older Telugu-speaking listeners with digital hearing aids, using Telugu CWLs as test stimuli, highlighted the importance of considering HFWLs alongside CWLs to assess the actual benefits of hearing aids, given the sloping HFHL commonly observed in elderly patients.[16] Further, HFWLs would be more suitable speech stimuli than CWLs to assess the benefits of hearing aids that employ frequency-lowering strategies, explicitly designed to enhance speech perception in individuals with HFHL, “including nonlinear frequency compression (NLFC), linear frequency transposition, and frequency translation”.[15]

Bukya et al.[17] examined the applications of Telugu speech perception tests (CWLs and HFWLs) to compare speech-perception benefit across conventional amplification and NLFC settings in an elderly patient with a sloping HFHL. The results suggested that NLFC made high-frequency speech cues more available to the listener than the conventional strategy. In addition, they reported that HFWLs are more responsive than CWLs in identifying the speech-perception advantage associated with NLFC and the genuine speech perception challenges individuals encounter due to HFHL. Because the earlier report was based on one case, its findings could not be readily extended to a wider clinical group.[17] Consequently, these findings indicate the need to examine aided speech-perception benefit in a larger group of elderly Telugu-speaking participants using Telugu speech perception tests.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In older adults, hearing sensitivity often declines more sharply at higher frequencies. While conventional hearing aid technology may not provide sufficient high-frequency energy in speech for listeners whose hearing loss is more pronounced at high frequencies[18], the NLFC strategy, compared to other frequency-lowering strategies, can shift otherwise inaccessible high-frequency cues into a frequency region that remains usable for the listener.[19] The HFWLs would be particularly suitable stimuli for detecting benefit from hearing aids that employ the NLFC strategy, compared with CWLs.[15] This study examined the utility of Telugu CWLs and HFWLs for measuring aided speech perception under conventional and NLFC strategies among elderly patients with sloping HFHL. This behavioural study used non-invasive data collection methods. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and ethics approval was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the University of Hyderabad vide IEC No: UH/IEC/2024/232. The following methods were used to achieve the study aim. The study activities, from recruitment through analysis, were completed within a six-month period. Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted from January 2025 to July 2025. Data analysis was completed following the data collection phase.

Participants

Participants were recruited using a stratified sampling technique during the defined study period. All participants were evaluated through case-history review, pure-tone audiometry, and immittance testing. Special attention was given to ruling out occupational and recreational noise exposure to reduce the chance that unrecognised noise-related hearing changes influenced the findings. The participants in the study were aged 66–72 years, had bilateral, sloping HFHL, postlingual hearing loss with gradual onset, used hearing aids with the NLFC strategy, and were native speakers of Telugu. Participants with unilateral or asymmetrical hearing loss, middle-ear pathologies, monaural hearing aid users, and a history of ototoxicity, noise exposure, or neurological disorders were excluded from the study. Finally, thirteen elderly patients (nine male and four female) were included in the study. Identifying participants who met highly specific inclusion criteria while maintaining sample uniformity significantly reduced the number of eligible participants during the recruitment period. Nonetheless, the study employed a within-subjects experimental design, in which each participant served as their own control. Such designs reduce interindividual variability and enable meaningful analysis with a smaller sample size than in between-group studies. Hence, the study can be interpreted as an exploratory investigation within a well-defined homogeneous subgroup.

Hearing Aids

The participants used a pair of receiver-in-the-canal hearing aids from the same manufacturer (Phonak Lumity 30 R RIC, Sonova Group, Stäfa, Switzerland), which could be optimized for both conventional and NLFC strategies. The participants’ hearing aids had already been programmed to determine the NLFC cutoff and compression settings for the NLFC strategy using the National Acoustic Laboratories-Nonlinear version 2 (NAL-NL2) fitting strategy. The NAL-NL2 is a hearing-aid fitting prescription that prescribes gain to support “speech intelligibility without exceeding a comfortable loudness level for the user”.[20] The participants regularly used the hearing aids with the NLFC strategy. Hence, the NLFC strategy settings were unchanged during the test sessions. However, a NOAH-compatible programming platform from the Hearing Instrument Manufacturers’ Software Association (HIMSA)[21] and Hi-Pro programming interface were used to switch the hearing-aid setting from the NLFC to the conventional strategy and fit the hearing aids in that strategy. Additionally, all other hearing aid fitting parameters, such as feedback suppression, noise reduction, and directional microphone features, were turned off during the test sessions. None of the participants underwent listening training using hearing aids with either a conventional or an NLFC strategy before the study.

Speech Perception Tests

Telugu speech perception tests, including CWLs[14] and HFWLs,[15] served as stimuli for deriving SIS (%), which was used as the behavioural index of speech perception. CWLs and HFWLs included words that were familiar to Telugu speakers and acceptable within the relevant linguistic and sociocultural context. The CWL set comprised four lists, each containing 25 CVCV disyllables. Each CWL was constituted with 64% of voiced consonantal phonemes, and the remaining consonants were voiceless ones. The HFWL materials included two original 25-word CVCV disyllabic lists, with additional randomised versions prepared for testing. Each HFWL was constituted with 84% of voiceless consonantal phonemes, and the remaining consonants were voiced ones. Previous validation work showed that the lists had acceptable reliability, validity, and comparable difficulty for measuring SIS (%) among native speakers of Telugu. Each HFWL was randomly generated once to create four HFWLs, that is, HFWL-1, HFWL-2, HFWL-3 (created by randomly generating HFWL-1), and HFWL-4 (created by randomly generating HFWL-2).

Procedure

Participants underwent audiological evaluation and hearing-aid programming in a sound-treated double-suite room, and ambient noise was verified against “ANSI/ASA S3.1-1999 (R2018)” limits.[22] Pure-tone and speech measures were obtained using a calibrated Maico MA 53 (Maico Diagnostic GmbH, Berlin, Germany) audiometer, while middle-ear status was checked with a calibrated GSI Tympstar Pro (Grason-Stadler, Minnesota, USA) immittance system. Speech perception was evaluated in terms of SIS (%) using four CWLs and four HFWLs across eight test conditions. (1) hearing aids in conventional strategy using CWL-1 in quiet listening condition; (2) hearing aids in conventional strategy using CWL-2 in +5 dB SNR listening condition; (3) hearing aids in conventional strategy using HFWL-1 in quiet listening condition; (4) hearing aids in conventional strategy using HFWL-2 in +5 dB SNR listening condition; (5) hearing aids in NLFC strategy using CWL-3 in quiet listening condition; (6) hearing aids in NLFC strategy using CWL-4 in +5 dB SNR listening condition; (7) hearing aids in NLFC strategy using HFWL-3 in the quiet listening condition; and (8) hearing aids in NLFC strategy using HFWL-4 in +5 dB SNR listening condition.

Stimuli were delivered from a loudspeaker positioned 1 m in front of the listener at 0° azimuth. The most comfortable level for each patient was determined using the bracketing method, with continuous discourse (running speech) as the stimulus. The stimuli were played via a computer routed through a calibrated dual-channel audiometer. Testing was performed at each participant’s most comfortable listening level in both quiet and noise with bilaterally fitted hearing aids. The noise constituted four-talker babble, recorded while two male and two female speakers read a passage. The speech and babble tracks were mixed at +5 dB SNR in Adobe Audition 3.0 (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA). A participant-masked design was established, in which the participant was unaware of the hearing aid settings to prevent bias regarding the relative benefits of different hearing aid strategies. To counteract any order and practice effects, the order of testing conditions was also randomized. Participants were given short breaks as needed. Oral open-set responses were recorded. Before formal data collection, participants completed a brief practice session in quiet and noise using ten CVCV words not included in the test lists.

Scoring

A score of 0 or 1 was assigned for wrong and correct responses, respectively. The number of correct responses was converted to a percentage, designated SIS (%). The SIS (%) was established separately for each participant at each testing condition.

Analysis

For each of the eight conditions, SIS (%) was summarised using the mean and standard deviation. Normality was checked for each comparison using descriptive indices and the Shapiro-Wilk test. A stricter criterion was applied, whereby skewness and kurtosis values within ±1 were considered indicative of normality. All datasets met this criterion. The Shapiro-Wilk results were non-significant (P > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric comparisons. Paired-samples t-tests were then used to compare mean SIS (%) across the planned within-participant contrasts. The following paired differences in mean SIS (%) were calculated between testing conditions: conventional vs. NLFC strategies for CWLs in quiet listening conditions; conventional vs. NLFC strategies for CWLs in noisy listening conditions; conventional and NLFC strategies for HFWLs in quiet listening conditions; conventional and NLFC strategies for HFWLs in noisy listening conditions; CWLs vs. HFWLs in the conventional strategy in quiet listening conditions; CWLs vs. HFWLs in the conventional strategy in noisy listening conditions; CWLs vs. HFWLs in the NLFC strategy in quiet listening conditions; CWLs vs. HFWLs in the NLFC strategy in noisy listening conditions; quiet vs. noisy listening conditions for CWLs in conventional strategy; quiet vs. noisy listening conditions for HFWLs in conventional strategy; quiet vs. noisy listening conditions for CWLs in NLFC strategy; quiet vs. noisy listening conditions for HFWLs in NLFC strategy. Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were prepared in Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

The following inferences have been formulated from the results: (1) CWLs overestimate the speech perception performance of elderly patients with sloping HFHL; (2) HFWLs address some of the genuine speech perception difficulties elderly patients face due to sloping HFHL; (3) the NLFC strategy offers improved audibility of high-frequency speech cues relative to the conventional strategy for individuals with sloping HFHL; (4) HFWLs were more suitable than CWLs for recognising the speech-perception advantage associated with NLFC over the conventional strategy in individuals with sloping HFHL; (5) the noisy listening conditions degraded the speech perception performance regardless of the speech stimuli (CWLs or HFWLs) and strategies used in the hearing aids (conventional and NLFC).

DISCUSSION

The commonest audiometric pattern in sensorineural loss is a gradual worsening of sensitivity as frequency increases. However, speech cues in the low-frequency region are often less affected than cues carried at higher frequencies, even at greater degrees of hearing loss.[23] This is because spectral and temporal processing may remain comparatively better preserved at lower frequencies, even at greater degrees of hearing loss.[24] Given that the participants in the current study had sloping HFHL, the severity of hearing loss was comparatively lower at low frequencies. Furthermore, the CWLs used as stimuli in the current study comprised 64% voiced consonants in each list, in contrast to only 16% of voiced consonants in HFWLs. Words containing voiced consonants may have provided redundant information in speech because of a relatively lower degree of hearing loss at low frequencies. Despite conventional hearing aids providing insufficient gain at high frequencies, satisfactory access to speech information at low frequencies can be achieved even with more severe hearing loss.[25] This may explain why participants in the present study showed markedly higher CWL scores than HFWL scores in the conventional condition (Figure 3 and Table 2). Thus, CWLs overestimate speech perception performance and fail to identify genuine speech perception difficulties caused by HFHL.

Individuals with sloping HFHL experience difficulties perceiving speech sounds that contain spectral energy mainly in the high-frequency regions.[6] The spectral power of consonants is spread across a wide range of frequencies. Among the consonants, voiceless consonants have significant spectral energy in the higher frequency regions.[7] Additionally, in everyday conversations, voiceless consonants often fall below the hearing thresholds because they contain less energy than voiced consonants.[26] Conventional amplification may provide inadequate audibility for “high-frequency speech components, particularly in severe high-frequency loss”.[25] Furthermore, the HFWLs used as stimuli in the current study contained 84% voiceless consonants per list, compared with 36% in CWLs. Therefore, participants with sloping HFHL might have had difficulty perceiving words containing voiceless consonants. Consequently, these participants performed more poorly on HFWLs than on CWLs when using the conventional program (Figure 3 and Table 2). Thus, HFWLs address some of the genuine speech-perception challenges individuals encounter due to HFHL that would otherwise have gone unrecognised if CWLs had been employed alone.

The high-frequency bandwidth is compressed by NLFC technology, which relocates inaudible high-frequency information into a nearby low-frequency region of audible hearing for a particular listener, thereby expanding the audible range.[27] It is therefore possible that the NLFC strategy shifted additional high-frequency cues into a listener-accessible frequency region and provided access to details that the conventional program may not have delivered with adequate audibility.[19] Improved access to high-frequency phonemic cues would be expected to improve speech perception performance.[28] Hence, participants achieved higher speech perception scores in the NLFC than with the conventional strategy for HFWLs (Figure 2 and Table 1). Thus, HFWLs respond more readily to suitable speech stimuli than CWLs, recognising some of the advantages of the NLFC strategy over the conventional strategy.

It is widely recognized that noisy listening conditions degrade speech quality and reduce speech perception. This is because, in addition to reduced hearing sensitivity, sensorineural hearing loss involves suprathreshold limitations in spectral and temporal resolution that deteriorate speech perception. While limited spectral and temporal resolution features may be sufficient for understanding speech in quiet environments,[29] more accurate spectral and temporal resolution features are required to comprehend speech in noisy environments.[30] When spectral and temporal coding is degraded, listeners struggle to separate speech from competing noise, especially in sensorineural hearing loss.[31] Hence, despite superior performance in quiet, noisy conditions degraded participants’ performance, regardless of the speech stimuli (CWLs or HFWLs) (Figure 4 and Table 3).

Apart from suprathreshold shortcomings arising from cochlear degeneration, ageing is another significant factor contributing to disproportionate difficulty when speech must be understood against background noise. The consequences of ageing on central auditory processing abilities are additionally expected to degrade speech perception in noisy backgrounds.[32] In noisy listening conditions, individuals must meet significant demands on attention, working memory, and executive control.[33] Therefore, elderly patients encounter greater difficulties understanding speech, especially in noisy environments, because cognitive capacities decline with age.[34] Hence, apart from peripheral and central deteriorations in auditory processing, possible reduced cognitive capacities due to ageing likely degraded participants’ speech perception performance, particularly in noisy listening environments, despite the amplification provided by conventional or NLFC strategies in the present study.

Limitations of the Study

Nonetheless, the existing Telugu speech perception tests (CWLs and HFWLs) do not yet fully characterise aided listening benefit in noise. It was predicted that cognitive decline due to ageing, beyond peripheral and central deterioration, would result in poor perception in noisy environments. However, the participants’ cognitive status was not assessed in the present study to determine whether poorer speech-in-noise scores were related to cognitive status. In addition, the lack of normative data on words-in-noise recognition in Telugu hinders comparisons with participants’ performance. Further, the study employed a fixed SNR rather than a variable SNR. As a result, the range of participants’ performance as a function of SNR could not be documented. In routine clinical settings, elderly patients often present with mixed aetiologies. As a result, it was challenging to identify participants who met extremely strict inclusion criteria while maintaining sample homogeneity, thereby reducing the number of eligible participants during recruitment. Hence, the small sample size limits generalisation to the broader population.

A Proposal for Speech-in-Noise Tests in the Telugu Language

To understand the challenges individuals with hearing loss encounter and assess whether hearing aids provide meaningful benefit in everyday listening situations, particularly when background noise is present, it is proposed to develop specifically designed speech-in-noise tests. Additionally, assessing cognitive functions in conjunction with speech-in-noise evaluations will be crucial in providing evidence-based hearing validation, counselling hearing aid users, and deciding candidacy for hearing training.

Speech-in-noise perception can be assessed using words, phrases, sentences, and other speech signals as test stimuli. Word-in-noise recognition depends strongly on peripheral auditory encoding. [35] Word-level recognition relies more on lower-level auditory processing and bottom-up information, thereby reducing the influence of higher-order cognitive support when speech is degraded by noise.[36] As a result, word-level tests, such as the words-in-noise test, reduce the influence of top-down processing and contextual cues, thus providing more precise insights into underlying auditory discrimination abilities and conveying information on specific phoneme errors to the clinicians. Words-in-noise tests provide valuable information beyond conventional pure-tone audiograms or speech-in-quiet tests. Furthermore, they can be administered and interpreted relatively quickly, making them feasible to incorporate into the routine audiological test batteries. More convincingly, it is reasonably possible to establish, dialectically and socioculturally, balanced words in Telugu suitable for native speakers to constitute a words-in-noise test. While isolated words are shorter in duration, convey limited semantic and syntactic content of language, and do not closely approximate natural conversational listening, sentences are longer in duration, approximate natural conversational listening more closely than isolated words, and convey greater semantic and syntactic content. Sentence-level recognition, particularly in noisy environments, utilises higher-order auditory functions that involve more top-down processing, including cognitive processes.[36] Besides, using sentences as speech stimuli helps assess co-articulation and temporal aspects of speech.[37] However, sentences are expected to be more predictable due to increased linguistic redundancy resulting from rich contextual cues. Hence, using them as speech stimuli would overestimate functional speech understanding in listeners with hearing impairment, especially in noisy listening environments.[38] Telugu spoken in India has four major dialects. The dialects differ in their phonology, vocabulary, grammar, pragmatics, and pronunciation. As a result, it is challenging to establish dialectically and socioculturally balanced sentences. Additionally, sentence word order can vary, often between dialects and among speakers of the same language. Thus, sentence-level tests compromise the naturalness and nativity of everyday speech of representatives of different dialects of the Telugu language.

While it is challenging to establish dialectically and socioculturally balanced sentences in Telugu, it is reasonably possible to develop phrases that consider both aspects. A phrase consists of a small group of words that operates as one grammatical unit within a sentence. In contrast to sentences, phrases are shorter in duration and have less semantic and syntactic content. However, phrases are easier to maintain with consistent word order, more closely approximate everyday conversation, and are relatively less redundant compared to sentences. In contrast to isolated words, phrases are longer and convey more semantic and syntactic content. Hence, it is anticipated that, to some extent, phrase recognition tests help to determine top-down processing effects involving higher-level auditory functions associated with speech-in-noise perception. Therefore, phrase-level tests can be considered alternatives to sentence-level tests for speech-in-noise assessments, especially in Telugu. In summary, given the intended purpose and convenience, words and phrases are suitable speech stimuli for assessing individuals’ speech-perception challenges in noisy environments, particularly in Telugu.

CONCLUSION

This study examined how Telugu CWLs and HFWLs perform as tools for evaluating aided speech perception under conventional and NLFC hearing-aid strategies among elderly patients with HFHL. The HFWLs serve as suitable speech stimuli to address some of the speech-perception challenges individuals face due to sloping HFHL and to highlight the benefits of NLFC strategies over the conventional strategy for individuals with HFHL across quiet and noise conditions, which would have been overlooked when CWLs were administered alone. Hence, HFWLs should be used routinely, along with CWLs, as test stimuli to measure SIS (%) in speech audiometry and during hearing aid fitting for individuals with HFHL. However, it is proposed that specifically designed speech-in-noise tests be developed in Telugu for a more complete estimate of aided benefit, especially when listening occurs in noise.
