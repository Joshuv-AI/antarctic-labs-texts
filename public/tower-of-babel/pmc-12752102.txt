Solvent-dependent variation in phenolic profiles, antioxidant and antibacterial activities across wild populations of Allium jesdianum
Authors: Zeinab Sharifi, Hamid Reza Eisvand, Atousa Aliahmadi, Hamid Ahadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllium jesdianum, an endangered medicinal plant endemic to Iran, exhibits notable pharmacological properties, including antioxidant and antibacterial effects. This study explored the influence of solvent polarity on the extraction efficiency of phytochemicals and their associated bioactivities in wild populations of A. jesdianum collected from six natural habitats (P1–P6). Aerial parts of the plant were extracted using methanol, followed by fractionation with ethyl acetate and n-hexane. Total phenolic and flavonoid contents were quantified via Folin–Ciocalteu and aluminum chloride methods. Antioxidant activity was assessed using DPPH and FRAP assays, while antibacterial potential was evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa using MIC and MBC assays. Despite its acknowledged medicinal significance, the variation in phytochemical composition and bioactivities of A. jesdianum at the population level has been largely overlooked. To fill this gap in knowledge, the current study offers the inaugural systematic and comparative assessment of various wild populations from six different natural environments, incorporating the influences of population origin and solvent polarity on the bioactive potential of the plant. The results showed that both solvent type and population origin significantly influenced phytochemical content and biological activity. Ethyl acetate and n-hexane fractions from the P5 and P6 populations yielded the highest levels of phenolics (70.37 mg GAE g−1) and flavonoids (234.75 mg Rutin g−1), along with the strongest antioxidant and antibacterial effects (IC₅₀ = 0.034 mg mL−1; MIC = 2 mg mL−1 against S. aureus). HPLC analysis identified ferulic acid and rutin as predominant bioactive compounds, whose accumulation correlated strongly with soil macronutrients (N, P, K), rather than altitude. Heat map and PCA analyses revealed pronounced phytochemical diversity among the wild populations, with P6 showing superior metabolite profiles. The high bioactive properties of populations P5 and P6 suggest their suitability for targeted conservation, selective cultivation, and use in pharmaceutical or nutraceutical products, offering valuable advice for population-specific approaches to preserve and capitalise on the medicinal value of A. jesdianum.Keywords: Bioactive compounds, Flavonoids, HPLC profiling, Phenolic content, Soil macronutrients, Solvent polarity

Introduction

The genus of Allium L. is the largest and important representative genus of the Amaryllidaceae family comprising more than 700 species. Nonetheless, they are close in biochemical, phytochemical, and nutraceutical properties [67]. Allium species, are known to synthesize an abundance of secondary metabolites, including saponins, organosulfur compounds, and flavonoids, which exhibit a range of bioactivities from antioxidant to anti-inflammatory properties [53].

Allium jesdianum is a crucial medicinal and edible species within the Amaryllidaceae family and is indigenous to Iran. It naturally grows at high altitudes (1800–2600 m) of Zagros Mountains. Local populations traditionally use its bulbs and aerial parts to treat colds, digestive ailments, rheumatic pain, and kidney disorders [38, 55]. Numerous studies have confirmed its broad pharmacological profile, including antioxidant [55], anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic [3], antifungal [45], analgesic [31], antibacterial [55], hepatoprotective [28], anticancer [76], protoscolicidal [23], and anti-toxicity activities [46].

The medicinal value of bulbous species such as A. jesdianum has rendered their populations and natural habitats increasingly vulnerable. Primary threats include uncontrolled bulb harvesting and habitat destruction, which are exacerbated by overgrazing, climate change, and insufficient conservation measures. Together, these factors disrupt reproductive processes and have caused significant population declines [25, 62, 79]. In particular, unregulated collection disproportionately removes mature individuals, resulting in fragmented populations that struggle to recover under mounting environmental stress [35, 40, 74]. These compounding threats highlight the urgent need to characterize phytochemical diversity and bioactivity across wild populations to identify resilient chemotypes capable of withstanding anthropogenic and environmental pressures.

The biosynthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites are regulated by multiple factors, including genetic makeup, developmental stage, and environmental conditions such as light, temperature, soil composition, and moisture [10, 36, 72, 73]. Environmental variability, particularly in wild populations, can lead to significant differences in the levels of active ingredients, thereby influencing their biological and pharmacological effects [29, 66]. Several recent studies have investigated the role of geographic origin and environmental conditions in shaping secondary metabolite profiles in medicinal plants [36, 72, 73, 75]

Another critical factor influencing the extraction of phytochemicals is the choice of solvent. Solvents with different polarities—such as methanol, ethyl acetate, n-hexane, and water—can selectively extract specific classes of compounds based on their solubility [4, 16]. Numerous studies have shown that solvent polarity significantly affects the yield, composition, and biological activity of extracts [2, 49]. For instance, Mehmood et al. [41] reported that different solvents resulted in varying concentrations of total phenolics and flavonoids in medicinal plant extracts, which in turn impacted their antioxidant and antimicrobial potential.

Although there has been a marked increase in research on the phytochemical and pharmacological properties of Allium species in recent years, comprehensive comparative studies investigating intraspecific variation among natural populations—particularly in bulbous geophytes such as A. jesdianum—remain notably underrepresented in the literature. Previous investigations have demonstrated a range of bioactivities in A. jesdianum, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic effects [55].

However, these studies have largely focused on single populations and have not systematically addressed how environmental variability may influence secondary metabolite profiles and associated biological activities. Previous studies have reported certain phytochemical variations among A. jesdianum populations. For instance, Pirbalouti et al. [55] provided preliminary evidence of differences in total phenolic content among geographically distinct populations in Iran. However, these investigations did not systematically examine how solvent polarity affects extraction efficiency and the resulting bioactivities, nor did they integrate multiple functional assays across diverse populations. Such limitations have restricted our understanding of environmental influences on the species’ pharmacological potential.

To address these gaps, the current research performs a comparative analysis of six wild populations through solvent fractionation, with the objective of clarifying the interactive influences of population origin and solvent polarity on the composition of phenolics and flavonoids, in addition to their antioxidant and antibacterial properties.

Given the medicinal importance of A. jesdianum, and considering the potential influence of environmental factors and solvent polarity on the extraction and bioactivity of phytochemicals, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the phytochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and antibacterial activity of extracts obtained using solvents of varying polarities (n-hexane, ethyl acetate, methanol, and water) across wild populations of A. jesdianum. In addition, HPLC profiling was performed to identify and compare key phenolic and flavonoid constituents among populations, and to determine the most promising population for future phytopharmaceutical applications.

Materials and methods

Plant materials collection

The leaf samples of A. jesdianum plant were collected from natural habitats in various regions of Iran, including Khoramabad, Semirom, Kohdasht, Sanandaj, Abdanan, and Ravansar. The geographical coordinates of each population were recorded (Table 1). Samples were collected from at least 50 wild-growing individuals per populations. The collection dates varied depending on the optimal sampling time (i.e. before flowering), due to differences in climatic conditions across the studied habitats. The sampling period extended from mid-April to mid-May 2023. Dried samples were placed in cloth bags and stored at 20–22 °C in a cool environment until experiments began in June 2023.

The geographical distribution of the A. jesdianum was determined using Flora Iranica [57] and local reports. Habitats characteristics, including altitude, latitude, and longitude, were recorded for each sampling site (Table 1).

Preparation of sample extracts

To prepare the samples for the extraction of bioactive compounds, the leaves were first washed with distilled water and then dried at room temperature (30 ± 2 ◦C) for 48 h. The dried leaves were subsequently ground into a fine powder using a grinder. The powdered aerial parts of each plant sample were suspended in 100% methanol at a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The mixture was stirred for 48 h and then filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The resulting filtrate was vacuum-dried. The methanolic extract, once dried, was then subjected to fractionation through a liquid–liquid partitioning method.The sample was systematically divided using n-hexane, then ethyl acetate, and ultimately water, resulting in fractions that are enriched with various phytochemicals [17]. Each fraction was evaporated to dryness under vacuum using a rotatory evaporator. The dried extracts were stored in dark glass vials, protected from moisture, and kept at 4 °C in a refrigerator until further analysis of total phenolic and flavonoid content and antioxidant activity.

Chemicals and reagents

Chemical solvents, including methanol, n-hexane, ethyl acetate, aluminum chloride, sodium carbonate, and HPLC-grade acetonitrile were purchased from Merck (Germany). DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl), 2,4,6-tri(2-pirydyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ), chlorogenic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, gallic acid (GA), and rutin standards were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (USA). HPLC-grade water was produced by a Mili-Q system (Millipore, USA).

Total Phenolic Content (TPC)

Total phenolic content of the sample extracts were determined using a modified version of the method described by Singleton et al. [65]. Briefly, 25 µL of each extract was mixed with 125 µL Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (10% v/v in distilled water) in 96-well microplates and incubated for 5 min. Then, 100 µL of sodium carbonate solution (Na2CO3 75 g L−1) was added. This mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 45 min, and the absorbance was read at 765 nm using a microplate reader. Quantification was performed using gallic acid as a standard phenolic compound at concentrations of 10, 50, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 mg L⁻1. The results were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry extract.

Total Flavonoids Content (TFC)

The total flavonoid content (TFC) of the extracts was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method described by Chang et al. [12]. The results were expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalents per gram of dried extract (mg RE g⁻1 DW). Briefly, 20 µL of appropriately diluted sample solution was mixed with 80 µL of distilled water and 6 µL of 5% (w/v) sodium nitrite (NaNO₂) in a 96-well microplate. After incubation for 6 min at room temperature, 6 µL of 10% (w/v) aluminum chloride (AlCl₃) was added. Following 5 min of incubation, 80 µL of 4% (w/v) sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and 8 µL of distilled water were added. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature, and absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader.

A calibration curve was constructed using rutin standard solutions in the concentration range of 10–250 mg L⁻1 (R2 > 0.99). The total flavonoid content of each sample was calculated from this calibration curve and expressed as mg rutin equivalents per g of dried extract. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Antioxidant activity by DPPH assay

The DPPH free radical scavenging activity was assessed using the method described by Chen et al. [13], with slight modifications. The assay was performed in a 96-well microplate using six different concentrations of each extract (10, 15, 20, 30, 50, and 60 µg/mL). A freshly prepared DPPH solution (100 µM in methanol) was used, and all experiments were performed in triplicate. Briefly, 100 µL of the DPPH solution was mixed with 100 µL of the extract solution in each well. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 45 min at 25 °C with gentle shaking using a Heidolph Titramax 1000 shaker and Incubator 1000 (Germany).

Antioxidant activity by FRAP assay

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed according to the method of Benzie and Strain [8], with minor modifications. The assay is based on the reduction of the Fe3⁺–TPTZ (2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine) complex to its ferrous form (Fe2⁺–TPTZ) by antioxidants under acidic conditions, resulting in the formation of an intense blue color, which was measured at 593 nm. The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared before each experiment by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl₃·6H₂O in a 10:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio. For the assay, 20 µL of each extract (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 200 µL of the FRAP reagent in a 96-well microplate. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a PowerWave XS microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instrum USA). A calibration curve was established utilizing FeSO₄·7H₂O (0.1–1.0 mM). Ascorbic acid acted as a positive control, while the extraction solvent functioned as a negative control. The outcomes were reported as millimoles of Fe2⁺ equivalents per gram of dried extract (mmol Fe2⁺ eq/g dry extract). All measurements were conducted in triplicate. To ensure consistent results and reproducibility, Ascorbic acid was employed as a positive control, while DMSO acted as a negative control in every FRAP assay.

Determination of phenolic compounds by HPLC

Phenolic compounds in the extracts were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a Waters 2695 system equipped with a Waters 996 photodiode array detector (Waters Corporation, USA). Separation was carried out on a SunFire C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 3.5 µm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of methanol (solvent A) and distilled water (solvent B), both containing 0.02% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The flow rate was maintained at 0.5 mL min⁻1, and the injection volume was 20 µL. The chromatographic separation was achieved using a binary gradient as follows: the initial mobile phase composition was maintained at 20% solvent A and 80% solvent B. The gradient was then programmed as follows: a linear increase to 30% A over 10 min, followed by a linear increase to 50% A over the next 10 min. This composition (50% A) was held isocratically for 20 min to ensure complete elution of mid-polarity compounds. Subsequently, the percentage of A was raised linearly to 100% over 2 min and maintained for 6 min to wash the column of strongly retained, non-polar components. Finally, the gradient was returned to the initial conditions (20% A) over 7 min and equilibrated for a subsequent analysis [1]. Standard solutions of chlorogenic acid (326 nm), p-coumaric acid (310 nm), ferulic acid (324 nm) and rutin (355 nm) were 10, 25, 50, 100, and 200 ppm to construct calibration curves and quantify the phenolic compounds content in the samples. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Millennium32 software (Waters Corporation, USA).

Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

The antimicrobial activity of various plant extracts was evaluated against Gram-negative Escherichia coli (ATCC11775), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATTC27853), and Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC12600). The bacterial strains were obtained from the bacterial culture collection of the Microbiology Laboratory, Medicinal Plants and Drugs Research Institute, Shahid Beheshti University (Tehran, Iran). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the broth microdilution method. Each plant extract was dissolved in 0.2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to a final concentration of 100 mg mL⁻1 (i.e., 100 mg of crude extract in 1 mL of DMSO). Bacterial suspensions were prepared by adjusting the turbidity to match a 0.5 McFarland standard, corresponding to approximately 5 × 105 to 1 × 10⁶ colony-forming units (CFU) per milliliter, using a spectrophotometer.

In a sterile 96-well microplate, 100 µL of Mueller–Hinton broth was dispensed into each well, except for the first column. Serial dilutions of the plant extracts were prepared in the wells by adding the appropriate volume of extract to the first column and performing two-fold dilutions across the row. Then, 100 µL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well. The microplate was incubated at 37 °C for 20 h.

The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of extract that visually inhibited bacterial growth (no turbidity). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), 5 µL aliquots from the wells with no visible growth were inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The MBC was recorded as the lowest concentration at which no colony growth was observed, indicating complete bactericidal activity. Resazurin was used as viability indicator for MIC assays. A solution of Resazurin in D.W. (5 mg mL-1) was sterilized by 0.2-micron syringe filter and 10 µL of this stock was added to each well to determine the MIC values. While growth positive wells resulted in pinkish color, the no growth wells remain dark blue. Cefixime was used as standard broad-spectrum antibiotic.

Data analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SAS software (version 9.2) to evaluate the effects of the experimental factors and their interaction. Duncan’s multiple range test was used to identify significant differences among treatment means at p < 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were calculated using SAS (version 9.2) to assess the relationships among bioactive compounds, soil elements, and altitude. Graphical representations and heatmaps were generated using Origin Pro 2025. The level of statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Total Phenolic Content (TPC)

Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in TPC among the fractions derived from different A. jesdianum populations (Table 3, p < 0.05). The TPC values ranged from 3.24 to 70.37 mg GAE g⁻1 of dry extract across all samples (Fig. 1). Among the tested solvents, the ethyl acetate fraction consistently showed the highest phenolic content in all populations, followed by the n-hexane fraction. In particular, the ethyl acetate extracts from populations P5 and P6 exhibited the highest TPC values, at 70.37 and 44.26 mg GAE g⁻1, respectively. These values were significantly higher than those obtained with more polar solvents. In contrast, aqueous fractions had the lowest TPC values. The P2 population showed the minimum phenolic content, with a TPC of 3.24 mg GAE g⁻1, which was not statistically different from the aqueous fraction of the P3 population (5.02 mg GAE g⁻1).

Antioxidant activity by DPPH assay

The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) free radical scavenging assay is a widely used method for evaluating the antioxidant activity of bioactive compounds. It is a simple, rapid, and sensitive approach for screening the antioxidant potential of plant extracts. A lower IC₅₀ value indicates a higher antioxidant efficacy, as it reflects a smaller quantity of extract needed to scavenge 50% of DPPH radicals. Conversely, a higher IC₅₀ value denotes lower scavenging activity, implying that a larger amount of extract is required to achieve the same effect [61].

Significant differences in IC₅₀ values were observed among different fractions and populations of A. jesdianum (Table 3, p < 0.05). Among the evaluated populations, the ethyl acetate and n-hexane fractions extracted from the leaves showed the strongest DPPH radical scavenging activity. IC50 of standard antioxidant (Trolox) was about 0.003 mg mL-1. However, the ethyl acetate fraction of population P6 exhibited the lowest IC₅₀ value (IC₅₀ = 0.034 mg mL⁻1), indicating the highest antioxidant potential. There was no statistically significant difference between the IC₅₀ values of ethyl acetate fractions from P6, P1, and P5 populations. In contrast, the aqueous extract from population P2 showed the weakest antioxidant activity, with the highest IC₅₀ value (IC₅₀ = 0.88 mg mL⁻1). Overall, the findings confirm that all tested extracts exhibited free radical scavenging capacity, though with varying degrees of effectiveness (Fig. 3).

Antioxidant activity by FRAP assay

The ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was employed as a complementary method to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of various solvent fractions obtained from different populations of A. jesdianum. The results revealed substantial variations in reducing power among the tested fractions, which were generally consistent with the trends observed in the DPPH radical scavenging assay. FRAP values among the samples ranged from 1.83 to 41.87 mmol Fe2⁺ eq/g dry extract. The ethyl acetate fraction from population P6 exhibited the highest ferric-reducing antioxidant activity (41.87 mmol Fe2⁺ eq/g dry extract), indicating a strong antioxidant potential.

While the ferric-reducing activity of the ascorbic acid standard was approximately 76.21 mM Fe2⁺ equivalents/g dry extract, the ethyl acetate fraction from population P6 showed the highest antioxidant reducing capacity (41.87 mM Fe2⁺ eq/g dry extract) among the tested sample. In contrast, the aqueous extract from population P2 showed the lowest FRAP value (1.83 mmol Fe2⁺ eq/g dry extract), suggesting weak reducing power and limited antioxidant capacity (Fig. 4).

Antimicrobial activity of A. jesdianum populations in different solvent extracts

In this experiment, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were determined to assess the antibacterial activity of different solvent extracts from A. jesdianum populations against standard strains of Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

The MIC results (Table 4) revealed that all extracts exhibited antimicrobial activity, with MIC values ranging from 2 mg mL⁻1 to > 16 mg mL⁻1. The MIC values varied depending on the type of extract and the bacterial species tested. Among the different solvent fractions, the ethyl acetate extract consistently demonstrated the strongest antibacterial activity, while the aqueous extract showed the weakest. Sensitivity of the bacterial species to the extracts also varied. S. aureus was generally more susceptible to the extracts compared to E. coli and P. aeruginosa. The ethyl acetate fractions from the P6 and P5 populations showed the lowest MIC values against S. aureus (2 mg mL⁻1 for both), and similarly low MICs against E. coli (2 and 4 mg mL⁻1, respectively). In contrast, the aqueous extract from the P3 population exhibited the highest MIC value (> 16 mg mL⁻1) against P. aeruginosa, indicating poor inhibitory activity.

Determination of phenolic compounds by HPLC

Based on the results of antioxidant and antimicrobial assays, the ethyl acetate fraction—which showed the highest antioxidant activity—was selected for further analysis of potent antioxidant compounds using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

The analysis revealed the presence of various phenolic acids and flavonoids, with notable variation in their concentrations among populations. Among the compounds detected, ferulic acid and rutin were the predominant phenolic acid and flavonoid, respectively, across the samples analyzed (Table 5, Fig. 5). Correlation and cluster analysis of phenolic compounds and environmental factors

Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations between soil macronutrients—particularly nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K)—and key phenolic compounds in A. jesdianum. Among the phenolic metabolites, rutin, ferulic acid, and chlorogenic acid exhibited the highest correlations with soil nutrients, particularly with phosphorus and potassium (r ≥ 0.90). Notably, phosphorus showed a strong correlation with rutin (r ≈ 0.93), while potassium was strongly correlated with ferulic acid (r ≈ 0.91), highlighting the critical role of soil nutrient availability in the biosynthesis of antioxidant metabolites. In contrast, altitude displayed weaker correlations with phenolic content (r < 0.60), suggesting that edaphic factors exert a more dominant influence than topographic features on phenolic compound accumulation (Fig. 6).

To further explore the variation in phenolic profiles among populations, hierarchical clustering coupled with a heat map was performed. This analysis revealed clear differences in the accumulation patterns of individual phenolic compounds across populations (Fig. 7). The P6 population was distinguished by a remarkably high level of rutin, setting it apart from all other populations and suggesting enhanced flavonoid biosynthesis potentially linked to specific edaphic or genetic factors. In contrast, the P2 population exhibited notably low levels of several phenolic compounds, particularly ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid, which may reflect either a limited biosynthetic capacity or environmental conditions less favorable to secondary metabolism.

The dendrogram also highlighted clusters among both populations and compounds. Populations P1, P3, and P4 formed a cluster with relatively moderate and similar phenolic profiles, while P5 showed a balanced but distinct pattern. Among the phenolic compounds, rutin and chlorogenic acid were grouped together, suggesting they may share regulatory or biosynthetic pathways. Similarly, ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid clustered closely, further supporting potential metabolic linkage.

Overall, these analyses underscore the significant influence of environmental (particularly soil-related) and possibly genetic factors on the phytochemical diversity observed among A. jesdianum populations. They also highlight the utility of multivariate approaches in uncovering subtle patterns of variation not captured by univariate comparisons alone.

Discussion

Effect of solvent extractions on TPC and TFC

The present study demonstrated that ethyl acetate, a semi-polar solvent, yielded significantly higher levels of total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) compared to more polar solvents such as methanol and distilled water. This trend can be attributed to the intermediate polarity of many phenolic and flavonoid compounds, which enhances their solubility in less polar organic solvents. These findings clearly highlight the crucial role of solvent polarity in the efficient extraction of phenolic compounds.

This observation aligns with prior studies emphasizing the influence of solvent selection on the recovery of bioactive phytochemicals [2, 49]. For example, ethyl acetate extracts have been reported to yield the highest phenolic contents in various plant species, including Cytisus triflorus [7], and Allium cepa [32].

Further supporting this trend, Ezealigo et al. [20] evaluated TPC and TFC in the methanolic extract and its fractions (n-hexane, ethyl acetate, n-butanol, and aqueous) of Desmodium ramosissimum. Their findings indicated that the ethyl acetate fraction exhibited the highest levels of both phenolic and flavonoid compounds. Similarly, Ri et al. [59] found that among the extracts of Helianthus aureus flowers using different solvents (hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, butanol, and water), ethyl acetate extract consistently showed the highest concentrations of phenolics and flavonoids, while water extract had the lowest.

These consistent outcomes across diverse plant species emphasize the selective extraction power of ethyl acetate, likely due to its ability to solubilize mid-polarity bioactive compounds. Moreover, the solubility of phenolic compounds depends not only on solvent polarity but also on the structural properties of the phenolics themselves, such as aromatic ring conjugation, distribution of hydroxyl groups, and the nature of intermolecular interactions with the solvent [21].

Interestingly, although flavonoids are generally considered mid- to high-polarity compounds, the n-hexane fraction in this study also exhibited a relatively high flavonoid content. This may be attributed to the partial solubility of certain lipophilic or methylated flavonoids in low-polarity solvents [19, 72, 73] a) and the possible formation of flavonoid–lipid or flavonoid–terpene complexes that facilitate co-extraction into n-hexane [71]. These interactions help explain the detectable levels of flavonoids in non-polar fractions and underscore the complexity of solvent–metabolite relationships during phytochemical extraction.

Therefore, the increased extraction efficiency observed with ethyl acetate in A. jesdianum may reflect a predominance of mid- to low-polarity phenolics in its phytochemical profile. The choice of solvent not only influences the quantity of compounds extracted but also affects the chemical diversity and biological activity of the resulting extract [56, 63]. Understanding these interactions is essential for optimizing extraction protocols aimed at maximizing the yield of antioxidant and therapeutic compounds from plant sources.

This discrepancy in activity may also stem from the collection of plant samples from distinct ecological environments. The findings suggest that geographic location plays a substantial role in determining the total phenolic content of plants. Variations in environmental factors—such as climate, altitude, and soil characteristics—can profoundly influence the metabolic profiles observed [36, 77].

As expected, the habitat environment significantly impacted the accumulation of secondary metabolites (SMs) in A. jesdianum. This was evidenced by the observed correlations between the concentrations of various SMs in the six populations collected from environmentally distinct regions. The heat map analysis (Fig. 7) further confirmed these differences, revealing substantial variation in the accumulation of key phenolic compounds among populations. Notably, the P2 population exhibited markedly lower levels of phenolics, particularly ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid, while P6 and P5 displayed higher concentrations of rutin and chlorogenic acid.

These findings underscore the influence of geographic and ecological factors on the regulation of secondary metabolism in A. jesdianum. Furthermore, the correlation analysis (Fig. 6) demonstrated significant positive relationships between soil macronutrients—especially nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K)—and the levels of phenolic compounds such as ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid. Although altitude also showed a positive correlation with rutin and p-coumaric acid, the effect of soil nutrient content appeared to be more substantial.

These results support the hypothesis that both edaphic and geographical factors contribute to the chemical diversity in A. jesdianum. An increasing number of studies have highlighted the role of plant nutrition in influencing secondary metabolism and antioxidant activity. For instance, Yang et al. (72, 73 a) emphasized the importance of nutrient availability in modulating phenolic biosynthesis. Similarly, Ibrahim et al. [26] demonstrated that nitrogen concentration significantly affected the production of total phenolics and flavonoids in Labisia pumila. These findings are also consistent with the earlier work by Zargoosh et al. [77], which reported positive correlations between phenolic content and environmental parameters such as altitude, organic matter, and soil nutrients in Scrophularia striata.

In addition to environmental influences, the variation noted in the phenolic and flavonoid levels among A. jesdianum populations may also indicate inherent genetic differences. Prior research has shown that populations belonging to the same species frequently display unique metabolic chemotypes, which arise from genetic differences in the biosynthetic pathways of secondary metabolites [43, 70]. This genetic differentiation can affect the expression of essential enzymes that play a role in phenolic biosynthesis, thus leading to metabolic profiles that are specific to different populations [60]. Consequently, the variations noted in compounds like ferulic acid, rutin, and chlorogenic acid across different populations may be partially ascribed to genotypic differences, alongside environmental adaptability.

Effect of solvent extractions on antioxidant activity

The current study provides strong evidence that the antioxidant capacity of A. jesdianum is significantly influenced by both the type of extraction solvent and the geographic origin of the plant populations. Among the six populations studied, the ethyl acetate and n-hexane fractions exhibited the highest antioxidant activities, as measured by both DPPH radical scavenging and FRAP assays. In contrast, aqueous extracts consistently showed the lowest antioxidant capacities across all populations.

The DPPH assay revealed that the ethyl acetate extract from the P6 population had the lowest IC₅₀ value, indicating the most effective free radical scavenging activity. Similarly, results from the FRAP assay confirmed that the highest ferric-reducing antioxidant power was also found in the ethyl acetate extract of P6. Conversely, the aqueous extract from the P2 population exhibited the weakest antioxidant response in both assays. These results are consistent with previous findings by Khalili et al. [32], Mohammed et al. [42], and Ezealigo et al. [20], who reported superior antioxidant activities in ethyl acetate fractions of A. cepa, D. ramosissimum, and C. triflorus, respectively.

The differences observed in DPPH radical scavenging activity among the various solvent extracts suggest that solvent polarity plays a crucial role in extracting specific bioactive compounds with antioxidant properties [47, 48]. In support of this, heat map analysis (Fig. 7) showed that populations such as P6 and P5 accumulate higher levels of phenolic compounds, including rutin, chlorogenic acid, ferulic acid, and p-coumaric acid. These populations also displayed the highest antioxidant capacities in both DPPH and FRAP assays. In contrast, P2 showed the lowest levels of phenolics and the weakest antioxidant performance, highlighting a strong correlation between phytochemical composition and biological activity.

Phenolic acids are known for their antioxidant properties, helping neutralize reactive oxygen species during environmental stresses [9, 14]. In this study, ethyl acetate extracts not only exhibited higher antioxidant activity but also contained greater concentrations of phenolics compared to methanol and hexane extracts. These findings affirm that the antioxidant potential of A. jesdianum is largely attributed to its phenolic and flavonoid content.

This conclusion is further corroborated by studies on Phoenix dactylifera [52] and indigenous herbs [44], where phenolic compounds and flavonoids were found to be the main contributors to antioxidant activity. In general, there exists a direct relationship between phenolic/flavonoid content and antioxidant efficacy—higher concentrations of these compounds lead to stronger antioxidant activity.

Additional support for this correlation comes from the Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 6), which showed strong positive associations between soil nutrients (N, P, K) and phenolic compounds such as ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid. Soil composition appeared to be the primary ecological driver of antioxidant potential in A. jesdianum. Populations grown in nutrient-rich soils (such as P6 and P5) synthesized higher levels of antioxidant compounds.

These findings underscore a biological link between environmental conditions and antioxidant capacity. Variability in soil nutrient content and ecological setting can shape the phytochemical profiles of wild medicinal plants, thereby influencing their pharmacological and therapeutic properties [36, 37, 54].

Antibacterial activity

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) analysis demonstrated that various extracts from six populations of A. jesdianum exhibited significant antibacterial properties against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. These findings are in line with earlier research confirming the antimicrobial potential of A. jesdianum [24, 45, 55]. A pronounced disparity in antibacterial efficacy was observed among the different solvent fractions. Specifically, the ethyl acetate and n-hexane fractions displayed the most potent inhibitory activity at relatively low concentrations, while the aqueous extracts showed only mild antibacterial effects.

This trend corroborates previous studies that reported the superior antimicrobial efficiency of ethyl acetate [32, 41] and n-hexane extracts [64]. The heat map analysis (Fig. 7) supports these findings, revealing that the P6 and P5 populations, which showed high antimicrobial activity, also contained greater concentrations of phenolic compounds—particularly rutin, chlorogenic acid, ferulic acid, and p-coumaric acid. In contrast, the P2 population exhibited the lowest levels of these bioactive compounds, which corresponded with its limited antibacterial performance.

Further validation is provided by Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 6), which demonstrated strong positive correlations between the accumulation of key phenolic acids (e.g., ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid) and soil macronutrients, especially nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.90. Although altitude also showed a positive association with phenolics such as rutin and p-coumaric acid, its impact was less significant compared to that of soil composition. These findings suggest that both ecological factors and extraction methods influence the observed variations in antibacterial activity by modulating the concentration and composition of bioactive metabolites.

Among the phenolic compounds, hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives—including ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, and chlorogenic acid—are well-documented for their antimicrobial and antioxidant properties [6, 15, 30]. Their high abundance in the P6 and P5 populations likely contributes to the enhanced antibacterial efficacy observed in these groups.

The close relationship between antioxidant capacity and antibacterial potential has been demonstrated in earlier studies, including those by Djeridane et al. [18] and Zhang et al. [78], who reported a direct association between phenolic content and biological activity. In this context, the role of solvent polarity is particularly important. Ethyl acetate, with its intermediate polarity, was especially effective in extracting moderately hydrophobic phenolics, resulting in significantly higher total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) compared to methanol and aqueous extracts.

Similar trends have been observed in other medicinal plants, such as A. cepa and D. ramosissimum [20, 32], where solvents with lower polarity enhanced the extractability and solubility of phenolic acids—particularly hydroxycinnamic acids—thereby increasing their antimicrobial effectiveness.

Finally, differences in antibacterial efficacy may also be partially attributed to the inherent resistance of bacterial strains. In this study, A. jesdianum extracts exhibited greater activity against Gram-positive bacteria, particularly S. aureus, than against Gram-negative strains such as E. coli and P. aeruginosa. This outcome is likely due to the outer membrane barrier in Gram-negative bacteria, which limits the permeability of external agents—a structural feature absents in Gram-positive bacteria [27, 39].

Conclusions

This study revealed pronounced phytochemical and functional variation among populations of Allium jesdianum, as evidenced by differences in total phenolic and flavonoid contents, antioxidant capacities, and antibacterial activities. Ethyl acetate and n-hexane extracts, derived from populations P6 and P5, consistently exhibited the highest levels of phenolic compounds—including rutin, chlorogenic acid, and ferulic acid—and demonstrated superior radical scavenging, ferric-reducing, and antibacterial efficacy. These findings underscore the influence of both ecological parameters (notably soil nutrient composition) and solvent polarity on the extraction and bioactivity of secondary metabolites. The strong correlation between phenolic accumulation and antioxidant/antimicrobial activities suggests that phenolics are key contributors to the observed bioactivities. The differential bioactivity among populations supports the potential use of selected wild genotypes as sources of pharmacologically relevant compounds.

The P5 n-hexane extract exhibited pronounced antibacterial activity, highlighting its potential as a natural alternative to synthetic preservatives and as a promising source of lead compounds for environmentally friendly antimicrobial products. Additionally, the P6 population demonstrated remarkable antioxidant capacity and contained substantial levels of phenolic compounds, particularly rutin and chlorogenic acid, underscoring its importance for both conservation and nutraceutical applications. Considering the increasing threat of genetic erosion in natural populations due to extensive bulb harvesting, it is imperative that P6 be prioritized in conservation and cultivation programs aimed at preserving elite genotypes with high bioactive potential. The diverse phytochemical composition of this population further indicates significant promise for the development of standardized nutraceutical formulations targeting oxidative stress–related disorder. Future work should focus on genotypic characterization, metabolic pathway elucidation, and in vivo validation of therapeutic effects to further develop A. jesdianum as a bioresource for medicinal and nutraceutical applications.
