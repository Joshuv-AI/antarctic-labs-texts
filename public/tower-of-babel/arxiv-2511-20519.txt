High-dimensional limits arising from hyperbolic Poisson k k -plane processes
Tillmann Bühler, Daniel Hug, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
We consider a stationary Poisson process of k k -planes in the d d -dimensional hyperbolic space ℍ d \mathbb{H}^{d} of constant curvature − 1 -1 , with d ≥ 4 d\geq 4 and 1 ≤ k ≤ d − 1 1\leq k\leq d-1 . It is known that, after centring and normalization, the total k k -volume of all intersections of k k -planes with a geodesic ball of radius R R converges in distribution, as R → ∞ R\to\infty , to a non-Gaussian infinitely divisible random variable Z d , k Z_{d,k} whenever 2 ​ k > d + 1 2k>d+1 . We investigate the distributional behaviour of Z d , k Z_{d,k} in the high-dimensional regime d → ∞ d\to\infty and depending on how fast k k grows in relation to d d . We derive precise conditions for the variance normalized sequence to converge in law to a standard Gaussian random variable or to a degenerate law, respectively, and show that an alternative rescaling of the Lévy measures yields an explicit non-Gaussian infinitely divisible limit for fixed codimension d − k d-k and a standard Gaussian limit for d − k → ∞ d-k\to\infty . Keywords: Hyperbolic stochastic geometry, infinitely divisible distribution, Poisson plane process, Poisson hyperplane process, stochastic geometry MSC: 60D05, 60F05

Introduction

In stochastic geometry, a central theme is the analysis of geometric structures generated by random point processes on spaces of geometric objects such as balls, general convex bodies or subspaces. Of particular interest are their geometric characteristics, including volumes, surface areas, and more general intersection measures. While much of the classical theory has been developed in the Euclidean setting, see
[
10
]
, there has recently been growing interest in extending these questions to spaces of constant curvature. In particular, in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, with its constant negative curvature and exponential volume growth, such random geometric structures often exhibit an interesting probabilistic behaviour that differs significantly from that in Euclidean space.

One class of models that has received considerable attention is that of Poisson processes of
k
-planes in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, where a
k
-plane is a totally geodesic submanifold of dimension
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, see
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
6
]
and the final chapter of
[
5
]
. To be formal, let
\eta_{d,k}
be an isometry invariant Poisson process on the space of
k
-planes in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Let
\mathbb{B}^{d}_{R}
denote a geodesic ball of radius
R>0
centred at a fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and define

F_{d,k,R}:=\sum_{E\in\eta_{d,k}}\vol_{k}(E\cap\mathbb{B}_{R}^{d}),

where
\vol_{k}(\,\cdot\,)
denotes the
k
-dimensional Riemannian volume in
E
. In other words,
F_{d,k,R}
measures the total
k
-dimensional volume contributed by all
k
-planes of
\eta_{d,k}
inside of
\mathbb{B}_{R}^{d}
.

In the Euclidean setting, for all choices of dimensions
d\geq 2
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
the random variables
F_{d,k,R}
satisfy a central limit theorem as
R\to\infty
under centring and variance normalization. In hyperbolic space, however, this asymptotic normality fails whenever
2k>d+1
, see
[
4
]
for the special case
k=d-1
and
[
1
]
for general
k
. In this dimension regime, it has been shown that
F_{d,k,R}
converges in distribution, after centring and normalization, to a centred non-Gaussian infinitely divisible random variable
Z_{d,k}
.
The characteristic function of
Z_{d,k}
is given by

\varphi_{Z_{d,k}}(t)=\exp\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{itx}-1-itx)\,\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)\Big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1.1)

with Lévy measure (the terms
Lévy measure
and
Lévy triplet
are defined in Section
2
)

\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)=\frac{\omega_{d-k}}{k-1}\;x^{-1-\frac{d-1}{k-1}}\bigl(1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\bigr)^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}\,\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(x)\,\dint x,

(1.2)

where
\omega_{b}=2\pi^{b/2}/\Gamma(b/2)
for
b\in\mathbb{N}
is the surface area of a Euclidean sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{b}
.
Note that the variance
\sigma_{d,k}^{2}:=\mathbb{V}(Z_{d,k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)
is finite but not equal to
1
in general, see (
4.3
) below.
The above normalization was chosen in
[
1
]
to obtain a representation with
\nu_{d,k}
concentrated in
(0,1)
.

The same limit law also arises in the study of more general intersection measures for hyperbolic Poisson
k
-plane processes, as demonstrated in
[
2
]
. Motivated by simulations in
[
2
, Section 6]
, it has been asked whether (or not), in the high-dimensional limit
d\to\infty
(with
2k>d+1
), the variance normalized random variables
Z_{d,k}
converge to a standard Gaussian one. Surprisingly, the cumulant analysis in
[
2
]
reveals a highly unconventional behaviour. After normalization, the higher-order cumulants of
Z_{d,k}
do not merely fail to vanish, they even diverge as
d\to\infty
. This stands in stark contrast to the standard central limit setting, where vanishing higher cumulants are the hallmark of Gaussian convergence. The result therefore suggests that
Z_{d,k}
should exhibit a non-Gaussian limit, if any. Remarkably, however, we will see that this intuition is misleading. Despite the divergence of its cumulants,
Z_{d,k}
can in fact converge to a Gaussian law. Our first theorem identifies the precise conditions under which this counter-intuitive phenomenon occurs.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence satisfying
k_{n}<d_{n}
,
2k_{n}>d_{n}+1
and
d_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Write
Z^{*}_{n}\coloneqq Z_{d_{n},k_{n}}/\sigma_{d_{n},k_{n}}
for the variance normalized version of
Z_{d_{n},k_{n}}
and abbreviate
r_{n}\coloneqq 2k_{n}-d_{n}-1
.

(a)

If
k_{n}/d_{n}\to 1/2
and
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{n}^{-1}\,r_{n}^{d_{n}/k_{n}}<e\pi
, then
Z^{*}_{n}
converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian law.

(b)

If
k_{n}/d_{n}\to 1/2
and
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{n}^{-1}\,r_{n}^{d_{n}/k_{n}}>e\pi
or if
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}k_{n}/d_{n}>1/2
, then
Z^{*}_{n}
converges in distribution to
0
.

Remark 2
.

Let
\gamma>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
be fixed and
\tau(d_{n})\in(0,1)
such that
k_{n}=\frac{d_{n}}{2}+\gamma d_{n}^{\beta}+\tau(d_{n})
is an integer. In this case, we get

d_{n}^{-1}r^{{d_{n}}/{k_{n}}}\sim 4\gamma^{2}d_{n}^{2\beta-1}\to\begin{cases}0,&\beta\in(0,1/2),\\
(2\gamma)^{2},&\beta=1/2,\\
\infty,&\beta\in(1/2,1),\end{cases}

where for two sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
Hence, for
\beta=1/2
, if
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{e\pi}
, then
Z_{n}^{*}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in distribution, and if
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{e\pi}
, we get
Z_{n}^{*}\to 0
in distribution. At the critical growth rate with
\beta=1/2
and
2\gamma=\sqrt{e\pi}
, the asymptotic behaviour of
Z_{n}^{*}
remains open.

Our second goal is to show that even when convergence to a normal distribution fails, a different type of scaling can still lead to a meaningful limit law.
Instead of considering
Z_{d,k}/\sigma_{d,k}
, we rescale the Lévy measure
\nu_{d,k}
and study the random variable
\widetilde{Z}_{d,k}

with Lévy triplet
(0,0,\nu_{d,k}/\sigma^{2}_{d,k})
.
Such a rescaling and associated random variables are considered when introducing infinitely divisible distributions and their Lévy triplets, see
[
3
, §17, Theorem 4]
,
[
9
, Theorem 7.10, Corollary 8.3]
and Section
2
.

It turns out that when the codimension
d_{n}-k_{n}=b\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed, the random variables
\widetilde{Z}_{d_{n},k_{n}}
converge in distribution to some non-Gaussian limit
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
(see Figure
1
for some illustrations).
On the other hand, when
d_{n}-k_{n}\to\infty
, then
\widetilde{Z}_{d_{n},k_{n}}
converges to a standard Gaussian distribution.

In the following, we write ‘
\log
’ for the natural logarithm with respect to base
e
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence satisfying
k_{n}<d_{n}
,
2k_{n}>d_{n}+1
and
d_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
(0,0,\nu_{d_{n},k_{n}})
denote the Lévy triplet of
Z_{d_{n},k_{n}}
and let
\widetilde{Z}_{n}
be a random variable with Lévy triplet
(0,0,\nu_{d_{n},k_{n}}/\sigma^{2}_{d_{n},k_{n}})
.

(a)

If the codimension
d_{n}-k_{n}=b\in\mathbb{N}
is constant, then
\widetilde{Z}_{n}
converges in distribution to an infinitely divisible random variable
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
with Lévy triplet
(0,0,\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)})
, where

\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)}(\dint x)=\Gamma({\textstyle\frac{b}{2}})^{-1}x^{-2}\bigl(-\log x\bigr)^{\frac{b-2}{2}}\;\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(x)\,\dint x.

In particular,
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
has finite moments of all orders and
\mathbb{V}(\widetilde{Z}^{(b)})=1
.

(b)

If
d_{n}-k_{n}\to\infty
, then
\widetilde{Z}_{n}
converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian law.

:
Densities of the random variables
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
(top line) and densities of the Lévy measure

b=1

.

1
. In Section

2
Background on infinitely divisible laws

A probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
is called
infinitely divisible
if, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability distribution
\mu_{n}
such that
\mu=\mu_{n}^{*n}
, where
\mu_{n}^{*n}
is the
n
-fold convolution of
\mu_{n}
with itself. A random variable
X
is called infinitely divisible if its distribution
\mu
satisfies this property. The class of infinitely divisible distributions is characterized by the
Lévy–Khintchine formula
. It says that the characteristic function
\varphi_{X}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,\mu(\dint x)
of an infinitely divisible random variable
X
with law
\mu
admits the unique representation

\varphi_{X}(t)=\exp\Big(i\gamma t-\frac{1}{2}at^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{itx}-1-itx\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\right)\nu(\dint x)\Big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

see
[
7
, Corollary 7.6]
.
Here,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
is called the drift term,
a\geq 0
is the Gaussian variance coefficient, and
\nu
is the
Lévy measure
, satisfying
\nu(\{0\})=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1\wedge x^{2})\,\nu(\dint x)<\infty
.
The triplet
(\gamma,a,\nu)
, which we call the
Lévy triplet
of
X
, is unique.
Convergence of infinitely divisible distributions can be described only in terms of the convergence of their associated Lévy triplets. In fact, the class of infinitely divisible distributions is closed under weak convergence, see
[
7
, Theorem 7.7 (i)]
.

The second moment of an infinitely divisible random variable
X
with Lévy measure
\nu
is finite if and only if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\nu(\dint x)<\infty
.
In this case, the above representation can be simplified to

\varphi_{X}(t)=\exp\Big(i\tilde{\gamma}t-\frac{1}{2}at^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{itx}-1-itx\right)\nu(\dint x)\Big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1)

which is the so-called
Kolmogorov representation
, cf.
[
3
, Equation (18.10)]
or
[
8
, Theorem 4.1.2]
, although the Lévy-Khintchine formula and the Kolmogorov representation are parametrized somewhat differently there.
Note that in general,
\gamma
is not equal to
\tilde{\gamma}
, as one needs to adjust for the missing indicator term
\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}
.

For our purposes, it suffices to treat infinitely divisible distributions whose characteristic functions are of the form (
2.1
), with
\tilde{\gamma}=0
and
a=0
.
For these, we now state and prove a simple convergence criterion.
While the proof can be derived from a deeper result about the convergence of infinitely divisible laws (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 7.7]
), we prefer to give a simple direct argument here to keep the paper self-contained. In what follows, we indicate convergence in distribution by the symbol
\xrightarrow{D}
and write
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for a standard Gaussian random variable.

Lemma 4
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of random variables.
Assume that their characteristic functions are of the form

\varphi_{X_{n}}(t)=\exp(\Psi_{n}(t))=\exp\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{itx}-1-itx)\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\Big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with measures
\nu_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}
that satisfy

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)=1.

(2.2)

(a)

If

\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\to 0\quad\text{for all}\quad\varepsilon>0,

(2.3)

then
X_{n}\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

(b)

If

\int_{\{|x|\leq\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\to 0\quad\text{for all}\quad\varepsilon>0,

(2.4)

then
X_{n}\xrightarrow{D}0
.

Proof.

We recall the following bound for the Taylor approximation of the exponential function from
[
7
, Lemma 6.15]
:

\Big|e^{ix}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(ix)^{k}}{k!}\Big|\leq\min\Big\{\frac{2|x|^{n}}{n!},\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\Big\},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.5)

To prove (a),
assume that (
2.3
) holds.
Note that by Lévy’s continuity theorem, it is enough to show that
\Psi_{n}(t)\to-t^{2}/2
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We thus fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and observe that by (
2.2
) and the triangle inequality,

|\Psi_{n}(t)-(-t^{2}/2)|=\left|\Psi_{n}(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(itx)^{2}}{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|e^{itx}-1-itx-\frac{(itx)^{2}}{2}\right|\,\nu_{n}(\dint x),

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Splitting the integral at an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and applying (
2.5
) to both parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|e^{itx}-1-itx-\frac{(itx)^{2}}{2}\right|\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\leq\int_{\{|x|\leq\varepsilon\}}\frac{|tx|^{3}}{6}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)+\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}|tx|^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|t^{3}|}{6}\int_{\{|x|\leq\varepsilon\}}\varepsilon x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)+t^{2}\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\leq\varepsilon\frac{|t^{3}|}{6}+t^{2}\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x),

where we used (
2.2
) in the last step.
Now (
2.3
) yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\Psi_{n}(t)-(-t^{2}/2)|\leq\varepsilon|t|^{3}/6+0.

Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, the first claim follows.

For part (b), we assume that (
2.4
) holds. Let again
t\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed.
By the triangle inequality, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we get that

|\Psi_{n}(t)|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|e^{itx}-1-itx|\,\nu_{n}(\dint x).

Splitting the integral at an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and applying (
2.5
) to both parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|e^{itx}-1-itx|\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\leq\int_{\{|x|\leq\varepsilon\}}\frac{|tx|^{2}}{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)+\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}2|tx|\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\int_{\{|x|\leq\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)+2|t|\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}\frac{x^{2}}{\varepsilon}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\int_{\{|x|\leq\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{n}(\dint x)+\frac{2|t|}{\varepsilon},

where we used (
2.2
) in the last step.
Now (
2.4
) yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\Psi_{n}(t)|\leq 0+2|t|/\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily large, it follows that
\Psi_{n}(t)\to 0
, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, so
X_{n}\xrightarrow{D}0
by Lévy’s continuity theorem.
∎

3
Estimates for beta functions

The
Beta function
is defined as

B(p,q):=\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)},\quad p,q>0,

(3.1)

where
\Gamma
is the usual Gamma function.
It is readily seen from the integral representation

B(p,q)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{p-1}(1-t)^{q-1}\dint t

that
B(p,q)
is decreasing in both arguments
p,q
.
It follows in particular that

B(p,q)\leq B({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}},{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{\Gamma(1)}=\pi,\quad p,q\geq\frac{1}{2},

where we used (
3.1
), as well as the fact that
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
and
\Gamma(1)=1
.
Thus,

\frac{\Gamma(p+q)}{\Gamma(p)}=\frac{1}{B(p,q)}\Gamma(q)\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\Gamma(q),\quad p,q\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(3.2)

The inequality

z^{1-t}\leq\frac{\Gamma(z+1)}{\Gamma(z+t)},\quad z\geq 0,\,t\in[0,1],

(3.3)

is due to Wendel, see
[
11
, Equation (4)]
(for
z=0
and
t=1
relation (
3.3
) holds with
0^{0}:=1
and for
z=t=0
it holds with
\Gamma(0)^{-1}=0
).

Lemma 5
.

(a)

If
p\geq 1
and
q\geq 0
, then
\Gamma(p+q)\geq\Gamma(p)(p-1)^{q}
.

(b)

If
p,q>0
, then
\Gamma(p+q)\leq\Gamma(p)(p+q)^{q}
.

(c)

If
q\geq 0
is fixed, then
\Gamma(p+q)\sim\Gamma(p)p^{q},\quad p\to\infty
.

Proof.

(a) Let
q=n+s
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
s\in[0,1)
. Repeated application of
\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
,
x>0
, yields

\Gamma(p+q)\geq\Gamma(p+s)(p+s)^{n}
. From (
3.3
) with
z=p+s-1\geq 0
and
t=1-s
, we obtain
\Gamma(p+s)\geq\Gamma(p)(p+s-1)^{s}
. Hence,
\Gamma(p+q)\geq\Gamma(p)(p+s)^{n}(p+s-1)^{s}\geq\Gamma(p)(p-1)^{n+s}=\Gamma(p)(p-1)^{q}
.

(b) Let
q=n+s
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
s\in(0,1]
. Repeated application of
\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
,
x>0
, and (
3.3
) with
z=p
and
t=s
yield
\Gamma(p+q)\leq(p+q)^{n}\Gamma(p+s)\leq(p+q)^{n+s}\Gamma(p+1)p^{-1}=\Gamma(p)(p+q)^{q}
.

(c) The last claim follows directly from parts (a) and (b).
∎

Finally, we recall from
[
12
, Section 12.33]
that

\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{z}}\left(\frac{z}{e}\right)^{z}\leq\Gamma(z)\leq e^{\frac{1}{12z}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{z}}\left(\frac{z}{e}\right)^{z},\quad z\geq 1,

(3.4)

which is the lower and upper
Stirling approximation
for the Gamma function.

3.1
Incomplete Beta function and Beta distribution

For
p,q>0
and
x\in[0,1]
, we write

B_{x}(p,q)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}u^{p-1}(1-u)^{q-1}\,\dint u

for the
incomplete Beta function
and

I_{x}(p,q)\coloneqq I(p,q;x)\coloneqq B_{x}(p,q)/B(p,q)

for the
regularized
(or normalized)
incomplete Beta function
.
We extend the domain of
I(p,q;\cdot)
to the whole of
\mathbb{R}
by setting
I(p,q;x)\coloneqq 0
for
x<0
and
I(p,q;x)\coloneqq 1
for
x>1
.
The probability distribution
\Beta(p,q)
on
\mathbb{R}
with cumulative distribution function
I(p,q;\,\cdot\,)
is called the
Beta distribution
with parameters
p,q
.
The mean and variance of
\Beta(p,q)
are given by
\mu:=\frac{p}{p+q}
and
\sigma^{2}:=\frac{pq}{(p+q)^{2}(p+q+1)}
, respectively.

Lemma 6
.

Let
p,q>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
write
\mu=\frac{p}{p+q}
.
Then
I_{x}(p,q)\leq\frac{1}{p(\frac{x}{\mu}-1)^{2}}
for
x<\mu
and
I_{x}(p,q)\geq 1-\frac{1}{p(\frac{x}{\mu}-1)^{2}}
for
x>\mu
.

Proof.

Let
Y
be a random variable with distribution
\Beta(p,q)
.
Then
\sigma^{2}/\mu^{2}\leq 1/p
, as follows from the expressions for
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
above.
For
x<\mu
, the Chebyshef inequality then yields

I_{x}(p,q)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq x)\leq\mathbb{P}(|Y-\mu|\geq|x-\mu|)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(x-\mu)^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\frac{1}{(\frac{x}{\mu}-1)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{p(\frac{x}{\mu}-1)^{2}}.

Likewise, for
x>\mu
we get

1-I_{x}(p,q)=\mathbb{P}(Y>x)\leq\mathbb{P}(|Y-\mu|>|x-\mu|)\leq\frac{1}{p(\frac{x}{\mu}-1)^{2}},

which concludes the argument.
∎

An application of Lemma
6
yields the following result, which in turn will be used below to establish Theorems
1
and
3
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1},(q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be sequences in
(0,\infty)
and
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
.
Write
\mu_{n}=p_{n}/(p_{n}+q_{n})
.

(a)

If
x_{n}/\mu_{n}\to\infty
and
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}p_{n}>0
, then
I_{x_{n}}(p_{n},q_{n})\to 1
.

(b)

If
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n}/\mu_{n}>1
and
p_{n}\to\infty
, then
I_{x_{n}}(p_{n},q_{n})\to 1
.

(c)

If
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n}/\mu_{n}<1
and

Before giving the proofs of Theorems

and

We call a pair of integers
(d,k)

1\leq k\leq d-1

2k>d+1
(a sequence of such pairs is admissible if each pair in the sequence is admissible).
Recall that for admissible
(d,k)

The variance
\sigma^{2}_{d,k}=\mathbb{V}(Z_{d,k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)

\nu^{*}_{d,k}(A)=\nu_{d,k}(\sigma_{d,k}A)

Then

The following is an immediate consequence of Lemma
4
.

Corollary 8
.

Let
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an admissible sequence. If
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 0
for all
\varepsilon>0

Z^{*}_{d_{n},k_{n}}\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, as
n\to\infty

J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 1
for all
\varepsilon>0

Z^{*}_{d_{n},k_{n}}\xrightarrow{D}0
, as
n\to\infty

Note that for any
\varepsilon>0

J(d,k,\varepsilon)=\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu^{*}_{d,k}(\dint x)=\int_{\{|x|>\sigma_{d,k}\varepsilon\}}\frac{x^{2}}{\sigma_{d,k}^{2}}\,\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)=\frac{\int_{\{|x|>\sigma_{d,k}\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\nu_{d,k}(\dint x)}.

Using (
1.2
) and substituting
u=x^{\frac{2}{k-1}}
, we can then express
J(d,k,\varepsilon)
as

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\{\sigma_{d,k}\varepsilon<x<1\}}x^{1-\frac{d-1}{k-1}}\bigl(1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\bigr)^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}\dint x}{\int_{0}^{1}x^{1-\frac{d-1}{k-1}}\bigl(1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\bigr)^{\frac{d-k-2}{2}}\dint x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\{(\sigma_{d,k}\varepsilon)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}<u<1\}}u^{\frac{2k-d-1}{2}-1}\,(1-u)^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}\dint u}{{\int_{0}^{1}u^{\frac{2k-d-1}{2}-1}\,(1-u)^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}\dint u}}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=I\left({\textstyle\frac{d-k}{2}},{\textstyle\frac{2k-d-1}{2}};1-(\sigma_{d,k}\varepsilon)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\right),

(4.2)

where we used the fact that
I(p,q;x)+I(q,p;1-x)=1
for
p,q>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
in the last line.

Note that by the same approach, one obtains

\sigma^{2}_{d,k}=\frac{\omega_{d-k}}{2}B\left(\textstyle\frac{2k-d-1}{2},\frac{d-k}{2}\right)=\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}+\frac{d-k}{2}\right)}=\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)},

(4.3)

where we used (
3.1
) for the second equality. The next lemma will ensure that
\sigma_{d_{n},k_{n}}\varepsilon
, and hence the last argument of
I(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,)
in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), lies between zero and one for large enough
n
.
We believe this to be noteworthy, even though it is not needed for the proofs of Theorems
1
and
3
.

Lemma 9
.

If
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an admissible sequence with
d_{n}\to\infty
, then
\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{d_{n},k_{n}}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Recall that
d_{n}-k_{n}\geq 1
and
2k_{n}-d_{n}-1\geq 1
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be given. We show that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
if
n
is large enough. In the following, we occasionally omit the index
n
.

It follows from (
4.3
), (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) that

\sigma^{2}_{d,k}=\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}+\frac{d-k}{2}\right)}\leq\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}+1}\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-k}{2}\right)}\leq\sqrt{d-k}\left(\frac{2e\pi}{d-k}\right)^{\frac{d-k}{2}}.

There is some
C_{0}>1
such that the upper bound is less or equal
\varepsilon_{0}
if
d-k>C_{0}
. Hence
\sigma_{n}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{n}-k_{n}>C_{0}
.

Now we consider all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{n}-k_{n}\leq C_{0}
. For any such
n
we have
k_{n}\geq d_{n}-C_{0}
and
2k_{n}-d_{n}-1\geq d_{n}-2C_{0}-1
.
Hence, if there are infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{n}-k_{n}\leq C_{0}
, then we can assume that
(2k_{n}-d_{n}-1)/2\geq 2
if
n
is large enough and
d_{n}-k_{n}\leq C_{0}
.
For any such
n
, the monotonicity of the Gamma function and Lemma
5
(a) yield

\sigma^{2}_{d,k}\leq\pi^{\frac{C_{0}}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\leq\pi^{\frac{C_{0}}{2}}\left(\frac{2k-d-3}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\pi^{\frac{C_{0}}{2}}\left(\frac{d-2C_{0}-3}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

If
n
is large enough, the right-hand side is at most
\varepsilon_{0}
.

Thus, if
n\in\mathbb{N}
is large enough, in any case we obtain
\sigma_{n}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
.
∎

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Throughout this section, we consider admissible sequences
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
Note that by Corollary
8
, the determination of
\lim_{n\to\infty}J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)
is sufficient for proving the assertions of Theorem
1
. We start with the case where
\liminf_{n\to\infty}d_{n}/k_{n}>1/2
. In what follows, we will frequently use the abbreviation
\sigma_{n}\coloneqq\sigma_{d_{n},k_{n}}
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an admissible sequence with
d_{n}\to\infty
, as
n\to\infty
.
Suppose that
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}k_{n}/d_{n}>1/2
. Then
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)=1
, for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}/d_{n}\eqqcolon\alpha\in(1/2,1]
exists.
Using (
4.2
), we have
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)=I(p_{n},q_{n};x_{n})
with

p_{n}=\frac{d_{n}-k_{n}}{2}
,
q_{n}=\frac{2k_{n}-d_{n}-1}{2}
and
x_{n}=1-(\sigma_{n}\varepsilon)^{\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}}
.
Dropping the index
n
where it is unlikely to cause confusion, we write

\displaystyle x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}

\displaystyle=(1-(\sigma\varepsilon)^{\frac{2}{k-1}})\frac{k-1}{d-k}=\Big(1-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\Big(\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{2k-d-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}+\frac{d-k}{2})}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k},

where we used (
4.3
) for the last step.
Using Lemma
5
(a) and the fact that
\frac{2k_{n}-d_{n}-3}{2}\geq\frac{2\alpha-1}{4}d_{n}
if
n
is large enough, we get that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(1-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\Big(\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}\frac{1}{(\frac{2k-d-3}{2})^{\frac{d-k}{2}}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(1-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\Big(\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}\frac{1}{(\frac{2\alpha-1}{4}d)^{\frac{d-k}{2}}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(1-\Big(\varepsilon^{\frac{4}{d-k}}\frac{4\pi}{2\alpha-1}\frac{1}{d}\Big)^{\frac{d-k}{2(k-1)}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k}.

If
\alpha<1
, then
p_{n}\to\infty
, and the above bound yields

\liminf_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(1-\Big(\varepsilon^{\frac{4}{d-k}}\frac{4\pi}{2\alpha-1}\frac{1}{d}\Big)^{\frac{d-k}{2(k-1)}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k}=(1-0)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}>1,

which implies
I(p_{n},q_{n};x_{n})\to 1
by Lemma
7
(b).

Let us now assume that
\alpha=1
.
Note that for any
c>0
, we have that

\varepsilon^{\frac{4}{d_{n}-k_{n}}}\frac{4\pi}{2\alpha-1}\frac{1}{d_{n}}\leq c

for all but finitely many
n
, which gives

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(1-\Big(\varepsilon^{\frac{4}{d-k}}\frac{4\pi}{2\alpha-1}\frac{1}{d}\Big)^{\frac{d-k}{2(k-1)}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k}\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(1-c^{\frac{d-k}{2(k-1)}}\Big)\frac{k-1}{d-k}.

Since
\frac{d-k}{2(k-1)}\to\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}=0

\lim_{t\to 0+}\frac{1-c^{t}}{2t}=-\frac{\log c}{2}
.

Since
c>0
was arbitrary, this yields

\liminf_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}\geq\sup_{c>0}-\frac{\log c}{2}=\infty,

which, together with
\liminf_{n\to\infty}p_{n}\geq 1/2>0

(a).
∎

We now focus on admissible sequences with

The asymptotic behaviour then depends on the growth of

.
If

is sufficiently small (which means that
k_{n}
is sufficiently close to

), then

tends to

\varepsilon>0
.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an admissible sequence such that
d_{n}\to\infty
and
k_{n}/d_{n}\to 1/2
.
Write
r_{n}:=2k_{n}-d_{n}-1
.
Then the following holds:

(a)

If
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{n}^{-1}r_{n}^{d_{n}/k_{n}}<e\pi
, then
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

(b)

If
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{n}^{-1}r_{n}^{d_{n}/k_{n}}>e\pi
, then
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

We fix
\varepsilon>0
and omit the index
n
. Then, we notice that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\frac{4}{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\pi^{\frac{d-k}{k-1}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}}\sim\pi^{\frac{\frac{d}{k}-1}{1-\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}}{(4\pi/(k-1))^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\frac{k-1}{2e}}\sim\frac{2e\pi}{k}\Gamma\left({\textstyle\frac{2k-d-1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k-1}},

where we used (
4.3
) and (
3.4
).
While
r=2k-d-1
may not tend to infinity, (
3.4
) still guarantees that

0<\inf_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{r}{2})}{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{r}}(\frac{r}{2e})^{\frac{r}{2}}}\leq\sup_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{r}{2})}{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{r}}(\frac{r}{2e})^{\frac{r}{2}}}<\infty,

which yields

\frac{2e\pi}{k}\Gamma\left({\textstyle\frac{2k-d-1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\sim\frac{2e\pi}{k}\left(\frac{r}{2e}\right)^{\frac{r}{k-1}}\sim\frac{2e\pi}{k}r^{\frac{r}{k-1}},

where the last step holds because

tends to zero.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\frac{4}{k-1}}\Big(\frac{k-1}{r}\Big)^{2}\sim\frac{2e\pi}{k}r^{\frac{r}{k-1}}\Big(\frac{k}{r}\Big)^{2}=e\pi\frac{2k}{r^{\frac{d-1}{k-1}}}\sim e\pi\frac{d}{r^{\frac{d}{k}}},

(4.4)

where we used

\frac{r^{\frac{d}{k}}}{r^{\frac{d-1}{k-1}}}=\Big(r^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\Big)^{1-\frac{d}{k}}\to 1^{-1}=1

in the last step.
By (
4.1
), we have
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)=1-I(p_{n},q_{n};x_{n})
with
p_{n}=\frac{r_{n}}{2}
,
q_{n}=\frac{d_{n}-k_{n}}{2}
and
x_{n}=(\sigma_{n}\varepsilon)^{\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}}
.

To prove (a), suppose that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}(d_{n}^{-1}r_{n}^{d_{n}/k_{n}})<e\pi
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that either the sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is bounded or
r_{n}\to\infty
. In the former case, we observe that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}p_{n}\geq 1/2
since
r_{n}\geq 1
and further, using (
4.4
),

\Big(x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}\Big)^{2}=\sigma^{\frac{4}{k-1}}\Big(\frac{k-1}{r}\Big)^{2}\sim e\pi\frac{d}{r^{\frac{d}{k}}}\geq e\pi\frac{d}{(\max_{n}r_{n})^{2}}\to\infty.

Lemma
7
(a) thus yields
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 0
. In the latter case,
p_{n}=r_{n}/2\to\infty
and (
4.4
) gives

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}\Big)^{2}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}e\pi\frac{d}{r^{\frac{d}{k}}}>1,

hence Lemma
7
(b) yields
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 0
.
Together with the previous consideration, this proves the first part of the proposition.

For part (b) suppose that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}(d_{n}^{-1}r_{n}^{d_{n}/k_{n}})>e\pi
.
Arguing as in the previous case then gives
\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\frac{p_{n}+q_{n}}{p_{n}}<1
.
Since
r_{n}^{2}\geq r_{n}^{\frac{d_{n}}{k_{n}}}\geq e\pi d_{n}
for all but finitely many
n
, we further have
p_{n}=r_{n}/2\to\infty
.
We can thus apply Lemma
7
(c) to obtain
J(d_{n},k_{n},\varepsilon)\to 1
.
∎

now follows directly by combining Proposition
10
, Proposition
11
and Corollary
8
.

Proof of Theorem
3

For
k<d
and
2k>d+1
let
f_{d,k}
denote the density of the Lévy measure in (
1.2
).
Using (
4.3
) and
\omega_{d-k}=2\pi^{\frac{d-k}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{d-k}{2})
, one obtains for

that

\frac{f_{d,k}(x)}{\sigma_{d,k}^{2}}=\frac{2}{k-1}x^{-1-\frac{d-1}{k-1}}(1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}})^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}+\frac{d-k}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d-k}{2}\right)}.

(4.5)

In the following, we consider an admissible sequence
(d_{n},k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as well as random variables

\widetilde{Z}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with respective Lévy triplets
(0,0,\nu_{d_{n},k_{n}}/\sigma^{2}_{d_{n},k_{n}})
.

From now on, we will abbreviate
f_{n}\coloneqq f_{d_{n},k_{n}}
,
\sigma_{n}\coloneqq\sigma_{d_{n},k_{n}}
and
\nu_{n}\coloneqq\nu_{d_{n},k_{n}}
to simplify the notation.
Sometimes the index
n
will be omitted from

and

for the sake of readability.

Let us first assume that
d_{n}-k_{n}=b\in\mathbb{N}
is constant.
We show that
\widetilde{Z}_{n}
converges to
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
in distribution.

Step 1: Pointwise limit of the rescaled densities.

Let
x\in(0,1)
be fixed.
Since
k_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, it follows by l’Hôpital’s rule that

1-x^{\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}}\sim(-2\log x)/(k_{n}-1)
, as
n\to\infty
.
Using these observations as well as Lemma
5

\frac{f_{n}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\sim\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}x^{-2}\left(-\frac{2\log x}{k_{n}-1}\right)^{\frac{b}{2}-1}\frac{\left(\frac{2k_{n}-d_{n}-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{b}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{b}{2})}.

Since
k_{n}-1=d_{n}-b-1\sim d_{n}
and
2k_{n}-d_{n}-1=d_{n}-2b-1\sim d_{n}
, it follows that

\frac{f_{n}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\to\Gamma({\textstyle\frac{b}{2}})^{-1}x^{-2}(-\log x)^{\frac{b-2}{2}},\quad n\to\infty,

so
f_{n}/\sigma^{2}_{n}
converges pointwise to the density of the Lévy measure
\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)}
.

Step 2: Integrable domination.

Let
g_{t}(x):=e^{itx}-1-itx
, for
t,x\in\mathbb{R}
.
We construct, for fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
, an integrable dominating function for

|g_{t}(x)|\,f_{n}(x)/\sigma^{2}_{n}
,
x\in(0,1)
.
Since
|g_{t}(x)|\leq t^{2}x^{2}
by (
2.5
), we get using (
4.5
) that

|g_{t}(x)|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\leq\frac{2t^{2}}{k-1}x^{1-\frac{d-1}{k-1}}(1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}})^{\frac{b}{2}-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}+\frac{b}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k-d-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)}.

Let us first assume that
b\geq 2
.
Since
\frac{d_{n}-1}{k_{n}-1}=\frac{d_{n}-1}{d_{n}-b-1}\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
, it holds for large
n
that

x^{1-\frac{d_{n}-1}{k_{n}-1}}\leq x^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in(0,1).

From
1+y\leq e^{y}
with
y=(2\log x)/(k-1)
, we get

1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\leq-\frac{2\log x}{k-1},\quad x\in(0,1).

Using these bounds as well as Lemma
5
(b), we obtain

|g_{t}(x)|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\leq\frac{2t^{2}}{k-1}x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(-\frac{2\log x}{k-1}\right)^{\frac{b}{2}-1}\frac{\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{b}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{b}{2})}=\frac{t^{2}}{\Gamma(\frac{b}{2})}x^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-\log x)^{\frac{b}{2}-1},\quad x\in(0,1),

for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The integrability of the right-hand side follows after substituting
x=e^{-u}
.

Now assume that
b=1
.
For
x\in(0,1]
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the mean value theorem implies that
\alpha(1-x)\leq 1-x^{\alpha}
.
Applying this with
\alpha=2/(k_{n}-1)
, which is in
[0,1]
since
k_{n}\geq 3
, gives

(1-x^{\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\Big(\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}(1-x)\Big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Bounding the other terms of
|g_{t}(x)|\,f_{n}(x)/\sigma_{n}^{2}
as above yields

|g_{t}(x)|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\leq\frac{2t^{2}}{k-1}x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{2}{k-1}(1-x)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}=\frac{t^{2}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}x^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-x)^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in(0,1),

for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This function is clearly integrable.

Step 3: Convergence of exponents and random variables.

Define

\Psi_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\int_{(0,1)}g_{t}(x)\,\nu_{n}(\dint x),\quad\Psi^{(b)}(t):=\int_{(0,1)}g_{t}(x)\,\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)}(\dint x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with
g_{t}(x)=e^{itx}-1-itx
as above.
By Step 1,
f_{n}(x)/\sigma^{2}_{n}\to\Gamma(b/2)^{-1}x^{-2}(-\log x)^{(b-2)/2}
for each
x\in(0,1)
, as
n\to\infty
, and by Step 2, for each fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the integrands
x\mapsto g_{t}(x)f_{n}(x)/\sigma^{2}_{n}
are dominated by an integrable function independent of
n
, provided that
n
is large enough.
Hence, the dominated convergence theorem yields for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
that
\Psi_{n}(t)\to\Psi^{(b)}(t)\in(0,\infty)
, as
n\to\infty
.

Let
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
be an infinitely divisible random variable with Lévy triplet
(0,0,\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)})
.
(The fact that
\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)}
is a valid Lévy measure, i.e., that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1\wedge x^{2})\,\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)}(\dint x)<\infty
, follows from (
4.6
) below.)
It then follows for the characteristic functions of
\widetilde{Z}_{n}
and
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
that

\varphi_{\widetilde{Z}_{n}}(t)=\exp(\Psi_{n}(t))\ \to\ \exp(\Psi^{(b)}(t))=\varphi_{\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}}(t)\qquad\text{for each }t\in\mathbb{R},

so
\widetilde{Z}_{n}\xrightarrow{D}\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
by Lévy’s continuity theorem.

For
m\geq 2
, the
m
-th order cumulant of
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
is given by

\kappa_{m}\bigl(\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}\bigr)=\int_{(0,1)}x^{m}\,\widetilde{\nu}^{(b)}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{b}{2})}\int_{0}^{1}x^{m-2}\,(-\log x)^{\frac{b-2}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\Big(\frac{1}{m-1}\Big)^{\frac{b}{2}},

(4.6)

since
\int_{0}^{1}x^{p-1}(-\log x)^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x=\Gamma(\alpha+1)/p^{\alpha+1}

(substitute
x=e^{-u/p}
).
In particular, the random variable
\widetilde{Z}^{(b)}
has finite moments of all orders and
\mathbb{V}(\widetilde{Z}^{(b)})=1
. This concludes the proof of the first part of Theorem
3
.

Let us now assume that
d_{n}-k_{n}\to\infty
, as
n\to\infty
.
Write
\widetilde{\nu}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\nu_{n}
.
For arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, it follows from (
4.5
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\widetilde{\nu}_{n}(\dint x)

\displaystyle=B\left({\textstyle\frac{2k-d-1}{2}},{\textstyle\frac{d-k}{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{2}{k-1}\int_{\{\varepsilon<x<1\}}x^{1-\frac{d-1}{k-1}}(1-x^{\frac{2}{k-1}})^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}\,\dint x

\displaystyle=I\left({\textstyle\frac{d-k}{2}},{\textstyle\frac{2k-d-1}{2}};1-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k-1}}\right),

where the second equality follows by adapting the arguments leading to (
4.2
).
We now apply Lemma
7
(c) with
p_{n}=\frac{d_{n}-k_{n}}{2}
,
q_{n}=\frac{2k_{n}-d_{n}-1}{2}
and
x_{n}=1-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}}
.
Note that
p_{n}\to\infty
by our assumptions on
d_{n}-k_{n}
.
Furthermore, we have

\frac{x_{n}}{\mu_{n}}=\frac{k_{n}-1}{d_{n}-k_{n}}(1-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k_{n}-1}})\sim-\frac{2}{d_{n}-k_{n}}\log\varepsilon\sim 0,

where second step follows from
\lim_{s\to 0+}(1-\varepsilon^{s})/s=-\log\varepsilon
and the last one from
d_{n}-k_{n}\to\infty
.
Hence, the conditions of Lemma
7
(c) are met, so

\int_{\{|x|>\varepsilon\}}x^{2}\,\widetilde{\nu}_{n}(\dint x)\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\widetilde{\nu}_{n}(\dint x)=1
by construction, Lemma
4
(a) yields that
\widetilde{Z}_{n}\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
This concludes the proof of the second part. ∎
