On the lattice of the weak factorization systems on a finite lattice
Yongle Luo, Baptiste Rognerud

Abstract
We consider the lattice of all the weak factorization systems on a given finite lattice. We prove that it is semidistributive, trim and congruence uniform. We deduce a graph theoretical approach to the problem of enumerating transfer systems. As an application we find a lower bound for the number of transfer systems on a boolean lattice.



1
Introduction

A
weak factorization system
on a category is a pair
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
of subclasses of the morphisms of the category satisfying a list of natural conditions (see
definition

2.1
). One can order the weak factorization systems on a given category by setting
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})\preceq(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
if
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. An interval in the poset of weak factorization system was called a
premodel structure
by Barton in
[
13
]
. A
model structure
, in the sense of Quillen, is a premodel structure which satisfies an additional property. In other words, the model structures are particular intervals in this poset. If one is interested in classifying model structures, it is tempting to start by classifying the weak factorization systems. Such a classification seems to be difficult to obtain in general but a lot of results have recently been obtained (see e.g.
[
16
]
for a very nice introductory article on the subject) in the particular case where the category is a
finite lattice
.

In that case, the two classes
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
of a weak factorization systems satisfy a very short list of properties: they are subposets of
(L,\leq)
which are respectively closed under pushouts (joins in lattice terminology) and pullbacks (meets). The class
\mathcal{R}
is called a
transfer system
and we call the class
\mathcal{L}
a
left saturated set
. We can order transfer systems by inclusion of relations and similarly, we order the left saturated sets by reverse inclusion. It is classical that the classes
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
determine each other. So, the projections onto each component induce injective morphisms of posets from the poset of weak factorization systems to the poset of transfer systems and of left saturated sets. By
[
21
, Theorem 4.13]
, the three posets are in fact isomorphic. Moreover by
[
21
, Proposition 3.7]
they are lattices where the meet is given by
intersection of the transfer systems
and the join is given by the
intersection of left saturated sets
. Surprisingly
transfer systems
have a different origin. There is a notion of
G
-transfer systems (see
definition

2.8
) where
G
is a finite group, which can be used to classify the so-called
N_{\infty}
operads up to homotopy (see
[
21
, Theorem 3.6]
and the references above it). In the particular case where the group
G
is abelian (or every subgroup is normal) a
G
-transfer system is nothing but a transfer system on its lattice of subgroups. This gives another motivation for the classification of the weak factorization systems on a given lattice. Many results in that direction have been recently obtained: for the cyclic
p
-groups see
[
37
]
, for the symmetric group
S_{3}
or the quaternion group
Q_{8}
see
[
38
]
, the elementary abelian
p
-groups of rank two see
[
8
]
, the Dihedral group
D_{p^{n}}
see
[
6
]
, see also
[
16
]
for more references.

The lattice of subgroups of a cyclic
p
-group is a total order and it has been proved by Roitzheim, Barnes and Balchin that the lattice of transfer systems is in this case isomorphic to the
Tamari lattice
(see
[
37
, Theorem 25]
, see also
section

10.1
for an alternative proof). The Tamari lattices are particular orderings of the Catalan numbers and they appear throughout mathematics. In representation theory, they are known to be the lattices of
torsion pairs
of the path algebras of equioriented quivers of type
A
(see e.g.
[
41
]
).

The notion of torsion pairs in an
abelian category
has been introduced by Dickson in
[
20
]
and has received a lot of attention recently due to its relation with the
\tau
-tilting theory
of Adachi, Iyama and Reiten
[
1
]
. The lattice properties of the lattice of torsion pairs in the category of finitely generated modules over an artinian algebra have been extensively studied in
[
19
]
. The fact that the Tamari lattice appears at the intersection of these two theories is the starting point for this article and our results can be summarized by saying that from the lattice point of view, the weak factorization systems of finite lattices and the torsion pairs of artinian algebras are extremely similar. The main difference lies in the fact there are generally
infinitely many torsion pairs
. A more detailed comparison of the properties is given in
section

11
.

Let us be more precise on the different results of this article, we refer to
figure

1
and
figure

2
for an illustration in the case where the lattice
L
is a commutative square. A lattice
(L,\land,\lor)
is said to be
distributive
if the meet distributes over the join and vice-versa. As main example, given a poset
(P,\leq)
we consider
\operatorname{Ideal}(P)
the set of
order ideals
of
P
(downwards closed subsets). The inclusion of ideals turns
\operatorname{Ideal}(P)
into a poset and since both the intersection and union of ideals are ideals, it is a lattice, and it is distributive by the ‘distributive laws’ in set theory. Moreover, by the Birkhoff
representation theorem
, any finite distributive lattice
L
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Ideal}(P)
where
P
is the subposet of
L
containing the
join-irreducible elements
of
L
. For our setting, this notion is too strict and rigid, indeed as first result we have a characterization of the distributive lattices of weak factorization systems.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
L
be a finite lattice. Then the lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is
distributive
if and only if
|L|\leq 2
.

One should note that for more general categories
\mathcal{C}
there are other examples of distributive lattices of weak factorization systems. This is for example the case of the
category of sets
(see the poset in
[
3
]
).

Semidistributive lattices
are a generalization of the distributive lattices introduced by Jónsson
[
26
]
who was inspired by Whitman’s solution to the word problem for free lattices involving the existence of a nice canonical form. We refer to
[
24
, Chapter 3]
for more details. We say that a lattice
(L,\land,\lor)
is
semidistributive
if
\forall a,b,c\in L

a\vee(b\wedge c)=(a\vee b)\wedge(a\vee c)\hbox{ whenever }a\vee b=a\vee c,

and

a\wedge(b\vee c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c)\hbox{ whenever }a\wedge b=a\wedge c.

For weak factorization systems we have:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is a
semidistributive
lattice.

In the semidistributive lattices we have the existence of a so-called
canonical join decomposition
for their elements. We refer to
section

5.1
for explicit definitions. Loosely speaking any element of a semidistributive lattice can be written in a unique way as the join of
join-irreducible elements
. This is a lattice version of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic which says that any integer can be written in a unique way as product of prime numbers.

The join-irreducibles in the poset of transfer systems are particularly easy to describe. We denote by
\operatorname{Rel}(L)
the set of all
relations of
(L,\leq)
. That is the set of
(a,b)
such that
a\leq b
. We denote by
\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
the set of the non-trivial relations on
L
i.e., the relations
(a,b)
with
a\neq b
. Then, we prove that sending a relation
(a,b)
to the smallest transfer system
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
containing
(a,b)
is a bijection between the non-trivial relations in
L
and the join-irreducible elements in the lattice of transfer systems on
L
. For two relations
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
we write
(a,b)\sqsubset(c,d)
if there is a pullback diagram

J

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. The poset of join-irreducible elements in the lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is isomorphic to
(\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L),\sqsubset)
.

We refer to
figure

for an illustration in the simple case where
L
is a commutative square.

As a corollary, we obtain the following result, which answers a question in
[
8
, remark 4.5]
:

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
L
and
L^{\prime}
be two finite lattices with isomorphic lattices of weak factorization systems. Then
L
and
L^{\prime}
are isomorphic.

More precisely, we show that
L\setminus\{0\}
(where
0
is the least element) is isomorphic to the largest principal ideal of
(\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L),\sqsubset)
.

For two relations
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
in
\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
, we write
(a,b)\boxslash(c,d)
if
(a,b)
lifts on the left
(c,d)
(see
section

2.1
for more details). Moreover, for every
S\subseteq\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
we say that
S
is an
elevating set
if for every
(a,b)\in S
and
(c,d)\in S
we have
(a,b)\boxslash(c,d)
and
(c,d)\boxslash(a,b)
. The existence of canonical join representations in the semidistributive lattice of weak factorization systems, implies the following result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. There is a bijection between the set of weak factorization systems on
L
and the set of elevating subsets of
\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
.

The bijection is moreover explicit: if
S
is an elevating set, then the corresponding transfer system is the smallest transfer system containing
S
. The inverse bijection is more subtle: in any semidistributive lattice there is a
labelling of the cover relations
by the join-irreducible elements of the lattice. For transfer systems a cover relation
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{R}
is labelled by the unique non-trivial relation of
\mathcal{R}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{1}
(see
lemma

5.3
). Given a transfer system
\mathcal{R}
, the corresponding elevating set is the set consisting of the labels of all the lower cover relations
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\lessdot\mathcal{R}
.

The set of all the canonical join-representations of the elements of a semidistributive lattice

can be naturally viewed as a

. It has been proved by Emily Barnard
[

that this simplicial complex is a
flag
. This means that it is the
clique complex
of its
1

elevating graph
of the lattice
L
. This is the
graph
whose
vertices
are the non-trivial relations of
(L,\leq)
and there is an
edge
between
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
if and only if
(a,b)\boxslash(c,d)
and
(c,d)\boxslash(a,b)
. For the commutative square this graph is illustrated in

on

of

10

.

Let

is the boolean lattice with

has only
65

Moreover any subgraph of a clique is also a clique, so if there is a clique of size
n

maximal size of a clique
, is the maximal size of an elevating set, hence it is the
maximal number of lower cover relations
of a transfer system, which we denote by
\operatorname{mcov}_{\downarrow}(L)
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. There are at least
2^{\operatorname{mcov}_{\downarrow}(L)}
weak factorization systems on
L
.

The lattice of weak factorization system is
not regular
in general and the number
\operatorname{mcov}_{\downarrow}(L)
seems difficult to determine in general, but in the special case of the boolean lattice we obtain the following lower bound:

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
L
be the boolean lattice of the subsets of
[n]
. Then, there are at least
2^{a_{n}}
transfer systems on
L
, where

1.

If
n
is odd,
a_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\binom{n}{j}\binom{n-j}{n-2j}
.

2.

If
n
is even,
a_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\binom{n}{j}\binom{n-j}{n+1-2j}.

Our next result involves the notion of
trim
lattice which was introduced by Hugh Thomas in
[
40
]
as a non-graded generalization of
distributive lattice
, we refer to
section

4.3
for the definition and for more details. The notions of trimness and semidistributivity are independent of each other: there are examples of trim lattices that are not semidistributive and conversely. However, it has been proved in
[
43
]
that a semidistributive lattice which is also
extremal
is trim. Here by an extremal, we mean a lattice whose length is equal to its number of join-irreducible elements and also to the number of meet-irreducible elements.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is a
trim lattice
.

As a comparison,
the lattice of torsion pairs
of artinian algebras is in general
not a trim lattice
. Trimness occurs for example for the lattice of torsion pairs of the path algebra of
Dynkin type
and for incidence algebras of posets of finite representation type (see
[
43
]
). For a counterexample, the lattice of torsion pairs of a
preprojective algebra
of Dynkin type is isomorphic to the weak Bruhat ordering of the corresponding Weyl group (
[
30
]
) and this is not a trim lattice.

Trim lattices have an important distributive sublattice which is called the
spine
. It consists of the elements on the chains of maximal length. We prove that a weak factorization system
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is on the spine if and only
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{R}=\operatorname{Rel}(L)
. In other words if and only if every morphism is either in
\mathcal{L}
or in
\mathcal{R}
. If
(a,b)
is a non-trivial relation of
L
, then it can be seen as the interval
\{x\in L\ |\ a\leq x\leq b\}
of the poset. These intervals can be naturally ordered by setting
(a,b)\preceq(c,d)
if and only if
a\leq c
and
b\leq d
. We obtain the following representation of the spine:

Proposition 1.10
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the spine of
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Ideal}\big((\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L),\preceq)^{op}\big)
.

The following results concern the
congruences
of the lattice of weak factorization systems. Recall that an equivalence relation compatible with the meet and the join of the lattice is called a congruence. The set
\operatorname{Con}(L)
of all congruences on
L
is also a lattice when ordered by refinement and it is known to be a
distributive
lattice. The Birkhoff theorem tells us that
\operatorname{Con}(L)=\operatorname{Ideal}(P)
where
P
is the poset of join-irreducible congruences. Roughly speaking a lattice
L
is
congruence uniform
when the irreducible congruences are in bijection with the irreducible elements of
L
, we refer to
section

6.1
for a more precise statement. By labelling the cover relations in the lattice of weak factorization systems (a consequence of semidistributivity), we are able to prove the following result.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is a
congruence uniform
lattice.

The non-trivial relations
a\leq b
on
L
, when seen as intervals of the poset
L
are also naturally ordered by containment
\subseteq
. Then we have:

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the lattice of congruences of the lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Ideal}\big((\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L),\subseteq)^{op}\big)
.

Finally, let us return to the
G
-transfer systems. They coincide with the transfer systems on the lattice
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
of the subgroups of
G
, when the group is abelian, but in general, this is a proper
sublattice
. We prove in
section

9
that it inherits the properties of the lattice of transfer systems on
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then the lattice of
G
-transfer systems is
semidistributive
,
trim
and
congruence uniform
.

Weak factorization systems and transfer systems

In this short section we recall the definitions and basic results on weak factorization systems and transfer systems on a finite lattice

.

2.1

2.2
Transfer systems

We now consider the case where
\mathcal{C}
is the category of a finite poset. Moreover since we want our categories to have limits and colimits we will assume that the poset is a
lattice
. Concretely if
(L,\leq)
is a finite lattice, it can be viewed as a category whose objects are the elements of
L
. There is a unique morphism from
x
to
y
if and only if
x\leq y
. Since a poset is reflexive, there is a unique morphism from
x
to
x
, which is nothing but the identity morphism at
x
. Moreover, the transitivity of the relation implies that our construction is a category. The antisymmetry of the relation implies that the only isomorphisms in this category are the identity morphisms. In particular there are no non-trivial retracts and we can remove the third item in the definition of weak factorization systems. A set of morphisms in this category is nothing but a
subrelation
of
(L,\leq)
. To say that such a class contains the
isomorphisms
means that the relation is
reflexive
and to say that it is closed under
compositions
means that this is a
transitive relation
. A subrelation of an antisymmetric relation is always antisymmetric. In other words, a composition closed set of morphisms that contains the isomorphism is a
subposet
of
(L,\leq)
. The definition of
right saturated
class of morphisms becomes:

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a lattice. A
transfer system

\lhd
for
L
is a relation of partial ordering on
L
such that:

1.

i\ \lhd\ j
implies
i\leq j
.

2.

i\ \lhd\ k
and
j\leq k
implies
(i\land j)\ \lhd\ j
.

Inclusion of relations naturally induces a poset structure on the set of transfer systems on
L
and we denote this poset by
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a lattice. The map sending a transfer system
\mathcal{R}
to
(\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R})
is an isomorphism between the poset of transfer systems and the poset of weak factorization systems.

Proof.

This is
[
21
, Theorem 4.13]
.

∎

Remark 2.7
.

It is clear that we can dualize this proof to obtain an isomorphism between the poset of weak factorization systems on
L
and the poset of left saturated sets ordered by reverse inclusion.

2.3
G
-equivariant topology

The notion of
N_{\infty}
-operad has been introduced in
[
15
]
as an equivariant analogue of
E_{\infty}
-operads. As the authors are not expert in this area, we simply refer to the
[
21
, Section 3]
for more informations about this notion and for the details. There is a notion of weak equivalences of
N_{\infty}
-operads, hence one can define the homotopy category of these operads. It turns out that this category is closely related to the notion of transfer systems.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
G
be a finite group. We denote by
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
the poset of the subgroups of
G
. Then a
G
-transfer system
is a subposet
\lhd
of the inclusion on

such that:

1.

and

, then
gHg^{-1}\lhd gKg^{-1}
.

2.

If
H\lhd K
and

, then
M\cap H\lhd M
.

The poset
\big(\operatorname{Sub}(G),\leq\big)
is a lattice and by forgetting the first item in

we see that a
G
-transfer system is in particular a
transfer system
on

in the sense of

is abelian or when all the subgroups of

are normal (e.g., the quaternion group

) a

-transfer system is nothing but a transfer system.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
G
be a finite group. The homotopy category of
N_{\infty}
-operads is equivalent to the poset of
G
-transfer systems (viewed as a category).

Proof.

This theorem involves the work of various groups of mathematicians, see
[
21
, Theorem 3.6]
and the references above it.
∎

It follows that understanding the transfer systems on finite lattices is important for
two reasons
: first they are the building blocks of the weak factorization systems, which is the first step towards the model structures on the finite lattices. Secondly, they classify the
G
-equivariants analogues of the

-operads up to equivalences. For the second case, we should focus on the lattices of subgroups of given finite groups.

In this section we investigate the properties of the poset of weak factorization systems on a finite lattice

Semidistributivity and trimness

4.1
Distributivity

The notion of
semidistributive
lattice is a weakening of the one of
distributive
lattice. Hence before proving that the lattice of transfer systems is always semidistributive, it is natural to ask under what conditions the lattice of transfer systems is distributive. Unfortunately for a finite lattice this is almost never the case.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
L
be a finite lattice. Then
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
is
distributive
if and only if
|L|\leq 2
.

Proof.

If
|L|=1
, then there is only one transfer system on
L
which is
\Delta(L)
. If
|L|=2
, since
L
is a lattice it has a greatest element and a least element, so it is a total order with
2
elements. There are two transfer systems
\Delta(L)
and
L
, and the lattice of transfer systems is also a total order with
2
elements. Both of these lattices are distributive.

Conversely, if
|L|\geq 3
since
L
has a greatest element and a least element, there is a chain (a set of pairwise comparable elements) of length at least
2
in
L
starting at the least element. Without loss of generality we assume that it is labelled by
0\to 1\to 2\to\cdots
, where
0
is the least element of
L
and
(0,1),(1,2)
are cover relations in
L
. Our objective is to construct from this chain a pentagonal sublattice of
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
and conclude that
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
is not distributive.

We consider
\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)
the smallest transfer system containing
(0,2)
. By
lemma

3.3
, we have:

\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)=\{ (0\land c,c)|c\leq 2\}\cup\Delta(L)=\{(0,x)\ |\ x\leq 2\}\cup\Delta(L).

It is join-irreducible and
\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}=\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{(0,2)}=\{(0,x)\ |\ x<2\}\cup\Delta(L)
.
Similarly, we have

\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)=\{\{ (1\land c,c)|c\leq 2\}\cup\Delta(L)\}=\{(1,2)\}\cup\{(0,c)\ |\ c\leq 2,1\not\leq c\}\cup\Delta(L)

Since
(0,2)\notin\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)
we have
\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)\cap\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)=\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)\cap\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}
. Moreover
\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)\lor\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)=\big(\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)\cup\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)\big)^{tc}=\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)\cup\{(1,2)\}
. Similarly,
\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)\lor\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}=\big(\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)\cup\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}\big)^{tc}=\{(1,2)\}\cup\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}\cup\{(0,2)\}=\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)\lor\{(1,2)\}
. Here
(0,2)=(0,1)\cdot(1,2)
with
(0,1)\in\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}
and
(1,2)\in\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)
. We have obtained the following sublattice

\textstyle{\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)\lor\operatorname{Tr}(1,2)}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Tr}(0,2)_{*}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

Proof.

The first step is to see that it suffices to prove that the lattice of transfer systems on

, that

is an isomorphism from the lattice of transfer systems on

to the lattice of left saturated sets on

(ordered by reverse inclusion). Moreover, by

taking the opposite of a left saturated set induces an anti-isomorphism from the lattice of left saturated sets on

\Phi
from
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
to
\operatorname{Trs}(L^{op})
. If
\operatorname{Trs}(L^{op})
is join-semidistributive, it follows that
\operatorname{Tr}(L)
is meet-semidsitributive.

Now we prove the join-semidistributivity. Let
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R}_{3}
be three transfer systems on

such that
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\mathcal{R}_{2}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\mathcal{R}_{3}
. Note that
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor(\mathcal{R}_{2}\land\mathcal{R}_{3})\leq\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\mathcal{R}_{2}
holds in any lattice. Conversely, we consider

. We assume that
(x,y)
is a cover relation in the poset

. By

3.4
, the poset

is the transitive closure of
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
. Hence, the cover relation
(x,y)
is either in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
or in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
. Since
P=\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\mathcal{R}_{3}=(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{3})^{tc}
, the relation
(x,y)
is also in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
or in
\mathcal{R}_{3}
. It follows that
(x,y)
is either in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
or in
\mathcal{R}_{2}\cap\mathcal{R}_{3}
and we have:

\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\mathcal{R}_{2}\subseteq\big(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup(\mathcal{R}_{2}\cap\mathcal{R}_{3})\big)^{tc}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\big(\mathcal{R}_{2}\land\mathcal{R}_{3}\big).

∎

in

, we have

(y\lor x)\land z=y\lor(x\land z).

A chain in a lattice is called
maximal
, when it is maximal with respect to inclusion.

Definition 4.4
.

A lattice
(L,\land,\lor)
is

if it has a maximal chain of
n+1
left modular elements, exactly
n
join-irreducibles and exactly
n
meet-irreducibles.

Trim lattices were introduced by Hugh Thomas in
[
40
]
as ungraded analogue to distributive lattices. In particular a graded trim lattice is distributive. The typical examples of trim lattices are the
cambrian lattices
associated to Dynkin diagrams (see
[
40
,
31
]
).

Let us recall that the
length
of a lattice is the maximum of the length of its chains. A lattice of length
n
has at least
n
join-irreducibles and
n
meet-irreducibles.

Definition 4.5
.

A lattice
(L,\leq)
is
extremal
if its length is equal to the number of join-irreducible elements and to the number of meet-irreducible elements.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the lattice of transfer systems on

is

.

Proof.

By
proposition

1.3
, the lattice
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
has
|\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)|
join-irreducible elements. Since there is an anti-isomorphism between
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
and
\operatorname{Trs}(L^{op})
and

has as many relations as

, this is also the number of meet-irreducible elements. If

is a chain, each inclusion is strict so at each step there is a relation in
\mathcal{R}_{i}
which is not in
\mathcal{R}_{i-1}
. Hence the length of such a chain is bounded by the total number of non-trivial relations on

.

Conversely, we need to construct a chain of length exactly

. Recall from

that in order to construct a cover relation

in

. We consider

with

is maximal in

. That is if there is another relation

in

such that

, then

. Suppose that there is a pullback diagram as follow, where

.

\textstyle{x\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
J
\textstyle{y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{u\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{v}

As

is non-trivial, so is

. Then

and

∎

Theorem 4.7

Let

5
Consequences of semidistributivity

In this section, we investigate some of the consequences of the semidistributivity of the lattice of weak factorization systems. We start by a discussion on the canonical join representations for such lattices, then we discuss the existence of a labelling of the Hasse diagram by the join-irreducible elements. In a semidistributive lattice there is always a canonical bijection between the set of join-irreducibles and the meet-irreducibles which is called
\kappa
. We describe this map for the lattice of weak factorization systems.

5.1
Canonical join representations and join-labelling

Let
(L,\leq)
be a lattice. A subset
B\subseteq L
is called a
join representation
of
a\in L
if
a=\lor B
. We say that
B
is
irredundant
if for all proper subset
C
of
B
we have
\lor C<\lor B
. If a set
B
is an irredundant join representation, it is an
antichain
in
L
( the elements are pairwise incomparable). We say that
C
is a
refinement
of
B
, if for every
c\in C
there exists
b\in B
such that
c \leq b
. This is a preorder on the subsets of
L
which restricts to a partial order on the antichains of
L
.

Definition 5.1
.

A join representation
a=\lor B
is
canonical
if it is irredundant and
\forall C\subseteq L
such that
a=\lor C
, we have that
B
is a refinement of
C
.

In other words a canonical join representation is the
least antichain
(if it exists) with respect to the refinement order among antichains joining to
a
. The existence of such join-representation is heavily related to semidistributivity, as we have:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
L
be a finite lattice. The following are equivalent:

1.

L
is a join-semidistributive lattice.

2.

Every element of
L
has a canonical join representation.

Proof.

See for example
[
22
, Theorem 2.24]
.
∎

Of course there is the dual notion of
canonical meet representation
and it is related to
meet-semidistributivity
.

Let
L
be a join-semidistributive lattice and
x\lessdot y
be a cover relation in
L
. It is known (see for example
[
42
]
) that there is a
unique join-irreducible

j\in L
such that

x\lor j=y\hbox{ and }x\lor j_{*}=x.

The join-irreducible
j
is called the
join label
of the cover relation
x\lessdot y
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then the
join label
of a cover relation
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{R}
is
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
where
(a,b)
is the unique non trivial relation in
\mathcal{R}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{1}
.

Proof.

By
lemma

3.7
, there is a unique non-trivial relation
(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{1}
. Hence
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and we have

\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\subseteq\mathcal{R}.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is a cover relation, we have
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)=\mathcal{R}_{1}
or
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)=\mathcal{R}
. As
(a,b)\notin\mathcal{R}_{1}
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)=\mathcal{R}
. Let us recall that
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}=\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\setminus\{a,b\}
and we have similarly,

\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}\subseteq\mathcal{R}.

If
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}=\mathcal{R}
we have
(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}
. By the second item of
lemma

3.7
the relation
(a,b)
is a cover relation in
\mathcal{R}
, hence we have
(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
or
(a,b)\in\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}
. This is a contradiction in both cases, hence we have
\mathcal{R}_{1}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}
. Since
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
is join-irreducible by
proposition

1.3
, it is the join label of
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Of course, we will simply label it as
(a,b)
instead of
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
, with the cover relation
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{R}
.

By duality if
L
is a meet-semidistributive lattice and
x\lessdot y
is a cover relation in
L
, there is a
unique meet-irreducible

m\in M
such that

y\land m=x\hbox{ and }y\land m^{*}=y.

This meet-irreducible element is called the
meet label
of
x\lessdot y
.
When the lattice is semidistributive, it has the two labels. Hence we can define the
map
\kappa
from the set of join-irreducible elements to the set of meet-irreducible elements of
L
by setting
\kappa(j)
as the meet label of the cover relation
j_{*}\lessdot j
. Dually we define the
map
\kappa^{d}
by setting for a
m
meet-irreducible element
\kappa^{d}(m)
as the join label of the cover relation
m\lessdot m^{*}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
L
be a finite semidistributive lattice. Then
\kappa
and
\kappa^{d}
are two inverse bijections between the set of join-irreducible elements and the set of meet-irreducible elements of
L
.

Proof.

See for example
[
42
, Proposition 9.2]
.
∎

5.2

By

and

the

of

are of the form

.

Let

The map
\kappa
is important for us because of the following result.

Theorem 5.7

(Barnard-Hanson)
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite semidistributive lattice. There is a bijection between the elements of
L
and the subsets

Since

for two sets of join-irreducible elements, then

and the set of all the canonical join representations of the elements of

. By

of join-irreducible elements is a canonical join representation if and only if it satisfies the condition of the theorem.

.

6
Congruences

Recall that an equivalence
\equiv
on a lattice
L
is called a
congruence
if it has the property: for any elements
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1}
and
y_{2}
in
L
such that
x_{1}\equiv y_{1}
and
x_{2}\equiv y_{2}
, then also
x_{1}\wedge x_{2}\equiv y_{1}\wedge y_{2}
and
x_{1}\vee x_{2}\equiv y_{1}\vee y_{2}
. We denote by
\operatorname{Con}(L)
the set of congruences on
L
, partially ordered by the refinement (
\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}
if and only if
\forall x,y\in L
,
x\equiv_{\theta_{1}}y
implies
x\equiv_{\theta_{2}}y
). It is well known that the lattice
\operatorname{Con}(L)
is distributive
[
23
]
. It follows from the Birkhoff theorem that
\operatorname{Con}(L)=\operatorname{Ideal}(P)
where
P
is the poset of
join-irreducible congruences
.

Given a congruence
\equiv
on
\operatorname{Con}(L)
, we say that
\equiv
contracts an arrow
q:x\rightarrow y
(
x\lessdot y
) of the Hasse diagram of
L
whenever
x\equiv y
. We define
\operatorname{con}(q)
to be the smallest congruence relation contracting
q
. That is,
\operatorname{con}(q)
is the meet of all congruences contracting
q
. By
[
24
, Proposition 9.5.14]
, the map sending a join-irreducible element
j\in L
to
\operatorname{con}(j_{*}\to j)
induces a surjective map from the set of join-irreducible elements of
L
to the set of join-irreducible congruences of
L
. Similarly, the map sending a meet irreducible element
m
to
\operatorname{con}(m\to m^{*})
induces a surjective map from the set of meet-irreducible elements of
L
to the set of meet-irreducible congruences of
L
.

Definition 6.1
.

A finite lattice
(L,\leq)
is
congruence uniform
if the maps
j\mapsto\operatorname{con}(j_{*}\to j)
and
m\mapsto\operatorname{con}(m\to m^{*})
are bijective.

By
[
18
]
a congruence uniform lattice is always semidistributive, and conversely knowing that a lattice is semdisitrbutive can be used to simplify the proof of the congruence uniformity.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. If
L
is semidistributive and the map
j\mapsto\operatorname{con}(j_{*}\to j)
from the set of join-irreducible elements of
L
to the set of join-irreducible congruences is injective, then
L
is congruence uniform.

Proof.

See the paragraph 7.2 of
[
34
]
.
∎

For two arrows
q
and
q^{\prime}
in the Hasse diagram of
L
, we say that
q
and
q^{\prime}
are
forcing equivalent
if
\operatorname{con}(q)=\operatorname{con}(q^{\prime})
. Moreover, there is a preorder on the set of all arrows in the Hasse diagram, called the
forcing preorder
. It is defined by

q\rightsquigarrow q^{\prime}\leftrightarrow\operatorname{con}(q)\geq\operatorname{con}(q^{\prime})\hbox{ in the lattice}\operatorname{Con}(L).

(a,b)

\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{*}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
. Hence,
(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
. Moreover, the transfer system
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
is in the interval
[\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}]
and it satisfies
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is join-irreducible in this interval, we have
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
or
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}
. Since
(a,b)\notin\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, so
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
. By symmetry, we also obtain the other inclusion, and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
.

It remains to prove that
\Psi
is surjective. Let
(a,b)\in\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(\mathcal{R}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{1})
and
\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\vee\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)

(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, it is immediate that
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
. Let also
\mathcal{R}_{*}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime}}\wedge(a,b)^{\boxslash}
. As
(a,b)\in\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{1}
, equivalently,
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq(a,b)^{\boxslash}
. So
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{*}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
. Moreover, by construction we have
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and since
(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and
(a,b)\notin\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}
, we have

\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{*}^{{}^{\prime}}\subsetneq\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime}}.

Let
X
be a transfer system such that
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq X\subsetneq\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime}}
. We will prove that
X\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}
. Since
X\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{*}^{{}^{\prime}}
if and only if
X\subseteq(a,b)^{\boxslash}
, to prove
X\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{*}^{{}^{\prime}}
, we only need to prove
X\subseteq(a,b)^{\boxslash}
. Note that
(a,b)\notin X
, otherwise we get a contradiction that
\mathcal{R}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\vee\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{1}\vee X=X
.

Let
(x,y)\in X
. In particular
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
, so the relation
(x,y)
is obtained by transitivity from relations in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
. Note that
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subseteq(a,b)^{\boxslash}
and
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}\subseteq(a,b)^{\boxslash}
. Hence,
\mathcal{R}_{1}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}\subseteq(a,b)^{\boxslash}
. So if all the relations appearing in the decomposition of

are in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
or in
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}
, we obtain that
(x,y)\in(a,b)^{\boxslash}
. The only relation of
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
which is not in
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}
is
(a,b)
, so let us assume that it appears in
(x,y)
, that is we have
(x,y)=x\to a\to b\to y
. If
x=a
, then by the pullback property of

, we have
(a,b)\in X
and this is a contradiction. So we have
x<a
, and there is no commutative diagram

\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{x\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{y.}

In particular we have
(x,y)\in(a,b)^{\boxslash}
and we have
X\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}
. This proves that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is join-irreducible in the interval
[\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}]
and that we have a cover relation
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}\lessdot\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. It remains to compute its join label. To do so, we recall that
X\mapsto\,^{\boxslash}X
is an isomorphism between the lattice of transfer systems and the lattice of left saturated sets (ordered by reverse inclusion). The inverse isomorphism is
Y\mapsto Y^{\boxslash}
. So, we have

\,{}^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}=\,^{\boxslash}\big(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap(a,b)^{\boxslash}\big)=\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\land\operatorname{Ls}(a,b).

Since the lattice of left saturated set is ordered by reverse inclusion, we have

(a,b)\in\operatorname{Ls}(a,b)\subseteq\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\land\operatorname{Ls}(a,b)=\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}.

Hence, we have

(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}.

So, the relation

is the unique non-trivial relation in this intersection, and it is the join-labelling of the cover relation
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{*}\lessdot\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. This concludes the proof of the surjectivity of the map
\Psi

∎

Every non-trivial relations

The relation

Then we have the following:

Proposition 6.5
.

The arrows
q
and

are forcing equivalent if and only they have the same join label.

Proof.

First, we prove that if two arrows have the same label, they are forcing equivalent. This is not particular to our situation, as it holds for any semidistributive lattice. If
j
is the join label of
q:x\to y
and
q^{\prime}:x^{\prime}\to y^{\prime}
. Then by
[

, Proposition 2.13]
, we have
x\land j=j_{*}
and since
j\leq y
, we have
y\land j=j
.

let
\equiv
be a congruence relation contracting
q
. Then we have

j=y\land j\equiv x\land j=j_{*}.

And

y^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\lor j\equiv x^{\prime}\lor j_{*}=x^{\prime}.

So
\equiv
also contracts the arrow
q^{\prime}
and by symmetry we see that
q
and
q^{\prime}
are forcing equivalent.

On the other hand, assume that the arrows
q:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{R}
and
q^{\prime}:\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
are forcing equivalent. We denote by
(a,b)
and
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
their join labels, respectively. The congruence
\equiv_{(a,b)}
defined above contracts

, so it contracts

. Then we have
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\equiv_{(a,b)}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. Hence it follows
(a,b)\subseteq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Conversely, we also have
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\subseteq(a,b)
, so we have
(a,b)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
.

6.2
Lattice of congruences

Let
(a,b)
be a non-trivial relation in
\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
. We denote by
\operatorname{Con}\big((a,b)\big)
the smallest congruence contracting
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
and
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)_{*}
. We have the following:

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
a<b<c
be elements in
L
. We denote by
q=(a,c)
and
q^{\prime}=(b,c)
the non-trivial relations of
\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
. Then
\operatorname{Con}(q)\subseteq\operatorname{Con}(q^{\prime})
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)\leq\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\vee\operatorname{Tr}(b,c)
since
(a,c)
is the composition of
(a,b)
and
(b,c)
. From
\operatorname{Tr}(b,c)\equiv_{\operatorname{Con}(q^{\prime})}\operatorname{Tr}(b,c)_{*}
, then it follows that

\mathcal{R}:=(\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\vee\operatorname{Tr}(b,c)_{*})\wedge\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)\equiv_{\operatorname{Con}(q^{\prime})}(\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\vee\operatorname{Tr}(b,c))\wedge\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)=\operatorname{Tr}(a,c).

Since
(a,c)\notin\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\vee\operatorname{Tr}(b,c)_{*}
, we have
\mathcal{R}\lneq\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)
, that is,
\mathcal{R}\leq\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)_{*}
. Then we have
\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)=\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)_{*}\equiv_{\operatorname{Con}(q^{\prime})}\mathcal{R}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)_{*}=\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)_{*}
. Note that
\operatorname{Con}(q)
is the smallest congruence contracting
\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)
and
\operatorname{Tr}(a,c)_{*}
. We have
\operatorname{Con}(q)\subseteq\operatorname{Con}(q^{\prime})
.

∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
a<b<c
be elements in

. We denote by

and

the non-trivial relations of

\operatorname{Con}(q)\subseteq\operatorname{Con}(q^{\prime})

. It also follows

.

.

7
The spine of the lattice of weak factorization systems

A trim lattice has an important distributive sublattice called the
spine
. It consists of the union of all the chains of maximal length. By
[
43
, Theorem 3.7]
, the spine is also the set of the
left-modular elements
of the lattice and by
[
40
, Lemma 6]
it is a
distributive sublattice
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice and
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
be a weak factorization system on
L
. Let
(a,b)\in\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
. Then
(a,b)\in\mathcal{L}
if and only if there is a chain

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{0}\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{1}\cdots\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{n}=L

such that
(a,b)
is the join label of
\mathcal{R}_{j}\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{j+1}
for some
0\leq j\leq n-1
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
be a weak factorization system and
(a,b)
a non-trivial relation in
\mathcal{L}=\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}
. Then it follows from
lemma

6.3
that there is a cover relation
\mathcal{R}_{j}\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{j+1}
in the inverval
[\mathcal{R},L]
whose join-label is
(a,b)
. Since
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
is finite and
\mathcal{R}_{j}\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{j+1}
is in the interval of
[\mathcal{R},L]
. Then the chain above exists.

On the other hand, suppose such a chain exists. Since
(a,b)
is the join label of
\mathcal{R}_{j}\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{j+1}
for some
j
, then it is clear that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{R}_{j+1}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{j}
. Certainly we have

(a,b)\in\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}_{j}\subseteq\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{L}.

∎

Lemma 7.2
.

(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice and
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
be a weak factorization system on
L
. Then

1.

The non trivial relations of
\mathcal{L}
are the join labels of the cover relations in the interval
[\mathcal{R},L]
.

2.

The non trivial relations of
\mathcal{R}
are the join labels of the cover relations in the interval
[\Delta(L),\mathcal{R}]
.

Proof.

Since
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is a weak factorization system, we have
\mathcal{L}=\,^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R}
. Hence
\mathcal{L}=\,^{\boxslash}R\cap L
. Similarly, we have
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}\cap\,^{\boxslash}\Delta(L)
. The result follows from
lemma

6.3
.
∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. Then a weak factorization system
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is on the spine of the lattice
\operatorname{Wfs}(L)
if and only if
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{R}=\operatorname{Rel}(L)
.

Proof.

We recall that the join label of two distinct cover relations in the same chain are distinct. Indeed if we have
x\lessdot y\leq z\lessdot t
and
j
denotes the join label of
x\lessdot y
, then
j\leq y
so
z\lor j=z
and
j
is not the join label of the cover relation
z\lessdot t
.

If

\mathcal{R}_{0}=\Delta(L)\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{2}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot\mathcal{R}_{n-1}\lessdot L=\mathcal{R}_{n}

is a chain of maximal length, then all the non-trivial relations of
L
appear as join label of the cover relations of this chain. So if
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is a weak factorization system on the spine, then by
lemma

7.2
,
\mathcal{R}
is one of the
\mathcal{R}_{i}
and the join labels of
[\Delta(L),\mathcal{R}_{i}]
are the non-trivial relations of
\mathcal{R}
and the join-labels of
[\mathcal{R}_{i},L]
are the non-trivial relations of
\mathcal{L}
. So, all the non-trivial relations of
L
are either in
\mathcal{L}
or in
\mathcal{R}
and we have
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{L}=\operatorname{Rel}(L)
.

On the other hand let
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
be a weak factorization system such that
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{R}=\operatorname{Rel}(L)
. Since a non-trivial relation cannot be in
\mathcal{L}
and in
\mathcal{R}
at the same time, this condition implies that
\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)=\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(\mathcal{L})\sqcup\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(\mathcal{R})
. By the proof of
proposition

4.6
, there is a chain of length
|\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(\mathcal{R})|
from
\Delta(L)
to
\mathcal{R}
. Dually in the poset of left saturated set, there is a chain of length
|\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(\mathcal{L})|
from
\mathcal{L}
to
\Delta(L)
. Applying the isomorphism, between left saturated sets and transfer systems, we obtain a chain of length
|\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(\mathcal{L})|
from
\mathcal{R}
to
L
. Hence, a chain of length
|\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)|
from
\Delta(L)
to
L
containing
\mathcal{R}
. So
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is on the spine.

∎

8
Galois graph of the lattice of weak factorization systems

For
distributive lattices
, we have the Birkhoff representation theorem which says that any finite distributive lattice can be represented as subsets of its set of join-irreducible elements. There is a similar, rather technical, result for finite semidistributive lattices
[
34
]
, involving
pairs of subsets
of the set of join-irreducible elements with a maximality property. For finite
extremal lattices
, we have the
Markowsky representation theorem

[
28
]
, which also represents the lattice as pairs of vertices of a
graph
on the set of join-irreducible elements.

Let
G
be a simple directed graph on
[n]=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
such that
i\to k
implies
k<i
. For
X\subseteq[n]
and
Y\subseteq[n]
such that
X\cap Y=\emptyset
, we say that
(X,Y)
is an
orthogonal pair
if there are no arrows from
x\in X
to
y\in Y
. We say that
(X,Y)
is a
maximal orthogonal pair
if
X
and
Y
are maximal for that property. We denote by
\operatorname{Pairs}(G)
the set of all maximal orthogonal pairs of
G
. This set can be viewed as a poset for the ordering

(X,Y)\preceq(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\hbox{ if }X\subseteq X^{\prime}\hbox{ and }Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y.

The poset
(\operatorname{Pairs}(G),\preceq)
is a lattice where the join is computed by taking the intersection of the second component, and the meet is given by taking the intersection of the first component. By Markowsky’s representation theorem
[
28
]
, this lattice is
extremal
and conversely every extremal lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of maximal orthogonal pairs of a direct graph associated to
L
called the
Galois graph
of
L
. See also
[
43
, Theorem 2.4]
. In our context, this result boils down to the following.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. The lattice of weak factorization systems on
L
is isomorphic to the lattice of maximal orthogonal pairs of the graph
G_{L}
whose vertices are the non-trivial relations of
L
and there is an arrow
(a,b)\to(c,d)
if and only
c\leq a
,
d\leq b
and
d\not\leq a
i.e., the morphism
c\to d
does not lift on the left the morphism
a\to b
.

Proof.

By Markowsky’s representation theorem, it is enough to show that the Galois graph of the lattice of transfer systems on
L
is isomorphic to the graph
G_{L}
. Since the lattice of transfer systems is both extremal and congruence uniform, we can use the description of the Galois graph given in
[
32
, Corollary A.1]
. The Galois graph of
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
is isomorphic to the graph whose vertices are the join-irreducible elements of the lattice
\operatorname{Trs}(L)
. Moreover, there is an arrow
j_{s}\to j_{t}
if and only if
j_{t}\leq(j_{t})_{*}\lor j_{s}
.

By
proposition

1.3
, the join-irreducible transfer systems are the
\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
for
(a,b)\in\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L)
. Since
\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
is the smallest transfer system containing
(c,d)
, when
R
is a transfer system we have
\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)\subseteq R
if and only if
(c,d)\in R
. So
\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)\subseteq\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
if and only if
(c,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
. By
lemma

3.3
, we have
\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)=\big(\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}\cup\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\big)^{tc}
. Moreover
\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}=\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)\setminus\{(c,d)\}
.

\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
is a transfer system and
(c,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
, so for every
x\in L
such that
c\leq x\leq d
, we have
(c,x)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
. Moreover if
x<d
we have
(c,x)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}
. So if
(c,d)\in\big(\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}\cup\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)\big)^{tc}
, there are
c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n-1}
such that
c=c_{0}<c_{1}<c_{2}<\cdots<c_{n-1}<c_{n}=d
such that
(c_{i},c_{i+1})\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}
or
(c_{i},c_{i+1})\in\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
. By the remark above
(c,c_{n-1})\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}
, and
(c_{n-1},d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
, otherwise we would have
(c,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}
. Hence we obtain a factorization
c\to d=c\to x\to d
where
c\leq x
,
(c,x) \in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}
,
(x,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
and
x\neq d
. In particular, we have a pullback diagram:

\textstyle{x\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
J
\textstyle{d\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b.}

So we have
c\leq x\leq a
,
d\leq b
and
x=a\land d
. Since
x\neq d
the last condition implies that
d\not\leq a
. So the morphism
c\to d
does not lift on the left
a\to b
.

Conversely, if
c\to d
does not lift on the left
a\to b
, we have
c\leq a
,
d\leq b
and
d\not\leq a
. We deduce the diagram

\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a\land d\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
J
\textstyle{d\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b.}

Since
d\not\leq a
, we have
a\land d<d
. As above,
(c,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
and
\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
is a transfer system so
(c,a\land d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)
. And since
a\land d\neq d
we have
(c,a\land d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}
. Moreover, by construction
(a\land d,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
hence
(c,d)\in\operatorname{Tr}(c,d)_{*}\lor\operatorname{Tr}(a,b)
.
∎

We deduce another characterization of the
spine
of the lattice of transfer systems. By
[
40
]
, the spine is a distributive lattice so it is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Ideal}(P)
where
P
is its poset of join-irreducible elements. By
[
43
, Proposition 2.6]
, the poset
P
is obtained by taking the transitive closure of Galois graph of the trim lattice. In our setting we recover the second natural way of ordering the intervals of a poset. For two relations, we set
(a,b)\preceq(c,d)
if there is a commutative diagram

\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{d\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b.}

Clearly, this is equivalent to
c\leq a
and
d\leq b
. Hence, we recover the product order on the intervals (of size at least two) of
L
.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. The
spine
of the lattice of transfer systems on
L
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Ideal}(\operatorname{Rel}^{*}(L),\preceq)
.

Proof.

If there is a path from
(a,b)
to
(c,d)
in the Galois graph of the lattice of transfer systems, then
c\leq a
and
d\leq b
.

Conversely, if
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
are two non-trivial relations such that
c\leq a
and
d\leq b
, then we have
c<d\leq b
, so
(c,b)
is a non-trivial relation in
L
. Moreover we have a commutative diagram:

\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{d\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b.}

Since
(c,d)
is a non-trivial relation, we have
d\not\leq c
and similarly
b\not\leq a
. If
b\neq d
and
c\neq a
, we have a path
(a,b)\to(c,b)\to(c,d)
in the Galois graph of
L
. If
b=d
, then the situation is even simpler: we have a commutative diagram

\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{b,}

and

since

is a non-trivial relation, so there is an arrow
(a,b)\to(c,b)
in the Galois graph of

. Similarly if

, there is an arrow
(a,b)\to(a,d)
in the Galois graph of
L
.
∎

9
Lattice of
G
-transfer systems

Let
G
be a finite group. Recall that a
G
-transfer system
is a transfer system on the lattice
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
of the subgroups of
G
which is
stable by conjugation
. We denote by
\operatorname{Tr}(G)
the poset of
G
-transfer systems ordered by inclusion of their relations. By
[
21
, Proposition 3.7]
, this is a lattice where the meet is given by the intersection. The join of two
G
-transfer systems is the smallest
G
-transfer system containing the union. The objective of this short section is to explain how the general properties of the lattice of weak factorization systems also apply to the lattice of the
G
-transfer systems.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then the lattice of
G
-transfer systems is a sublattice of the lattice of transfer systems on
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
.

Proof.

Since the meets in both lattices are the intersection, we only have to check that if
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
are two
G
-transfer systems, the joins of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
in the two lattices are equal.

By
[
38
, Theorem A.2]
, the smallest
G
-transfer system containing a set of relations
S
is obtained by closing
S
under conjugation, then pullback and then by transitivity. As in the proof of
lemma

3.3
, we see that the join of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
in
\operatorname{Tr}(G)
is given by
(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime})^{tc}
. This is also the join of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
in the lattice of transfer systems on
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
.
∎

Note that a
sublattice
of a trim lattice is
not a trim lattice
in general, hence for trimness we need another argument.

Let us recall that a poset
(X,\leq)
is called a
G
-poset
if there is a monotone
G
-action on it. That is
X
is a
G
-set and for every
x\leq y\in X
, and every
g\in G
we have
g\cdot x\leq g\cdot y
.

A lattice
(L,\leq)
is a
G
-lattice
if it is a
G
-poset and the action is compatible with the meets and the joins of the lattice. That is
g\cdot(x\land y)=g\cdot x\land g\cdot y
and
g\cdot(x\lor y)=g\cdot x\lor g\cdot y
, for every
x,y\in L
and
g\in G
.

We start by an easy lemma.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a lattice which is also a
G
-poset. Then
L
is a
G
-lattice.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in L
. Then
x\leq x\lor y
so
g\cdot x\leq g\cdot(x\lor y)
. Similarly
g\cdot y\leq g\cdot(x\lor y)
, hence
g\cdot x\lor g\cdot y\leq g\cdot(x\lor y)
. Moreover we have
g\cdot x\leq g\cdot x\lor g\cdot y
, hence
x\leq g^{-1}\cdot\big(g\cdot x\lor g\cdot y\big)
, and similarly for
y
. So
x\lor y\leq g^{-1}\cdot\big(g\cdot x\lor g\cdot y\big)
, and we get
g\cdot(x\lor y)\leq g\cdot x\lor g\cdot y
. The proof for the meet is dual.
∎

If
(L,\leq)
is a
G
-lattice and
\mathcal{R}
is a transfer system on
L
, we denote by
g\cdot\mathcal{R}
the relation on
L
defined by

x(g\cdot\mathcal{R})y\Longleftrightarrow(g^{-1}\cdot x)\mathcal{R}(g^{-1}\cdot y).

We use
g^{-1}
instead of
g
in order to have an action of
G
on the left.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a
G
-lattice, then the lattice of transfer systems on
L
is a
G
-lattice for the action defined above.

Proof.

Let
R
be a transfer system on
L
. It is clear that
g\cdot R
is reflexive and transitive. Moreover if
x(g\cdot R)y
, we have
(g^{-1}\cdot x)R(g^{-1}\cdot y)
, so
g^{-1}\cdot x\leq g^{-1}\cdot y
. Multiplying by
g
, we get
x\leq y
. Hence
g\cdot R
is a subposet of
(L,\leq)
.

Now we consider a pullback diagram in
(L\leq)

\textstyle{x\land z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
J
\textstyle{z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{x\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\in g\cdot\mathcal{R}}

such that
x(g\cdot\mathcal{R})y
. We have
g^{-1}\cdot z\leq g^{-1}\cdot y
, and a pullback diagram

\textstyle{(g^{-1}\cdot x)\land(g^{-1}\cdot z)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

, we get
g^{-1}\cdot(x\land z)\mathcal{R}g^{-1}\cdot z
, so

.

It remains to see that the action is an action of
G
-poset, since this is straightforward we leave the details to the reader.
∎

When
(L,\leq)
is a
G

the sublattice of
L
consisting of the

.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group and

is a

It is clear that the lattice of subgroups of

10.1
Case of a total order

There are no new results in this section, however we would like to point out that the notion of weak factorization systems on a total order can be easily related to the
classical poset point of view on the binary trees
. This gives an alternative proof of
[
37
, Theorem 25]
.

Binary search trees
are binary trees with a particular labelling of their vertices. They were introduced in computer science in the sixties and it is known since
[
14
,
25
]
that they are central objects for the combinatorics of the Tamari lattice viewed as a quotient of the lattice of weak ordering of the permutations.

Let
T
be a
binary tree
with
n
inner vertices, we label its vertices by the integers
1,2,\cdots,n
in such a way that if
x
is a vertex in the left (right) subtree of
y
then the label if
x
is strictly smaller (larger) than the label of
y
. There is a unique way of viewing a binary tree as binary search tree which can be obtained by an in-order traversal of the tree (recursively visit left subtree, root and right subtree). We refer to
figure

3
for an illustration.

4
2
1
3
5

Figure 3:
Binary search tree of size 5 labeled by the in-order algorithm.

If
T
is such a binary search tree, it induces a partial order
\lhd_{T}
on
[n]
by setting
i\lhd_{T}j
if the vertex labelled by
i
is in the subtree with root the vertex labelled by
j
. Conversely, it is not difficult to classify the posets coming from such a binary search tree.

Proposition 10.1
.

A poset
\lhd
on
[n]
is of the form
\lhd_{T}
for a binary tree
T
if and only if for every
x,y\in[n]
such that
x<y
we have:

1.

If
x\lhd y
and
x\leq z\leq y
, then
z\lhd y
.

2.

If
y\lhd x
and
x\leq z\leq y
, then
z\lhd x
.

3.

For every
x\leq y\in[n]
,there exists
z\in[n]
such that
x\leq z\leq y
and
x\lhd z
and
y\lhd z
.

Proof.

This is
[
17
, Proposition 2.21]
.
∎

Since
[n]
is a total ordering for the usual ordering, we can split the relations of
\lhd_{T}
into two sets
\operatorname{Inc}(T)
and
\operatorname{Dec}(T)
. The first set is the set of
increasing relations
of
\lhd_{T}
: the relations
x\lhd_{T}y
for which
x\leq y
. The second set is the set of
decreasing relations
of
\lhd_{T}
: the relations
y\lhd_{T}x
for which
x\leq y
.

The first item of
proposition

10.1
tells us that
\operatorname{Inc}(T)
is a
left-saturated set
for the total ordering
[n]
. Similarly, the second item tells us that
\operatorname{Dec}(T)
is a
left-saturated set
for the
opposite total ordering
on
[n]
, so
\operatorname{Dec}(T)^{op}
is a
transfer system
on
[n]
. The third item is better rephrased using a diagram:

\textstyle{x\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{Inc}(T)\ni}
\textstyle{y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\in\operatorname{Dec}(T)}
\textstyle{\exists z}

and we see that
(\operatorname{Inc}(T),\operatorname{Dec}(T)^{op})
is a weak factorization system on
[n]
. Conversely, if
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is a weak factorization system on
[n]
, then
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{R}^{op}
is a poset satisfying the three conditions of
proposition

10.1
, hence it comes from a binary tree.

Theorem 10.2
.

Let
L
be the usual total ordering on
[n]
. The poset of weak factorization systems of
L
is isomorphic to the Tamari lattice.

Proof.

The map sending a binary tree
T
to
(\operatorname{Inc}(T),\operatorname{Dec}(T)^{op})
is a bijection between the set of binary trees with
n
vertices and the weak factorization systems on
[n]
. It is well-known that for two binary trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
we have
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
in the Tamari lattice if and only if
\operatorname{Dec}(T_{1})\subseteq\operatorname{Dec}(T_{2})
and
\operatorname{Inc}(T_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Inc}(T_{1})
(see e.g. By
[
17
, Proposition 2.22]
). Hence this bijection is an isomorphism of posets.
∎
