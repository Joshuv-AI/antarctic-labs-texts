Association between lipid accumulation product index (LAP index) and coronary artery disease risk
Authors: Kaiyou Liu, Yucheng Zhong, Hongwei Zhang, Shaoming Qin, Ling Liu, Qingkuan Li, Guihua Li, Junjun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a highly prevalent condition that predominantly affects obese individuals and those with metabolic abnormalities. The lipid accumulation product (LAP), combining waist circumference (WC) and triglyceride (TG) measurements, provides a clinically relevant metric for evaluating adipose tissue deposition. However, existing research findings regarding its association with CAD remain inconsistent. Therefore, this study further investigates the relationship between LAP and CAD to elucidate whether the LAP index can serve as a reliable screening tool for identifying individuals at high risk for CAD. Utilizing the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2017–2020 database, we performed logistic regression analysis to investigate the association between LAP and CAD. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied to assess the predictive performance of LAP and other metabolic parameters in relation to CAD. Additionally, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to detect possible effect modifiers and verify the robustness of findings across different population strata. Our study recruited 7860 participants, among whom 499 individuals were diagnosed with CAD. A statistically significant association between LAP and CAD was established through regression analysis (OR: 4.12, 95% CI: 2.06–6.23, P < .001). ROC analysis found that LAP demonstrates moderate-to-good discriminatory power for CAD (AUC: 0.789, 95% CI: 0.773–0.805, P < .001) when compared to other metabolic markers. Notably, a statistically significant elevation in LAP levels is observed among CAD patients when compared to their counterparts without CAD. These results support its integration into clinical protocols as a gatekeeper screening mechanism for primary CAD prevention.

1. Introduction

As the global prevalence of cardiovascular diseases continues to surge, patient numbers have escalated from 270 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019. Projections indicate a 90.0% rise in crude prevalence and a 73.4% increase in crude mortality rates by 2050, with particularly pronounced increases observed in Asia, notably China.[1] Among such populations, individuals with CAD constitute a significant proportion. This global surge in CAD prevalence is closely associated with population aging, lifestyle transformations, and environmental factors.

Significantly, both obesity and excess weight emerge as critical determinants in the development and exacerbation of CAD. Anthropometric indices commonly employed in obesity assessment include body mass index (BMI), WC, and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), all of which maintain widespread clinical utility. However, these indicators demonstrate limited practical utility due to their inefficacy in predicting disease progression.[2,3] In recent years, ectopic fat accumulation and metabolic dysregulation have become frontier areas in CAD research, coinciding with the obesity and metabolic syndrome epidemics. Against this backdrop, the LAP, a novel metabolic risk indicator, has gained increasing attention for its high sensitivity to visceral adiposity and lipid metabolic disorders.[4] Studies suggest that compared to BMI, LAP more accurately identifies individuals at high risk of hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), and metabolic abnormalities,[5-7] especially those with visceral fat accumulation and lipotoxicity.

However, current studies have yielded inconsistent conclusions regarding the predictive capability of LAP versus traditional indicators for CAD. Meanwhile, these studies are mostly confined to a single ethnic group or region.[8-11] So this study aims to utilize a large-scale database that is more representative, encompassing multiple ethnic groups, to evaluate the independent association between LAP and the risk of CAD, and to explore its potential utility as a simple and effective population-level screening tool. In our study, we also compared the predictive performance of LAP with other commonly used obesity-related metabolic indicators, providing more direct evidence for selecting the optimal indicator in clinical practice. Furthermore, this study aims to identify key risk factors for CAD through logistic regression analysis, thereby facilitating the development of targeted prevention and early intervention strategies in clinical practice.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

Our analysis utilized cross-sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017–2020 waves. NHANES employs a multimodal assessment protocol including resident interviews, comprehensive physical examinations, and biomarker analysis to establish national health benchmarks. Detailed technical specifications of the NHANES research design, including sampling protocols and data collection methodologies, are publicly available at http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm. Initially, 9445 participants were recruited. Participants were excluded according to predefined eligibility criteria, including: individuals aged < 18 years; cases with incomplete serum TG or WC; subjects with preexisting malignancies. Additionally, participants with pregnancy status, missing other relevant biochemical parameters, or lacking cardiovascular disease history information were excluded. The final analytical cohort comprised 7860 eligible individuals (Fig. 1). Each NHANES research protocol obtained the endorsement of the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and each participant provided a signed informed consent document. This study, based on retrospective analysis and lacking personal identification information, did not require approval from an ethics committee or informed consent.

Flowchart of the study. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, TG = triglycerides, WC = waist circumference.

2.2. Anthropometric and general data collection

Baseline demographic data were systematically gathered through structured household interviews. The diagnosis of hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease was determined by self-reported “Yes” responses to the multiple-choice questionnaire (MCQ) asking: “Has a doctor or other healthcare provider ever informed you that you have hypertension, diabetes, or coronary heart disease?”

2.3. Definition of LAP

The LAP calculation employed sex-specific formulations as defined by Kahn et al[4]:

Males: LAP = [[WC (cm)] × [TG (mmol/L)]

Females: LAP = [WC (cm) − 65] × [TG (mmol/L)].

Based on the median of the LAP index, participants were classified into 2 groups: Group L1 (LAP < 88.73) and Group L2 (LAP ≥ 88.73). The median value was chosen as the cutoff to divide the study population into 2 equal groups, ensuring balanced statistical power for inter-group comparisons.

2.4. Covariates

We employed multivariate adjustment models to assess potential variables that might introduce confounding effects on the association between the LAP and the onset of CAD. Our analysis took into account various changes in covariates, including gender, age, blood pressure, BMI, HTN, DM, heart failure (HF), total cholesterol (TC), WC, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), TG, serum creatinine (Scr), uric acid (UA), fasting blood glucose (FBG), WHR, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR).

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical computations were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.1) and IBM SPSS (version 22.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons between 2 independent groups were performed using Student t test. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were shown as proportions, and inferential analyses were conducted using the Pearson chi-square test of association. To evaluate the relationship between LAP and CAD, logistic regression modeling was implemented, with subsequent calculation of crude odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals. The dependent variable was CAD (yes/no). The independent variables were selected based on clinical discretion. LAP was treated as a continuous variable. The adjusted ORs were generated using logistic regression after adjusting for gender, age, DM, HF, and HTN status. The diagnostic performance of LAP in predicting CAD was evaluated using the area under the ROC curve using LAP, WHR, WHtR, WC, and BMI. The ROCs were compared using DeLong’s test. Sensitivity subgroup analyses were conducted to stratify patients by gender, HTN status, DM status, and HF status. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic and clinical profile of study participants

We analyzed data from 7860 participants (Table 1), with males comprising 48.33% of the cohort. Among these, 499 participants had CAD, with a mean age of (67.78 ± 10.93) years, while 7361 were non-CAD participants, with a mean age of (49.84 ± 17.23) years. Patients with CAD were older and exhibited significantly higher levels of SBP, FBG, WC, LDL-C, TG, TC, Scr, and UA. Additionally, among individuals with CAD, males represented a significantly larger cohort, while comorbidities including HTN, HF, and DM were more commonly observed (P<.001). Compared to non-CAD patients, CAD patients demonstrated significantly elevated LAP, WHR, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) values (P < .001).

3.2. Investigating the association between LAP and CAD risk via logistic regression

The regression analysis identified a panel of modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors significantly associated with CAD, including demographic parameters (gender and age), hemodynamic measures (SBP), metabolic panel components (TC, TG, LDL-C, FBG, UA, Scr), and advanced anthropometric indices (WHR, WHtR, LAP). In model 1, adjusting for gender and age, and in model 2, adjusting for gender, age, DM, HF, and HTN, the LAP index remained significantly associated with CAD (OR: 4.12, 95% CI: 2.06–6.23, P < .001 in model 2; Table 2).

3.3. ROC analyses of LAP and CAD risk

As shown in Figure 2, in the ROC analysis, LAP exhibited statistically significant predictive capability for CAD, yielding an AUC of 0.789 (95% CI: 0.773–0.805, P < .001) alongside 60.1% specificity and 86.0% sensitivity. Compared to the AUC of BMI 0.540 (95% CI: 0.516–0.564), WC 0.621 (95% CI: 0.599–0.643), WHR 0.698 (95% CI: 0.675–0.720), and WHtR 0.606 (95% CI: 0.583–0.629), LAP exhibited superior predictive value, with Delong’s test showing the differences were statistically significant (P < .001).

3.4. Subgroup analysis

We performed stratified subgroup analyses and interaction assessments in CAD populations grouped by demographic/clinical factors (gender, age, HTN, DM, HF). Based on the median value of LAP, the cohort was divided into 2 groups (group 1: LAP < 88.73, group 2: LAP ≥ 88.73). We demonstrated that the association between higher LAP levels and increased CAD risk remained consistent across all subgroups, with no statistically significant interaction effects detected (all P for interaction > .05), indicating the robustness of LAP as a predictive biomarker for CAD across diverse clinical populations (Table 3).

4. Discussion

This study represents the first investigation utilizing the NHANES database to explore the predictive value of LAP for CAD. We observed a gradual increase in CAD risk with elevated LAP levels, and compared to BMI, WHR, and WHtR, LAP demonstrated superior predictive value for CAD. Specifically, the AUC for LAP in predicting CAD was 0.789 (95% CI: 0.773–0.805, P < .001), with corresponding sensitivity and specificity of 86.0% and 60.1%, respectively.

Previous studies have indicated that CAD risk rises with increasing LAP levels,[12-16] which aligns with our research finding. Tan[12] demonstrated that compared to the first quartile (Q1) of LAP, the second, third, and fourth quartiles (Q2–Q4) were associated with 1.15-, 1.29-, and 1.39-fold increases in CAD incidence, respectively, and further highlighted LAP’s superior predictive capacity for CAD over BMI. Hosseinpanah et al[13] identified LAP as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events even among individuals with normal BMI, though it did not outperform other anthropometric measures. In a 5- and 10-year follow-up of healthy adults from the KERCADR cohort, Jafari et al[14] reported that LAP, TyG, and visceral adiposity index (VAI) were stronger CAD risk predictors, with LAP showing greater predictive power than the other 2 indices. Based on the ATTICA cohort, Kyrou et al[15] investigated the association between LAP and 10-year CAD incidence in Caucasian adults, observing a direct correlation between higher LAP values and elevated CAD risk, with LAP outperforming traditional anthropometric indices (BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR) in predictive efficacy.

LAP integrates WC, which reflects visceral fat accumulation, and TG, which indicate blood lipid levels, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of central obesity. While BMI was among the earliest and most widely adopted anthropometric measures, it lacks specificity in differentiating adipose tissue from lean muscle mass and fails to provide precise insights into regional fat deposition patterns. Similarly, WC lacks precision in assessing visceral fat, which is closely linked to metabolic dysregulation.

Compared to WC and BMI, LAP exhibits superior efficacy in identifying CAD risks. Across relevant studies, LAP has been validated as an accurate and straightforward predictor of insulin resistance (IR), metabolic syndrome (MetS), and CAD incidence, surpassing traditional BMI.[17-19] Individuals presenting with elevated LAP scores frequently exhibit visceral adiposity, a clinically significant phenotype demonstrating robust correlations with metabolic dysfunction, including greater vulnerability to abnormal lipid profiles, blood glucose dysregulation, heart-related complications, and MetS.[20,21] Evidence suggests that obesity and MetS promote atherosclerosis, ultimately resulting in significantly increased cardiovascular mortality.[22-24] Additionally, preadipocytes and macrophages in adipose tissue of obese individuals secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, which induce endothelial dysfunction via inflammatory mediators, driving CAD progression.[25-28] Furthermore, visceral adipose tissue accumulation enhances the production of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL),[29] promoting monocyte chemotaxis, inflammation, endothelial damage, vascular remodeling – key processes accelerating atherosclerosis.[30]

This study utilized the NHANES database to investigate the predictive value of LAP for CAD, further confirming its predictive efficacy. The strengths of this study are that we utilized the nationally representative, multi-ethnic NHANES database, which provides evidence on the association between LAP and CAD from a broader population base, differing from previous cohorts limited to single ethnic groups or regions. Meanwhile, we systematically and directly compared the predictive performance of LAP with traditional obesity indicators such as BMI, WHR, and WHtR. This offers more direct evidence for selecting the optimal indicator in clinical practice. However, our study also has some limitations: Firstly, as a cross-sectional study, it can only elucidate associations between biomarkers and CAD, without establishing causality. Secondly, baseline clinical data were collected, but fluctuations in LAP over time and their correlation with CAD incidence were not assessed. Thirdly, reliance on participant self-reports to identify conditions such as HTN, CAD, and HF may potentially lead to missed or incorrect diagnoses. Additionally, there was a lack of imaging (e.g., CT, MRI) or experimental validation (e.g., hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique) to confirm the concordance between LAP and insulin resistance, visceral adipose tissue content, and other metabolic parameters.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study leveraged the NHANES database to demonstrate that individuals with higher LAP indices exhibit elevated risks of CAD. Therefore, LAP can serve as a valuable tool for early screening of high-risk populations, enabling enhanced early prevention strategies and targeted interventions.
