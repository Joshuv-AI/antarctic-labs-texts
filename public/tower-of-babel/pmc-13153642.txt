Risk stratification for malignant upgrade in breast atypical hyperplasia: a Korean multi-institutional analysis from academic hospitals
Authors: Hyobin Kim, Jung Ho Park, Min Kyoon Kim, Chihwan Cha, Hocheol Lee, Se Jeong Oh, Hoon Choi, Jae Pak Yi, Su Hyun Lim, Eun Young Kim, Young-Joon Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAtypical hyperplasia (AH) management remains controversial due to variable malignant disease progression rates. While Western studies report 10% to 25% upgrade rates, data from Asian populations, particularly from referral academic centers, are limited. We aimed to identify predictive factors for malignant upgrade in Korean women with AH at academic hospitals.MethodsThis retrospective multi-institutional study analyzed 340 patients diagnosed with AH on initial biopsy who underwent subsequent excision at five Korean academic hospitals from 2000 to 2022. Malignant upgrade was defined as ductal carcinoma in situ or invasive cancer on the final pathology. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of upgrades.ResultsAmong 340 patients (319 atypical ductal hyperplasia, 20 atypical lobular hyperplasia, and 1 mixed), 128 (37.6%) experienced a malignant upgrade, 98 (76.6%) to ductal carcinoma in situ, and 30 (23.4%) to invasive cancer. In multivariate analysis, multifocal atypia (odds ratio [OR], 25.61; 95% confidence interval [CI], 11.20–58.55; P<0.001) and Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System 4c-5 lesions (OR, 11.02; 95% CI, 1.43–84.86; P=0.021) were significant predictors. Multifocal atypia showed an 84.4% upgrade rate. Core needle biopsy had higher upgrade rates than vacuum-assisted biopsy (45.2% vs. 20.0%; P<0.001). The upgrade rates decreased from 50% to 25% over the study period (P<0.05).ConclusionThe 37.6% upgrade rate in this tertiary referral cohort exceeded that in Western reports, with multifocal atypia emerging as the strongest predictor. These findings support immediate excision for multifocal atypia while allowing individualized management for unifocal lesions with favorable imaging in Korean tertiary care settings.Keywords: Breast neoplasms, Risk factors, Precancerous conditions, Carcinoma, intraductal, noninfiltrating

INTRODUCTION

Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) and atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH) are breast lesions characterized by abnormal cellular proliferation within the ductal and lobular epithelium, respectively. These lesions are regarded as high-risk for breast cancer development, necessitating complete tissue analysis of the entire lesion due to their potential to be reclassified as malignant upon further investigation. Histologically, differentiating between low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and ADH can be challenging, often requiring excision for a definitive diagnosis.

Initial diagnoses of ADH or ALH are typically made through core needle biopsies (CNBs), but the limited sample size inherent to these procedures can lead to underestimation of lesion severity [1,2]. This diagnostic uncertainty has led to the American Society of Breast Surgeons recommending surgical excision for comprehensive evaluation of the entire lesion to rule out malignancy [3]. However, these recommendations are being challenged by emerging evidence suggesting that not all patients with atypical hyperplasia (AH) require surgical intervention. The rate at which AH upgrades to DCIS or invasive breast cancer upon excisional biopsy varies considerably, with previous studies reporting upgrade rates ranging from 0% to 37.5%, depending on many factors [4–9]. The controversy arises from the observation that the majority of ADH lesions do not upgrade to malignancy after surgical excision. The reported upgrade rates were lower in carefully selected low-risk patients [10,11]. While several studies have attempted to identify predictive factors for malignant upgrade, the results have been inconsistent. The applicability of these findings to Asian populations, including Korean patients, remains unclear because of potential differences in breast cancer biology, healthcare systems such as screening, and genetic factors. Asian women often have breasts that are smaller and denser than those of Western women, which makes it more difficult to detect and treat these lesions.

Previous studies in Korea investigating AH have been limited by single-center cohorts and relatively small sample sizes [12,13]. We conducted a multi-institutional retrospective cohort study to analyze data from five academic tertiary medical centers in Korea, where patients presented after initial evaluation and filtering through primary and secondary healthcare facilities. This patient cohort raises questions about whether the known characteristics of AH can be applied. We aimed to analyze factors that can guide clinical decision-making for Korean patients with AH who visited tertiary hospitals.

METHODS

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective multi-institutional cohort study collected data from five academic hospitals across Korea from January 2000 to December 2022. Patients were included in this study if they had: (1) a diagnosis of ADH or ALH on initial biopsy, and (2) subsequent excision by surgery or vacuum assistance. We excluded patients with (1) concurrent diagnosis of malignancy (DCIS or invasive carcinoma) in the ipsilateral breast at initial biopsy, (2) previous history of breast cancer, and (3) insufficient pathological or clinical information for analysis. One hospital had only 10 cases, and all these cases were excluded during the exclusion process. As a result, data from four hospitals were ultimately included in the statistical analysis. One case with both ADH and ALH features was classified as ADH based on the predominant pattern.

Data collection and definitions

We collected demographic data (age, body mass index [BMI], menopausal status, and reproductive history), lesion characteristics (size, location, and multifocality), diagnostic information (biopsy method and imaging findings), and pathological outcomes. Radiological findings were categorized according to the Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) classification. Malignant upgrade was defined as the presence of DCIS or invasive breast cancer in the final excision specimen following an initial tissue diagnosis of AH. Multifocal atypia was defined as AH with multiple foci within the same breast.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Core Team, 2023). Missing data ranged from 2.1% (age) to menopausal age (83.8%), variables with excessively high missing rates were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, univariate and multivariate analysis was performed by excluding missing data for each variable individually. Chi-square or Fisher exact tests were used for categorical variables, and Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. For BI-RADS categorization, we used a three-tier system (0-3/4a-b/4c-5) based on clinical relevance. Univariate logistic regression identified factors associated with upgrade, and variables with P<0.1 were included in multivariate analysis. We analyzed temporal trends using the year of diagnosis as a continuous variable and examined operation delay effects by categorizing patients into <31, 31–70, and >70 days. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

RESULTS

Study population and patient characteristics

Of the 589 initially collected cases, 340 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The cohort included 319 (93.8%) patients with ADH, 20 (5.9%) with ALH, and one (0.3%) with both features. Overall, the initial pathology remained unchanged in 212 patients (62.4%), whereas 128 patients (37.6%) experienced malignant upgrades: 98 (76.6%) to DCIS and 30 (23.4%) to invasive cancer (Fig. 2). The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age at operation was 47 years (IQR, 41–54 years). Significant differences between the upgrade and no-upgrade groups were observed for postmenopausal status (P=0.002), age >55 years (P<0.001), BRCA mutation positive (P=0.043), ultrasonography (USG) BI-RADS 4c, 5 (P<0.001), and multifocal atypia (P<0.001). In addition, ADH cases had significantly higher upgrade rates than ALH cases (39.2% vs. 15.0%; P=0.030). Among 295 cases with tissue sampling, CNB was associated with higher upgrade rates than vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB) (45.2% vs. 20.0%; P<0.001).

Risk factor analysis

Univariate analysis identified several factors associated with a malignant upgrade (Table 2). Significant predictors included BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (odds ratio [OR], 1.74; P=0.047), age >55 years (OR, 2.83; P=0.007), postmenopausal status (OR, 2.24; P<0.001), CNB (OR, 3.30; P=0.020), BI-RADS 4c-5 (OR, 13.50; P<0.001), and multifocal atypia (OR, 14.85; P<0.001). In multivariate analysis, only two factors remained independently associated with upgrade (Table 3): multifocal atypia (OR, 25.61; 95% confidence interval [CI], 11.20–58.55; P<0.001) and BI-RADS 4c-5 (OR, 11.02; 95% CI, 1.43–84.86; P=0.021). Multifocal atypia was present in 91 cases (28.8%), with an upgrade rate of 81.3%.

Sensitivity analysis for missing data handling

To evaluate the stability of the results under different approaches to handling missing data, we compared original analysis with complete case analysis for multivariate logistic regression (Supplementary Table 1). Among 340 cases, 211 cases (62.1%) were available for complete case analysis. The two independent predictors—multifocal atypia (OR, 40.12; 95% CI, 15.19–105.99; P<0.001) and BI-RADS 4c-5 (OR, 8.78; 95% CI, 1.04–74.13; P=0.046)—remained statistically significant with consistent direction of effect in the complete case analysis. However, the overall upgrade rate was higher in complete cases, compared to original analysis (44.5% vs. 37.6%).

DISCUSSION

The management of AH remains challenging due to variable upgrade rates and inconsistent risk assessment criteria [11,14]. In this multi-institutional cohort of 340 patients, we observed a 37.6% upgrade rate and identified multifocal atypia as the strongest predictor of malignant upgrade.

The overall upgrade rate of 37.6% falls within the higher range of previously reported rates (0%–37.5%) but exceeds recent Western studies reporting 10% to 25% [15,16]. This difference likely reflects our tertiary center population, where patients were referred after the initial evaluation at primary and secondary facilities, representing a preselected higher-risk cohort.

Our multivariate analysis identified multifocal atypia as the strongest independent predictor (OR, 25.61; P<0.001), representing one of the most powerful associations reported in the literature [17]. This suggests that the spatial distribution of atypical lesions is more predictive than individual lesion characteristics. High-suspicion ultrasonographic findings (BI-RADS 4c-5) also showed a significant association (OR, 11.02; 95% CI, 1.43–84.86; P=0.021), consistent with previous studies demonstrating the importance of imaging characteristics in predicting upgrade risk [18,19]. In the sensitivity analysis for 211 complete cases without missing values, multifocal atypia (OR, 40.12; 95% CI, 15.19–105.99; P<0.001) and BI-RADS 4c-5 (OR, 8.78; 95% CI, 1.04–74.13; P=0.046) remained statistically significant, supporting the robustness of our findings. However, the higher upgrade rate in complete cases (44.5%) compared with the overall cohort (37.6%) suggests an MNAR (missing not at random) pattern, where patients with complete data may represent a higher-risk subgroup. This pattern may have influenced the magnitude of effect estimates, although the direction and significance of key predictors remained consistent across analytical approaches.

The wide CIs observed for BI-RADS 4c-5 in both analyses reflect sparse data bias. Although the BI-RADS 4c-5 category contained few cases, the upgrade rate in this subgroup was disproportionately high, resulting in inflated ORs with wide CIs. Consequently, uncertainty and reduced reliability in the results were inevitable.

CNB demonstrated a significantly higher upgrade rate than VAB (OR, 3.30; P=0.020) in the univariate analysis, but this significance was attenuated in the multivariate model (OR, 2.08; P=0.379). This change in significance is attributable to multicollinearity among the independent variables, which interferes with the independent estimation of each predictor. In clinical practice, lesions with BI-RADS 4c or higher are more frequently diagnosed by CNB rather than VAB. Additionally, the small number of VAB cases (8.5%) relative to CNB (91.5%) caused the separation effect, which has further increased estimation uncertainty.

Consistent with prior studies, we confirmed that ADH has significantly higher upgrade rates than ALH (39.2% vs. 15.0%) [20]. However, several previously recognized risk factors did not maintain significance in this analysis. Age, which has been positively associated with upgrade risk in previous studies, lost significance when adjusted for other factors [21]. Similarly, palpable masses, lesion size, and microcalcifications were not independently associated with upgrade in this cohort [22,23]. This discrepancy may reflect the tertiary referral nature of our population, where patients may have been preselected based on high-risk imaging characteristics. The absence of significance for factors such as co-existing columnar cell lesions and detailed histopathological characteristics may be attributed to missing data and variations in pathological reporting across institutions and time periods [21].

The significant decline in upgrade rates from approximately 50% in the early 2000s to 25% in recent years likely reflects improvements in imaging technology and biopsy techniques [24–26]. Enhanced imaging resolution and larger tissue sampling may contribute to a more accurate initial diagnosis, reducing the likelihood of missed concurrent malignancy [26,27]. The finding that operation delay did not impact upgrade rates provides reassurance that reasonable delays for multidisciplinary consultation do not compromise outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design inherently carries the risks of selection and information bias. Missing data were substantial for several key variables: menopausal age (83.8%), reproductive history including parity (59.1%), mammographic findings (71.8%), sonographic characteristics (64.1%), and pathologic lesion size (62.6%). Variables with excessively high missingness were inevitably excluded from the analysis, which have limited our ability to identify additional risk factors and develop more robust prediction models. The small number of ALH cases (n=20, 5.9%) restricts the generalizability of the findings to this subgroup and limits the statistical power for meaningful comparisons with ADH cases.

The declining upgrade rates over time may introduce temporal bias, as cases diagnosed in earlier years may have different baseline characteristics and diagnostic criteria than more recent cases. Variations in pathological interpretation between institutions and across the study period may have influenced diagnostic consistency, particularly given the subjective nature of distinguishing AH from low-grade DCIS. The exclusion of cases initially diagnosed by surgical excision, while methodologically appropriate, may have excluded high-risk lesions that warranted immediate excision based on imaging characteristics, potentially underestimating the upgrade rates in the highest-risk population.

Furthermore, our findings may not be generalizable to community practice settings or screening-detected lesions, as the tertiary referral nature of our cohort likely represents a preselected higher-risk population. The upgrade rates ranged from 4.5% to 60.2% across institutions (sample size range: n=44 to n=129), showing the differences in upgrade rates among the hospitals. There may be variations depending on the tendencies of pathologists, but formal statistical comparison was not performed due to the relatively small sample size at each institution. Future studies should focus on standardized data collection across multiple academic centers to develop more robust risk assessment models specific to referred patient populations.

In conclusion, this multi-institutional study demonstrated that multifocal atypia is the strongest independent predictor of malignant upgrade in Korean women with AH diagnosed in tertiary care settings. The 37.6% upgrade rate reflects the preselected nature of patients referred to academic hospitals. These results provide evidence-based guidance for AH management in referred care settings, supporting immediate excision for multifocal atypia while allowing individualized management for unifocal lesions with favorable imaging characteristics. Standardized data collection across academic centers is required to develop reliable decision-making criteria for this specific patient population.
