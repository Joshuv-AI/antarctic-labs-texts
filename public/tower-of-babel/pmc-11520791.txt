GSK3179106 ameliorates lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation and acute lung injury by targeting P38 MAPK
Authors: Bin Zheng, Mengying Li, Enhong Lan, Wenting Ding, Lijiao Gao, Yue Tang, Xinyi Wu, Bing Zhang, Yali Zhang, Xiaona Zhu, Hui Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is a serious acute respiratory disease that can cause alveolar-capillary barrier disruption and pulmonary edema, respiratory failure and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. However, there is no effective drugs in clinic until now. GSK3179106 has been reported can alleviate intestinal stress syndrome, but the protective effect of GSK3179106 on ALI has not been elucidated. The present study will evaluate the pharmacological activity of GSK3179106 on lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation and lung injury and clarify its underlying mechanism. We found that GSK3179106 significantly attenuated LPS-induced lung injury in vivo, accompanied by inhibited infiltration of inflammatory cells and reduced expression of inflammatory cytokines. Meanwhile, GSK3179106 dose-dependently reduced the LPS-induced IL-6 expression both in protein and gene levels in macrophages. Mechanistically, GSK3179106 could inhibited the phosphorylation of P38 MAPK induced by LPS. Importantly, results showed that there is a direct combination between GSK3179106 and P38 MAPK. Together, our findings not only clarified the anti-inflammatory activity of GSK3179106 but also discovered its new clinical indications. Therefore, compound GSK3179106 may be a potential candidate for the treatment of acute inflammatory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-024-03012-9.Keywords: Acute lung injury, P38 MAPK kinase, Sepsis, GSK3179106, Lipopolysaccharide

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI), which main feature is acute diffuse inflammatory lung injury, resulting in increased alveolar capillary permeability, alveolar edema, and interstitial edema, is a clinical serious respiratory disease caused by sepsis, infection, shock, trauma, and other factors [1, 2]. Currently, mechanical ventilation, steroid therapy, nitric oxide, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation are generally used for treatment, but there are no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved effective treatments [3, 4]. The morbidity and mortality of ALI and its severe form acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains very high [5, 6]. It is urgent to discover new drugs for treating ALI.

Cytokines storm, characterized by the release of large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are closely related to pulmonary edema, multiple organ failure and shock, played an important role in acute inflammatory lung injury [7, 8]. Persistent elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) associated with the poor outcome of ARDS [9].Tocilizumab, anti-IL-6 receptor antibody, has been reported can effectively treat severe COVID-19-infected patients through inhibiting CRP gene transcription and suppressing inflammatory response [10]. Thus, targeting pro-inflammatory cytokines has been considered as means for treating hyperinflammation and lung injury.

Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), p38 MAPK, and c-Jun NH2-terminal kinase (JNK), are activated by various stimuli and mediate biological processes such as proliferation, differentiation, inflammation and survival [11, 12]. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI, MAPKs are phosphorylation to activate transcription factor activator protein-1(AP-1), thereby promoting the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β et al. [13, 14]. There are numerous publications in PMC-NCBI have shown that inhibiting the activation of MAPK signaling pathway could attenuate ALI in laboratory and clinical study. GSK3179106, a multikinases inhibitor, could alleviate intestinal stress syndrome through attenuation of inflammatory and stress-induced visceral hypersensitivity by inhibiting REarranged during transfection (RET) kinase activity in mice [15, 16]. In addition to RET, we also found that GSK3179106 had significant inhibitory activity against several kinases within the MAPK signaling pathway. While GSK3179106’s inhibitory effects on MAPK kinases are evident, it remains unclear whether it can modulate the MAPK signaling pathway and thereby alleviate ALI. This study will investigate pharmacological activity of GSK3179106 on ALI and clarify its underlying molecular mechanism.

Materials and methods

Reagents

LPS was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St.Louis, MO, USA). Antibodies for p-P38, P38, p-ERK, ERK, p-JNK, JNK, p-P65, P65, IκB-α, p-STAT3 and STAT3 were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). The mouse IL-6 enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit was purchased from eBioscience (San Diego, CA, USA). SB203580 was purchased from To-pscience Biochem Technology (Shanghai, China). SR11302 was purchased from GlpBio (Montclair, USA). GSK3179106 was purchased from Selleck (Texas, USA). In vivo studies, GSK3179106 was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted with olive oil into appropriate concentrations (final concentration of DMSO was less than 1%). In vitro studies, GSK3179106 was dissolved in DMSO (final concentration of DMSO was less than 1‰), and the same volume of DMSO alone was used as the vehicle control.

Cell culture

J774A.1 macrophages (Procell CL-0370) were kindly provided by Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Eggenstein, Germany) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin Solution (P/S) at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2.

RAW264.7 macrophages purchased from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% P/S.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from Clonetics (San Diego, CA, USA) were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% P/S.

Animal care and preparation

Male C57BL/6 mice (18–20 g), 6–8 weeks old, were obtained from the Animal Centre of Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou, China). Animals were housed at a constant room temperature with a 12:12 h light–dark cycle and fed with a standard rodent diet for at least 7 days before used. All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Wenzhou Medical University Animal Policy and Welfare Committee (Approval Document No. wydw2019-0438). All animals received humane care according to the National Institutes of Health (USA) guidelines.

Sepsis-induced ALI model Thirty C57BL/6 mice were randomly separated into five groups (n = 6 per group): vehicle control group (CON), LPS-induced sepsis group (LPS), low-dose GSK3179106 pretreatment sepsis group (LPS + GSK 3), high-dose GSK3179106 pretreatment sepsis group (LPS + GSK 6), and GSK3179106 pretreatment-only group (GSK 6). Mice in the LPS group received 10 mg/kg LPS (100 μL, in saline) via the tail vein. Mice in the LPS + GSK 3 and LPS + GSK 6 groups were administered 3 mg/kg and 6 mg/kg GSK3179106 (100 μL, 1% DMSO in olive oil), respectively, via intraperitoneal injection, 12 h and 1 h prior to 10 mg/kg LPS (100 μL) administration. Mice in the GSK 6 group received 6 mg/kg GSK3179106 (100 μL, 1% DMSO in olive oil) via intraperitoneal injection of 12 h and 1 h before saline administrated. Mice in the CON group received an equivalent volume of 1% DMSO in olive oil and saline. Mice were euthanized under anesthesia 12 h post-LPS administrated by intraperitoneal injection of 4% chloral hydrate. BALF and blood samples were collected, and lung tissue was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

ALI mouse model Mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 5 per group): control group (CON), ALI group (ALI), and ALI + GSK3179106 group (ALI + GSK 3). Mice in the ALI group received tracheal instillation of 5 mg/kg LPS (20 μL, in saline). Mice in the ALI + GSK 3 group received 3 mg/kg GSK3179106 (100 μL, 1% DMSO in olive oil) via intraperitoneal injection 12 h and 1 h prior to the administration of 5 mg/kg LPS (20 μL). Mice in the CON group received an equivalent volume of 1% DMSO in olive oil and saline. Mice were euthanized under anesthesia 6 h post-LPS administrated by intraperitoneal injection of 4% chloral hydrate. BALF and blood samples were collected, and lung tissue was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

MTT assay

J774A.1 and RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 0.8 × 104 cells per well. Cells were incubated with different concentrations of GSK3179106 (0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10 and 20 µM) for 24 h. Then, 20 μL MTT (5 mg/mL) was added to all wells and the plate was incubated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C for further 4 h. Cells were dissolved with 150 µL DMSO and analyzed in a multi-well-plate reader at 490 nm.

Determination of cytokines

The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in culture medium or mouse BALF and serum were determined with ELISA kits according to manufacturer's instructions (Bioscience, San Diego, CA). The total amount of IL-6 in medium was normalized to the protein concentration of lysates.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from J774A.1, RAW264.7 cells and lung tissues (10–20 mg) using TRIZOL (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Both reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using IQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). QuantStudio®3 Real Time PCR Systems (ABI, CA, USA) was used for qPCR analysis. Western blot assay

Collected cells or homogenized lung tissues were then lysed. Concentration of total proteins was determined using the Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

After administration of GSK3179106 for 30 min, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 0.5 μg/mL LPS for 30 min. Then, the nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using the Nucleoprotein and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime P0028) and the protein concentration were determined by the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CESTA)

RAW264.7 cells were treated with GSK3179106 (5 μM) for 6 h, lysed with RIPA buffer to collect total protein. The protein was evenly divided into six portions and heated to 44, 48, 52, 56, 60 and 64 °C for 5 min, then left at room temperature for 3 min before being transferred to ice for further use. Following heating, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant (50 μL) was mixed with 50 μL of 2 × loading buffer, heated on a metal bath at 100 °C for 10 min, and then collected. Protein levels were determined by Western blotting [17]. (10..1038/s41598-018–27834-y).

Drug affinity response target stability assay (DARTS assay)

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated by LPS (0.5 μg/mL) for 5 h. Total protein was extracted and incubated with GSK3179106 (5 μM) at 4 °C for overnight. Then pronase (25 ng/μg total protein) was added and cells were incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Western blot samples were prepared through adding 2*loading buffer and boiling at 100 °C for 10 min [18].

BALF collection and flow cytometric analysis

BALF was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was immediately stored at -80 °C. The cell pellet was then resuspended in cold PBS for counting using the Count Star system (Shanghai, China) and subsequently analyzed via flow cytometry on a Beckman Coulter Cytoflex instrument (CA, USA). For the identification of neutrophils in our experiments, we employed a panel of antibodies, including APC-Cy7 anti-mouse CD45, PE-Cy7 anti-mouse CD11b, and PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-mouse Ly6G, as outlined in the schematic provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Histopathology and immunofluorescence

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. After dehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and were evaluated for general histopathological damage using light microscopy (Nikon, Japan). According to previous study, the severity of lung impairment was evaluated using a lung injury score, which ranged from 0 (normal) to 4 (severe) across four categories: neutrophil infiltration, hemorrhage, alveolar congestion, and alveolar wall damage [19, 20]. Grading was performed by a pathologist who was blinded to the study. The individual scores for each category were factored into the lung injury score. For each animal, the lung injury score was calculated as the sum of five sections. Paraffin sections were also used to perform immunofluorescence staining for LY6G using routine techniques. Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies. Anti-LY6G (Cat#: 560,602, 1:200 dilution, BD Pharmingen) was used to label neutrophils. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope equipped with DP2-BSW software (version 2.2, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The percentage expression was measured using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Five lung tissue sections were obtained from each group using 200X magnification.

Myeloperoxidase

The myeloperoxidase (MPO) test kit was used to measure MPO activity in lung tissue. The lung tissue was homogenized according to the kit’s instructions, and was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was then taken for MPO activity detection and indicated in the form of units per gram of protein. BCA assay was used to determine the total protein content in the sample.

Molecular docking of GSK3179106 to P38

The crystal structure of P38 (6OHD) was obtained from Protein Data Bank for the docking simulation. Schrodinger software Protein Preparation Wizard module is selected. The protein was pretreated, including hydrogenation, dewatering, supplementing the missing loop amino acid, supplementing the missing side Chain, and so on. The structure of GSK3179106 was processed with the Schrödinger software LigPrep module, including 3D conformational generation, hydrogenation, ionization state calculation, and energy minimization conformational optimization. The hydrogen bonds and bond lengths within the possibility of protein binding to small molecules was analyzed [21].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were randomized and blinded. In vitro experiments repeated at least three times. All data are expressed as mean ± SEM. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Pro Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA). We used one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test when comparing multiple independent groups. Differences between group means were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

GSK3179106 attenuates septic lung injury in mice

Firstly, the effects of GSK3179106 in the mouse model of sepsis-induced lung injury were assessed. Mice were injected with LPS (10 mg/kg) via tail vein to generate a sepsis model (Fig. 1A). Histological analysis revealed significant infiltration of inflammatory cell and aggravation thickening of the alveolar wall after LPS exposure, which was significantly reduced upon GSK3179106 pretreatment (Fig. 1B, C). Given that increased pulmonary capillary vascular permeability is a central feature of acute lung injury [22], the total protein concentration and the number of cells in BALF were evaluated. The results showed that GSK3179106 could substantially inhibit LPS-induced protein levels in BALF (Fig. 1D). This effect may be attributed to GSK3179106 targeting endothelial cells to restore barrier function, as evidenced by its inhibition of Occludin expression in HUVECs induced by LPS (Fig. S2). Furthermore, pretreatment with GSK3179106 signifcantly reduced the numbers of total cells and neutrophils (Fig. 1E–G). Immunofluorescence labelling of the common neutrophil markers Ly6G showed that, LPS-induced ALI mice had marked neutrophil accumulation in their lungs compared to control mice, however, these increases were signifcantly reduced in GSK3179106 treatment group (Fig. 1H, I). At the early stage of lung injury, neutrophils are the first cells to reach the affected area, with MPO serving as a marker of neutrophils activity [23, 24]. Results showed that MPO activity in lung tissues increased in the LPS group and decreased with GSK3179106 pretreatment (Fig. 1J). Thus, GSK3179106 can ameliorate sepsis-induced lung injury through attenuating pathological damage, decreasing vascular permeability and inhibiting the recruitment of inflammatory cells.

GSK3179106 inhibits sepsis-induced inflammation in mouse lung tissue

To further evaluate the protective effects of GSK3179106, we analyzed the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in vivo. The level of TNF-α in BALF was significantly higher in the LPS group compared to the control group, yet this increase was mitigated by GSK3179106 pretreatment (Fig. 2A). We also observed that the LPS group exhibited higher secretion of IL-6 compared to the control group, whereas, pretreatment with GSK3179106 at 6 mg/kg significantly decreased LPS-induced secretion of IL-6 (Fig. 2B). Additionally, administration of GSK3179106 reduced the amount of IL-6 in serum (Fig. 2C). We then examined the mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β by RT-qPCR. Results in Fig. 2D–F showed that pretreatment with GSK3179106 at 6 mg/kg attenuated LPS-induced elevation of inflammatory gene expression. Collectively, these results suggest that GSK3179106 can alleviate sepsis-induced lung inflammation and injury.

GSK3179106 alleviates LPS-induced ALI

To verify the anti-inflammatory activity of GSK3179106, an LPS-induced ALI mouse model was established (Fig. 3A). Our assessment of the total cell count in BALF from LPS-induced ALI mice revealed that GSK3179106 significantly reduced the total cell count in the BALF (Fig. 3B). According to H&E staining, LPS challenge induced significant inflammatory cell infiltration and pulmonary edema, which were significantly improved by the pretreatment with GSK3179106 (Fig. 3C, D). Furthermore, levels of IL-6 (Fig. 3E, F) and TNF-α (Fig. 3G, H) in serum and BALF were noted to be significantly increased but reduced following GSK3179106 pretreatment. Then, RT-qPCR was used to evaluate the gene expression of TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. 3I, J). As expected, the LPS-induced gene expressions of inflammatory cytokines were reduced when pretreated by GSK3179106. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis of BALF demonstrated a significant reduction in neutrophil populations within the BALF in animals treated with both LPS and GSK3179106, compared to those exposed solely to LPS (Fig. 3K, M). Additionally, our findings indicated that MPO activity in lung tissues was elevated in the LPS group and was subsequently reduced with GSK3179106 pretreatment (Fig. 3L). Thus, these findings indicate that GSK3179106 can attenuate LPS-induced ALI.

Effect of GSK3179106 on LPS-inflammation in macrophages

Given GSK3179106 effectively showed protection against LPS-induced sepsis and ALI, we further assessed its anti-inflammatory activity in vitro and its potential mechanism. Firstly, the cytotoxicity of GSK3179106 on macrophages was determined using MTT assay. Results showed that GSK3179106 at 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, and 5 μM did not produce obvious cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 and J774A.1 cells (Fig. 4A, D). Thus, these concentrations were chosen to investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of GSK3179106. After a 30-min pretreatment with different concentrations of GSK3179106, macrophages were stimulated with LPS for 24 h and the ELISA assay was used to measure the release of IL-6 in the culture medium. GSK3179106 effectively inhibited LPS-induced production of IL-6 both in RAW264.7 and J774A.1 cells, with IC50 values of 0.1089 and 0.2048 μM, respectively (Fig. 4B, E). RT-qPCR analysis showed that LPS-induced IL-6 mRNA expression was reduced by GSK3179106 in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 1.271 and 0.01627 μM in RAW264.7 and J774A.1, respectively (Fig. 4C, F). Taken together, these results showed that GSK3179106 exhibited obvious anti-inflammatory effect in macrophages.

GSK3179106 exerted its anti-inflammatory response through binding to P38

GSK3179106 has been reported as an inhibitor of RET kinase [15], so we detected the RET expression through western blot assay. However, the expression of RET decreased in ALI mouse lung tissues when compared with CON group and the phosphorylation of RET was hard to detect (Supplementary Fig. S3). Thus we speculate that GSK3179106 exert its anti-inflammatory activity not through targeting RET. MAPKs-AP-1 and NF-κB signaling, which are two classic inflammatory signal pathways downstream of LPS/TLR4, were examined to elucidate the underlying anti-inflammatory mechanism of GSK3179106. Results showed that LPS induced an increase in the phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, P38, c-JUN and P65 and the degradation of IκB-α. GSK3179106 pretreatment significantly attenuated the phosphorylation of P38 and c-JUN in a dose-dependent manner in RAW264.3 cells (Fig. 5A and S4C). Additionally, GSK3179106 also can inhibit LPS-induced activation of P38 in J774A.1 cells (Fig. 5B). However, GSK3179106 had no obvious impact on LPS-mediated ERK, JNK and P65 phosphorylation or IκB-α degradation (Fig. S4A-E). Meanwhile, our analysis revealed that kinases exhibiting an inhibition rate of GSK3179106 > 50% were significantly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway, as identified through KEGG enrichment analysis of Eidam’s kinase spectrum data (Fig. S5) [15]. This suggests that P38 may serve as an anti-inflammatory target for GSK3179106. Consequently, we conduceted molecular docking studies to explore the interaction between GSK3179106 and P38. GSK3179106 can directly bind to P38 and form hydrogen bonds with Thr-106, His-107 and Met109, with a binding energy of -9.2 kcal/mol (Fig. 5D). Meanwhile, we found that GSK3179106 protected P38 from pronase-mediated protein degradation through DARTS approach (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, CETSA confirmed that GSK3179106 directly bound with P38 to enhance its thermal stability at 56 °C (Fig. 5E). In addition, GSK3179106 treatment obviously inhibited the phosphorylation of P38 in both ALI and sepsis mouse lung tissue (Fig. 5F–G and S6). Collectively, these findings suggested that GSK3179106 suppressed inflammation by directly inhibiting P38 signaling.

Discussion

In this study, we discovered an important role of GSK3179106 in alleviating LPS-induced lung injury in both sepsis and ALI mice. In addition, GSK3179106 treatment was found to suppress inflammatory responses in injured lung tissues and activated macrophages. Moreover, mechanism study showed that P38 MAPK/AP-1 signaling pathway was discovered to be involved in the anti-inflammatory activity of GSK3179106. Interestingly, molecular docking revealed that GSK3179106 can interact with P38 MAPK directly. Furthermore, GSK3179106 pretreatment had a notable effect on the inhibition of STAT3 activation.

Inflammatory response plays an important role in the LPS-induced ALI and sepsis, many cytokines are produced, leading to lung injury. IL-6, mainly secreted by monocytes, macrophages, endothelial cells, B cells, and T cells, plays an important role in the acute phase of inflammation [25, 26]. High levels of IL-6 are associated with encephalitis in children infected with Enterovirus 71 and in immunodeficient mouse models [27, 28]. In mice with acute kidney injury, circulating IL-6 promotes lung injury through mediating CXCL1 production [29]. Interleukin-6 mediates lung injury following ischemic acute kidney injury or bilateral nephrectomy [30]. There is some early evidence that IL-6 can be used as an inflammatory marker for severe COVID-19 infection with poor prognosis in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. In this study, we demonstrated that GSK3179106 inhibited the expression of inflammatory genes in the lung tissue of septic mice and decreased IL-6 levels in serum and alveolar lavage fluid of septic mice, and the same effect was shown in the ALI mouse model. In addition, GSK3179106 can also reduce IL-6 levels in macrophages.

P38 MAPK, a member of MAPK signaling pathway, can be activated in the presence of certain pathogenic stimuli or extracellular stimuli. Some P38 MAPK inhibitors and blockers have been reported can be considered as important anti-inflammatory strategies [31–35]. Our study revealing that GSK3179106 significantly inhibits the phosphorylation of P38 MAPK in macrophages, without exerting notable inhibitory effects on the phosphorylation of ERK and JNK. Additionally, it markedly inhibits the phosphorylation of P38 in mouse lung tissue, while showing no obvious impact on the reported target RET. Meanwhile, Eidam et al. reported that GSK3179106 showed obvious inhibitory activity on P38α and p38β subtypes at 1 μM, which were 70.4% and 81.6%, respectively, but not on P38γ and p38δ subtypes [15]. These results indicated that P38 is involved in the anti-inflammatory effects of GSK3179106. Computer molecular simulation docking experiment showed that GSK3179106 directly inserted the ATP binding pocket of P38 MAPK and forms hydrogen bind with THR-106, MET-109 and VAL-30. THR-106 residue, known as gatekeeper residue in P38α/β, was found to be the direct binding site of some P38 inhibitors, such as SB203580, SB206718 and 4-Anilinoquinazoline analog [36–38]. MET-109 residue also was reported as direct binding site of some P38 inhibitors, such as Aminobenzophenones and 4-Anilinoquinazoline analog [36, 39]. DARTS and CESTA methods also confirmed that there was a direct binding between P38 and GSK3179106. As GSK3179106 can directly bind to P38 and form hydrogen bonds with the key amino acids in the P38 ATP pocket, P38 was considered to the anti-inflammatory target of GSK3179106.

Conclusion

In summary, we showed that GSK3179106 can significantly protect lung tissue damage caused by LPS injection in mice. These findings extend the potential application range of GSK3179106. We also demonstrated that P38 may be the anti-inflammatory target of GSK3179106.
