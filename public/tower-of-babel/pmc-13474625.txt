What is the clinical question being addressed?

Can a large language model verify site-reported baseline past medical history data in multicenter trial records?

What is the main finding?

Large language model-based source document verification achieved 96% agreement with a reference reviewer, approaching human review performance.

Multicenter clinical trials underpin cardiovascular practice but are increasingly expensive, with some pivotal trials exceeding $500 million.1 Trial monitoring is among the most labor-intensive activities, accounting for up to one-third of total costs.2,3 A central component of monitoring is source document verification (SDV), in which sponsor or contract research organization staff compare electronic case report forms (eCRFs) to participant medical records to verify data elements. SDV may occur during in-person site visits or remotely. Automating SDV could reduce costs substantially and flag unsubstantiated data earlier. Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong performance in data verification tasks and can extract structured variables from clinical trial documents at scale.4 In this study, we developed an LLM-based SDV workflow to verify site-reported past medical history (PMH) variables and evaluated its agreement with human reviewers in INVESTED (Influenza Vaccine to Effectively Stop Cardio Thoracic Events and Decompensated Heart Failure) trial.

The INVESTED trial enrolled 5,260 patients with a myocardial infarction within 1 year or heart failure hospitalization within 2 years and at least 1 additional risk factor at 157 North American sites (September 2016-January 2019).5 Local Institutional Review Boards approved the protocol, and all participants provided informed consent. Investigators entered baseline characteristics into an eCRF, including 24 PMH variables: 23 binary comorbid conditions and 1 numerical variable (ejection fraction). For approximately 5% of participants (n = 293) selected randomly, investigators were required to submit medical records from the qualifying hospitalization as portable document format files for central eligibility assessment.

We built an LLM-based SDV workflow that ingested paired eCRF data and the medical records. Portable document formats were converted to text using Apple Vision OCR. We prompted the OpenAI o4-mini LLM to determine, for each PMH variable in each participant, whether the eCRF entry was supported by the medical record. For eCRF-positive entries, the LLM returned “verified” when the condition was documented and “not verified” when it was absent or negated; for eCRF-negative entries, it returned “verified” when the condition was not documented or negated in the submitted records and “not verified” when it was positively mentioned. Among the 293 participants with available medical records, 125 randomly selected participants were partitioned into a held-out validation set; a subset of the remaining participants was used for prompt refinement. A single prompt was used for verification of all variables and was identical for all patients. The model was executed in a secure Microsoft Azure environment.

Two blinded clinical reviewers and the LLM independently annotated a randomly selected held-out validation set of 125 INVESTED participants. Six participants with medical records in French were excluded, restricting the primary analysis to 119 English-language records. One clinical reviewer was designated as the reference reviewer for analyses of LLM-human agreement, and the second reviewer was used to assess human-human agreement; disagreements were not adjudicated. Because disagreements were not adjudicated, the reference reviewer comparison was interpreted as concordance with a prespecified clinical reviewer rather than as a gold standard assessment of truth. Agreement was defined as the raw proportion of variable assessments for which 2 reviewers assigned the same SDV classification. Agreement between reviewers and the LLM was assessed in the pooled cohort and for each PMH variable individually. For the pooled analyses, 95% CIs were estimated by fitting an intercept-only logistic regression model to the agreement indicator (1 for agreement and 0 for disagreement) with cluster-robust SEs at the participant level to account for multiple PMH variables contributed by each individual. Positive and negative predictive values of LLM SDV verification were calculated relative to the reference reviewer. Positive predictive value was defined as the proportion of LLM-verified entries that were also verified by the prespecified reference human reviewer. Negative predictive value was defined as the proportion of LLM-nonverified entries that were also not verified by the reference human reviewer.

Among 119 participants in the validation set, the mean age was 65.4 years (SD 12.1), and 29 (24%) were female; 61 (52%) were enrolled at U.S. Veterans Administration sites, 22 (19%) at non-Veterans Administration U.S. sites, and 36 (30%) at Canadian sites. Among 2,856 variable-level observations (119 patients x 24 PMH variables), the PMH was recorded in the eCRF as present (“eCRF-positive”) in 520 (19%) and as absent (“eCRF-negative”) in 2,217 (81%), with 119 additional numerical ejection fraction entries.

Overall agreement between the LLM and the reference human reviewer was 95.6% (95% CI: 94.2%- 96.6%), compared with 97.4% (95% CI: 96.7%-97.9%) agreement between the 2 human reviewers. Agreement between the LLM and the second reviewer was 96.1% (95% CI: 94.7%-97.1%). The LLM verified 90% of eCRF entries, and the reference human reviewer agreed with these verifications with a positive predictive value of 98.5% (95% CI: 97.9%-98.9%). The negative predictive value of nonverification was 69.6% (95% CI: 60.5%-77.3%). These results indicate that LLM demonstrated high positive predictive value when it verified data but missed some verification opportunities that the reference reviewer found. The LLM generated identical results on 5 repetitions.

Agreement for both LLM-human and human-human comparisons was greater in eCRF-negative compared to eCRF-positive data entries. When the eCRF reported no PMH of the condition, agreement was 97.2% (95% CI: 95.6%-98.2%) between LLM and human reviewers and 98.3% (95% CI: 97.6%-98.8%) between human reviewers. Among eCRF-positive data entries, agreement was 91.0% (95% CI: 87.9%-93.3%) between the human reviewer and LLM and 94.4% (95% CI: 91.8%-96.2%) between human reviewers. The LLM verified 75% of PMH-positive eCRF entries, and 97% of those were confirmed by the human reviewer, indicating high positive predictive value and avoidance of false verifications. Among the 25% the LLM did not verify, 73% were also not found by the human reviewer, suggesting that in most cases the finding was missing in the documentation rather than overlooked by the model.

LLM agreement with the reference reviewer exceeded 85% for every individual PMH variable (Table 1). Consistent with the lower observed agreement in eCRF-positive observations, agreement between LLM and human reviewers was lower for more common conditions like dyslipidemia and hypertension. This post hoc analysis of the INVESTED trial found that an automated LLM-based approach verified site-reported PMH data in participant medical records with high agreement relative to a prespecified reference reviewer. Human-human agreement was also high and is reported as contextual information. LLM data verification demonstrated high positive predictive value. The model was more conservative than human reviewers in assigning verification; nonverification by the LLM should not be interpreted as evidence of eCRF error but rather as identifying entries requiring human review. These results suggest that LLMs may help streamline labor-intensive and costly clinical trial monitoring.

A practical workflow for implementing automated LLM-based monitoring would use the LLM to support human monitors by conducting immediate first-pass verification and route only discordant or low-confidence items to human review. This approach could substantially reduce monitoring effort, identify variables or sites generating nonverifiable data earlier than intermittent site visits, and integrate with contemporary risk-based monitoring strategies. Our results suggest that inappropriate data verification by the LLM was rare (< 2% of data entries).

This study has limitations. The absence of documentation does not necessarily mean the medical condition is absent. Medical records in INVESTED were collected to confirm eligibility, not to support SDV, so some eCRF entries may have been present but not documented in the submitted dossier. Missing source document information was handled according to the SDV task: absent documentation led to “not verified” for eCRF-positive entries and “verified” for eCRF-negative entries. We evaluated only baseline PMH variables; performance may differ for longitudinal outcomes, adverse events, medications, procedures, endpoint adjudication, multilingual records, or lower-quality scanned documents, and extension to these use cases will require additional prompting strategies and validation. Moreover, validation was performed only in 1 clinical trial conducted in North America; generalizability to other trials or regions is unknown. LLM-based SDV requires centralized submission of medical records, which is not performed in all trials.

In conclusion, in this proof-of-concept study, an LLM-based SDV tool verified baseline medical history data with high agreement relative to human review in a multicenter clinical trial. Rather than replacing human monitors, partial SDV automation—using LLMs for first-pass verification and routing unverified or clinically ambiguous items to human monitors—could reduce trial costs and surface inaccurate data earlier.
