On Randomized Computational Models and Complexity Classes: a Historical Overview
Melissa Antonelli, Ugo Dal Lago, Paolo Pistone

Abstract
Since their appearance in the 1950s, computational models capable of performing probabilistic choices have received wide attention and are nowadays pervasive in almost every areas of computer science. Their development was also inextricably linked with inquiries about computation power and resource issues. Although most crucial notions in the field are well-known, the related terminology is sometimes imprecise or misleading. The present work aims to clarify the core features and main differences between machines and classes developed in relation to randomized computation. To do so, we compare the modern definitions with original ones, recalling the context in which they first appeared, and investigate the relations linking probabilistic and counting models.

2.2.4
Comparing Santos’ and Gill’s Machines

In fact, there exist relevant differences between Santos’ and Gill’s PTMs.
First, the latter ones are presented as (deterministic) TMs accessing an oracle source of randomness, while the former are defined as NTMs with special transition functions returning a probability value.
Proving that oracle machines and machines accessing probability functions are analogous (even assuming that the source of randomness they access is equivalent, e.g. corresponding to fair coin tossing) is not trivial, see e.g.
[
2
]
.
16
16

16

Indeed, although it is more or less folklore to consider the oracle- and probability function-models as analogous,
concretely
relevant dissimilarities depend on the way in which the machine accesses its source of randomness; for instance, showing the equivalence between the class of probabilistic recursive functions
\mathcal{PR}

[

]
– inspired by “Santos-style PTMs” and based on probabilistic functions – and of oracle recursive functions

– based on the “oracle” model – is not straightforward, see
[
2
]
.

Second, in Gill’s PTMs only unbiased choices are allowed, whereas the “kind of randomness” defined by Santos is more general.
Indeed, the source of randomness considered in
[

[

Gill himself was aware of this difference as he claimed that, by relaxing his model so to allow arbitrary bias, one rather obtains machines equivalent to Santos’ ones, i.e. in which transitions can be associated with any probability:

[

He also presented his PTMs as a special case of Santos’ one
[
12

Another relevant dissimilarity between the two machines is that, when entering a non-deterministic state, Gill’s PTM chooses between two possible next states only, while Santos’ transition function may lead to several subsequent configurations.

On the other hand, despite being more limited, Gill’s PTMs – in which, as seen, the source of randomness is confined to the probability of independent and fair coin tosses – are somehow more realistic, as effectively implementable.
Nonetheless, both these original, alternative presentations have paved the way to
different

2.3.2
On Valiant’s Counting Machines

Counting machines were developed to analyze probabilistic and counting complexity as well.
In
The Complexity of Computing the Permanent
(1979), Valiant introduced them to define the class
\sharp\mathbf{P}
.
Actually, he started by considering a specific problem – that of computing the permanent of an
n\times n
matrix – and by claiming that it is at least as complex as counting the number of accepting computations of a poly-time NTM
[
40
, p. 189]
.
It was to express (and prove) this statement in a precise way that Valiant introduced counting machines and the corresponding (function) class
\sharp\mathbf{P}
.
18
18

18

He also generalized these notions establishing a framework to classify counting problems and defining a corresponding hierarchy
[
40
, p. 190]
.

More precisely, in
[
40
,
Def. 2.1
, p. 191]
, a counting machine is defined as a (poly-time) NTM such that, given an input, it returns
the number
of the corresponding accepting paths (in binary notation):

A
counting Turing machine
is a standard nondeterministic TM with an auxiliary output device that (magically) prints in binary notation on a special tape the number of accepting computations induced by the input.
[
40
, p. 191]

Then,
\sharp\mathbf{P}
is presented as the
counting
class of functions that can be computed by a counting machine in polynomial time.
Finally, related notions of reduction and of complete problems are introduced.

2.3.3
Comparing Simon’s and Valiant’s Machines

Simon and Valiant themselves noticed the existence of mutual similarities
between their machines and with PTMs.
In
[
30
,
Th.
4.4, p. 91]
, Simon compared his machine model with Gill’s ones, showing that the class of languages recognized by poly-time threshold machines and by poly-time PTMs were the same.
On the other hand, Valiant noticed that threshold machines were very close – or “essentially equivalent”
[
40
, p. 190]
– to Gill’s and Simon’s ones.

Actually, as we have seen, although the two terms are often used as interchangeable in the literature, there is a crucial difference between counting and threshold machines: given an input, Simon’s machine
accepts
it when the number of accepting paths is greater than a given threshold, whereas Valiant’s machine
returns
the number (in binary) of the corresponding accepting paths.
Consequently, the class defined by Simon’s machines, called
k
-
\mathbf{NP}
, is a class of problems, while
\sharp\mathbf{P}
is a function class.

3.2
The Counting Hierarchy

Since then, studies on probabilistic and counting complexity have spread
and several definitions for such classes have been introduced.
It was in this context that, in the 1980s,
\mathbf{CH}
was introduced for the first time both (and independently) by Wagner
[
42
]
and by Parberry and Schnitger
[
18
]
.
As for
\mathbf{PH}
, there exist (at least) two main,
equivalent
presentations of this hierarchy:
Wagner’s original one
[
41
,
42
]
, in terms of alternating quantifiers, and Torán’s oracle characterization
[
38
,
39
]
.
The latter is very similar to the corresponding polynomial version, but its building blocks are obtained by replacing
\mathbf{NP}
with
\mathbf{PP}
.

In 1984/86, starting from the investigation of languages for succinct representation of combinatorial problems, Wagner introduced
\mathbf{CH}
to classify natural problems in which counting is involved
[
41
,
42
]
.
His characterization relied on the notion of counting operator over classes of languages,
C
, which was inspired by the definition of threshold machine:

\textsf{C}^{p(n)}_{f(x)}yP(y)
expresses that there are at least
f(x)
strings
y
of length
p(n)
satisfying
P
.
In particular, a clear presentation was offered in
The Complexity of Combinatorial Problems with Succinct Input Representation
(1986), where, in Section 3, so-called
{CPH}
was introduced as an extension of
\mathbf{PH}
obtained by adding
C
to standard existential and universal quantifiers
[
42
, p. 335]
.
Then,
\mathbf{CH}
was defined as the smallest family of classes of languages including
\mathbf{P}
and closed under existential, universal and counting operators.
For each level in the hierarchy, Wagner showed how to construct a complete set for it
[
42
, p. 338]
.

Some years later, an alternative, but equivalent oracle characterization for
\mathbf{CH}
was introduced by Torán
[
38
,
39
]
.
First, he presented a slightly modified notion of
C
, the so-called
exact counting quantifier
:

\dots
for the function
f:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
,
f\in\mathbf{FP}
, a polynomial
p
, and a two argument predicate
P
,

\textsf{C}^{p}_{f(x)}y:P(x,y)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad||\{y:|y|\leq p(|x|)\text{ and }P(x,y)\}||\geq f(x).

We alternate now the polynomial counting quantifier
C
with the existential and universal quantiiers in order to define the counting hierarchy.

[
38
, p. 215]

Then, in
[
38
, Sec. 4]
, the oracle characterization was introduced.
This definition is close to the oracle characterization of
\mathbf{PH}
, but here PTMs are used instead of NTMs
[
38
, p. 219]
:

One way to define the counting hierarchy is to take the usual definition of the polynomial hierarchy:

•

\Sigma^{p}_{0}=\mathbf{P}

•

\Sigma^{p}_{k+1}=\mathbf{NP}^{\Sigma^{p}_{k}}
for
k\geq 0

and replace “
\mathbf{NP}
” with “
\mathbf{PP}
”. […]
That gives us the definition:

•

\mathbf{C}^{p}_{0}=\mathbf{P}

•

\mathbf{C}^{p}_{k+1}=\mathbf{PP}^{\mathbf{C}^{p}_{k}}
for
k\geq 0
.

Thus
\mathbf{C}^{p}_{1}=\mathbf{PP}
,
\mathbf{C}^{p}_{2}=\mathbf{PP}^{\mathbf{PP}}
, and so on.

[
3
, pp. 182-183]

Torán also proved this characterization to be equivalent to Wagner’s one.

Observe also that Wagner’s counting operator was not the only “probabilistic” (class) quantifier that appeared in those decades to characterize complexity classes.
For example, Papadimitriou introduced a
probabilistic quantifier
to capture
\mathbf{PSPACE}

[
16
]
, Zachos and Heller defined
random quantifiers
to characterize
\mathbf{BPP}

[
44
]
, and, in
[
43
]
, (again) Zachos considered the
overwhelming
and
majority
quantifiers.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we have surveyed some of the most relevant machine models and classes related to probabilistic and counting computation, which in turn is increasingly important in computer science.
We have shown that for several of these non-standard models more than one definition exists, and that the corresponding terminology is often ambiguous.
Such ambiguity is rooted in the genesis of these notions and in their historical development.
By inspecting historical sources, we have tried to identify the original features of the mentioned models so to avoid future misunderstanding and to enhance the comprehension of randomized computation.

For instance, we have shown that the very-first presentations of PTMs
are different – as said, PTMs are defined either as deterministic TMs accessing an oracle source of (unbiased) randomness or as NTMs with probabilistic transition functions
– and that the proof of their equivalence is not obvious (see
[
2
]
).
We have also seen that, although these terms are used more or less interchangeably in the literature, threshold and counting machines are not the same.
These differences also reflect on the way in which the corresponding complexity classes were defined.
For example, we have clarified the distinction between the counting class
\sharp\mathbf{P}
, on the one hand, and the (equivalent) classes
\mathbf{PP}
, defined by Gill in relation with his PTM, and
k
-
\mathbf{NP}
, introduced by Simon relying on the threshold model, on the other.
