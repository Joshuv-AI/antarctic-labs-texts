Central hypoventilation with hypercapnia: the silent face of ventilatory control
Authors: Izabella M Brito Marques, Mariana Lima Lafetá, Eloara V M Ferreira
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Central hypoventilation arises from impaired neural regulation of ventilation, resulting in inadequate alveolar ventilation to meet metabolic requirements and subsequent carbon dioxide retention. This condition typically manifests without significant parenchymal lung disease, neuromuscular disorders, or mechanical abnormalities of the respiratory system that could otherwise account for hypoventilation. 1 , 2 Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) serves as a valuable diagnostic tool for patients with unexplained dyspnea or hypoxemia, enabling a comprehensive assessment of ventilatory, cardiovascular, and metabolic responses during exercise. 3

OVERVIEW

A 51-year-old non-obese woman (BMI = 23.3 kg/m²) with hypertension and diabetes, treated with losartan, metoprolol, and metformin, presented with a history of two respiratory infections associated with hypoxemia requiring oxygen therapy during hospitalization. In the outpatient setting, she exhibited mild dyspnea and exercise-induced hypoxemia during a two-minute step test (SpO2, 91% at rest and 78% at peak exercise). Spirometry revealed reduced FVC (68% of the predicted value) and FEV1 (1.99 L; 75% predicted), with a normal FEV1/FVC ratio (0.89). Blood workup, chest CT angiography, and microbubble echocardiography were unremarkable. Two years later, CPET demonstrated low VO2peak with a normal anaerobic threshold and oxygen pulse. Peak minute ventilation was low with normal Vt and no change in respiratory rate, accompanied by a reduced Ve/VCO2 slope (Ve/VCO2 = 12) and a progressive increase in end-tidal carbon dioxide pressure (PetCO2) from 44 mmHg at rest to 69 mmHg at peak exercise. Exercise-induced hypoxemia was also observed (SpO2, 95% at rest and 68% at peak exercise), suggesting a ventilatory control disorder (Figure 1A).

Polysomnography identified both central (61 events) and obstructive (353 events) sleep apnea, raising suspicion for a central hypoventilation syndrome. Blood gas analysis revealed PaCO2 of 62 mmHg and bicarbonate of 34 mEq/L. Following initiation of BiPAP therapy, repeat CPET with arterialized earlobe blood gas sampling showed persistent hypoventilation, chronic respiratory acidosis, and hypoxemia, with a normal alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient (Figure 1B).

In patients with unexplained dyspnea and hypoxemia, disorders of ventilatory control should be considered. Hypoventilation syndromes comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by hypercapnia (PaCO2 ≥ 45 mmHg at sea level) due to impaired respiratory homeostasis. The differential diagnosis for central hypoventilation includes congenital central alveolar hypoventilation (Ondine syndrome), idiopathic hypoventilation, and medication-induced causes, such as chronic opioid or benzodiazepine use. Additional considerations include obesity hypoventilation syndrome, neuromuscular dystrophies, post-polio syndrome, and restriction of thoracic or abdominal expansion.

In the present case, the progressive increase in PetCO2 with a normal Vt during exercise was consistent with alveolar hypoventilation secondary to impaired ventilatory control. Pulmonary ventilation is regulated by the respiratory center in the brainstem, which integrates input from central and peripheral chemoreceptors. Central chemoreceptors primarily respond to changes in PaCO2, which is a major determinant of ventilatory drive. 1 , 2 When this stimulus is diminished, alveolar ventilation becomes inadequate to eliminate muscle-derived CO2, resulting in increased PetCO2 in expired air and, with worsening hypoventilation, hypercapnia. 3 , 4

Chronic hypercapnia also induces renal compensatory mechanisms, including increased bicarbonate reabsorption and partial metabolic compensation for respiratory acidosis (Figure 1C). 4

From a pathophysiological perspective, the alveolar gas equation, as follows:

PAO2=[Pb–PH2O]×FiO2–PaCO2/RER

where Pb is barometric pressure, PH2O is water vapor pressure, FiO2 is the fraction of inspired oxygen, and RER is the respiratory exchange ratio, illustrates how these parameters contribute to hypoxemia. Hypoxemia may result from reduced oxygen uptake in the lungs due to environmental factors such as high altitude or low inspired oxygen fraction, as well as from an altered ventilatory response relative to CO2 output. Reduced alveolar ventilation decreases the delivery (minute ventilation) of fresh air to the alveoli, lowering PAO2, reducing oxygen diffusion into the pulmonary capillary blood, and causing a progressive decline in PaO2. In this context, the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient typically remains normal or only mildly elevated, a hallmark of alveolar hypoventilation.

CLINICAL MESSAGE

Disorders of ventilatory control can result in inadequate alveolar ventilation relative to metabolic CO2 production, leading to hypercapnia and hypoxemia even in the absence of structural lung disease. CPET with blood gas analysis may provide critical physiological insights by revealing an inadequate ventilatory response to metabolic demand during exercise. 3
