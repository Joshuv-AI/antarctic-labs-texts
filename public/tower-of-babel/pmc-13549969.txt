Hereditary cancer: Germline testing practices across ERN GENTURIS member countries
Authors: Milena Kiljańczyk, Zanda Daneberga, Mikk Tooming, Katarzyna Urbańczyk, Minna Pöyhönen, Tiina Kahre, Lenka Foretova, Emma Tham, Tamara Milagre, Béla Melegh, Maria K Haanpää, Ana Blatnik, Katharina Wimmer, Robin de Putter, Karin Wadt, Claude Houdayer, Elke Holinski-Feder, Antonis Kattamis, Barbara Klink, Hildegunn Høberg-Vetti, Ignacio Blanco Guillermo, Nicoline Hoogerbrugge, Jan Lubiński
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGermline genetic testing practices for hereditary cancer vary across the European Reference Network on Genetic Tumour Risk Syndromes (ERN GENTURIS) member countries. We surveyed experts in genetic testing from 20 EU member countries and Norway to assess multi-gene panel usage, availability of genome-wide sequencing, first-tier testing approaches, implementation of polygenic risk scores, and the roles of non-genetic healthcare professionals. National experts and members of the ERN GENTURIS completed a structured questionnaire covering founder germline pathogenic variants (gPV) testing, panel testing for common genetic tumour risk syndromes, use of whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing, polygenic risk score implementation, use of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumour samples, laboratory accreditation, and the clinical roles of physicians, genetic counselors and nurses. Significant inter-country heterogeneity was observed. Most countries rely on next-generation sequencing (NGS) multi-gene panels. Founder gPV testing is first-line in a few high-prevalence populations (e.g., BRCA1/2 founders). All 21 countries offer NGS panel tests for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, and ≥19 countries do so for colorectal and prostate cancers. However, NGS panel size and gene composition exhibit substantial variability. WES is available in 12 countries on a routine basis. Most countries implemented genetic testing on stored tumour tissue from deceased patients. In all countries, clinical geneticists can order germline genetic tests, and in 9 countries, any physician can do so. These findings show differences in accessibility to germline genetic testing of hereditary cancer in Europe. We propose EU-wide guidance via pathways, standards of care, and sharing of best practices to further optimize access to hereditary cancer genetic molecular diagnostics.Subject terms: Genetics research, Cancer genetics, Cancer genomics, Genetic association study, Cancer genetics

Introduction

Germline pathogenic variants (gPV) in cancer predisposition genes (such as BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, MSH2, etc.) are causative for various genetic tumour risk syndromes, which not only confer high risks of cancer development, but also make cancer risk reduction possible in relatives, and lead to optimized cancer treatment options for the patient. With the advances of next-generation sequencing (NGS), genetic testing for hereditary breast, ovarian, colorectal, prostate, and other genetic tumour risk syndromes have become increasingly common. However, genetic testing practices are not yet uniform across the European Reference Network on Genetic Tumour Risk Syndromes (ERN GENTURIS) members, reflecting differences in population genetics, healthcare infrastructure, and national guidelines. Recent analyses of genetic testing practices across Europe, including the ESMO study by Bayle et al. [1], have demonstrated substantial between-country inequalities in the availability and real-world accessibility of biomolecular technologies and molecular diagnostics in oncology. These disparities are driven not only by technical capacity but also by reimbursement mechanisms and organizational pathways, and may translate into unequal patient access to guideline-based care. In this context, mapping of germline testing practices for genetic tumour risk syndromes is needed, including first-tier strategies and system-level factors such as test ordering pathways, laboratory standards, and the availability of whole-exome sequencing (WES), whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and polygenic risk scores (PRS) testing.

A few countries, which have a high prevalence of founder gPVs (e.g., characteristic BRCA1/2 variants in Ashkenazi Jewish, Greek, Latvian, Lithuanian, Polish or Portuguese populations) are using targeted founder testing as a first-tier approach, whereas others have moved directly to multigene panel sequencing. The breadth of genes included in routine panels and the adoption of broader genomic tests like WES or WGS vary considerably. Moreover, the integration of emerging tools for the risk assessment of moderate or low risk genetic variants, such as PRS into clinical practice is not implemented, across countries.

Beyond different testing strategies, national healthcare system, resourses, and legal frameworks differ. Countries have diverse policies on whether formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumour tissue from deceased relatives can be tested to identify gPVs. Laboratory standards and accreditation requirements (e.g. ISO 15189 certification) may be mandatory in some jurisdictions and voluntary in others. The right to order genetic testing also varies among countries: in some cases, any physician may order certain genetic tests, while elsewhere, only clinical geneticists have these rights. The role of non-physician professionals (genetic counselors and genetic nurses) is expanding, but their formal recognition, autonomy in test ordering, and training opportunities differ widely across Europe [2].

Our objective was to systematically document differences between ERN GENTURIS members through a comprehensive survey among expert institutes. We aimed to identify gaps and best practices in: (i) first-tier genetic testing strategies (ii) availability of advanced genomic testing (WES/WGS) and PRS in routine care, (iii) laboratory and sample usage policies (use of alternate tissues, accreditation, quality assurance), and (iv) genetic services infrastructure (test ordering permissions and roles of genetic counselors/nurses). By highlighting the current landscape, the ERN GENTURIS [3] seeks to harmonize recommendations for hereditary cancer germline genetic services across Europe, ensuring equitable access to high-quality genetic testing and counseling.

Materials and methods

Survey design

A structured questionnaire was developed by a European collaborative working group on genetic tumour risk syndromes, all members of a Task Force 2 of ERN GENTURIS. The survey comprised multiple-choice and short-answer questions across several domains: 1. availability of founder gPV testing for genetic tumour risk syndromes; 2. use of multigene NGS panel tests for breast/ovarian, colorectal (Lynch syndrome), prostate, and pan-cancer predisposition (including typical gene content); 3.integration of WES/WGS into clinical diagnostics; 4. implementation of PRS analysis in routine care; 5. legal permission and national health coverage for testing FFPE or frozen tumour tissues from deceased relatives or from proband tumours for germline findings; 6. accepted DNA sample types (extracted from blood, buccal swab, saliva, etc) for germline testing in each country; 7. laboratory accreditation and quality assurance requirements (specifically ISO 15189:2022 compliance); 8. policies on tests (clinical geneticists, other specialists, general practitioners); which health professionals can order genetic and 9. roles and recognition of genetic counselors and genetic nurses (including their authority to order tests under supervision, if any). The survey focused on organizational and structural aspects of hereditary cancer genetic services available and reimbursed by public health insurance. One of the main aims was to map what is available and provided within public healthcare; therefore, services offered exclusively by private providers and paid for out-of-pocket by patients were excluded. The detailed mapping of national eligibility criteria for germline testing, such as specific referral thresholds, tumour-based screening algorithms, or formal diagnostic pathways, was not included. In particular, the survey did not include questions on the implementation of universal tumour screening strategies, such as mismatch repair immunohistochemistry (MMR IHC) or microsatellite instability (MSI) testing in colorectal cancer, nor on the precise criteria triggering subsequent germline analysis. The survey also included optional open-text comments on challenges and best practices.

Data collection

The survey targeted all 23 GENTURIS member countries represented in the ERN GENTURIS by full members or affiliated expert centers [1]. All 22 EU member states and Norway were contacted, and 21 provided complete responses. Therefore, the present analysis includes 21 countries (Austria, Belgium, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden).

Each participating country was represented by its designated ERN GENTURIS national representative. Within ERN GENTURIS, national representatives are formally appointed through the network’s governance structure and confirmed by the central European coordinator (Prof. Nicoline Hoogerbrugge), based on recognized expertise in hereditary cancer genetics and formal affiliation of their centre within the network. National representatives are expected to provide an overview of country-level organization of hereditary cancer services, including policies, reimbursement models, regulatory frameworks, and commonly implemented clinical pathways (see Appendix A for the full questionnaire).

Respondents were asked to report national-level practices within the public healthcare framework, rather than institutional practice at their own center only. As germline testing is typically performed by multiple laboratories within each country, national representatives were requested to summarize practice across centers to the best of their knowledge.

The questionnaire was completed based on current national practice, routine clinical pathways, and applicable policy or regulatory frameworks. Clarifications were sought when responses were ambiguous, incomplete, or appeared inconsistent, and follow-up communication was undertaken to ensure interpretative consistency and cross-country comparability. In 2024, the same questionnaire was re-distributed to the same respondents to capture any changes in national practice since the first round. The full wording of the questionnaire is provided in Appendix A to allow readers to assess potential framing effects and ensure transparency.

Analysis

We summarized responses for each question domain, treating each country as a single unit of analysis. As a consequence, within- country regional variation and potential inequities in access could not be assessed. Quantitative data (e.g., number of countries with a given practice) are reported as counts and percentages. Qualitative descriptions (e.g. names of genes in panels, specific legal conditions) were aggregated to identify common themes and notable outliers. We compiled illustrative examples from specific countries to highlight variation. No formal statistical comparisons were made due to the descriptive nature of the study.

Hereditary breast/ovarian cancer (HBOC) panels

All 21 countries offer gene panel testing for HBOC. In 10 countries, the standard panel is relatively small ( < 20 genes), usually centred on high-risk BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2 and TP53, but also including moderate-risk gene CHEK2. In five countries, more extensive panels ( > 20 genes) are used, often adding moderate-risk genes such as ATM, BARD1, RAD51C/D, BRIP1, and others. The remaining countries report variable panel size or lack precise data on gene count. Notably, BRCA1/2 are always included, and most panels include at least the core genes PALB2 and CHEK2.

Colorectal cancer panels

Germline panel testing for Lynch syndrome (and other colorectal cancer predispositions) is available in 20 of 21 countries (all except Greece). Eight countries use focused panels primarily containing the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) and sometimes EPCAM deletions, as well as a few polyposis genes such as APC and MUTYH. Another 6 countries employ broader colorectal panels ( ≥ 20 genes) that include Lynch genes and additional genes for polyposis and other syndromes that may predispose to colorectal cancer. The remaining countries did not specify panel details. All panels universally include the four major MMR genes. Although respondents reported broad alignment with international recommendations (e.g., ESMO, NCCN), a structured comparison of national tumour screening algorithms (such as universal MMR IHC/MSI testing in colorectal cancer) or detailed referral criteria for germline testing was not performed. Some countries explicitly integrate genes for adenomatous polyposis (e.g., APC) and hamartomatous polyposis in the same test, whereas others keep Lynch testing separate from polyposis testing.

Prostate cancer panels

19 countries offer germline panel testing for familial prostate cancer (excluding Greece and Lithuania). These panels typically include BRCA2 (considered a high-priority gene for aggressive prostate cancer), and often BRCA1, ATM, CHEK2, PALB2, and NBN, among others. In the survey, the question on availability of germline panel testing in prostate cancer for patients meeting national clinical and/or familial criteria was included; however, the framework of guidelines or detailed eligibility thresholds each country applies were not included. The analysis indicated that criteria are broadly similar across respondent countries and generally follow international guidelines (e.g., ESMO/NCCN), with minor local adaptations (e.g., requiring ≥2 prostate cancer cases in a family, considering aggregation through the maternal line in families overlapping with HBOC, or allowing testing in a single early-onset case). Seven countries use smaller panels ( < 20 genes) that usually centre on BRCA2 and a few additional genes. Six countries have more extensive prostate panels (adding genes like HOXB13 or common risk SNPs such as 8q24 if applicable). Five countries did not provide specific composition data. For the gene HOXB13, associated with hereditary prostate cancer, however, the cumulative lifetime risk has not yet been established and this gene is not yet included in most countries’ clinical guidelines.

Other genetic tumour risk syndromes or Pan-Cancer Panels: Sixteen countries (about 76%) have introduced broad “pan-cancer” gene panels that cover a wide spectrum of hereditary cancer genes in one test. The questionnaire primarily captured the availability of pan-cancer panels and did not require respondents to report the exact number of genes included. Where respondents voluntarily provided panel size, these values are reported in Table 1 (e.g., “up to 226 genes”, “16–46 genes”), while for other countries panel size was not specified. These panels are typically used when phenotype overlaps or if patients present with rare cancers that are suggestive of multiple syndromes.

First-tier testing strategies

Founder gPV testing

A minority of countries use targeted founder gPV tests as the first-tier approach in hereditary cancer genetics. Notably, Greece, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland and Portugal rely on founder-panel testing for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer [4, 5]. In Poland, an initial panel of five recurrent BRCA1 pathogenic variants is commonly used as a rapid first-tier test, as three major founder variants account for approximately 86% of all BRCA1/2 gPVs identified in the Polish population [6] This targeted approach enables rapid identification of carriers, with turnaround times often as short as three days, before proceeding to broader sequencing when clinically indicated.

Portugal similarly begins testing with a targeted approach. Three founder BRCA1/2 gPVs account for approximately 50% of variants identified in high-risk Portuguese families [7, 8]. The BRCA2 Alu insertion, unique to Portugal, alone accounts for one-third of all identified cases [9–11] National guidelines in Portugal recommend starting with this founder screen before proceeding to broader testing.

Lithuania historically used an eight-variant BRCA1/2 founder panel that captured approximately 86% of BRCA1 gPVs identified in the Lithuanian population, although testing strategies have more recently transitioned towards broader sequencing-based approaches [12, 13].

Latvia applies a founder-based testing strategy initially focused on screening for the three most common BRCA1 gPVs, two of which account for more than 80% of BRCA1 variants identified in the Latvian population [14–16]. More recent studies have also identified additional recurrent BRCA1/2 variants, including a relatively frequent BRCA1 missense gPV [16, 17]. Since 2024, the Latvian founder panel has been expanded to include 20 recurrent BRCA1/2 gPVs [18–22].

Large-scale testing initiatives have also illustrated the efficiency of founder-variant strategies in populations with strong founder effects. For example, in Poland more than 500,000 individuals have been tested for recurrent BRCA1 variants since the late 1990s, leading to the identification of over 9000 carriers. These data highlight the high diagnostic yield of founder-based approaches in appropriate populations.

Utilization of WES and WGS in clinical testing

The adoption of WES and WGS for germline genetic diagnostics of hereditary cancer varies. Overall, 12 of 21 countries (57%) report that WES is offered in the routine clinical setting. In most countries, WES or WGS functions primarily as a sequencing approach. In ERN GENTURIS member countries, WES is typically used for complex cases where panel testing was uninformative, or for phenotypes suggestive of very rare genes not covered on standard panels [23].

By contrast, WES remains unavailable or not covered by the national healthcare service in at least 6 ERN GENTURIS member countries.

The emerging use of WGS reflects its higher cost and data interpretation burden compared to WES [24, 25]. Therefore, WGS is even less commonly used: only 6 countries use WGS in routine diagnostics for cancer patients, and in those it is often limited to specific scenarios or pilot programs. Even among adopters, WGS is usually restricted to selected cases (for example, exploring non-coding variants in high-risk families after negative WES, or concurrent comparison of tumour DNA and germline DNA sequencing for rare cancer presentations).

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) in clinical use

The use of PRS - aggregate scores of multiple common low or moderate risk genetic variants associated with cancer risk - is minimal in current clinical practice [26–29]. Nineteen out of 21 (90%) ERN GENTURIS member countries reported that PRS testing is not part of routine care for hereditary cancer risk assessment.

Clinical implementation was reported only in Germany and Austria, where PRS is used for breast cancer risk estimation. No country indicated incorporation of PRS as a mandatory or universally recommended component of national hereditary cancer testing guidelines.

Use of FFPE tumour tissue for germline testing

We investigated the use of testing tumour tissue (especially archived pathology specimens) to infer germline gPVs when conventional testing of a living individual or recently obtained sample is not possible. This scenario commonly arises when a patient has died but their preserved tumour tissue (often FFPE blocks) could be tested to inform surviving relatives, or when only tumour tissue is available from an index case and a germline origin needs confirmation [30, 31].

Legal permission

Such testing of deceased individuals’ tissues is legally permitted in 19 out of 21 countries ( ~ 90%). In most of the countries, there are provisions allowing analysis of stored pathological specimens for hereditary gPV testing with appropriate consent (usually from next of kin or by satisfying certain conditions). Likewise, testing an index patient’s own tumour to look for germline gPVs (for example, sequencing tumour DNA and then checking if a pathogenic variant is constitutional) is widely allowed (20/21 countries, ~95%).

Germline testing materials and laboratory practices

All respondents indicated that their laboratories primarily use a peripheral blood sample as the standard for germline testing, which is expected, as blood provides high-quality DNA and is considered the gold-standard sample. However, the survey also proves the acceptance of less invasive or alternate sample types in the routine clinical practice:

Buccal swabs and saliva

These are widely accepted biological materials. Buccal swabs are used for germline testing in 19 of 21 countries (90.5%), and saliva in 16 of 21 (76.2%). Thus, the vast majority have incorporated buccal swabs or saliva collection kits in routine practice, which can facilitate accessibility of testing in remote regions, pediatric cases, or for individuals averse to phlebotomy.

Skin fibroblasts

Fibroblast cultures are used in germline testing if a mosaic condition is suspected or if a patient has a hematological malignancy. Fibroblasts are accepted as testing material in 19 of 21 countries (90.5%) when indicated. This suggests widespread capability to perform tissue culturing and highlights that specialized protocols exist in many labs for unusual scenarios (e.g. suspicion of mosaic neurofibromatosis or other genetic tumour risk syndromes).

Other tissues

Several countries reported utilization of more unconventional specimens on a case-by-case basis. These include fresh or fresh-frozen surgical specimens, hair follicles (as a source of DNA when blood is not usable), nail clippings, bone marrow, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-transformed lymphocyte cell lines, and FFPE or fresh-frozen tumour tissues (discussed above).

Laboratory accreditation status

The survey inquired information about accreditation status of molecular genetic testing laboratories, specifically ISO 15189:2022 (Medical laboratories – requirements for quality and competence), which is the international standard for all medical laboratories including those providing genetic testing for clinical use [32, 33].

Out of 21 countries, 11 indicated that ISO 15189 accreditation of genetic testing labs is mandatory by law or national policy. These include Austria, Belgium, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Hungary, Latvia, the Netherlands, Poland, Spain. In the remaining countries, accreditation is either not compulsory or the responses implied it is “recommended/expected” but not strictly mandated by authorities. Slovenia noted a somewhat different system: rather than ISO 15189 per se, labs undergo a mandatory quality assessment that aligns with similar principles (effectively an alternative national accreditation).

Quality assurance practices

Even where formal accreditation isn’t mandated, all countries reported participation in external quality assurance (EQA) schemes for genetic testing. EQA (or proficiency testing) involves laboratories periodically testing blinded samples for specific genes or variants to ensure accuracy and clinical interpretation of results. Many respondents referenced their enrolment in European EQA programs (for example, schemes provided by the European Molecular Genetics Quality Network, EMQN). This indicates a broad commitment to quality, even in the absence of formal accreditation. However, accreditation encompasses more than EQA - it covers staffing qualifications and competence, validation processes, result reporting standards, and other related aspects.

Roles of healthcare professionals in genetic testing

Ordering of genetic tests by physicians

Although national regulations differ regarding who may request germline genetic testing in the clinical setting, certain common practices have become apparent.

Beyond clinical geneticists, the policies diverge

In 9 of 21 countries, any licensed physician (regardless of specialty) may order at least certain germline genetic tests. In practice, however, general practitioners or non-specialists seldom order complex germline cancer genetic tests independently; they tend to refer patients to specialists. For instance, Belgium technically allows all doctors to request tests, but guidelines suggest that non-geneticists should involve a genetic centre for predictive testing. Sweden, Finland and Norway also permit broad physician ordering, and these countries have well-established frameworks that support pre-test counseling with informational material and/or genetic counselors if a non-geneticist orders a test.

Most countries indicated that specific medical specialties - such as medical oncologists, breast surgeons, gynecologists, or gastroenterologists - are authorized to request germline genetic testing for patients under their care, typically in the context of diagnostic testing for cancer patients (a practice often referred to as “mainstreaming”). A nearly universal principle across ERN GENTURIS member countries is that predictive or presymptomatic testing (i.e., genetic testing in an unaffected individual to estimate future cancer risk, such as BRCA1/2 testing in a healthy relative) remains under the supervision of a clinical geneticist. In 20 out of 21 ERN GENTURIS member countries, predictive testing is conducted only after formal genetic counseling and typically under the oversight of a clinical geneticist. In all surveyed countries, clinical geneticists (MDs specializing in genetics) have the right to order both diagnostic (for patients with cancer or symptoms) and predictive (for presymptomatic at-risk relatives) genetic tests. In other countries, other medical specialists are authorized to order such tests. Availability and recognition of genetic counselors (GCs) and nurses

Out of 21 ERN GENTURIS member countries, approximately half have formally recognised the profession of genetic counsellor (GC) and/or genetic nurse, meaning that the role is defined nationally, often with a dedicated training programme and/or certification. Countries such as Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Slovenia, and Sweden report genetic counselors and/or genetic nurses in clinical practice, with at least some official recognition within their healthcare systems; however, formal regulation and scope of practice still vary across countries.

Even in countries with genetic counselors, most operate under the supervision of clinical geneticists or within multidisciplinary teams rather than independently. Only a few countries grant GCs a higher degree of autonomy.

Discussion

Genetic testing availability is a crucial component for timely diagnosis, therapy choice, risk-specific surveillance and reproductive decision-making in genetic tumour risk syndromes. The rapid advances in genetic testing techniques underscore a continuous need for situational analysis regarding the availability of testing for genetic tumour risk syndromes across the EU. Our study provides contemporary data on testing availability from the perspective of ERN GENTURIS member countries experts.

The ESMO study (Bayle et al., 2023) provided a pan-European assessment of biomolecular technology availability and accessibility, demonstrating that although basic single-gene techniques are widely available, access to advanced approaches (including large NGS panels and comprehensive genomic profiling) remains highly heterogeneous and often restricted in routine practice. Importantly, their analysis highlights the gap between technical availability and real-world accessibility, with reimbursement and organizational factors repeatedly emerging as key barriers.

In line with this access-oriented perspective, our survey suggests that comparable structural inequities extend to germline testing for hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes. Beyond differences in first-tier testing strategies and gene content, several system-level levers identified here - test ordering pathways (including mainstreaming), formal recognition and supervised autonomy of genetic counselors and nurses, laboratory accreditation requirements (e.g., ISO 15189), and policies enabling the use of alternative materials such as FFPE tissue or tissue from deceased individuals - may materially influence timely identification of clinically actionable germline variants, cascade testing, and implementation of tailored surveillance and prevention.

In our study, the observed cross-country differences in the availability of germline genetic testing for rare and complex disorders such as genetic tumour risk syndromes show diversity in access among individuals and families at high hereditary risk of cancer across ERN GENTURIS member countries [3]. In practical terms, a person’s country of residence determines whether a clinically actionable gPV is identified in time to inform surveillance, risk-reducing interventions and tailored therapy. Our cross-country analysis of expert centres? indicates several differences.

First, the technological approaches employed are inconsistent. While multigene NGS panels are routinely utilized, the specific genes analyzed - particularly those associated with moderate-risk - vary considerably, already in the variant reporting level, contributing to diagnostic disparities.

Second, first-tier testing strategies remain heterogeneous. In a minority of countries where founder effects are present, a targeted founder approach can function as an efficient and equitable gateway to care: a small set of recurrent variants captures a large fraction of carriers, enabling rapid, affordable ascertainment and broad eligibility for testing [34]. However, it is important that families with suspected hereditary tumour predisposition are offered add-on testing with wider gene panels if the initial founder test is negative.

Third, processes and governance for these rare diseases, which genetic tumour risk syndromes are, need harmonization, specifically for complex technology using DNA from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded or fresh frozen tissue, including tissue from deceased individuals, to reliably clarify germline status. Additionally, while laboratory participation in external quality assessment is widespread, mandatory ISO 15189 (or equivalent) accreditation is not yet universal. In parallel, mainstreaming policies and the formal roles of genetic counselors and genetic nurses in addition to clinical geneticists differ and require optimization.

The role of PRS in cancer risk assessment remains limited in routine clinical practice across ERN GENTURIS member countries. In our dataset, only Germany and Austria reported clinical implementation of PRS, restricted to breast cancer risk assessment, while other countries did not report routine use. Although several professional bodies have published position statements or guidance documents on PRS, formal integration into national cancer testing guidelines remains heterogeneous and, in many jurisdictions, cautious or absent. Implementation of PRS requires robust governance frameworks, population-specific validation, clear communication strategies, and careful consideration of equity and transferability across ancestries. The evolving and variable nature of recommendations in this area likely contributes to the currently limited and uneven uptake observed across ERN GENTURIS member countries.

The results indicate an improvement in general availability of NGS technique across countries compared to the Europe-wide assessment of biomolecular technology availability and accessibility reported by Bayle et al. [1], yet they still highlight significant differences among countries. While this study did not analyse the reasons for disparities in the choice of testing technique, number of tested genes, and policies for used type of biological materials, the results clearly outline inequalities in testing availability, which directly affect timely diagnosis, treatment, and identification of high-risk gPV carriers.

The quality of testing and results interpretation is another crucial aspect that our study addresses. Although several disease-specific and general genetic testing guidelines are available at the European level, data on their implementation at the national level are scarce or not well visible internationally.

Strengths of this study include its geographic breadth (21 ERN GENTURIS member countries), its focus on real-world organizational practice across technology, quality assurance, and expert input from the ERN GENTURIS network. The national representative model allows identification of system-level factors shaping the germline testing availability and organization, rather than isolated institutional practices.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study relied on self-reported national practices provided by designated national representatives. Although respondents were asked to summarize country-level policies within the public healthcare framework, intra-country regional variation and institutional heterogeneity may not be fully represented. Secondly, genetic testing technologies and policies evolve rapidly; thus, the landscape may already have changed since the survey was conducted in 2023 and 2024. Also, the exact number of genes included in pan-cancer panels was not collected, restricting formal comparison of panel extent.

The survey focused primarily on structural availability rather than detailed clinical pathways. We did not conduct a systematic comparative analysis of national eligibility criteria, referral thresholds, or tumour testing algorithms. This includes, for example, implementation of universal MMR IHC/MSI testing in colorectal cancer subsequent germline testing referral. Similarly, for prostate cancer, we assessed availability of panel testing but did not collect structured data on country-specific guideline sources or detailed eligibility thresholds.

Importantly, consistent with the distinction between availability and accessibility, the quantitative health-system metrics such as number of laboratories, regional coverage indicators, waiting times, reimbursement mechanisms, or funding differences were not collected. Therefore, the centralization, capacity constraints, or within- and between-country access inequities were not analysed. Finally, the study did not include patient-level outcome data (e.g., stage at diagnosis, cascade testing uptake, or preventive intervention rates), which would be required to assess the clinical impact of observed structural differences.

The findings point to the critical need for pragmatic standards and pathways that ERN GENTURIS could lead in collaboration with other European societies, such as ESMO.

Conclusion

Optimizing equal access to germline testing for rare genetic tumour risk syndromes is the necessary next step to achieve timely diagnosis, prevention, and tailored care for individuals at high hereditary risk of cancer across ERN GENTURIS member countries [3]. We recommend a coordinated, ERN GENTURIS - led harmonization of standards and clinical pathways to ensure that comprehensive molecular germline genetic testing is universally available when clinically indicated. Taking this action will significantly narrow inequities, increase identification of at-risk families, and accelerate the delivery of precision cancer prevention throughout the continent.
