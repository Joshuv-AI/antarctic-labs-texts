The effect of adding different proportions of black wolfberry residues to the diet on the production performance, egg quality and fecal microorganisms of laying hens
Authors: Youli Yao, Haifeng Tong, Peilin Ye, Rong Li, Yong Shi, Guofang Wu, Xuan Luo, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study investigated the impacts of adding varying proportions of black wolfberry residues to the diet on the production performance, egg quality, and intestinal health of Hy-Line Brown laying hens. A total of 600 healthy 20‑week‑old hens were randomly allocated to three groups (10 replicates/group, 20 hens/replicate) and fed either a basal diet (CK) or a basal diet supplemented with 1.0% (T1) or 1.5% (T2) black wolfberry residues for 60 days. Measurements included growth performance (feed intake, egg production, feed conversion ratio), egg quality (yolk color, Haugh unit, eggshell strength), egg nutrients (amino acids, total cholesterol, minerals Na, P, Ca, Fe, Zn, Se, and vitamins VA, VD₃, VE, VB₂, VB₉), fatty acid profile, and cecal microbiota (16S rRNA sequencing). The results showed that black wolfberry residues significantly increased average daily feed intake (P < 0.01). In the T2 group, yolk color and total amino acid content in eggs significantly increased (P < 0.01), while total cholesterol content significantly decreased (P < 0.01). At 30 days, compared with the CK group, the T1 group increased the contents of P, Ca, Fe, Zn, Se and VB2, decreased the Na content in eggs (P < 0.01). Meanwhile the T2 group increased the contents of Na, P, K, and Se in eggs (P < 0.01). At 60 days, both test groups had significantly increased Na and K contents (P < 0.01), but significantly decreased P, Ca, Fe, Zn, and VD3 contents in eggs (P < 0.01). Compared with the CK group, the T1 group showed significantly increased methyl oleate content and decreased levels of palmitic acid, stearic acid, linoleic acid, and arachidonic acid in eggs at all measured time points. In contrast, the T2 group elevated both methyl oleate and DHA contents, while reducing the levels of myristic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, and arachidonic acid in eggs. 16S rRNA sequencing analysis indicated that adding black wolfberry residues enhanced the richness of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, and reduced the relative abundance of Fusobacteria. In conclusion, dietary supplementation with black wolfberry residues, particularly at 1.5%, improved egg quality by increasing yolk color, total amino acids, DHA, and minerals (Se, Zn) while reducing total cholesterol. These changes suggest enhanced antioxidant capacity and potential nutritional benefits for consumers, such as improved dietary intake of essential micronutrients. Furthermore, modulation of cecal microbiota (increased beneficial Romboutsia, decreased Fusobacterium) may contribute to the observed effects on egg composition.

Introduction

Qinghai, as one of the main wolfberry production areas in China, has an annual wolfberry output of 8.27 × 104 t (Luo, 2019). However, large amounts of wolfberry residues are generated during harvesting and processing. Currently, this residue is not effectively utilized and is often discarded, leading to resource wastage and environmental pollution. Therefore, exploring the development of wolfberry residue as an unconventional feed resource can not only achieve resource utilization of waste, but also effectively alleviate the shortage of feed resources, providing a new path for the sustainable development of animal husbandry.

Black wolfberry fruit exhibits biological activity due to its rich content of various nutrients (anthocyanins, carotenoids, various vitamins, and eight essential amino acids for the human body) (Chen et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2019). Studies have shown that the total amino acid content in black wolfberries is as high as 4.23 g/100 g, among which the aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and proline contents are particularly prominent (Ding et al., 2016b). These nutrients are not only indispensable components for growth and development but also play a crucial role in maintaining normal physiological functions and improving the growth performance of animals.

“Studies have demonstrated that black wolfberry possesses multiple health benefits, including anti‑tumor, anti‑oxidation, prevention of hypertension, cardiovascular protection, and skin‑beautifying effects (Guo et al., 2020). These findings suggest that black wolfberry may also confer beneficial effects on the health and performance of laying hens, although direct evidence in poultry is still limited. Notably, black wolfberry has an extremely powerful ability to eliminate free radicals, which is 20 times that of VC and 50 times that of VE, making it a natural and highly efficient antioxidant (Liu et al., 2013; Peng et al., 2014; Song et al., 2014; Zhan et al., 2018). In addition, its extracts (such as flavonoids and high-purity anthocyanins) perform excellently in reducing blood lipids, and its effect is even better than that of simvastatin, a commonly used clinical drug. Extracts, such as purified anthocyanins, can not only significantly inhibit the inflammatory response and improve the symptoms of atherosclerosis, but also effectively reduce weight by regulating the fatty acid metabolism pathway (Wang et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019; Zhao, 2020).

Studies have found that black wolfberries collected from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau not only possess rich nutrients, phenols, polysaccharides and betaine, but also exhibit significantly higher antioxidant activity compared to other regions (Chi et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2020). Research data shows that the total flavonoid content (11.09 mg CAE/g) and condensed tannin content (20.87 mg CAE/g) of black wolfberries are much higher than those of red wolfberries (2.83 mg CAE/g and 1.08 mg CAE/g respectively) (Islam et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2022). Studies have confirmed that after obese mice were fed anthocyanin derivatives, their body weight decreased significantly, the secretion levels of adiponectin and leptin increased remarkably, and the content of white adipose tissue in their bodies decreased significantly (Yin and Wu, 2017). This finding reveals the crucial role of the bioactive components in black wolfberries in regulating lipid metabolism and controlling body weight.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the potential biological activities of black wolfberry leaves, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, gastrointestinal regulatory, hypoglycemic, anti-fatigue, radioprotective, immunomodulatory, and toxicological effects. (Liu et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). Moreover, black wolfberry leaf extracts exhibited better antioxidant activity than the other extracts, indicating that the extracts from the leaves and stems are also rich sources of polyphenols, flavonoids, and condensed tannins (Kumar et al., 2022). However, most of these studies were conducted using purified extracts in cell or rodent models, and the direct application of black wolfberry residue (a processing byproduct that still contains fruit and leaf components) in poultry feeding has received little attention. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate, for the first time, the effects of dietary black wolfberry residue (at 1.0% and 1.5%) on production performance, egg quality (including physical properties, amino acids, total cholesterol, minerals, vitamins, and fatty acids), and cecal microbiota in Hy-Line Brown laying hens over a 60-day feeding period. Exploring the application of black wolfberry residues in feed is of practical significance for improving egg production and quality and enhancing the overall value of livestock products.

Materials and methods

Animals, diets and experimental design

A total of 600 20-week-age Hy - Line brown laying hens were randomly divided into three groups, with 10 replicates in each treatment group and 20 laying hens in each replicate. The CK group served as the control group and was fed a basal diet; the T1 and T2 group were fed a basal diet supplemented with 1.0% or 1.5% black wolfberry residues, respectively. Black wolfberry residues were provided by Xinlong Village, Balong Township, Dulan County, (Qinghai, China). The residues were analyzed for proximate composition (dry matter, crude protein, crude fiber, ether extract, ash, calcium, phosphorus) and total polyphenols/anthocyanins (detailed in Supplementary Table S1). The experimental animals and experimental site were provided by the Qinghai Shangen Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Technology Co. Ltd. The composition and nutritional value of the basic feed formula are listed in Table 1. The entire experiment lasted for 60 days. The light cycle was set as 16L:8D, and the appropriate temperature (20-24°C), humidity (50%−65%) and good ventilation in the chicken coop were maintained. Feed was provided twice daily (07:30 and 17:00) using a two‑step procedure: each morning, the orts (leftover feed) from the previous day were collected and weighed. The total feed offered during the day was recorded, and daily feed consumption per replicate was calculated as. Weekly feed intake was summed and divided by the number of hens in the replicate and by the number of days to obtain average daily feed intake (ADFI). Daily at 09:00, the number of eggs laid in each replicate was counted on a hen‑day basis. All eggs from each replicate were collectively weighed using an electronic scale (model JY10002, Ohaus, USA; accuracy 0.1 g) to calculate the average egg weight per replicate. Defective eggs (cracked, soft‑shelled, misshapen, or otherwise abnormal) were counted daily per replicate, and their percentage relative to total eggs was calculated. Mortality was recorded as it occurred, and mortality rate was calculated at the end of the trial. Hen‑day egg production (%) was calculated as (total eggs recorded / number of hens alive that day) × 100. Egg mass (g/hen/day) was derived as (egg production % × average egg weight) / 100. All data were recorded daily and aggregated weekly for statistical analysis. Sample collection and detection

Production performance measurement

At the beginning and end of the experiment, the laying hens were weighed. The feed intake, egg production and egg weight of each replicate were recorded daily. The average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), average number of eggs laid, total egg weight, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated. Egg quality determination

On the 30th and 60th days of the formal test period, 15 eggs with similar shapes and without eggshell contamination or damage were selected from each replicate. Egg quality measurements were carried out within 12 h. The transverse and longitudinal diameter of the eggs were measured with a vernier caliper (SYEETH, Liaoning, China), and the egg shape index was calculated. The eggshell strength was measured using an eggshell strength tester (RH-DQ200, Guangzhou, China). The color grade of the yolk was determined using a Roche colorimeter (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). Finally, a piece of eggshell was taken from the blunt end, middle part, and pointed end of the eggshell respectively to measure the eggshell thickness. The calculation formula is as follows:Egg shape index = egg length (mm) / egg width (mm).

Egg shape index = egg length (mm) / egg width (mm).

The various egg quality indicators were determined by Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All egg quality analyses were conducted following the protocols detailed below, with specific attention to the use of either whole egg, yolk, or albumen as specified for each parameter.

Crude Protein: Crude protein content was determined in whole egg samples according to the Chinese National Standard Method GB 5009.5-2016 (Kjeldahl method). Briefly, approximately 0.5 g of homogenized sample was digested with 10 g of a catalyst mixture (copper sulfate and potassium sulfate) and 20 mL of sulfuric acid. The digest was distilled, and the liberated ammonia was collected in a boric acid solution and titrated with 0.05 mol/L hydrochloric acid. The nitrogen content was converted to crude protein using a conversion factor of 6.25.

Total Amino Acids (TAA): TAA content was measured in whole egg samples using a microplate method with a commercially available kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). In brief, 0.1 g of sample was homogenized in 1 mL of provided extraction buffer and boiled for 15 min. After centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C), the supernatant was reacted with a chromogenic agent (containing ninhydrin) at 100°C for 15 min. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm. A standard curve was prepared using a cysteine standard (10 µmol/mL), and the total amino acid content was calculated and expressed as µmol/g of fresh whole egg.

Amino Acid Profile: The profile of individual amino acids was analyzed in yolk samples using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC1200, Agilent, USA) with a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 0.5 μm) and VWD detection at 360 nm. Sample preparation involved hydrolysis of 0.1 g of yolk with 2 mL of 6 mol/L HCl at 105°C for 24 h, followed by neutralization with 6 mol/L NaOH and derivatization with DNFB (2,4-dinitrofluorobenzene) at 60°C for 60 min in the dark. The final solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before HPLC analysis. The mobile phase consisted of a gradient of A (acetonitrile:methanol=90:10) and B (0.02 mol/L sodium dihydrogen phosphate-disodium hydrogen phosphate) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Concentrations were quantified against external standards.

Total Cholesterol (TC): TC content was determined in yolk samples using a microplate-based enzymatic colorimetric assay kit. Briefly, 0.1 g of yolk was homogenized in 1 mL of isopropanol (extraction solution) and centrifuged (10,000 g, 10 min, 4°C). The supernatant was then reacted with a working solution containing cholesterol esterase and cholesterol oxidase at 37°C for 15 min. The absorbance of the resulting quinone pigment was measured at 500 nm. A standard curve was established using serially diluted cholesterol standards (0.078 – 2.5 µmol/mL). The TC concentration was calculated and expressed as µmol/g of fresh yolk.

Mineral Elements: Mineral analysis was performed on liquid egg samples using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (PE NexION 300D ICP-MS, PerkinElmer, USA). Approximately 1.4 g of liquid egg was accurately weighed into a PTFE microwave digestion vessel and pre-digested with 10 mL of ultrapure concentrated nitric acid at 120°C for 20 min. Following pre-digestion, a further 5 mL of nitric acid was added, and the vessel was sealed for microwave digestion. After digestion, the solution was heated to 180°C to evaporate the acid to approximately 1 mL. The concentrate was then diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water, filtered (0.22 μm), and analyzed by ICP-MS.

Fat-Soluble Vitamins (VA, VD₃, VE): Fat-soluble vitamins were analyzed in yolk samples using HPLC (HPLC1200, Agilent, USA) equipped with a DAD detector and a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at 30°C. The sample (2 g) was saponified with 10 mL ethanol, 0.1 g BHT, 1 g ascorbic acid, and 15 mL 50% KOH at 80°C for 30 min in the dark. The saponified solution was extracted three times with petroleum ether. The combined extract was washed with water, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted in 1 mL of methanol, filtered (0.22 μm), and analyzed. The mobile phase was 100% methanol at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, and detection was performed at 300 nm.

Water-Soluble Vitamins (VB₂, VB₉): VB₂ and VB₉ were analyzed in yolk and whole egg samples, respectively, using HPLC.

VB₂ (yolk): Samples (1 g) were extracted with 3 mL ultrapure water and 2.5% perchloric acid, sonicated for 30 min, and centrifuged (4000 rpm, 10 min). The supernatant was filtered (0.45 μm) and analyzed using a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) with VWD detection at 266 nm. The mobile phase was a gradient of A (0.05 mol/L KH₂PO₄, pH 6.1) and B (methanol) at 0.8 mL/min.

VB₉ (yolk): Samples (0.5 g) were autoclaved with 8 mL of 0.1 mol/L HCl at 121°C for 30 min. After cooling and adjusting to pH 6.0-6.5 with 1 mol/L NaOH, the sample was incubated with an enzyme mixture at 37°C overnight. The digest was then filtered (0.22 μm) and analyzed using the same HPLC conditions as for VB₂.

Fatty Acid Profile: The fatty acid profile was determined in yolk samples using gas chromatography (GC6890, Agilent, USA). Lipids were extracted by vortexing 0.5 g of yolk with 3 mL of chloroform-methanol, followed by sonication at 50°C for 30 min. The extract was washed with saturated NaCl solution, and the organic phase was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and evaporated under nitrogen. The extracted fat was methylated with 5 mL hexane and 3 mL CH₃OH-KOH (0.5 mol/L) at 60°C for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was analyzed on an Agilent DB-23 column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The GC was operated with a temperature program starting at 50°C (1 min), ramping to 175°C at 25°C/min, then to 230°C at 4°C/min (hold 10 min). Fatty acids were identified and quantified by comparison with external standards.

Quality Control: All analyses were performed in duplicate. Results are expressed as mean values. For chromatographic methods (HPLC, GC, ICP-MS), peaks were identified by comparing retention times with known standards. In all analytical runs, standard curves were used for quantification. Values below the limit of detection are denoted as "N.D." as indicated in the original test reports.

Bacterial community analyses

Fecal samples were collected on day 30 and day 60 of the experiment, pre-processed, and stored at −80°C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis. After the total DNA of the sample was extracted, primers were designed according to the conserved region, and sequencing joints were added to the end of the primers, PCR amplification was performed, and the products were purified, quantified and homogenized to form sequencing libraries. The constructed libraries were first inspected, and qualified libraries were sequenced by Illumina Nova-Seq 6000. The primer sequences of the V3 + V4 region were F: ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA; R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT. First, Trimoraic v0.33 software was used to filter the sequenced Raw Reads. The cut adapt 1.9.1 software was used to identify and remove the primer sequences to obtain Clean Reads that did not contain the primer sequences, and the dada2 method in QIIME2 2020.6 was used to denoise and remove the chimeric sequences to obtain the final effective data.

Statistical analyses

SPSS software (version 20.0) was used for data processing and statistical analysis. One-way ANOVA was used to test and analyze the significance of the differences (P < 0.05). Microbial community data was completed using the Wekemo Bioincloud (https://www.bioincloud.tech).

Discussion

Study on the production performance of laying hens with different proportions of black wolfberry residues

Production performance is the main index for evaluating the benefits of laying hens. Improving the egg production rate of laying hens in the later stages of egg production through feeding and nutritional factors will effectively prolong the egg production cycle and greatly improve profit and competitiveness. Unconventional feed can effectively improve the production performance of laying hens (feed conversion rate, egg production rate, and egg quality). The efficient application of unconventional feed is of great significance for improving the efficiency of laying hens and promoting the sustainable development of feed resources. In the present study, dietary supplementation with black wolfberry residue significantly increased average daily feed intake (ADFI) in both T1 and T2 groups compared with CK, but did not significantly affect egg production rate or feed conversion ratio (FCR). The increase in ADFI may be attributed to the palatability of the residue or a mild stimulatory effect of its bioactive compounds (polyphenols, polysaccharides) on feed intake. Similar observations have been reported for Lycium barbarum polysaccharides in laying hens (Wu et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020). The addition of black wolfberries to the diet did not have a positive effect on the production performance of laying hens. It may be that the additive raw materials experienced high-temperature pressing during processing, resulting in denaturation, loss, or weakening of active ingredients that can effectively promote the production performance of laying hens.

Study on the quality of eggs with different proportions of black wolfberry residues

The external quality of eggs, such as egg weight, egg color, and egg shape, are the most important factors that affect consumers when purchasing eggs, while indicators such as egg white height, egg yolk color, and egg yolk ratio are indicators for evaluating egg freshness, shelf life, and protein content in egg white (Ammar et al., 2021; Kwame et al., 2021). The Haugh unit is a measure of egg freshness, which is related to shelf life (Özek et al., 2011), and the shell thickness is related to the proportion of eggs damaged during transportation and handling (Anderson et al., 2004). The significantly higher yolk color score in T2 group (Table 3) is directly linked to the carotenoids and anthocyanins present in black wolfberry residue. These pigments are deposited in the yolk and contribute to its color.In this experiment, at 30th days, the protein height of the two groups was lower than that of the control group, but higher than that of the CK group at 60th days, and with time changes, the egg yolk color of the three groups became lower, and the egg yolk color of T2 group was significantly higher than that of the T1group (P < 0.01), indicating that feeding time will affect the protein height, and different egg-laying days affect the egg yolk color. In a study conducted by Long et al. (2016), 14% high-level double-low rapeseed meal was added to the diet, the egg yolk color value was significantly reduced, indicating that the level of double-low rapeseed meal significantly affected egg yolk color. This may be related to the type, quality, and quantity of fat in the diet and its oxidative status affecting coloring. In addition, studies have shown that hens usually have lower egg weights at the beginning of production, and the weight increases with age. Generally, the speed slows after 60 days, reaching a limit value of approximately 300 days (Ketelaere et al., 2002). Egg quality is related to protein viscosity. The higher the viscosity, the higher the Haugh unit, and the better the protein quality. Generally, Haugh units higher than 72 are classified as AA and eggs from 62 to 72 are classified as A (Zhang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2012). In this experiment, while the addition of black wolfberry residues didn't exert significant impacts on Haugh units and egg shape index (SI), it still ensured that Haugh units consistently stayed above 88%, This indicates its potential stabilizing influence on egg quality - related indices.

Effects of different proportions of black wolfberry residues on the content of amino acids, crude protein and total cholesterol in eggs of laying hens

As the main nutritional tonic ingredient of functional products, the analysis of black wolfberry content and components is important for monitoring the active ingredients of functional products. Studies have shown that the total flavonoids of black wolfberry leaves have protective hemolysis of erythrocytes, significant effects in scavenging mouse serum hydroxyl free radicals, and inhibiting mouse liver malondialdehyde (MDA) production, and its extracts (such as flavonoids) have the effect of lowering blood lipids (Li, 2009). In addition, Akbarian et al. (2011) reported that phenolic compounds in plant extracts have inhibitory effects on 3‑hydroxy‑3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A, which is necessary for cholesterol synthesis. In this study, at 30th days, the total cholesterol content in the eggs of the T2 group decreased, and the total amino acid and cystine contents increased. This may be due to the inhibition of malondialdehyde production by flavonoids in black wolfberry residues or the inhibition of 3‑hydroxy‑3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme a by phenols, resulting in a decrease in total cholesterol content.

In addition, black wolfberry contains a variety of amino acids. The total content of amino acids in black wolfberry is 4.23 g/100 g, of which aspartic acid, glutamic acid and proline are higher, the content of pharmacodynamic amino acids accounts for 63.3% of the total amino acid content, and the essential amino acids of the human body account for 38.1% of the total amino acid content. It is an ideal protein supply for the human body (Ding et al., 2016a). In this study, the total amino acid content of the T2 group was significantly higher than that of the CK group and T1 group and the total amino acid content of the T1 group decreased with time, indicating that the active substances in black wolfberry were affected by the amount added. The study found that the total flavonoids in the leaves of black wolfberry have antioxidant activity in vitro, and the antioxidant activity has a dose-effect relationship with its concentration, which is consistent with the results of this study (Li et al., 2010). Studies have shown that the nutritional value of animal-derived products depends on the diet, genetics, age, management and feeding of animal origin (Devi et al., 2018). In this study, there was no significant difference in the crude protein content of eggs at 30th days, while the crude protein content of the test group at 60th days was significantly higher than that of the CK group, which is consistent with the report of (Devi et al., 2018).

Effects of different proportions of black wolfberry residues on the contents of Mineral elements in eggs of laying hens

The increased egg contents of P, Ca, Fe, Zn, Se (T1 at day 30) and Na, P, K, Se (T2 at day 30) suggest that black wolfberry residue enhances the intestinal absorption of these minerals. Polyphenols can act as chelators, forming soluble mineral-polyphenol complexes that protect minerals from precipitation in the gut lumen, thereby increasing bioavailability (Seuss-Baum and Nau, 2011). The higher Se content is particularly notable because Se, together with vitamin E, contributes to antioxidant defense; the sparing effect of polyphenols on vitamin E may also reduce Se consumption, leading to higher Se deposition (Wang et al., 2018). The age-dependent decrease in several minerals at day 60 likely reflects normal physiological changes in late laying period, but the fact that T1 and T2 groups maintained higher Na and K than CK suggests a persistent positive effect. Researcher (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019) found that selenium can also be related to factors such as improving the antioxidant capacity and lipid metabolism function of laying hens, further enhancing the additional nutritional value of eggs. Meanwhile, eggs with a high selenium content play a huge role in delaying aging, enhancing immunity, and anti-cancer effects (Liu, 2021).

Effects of different proportions of black wolfberry residues on vitamin content in eggs of laying hens

Eggs, as a vitamin-rich food containing all vitamins except vitamin C (ascorbic acid), have been identified as the lowest cost animal source of protein, vitamin A (retinol), iron, vitamin B12, vitamin B2 (riboflavin), choline, and the second lowest cost source of zinc and calcium (Drewnowski, 2010). The lack of VC in eggs may be due to poultry's ability to synthesize vitamin C from glucose, and as such, they are dietary sources (fruits and vegetables) that rely on VC. Egg yolk contains a lot of VA, VD, VE, VK, VB1, VB2, VB5, VB6, VB9 and VB12, while egg white contains a lot of VB2, VB3 and VB5, but also contains a lot of VB1, VB6, VB8, VB9 and VB12 (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). VA has a wide range of physiological effects. In addition to maintaining visual function, it also plays an important role in maintaining normal growth and development, reproductive performance and immune function of animals. This study found that at 60th days, the content of VA, VE and VB9 in the T1 group was higher than that in the other two groups, while the content of VD3 was significantly lower than that in the control group, indicating that the addition of 1.0% black wolfberry residues to the diet of laying hens can improve the antioxidant effect of eggs.

Effects of different proportions of black wolfberry residues on fatty acid content in eggs of laying hens

The addition of black wolfberry to the diet of laying hens can significantly increase the total amount of fatty acids in eggs, especially polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA). This study found that adding 1% black wolfberry residues to the diet of laying hens can increase the content of methyl oleate in eggs and reduce the content of palmitic acid, stearic acid, linoleic acid, arachidonic acid, etc.; More importantly, the increased DHA content in T2 eggs (Table 6) can be explained by the antioxidant properties of anthocyanins and flavonoids. DHA is highly susceptible to lipid peroxidation; dietary polyphenols can protect n-3 PUFA from oxidative damage during intestinal absorption, transport, and yolk deposition, thereby increasing their final concentration in eggs (Lisa, 2020). This mechanism is supported by the concurrent increase in yolk vitamin E (Table 5, T1 at day 60), which also functions as a lipophilic antioxidant and is spared by polyphenols. In addition, studies have shown that the addition of Saccharicter-Penin and Lactobacillus plantarum complexes (Qian et al., 2023) and shiitake mushrooms (Gao et al., 2023) to the diet also increases the content of PUFA in eggs, and these results are consistent with the reduction of cholesterol. DHA, known as "brain gold", belongs to omega-3 PUFA. DHA plays an important role in the human body and can prevent cardiovascular diseases, protect the retina, and improve vision (Dong et al.). The results of this study show that the DHA content in the added black wolfberry residues group is higher than that in the control group, indicating that black wolfberry residues has a protective effect on vision.

Effects of different proportions of black wolfberry residues on fecal microorganisms of laying hens

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating the host's immunity, nutrient absorption, and intestinal barrier function, thus having a significant impact on the host's health. (Yang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2019). Combined with the analysis of the diversity indices, black wolfberry residues enhanced the species richness of the gut microbiota. A microbial community with higher richness contributes to improving the stability of the community (Zhen et al., 2021).

The further analysis of the microbial community composition showed that, after adding black wolfberry residues for 60th days, the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were higher than those in the control (CK) group, while the abundance of Fusobacteriota was lower than that in the CK group. This indicates that the gut microecosystem became more stable, mature, and healthy with the passage of time, which is consistent with previous studies (Mi-Young et al., 2020; Shobharani and Halami, 2016). Bacteroidota and Firmicutes play important roles in the digestive process of the animal gastrointestinal tract. Firmicutes is mainly involved in the degradation of structural carbohydrates, while Bacteroidota is the most important phylum in the degradation process of non-fiber substances. These two phyla play a vital role in the nutritional metabolism of animals (Khafipour et al., 2016).

In addition, at day 30, the abundances of Limosilactobacillus and Lactobacillus in group T1 were higher than those in the control group. Lactobacillus has the functions of regulating the intestinal microecological balance and synthesizing nutrients. It can inhibit the growth of harmful bacteria by producing substances such as lactic acid and bacteriocins. Some Lactobacillus strains can synthesize VB (Krysiak et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023), which may be the reason why the contents of VB2 and VB9 in group T1 were higher than other two groups at day 30. In this experiment, the abundance of Deferribacterota in group T2 was relatively high at day 30. Studies have shown that Deferribacterota is related to iron metabolism, and the low-valent iron produced by Deferribacterota can be utilized by other microorganisms (Mayneris-Perxachs et al., 2022), which may also be the reason for the decrease in the iron content in eggs. After continuously fed mice with blueberry polyphenol extract (PPE) for 12 weeks, they found that PPE changed the composition of the intestinal microbiota of mice and regulated Deferribacteres, etc. (Jiao et al., 2018). This may be because both blueberry polyphenol extract and black wolfberry are rich in antioxidant substances such as polyphenols and anthocyanins. These components may reduce the oxidative stress damage in the intestine, provide relatively stable living conditions for Deferribacterota bacteria, and thus indirectly promote their proliferation. At day 60th, the relative abundance of Romboutsia in test groups were consistently higher than CK group. Romboutsia can interact with other intestinal microorganisms to maintain the stability of the intestinal microecology, help resist colonization and infection by harmful bacteria, and improve immunity. At the same time, they play important roles in improving digestive function, fermenting dietary fiber, producing beneficial short-chain fatty acids, and promoting intestinal barrier function. Romboutsia are closely related to fat metabolism (Gerritsen et al., 2019; Song et al., 2024) found that Romboutsia ilealis can improve the immunity of broiler chickens and activate the TLR2/NF-κB signaling pathway. Studies also found that Romboutsia lituseburensis JCM1404 is related to a lower level of inflammation and a higher level of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which helps maintain the normal metabolic function of the body (Yin et al., 2023).

The Lefse results showed that at day 30, Selenomonadales, Selenomonadaceae 42771, and Meganmonas played important functions in group T2. Some studies have found that Selenomonadales can help host animals digest food or interact with the host animals' immune systems. In the intestines of some animals, it can produce some substances beneficial to the host, such as certain vitamins (Yang, 2024). Zhang et al. (2021) found that there was a negative correlation between Selenomonadales and the residual feed intake (RFI) of sheep, so they believed that Selenomonadales might play a role in nutrient supply. However, In a study conducted by Do et al. (2017), Selenomonadales in the rumen of ruminants and pointed out that it had nothing to do with feed efficiency, which was consistent with the results of this experiment. Yang et al. (2023) used a laying hen model of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) to study the effect of inulin on improving MAFLD, they found that the genus Megamonas rich in inulin was negatively correlated with the parameters related to hepatic steatosis. Wu et al. (2024) found that Megamonas had a strong correlation with obesity because this bacterium can degrade inositol, enhance the intestinal absorption capacity of fat, and ferment various carbohydrates. The end products are acetic acid, propionic acid, and lactic acid, which may be the reason why the body weight of group T2 was greater than that of the other two groups in this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of our study indicate that supplementing the diet with different proportions of black wolfberry residues has a positive impact on the laying performance, egg quality, and intestinal health of laying hens. Among them, adding 1.5% black wolfberry residues shows significantly better effect than other treatments.
