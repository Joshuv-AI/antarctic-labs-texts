The Türkiye orthorexia nervosa scale (TONS-29): development and psychometric validation for adult populations
Authors: Hasan Kaan Kavsara, Indrani Kalkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOrthorexia Nervosa (ON), first described by Steven Bratman in 1997, refers to a pathological obsession with healthy eating, often resulting in restrictive dietary behaviors, nutritional deficiencies, and impaired psychosocial functioning. In the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria, this study aimed to develop and validate the Türkiye Orthorexia Nervosa Scale (TONS-29).MethodsAfter excluding individuals with a diagnosed eating disorder (n = 7) or without informed consent (n = 8), a total of 720 adults (489 women, 231 men) participated. Scale development was guided by expert opinions from professionals in nutrition, psychiatric nursing, and Turkish linguistics. Construct validity was assessed through Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA; n = 360) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA; n = 360) on randomly divided samples. A test-retest analysis was conducted with 128 participants.ResultsThe final scale includes 29 items across five subfactors, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.941 and explained variance of 49.63%. Subdimension reliability was good to excellent (α = 0.724–0.908). CFA results indicated good model fit (chi-square/degrees of freedom [χ²/df] = 2.319, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMSEA] = 0.061, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR] = 0.052, Incremental Fit Index [IFI] = 0.907, Comparative Fit Index [CFI] = 0.906, Goodness of Fit Index [GFI] = 0.855, and Tucker–Lewis Index [TLI] = 0.907). The scale demonstrated temporal stability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 75.1% (95% CI: 0.647–0.824).ConclusionThe findings support the TONS-29 as a valid and reliable tool for assessing ON tendencies in Turkish adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03634-8.

Introduction

Orthorexia Nervosa (ON) was first introduced by family physician Steven Bratman in 1997 to describe individuals’ pathological obsession with “correct” eating [1]. Derived from the Greek words orthos (correct) and orexis (appetite), the term refers to a condition characterized by an excessive focus on the purity and nutritional content of foods, which can lead to severe dietary restrictions and impairments in psychosocial functioning [1, 2]. In 2016, Dunn and Bratman further conceptualized ON as a condition marked not merely by a desire for a healthy lifestyle, but by rigid dietary rules, malnutrition, and psychosocial dysfunction [3].

Despite its frequent use in both academic literature and daily life, ON still lacks standardized diagnostic criteria. A 2022 consensus document noted that although various diagnostic proposals exist, ON has yet to be officially recognized within international disease classification systems such as the DSM or ICD [2]. This lack of consensus complicates clinical diagnosis and raises questions about ON’s classification as a distinct psychopathological disorder. ON has been suggested to share characteristics with obsessive-compulsive disorder, eating disorders, and somatoform disorders [4, 5], though its distinction from these conditions remains unclear [6].

Current studies report widely varying ON prevalence rates, ranging from 6% to 90%, depending on the population studied and the measurement tools used [7]. For instance, a Turkish study using the Turkish version of the Düsseldorf Orthorexia Scale (TR-DOS) reported a prevalence of 7.3% among university students [8], while studies using ORTO-15 showed rates as high as 71% in Poland [9] and 83% in the United States [10]. These discrepancies highlight the need for culturally sensitive and psychometrically robust tools for ON assessment.

In Türkiye, currently and commonly used ON assessment tools include the TR-DOS [8], Barcelona Orthorexia Scale (BOS) [11], Orthorexia Nervosa Inventory (ONI) [12], and ORTO-11 [13]. However, these are adaptations of scales originally developed in other cultural contexts, and they may not fully capture ON-specific behaviors and attitudes within the Turkish population. Despite its widespread use, ORTO-11 has been criticized for failing to encompass the psychological and social factors of ON [8]. Moreover, widely used instruments such as ORTO-15 have shown inconsistent psychometric performance across studies [14–16].

These methodological challenges have led to calls for new measurement tools that reflect ON’s multifactorial structure and cultural specificity. A valid scale should assess not only eating behaviors but also the emotional and psychosocial impacts of ON [15, 17]. Therefore, this study aims to develop a reliable and valid instrument specifically designed to assess orthorexic tendencies in the Turkish adult population.

The primary objectives of this study were:

To develop a culturally appropriate and original measurement tool for ON in Türkiye.

To determine the scale’s factor structure.

To evaluate its psychometric properties, including validity and reliability.

To examine the relationship between ON and other eating-related constructs through comparisons with the Eating Attitudes Test-26 (EAT-26), a widely used screening tool for disordered eating behaviors, and ORTO-11, a measure of orthorexic tendencies.

To assess the prevalence and distribution of ON tendencies among adults in Türkiye.

It is expected that the development of this instrument will provide a valuable tool for future academic research and clinical evaluation of ON within the Turkish context.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a methodological and descriptive design to develop and validate a culturally appropriate instrument for assessing ON tendencies among Turkish adults. The Türkiye Orthorexia Nervosa Scale was created in Turkish and structured to reflect obsessive healthy eating behaviors within a culturally sensitive framework.

The development process began with a comprehensive literature review across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Keywords included “orthorexia nervosa,” “orthorexia scale,” “assessment tool,” “psychometric validation,” “eating behavior scale,” “healthy eating obsession,” and “Turkish adaptation.” The retrieved materials were organized in the Mendeley library and used to identify existing ON measurement tools, diagnostic criteria, and conceptual frameworks. Limitations of current scales, particularly the widely used ORTO-11 in Türkiye, regarding cultural relevance and multidimensionality, informed the conceptual and methodological direction of the present study.

An initial item pool was generated based on constructs identified in the literature, expert insights, clinical observations, and qualitative data. The draft of 56 items was evaluated by a panel of 21 experts specializing in nutrition, psychiatric nursing, and Turkish linguistics. The initial 56-item pool and the applied 46-item version used for psychometric testing are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Full details of the experts, including name, title, institution, and area of expertise, are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Each item was assessed for clarity, relevance, and representativeness. Items failing to meet the minimum content validity ratio criteria of 0.429 for 21 experts at the α = 0.05 significance level [18], as outlined by Lawshe and further adapted by Boateng et al. [19], were revised or removed to enhance content validity.

Following this review, a pilot study was conducted with the preliminary 46-item version of the scale among 30 participants to assess linguistic clarity and item comprehensibility, in line with the recommendations of Johanson and Brooks [20]. Minor wording adjustments were made in response to participant feedback to improve clarity, but no items were excluded at this stage.

The final version of the scale was administered to a larger sample (N = 720). Data were collected in two stages: the first phase focused on item-level psychometric evaluation using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis (EFA and CFA). For the second phase, a subgroup of 128 participants who had indicated their willingness at baseline to be recontacted for a follow-up assessment were invited to complete the scale again after a 2–4 week interval. This subsample was used to evaluate test–retest reliability and temporal stability, and the intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated, in line with established psychometric standards by DeVellis [21].

Psychometric analyses included EFA to determine the underlying factor structure, CFA to verify model fit, and Cronbach’s alpha to assess internal consistency for the overall scale and its subscales. Construct and criterion validity were examined through correlation analyses with established instruments. Based on these results, items that failed to contribute meaningfully to the scale’s reliability and validity were revised or excluded.

Participants

The study population consisted of adults aged 18–65 residing in Türkiye. Individuals within this age range who voluntarily agreed to participate were included. Participants were excluded if they were outside the specified age range, reported following a medical or therapeutic diet, or self-reported a clinical diagnosis of a psychiatric or eating disorder. In the initial phase, participants were asked whether they were willing to participate in the follow-up retest; those who declined were excluded from the second phase.

A convenience sampling method was employed. Although 735 individuals participated, 7 respondents who reported a clinical diagnosis of an eating disorder and 8 who did not provide informed consent were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 720 participants for analysis.

In scale development studies, it is recommended that the sample size be at least 5–10 times the number of items [22]. According to Comrey and Lee [23], sample sizes of 100 are considered poor, 200 fair, 300 good, 500 very good, and 1,000 excellent. In this study, the scale initially consisted of 46 items. To perform both EFA and CFA, the total sample of 720 participants was randomly divided into two equal subsamples (n = 360 each), in line with the SOLOMON method [24]. This approach was chosen to avoid overfitting and to enable independent testing of the factor structure, thereby increasing the robustness of the validation process. Although splitting the sample reduced the number of participants per analysis, the resulting participant-to-item ratio of approximately 7.8:1 in each subsample still exceeded recommended psychometric thresholds, supporting the adequacy and stability of factor solutions.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Yeditepe University Clinical Research Ethics Committee for Non-Interventional Studies (decision number E. 83321821-805.02.02.02.02.03–276, dated November 10, 2023). Permissions for the use of the EAT-26 and ORTO-11 were obtained. Informed consent was acquired from all participants before data collection using informed consent forms. Data were collected between December 11, 2023, and June 11, 2024, through a combination of face-to-face and online (Google Forms) surveys.

Instruments

EAT-26, originally developed by Garner and colleagues [25] to assess eating behaviors associated with anorexia nervosa, the 40-item version was later reduced to 26 items without compromising psychometric properties [26, 27]. The Turkish validation of the EAT-26 was conducted by Ergüney-Okumuş & Sertel-Berk [28]. It is a 6-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 0 (never/rarely) to 3 (always), with scores of 21 or higher indicating a potential disordered eating pattern. In the Turkish validation study, factor analysis supports a three-factor structure: Food Preoccupation, Dieting, and Social Pressure, explaining 38.5% of the variance. Internal consistency was Cronbach’s α = 0.84; test-retest reliability was 0.78 [28].

ORTO-11 is a shortened version of the original ORTO-15 scale which was developed by Donini et al. [29] to identify ON, based on Bratman’s earlier diagnostic ideas [30]. The Turkish version, ORTO-11, was validated by Arusoğlu et al. [13] by removing four items from the original 15-item scale. The scale consists of 11 items, each rated on a four-point Likert scale (“always,” “often,” “sometimes,” “never”). Items reflecting orthorexic behaviors are scored as 1, while items indicating normal eating are scored as 4, so that lower total scores represent stronger orthorexic tendencies. The possible score range is 11 to 44. While the original ORTO-15 used a cut-off point of ≤ 40 to indicate orthorexic risk [29], Arusoğlu et al. [13] proposed a cut-off of ≤ 27 for the Turkish version (ORTO-11). In their study, the 11-item version demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.62, and a two-factor solution was identified, explaining 24.94% and 12.25% of the variance, respectively. However, due to the low reliability of the second factor (α = 0.31), the authors recommended treating the scale as unifactorial [13].

Scoring and interpretation of the TONS-29

The final version of the Türkiye Orthorexia Nervosa Scale (TONS-29) consists of 29 positively phrased items provided in Supplementary Table 4; each rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Accordingly, the total score that a participant can obtain from the scale ranges between 29 and 145. Higher scores reflect a stronger tendency toward orthorexic behaviors.

To interpret the scale scores, the mean item score was used as the basis for classification. This approach allows for a more standardized interpretation across individuals with varying response patterns. Based on prior research utilizing similar mean-based classification frameworks [31–33], three interpretive categories were defined. A mean score between 1.00 and 2.60 indicates no risk of ON, scores between 2.61 and 3.40 reflect being at risk, and scores between 3.41 and 5.00 represent the presence of orthorexic tendencies.

These mean-based categories correspond to total score ranges of 29–75 for “no risk,” 76–98 for “at risk,” and 99–145 for “ON present.” This classification allows researchers and clinicians to evaluate ON risk levels in individuals and supports the use of the TONS-29 as a practical screening and monitoring tool in both research and applied health settings.

Content validity based on expert opinions

A qualitative approach was adopted to assess content validity. Expert opinions were converted into quantitative data to evaluate the agreement between evaluators and to conduct a systematic analysis. For this purpose, methods developed by Cohen [34], Davis [35], Ayre and Scally [18], and particularly Lawshe [36] were referenced. Lawshe’s Content Validity Ratio (CVR) method was preferred for its simplicity and practicality. Although some criticisms and modifications of this method have been suggested by scholars like Wilson et al. [37] and Ayre & Scally [18], it remains widely used [38]. In this study, the modified Lawshe technique was applied to calculate both the CVR and Content Validity Index (CVI) for each item. In Lawshe’s original format [36], each item is rated as “essential,” “useful but not essential,” or “not necessary.” In the modified version, these categories were adapted to “Not appropriate,” “Appropriate with minor revision,” and “Appropriate.” Experts were instructed to mark one option per item, with an additional section provided for qualitative feedback on wording, cultural relevance, or clarity.

According to Ayre and Scally [18], the minimum acceptable CVR value for 21 experts at the 0.05 significance level is 0.429. Items falling below this threshold were removed.

Following this, the CVI was computed by averaging the CVR values across retained items, providing an overall index of agreement regarding the relevance of the scale items. The CVI thus represents the proportion of items considered content valid by the panel.

To ensure objectivity in content validity calculations, the qualifications and number of experts (recommended: 5–40) are critical [18, 36]. Experts from various universities across Türkiye were invited. In total, 21 experts participated: 15 in Nutrition and Dietetics, 2 in Psychiatric Nursing, and 4 in Turkish Language and Literature. Supplementary Table 3 presents the panel of experts consulted for language and content validity.

Statistical analysis

EFA was used to identify latent structures underlying observed variables [39]. The correlation matrix was examined to ensure that most inter-item correlations exceeded 0.30 [40]. Suitability for factor analysis was assessed using Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity [41] and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy, with values above 0.50 considered acceptable [42]. Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) was used, and factors with eigenvalues >1 were retained. Direct Oblimin rotation was applied to improve interpretability. For EFA, items with factor loadings ≥ 0.30 were considered acceptable, with ≥ 0.40 preferred as the cut-off for retention, while those below this threshold were excluded [40].

For reliability analyses, internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable). Item-total correlations were evaluated, with values above 0.20 considered indicative of item fit [43]. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC ≥ 0.70) [44].

CFA was performed on an independent subsample (n = 360) to test the five-factor structure identified in EFA. The analysis was conducted using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method in IBM SPSS AMOS 24.0. For CFA, items with factor loadings ≥ 0.50 were considered acceptable. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df ≤ 3.0), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.90), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.90), Incremental Fit Index (IFI ≥ 0.90), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI ≥ 0.90), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08).

Descriptive and inferential data analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26. Descriptive statistics included frequencies (n), percentages (%), means (M), standard deviations (SD), medians (Md), and minimum–maximum values. Shapiro–Wilk tests and skewness/kurtosis values were used to assess normality. Independent samples t-tests and ANOVA were used to compare group means. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. Pearson correlation coefficients assessed relationships between continuous variables. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used throughout.

Results

Content validity

Content Validity Ratio was calculated using Lawshe’s formula: CVR = (Na – N/2)/(N/2), where Na represents the number of experts who rated the item as “appropriate,” and N is the total number of experts (n = 21).

Item 46 was excluded due to a negative CVR value. According to Ayre and Scally (2014), the minimum acceptable CVR value for 21 experts at the α = 0.05 significance level is 0.429. Items 6, 18, 22, 25, 30, and 38 had CVR values below this threshold and were subsequently removed from the scale.

Following these exclusions, the number of items was reduced from 56 to 49. After recalculating the CVI for the remaining 49 items, the value increased from 0.673 to 0.740, exceeding the minimum threshold of 0.429 and indicating statistically significant content validity [36, 38]. This improvement reflects better overall alignment between the items and the construct being measured. Items flagged for revision by at least one expert were modified accordingly.

Additionally, Item 37, originally phrased as “My eating habits are superior to others,” was revised to “I enjoy paying more attention to healthy eating than most people,” aligning it more closely with orthorexic traits and avoiding overemphasis on superiority. Items related to weight loss or fear of weight gain, adapted from the BOS scale for differential diagnosis, were also excluded (Items 52, 53, and 54) based on expert consensus and the use of EAT-26 in this study for distinguishing ON from other eating disorders. In total, 10 items were removed due to low CVR values or redundancy, resulting in a revised draft scale consisting of 46 items. The final CVI increased to 0.752 for the 46-item version, reflecting improved content alignment. The initial 56-item pool and the applied 46-item version used for psychometric testing are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Subsequent factor analyses and refinement steps led to the exclusion of additional items. The final validated version of the scale retained 29 items, with all items demonstrating CVR values above the critical threshold (0.429) and a CVI of 0.767, indicating robust content validity for the final version. Detailed CVR and CVI results of the final 29-item version are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Characteristics of participants

A total of 720 individuals participated in the study. The mean age of the participants was 32.53 years (SD = 9.90), with a median of 31 years, ranging from 18 to 65 years. The majority of participants were women (n = 489, 67.9%), while men comprised 32.1% (n = 231) of the sample.

The average height was 168 cm (SD = 9 cm), with a median of 170 cm (range: 150–190 cm). The mean body weight was 69.38 kg (SD = 14.78), with values ranging from 37 to 120 kg (median = 68 kg). Participants’ average body mass index (BMI) was 24.55 kg/m² (SD = 4.36), with a median of 24.1 and a range between 14.5 and 42.8 kg/m².

Regarding health-related behaviors, 60.8% (n = 438) of participants reported no alcohol use, while 39.2% (n = 282) reported current alcohol consumption. In terms of smoking status, the majority were non-smokers (63.9%, n = 460), followed by current smokers (28.6%, n = 206) and former smokers (7.5%, n = 54).

Construct validity

To evaluate the scale’s construct validity, EFA was conducted on data from 360 participants randomly selected from the total sample (N = 720). The initial item pool of 46 items was reduced to 34 based on factor loading criteria (Removed items 5, 11, 13, 18, 20, 24, 30, 31, 32, 36, 39, 46). The full factor loading matrix of the retained 34 items is presented in Supplementary Tables 6, and the scree plot of eigenvalues supporting the five-factor solution is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The KMO measure was 0.940, indicating excellent sampling adequacy. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 5121.164, df = 406, p < 0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis. The five-factor structure explained 49.63% of the total variance.

As shown in Fig. 2, the scree plot indicated an elbow point after the fifth component, supporting a five-factor solution. The rotated component matrix confirmed this structure. The naming of the sub-dimensions was guided by the thematic content of the items within each factor. The first factor, Guilt and Dietary Perfectionism (9 items), captured perfectionistic attitudes toward food rules and feelings of guilt when deviating from them, with loadings ranging from 0.527 to 0.729. The second factor, Social Isolation and Control Over Food (6 items), reflected withdrawal from social contexts and rigid monitoring of food preparation, with loadings between 0.370 and 0.680. The third factor, Health Anxiety and Nutritional Awareness (5 items), included concerns about nutritional value and anxiety around meal quality, with loadings from 0.362 to 0.592. The fourth factor, Nutritional Vigilance and Avoidance (5 items), represented restrictive eating and avoidance of foods deemed unhealthy, with loadings ranging from 0.420 to 0.570. The fifth factor, Rigid Dietary Control and Food Judgement (4 items), captured strict dietary rules and judgment toward others’ food choices, with loadings from 0.385 to 0.546.

Confirmatory factor analysis results for the TONS-29

CFA was conducted to validate the five-factor structure previously identified through EFA. The analysis was performed on a separate sample of 360 participants randomly selected from the total study group (N = 720). Initially, a 34-item model was tested, but fit indices indicated an inadequate model fit. Based on modification indices and theoretical considerations, five items (Items 23, 26, 29, 35, 43) were removed. The final CFA was then conducted on a refined model with 29 items. A summary of all excluded items, including the stage and reason for exclusion, is provided in Supplementary Table 7.

As shown in Table 4, the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), RMSEA, SRMR, IFI, TLI, CFI, and GFI. The χ²/df ratio was 2.319, indicating an acceptable model fit. The RMSEA value was 0.061 and the SRMR was 0.052, both within acceptable limits (< 0.08). Values for IFI (0.907), CFI (0.906), and TLI (0.907) exceeded the 0.90 threshold, while the GFI value of 0.855 was also deemed acceptable.

Criterion-Related validity

Criterion validity was assessed through correlation analyses between the TONS-29 and the EAT-26 (including its subfactors) and the ORTO-11 scale. Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated the TONS-29 for assessing orthorexic tendencies among adults. The scale demonstrated robust content validity through expert review, and EFA revealed a five-factor structure, which was subsequently confirmed by CFA with acceptable fit indices. Criterion validity was supported by significant positive correlations with the EAT-26 and negative correlations with the ORTO-11, while internal consistency and test–retest reliability analyses indicated strong psychometric stability. Collectively, these findings highlight the TONS-29 as a valid, reliable, and culturally adapted tool that advances research and clinical evaluation of ON in Türkiye.

Validity and reliability of the TONS-29

Content validity and expert review

Content validity is a critical component in scale development, ensuring that the items reflect the construct under investigation. An expert review was conducted with 21 academics from nutrition and dietetics, psychiatric nursing, and Turkish language and literature. The CVR method was used to evaluate item relevance [18, 36], and the initial item pool was refined from 56 to 46 items based on expert ratings.

Items with CVR values below the critical threshold (0.429) or negative scores were excluded (e.g., item 46), and the final CVI increased from 0.673 to 0.752, reflecting improved content alignment. Some items were revised for clarity and relevance. For instance, item 37 was revised from “My eating habits are superior to others” to “I enjoy paying more attention to healthy eating than others,” aligning better with ON diagnostic frameworks [2, 45].

Additionally, items related to weight gain or loss anxiety, often associated with anorexia nervosa, were removed based on expert feedback and literature suggesting that ON should be assessed independently from other eating disorders [46, 47]. This refinement supports the scale’s construct purity and improves interpretability.

Factor structure and construct validity

EFA supported a five-factor solution, explaining 49.63% of the total variance. The KMO value of 0.940 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 5121.164, p < 0.001) indicated excellent sampling adequacy and suitability for factor analysis.

The five factors—Guilt and Dietary Perfectionism, Social Isolation and Food Control, Health Anxiety and Nutritional Awareness, Nutritional Vigilance and Avoidance, and Rigid Diet Control and Food Judgment—align with current theoretical models of ON and reflect its multifactorial nature [6, 48–50].

Confirmatory factor analysis

To validate the factor structure, CFA was conducted on a separate subsample (n = 360). After removing five items based on modification indices, the revised 29-item model demonstrated an acceptable to good fit: χ²/df = 2.319, RMSEA = 0.061, SRMR = 0.052, CFI = 0.906, TLI = 0.907, and GFI = 0.855. These values meet established cutoffs for model adequacy [51, 52].

Convergent and discriminant validity

The TONS-29 was positively and significantly correlated with EAT-26 total scores (r = 0.304, p < 0.001) and its subfactors. This supports convergent validity, as some ON features overlap with disordered eating tendencies, particularly in the Guilt and Dietary Perfectionism subscale (r = 0.385 with EAT-26 F1).

Conversely, strong negative correlations were observed with the ORTO-11 scale (r = − 0.500, p < 0.001), which supports construct validity due to inverse scoring in ORTO-11. These patterns confirm that TONS-29 captures ON-specific behaviors, while also distinguishing it from broader eating pathology.

TONS-29 offers a multifactorial alternative to earlier tools that have shown psychometric limitations. ORTO-15 has been widely criticized for low reliability and inconsistent factorial structure [15]. ORTO-R and DOS improve upon these limitations but do not fully address the cognitive, social, and emotional aspects of ON. While tools like BOS and ONI provide broader coverage, their length and cultural limitations restrict practical use. TONS-29 bridges this gap by offering a reliable, concise, and culturally relevant measure tailored to the Turkish population.

Reliability

Internal consistency and test–retest reliability were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and ICC, respectively. The TONS-29 exhibited excellent internal consistency (α = 0.941), with all five subscales exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70 [21]. Subscale values ranged from α = 0.724 to 0.908, indicating coherent item clusters.

Test–retest reliability, conducted on a subsample (n = 128), yielded an overall ICC of 0.751 (95% CI: 0.647–0.824), suggesting strong temporal stability. No statistically significant differences were found between initial and follow-up scores (p > 0.05), further confirming the instrument’s consistency over time.

Compared with existing tools such as ORTO-15 (α = 0.62), ORTO-R (α = 0.696), and DOS (α = 0.85), the TONS-29 demonstrates superior reliability while offering a more comprehensive and culturally adapted structure [8, 13, 53].

Theoretical contributions and future applications

The identification of five distinct ON factors supports the conceptualization of ON as a multifaceted condition encompassing cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and social elements. The inclusion of factors such as social isolation, guilt, and food-related perfectionism enhances our understanding of ON beyond dietary restraint.

Given its robust psychometric properties, TONS-29 is well-positioned for use in clinical screening, epidemiological research, and intervention studies. Its multifactorial nature allows for detailed assessment of ON severity and subtype differentiation. In light of increasing ON prevalence among university students, health professionals, and athletes [54–56], the scale may be instrumental in identifying at-risk individuals and tailoring interventions.

Further cross-cultural validation studies are needed to assess the scale’s generalizability. Longitudinal research may also explore ON’s progression and associations with psychiatric comorbidities, such as obsessive-compulsive traits, anxiety, and depression [57, 58].

Comparative psychometrics with existing ON instruments

Several instruments have been developed internationally to assess orthorexic tendencies, each with different structures, psychometric qualities, and cultural adaptations. The ORTO-15, developed in Italy by Donini et al. (2005), was the first widely used tool [29]. However, its original psychometric quality was not well established, and its Turkish adaptation demonstrated limited reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.62), with criticisms regarding inconsistent factor structures and inadequate coverage of psychosocial aspects of ON [13, 15].

The Düsseldorf Orthorexia Scale, introduced in Germany in 2015 by Barthels et al. [49], is a concise 10-item single-factor instrument with good internal consistency (α = 0.84). The Turkish adaptation [8] confirmed its unidimensionality with strong reliability (α = 0.854). While practical for screening, DOS primarily emphasizes behavioral aspects of ON and may not capture the broader psychosocial dimensions.

The Barcelona Orthorexia Scale, developed in Spain in 2019 by Bauer et al. [46], represents one of the most comprehensive instruments, comprising 64 items across six dimensions (rational, emotional, behavioral, negative health consequences, negative social/academic consequences, and differential diagnosis). Its Turkish validation [11] demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.96). Nevertheless, its considerable length limits feasibility for routine use, particularly in large samples or clinical screening contexts.

The Orthorexia Nervosa Inventory, developed in the United States in 2021 by Oberle et al. [50], contains 24 items across three dimensions: behaviors, impairments, and emotions. The original validation demonstrated high reliability (total α = 0.94; subscales 0.88–0.90). ONI is shorter than BOS yet still multidimensional, though it was not originally designed for the Turkish context.

In comparison, the TONS-29 offers a balance between comprehensiveness and feasibility. With 29 items, it is shorter than BOS yet more multifactorial than DOS and ORTO-11. The five-factor structure captures guilt and dietary perfectionism, social isolation, health anxiety, nutritional vigilance, and rigid dietary control, thereby reflecting cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and social components of ON. Its internal consistency (α = 0.941) surpasses ORTO-11 and DOS and approaches that of ONI and BOS, while test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.751) confirms temporal stability. Importantly, unlike adapted tools, TONS-29 was developed and validated directly in Turkish with expert review, ensuring cultural specificity. Taken together, TONS-29 complements existing measures by providing a psychometrically robust, culturally relevant, and moderately concise instrument that can be applied in both research and clinical screening within Türkiye.

Cultural adaptation of items in the TONS-29

An important contribution of the TONS-29 lies in its cultural adaptation, ensuring that orthorexic behaviors are expressed in ways that are meaningful and recognizable within the Turkish context. During item development, linguistic phrasing and cultural nuances were carefully considered to avoid ambiguity and to capture behaviors that align with Turkish dietary traditions and social norms.

For instance, items such as “Besinlerin etiket bilgilerini kontrol etmeden besini tüketmem” (I do not consume foods without checking their labels; Item 7) reflect the growing emphasis in Türkiye on food labeling and the popularity of “organic” or “natural” claims in the local market. Similarly, the inclusion of an item on avoiding foods not labeled as “organik” or “doğal” (Item 25) acknowledges the cultural weight given to these terms in Turkish food discourse, where such labels strongly influence consumer trust.

Social dimensions of eating were also culturally phrased. The item “Servis edilen yiyeceklerin içeriğinden emin olmadığım sosyal etkinliklerden genellikle kaçınırım” (I usually avoid social events where I am not sure about the content of the food served; Item 10) highlights the centrality of shared meals in Turkish culture, where refusal or avoidance may have strong social implications. Likewise, “Beslenmeyle ilgili değerlerimi kimse anlamadığı için kendimi yalnız hissederim” (I feel lonely because no one understands my dietary values; Item 17) captures the experience of social isolation specific to collectivist cultural settings where conformity to group eating practices is often expected.

In addition, items were designed to differentiate orthorexic tendencies from weight-focused concerns, which are culturally sensitive in Türkiye, given the high visibility of dieting culture. For example, Item 2 (“Zayıf olmaktan daha çok sağlıklı olmak konusunda endişelenirim”; I worry more about being healthy than being thin) ensures that health anxiety is emphasized over thinness, distinguishing ON from anorexia nervosa–related concerns.

Overall, the cultural phrasing of the TONS-29 items strengthens the ecological validity of the instrument. By embedding locally resonant expressions, such as organik/doğal food preferences, social event avoidance, and collectivist dynamics, the TONS-29 provides a nuanced assessment of orthorexic tendencies that is both psychometrically sound and culturally meaningful for the Turkish population. The full set of culturally phrased and validated items of the TONS-29 is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Strengths and limitations

The TONS-29 is a psychometrically robust scale developed to assess orthorexic tendencies within a culturally appropriate framework. Its development followed a rigorous multi-step process including expert validation, content validity assessment, EFA, CFA, and reliability testing. The five-factor structure confirmed by CFA reflects the multidimensional nature of ON, cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and social components, while demonstrating excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.941) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.751).

Unlike earlier instruments such as ORTO-15, which suffer from poor reliability and factor instability, the TONS-29 provides a more structured and comprehensive tool. Particularly, the inclusion of a social isolation dimension highlights the psychosocial impact of ON beyond dietary rigidity. The large sample size (N = 720) enhances the statistical power and generalizability of findings. Moreover, convergent and divergent validity were established through positive correlations with EAT-26 and negative correlations with ORTO-11, supporting the scale’s specificity and discriminant validity.

Importantly, the scale holds clinical utility for early identification and monitoring of individuals with orthorexic tendencies, despite ON not yet being formally classified in the DSM-5. However, the TONS-29 should be interpreted as a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool, since clinical validation studies and structured psychiatric interviews were not conducted. Establishing diagnostic cutoffs and confirming its application in clinical practice will require future studies with clinical populations.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample consisted of non-clinical adults aged 18–65; therefore, the scale’s applicability in clinical settings remains to be confirmed. Second, although individuals with diagnosed eating disorders were excluded, this information was based on self-report only, without structured psychiatric interviews. This reliance on self-reported exclusion criteria may not fully eliminate undiagnosed cases and represents a limitation regarding sample purity. Third, the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences and understanding of ON progression over time. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess risk trajectories and the scale’s predictive validity. Fourth, although the scale was designed to be culturally sensitive for the Turkish population, its applicability to other cultural contexts remains unknown. Further cross-cultural adaptation and validation studies are required to assess the scale’s utility in diverse populations and to compare ON manifestations across different sociocultural settings. Fifth, all items were positively phrased, and no reverse-coded items were included, which may increase the risk of response bias.

Additionally, reliance on self-report data introduces potential social desirability bias, particularly given the socially accepted nature of “healthy eating.” Incorporating objective or clinician-administered assessments in future studies could strengthen measurement accuracy. Finally, the absence of a clinical cutoff restricts its diagnostic application. Further research with clinical populations is needed to define threshold scores for subclinical and pathological ON.

Future studies should focus on clinical validation, longitudinal designs to track ON progression, and cross-cultural adaptations to enhance generalizability. Such efforts will strengthen both scientific understanding and practical applications of the scale.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated the TONS-29, a culturally adapted and multifactorial tool for assessing orthorexic tendencies in adults. The scale demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with a stable five-factor structure confirmed by EFA and CFA. Internal consistency and test–retest analyses indicated excellent reliability, while convergent and divergent validity were supported through significant associations with established eating behavior measures. Collectively, these findings confirm that the TONS-29 is a valid, reliable, and culturally appropriate instrument suitable for research and clinical applications in the assessment of ON.
