Comparative effects of central adiposity and BMI trajectories on cardiometabolic diseases in Chinese adults: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Limei Cao, Huifan Jia, Xinyu Si, Kexin Cao, Qian Gao, Yongxia Ding, Peili Zhang, Juping Wang, Tong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether long-term trajectories of central adiposity indices predict cardiometabolic diseases better than BMI remains uncertain. We compared trajectories of central adiposity indices and BMI in relation to cardiometabolic outcomes and estimated potential population benefits from trajectory improvement.MethodsAmong 4,295 adults from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, group-based trajectory modeling identified three trajectories for each obesity index. BMI, waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and body roundness index (BRI) showed Low-stable, Moderate-stable, and High-stable patterns, whereas the high ABSI (a body shape index) group followed a High-to-low pattern. Cox models examined associations with incident diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) between 2015 and 2020. Δβ bootstrap tests compared association strength across indices, and two one-sided tests (TOST) assessed equivalence within ± 10–15%. Population impact fractions (PIFs) estimated preventable events under counterfactual trajectory-shift scenarios.ResultsDuring follow-up, 379 diabetes, 458 heart disease, 252 stroke, and 84 CMM events occurred. Compared with the Low-stable group, the High-stable groups of BMI, WC, WHtR, and BRI were associated with higher risks of all outcomes, with hazard ratios of approximately 2.7–3.5 for diabetes and broadly elevated risks for heart disease, stroke, and CMM, whereas ABSI showed no significant associations. Δβ contrasts indicated that WHtR and BRI had significantly stronger associations with diabetes than BMI and WC, while differences among indices for heart disease and stroke were small and often within TOST equivalence margins. Under the realistic partial-shift scenario, PIFs suggested that improving trajectories could avert about 6% of diabetes cases and 4–7% of CMM events, with smaller but meaningful reductions in heart disease and stroke.ConclusionsCentral adiposity indices, particularly WHtR and BRI, demonstrated favorable trajectory-based discrimination for diabetes, whereas differences among anthropometric measures were modest for cardiovascular outcomes. These findings support an outcome-specific and context-sensitive approach to adiposity assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02948-9.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) continue to be a significant cause of illness and death globally [1], and account for approximately 32% of all deaths [2]. In 2021, over 530 million adults worldwide were affected by diabetes [3]. In China, due to the rapidly aging population and ongoing lifestyle changes, there has been a continuous increase in diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) has been steadily increasing.

In this study, CMM was defined as the coexistence of at least two incident clinical cardiometabolic diseases—specifically diabetes, coronary heart disease, or stroke. This definition explicitly treats these conditions as clinical disease outcomes rather than metabolic risk factors. Accordingly, combinations consisting solely of metabolic abnormalities, without an incident cardiovascular disease event, were not classified as CMM. It is estimated that approximately 330 million Chinese people have cardiovascular conditions, and 140 million adults have diabetes, posing a significant challenge to the healthcare system [4].

Obesity is a crucial upstream risk factor for cardiometabolic diseases [5]. The traditional measure, BMI, does not capture body fat distribution [6]. Alternative indices such as waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and body roundness index (BRI) may better reflect central adiposity and related metabolic disorders [7–9], while a body shape index (ABSI) further attempts to capture body shape geometry beyond BMI and WC [10, 11]. However, most epidemiological evidence has relied on single-time-point measurements, which cannot reflect the dynamic nature of obesity over time [5, 12], even though long-term changes in body weight and shape may provide a more physiologically relevant exposure metric for risk stratification [13].

Recent cohort studies have therefore modeled trajectories of BRI and reported that persistently high BRI patterns are associated with increased risks of CMDs [14] and mortality among Chinese adults [15]. Yet these longitudinal analyses have focused on a single obesity index and mainly composite cardiovascular outcomes, without directly comparing multiple central adiposity indices with BMI within the same population or across distinct cardiometabolic endpoints. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the added complexity of alternative indices confers a meaningful advantage over BMI, or whether some indices are effectively interchangeable in clinical and public health practice.

To address these doubts, we utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older adults. We first applied group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to identify long-term trajectories of BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, and ABSI. We then prospectively examined their associations with incident CMDs and CMM. Next, we implemented a direct-comparison framework using pairwise Δβ bootstrap tests and two one-sided tests (TOST) to quantify differences in effect sizes and assess equivalence [16]. Finally, we estimated the population impact fractions (PIF) and the number of avoidable events under several realistic trajectory-modification scenarios [17, 18]. Collectively, these analyses provide robust evidence for evaluating obesity-related cardiometabolic risk and informing public health interventions [19].

Methods

Study design and population

The CHARLS is an ongoing, nationally representative cohort of community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 45 years in China, with multistage probability sampling and follow-ups every 2–3 years since 2011. Details of the study design have been published previously [20]. All waves were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This study employed a prospective cohort design using data from all five CHARLS waves (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020). The analytical timeline is depicted in Fig. 1. The trajectory identification period consisted of three waves (2011, 2013, and 2015) used to model long-term patterns in obesity indices. The outcome assessment period commenced in 2015, designated as the baseline for risk follow-up, and continued until 2020, during which incident events of CMDs were ascertained.

To minimize potential reverse causality, participants who reported a diagnosis of any CMDs during the trajectory identification period were excluded. Therefore, all participants included in the final analysis were free of CMDs at baseline (2015).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 45 years; (2) having a prior diagnosis of any CMDs during the trajectory identification period or missing information necessary for determining pre-existing CMDs status; (3) missing or implausible anthropometric data (height, weight, WC) from 2011 to 2015; (4) missing data on key baseline covariates at 2015 (sex, marital status, educational level, smoking status, drinking, and sleep duration); (5) absence of prospective follow-up information in both the 2018 and 2020 survey waves. After applying these criteria, a total of 4,295 participants were included in the final analysis.

Definition of obesity indices

This study assessed the following five established obesity indices as primary exposure variables:

BMI, calculated as body weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m²); WC was measured horizontally at the umbilical level at the end of normal expiration; WHtR was calculated as WC (m) divided by standing height (m); BRI, computed using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:BRI=364.2-365.5\times\:\sqrt{1-\frac{{\left(WC\left(m\right)/2\pi\:\right)}^{2}}{{\left(0.5\times\:\mathrm{height}\left(m\right)\right)}^{2}}}$$\end{document}

and ABSI, defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ABSI=\frac{WC\left(m\right)}{BMI{\left(kg/{m}^{2}\right)}^{2/3}\times\:height(m{)}^{1/2}}$$\end{document}

These indices represent general adiposity (BMI), central adiposity (WC and WHtR), and geometric characteristics of visceral fat distribution (BRI and ABSI) [21, 22].

Assessment of CMDs and CMM

The outcomes of interest were incident diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and CMM. All outcomes were ascertained based on self-reported physician diagnoses using standardized CHARLS questionnaires, consistent with previous CHARLS-based studies [23]. CMM was defined as the occurrence of at least two incident cardiometabolic diseases during follow-up from a defined set: diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke. To ensure conceptual precision, we treated these as clinical disease endpoints. Combinations of only metabolic risk factors (e.g., diabetes plus hypertension alone) in the absence of a confirmed cardiovascular disease event were therefore excluded from the CMM classification. Since all participants were free of these diseases at baseline, the risk set for CMM began at enrollment, and incident CMM was defined as the date of diagnosis of the second qualifying disease.

The CMM event date was assigned as the interview date when the second condition was first reported. In cases where two CMDs were first reported at the same follow-up wave, the event date was defined as the interview date of that wave. Participants were followed from baseline (2015) until the occurrence of the outcome of interest, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of follow-up (2020), whichever occurred first.

Covariates

All covariates considered in this study were measured at the 2015 baseline and included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical history. Data on demographic and lifestyle factors were collected using a standardized questionnaire that included age, sex, educational level, marital status, smoking status, drinking, and sleep duration. Medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), and physician-diagnosed kidney, lung, and liver diseases.

Hypertension was primarily defined according to the 2023 Chinese guidelines (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, physician diagnosis, or use of antihypertensive medication), with reference to the 2017 AHA/ACC criteria for comparability [24]. Dyslipidemia was defined according to the 2016 Chinese guidelines (TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG ≥ 200 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL, physician diagnosis, or lipid-lowering therapy), with reference to the US ATP III criteria [25, 26]. IGT was defined as impaired glucose regulation (FPG ≥ 110 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%) among participants without a prior diagnosis of diabetes [27, 28]. Other medical conditions were self-reported. A composite dichotomous variable (yes/no) was constructed and included in the adjusted models.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Group differences were assessed using analysis of variance, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, or chi-square tests, as appropriate.

GBTM was employed to identify distinct subgroups of participants following similar longitudinal patterns of change in obesity indices (BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, ABSI). For each index, candidate models with 2 to 4 trajectory groups were fitted, and both quadratic and cubic polynomial terms were evaluated. The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined based on a combination of model fit and classification diagnostics, including the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), Akaike information criterion (AIC), average posterior probability (AvePP) for each group, odds of correct classification (OCC), minimum group size (> 5% of the sample), and graphical interpretability. Models were considered acceptable when AvePP exceeded 0.70 and OCC exceeded 5.0 for all trajectory groups [29].

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between adiposity-trajectory groups and incident CMDs. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was formally evaluated for each Cox model (Models 2 and 3) using Schoenfeld residual tests with the Kaplan-Meier transform. For each model, we report the global χ² statistics and corresponding two-sided P-values. In addition, separate tests were conducted for the trajectory-group variables. A two-sided P-value > 0.05 was considered to indicate no statistically significant violation of the PH assumption.

Models were adjusted sequentially: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors; Model 3 additionally adjusted for medical history (hypertension, dyslipidemia, IGT, and chronic kidney, lung, and liver diseases). Model 2 was prespecified as the primary model because it controls for plausible confounders while avoiding over-adjustment for potential mediators (e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia) included in Model 3, which was reserved for sensitivity analysis. Given this potential mediating role, we additionally conducted formal mediation analyses with hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT as mediators between obesity trajectories and CMD outcomes. To preserve temporal ordering, mediators were defined using measurements obtained at the end of the trajectory assessment period, corresponding to the final wave used for trajectory modeling, and incident CMD outcomes were subsequently ascertained during follow-up.

Given the limited number of CMM events (n = 84), Firth’s penalized Cox proportional hazards regression was applied for CMM analyses to reduce potential small-sample bias and mitigate issues related to sparse data or separation. Penalized partial likelihood estimation was implemented using the coxphf package in R, which applies Firth’s bias-reduction method. Default penalization settings and convergence criteria provided by the package were used. HRs and 95% CIs were derived from penalized likelihood estimates.

To directly compare the strength of associations between indices for a given outcome, we contrasted the Model 2 log-HRs for the High-stable vs. Low-stable trajectory comparison. To quantify the potential public health benefit of reducing population-level adiposity, we calculated the PIF under three counterfactual scenarios. The PIF represents the proportional reduction in disease incidence that would occur if the population’s exposure distribution shifted from the observed pattern to a Low-stable pattern. The three scenarios were: (S1) all individuals shifted to the Low-stable trajectory group (theoretical maximum benefit); (S2) all individuals in the High-stable trajectory group shifting to the Moderate-stable trajectory group; and (S3) a realistic scenario where 10% of Moderate-stable group moved to the Low-stable group and 25% of High-stable group individuals moved to the Moderate-stable group. For each outcome and index, we obtained PIF point estimates and 95% CIs using a parametric bootstrap with 10,000 replicates, sampling log-HRs from normal distributions defined by their means and standard errors. The number of avoidable events was calculated by multiplying the PIF and its 95% CI limits by the observed number of incident cases.

Robustness and potential effect modification were evaluated in prespecified subgroups defined by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, smoking, drinking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT. Subgroup analyses were also based on Model 2, and the subgroup-defining variables were used solely for stratification rather than additional adjustment, so that primary causal interpretations rely on the overall Model 2 estimates. We also calculated E-values to assess the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding needed to fully explain the observed associations [30].

Missing data were handled according to variable type and analytical role. Participants with missing anthropometric measurements required for trajectory modeling, missing baseline covariates included in Model 2, or missing follow-up information were excluded using a complete-case approach, as illustrated in Fig. 1. For biochemical indicators used to define baseline hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT, missing values were imputed under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption using multiple imputation by chained equations. Five imputed datasets were generated, incorporating all variables included in the analytic models as well as outcome indicators to improve the plausibility of the MAR assumption. Estimates from analyses involving these variables (Model 3 and mediation analyses) were combined using Rubin’s rules. Primary analyses based on Model 2 were not affected by biochemical imputation.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3), and two-sided p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

We further compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants (Supplementary Table S5). Included participants were younger and exhibited lower SBP and DBP, as well as a lower prevalence of hypertension at baseline. Standardized mean differences indicated moderate imbalances in age, marital status, SBP, and hypertension. Overall, excluded participants demonstrated a relatively higher baseline cardiometabolic risk profile.

Trajectories of obesity indices

Model fit statistics comparing 2-, 3-, and 4-group trajectory solutions are presented in Supplementary Table S6. Overall, the 3-group model provided the best balance between model fit and parsimony across all obesity indices, supported by lower BIC values compared with the 2-group model and acceptable classification quality. Specifically, the minimum AvePP for each group exceeded 0.70, the minimum OCC was all > 5.0, and the smallest group proportion was consistently above 5%, indicating adequate model discrimination and stability. Therefore, a 3-group solution was selected for all indices to ensure comparability across measures.

Based on the selected 3-group models, BMI, WC, WHtR, and BRI each exhibited three relatively stable trajectory patterns, which were labeled as Low-stable, Moderate-stable, and High-stable (Fig. 2A–D). The proportions of participants in the Low-stable, Moderate-stable, and High-stable groups were 34.7%, 39.8%, and 25.5% for BMI; 39.4%, 42.1%, and 18.5% for WC; 38.4%, 41.2%, and 20.0% for WHtR; and 35.7%, 43.8%, and 20.5% for BRI, respectively. In contrast, ABSI demonstrated a distinct pattern, with one decreasing trajectory (High-to-low) and two relatively stable trajectories (Fig. 2E), accounting for 42.8%, 44.3%, and 12.9% of participants across the three groups, respectively. The 95% confidence bands around the trajectory curves further supported the separation and distinctiveness of the identified groups.

Trajectories of obesity indices and the risk of CMDs

During 20,889 person-years of follow-up, 379 cases of diabetes, 458 of heart disease, 252 of stroke, and 84 of CMM occurred. The global χ² statistics across Models 2 and 3 ranged from 8.54 to 20.05, and all corresponding two-sided P-values exceeded 0.05 (Supplementary Table S7). Separate tests for the trajectory-group variables likewise yielded P-values greater than 0.05 across all outcomes and models.

Visual inspection of the log-log survival curves showed no obvious departure from parallelism among trajectory groups (Supplementary Fig. S1). Taken together, these findings provide no evidence of violation of the PH assumption.

In Model 2, High-stable trajectories of BMI, WC, WHtR, and BRI were consistently associated with higher risks of all outcomes, whereas ABSI trajectories were null. For diabetes, central adiposity indices showed the steepest gradients: High-stable versus Low-stable WHtR (HR 3.32, 95% CI 2.48–4.44) and BRI (3.53, 2.61–4.77) exceeded BMI (2.66, 2.02–3.50) and WC (2.62, 1.97–3.48) (Fig. 3). For heart disease, risk elevations were modest but similar across indices (HRs ≈ 1.5–1.6). For stroke, HRs were around 2.1–2.3 for all four indices. For CMM, High-stable versus Low-stable trajectories yielded HRs of roughly 2.8–3.7. In Model 3, further adjusting for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT, estimates were attenuated but remained statistically significant for BMI, WC, WHtR, and BRI across outcomes, while ABSI stayed null; patterns were consistent with unadjusted models (Supplementary Tables S8–S11).

In mediation analyses, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT together accounted for about 7–18% of the associations between High-stable obesity trajectories and cardiometabolic diseases, consistent with their role as partial intermediates (Supplementary Table S12). Accordingly, Model 2, which does not adjust for these variables, was retained as the primary model, with Model 3 providing complementary controlled direct effects.

Pairwise Δβ comparisons (bootstrap) and TOST equivalence

Pairwise Δβ bootstrap comparisons of log-HRs showed clear differences for diabetes: WHtR and BRI were significantly stronger than BMI and WC after FDR correction (all q < 0.05), indicating greater risk gradients for central adiposity indices. No significant between-index differences emerged for heart disease or stroke, and contrasts for CMM were too imprecise to rank indices.

Equivalence testing supported practical interchangeability of some indices. WHtR and BRI met TOST criteria within ± 10% for heart disease and within ± 15% for stroke (Table 2), suggesting that these central adiposity measures perform similarly for macrovascular outcomes. Under the more realistic scenario (S3), PIFs were modest but non-trivial. For diabetes, BRI and WHtR yielded PIFs of 6.1% and 5.9% (≈ 23 and 22 avoidable cases); for CMM, 6.8% and 6.2% (≈ 6 and 5 cases). Sensitivity analysis

Because of limited CMM events, subgroup analyses focused on individual CMDs. Associations of Moderate-stable and High-stable trajectories of BMI, WC, WHtR, and BRI with higher CMD risk were broadly consistent across strata defined by age, sex, smoking, drinking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT (Supplementary Tables S14–S16).

Given the potential influence of menopausal status on body fat redistribution, we conducted an additional women-only sensitivity analysis with further adjustment for menopausal status. The associations between obesity trajectory groups and incident CMD outcomes remained largely unchanged after this adjustment (Supplementary Table S17), indicating that menopausal status did not materially confound the observed associations among women.

Significant interactions (P < 0.05) were observed for diabetes by sex (stronger WHtR and BRI associations in men), for heart disease by hypertension (stronger associations for all four indices in non-hypertensive participants), and for stroke by dyslipidemia (stronger associations in non-dyslipidemic participants) (Supplementary Tables S18–S20). Notably, the associations between High-stable adiposity trajectories and heart disease were evident primarily among participants without baseline hypertension, whereas corresponding associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant among those with hypertension. A similar pattern was observed for stroke in relation to dyslipidemia status. A forest plot (Fig. 4) for heart disease subgroups further illustrates these differences.

Sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of the main findings. For CMM, Firth’s penalized logistic models produced estimates similar in direction and magnitude to standard Cox models (Supplementary Table S21). When ABSI was analyzed as a continuous variable in 2015, no significant associations with any CMD were observed (Supplementary Table S22). E-values from Models 2 and 3 indicated that relatively strong unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain the observed associations, suggesting that the results are reasonably robust to unmeasured confounding (Supplementary Table S23).

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, persistently High-stable trajectories of BMI, WC, WHtR, and BRI were consistently associated with increased risks of incident diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and CMM. In contrast, trajectories of ABSI were not materially associated with these outcomes. Beyond index-specific estimates, we conducted within-cohort, head-to-head comparisons by contrasting bootstrap distributions of log-hazard ratios (Δβ) across indices and assessed practical interchangeability using equivalence testing. This framework distinguishes statistically detectable differences from clinically meaningful ones and supports outcome-specific selection of adiposity metrics rather than reliance on a single universal indicator.

Our findings align with growing trajectory-based evidence showing that long-term adiposity patterns provide clinically relevant information beyond single-time measurements. Population-based studies have linked adverse adiposity trajectories to increased cardiovascular risk, with heterogeneity across demographic or hormonal strata [31]. Prospective analyses of multiple adiposity indices similarly demonstrate that longitudinal phenotypes predict cardiovascular disease and mortality [32]. Evidence from the CHARLS further indicates that higher BRI trajectories are associated with incident cardiovascular disease and may improve risk stratification [14]. Together, these studies contextualize our findings and underscore the value of comparing adiposity indices within a unified analytic framework.

The stronger associations of WHtR and BRI with diabetes likely reflect their closer approximation of visceral and ectopic adiposity—metabolically active fat depots directly implicated in insulin resistance and dysglycemia. WHtR standardizes waist size by height and may better capture disproportionate abdominal fat accumulation [9, 33]. BRI, derived from waist and height through a geometric model, correlates with overall and visceral adiposity and may better represent abdominal volume than BMI alone [34]. Excess visceral and ectopic fat promotes hepatic steatosis, chronic inflammation, adipokine dysregulation, and lipotoxicity, thereby impairing insulin signaling and accelerating progression to type 2 diabetes [35, 36]. These mechanisms may be particularly relevant in Asian populations, where visceral fat accumulates at lower BMI levels. Accordingly, central adiposity trajectories may more directly capture glycemia-related risk pathways than general adiposity measures.

The selection of Model 2 as the primary model was based on a causal framework. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and IGT may lie along the pathway linking long-term adiposity to cardiometabolic events. Adjusting for such intermediates can attenuate total effects and obscure etiologically relevant mechanisms [37, 38]. Model 2 therefore aimed to estimate a more policy-relevant total association, whereas Model 3—treating these factors as confounders—approximates a controlled direct effect. In our analyses, additional adjustment (Model 3) attenuated effect sizes without reversing directions, consistent with a pattern suggestive of partial mediation rather than complete confounding. However, given the observational design and the limited number of repeated measurements, some degree of temporal overlap between exposure trajectories and mediators cannot be entirely ruled out given the limited frequency of repeated measurements, although mediators were defined to precede outcome ascertainment. Therefore, mediation findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

We observed effect modification for heart disease by hypertension status and for stroke by dyslipidemia status, with stronger associations among participants without these conditions. Hypertension and dyslipidemia are strong independent drivers of macrovascular events; once present, baseline risk is elevated, and the relative contribution of adiposity trajectories may appear smaller. Moreover, if these conditions partially mediate adiposity effects, their presence may indicate that part of the pathway has already been activated. Finally, individuals with diagnosed cardiometabolic conditions may receive more intensive treatment and lifestyle counseling, potentially attenuating subsequent vascular risk and observed associations.

Age-related body composition may further influence CMM development. Sarcopenic obesity—the coexistence of excess adiposity and reduced muscle mass or function—is associated with worse cardiometabolic outcomes than either condition alone [39–41]. While central adiposity indices capture abdominal fat, they do not quantify muscle loss; thus, sarcopenic obesity may represent an overlapping pathway amplifying progression from single cardiometabolic disease to multimorbidity. Although certain pathways (e.g., sarcopenic obesity) could not be formally evaluated as mediators in this study, this hypothesis warrants evaluation in studies incorporating repeated adiposity and muscle measurements.

Some Low-stable trajectory groups showed higher prevalences of smoking and alcohol use. This pattern may reflect behavioral clustering, residual confounding, or reverse causation (e.g., weight gain after smoking cessation alongside reduced smoking-related vascular risk), underscoring the importance of time-updated lifestyle data in future research.

The null associations for ABSI warrant consideration. ABSI was designed to capture body shape independently of BMI and complement general adiposity measures [42]. However, this mathematical independence may also limit its ability to reflect absolute central fat accumulation. In contrast, indices that directly incorporate waist circumference (such as WHtR and BRI) may more closely approximate visceral adiposity, which is more strongly linked to cardiometabolic risk in this population. Prior evidence suggests that ABSI may perform more consistently for mortality prediction than for specific cardiometabolic outcomes [43]. In older adults, sarcopenia, fat redistribution, and height loss may further weaken the link between ABSI and metabolically harmful visceral fat. WHtR and BRI more directly reflect height-standardized abdominal volume, closely linked to visceral adiposity. This distinction between body shape and visceral fat burden may explain why central adiposity indices showed clearer gradients for CMM risk in our cohort.

Using Δβ contrasts and equivalence testing, we found that differences between indices for heart disease and stroke were small and frequently fell within prespecified equivalence margins. These findings indicate limited practical differences among measures for macrovascular risk assessment. Statistical equivalence, however, does not imply identical clinical performance [16]. When effect estimates cluster near unity, large samples may detect small differences unlikely to influence clinical decisions, whereas results may be sensitive to precision when confidence intervals approach equivalence boundaries. Thus, our findings suggest pragmatic comparability rather than strict interchangeability across settings.

A further strength of this study is the translation of relative associations into potential population-level benefits using the PIF. Under hypothetical trajectory-shift scenarios, if individuals maintained stable Low-stable central adiposity trajectories, a proportion of future CMM events could theoretically be prevented under the model assumptions [17, 44]. This framework links trajectory patterns to actionable prevention targets. However, PIF estimates inherently rely on causal assumptions that cannot be fully verified in observational research. Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted as scenario-based projections under specified assumptions rather than precise causal effects or guaranteed clinical outcomes.

From a clinical and public-health perspective, our findings support a pragmatic measurement strategy rather than identification of a single superior index. Despite its recognized limitations in capturing fat distribution, BMI remains widely standardized and used across heterogeneous cardiovascular populations, including acute coronary syndrome settings [45]. Rather than replacing BMI, central adiposity indices may complement it when greater discrimination is required for specific outcomes. In primary care and community screening, WC and WHtR can be measured rapidly with minimal resources and may provide actionable information, particularly when glycemic or multimorbidity prevention is prioritized. A tiered approach—BMI for general risk communication, with WHtR or BRI added for refined metabolic risk stratification—may enhance targeted prevention and improve efficiency at scale.

Advantages and limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, we leveraged a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults with repeated anthropometric measurements, allowing trajectory-based characterization of long-term adiposity patterns rather than reliance on single-time-point indicators. This longitudinal perspective enhances etiological interpretability and better reflects real-world exposure dynamics. Second, we implemented a unified within-cohort, head-to-head comparison framework—including Δβ contrasts, equivalence testing, and impact projections based on the PIF, which enabled a more rigorous evaluation of both relative effect sizes and practical interchangeability across adiposity indices. By integrating statistical comparison with translational metrics, our approach provides a structured template for aligning measurement choice with prevention priorities in population-based research and clinical practice.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the number of incident CMM events was modest, which may limit statistical precision and widen confidence intervals, particularly in subgroup analyses. Although Firth-type penalized methods were applied to reduce small-sample bias, CMM-related estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution and confirmed in studies with larger event numbers and longer follow-up durations. Second, outcomes were based on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce nondifferential misclassification. Reassuringly, associations were directionally consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. Third, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded because certain lifestyle factors (e.g., detailed diet quality, physical activity intensity, and medication adherence) were not comprehensively captured or available as time-updated variables. Finally, the limited number of repeated anthropometric assessments and the spacing between survey waves constrained our ability to formally evaluate high-frequency weight cycling. Future studies with denser measurement intervals and extended follow-up will be important to further clarify dynamic adiposity variability and its cardiometabolic consequences. In addition, datasets incorporating repeated biochemical measurements and comprehensive anthropometric assessments—including hip circumference—would enable evaluation of indices such as the visceral adiposity index (VAI) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), thereby providing a more comprehensive characterization of cardiometabolic risk[46].

Conclusions

Overall, in this nationally representative Chinese cohort, persistently High-stable trajectories of general and central adiposity were associated with elevated risks of CMDs and CMM, whereas ABSI trajectories showed limited discriminatory value. Head-to-head comparisons suggest that central adiposity indices may be particularly informative for glycemic risk, while several measures appear broadly comparable for macrovascular outcomes within the precision of this study. These findings support an outcome-specific, pragmatic approach to adiposity assessment in routine clinical and public-health practice.
