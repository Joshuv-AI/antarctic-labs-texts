Noncovalent SARS-CoV-2 main protease inhibitors targeting the catalytic dyad and primed substrate binding subsites
Authors: Dipendra Bhandari, Katerina Kovalevskaya, Leighton Coates, Colleen B. Jonsson, Jyothi Parvathareddy, Kevin L. Weiss, Annie Aniana, John M. Louis, Peter V. Bonnesen, Andrey Kovalevsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SARS-CoV-2 main protease (MPro) is a proven target for drug discovery of small-molecule antiviral agents due to its crucial role in viral polyprotein processing, high structural conservation across numerous divergent variants, and the lack of similar human enzymes. Unlike covalent compounds, noncovalent inhibitors of MPro do not modify the enzyme's active site, and may offer improved safety profiles, greater chemical tractability, and better oral bioavailability without the need for pharmacokinetic enhancement. In this study, we designed, synthesized and characterized thirteen noncovalent nonpeptidic SARS-CoV-2 MPro inhibitors clustered into two series (KK and KB) of compounds. The inhibitors were designed based on our recently discovered Mcule-5948770040 and its analogue HL-3-68 designed through the structure–activity relationship study. To obtain atomic details of the inhibitors' binding we solved room-temperature X-ray structures of the MPro/inhibitor complexes, and to quantify their binding and antiviral properties we performed in vitro DSF and ITC measurements and TCID50 antiviral assays. In addition, a room-temperature neutron structure of the MPro/KB-5 complex allowed direct determination of hydrogen positions, mapping intermolecular interactions and directly visualizing the protonation states and hydrogen bonding. Improved binding affinities of KK-7 and KB-3 through KB-6 could be attributed to the observed nonconventional S–H⋯F hydrogen bond and an additional conventional hydrogen bond between the carboxamide moieties and Q189. Our study provides binding details for the designed compounds and demonstrates the feasibility of our joint X-ray/neutron structure-assisted drug design approach to generate more potent noncovalent nonpeptidic MPro inhibitors.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by the novel severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has resulted in unprecedented global health and economic damage since its emergence in late 2019.1–3 While the World Health Organization officially ended the global emergency status for COVID-19 several years ago, the virus has not disappeared but rather transitioned into an endemic phase, characterized by persistent waves of infection and the looming threat of “Long COVID” sequelae.4 In response, extensive efforts have been directed towards disease mitigation, including the rapid development of vaccines and antiviral agents. Although vaccines have helped to substantially reduce the severity and transmission of the disease, their effectiveness has been challenged by the rapid evolution of SARS-CoV-2, especially due to mutations in the spike protein.5–8 The rise of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOCs), from Alpha to the highly transmissible Omicron sub-lineages, has demonstrated the virus's ability to escape neutralizing antibodies generated by vaccination or previous infection.9 This ongoing antigenic drift, with the appearance of the highly divergent Omicron strain called Cicada (BA.3.2),10,11 demonstrates that approaches relying chiefly on vaccine-induced immunity are inherently fragile, as they can be compromised by antigenic changes in the spike protein. Consequently, there remains a critical need for potent direct-acting antivirals that target conserved viral components less susceptible to resistance, mutation, and evolutionary pressure.

Across the spectrum of SARS-CoV-2 proteins targeted for the design and development of therapeutic agents, including RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), papain-like protease (PLpro), helicase (nsp13), endoribonuclease (nsp15), methyltransferases (nsp14 and nsp16), and various accessory factors, the main protease (MPro, also known as 3CLpro) has emerged as the most promising candidate for small-molecule antiviral strategies.12–14 Currently, MPro remains a central target for drug discovery due to its recognized vital role in viral polyprotein processing, high structural conservation across vaccine-resistant variants, and absence of closely related analogs in humans.14,15 Upon host cell entry, the viral genomic RNA is translated into two large polyproteins, pp1a and pp1ab, which contain the non-structural proteins (nsps) required for the viral replication–transcription complex assembly.13 MPro, expressed as part of both pp1a and pp1b, cleaves these large polyproteins at no fewer than 11 specific sites, releasing functional proteins nsp4 through nsp16 (ref. 13, 16 and 17) after it autoprocesses its own release.18 Without the MPro's proteolytic activity, the virus is unable to replicate. Critically, MPro recognizes a highly specific cleavage motif that requires a glutamine residue at the P1 position.19,20 Because no human protease is known to share this strict glutamine-directed specificity, inhibitors targeting MPro offer a strong potential for high selectivity and reduced risk of host-related toxicity,21–23 even though MPro is capable of cleaving human immune signaling proteins potentially contributing to the virus's ability to deregulate the host immune response.24

Structurally, SARS-CoV-2 MPro is a chymotrypsin-like cysteine protease25,26 that functions as a homodimer in its mature form. Each protomer is composed of three domains: domains I (residues 8–101) and II (residues 102–184) constitute the catalytic domain that adopts an antiparallel β-barrel fold and creates the chymotrypsin-like substrate-binding cleft, whereas the helical domain III (residues 201–306) is a cluster of five α-helices that regulates dimerization.27 At the heart of the active site lies the catalytic dyad comprised of C145 and H41. The peptide bond cleavage reaction mechanism proceeds via general-base catalysis, in which the deprotonated C145 and protonated H41 catalytic residues enable efficient attack on the substrate by the thiolate nucleophile and downstream proton transfers.28–30 During catalysis, the oxyanion intermediate is stabilized by the “oxyanion hole”, formed by the backbone amide NH groups of G143, S144, and C145.31

Historically, the development of protease inhibitors has been a cornerstone of antiviral therapy, most notably in the management of HIV and the hepatitis C virus (HCV). In both HIV and HCV, blocking the viral protease effectively disrupts the maturation of essential viral proteins, thereby halting replication.32,33 This therapeutic principle quickly guided efforts to battle SARS-CoV-2, with multiple classes of MPro inhibitors pursued, including both covalent and noncovalent small molecules.23,34–37 For instance, the covalent peptidomimetic inhibitor nirmatrelvir (NMV), marketed as PaxlovidTM by Pfizer,38 was designed based on the chemical scaffold of HCV protease inhibitor boceprevir. Nirmatrelvir features a nitrile “warhead” that forms a reversible, yet highly stable,18 thioimidate adduct with the catalytic C145. In addition, a nonpeptidic noncovalent inhibitor, ensitrelvir (ESV, sold under the brand name Xocova®),39 is approved for clinical use to treat COVID-19 in Japan and for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) in the United States. The covalent peptidomimetic inhibitors bearing electrophilic warheads (e.g., aldehyde, α-ketoamide, nitrile, etc.) designed to react with C145 often face significant limitations in drug development. These challenges include intrinsic chemical reactivity that can lead to indiscriminate binding to host proteins, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation by host enzymes, and suboptimal physico-chemical properties that necessitate complex formulation strategies.40 For instance, NMV exhibits low metabolic stability and rapid clearance, necessitating co-administration with ritonavir, a potent inhibitor of cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4).41,42 Additionally, the widespread use of a single agent inevitably imposes selective pressure on the virus. Recent surveillance data and in vitro evolution studies have already identified MPro mutations (e.g., E166V, L50F) that confer partial resistance to NMV, threatening the longevity of the current therapeutic arsenal.43–46

In contrast, noncovalent small-molecule inhibitors avoid covalent protein modification and generally offer improved safety profiles, greater chemical tractability, and better prospects for oral bioavailability without the need for pharmacokinetic enhancement.47–49 By relying on optimized van der Waals interactions, hydrogen bonding networks, and π–π stacking interactions within the substrate-binding cleft, noncovalent inhibitors can exploit unique binding modes, potentially maintaining their effectiveness against mutant variants resistant to covalent binders.50–52 The recent design and development of ESV demonstrate the viability of the noncovalent approach.39 However, the global chemical space for noncovalent MPro scaffolds remains underexplored compared to their covalent counterparts. The design of novel noncovalent MPro inhibitors with high potency, broad-spectrum activity against VOCs, and standalone oral bioavailability represents a critical frontier in medicinal chemistry. As a result, noncovalent MPro inhibitors have emerged as an attractive and increasingly prioritized strategy for developing next-generation antiviral therapeutics against SARS-CoV-2.52,53

In this study, we report on the design, synthesis and characterization of noncovalent SARS-CoV-2 MPro inhibitors, code-named the KK and KB series (Fig. 1). Both inhibitor series were designed based on our recently reported noncovalent small molecule Mcule-5948770040 (HL-3-47), discovered through a scalable high-throughput virtual screening,54 and its subsequent structure-assisted expansion via a structure-activity relationship study.55 To elucidate the inhibitors' binding modes within the MPro active site, we obtained room temperature X-ray structures of the enzyme–inhibitor complexes at resolutions of 1.8–2.0 Å. In addition, a room-temperature neutron structure of MPro in complex with KB-5 was obtained at the resolution of 2.3 Å. The neutron structure permitted us to determine the positions of all hydrogen atoms, mapping atomic details of the inhibitor interactions with the enzyme and directly visualizing the protonation states and hydrogen bonding. We detected nonconventional S–H⋯Halide (F, Cl) hydrogen bonds formed between the P1 group of inhibitors KK-1, KK-2, KK-7, KB-4, KB-5, KB-6 and the C145 thiol group, and additional noncovalent interactions with the substrate binding subsites S1′ and S2′ made by the sulfonamide or amide moieties of the inhibitors in the KB series. Inhibitor KK-7, and inhibitors KB-3 through KB-6 demonstrated improved in vitro binding properties relative to the parent compounds, as evidenced by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements, and showed moderate inhibition of the virus replication in the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assays lowering the viral load by ∼2–3 log units. Thus, our study provides atomic resolution structural and ligand binding details for the designed noncovalent inhibitors of SARS-CoV-2 MPro and shows the viability of our approach to expand their structure into the primed substrate binding subsites.

Results

Design of KK and KB inhibitor series

We previously reported the discovery of Mcule-5948770040 using a scalable high-throughput virtual screening framework and a targeted compound library (Fig. 1A).54 Mcule-5948770040 (HL-3-47) is a noncovalent inhibitor of MPro, consisting of the uracil P1 group, piperazine linker, and aromatic P2 moiety that are positioned to interact with the substrate binding subsites S1 and S2, and the oxyanion hole in the catalytic site (Fig. 1B). We then initiated a structure–activity relationship study designing substitutions in the P2 group and chemically modifying piperazine and uracil moieties55 resulting in compound HL-3-68. HL-3-68 demonstrated slightly improved binding affinity to MPro compared to the parent HL-3-47 molecule, which nonetheless was around 1 μM by ITC. To further improve binding affinity, additional substituents must be introduced into the structures of these molecules that would interact with other binding subsites. In fact, a previous study led by Zhan and colleagues56 built upon our HL-3-47 discovery by replacing the P1 uracil with different heterocycles and adding N-substituted carboxamide groups to the piperazine carbon vicinal to the aniline nitrogen bonded to the aromatic P2 group. Some of the Zhan group's compounds showed improved MPro binding affinities compared to the parent molecule, with the added substituents protruding into subsite S4.

In this study, our noncovalent inhibitor design strategy was inspired by the possibility of further engaging the catalytic dyad through new noncovalent interactions with the P1 group and by the opportunity to explore binding to the primed substrate binding subsites S1′–S2′. The MPro active site recognizes substrates containing chemical groups at positions P5 through P3′ that occupy substrate binding subsites S5–S3′ (Fig. 1B),57–59 but few inhibitors have been designed to target the primed subsites.39,51,60 We, therefore, initially designed the KK inhibitor series using HL-3-68 as the parent molecule (Fig. 1A). We introduced substituents to the P1 uracil group specifically to interact with C145 (i.e. halogens) and also to probe whether bulkier chemical groups (i.e. CH3 and OCH3) affect inhibitor binding. For example, single halogen substitutions to a P1′ group have been shown to improve inhibitor binding affinity through specific interactions with MPro residues.61 In addition, for KK-6 and KK-7 compounds, we applied the deuterium switch by replacing the piperazine ring with its perdeuterated isotopologue (piperazine-d8). Next, we modified the aromatic dichlorophenyl P2 group of HL-3-47 as the parent compound to create the KB inhibitor series by adding sulfonamide or carboxamide substitutions at the ortho-to-piperazine position. This was achieved through an initial introduction of an NO2 moiety on the aromatic nucleus, and subsequent reduction to an NH2 group, to provide a site for the attachment of the selected sulfonyl or acyl moiety (Fig. 1A), prior to the attachment of orotic acid (KB-1, KB-2, KB-3), or 5-fluoroorotic acid (KB-4, KB-5, KB-6). For comparative purposes, the synthetic intermediates KB-0-1, containing the nitro group, and KB-0-2, with the unfunctionalized amine attached to the P2 group, were also screened for MPro inhibition. As 1,2,3-trichloro-5-fluoro-4-nitrobenzene was not available, we did not prepare analogous derivatives of HL-3-68. Deuteration was also incorporated into the KB inhibitor series, with KB-5 containing piperazine-d8 and cyclopropyl-d5. Therefore, compounds KK-6, KK-7 and KB-5 are bioisosteric to HL-3-47, KK-1 and KB-4, respectively. Deuteration of small-molecule drugs has been shown to increase their chemical stability to oxidative metabolism caused by cytochrome P450 and aldehyde oxidase enzymes, and to slow down enantiomerization and epimerization of chiral carbon centers.62–64 In some cases, such as a notable example of deutetrabenazine prescribed for chorea associated with Huntington's disease and tardive dyskinesia treatment, deuteration significantly improved the drug's pharmacokinetic properties.65 As a result, several deuterated SARS-CoV-2 MPro inhibitors, including ESV66 and nirmatrelvir (NMV),67 have already been designed,68,69 with some showing improved human microsomal stability.69 On the other hand, deuteration is not expected to influence the small-molecule inhibitor's affinity to the target protein, because of similar electronic properties, shape, size and conformational flexibility of the deuterated analogue relative to its hydrogenated counterpart, although some recent studies challenge this conventional wisdom.70

Inhibitor binding by DSF

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) profiles of MPro in the absence and presence of inhibitors are plotted in Fig. 2, and the corresponding Tm and ΔTm values are listed below the plots. Tm of MPro with no inhibitor added is 53.9 °C in agreement with the previously published values,71,72 and any increase in Tm relative to this value in the presence of an inhibitor correlates with the strength of binding. Binding of HL-3-68, the parent compound for the KK series, increases the melting temperature by ΔTm of 5.2°. Addition of F to the uracil P1 group in KK-1 and/or replacement of piperazine with piperazine-d8 in KK-6 and KK-7 does not have an additional effect on ΔTm. Conversely, ΔTm for KK-3 and KK-5 are very small and indicative of weak binding, as anticipated for bulkier substituents CH3 and OCH3 introduced into the P1 group. Substitution of F, as in KK-1 and KK-7, with sterically larger halides Cl (KK-2) or Br (KK-4), acts unfavorably with decreased ΔTm values, as expected. HL-3-47 is the parent compound for the KB series. It showed a slightly lower ΔTm of 3.6° than did HL-3-68. The sulfonamide derivatives KB-1 and KB-2 did not demonstrate sizable increases in ΔTm relative to HL-3-47. Conversely, binding of the carboxamide analogues (KB-3–KB-6) increased the MPro resistance to unfolding in the range of 5.3–7.3°, comparable or slightly better than that for KK-6 and KK-7 inhibitors. For comparison, the ΔTm value for MPro/ESV complex is 18.9°,71,72 implying this clinical inhibitor has a substantially better binding affinity to MPro than do the KK or KB compounds.

Binding thermodynamics and inhibitor dissociation constants (Kd) by ITC

To determine the dissociation constants (Kd) and assess the binding thermodynamics of the designed noncovalent inhibitors we performed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) with the parent compounds and selected molecules from the KK and KB series (Table 1 and Fig. S1). We also carried out ITC measurements with compounds KB-0-1 and KB-0-2 to gauge the possible improvement in binding properties of the KB series relative to the two precursor molecules. The parent compounds HL-3-47 and HL-3-68 have high-nM binding affinity to MPro, with Kd values of 0.7 ± 0.07 and 0.75 ± 0.11 μM, respectively. Introduction of F to the P1 uracil group and substitution of the protiated linker piperazine with its deuterated version had a modest positive effect on the binding affinity of KK-7 (Kd = 0.47 ± 0.07 μM) compared to HL-3-68, even though the ΔTm was largely unchanged. When the P2 substituent of HL-3-47 is derivatized with NO2 or NH2 groups to obtain KB-0-1 and KB-0-2 precursors of the KB series, the Kd values increase by about 3-fold, indicating such substitutions alone are unfavorable to the binding affinities. Surprisingly, adding tosyl group to the P2 aromatic amine to generate the sulfonamide compound KB-1 further reduced the binding affinity, which nonetheless agreed well with its very small ΔTm of 1.8°.

However, replacing the tolyl moiety in KB-1 with cyclopropyl (KB-2) resulted in binding affinity equivalent to HL-3-47. Replacing the sulfonamide linkage in KB-2 with a carboxamide linkage (KB-3) resulted in a marked improvement in the binding affinity, compared to both KB-0-2 and HL-3-47 (Table 1). Further improvements were observed for carboxamide inhibitors KB-4 through KB-6. In agreement with the increased ΔTm values, Kd for KB-3 (0.25 ± 0.02 μM) and KB-4 (0.26 ± 0.04 μM), both containing cyclopropane carboxamide groups, improved by about 3-fold relative to HL-3-47 and by almost 7-fold relative to KB-0-2. Modifications to KB-4, specifically deuteration of both the piperazine and cyclopropyl moieties to form KB-5 (0.20 ± 0.02 μM) or swapping the cyclopentyl for a cyclopropyl group to yield KB-6 (0.19 ± 0.03 μM), resulted in a slight further improvement in their binding affinities. Of note, the Kd values for KB-5 and KB-6 are an order of magnitude lower than those for KB-0-1 and KB-0-2 and are comparable to that of the covalent inhibitor GC-373 (0.15 ± 0.03 μM).18

A very unusual property of the noncovalent inhibitors, including the clinical drug ESV, is a considerable unfavorable binding entropy (ΔS) that has to be offset by a large binding enthalpy (ΔH) according to ITC. This is in contrast to many covalent MPro inhibitors that show moderately favorable ΔS of binding.31,72 Only the sulfonamide-containing KB-1 compound binds to MPro with a favorable ΔS, but the small ΔH of binding leads to a weak binding affinity (Kd = 2.8 ± 1.4 μM).

Antiviral assays

To assess the potency of the synthesized compounds against the wild-type SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan strain) we tested KK7 and KB-3–KB-6 in Vero E6 cells using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay. The results are plotted in Fig. S2. All compounds demonstrated a measurable antiviral activity, with the viral load reduction of ∼2–3 log units. Importantly, the parent compounds showed no antiviral activity, as reported previously.55 KB-5 and KB-6 demonstrated a slightly higher inhibition potency relative to the other molecules, in agreement with their increased binding affinity to MPro. The noncovalent clinical drug ESV, which was chosen as a positive control, inhibited the virus replication with the titer reduction of ∼6 log units.

Substituted P1 group in KK inhibitor series makes new interactions with catalytic C145

To shed light on the possible structural effects caused by adding substituents to the uracil P1 group of HL-3-68 we have generated room-temperature X-ray structures of MPro co-crystallized with KK-1, KK-2, KK-3 and KK-7 at the resolutions of 1.8–2.0 Å (Table S1, Fig. 3 and S3). KK-7, a deuterated analogue of KK-1, was chosen to visualize any structural perturbations due to replacing the hydrogenated linker piperazine with piperazine-d8. We did not obtain crystal structures with the other compounds from the KK series because they were significantly less potent. Comparisons of these structures with MPro/HL-3-68 (PDB ID 7RLS)55 provide insights into whether substitutions in the uracil P1 group and deuteration alter binding of the KK compounds and correlate with their inhibitory potencies.

KK inhibitors in the four determined structures bind in an identical fashion, spanning subsites S1 and S2, and the catalytic site, including the oxyanion hole. The inhibitors are anchored by direct conventional H bonds made by the uracil P1 groups with H163 and E166, and by the linker's carbonyl with the oxyanion hole's G143 NH amide (Fig. 3 and S3). The P1 groups of KK-1, KK-2 and KK-7 also engage the side chains of N142 through water-mediated contacts, which are not invariant and are replaced with a direct H bond in the KK-3 complex. In addition, the P1 group's fluorine substituents in KK-1 and KK-7 participate in possible nonconventional S–H⋯F H bonds of 3.2 Å in both structures with the thiol side chains of C145. The P1 group's chlorine in KK-2 forms a similar S–H⋯Cl interaction of 3.3 Å. Nevertheless, such interactions are considered significantly weaker than conventional H bonding.73,74 The P1 group's CH3 substituent in KK-3 cannot form an H bond with C145, instead making a hydrophobic contact with the thiol side chain, with the S⋯C distance of 3.3 Å. The trichlorobenzene P2 groups fit securely within the S2 subsites, sandwiched between H41 and Q189, except in the KK-2 complex, and surrounded by C44, M49, P52, and Y54 (Fig. 3 and S3). As expected, deuteration of the piperazine linker has no effect on the KK-7 binding to MPro relative to that of KK-1, and the two complexes MPro/KK-1 and MPro/KK-7 superimpose with an RMSD of 0.08 Å. The overall enzyme structures are not affected by the F or CH3 substitutions in the P1 groups of the KK inhibitors, with the MPro/KK-1, MPro/KK-2 and MPro/KK-3 superimposing onto MPro/HL-3-68 with RMSDs of 0.14, 0.20 and 0.13 Å. The slight shifts in all KK complexes are for the P1 groups of 0.2-0.3 Å away from H163 and C145 leading to minor and likely insignificant elongations in the H bonds with H163 compared to those in MPro/HL-3-68 (Fig. S3), considering the resolutions of the X-ray structures. In the KK-2 and KK-3 complexes, C145 thiols move significantly by 0.5–0.6 Å away from the P1 groups, concurrently with the P1 group motion, which is necessary to accommodate the bulkier substituents.

Inhibitors in KB series interact with subsite S1′–S2′ region

To gain structural insights into the marked binding affinity differences between the sulfonamide and carboxamide derivatives within the KB inhibitor series, and to compare their binding modes to that of the parent HL-3-47, we have obtained X-ray structures of MPro co-crystallized with all KB inhibitors at the resolutions of 1.8–2.0 Å (Table S1, Fig. 4 and S4), and a joint X-ray/neutron structure of the MPro/KB-5 complex as described below. For comparison, crystal structures of MPro complexed with KB-0-1 and KB-0-2 were also determined (Table S1), showing strong electron density for these precursor molecules (Fig. S4).

The sulfonamide derivatives KB-1 and KB-2 bind to the MPro active site in a manner similar to HL-3-47, KB-0-2, and KK inhibitors, forming comparable hydrogen bonds. The extended sulfonyl groups, however, were observed in two alternate conformations with the occupancies of 0.5/0.5 (Fig. S5). In one orientation, the KB-1 tolyl and KB-2 cyclopropyl sulfonyl groups are rotated below the aromatic P2 group's plane, pointing into the S1′–S2′ region containing a distinctive threonine-rich β-hairpin motif that stretches from T21 to T26. The tolyl substituent makes a hydrophilic contact with the main chain carbonyl of T26 and several short hydrophobic interactions with T25, L27 and H41, some of which might be repulsive as the C⋯C distances are measured below 4 Å. The cyclopropyl sulfonyl substituent, owing to its much smaller steric volume, closely interacts with the main chain carbonyls of C44 and H41 while also being very close to the H41 side chain. As with tolyl, such close contacts are likely unfavorable. In the other orientation, which is related by an almost 180° flip of the NH group, both sulfonamides are placed above the P2 group's plane within the water-exposed S3 subsite and are near the side chains of M49 and Q189. The likely unfavorable hydrophobic contacts formed by KB-1 may explain the significant loss of binding affinity relative to the parent compound as measured by ITC (Table 1).

Unlike KB-1 and KB-2, the electron density maps of the carboxamide-containing inhibitors KB-3, KB-4 and KB-6 indicate no positional disorder of the carboxamide arm (Fig. 4 and S6). Because the amide group is planar, the cyclopropyl substituents of KB-3 and KB-4, and the cyclopentyl group of KB-6, extend farther into the S2′ site than the sulfonamide groups. Again, the H bonds formed between the P1 uracil and the central carbonyl with the S1 subsite and the oxyanion hole, respectively, are similar to those made by other inhibitors. As observed in the KK-1 and KK-7 complexes, inhibitors KB-4 and KB-6 form nonconventional S–H⋯F hydrogen bonds connecting the P1 uracil to the catalytic C145 that is absent in the KB-3 complex. In addition, the carboxamide groups of KB-4 and KB-6 form direct hydrogen bonds with Q189 side chain and have water-mediated contacts with both side and main chains of H41. These extra hydrophilic interactions may be the reason for the improved binding affinities of the four carboxamide analogues compared to the other inhibitors studied. Importantly, neither cyclopropyl nor cyclopentyl groups have close, unfavorable, hydrophobic interactions with the nearby residues.

Protonation states and hydrogen bonding in MPro/KB-5 complex

To visualize noncovalent interactions and map the electrostatic profile of the MPro active site upon inhibitor binding we determined a 2.3 Å room-temperature neutron structure of the MPro/KB-5 complex jointly refined with the 1.9 Å room-temperature X-ray data collected from the same crystal. MPro was partially deuterated and KB-5 contains piperazine-d8 and cyclopropyl-d5 substituents that improve the diffraction signal-to-noise and detection of H atoms observed as deuterium atoms (D) in the neutron scattering length density maps. The neutron structure revealed the positions of D atoms, allowing accurate assignment of protonation states on the ionizable amino acid side chains. For clarity, the hydrogen bonds visualized in the neutron structure are described by measuring the distances between a D atom belonging to a hydrogen bond donor and a heavy atom from an H bond acceptor, as opposed to distances between heavy atoms normally reported for X-ray structures that lack H atom positions.

Fig. 5 depicts the neutron scattering density maps for KB-5, several active site residues that determine electrostatics, including catalytic C145 and H41, ionizable H163 and H164, and hydration water molecules as D2O. The D atoms on piperazine-d8 and cyclopropyl-d5 are clearly visible. The substituted P1 uracil group inserts into the S1 subsite making a 1.9 Å H bond with H163, which is doubly protonated, and thus positively charged (Fig. 5A). The orientation of H163 is anchored by an additional H bond with Y161 and a closely positioned neutral H172 (Fig. 5B). C145 is observed in the protonated, neutral thiol state, with the S–D bond directed towards the P1 uracil F substituent. The S–D⋯F distance is measured at 2.6 Å indicating the nonconventional H bond formation. P1 uracil is also engaged in a water-mediated interaction with N142, which is situated next to the oxyanion hole. The carbonyl group connecting the P1 uracil and piperazine is placed inside the oxyanion hole, but it hydrogen bonds only with the main-chain amide ND of G143, with a distance of 2.0 Å (Fig. 5B). This carbonyl is also hydrated by a chain of water molecules (W1–W3) positioned along the piperazine linker. W1 accepts the C145 main-chain amide D in a 1.8 Å hydrogen bond. W1 forms an additional hydrogen bond with W2, which does not interact with W3. W3 donates its D to form a hydrogen bond with H41 (Fig. 5C). The carbonyl of the carboxamide substituent in KB-5 forms a 2.0 Å H bond with Q189 whose side chain lies parallel to the inhibitor's aromatic plane. The catalytic H41 is singly protonated on its Nε2 atom, which makes a 2.0 Å H bond with the H164 main-chain carbonyl, determining the H41 side-chain imidazole orientation. H164 is also singly protonated, and its imidazole side chain adopts a tautomeric form identical to that of H41. The active site protonation states observed in the MPro/KB-5 complex are identical to those found in our previous neutron structure of MPro in complex with HL-3-47, with only H163 possessing a positive charge.55

(A–C) Neutron scattering length density maps contoured at the 1.2 σ level are depicted as brown mesh, with hydrogen bonds shown as black dashed lines. Atoms are colored by atom type, with carbon atoms in yellow for KB-5 and green for protein residues, and D atoms in light gray. Fluorine in uracil groups is colored cyan. Distances are in Ångstrom.

Discussion

Although the COVID-19 pandemic ended a few years ago,75 the most prudent option to prepare for the inevitable future is to continue the design and development of small-molecule antiviral compounds against SARS-CoV-2 and its perpetually evolving variants. SARS-CoV-2 MPro has demonstrated to be a valuable target for therapeutics23 that are not prone to the mutations in the virus's spike protein conferring resistance to available vaccines or prior infections. Studying structure, function and modes of MPro inhibition, including covalent and noncovalent ligand binding, at the atomic level is crucial to design novel antiviral agents. New inhibitors need to exploit not only conventional noncovalent interactions, such as H bonds and hydrophobic contacts, but also nonconventional interactions, including C–H⋯O H bonds, various X–H⋯π or π⋯π interactions, where X = C, O, N, etc., and interactions involving halogens.76 We have been using room-temperature X-ray and neutron crystallography to develop a detailed understanding of the MPro active site in both inhibitor-free and inhibitor-bound forms.28,31,55,77 In the current study, we expanded on the design of noncovalent inhibitors, exploring substitutions in the P1 and P2 groups, and deuteration of noncovalent, nonpeptidic HL-3-47 and HL-3-68 compounds to generate two series of inhibitors (Fig. 1A). Adding substituents that face the catalytic C145 thiol to the P1 uracil within the KK series mostly had detrimental effects on the inhibitors' binding strength, except for fluorine, due to their steric bulkiness (Fig. 2, Table 1). Together with the deuteration of the piperazine linker, having fluorinated P1 uracil in KK-7 led to a modest increase in its binding affinity relative to the parent HL-3-68 compound. Surprisingly, incorporation of tolyl or cyclopropyl sulfonamides in the aromatic P2 group resulted in a similar reduction in potency for KB-1 and KB-2 compared to the parent HL-3-47. Several potentially unfavorable interactions between the sulfonamides and MPro active site residues observed in the X-ray structures may explain the reduced potency of KB-1 and KB-2 (Fig. S4). Conversely, replacing sulfonamide with carboxamide functionality improved the binding affinities of inhibitors KB-3 through KB-6 by 3-4-fold. The latter inhibitors, and KK-7, also demonstrated modest antiviral properties that their parent compounds lacked.55 By examining the neutron structure of the MPro/KB-5 complex, we attribute the improved binding properties to the formation of a nonconventional S–H⋯F hydrogen bond between the fluorinated P1 uracil and protonated C145 thiol, and a conventional N–H⋯O hydrogen bond made by the inhibitor's carboxamide carbonyl and the Q189 side chain amide (Fig. 5).

Conclusions

In summary, we designed two series of noncovalent, nonpeptidic MPro inhibitors based on the chemical structures of the previously discovered Mcule-5948770040 (HL-3-47)54 and subsequently designed HL-3-68.55 Our study combined chemical synthesis, room-temperature X-ray and neutron crystallography, in vitro DSF and ITC measurements, and antiviral assays to obtain atomic structures of MPro/inhibitor complexes and their binding and antiviral properties. We directly visualized conventional and nonconventional interactions, including observing an S–H⋯F hydrogen bond for the first time. Inhibitors in the KB series containing carboxamide moieties on their aromatic P2 groups and fluorine-substituted P1 uracil demonstrated improved MPro binding characteristics relative to the parent compounds. Our results provide atomic details of inhibitor binding and offer insight into the future design of noncovalent antiviral therapeutics.

Synthesis of KK and KB inhibitor series

Both the KK and the KB series of inhibitors were synthesized by first coupling tert-butyl piperazine-1-carboxylate with either 1,2,3-trichloro-5-fluorobenzene (KK series, Scheme S1), or 1,2-dichloro-4-fluoro-5-nitrobenzene (KB series, Scheme S2), by heating in DMF in the presence of dry ground K2CO3, as previously described.55 The lability of the fluorine substituent is influenced by both the electron-withdrawing nature and the location of the other substituents (–Cl or –NO2) on the aromatic nucleus. In the case of 1,2-dichloro-4-fluoro-5-nitrobenzene, F is sufficiently labile for the coupling reaction to proceed essentially quantitatively (overnight at 100 °C) to form the intermediate tert-butyl 4-(4,5-dichloro-2-nitrophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate (KB-Int-1H; complete synthetic details can be found in the SI). The deuterated analog tert-butyl 4-(4,5-dichloro-2-nitrophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate-2,2,3,3,5,5,6,6-d8 (KB-Int-1D) could also be prepared in high yield (95%) under the same conditions. Conversely, the coupling of tert-butyl piperazine-1-carboxylate with 1,2,3-trichloro-5-fluorobenzene to form tert-butyl 4-(3,4,5-trichlorophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate (KK-Int-1H) for the KK series required more forcing conditions (120 °C for 10 days), due to possible absence of the strongly deactivating properties of the nitro group ortho to F. Under these conditions, some decomposition was also observed, and chromatography was required for purification. The deuterated analog tert-butyl 4-(3,4,5-trichlorophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate-2,2,3,3,5,5,6,6-d8 (KK-Int-1D) was prepared under similar reaction conditions.

For the KK series of inhibitors, the tert-butoxycarbonyl (BOC) group could be conveniently removed from the KK-Int-1H and KK-Int-1D intermediates using TFA to afford the free piperazine bases KK-Int-2H and KK-Int-2D in yields of 99% and 98%, respectively. Subsequent reaction of KK-Int-2H (KK-1, KK-2, KK-3, and KK-5) or KK-Int-2D (KK-6 and KK-7) with the appropriate orotic acid derivative utilizing HOBt·H2O (catalytic) and EDC·HCl in DMF at room temperature overnight afforded the products. In the case of KK-4, the very low solubility of 5-bromoorotic acid in neat DMF necessitated employing a 5 : 2 DMSO–DMF solvent mixture. All the KK inhibitors have low aqueous solubility, and purification was accomplished by repeated precipitation into deionized water. KK-4, however, required additional purification via column chromatography (3 : 2 ethyl acetate : acetone). Yields ranged from low (17% for KK-4 and 37% for KK-7) to moderate to good (60% for KK-1 and 80% for KK-5) to high (88% for KK-3, 94% for KK-6, and 95% for KK-2), with purities ≥95% by NMR.

For the KB series of inhibitors, as the first step, the nitro group in tert-butyl 4-(4,5-dichloro-2-nitrophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate (KB-Int-1H) and tert-butyl 4-(4,5-dichloro-2-nitrophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate-2,2,3,3,5,5,6,6-d8 (KB-Int-1D) needed to be reduced to the amine. This could be accomplished by overnight treatment with NH4Cl and zinc powder in 3 : 1 1,4-dioxane : H2O (Scheme S2), under anoxic conditions. Yields of tert-butyl 4-(2-amino-4,5-dichlorophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate (KB-Int-2H) and tert-butyl 4-(2-amino-4,5-dichlorophenyl)piperazine-1-carboxylate-2,2,3,3,5,5,6,6-d8 (KB-Int-2D) were respectively quantitative, and 98%. Functionalization of the amine group with sulfonyl chlorides or acyl chlorides required somewhat forcing conditions (4 days at 90 °C in pyridine; Scheme S3), due to the lowered basicity from the chlorine substituents. Following purification by column chromatography, the BOC-protected intermediates were deprotected using TFA, and coupled to the appropriate orotic acid derivative employing the same reaction conditions as for the KK series. Following purification by precipitation into water, the KB series of inhibitors (KB-1 to KB-4, and KB-6) were obtained in yields ranging from 31% to 91% in ≥95% purity. For KB-5, a portion of the bulk material of 90–92% purity was further purified by column chromatography to increase the purity to ≥95%.

The precursor inhibitor KB-0-1 was also prepared by coupling orotic acid to the deprotected KB-Int-1H (Scheme S4). We initially attempted to prepare amine KB-0-2 by Zn/NH4Cl reduction of KB-0-1, following the same procedure employed for reducing KB-Int-1H/D to KB-Int-2H/D. Due to extremely low solubility of KB-0-1 in 1,4-dioxane, we attempted the reduction by replacing 1,4-dioxane with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), in which KB-0-1 is soluble. However, after 24 h reaction time, the desired product was observed only in extremely low yield (<5%) with substantial decomposition. A shorter reaction time (1 h) did lead to the isolation of product though still in low (8%) yield following purification. Since we were aware of the low reactivity of the aniline –NH2, based on the forcing conditions required to form the KB sulfonamides and carboxamides, we rationalized that orotic acid would couple to the much more basic piperazine nitrogen preferentially over, and without interference from, the aniline –NH2. Hence, we endeavored to prepare KB-0-2 via direct coupling of orotic acid to deprotected KB-Int-2H (KB-Int-4). This was successful, affording KB-0-2 in 81% yield with a purity of ≥95%.

Expression and purification of MPro

The design of SARS-CoV-2 MPro (nsp5) plasmids, and protein expression and purification procedures have been carried out according to the established protocols.46,55,78,79 The MPro gene sequence was codon-optimized and cloned into a pD451-SR plasmid harboring a kanamycin resistance cassette (Atum, Newark, CA), featuring maltose binding protein (MBP) upstream and a 6 His tag downstream of the MPro gene. In the second construct MPro was appended to 102 amino acids of nsp4 and GP-6H tag at the N- and C-termini, respectively. A native N-terminus is attained using both constructs during expression through an MPro autoprocessing site corresponding to the cleavage between nsp4 and nsp5 in the viral polyprotein, SAVLQ↓SGFRK, where ↓ denotes the cleavage site. A native C-terminus is produced through an HRV-3C protease cleavage site (SGVTFQ↓GP). MPro was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) and purified using Ni-affinity chromatography. Protein yields for MPro preparations averaged ∼5 mg per 1 g cells and were stored at −30 °C. For neutron crystallography, the partially deuterated MPro was expressed in a bioreactor using minimal media made with 99.8 % D2O and supplied with hydrogenated glycerol as carbon source and then purified as described recently.77,78

Crystallization of MPro in complex with KK and KB inhibitor series

Protein for co-crystallization was concentrated to 5 mg mL−1 in 20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP (tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine) pH 8.0. 50 mM stocks of inhibitor compounds were prepared in 100% DMSO for crystallization purposes and stored at −30 °C. MPro was mixed with the ligands at 1 : 5 molar ratio and allowed to incubate at room temperature for a minimum of 30 minutes. Before setting up crystal trays, the MPro-inhibitor mixtures were passed through a 0.2 μm centrifuge filter. All crystals for room-temperature X-ray diffraction were grown by the sitting drop vapor diffusion methodology with 18–21% PEG3350, and either 0.1 M bis-Tris pH 6.5,7.0, or 0.1 M HEPES pH 7.0, 7.5 as the precipitant solution. Crystallization drops with volumes of 20 μL at 1 : 1 of MPro complex to well solution ratio were microseeded with a seeding tool from Hampton Research. Crystallization trays were left undisturbed in an incubator set to 14 °C for approximately 4 weeks to produce MPro/inhibitor crystals suitable for room temperature X-ray and neutron crystallography experiments.

For neutron crystallography, partially deuterated MPro at 5 mg mL−1 was mixed with KB-5 at a 1 : 5 molar ratio, incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, then filtered through a 0.2 μm centrifugal filter. Crystals of the MPro/KB-5 complex were grown in a Hampton 9-well sandwich box set up with 150 μL drops at a 1 : 1 ratio of protein to 17% PEG3350, 0.1 M HEPES pH 7.0 reservoir solution. Crystals grew using streak seeding technique to the largest size of 1 mm3 after 4 weeks of incubation at 14 °C. A crystal was then mounted in a quartz capillary with 20% PEG3350 stabilizing solution prepared in 99.8 % D2O to exchange labile hydrogens. Neutron diffraction data collection was initiated after 2 weeks of H/D exchange. The same crystal was used to collect complementary room-temperature X-ray data used in the joint X-ray/neutron refinement.

Room temperature X-ray diffraction data collection and structure refinement

All room temperature X-ray crystallographic data were collected on a Rigaku HighFlux HomeLab instrument equipped with a MicroMax-007 HF X-ray generator, Osmic VariMax optics, and a DECTRIS Eiger R 4M hybrid photon counting detector. Diffraction data were integrated using the CrysAlis Pro software suite (Rigaku Inc., The Woodlands, TX) and then reduced and scaled using Aimless80 from the CCP4 suite.81 The structures were solved by molecular replacement using PDB code 7RLS55 with Phaser82 from CCP4. Each model was iteratively refined using Phenix.refine from PHENIX suite83,84 and COOT85,86 graphics program aided by Molprobity87 for geometry validation. All ligand restraints were generated with eLBOW88 using geometry optimized by quantum mechanical calculations in Gaussian 16 at B3LYP/6-31g(d,p) level of theory.89 Final data collection and refinement statistics are organized in Table S1. All structural comparisons were performed by superimposing all main chain atoms in the structures with the COOT program.

Neutron diffraction data collection

Room temperature neutron diffraction data were collected using the Macromolecular Neutron Diffractometer Instrument (MaNDi) at the Spallation Neutron Source of Oak Ridge National Laboratory.90–92 The crystal was held stationary at room temperature while diffraction data were collected for 23 hours, using all neutrons with wavelengths between 2.0 and 4.0 Å. Following this, the crystal was rotated in steps by Δϕ = 10° to collect subsequent data frames. A total of 15 diffraction frames were collected in the final neutron data set. Diffraction data were reduced using the Mantid package, with integration carried out using three-dimensional TOF profile fitting.93 Wavelength normalization of the Laue data was performed using the Lauenorm program from the Lauegen suite.94,95 Neutron data collection statistics are shown in Table S2.

Joint X-ray/neutron (XN) refinement

Joint XN refinement of deuterated MPro/KB-5 complex was performed using nCNS,95,96 and the structure was manipulated in COOT.85,86 After initial rigid-body refinement, several cycles of positional, atomic displacement parameter, and occupancy refinement were run. Correctness of sidechain conformations, hydrogen bonding, and orientations of D2O water molecules in the structure were based on the mFO–DFC difference neutron scattering length density maps. The 2mFO–DFC and mFO–DFC neutron scattering length density maps were then examined to determine the correct orientations of hydroxyl (Ser, Thr, Tyr), thiol (Cys), and ammonium (Lys) groups, and protonation states of the enzyme residues and KB-5. The protonation states of some disordered side chains on the protein surface could not be obtained directly and remained ambiguous. Water molecules were refined as D2O where water oxygen atoms were centered on their electron density peaks and each molecule was rotated in accordance with the neutron scattering length density maps. Hydrogen positions in the protein were modeled as deuterium atoms because the protein was partially deuterated. Occupancies of D atoms were refined individually within the range of −0.56 (pure H) to 1.00 (pure D) because the neutron scattering length of H is −0.56 times that of D. Before depositing the neutron structure to the PDB, coordinates of each D atom were split into two records corresponding to an H and a D partially occupying the same site, both with positive partial occupancies that add up to unity. The percent D at a specific site is calculated according to the following formula: % D = {occupancy(D) + 0.56}/1.56.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Stock solutions (100 mM in 100% DMSO) of compounds were diluted to a concentration of 1 mM and 10 % DMSO in 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 20 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP (ITC buffer). At a final 100 μM concentration in ITC buffer and 1 % DMSO, the compounds remain soluble at room temperature as verified by the absence of any precipitation for at least a day. Previous studies had shown that >95% of the Tm increase was observed when adding 10 to 20-fold molar excess of inhibitor above the protein concentration of 10 μM.97 Once it was established that preliminary scans of the protein and its complexes display distinct fluorescence increases indicative of unfolding, samples in duplicate were prepared with SYPRO orange dye (5000× in 100% DMSO, Millipore Sigma, product number S5692, Burlington, MA) to yield a final concentration of 10 μM mature wild type MPro, 100 μM inhibitor and 5× dye in 25 μl of ITC buffer. The fluorescence signal was monitored as a function of temperature in a Bio-Rad C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler and data were processed with the software provided with the instrument. Experiments were repeated at least twice. Typically, duplicate traces are very similar72 and therefore, the mean of the data points were plotted using SigmaPlot (Grafiti LLC, San Jose, CA). Observed Tm error range is ±0.1 − 0.2 °C and Tm changes below ≤0.5 °C were not considered to be of significance. The same stock solutions of MPro were used for ITC experiments.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Purified MPro (∼80 μM) was dialyzed overnight against 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 20 mM NaCl and 1 mM TCEP at 4 °C. Protein concentration was estimated at least twice based on its 280 nm absorbance (ε/1000 = 31.15). Stock solutions (100 mM) of inhibitors dissolved in 100% DMSO were freshly diluted in ITC buffer to a concentration of 0.9 mM containing 9% DMSO in ITC buffer. Titrations were carried out on an iTC200 microcalorimeter (Malvern Instruments Inc., Westborough, MA) with 18 μM MPro in the cell, and 90 μM inhibitor in the syringe, diluted from the 0.9 mM stock solution in ITC buffer. DMSO concentration in the cell was matched to that of the inhibitor in the syringe. Control titrations of buffer with each of the inhibitors showed negligible thermal response. Data were processed and plots were generated using the Origin software provided with the instrument. For competitive inhibitors that bind at only one site, the dissociation constant (Kd = 1/Ka) is equivalent to the inhibition constant measured by enzyme kinetics (Ki).

Titer reduction assay

Vero E6 cells were seeded into 24-well tissue culture plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2.98 The next day, the cells were washed once with Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) and pretreated for 1 h at 37 °C with Eagle's minimum essential medium (EMEM) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) containing the test inhibitors at the indicated concentrations. After pretreatment, the inhibitor-containing medium was removed. The cells were infected with the SARS-CoV-2 WA2020 strain in EMEM supplemented with 2% FBS for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. During the adsorption period, the plates were gently rocked every 15 min. After adsorption, the viral inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed once with DPBS. Then, fresh EMEM with 2% FBS, and the appropriate concentration of each compound was added to each well. Sham-treated, virus-infected wells served as the virus control in each experiment. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, culture supernatants were collected for virus titration. Virus titers of the collected supernatants were measured in 96-well plates by the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay, with CellTiter-Glo (CTG) used to assess cell viability as the assay endpoint. Endpoint virus titers were calculated using the Reed–Muench method and expressed as log10 TCID50 per mL.99
