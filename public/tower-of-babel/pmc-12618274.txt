Biphasic Synovial Sarcoma of the Palatine Tonsil: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Ramiza Ramza Ramli, Shahzaib Maqbool, Abdur Rehman, Sadia Chaudhry, Imran Khan, Rahmat Gul Omarzai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSynovial sarcoma (SS) is a rare soft tissue tumor, accounting for 5%–10% of all soft tissue sarcomas, with an unusual occurrence in the head and neck region. This report presents a 29‐year‐old male with a biphasic SS of the left palatine tonsil, manifesting as progressive dysphagia, odynophagia, hoarseness, and left cervical lymphadenopathy. Clinical examination and imaging revealed a 4 × 4 mass arising from the left tonsil and obstructing the oropharyngeal inlet. Left tonsillectomy was done and the histopathological evaluation revealed a biphasic SS and negative margins. The diagnosis was further supported by a positive transducin‐like enhancer of split‐1 (TLE‐1), SS translocation on chromosome 18 (SS18), and epithelial membrane antigen (EMA). The patient underwent chemotherapy and external beam radiotherapy (60 Gy in 30 fractions), with significant clinical improvement. This case highlighted the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosing and managing a rare head and neck SS. Furthermore, this is the first reported case from Pakistan, emphasizing the need for awareness in atypical locations.

Introduction

Synovial sarcoma (SS) is a rare and aggressive soft‐tissue malignancy, accounting for approximately 5%–10% of all soft tissue sarcomas [1]. Although SS most commonly arises from the articular surfaces adjacent to the knee and foot, head and neck SS is very rarely reported and is considered a rare diagnosis originating from a very unusual location [2]. Among these, only 3%–5% arise in the head and neck region, with primary tonsillar involvement reported in just a handful of cases to date [3]. The tumor's propensity for local invasion and distant metastasis underscores the importance of early diagnosis and multimodal management.

Patients with head and neck SS often present with nonspecific symptoms such as dysphagia, odynophagia, or a painless mass, which can delay diagnosis [4]. Imaging typically reveals an ill‐defined, heterogeneous mass, and definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology and cytogenetic confirmation of the characteristic SS18–SSX fusion [5].

Given the scarcity of tonsillar SS, optimal treatment paradigms are extrapolated from extremity cases, emphasizing wide surgical excision combined with adjuvant radiotherapy to improve local control and survival [5]. Here, we present a case of primary tonsillar SS managed with surgical resection and adjuvant external beam radiotherapy.

Case History

A 29‐year‐old male patient presented to Benazir Bhutto Hospital, Rawalpindi with a complaint of a gradual change in the quality of his voice that was deep and muffled, also known as a “hot potato” voice. This was associated with fever, sore throat, relative dysphagia to solids, and left‐sided neck swelling. Over time, he experienced progressively worsening odynophagia and dysphagia, both to solid food and liquid. During his admission, the patient developed inspiratory stridor and a tracheostomy was done. The patient was a social smoker but had no history of tuberculosis, contact weight loss, appetite loss or episode of night sweat.

Methods

On throat examination, a lobulated and spherical mass measuring approximately 4 × 4 cm was visible in the left tonsillar fossa. The mass extended to the left anterior tonsillar pillar and to the base of the tongue, occupying more than 70% of the oropharyngeal inlet as shown in Figure 1. Additionally, a level II lymph node swelling was palpable, measuring 1 × 1.5 cm. There was no evidence of lymph node involvement on the right side. Similarly, a fibreoptic laryngoscopy showed a significant obstruction of the oropharynx.

Showing the gross appearance of the left tonsillar mass occupying the oral cavity (Blue arrow shows the right edge of the mass. The small amount of opening on the left can be appreciated).

A comprehensive workup for suspected malignancy was initiated. The baseline blood investigations (i.e., complete blood count, renal, liver and coagulation profiles) were within normal range. A contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) scan of the neck demonstrated a well‐defined, lobulated, and heterogeneously enhancing soft tissue lesion centered at the left tonsillar fossa. Magnetic resonance imaging was not done due to unavailability. The lesion extended to the left lingual tonsil and crossed the midline at the level of the oropharynx, causing significant obstruction of the oropharyngeal airway as shown in Figure 2.

Showing the CECT findings of the patient with left‐sided tonsillar mass crossing the midline (axial view) (Orange arrow shows the mass extending from the left into the oropharynx.)

Left tonsillectomy was performed and the procedure was completed without intraoperative or postoperative complications. The surgical field was highly vascular due to tumor infiltration, and careful hemostasis was maintained throughout. Surgeons encountering similar cases should ensure a secured airway preoperatively, anticipate significant bleeding risk, and coordinate closely with anesthesia and oncology teams for perioperative planning.

Histopathological examination revealed the tissue fragments had features of erosion with partially ulcerated surfaces and areas of necrosis containing a mosaic of both epithelial and spindle cells. The underlying stroma displayed inflamed granulation tissue and nuclear abnormalities, including increased chromatin and apoptotic bodies with spindle cells and hyperchromatic nuclei giving the diagnosis of biphasic SS. The histopathological study also revealed interspersed glandular tissue with spindle‐shaped cells as given in Figure 3.

Showing the histopathological analysis of the biopsy sample representing spindle‐shaped cells admixed with a biphasic glandular structure on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain.

Immunohistochemical staining, using markers such as TLE‐1, SS18, FLT‐1, and EMA, was all positive and highly specific for the diagnosis of SS. The tumor was graded per the Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) system, which scores three parameters—histologic differentiation (1–3), percentage of tumor necrosis (0–2), and mitotic count per 10 high‐power fields (1–3)—for a total score correlating to Grade 1–3 [6]. In this case, differentiation scored 3, necrosis 2, and mitotic count 3 (total = 8), making the final diagnosis biphasic SS FNCLCC Grade 3. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was unavailable due to institutional constraints. This is a major diagnostic limitation as this test is the diagnostic test of choice in this scenario.

Conclusion and Results

The patient underwent a wide local excision of the left tonsil via tonsillectomy followed by a combination of chemoradiotherapy as part of a trimodal therapy. Adjuvant chemoradiotherapy was indicated due to the tumor's high histologic grade (FNCLCC Grade 3) and the risk of microscopic residual disease, despite negative surgical margins. This multimodal approach aimed to reduce the risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis. A combination of Ifosfamide and Doxorubicin was given in 4–6 cycles together with radiotherapy (60 Gy over 30 fractions, 2 Gy per fraction, once daily, 5 days a week) over a 6‐week duration. Each chemotherapy cycle consisted of Ifosfamide 1.8 g/m2/day administered over five consecutive days and Doxorubicin 75 mg/m2 given as a single dose on Day 1 of each cycle, in accordance with standard soft tissue sarcoma protocols.

The patient was referred to the Nuclear Medicine, Oncology, and Radiotherapy Institute (NORI) Hospital for External Beam Radiotherapy (EBRT) of the tonsillar fossa. The tumor regressed significantly and the patient completed the chemoradiotherapy without complication. Upon further follow‐up, the patient was reported to have significant improvement and no evidence of recurrence. The patient was followed up clinically and radiologically every 3 months for the first year. At the 12‐month follow‐up, there was no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis, and the patient remained asymptomatic with good functional recovery.

Discussion

SS arises from the connective tissue and is a rare malignant tumor making up 5%–10% of soft tissue sarcomas [5]. Histopathologic examination distinguishes that SS of the head and neck region is found in three principal subtypes: biphasic, monophasic, and poorly differentiated. The biphasic type, as in our patient, consists of a combination of spindle cells growing in fascicles, interspersed with well‐developed epithelial (glandular) elements that create gland‐like or cleft‐like structures [1, 2]. The monophasic type is mainly composed of homogeneous spindle‐shaped cells with scant cytoplasmic content and elongated nuclei, which are arranged in intersecting fascicles. This subtype shows an absence of definite epithelial differentiation, thus making it difficult to differentiate from other spindle cell neoplasms. In contrast, the poorly differentiated type is constituted by clusters of small, round to oval undifferentiated cells with high nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratios, marked mitotic activity, and often accompanying necrosis, sometimes mimicking other small round blue cell tumors [1, 2, 5]. Immunohistochemically, they all typically stain for TLE‐1, EMA, and SS18 with scattered cytokeratin positivity, and the diagnosis can be confirmed by identification of the SS18: SSX fusion gene by molecular studies if available. Accurate recognition of these histologic patterns is critical because of the rarity and diagnostic difficulty of SS in the head and neck region [1, 2, 5]. The name can be misleading, as these tumors do not arise from the synovial cells themselves. A vast majority (80%–95%) of the cases are found in the extremities, while the midline structures, i.e., the head and neck, the chest, and the abdomen, are not stricken often [3, 7, 8, 9, 10].

SS occurring in the head and neck region is often considered aggressive. The standout anatomical site in this region is the hypopharynx [2, 11]. But it can also occur in the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, parotid glands, as well as other soft tissue sites [9]. The patient usually presents with a painless mass, which may or may not be associated with dysphagia, a change in voice quality, or even dyspnoea depending on the exact anatomical location of the tumor [2]. Our patient's mass gradually obstructed his oropharynx and caused progressive dysphagia.

A combination of MRI and a CECT scan is the ideal imaging modality to assess the extent of the disease whereas biopsy is vital for histopathological diagnosis. A characteristic chromosomal translocation that causes the fusion of the SYT gene on chromosome 18 with either the SSX1, SSX2, or SSX4 gene on chromosome X is the diagnostic marker for SS. We can look for this fusion through molecular testing to help guide our diagnosis [10].

From the literature, over 150 cases of SS in the head and neck region have been reported. However, only 21 of these cases reported the involvement of the palatine tonsils, 20 of the reported cases were primary SS and one was reported to be metastatic SS. All of the cases were male patients, including this case, highlighting the male predisposition of the disease, and most of them were biphasic subtypes. This is interesting because a recent review showed an almost equal predominance of SS among the two genders [1]. The management of head and neck SS requires a multimodal approach [1]. Complete surgical excision with histologically negative margins, combined with adjuvant radiotherapy, remains the primary treatment strategy to optimize local control [1, 29]. Cervical lymph node dissection is reserved for cases with confirmed nodal metastasis, which occurs in approximately 10%–15% of HNSS cases [30]. Adjuvant radiotherapy is strongly recommended for tumors > 5 cm or those with close/positive margins, reducing local recurrence risk by 30%–50% [29].

While systemic therapies like doxorubicin and ifosfamide are used in advanced or metastatic disease, their benefit in localized HNSS is debated. Studies in adults show no significant survival improvement with adjuvant chemotherapy, particularly in low‐risk tumors [31, 32]. However, neoadjuvant chemotherapy may be considered for large tumors (> 10 cm) or those involving critical structures to facilitate resection [1]. Postoperative adjuvant therapy (chemotherapy or radiation) is then employed to target residual microscopic disease [33].

Radiotherapy alone is reserved for unresectable tumors or patients with medical contraindications to surgery, achieving 60%–70% local control in palliative settings [1]. New targeted therapy, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immunotherapy i.e., PD‐1, which are particularly effective in refractory cases [34]. Furthermore, the FDA has recently approved a brand new genetically modified autologous T cell immunotherapy for refractory cases of SS [34]. There is still no established treatment protocol for tonsillar SS, attributed to its rarity. Therefore, the treatment currently is tailored according to a case‐to‐case basis [2, 22, 35, 36].

Finally, the prognosis, depends largely on individual tumor characteristics and the effectiveness of the initial treatment. Long‐term outcomes are affected by a range of variables, i.e., tumor size, histological subtype, metastatic status, surgical margin extent, patient demographics, and tumor biology among others. The study by Wushou et al. specifically established that a tumor size of greater than 5 cm was an adverse prognostic indicator in the head and neck SS [37]. A multidisciplinary approach, i.e., the Otorhinolaryngology (ORL), Anesthesia, Oncology, Radiology, and Pathology team, is essential to provide a good outcome in the treatment care. Additional multicentre collaborative research is necessary to establish disease‐specific prognostic biomarkers, refine risk stratification paradigms, and develop evidence‐based treatment protocols for tonsillar SS. Considering the tumor's rarity and heterogeneous clinical behavior, priority areas of research should encompass molecular profiling of SS18::SSX fusion variants, validation of histopathologic grading schemes in head and neck patients, and prospective assessment of novel immunotherapeutic drugs in this anatomical subset. These endeavors are essential to fill ongoing knowledge deficiencies for the determinants of survival and to optimize multimodal treatment strategies for this aggressive cancer [38, 39].

Since there is sparse data on this very malignancy, each report of a tonsillar SS has immense significance. Particularly as the first to be reported from Pakistan, it serves to remind us that, albeit rare, this can occur in any corner of the globe. Thus, it is important that the potential to be confronted with such an uncommon neoplasm at this singular anatomical site remains at the forefront of the minds of all clinicians who encounter such patients on a routine basis. With every documented case, we get closer to a more refined knowledge of the course and prognosis of the disease and, ultimately, to the institution of effective treatment regimens and guidelines.

In conclusion, palatine tonsil SS is an extremely uncommon occurrence that demands a very good degree of suspicion for accurate diagnosis. The case highlights the importance of adequate assessment, histological diagnosis, and use of immunohistochemical techniques. A multimodal approach including surgical excision and radiotherapy is essential in achieving optimal patient care and results. Prompt identification of such atypical manifestations enables earlier diagnosis and treatment. Additional research is warranted to establish consistent guidelines for the management of tonsillar SS.
