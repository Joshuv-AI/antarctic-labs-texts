A longitudinal evaluation of a four-level practical engineering education framework based on OBE-CDIO principles
Authors: Fei Chen, Peng Chen, Jianjun Yang, Siyu Liu, Xianke Ma, Zhongwei Meng, Yuan Zhang, Jia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transition of engineering education toward competency-oriented training is critical for modern intelligent manufacturing, yet empirical models for resource-constrained local universities remain underexplored. Using a national-level automotive engineering experimental teaching demonstration center in China as a case, this study designed and evaluated a progressive four-level practical teaching system. The system integrates Outcome-Based Education (OBE) and the Conceive-Design-Implement-Operate (CDIO) approach and is supported by a Teaching-Learning-Competition-Innovation (TLCI) linkage mechanism. To evaluate its operation, this study conducted a design-based longitudinal investigation using graduate administrative tracking, competency self-assessment, and employer feedback. Multi-source descriptive evidence showed that, during implementation, the graduate employment rate remained around 95% for four consecutive years, and job-major alignment was approximately 80.0%. In addition, the average starting salary of the 2023 cohort increased by 42% compared with the 2020 cohort, students reported relatively high perceived engineering problem-solving ability, and national competition performance also showed positive trends. These findings suggest that combining industry practice with a competition-driven closed-loop mechanism may provide practical reference for improving the quality of engineering talent cultivation and offer an implementable pathway for educational reform in resource-constrained universities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56297-9.

Introduction

The rapid development of technologies such as Industry 4.0, renewable energy, and intelligent manufacturing has created new challenges for engineering education1. Contemporary engineers not only need solid disciplinary knowledge but also require interdisciplinary integration skills to solve real-world engineering problems with blurred boundaries and complex contexts2. Accordingly, engineering education in many countries has gradually shifted from a knowledge-transmission paradigm to a competency-oriented framework3. Practical teaching plays a key role in this transition by helping bridge the gap between theoretical learning and professional practice4.

Previous studies have extensively examined pedagogical innovation at the curriculum level. For example, Dziuban et al. described blended learning, namely the combination of online and face-to-face instruction, as the “new normal” in higher education5. Related studies have shown that, when information and communication technologies are effectively integrated, blended teaching can improve students’ learning performance and satisfaction6. Systematic reviews of instructional organization and support strategies in engineering education during the COVID-19 pandemic also indicate that engineering educators maintained a certain degree of teaching quality and learning continuity by adjusting teaching strategies, innovating course design, and optimizing assessment methods7,8. These studies suggest that the purposeful integration of digital tools with traditional teaching has become an important component of modern engineering talent cultivation.

Beyond micro-level pedagogical innovation, Outcome-Based Education (OBE) and the Conceive-Design-Implement-Operate (CDIO) model have become important macro-level frameworks for engineering education reform and quality assurance9. Both have been widely adopted in European and American universities, promoting systematic reforms centered on competency mapping, curriculum structure, and learning experience10. Prior research also emphasizes that successful engineering education reform depends not only on course design itself but also on sustained faculty development, operational evaluation mechanisms, and evidence-based feedback improvement processes11.

At the implementation level, Project-Based Learning (PBL) and experiential learning models have been shown to facilitate knowledge transfer, interdisciplinary collaboration, and complex problem solving12. Incorporating sustainability competencies and authentic engineering challenges into training objectives can also enhance students’ sense of social responsibility and systems thinking13. Meanwhile, virtual simulation-supported practical activities can improve students’ preparedness and engagement under certain conditions14. From the perspective of learning motivation, support strategies based on frameworks such as self-determination theory help explain and enhance student engagement and sustained participation in project-based learning environments15.

Although prior studies have provided an important foundation for OBE-CDIO, practical teaching, project-based learning, and digital instruction, two gaps remain in the existing literature. First, systematic and longitudinal evidence remains limited. Many studies focus on individual courses, local teaching components, or short-term interventions, with limited explanation of how to construct a coherent, multi-level practical teaching system across the entire undergraduate training cycle. They also rarely link the operation of practical teaching platforms with graduates’ career development outcomes. Second, localized implementation research remains insufficient. Existing empirical studies are more often drawn from resource-rich universities or mature engineering education systems, while discussion of how resource-constrained local universities can continuously implement and iterate OBE-CDIO principles remains limited16. Rather than repeating prior course-level or single-intervention studies, this study builds on this literature by combining long-term institutional development, practical platform operation, and graduate outcome tracking to further analyze the operating mechanism and conditional transferability of an OBE-CDIO practical teaching system in resource-constrained local universities.

In response to industrial transformation, the Ministry of Education of China launched the “New Engineering” reform agenda, with key measures including curriculum optimization and deepened industry-education integration17. Within this institutional framework, national experimental teaching demonstration centers have become important carriers of engineering education reform. This study focuses on the National Automotive Engineering Experimental Teaching Demonstration Center at a university in Southwest China. Over more than a decade, the Center has combined OBE-CDIO principles with the development of a Teaching-Learning-Competition-Innovation (TLCI) mechanism and has constructed a progressive four-level practical teaching system. This system aims to improve the quality of the cultivation of automotive engineering talent by leveraging disciplinary strengths and standardized practical teaching resources.

This study aims to develop and implement a four-level practical teaching system based on OBE-CDIO principles and to conduct a descriptive evaluation using multi-source evidence, with particular attention to its operating mechanism in the context of resource-constrained local universities, its relationship with student competency development, and its conditional transferability:How can the OBE and CDIO frameworks be integrated to design a coherent four-level practical teaching system suitable for automotive engineering?To what extent do multi-cohort longitudinal data and external feedback indicate that the TLCI-based four-level practical teaching system is associated with improving trends in students’ perceived core competencies and employment outcomes?Under what institutional conditions does this teaching model have adaptability and transferability, particularly for resource-constrained local universities?

To answer these questions, this study adopts a design-based case study approach combined with a mixed-methods evaluation strategy18–20. First, it clarifies the overall goals and core structure of the four-level practical teaching system. Second, it details the institutional development strategies and implementation pathways. Third, it integrates student self-assessments, project performance records, employer feedback, and national employment statistics for multidimensional descriptive evaluation. Finally, it compares this model with mainstream international engineering education frameworks and discusses the practical reference value of the OBE-CDIO-TLCI integrated model for engineering education reform in resource-constrained universities.

Proposed framework and implementation

Objectives and the TLCI mechanism

China established National Experimental Teaching Demonstration Centers to improve practical teaching standards and pilot experimental curriculum reforms. The main function of these centers is to meet national and regional industrial needs. By adopting student-centered teaching methods, they cultivate students’ core engineering skills, autonomous learning ability, and interdisciplinary competencies. To address the mismatch between automotive engineering graduates and industry demands, the Center constructed a systematic talent cultivation framework (Fig. 1). This framework integrates strategies such as curriculum optimization, pedagogical innovation, participation in industry competitions, and deepened industry-university-research collaboration21. Figure 1 is a conceptual framework independently constructed and drawn by the authors based on the Center’s development practice and related research. It is used to illustrate the logical relationships among practical teaching objectives.

The four-level practical teaching system

At the National Automotive Engineering Experimental Teaching Demonstration Center, student competency development runs throughout the entire four-year undergraduate curriculum and follows a structured, phased pathway. This staged arrangement is consistent with systemic PBL research on the progression of students’ self-assessed learning outcomes, which suggests that competency development should be understood as a longitudinal and program-level process rather than as the result of isolated course activities25. To further improve training quality, the Center established a progressive four-level practical teaching system, as shown in Fig. 2. Figure 2 is a systematic representation, independently constructed and drawn by the authors, based on the Center’s four-year practical teaching operational experience. Its hierarchical logic is inspired by OBE-CDIO and practical teaching research, but the specific levels, implementation pathways, and activity arrangements are derived from the long-term practice at the study site.

Practical Teaching Support System.

Both physical and human resources require sustained funding and strong institutional support. To cultivate integrated teaching teams, the Center has adopted measures such as establishing specialized practical teaching platforms, upgrading experimental equipment, and implementing a joint guidance model that involves industry mentors and full-time faculty members. The Center deepens industry-university-research collaboration by hiring industry experts, encouraging faculty participation in industry practice, and building a comprehensive faculty development system. These measures provide continuous support for the quality of talent cultivation28,29. This is consistent with a recent systematic review emphasizing that formal, in-depth industry partnerships and dual-mentor mechanisms are essential to ensuring the sustainability of engineering education and promoting the professional growth of faculty members30. In terms of student career development support, this system does not treat the curriculum scheme itself as the sole driving factor. Instead, it provides students with professional knowledge, project experience, professional norms, and continuous feedback through the collaborative roles of campus faculty, industry experts, and practical platforms. Specifically, through sustained alignment among course objectives, project tasks, industry mentor feedback, and professional norm training, the four-level practical teaching system translates specialized training objectives into career competencies that students can perceive, practice, and develop sustainably.

Core construction strategies

The Center adopts a holistic educational philosophy that integrates knowledge transmission, competency building, professional literacy, and collaborative development. Guided by this philosophy, the Center continuously iterates its experimental teaching system to improve students’ engineering practice skills and innovative thinking, optimizing the training model to match the multifaceted competency structure required by industrial digitalization and intelligent transformation31.

Theory-practice integration

The Center has implemented systematic reforms across three dimensions: content, methods, and evaluation. At the implementation level, the Center funds approximately five teaching reform projects each year to balance the transmission of foundational knowledge with the cultivation of higher-order competencies. In terms of interdisciplinary integration, the Center promotes the intersection of automotive engineering and computer science. By incorporating frontier industry practices into the curriculum, this approach responds to the need in interdisciplinary engineering education to “integrate different knowledge domains to solve real problems.” In addition, through cooperation with domestic and international universities, nontechnical elements such as professional ethics and social responsibility have been incorporated into the curriculum, supporting the systematic development and deepening of engineering ethics education32.

To improve students’ ability to solve complex engineering problems, the Center optimized the curriculum structure. While maintaining the balance of total credit hours, it increased the proportion of comprehensive and innovative experiments. As a result, experimental courses now account for more than 27.1% of the total curriculum. In instructional design, the Center adopts a multidimensional structured model:At the micro level, it focuses on in-depth analysis of key concepts and inquiry-based questions;At the meso level, it establishes internal logical coherence within units and horizontal connections across disciplines;At the macro level, it integrates disciplinary knowledge domains with holistic competency cultivation. By combining situated learning and flipped classrooms, this hierarchical design aims to stimulate students’ intrinsic motivation and engagement, thereby better aligning with the competency requirements of industrial transformation33.

In experimental courses, the Center adopts a “virtual-physical integration” teaching model. This approach not only enhances instructional interaction, standardizes operational procedures, and deepens theoretical understanding but also prompts students to reflect on the development trends and transformation pathways of the automotive industry in the Internet era. For example, the automotive structure course combines physical disassembly experiments with virtual disassembly exercises and requires students to complete component measurement and drawing tasks. This hybrid model leverages the benefits of repeated practice and process visualization to improve students’ operational skills and, to some extent, learning outcomes.

Virtual–physical integration

The Center’s experimental system follows a teaching strategy of “physical practice as the main component and virtual simulation as a supplement.” Using virtual experiments, simulation, and immersive technologies, the Center applies virtual methods specifically to complex scenarios involving high risk, high cost, or unobservable dynamic processes. This approach ensures a “safe, visible, and repeatable” practical learning environment34. In addition, comparative research on the complementarity of physical, simulated, and remote laboratories in terms of learning outcomes and resource efficiency provides an important theoretical basis for this approach35. Related platforms are mainly used for process training, observation of task completion, and recording of competency performance. They provide process evidence for monitoring student competencies rather than replacing the comprehensive evaluation of faculty and industry mentors.

The Center has built a multi-level ecosystem of simulation tools. At the commercial software level, industry-standard tools are included, such as CarSim (vehicle dynamics), PreScan (autonomous driving testing), VISSIM (traffic flow simulation), Simulink (control modeling), and PC-Crash (accident reconstruction), to ensure that the teaching content aligns with industry standards. Science–education–research synergy

Transforming faculty research resources into teaching content is a core educational strategy of the Center. Following the principle of research-teaching integration, the Center has established a continuous pathway from undergraduate practice to academic research since 2009. In its implementation, the Center has established an institutional mechanism that supports students’ direct participation in faculty-led research projects. For example, the Deep Traffic Accident Investigation project recruits approximately 20 undergraduate and master’s students for research each year. Similarly, in frontier areas such as the hydrogen fuel cell bus battery platform, project teams assign approximately 20 students each year to related work. In addition, research teams in intelligent vehicles, automotive powertrains, clean energy, heavy transportation, and special-purpose vehicles also actively recruit students. Statistics show that approximately 50% of students at the Center join at least one of 16 existing research teams each year for in-depth practical learning. During the research process, mentors guide students in identifying and solving complex engineering problems and help them master frontier professional knowledge and advanced technical skills. It should be noted that the preceding references to “approximately 20 students” are used to describe the annual participation scale of specific research projects. Because participation in such projects varies with project cycles, research tasks, and team arrangements, this study treats these figures only as descriptive information on the intensity of project operations and does not calculate percentages or conduct further statistical inference based on them.

Beyond the internal drivers of academic research, the Center prioritizes feedback loops from external industrial demand. To ensure that the practical teaching system remains aligned with industry development, the Center collects external feedback through multiple channels, including annual graduate tracking surveys, alumni associations, and alumni forums. This “external stakeholder participation-feedback-improvement” mechanism is consistent with systematic review evidence on community and industry engagement in engineering education37. From 2021 to 2024, the Center systematically consulted numerous partner enterprises and more than 100 alumni on issues such as course design and talent cultivation objectives. In response to industry demand for stronger customer communication and technical service capabilities, the Center optimized relevant course content, innovated teaching methods, and placed greater emphasis on simulation training based on authentic service scenarios. These measures effectively improved students’ practical skills and workplace adaptability.

Student innovation and interdisciplinary practice

The Center adheres to the philosophy of “competition-driven learning” and has established a diversified competition system that includes Baja SAE, autonomous driving challenges, and transportation technology competitions. Using this framework, the Center has formed 13 interdisciplinary teams spanning 21 majors, collectively involving approximately 470 students. This collaborative model is consistent with the practical logic in engineering education that emphasizes “collaborative solving of complex problems”38. Here, “approximately 470 students” refers to the cumulative participation across multiple competition teams across different majors and primarily reflects the coverage of the interdisciplinary practice platform. Because this figure represents cumulative cross-team participation rather than a proportion based on a single, stable annual student population, this study does not calculate a percentage.

Under faculty guidance, these teams engage in project-oriented practice and complete the full process from conceptual design, manufacturing, and assembly to on-site presentation and defense over a cycle of approximately ten months. This intensive, closed-loop engineering training helps improve students’ technical integration ability and systems thinking and is also consistent with industry demand for interdisciplinary engineering talent.

The Center further established a “competition-innovation integration” mechanism, encouraging students to transform engineering challenges encountered in competitions into entrepreneurship projects, such as those for the “Internet+” and “Challenge Cup” competitions. Through continuous iteration and improvement, students have successfully bridged the gap between pure engineering practice and innovation incubation. Recently, a student team from the Center won a national bronze award in the National “Internet+” College Student Innovation and Entrepreneurship Competition. This achievement provides illustrative evidence of the model’s practical value and suggests that students can perform competitively in high-level innovation practice.

To adapt to the service-oriented transformation of the automotive industry, the Center expanded the scope of its innovative practice from technological research and development to after-sales service. Based on industry feedback, the Center introduced modules on service process design and user needs analysis into the curriculum. Through school-enterprise collaborative projects simulating real business scenarios, students can strengthen customer communication and technical service capabilities while mastering technical skills. This dual-track “product and service” innovation practice broadens students’ career adaptability and promotes steady improvement in the quality of employment in graduate service positions.

Resource sharing and outreach

The Center not only conducts diverse on-campus experiments but also actively fulfills social responsibility by expanding science outreach and regional cooperation. Since 2020, the Center has successfully hosted more than 400 public welfare activities and received more than 6,000 visitors from primary and secondary schools, universities, government agencies, and enterprises. These activities take diverse forms, including research facility visits, science outreach activities, and laboratory open days, such as those held during Science and Technology Week, the Chengdu Creative Design Exhibition, and youth maker activities (Table 1). As a result, the Center has become an important regional platform for disseminating scientific knowledge and showcasing achievements in engineering education. This community-oriented participation is consistent with systematic evidence on the benefits of community engagement and service-oriented practice in engineering education.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics statement

The research protocol and all procedures involving human participants followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Automotive and Transportation Engineering at the host university (Approval No.: 2024LL(05); approval date: May 20, 2024). The ethical review covered the prospective questionnaire survey and the retrospective analysis of de-identified administrative records. For the survey, participants were provided with a study description, and electronic informed consent was obtained before data collection. For the secondary analysis of administrative data, the committee approved a waiver of informed consent because the data contained no direct personal identifiers and were reported only in aggregate form.

Data collection sources

To describe and evaluate the operation of the four-level practical teaching system, this study employs a descriptive evaluation framework grounded in a multi-source chain of evidence. All evaluation activities are centered on the operation of the Center’s four-level practical teaching system. This evaluation framework has motivational, diagnostic, corrective, and forward-looking functions: it is used to stimulate students’ intrinsic motivation to participate in practical learning, identify students’ competency development needs and course implementation problems, adjust practical activity arrangements based on feedback, and provide a basis for subsequent optimization of the training program. The selection of evaluation tools follows three principles: first, alignment with the objectives of the four-level practical teaching system; second, coverage of both student self-assessment and external evaluation; and third, a verifiable basis for reliability and validity.

On this basis, this study uses multiple forms of evidence, including student questionnaires, longitudinal administrative archives, and employer feedback, for cross-validation. Statistical analysis

This study used SPSS to analyze the survey data. For the three dimensions of the student competency questionnaire, namely engineering problem solving, interdisciplinary collaboration, and engineering literacy, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.842, 0.857, and 0.889, respectively. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) values were 0.816, 0.826, and 0.882, and Bartlett’s tests of sphericity were significant for all dimensions (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Principal component analysis showed that a single principal factor was extracted for each dimension, with cumulative variance explained of 67.84%, 70.06%, and 69.22%, respectively, suggesting acceptable convergent validity. In addition, this study conducted item-deletion sensitivity analysis. The results showed that Cronbach’s α did not increase after deleting any single item in any dimension: the α values after deleting individual items were 0.788–0.810 for engineering problem solving, 0.813–0.827 for interdisciplinary collaboration, and 0.862–0.868 for engineering literacy, all lower than the original α values for the corresponding dimensions. No obvious redundant item was found that could be deleted to improve internal consistency.

To avoid interpreting the data as causal improvement in a post-test design, this study uniformly reports Likert means, standard deviations (SDs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and strictly interprets them as students’ perceived levels of competency improvement41. In addition, descriptive statistics and frequency analyses were used to characterize the distribution of high-score categories. The item-deletion analysis supports the internal consistency of the current item set, but measurement invariance and cross-sample stability still require further examination in future research.

Methodological statement and limitations

All analyses in this study should be interpreted as descriptive and correlational evidence. Because of the absence of a rigorous control group or quasi-experimental design, observed differences across cohorts or groups may be affected by multiple external factors, including macroeconomic fluctuations, changes in industry demand, the COVID-19 pandemic, student self-selection bias, and changes in regional employment structure. Therefore, this study uses these results only to describe trends, provide contextual reference, and form multi-source corroboration, rather than to infer the net causal effect of the TLCI mechanism. The relevant statistical criteria and sample information are specified in the titles or footnotes of the corresponding tables in the Results section.

The virtual learning space in this study primarily refers to a virtual simulation environment for engineering practice training. Its purpose is to reduce experimental risk, improve the repeatability of training, and expand access to practical resources, rather than to discuss an online psychosocial space in the sense of anonymous interaction or identity presentation.

Results

In recent years, the Center has offered more than 90 undergraduate experimental courses each year, covering over 270 experimental projects and completing approximately 120,000 student-experiment sessions. Notably, 90% of these experiments are classified as comprehensive, design-oriented, or innovative. These figures indicate that the Center has built an integrated practical teaching supply chain spanning courses, projects, and platforms, with a structural feature characterized by a high proportion of comprehensive, design-oriented, and innovative experiments.

Competition participation and academic achievements

The school has established seven specialized student competition teams focused on core research and practice in fields such as internal-combustion-engine vehicles, electric vehicles, and autonomous driving technologies. Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This study constructed and implemented a four-level practical teaching system within the OBE-CDIO framework. Multi-source descriptive evidence suggests that this model is associated with improving trends in graduates’ perceived core engineering competencies, competition participation, and employment quality indicators. In the survey results, students rated engineering problem-solving ability highest, which may be related to the project-driven and virtual-physical integration strategies in the curriculum reform. Meanwhile, the positive association between competition experience and employment rate suggests that competition-driven learning within the TLCI mechanism warrants further study. Alumni tracking and employer feedback jointly indicate that students who participated in research and competition projects performed well in engineering practice skills, interdisciplinary communication, and workplace adaptation, but these results should still be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than strict causal effects.

Comparison with international engineering education models

Compared with internationally common paradigms, the Center’s four-level practical teaching system is not a simple replication. Instead, it represents localized adaptation and structural reconstruction of core OBE-CDIO principles, tailored to the context of a regionally oriented, application-focused university.

To enhance the system’s transferability, the Center has gradually developed standardized implementation and optimization tools, including practical teaching feedback standards and course evaluation operational manuals. Through peer exchange and regional cooperation, relevant experience has provided a reference for practical teaching reform at some universities. For resource-constrained local universities, the reference value of this model does not lie in replicating a single hardware platform, but in drawing on its four-level progressive structure, TLCI feedback loop, virtual-physical integration, dual-mentor support, and resource-sharing mechanism, and adapting these elements locally according to different universities’ funding conditions, faculty base, and industrial environment.

Sustainability and social impact

The four-level practical teaching system incorporates the principle of “engineering for the benefit of society” into its design and emphasizes the social value of technical skills. Specific examples include the traffic accident investigation team cooperating with local institutions to convert professional data into traffic safety teaching materials for primary and secondary school students, and the intelligent driving team developing assistive systems for people with visual impairments. These projects illustrate how engineering education can respond to the needs of vulnerable groups and cultivate students’ awareness of engineering ethics. Prior research shows that learning experiences oriented toward social contexts and public welfare can promote students’ sociotechnical integration thinking and sense of responsibility46.

To assess graduates’ performance in social responsibility, a targeted follow-up survey of the 2020–2024 cohorts showed that approximately 42.5% of respondents had participated in public welfare projects or corporate social responsibility (CSR) activities within three years after graduation. In response to these findings, the Center updated courses such as Engineering Ethics and Social Responsibility over the past two years and introduced a modular Corporate Social Responsibility Practice Training component. Related research indicates that embedding ethical norms into engineering courses with assessable learning outcomes can support students’ gradual development from normative awareness to responsible engineering judgment in complex situations47.

Conclusions

This study developed and implemented a four-level practical teaching system under the OBE-CDIO framework, with the TLCI mechanism as its core driver, and descriptively evaluated its operation using multi-source evidence, including longitudinal administrative records, perceived competency surveys, and employer feedback. The findings show that the system is associated with positive changes in indicators such as students’ perceived engineering competencies, competition participation, and employment quality, but the results should still be interpreted as descriptive and correlational evidence rather than strict causal effects.

This work makes three main contributions:It presents student perceived competency results in a standardized manner while avoiding over-causal interpretation of the results;It clarifies the implementation pathway of the TLCI mechanism in a real engineering education context;It provides a descriptive evaluation framework that can be further validated and locally adapted in subsequent research.

Future research will advance in three main directions: first, developing a cloud-based virtual simulation sharing platform to improve access to practical teaching resources for resource-constrained universities; second, exploring artificial intelligence-driven personalized preparatory courses to support competency bridging for students with different academic foundations; and third, expanding sample sources, conducting cross-institutional and cross-regional external validation, and further streamlining and reviewing the evaluation scale to examine the applicability of this model under different resource conditions. Overall, this study provides an implementable pathway and evaluation framework for practical engineering education reform in resource-constrained local universities.
