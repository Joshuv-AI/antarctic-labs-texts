The effect of post-operative continuous negative pressure therapy after debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention for acute periprosthetic joint infection
Authors: Hisham Alayed, Allen Herng Shouh Hsu, Kuan-Ting Wu, Cheng-Ta Wu, Po-Chun Lin, Feng-Chih Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a serious complication after total knee and hip replacement, with varying success rates in its management using debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR). We hypothesize that the application of post-operative continuous negative pressure therapy (CNPT) can improve the success rate of DAIR in the treatment of acute knee and hip PJI.MethodsIn this retrospective study from October 2018 to December 2022, 62 patients diagnosed with acute PJI were divided into two groups: 24 patients treated with DAIR and CNPT, and 38 patients treated with DAIR alone. The groups were comparable in terms of patient characteristics, organism profile, and clinical findings. The primary outcome was treatment failure based on the Delphi consensus criteria for 1-year follow up. The statistical analysis involved univariate and multivariate regression.ResultsAfter a year of follow-up, the CNPT group had a significantly lower treatment failure rate (25%) compared to the control group (55.3%, p = 0.019). After controlling the confounding factors, the use of CNPT remained as a significant factor with a lower OR (adjusted OR: 0.13; 95% CI 0.02–0.86, p = 0.034), indicating its potential effectiveness in improving DAIR outcomes.ConclusionThe study suggests that CNPT could substantially enhance the success rate of DAIR in treating acute PJI. This intervention may represent a significant advancement in post-operative care, potentially reducing the failure rate of DAIR treatment and improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, DAIR, Continuous negative pressure therapy, Total knee arthroplasty, Total hip arthroplasty

Background

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is one of the most common causes of early and late revision arthroplasty. The most devastating complication is severely limits joint function and consistently difficult to eradicate and a leading cause of morbidity following total joint arthroplasty [1, 2]. The number of PJI cases is increasing rapidly due to the increasing numbers of arthroplasty surgery and increases the economic burden and financial cost on the health system [3, 4].

The management of acute PJI frequently involves a timely combination of debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR). Literature reveals various factors and methods that influence the success rate of DAIR, such as timing of the procedures, the exchange of modular parts of the implant, the use of antibiotic-loaded bone cement beads, or the repetition of the DAIR procedure [5–8]. However, the success rate reported in each study varies, from 16% to 82%. Previous literature has indicated that continuous post-operative negative pressure therapy is highly effective in treating acute septic arthritis of the shoulder [9]. However, this straightforward surgical technique has not been widely adopted in the treatment of acute PJIs of the hip and knee.

We hypothesize that the application of post-operative continuous negative pressure therapy (CNPT) can improve the success rate of DAIR in the treatment of acute knee and hip PJI.

Methods

After receiving approval from the Institutional Review Board, we conducted a retrospective review of patients who underwent DAIR for acute post-operative or acute hematogenous PJIs following total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA). This study included cases from a single academic center spanning from October 2018 to December 2022. Patients who did not fit the 2018 the International Consensus Meeting (ICM) criteria for acute (PJI) [10], those who underwent reimplantation surgery, and those with incomplete surgical or demographic data were excluded from the study. Acute post-operative PJI was defined as occurring when the DAIR procedure was performed within 3 months of the index surgery. Meanwhile, acute hematogenous infections were characterized by DAIR being conducted more than 3 months after the index surgery, accompanied by abrupt symptom onset lasting less than 3 weeks in a well-fixed and functioning THA or TKA. A total of 62 patients were included in this study, comprising 24 patients who received post-operative CNPT and 38 patients who did not receive this therapy following surgery. All patients were followed up at least of one year.

For acute knee PJIs, irrigation and debridement (I&D) were carried out using a medial parapatellar approach. In cases of acute hip PJI, the approach matched that of the initial surgery, which included posterolateral, direct anterior, or direct lateral approach. Each case involved a radical and thorough I&D of all infected and necrotic osseous and soft tissues. However, due to constraints imposed by the medical insurance system, the modular components—specifically, the polyethylene component for knees and both the femoral head and polyethylene liner for hips—were not replaced with new components. These polyethylene components were retrieved and disinfected with alcohol-based salutation (povidone iodine alcohol or 2% chlorhexidine gluconate in 70% isopropyl alcohol). The joint cavity was thoroughly irrigated with 3 to 6 L of diluted betadine solution at a concentration of 0.35%. Subsequently, a 1/8 inch drain (Hemovac®, Zimmer, Inc, Warsaw, IN) was placed in the joint. After that, a large diameter chest tube was inserted into the joint space, utilizing a 20 Fr. size for the knee (Fig. 1) and a 24 Fr. size for the hip (Fig. 2). A continuous negative pressure of 75 to 100 mmHg was then applied to the large diameter drain using a wall-suction system. The criteria for drain removal were established as a drainage volume of less than 30 cc per day over a three-day period coupled with clinical improvement in symptoms. Typically, the chest tube drain was removed first, followed by the hemovac drain. After removal of the chest tube, the wound of the insertion site was not sutured and cared with antibiotic ointment until it was healed. Postoperatively, all patients were administered systemic antibiotics, either intravenously or orally, for a total duration of 6 weeks, according to the results of intraoperative cultures. Postoperative surveillance was conducted according to our standard institutional practice. It involved routine clinical evaluations, measurement of inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR), and radiographic imaging at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and at least 1 year.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome for treatment failure was established based on the Delphi consensus criteria [11]. This includes three key parameters: (1) infection recurrence caused by the same organism strain; (2) PJI-related mortality, such as death due to sepsis; and (3) need for additional surgical interventions for infection following DAIR. The secondary outcome was blood transfusion. Blood transfusion criteria were established based on hemoglobin (Hb) levels: a threshold of less than 8 g/dl in healthy patients or between 8 and 9 g/dl when accompanied by clinical anemia symptoms such as dizziness, dyspnea, or palpitations. For patients with cardiovascular disease, the transfusion threshold was set at a higher Hb level of 9 g/dl [12].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and continuous data as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were analyzed using either the chi-square test or the Fisher exact test. As a preliminary Kolmogorov-Smirnov test did not demonstrate a normal distribution for our small sample size, we decided to use the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test to compare the continuous variables. To rigorously assess the impact of CNPT as a predictor of successful treatment for PJI, we included covariates that demonstrated an association with PJI at a significance level of p < 0.2 in the univariate analysis into a multivariate logistic regression model. An alpha of 0.05 was used to evaluate significance. All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc version 17.9.2 software (Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Of the 62 patients with acute PJI who underwent DAIR, 27 (43.5.%) demonstrated treatment failure at 1 year of follow-up on the basis of the Delphi criteria. The failure rate was 25% (6 of 24) for the CNPT group compared with 55.3% (21 of 38) for the control group (p = 0.019). The duration of drain retention varied, with the small diameter suction drain remaining in place for an average of 8.7 days (range: 6 to 22 days) and the chest tube drain for an average of 5.6 days (range 3 to 8 days). Following the removal of the continuous negative pressure, the Hemovac drain was kept for an additional 3 days. Despite the variations in treatment outcomes, all wounds eventually healed without complications through secondary healing. There were no significant differences between the CNPT group and the control group with respect to patient characteristics, serum lab data, synovial WBC and PMN, and blood transfusion (Table 1). No significant differences were observed in the types of infecting organisms between the two groups (Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that CNPT could substantially enhance the success rate of DAIR in treating acute PJI. The application of CNPT plays a pivotal role in enhancing wound healing in plastic surgery, including to augments wound contraction, facilitates the drainage of exudate removes debris [13, 14]. Jung et al. demonstrated a 98.5% cure rate in treating septic arthritis of the shoulder using CNPT after open arthrotomy irrigation and debridement [9]. We employed the same technique of CNPT in treating patients with acute PJI. This approach yielded notably better treatment outcomes compared to those achieved in patients who did not receive this therapy.

Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT) has been utilized in various studies to treat acute PJI with mixed results. Lehner et al. reported an 85% success rate (17 out of 20 cases) in treating acute PJI in hip and knee joints using NPWT with instillation [15]. In contrast, Beckmann et al. observed a 67.7% success rate in 31 acute hip PJI cases using the same method of NPWT with instillation [16]. However, their approach involved a commercial, and consequently more expensive, NPWT with instillation system. In our approach, we employed a simpler, cost-effective method of negative pressure therapy using a chest tube. This technique demonstrated a relatively lower rate of infection elimination, but importantly, without incurring additional costs for the acute PJI patients. The mechanism of continuous negative suction operates through primary and secondary effects. The primary mechanism involves macro-deformation and micro-deformation at the dressing-wound interface, which results in wound shrinkage, fluid removal, and stabilization of the wound environment [17]. The secondary effects include processes such as angiogenesis, neurogenesis, granulation tissue formation, and cellular activities like proliferation, differentiation, and migration [14]. In the context of an infective status, soft tissue edema can impede wound healing and exacerbate bacterial growth [9]. Our simple method effectively stabilizes osmotic and oncotic pressure gradients in hip and knee joints, enhancing infection control. This is achieved through the removal of excess fluid utilizing a cost-effective, helpmate negative wound pressure device.

Our method utilized a standard chest tube and hospital wall suction system, which are inexpensive and readily available, in contrast to commercial NPWT devices with instillation that are substantially more costly. At our institution, the material cost of the CNPT setup is approximately 10–15 USD, compared with 600–700 USD for a commercial NPWT system. Although we did not conduct a formal cost-effectiveness analysis, we highlighted the relative difference in equipment costs. While this qualitative comparison indicates a markedly lower material cost, future studies incorporating formal health economic evaluations are necessary to validate the cost-effectiveness of this approach.

Numerous surgical techniques and innovations have been documented in the literature to enhance the success rate of DAIR. Double DAIR, which implemented a 2-stage debridement protocol that includes the use of high-dose antibiotic beads between stages for the treatment of acute PJI [18]. With this technique, from two previous studies, there were a high infection-control rates of 87% and 90% after a mean follow-up of 3.5 years [19, 20]. However, this surgical technique necessitates a second surgery, thereby exposes acutely ill PJI patients to additional anesthesia and surgical risks. With our method, patients avoid an additional surgery under the risk of anesthesia; the treatment involves only the removal of the drainage tube during hospitalization once the drainage volume has sufficiently decreased.

The literature has identified several risk factors that impact the treatment outcomes of DAIR. These include the timing of debridement [21], particularly the importance of early intervention [22], as well as the presence of acute post-operative infection [23] and exchange of all modular components [22, 24, 25]. Additional factors influencing outcomes are patient-specific conditions such as obesity and diabetes mellitus [26, 27], erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) values at presentation [28, 29], along with the type of infecting organism, notably Staphylococcus aureus, gram-negative bacteria, or polymicrobial infections [21, 30–32]. In our study, a thorough analysis revealed no significant statistical differences in patient demographics, surgical variables, or laboratory data. Notably, only revision implant and the application of negative pressure therapy emerged as strong predictive factors for treatment outcomes. Although the direction of the associations (e.g., revision prosthesis) remains clinically meaningful, the wide confidence intervals limit the precision of these estimates, and thus the findings should be interpreted with caution.

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, it is a small-scale, single-center retrospective cohort study, which may limit the external validity and generalizability of our findings. These factors inevitably reduce the strength of causal inference and restrict the applicability of our results to broader clinical populations. Nevertheless, given the scarcity of literature evaluating CNPT in acute PJI after DAIR, we believe our study provides valuable preliminary evidence and may serve as a basis for future multicenter, prospective investigations with larger cohorts. Secondly, our methodology did not routinely exchange all of the modular components, a factor that could potentially influence the surgical outcomes of DAIR as suggested in other literature. However, this viewpoint is not universally supported, as evidenced by several studies which suggest that modular component exchange does not necessarily correlate with higher DAIR success rates [24, 33–38]. It is pertinent to note that recent systematic reviews and meta-regression analyses have not demonstrated a universally beneficial effect of component exchange on the treatment outcomes of DAIR for hip and knee PJIs [39]. Future research should therefore aim to conduct larger-scale comparative studies on outcomes of DAIR with and without component exchange in conjunction with CNPT. Such investigations are crucial to definitively ascertain the role of component exchange in enhancing the efficacy of continuous negative pressure therapy in DAIR.

Conclusion

Our innovative technique of integrating CNPT with DAIR resulted in a higher success rate at 1 year compared to those without CNPT. This intervention may represent a significant advancement in post-operative care, potentially reducing the failure rate of DAIR treatment and improving patient outcomes. Further research with larger samples is recommended to validate these findings and explore the broader applicability of CNPT in PJI management.
