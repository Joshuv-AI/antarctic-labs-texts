Energetic Characteristics of Hydrophobic Porous Materials as Candidates for Manufacturing of Nanorockets
Authors: Alexander
R. Lowe, Mirosław
A. Chora̧żewski, Yaroslav Grosu, Yuriy G. Bushuev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A new propulsion mechanism for nano- and microrocket engines is hypothesized. It is based on the instantaneous expulsion from hydrophobic nanopores triggered by irradiation from electromagnetic microwaves, ultrasound, or sudden pressure release. A large energy output is needed for the propulsion of a nanoparticle, and the value can be determined experimentally and by means of atomistic simulations. As such, we measured the heat of intrusion of water into ITQ-29 (LTA) pure silica zeolite with cage structure of pores. The heat effect is exothermic and equal to −7.3 ± 0.8 J/g of zeolite. Similar values were reported for chabazite, ZIF-8, and grafted mesoporous silica EVA. All these materials have cage structures of pores. In contrast, silicalite-1 (MFI) zeolite with a channel structure of pores exhibits endothermic intrusion. Molecular dynamics simulations of pure silica zeolites with LTA, CHA, and MFI topologies at a broad range of water loadings show that water becomes thermodynamically stable in cage-shaped pores while it is unstable in channel-shaped pores. A large energy release is expected during water expulsion from channel-type pores.

The design and manufacturing of nanoscale rockets and motors are fast-developing branches of nanoscience, environmental science, defense, and industry.1−4 A nanorocket is a vehicle that is thrust-driven by a tubular engine. Several propulsion mechanisms are explored to stimulate nanoparticle movement, which can be used as drug-delivering vehicles for killing cancer cells and bacteria while also being used as chemical sensors. Catalytic reactions are known to work as a propulsion mechanism for nanorockets. For example, gas bubbles are formed after urea, or H2O2 decomposes at the catalytic centers on the inner or outer surface of the particle. Ultrasound propulsion is based on a pressure gradient formed at the concave ends of the particles.5 Magnetic, electric fields and light are often used6 to create a steering movement for particles. Detailed information on existing propulsion mechanisms is summarized in many review articles.3,6,7

The present work supports and develops the hypothesis formulated in the previous article,8 about a new propulsion mechanism based on fast water expulsion from hydrophobic nanoporous materials. Heterogeneous lyophobic systems (HLSs) are used to store mechanical and thermal energy and convert one into the other.9−11 They consist of a combination of hydrophobic porous solids and water. Water can penetrate a hydrophobic porous material only at elevated pressure.

It was demonstrated that water in hydrophobic pores is in a metastable state12 and is expelled from pores with high velocity.9,10 Based on the reported kinetics of water extrusion from pure silica zeolite (PSZ) tubes representing the hexagonal 18-membered ring (18MR) channel with a pore aperture of 15.3 Å, it was demonstrated that the exit velocity of water molecules is about 100 m/s.10 Another example8 of the fast water expulsion from the hydrophobic tube with a square cross-section triggered by a decrease in pressure is shown in Figure 1. An estimated molecular velocity is 80 m/s. The simulation methods of particles immersed in water are presented in the original articles.8,10

These velocities exceed the nanofluidic measurements of steady-state flow in hexagonal boron nitride nanoscale channels (10–2 m/s) and graphite capillaries (1 m/s).11 It was demonstrated that friction is governed by interactions of flowing molecules with confining surfaces. Solid–liquid friction was studied using computer simulations for water in boron nitride and carbon nanotubes.12 A large, pore radius-dependent hydrodynamic slippage was found for both materials. Water experiences lower friction on carbon surfaces compared to hydrophilic boron nitride. These materials are wetted at ambient pressure. Thus, fast expulsion of metastable water from more hydrophobic nanotubes triggered by pressure drop or local heating is expected.

These observations suggested that tubes, capped from one side, can be used as nanorockets propelled by ejected water. Bunches of tubes may cover nano and micro scale. However, it is unclear how the topology of porous materials and the aperture of pores affect water expulsion velocity.

The analysis of the structure of water in hydrophobic nanopores has begun with the following studies: clustering of water, stability of clusters, and the establishment of the role of dipole–dipole orientations.8 This allowed us to assume that the maximum microwave absorption band of confined water is significantly moved toward lower frequencies compared to bulk water. Thus, it is possible to heat only the confined water. Meanwhile, net water is more transparent in this frequency band. After a short microwave irradiation impulse, the heated metastable water is ejected from the material, pushing a rocket. Following temperature relaxation, bulk water reenters the pore, and the process can become a cycle. The direction of movement of the particle coincides with the direction of microwaves because water heating occurs only in pores lying along the radiation beam. Due to the metastability of confined water in nanochannels, it is expected that ultrasound can trigger ejection, too. For some HLSs, water extrusion occurs when pressure is released.

The motion of micro-objects in liquids is characterized by very low Reynolds numbers, representing the ratio of inertial to viscous forces.13,14 The number is proportional to the characteristic size of the moving object. The viscous drag and Brownian motion role is less influential for micro- than nanoparticles. The proposed mechanism is expected to be exploited for microrockets due to the scalability of porous materials, which can be zeolite crystals or grafted silica mesoporous particles. These materials are chemically and biologically inert and are widely exploited.

Many experimental investigations and calculations must be performed to support this hypothesis. To increase the particle momentum, the water intruded in the hydrophobic pores must store maximum mechanical and heat energy to release during extrusion. Materials with high energy performance are needed to transfer the energy into the fast, directed motion of water, which will push the rocket.

Pure silica zeolites are crystalline microporous materials that contain only silicon and oxygen atoms if one neglects the possible silanol defects of their frameworks. Zeolites possess a simple chemical composition and an arrangement of pores of various sizes and shapes. They are attractive materials for experimental investigations and computer simulations. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are also microporous materials with significantly different properties. They have complex chemical compositions and more flexible frameworks that respond to pressure. Mesoporous silica is a class of hydrophilic amorphous materials that can be hydrophobized using silane chemistry methods. Large pore apertures, irregular structures, and complex compositions prevent atomistic simulations of these materials to a large extent. Thus, we have focused on simulations of PSZs presented in Figure S1 as simple models and tried to explain the experimental results obtained for MOF ZIF-815 and grafted mesoporous silica EVA.9

In contrast to mechanical work spent for intrusion, calculated from a pressure–volume intrusion isotherm obtained by porosimetry (Figure S2), the heat of water intrusion has been measured only for a small number of HLSs. A high-pressure scanning transitiometer is a calorimeter to study heat effects in a wide range of pressures and temperatures.16 Presently, this equipment has been exploited to measure the heat of water intrusion into pure silica zeolite ITQ-29 (LTA). The zeolite was synthesized and characterized at ITQ (Valencia, Spain).17,18 One gram of the material was donated for us to study. The experimental methodology, raw data, and final heat of intrusion value are presented in the Supporting Information (SI). The ITQ-29 is the third zeolite for which the heat of intrusion is reported.

The heat effects of water intrusion into chabazite (CHA) and silicalite-1 (MFI) crystals have been previously reported in the literature, and they have opposite signs.9,16,19,20 Structures of these zeolites are presented in Figure S1. CHA is composed of d6r and cha building units. Water can penetrate through its 8MR windows, which connect cages. The MFI-type framework comprises two different 10MR channels. Straight channels with an elliptical cross-section of about 5.2–5.7 Å are interconnected by sinusoidal channels with a diameter of 5.4 Å. LTA-type zeolite consists of α and sodalite cages of 13 and 6 Å in diameter. The α cages are interconnected through 8MR windows. Our computer simulations show that water penetrates only into α cages. Thus, CHA and LTA have cage-shaped pores, unlike MFI, whose pores are 3D intersecting channels. Three behaviors of HLSs are observed. Extrusion can occur at the same pressure as intrusion, Pext≈ Pint. The pressures are defined as the pressures of half-loading from maximum values (Figure S2). These systems are called molecular springs. More often, HLSs demonstrate intrusion-extrusion hysteresis, Pext< Pint. If Pext > 0, they are shock absorbers; overwise, they are bumpers. MFI and CHA-type PSZs demonstrate small hysteresis, and they are springs. The MOF ZIF-8 and mesoporous silica EVA are shock absorbers, while ITQ-29 (LTA) is a bumper.

Classical molecular dynamics (MD) was exploited to simulate water-zeolite systems with a broad range of water loadings. Simulations were performed with the General Utility Lattice Program (GULP)21 version 6.2.0 according to Procedure S1. The parameters and functional forms of the force field, parameters used for MD simulations, and starting molecular configurations are presented in SI as input files for the GULP computer program.

The estimated heat effects are approximately three times larger in magnitude than the experimental ones. There are several reasons for this difference. In models, one has an ideal crystal without defects, but actual crystalline grains contain some broken −Si–O–Si– bridges terminated by hydroxyl groups, which decrease the hydrophobicity of the material. Any force field always reproduces experimental properties with limited accuracy, and sometimes, the final results of computations only qualitatively correspond to the experiment. The experimental errors also affect the discrepancy. The purity of the material, the silanol defects, the clogging of some pores, and other factors that are hardly countable increase the uncertainties.

Another factor explaining the discrepancy between the calculated and experimental data is that the minimums of internal energy are at partially reachable loadings for actual systems. From a thermodynamics point of view, the difference in Gibbs free energy controls loadings. Our calculations show that energies are negative at Nw from 21 to 30 for LTA and from 34 to 47 for CHA. Numerous calculations and experimental measurements of intruded volume in MFI-type PSZ show that the number of water molecules per unit cell is approximately 38–45 in the pressure region of pressures where actual calorimetric experiments are performed.27−29 One of the reasons is the spontaneous formation of voids in narrow channels.8 Direct MD simulations of water intrusion in porous crystals provide information about intrusion isotherms and loadings at each pressure.

Another limitation of accessibility of loadings, corresponding to the minima energy region, is the kinetics of penetration of water into the crystalline grains. According to Figure 2a, for cage zeolites, there is a large energy gap between the extrema of the functions ΔU(Nw). It is about 75 J/g for LTA-type zeolite. It must be overcome when water penetrates from the filled cages to the empty neighboring cages. The barrier is smaller for cages on the surface of the crystal in contact with bulk water. As a result, some cages in the bulk crystal can be empty even at high pressure. It may increase the uncertainties of the experimental values. The avalanche mechanism of water penetration into ITT-type zeolite with 3D channel structure of pores was recently described.29 Water intrusion started at the surface of crystallites and advanced toward their center.

The reasons for the bumper behavior of the water-LTA system are the large energetic barrier that hinders water flux from the filled to the empty cages and the low intrusion pressure (20 MPa). The barrier to water in CHA is lower, and only the 10 MPa gap between intrusion and extrusion isotherms.23 The observable hysteresis is smaller for MFI in accordance with our results, showing the absence of a reachable stable state.

This effect and the high-energy barrier prevent fast water expulsion when the pressure decreases during extrusion.

Due to weak water interaction with hydrophobic materials, heat effects are preferentially defined by changes in the structure of water. The case of tubes immersed in water was discussed in the previous publication.8 Discussed zeolites have pores with different shapes, sizes, and topologies. These characteristics of pores affect the structure of water. One of the main properties of the water structure is the statistics of hydrogen bonds. If molecules form a network of H-bonds randomly with probability p = nHB/j, where nHB is the mean number of H-bonds per molecule, j is the maximum number of bonds formed by one molecule with neighboring molecules, and (1 – p) is the probability of finding the broken bond, then the fraction of molecules with i bonds is defined as335In this work, we apply two definitions of H-bonds.34 According to the energetic threshold criterion, two molecules are considered bonded if the energy of their interactions is less than −3.5 kcal/mol (EHB < −14.64 kJ/mol).8 Here, we take into account only a small part of intermolecular interactions, the interactions of pairs at short distances. Another applied criterion is geometrical. We consider the molecules bonded if the distance between oxygens (ROO) is less than 3.3 Å and simultaneously ROH < 2.41 Å.26,34 Using this definition, it is possible to easily count H-bonds with a zeolite, which is one supermolecule. However, at large zeolite loadings, the short interatomic distances appear due to a packing effect and low mobility of molecules. As a result, the energy of molecular pairs can be large relative to the adopted EHB.

Statistics of H-bonds for water in LTA-type zeolite are presented in Figure 5. The average number of H-bonds between water molecules (nww) increases with loading and reaches a plateau at Nw = 24 in the case of energetic definition, staying lower than in bulk water, for which nww = 3. With an increase in loadings, additional strong and medium-strength bonds (E < – 14.64 kJ/mol) cannot be formed in the α cage of LTA due to more tight molecular packing preventing the formation of directed H-bonds. At Nw > 28 the newly added molecules destabilize the system, causing an increase in total potential energy (Figure 2a). The geometric definition characterizes a number of interatomic contacts of oxygens and hydrogens. An increase in nww is expected at high loadings. The number of water-zeolite H-bonds nwz is low in the whole range of loadings because of the hydrophobicity of a zeolite. Thus, we can conclude that water in cage-shaped pores is more ordered than in bulk despite a decrease in nww. Strengthening of H-bonds and long-range intermolecular interactions decrease the energy of the system.

The fractions of molecules with i H-bonds (fi), calculated with the energetic definition and presented in Figure 5b, highlight the alteration of the structure of water under confinement. A deviation calculated from theoretically predicted values (eq 5) is negligibly small for bulk water. The fractions of molecules with one (f1) and four H-bonds (f4) for confined water are systematically lower than the theoretical ones. The gap is the same at all loadings. Meanwhile, an increase in f2 is observed in all Nw, but for f3 only at Nw > 16. At low loadings, molecules form chains in the α cage of LTA, and each molecule predominantly has two H-bonds. Near the most probable loading (Figure S4), a degeneration of states is observed, f1 ≈ f4 and f2 ≈ f3, the same as theoretically predicted but shifted toward larger nww. In this region, nww increases with loading.

The arrows in Figure 5b show the difference between bulk and confined water in fractions with i = 2 and 4 at high loadings. Under confinement, the number of molecules with two bonds significantly increases, in contrast to those with four H bonds. However, the fractions in f1 and f3 are close to those in the bulk water. Thus, the main change in the water structure for tightly packed water in the cage is a shift of the dynamic equilibria between molecules with two and four H-bonds. The same effect of confinement was observed for water in hydrophobic tubes.8 However, for cage-shaped pores, the number of water molecules with the single H-bond is smaller than in bulk water, which is in contrast to that when water is in straight tubes. The alteration of the inherent structure of water due to confinement into hydrophobic pores explains heat effects. Distributions of molecules with respect to the number of H-bonds they formed show specific effects, which depend on the shape of pores.

Generally, the intrusion is only partially reversible, and HLSs generate additional heat during extrusion.9 The proposed propulsion mechanism for nano- and microrockets is based on fast water expulsion from pores. This phenomenon was observed for water confined in materials with a 1D channel system of pores. The MFI-type PSZ with intersected channels exothermically expels water, indicating fast molecular motion that can propel a crystal grain. The fast and directed movement of water molecules releases significant storage energy, which produces heat and work. Shock absorbers, the systems with hysteresis, produce less work than the work spent on water intrusion. For 1D channel materials, hysteresis was demonstrated to increase with the diameter of the pore.8 Thus, these systems can produce more heat than is absorbed during intrusion. The released energy, by jets of expulsed molecules, heats bulk water.

The results of our work show that the shape and geometric parameters of the pores control the heat effects. Taking into account that its magnitude depends on the energetic stabilization/destabilization of the water, we estimate the magnitude of the heat effects to be less than 30 J/g in any case. These absorbed/released thermal energies are for one extrusion. According to our simulations, intrusion/extrusion into/from nanoparticles occurs within 10–8 s. It is supposed that pulsing radiation can trigger the expulsion a thousand times per second. Nanorockets will utilize energy from external sources.

Exothermic heat of intrusion is observed for HLSs with cage structure of pores, such as mesoporous silica EVA, MOF ZIF-8, LTA, and CHA-type zeolites. The energetic stabilization of confined water with respect to bulk water explains this behavior. Depending on the reversibility of intrusion, these materials absorb some heat during extrusion and cannot be recommended as a material for nanorockets. The experimental data and the results of computer simulations show that maximum storage of mechanical and heat energy due to the intrusion of water in hydrophobic porous materials is possible to reach if they have channel structures. These materials can be recommended for the manufacture of rockets.

Hydrophobic zeolites and metal–organic frameworks have elevated intrusion and extrusion pressures, and the propulsion of particles may be observed only at these pressures. Mesoporous silica materials with straight channels grafted with organic chain molecules are better suited for tuning the working pressure. Grafting compounds and a treatment protocol regulate their hydrophobicity and pore size, thus intrusion-extrusion pressures and hysteresis. Another method of the regulation of hydrophobicity is the topological tuning.10,29 Thus, the working pressure can be close to ambient. Pulsed electromagnetic or ultrasonic waves may trigger the expulsion of metastable water, and the intrusion-extrusion process can be cyclized. The proposed computational method will be critical for the search for materials with high energy output.
