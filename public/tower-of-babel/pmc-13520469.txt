MnSb-Based Quantum Dots: Defect-Mediated Photoluminescence in Multiphase Nanostructures via Green Colloidal Synthesis
Authors: Rahma Dika Nasution Aulia, Evelyna Ikhza Wafiyah, Juvani Indah Putri, Kasmita Kasmita, Zamahsary Martha, Veinardi Suendo, Davin Philo, Deski Beri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MnSb-based quantum dots are an emerging class of lead-free luminescent nanomaterials with potential for sustainable photonic applications. In this work, defect-emissive Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures were synthesized via a green ethanol-assisted colloidal reflux method using oleic acid as a surface-passivating ligand under an inert nitrogen atmosphere. The synthesized nanostructures exhibited an average particle size of ∼5.3 nm with good colloidal dispersibility and narrow size distribution. Structural characterization by XRD, TEM, SAED, XRF, Raman spectroscopy, and XPS collectively suggests the formation of Mn–Sb-related nanocrystalline domains embedded within MnOx-rich surface environments, indicating a structurally heterogeneous and defect-rich nanoscale system. Optical measurements showed a pronounced absorption feature near 230 nm and effective optical transition energies of 3.9–4.1 eV. Because the synthesized material is structurally heterogeneous and contains MnOx-rich surface environments, the estimated optical transition energies should be regarded as effective optical transition energies rather than a true semiconductor band gap. Broad green photoluminescence centered at 518–525 nm was observed, with a maximum photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of 62.3%. The emission is attributed to defect-mediated and interface-assisted radiative recombination associated with Mn–Sb-related nanodomains, MnOx-rich surface species, and ligand-passivated defect states. These findings demonstrate that defect engineering in MnSb-based multiphase quantum dots provides an effective strategy for designing efficient lead-free luminescent nanomaterials.

Introduction

Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) are nanoscale materials that can exhibit size-dependent electronic and optical properties due to spatial confinement effects, typically 2–10 nm in diameter, that confine charge carriers in all three spatial dimensions, resulting in discrete electronic states governed by the quantum confinement effect. This phenomenon enables precise tuning of optical absorption and photoluminescence (PL) properties through control of nanocrystal size, composition, and surface chemistry, thereby allowing emission properties to be engineered for a wide range of optoelectronic, photovoltaic, and bioimaging applications. , The large surface-to-volume ratio of QDs further introduces abundant surface states that influence charge-carrier dynamics, radiative recombination, and colloidal stability.

Growing concern over environmental and human-health risks associated with heavy-metal QDs particularly cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb), has accelerated the search for low-toxicity alternatives. Cadmium- and lead-based nanocrystals can release toxic ions during degradation, leading to bioaccumulation and cytotoxicity. , Consequently, research has shifted toward RoHS-compliant, environmentally benign QDs, including systems based on InP, CuInS2, and other low-toxicity semiconductors. , Meanwhile, emerging transition-metal antimonide nanomaterials offer promising opportunities due to hybridization between transition-metal d orbitals and pnictogen p orbitals, which generates tunable electronic and magnetic properties, thereby enabling the integration of optical, electronic, and magnetic functionalities within a single nanoscale platform. −

Quantum confinement significantly alters the electronic structure of semiconductor nanocrystals, enabling band gap modulation and enhanced photoluminescence efficiency. , In Mn–Sb-containing nanostructures, interactions between Mn-derived electronic states, Sb-related domains, and surface defect states may strongly influence carrier relaxation pathways and light-emission processes. , Whereas existing literature on Mn-based nanocrystals predominantly focuses on Mn-doped chalcogenides such as Mn:ZnS and Mn:CdSe where Mn2+ acts as an internal luminescent center, reports on colloidal MnSb-based quantum dots remain largely unexplored. These nanocrystals represent an opportunity to probe photoluminescence arising from Mn–Sb-related domains and associated defect states rather than dopant-derived emission. ,

The Mn–Sb system is of particular interest because it combines semiconducting and magnetic characteristics, offering potential magneto-optical functionality at the nanoscale. ,− Mn-containing materials can exhibit enhanced red emission, long PL lifetimes, and improved thermal stability, while Sb contributes to band-structure modulation and nonlinear optical behavior. Despite increasing interest in transition-metal-based luminescent nanomaterials, the relationship between structural heterogeneity, interfacial chemistry, and photoluminescence behavior in Mn–Sb-containing nanoscale systems remains poorly understood. In particular, the role of multiphase Mn–Sb/MnOx nanostructures and defect-rich interfaces in generating efficient visible-light emission has received limited attention. The formation of MnOx-rich surface environments surrounding Mn–Sb-related nanodomains may introduce localized electronic states that substantially modify radiative recombination pathways. Systematic studies that directly correlate structural heterogeneity, surface oxidation, and photoluminescence behavior in colloidal Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures remain limited.

In this work, we investigate the formation, structural evolution, surface chemistry, and optical properties of Mn–Sb-based quantum nanostructures synthesized through a green colloidal route using ethanol as a low-toxicity solvent and oleic acid as a surface-stabilizing ligand. Rather than focusing solely on the formation of phase-pure MnSb quantum dots, this study aims to understand the origin of defect-mediated photoluminescence arising from the coexistence of Mn–Sb-related nanodomains and MnOx-rich interfacial environments. Understanding how interfacial defects and partial surface oxidation govern radiative recombination in Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures is essential for the development of next-generation lead-free luminescent materials.

Experimental Section

The chemicals used include manganese­(II) chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O, 99%, Merck), antimony trichloride (SbCl3, 99%, Merck), 96% ethanol, oleic acid (C18H34O2, 90%, Merck), n-hexane (CH3(CH2)4CH3, 99%, Merck), and nitrogen gas (N2n, 99.99%). All materials were used without further purification. Ethanol was selected as a low-toxicity and environmentally benign solvent capable of dissolving metal precursors while supporting a green colloidal synthesis approach. In addition, its moderate boiling point (78 °C) enables controlled precursor conversion under reflux conditions and minimizes the use of high-boiling organic solvents commonly employed in conventional quantum-dot synthesis. Whereas, oleic acid was employed primarily as a surface-passivating and growth-regulating ligand to improve colloidal stability and limit nanoparticle aggregation during synthesis.

A total of 1.98 g of MnCl2·4H2O (0.01 mol) was dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol in a 250 mL three-neck flask under a nitrogen atmosphere. The solution was stirred for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution of the precursor while continuously purging with nitrogen to reduce dissolved oxygen. Subsequently, the solution was heated to 78 ± 2 °C under reflux and constant stirring conditions. A solution containing 2.28 g of SbCl3 (0.01 mol) dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol was added dropwise over 15 min using a syringe, corresponding to an initial Mn molar ratio of 1:1. The reaction mixture was maintained under reflux to promote precursor conversion and nanostructure formation.

After the designated reaction period, 6 mL of oleic acid was slowly introduced over 5 min as a surface-passivating and growth-regulating ligand. The reaction was further maintained under reflux, during which the solution gradually developed a brownish-yellow coloration, indicating the formation of colloidal Mn–Sb-containing nanostructures. Upon completion of the reaction, the mixture was cooled to room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere to minimize oxidation during cooling.

The resulting suspension was transferred to a separatory funnel and washed with n-hexane to remove excess ligands, residual precursor species, and reaction byproducts. Following phase separation, the upper organic phase containing ligand-stabilized colloidal nanostructures was collected and used for subsequent characterization. Schematic illustration of the green colloidal synthesis and purification procedure for Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures. MnCl2·4H2O and SbCl3 precursors were treated in ethanol under nitrogen atmosphere, followed by oleic acid-assisted growth, purification by n-hexane extraction, and collection of luminescent colloidal nanostructures exhibiting blue-green emission under 365 nm UV irradiation.

The optical properties of the synthesized Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures were investigated using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. Photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) measurements were performed to evaluate the emission efficiency of the nanostructures. Structural characterization was carried out using X-ray diffraction (XRD) to determine the crystalline phases and structural features of the synthesized materials. The morphology, particle size, and size distribution were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate vibrational characteristics associated with Mn–Sb-related structures, surface disorder, oxide species, and ligand-derived surface functionalities. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was further used to analyze the surface chemical composition and oxidation environments of the synthesized nanostructures.

Results and Discussion

Ethanol primarily functions as a polar coordinating solvent that facilitates precursor dissolution and homogeneous mixing rather than acting as a strong reducing agent. Under reflux conditions (78 °C), precursor conversion is governed mainly by thermal and coordination-driven processes. SbCl3 may undergo partial hydrolysis and coordination interactions in polar media, generating Sb-containing intermediate species that can interact with Mn2+ ions during nanostructure formation. Consequently, the formation of Mn–Sb-containing quantum nanostructures is likely controlled by precursor conversion kinetics and ligand-mediated coordination rather than direct chemical reduction.

Oleic acid serves as a surface-passivating and growth-regulating ligand by coordinating with metal ions, reducing surface energy, and suppressing uncontrolled aggregation. Such ligand-assisted processes promote the formation of Mn–Sb-nanodomains while maintaining colloidal stability. Furthermore, the relatively mild reaction environment may facilitate partial surface oxidation of manganese-containing domains, consistent with the Mn–O species observed in XPS analysis. The coexistence of Mn-containing antimonide domains and MnOx-rich surface environments is therefore considered a plausible outcome of the green colloidal synthesis process employed in this work.

Particle size analysis revealed an average particle diameter of approximately 5.3 nm, indicating the formation of nanoscale colloidal particles (Figure ). These results are consistent with TEM observations, which show nearly spherical Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures with an average diameter of 5.28 ± 0.7 nm and a narrow size distribution. ,, The particles are uniformly dispersed with minimal agglomeration, suggesting effective stabilization by oleic acid during nanocrystal growth. Such size uniformity indicates controlled nucleation and growth processes under the employed colloidal synthesis conditions.

The particle dimensions fall within the nanoscale regime (<10 nm), where surface effects and size-dependent electronic structure modifications become significant. The corresponding SAED pattern exhibits distinct diffraction rings, confirming the polycrystalline nature of the synthesized nanostructures. The observed diffraction features are consistent with Mn–Sb-related crystalline domains and agree with the phase analysis obtained from XRD. Although lattice fringes are not clearly resolved in the conventional TEM images, the combined TEM and SAED results indicate the formation of crystalline Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures with good colloidal dispersibility, ,,

XRD analysis (Figure ) reveals diffraction features consistent with nanoscale crystalline domains related to the hexagonal NiAs-type MnSb structure (space group P63/mmc). The broad diffraction peaks indicate small crystallite size, lattice distortion, and structural disorder, which are commonly observed in quantum-sized intermetallic nanostructures synthesized via solution-phase routes. Such peak broadening is characteristic of nanocrystalline materials and suggests the presence of finite coherent scattering domains. ,

XRF analysis (Table ) reveals Mn as the dominant element, whereas Sb is detected at a substantially lower concentration than expected for stoichiometric MnSb. Because XRF provides bulk elemental information without distinguishing between crystalline phases or oxidation states, the measured Mn signal likely includes contributions from both Mn-containing antimonide domains and manganese oxide species. Such compositional deviations are commonly observed in colloidal nanomaterials synthesized through solution-phase routes, where surface oxidation, precursor conversion inefficiencies, and nanoscale structural heterogeneity influence the overall elemental composition. − The higher Mn content detected by XRF is therefore attributed to the coexistence of Mn–Sb-containing domains and MnOx-rich surface environments, consistent with the multiphase characteristics identified by XRD, SAED, and XPS analyses. − Consequently, the observed nonstoichiometric composition is interpreted as a manifestation of phase coexistence and structural heterogeneity rather than a single homogeneous MnSb phase. Nevertheless, the simultaneous detection of Mn and Sb, together with diffraction features associated with Mn–Sb-containing domains, supports the formation of defect-rich Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures. −

The UV–Vis spectra (Figure A) exhibit a pronounced absorption band centered near 230 nm for all synthesis durations. The absorption intensity increases with reaction time, suggesting progressive nanostructure evolution and an increased density of optically active states. The broad absorption profile is consistent with nanoscale structural heterogeneity and surface-state contributions commonly observed in colloidal quantum nanostructures. −

Optical properties of Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures synthesized at different reaction times. (A) UV–Vis absorption spectra. (B) Photoluminescence spectra as a function of synthesis time.

The PL spectra (Figure B) display broad green emission centered at 518, 521, and 525 nm for synthesis times of 12, 18, and 24 h, respectively. The gradual red shift suggests evolution of the local electronic environment during nanostructure growth. , The broad emission profiles are characteristic of defect-mediated recombination involving surface states, structural disorder, and MnOx-related interfacial defects. −

The PLQY increases from 36.46% (12 h) to 47.02% (18 h), reaching 62.3% after 24 h of synthesis. This improvement indicates progressive suppression of nonradiative recombination pathways through enhanced structural ordering and more effective oleate-mediated surface passivation. − The high PLQY obtained after prolonged synthesis suggests that defect-assisted radiative recombination dominates the emission process in the Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures.

Optical transition energies estimated from Tauc plots decrease slightly with increasing synthesis time. Because bulk MnSb exhibits semimetallic behavior, these values should be regarded as effective optical transition energies rather than intrinsic semiconductor band gaps. The observed transitions are attributed to nanoscale electronic structure modification arising from surface states, ligand–metal interactions, structural disorder, and MnOx-related interface states. −

XPS analysis (Figure ) reveals Mn-, O-, and carbon-containing surface species. The Mn 2p spectrum exhibits characteristic Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 peaks at 641.4 and 652.6 eV, while the C 1s and O 1s spectra indicate oleate-derived surface functionalities and Mn–O species. These results suggest chemically heterogeneous manganese environments together with partial surface oxidation and ligand-passivated interfaces, supporting the presence of localized electronic states involved in radiative recombination. No distinct Sb 3d signal was observed, likely due to the relatively low Sb concentration detected by XRF and the surface-sensitive nature of XPS

The Raman spectrum in Figure exhibits broad vibrational features below 600 cm–1 together with pronounced bands in the 1350–1600 cm–1 and ∼3000 cm–1 regions. − The low-frequency features are consistent with Mn–O- and Mn–Sb-related vibrations as well as disorder-induced lattice modes commonly observed in transition-metal nanostructures. The bands at 1350–1600 cm–1 and ∼3000 cm–1 originate from carbonaceous species and C–H stretching vibrations associated with oleate ligands. These results confirm the presence of surface-bound organic ligands and are consistent with the structurally heterogeneous nature of the synthesized Mn–Sb/MnOx nanostructures.

Because bulk MnSb exhibits semimetallic behavior, the optical transitions observed in the present system should not be interpreted as the intrinsic band gap of MnSb. Instead, the optical response is attributed to nanoscale electronic structure modification arising from structural heterogeneity, surface states, and MnOx-rich interfacial environments. The coexistence of Mn–Sb-containing nanodomains and partially oxidized surface species is expected to generate localized electronic states that facilitate carrier trapping and radiative recombination, giving rise to defect-mediated green photoluminescence.

Based on the combined structural and optical characterization results, a defect-mediated emission mechanism is proposed (Figure ). Partial surface oxidation produces MnOx-rich interfaces surrounding Mn–Sb-containing nanodomains, creating defect states associated with interfacial disorder. These states act as radiative recombination centers, resulting in green emission at 518–525 nm. The progressive increase in PLQY with synthesis time suggests improved oleate-mediated surface passivation and suppression of nonradiative recombination pathways.

Proposed emission mechanism in Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures. Formation of MnOx-rich interfacial regions around Mn–Sb nanodomains generates defect states that act as carrier trapping and radiative recombination centers, giving rise to efficient green photoluminescence (518–525 nm).

Although XRD, SAED, XRF, Raman, and XPS collectively support the formation of Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures, direct identification of Sb-containing local environments remains challenging because Sb signals are weak in surface-sensitive measurements. Future studies employing high-resolution STEM-EDS mapping, synchrotron-based XPS, or X-ray absorption spectroscopy would provide deeper insight into the local atomic structure and electronic interactions governing defect-assisted emission.

Conclusion

Defect-emissive Mn–Sb/MnOx quantum nanostructures were successfully synthesized through a green ethanol-assisted colloidal reflux route. The nanostructures exhibited an average particle size of ∼5.3 nm and broad green photoluminescence centered at 518–525 nm with a maximum PLQY of 62.3%. Combined XRD, SAED, XRF, Raman, and XPS analyses suggest the presence of Mn–Sb-rich domains embedded within MnOx-rich surface environments, resulting in a structurally heterogeneous nanoscale system. The optical response is attributed to defect-mediated and interface-assisted radiative recombination associated with surface oxidation, interfacial disorder, and ligand-passivated defect states. These results demonstrate that interfacial defect engineering provides an effective route toward efficient light emission in lead-free transition-metal-based nanostructures.
