Comparison of vertical releasing incisions and horizontal extending incisions for periodontal accelerated osteogenic orthodontics in the anterior region: a randomized controlled trial with clinical and radiographic outcomes
Authors: Yifan Chen, Kangli Jiang, Zirui Bian, Xiaoyu Chen, Qian Lin, Yuxuan Yang, Zhuoming Zhang, Boxiu Li, Gang Sun, Jingyi Tan, Yanmin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontal accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) requires precise flap design for aesthetic preservation, functional stability, and rapid recovery. This study compared two additional flap incisions—vertical releasing incision (VRI) and horizontal extending incision (HEI) for PAOO in the anterior region.MethodsTwenty-two orthodontic patients requiring PAOO were randomized into the VRI or HEI group. Immediately after surgery, the surgical time and graft material weight were recorded. Wound healing was evaluated at 1 and 2 weeks post-surgery. Soft-tissue parameters, such as probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), keratinized gingival width (KGW), and gingival recession depth (GRD), were assessed at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months. To assess hard-tissue changes, including root length, vertical bone level (VBL), and bone width measured at 2 mm (BW2), 4 mm (BW4), and 6 mm (BW6) below the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ), cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were performed at baseline, 6, and 12 months. Lateral cephalometric analysis was performed to evaluate tooth movement before and after orthodontic treatment.ResultsPatient-level analyses revealed that the HEI technique had shorter surgical time (57.66 ± 5.01 min vs. 68.50 ± 13.50 min, p = 0.029) and a lower incidence of scarring (10% vs. 60%) than VRI. At the tooth-level, HEI achieved significantly greater labial bone width (BW2, BW4, BW6; all p < 0.001). Both groups showed comparable KGW increases (VRI: 0.71 ± 1.15 mm; HEI: 0.85 ± 1.13 mm) and VBL changes (VRI: 2.94 ± 2.79 mm; HEI: 2.69 ± 2.96 mm) at 12 months. No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding postsurgical pain or mucosal condition.ConclusionsBoth flap techniques were effective in improving KGW and bone augmentation (VBL, BW2, BW4, BW6), with HEI showing advantages in terms of surgical time, reduced scar formation, and greater bone width in PAOO surgery.Trial registrationRegistered on clinicaltrial.gov; NCT05441683, 13/6/2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07270-x.

Introduction

Healthy periodontium serves as the fundamental prerequisite for orthodontic treatment [1]. To mitigate periodontal risks (e.g., dehiscence, fenestration, and gingival recession) in orthodontic patients, periodontal accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) has emerged as a widely adopted therapeutic approach [2]. Recent controlled trials have further supported its safety and efficacy in improving periodontal conditions [3–5]. Despite its benefits, this invasive surgical procedure carries risks of postoperative complications (pain, infection, swelling) and crestal bone loss, factors known to adversely affect bone regeneration potential [6]. The procedure remains highly technique-dependent and demands appropriate flap design for successful surgical intervention. This design should offer access to alveolar bone, cover particulate grafts, preserve interdental tissue height and volume, and enhance gingival aesthetics when needed [7].

To manage flap contraction and bone volume augmentation in PAOO, additional vertical releasing incisions (VRIs) are often employed to alleviate flap tension [4, 8–10]. Additionally, VRI can improve the visibility of the surgical field while minimizing the exposure of bone in nondiseased areas in periodontal surgeries [11, 12]. However, VRI is prone to unesthetic scars postoperatively, so it often fails to achieve ideal results for esthetic zone or patients with esthetic demands [13, 14]. Furthermore, the flap integrity and vascularization may be compromised by VRI, which can induce soft tissue trauma, greater postoperative inflammation, and delayed healing [15].

To achieve a tension-free flap while overcoming the limitations of vertical incisions, we employed horizontal extending incisions (HEIs), bilaterally lengthening the envelope flap two teeth per side beyond the cortical osteotomy area. This type of incision has been demonstrated to provide good esthetic results and preserve the lateral distribution of the vascular supply in the treatment of gingival recession [14, 16]. Moreover, compared to vertical incisions, envelope flaps can significantly shorten the surgical time [17]. This increased efficiency can decrease the duration of tissue exposure and desiccation, which is crucial for mitigating postoperative pain and swelling in periodontal surgeries [18, 19].

We aimed to determine whether bilateral releasing incision design (VRI vs. HEI) influences PAOO outcomes, specifically in patients with anterior alveolar bone defects. The primary objective was to compare the surgical time depending on the flap design. The secondary objectives included assessing scar formation, postoperative pain, mucosal conditions, and long-term periodontal soft- and hard-tissue stability.

Materials and methods

Trial design and setting

Between July 2022 and September 2024, a single-center, parallel-group design clinical trial was conducted. The experimental procedures complied with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki 1975, as revised in 2013. The study was approved by the Human Subject Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (no. 2022–0436), registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05441683). The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guideline was used for the designing and reporting of this trial. All participants permitted this study and signed an informed consent agreement.

Participants

Patients undergoing orthodontic treatment requiring PAOO were recruited. The inclusion criteria were: 1) aged 18 to 40 years; 2) with thin labial alveolar bone (< 0.5 mm) and/or the presence of dehiscence/fenestration in the maxillary or mandibular incisor and canine regions; 3) with expected labial movement of one or more anterior teeth beyond the labial alveolar bone, as assessed by the orthodontist on pre-treatment CBCT scans; 4) adequate oral hygiene, the full-mouth plaque and bleeding scores ≤ 25%; and 5) no previous periodontal surgery in the experimental area.

Patients meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: 1) smokers; 2) pregnant or lactating; 3) uncontrolled periodontal infection; 4) presence of < 2 mm keratinized gingival width [20]; 5) systemic diseases (e.g., diabetes mellitus, nephrosis, hepatopathy, hypertension, neutropenia) or use medications (immunosuppressive drugs, bisphosphonates, etc.); and 6) poor adherence to completing the one-year follow-up.

Randomization, allocation concealment and blinding

Randomization was performed by means of a simple randomization method using a 1:1 allocation ratio. An individual unrelated to the clinical portion of the study created a list of participant numbers (1–22) in Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and generated corresponding random numbers, which were then sorted in ascending order and split into two groups. Surgical incision names (HEI or VRI) were placed in opaque envelopes. An assistant enrolled participants based on the inclusion criteria and allocated participant numbers in the order of their visits. During the surgery, this assistant provided the sealed envelope with the participant number to the surgeon (W.Y.M.). Two blinded examiners (J.K.L. and C.Y.F.) were calibrated by performing duplicate measurements on 10 non-study participants involving 40 sites at a two-week interval. It was not possible to mask either the surgeon or the participants regarding the incision position.

Treatment procedures

The study was coordinated with the orthodontist to optimize the treatment plan based on the patient's condition, considering tooth extraction and anchorage design when necessary. For patients undergoing fixed appliance therapy, a standard archwire sequence was used (0.014-inch round, 0.018-inch round, 0.018 × 0.025-inch rectangular nickel-titanium alloys, and 0.019 × 0.025-inch stainless steel). Patients were scheduled for follow-up appointments at 4-week intervals. For patients treated with clear aligners, digital impressions were obtained using an iTero intraoral scanner (Align Technology, Tempe, AZ) for treatment planning via the ClinCheck (Invisalign) software. Each patient was instructed to wear the aligners for 20–22 h per day, advancing to the next set every two weeks. Clinical monitoring was performed at 4-week intervals.

Prior to surgery, all patients underwent orthodontic leveling and aligning. Preoperative preparations included radiographic examination and routine tests (blood tests, coagulation function tests, and infectious disease tests). Routine oral hygiene education and initial periodontal therapy were provided under the supervision of the periodontist. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon W.Y.M. under local infiltration anesthesia.

In the VRI group, a papilla preserving incision was performed on the labial side of the anterior teeth, preserving the interdental gingival tissues and extending one tooth beyond the canine (typically from the first premolar to the first premolar). Vertical incisions were then added bilaterally to the distal side of the first premolar. They were placed mesial to the papilla rather than through the interdental papilla or midradicularly. The incision formed an oblique trapezoidal flap with a wider base, which facilitated blood supply. Eventually, the vertical incisions extended beyond the mucogingival junction into the alveolar mucosa, with careful attention to preserving neurovascular structure (Fig. 1A). The full-thickness flaps were elevated approximately 5 mm beyond the apices of the anterior teeth. Next, vertical interproximal cortical bone incisions were made below the alveolar bone crest to a depth of 2 to 3 mm using piezoelectric bone surgery device, typically starting 1–2 mm below the alveolar crest. Bone mineral (Bio-Oss, Geistlich Biomaterials AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) was then placed above the activated alveolus. The volume of the graft material used is dictated by the direction and amount of tooth movement predicted, the pretreatment thickness of the alveolar bone, and the need for labial support by the alveolar bone. The graft area was covered with resorbable collagen membranes (HaiAo, HaiAo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), which were sutured to the periosteum to maintain the stability of bone graft materials. A split-thickness dissection was performed in the apical parts of the surgical site. Gingival flaps were coronally repositioned at the original pre-surgical site. The flaps were secured with a single-sling suture and interrupted interdental sutures using non-absorbable 5–0 Prolene (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA). Fig. S1 provides clinical photographs of all procedural steps for both surgical interventions.

Standard postoperative care included cefuroxime axetil 250 mg BID (7 days) and compound paracetamol 450 mg PRN. Chlorhexidine mouth rinses (0.12%) were recommended twice daily for 2 weeks. Sutures were removed 2 weeks postoperatively, and patients were able to resume orthodontic treatment 2 weeks after surgery.

Outcomes

Surgical time

The operative duration was recorded using a calibrated timer, from the initiation of the first incision to the completion of the final suture placement. The individual responsible for recording was located outside the operating room, with the start and end signals conveyed by the same nurse.

Particulate bone grafting material weight

The net weight of the bone graft material before surgery was recorded as W1. After surgery, the remaining bone graft material was collected, dried, and weighed by the same electronic scale as W2. The amount of bone graft material used during surgery was calculated as ΔW = W1 − W2.

Wound healing and scar formation

The following parameters were scored at 1 and 2 weeks after PAOO (T1w and T2w) [21]: facial swelling: "0" for barely facial swelling, "1" for mild facial swelling, "2" for severe facial swelling; mucosal color: "0" for no redness along the incision, "1" for slight redness, "2" for obvious redness; mucosal edema: "0" for no edema around the surgery area, "1" for mild edema, "2" for severe edema; membrane exposure (yes/no); pain level: measured with visual analog scale (VAS) score 0 (no pain/normal sensation) to 10 (intolerable pain/complete numbness). Gingival scarring was evaluated at 12 months through visual inspection. Participants were classified as scar-positive if any visible scar was present at the surgical site, including normotrophic scars (flat and white) and pathological scars (raised or irregular in shape).

Periodontal soft tissue measurements

The following clinical periodontal indicators were measured on the mid-buccal aspect of teeth at different time points (T0: before surgery; T1m: 1 month; T3m: 3 months; T6m: 6 months; and T12m: 12 months after surgery): probing depth (PD), keratinized gingival width (KGW), gingival recession depth (GRD), and clinical attachment level (CAL, sum of PD and GRD). All data were measured twice by the same calibrated examiner using a manual probe (PCP-UNC 15, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, Illinois, USA), and the average value was used.

Radiographic measurements

All anterior teeth were measured using Mimics software (version 21.0; Materialise Mimics Medical, Belgium). All cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were performed by the same experienced radiologist using the Planmeca ProMax 3D (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland). Identical imaging parameters were employed: 90 kV, 6.0 mA, 0.160 mm voxel size, 10 × 8 cm field of view (FOV). The detailed protocol of CBCT measurements was modified based on previous studies [3, 22], which were described in Fig. 2. Sample size

An independent two-sample t-test model was performed based on the results of a previous study [17]. The surgical time of coronally advanced flap procedures with or without VRI were 34.6 and 28.7 min, respectively. The standard deviations were 3.9 and 2.2 min. The type I error probability (α) was set at 0.05, and the statistical power (1 − β) was at 95%. Referring to these data, G*Power 3.1 software (University of Duisburg-Essen, Germany) determined that 9 patients per group were required. Assuming a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 11 patients per group, totaling 22 patients for the study.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) at both patient- and tooth-level. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons between groups using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Quantitative data including periodontal and radiographic measurements were expressed as mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR).

Intergroup comparisons utilized independent t-tests (normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-normal). The median differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the skewed data were estimated using the Hodges-Lehmann method. Changes over time were evaluated using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. All tests were conducted with a two-tailed hypothesis, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. To evaluate measurement reliability, intra- and intergroup differences were analyzed using the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from duplicate assessments (40 sites, 2-week interval).

Results

From July 2022 to August 2023, this study recruited 22 eligible adult participants. The follow- up period lasted until 12 months post-surgery. After randomization, one in the VRI group was excluded due to postoperative smoking, and one participant in the HEI group withdrew due to pregnancy planning. In total, 20 patients and 117 anterior teeth (two central incisors and one lateral incisor in two patients were congenitally missing) were included in the present study (Fig. S2). The postoperative orthodontic treatment length for completed cases was 18.41 ± 7.08 months in the VRI group (n = 9) and 22.10 ± 7.20 months in the HEI group (n = 8). Post-surgical outcomes

Membrane exposure and pain levels are shown in Table 2. The incidence of membrane exposure (VRI: 10% vs. HEI: 30%) showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.582). For the pain score, we examined the changes from T1w to T2w, which showed a significant reduction in both groups. Nevertheless, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups at either T1w (p = 0.709) or T2w (p = 0.838).

The healing scores of facial swelling, mucosal color, and mucosal edema are described in Table S1. Nearly all patients experienced mild postoperative mucosal redness and swelling during the first week. Facial swelling was significantly reduced at T2w compared to T1w in both groups (p < 0.05). The VRI group showed a significant reduction in mucosal edema from T1w to T2w (p < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups over the two-week period. No cases of postoperative numbness or infection were reported in any of the study participants.

Long-term follow-ups

A clinically apparent difference in scarring was observed between groups (Fig. 3). The VRI group presented a higher incidence of noticeable scars (60%, 6/10) compared to the HEI group (10%, 1/10). Discussion

Existing evidence demonstrates that periodontal flap design significantly affects both postoperative complications [19, 23] and long-term esthetic/functional outcomes [13].

However, the impact of the PAOO flap design on clinical outcomes has not been clearly reported. To our knowledge, this present RCT may be the first to systematically evaluate and compare technical efficiency, patient-centered outcome, and tissue-level response between two flap incision techniques (VRI and HEI) for PAOO. In this study, the average surgical time for the HEI group was 10.84 min shorter than that of the VRI group, which was in line with the findings of Zucchelli et al. [17]. This shortening may be due to the continuous horizontal incisions and simplicity of the suturing alignment. Using VAS measurement at postoperative days 0, 3, 5, and 7, Tan et al. [24] identified longer surgery time (≥ 60 min) as a key determinant of worse patient-reported outcomes (bleeding/swelling/pain/bruising). However, there were no significant differences in pain level, facial swelling, mucosal edema, or mucosal color between VRI and HEI groups during the first and second weeks post-surgery. From an anatomical perspective, Koymen et al. [12] observed all the intra-oral vascular territories in 10 cadaver specimens through both macroscopic and stereo-microscopic assessments, and concluded that the vascular richness of the oral mucosal area provides sufficient healing potential.

Notably, the mental neurovascular bundle, which typically exits the mental foramen (usually located apical to the second mandibular premolar), is at risk during vertical incisions in PAOO surgery, with the potential for temporary or permanent postoperative complications such as paresthesia, hypoesthesia, or dysesthesia of the lower lip and chin [25]. In contrast, the HEI technique poses no risk of direct mental nerve injury, thereby enhancing patient safety. Consequently, preoperative radiographic evaluation is imperative to determine the precise size, location, and any anatomical variations of the mental foramen, which informs the development of a surgical strategy for nerve protection [26].

Another observation in the present study was the incidence of scarring was higher in the VRI group (60% vs. 10%), which may be attributed to the impaired blood supply. Given the biologic and esthetic drawbacks, many periodontal surgeons have designed flap procedures without VRI [14, 27, 28]. Zucchelli et al. [17] found that there was no difference in patient satisfaction with regard to aesthetics for the treatment of gingival recession with or without VRI, whereas an independent expert periodontist provided better esthetic evaluations for flaps without VRI. Since the scars were located at the distal of the first premolar, they exhibited limited impact on both aesthetics and function. However, this finding warrants clinical attention due to potential patient-reported concerns.

It is worth noting that membrane exposure occurred more frequently in the HEI group (30%, 3/10 cases) at the 2-week follow-up, all of which were in the canine regions. This phenomenon was potentially due to restricted apical visibility during flap elevation, leading to increased flap tension. The single exposure case in VRI group was located in the anterior mandible, likely resulting from over-coronal graft positioning and excessive flap advancement. Subsequent observations confirmed this issue did not affect membrane stability or wound healing. Traboulsi-Garet et al. [15] conducted a randomized, split-mouth study on pig mandibles, demonstrating that trapezoidal flaps with bilateral VRI had a significantly greater flap extension compared to envelope flaps without VRI. Therefore, performing only HEI should sufficiently prevent flap tearing or excessive tension, and tension-free primary suturing should be applied. Kim et al. [28] successfully achieved sufficient flap advancement without vertical incisions in implant cases, using extended incisions, periosteal release and selective muscle relaxation. Their study also reported membrane exposures after surgery (2 of 40 guided bone regeneration sites), attributed to physical irritation from removable prostheses.

In this study, we analyzed periodontal soft-tissue parameters, including PD, CAL, KGW, and GRD between the two groups, but no significant differences were observed. Both surgeries achieved a significant increase in the width of keratinized gingiva at 12 months post-treatment (VRI group: 0.71 ± 1.15 mm, HEI group: 0.85 ± 1.13 mm). This is consistent with Wilcoko et al. [29], who reported that among orthodontic patients treated with alveolar corticotomy and bone grafting, a 0.78 mm average gain in keratinized gingival tissue height labial to the mandibular incisors was observed at 19.4 months after debonding. They considered the stretching of the full-thickness periosteal flap and the increased alveolar dimension from the augmentation bone grafting resulted in this value-added outcome. Building on this, Ferguson et al. [30] further demonstrated through a case–control study that mandibular incisor movement (inclination, prominence, and vertical height changes) did not affect the increase in labial keratinized gingival width following PAOO. Additionally, the HEI group showed a slight increase in gingival recession (p = 0.079), which may be correlated with its higher membrane exposure incidence. The established evidence confirms that the absence of primary and tension-free wound closure directly contributes to flap dehiscence, premature membrane exposure, and gingival recession [23].

Accounting for tooth displacement and root resorption during orthodontic treatment, the CEJ was chosen as the anatomical reference point for measurements. Furthermore, standardized CBCT imaging parameters and consistent measurement planes were implemented to ensure accurate measurements with high data stability and comparability. Previous studies [4, 9] have shown minimal differences in alveolar bone changes between incisors and canines after PAOO. Therefore, this trial measured periodontal hard-tissue parameters uniformly across all anterior teeth based on a CBCT method. Root resorption during orthodontic treatment is unavoidable [31]. Our measurements revealed root resorption in both study groups. This finding is consistent with the study by Ahn et al. [9], who reported significant root length reduction (mean −0.6 mm) in mandibular anterior teeth among orthodontic patients with or without augmented corticotomy. However, some studies have reported no significant root resorption following corticotomy, and the incorporation of bone grafts had little impact on the incidence or amount of root resorption. They speculated that this could be attributed to local, transient, clinically induced osteoporosis and shorter treatment duration [32, 33].

Studies have shown a trend of increasing buccal bone thickness towards the apex following PAOO, indicating that implanting particulate grafting material into cervical defects remains challenging [3, 4, 8, 9]. In this study, we measured VBL and BW at 2, 4, and 6 mm below the CEJ, as these measurements correspond to the mid-to-upper cortical bone region. Both surgical techniques increased alveolar height in the anterior region. However, the HEI group consistently outperformed the VRI group at all levels of bone thickness, suggesting that the HEI approach more effectively stabilized the bone graft, reducing the risk of fenestration and dehiscence during orthodontic treatment, possibly due to the pouch-like structure formed. The periosteal pocket flap technique, introduced by Steigmann et al. [34], promotes horizontal bone regeneration. Ma et al. [35] applied periosteal covering in PAOO and observed vertical bone augmentation in the buccal anterior mandibular region, but no increase in horizontal bone thickness. In contrast, Liu et al. [10] combined collagen membranes with periosteum to form a pouch in PAOO, resulting in a significant increase in both horizontal bone thickness (0.84 mm) and VBL 2.35 mm), which was significantly greater than the control group.

The positional change of anterior teeth may represent a confounding factor in evaluating hard and soft tissue responses. Although some cases required first premolar extraction followed by anterior retraction (VRI: 4/10 cases, HEI: 3/10 cases), the distribution did not differ significantly between groups. Overall, cephalometric analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in incisor inclination or protrusion within or between the groups. This finding further demonstrates the efficacy of PAOO in enhancing hard and soft tissue dimensions, suggesting the differences in bone thickness between groups may be independent of incisor positional changes.

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the soft tissue contours in the esthetic zone were not evaluated. Furthermore, the 12-month follow-up may not reflect final outcomes, as most patients were still undergoing active orthodontic treatment. Longer-term evaluation after orthodontic completion is needed to assess stability. Additionally, a larger sample size is recommended in future research to validate these findings, as the present study was not adequately powered to detect small but potentially clinically important differences in the secondary outcomes. This trial was not designed to compare outcomes across specific types of malocclusion or orthodontic appliances. Therefore, more prospective clinical trials should be conducted on populations with different treatment scenarios to improve the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Compared with the VRI technique, HEI offers advantages in terms of surgical efficiency, esthetic results, and bone augmentation in PAOO. However, ensuring tension-free closure is crucial when employing HEI. Strategic selection of incision design can help minimize complications and improve long-term treatment outcomes.
