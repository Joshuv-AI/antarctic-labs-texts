Cancer Risk Among Young Adults With Clinically Diagnosed Fatty Liver Disease: A Nationwide Cohort Study in South Korea
Authors: Sun Ju Lee, Yongho Jee, Ho Soo Chun, Hyun‐Young Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a growing public health concern, particularly among young adults. While MASLD is a well‐established risk factor for liver‐related complications, growing evidence suggests an association with several extrahepatic cancers. However, data regarding cancer risk among young adults with MASLD remain limited. This study investigates the association between MASLD and cancer risk in young adults in South Korea.MethodsA nationwide cohort study was conducted using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, including individuals aged 20–39 years who underwent health screenings (2004–2007). Clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease was identified using ICD‐10 code K76.0, and incident cancers were identified using ICD‐10 malignant neoplasm codes recorded in the National Health Insurance Service claims database through 2022. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for cancer risk, adjusting for confounders.ResultsAmong 4,094,241 participants, 254,897 (6.22%) had clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease. Clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease was significantly associated with increased all‐cancer incidence in both men (HR: 1.48, 95% CI 1.45–1.51) and women (HR: 1.23, 95% CI 1.20–1.27). The highest risks were observed for liver cancer (men, HR: 2.39, 95% CI 2.28–2.50, women, HR: 2.88, 95% CI 2.56–3.23), thyroid cancer (men, HR: 1.49, 95% CI 1.43–1.55, women, HR: 1.18, 95% CI 1.13–1.24). BMI‐based fatty liver disease (BMI < 25 kg/m2) showed a higher liver cancer risk. Non‐drinkers with clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease also had increased cancer risk.ConclusionsClinically diagnosed fatty liver disease in young adults was associated with a higher risk of both hepatic and extrahepatic cancers. Further studies are needed to determine appropriate risk‐stratified prevention and surveillance strategies in this population.

Introduction

Metabolic Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) is a disease caused by hepatic steatosis and closely related to obesity, diabetes, and metabolic disorders [1]. The prevalence has increased worldwide; there are reports that the prevalence of MASLD is up to 33.3% in the Republic of Korea and more than 25% worldwide [2, 3, 4]. Body mass index (BMI) significantly affected MASLD prevalence (≤ or > 25 kg/m2, 12.3% vs. 41.7%, p < 0.001), and the age under 50 was 39.8% in men and 17.0% in women, and the age over 50 it was 43.5% in men and 25.8% in women [5, 6].

Recently, the awareness of MASLD has increased, and the clinical burden of MASLD is significant as it progresses to metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), liver cirrhosis, and liver cancer, resulting in an increase in mortality or other diseases [7].

Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that MASLD is associated not only with liver cancer but also with several extrahepatic malignancies, including colorectal, thyroid, pancreatic, and breast cancers [8, 9, 10]. Previous studies have reported that the incidence of extrahepatic cancers among patients with MASLD may exceed that of hepatocellular carcinoma. Proposed mechanisms include chronic metabolic inflammation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and DNA damage, all of which may contribute to carcinogenesis [11].

In a study on MASLD and the risk of cancer, the age at diagnosis of MASLD plays an important role in cancer incidence. Although several studies have reported an association between MASLD and cancer risk, most investigations have focused on middle‐aged or older adults. Evidence specifically addressing cancer risk among young adults with MASLD remains limited, and existing findings have been inconsistent [8, 9, 12].

In addition, it is commonly known that the prevalence of MASLD increases in obese patients, but there are reports that patients with BMI‐based MASLD have not only a higher incidence of cardiovascular disease and cancer, but also higher all‐causes mortality [13, 14, 15].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease and incident cancer among young adults using a nationwide Korean cohort.

We further examined whether cancer risk differed between participants with fatty liver disease and BMI < 25 kg/m2 and those with fatty liver disease and BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study utilized data from the South Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS) database, a comprehensive national health insurance system overseen by the government, which covers about 97% of the population. The KNHIS database encompasses demographic information, medical treatments, procedures, prescriptions, diagnoses based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM), hospital utilization, and results from national health screenings. The standardized National Health Screening Program, offered biennially by KNHIS, targets all insured individuals aged 20 and above, including all employees irrespective of age, with a participation rate of approximately 76%. This health screening includes anthropometric measurements, laboratory tests, and a self‐administered questionnaire covering medical history and lifestyle habits Figure 1 depicts the selection process for young adults aged 20–39 years.

Flowchart of study population. Participants were excluded if they had a history of cancer before cohort entry, heavy alcohol consumption (≥ 30 g/day for men or ≥ 20 g/day for women), or missing information on key study covariates. Definition of Fatty Liver Disease

Because the study period preceded the recent MASLD nomenclature and consensus criteria, clinically recognized fatty liver disease was operationally identified using the ICD‐10 code K76.0 (fatty liver disease, not elsewhere classified) recorded during hospitalization or outpatient visits between 2004 and 2007.

Administrative claims data do not contain the detailed metabolic information required for contemporary MASLD diagnosis. Therefore, the exposure in this study should be interpreted as a claims‐based diagnosis of clinically recognized fatty liver disease rather than a strict implementation of the current MASLD definition.

Measurement of Smoking and Alcohol

Smoking history was categorized into non‐smokers, former smokers, and current smokers. Alcohol consumption was categorized as non‐drinkers and current drinkers based on self‐reported responses obtained though the National Health Screening Program questionnaire. For current drinkers, alcohol intake was estimated using drinking frequency and the amount of alcohol consumed per occasion. Drinking frequency was converted to weekly drinking frequency, and the amount consumed per occasion was converted into the number of standard drinks. Average alcohol consumption was calculated as weekly drinking frequency × number of drinks consumed per occasion × 13 g of ethanol, divided by 7 to estimate daily alcohol intake (g/day). To minimize potential misclassification related to alcohol‐associated liver disease, individuals with alcohol consumption ≥ 30 g/day for men or ≥ 20 g/day for women were excluded from the analysis. Alcohol consumption was included as a covariate in the multivariable analyses and was further evaluated in subgroup analyses according to alcohol intake levels.

Outcome Ascertainment

Participants with a history of cancer before cohort entry were excluded from the study. Cancer incidence was ascertained using claims records from the National Health Insurance Service database. Incident cancer was defined by the presence of an ICD‐10 malignant neoplasm code (C00–C99) recorded as either the primary or secondary diagnosis during follow‐up through 2022. Site‐specific cancers were identified using predefined ICD‐10 codes, and the earliest medical care start date associated with the corresponding cancer diagnosis was considered the incident date. 2.5.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were evaluated using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and the chi‐square (χ2) test for categorical variables. The incidence rates of digestive cancers were determined by dividing the number of new cases by the total follow‐up duration, and the results were expressed per 100,000 person‐years. The risk of cancer incidence associated with fatty liver disease was estimated using hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals through a Cox proportional hazards model, adjusting for confounding variables. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 4,094,241 participants were included in the study, of which 254,897 (6.2%) were diagnosed with fatty liver disease (ICD‐10 code K76.0) between 2004 and 2007. The baseline characteristics of the study population, stratified by fatty liver disease status, are presented in Table 1. Participants with fatty liver disease were older on average, with a mean age of 32.98 years compared to 30.84 years in those without fatty liver disease (p < 0.0001). Fatty liver disease participants also had a significantly higher mean body mass index (BMI) of 24.29 kg/m2 compared to 22.73 kg/m2 in non‐fatty liver disease participants (p < 0.0001). Fasting serum glucose levels were higher in the fatty liver disease group (96.12 mg/dL vs. 89.08 mg/dL, p < 0.0001), as were total cholesterol levels (196.85 mg/dL vs. 182.49 mg/dL, p < 0.0001). Similarly, liver enzyme levels, including GOT, GPT, and GGT, were significantly elevated in participants with fatty liver disease (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons). Regarding anthropometric measurements, individuals with fatty liver disease had a higher mean weight (73.36 kg vs. 64.35 kg, p < 0.0001).

Association of Fatty Liver Disease With Major Cancer Incidence by Gender

Table 2 presents the association between fatty liver disease and the incidence of major cancers, stratified by gender. For men, the incidence rate of all cancers was higher in the fatty liver disease group compared to those without fatty liver disease, yielding a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.48 (95% CI: 1.45–1.51). Similarly, women with fatty liver disease had a higher incidence rate of all cancers compared to non‐fatty liver disease women, with an HR of 1.23 (95% CI: 1.20–1.27). Among specific cancers, liver cancer (ICD‐10: C22) showed a particularly strong association with fatty liver disease in both men and women. Men with fatty liver disease had an HR of 2.39 (95% CI: 2.28–2.50) compared to those without fatty liver disease, while women had an HR of 2.88 (95% CI: 2.56–3.23). Fatty liver disease was also associated with an increased risk of thyroid cancer (ICD‐10: C73) in both men (HR: 1.49, 95% CI: 1.43–1.55) and women (HR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.13–1.24).

Cancer Risk by Fatty Liver Disease and Obesity Status Stratified by Alcohol Consumption

Tables S1 and S2 present the association between clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease and cancer risk within strata of alcohol consumption, separately for men and women.

Among men, clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease was associated with a higher risk of all cancer both among non‐drinkers (HR. 1.59; 95% CI: 1.53–1.64) and drinkers (HR. 1.44; 95% CI: 1.40–1.47), each compared with participants without fatty liver disease within the corresponding drinking stratum. Similarly, liver cancer risk was higher in participants with fatty liver disease than in those without fatty liver disease among both non‐drinkers (HR. 2.60; 95% CI: 2.40–2.81) and drinkers (HR. 2.27; 95% CI: 2.15–2.41). Among non‐drinkers, fatty liver disease was also associated with an increased risk of prostate cancer (HR. 1.69; 95% CI: 1.49–1.92) (Table S1).

Among women, clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease was likewise associated with an increased risk of all cancer in both non‐drinkers (HR. 1.26; 95% CI: 1.21–1.30) and drinkers (HR. 1.19; 95% CI: 1.14–1.25), compared with participants without fatty liver disease within each drinking stratum. Fatty liver disease was also associated with an increased risk of liver cancer among both non‐drinkers (HR. 3.43; 95% CI: 2.98–3.94) and drinkers (HR. 2.07; 95% CI: 1.67–2.56), and with an increased risk of thyroid cancer among non‐drinkers (HR. 1.20; 95% CI: 1.13–1.28) and drinkers (HR. 1.12; 95% CI: 1.04–1.21) (Table S2).

Sensitivity Analyses

After additional adjustment for baseline hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and cirrhosis, the association between clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease and liver cancer was attenuated but remained statistically significant in both men (HR. 1.65; 95% CI: 1.57–1.73) and women (HR. 2.31; 95% CI: 2.05–2.59).

In landmark analyses excluding the first year of follow‐up, clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease remained associated with all‐cancer risk in men (HR. 1.44; 95% CI: 1.41–1.47) and women (HR. 1.21; 95% CI: 1.17–1.24). For liver cancer, the corresponding estimates after additional adjustment for viral hepatitis and cirrhosis were 1.57 (95% CI: 1.49–1.65) in men and 2.20 (95% CI: 1.94–2.50) in women. Exclusion of the first two years of follow‐up yielded similar, although further attenuated, associations for liver cancer in men (HR. 1.48; 95% CI: 1.40–1.56) and women (HR. 2.14; 95% CI: 1.87–2.45).

When fatty liver disease was restricted to participants with K76.0 recorded on at least two distinct medical‐care dates, the associations remained significant for all cancer in men (HR. 1.22; 95% CI: 1.20–1.23) and women (HR. 1.14; 95% CI: 1.11–1.18), and for liver cancer in men (HR. 1.13; 95% CI: 1.09–1.17) and women (HR. 1.57; 95% CI: 1.43–1.73) (Table S5).

Discussion

Our study found that young adults diagnosed with clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease had an increased incidence of various cancers, including liver cancer (Figure 2). Specifically, the risks of liver cancer, kidney cancer, and thyroid cancer were found to be elevated. Additionally, the study observed an increased incidence of bladder cancer in men and pancreatic cancer in women. The attenuation of the liver cancer estimates after adjustment for viral hepatitis and cirrhosis indicates that competing liver disease etiologies explained part of the association observed in the primary analysis. Nevertheless, the association remained statistically significant after adjustment and after exclusion of the early follow‐up period. Similarly, a more specific exposure definition requiring repeated K76.0 diagnoses yielded smaller but directionally consistent estimates. Collectively, these findings suggest that residual confounding, surveillance bias, reverse causation, and exposure misclassification may have contributed to the magnitude of the primary estimates, although they did not fully account for the observed associations. The direction of the association was robust across sensitivity analyses, although the magnitude was sensitive to the exposure definition and adjustment for underlying liver disease.

Previous studies have reported associations between the age of MASLD diagnosis and the subsequent development of cancer. It has been shown that the occurrence of MASLD at a younger age significantly increases the risk of cancer (average hazard ratio [AHR], 1.52; 95% CI, 1.09–2.12), with a particularly pronounced effect observed in digestive system cancers and lung cancers (AHR 2.00; 95% CI, 1.08–3.47 and AHR 2.14; 95% CI, 1.05–4.36) [16]. Additionally, the longer the duration since the onset of MASLD, the greater the risk of developing cancer, although this risk decreased with increasing onset age of MASLD (ages 45–54 years: AHR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.15–1.97; ages 55–64 years: AHR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.97–1.33; ages > 65 years: AHR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.45–1.27) [17]. Another study reported an increased incidence of thyroid cancer among young adults having MASLD, with the risk escalating in line with higher Fatty Liver Index (FLI) scores [18]. These findings suggest a correlation between the age at MASLD (then‐called NAFLD) diagnosis and genetic variants implicated in liver disease progression [16, 19]. Moreover, the prevalence of MASLD at a younger age has been linked to liver cancer through mechanisms involving long‐term liver damage, which not only affects hepatocytes but also impairs the function of cells in other organs, influencing metabolic and systemic diseases [16].

The association between MASLD and the development of liver cancer has been widely reported in numerous studies. Research has shown that MASLD patients first diagnosed at a young age, particularly those under 45, have a higher prevalence of liver cancer (AHR 2.66, 95% CI 1.16–6.11) [16]. Another study, using a 1:2 matched cohort based on age, sex, race, BMI, and type 2 diabetes, reported a liver cancer incidence of 89 per 100,000 person‐years in the MASLD group compared to 0 in the control group, highlighting a significantly higher incidence in the MASLD group [19].

In our study, an increase in liver cancer incidence was observed among young adults with fatty liver disease. Notably, the incidence was higher in individuals with a BMI below 25 compared to those with a BMI above 25. While obesity is recognized as a risk factor for progression from MASLD to liver cancer [17], there are many studies suggesting that lean MASLD has a poor prognosis for death, including all‐cause mortality, liver‐related mortality, and cardiovascular‐related mortality, than non‐lean MASLD [20, 21]. This finding might be explained by the fact that having a normal weight not only do they have more co‐morbid conditions such as central obesity, sarcopenia, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and hypertension, but also may progress to MASH, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, and HCC [20, 22, 23]. Lean MASLD is also suggested by genetic disorders associated with the PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 genes [24]. Further research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying lean MASLD and non‐lean MASLD for the proper approach and management of people with MASLD. However, BMI‐based subgroup analyses were defined using body mass index alone and did not incorporate detailed metabolic characteristics; therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as reflecting fully characterized lean MASLD phenotypes.

There have been several studies reporting on the occurrence of non‐hepatic cancers in patients with MASLD. Some studies suggest that the incidence of non‐liver cancers may be higher than that of liver cancer in MASLD patients, although the mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear [8]. It is hypothesized that various processes, such as metabolic disorders like obesity and diabetes, chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, extrahepatic injury, and gut dysbiosis, may contribute to the transformation into cancer cells. A systematic review reported that in MASLD patients, the hazard ratio (HR) for thyroid cancer was 2.63 (95% CI, 1.27–5.45) with moderate evidence grade; for pancreatic cancer, HR was 1.98 (95% CI, 1.47–2.65); for breast cancer, HR was 1.28 (95% CI, 1.00–1.63); and for lung cancer, HR was 1.25 (95% CI, 1.11–1.40) with low evidence grade [8].

Another study found that among those diagnosed with MASLD before the age of 45, the cancers that were most prevalent were digestive system cancers and lung cancers (AHR 2.00; 95% CI: 1.08–3.47 and AHR 2.14; 95% CI: 1.05–4.36, respectively) [16]. A meta‐analysis of observational cohort studies indicated that MASLD is associated with a 1.5‐ to 2‐fold increased risk of developing gastrointestinal cancers (esophagus, stomach, pancreas, or colorectal cancers) and approximately a 1.2‐ to 1.5‐fold increased risk of developing lung, breast, gynecological, or urinary system cancers [22].

Regarding kidney and bladder cancers, a previous meta‐analysis found that MASLD is associated with a nearly 30% increased risk of urinary system cancers (kidney or bladder cancers) (pooled random‐effects HR 1.33; 95% CI: 1.04–1.70; I 2 = 10.4%) [15]. Another study in Israel reported that the kidney cancer in patients with MASLD had a higher incidence compared to the general population (0.8% vs. 0.5%, p‐value < 0.001) [23]. A study from Sweden found that the risks of kidney cancer (aHR 2.12) and bladder cancer (aHR 2.51) were increased compared to the people of MASLD [25]. Regarding bladder cancer, our study found a significant association in men but not in women. Similar results showed a positive association between bladder cancer and MASLD (OR 2.61; 95% CI: 1.30–5.22) and that males had the association (OR 2.34, 95% CI: 1.61–3.41) in the study from Taiwan [26]. These results indicate that further research is needed to explore the gender differences in the relationship between MASLD and bladder cancer.

Concerning thyroid cancer, this study found an increased risk in both men and women, with a higher risk observed in men (HR 1.49 for men, HR 1.18 for women). A meta‐analysis also reported that MASLD is associated with an approximately 2.5‐fold increased risk of thyroid cancer (HR 2.63; 95% CI: 1.27–5.45) [10]. The risk was also higher in those with a high vs. low FLI (HR 1.491 vs. HR 1.347). A Korean study focusing on young adults found that compared with participants with an FLI of < 30, those with an FLI of 30 to 60 had a significantly higher risk of thyroid cancer (men: HR 1.36; 95% CI: 1.22–1.51 and women: HR 1.44; 95% CI: 1.21–1.70), with even higher risks in those with an FLI of ≥ 60 (men: HR 1.71; 95% CI: 1.53–1.92 and women: HR 1.81; 95% CI: 1.46–2.25) [12]. In our study, the risk of thyroid cancer was notably higher in obese young adults, reconfirming a significant correlation between obesity and the development of thyroid cancer [27].

Regarding pancreatic cancer, this study found a 1.71‐fold increased risk in men and a 2.30‐fold increased risk increase in women. Other studies have reported an approximately 85% increased risk of pancreatic cancer (pooled random‐effects HR 1.84; 95% CI: 1.23–2.74; I 2 = 0%) [10] and in the UK biobank study, the association of Metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) with pancreatic cancer was HR 1.32, 95% CI: 1.05–1.67 only in men, and was not significant in women [28]. In another analysis of Mendelian randomization, there was no association between genetically predicted MASLD and pancreatic cancer [29]. To elucidate the association between pancreatic cancer and MASLD, more research is needed in the future.

In the alcohol‐stratified analyses, clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease remained associated with an increased risk of overall site‐specific cancers in both drinkers and non‐drinkers. The magnitude of the association tended to be greater among non‐drinkers than drinkers for several cancers, suggesting that drinking stratum may modify the association between fatty liver disease and cancer risk. However, because these analyses compared participants with and without fatty liver disease within each drinking stratum, rather than directly comparing drinkers with non‐drinkers, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that alcohol consumption itself reduces cancer risk among individuals with fatty liver disease. Further studies specifically designed to evaluate the independent effects of alcohol consumption are warranted [30, 31].

The prevalence of clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease observed in our cohort (6.2%) was lower than estimates reported in population‐based screening studies. This difference is likely attributable to the use of an administrative claims‐based definition, which identifies clinically recognized cases rather than all individuals with hepatic steatosis. Unlike imaging‐based studies or investigations using validated surrogate markers such as the Fatty Liver Index, claims‐based approaches depend on healthcare utilization and physician diagnosis and therefore may yield lower prevalence estimates, particularly among asymptomatic young adults. Accordingly, the prevalence reported in this study should be interpreted as the prevalence of clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease rather than the overall burden of steatotic liver disease in the general population. In addition, individuals with clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease may have more frequent healthcare utilization and diagnostic evaluations than those without fatty liver disease. This increased medical surveillance could have enhanced opportunities for cancer detection and may have partially contributed to the observed associations.

This study has several limitations. First, fatty liver disease was identified using the KCD code K76.0 recorded in administrative claims data. Although this approach has been widely used in administrative database studies, it does not fully capture the contemporary consensus definition of MASLD, which additionally requires evidence of metabolic dysfunction and consideration of alcohol intake. Therefore, some degree of exposure misclassification is possible. Furthermore claims‐based definitions generally identify clinically recognized cases and may underestimate the true prevalence of fatty liver disease compared with screening‐based studies using imaging or validated surrogate biomarkers.

In addition, because claims‐based definitions depend on healthcare utilization and physician recognition, they may preferentially identify individuals who seek medical attention, potentially introducing selection bias toward clinically recognized disease. In addition, differences in healthcare utilization patterns may have influenced the likelihood of cancer detection, potentially introducing surveillance bias.

Additionally, the study lacks information on the severity of fatty liver disease. Moreover, as an observational cohort study, this research is more focused on identifying associations rather than establishing causality. In addition, although major demographic and metabolic factors were adjusted for, residual confounding from competing etiologies of liver cancer, including chronic hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and pre‐existing cirrhosis, cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the observed association between fatty liver disease and liver cancer should be interpreted with caution. There may be residual confounding that prevents full control and adjustment for risk factors related to various cancers.

Nevertheless, the strengths of this study include its use of the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) big data in the republic of Korea, which allowed for a comprehensive analysis of all young adults in their 20s and 30s who underwent national health examinations. The study is distinguished by its consistent follow‐up on the occurrence of cancer from the point of initial fatty liver disease diagnosis. Furthermore, as South Korea has universal health coverage, this study represents a full‐population analysis of patients with clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease identified during routine healthcare processes, making it a valuable long‐term observational study using Korean healthcare data.

Conclusions

In this nationwide cohort study, clinically diagnosed fatty liver disease in young adults was associated with an increased risk of several cancers, including liver, kidney, thyroid, and pancreatic cancers. These findings suggest that young adults with clinically recognized fatty liver disease may represent a population at elevated long‐term cancer risk. However, given the observational nature of the study and the limitations inherent to claims‐based disease ascertainment, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying biological mechanisms and to determine appropriate risk‐stratified prevention and surveillance strategies for young adults with fatty liver disease.
