Extended Janeway lesions in Enterobacter infective endocarditis: a case report
Authors: Fadi Abou Deb, George Abou Deb, Badie Alkhouli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfective endocarditis is a rare but serious infection of the heart valves, with various clinical manifestations. Diagnosis relies on the modified Duke criteria, in which Janeway lesions can be an important diagnostic feature.Case presentationWe report a case of a Middle Eastern male patient in his mid-60s with history of ischemic heart disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and hypertension who presented with respiratory symptoms and extensive skin lesions. A skin biopsy confirmed the lesions as Janeway lesions, assisting in the diagnosis of infective endocarditis caused by Enterobacter species. Despite aggressive antimicrobial therapy, the patient’s clinical condition failed to improve, highlighting the challenges in managing infections caused by this organism.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of recognizing atypical manifestations of infective endocarditis and utilizing diagnostic tools, such as skin biopsy, for accurate diagnosis. It emphasizes the need for effective management strategies in extended cases caused by uncommon organisms such as Enterobacter.

Background

Infective endocarditis (IE) constitutes a rare yet severe infection affecting the endocardium and heart valves [1] with annual incidence ranging from 3 to 10 cases per 100,000 individuals [2]. Primary predisposing factors for IE include prosthetic valve insertion, prior occurrences of IE, untreated cyanotic congenital heart disease, bicuspid aortic valve, mitral valve prolapse, cardiac implantation, advanced age, intravenous drug use, dental procedures, corticosteroid administration, HIV infection, and malignancies [3, 4].

Clinical manifestations of IE vary; it may present acutely with rapid progression or manifest as a subacute or chronic presentation with mild fever and nonspecific symptoms [5]. Predominant symptoms include fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, night sweats, myalgia, dyspnea, and abdominal discomfort [6]. Additionally, characteristic signs such as newly developed cardiac murmurs, splenomegaly, petechiae, splinter hemorrhages, Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, and Roth spots may be evident in IE patients [7]. Moreover, IE may present with complications such as valve regurgitation, heart failure, embolic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, brain abscess, or septic emboli [8].

Staphylococcus aureus is the most frequently isolated organism in blood cultures of IE patients [9]. Other causative agents include viridans group streptococci, enterococci, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Streptococcus bovis, fungi, and the HACEK group [10]. Enterobacter species are a rare cause of infective endocarditis, with very few reported cases in literature. In a meta-analysis of 2761 confirmed cases of endocarditis, only two were linked to Enterobacter infections. It was observed that more than half of patients with non-HACEK Gram-negative bacterial endocarditis had healthcare-associated infections. A significant number of these patients had endovascular devices, such as prosthetic valves or permanent pacemakers and defibrillators, which may have contributed to the risk of infection [11].

Diagnosis of IE typically employs the modified Duke criteria; a histological demonstration of pathological lesions indicating endocarditis suffices for diagnosis, or the presence of two major clinical criteria, one major and three minor clinical criteria, or five minor clinical criteria is required [12]. Major criteria include positive blood cultures (two separate cultures revealing typical microorganisms of IE, such as Staphylococcus aureus, viridans streptococci, Streptococcus gallolyticus, and HACEK group bacteria, or at least two positive cultures drawn more than 12 h apart displaying organisms commonly implicated in IE, or the majority of four or more separate cultures yielding agents more likely to be skin contaminants, or a single positive culture for Coxiella burnetii, or an immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody titer greater than 1:800), and evidence of endocardial involvement on echocardiography (vegetation, abscess, or new partial dehiscence of prosthetic valve) or new valvular regurgitation. Minor criteria include predisposing factors, fever, vascular phenomena, immunologic phenomena, and microbiologic evidence not meeting major criteria [13].

Janeway lesions manifest as asymmetrical, painless hemorrhagic spots located on the palms, soles, thenar, and hypothenar eminences of the hands, as well as the plantar surfaces of the toes. These lesions typically persist for days to weeks and are commonly associated with the acute presentation of bacterial endocarditis. They are presumed to originate from septic microemboli arising from valvular pathology. Typically, cultures of biological samples yield positive results. Histologically, Janeway lesions exhibit microabscess formation within the dermal layer along with thrombosis of small blood vessels, albeit devoid of vasculitis [14].

This case underscores the atypical presentation of extended Janeway lesions in a patient with infective endocarditis caused by the rare organism Enterobacter, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize that symptoms may diverge from established clinical profiles. The identification of such unusual manifestations is critical in preventing the oversight of serious conditions such as infective endocarditis, particularly when the clinical presentation is atypical. This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a comprehensive and flexible approach in clinical evaluations. By disseminating these findings, we aim to enhance the understanding of infective endocarditis, facilitating earlier diagnosis and improved patient outcomes for individuals presenting with similar atypical signs.

Case presentation

A Middle Eastern man in his mid-60s, with significant smoking history of 50 pack-years and medical history that included coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) for ischemic heart disease (IHD) 10 years prior, followed by implantation of a dual-chamber pacemaker, chronic kidney disease (CKD), hyperthyroidism, hypertension (HTN), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), was admitted to the acute medical unit.

The patient’s complaints commenced 10 days prior to admission with respiratory symptoms, including a yellow productive cough and dyspnea. Despite these symptoms, he did not seek medical attention. However, 5 days prior to admission, he noticed a painless, rapidly expanding black purpura on his legs and hands, which progressed to ulceration 2 days later, accompanied by rigors and subjective fever.

On admission, his vital signs were within normal limits. The physical examination revealed an elevated jugular venous pressure, pallor, and widespread purpura affecting both legs and arms, including the dorsal aspects of the feet and hands, extending over the extensor surfaces of the extremities (Figs. 1, 2). There was ulceration within the purpura on the dorsal aspect of the left hand. Peripheral pulses, including the radial, brachial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and dorsalis pedis arteries, were all present. An electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated sinus rhythm with left-axis deviation, PR interval prolongation (220 ms), a trifascicular block, and pacemaker failure to capture (Fig. 3). Blood tests revealed leukocytosis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and raised C-reactive protein (CRP), while thyroid function tests were normal. Peripheral blood cultures yielded growth of Enterobacter species after 48 h.

Further investigations revealed normal p-ANCA, c-ANCA, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and complement component 3 (C3) levels. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was 7.2%, indicating suboptimal glycemic control.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) demonstrated left ventricular global hypokinesis, with an ejection fraction (EF) of 25–30%, advanced diastolic dysfunction (grade 3), moderate mitral regurgitation, mild aortic regurgitation, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and a pacemaker wire in the right ventricle, without features of infective endocarditis. Doppler ultrasound of the vessels demonstrated normal arterial and venous flow, with no evidence of stenosis or thrombosis. Given the extensive cutaneous manifestations, a skin biopsy was performed on the purpuric lesions of the knees, feet, left elbow, and left hand, targeting the edge of a well-demarcated ulcer. Histopathological examination demonstrated pustules containing bacterial colonies, spongiosis, keratinocyte necrosis, and interface changes, with a mild superficial perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate in the upper dermis, consistent with Janeway lesions, and no evidence of vasculitis.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnoses, based on the patient’s signs and symptoms, included infective endocarditis with septicemia, vasculitis, and diabetic-related skin conditions, such as necrobiosis lipoidica and diabetic dermopathy. Infective endocarditis was confirmed by positive blood cultures and TOE findings, further supported by histopathological evidence from the skin biopsy. Vasculitis and diabetes-related dermatoses were excluded through the biopsy and other additional investigations.

Treatment

Upon admission, the patient was commenced on empirical antimicrobial therapy, consisting of intravenous vancomycin 1 g twice daily alongside piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g four times daily. Following the blood culture results, piperacillin–tazobactam was replaced with meropenem 1 g every 12 h. Additionally, tramadol was administered for pain relief, and diuretic therapy was initiated with intravenous furosemide 80 mg twice a day.

Follow-up and outcome

Despite aggressive medical therapy, there was no improvement in the patient’s clinical or laboratory parameters after 12 days of admission, and the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, leading to the necessity for transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). The pacemaker was removed percutaneously, and the multidisciplinary team recommended repeating the TOE to assess for any newly developed abscesses or large vegetations that may have required surgical intervention. However, owing to the patient’s deteriorating condition, it was deemed unfeasible to repeat the TOE, and surgical intervention was considered too high risk. Therefore, the decision was made to continue medical treatment in the ICU, with close monitoring for complications, along with ongoing assessment and management by the multidisciplinary team. Unfortunately, despite all treatment and monitoring, the patient passed away after 6 days in the ICU.

Discussion

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a severe infection affecting the endocardium and heart valves, presenting with a wide range of clinical manifestations. Janeway lesions, characterized by painless hemorrhagic spots, typically found on the palms, soles, and other regions of the extremities, are considered a clinical hallmark of acute bacterial endocarditis [15, 16]. These nontender, small erythematous and hemorrhagic skin lesions result from septic emboli lodging in cutaneous blood vessels, leading to localized skin damage, characteristic of Janeway lesions [14]. However, the occurrence of extensive Janeway lesions, extending beyond their usual distribution, is an exceedingly rare phenomenon, with no documented cases in literature to date.

This case report describes a unique instance of extensive Janeway lesions in a patient with infective endocarditis. The patient, who had multiple comorbidities, presented with respiratory symptoms and purpura with ulceration in the extremities. Given the unusual presentation of the Janeway lesions, a comprehensive differential diagnosis was pursued to ascertain the underlying etiology. The diagnosis of infective endocarditis was confirmed on the basis of the Duke criteria, with visualization of vegetation along the pacemaker wire evident on TOE [17].

The management of infective endocarditis generally involves a combination of antimicrobial therapy and, in some cases, surgical intervention [18]. However, despite receiving culture-guided antimicrobial therapy, our patient showed no improvement in clinical or laboratory parameters. The poor prognosis in this case was significantly impacted by the involvement of Enterobacter species, alongside the patient’s advanced heart failure, CKD, hypertension, smoking, and T2DM [19]. Additionally, while the extensive Janeway lesions are not directly linked to prognosis, they may reflect a heavy embolic burden and an aggressive disease course [20]. These factors collectively highlight the poor prognosis in cases of infective endocarditis caused by drug-resistant pathogens and compounded by significant comorbidities, which limit both surgical and medical treatment options [21].

Enterobacter species is an exceptionally rare cause of infective endocarditis. It is associated with poor outcomes, and mortality rates exceed 40% despite targeted antimicrobial therapy [22]. The organism’s inherent resistance to multiple antibiotics, along with the ability to form biofilms on intracardiac devices, contributes to this high mortality rate [23]. However, we cannot definitively conclude that the extent of the Janeway lesions in this case is directly related to the Enterobacter species. To the best of our knowledge, there are no previous reports in literature associating extended Janeway lesions with Enterobacter-related infective endocarditis. This makes it an intriguing area for future research to explore the mechanisms contributing to such unusual clinical presentations in infective endocarditis.

The role of skin biopsy in this case was pivotal. Although the diagnosis of infective endocarditis had already been established prior to the biopsy, it played a crucial role in confirming the nature of the skin lesions and excluding alternative etiologies. Initially, conditions such as vasculitis or diabetes-related skin disorders were considered owing to the unusual appearance and ulceration of the lesions. However, the biopsy ultimately confirmed the diagnosis of Janeway lesions, further supporting the diagnosis of infective endocarditis. This underscores the importance of incorporating skin biopsy into the diagnostic workup in cases presenting with atypical skin manifestations associated with infective endocarditis, facilitating accurate diagnosis and appropriate management [24, 25].

The presence of extended Janeway lesions on the hands, feet, knees, and elbows, along with ulceration, in our patient represents an exceptionally rare phenomenon. While Janeway lesions are acknowledged as a hallmark of acute bacterial endocarditis, their extension beyond the typical distribution on the palms and soles, coupled with ulceration, is exceedingly uncommon [14]. The precise pathophysiological mechanism underpinning the development of extended Janeway lesions in this context remains elusive and warrants further investigation.

The rarity of cases featuring extensive Janeway lesions reported in literature highlights the uniqueness of this presentation, emphasizing the need for increased awareness among healthcare professionals to consider atypical manifestations as potential diagnostic indicators of infective endocarditis. The scarcity of such cases further underscores the importance of thorough evaluation and investigation when faced with atypical signs, as these unique presentations can provide valuable insights into the underlying disease process and inform treatment decisions.

The publication and dissemination of this case report contribute to the existing medical literature by shedding light on a rare and unique clinical phenomenon of extensive Janeway lesions in infective endocarditis. It serves as a reminder to clinicians to maintain vigilance for unusual presentations and manifestations, even within well-established conditions such as infective endocarditis. Furthermore, it encourages further research and reporting of similar cases to enhance our understanding of the diverse clinical spectrum of infective endocarditis and its associated complications.

Conclusion

This case report presents a rare presentation of infective endocarditis caused by Enterobacter species, complicated by unusually large and extended Janeway lesions. Such an atypical manifestation has not been documented previously in literature. This case underscores the critical importance of recognizing atypical manifestations such as extended Janeway lesions in patients with infective endocarditis. The utilization of diagnostic tools, such as skin biopsy, proved vital in confirming the diagnosis. Despite the initiation of aggressive treatment, significant management challenges persisted, highlighting the necessity for comprehensive and multidisciplinary care strategies. Clinicians should remain alert to unusual presentations to ensure prompt intervention and improve patient outcomes. Additionally, future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms that link rare pathogens to atypical skin manifestations, with the aim of advancing our understanding and management of such complex clinical scenarios.
