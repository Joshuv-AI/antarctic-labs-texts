Connection formulae for the radial Toda equations II Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author.
Martin A. Guest, Alexander R. Its, Maksim Kosmakov, Kenta Miyahara ∗, Ryosuke Odoi

Abstract
. This is a continuation of [ GIKMO ] in which we computed the asymptotics near x = ∞ x=\infty of all solutions of the radial Toda equation. In this article, we compute the asymptotics near x = ∞ x=\infty of all solutions of a “partner” equation. The equations are related in the sense that their respective monodromy data constitute connected components of the same “monodromy manifold”. While all solutions of the radial Toda equation are smooth, those of the partner equation have infinitely many singularities, and this makes the Riemann-Hilbert nonlinear steepest descent method (and the asymptotics of solutions) more involved. Keywords : Toda equation; Painlevé III equation; isomonodromic deformation; Riemann-Hilbert problem; steepest-descent method



1 Introduction and Main Results

In
[
GIKMO
]
, we studied the large and small
x
asymptotic behaviors of the solutions of the radial Toda equation of type
A_{2}
, namely

(1.0.1)

\displaystyle(w_{0})_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}(w_{0})_{x}=2e^{-2w_{0}}-2e^{4w_{0}},

where
w_{0}(x)
is a real-valued function on the positive real line. We performed the desired asymptotic analysis by using the Lax-integrability of equation (
1.0.1
), a fact that goes back to Mikhailov
[
Mik
]
:

(1.0.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Psi_{\zeta}=\left(-\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}W-\frac{x}{\zeta}w_{x}-x^{2}W^{T}\right)\Psi\\
\Psi_{x}=\left(-w_{x}-2x\zeta W^{T}\right)\Psi,\end{cases}

where

(1.0.3)

\displaystyle w=\diag(w_{0},0,-w_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad W=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-w_{0}}&0\\
0&0&e^{-w_{0}}\\
e^{2w_{0}}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Our study of the radial Toda equation was motivated by the series of works on the tt*-Toda equations by Guest-Its-Lin
[
GuLi12
,
GuLi14
,
GIL1
,
GIL2
,
GIL3
,
GIL4
]
. The global solutions are physically important
[
CV1
,
CV2
,
CV3
]
and we have been trying to understand the asymptotic behaviors of more general real-valued radial solutions on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. For both the radial Toda equations and the tt*-Toda equations, we have used a fusion of the Iwasawa factorization method in loop group theory and the Riemann-Hilbert nonlinear steepest descent method of Deift and Zhou
[
DZ
]
.

In
[
GIKMO
]
we observed that, for a given solution of (
1.0.1
), the corresponding monodromy data for the first linear equation of (
1.0.2
) belongs to a
2
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Explicitly, this manifold can be described by four real parameters
(A^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},x^{\mathbb{R}},y^{\mathbb{R}})
satisfying two algebraic equations:

(1.0.4)

\displaystyle(A^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}A^{\mathbb{R}}=(x^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}+(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2},

\displaystyle(1+s^{\mathbb{R}})A^{\mathbb{R}}+2x^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{3}.

This has two connected components, given (respectively) by
A^{\mathbb{R}}>0
and
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
. (See Figure
1
for a representation of the manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.)

.

\mathcal{M}

In
[
GIKMO
]
we showed that the (real) solutions
w_{0}(x)
of (
1.0.1
) correspond to the component
A^{\mathbb{R}}>0
, and (based on the Riemann-Hilbert method) we observed that points in the component
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
would arise from the monodromy data of non-real solutions
w_{0}(x)=v_{0}(x)+\frac{i\pi}{2}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, where
v_{0}(x)
is real. In other words, the function
v_{0}(x)
is a real-valued solution of the following “new” differential equation

(1.0.5)

\displaystyle(v_{0})_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}(v_{0})_{x}=-2e^{-2v_{0}}-2e^{4v_{0}},

which could be regarded as a “monodromy partner” of (
1.0.1
). The main result of this paper is to prove that the (real) solutions
v_{0}(x)
of (
1.0.5
) correspond precisely to the component
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
, by means of a detailed examination of the asymptotics of all solutions near
x=\infty
. Thus the relation between connected components of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
and equations (
1.0.1
) and (
1.0.5
) is not trivial and of some interest in its own right.

Theorem 1.1
.

The monodromy manifold corresponding to the solutions of (
1.0.1
) is the connected component of
\mathcal{M}
defined by (
1.0.4
) with
A^{\mathbb{R}}>0
. The monodromy manifold corresponding to the solutions of (
1.0.5
) is the connected component of
\mathcal{M}
defined by (
1.0.4
) with
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
.

The radial Toda equation (
1.0.1
) is known to be equivalent to a special case of the third Painlevé equation (PIII) of type
D_{7}
, see
[
GIKMO
, section 2]
. The same holds for the partner equation, see Corollary
1
(as well for the tt*-Toda equation, in fact). From the Painlevé property, it follows that the radial Toda equation, its partner equation (and the tt*-Toda equation) can have only isolated logarithmic poles as “movable singularities” between zero and infinity. In the case of the radial Toda equation, it is easy to verify that these singularities cannot be real (see
[
GIKMO
, Proposition 2.1]
) by general ODE arguments. In the case of the partner equation, there is no such restriction, indeed our asymptotic analysis shows that they appear in the generic solutions of those equations.

The equation PIII(
D_{7}
) is also known as the degenerate Painlevé III equation and has been studied in several contexts. One of the important global aspects is to find the large and small
x
asymptotics and to connect the asymptotic parameters. In 1987, A. Kitaev
[
Kitaev
]
did pioneering work in this direction for a particular choice of parameters, and later Kitaev-Vartanian
[
KitV1
,
KitV2
]
extended the result for arbitrary choice of parameters for purely real solutions and purely imaginary solutions. They used an alternative
2\times 2
Lax pair and applied the WKB method to the associated direct monodromy problem.

Although the result by Kitaev-Vartanian already captures the singular solutions of the partner equation (
1.0.5
), our Riemann-Hilbert approach will give another proof of the large
x
asymptotics, and along the way prove Theorem
1.1
.
More importantly, the approach taken in
[
KitV1
,
KitV2
]
does not readily generalize to tackling the asymptotics of the partner equations of the radial Toda equation and the tt*-Toda equation of type
A_{n}
for general
n>1
, which is our overarching goal. In contrast, our
3\times 3
Riemann-Hilbert approach for
n=2
is more promising to generalize to the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
problem for general
n
in principle.

It is important to notice that this paper deals with a
3\times 3
Riemann-Hilbert problem with singularities which gives extra complication in the Riemann-Hilbert analysis, see section
3
. In the
2\times 2
situation, there are several prior studies
[
FIS
,
BothnerIts
]
, but this paper will be the first to apply the scheme of those works to the higher rank problems.

Our formulation of the large
x
asymptotics of (
1.0.5
) is as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
(A^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},x^{\mathbb{R}},y^{\mathbb{R}})
,
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
satisfying (
1.0.4
), there is a real solution
v_{0}(x)
of

\displaystyle(v_{0})_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}(v_{0})_{x}=-2e^{-2v_{0}}-2e^{4v_{0}},

such that

(1.0.6)

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\vartheta(x)}{2-\sin\vartheta(x)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1}),\quad x\to\infty,

where

\displaystyle\vartheta(x)=-2\sqrt{3}x+i\nu_{0}\ln(24\sqrt{3}x)-\frac{\pi}{2}-\arg(x^{\mathbb{R}}+iy^{\mathbb{R}})-\arg\Gamma(-\nu),

\displaystyle\nu=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\ln 3A^{\mathbb{R}}=-\frac{1}{2}+\nu_{0},\quad\nu_{0}\in i\mathbb{R}.

The asymptotics is uniform outside of the small neighborhood of each singularity appearing in (
1.0.6
).

Remark
.

The asymptotic formula (
1.0.6
) implies that if we label the singularities of
\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\vartheta(x)}{2-\sin\vartheta(x)}\right)
by
x_{n}
in the increasing order on the real line, then
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
large enough, one has

(1.0.7)

\displaystyle x_{n}=\frac{2n\pi+\pi/3+i\nu_{0}\ln(24n\pi)-\arg\left(x^{\mathbb{R}}+iy^{\mathbb{R}}\right)-\arg\left(\Gamma(-\nu)\right)}{2\sqrt{3}}+o(1).

This describes asymptotic locations of movable singularities of
v_{0}(x)
for
x
large.

Corollary 1
.

The change of variables

(1.0.8)

\displaystyle x=\frac{3}{4}s^{\frac{2}{3}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{w}=s^{\frac{1}{3}}e^{-2v_{0}}

transform (
1.0.5
) into the following
\mathrm{PIII}(D_{7})
equation with
(\alpha,\,\beta,\,\gamma,\,\delta)=(1,0,0,1)
in the standard notation,

(1.0.9)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{ss}=\frac{\tilde{w}_{s}^{2}}{\tilde{w}}-\frac{\tilde{w}_{s}}{s}+\frac{\tilde{w}^{2}}{s}+\frac{1}{\tilde{w}},

and vice versa.
Then, it follows from Theorem
1.2
that the large
x
asymptotics of a solution to (
1.0.9
) is given by
the formula

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(s)=s^{1/3}\,\frac{2-\sin\tilde{\vartheta}(s)}{1+\sin\tilde{\vartheta}(s)}+\mathcal{O}\left(s^{-2/3}\right),\quad s\to\infty.

where
\tilde{\vartheta}(s)=\vartheta\left(\frac{3}{4}s^{2/3}\right)
and
\vartheta
is defined in Theorem
1.2
.

2 Direct Monodromy Problem

2.1  The Partner Equation

First, we recall from (2.1.3) of
[
GIKMO
]
the Lax pair

(2.1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Psi_{t}=\left(w_{t}+\frac{1}{\lambda}W\right)\Psi\\
\Psi_{\bar{t}}=\left(-w_{\bar{t}}-\lambda W^{T}\right)\Psi\end{cases}

for the Toda equation
2(w_{0})_{t\bar{t}}=e^{-2w_{0}}-e^{4w_{0}}
, where
w_{0}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
. The partner equation

(2.1.2)

\displaystyle 2(v_{0})_{t\bar{t}}=-e^{-2v_{0}}-e^{4v_{0}}

where
v_{0}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
has an analogous Lax pair

(2.1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\Psi}_{t}=\left(v_{t}+\frac{1}{\lambda}V\right)\tilde{\Psi}\\
\tilde{\Psi}_{\bar{t}}=\left(-v_{\bar{t}}-\lambda JV^{T}J\right)\tilde{\Psi}\end{cases}

where
v=\diag\left(v_{0},0,-v_{0}\right)
,
J=\diag(-1,1,-1)
, and

(2.1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-v_{0}}&0\\
0&0&e^{-v_{0}}\\
e^{2v_{0}}&0&0\end{pmatrix}=e^{-v}\Pi e^{v},\quad\Pi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}

We remark that (
2.1.1
) and (
2.1.3
) are associated to different real forms of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}}_{3}\mathbb{C}
consisting of
3\times 3
complex traceless matrices.
For the Toda equation, if we restrict
\lambda
to the circle
|\lambda|=1
, then the
1
-form
d\Psi\Psi^{-1}
takes values in the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}}_{3}
, i.e. the set of matrices
X\in{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}}_{3}\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\bar{X}^{T}=-X
.
In contrast, for the Lax pair (
2.1.3
) of the partner equation, the
1
-form
d\tilde{\Psi}\tilde{\Psi}^{-1}
takes values in the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}}_{2,1}
, given by the condition
J\bar{X}^{T}J=-X
.

The difference in real forms corresponds to the “change of variable”
v_{0}=w_{0}-\frac{i\pi}{2}
in the equations for
w_{0},v_{0}
. The Lax pairs are related in the same way. Namely,
e^{w}=e^{v}K
where
K=\diag(i,1,-i)
, so we have

(2.1.5)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=e^{-w}\Pi e^{w}=K^{-1}e^{-v}\Pi e^{v}K=K^{-1}VK,

(2.1.6)

\displaystyle W^{T}

\displaystyle=Ke^{v}\Pi^{T}e^{-v}K^{-1}=K^{-1}JV^{T}JK.

It follows that (
2.1.3
) is obtained — formally — from (
2.1.1
) by putting

(2.1.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}=K\Psi.

However, it is important to note that we assume
w_{0}
is real in (
2.1.1
), whereas
v_{0}
is real in (
2.1.3
), and these cannot hold simultaneously.
Therefore we interpret the formula
v_{0}=w_{0}-\frac{i\pi}{2}
as a change of
complex
variables, and impose the reality conditions later.

Let us now impose the radial conditions
w_{0}(t,\bar{t})=w_{0}(x)
and
v_{0}(t,\bar{t})=v_{0}(x)
where
x=|t|
, so

(2.1.8)

\displaystyle(w_{0})_{t\bar{t}}=\frac{1}{4}\left((w_{0})_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}(w_{0})_{x}\right),\quad(v_{0})_{t\bar{t}}=\frac{1}{4}\left((v_{0})_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}(v_{0})_{x}\right).

For the Toda equation, this gives rise to the isomonodromic Lax pair (
1.0.2
); the analogue of this for the partner equation is

(2.1.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\Psi}_{\zeta}=\left(-\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}V-\frac{x}{\zeta}v_{x}-x^{2}JV^{T}J\right)\tilde{\Psi}\\
\tilde{\Psi}_{x}=\left(-v_{x}-2x\zeta JV^{T}J\right)\tilde{\Psi}\end{cases}

As in the case of equation (2.2.1) of
[
GIKMO
]
, therefore, our first task will be to discuss the monodromy data of the equation

(2.1.10)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{\zeta}=\left(-\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}V-\frac{x}{\zeta}v_{x}-x^{2}JV^{T}J\right)\tilde{\Psi}.

The simple relation (
2.1.7
) makes this particularly straightforward. In particular, we may use the same Stokes sectors
\Omega_{n}^{(0,\infty)}
where
n=1,2
as defined in
[
GIKMO
]
, and obtain analogous formal solutions

(2.1.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{\Psi}_{f}^{(0)}(\zeta)&=e^{-v}\Omega(I+\mathcal{O}(\zeta))e^{\frac{1}{\zeta}d_{3}},\\
\tilde{\Psi}_{f}^{(\infty)}(\zeta)&=Je^{v}\Omega^{-1}\left(I+\mathcal{O}\left(\zeta^{-1}\right)\right)e^{-x^{2}\zeta d_{3}}\end{aligned}

where

(2.1.12)

\displaystyle\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&\omega&\omega^{2}\\
1&\omega^{2}&\omega\end{pmatrix},\quad d_{3}=\diag(1,\omega,\omega^{2}),\quad\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}

from
\Psi^{(0)}_{f}
and
\Psi^{(\infty)}_{f}
in (2.2.2) and (2.2.3) of
[
GIKMO
]
by the above change of variable and (
2.1.7
).
In each Stokes sector, there is a unique canonical solution that satisfies

(2.1.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\tilde{\Psi}_{n}^{(0)}(\zeta)\sim\tilde{\Psi}_{f}^{(0)}(\zeta),\quad\zeta\to 0,\quad\zeta\in\Omega_{n}^{(0)},\\
&\tilde{\Psi}_{n}^{(\infty)}(\zeta)\sim\tilde{\Psi}_{f}^{(\infty)}(\zeta),\quad\zeta\to\infty,\quad\zeta\in\Omega_{n}^{(\infty)}.\end{aligned}

Then, we define Stokes matrices
\tilde{S}^{(0)}_{n},\tilde{S}^{(\infty)}_{n}
and connection matrices
\tilde{E}_{n}
for (
2.1.9
), just as we defined
S^{(0)}_{n},S^{(\infty)}_{n}
and
E_{n}
for (
1.0.2
).

Proposition 2.1
.

The symmetries satisfied by
\tilde{S}^{(0)}_{n},\tilde{S}^{(\infty)}_{n},\tilde{E}_{n}
are identical to the symmetries satisfied by
S^{(0)}_{n},S^{(\infty)}_{n},E_{n}
(that is, the former symmetries are obtained from the latter symmetries by adding tildes).

Proof.

We omit the proof, which is entirely analogous to the computations in
[
GIKMO
]
of the symmetries satisfied by
S^{(0)}_{n},S^{(\infty)}_{n},E_{n}
.
∎

2.2  More about the Monodromy Data

The symmetries of
\tilde{S}^{(0)}_{n},\tilde{S}^{(\infty)}_{n},\tilde{E}_{n}
imply that they can be parametrized by the same monodromy data
m=(A^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},x^{\mathbb{R}},y^{\mathbb{R}})
subject to (
1.0.4
) as defined in
[
GIKMO
]
, the only difference (as we explain later) being that we have

A^{\mathbb{R}}>0
for the radial Toda equation and
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
for the partner equation.
In particular,

(2.2.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{n}^{(\infty)}=\tilde{Q}_{n}^{(\infty)}\tilde{Q}_{n+\frac{1}{3}}^{(\infty)}\tilde{Q}_{n+\frac{2}{3}}^{(\infty)},\quad\tilde{S}_{n}^{(0)}=\tilde{Q}_{n}^{(0)}\tilde{Q}_{n+\frac{1}{3}}^{(0)}\tilde{Q}_{n+\frac{2}{3}}^{(0)}

where

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{(\infty)}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{1\frac{1}{3}}^{(\infty)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&-a\omega^{2}&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{1\frac{2}{3}}^{(\infty)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
a&0&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{(\infty)}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-a\omega^{2}&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{2\frac{1}{3}}^{(\infty)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&a\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{2\frac{2}{3}}^{(\infty)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-a\omega^{2}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{(0)}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-a&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{1\frac{1}{3}}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&a\omega^{2}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{1\frac{2}{3}}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-a\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{(0)}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\omega^{2}&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\tilde{Q}_{2\frac{1}{3}}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\

0&1&0\\
a\omega^{2}&0&1\end{pmatrix},

with

, and

(2.2.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\mathbb{R}}&B&\overline{B}\\
B&\omega s^{\mathbb{R}}A^{\mathbb{R}}-\omega^{2}s^{\mathbb{R}}B+\overline{B}&A^{\mathbb{R}}\\
\overline{B}&A^{\mathbb{R}}&\omega^{2}s^{\mathbb{R}}A^{\mathbb{R}}+B-\omega s^{\mathbb{R}}\overline{B}\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{E}_{2}=\frac{1}{9}d_{3}\tilde{E}_{1}^{-1}d_{3}

where

and
\omega B=x^{\mathbb{R}}+iy^{\mathbb{R}}
.

We shall see that the sign of
A^{\mathbb{R}}
has a significant impact on the implementation of the Riemann-Hilbert analysis of solutions.

Before getting to that point, we will give a detailed parametrization of the monodromy manifold
\mathcal{M}
corresponding to
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
. (For a parametrization of the part of
\mathcal{M}
corresponding to
A^{\mathbb{R}}>0
, see
[
GIKMO
, Proposition 2.14 and the following paragraphs]
.)
From (
1.0.4
),

(2.2.3)

\displaystyle(1+s^{\mathbb{R}})A^{\mathbb{R}}+2x^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{3}\iff x^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1-3(1+s^{\mathbb{R}})A^{\mathbb{R}}}{6},

and

(2.2.4)

\displaystyle(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}=(A^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}A^{\mathbb{R}}-(x^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}.

Substituting (
2.2.3
) into (
2.2.4
) gives

(2.2.5)

\displaystyle(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}=\frac{(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})}{4}(A^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}-\frac{1-s^{\mathbb{R}}}{6}A^{\mathbb{R}}-\frac{1}{36}

As we are assuming
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
, this formula shows that
s^{\mathbb{R}}<1
.
Solving (
2.2.5
) for
A^{\mathbb{R}}
gives

(2.2.6)

\displaystyle A^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})\pm\sqrt{(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}+(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})(1+36(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2})}}{3(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})}

when
s^{\mathbb{R}}\neq-3
, and

(2.2.7)

\displaystyle A^{\mathbb{R}}=-\frac{3(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{24}

for

.
When
-3<s^{\mathbb{R}}<1
, only the negative sign in (
2.2.6
), i.e.,

\displaystyle A^{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{1}{3(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})}-\frac{2}{3+s^{\mathbb{R}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{36}+\frac{3+s^{\mathbb{R}}}{1-s^{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\frac{1}{36}+(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}\right)}.

gives

and we have no restrictions on

.
Thus,

\mathcal{M}
with

given by
s^{\mathbb{R}}\geq-3
.

For
s^{\mathbb{R}}<-3
, we need a

By (

(2.2.8)

\displaystyle(y^{\mathbb{R}})^{2}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{-(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})}A^{\mathbb{R}}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1-s^{\mathbb{R}}}{-36(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})}}\right)^{2}=\frac{s^{\mathbb{R}}-1}{36(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})}-\frac{1}{36}

and one can introduce
\psi^{\mathbb{R}}\in(0,2\pi]
to write

(2.2.9)

\displaystyle y^{\mathbb{R}}=R\sin\psi^{\mathbb{R}},

(2.2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{-(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})(1-s^{\mathbb{R}})}A^{\mathbb{R}}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1-s^{\mathbb{R}}}{-36(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})}}=R\cos\psi^{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle R=\sqrt{\frac{s^{\mathbb{R}}-1}{36(3+s^{\mathbb{R}})}-\frac{1}{36}}.

Hence,
(s^{\mathbb{R}},\psi^{\mathbb{R}})\in(-\infty,-3)\times(0,2\pi]
parameterize the subspace of
\mathcal{M}
with
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
given by
s^{\mathbb{R}}<-3
.

This can be summarized in the following proposition:

Proposition 2.2
.

The points
(A^{\mathbb{R}},s^{\mathbb{R}},x^{\mathbb{R}},y^{\mathbb{R}})
of the connected component of the monodromy manifold
\mathcal{M}
with
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
can be parametrized as follows.

(1)

For
s^{\mathbb{R}}\geq-3
, we have
(s^{\mathbb{R}},y^{\mathbb{R}})\in[-3,1)\times\mathbb{R}

3.2  Analogies and Differences in the Radial Toda and Partner Equations

Most calculations for the
\hat{\Psi}
-problem will go in parallel with the ones in
[
GIKMO
]
. To avoid repetition, we just sketch the main steps of the coinciding part. Let us briefly indicate them.

Applying a series of transformations described in sections 3.1 and 3.2 of
[
GIKMO
]
to the
\hat{\Psi}
-problem will give the
\check{Y}
-problem eventually. To solve the
\check{Y}
-problem asymptotically, one can construct globally and locally approximating solutions as in
[
GIKMO
]
. In other words, the
global parametrix

\check{Y}^{D}(\zeta)
and the
local parametrices

P^{(u)}
near the stationary points
u=\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\overline{\omega}
are given by the same formulae as in the radial Toda case.

The next step is to consider a RHP for an error function
R(\zeta)
by comparing
\check{Y}(\zeta)
with its parametrices. This step for the partner equation will completely differ from that for the radial Toda equation.

Recall that for the radial Toda case, we assumed
A^{\mathbb{R}}>0
(which was consistent with the analysis of the solution near
x=0
). Under this condition, jump matrices for the error function
R(\zeta)
tend to the identity matrix as
x\rightarrow\infty
, i.e., the global parametrix and each local parametrix matches well on the boundary of a small neighborhood around each stationary point. Then, we apply the small norm theorem, see
[
FIKN
, Theorem 8.1]
, to the
R
-problem to guarantee the large
x
solvability of the RHPs. However, in this paper, we assume
A^{\mathbb{R}}<0
, and it turns out that we don’t have the “matching up” condition for
R(\zeta)
. In other words, jump matrices
G_{R}^{(u)}
on the boundary of each small neighborhood around a stationary point
u
will not be close to the identity matrix in the
L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}
norm:

\displaystyle G_{R}^{(u)}=\check{Y}^{D}(\zeta)\left(P^{(u)}(\zeta)\right)^{-1}=\left[E^{(u)}(\zeta)\right]^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1}),

\displaystyle\bigg|\bigg|\left[E^{(u)}(\zeta)\right]^{-1}-I\bigg|\bigg|\not\to 0,\;\text{ as }\;x\to\infty.

Because of that, we cannot apply the small norm theorem directly. Such a situation has appeared for
2\times 2
matrix RHPs in
[
BothnerIts
]
when they considered certain singular solutions of the Painlevé II equation. Encountering a non-small norm RHP after standard construction of the global and local parametrices seems to be one feature of studying singular solutions of the corresponding differential equations. In
[
BothnerIts
, section 2]
, a certain “dressing” procedure was applied to resolve this issue. We follow the same idea with some modifications due to the
3\times 3
nature of our problem.
