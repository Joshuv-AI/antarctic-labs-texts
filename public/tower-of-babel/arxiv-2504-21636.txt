Abstract
Simulation of fermionic systems is one of the most promising applications of quantum computers. It spans problems in quantum chemistry, high-energy physics and condensed matter. Underpinning the core steps of any quantum simulation algorithm, fermion-qubit mappings translate the fermionic interactions to the operators and states of quantum computers. This translation is highly non-trivial: a burgeoning supply of fermion-qubit mappings has arisen over the past twenty years to address the limited resources of early quantum technology. Previous literature has presented a dichotomy between ancilla-free fermion-qubit mappings, which minimise qubit count, and local encodings, which minimise gate complexity. We present two computational approaches to the construction of general mappings while working with a limited number of qubits, striking a balance between the low-qubit and low-gate demands of present quantum technology. The first method frames the order of fermionic labels as an instance of the quadratic assignment problem to minimize the total and maximum Pauli weights in a problem Hamiltonian. We compare the order-optimized performance of several common ancilla-free mappings on systems of size up to 225 fermionic modes. The second method is a computational approach to incrementally add ancilla qubits to Jordan–Wigner transformations and further reduce the Pauli weights. By adding up to 10 ancilla qubits, we were able to reduce the total Pauli weight by as much as 67% in Jordan–Wigner transformations of fermionic systems with up to 64 modes, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art ancilla-free mappings. Reproducibility: source code and data are available at https://github.com/cameton/QCE_QubitAssignment

Our contribution:
In this work, we present two new strategies to further reduce the Pauli weight costs of fermion-qubit mappings in the absence of scaling qubit resources. We extend the method in

Through converting the task to a quadratic assignment problem, we also expand the scope of our strategy to fermionic systems with any hopping and interaction pattern, beyond just the square-lattice Fermi–Hubbard model.

Our second protocol generalises the two-ancilla qubit strategy from

In the process, we demonstrate that with sufficient ancilla qubits, we can reduce the Pauli weight associated with the Jordan–Wigner transformation by as much as 67%, improving upon even the best known ancilla-free transformations.
Furthermore, we determine optimal or near-optimal mappings for systems with several hundred fermions.
This work demonstrates that there is ample room for further improvement in fermionic simulation for near-term quantum computing, driving down the cost of quantum simulation avoiding significant overheads in quantum resources, through computationally-assisted searches and optimization.

Related work

In recent years, there have been various approaches to optimizing ancilla-free fermion-qubit mappings targeting different cost functions. In contrast to our approach in Section IIIA, in which we take a linear encoding and reduce total and average Pauli weight by optimizing over fermionic order, the space of linear encodings itself has been used as an optimization space to reduce costs such as the number of entangling gates in fermionic simulation

III-A

Fermionic Systems vs Qubit Systems

Fermionic systems consist of
modes
which represent lattice sites of quantized space in high energy and condensed matter physics or electronic orbitals in quantum chemistry. Modes can be occupied or unoccupied by single fermions, indistinguishable particles obeying the Pauli exclusion principle.

A mathematical description of fermionic statistics requires choosing an ordering
i\in[n]
for the modes: while physically unimportant, the order enables the description of the
(2^{n})
-dimensional Hilbert space of the system as
\mathcal{H}_{f}^{n}=\text{span}\{|{\mathbf{f}})\mid\mathbf{f}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{n}\}
, where the
Fock basis state

|{\mathbf{f}})
is the antisymmetrized wavefunction of an
n
–particle system with mode
i
occupied if and only if
f_{i}=1
. The algebra of fermionic operators
B(\mathcal{H}_{f}^{n})
derives from the
creation and annihilation operators

a_{i}^{(\dagger)}
for
i\in[n]
, which act on the Fock basis states via

\displaystyle a_{i}^{(\dagger)}|{\mathbf{f}})=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\sum_{j<i}f_{j}}|{(\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{e}_{i})\bmod 2})&f_{i}=1\,(0)\\
0&f_{i}=0\,(1)\,,\end{cases}

(1)

representing the addition or removal of a fermion in the
i
th mode from the overall system. Equation (
1
) ensures the Pauli exclusion principle, which the canonical anticommutation relations express mathematically as

\displaystyle\{a_{i},a_{j}\}=0\,,\quad\{a_{i}^{\dagger},a_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\,,

(2)

where
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
for any two operators
A
and
B
.

A

is a Hermitian operator
H_{f}
in

that fully describes the fermionic system. It may contain
number operators

c_{ii}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}
, the cost of single-particle tunnelling of fermions between modes via

c_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+c_{ij}^{*}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}
, and the repulsive forces between fermions via

c^{ii}_{jj}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}
for real coefficients

and the possibly complex values

.

In contrast, models for quantum computation assume access to
qubits
, distinguishable spin-
\frac{1}{2}
systems with state space
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\text{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
. A quantum computer making use of an
m
-qubit register has access to the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes m}
, which has the
computational basis

\{\ket{\vec{f}}\coloneqq\ket{f_0}\dots\ket{f_{n-1}}\mid\mathbf{f}\in\mathds{Z}_{2}^{n}\}
. Quantum computation consists of acting on qubit qubits with unitary operators, such as the Pauli matrices

\displaystyle X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3)

We denote multi-qubit Pauli operators such as
X\otimes Y\otimes Z
with the short-hand notation
X_{0}Y_{1}Z_{2}
.

Fermion-qubit mappings and the Jordan–Wigner transformation

Quantum simulation algorithms require a qubit Hamiltonian with the same spectral properties as the fermionic Hamiltonian of interest. This demands a unitary equivalence

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:|{\psi})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\ket{\psi}\,,

\displaystyle|{\psi})

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{H}_{f}^{n}\,;

(4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:a_{i}^{(\dagger)}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathfrak{m}\cdot a_{i}^{(\dagger)}\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}\,,

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\in[n]\,.

(5)

The canonical example of an ancilla–free fermion-qubit mapping is the

]

to the corresponding computational basis state:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{\text{JW}}:\mathcal{H}_{f}^{n}\ni|{\mathbf{f}})\longmapsto\ket{f_0}\ket{f_1}\dots\ket{f_{n-1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n}\,.

into sums of Pauli operators acting on up to

III-C

Optimization problems for fermion-qubit mappings

The hopping and interaction terms in a fermionic system give a pattern of couplings between modes that can be described by a graph structure
G=(V,E)
where
V
is a set of unordered vertices and
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
is a set of edges between vertices.
In order to facilitate mapping our fermionic Hamiltonian to a qubit Hamiltonian, it is useful to fix an
order

\sigma\colon V\to\left[\lvert V\rvert\right]
for the vertices. Given
\sigma
, we define

\displaystyle E_{\sigma}\coloneqq\left\{\left(\sigma(v),\sigma(u)\right)\mid uv\in E,\sigma(v)<\sigma(u)\right\}

(8)

to be the set of start and end positions of each edge with respect to the order
\sigma
.

A fermionic Hamiltonian corresponding to the graph
G
takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{f}&=\overbrace{\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}}^{\text{number terms}}+\overbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j)\in E_{0}\end{subarray}}c^{ii}_{jj}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}}^{\text{interaction terms}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j)\in E_{0}\end{subarray}}(c_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+c_{ij}^{*}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}}_{\text{hopping terms}})\end{split}

(9)

where
E_{0}
corresponds to an initial ordering of the fermionic labels.
Equation (
9
) describes a large variety of quantum systems, including the Fermi–Hubbard model
[
25
]
as well as quantum chemical Hamiltonians
[
2
]
.

To generate a qubit representation of the fermionic Hamiltonian, consider applying a fermion-qubit mapping
\mathfrak{m}
to obtain

\displaystyle H_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}+\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{0}}c^{ii}_{jj}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}

(10)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{0}}(c_{ij}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{j}+c_{ij}^{*}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{i})\,,

where
A_{i}^{(\dagger)}=\mathfrak{m}\cdot a_{i}^{(\dagger)}\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
. We can assess the quality of the fermion-qubit mapping
\mathfrak{m}
in simulating
H_{f}
in terms of the following two cost functions of the qubit Hamiltonian:

1.

The
total Pauli weight
, which is equal to the sum of the number of qubits upon which each term in
H_{q}
acts. The total Pauli weight could correspond to the number of single-qubit measurements required to measure each term of the Hamiltonian once, relating, for example, to ground-state estimation as part of a VQE procedure.

2.

The
maximum Pauli weight
, which is equal to the maximum number of qubits upon which any one term in
H_{q}
acts. The maximum Pauli weight corresponds to the non-locality of the Hamiltonian, a time complexity parameter of simulation algorithms such as Trotterization.

For example, under the Jordan–Wigner transformation, the number, hopping and interaction terms in (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{\text{JW}}:{a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}^{\otimes n}-Z_{i})

\displaystyle{c_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+c_{ij}^{*}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\big(\text{Re}(c_{ij})(X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j})

(12)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\text{Im}(c_{ij})(X_{i}Y_{j}+Y_{i}X_{j})\big)Z_{[i+1,j-1]}

\displaystyle{a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\frac{1}{4}(\mathds{1}^{\otimes n}-Z_{i})(\mathds{1}^{\otimes n}-Z_{j})\,.

Each hopping term maps to a sum of four Pauli operators, each with weight
|i{-}j|{+}1

The presence of hopping terms means that overall, the Jordan–Wigner transformation produces terms of weights up to

produce qubit representations of the hopping terms with weights up to

III-D

Majorana operators and linear encodings

In this paper, we will primarily work with the fermionic operators known as
Majorana operators

\gamma_{i},\overline{\gamma}_{i}
, which are defined for
i\in[n]
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq a_{i}+a_{i}^{\dagger}\,,

\displaystyle\overline{\gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\left(a_{i}-a_{i}^{\dagger}\right)\,.

(14)

In the Majorana basis, for
i,j\in[n]
the canonical anticommutation relations of (
2
) translate to

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{i},\overline{\gamma}_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}\,,\quad\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}=\{\overline{\gamma}_{i},\overline{\gamma}_{j}\}=0\,;

(15)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{i}\,,\quad\overline{\gamma}_{i}^{\dagger}=\overline{\gamma}_{i}\,.

(16)

Linear encodings
are a prominent class of fermion-qubit mappings that act as binary codes of the Fock basis, yielding the transformation from fermionic to qubit states

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{U}:\mathcal{H}_{f}^{n}\ni|{\mathbf{f}})\longmapsto\ket{U \vec{f}}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n}

(17)

for an invertible binary matrix
U\in\text{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. For example, the
n
-qubit Jordan–Wigner transformation is trivially the linear encoding with
U=\mathds{1}
, the
n{\times}n
identity matrix.

Fermionic operators transform predictably under linear encodings
[
16
,
15
]
. The Majorana representations of the mapping in (

) are

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{U}:\begin{cases}\gamma_{i}\longmapsto X_{U(i)}Z_{P(i)}\\
\overline{\gamma}_{i}\longmapsto X_{U(i)}Z_{R(i)}\,.\end{cases}

(18)

Here, the expression
U(i)
denotes the row indices of nonzero elements in the
i
th column of
U
. If the expression
F(i)
denotes the column indices of nonzero elements in the
i
th row of

, then
P(i)=F(0)\,\triangle\,F(1)\,\triangle\,\dots\,\triangle\,F(i-1)
and

, where

denotes the symmetric difference of two sets:

.

In addition to the Jordan–Wigner transformation, there are three other well-known linear encodings. The

takes the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{\text{PB}}:|{\mathbf{f}})\longmapsto\ket{L \vec{f}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{\text{BK}}:|{\mathbf{f}})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\ket{U_\text{BK} \vec{f}}\,,

where

\displaystyle U_{\text{BK}}^{(0)}=1\,,\quad U_{\text{BK}}^{(i+1)}=\begin{pmatrix}U_{\text{BK}}^{(i)}&\scalebox{0.5}{$\begin{matrix}0&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&\dots&0\\

\scalebox{0.5}{$\begin{matrix}0&0&\dots&0\\

The

]
has a canonical definition when

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{\text{TT}}:|{\mathbf{f}})\longmapsto\ket{U_\text{TT} \vec{f}}\,,

where

, the

\displaystyle U_{\text{TT}}^{(0)}=1\,,\quad U_{\text{TT}}^{(i+1)}=\begin{pmatrix}U_{\text{TT}}^{(i)}&0&0&0\\

Theorem 1
.

(Adapted from
[

]

Let

be an
n
-mode fermionic Hamiltonian and let
H_{q}
be an
m
-qubit Hamiltonian, where
m\geq n
. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

There is a fermion-qubit mapping
\mathfrak{m}:\mathcal{H}_{f}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\leq\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes m}
between the two Hamiltonians, i.e. a unitary equivalence

\displaystyle H_{q}=\mathfrak{m}\cdot H_{f}\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}\,,

(24)

where the
(2^{n})

\mathcal{H}
is the space stabilized by any qubit operator in the kernel of
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}

is an image of the fermionic identity in the sense that

for all
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}

2.

Let the fermionic Hamiltonian have the decomposition into the sum of products of Majorana operators

\displaystyle H_{f}=\sum_{\mathclap{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\subset[n]}}(c_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}})\gamma_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\,,\,\text{ where }\gamma_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{I}\\
j\in\mathcal{J}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{i}\overline{\gamma}_{j}\,.

(25)

Then, the qubit Hamiltonian has a similar decomposition into Pauli operators, i.e.

\displaystyle H_{q}=\sum_{\mathclap{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\subset[n]}}(c_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}})P_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\quad\text{for Paulis }P_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\,.

(26)

\displaystyle(\gamma_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}})^{\dagger}=\pm\gamma_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\iff(P_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}})^{\dagger}=\pm P_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\,,

(27)

and the same commutation relations:

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}},\gamma_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}\}=0

\displaystyle\iff\{P_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}},P_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}\}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle[\gamma_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}},\gamma_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}]=0

\displaystyle\iff[P_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}},P_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}]\,.

(29)

Figure

displays several terms from the Bravyi–Kitaev superfast
[

]
and Verstraete–Cirac transformations
[
17
]
, examples of local encodings that produce
\mathcal{O}(1)
-weight hopping terms using
n{+}\mathcal{O}(n)
qubits.
The Bravyi–Kitaev superfast method constructs an
m
-qubit Hamiltonian where
m
is the number of edges in the Hamiltonian graph,
and carefully chooses Pauli operators obeying statement
2
of Theorem
1
that act only locally with weight
\mathcal{O}(1)
upon each edge of the graph. The fermion-qubit mapping thus gives a qubit Hamiltonian with significant reduction upon the
\mathcal{O}(n)
-weight hopping terms of the Jordan–Wigner transformation from Section
III-C
.

While local encodings promise far simpler qubit Hamiltonians than ancilla-free mappings, these results are only beginning to bear fruit on near-term devices
[
26
,
27
]
. The requirement of
\mathcal{O}(n)
ancilla qubits can be a heavy price to pay for Pauli weight reduction when logical qubits themselves are a scarce resource.

In Section
IV-B
, we generalize a method from
[
29
]
into a strategy for incrementally adding ancilla qubits to Jordan–Wigner transformations, resulting in
constant-ancilla mappings
. Figure

gives a two-ancilla mapping as an example.

III-F

Order Optimization

Regardless of the linear encoding at hand, the fermionic order yields qubit Hamiltonians with different total and maximum Pauli weights, despite being a physical symmetry of the fermionic system in question. As Figure
5
illustrates, our approach in Section
IV-A
exploits the freedom in fermionic order to minimize total or maximum Pauli weight.

The problem of ordering the vertices of a graph to minimize some given cost is ubiquitous, exemplified by the well studied Quadratic Assignment Problem
[
37
]
. For a given graph
G=(V,E)
and distance matrix
\mathcal{D},
the unweighted form of the quadratic assignment problem can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma}\sum_{uv\in E}\mathcal{D}_{\sigma(u)\sigma(v)}.

There are a variety of search based methods for solving Quadratic Assignment in its most general form
[
38
]
. However, for certain choices of distance matrix
\mathcal{D},
Quadratic Assignment admits efficient heuristic approaches. For example, Minimum Linear Arrangement (MLA) is a special case of Quadratic Assignment with
\mathcal{D}_{ij}=\lvert i-j\rvert
. One can find high quality solutions for MLA very quickly by utilizing multilevel methods, even for large graph inputs
[
39
,
40
]
.

IV
Our Methodology

We consider two approaches to optimization over fermion-qubit mappings to reduce the maximum and total Pauli weights of Hamiltonians of the form in (
10
). The first approach, in Section
IV-A
, fixes an arbitrary linear encoding
\mathfrak{m}_{U}
and optimizes over the fermionic order
\sigma
to minimize the Pauli weight of the qubit Hamiltonian.
This generalizes the method from
[
29
]
which previously only applied to the Jordan–Wigner transformation.

The second approach, in Section
IV-B
, introduces a new strategy to incrementally add ancilla qubits to the Jordan–Wigner transformation in order to produce a qubit Hamiltonian with lower maximum and total Pauli weights than the mappings with optimized orderings from Section
IV-A
. This method generalises the approach in
[
29
]
to add more than two qubits, and applies to arbitrary fermionic Hamiltonian graphs rather than just the square lattice.

The following fermionic operator identities are helpful:

\displaystyle a_{i}^{(\dagger)}=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{i}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\gamma}_{i})\,,\quad a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\gamma_{i}\overline{\gamma}_{i})

(30)

\displaystyle c_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+c_{ij}^{*}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}=\text{Re}(c_{ij})(\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}+\overline{\gamma}_{i}\overline{\gamma}_{j})

(31)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\,\text{Im}(c_{ij})(\gamma_{i}\overline{\gamma}_{j}-\overline{\gamma}_{i}\gamma_{j})\,.

IV-A

Fermionic Order as Quadratic Assignment

While a fermion-qubit mapping may produce the Hamiltonian
H_{q}
in (
10
) from the fermionic Hamiltonian
H_{f}
in (
9
),
as outlined in Section
III-A
, the order of the fermionic mode is arbitrary and does not affect the underlying dynamics of the model
[
29
]
.
Thus,
we have the freedom to relabel the fermionic modes and consider qubit Hamiltonians of the form

\displaystyle H_{q}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}+\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}c_{jj}^{ii}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}

(32)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}(c_{ij}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{j}+c_{ij}^{*}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{i})\,.

(33)

for any order
\sigma:V\rightarrow[n]
. For example, Figure
5
demonstrates the hopping terms arising from the Jordan–Wigner transformation of the Fermi–Hubbard model using three distinct fermionic labelings.

Let
\mathfrak{m}_{U}
be a linear encoding for some
n{\times}n
invertible binary matrix
U
. Using (
18
) and (
31
), it is straightforward to evaluate the representations of hopping terms under
\mathfrak{m}_{U}
:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\left(\imaginary(c_{ij})(X_{U_{i\triangle j}}Z_{P_{i\triangle j}}+X_{U_{i\triangle j}}Z_{R_{i\triangle j}})\right.

(34)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left.-\real(c_{ij})(X_{U_{i\triangle j}}Z_{P_{i}\triangle R_{j}}-X_{U_{i\triangle j}}Z_{R_{i}\triangle P_{j}})\right)

where we have used the shorthand
U_{i}
and
U_{i\triangle j}
to denote
U(i)
and
U(i)\,\triangle\,U(j)
, respectively, and likewise for the
F
and
R
sets. We have also used the rules that
X_{U(i)}Z_{P(i)}=Z_{P(i)}X_{U(i)}
and
X_{U(i)}Z_{R(i)}=-Z_{R(i)}X_{U(i)}
from Lemma 7.2 of
[
15
]
. Using (
31
), the interaction terms transform to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\mathds{1}-(Z_{P_{i}\triangle R_{i}}+Z_{P_{j}\triangle R_{j}})+(Z_{P_{i}\triangle R_{i}}Z_{P_{j}\triangle R_{j}}))\,.

(35)

Given an invertible binary matrix
U\in\text{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
, define the binary matrices

\displaystyle F=U^{-1}\,,\quad P=LF\,,\quad R=P+F

where
L_{ij}=1
if
i<j
and
0
otherwise. Each column of
U
and each row of
F,P,R
then correspond to the set families of the same name in (
18
), e.g.
U_{\ast j}=U(j)
and
F_{i}=F(i)
.

Using (
34
) and (
35
), we may use the matrix definitions of
F
,
P
and
R
to define a matrix
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
representing the total (
\oplus=+
) or maximum (
\oplus=\max
) Pauli weight of
H_{q}(\sigma)
consisting of the elementwise
\oplus
-sum of the weight of number terms, hopping terms with a nontrivial real part, hopping terms with a nontrivial complex part, and interaction terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Num}_{i}&\coloneqq\lVert F_{i}\rVert_{0}\\
\text{ReHop}_{ij}&\coloneqq\lVert\max(P_{i}+R_{j},U_{\ast i}+U_{\ast j})\rVert_{0}\oplus\phantom{123}\\
&\phantom{\coloneqq}\qquad\lVert\max(R_{i}+P_{j},U_{\ast i}+U_{\ast j})\rVert_{0}\\
\text{ImHop}_{ij}&\coloneqq\lVert\max(P_{i}+P_{j},U_{\ast i}+U_{\ast j})\rVert_{0}\oplus\phantom{123}\\
&\phantom{\coloneqq}\qquad\lVert\max(R_{i}+R_{j},U_{\ast i}+U_{\ast j})\rVert_{0}\\
\text{Inter}_{ij}&\coloneqq\lVert F_{i}\rVert_{0}\oplus\lVert F_{j}\rVert_{0}\oplus\lVert F_{i}+F_{j}\rVert_{0}\end{split}

(36)

A particular model may include any or all components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{ij}=\text{ReHop}_{ij}\oplus\text{ImHop}_{ij}\oplus\text{Inter}_{ij}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{ii}=\text{Num}_{i}\,.

We assume that the hopping terms of the problem Hamiltonian
H_{f}
have either (1) purely real or imaginary coefficients, so that one of ImHop and ReHop is zero, or (2) complex coefficients in which case both ImHop and ReHop are necessary to calculate Pauli weights of hopping terms.
In cases that combine these two scenarios, the expression
\mathcal{D}_{ij}
acts as an upper bound.
The total or maximum Pauli weight is thus

\displaystyle\left(\bigoplus_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}\mathcal{D}_{ij}\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i\in V_{\sigma}}\mathcal{D}_{ii}\right)\,.

(37)

We give examples for
\mathcal{D}
with
\oplus=\max
in Figure
6
for the Jordan–Wigner, Bravyi–Kitaev, Ternary Tree and Parity Basis transformations. This yields the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma}\left(\bigoplus_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}\mathcal{D}_{ij}\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i\in V_{\sigma}}\mathcal{D}_{ii}\right)\,.

(38)

This is the problem that we solve in Section
V-A
.
Minimizing the total Pauli weight corresponds to quadratic assignment, while minimizing the maximum Pauli weight relates to the bandwidth problem for Minimum Linear Arrangement.

IV-B

Incremental addition of ancilla qubits

In this section we generalize the approach in
[
29
]
to a procedure for incrementally adding ancilla qubits to Jordan–Wigner transformations.
We first show how a mapping using a single extra ancilla qubit can produce a Hamiltonian consisting of terms with lower maximum and total Pauli weights; the incremental procedure proceeds inductively.
Theorem
1
is the theoretical bedrock of our method;
we make frequent use of statement
2
to ensure the validity of the modified qubit Hamiltonian as a representation of the target fermionic system.

Begin with an
n
-mode fermionic Hamiltonian
H_{f}
of the form in (
9
) and the resulting qubit Hamiltonian generated by the
n
-qubit ancilla-free Jordan–Wigner transformation,

\displaystyle H_{q}(\sigma)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}\underbrace{(\dots)_{ij}Z_{[i+1,j-1]}}_{\text{hopping term}}\,+\,\dots\,,

(39)

where
\sigma:V\rightarrow[n]
is an optimized fermionic label order for total or maximum Pauli weight from Section
IV-A
, depending on the cost function of choice. Unlike in (
12
), Equation (
39
) conceals the linear combination of the two-qubit operators
X_{i}X_{j}
,
Y_{i}Y_{j}
,
X_{i}Y_{j}
and
Y_{i}X_{j}
as
(...)_{ij}
for brevity. The unwritten terms in (
39
) are the the number and interaction terms, which are local under the Jordan–Wigner transformation. As per statement
2
, the terms of
H_{q}(\sigma)
have the same Hermiticity and commutation relations relative to the terms in
H_{f}
.

Fig. 7
:
One iteration of the method for incrementally adding ancilla qubits to Jordan–Wigner transformations and the corresponding reduction in Pauli weights of a qubit Hamiltonian. The ancilla comes with the user’s choice of Pauli string
Z_{\mathcal{I}}
: in constructing the Hamiltonian
H_{q}^{\prime}
, which is equivalent to
H_{q}(\sigma^{\prime})
, the user has the choice to modify each term of the original Hamiltonian by
Z_{\mathcal{I}}
or leave it unchanged up to local action on the ancilla, leading to a reduction in Pauli weights.

We wish to construct an
(n{+}1)
-qubit Hamiltonian
H_{q}^{\prime}
that also satisfies statement
2
, while reducing the Pauli weights of some of the hopping terms.
Define
H_{q}^{\prime}
to be an
(n{+}1)
-qubit Hamiltonian with identical number and interaction terms to
H_{q}(\sigma)
, but with each hopping term modified via

\displaystyle(\cdots)_{ij}Z_{[i+1,j-1]}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\cdots)_{ij}Z_{[i+1,j-1]}\kappa_{ij}.

(40)

The Pauli operator
\kappa_{ij}
may take one of two forms for each individual term, chosen to minimize the Pauli weight of the resulting term. These options are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{(Option 1)}\qquad\kappa_{ij}&\coloneqq\begin{cases}Z_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes Y_{n}&\lvert\{i,j\}\cap\mathcal{I}\rvert=1\\
Z_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes Z_{n}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\
\text{(Option 2)}\qquad\kappa_{ij}&\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathds{1}^{\otimes n}\otimes X_{n}&\lvert\{i,j\}\cap\mathcal{I}\rvert=1\\
\mathds{1}^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{n}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{split}

(41)

Options are independent for each hopping term of the Hamiltonian in order to minimize Pauli weight – one term utilizing Option 1 does not exclude another from utilizing Option 2.

In Option 1, the operator
\kappa_{ij}
is the product of
Z_{\mathcal{I}},
a string of Pauli
Z
-gates acting on some subset
\mathcal{I}\subset[n]
of qubits, and a single-qubit Pauli operator acting on the ancilla. In Option 2, the operator
\kappa_{ij}
is simply a Pauli operator acting on the ancilla qubit. The string
Z_{\mathcal{I}}
serves the purpose of reducing the Pauli weight of the hopping terms, while the ancilla gates preserve the commutation relations of the terms of
H_{q}(\sigma)
in the terms of
H_{q}^{\prime}
as per the requirements of statement
2
. Figure
7
illustrates the process of generating the modified Hamiltonian
H_{q}^{\prime}
given the terms of
H_{q}(\sigma)
.

The question is then, how much does
\kappa_{ij}
affect the Pauli weight of a Jordan–Wigner hopping term? For Option 2, this is fairly trivial: the weight of an
(i,j)
hopping term in the new Hamiltonian
H_{q}^{\prime}
increases by
|\{i,j\}\cap\mathcal{I}|\bmod 2
, corresponding to the case where a nontrivial gate is placed on the ancilla.

For Option 1, we may ignore any
Z
s applied to the endpoint qubits
i
and
j
of the
(i,j)
hopping term since
XZ\propto Y
and
YZ\propto X.
The Pauli weight is then reduced by the number of indices in
\mathcal{I}
falling strictly between
i
and
j,
but increased by the number falling outside that range, plus one for the ancilla gate. The weight of the
(i,j)
hopping term in
H_{q}^{\prime}
increases by the possibly negative quantity

\displaystyle d_{ij}(\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lvert\mathcal{I}\cap([n]\setminus[i,j])\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}\cap[i+1,j-1]\rvert+1\,.

(42)

Overall, this yields an optimization problem to find the choice of qubits
\mathcal{I}
that minimizes the increase in Pauli weights of the terms in
H_{q}^{\prime}
. The solution
minimizes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\mathcal{I}\subset[n]}\quad&\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}\alpha_{ij}\min\Big(\lvert\{i,j\}\cap\mathcal{I}\rvert\bmod 2,d_{ij}(\mathcal{I})\Big)\end{split}

(43)

where the weighting

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}4&\text{Im}(c_{ij})\neq 0\neq\text{Re}(c_{ij})\\
0&\text{Im}(c_{ij})=0=\text{Re}(c_{ij})\\
2&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

takes into account the different number of hopping terms if
c_{ij}
is purely real or imaginary.

Because the Hamiltonian
H_{q}^{\prime}
satisfies statement
2
, statement
1
implies the existence of a fermion-qubit mapping
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}_{f}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}<\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes(n+1)}
,
where
\mathcal{H}
is the
(2^{n})
-dimensional subspace of the
(n+1)
-qubit register stabilized by
Z_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes Z_{n}
, that gives the unitary equivalence
H_{f}=\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\cdot H_{q}^{\prime}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})^{\dagger}
. Thus, a quantum computer may use
H_{q}^{\prime}
to study
H_{f}
instead of
H_{q}(\sigma)
, reducing the total and maximum Pauli weight cost of simulation at the expense of a single additional qubit.

Subsequent ancilla qubits can be added by renaming
H_{q}^{\prime}
to
H_{q}(\sigma)
and iteratively applying the procedure described above. In introducing the
k
th ancilla qubit, choose a qubit subset
{\mathcal{I}_{k}}
and define
\kappa_{ij}
as in (
41
), with
Z_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}
acting on the first
n
qubits and the single-qubit Pauli acting on the
k
th ancilla qubit, leaving the previous ancilla qubits untouched. Perform the conditional modification of the hopping terms via Option 1 and Option 2 to produce an
(n{+}k)
-qubit Hamiltonian with lower Pauli weights.
Continue to add ancilla qubits incrementally until exhausting the available supply, or if there is no further reduction in the total or maximum Pauli weight of the hopping terms in the modified qubit Hamiltonian.

Naturally, this leads to the question of whether it is possible to find an optimal arrangement of multiple qubit subsets
\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots\mathcal{I}_{p-1}
. While there was no requirement for the sets
\mathcal{I}_{k}
to be pairwise disjoint, making this slight restriction simplifies the scenario in which both
Z_{\mathcal{I}_{j}}
and
Z_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}
are applied to the same hopping term in repeated instances of Option 1. This non-intersecting scenario yields the optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\mathclap{\mathcal{I}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{p-1}}}\quad&\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\sigma}}\alpha_{ij}\min\Big(\lvert\{i,j\}\cap\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert\bmod 2,d_{ij}(\mathcal{I}_{k})\Big)\\
\text{st}\quad&\forall i,j\in[p]:\mathcal{I}_{i}\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}=\emptyset\,.\end{split}

This is problem that we solve in Section
V-B
.

IV-C

Constant-ancilla method for other mappings

Our method for constructing constant-ancilla fermion-qubit mappings in Section
IV-B
begins by using the Jordan–Wigner transformation to generate the base ancilla-free Hamiltonian in (
39
).
If we had instead used a different linear encoding, we predict that it would generally be computationally hard to devise an effective set of commuting Pauli strings
P_{0},P_{1},\dots,P_{p-1}
for incremental ancilla addition because number, hopping and interaction terms map to Pauli strings with less obvious nonlocal structure than the
Z_{i+1}\dots Z_{j-1}
factors of the Jordan–Wigner transformations. In modifying the Hamiltonian terms from
H_{q}(\sigma)
to
H_{q}^{\prime}
with the addition of the
k
th ancilla qubit,
the protocol would need to search for Pauli operators
P_{k}=X_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}Y_{\mathcal{J}_{k}}Z_{\mathcal{K}_{k}}
such that multiplying the terms of
H_{q}(\sigma)
by
P_{k}
reduces the total or maximum Pauli weight. As discussed elsewhere
[
19
,
29
]
a new requirement emerges that
[P_{j},P_{k}]=0
for all
j\in[k]
in order to satisfy statement
2
.
Again, the reason this was straightforward for the Jordan–Wigner case was because we knew
a priori
that the problematic parts of the hopping terms would consist only of
Z
matrices. Nevertheless, we see this as a worthwhile line of inquiry because of the potential for other mappings to reduce the weight of qubit Hamiltonians from the outset.

V-B

Jordan–Wigner transformations with optimal ancilla use

In this section, we examine the improvement to the Jordan–Wigner encoding scheme provided by our method of incrementally adding ancilla qubits. All experiments provided in this section concern themselves only with the total Pauli weight of the hopping terms, i.e.
\mathcal{D}=\text{ReHop}+\text{ImHop}

VI
Conclusion

We have introduced a cost model for
the total and maximum Pauli weight of the output of linear encodings, a type of ancilla-free fermion-qubit mapping, and have found optimized fermionic orderings for several popular transformations using quadratic assignment. Empirically, these optimized orderings are able to recover known optimal solutions in the case of the Jordan–Wigner mapping. Moreover, we find an optimal strategy to cancel out disjoint strings of Pauli-
Z
gates, utilizing
ancilla qubits to reduce the Pauli weight of qubit Hamiltonians associated with the Jordan–Wigner transformation. According to our results, the latter strategy requires only of 10 ancilla qubits to reduce Hamiltonian Pauli weight by as much as 67%, outperforming the optimized ancilla-free Ternary Tree transformation. Future work might expand our approach to more complex cost functions that incorporate gate depth and circuit compilation, and generalise our incremental ancilla method to mappings beyond the Jordan–Wigner transformation.
