The Interconnected Factors of Emotional Regulation, Trauma, and Substance Use in Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury: A Case Study and Literature Review
Authors: Sakshi Prasad, Diana Sofia Galarza, Vasudha Sharma, Saad Ather, Wafa Sosal, Amina Hanif, Sasidhar Gunturu, Souparno Mitra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNon‐suicidal self‐injury (NSSI) is a complex and often misunderstood behavior linked to emotional dysregulation, trauma, and substance use. In this case report we present a 19‐year‐old female with a history of childhood trauma and substance use, who engaged in self‐injurious behavior as a coping mechanism to manage distress and maintain sobriety. Through her narrative and clinical course, we explore the multifaceted drivers of NSSI, its diagnostic considerations, and therapeutic interventions including dialectical behavior therapy. This case report underscores the importance of trauma‐informed, individualized care and highlights emerging insights into the neurobiological underpinnings of self‐injury. This case advocates for early recognition, compassionate engagement, and integrated treatment approaches to support recovery.

Introduction

Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury (NSSI) is a behavior in which an individual repeatedly inflicts injuries which are often painful, on their own body without any suicidal intent. This is done with the intent of obtaining relief from negative feelings/cognitive state, induce a positive feeling or to resolve an interpersonal conflict. Cutting, burning, biting, stabbing, scratching or self‐hitting or banging are common modes to achieve bleeding, pain or bruising of the skin. The behavioral pattern often includes urgency, craving and dependency, akin to that of addiction [1].

While Non suicidal self‐harm was majorly studied as a symptom of multiple psychiatric disorders like major depressive disorder or borderline personality disorder, the presence or absence of suicidal intent was often not a focus of clinical inquiry [2]. NSSI was first added as a ‘Condition for Further Study’ under Section III in Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)‐5 [3], later shifted to ‘Other Conditions That May Be a Focus of Clinical Attention’ in DSM 5‐TR [1]. The highlighting of this disorder in the DSM criteria allowed the clinicians to take note of this clinically important behavior, especially when independent of any psychiatric diagnosis in absence of a suicidal intent. This change in categorization aims to encourage research targeting tailored treatment as well as study the motives for self‐harm sans suicidality [4]. NSSI was added to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐ 10 Clinical Modification (CM) under codes R45.88, for current non‐suicidal self‐injury, and Z91.52, for history of nonsuicidal self‐injury [5].

Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury (NSSI) has an estimated global lifetime prevalence of 18%, calculated through a meta‐analysis [2] of studies ranging from 5.5% in Hungary [6] to 30.7% in Belgium [7]. Adolescence and female gender (female: male OR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.29–1.98) represents the at‐risk population [8]. Additionally, bullying, history of childhood abuse/neglect, insecure peer relationships and comorbid mental illnesses increase the likelihood of self‐harming behaviors [9]. Depressive disorders, traumatic disorders, personality disorders and eating disorders are all comorbidities associated with NSSI [10]. While this disorder explicitly implies an absence of suicidal intent, research show NSSI to be a risk factor for future suicide attempts [2, 11], especially with longer history of NSSI, increased frequency of self‐harm, multiplicity of methods of harm and absence of pain during self‐injury [12, 13].

Devising a screening protocol at the first point of contact, understanding the associated risk for suicidality, and individualizing management for self‐harming patients are future avenues of research in NSSI. This requires exploring the motivations behind NSSI and figuring out alternative coping strategies. We present a case study on one such motivation for non‐suicidal self‐injurious behavior, namely, replacing the euphoria of substance use with the release of endorphins due to the pain of self‐injury.

Case History

The patient is a 19‐year‐old female, currently unemployed and residing in a youth shelter since 2022, was brought into the comprehensive psychiatric emergency program (CPEP) by Emergency Medical Services, alerted by the shelter due to self‐injurious behavior, evidenced by numerous superficial blade cuts across her forearms, legs, and thighs, accomplished through retrieving a razor from a pencil sharpener and scratching her cuts until they bled. There were numerous cuts of varying lengths and depth observed across her forearms at various stages of healing. She had past medical history of asthma and past psychiatric history inclusive of major depressive disorder, autism, and substance use involving heroin, cannabis, cigarettes, and ketamine. The urine toxicology test was positive for cannabinoids and negative for other illicit substances at the time of admission.

Investigations and Treatment

During assessment, the patient displayed a well‐groomed appearance and willingness to engage in interviews with the treatment team, with limited eye contact, that she attributed to her autism spectrum disorder. When queried about the circumstances leading to her admission, she unveiled a history of self‐harm initiation at the age of 14, underpinned by distressing events involving her mother and traumatic experiences during her childhood. The patient recounted instances of her mother being physically abusive towards her and her pet animals, which included a distressing incident where her cat's fur was cut with knives, ultimately resulting in the cat's death due to subsequent infections.

In narrating the motivations behind her recent self‐injury, she highlighted her endeavor to sustain sobriety from substances, which included ketamine, cocaine, marijuana and cigarettes. She delineated a correlation between the physical pain of cutting and the euphoric high she sought, emphasizing her attraction to observing the release of blood as a source of comfort and relief from anxiety. The patient elucidated on the pain's role in providing waves of relief, thus becoming her primary coping mechanism. While expressing a sense of remorse regarding hospitalization, the patient appeared uncertain about exploring alternative coping strategies, spotlighting her dependency on self‐harm for stress management and emotional solace since childhood.

The patient additionally reported frequently feeling overwhelmed with negative emotions, and was noted picking at her healing scars while talking to the writer. The patient required immediate administration of Olanzapine 10 mg intramuscular on her first day of admission after displaying behavior such as banging her head against the wall, scratching her wounds, and removing bandaids, resulting in further bleeding. Venlafaxine was initiated and gradually increased to 150 mg per day during her stay. She reported needing additional assistance with sleep, for which Ramelteon 8 mg was administered on an as‐needed basis at bedtime. She started to attend group sessions, which included dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) based group sessions and was more compliant with medication. She was also able to refrain from inflicting pain or picking at her wounds during her stay. As per the patient, “It helped having someone guide through the extreme emotions I felt. This is a hospital, and people come to speak with you. Just having someone coming to see me makes me feel heard. It has been a while since my father has also come to check up on me. In shelter, you are on your own”.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

With further interventions and motivational interviewing, the patient started to actively participate in discharge planning and consented to participate in an outpatient rehabilitation program. She acknowledged the necessity of finding healthier coping mechanisms for her emotions and expressed willingness to seek psychotherapy. As of this update, there have been no readmissions to the hospital for the patient.

Discussion

The Interconnected Factors of Emotional Regulation, Trauma, and Substance Use in Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury

Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury (NSSI) emerges as a complex behavior deeply intertwined with emotional regulation, trauma, and substance use, reflecting a multifaceted interplay of psychological pain and coping mechanisms. Individuals who engage in NSSI often struggle with intense emotional dysregulation, where overwhelming feelings become unmanageable, prompting self‐harm as a means to regain a semblance of control or relief. Traumatic experiences, particularly in childhood, exacerbate this struggle, as they can instill deep‐seated patterns of negative self‐perception and maladaptive coping strategies that linger into adulthood. Additionally, a direct association is observed between substance use and NSSI [12, 14, 15, 16], with these behaviors increasing under intoxicated states, especially for men [17]. While substance abuse cannot independently predict self‐harm, it significantly increases the odds (OR = 30.5, p < 0.05) of NSSI [18]. Non‐suicidal self‐injurious behavior is often compared to drug addiction; however, NSSI primarily operates through negative reinforcement, lacking the positive reinforcement or craving typically associated with substance dependence [19, 20, 21]. This resemblance is further supported by observations that a decrease in NSSI behavior upon treatment may lead to an increase in the frequency of substance use [22], demonstrating the swapping of one maladaptive coping mechanism for another. This intricate web of emotional turmoil, past trauma, and substance dependency necessitates a multidimensional treatment approach that not only addresses self‐injurious behavior but also considers all unhealthy behaviors during monitoring and explores alternative healthy coping mechanisms tailored to the patient, fostering long‐term recovery. However, positive reinforcement can also play a role. Some individuals report engaging in NSSI to generate positive feelings, such as satisfaction or stimulation, or to elicit social responses from others, which are forms of positive reinforcement.

Demographics, Comorbidities and Pathogenesis Related to NSSI

Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury (NSSI) typically presents early in life, with an onset age estimated between 12 and 14 years, as indicated by a statistical review of 53 worldwide studies [10]. This review highlights the prevalence of NSSI across different age groups: 7.5% to 46.5% among adolescents, 38.9% in university students, and 4% to 23% in adults [10]. Females and adolescents are identified as particularly at‐risk populations [10, 14, 17, 23]. Specific demographic factors also influence risk, with individuals identifying as Multiracial (20.8%), Caucasian (16.8%), or Hispanic (17%) showing an increased likelihood of engaging in NSSI, while African Americans exhibit a lower risk [10, 23]. Additionally, adherence to religions that view suicide as sacrilege—such as Islam and certain Christian denominations—lowered the risk of self‐harm [23]. Conversely, identifying as anything other than heterosexual has been associated with an increased risk of self‐harming behaviors [17]. Within this context, cutting emerges as the most common method of self‐injury, utilized by 70% of individuals who self‐harm, with women primarily engaging in methods that involve blood, while men often resort to hitting, burning, or banging their heads [10, 14]. Our 19‐year‐old Caucasian female patient who engages in cutting epitomizes this demographic.

The presence of psychiatric comorbidities significantly correlates with NSSI, with borderline personality disorder and eating disorders being the most common associations [1, 10, 24, 25]. A range of other psychiatric conditions, including post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), dissociative disorders, conduct disorders, obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD), anxiety, mood disorders, substance use disorders, and dissociative identity disorder, increase the risk of deliberate self‐harm [9, 10, 24, 25]. It is theorized that adverse childhood experiences might predispose individuals to both psychiatric comorbidities and NSSI itself [24, 25], emphasizing the intricate link between emotional distress and self‐injurious behaviors.

Franz et al. [26] suggest that NSSI may serve as a mechanism for individuals to attain positive emotional states, such as euphoria, potentially mediated by the release of endogenous opioids during acts of self‐injury. A meta‐analysis by Wolff et al. [27] supports this idea, indicating that the endorphin release associated with self‐injury may function as a maladaptive coping mechanism that temporarily alleviates intense emotional distress. The brain's reward system plays a pivotal role in reinforcing NSSI behaviors; Claes et al. [28] highlight the association between NSSI and the brain's positive valence systems and the euphoria it can induce. This phenomenon aligns with the experience of our patient, who found temporary emotional relief through self‐harm amid her struggle with sobriety. Additional research by Klonsky et al. [29] identifies affect regulation and social functions as central drivers of NSSI, resonating with the patient's history of childhood trauma, strained relationships, and a lack of alternative coping strategies. Together, these factors underscore the complex interplay between emotional regulation, trauma, and self‐injurious behaviors.

Intervention Strategies for NSSI

A non‐judgmental approach is essential in managing NSSI, as negative reactions from adults can deter adolescents from seeking help in the future [30]. Effective interventions for adolescent NSSI include dialectical behavior therapy for adolescents (DBT‐A), cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and mentalization‐based treatment (MBT‐A) [31]. While evidence for psychiatric medications in treating NSSI remains limited, sedative medications may help manage acute high‐tension states in inpatient settings [32].

N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) has shown promise as a potential treatment for NSSI in individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). It is safe, well‐tolerated, and effective in reducing irritability, self‐injury, and anxiety‐like behaviors while enhancing social awareness [33, 34]. NAC, a precursor to glutathione, enhances antioxidant levels and reduces oxidative stress, linked to the pathology of NSSI and other psychiatric conditions. NAC also impacts the glutamate system by modulating synaptic and extrasynaptic glutamate levels; this alters the connectivity in brain circuits related to emotion regulation and reward processing, particularly the amygdala and nucleus accumbens therefore highlighting its potential as a novel intervention for NSSI in ASD [35].

Conclusion

This case illustrates the complexity of Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury (NSSI) as a maladaptive coping mechanism, driven by emotional dysregulation, trauma, and substance use. The patient's use of self‐injury as a substitute for substance abuse highlights the dual psychological and neurobiological factors that reinforce this behavior. Emerging research suggests that the release of endogenous opioids and activation of the brain's reward system, particularly the euphoria associated with self‐harm, contribute to the persistence of NSSI. A non‐judgmental approach is crucial, with therapies like DBT‐A, CBT, and MBT‐A being effective for adolescents. While psychiatric medications show limited evidence, N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) may reduce NSSI in individuals with autism spectrum disorder. This case highlights the need for individualized, trauma‐informed treatment plans addressing both emotional and neurobiological aspects of NSSI. Future research should refine screening protocols and explore alternative coping strategies, while a multidisciplinary approach remains key to managing NSSI and reducing its long‐term impact.
