Abstract
. With the rapid progress of deep learning and large language models (LLMs), companies now spend enormous sums executing GPU kernels. These kernels have, therefore, become prime targets for aggressive optimization. Recent efforts increasingly leverage LLMs to generate GPU kernels, but make no formal guarantees about the generated kernels. We present the first equivalence checker for GPU kernels and use it to formally verify the correctness of machine learning (ML) kernels optimized by hand, by LLMs, and by compilers. We show that our equivalence checker is sound and, for a well-defined class of GPU kernels which includes the programs of interest, complete. Our implementation, Volta , can verify ML computations such as convolutions, matrix multiplications, and various attention mechanisms.

Introduction

Given two GPU kernels—a reference implementation and an optimized counterpart—our goal is to prove their semantic equivalence, i.e., that they produce identical outputs for all valid inputs, thereby verifying the correctness of the optimized kernel.

Given the increasing reliance on aggressively optimized GPU kernels, we believe it would be beneficial to formally verify their correctness.
GPU kernels execute thousands of threads in parallel on runtimes such as NVIDIA’s CUDA

One major source of such errors is data races, which may appear only in extremely rare executions or may remain latent when kernels rely on implicit synchronization that happens only for a specific version of the test runtime or the test hardware but is not guaranteed to hold in general. For example, a widely cited GPU programming tutorial
(

(

GPU optimizations often reorder arithmetic operations, causing small numerical differences between the outputs of the reference and optimized implementations. Such variations are usually tolerated in tests, yet similar deviations can arise from genuine semantic bugs. For instance, an unsound optimization that omits a clipping operation may seem acceptable for some test inputs even though it reduces overall model accuracy. While compilers rarely introduce such mistakes, they are plausible when kernels are hand-optimized by human programmers or generated by automated agents.

In an optimized kernel, there may be no one-to-one correspondence between its threads and the threads in the reference implementation. In addition, existing techniques have no support for synchronization among multiple threads.

Although equivalence checking is undecidable in general and GPUs allow programmers to write arbitrary code, efficient GPU kernels typically follow structural patterns that make formal reasoning feasible.
We assume that the programs analyzed by our equivalence checker are
structured-CTAs

Finally, our work focuses on kernels targeting NVIDIA GPUs due to their widespread adoption, although the techniques are applicable to other GPU runtimes as well.

The core of our equivalence checker is symbolic evaluation.
Consider a tensor computation—a workload that dominates machine learning. We symbolically execute the reference computation under an arbitrary, fixed
schedule
to derive an expression over the mathematical reals that represents the output tensor as a function of the input tensors. Is it possible that a different scheduling of the threads could lead to a different expression?
Our main technical result,
confluence
, guarantees that all symbolic evaluator schedules lead to the same expression. If the kernel contains a deadlock or a data race, the symbolic evaluator is guaranteed to catch it and produce a diagnostic report. The same procedure is applied to the optimized computation. Finally, we develop a procedure to decide equality of the two symbolic expressions and, if they are equal, we conclude that the optimized kernel is semantically equivalent to the reference kernel over the reals, for all schedules. We are not aware of a proof in the literature that the symbolic identities we need to decide are, in fact, decidable (though there is work on related problems, e.g.,
(
41

). We prove that these identities are decidable in Section
4.4

It is worth noting that optimized kernels are almost never bit-wise equivalent to the reference implementation in floating point arithmetic, since most optimizations reorder arithmetic operations when distributing work among GPU threads.

3.
Background

We begin with necessary background on GPUs. To execute a task on a GPU accelerator, the CPU host launches a grid of cooperative thread arrays (CTAs), also known as
thread-blocks
. All CTAs share access to
global memory
(HBM), typically sized in gigabytes. Different CTAs in a grid usually write to disjoint regions of global memory due to its weak consistency model. The GPU hardware schedules each CTA on a
streaming multiprocessor
(SM); a modern GPU may contain hundreds of SMs.
The resources assigned to each SM are limited and hence each CTA is tasked to compute only a
tile
of the output tensor.
Each CTA consists of 128 to 1024
threads
, depending on the hardware generation. Within a CTA, threads are grouped into
warps
, each consisting of 32 threads. Every thread maintains its own
registers
for local storage, while
shared memory
serves as a software-managed cache accessible to all threads in the same CTA.
To coordinate access to shared memory, threads must use explicit
synchronization
operations. Missing synchronization leads to data races and over synchronization leads to loss of performance. The shared memory available per CTA ranges from 48 KB to 256 KB, depending on the GPU architecture. In a typical CTA, threads first load data from global memory into shared memory, perform arithmetic operations while keeping intermediate values in registers or shared memory, and finally write the results back to global memory.
Writing code for efficient CTAs is challenging because programmers must reason about massive thread-level parallelism and low-level hardware details.
Volta
aims to statically identify incorrect or unsafe optimizations. We next describe the synchronization mechanisms available on GPUs.

3.1.
Synchronization operations

NVIDIA GPUs support independent thread scheduling, meaning that in the absence of explicit synchronization, each thread executes independently. The fundamental synchronization primitive is
syncthreads
, a barrier that spans all threads within a CTA. Threads that reach the barrier earlier are suspended until all threads in the CTA arrive, at which point execution resumes for all of them.

Another synchronization primitive is
syncwarp
, which operates on a user-specified subset of threads within a single warp. This mechanism is commonly used when programming tensor cores, where CUDA intrinsics such as
wmma::mma_sync
synchronize all threads in a warp to perform matrix multiplication. Most prior verification techniques for GPU kernels support only
syncthreads
and therefore do not reason about machine learning kernels that rely on warp-level synchronization. While
syncthreads
guarantees deadlock freedom,
syncwarp
can introduce potential deadlocks.

In summary, the synchronization mechanisms we consider are barriers that block subsets of the available threads. Asynchronous primitives such as
arrive
and
wgmma
are beyond the scope of this work and left for future research.

3.2.
Practical considerations

Here, we discuss the assumptions that
Volta
makes over the input kernels at a high level. These assumptions are checked:
Volta
raises an exception if a kernel does not satisfy these requirements (Section
5
). We first state these assumptions and then provide motivation for them.

Volta
checks equivalence of two CTAs that operate over tensor tiles whose sizes are statically known. For each thread of a CTA, given its
tid
, all branch targets and addresses used in memory accesses are statically fixed.
Later we define structured-CTAs that formally encode these assumptions (Section
4.1
) and prove the completeness of
Volta
for this class of programs (Section
4.3
).

Although these assumptions arise from domain knowledge of machine learning, they also reflect well-established best practices for achieving high GPU performance. Empirical studies support these assumptions. A large-scale analysis of GPGPU kernels in the wild found that most memory accesses are
control independent
and
data independent

(
38
)
(59.5% of 2770 programs). Similarly, a study of the CUDA SDK showed that most programs exhibit
access invariance

(
32
)
, meaning that threads perform the same memory accesses for all inputs (52 out of 76). Moreover,
Weft

(
57
)
, a race detector for scientific and cudaDMA kernels, makes all of the above assumptions and confirms that
all
26 scientific computations studied by
Weft
fall in that class. Next, we provide some background on ML GPU kernels and motivate why they fall in this class.

We focus on kernels that form the performance bottlenecks in training or inference of machine learning (ML) models, such as matrix multiplications, convolutions, and multi-head attention. In these workloads, the structure of the ML model is fixed: tensor entries depend on runtime inputs, but tensor sizes are statically known. Kernels are therefore specialized for particular tensor dimensions to achieve maximum performance. Consequently, we assume that the sizes of all arrays accessed by the kernel are statically known to
Volta
.

Once array sizes are known, all loop bounds can also be determined, since the bounds in tensor operations depend on tensor shapes.
Volta
can therefore unroll these loops statically. We also require that the targets of conditional branches can be statically resolved; note this restriction exploits the fact that efficient machine learning kernels do not make truly data dependent runtime decisions. Under these assumptions, the code executed by each individual thread can be represented as
straight-line
, containing no dynamic branches or loops. It is common for threads to branch on predicates of their thread index; however, such control flow still preserves the straight-line property for each thread’s execution.

Efficient GPU kernels carefully orchestrate memory transfers by coalescing accesses to global memory and avoiding shared memory bank conflicts. As a result, GPU programmers typically define memory access patterns statically, making memory addresses independent of the tensor contents. Consequently, when a straight-line thread accesses an array element, both the base array and the exact index can be determined statically. This property allows the symbolic evaluator to record memory accesses precisely and to detect data races accurately.

The ML kernels launch a grid of CTAs where the code of each CTA is identical and differs only in the instantiation of free variables
blockIdx.x
,
blockIdx.y
, and
blockIdx.z
. Each CTA is responsible for computing a fixed-size
tile
of the output tensor. Many GPU optimization focus on this CTA-level code. For instance, the CTA code can be modified to use tensor cores. When checking a reference kernel against an optimized kernel, we focus on proving the equivalence between the code of a CTA from the reference kernel with the code of the corresponding CTA in the optimized kernel. Hence, correctness checking of optimizations that change the number of CTAs launched in a grid is beyond the scope of this work.
Since each pair of CTAs can be checked independent of each other, following prior work
(
57
)
, our evaluation focuses on checking the equivalence of CTAs with
blockIdx.x
=
blockIdx.y
=
blockIdx.z
= 0 in the reference and the optimized kernels.

A sound equivalence checker operating over floating-point arithmetic would likely reject many aggressively optimized kernels. In practice, both developers and domain-specific compilers treat tensor values as real numbers when applying optimizations. Such transformations, similar in spirit to the
--ffast-math
option in
gcc
, are unsound for floating point arithmetic.
Volta
follows the same convention as existing optimizers and models tensor elements as reals.

4.
Algorithm

Our equivalence checker proceeds in two phases. The first phase symbolically executes a pair of programs, determining whether they are data race free and, if so, expresses their output values as terms of symbolic input tensors. If both programs are data race free, we proceed to the second phase, which verifies that, for each output element, the symbolic terms derived for both programs are equal. For example, when analyzing Listing
1(a)
, the symbolic evaluator starts with environment
\{x[0]\coloneqq\alpha,x[1]\coloneqq\beta,x[2]\coloneqq\gamma,x[3]\coloneqq\delta\}
for fresh symbols
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
. It determines there is no data race and outputs a symbolic state with
y[0]\coloneqq e^{\alpha}/(e^{\alpha}+e^{\beta}+e^{\gamma}+e^{\delta})
.

\varphi(\mathcal{G}_{Q})=\mathcal{G}_{Q}^{\prime}

\mathcal{G}_{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{Q}^{\prime}
, then
\varphi(\mathcal{G}_{P})=\mathcal{G}_{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{Q}^{\prime}=\varphi(\mathcal{G}_{Q})
, a contradiction. Hence,
\mathcal{G}_{P}^{\prime}\neq\mathcal{G}_{Q}^{\prime}
as desired.
∎

4.4.
Decision procedure

Thus far in the formalism, we have assumed, without loss of generality, a single binary operator
\oplus
. In the decision procedure, we support expressions involving additions, multiplications, and exponentiations over reals and, in this section, we show that it is possible to decide equalities of the form
f_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
involve such expressions.
Although we do not give a formal account of other functions such as
\max
(which can be handled by case splits and do not affect decidability) here, the implementation supports them. We are not aware of a proof of decidability for our desired equalities, though there is work on related problems, such as on Tarski’s exponential function problem
(
41
)
.

If there were no exponentiations, decidability would be immediate. Because
f_{1}=f_{2}
iff
f_{1}-f_{2}
is identically zero, and
f_{1}-f_{2}
can be rewritten as a sum of monomials and such a sum is identically zero iff the coefficients of all the monomials are zero. We show that even with exponentiations, identities
f_{1}=f_{2}
are decidable. We first prove the following result in

Appendix
A
:

Theorem 11.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Suppose that
p_{1}(x),\ldots,p_{m}(x)
and
h_{1}(x),\ldots,h_{m}(x)
are elements of
\mathbb{R}[x]
(real polynomials) satisfying
\forall i\neq j.\ h_{i}(x)\neq h_{j}(x)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(x)e^{h_{i}(x)}=0
, then it must be that
p_{1}(x)=\cdots=p_{m}(x)=0
.

Hence, in the uni-variate case, identity checking with exponentiations reduces to identity checking without exponentiations, which is decidable as discussed earlier. The above theorem for uni-variates generalizes to multi-variate polynomials.
Let
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be an
n
-tuple of formal variables. We need to decide identities of the form
\sum_{i}p_{i}(\bar{x})e^{h(\bar{x})}=0
. We prove the following in
Appendix
A
:

Corollary 12.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Suppose that
p_{1}(\bar{x}),\ldots,p_{m}(\bar{x})
and
h_{1}(\bar{x}),\ldots,h_{m}(\bar{x})
are elements of
\mathbb{R}[\bar{x}]
(real multivariate polynomials) satisfying
\forall i\neq j.h_{i}(\bar{x})\neq h_{j}(\bar{x})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(\bar{x})e^{h_{i}(\bar{x})}=0
, then it must be that
p_{1}(\bar{x})=p_{2}(\bar{x})=\ldots=p_{m}(\bar{x})=0
.

Given our interest in proving completeness of the overall equivalence checking procedure, we do not focus on the computational complexity of the decision procedure and leave it for future work. Our implementation (Section
5
) is able to handle ML kernels that arise in practice (Section
6
). Finally, to make good on our claim in the proof of completeness for equivalence checking that
\forall a,b:\mathbb{V}
such that
a\neq b
there exists a map
\varphi:(\mathbb{V},\oplus)\to(\mathbb{R},\oplus)
(more precisely
(\mathbb{V},+,\cdot,\exp)\to(\mathbb{R},+,\cdot,\exp)
) such that
\varphi(a)\neq\varphi(b)
, we remark that
a,b
can be viewed as polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[\bar{x}]
for some
\bar{x}
. By contraposition of the above theorem, recalling that each
p_{i}
can be written as a sum of monomials, if
a\neq b
(that is,
a-b
is not formally
0
), they must be unequal on some input
\bar{r}
. Then,

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}r_{i}&\text{if }x=x_{i}\text{ for some }x_{i}\in\bar{x}\\
x&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

can be extended in the obvious way to the desired map
(\mathbb{V},+,\cdot,\exp)\to(\mathbb{R},+,\cdot,\exp)
.

5.
Implementation

We implement our techniques in a proof-of-concept tool called
Volta
.
Given a pair of PTX assembly programs
P
,
Q
to be checked for equivalence,
Volta
proceeds as follows. First, the implementation could raise an exception because the given PTX programs are not structured-CTAs.
Next, the symbolic execution could report a deadlock or a data race. If the symbolic execution succeeds, then for
P,Q
we obtain the environments
(\mathcal{G}_{P},\mathcal{R}_{P})
,
(\mathcal{G}_{Q},\mathcal{R}_{Q})
. The verification conditions (VCs) corresponding to the output tensors are given to the decision procedure that checks for their validity. If a VC is found to be invalid, then an equivalence bug is reported. If all VCs are provably valid, then the two programs are declared equivalent, i.e., they are guaranteed to compute semantically equivalent expressions over reals for all schedules.

At a high level,
Volta
is comprised of two main components: a simulator that performs symbolic execution to generate decision problems, and a decision procedure that verifies their validity. For a program where each thread runs at most
N
statements, the simulator’s runtime is linear in
N
, whereas the decision procedure exhibits superlinear complexity in
N
. Accordingly, we place greater emphasis on optimizing the latter. The current simulator implementation, described below, is not optimized for performance and could benefit from additional engineering. Nevertheless, because equivalence checking is not typically required in real time, the existing prototype is sufficient to demonstrate the core contributions of our work.

Volta
operates on CTAs written in PTX ISA 9.0
9
9

9

https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/parallel-thread-execution/
. The scheduler also accepts user-provided annotations that specify parameters such as the number of threads and the arrays designated as input/output tensors. The PTX file is first parsed into a low-level intermediate representation consisting of a list of
statements
. Examples of statements include memory reads and writes, arithmetic operations, jumps, and labels. Each statement is encoded as a record containing an opcode, a destination, a list of arguments, and an optional predicate for conditional execution. The parser currently supports all PTX instructions that appear in our benchmarks, and support for additional instructions is being added incrementally.

The list of statements corresponding to a thread block is provided to the scheduler. If the thread block contains threads with identifiers
0,\ldots,N-1
, the scheduler maintains a queue of
N
program counters along with a single shared copy of the statement list. Initially, the queue is
[(0,0),(1,0),\ldots,(N-1,0)]
, where each pair
(t,c)
indicates that thread
t
currently has program counter
c
. During execution, the scheduler repeatedly dequeues a program counter, symbolically evaluates the corresponding thread until it becomes blocked while updating the program state (discussed further below), and then enqueues the updated program counter. This round-robin scheduling continues until all threads reach
return
.
If the symbolic evaluator detects reads from undefined registers or uninitialized memory locations then
Volta
raises an exception. For arrays, an out of bound memory read or write also cause
Volta
to raise an exception.

The program state consists of
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{G}
, and
\mathcal{R}
, as introduced in Section
4
. It is implemented as a Python class that uses
SymPy
10
10

10

https://www.sympy.org/en/index.html
expressions to uniformly represent both symbolic and concrete values.
Volta
leverages
SymPy
’s built-in simplification capabilities to perform arithmetic operations during simulation, such as computing array offsets for memory reads and writes or when evaluating branch predicates that determine the next instruction to execute. The state also maintains information to ensure that all threads expected to reach a synchronization barrier have done so. If a data race or deadlock is detected then a report is generated.

After the simulation completes, the final
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{R}
are written to a file. Because
SymPy
expressions can be large, they are converted into a custom serialization format, and only the symbolic expressions corresponding to the output tensors are stored.
This process is performed for both programs.

For each element of an output tensor, the decision procedure uses the stored files to construct a verification condition (VC). Since deciding all VCs is an embarrassingly parallel task, our implementation employs multi-threading to evaluate them concurrently. Given a VC of the form
f=g
, where
f
and
g
are symbolic expressions over the input tensors,
Volta
constructs a
SymEngine
11
11

11

https://github.com/symengine/symengine
expression representing
f-g
and checks whether it canonicalizes to zero. An earlier version of the decision procedure, implemented in Python with
SymPy
, did not scale to our benchmarks, motivating a re-implementation in C
+
+
using
SymEngine
. Unlike
SymPy
, which provides a large set of built-in simplification rules,
SymEngine
offers a minimal core that enables us to implement only the simplification rules required for our domain.

Volta
traverses each node of the abstract syntax tree (AST) for
f-g
exactly once, in a bottom-up manner, applying a sequence of simplification rules. For internal nodes,
SymEngine
’s internal ordering is used to sort child nodes, ensuring a canonical representation. In addition,
Volta
applies a collection of rewrite rules when applicable. Examples include
\max(-\infty,x)=x
,
\max(x,x)=x
,
\max(\max(a,b),c)=\max(a,b,c)
,
E+(-E)=0
, and
e^{a}\times e^{b}=e^{a+b}
. The system also distributes multiplication over addition to find the coefficient of each monomial term. This operation, in principle, may cause exponential blowup. Since machine learning workloads do not typically have computations with high multiplicative depth, this blowup does not happen in practice. Finally,
Volta
employs caching to eliminate redundant computations and improve efficiency. Caching is unpredictable and hampers the interpretability of the running times. In the following evaluation, when the reported time is lower than
expected, it is mainly because of caching.

6.
Evaluation

There are three main ways to create efficient GPU kernels for machine learning workloads: manual optimization (Section
6.1
), generation by LLMs (Section
6.2
), and compilation by domain-specific compilers (Section
6.3
). Since our equivalence checker operates as a black box and makes no assumptions about the optimization process, it can verify the correctness of optimized kernels produced by all three approaches. We evaluate on reductions, matrix multiplications, convolutions, and various attention mechanisms. We report the time to solve the verification conditions (VCs) on a machine with a 24-core 2.64 GHz AMD EPYC 7413 processor and 220 GB of RAM.
We show that
Volta
scales to realistic machine learning workloads and effectively identifies correctness issues when they exist. In particular,
Volta
detects data races that have been previously reported in open-source GitHub repositories (Section
6.4
).

8.
Conclusion and future work

We have presented
Volta
, the first equivalence checker for GPU ML kernels. We formally prove that
Volta
is sound and complete for structured-CTAs.
Volta
scales to realistic ML benchmarks.
In particular, our work is the first formal verification tool which handles programs that use GPU tensor cores.
We prove the decidability of identities with expressions over Real algebra and exponentiations, a result that may be of independent interest.
This paper focuses on verification of programs that synchronize with blocking operations. We leave asynchronous CUDA intrinsics such as
pipeline
and
tma
for future work.

,
we have
p_{1}(x)e^{h_{1}(x)}=0
. Since
e^{h_{1}(a)}>0
for all
a
, it must be that
\forall a.p_{1}(a)=0
and hence
p_{1}(x)=0
.

For the induction case, assume that the result holds for
(m-1)
. Suppose WLOG that the leading coefficient of
h_{m}(x)
is the largest among the leading coefficients of the
h_{i}(x)
’s. Then the leading coefficient of
h_{i}(x)-h_{m}(x)
is negative for all
i<m
. This implies,
\forall i<m.\lim_{x\to\infty}e^{h_{i}(x)-h_{m}(x)}=0
.

We are given,
p_{1}(x)e^{h_{1}(x)-h_{m}(x)}+p_{2}(x)e^{h_{2}(x)-h_{m}(x)}+\ldots+p_{m-1}(x)e^{h_{m-1}(x)-h_{m}(x)}+p_{m}(x)=0
.
Taking limits as
x
approaches
\infty
, we have:

\left(\sum_{i<m}\lim_{x\to\infty}p_{i}(x)e^{h_{i}(x)-h_{m}(x)}\right)+\lim_{x\to\infty}p_{m}(x)=0

Therefore,
\lim_{x\to\infty}p_{m}(x)=0
. But for a non-zero polynomial
p
,
\lim_{x\to\infty}p(x)
is either
\infty
if the leading coefficient of
p
is positive and
-\infty
if negative. Hence,
p_{m}(x)=0
.

We will typically assume that the polynomial in the exponent has a zero constant, i.e.,
h(0)=0
. If
h(0)\neq 0
then we can rewrite
p(x)e^{h(x)}
as
p^{\prime}(x)e^{h^{\prime}(x)}
where
p^{\prime}(x)=p(x)e^{h(0)}
and
h^{\prime}(x)=h(x)-h(0)
.

Corollary 6.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Suppose that
p_{1}(\bar{x}),\ldots,p_{m}(\bar{x})
and
h_{1}(\bar{x}),\ldots,h_{m}(\bar{x})
are elements of
\mathbb{R}[\bar{x}]
(real multivariate polynomials) satisfying
\forall i\neq j.h_{i}(\bar{x})\neq h_{j}(\bar{x})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(\bar{x})e^{h_{i}(\bar{x})}=0
, then it must be that
p_{1}(\bar{x})=p_{2}(\bar{x})=\ldots=p_{m}(\bar{x})=0
.

Consider the substitution
x_{1}=\alpha_{1}y
,
x_{2}=\alpha_{2}y
,
\ldots
,
x_{n}=\alpha_{n}y
where each
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are chosen independently at random. Making this substitution, we get a univariate functional identity of the form

(2)

\hat{p}_{1}(y)e^{\hat{h}_{1}(y)}+\hat{p}_{2}(y)e^{\hat{h}_{2}(y)}+\ldots+\hat{p}_{m}(y)e^{\hat{h}_{m}(y)}=0

where for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
\hat{p}_{i}(y)=p_{i}(\alpha_{1}y,\alpha_{2}y,\ldots,\alpha_{n}y)

and
\hat{h}_{i}(y)=h_{i}(\alpha_{1}y,\alpha_{2}y,\ldots,\alpha_{n}y)
.

From our convention,
\hat{h}_{i}(0)=h_{i}(0,0,\ldots,0)\neq 0
.
For
i\neq j
, consider
\hat{h}_{i}(y)-\hat{h}_{j}(y)=h_{i}(\alpha_{1}y,\ldots,\alpha_{n}y)-h_{j}(\alpha_{1}y,\ldots,\alpha_{n}y)
. Let the degree of
h_{i}(\bar{x})-h_{j}(\bar{x})
be
d
and the largest degree homogeneous component of
h_{i}(\bar{x})-h_{j}(\bar{x})
be
g_{d}(\bar{x})\neq 0
. Then,
\hat{h}_{i}(y)-\hat{h}_{j}(y)
is a polynomial of degree at most
d
and the coefficient of
y^{d}
in
\hat{h}_{i}(y)-\hat{h}_{j}(y)
is
g_{d}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
. By Schwartz Zippel Lemma,
g_{d}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\neq 0
almost surely and so almost surely (over the random choice of
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
) we have
\forall i\neq j:\hat{h}_{i}(y)\neq\hat{h}_{j}(y)
.
Applying the theorem to Equation
2
, we get almost surely (over the random choice of
\bar{\alpha}
,
\hat{p}_{1}(y)=\ldots=\hat{p}_{m}(y)=0
. But
\hat{p}_{i}(y)=p_{i}(\alpha_{1}y,\ldots,\alpha_{m}y)
and this must imply that
p_{i}(\bar{x})=0
(via Schwartz Zippel Lemma applied to the largest homogeneous component of
p_{i}(\bar{x})
. Thus,
\forall 1\leq i\leq m.p_{i}(x)=0
.
