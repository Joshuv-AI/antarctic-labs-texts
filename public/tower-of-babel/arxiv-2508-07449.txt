Slip over liquid-infused gratings in the singular limit of a nearly inviscid lubricant
Gunnar G. Peng 1 , Ehud Yariv 2 , Ory Schnitzer 3

Abstract
We consider shear-driven longitudinal flow of an exterior fluid over a periodic array of rectangular grooves filled with an immiscible interior fluid (the “lubricant”), the grooves being formed by infinitely thin ridges protruding from a flat substrate. The ratio λ \lambda of the effective slip length to the semi-period is a function of the ratio μ \mu of the interior to exterior viscosities and the ratio h h of the grooves depth to the semi-period. We focus on the limit μ ≪ 1 \mu\ll 1 , which is singular for that geometry. We find that the viscous resistance to the imposed shear is dominated by a boundary layer of exponentially small extent about the ridge tips, resulting in the effective slip length scaling as μ − 1 / 2 \mu^{-1/2} — not μ − 1 \mu^{-1} as implied by intuitive arguments overlooking the tip contributions (and by proposed approximations in the literature). Analyzing that exponential region in conjunction with an integral force balance, we find the simple asymptotic approximation λ ≈ μ − 1 / 2 \lambda\approx\mu^{-1/2} ; using conformal mappings, we also calculate the leading-order correction to that result, which introduces a dependence upon h h . The ensuing asymptotic expansion breaks down for h = O ⁡ ( μ 1 / 2 ) h=O(\mu^{1/2}) , upon transitioning to a lubrication geometry. We accordingly conduct a companion asymptotic analysis in the distinguished limit of small μ \mu and fixed H = h / μ 1 / 2 H=h/\mu^{1/2} , which gives λ ≈ μ − 1 / 2 H / ( 1 + H ) \lambda\approx\mu^{-1/2}H/(1+H) as well as a closed-form leading-order correction to that approximation; the intuitive μ − 1 \mu^{-1} scaling is accordingly only relevant to the regime H ≪ 1 H\ll 1 corresponding to extremely shallow grooves. We demonstrate excellent agreement between our predictions and numerical solutions constructed using a boundary-integral formulation.



I
Introduction

There is continued interest in the use of engineered microstructured surfaces to reduce hydrodynamic resistance at small scales. Such drag reduction typically hinges upon the stable entrapment of a relatively inviscid lubricant within the microstructure cavities. Traditionally, research has focused on superhydrophobic surfaces
(
Rothstein 2010
;
Bocquet and Lauga 2011
)
— made of or coated with hydrophobic materials, and characterized by small-scale roughness, either random or periodic. When these surfaces come into contact with liquid, air becomes trapped in a “Cassie state” within the cavities, creating a composite surface consisting of the solid substrate and the liquid-air menisci
(
Quéré 2008
)
. Recently, a promising alternative has emerged: “slippery liquid-infused porous surfaces” (SLIPS), which are designed to spontaneously imbibe a liquid lubricant through wicking
(
Lafuma and Quéré 2011
;
Wong et al. 2011
;
Hardt and McHale 2022
)
. While the liquid lubricant cannot match air’s low viscosity, SLIPS generally offer superior robustness compared with superhydrophobic surfaces.

Towards characterizing the drag-reduction efficacy of a given microstructured surface, it is expedient to consider a canonical problem
(
Davis and Lauga 2009a
)
where the liquid adjacent to the surface is exposed to a far-field simple shear flow, parallel to the surface,

\mathbf{u}_{*}\sim\hat{\mathbf{s}}\,G_{*}y_{*}\quad\text{as}\quad y_{*}\to\infty,

(1)

where
y_{*}
is the distance from the surface,
G_{*}
is the shear rate of the imposed flow, and
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
is its direction. (Henceforth, an asterisk decoration denotes a dimensional quantity.) Since the imposed flow (
1
) vanishes at
y_{*}=0
, it constitutes an exact solution in the case of a smooth solid surface. The microstructure disturbs that flow, inducing a uniform “slip” stream, say
\mathbf{u}^{s}_{*}
, at large distances whereby the far-field expansion (
1
) extends as

\mathbf{u}_{*}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}\,G_{*}y_{*}+\mathbf{u}^{s}_{*}+o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad y_{*}\to\infty.

(2)

With negligible inertia, the flow is governed by the linear Stokes equations. Assuming that the shape of the menisci is determined by capillarity (and, in particular, is essentially unaffected by the flow), the entire problem is linear and homogeneous in
\hat{\mathbf{s}}G_{*}
. The slip velocity must therefore possess the form
\mathbf{u}^{s}_{*}=G_{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{s}}
, wherein the effective slip length
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{*}
is a two-dimensional (2D) second-order tensor depending on the surface geometry and the viscosity ratio of the two fluids. The most common microstructure configuration, used for both superhydrophobic surfaces and SLIPS, consists of a periodic array of ridges protruding perpendicularly from a flat solid substrate, with the lubricant filling the resulting grooves of rectangular cross section. For such configurations, the effective slip length adopts the simplified anisotropic form
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\lambda^{\parallel}_{*}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{3}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{3}+\lambda^{\perp}_{*}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
, in which the longitudinal slip length
\lambda^{\parallel}_{*}
and transverse slip length
\lambda^{\perp}_{*}
can be independently found by considering canonical flow problems where the shear is applied parallel (
\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{3}
) or perpendicular to the ridges (
\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
), respectively.

Given the minute viscosity of air, the menisci of superhydrophobic surfaces have traditionally been modeled as free surfaces
(
Batchelor 1967
)
. The canonical effective-slip problem then reduces to a liquid-flow problem, decoupled from the flow inside the cavities. Accordingly, the microstructure geometry is manifested only through the partitioning of the liquid boundary into solid-liquid and gas-liquid interfaces. Assuming flat menisci, the imposed far-field stress is resisted only by viscous shear stresses at the solid-liquid interfaces. Hence, the limit where the solid fraction of the compound liquid interface vanishes is singular. In particular, the effective slip length diverges in that limit. The rate of this divergence — scaling logarithmically with the solid fraction for grooved or meshed surfaces, or algebraically for post arrays — has been elucidated through numerous analytical and asymptotic studies
(
Philip 1972a
;
Lauga and Stone 2003
;
Ybert et al. 2007
;
Davis and Lauga 2009b
;
Davis and Lauga 2010
;
Schnitzer 2016
;
Schnitzer 2017
;
Yariv 2017
;
Schnitzer and Yariv 2018a
;
Schnitzer and Yariv 2018b
;
Yariv and Schnitzer 2018
)
. While giant slip lengths have indeed been measured using small-solid-fraction superhydrophobic surfaces
(
Choi and Kim 2006
;
Lee et al. 2008
)
, the prospects of such surfaces are considered limited since the Cassie state inevitably collapses if the solid fraction is too low
(
Extrand 2004
;
Sbragaglia et al. 2007
;
Lee et al. 2016
)
.

Unlike in the superhydrophobic scenario, models of flow over SLIPS typically account for a finite lubricant viscosity. The effective-slip problem then involves the coupling of the exterior flow outside the cavities and the lubricant flow inside them. This coupling introduces a dependency not just on the viscosity ratio of the two fluids, but also on the interior geometry of the microstructure cavities; for “rectangular” grooves, the extra geometric parameter, in addition to the solid fraction, is the ridge height (i.e., groove depth). Very few analytical studies of the effective slip problem have included the effect of the interior fluid. The only systematic analysis we are aware of is that of
Crowdy 2017
, who derived analytical formulas for the effective slip length by perturbing about Philip’s classical complex-variable solutions for an inviscid lubricant
(
Philip 1972b
)
. Other investigators, including
Schönecker et al. 2014
and
Nizkaya et al. 2014
, have employed a mix of exact solutions and heuristic modeling assumptions, demonstrating good agreement with numerical simulations over a wide parameter range.

Having a finite lubricant viscosity regularizes the small-solid-fraction singularity. Indeed, the imposed shear is then resisted not just by the exterior solid boundaries, as in the superhydrophobic scenario, but also by the liquid-liquid interfaces. (Alternatively, one could interpret this as the imposed shear being resisted by both the exterior and interior solid boundaries.) Although the effective slip length no longer diverges as the solid fraction vanishes, it is still expected to increase in magnitude in that limit. Moreover, from a practical viewpoint, the SLIPS are stable even at very low solid fraction
(
Smith et al. 2013
)
. This motivates theoretical analysis of idealized
zero-solid-fraction
configurations. In particular, it is natural to consider the canonical rectangular-grooves geometry in the case of zero-thickness ridges — henceforth referred to as a “grating.” Indeed, this geometry
has been studied both in the transverse problem
(
Hocking 1976
)
and the longitudinal problem
(
Bechert and Bartenwerfer 1989
)
. (The latter problem was actually studied for a single-phase flow, corresponding to the special case where the viscosities are matched.) This geometry has also been employed by
Yan and Kowal 2024
in an analysis of transverse flow over a liquid-infused surface, with the effective slip length estimated using a heuristic approach similar to those of
Schönecker et al. 2014
and
Nizkaya et al. 2014
.

For the zero-solid-fraction grating geometry, the imposed shear is entirely supported by the lubricant. Accordingly, this scenario introduces a new
singular
limit in which the effective slip length diverges: that where the lubricant viscosity is vanishingly small. This previously unexplored singular limit is practically significant as it characterizes the maximum potential for drag reduction of SLIPS. As such, the motivation for studying it parallels that for the singular small-solid-fraction limit of the inviscid-lubricant scenario. In light of the above, the majority of our paper is concerned with an asymptotic analysis of the grating geometry in the singular limit of a nearly inviscid lubricant. We shall focus on the longitudinal grating problem, which is simpler and associated with larger slip lengths than its transverse counterpart. Similarly to analyses of the above-mentioned small-solid-fraction limit, we shall employ matched asymptotic expansions
(
Hinch 1991
)
in conjunction with conformal mappings
(
Brown and Churchill 2003
)
.

While the singular nature of the nearly-inviscid limit is evident, the corresponding scaling of the effective slip length is not. Let
\lambda_{*}
denote this scaling, and
\mu=\mu_{*}^{-}/\mu_{*}^{+}
the ratio of the interior-to-exterior viscosities. By conservation of momentum, the imposed shear stress
\mu_{*}^{+}G_{*}
must balance the average stress at the liquid-liquid interface. In the limit
\mu\ll 1
, it is intuitive to anticipate that the exterior flow is dominated by a uniform slip stream, which in light of (
2
) should scale as
v_{*}=G_{*}\lambda_{*}
. Since both the velocity and shear stress are continuous at the interface, the latter can be estimated as
\mu^{-}_{*}v_{*}/\ell_{*}
, where
\ell_{*}
is a length scale characterizing the interior flow — perhaps the groove width, or its depth? This argument, which follows the classical scaling approach of
Ybert et al. 2007
, yields the singular scaling
\lambda_{*}/\ell_{*}=O(1/\mu)
. This scaling is built into Yan and Kowal’s model for slip over a grating geometry
(
Yan and Kowal 2024
)
. Some scrutiny reveals that it is also consistent with the more general models proposed by
Schönecker et al. 2014
and
Nizkaya et al. 2014
, when these are degenerated to a grating geometry and reduced in the nearly-inviscid limit.

Nonetheless, our analysis will reveal that the
\mu^{-1}
scaling is actually
erroneous
, except in a lubrication-like limit corresponding to extremely shallow grooves. Indeed, the above scaling arguments overlook the formation of boundary layers, of
exponentially small
extent, about the tips of the zero-thickness ridges. At leading order, the applied shear is entirely supported by the localized flow in these tip regions. This delicate balance, in turn, implies an effective slip length that turns out to scale as
\mu^{-1/2}
.

The paper is arranged as follows. In the next section we formulate the problem for longitudinal shear flow about a grating and derive a useful integral force balance. We also briefly discuss a numerical scheme used to solve this “exact” problem. The intuitive lubrication limit corresponding to extremely shallow grooves is briefly addressed in Sec.
III
. In preparation for the analysis of the singular limit
\mu\ll 1
, we perform in Sec.
IV
a local analysis of the flow problem in the vicinity of the tips. The limit
\mu\ll 1
is analyzed in Sec.
V
, where the identification and analysis of the localized tip layers furnishes the leading-order slip length. In Sec.
VI
we derive a functional expression for the
\ord(1)
correction to the slip length.
In Sec.
VII
we identify a distinguished limit of shallow grooves and a nearly-inviscid lubricant and calculate the associated slip length. We conclude in Sec.
VIII
, addressing deviations from a grating geometry to finite solid fractions.

II
Problem formulation

The problem is depicted in Fig.
1
(a,b). The underlying solid substrate is a grating of period
2\ell_{*}
. The ridges are assumed infinitely thin; their height is
h\ell_{*}
. The grooves bounded by the ridges and the bottom substrate are filled with an “interior” lubricant, of viscosity
\mu^{-}_{*}
. The resulting compound surface is brought into contact with an “exterior” liquid of viscosity
\mu_{*}^{+}
. We assume that the menisci, pinned at the tips of the ridges, are flat (zero protrusion angle). At large distances from the surface, the flow approaches a longitudinal shear flow of shear-rate magnitude
G_{*}
; the velocity field thus satisfies the far-field condition (
1
), with the unit vector
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
parallel to the grooves.

We utilize a dimensionless notation where length variables are normalized by
\ell_{*}
and velocities by
\ell_{*}G_{*}
.
We employ Cartesian coordinates
(x_{1}=x,x_{2}=y,x_{3})
, with the imposed shear in the
x_{3}
-direction and the ridges located at
x=2n
with integer
n
. The plane
y=0
passes through the ridge tips; the external and internal fluids occupy the domains
y>0
and
-h<y<0
, respectively. Since the flow is unidirectional, the velocity fields in the interior and exterior domains can be written as
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{3}w^{\pm}(x,y)
, where
\pm
indicates the sign of

. Due to the discrete-translational (periodic) and mirror symmetries, it suffices to focus upon a single “semi-unit cell”, say, extending from the ridge at
x=0
to the center of the groove at
x=1
, see Fig.
1
(c).

The equations governing the velocity fields
w^{\pm}
are then given as follows. Due to unidirectionality, the Navier–Stokes equations for the flows simplify to Laplace’s equation in each domain,

\frac{\partial^{2}w^{\pm}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}w^{\pm}}{\partial y^{2}}=0\quad\text{for}\quad y>0\ (+)\quad\text{or}\quad{-h}<y<0\ (-).

(3)

The two fields are linked by the interfacial conditions of continuous velocity,

w^{+}=w^{-}\quad\text{at}\quad y=0,

(4a)

and continuous shear stress,

\quad\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial y}=\mu\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial y}\quad\text{at}\quad y=0,

(4b)

where
\mu=\mu_{*}^{-}/\mu_{*}^{+}
. The interior field
w^{-}
further satisfies the symmetry condition

\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial x}=0\quad\text{at}\quad x=1,

(5)

and the no-slip condition at the ridge and at the bottom,

w^{-}=0\quad\text{at}\quad x=0,\qquad w^{-}=0\quad\text{at}\quad y=-h.

(
6
a,b)

The exterior field
w^{+}
further satisfies the two symmetry conditions

\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial x}=0\quad\text{at}\quad x=0,\qquad\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial x}=0\quad\text{at}\quad x=1,

(
7
a,b)

the imposed shear in the far field,

w^{+}\sim y\quad\text{as}\quad y\to\infty,

(8)

and the no-slip condition at the tip of the ridge,

\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}w^{+}=0.

(9)

The problem posed by (
3
)–(
9
) uniquely determines
the flow fields
w^{\pm}(x,y)
, depending on the governing parameters
\mu
and
h
. In particular, it determines the “slip length”,

\lambda=\lim_{y\to\infty}(w^{+}-y),

(10)

namely the constant correction to the shear in the far-field behavior (
8

1
(d)]

w^{+}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}=}y+\lambda+o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad y\to\infty.

(11)

The goal is to calculate
\lambda
as a function of
\mu
and
h
.

It is useful to define the integral quantity

\mathcal{D}=\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx,

(12a)

which can be interpreted as the net viscous drag exerted by the exterior fluid on the interior fluid, through viscous shear stresses at the interface. Using (
4b
), we can also express this drag in terms of the interior field as

\mathcal{D}=\mu\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx.

(12b)

Integral conservation of momentum can be used to derive alternative expressions for
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be any simple curve in the exterior domain extending from
x=0
to
x=1
. By integrating Laplace’s equation (
3
) over the domain bounded between
y=0
and
\mathcal{C}
(within the semi-unit cell), and making use of the 2D variant of Gauss’s theorem in conjunction with the symmetry conditions (
7
), we find

\mathcal{D}=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}w^{+}\,dl,

(13)

where
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
is the unit normal to
\mathcal{C}
pointing away from the domain,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\partial/\partial x+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\partial/\partial y
and

is the differential arc length along
\mathcal{C}
.

The viscous drag at the interface must balance with the viscous traction at large distances from the surface, which is set by the external shear flow. Indeed, following Schnitzer
Schnitzer 2016
;
Schnitzer 2017
, we shift
\mathcal{C}
to infinity in (
13

8
) to find

\mathcal{D}=1.

(14)

Substituting this result into (
12

13

\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx=1,\quad\mu\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx=1,\quad\int_{\mathcal{C}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}w^{+}\,dl=1.

(
15
a,b,c)

As these balances follow from the problem formulation, they do not provide independent constraints. Nonetheless, they will be found to be useful in the subsequent analysis. In particular, (
15

We can also derive an integral expression for the slip length in terms of the velocity distribution at the interface. We first note from (
13

12a
) can be generalized to arbitrary
y\geq 0

\mathcal{D}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial y}\,dx.

(16)

Upon interchanging integration and differentiation, this extension gives

\mathcal{D}=\frac{d}{dy}\int_{0}^{1}w^{+}(x,y)\,dx.

(17)

Then, by integrating (
17

14

\lambda=\int_{0}^{1}w^{+}(x,0)\,dy.

Our main goal in this paper is to derive approximations for the slip length

\mu\ll 1

III
Shallow grooves

We begin by briefly considering the limit of shallow grooves,
h\ll 1
. It is readily seen that the one-dimensional linear profiles

w^{+}=y+\frac{h}{\mu},\qquad w^{-}=\frac{y+h}{\mu},

(
19
a,b)

satisfy the governing equations (
3
)–(
9
), except for the no-slip conditions (
6
) and (
9
) on the ridge. This suggests that (
19
) should be interpreted asymptotically as leading-order “outer” approximations in the limit
h\ll 1
with
y/h
fixed
(
Hinch 1991
)
, which break down near the ridge. The definition (
10
) then yields the slip length

\lambda\approx\frac{h}{\mu},

(20)

which is small (as long as
h\ll\mu
). This is to be expected, as the flow of the lubricant is resisted by the bottom boundary.

While the lubrication-type approximation (
20
) is simple and intuitive, we shall see that its validity for small
\mu
is severely limited.

IV
Local analysis near the ridge tip

We next analyze the local structure of the velocity field in the vicinity of the ridge tip
(x,y)=(0,0)

(\rho,\phi)

(x,y)=(\rho\cos\phi,\rho\sin\phi)

These include Laplace’s equation [cf. (

\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left(\rho\frac{\partial w^{\pm}}{\partial\rho}\right)+\frac{\partial^{2}w^{\pm}}{\partial\phi^{2}}=0\quad\text{for}\quad 0<\pm\phi<\frac{\pi}{2};

(21)

the conditions of continuous velocity and shear stress [cf. (
4

w^{+}=w^{-}\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=0,\qquad\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial\phi}=\mu\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial\phi}\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=0;

(
22
a,b)

the requirements at

6
) and (
7
)],

w^{-}=0\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad\frac{\partial w^{+}}{\partial\phi}=0\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=\frac{\pi}{2};

(23a,b)

and the tip condition [cf. (
9
)],

\lim_{\rho\to 0}w^{+}=0.

(24)

The remaining conditions [(
5
), (
6
), (
7
) and (
8

We now seek a leading-order approximation of the velocity field as
\rho\searrow 0
. We denote this approximation by
w_{L}^{\pm}(\rho,\phi)
and assume that it is a power law in
\rho
, i.e., proportional to
\rho^{\alpha}
,
where the unknown exponent
\alpha
is positive in order to satisfy (

w_{L}^{\pm}(\rho,\phi)=\rho^{\alpha}\left[A^{\pm}\cos(\alpha\phi)+B^{\pm}\sin(\alpha\phi)\right].

(25)

Continuity of velocity, (
22

A^{+}=A^{-}
; we denote this common value by
A

B^{+}=\mu B^{-},\quad A\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}-B^{-}\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}=0,\quad-A\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}+B^{+}\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}=0.

Upon eliminating

\tan(\alpha\pi/2)=\pm\mu^{1/2}

\alpha=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\mu^{1/2}.

(27)

The associated solution is

(28a,b)

The constant

We conclude that, on the interface

w_{L}^{+}=w_{L}^{-}=Ax^{\alpha},\qquad\frac{\partial w_{L}^{+}}{\partial y}=\mu\frac{\partial w_{L}^{-}}{\partial y}=A\alpha\mu^{1/2}x^{\alpha-1}.

(
IV

\mu\int_{0}^{\delta}\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx\approx A\mu^{1/2}\delta^{\alpha}.

(30)

We shall employ this estimate for scaling purposes.

Nearly inviscid lubricant

\mu\ll 1

V.1
Scaling arguments based on local analysis

While the local analysis of Sec.

\mu^{1/2}\ln\delta
is small). Assuming that this forms a non-negligible part of the total drag, (
14
) suggests that
A=\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
. [In fact, it will turn out that this is the sole contribution to the drag integral at
\ord(1)
, so that the force balance (
15
) gives
A\sim\mu^{-1/2}
.]

V.2
Asymptotic structure for nearly inviscid lubricant

For
\mu\ll 1
, the intuitive arguments in Sec.
V.1
suggest that velocities should be
\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
in order to result in an
\ord(1)
drag. We therefore postulate the expansions

\displaystyle w^{\pm}

\displaystyle\sim\mu^{-1/2}w_{-1}^{\pm}(x,y)+w_{0}^{\pm}(x,y)+\mu^{1/2}w_{1}^{\pm}(x,y)+\cdots,

(36)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\sim\mu^{-1/2}\lambda_{-1}+\lambda_{0}+\mu^{1/2}\lambda_{1}+\cdots,

(37)

as
\mu\to 0
. The far-field condition (
11
) then yields

w_{-1}^{+}\sim\lambda_{-1},\qquad w_{0}^{+}\sim y+\lambda_{0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1},}

(
38
a,b)

as
y\to\infty
, and continuity of shear stress (
4b
) yields

\frac{\partial w_{-1}^{+}}{\partial y}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial w_{0}^{+}}{\partial y}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial w_{1}^{+}}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial w_{-1}^{-}}{\partial y},

(
39
a,b,c)

at
y=0
. The remainder of the equations (
3
)–(
9
) simply retain their form at each order.

It may appear from (
39
a,b) and (
12
) that the drag
\mathcal{D}
is
O(\mu^{1/2})
, seemingly too small to satisfy (
14
). The resolution to this apparent contradiction is that the expansions (
36
) represent an outer asymptotic region where
(x,y)=\ord(1)
, whereas the dominant force is provided by an inner asymptotic region about the tip of the ridge,
(x,y)=(0,0)
. (The scaling of the thickness of this inner region will be determined later.) The no-slip condition at the tip, (
9
), is accordingly omitted from the outer problems posed above, as it rather applies to the inner region. Similarly, the shear-stress-continuity conditions (
39
) are henceforth understand to hold for
x>0
.

An alternative (perhaps more tempting) resolution would be to assume that the velocities and slip length are
\ord(\mu^{-1})
instead, to directly yield an
\ord(1)
shear stress at the interface. However, following such a calculation through, or appealing to (
35
), would reveal an
\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
contribution from near the tip to the drag integral (
12
), in contradiction to (
14
). Attempting any other large scaling for
w^{\pm}
and
\lambda
similarly results in a viscous drag of the wrong order; only the scaling (
36
) yields a self-consistent result.

We now proceed to calculate the leading-order slip length
\lambda_{-1}
by considering the outer approximation in the exterior domain, the inner approximation in both the exterior and interior domains, and the force balances (
15
).

V.3
Outer approximation in the exterior domain

We first consider the leading-order outer flow in the exterior domain,
w_{-1}^{+}
. It satisfies Laplace’s equation (
3
) together with the lateral symmetry conditions (
7
), the far-field streaming condition (
38
), and the stress-free interface condition (
39
). This outer problem suggests the uniform flow

w^{+}_{-1}\equiv\lambda_{-1}.

(40)

Although not necessary for determining the leading-order slip length, we study the asymptotic correction
w_{0}^{+}
briefly. At
\text{ord}(1)
, the generalized drag expression (
16
) gives

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial w_{0}^{+}}{\partial y}\,dx=1.

(41)

While the exact expression (
16
) holds for all
y\geq 0
, it only implies (
41
) for
y>0
, since the outer expansion (
36
) fails at the origin
(x,y)=(0,0)
. The ambiguity of (
41
) at
y=0
, in conjunction with the integral apparently vanishing there owing to the shear-free condition (
39
), suggests that
w_{0}^{+}
has a singularity at the origin.

To characterize the singularity, we employ the generalized force balance (
15
) at
\text{ord}(1)
,

\int_{\mathcal{C}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}w_{0}^{+}\,dl=1,

(42)

with
\mathcal{C}
the union of a quarter-circle of radius
0<\delta<1
centered at the origin (within the exterior semi-unit cell) and the interval
x\in(\delta,1)
on
y=0
. Given the stress-free interface condition (
39
), only the quarter-circle has a non-zero contribution. By letting
\delta\to 0
, we see that
the total force emanating from this “corner” is
1
. We therefore expect the singularity to be a “source of strength
4
”, i.e.,

w^{+}_{0}\sim\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\rho\quad\text{as}\quad\rho\to 0.

(43)

In principle, this asymptotic behavior could include more singular terms (without modifying the net force); these, however, can be ruled out via matching considerations.

V.4
Inner tip region

To determine the scaling of the inner region near the tip, we consider the local behavior as
\rho\to 0
of the first two terms in the expansion (
36
) for
w^{+}
. Equations (
40
) and (
43
) show that the expansion breaks down once the exponentially small scale set by
\ln(1/\rho)=\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
is reached; on that scale, the second term becomes asymptotically comparable to the first.

This observation suggests that an appropriate rescaled coordinate
\varrho
for the inner region near the tip is given by

\varrho=\mu^{1/2}\ln\rho.

(44)

The coordinate
\varrho
is negative, with the tip
\rho=0
located at
\varrho=-\infty
. Matching to the outer region
\rho=\ord(1)
takes place for small
\varrho
, specifically on the intermediate scale
\mu^{1/2}\ll\varrho\ll 1
.

We define the tip-scale velocity fields
\omega^{\pm}(\varrho,\phi)=w^{\pm}(\rho,\phi)
. They satisfy the following equations [cf. (
21
)–(
24
) in the local analysis]:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\omega^{\pm}}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}\omega^{\pm}}{\partial\varrho^{2}}=0\quad\text{for}\quad 0<\pm\phi<\frac{\pi}{2},

(45)

\displaystyle\omega^{+}=\omega^{-}\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=0,\qquad\frac{\partial\omega^{+}}{\partial\phi}=\mu\frac{\partial\omega^{-}}{\partial\phi}\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=0,

(46a,b)

\displaystyle\omega^{-}=0\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad\frac{\partial\omega^{+}}{\partial\phi}=0\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=\frac{\pi}{2},

(47a,b)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varrho\to-\infty}\omega^{+}=0.

(48)

We accordingly posit the inner expansions

\omega^{\pm}(\varrho,\phi)\sim\mu^{-1/2}\omega_{-1}^{\pm}(\varrho,\phi)+\omega_{0}^{\pm}(\varrho,\phi)+\mu^{1/2}\omega_{1}^{\pm}(\varrho,\phi)+\cdots\quad\text{as}\quad\mu\to 0,

(49)

and seek to calculate
\omega_{-1}^{\pm}
.
The set of homogeneous equations (
45
)–(
48
) is supplemented at each order with the requirement of matching to the outer region.
In particular, at leading order, the uniform solution (
40
) implies the inhomogeneous matching condition

\lim_{\varrho\to 0}\omega^{+}_{-1}=\lambda_{-1}.

(50)

We start with the exterior domain.
It is easy to see from the governing equation (
45
) with the symmetry condition (
47
) at
\ord(\mu^{-1/2})

that
\omega^{+}_{-1}

cannot depend upon
\phi
:

\omega_{-1}^{+}=\omega_{-1}^{+}(\varrho).

(51)

Thus, the governing equation (
45
) at
\ord(\mu^{1/2})
becomes

\frac{\partial^{2}\omega_{1}^{+}}{\partial\phi^{2}}=-\frac{d^{2}\omega_{-1}^{+}}{d\varrho^{2}}.

(52)

Integrating this over
0\leq\phi\leq\pi/2
and using
the symmetry condition (
47
) yields

\frac{\partial\omega^{+}_{1}}{\partial\phi}=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{d^{2}\omega_{-1}^{+}}{d\varrho^{2}}\quad\text{at}\quad\phi=0.

(53)

Consider now the interior domain at
\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
. Solving the governing equation (
45
)
with the no-slip condition (
47
) and continuity condition (
46
) gives

\omega^{-}_{-1}(\varrho,\phi)=\left(1+\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\right)\omega^{+}_{-1}(\varrho).

(54)

Substitution of (
53
) and (
54
) into the shear-stress condition (
46
) at
\ord(\mu^{1/2})
then gives an ordinary differential equation for
\omega_{-1}^{+}
,

\frac{d^{2}\omega_{-1}^{+}}{d\rho^{2}}=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\omega_{-1}^{+}.

(55)

The solution that satisfies the no-slip tip condition (
48
) and matching condition (
50
) is

\omega^{+}_{-1}=\lambda_{-1}e^{2\varrho/\pi}.

(56a)

The corresponding leading-order interior solution (
54
) is

\omega^{-}_{-1}=\lambda_{-1}\left(1+\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\right)e^{2\varrho/\pi}.

(56b)

[Reassuringly, the form of the inner solutions (
56
) agrees with the small-
\mu
expansion (
V.1
) of the results from the local analysis.]

V.5
Leading-order drag and slip length

It remains to determine the slip length
\lambda_{-1}
. We turn to the viscous-drag integral (
15
), and split it into separate contributions from the tip and outer regions,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}+\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}
, where

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}=\mu\int_{0}^{\delta}\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx,\qquad\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}=\mu\int_{\delta}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\,dx{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}.}

(
57
a,b)

By virtue of the force balance (
15
),

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}+\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}=1.

(57c)

The splitting point
x=\delta
is chosen to be on an “intermediate” scale between the outer scale
x=\ord(1)
and the inner scale
\varrho=\ord(1)
[see (
44
)]. This intermediate scale is represented by the conditions

\delta\ll 1\quad\text{ and }\quad\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\ll\mu^{-1/2}.

(
58
a,b)

Since the outer solution is
w^{-}=\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
[cf. (
36
)], the outer contribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}
scales as
\ord(\mu^{1/2})
and does not contribute at leading order to (
57c
).

For the tip contribution, we transform via polar coordinates
(\rho,\phi)
into tip coordinates
(\varrho,\phi)
using (
44
), and obtain

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}=\mu\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{\rho}\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\phi=0}\,d\rho=\mu^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu^{1/2}\ln(1/\delta)}\left.\frac{\partial\omega^{-}}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\phi=0}\,d\varrho.

(59)

Using the leading-order tip solution (
56b
) and replacing the upper integration limit with
0
[due to (
58
)], we obtain the result

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}\sim\frac{2\lambda_{-1}}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2\varrho/\pi}\,d\varrho=\lambda_{-1}.

(60)

We conclude from the force balance (
57c
) that the leading-order slip length is simply given by

\lambda_{-1}=1,

(61)

that is,

\lambda\sim\mu^{-1/2}\quad\text{for}\quad\mu\ll 1.

(62)

[An alternative derivation makes use of van Dyke matching
(
Van Dyke 1964
)
: Writing (
56a
) for
\omega_{-1}^{+}
in terms of the outer coordinate
\rho
and expanding for
\mu\ll 1
gives

\mu^{-1/2}\lambda_{-1}+\frac{2\lambda_{-1}}{\pi}\ln\rho+\cdots.

(63)

Comparison with (
43
) then yields the result (
61
).]

The leading-order results for
\mu\ll 1
can be summarized intuitively as follows. The velocity in the exterior domain
y>0
is approximately uniform,
w^{+}\sim\lambda
[see (
40
)]. Given the need to satisfy the no-slip condition, this uniform “slip flow” is disturbed in an exponentially small inner region about the tip, see (
56
). This disturbance, in turn, results in a viscous drag
\sim\mu^{1/2}\lambda
. Other contributions to the drag only enter at higher order, so the balance (
15
) between the viscous drag and the unity imposed shear yields the result
\lambda\sim\mu^{-1/2}
[see (
62
)].

VI
First correction to slip length

In the previous section, we determined the first term in the expansion

\lambda\sim\mu^{-1/2}+\lambda_{0}\quad\text{for}\quad\mu\ll 1.

(64)

We proceed here to determine the correction,
\lambda_{0}
, which depends only upon the groove depth
h
. This involves calculating the first corrections (smaller by an order
\mu^{1/2}
) to the outer approximation in the exterior domain and the inner (tip) approximation in both domains,
as well as a leading-order outer approximation in the interior domain. We subsequently use these results to extend expansion (
60
) for
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}
to higher order, and to calculate the leading-order contribution to
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}
from the interior solution.

VI.1
First correction to the outer approximation in the exterior domain

We begin with the asymptotic correction
w_{0}^{+}
to the uniform leading-order outer approximation in the exterior domain, (
40
). To aid the calculation, we introduce a shifted field,

\tilde{w}_{0}^{+}=w_{0}^{+}-\lambda_{0},

(65)

so that
\lambda_{0}
is eliminated from the far-field condition (
38
):

\tilde{w}_{0}^{+}=y+o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad y\to\infty.

(66)

The problem governing
\tilde{w}_{0}^{+}
is given by Laplace’s equation [recall (
3
)]; homogeneous Neumann conditions at
x=0
,
x=1
and
y=0
, excluding the origin [cf. (
7
) and (
39
)]; the far-field condition (
66
); and (
43
), which follows from matching to the tip region [see (
63
)]. Hence,
\tilde{w}_{0}^{+}
has a unique solution that is
universal
, i.e., does not depend on any parameters. In particular, it follows from the local behavior (
43
) that we can define the pure number

\beta=\lim_{\rho\to 0}\left(\tilde{w}_{0}^{+}-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\rho\right),

(67)

and deduce that

w_{0}^{+}\sim\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\rho+\lambda_{0}+\beta\quad\text{as}\quad\rho\to 0.

(68)

Expression (
68
) needs to match the inner region at the next order.

The universal problem possesses the explicit solution
(see Appendix
B
)

\tilde{w}_{0}^{+}=\frac{1}{\pi}\ln\left[4\left(\sin^{2}\frac{\pi x}{2}+\sinh^{2}\frac{\pi y}{2}\right)\right],

(69)

whereby (
67
) gives

\beta=\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\pi.

(70)

VI.2
First corrections to the inner tip approximations

Continuing to the tip region, we observe that the analysis in Sec.
V.4
can be repeated at one higher order, to yield

\omega^{+}_{0}=C_{0}e^{2\varrho/\pi},\qquad\omega^{-}_{0}=C_{0}\left(1+\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\right)e^{2\varrho/\pi}.

(
71
a,b)

The constant
C_{0}
is determined by 2–2 van Dyke matching
(
Van Dyke 1964
)
to the outer exterior solution, as follows. Taking the inner field

\omega^{+}
up to
\ord(1)
[i.e., (
56a
) and (
71
), making use of (
61
)], rewriting in terms of the outer coordinate
\rho
and expanding for small
\mu
gives, when retaining terms up to
\ord(1)
,

\mu^{-1/2}+\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\rho+C_{0}.

(72)

Comparing with the outer behavior [i.e., (
40
) and (
68
)], we conclude that
C_{0}=\beta+\lambda_{0}
, and hence (
71
) becomes

\omega^{+}_{0}=(\beta+\lambda_{0})e^{2\varrho/\pi},\qquad\omega^{-}_{0}=(\beta+\lambda_{0})\left(1+\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\right)e^{2\varrho/\pi}.

(
73
a,b)

To complete the calculation and determine
\lambda_{0}
, we need to revisit the viscous-drag integral (
57
).

VI.3
Corrections to the viscous drag

We first evaluate the tip contribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}
in (
57
) to
\ord(\mu^{1/2})
using the first two terms in the expansion (
49
). The leading-order approximation (
56b
) contributes, using (
61
),

\int_{-\infty}^{\mu^{1/2}\ln\delta}\frac{2}{\pi}e^{2\varrho/\pi}\,d\varrho\sim 1+\mu^{1/2}\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\delta,

(74)

where we again use the fact that
\mu^{1/2}\ln\delta\ll 1
, from (
58
). Similarly, the correction (
73
) contributes

\mu^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\mu^{1/2}\ln\delta}(\beta+\lambda_{0})\frac{2}{\pi}e^{2\varrho/\pi}\,d\varrho\sim\mu^{1/2}(\beta+\lambda_{0}).

(75)

Hence, the tip contribution is

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tip}}\sim 1+\mu^{1/2}\left(-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}+\beta+\lambda_{0}\right).

(76)

The outer contribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}
in (
57
) depends on the leading-order outer approximation in the interior domain,
w_{-1}^{-}
. Prior to calculating this approximation, we determine an expression for the desired correction
\lambda_{0}
in terms of
w_{-1}^{-}
.

Given that
w_{-1}^{-}
matches to the tip solution
\omega_{-1}^{-}
[given by (
56b
) and (
61
)], we can deduce its local behavior

w_{-1}^{-}\sim 1+\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\quad\text{as}\quad\rho\to 0.

(77)

Consequently, the shear at the interface has the near-tip behavior

\left.\frac{\partial w_{-1}^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}\sim\frac{2}{\pi x}\quad\text{as}\quad x\to 0,

(78)

which results in a logarithmic divergence in the drag integral (
57
) as
\delta\to 0
. We subtract this singularity from the integral to obtain

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}\sim\mu^{1/2}\left[\int_{\delta}^{1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial w_{-1}^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}-\frac{2}{\pi x}\right)\,dx-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\delta\right].

(79)

This motivates defining a “regularized” drag contribution

\mathcal{F}(h)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial w_{-1}^{-}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}-\frac{2}{\pi x}\right)\,dx,

(80)

where we make it explicit that
\mathcal{F}
depends only on the groove depth
h
. Equation (
79
) then simplifies to

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{outer}}\sim\mu^{1/2}\left[\mathcal{F}(h)+\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right].

(81)

Upon substitution of the drag contributions (
76
) and (
81
) into the force balance (
57c
), the terms involving
\ln\delta
cancel out, as they should, and the remaining terms at
\ord(\mu^{1/2})
determine the correction to the slip length,

\lambda_{0}=-\mathcal{F}(h)-\beta,

(82)

where
\beta
is given by (
70
).

VI.4
Leading-order outer approximation in the interior domain

At this stage all that remains is to calculate the leading-order outer approximation in the interior domain,

w^{-}_{-1}
, and use (
80
) and (
82
) to calculate
\lambda_{0}
.

The field
w^{-}_{-1}
satisfies Laplace’s equation [cf. (
3
)], no-slip conditions at
x=0
and
y=-h
[cf. (
6
)], and the symmetry condition (
5
) at
x=1
. The flow is driven by the leading-order uniform exterior flow
w_{-1}^{+}\equiv 1
[cf. (
40
) and (
61
)] via continuity of velocity at the interface (
4a
), which yields

w^{-}_{-1}=1\quad\text{at}\quad y=0.

(83)

[The field
w_{-1}^{-}
must also satisfy a matching condition to the inner solution (
56b
) near the origin. A local analysis near the origin reveals, however, that the matching condition is trivially satisfied provided that
w_{-1}^{-}
remains bounded.] We thus have a well-posed problem governing
w^{-}_{-1}
for all values of the depth
h
.

We first consider the limiting case of infinitely deep grooves,
h=\infty
, for which the no-slip condition at
y=-h
is replaced by the decay condition

\lim_{y\to-\infty}w^{-}_{-1}=0.

(84)

The associated calculation of
w^{-}_{-1}
is carried out in Appendix
C.1
. It results in

\left.\frac{\partial w^{-}_{-1}}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}=\frac{1}{\sin(\pi x/2)},

(85)

which indeed blows up as
x\to 0
according to (
78
). Substitution of (
85
) into (
80
) yields

\mathcal{F}(\infty)=\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{4}{\pi},

(86)

whereby, from (
82
),

\lim_{h\to\infty}\lambda_{0}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln 4.

(87)

Thus, for
h=\infty
, (
62
) is refined to

\lambda\sim\mu^{-1/2}-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln 4\quad\text{for}\quad\mu\ll 1.

(88)

In Fig.
3
(a), we compare the leading-order approximation (
62
) and two-term approximation (
88
) with the slip length calculated using the numerical scheme. Figure
3
(b) shows the variation with
\mu
of the difference
\mu^{-1/2}-\lambda
, where
\lambda
is the numerically calculated slip length; it is evident that this difference indeed approaches (
87
).

Another limit of interest is that of shallow grooves,
h\ll 1
. Here the approximate solution
w^{-}_{-1}\sim 1+y/h
[analogous to (
19
)] is readily obtained by disregarding the no-slip ridge condition (
6
). At leading order in
h
we then find, from (
80
) and (
82
),

\lambda_{0}\sim-\frac{1}{h}\quad\text{for}\quad h\ll 1.

(89)

In principle, asymptotic corrections to
(
87
) and (
89
)
could be derived by perturbative analyses of the interior problem governing
w_{-1}^{-}
. In particular, the correction to the shallow-grooves approximation (
89
) could be obtained by analyzing the end region near
x=0

(
Cormack et al. 1974
;
Sen and Davis 1982
)
and its contribution to the integral (
80
). It turns out, however, that the problem for
w_{-1}^{-}
can be solved
exactly
.

The solution for arbitrary
h
is derived in Appendix
C.2
using a Schwarz–Christoffel mapping. It results in the regularized shear force

\mathcal{F}(h)=\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,K(k^{2})},

(90)

where
K
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind and the modulus
k
is given by the implicit relation

\frac{K(1-k^{2})}{K(k^{2})}=h.

(91)

Substitution of (
90
) into (
82
) finally yields

\lambda_{0}=\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,K(k^{2})}{2\pi},

(92)

and hence the two-term approximation for the slip length, valid for arbitrary (fixed)
h>0
, is

\lambda\sim\mu^{-1/2}+\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,K(k^{2})}{2\pi}\quad\text{as}\quad\mu\to 0,

(93)

where
k
depends on
h
via (
91
).

Revisiting the limits of deep and shallow grooves, we can expand (
92
) [with (
91
)] using the asymptotic behavior of the elliptic integral
(
Abramowitz and Stegun 1965
)
. For deep grooves, where
k\searrow 0
, we obtain

\lambda_{0}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln 4-\frac{8}{\pi}e^{-\pi h}+O(e^{-2\pi h})\quad\text{for}\quad h\gg 1

(94)

which provides a refinement of (
87
).
For shallow grooves, where
k\nearrow 1
, we obtain

\lambda_{0}=-\frac{1}{h}+\frac{2}{\pi}\ln\frac{1}{h}+O(e^{-\pi/h})\quad\text{for}\quad h\ll 1,

(95)

which provides a refinement of (
89
).

The variation with
h
of
-\lambda_{0}
is shown in Fig.
4
. Unsurprisingly, the agreement between the asymptotic and numerical results improves as
\mu
diminishes. We note, however, that the asymptotic scheme fails as
h
becomes small, even for small values of
\mu
. This failure motivates a careful look at the scenario where both
\mu
and
h
are small.

VII
Shallow grooves: Distinguished limit and slip length

According to (
95
), the correction
\lambda_{0}
in the slip-length expansion (
64
) grows in magnitude as
h
decreases. Hence, the expansion breaks down for
h
on the order of
\mu^{1/2}

VIII
Concluding remarks

We have investigated the canonical problem of longitudinal shear over a liquid-infused grating formed of infinitely thin ridges. With lengths normalized by the grating half-period, the slip length
\lambda
depends upon two dimensionless parameters: the interior-to-exterior viscosity ratio
\mu
and the groove depth
h
.
This assumes that the ridges are infinitely thin, that the liquid-liquid interfaces are flat and pinned to the ridge tips, and that the flow is laminar. (Since the flow is unidirectional, the inertial terms trivially vanish for any Reynolds number
(
Batchelor 1967
)
.) Our analysis has focused on the nearly-inviscid limit
\mu\ll 1
, which is singular in the above zero-solid-fraction scenario. In that limit, we have shown that
\lambda
possesses the asymptotic expansion

\lambda\sim\mu^{-1/2}+\lambda_{0}(h)\quad\text{as}\quad\mu\searrow 0,

(112)

where the
\ord{(1)}
correction
\lambda_{0}(h)
is provided by (
92
) in terms of an elliptic integral. Its small-
h
behavior (
95
) reveals a breakdown in the asymptotic expansion when
h
becomes comparable to
\mu^{1/2}
. Proceeding with the distinguished shallow-grooves limit of
\mu\ll 1
and
H=h/\mu^{1/2}
fixed, we have obtained the closed-form two-term approximation (
109
), which again starts at
\ord(\mu^{-1/2})
but now with the leading term depending upon
H
. For
H\ll 1
, namely, for extremely shallow grooves, this leading term reduces to
h/\mu
, revealing a transition of the scaling of
\lambda
with
\mu
across the distinguished regime. We have found excellent agreement between our asymptotic approximations and numerical solutions based upon a boundary-integral formulation.

As discussed in the Introduction, the
\mu^{-1/2}
scaling of the effective slip length is at odds with both intuitive scaling arguments and previous heuristic models; these predict the scaling
\mu^{-1}
, which, as noted above, is only valid for extremely shallow grooves. Our analysis reveals that the
\mu^{-1/2}
scaling arises from a unique asymptotic structure where the velocity of both liquids varies rapidly in exponentially small regions about the tips of the infinitely thin ridges. In particular, these regions resolve the incompatibility between a uniform stream in the exterior domain — associated with an asymptotically large effective slip length and a nearly inviscid lubricant — and the need to satisfy a no-slip condition at the tips. For
\mu\ll 1
, the net viscous force supported by the liquid-liquid interface, which balances the imposed far-field shear, is dominated by the contribution from these exponentially small regions. This contradicts the naive scaling argument mentioned in the Introduction, where the viscous force was associated with a geometric length scale, independent of
\mu
. As the groove depth
h
is decreased, however, the contribution to the net viscous force from the groove scale becomes increasingly important. In fact, the distinguished limit where
H=h/\mu^{1/2}
is held fixed corresponds to the regime where the contributions from the exponential tip regions and the groove scales become comparable. Only for extremely shallow grooves,
H\ll 1
, does the contribution from the groove scale become dominant and the intuitive
\mu^{-1}
scaling for the effective slip length is recovered.

Admittedly, the leading-order role played by exponentially small regions near the ridge tips suggests that the theory developed herein for infinitely thin ridges and a nearly inviscid lubricant may be sensitive to perturbations. In particular, it is clear from our analysis that even an exceedingly small solid fraction
\epsilon
— exponentially small in
\mu
— may induce a leading-order effect by modifying the inner problem governing those regions. More specifically, we expect finite-solid-fraction effects to become important once the ridge thickness is comparable to the exponential length scale set by the small viscosity ratio, corresponding to the condition that
\mu^{1/2}\ln\epsilon
is of order unity. In that distinguished limit, the order of magnitude of the effective slip length must still be
\mu^{-1/2}
. At larger (but still small) solid fractions, we expect a transition to the classical
\ln\epsilon
scaling for an inviscid lubricant
(
Philip 1972b
)
. Finally, for non-small solid fractions, the nearly inviscid limit ceases to be singular; indeed, that scenario was studied by
Crowdy 2017
via a regular perturbation expansion.

Another important perturbation involves our assumption that the liquid-liquid interface is pinned to the ridge tips: in some scenarios, the interface may instead lie slightly above or below them. Similar to the small-solid-fraction scenario, our analysis for infinitely thin ridges and a nearly inviscid lubricant hints to a leading-order effect once the vertical displacement of the interface becomes comparable to the exponentially small scale set by the lubricant viscosity. The case where the interface lies above the microstructure is primarily relevant to liquid-infused surfaces (SLIPS) in an “encapsulated state,” which is known to facilitate high drop mobility
(
Smith et al. 2013
)
. In that case, the limit of an inviscid lubricant is singular even for a finite solid fraction, though it is intuitive that the scaling of the effective slip length would become more singular at sufficiently small solid fraction. The alternative case, where the exterior liquid partially invades the grooves, is relevant to superhydrophobic surfaces in the scenario where the liquid-gas menisci are depinned owing to a sufficiently high liquid pressure. In the latter scenario, the protrusion of the ridges into the liquid renders the inviscid-lubricant limit regular even for infinitely thin ridges
(
Crowdy 2021
)
; the pertinent singular limit is then that of a small invasion depth
(
Yariv 2023a
;
Yariv 2023b
;
Yariv 2024
)
.

Beyond addressing the singular perturbations discussed above, it would be valuable to extend the present theory to other microstructure geometries and flow configurations. With regards to the grating geometry, preliminary analysis suggests that the asymptotic structure of the transverse problem is similar to that found here for the longitudinal problem. However, a detailed analysis would entail solving more complex variants of the reduced Stokes-flow problems associated with this asymptotic structure (e.g., the local analysis near the tip ridges, and the driven-cavity problem) — now governed by the biharmonic equation instead of Laplace’s equation. Last, we comment on another common microstructure geometry: a doubly periodic array of posts protruding from a flat solid substrate. In contrast to the grating geometry, a finite lubricant viscosity does
not
regularize here the effective slip length problem at zero solid fraction (i.e., infinitely thin posts). Accordingly, it would not make sense to attempt the limit process considered in this paper, of small lubricant viscosity at zero solid fraction. Instead, it would be of interest to study the asymptotic regime where both the solid fraction and the lubricant viscosity are small. This would constitute a generalization of preceding small-solid-fraction analyses of the posts configuration which were limited to an inviscid lubricant
(
Davis and Lauga 2010
;
Schnitzer and Yariv 2018a
)
.

A.2
Discretization

In order to solve the equation (

. There is no dependence on

We use an exponentially stretched grid to resolve the small scale near the ridge tip

, such that

to

in approximately uniform steps of

Having divided the interval

into cells, we sample

,

and

at the cell midpoints. The integrals in (

), we remove the logarithmic singularity in

at

using the integral

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\left[\left(\frac{4}{\pi(x-x^{\prime})}\right)^{2}\right]\,q(x)\,dx^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\pi}\left[1+\ln\frac{4}{\pi}-x\ln x-(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right]q(x).

(128)

By subtracting the integrand in (
128

121a
), and then adding back the right-hand side of (

in terms of

where the integrand is bounded, resulting in second-order accuracy when evaluated using the midpoint rule. In order to further increase accuracy for

close to
0
or

where the logarithmic singularity at

or

is close to (albeit still outside of) the range of integration, we also perform the corresponding removal procedure for those two singularities (regardless of the value of

).

The derivative in (

x
at the cell boundaries. We continue to evaluate the integral using the midpoint rule, i.e., with
x^{\prime}
at the cell midpoints. Due to

being staggered in this way, direct evaluation of the integral results in the Cauchy principal value being obtained automatically for the singularity at

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left[\cot\frac{\pi(x-x^{\prime})}{2}+\cot\frac{\pi(x+x^{\prime})}{2}\right]w(x,0)\,dx^{\prime}=0.

(129)

This results in an integral of unchanged value but now finite integrand, whose numerical evaluation is again accurate to second order.

As a result of (
128

For small

where
