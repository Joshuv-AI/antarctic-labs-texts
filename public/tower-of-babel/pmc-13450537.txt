A clinically contextualized, hands-on biostatistics workshop for rheumatology fellows-in-training: a quasi-experimental pre–post educational intervention study
Authors: Ertugrul Cagri Bolek, Rabia Deniz, Ali Ekin, Fatih Yildirim, Aysenur Yilmaz, Cemal Bes, Nilufer Alpay Kanitez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiostatistics and statistical literacy are essential competencies in postgraduate medical education, yet structured, clinically relevant training remains limited in many subspecialty settings. Evidence in postgraduate rheumatology biostatistics education is particularly scarce. This study evaluated the educational impact of a hands-on introductory biostatistics workshop delivered to rheumatology fellows using clinically contextualized examples relevant to rheumatology practice and research.MethodsThis was a single-group pre-test/post-test quasi-experimental educational study with post-course evaluation and qualitative feedback analysis. The workshop was designed for rheumatology fellows and early-career rheumatologists and focused on clinically contextualized, applied biostatistics. Objective knowledge was assessed using a 15-item dichotomously scored test (score range 0–15), and self-efficacy was assessed using an 8-item Likert-type scale (score range 8–40). Matched pre–post changes in objective knowledge and self-efficacy were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Course ratings were summarized descriptively, and open-ended responses were analyzed using descriptive content analysis.ResultsA total of 48 participants attended the workshop; 30 completed the pre-test, 41 completed the post-test, and 23 completed both assessments. Objective knowledge scores were significantly higher after the workshop, with the median increasing from 6.0 to 12.0 and the mean from 6.3 ± 3.5 to 10.0 ± 3.4 (p < 0.001; Z = -3.74; r = 0.82). Total self-efficacy scores were also significantly higher, with the median increasing from 14.0 to 31.0 and the mean from 18.4 ± 10.6 to 30.0 ± 8.0 (p < 0.001; Z = -4.11; r = 0.88). Post-course evaluations were favorable, with the highest ratings for preference for practical training (mean 4.5 ± 0.9) and overall satisfaction (mean 4.3 ± 1.1). Qualitative feedback emphasized hands-on learning, interactive teaching, instructor support, the need for more practice, and interest in advanced follow-up content.ConclusionsThis hands-on, clinically contextualized introductory biostatistics workshop was well received and associated with significant short-term improvements in objective knowledge and statistical self-efficacy, suggesting that this approach may be feasible and acceptable as an entry point for postgraduate rheumatology biostatistics training. Background

Biostatistics and statistical literacy are essential competencies in modern postgraduate medical education, underpinning evidence-based clinical practice, critical appraisal of research, and informed decision-making [1–5]. As the complexity of statistical methods in the medical literature continues to increase, the ability to interpret data has become increasingly important for clinicians to provide high-quality care, critically appraise evidence, and interpret claims in the medical literature and related information sources [1, 6]. However, widespread deficiencies in statistical literacy persist among medical trainees across various specialties, leading to poor interpretation of research, reduced confidence in engaging with the scientific literature, and important barriers to academic productivity [1–5].

Despite the recognized importance of these skills, many residents and fellows report a lack of structured, formal training during their subspecialty education [2–5, 7]. Objective assessments consistently reveal low proficiency in basic statistical concepts, with mean scores typically ranging from 41% to 55% [2, 3, 7]. Furthermore, a significant challenge in medical education is the “metacognitive gap”, a misalignment where perceived confidence or seniority does not necessarily correlate with objective performance [7–9]. This discrepancy suggests that educational interventions must address not only knowledge acquisition but also self-efficacy and the calibration of professional confidence [8, 9].

Traditional didactic lectures often fall short of addressing these needs [6, 10, 11]. Indeed, more than 95% of medical students prefer practical statistics teaching tailored to their specific field of study for future curricula [10]. Current evidence suggests that applied, hands-on, and context-specific educational models can improve learners’ objective knowledge of biostatistics, statistical self-efficacy, and overall satisfaction [6, 10–13]. For subspecialty trainees, particularly in certain clinical disciplines such as oncology, specialty-tailored workshops that link didactic content with interactive discussions of discipline-relevant clinical data and publications have been shown to enhance perceived relevance, engagement, and educational value [10, 13, 14].

Despite promising findings from applied educational models, there is a substantial gap in the literature on postgraduate subspecialty training, particularly in data-intensive fields such as rheumatology. In this study, by examining a clinically contextualized, hands-on biostatistics teaching model in a postgraduate rheumatology training setting, we address an underexplored area in the literature. It may inform the development of similar educational interventions in other specialty contexts.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study evaluated the short-term educational outcomes of a rheumatology-oriented, hands-on introductory biostatistics workshop at the ‘Annual Fellow Course’ [15] delivered as part of the Turkish Society for Rheumatology (TSR) [16]. Therefore, the study setting was not a single institutional training program but a society-based postgraduate educational course embedded in the broader rheumatology training context. Rheumatology subspecialty training in Turkiye is conducted within accredited training institutions in accordance with national subspecialty training requirements. At the same time, exposure to formal research methods, critical appraisal, and applied biostatistics may vary across institutions and individual training pathways.

The workshop targeted rheumatology fellows and early-career rheumatologists/specialists and was developed to address practical statistical skills needed in routine academic and clinical research. All workshop attendees were invited to participate in the educational evaluation. The pre-test form was made available before the workshop, and the post-test form was made available immediately after. Participation in the evaluation was voluntary and was not required for course attendance or completion. Participants were informed that non-participation would not affect their course participation, training evaluation, professional standing, or relationship with the instructors.

The intervention was designed as a single-group pre-test/post-test quasi-experimental educational study, with additional post-course reaction evaluation and qualitative feedback analysis. NAK coordinated the course with design and delivery provided by five instructors (ECB, RD, AE, FY, and AY). The faculty composition was intentionally balanced to include early-career academic rheumatologists (ECB and AE), recently qualified rheumatologists working in mandatory medical service for the government (RD and FY), and a rheumatology fellow (AY), thereby combining academic, practical, and near-peer educational perspectives.

The workshop was delivered in two consecutive sessions on the same day, totaling 3 h. Teaching methods included brief didactic explanations, live demonstrations, guided hands-on practice, and interactive discussions of rheumatology-relevant clinical and research examples around predefined learning objectives. The content covered study designs, variable classification, data entry and coding, normality assessment, descriptive statistics, selection of appropriate statistical tests for two-group comparisons (Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, and chi-square test), and interpretation of p-values and confidence intervals. The slide sets and practical materials were shared before the session. Participants were encouraged to follow the practical exercises on their own computers and perform the same analytic steps simultaneously on the practice datasets used during the session.

The primary purpose was to assess short-term educational outcomes in terms of perceived competence and objective learning, while also generating implementation feedback as part of a first-cycle, needs-informed curriculum development process to refine the structure, content, and level of future iterations of this newly developed course. To enhance reporting transparency, the educational intervention and its evaluation were described with attention to key reporting elements (GREET; [17]) for educational interventions, including the learners, faculty, content, delivery, outcome measures, and analytic approach.

Evaluation

Evaluation

Data Collection: Data were collected using digitally administered pre-test and post-test forms in Turkish. The original instruments were developed and administered in Turkish; the English versions provided in the Supplementary Appendix I are author-translated versions for international readers and were not used for data collection.

The evaluation framework comprised four domains: (1) information and consent, (2) self-efficacy, (3) objective knowledge, and (4) course evaluation. The pre-test form included participant information/consent, self-efficacy items, and the knowledge test. The post-test form included repeated self-efficacy items, the repeated knowledge test, course evaluation items, and open-ended feedback prompts. A self-generated participant code was collected in both the pre-test and post-test forms to enable matching of responses across the two assessments; the analytical dataset was de-identified before analysis.

b.Objective Knowledge Assessment: The knowledge test consisted of 15 dichotomously scored items (total score range 0–15), including 5 concept-based questions and 10 clinical vignette-based questions specifically contextualized to rheumatology practice. All items were written collaboratively by the instructor team, distributed according to a blueprint aligned with the course learning objectives, reviewed by all instructors for relevance and clarity, and completed by the instructors themselves before implementation as a practical pre-administration check. Each correct answer was scored as 1, and each incorrect answer as 0, yielding participant-level pre-test and post-test total knowledge scores.

Secondary outcomes included item-level correct response rates, post-course satisfaction ratings, and themes derived from open-ended feedback.

For analysis purposes, participants were categorized according to response availability: Group A included participants with matched pre-test and post-test data and was used for the primary pre-post learning analyses; Group B included participants with pre-test data only and was used for attrition analysis; and Group C included participants contributing post-course reaction/evaluation data without matched paired data and was used, together with Group A where appropriate, for post-course satisfaction analyses and selected post-test psychometric summaries. No separate control group was used.

A pilot administration was not conducted before the workshop because the instruments were locally developed for this course. However, the items were iteratively reviewed by the instructor panel for clarity, relevance, and alignment with the intended course content before implementation. We sought preliminary evidence of validity, focusing on content, response process, internal structure, and relations to other variables, in line with the contemporary validity frameworks of Cook and Beckman for educational measurement [18].

Response process evidence was strengthened by using the same digital platform, the same instructions, and the same time conditions at both administrations. To minimize priming, recall, and within-test learning effects, the same knowledge items were used in the pre-test and post-test, but the order of questions and response options was randomized at the post-test.

An exploratory item analysis was performed for the 15-item knowledge test. Item difficulty was defined as the proportion of correct responses among participants with valid responses, and item discrimination was evaluated using the corrected item-total correlation. Because the same items were administered at both time points, item performance was examined separately for the pre-test and post-test administrations. Pre-test discrimination was considered particularly informative because baseline knowledge was expected to be more heterogeneous and less affected by ceiling effects. In contrast, post-test item difficulty was examined mainly to identify content areas that became easier following the workshop.

Open-ended responses from the post-test questionnaire were analyzed using a descriptive content analysis approach. Two investigators independently reviewed all open-ended responses, generated initial codes, compared coding decisions, and developed a consensus-based coding framework through discussion. Coding was primarily inductive, and disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Similar codes were grouped into broader categories and themes that reflected the workshop’s perceived strengths and suggestions for future improvement. Representative quotations were selected for reporting; Turkish quotations were retained in the original language, and English translations were prepared by the authors (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 in Supplementary Appendix II).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted as a minimal-risk educational evaluation embedded within a structured rheumatology training activity. Ethical approval was obtained from the Gazi University Ethics Commission (Approval No: 2026 − 700). Before participation, attendees were verbally informed about the purpose of the evaluation and provided written information in the digital pre-test and post-test forms. Only those who indicated informed consent proceeded to the questionnaires. Participation was voluntary, and participants were explicitly informed that non-participation would not affect their course attendance, training evaluation, professional standing, or relationship with the instructors. Data were de-identified, collected, and stored in digital files before analysis and reporting.

Statistics

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range: IQR), depending on distributional characteristics; categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages). Normality was assessed using analytical (Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests) and visual methods, including histograms and Q–Q plots.

For the primary pre-post comparisons among matched participants (Group A), changes in total knowledge and self-efficacy scores were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, given the small matched sample size and the distributional characteristics of the paired differences. For Wilcoxon signed-rank analyses, the effect size was calculated as r = |Z|/√N, where N denotes the number of non-zero paired observations.

Associations between knowledge and self-efficacy measures were examined using Spearman correlation analysis, with 95% confidence intervals reported for correlation coefficients. As a sensitivity/attrition analysis, baseline pre-test knowledge scores and pre-training total self-efficacy were compared between matched completers (Group A) and pre-test-only participants (Group B) using the Mann–Whitney U test to assess potential attrition bias.

Item-level pre-post changes in the dichotomously scored knowledge test were examined among matched participants using McNemar exact tests, with Holm correction applied where relevant to account for the exploratory nature of multiple item-level comparisons.

Internal consistency of the 8-item self-efficacy scale was assessed separately for pre-test and post-test administrations using Cronbach’s alpha. For the dichotomously scored 15-item knowledge test, internal consistency was assessed separately at each time point using the Kuder–Richardson 20 (KR-20) coefficient. Factor analysis was not performed because the sample size was limited and the study was not designed for formal psychometric validation.

As an exploratory subgroup analysis, knowledge gain and self-efficacy gain were compared between first-year fellows and participants at other training levels using the Mann–Whitney U test. As an additional exploratory analysis, Hake’s normalized gain was calculated for each matched participant as g = (post-test knowledge score − pre-test knowledge score) / (15 − pre-test knowledge score) and summarized descriptively [19, 20].

Post-course satisfaction items were analyzed descriptively, with Likert-type responses summarized using measures of central tendency and dispersion and, where helpful, frequency distributions. Open-ended responses were analyzed qualitatively as described above. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Participant flow and analytic samples

A total of 48 participants attended the workshop. Of these, 30 completed the pre-test, and 41 completed the post-test. Twenty-three participants completed both assessments and constituted the matched analytic sample used for the primary paired pre-post analyses (Group A). Seven participants completed only the pre-test (Group B), and 18 contributed post-test data without matched pre-test data (Group C). Thus, post-course evaluation analyses were based on all available post-test respondents (Groups A and C, n = 41), whereas primary paired analyses were restricted to the matched sample (n = 23) (Fig. 1). Reasons for incomplete or unmatched assessments were not systematically recorded. In this real-world course setting, differences in pre-test and post-test completion may have reflected logistical factors such as late arrival, incomplete form submission, or inconsistent self-generated participant codes.

Primary educational outcomes

Among matched participants, objective knowledge scores increased significantly after the workshop (Table 2; Fig. 2). The median (IQR) knowledge score increased from 6.0 (6.0) at pre-test to 12.0 (6.0) at post-test, and this change was statistically significant by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Z = -3.74, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (r = 0.82).

Total self-efficacy scores were also significantly higher following the intervention. In the matched sample, the median (IQR) total self-efficacy score increased from 14.0 (21.0) to 31.0 (14.0), and the improvement was statistically significant (Z = -4.11, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (r = 0.88). Item-level self-efficacy metrics showed the same pattern: both the mean and median item scores increased significantly from pre-test to post-test (p < 0.001 for both), with similarly large effect sizes of r = 0.88 (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). For completeness, all available descriptive statistics for pre-test and post-test knowledge and self-efficacy outcomes are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Post-course evaluation and learner perceptions

Post-course evaluation ratings were generally favorable across all six evaluation domains (Table 3). Mean ratings ranged from 3.9 to 4.5 on a 5-point scale. The highest-rated domain was preference for practical training (mean 4.5 ± 0.9), followed by overall satisfaction/recommendation to colleagues (mean 4.3 ± 1.1) and instructor competence and teaching skills (mean 4.2 ± 0.9). Content quality and relevance, duration and timing, and training materials were also rated positively, with a mean rating of 3.9, 4.0, and 4.0, respectively. Figure 3 illustrates that ratings were concentrated toward the higher end of the scale across domains.

Among 41 eligible participants from Groups A and C, 19 (46.3%) responded to at least one open-ended question; 19/41 (46.3%) answered the first question, and 15/41 (36.6%) answered the second. Because only a small number of Group C participants provided open-ended responses, subgroup comparison was not considered appropriate; therefore, content analysis was conducted on the combined responses from Groups A and C. Qualitative feedback highlighted four main themes: (1) practical, hands-on learning was perceived as the most valuable component of the workshop; (2) the interactive teaching format and immediate instructor support were seen as important facilitators of learning; (3) participants expressed a need for more practice and stronger reinforcement of core concepts, particularly in linking research questions, variable types, and test selection; and (4) several respondents suggested that future workshops should expand toward more advanced statistical topics and next-level training (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 in Supplementary Appendix II).

Reliability and supporting measurement findings

Internal consistency of the 8-item self-efficacy scale was excellent at both administrations (Cronbach’s α = 0.97 at pre-test and 0.95 at post-test). Internal consistency of the 15-item dichotomously scored objective knowledge test was acceptable at both time points (KR-20 = 0.74 at pre-test and 0.76 at post-test) (Supplementary Table 4).

Exploratory item analysis of the knowledge test is presented in Supplementary Table 5. In general, most items became easier at the post-test, consistent with learning following the intervention. Discrimination indices were more variable, with several items showing acceptable-to-good corrected item-total correlations. In contrast, some items showed weak or negative post-test discrimination, likely reflecting reduced heterogeneity and possible ceiling effects after training. At the item level, McNemar exact tests showed nominally significant pre-post changes for Items 5, 6, 10, and 13; however, after Holm correction for multiple comparisons, only Item 13 remained statistically significant (Supplementary Table 6).

Exploratory analyses

Exploratory correlation analyses showed that the post-training total self-efficacy score was positively associated with the post-training knowledge score among post-test respondents (Spearman ρ = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.48 to 0.82, p < 0.001). Baseline total self-efficacy score was also positively associated with baseline knowledge among pre-test respondents (ρ = 0.74, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.87, p < 0.001). In the matched sample, baseline knowledge score showed a negative association with knowledge gain (ρ = -0.42, 95% CI -0.71 to 0.01, p = 0.04). In contrast, the association between gain in self-efficacy score and gain in knowledge was positive but did not reach statistical significance (ρ = 0.39, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.70, p = 0.07; Supplementary Tables 7 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Gain scores were calculated as post-test minus pre-test values. In addition, Hake’s normalized gain analysis suggested a moderate overall gain in objective knowledge among matched participants (mean g = 0.41; median = 0.44), with positive gain observed in 19 of 23 participants (82.6%) (Supplementary Table 8).

An attrition analysis comparing matched completers (Group A) with pre-test-only participants (Group B) showed no significant baseline differences in pre-training knowledge (p = 0.95) or pre-training total self-efficacy (p = 0.32), providing no clear evidence of marked attrition bias in the measured learning variables. However, this analysis was limited by the small number of pre-test-only participants, the lack of baseline data for post-test-only participants (Group C), and the absence of systematically recorded reasons for incomplete assessments; therefore, residual attrition or selection bias cannot be excluded (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Exploratory subgroup analyses by training year did not identify significant differences between first-year fellows and participants at other training levels for either knowledge gain (p = 0.86) or total self-efficacy gain (p = 0.30) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental pre–post study, a hands-on introductory biostatistics workshop delivered to rheumatology trainees was associated with significant improvements in objective knowledge and statistical self-efficacy among matched participants. Favorable post-course evaluations and qualitative feedback further supported the value of hands-on, interactive teaching. These findings suggest that, within the context of a short-term real-world educational evaluation, a clinically contextualized workshop may help address an important educational need in postgraduate rheumatology training and may inform the design of similar curricula in other specialty settings [13, 21, 22]. This study should therefore be interpreted as a first-cycle, real-world educational evaluation of a newly implemented workshop, rather than as a definitive controlled trial of educational effectiveness.

The observed improvement in objective knowledge is consistent with prior literature showing that applied, interactive, and clinically integrated teaching formats can improve knowledge and research-related skills in health professions education, particularly when linked to authentic clinical problems [13, 21, 23]. Our findings also align with smaller pre–post workshop studies suggesting that even brief, focused interventions can produce measurable short-term gains in understanding core statistical concepts [11]. Together, these findings support the educational value of teaching introductory biostatistics through a clinically contextualized format rather than as an abstract, stand-alone subject.

When situating our findings within the broader medical education literature, we note that previous studies have evaluated diverse approaches to teaching biostatistics, evidence-based practice, and research methodology, including didactic sessions, journal clubs, online modules, and interactive workshops [21, 23]. Systematic overviews suggest that multifaceted, interactive, and clinically integrated approaches are more likely to improve learners’ knowledge, skills, and engagement than isolated or purely didactic teaching formats [21, 23]. However, generic statistics teaching may be perceived as abstract or insufficiently connected to learners’ clinical and scholarly work, thereby limiting engagement and perceived relevance [1, 22]. Our study extends this literature by evaluating a clinically contextualized, hands-on workshop embedded within a postgraduate rheumatology training course. This approach is consistent with targeted interventions in other specialty settings, such as clinical oncology, where integrating biostatistics and research-methods teaching with discipline-relevant literature and clinical examples enhanced trainees’ perceived relevance and engagement [13]. Compared with many workshop evaluations that rely primarily on satisfaction or self-reported confidence, our study combined objective knowledge testing, self-efficacy assessment, post-course evaluation, qualitative feedback, and preliminary measurement analyses [24]. These findings suggest that linking introductory statistical concepts to clinically familiar examples may help bridge the gap between abstract statistical theory and authentic postgraduate clinical research practice.

The improvement in self-efficacy is also consistent with prior work showing that practical and experiential training can strengthen confidence in using research methods and statistics, particularly when learners actively apply concepts [24–26]. In our study, increased self-efficacy was accompanied by favorable perceptions of the course structure and teaching approach, suggesting an effect on both competence-related beliefs and the immediate learning experience. This matters educationally because self-efficacy may influence whether trainees engage with research questions, seek appropriate statistical support, and approach academic tasks with greater willingness and persistence [8, 9, 25, 26].

Our exploratory correlation findings support a more nuanced interpretation of self-efficacy. Although self-efficacy and knowledge were positively associated at baseline and post-training, changes in self-efficacy did not significantly track changes in knowledge. This pattern is consistent with broader medical education literature cautioning that self-reported confidence should not be treated as a direct surrogate for competence, particularly immediately after an intervention, when responses may be influenced by social desirability, satisfaction with the learning experience, or immediate enthusiasm [8, 24, 27]. It reinforces the value of combining objective testing with self-efficacy measures and suggests that increased confidence may still require reinforcement through continued practice and feedback.

The post-course evaluation and qualitative findings further support the workshop’s educational value. Participants rated the workshop positively across all domains, particularly the practice-oriented format and willingness to recommend it to colleagues. The qualitative themes closely mirrored these findings: trainees valued hands-on work, stepwise guidance, and an interactive teaching environment, while also requesting more practice and more advanced follow-up content. This pattern is consistent with prior literature indicating that learners in postgraduate and specialty training prefer applied, interactive, and context-specific teaching over lecture-dominant formats [13, 22]. It also supports the broader educational principle that relevance matters: when learners perceive direct connections between the curriculum and the clinical or scholarly problems they face in their own field, engagement and perceived value tend to increase. This clinically contextualized approach is consistent with adult learning and experiential learning theories, including Knowles’ andragogical principles and Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, which collectively emphasize relevance to learners’ professional roles, problem-centered learning, active experimentation, and application to authentic tasks [28–34].

The main contribution of the present study is not that hands-on or clinically contextualized teaching is novel in itself, but that this approach was implemented and evaluated within a postgraduate rheumatology training setting, where applied biostatistics education remains underrepresented. Although the introductory statistical concepts taught in the workshop are broadly transferable across specialties, the intervention was delivered to a rheumatology trainee audience using examples and scenarios relevant to their clinical and research context [13, 22]. Because the study was conducted within the Turkish rheumatology fellowship course context, generalization to other settings should be cautious; nevertheless, the underlying educational model, brief didactic input combined with guided hands-on practice and clinically contextualized examples, may be adaptable to rheumatology or other subspecialty training settings if local training structures, learner baseline experience, and curricular priorities are considered.

Much of the rheumatology education literature has focused on clinical skills, procedures, patient involvement, or broader competency-based training, rather than on instruction in applied biostatistics or research methods [35–37]. In addition, substantial heterogeneity in rheumatology training structures across settings suggests that exposure to research methods and biostatistics may vary considerably between programs [38, 39]. Against this background, our study helps address a small but important gap by providing data from a rheumatology-focused cohort in which objective knowledge, self-efficacy, learner reaction, and qualitative feedback were assessed together.

The study also has practical implications for curriculum development. Our findings support the feasibility and perceived usefulness of integrating clinically contextualized introductory biostatistics training into rheumatology fellowship education, while recognizing that evidence of sustained competence, behavioral change, and transfer to scholarly practice requires longitudinal evaluation. However, the combination of favorable evaluations and requests for further practice suggests that a single workshop is better viewed as an entry point than a complete curriculum. Consistent with prior literature, future iterations may benefit from reinforcement, longitudinal integration, repeated case-based exercises, supervised application to trainee projects, and follow-up modules on more advanced topics [22, 24]. Future evaluations could include delayed follow-up assessments, for example, at 3–6 months, to examine knowledge retention, sustained self-efficacy, and transfer of biostatistical learning to trainees’ own research activities. Longer-term curriculum development may also include sequential modules progressing from basic test selection and interpretation to multivariable modeling, critical appraisal of published studies, and supervised analysis of trainee-led research projects. Beyond rheumatology, this evaluation may be relevant to other subspecialty training programs that face similar challenges in integrating applied biostatistics, critical appraisal, and research methods teaching within limited curricular time.

Our exploratory analyses provide additional, albeit cautious, educational insight. Lower baseline knowledge was associated with greater subsequent gain, although this may partly reflect mathematical coupling and regression to the mean. At the item level, several questions became easier after training, while some post-test discrimination indices weakened, likely because improved overall performance reduced score variability. McNemar analyses were broadly consistent with this pattern: several items showed nominal improvement, but only one remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Overall, these findings suggest that the knowledge test was sensitive to learning and identified items that may warrant future refinement.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-group quasi-experimental pre–post design without a control group limits causal inference. It does not exclude alternative explanations for the observed improvements, including test familiarity, maturation, selection effects, and other unmeasured contextual factors [21, 23, 40]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as short-term changes associated with participation in the workshop rather than as definitive causal evidence of the intervention’s effectiveness. Second, the matched analytic sample was modest in size, limiting precision, subgroup inference, and generalizability. At the same time, the sample size reflects the practical challenges of evaluating educational interventions among subspecialty fellows, who represent a smaller and less readily accessible postgraduate learner population than undergraduate medical students or broader resident cohorts. Third, outcomes were assessed only immediately after the intervention, precluding conclusions about knowledge retention, the durability of self-efficacy gains, or the longer-term transfer of biostatistical skills to research practice. Immediate post-intervention gains may attenuate without continued reinforcement, and future studies should include delayed follow-up assessments to evaluate sustained competence over time [41, 42]. Fourth, although the study incorporated both objective and subjective measures, the instruments were locally developed for this course and were not intended as fully validated psychometric tools. In addition, self-efficacy is a subjective measure and may be influenced by social desirability, immediate enthusiasm, or satisfaction with the workshop; therefore, it should not be interpreted as evidence of durable competence. At the same time, preliminary evidence of validity was sought, and internal consistency was acceptable to excellent; further validation in larger samples would strengthen interpretability. Finally, participation depended on completing the survey. Although the attrition analysis did not suggest marked baseline imbalance in measured learning variables between matched completers and pre-test-only participants, only 23 of 48 workshop attendees completed both assessments. This limits the representativeness of the matched analytic sample. Reasons for incomplete or unmatched assessments were not systematically recorded and may have included logistical factors such as late arrival, incomplete form submission, or inconsistent self-generated participant codes. In addition, the number of pre-test-only participants was small, and baseline data were unavailable for the post-test-only group (Group C). Therefore, residual attrition and selection bias cannot be excluded. The same knowledge test was administered at both time points. Although item order and response option order were randomized at post-test to reduce recall effects, familiarity with the items may still have contributed to some of the observed improvement. We also did not systematically collect information on participants’ prior exposure to biostatistics training, previous research methods coursework, experience with statistical software, or prior publication record. These unmeasured baseline differences may have influenced initial knowledge, self-efficacy, and the magnitude of observed gains.

Despite these limitations, the study also has notable strengths. It evaluated a real-world educational intervention in an underexamined rheumatology training setting, used matched pre–post analysis for the primary outcomes, combined objective knowledge assessment with self-efficacy measurement, and incorporated both quantitative and qualitative learner feedback. This mixed evaluation framework provides a richer understanding of educational impact than reliance on satisfaction measures alone. It may be particularly useful for early-stage curriculum development in postgraduate medical education.

Overall, our findings suggest that a hands-on introductory biostatistics workshop delivered to rheumatology trainees through clinically contextualized examples was associated with short-term improvements in both objective knowledge and statistical self-efficacy and was well received by learners. These findings support further development of clinically contextualized biostatistics teaching in rheumatology training and suggest that future studies should use longitudinal, multi-session, and preferably controlled designs to assess retention, transfer to scholarly practice, and the relationship between confidence and sustained competence.

Conclusions

A hands-on introductory biostatistics workshop delivered to rheumatology trainees through clinically contextualized examples was associated with significant immediate improvements in both objective knowledge and statistical self-efficacy. As a first-cycle educational evaluation, these findings suggest that this approach may be feasible and acceptable as an entry point for postgraduate biostatistics training in rheumatology. Future longitudinal studies using larger samples and controlled designs are needed to evaluate knowledge retention, transfer to research practice, generalizability across training settings, and longer-term educational impact.
