Efficacy and safety of ciprofol for general anesthesia induction in female patients with frailty: a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yu Wei, Lijie Jia, Shiping Cheng, Wei Ma, Xiaohu An, Zifeng Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCiprofol, a recently developed anesthetic agent, is not inferior to propofol in terms of efficacy and safety. However, most previous clinical experience with ciprofol was based on a robust population. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in female patients with frailty.MethodsThis prospective randomized controlled trial included patients with frailty undergoing elective general anesthesia for gynecological surgery. Frailty was assessed using the modified frailty index. The patients were randomly administered propofol (2 mg/kg) or ciprofol (0.5 mg/kg) during anesthesia induction. The depth of anaesthesia was continually monitored by the bispectral index. The primary outcome was the lowest systolic blood pressure (SBP) during anesthesia induction. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of general anesthesia induction failure and adverse events.ResultsAmong the 69 enrolled patients with frailty, 67 were included in the final analysis. The success rate of anesthesia induction was 100% in both groups. The lowest SBP was significantly higher in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group (103 [96–110] vs. 90 [85–98] mmHg, respectively; P < 0.001), suggesting that the former caused less inhibition of hemodynamics during anesthesia induction in patients with frailty. The incidence of injection pain was lower in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group (3 [8.8%] vs. 11 patients [33.3%], respectively; P = 0.014).ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that the efficacy of ciprofol for inducing general anesthesia in patients with frailty is comparable to that of propofol, with more stable hemodynamics.Trial registrationThe trial was registered, before patient enrollment, in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn) (Clinical trial number: ChiCTR2300075271; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=205160, principal investigator’s name: Zifeng Xu, date of registration: 31/08/2023).Keywords: Ciprofol, Patients with frailty, General anesthesia

Background

Propofol is the most widely used intravenous anesthetic agent for general anesthesia induction because of its rapid action and ease of control. However, it significantly inhibits the cardiovascular system and is usually associated with hypotension during general anesthesia induction [1]. A drop in blood pressure, particularly in older patients with frailty, might limit its clinical application; therefore, alternative drugs are clinically required [2, 3].

Frailty is a complex and multisystem syndrome characterized by reductions in physiological reserve, which could be assessed by calculating the frailty index according to the “accumulated deficits” [4, 5]. Frailty is usually associated with increased risks for perioperative complications, such as stroke, and prolonged postoperative recovery [5–7]. The intraoperative considerations for frailty management significantly impact the prognosis in patients with frailty undergoing surgery [3, 8, 9]. Compared with robust patients, the maintenance of cardiovascular parameters under general anesthesia may be more challenging in patients with frailty [2, 8, 9]. Applying appropriate general anesthetics that can reduce the challenges posed by frailty is therefore paramount.

Ciprofol is an intravenous anesthetic, recently developed by modifying the chemical structure of propofol, with the aim of keeping the pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic profiles of propofol but reducing adverse drug reactions [10–13]. Ciprofol has been approved for sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy, bronchoscopy, intensive care, and for induction and maintenance of general anesthesia, and has been reported to be non-inferior to propofol in terms of efficacy and safety [14–16]. Although the incidence of hypotension was not the primary outcome in recent studies that used ciprofol in general anesthesia for gynecologic surgery, it seemed lower in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group, suggesting ciprofol as a potential candidate for hemodynamics maintenance during general anesthesia [17, 18]. The participants were generally robust, and the average age was less than 45 years in both these previous studies [17, 18]; however, the efficacy and safety of ciprofol in patients with frailty remain unknown.

This randomized, double-blind clinical trial recruited patients with frailty undergoing elective general anesthesia for gynecological surgery. This study aimed to explore the efficacy and safety of ciprofol in general anesthesia induction by comparing the anesthetic effects and adverse events of ciprofol and propofol in a frail population.

Methods

The participants were recruited from September 5, to December 20, 2023. Women aged 45–75 years who were scheduled to undergo elective gynecological surgeries under general anesthesia were screened for eligibility for the trial. Frailty increases with age; women in presenium (45–60 years) and geriatric (61–75 years) ages were selected because of their proclivity for frailty [19], and the Ethics Committee suggested not exceeding the age of 75 years for participants of this drug trial. The modified frailty index (mFI), calculating based on the presence of specific comorbidities and functional deficits, was used for the frailty assessment. Eligible patients had an mFI > 0.18, a body mass index (BMI) of 18–30 kg/m2, and an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of 2–3. The exclusion criteria included cardiovascular accidents within 3 months; allergy to soy or propofol; significant cardiovascular, respiratory, hepatic, and renal diseases; a history of substance use; and induction failure. Patients who declined to participate were additionally excluded.

Frailty was assessed by trained investigators calculating the mFI according to 11 items on comorbidities and one item on functional status, which could be easily acquired from routine clinical practice [20, 21]: (1) history of diabetes mellitus, (2) history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, (3) history of congestive heart failure, (4) history of myocardial infarction, (5) history of peripheral vascular disease, (6) history of cerebrovascular accident or transient ischemic attack, (7) history of hypertension requiring medication, (8) impaired functional status (defined as requiring assistance with activities of daily living), (9) history of angina, (10) history of kidney disease, and 11) history of arthritis. Each variable was dichotomized into 0 (the absence of a deficit) or 1 (the expression of the deficit). The mFI was calculated for each patient by dividing the sum of the deficits by the total number of deficits measured (i.e., 11). It is an ordinal variable that increases stepwise from 0 to 1, with a higher value indicating a worse frailty status. With reference to a previous study, patients with an mFI > 0.18 (roughly equivalent to two deficits) were recruited [20].

One day before the operation, the research fellow assigned the enrolled patients to either the propofol or ciprofol group, according to a random number table. The group information was placed in an envelope opened by the nurse anesthetist on the operative day to prepare the study medications in the anesthesia preparation room. The nurse anesthetist was not directly involved in the perioperative care, and the anesthesiologist who administered the anesthesia was blinded to the study group. Ciprofol looks very identical to propofol, anesthesiologists therefore cannot distinguish ciprofol from propofol according to their exterior features.

All patients fasted overnight. Upon arrival at the operating room, the patients underwent standard monitoring, including non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiogram, pulse oximetry, and bispectral index (BIS). After intravenous access to the upper limb was established, lactated Ringer’s solution was administered intravenously, and oxygen at 3 L/min was administered using a nasal cannula. The anesthesia induction included no premedication and started with an infusion of propofol or ciprofol. Either propofol (2 mg/kg; Fresenius Kabi AB) or ciprofol (0.5 mg/kg; Liaoning Haisco Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd) was administered using the identical syringes and same infusion pumps (BeneFusion eDS ex, Mindray) at a fixed infusion speed of 1,200 mL/h. The infusion volume according to body weight (kg) was also same between the two groups. Notably, the doses of both ciprofol and propofol were reduced by 25% for patients aged ≥ 65 years.

The time of loss of consciousness wad defined from the initiation of anesthesia induction to the patient not responding to any commands, which was assessed every 10 s, by calling the patient for eye-opening or by mild shaking. When the patient reached the modified observer’s assessment of alertness/sedation (MOAA/S) ≤ 1 (i.e., no response after mild prodding or shaking), remifentanil (effect-site concentration of 5 ng/mL by target-controlled infusion) and rocuronium (0.9 mg/kg) were administered. The face mask was used with 6 L/min when anesthesiologists started the induction. After spontaneous breathing disappeared, the patient was mechanical ventilated (tidal volume 6 ml/kg, 15 per min). An endotracheal tube was inserted and properly fixed when the neuromuscular blockade was achieved, followed by mechanical ventilation. If the patient failed to achieve MOAA/S ≤ 1 within 1 min after the full induction dose of propofol or ciprofol, one-half of the initial dose was administrated. Subsequently, if the MOAA/S ≤ 1 was still not reached within 1 min, after the supplemental dose, it was defined as the failure of general anesthesia induction.

The blood pressure was measured every minute from the start of induction till the induction was complete, lasting for about 10 min. It was performed by first setting the monitor at “auto NIBP (measurement interval was 1 minute)”. Then, the auto NIBP program was turned on when the anesthetics were administered, and turned off when the parameters at 10 time-points had been recorded. Thereafter, the blood pressure measurement interval was changed from every 1 min to every 5 min. The BIS and heart rate (HR) were continuously monitored from the onset of anesthesia induction and recorded every minute for 10 min. In case of a systolic blood pressure (SBP) increase or decrease by 30% of the baseline value, a vasoactive agent (perdipine [0.5 mg] or ephedrine [5 mg]) was available for use as needed. Atropine (0.5 mg) was administered if the HR was less than 50 beats/min. Adverse events, including bradycardia (HR < 50 beats/min), tachycardia (HR > 90 beats/min), hypotension (30% SBP reduction compared with baseline), injection pain (defined as patient complaint or unconscious limb movement), and intubation response (swallowing, cough, or tears), were recorded.

The primary outcome was the lowest SBP during anesthesia induction of each group. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of general anesthesia induction failure, time of loss of consciousness, and incidence of adverse events. The sample size was calculated from preliminary patients (not including the repeated measures), anticipating that a minimum of 33 eligible patients per group would be adequate to detect a 15-mmHg difference (standard deviation [SD], 20 mmHg) in SBP between groups during anesthesia induction, with a power of 0.90 at a significance level of 0.05 (two-sided). Assuming a 10% loss during the study period, 72 patients were recruited. The data from the preliminary study were excluded from the final analyses.

All values are expressed as mean (SD), median (interquartile range), or number (%). Hemodynamic data (including ten time-points) were compared using a two-way analysis of variance for repeated measurements, followed by the LSD multiple comparison test and without any other adjustments. Quantitative data were compared using an independent t-test for normally distributed variables or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Count data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 72 participants scheduled to undergo elective gynecological surgery were assessed for eligibility, and 69 women were ultimately enrolled and randomly allocated to the two groups (Fig. 1). One of these participants was excluded after enrollment because of a change in surgical schedule, and another because of allergy to rocuronium. The demographic characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the propofol and ciprofol groups in terms of age, height, body weight, BMI, ASA physical status, mFI, or baseline hemodynamics (Table 1).

The baseline blood pressure was comparable between the groups. The trend of blood pressure change during anesthesia induction was also similar between the groups, which mostly decreased within 4 min after anesthetic administration and then gradually stabilized (some participants were administered ephedrine). The lowest SBP was observed 3 min after ciprofol or propofol administration. The lowest SBP was significantly different between the two groups (103 [96–110] mmHg vs. 90 [85–98] mmHg, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). The lowest DBP (55 [50–58 mmHg vs. 53 [46–56] mmHg, P < 0.001, Fig. 2B) and MAP (71 [66–76] mmHg vs. 65 [59–70] mmHg, P = 0.002, Fig. 2C) were also significantly different between the groups.

The success rate of anesthesia induction was 100% in both groups, without any top-up doses or rescue therapy. The duration of loss of consciousness in the ciprofol group was 55.1 (4.3) s, which was slightly longer than that in the propofol group (53.1 [5.1] s), though not significantly (P = 0.082). In addition, participants in the ciprofol group showed a pattern of BIS changes similar to those of participants in the propofol group (Fig. 3). However, the BIS values were higher in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group 2 (54.5 [50–55] vs. 48 [45–50], P < 0.001, Figs. 3) and 3 (49.5 [45–52] vs. 44 [43–45], P = 0.003, Fig. 3) min after anesthetic administration. The lowest BIS in the ciprofol group (occurring 4 min after administration) was not different from that (occurring 3 min after administration) in the propofol group (45 [40–46] vs. 44 [43–45], P = 0.872, Fig. 3).

In terms of adverse events, 25 participants were administered ephedrine, of whom 6 (17.6%) were in the ciprofol group and 19 (57.6%) were in the propofol group. The use of vasopressors was significantly less frequent in the ciprofol group than that in the propofol group (P = 0.001, Table 2). The incidence of injection pain was significantly lower in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group (3 patients [8.8%] vs. 11 patients [33.3%], P = 0.014, Table 2). No participant in either group had tachycardia or was administered atropine (Fig. 2D). There was no incidence of intubation response in both groups. No severe adverse event occurred during the study period.

Discussion

In this prospective randomized controlled study, ciprofol was found to be as effective as propofol for general anesthesia induction in patients with frailty. Furthermore, ciprofol provided significantly greater hemodynamic stability (e.g., blood pressure) in frail patients compared to propofol during general anesthesia induction. A serious drop in blood pressure is associated with perioperative adverse events. However, hemodynamics maintenance in patients with frailty usually challenges the anesthesiologist, particularly during anesthesia induction. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to report that ciprofol can cause better hemodynamics in patients with frailty than propofol, which is the most commonly used general anesthetic during anesthesia induction.

Frailty has been shown to be associated with the risk of postoperative complications and mortality [22, 23], and has been recognized as an independent predictor of postoperative disability development and length of hospital stay [24]. Notably, patients with frailty have been found to have autonomic dysregulation in the form of intraoperative hemodynamic instability (e.g. particularly profound, prolonged hypotension), which contributes to increased postoperative adverse events [3, 9]. Therefore, intraoperative hemodynamic stability is paramount for the recovery of patients with frailty but is a challenge for anesthesiologists [8]. Optimal intraoperative management of patients with frailty includes sufficient considerations of anesthetic technique and agents [9]. According to the results of the current study, ciprofol, compared to propofol, provided significantly greater hemodynamic stability, and might be a promising general anesthetic for patients with frailty.

Ciprofol, an isomer of propofol, causes less cardiovascular inhibition in generally healthy patients [17, 18]. Given that hypotension during anesthesia induction is a common adverse event resulting from propofol administration, ciprofol was used for anesthesia induction in patients with frailty. In robust patients undergoing elective general anesthesia for gynecological surgery, 0.5 mg/kg ciprofol had an identical success rate of anesthesia induction, compared with 2 mg/kg propofol [18]. With reference to this study and the recommended dose in drug instructions, we applied similar doses in the current study. Our results showed identical trends in blood pressure in the two groups. However, the lowest SBP was significantly higher and the use of vasopressors was significantly less frequent in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group, suggesting that ciprofol could result in more stable hemodynamics during anesthesia induction compared with propofol in patients with frailty.

Similar to previous studies [14, 15], our results showed comparable efficacy of general anesthesia induction between the equipotent dose of ciprofol and propofol in patients with frailty. Although BIS values of the two groups (at 1 to 3 time-points) suggested ciprofol seemed to be slightly slower in action compared with propofol, the values (representing the depth of anesthesia) finally achieved to the same level 4 min after injection, indicating that the effect of ciprofol occurred slightly later than that of propofol, but still within 1 min. The duration of loss of consciousness in the ciprofol group was also slightly longer than that in the propofol group, but without statistical significance. It has been attributed to the structure of ciprofol leading to lower lipophilicity, causing a lower free ciprofol concentration and a lower rate of blood-brain barrier crossing [10, 18]. The modified chemical structure could also contribute to injection pain reduction. In the current study, the incidence of injection pain was significantly lower in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group, which is similar to the results reported in previous studies [17, 18].

The current study had some limitations. First, the sample size was not big. The reason should be attributed to the significant difference of the lowest SBP between the two groups during induction in frail patients. In relative robust patients, however, the difference of lowest SBP might be not with such significance between propofol induction and ciprofol induction. The current conclusion should be further verified in multicenter studies and big-size participants before its general application. Second, the mFI used in this study is a frequently used screening tool to categorize the frail state and might be less accurate than the Edmonton Frail Scale but has a higher predictive ability for adverse clinical events than other frailty measurements and is time-efficient in clinical practice [20, 25]. The Ethics Committee strictly reviewed the current study, because of the novel drug, for example the age should not exceed 75. Further researches on the older patients, passible with more deficits, are definitely needed. Nevertheless, the frail patients (if mFI > 0.18) included in this study were assessed according to the previous reports [20, 21], and were comparable between the two studied groups. Third, invasive hemodynamic monitoring (e.g., arterial and central venous catheters) was usually performed after anesthesia induction at our institute. Moreover, such invasive hemodynamic parameters were not obtained in the current study. Fourth, only female patients were recruited for this study. Women aged 45–79 years have been reported to have a higher mean frailty index and a higher prevalence of frailty, compared with men [4]. Although no difference has been demonstrated between the sexes in the association between frailty index and all-cause mortality [4], the efficacy and safety of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in frail men should be further investigated.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that the efficacy of ciprofol for general anesthesia induction in patients with frailty is comparable to that of propofol, with less hemodynamic inhibition. These findings may help further promote the management of perioperative frailty and indicate a promising future for ciprofol use, particularly in frail populations.
