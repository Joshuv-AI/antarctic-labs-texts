Closing Wedge Osteotomy With Cannulated Screw Fixation in Clinodactyly: A Surgical Technique Guide
Authors: Omid Nazifi, Dirck Martin Ananos Flores
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinodactyly is a congenital angular deformity of the finger, most commonly involving the little finger. Although mild deformities are well tolerated, progressive angulation may result in both cosmetic and functional limitations. This article describes a modified closing wedge osteotomy technique for correction of clinodactyly, using a cannulated headless compression screw to provide stable fixation, facilitate early mobilization, and optimize outcomes. The surgical anatomy, indications, contraindications, technique, and postoperative management are discussed, along with technical pearls and potential pitfalls.Key words: Cannulated, Clinodactyl, Osteotomy, Screw, Wedge

Clinodactyly is a congenital angular deformity of a finger, most commonly involving the little finger, where deviation occurs in the coronal plane. It is usually caused by an abnormal morphology of the middle phalanx, typically a delta phalanx or trapezoidal bone secondary to a longitudinal epiphyseal bracket restricting growth on one side of the physis.1,2 Although mild deformities are often well tolerated, increasing angulation may cause both cosmetic concern and functional limitation, particularly with fine motor tasks such as musical instrument use or typing.3

Surgical correction is indicated for symptomatic or progressive deformity, with several osteotomy techniques described—including closing wedge, opening wedge, and reverse wedge osteotomies.4 The closing wedge osteotomy remains the simplest and most reproducible technique, providing reliable correction at the expense of minimal bone shortening. When combined with cannulated screw fixation, it provides rigid stability, eliminates the need for transarticular pinning, and facilitates early mobilization and improved postoperative function.5

This article presents a modern adaptation of the closing wedge osteotomy technique for correction of clinodactyly, using a cannulated headless compression screw for stable fixation.

Surgical anatomy

The deformity typically involves the middle phalanx of the little finger, which assumes a wedge or trapezoidal shape. The deviation occurs toward the adjacent finger—usually ulnar deviation of the index or radial deviation of the little finger. The volar neurovascular bundles and extensor mechanism lie in close proximity and must be carefully preserved.

Surgical exposure is typically performed from the concave side of the deformity—that is, the ulnar side in cases of radial deviation. The extensor mechanism, including the conjoined lateral bands, central slip, and terminal tendon, must be identified and protected throughout the procedure. Accurate localization of the proximal interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints is critical to avoid iatrogenic joint violation. The periosteum should be preserved to maintain vascularity and enhance healing.

Surgical technique

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this surgical technique and case with accompanying images. The patient is placed supine with the affected limb on a hand table under tourniquet control. Prophylactic antibiotics are administered. A longitudinal or gently curved ulnar incision is made over the middle phalanx laterally, centered over the deformity. The extensor mechanism is identified and retracted dorsally, and a subperiosteal dissection is performed to expose the bone while preserving the volar neurovascular bundles (Fig. 1).

Surgical approach to the little finger.

Under fluoroscopic guidance, the closing wedge osteotomy is planned with its apex toward the concave side (ulnar in radial deviation). A 0.6-mm (0.023 in) Kirschner (K) wire is passed through the centre of the middle phalanx with the DIP flexed to allow safe passage through the head of the middle phalanx while avoiding articular violation, and 2 subsequent wires are used to plan the opening wedge (Fig. 2).

Planned closing wedge osteotomy planned with K-wires.

A small oscillating saw or a fine osteotome is used to excise a predetermined bone wedge based on the angle of the deformity, ensuring parallel cuts to correct the deformity to neutral alignment (Fig. 3).

Closing wedge osteotomy performed.

Once the wedge is removed, the distal fragment is realigned, closing the wedge to achieve correction, and temporary fixation is obtained with fine K-wires. The correction is confirmed fluoroscopically (Fig. 4).

K-wire correction and temporization.

With the DIP flexed and the 0.6-mm guidewire holding reduction in the middle phalanx longitudinally, a 1.7-mm (0.066 in) diameter partially threaded 9-mm headless compression cannulated screw (APTUS SpeedTip, Medartis AG) is then inserted across the osteotomy to achieve stable fixation and compression. The K-wire is removed once stability is confirmed. Bone grafting is typically unnecessary because of the closing wedge geometry (Fig. 5).

Headless compression screw is used to maintain fixation.

The wound is irrigated and closed in layers with absorbable sutures, followed by application of a digit-based thermoplastic splint maintaining the finger in slight flexion.

Postoperative management

The patient is reviewed at 7 days for wound inspection and commencement of a home exercise program or supervised hand therapy. A protective orthosis is maintained for 4 weeks.

Active motion of the metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints is initiated early to prevent stiffness, whereas motion at the DIP joint is encouraged early at the 1-week mark. Full activities are resumed at approximately 6–8 weeks, depending on union and comfort.

Case illustration

A 22-year-old man presented to the clinic with bilateral little finger clinodactyly with his DIP deviating radially, resulting in some ulnar-sided pain and cosmetic dissatisfaction (Fig. 6). He has a background history of fetal alcohol syndrome and Crohn’s disease. His middle phalanx of the little finger bilaterally had a 30° angulation radially (Fig. 7). A closing wedge osteotomy was performed with the technique above with a cannulated screw (Fig. 8). After surgery, the deformity improved to approximately 15°, with restoration of a functional digital cascade and resolution of symptoms within 6 weeks.

Although full radiographic correction was not achieved, the primary goal of surgery was functional realignment rather than absolute angular correction, which is well tolerated. The patient reported high satisfaction with function, rehabilitation, and appearance. He subsequently has elected to undergo correction of the contralateral side (Fig. 9).

In summary, clinodactyly is a congenital finger deformity, and a closing wedge osteotomy is a surgical method used to correct this deformity by removing a wedge of bone and realigning the finger.
