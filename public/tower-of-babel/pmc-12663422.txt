Effect of boric acid on the color stability and mechanical properties of 3D-printed permanent resins
Authors: Dalndushe Abdulai, Rafat Sasany, Raghib Suradi, Mehran Moghbel, Seyed Ali Mosaddad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effect of incorporating boric acid (BA) at different concentrations on the flexural strength, color stability, and microhardness of 3D-printed permanent crown resins. Disk-shaped specimens (15 × 2 mm) were fabricated for color stability and Vickers microhardness testing, and bar-shaped specimens (25 × 2 × 2 mm) were prepared for flexural strength evaluation using a masked stereolithography (MSLA) printer. BA was incorporated into the resin at 0% (control), 1%, 2%, and 3% (w/w). Flexural strength (n = 7 per group) was measured using a three-point bending test, microhardness (n = 7 per group) was assessed with the Vickers method, and color stability (n = 22 per group) was evaluated using ΔE₀₀ values at 3 time points: immediately after post-curing (T0), 30 min after post-curing (T1), and 60 min after post-curing (T2). Surface morphology was examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). Data were analyzed using one-way and two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests (α = 0.05). BA incorporation significantly affected flexural strength (p < 0.05), with the 3% BA group showing the highest mean value (177.92 ± 38.58 MPa), significantly higher than the control (p = 0.001), 1% (p = 0.002), and 2% (p = 0.011) groups. Microhardness showed an increasing trend with higher BA concentrations but did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). Color stability was adversely affected by BA addition (p < 0.001), as ΔE₀₀ values increased progressively from T0 to T2, with the 3% BA group consistently exceeding both perceptibility (ΔE₀₀ > 0.8) and clinical acceptability thresholds (ΔE₀₀ > 1.8) at all time points. BA incorporation enhanced flexural strength in a concentration-dependent manner, while microhardness improvements were not statistically significant, and color stability was compromised at higher concentrations. BA-modified resins may be suitable for posterior restorations where mechanical performance is prioritized.

Introduction

The evolution of digital dentistry and additive manufacturing (AM) has transformed the development of dental restorative materials, particularly in the field of three-dimensional (3D) printing for definitive and provisional prosthetic restorations1. Compared with subtractive manufacturing, which removes material from a solid block, AM enables the layer-by-layer fabrication of restorations directly from computer-aided design (CAD) models, offering advantages such as reduced material waste, enhanced design flexibility, and shorter fabrication times2,3. These benefits have positioned 3D printing as a promising alternative for fabricating long-term provisional and permanent restorations.

Despite these advantages, concerns persist regarding the mechanical performance and optical stability of 3D-printed dental materials, which remain inferior compared with milled and conventional counterparts4–6. In particular, limitations in flexural strength, hardness, and color stability restrict their widespread adoption in definitive restorations7–9. These shortcomings have prompted research into strategies for improving the structural and esthetic properties of additively manufactured permanent crown resins, including material reinforcement, optimization of post-curing protocols, and the incorporation of bioactive fillers7–9.

Boric acid (BA), a naturally occurring boron-based compound, has attracted increasing interest as a potential additive due to its antibacterial, antifungal, and reinforcing properties10–12. Previous studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of boron derivatives into polymeric matrices can enhance flexural strength, microhardness, and wear resistance, while maintaining biocompatibility10,13–15. For instance, the incorporation of boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs) improved surface smoothness and reduced free energy without altering the color of dental sealants16. Similarly, hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) in polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) enhanced surface hardness and flexural properties, changes that likely contribute to improved optical stability through enhanced surface integrity17. More recently, boron-rich nanocomposites combining BN nanosheets with antibacterial agents have demonstrated multifunctional enhancements spanning mechanical, surface, and optical domains18. The incorporation of boron derivatives into PMMA denture base resins has also been shown to significantly improve flexural strength and hardness; a study reported that 1% colemanite enhanced overall mechanical performance, while 3% borax produced marked increases in both hardness and flexural strength13.

However, despite these promising results, there is limited evidence regarding the influence of BA incorporation on 3D-printed permanent crown resins, particularly with respect to post-curing color stability and mechanical performance19,20. Color stability remains a critical factor in esthetic dentistry, as even minor discoloration can compromise patient satisfaction and treatment success9.

Unlike conventional PMMA-based or adhesive systems, 3D-printed permanent resins are formulated from photo-curable oligomers and dimethacrylate monomers that polymerize under controlled light exposure during layer-by-layer fabrication21. This AM process produces a polymer network with a different degree of conversion, cross-link density, and interfacial energy than chemically or heat-polymerized resins22. Therefore, the incorporation of boric acid into this type of matrix may interact differently with photoinitiators and reactive groups, influencing light transmission, polymerization kinetics, and filler–matrix bonding10,11. These distinctions make it essential to investigate whether the reinforcing and optical effects of boric acid observed in PMMA or adhesive systems can be replicated or altered in 3D-printed permanent resins. In photo-curable resin systems, the incorporation of raw, non-functionalized BA presents distinctive chemical and physical considerations compared to functionalized or silane-treated boron compounds. Without methacrylate or silane groups, BA is unlikely to form covalent bonds with the polymer matrix and instead interacts primarily through physical dispersion and secondary interactions such as hydrogen bonding with hydroxyl or carbonyl moieties23. Within photopolymerized materials, these additives can influence viscosity, light transmission, and polymerization kinetics24–26. The presence of micron-scale inorganic particulates may enhance light scattering due to refractive index mismatch, potentially affecting energy distribution, degree of conversion, and early color development15. Furthermore, although limited chemical coupling restricts interfacial adhesion, mechanical reinforcement may arise through stress transfer, crack deflection, or micro-filler effects10,13,27. These photochemical and mechanical behaviors differ fundamentally from those observed in heat- or chemically cured systems such as PMMA or dental adhesives, warranting further investigation in photo-cured additive manufacturing materials.

Previous reports have identified polymerization efficiency, curing protocols, and staining challenges as major contributors to discoloration in dental resins9. Moreover, BA addition could potentially alter the resin matrix structure and affect light interaction, influencing both mechanical performance and optical outcomes after post-curing and aging10,11. Therefore, it is essential to investigate how BA-modified 3D-printed resins behave under clinically relevant conditions to determine their suitability for long-term restorations. Therefore, the aim of this in vitro study was to evaluate the effects of boric acid incorporation on the color stability, flexural strength, and microhardness of 3D-printed permanent crown resins. The null hypothesis was that the incorporation of boric acid would not significantly affect the flexural strength, microhardness, or color stability of the 3D-printed permanent resin materials.

Materials and methods

In the present study, experimental resins were prepared by modifying a commercially available material through the incorporation of boric acid (BA) powder. Rather than synthesizing a novel resin formulation, the approach focused on enhancing the properties of an established resin by reinforcing it with BA. A total of 144 specimens were fabricated and divided into 4 groups according to BA concentration: C0 (control, 0%), C1 (1 wt%), C2 (2 wt%), and C3 (3 wt%). For color stability testing, 88 disc-shaped specimens (n = 22 per group) were fabricated; for Vickers microhardness evaluation, 28 disc-shaped specimens (n = 7 per group) were prepared; and for flexural strength testing, 28 bar-shaped specimens (n = 7 per group) were fabricated. The base resin material used was a light-polymerizing resin designed for permanent dental restorations (Saremco Print CROWNTEC, shade A2; Saremco Dental AG, Rebstein, Switzerland). The BA powder used was analytical grade (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) with an average particle size of < 10 μm, as specified in the manufacturer’s technical data sheet. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the BA powder had a median particle diameter (D50) of approximately 6.8 μm, with a particle size range of 2–10 μm. Morphological characterization indicated irregular polygonal particles with slightly angular contours, typical of mechanically milled boric acid. The powder was used in its raw form without any additional surface treatment to evaluate the intrinsic effect of BA incorporation on the mechanical and optical properties of the resin. This approach could assess the baseline influence of BA addition without introducing other modifying variables such as silanization or particle coating, as detailed in Table 1. For specimen preparation, BA powder was accurately weighed using a high-precision digital analytical balance (Precisa XB 220A, Switzerland) to obtain target concentrations of 1 wt% (C1), 2 wt% (C2), and 3 wt% (C3). The measured BA powder was gradually incorporated into the resin under controlled laboratory conditions. To ensure homogeneous particle distribution, the mixtures were subjected to 24 h of continuous magnetic stirring (WF-MIA1 Model, Weightlab Instruments, Turkey). Following this initial dispersion, an additional 20 min of ultrasonic homogenization was performed using a 250 W ultra-homogenizer (Ultraturrax, Janke & Kunkel, Staufen, Germany). This sonication step was performed in a darkened environment to prevent premature light-induced polymerization and further improve the uniformity of BA dispersion within the resin matrix. To avoid unintended activation or depletion of photoinitiators during mixing, BA–resin blends were prepared in opaque, light-shielded containers under ambient temperature (< 25 °C). No solvents or diluents were used. After 24 h of magnetic stirring and 20 min of ultrasonic homogenization, the material exhibited no gross change in handling (identical slicing/printing parameters and successful builds). However, viscosity and initiator content were not quantified instrumentally. For specimen manufacturing, digital models were designed using a software program (CHITUBOX 64; Shenzhen CBD Technology Co., Ltd, China) and exported in Standard Tessellation Language (STL) format to ensure compatibility with the selected 3D printing system. Two types of specimen geometries were prepared: disk-shaped specimens (15 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness) for color stability and Vickers microhardness (VHN) assessments were arranged in a grid pattern with a uniform spacing of approximately 2 mm between specimens. All disks were positioned horizontally and directly attached to the build platform without support structures to maintain surface flatness (Fig. 2A). For flexural strength evaluation, bar-shaped specimens (25 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm) were oriented vertically and uniformly distributed across the build platform to optimize printing accuracy and minimize distortion (Fig. 2B). Both layouts were sliced into .ctb format with a layer thickness of 50 μm and standard exposure settings recommended by the resin manufacturer. The specimens were fabricated using a Masked Stereolithography (MSLA) printer (Phrozen Sonic Mini 4K; Phrozen Technology, Xiangshan District, Hsinchu, Taiwan). After printing, all specimens were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath containing 96% isopropyl alcohol to remove uncured resin residues, air-dried, and subsequently subjected to a post-curing protocol using an ultraviolet (UV) curing unit (Wash and Cure 2.0; Anycubic Technology, Shenzhen, China) for 5 min at a wavelength range of 390–405 nm as recommended by the manufacturer’s guidelines.Fig. 2Digital layouts of specimen manufacturing: (A) Disk-shaped specimens (15 mm × 2 mm) arranged in a grid pattern with ~ 2 mm spacing for color stability and Vickers microhardness testing, positioned horizontally and directly attached to the build platform without support structures; (B) Bar-shaped specimens (25 × 2 × 2 mm) oriented vertically and uniformly distributed across the build platform for flexural strength evaluation.

Digital layouts of specimen manufacturing: (A) Disk-shaped specimens (15 mm × 2 mm) arranged in a grid pattern with ~ 2 mm spacing for color stability and Vickers microhardness testing, positioned horizontally and directly attached to the build platform without support structures; (B) Bar-shaped specimens (25 × 2 × 2 mm) oriented vertically and uniformly distributed across the build platform for flexural strength evaluation.

The manufactured specimens were subsequently subjected to a standardized finishing protocol to ensure uniform surface quality across all samples. Both surfaces of each specimen were sequentially polished using silicon carbide abrasive papers with 800-, 1000-, and 1200-grit sizes (Struers, Copenhagen, Denmark), applied for 30 s per side under continuous water cooling to minimize heat generation and surface damage. Following mechanical polishing, the specimens were sandblasted at 2.5 bar using 110 μm aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) particles (Easy Blast system; BEGO, Bremen, Germany) to remove the superficial oxygen-inhibition and smear layer formed during printing and polishing, and to homogenize surface microtopography across specimens. This step was selected to simulate minor external adjustments or surface cleaning procedures that may be performed clinically on definitive printed restorations before final polishing. Finally, all specimens underwent a final polishing step with polishing paste (Eve Diacomp; Eve Ernst Vetter, Pforzheim, Germany) to achieve a standardized, smooth surface finish. Upon completion of surface treatments, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C to simulate oral environmental conditions prior to mechanical and optical testing.

Flexural strength test

Flexural strength was evaluated using a three-point bending test performed in accordance with ISO 4049 guidelines28. For this analysis, bar-shaped specimens (n = 7 per group) measuring 25 × 2 × 2 mm (length × width × thickness) were tested using a universal testing machine (Lloyd LRX; Fareham, UK) with a support span of 20 mm. The dimensions of all specimens were verified prior to testing using a digital caliper to ensure measurement accuracy. Each specimen was centrally positioned on the support fixture and loaded until fracture at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min. Flexural strength (σ) was calculated using the formula σ = 3FL/(2bh2), where F is the fracture load (N), L is the support span (mm), b is the specimen width (mm), and h is the specimen thickness (mm). The results were expressed in megapascals (MPa)29.

Color stability

Color stability was evaluated using a clinical spectrophotometer (VITA EasyShade IV; VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany) operating in “tooth single” mode under D65 illumination (0°/45° geometry) and based on the CIEDE2000 color system. The control group (C0) served as the baseline reference. The instrument was calibrated before each measurement session according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure accuracy. On each specimen (n = 22 per group), color measurements (CIE L*, a*, b*) were performed at 3 time points: immediately after specimen fabrication (T0), after 30 min of storage (T1), and after 60 min of storage following post-curing (T2) on a neutral gray background by the same trained examiner (D.A.), and the mean value was used for analysis. Baseline color coordinates (L₀, a₀, b₀) were obtained from the control group, while the corresponding values for the experimental groups were recorded as L₁, a₁, b₁, and L₂, a₂, b₂. Perceptibility and acceptability thresholds for ΔE₀₀ values were defined as follows: not perceptible (ΔE₀₀ ≤ 0.8), perceptible but clinically acceptable (ΔE₀₀ ≤ 1.8), moderately unacceptable (ΔE₀₀ ≤ 3.6), clearly unacceptable (ΔE₀₀ ≤ 5.4), and extremely unacceptable (ΔE₀₀ > 5.4)31–33.

Vickers hardness test

Microhardness was evaluated in accordance with ISO 6507-1:202334, using a digital Vickers hardness tester (HVS-1000; Laizhou Huayin Testing Instrument Co., Ltd., China) equipped with a pyramid-shaped diamond indenter. Three indentations were made on the same polished surface of each specimen (n = 7 per group), maintaining a minimum spacing of 500 µm between indentations to avoid overlapping stress zones. A load of 300 g (2.94 N) was applied for 15 s at each indentation point, and the average value of the 3 measurements was used to determine the Vickers hardness number (VHN, kg/mm2) for each specimen. Group means were then calculated for statistical analysis. Scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM)

One representative specimen from each group (n = 4) was prepared using the same manufacturing protocol described in this study for surface morphology evaluation under a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (ZEISS Gemini 500; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Prior to imaging, each specimen was sputter-coated with a gold–palladium alloy (80% Au, 20% Pd) (Polaron SC7620 Mini Sputter Coater; Quorum Technologies, East Sussex, UK) for 15 s, resulting in an approximate coating thickness of 45 Å (deposition rate ≈ 3 Å/s). The coated specimens were then examined under the FE-SEM at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV, a working distance of ~ 4.1 mm, and magnifications of × 100 and × 2500 to assess surface topography and potential morphological changes associated with different boric acid concentrations.

Statistical analysis

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), assuming a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and 80% power to detect group differences. For flexural strength, previous studies on 3D-printed crown resins reported standard deviations between 10 and 30 MPa; therefore, a conservative estimate of σ ≈ 20 MPa and a detectable difference of ∆ = 35 MPa were used36–38, resulting in a required sample size of n = 6 specimens per group, which was increased to n = 7 after applying a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple pairwise comparisons. For Vickers microhardness, an estimated σ ≈ 4 VHN and ∆ = 7 VHN36,39 yielded the same requirement of n = 7 specimens per group. For color stability (ΔE₀₀), detecting a perceptible yet near-acceptable difference (Δ = 1.2) with σ ≈ 1.4 at the final time point (T₂) required n = 22 specimens per group36. Because color stability was analyzed using a repeated-measures design across 3 time points (T₀, T₁, T₂), this estimate was conservative, as within-subject correlation typically improves statistical efficiency. The chosen sample sizes (n = 7 for flexural strength and microhardness, n = 22 for color stability) were therefore adequate to achieve the planned 80% power.

All specimens were randomly assigned to their respective groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The operator performing the tests was blinded to group allocation during all experimental procedures, including flexural strength, microhardness, and color stability measurements, to minimize potential bias.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was verified using the Levene test. For the flexural strength and Vickers microhardness measurements, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare the 4 groups, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons when significant differences were detected. For color stability (ΔE₀₀ values), a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed, with material (C0, C1, C2, C3) as the between-subject factor and time (T0, T1, T2) as the within-subject factor. When significant main effects were observed, Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests. Effect sizes (partial η2) were calculated for the main outcomes (color stability and flexural strength) to quantify the magnitude of group differences.

For color stability (ΔE₀₀ values), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of material (F = 105.60, p < 0.001) and time (F = 101.33, p < 0.001), with no significant material × time interaction (F = 1.57, p = 0.158). Across all time points, ΔE₀₀ values increased in the order C0 < C1 < C2 < C3 (Table 4), and Tukey HSD comparisons confirmed significant differences among all materials at each time interval (p < 0.05). Within each material, ΔE₀₀ values increased progressively from T0 to T2 (Fig. 4). The change from T0 to T1 was not statistically significant (p = 0.057), whereas the increases from T1 to T2 (p = 0.004) and from T0 to T2 (p = 0.002) were statistically significant. From a clinical perspective, the C0 group was perceptible but clinically acceptable (ΔE₀₀ ≤ 1.8) at T0, but exceeded the acceptability threshold by T2. The C1 and C2 groups exceeded the threshold from T0, with C2 approaching the clearly unacceptable range (ΔE₀₀ > 5.4) at T2. The C3 group consistently exhibited the highest discoloration, remaining in the clearly unacceptable category across all time points. These findings indicate that both material composition and post-curing time significantly influenced color stability, with C3 showing the greatest susceptibility to discoloration. Discussion

The present study investigated the effect of boric acid (BA) incorporation on the color stability, flexural strength, and microhardness of 3D-printed permanent crown resins. Based on the findings, the null hypothesis stating that BA incorporation would not influence the mechanical and optical properties of the resin was partially rejected. While BA addition, particularly at higher concentrations, significantly improved flexural strength, it negatively affected color stability, and the observed increases in Vickers hardness did not reach statistical significance. These results are clinically relevant given the increasing adoption of 3D-printing technologies in restorative dentistry, where clinicians seek materials that combine mechanical durability with esthetic stability.

Flexural strength is a critical property of resin-based restorative materials, as it determines their ability to withstand occlusal forces and masticatory stresses. In the present study, BA incorporation improved flexural strength in a concentration-dependent manner, with the 3% BA group exhibiting the highest mean values, significantly outperforming the control and lower concentration groups. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that boron derivatives reinforce the polymeric network of resin-based materials. Demirci et al. showed that boron-containing compounds enhance structural integrity by increasing cross-linking density and improving resistance to crack propagation12. Similarly, Uçuk et al. reported that boron-based fillers improve fracture toughness and flexural performance through enhanced filler–matrix bonding10. Extending these findings to AM workflows, the present results suggest that BA can act as a reinforcing additive in 3D-printed resins. Comparable enhancements in flexural performance have also been achieved with other bioactive fillers such as nano-hydroxyapatite, silica, and zirconia nanoparticles, with improvements attributed to mechanisms like particle-induced stress transfer and crack deflection40–42. Tah et al.43 demonstrated that zirconia–silica nanofibers (2.5–5 wt%) enhanced flexural strength and fracture energy through fiber bridging and crack deflection, while Mirajkar et al.44 reported synergistic reinforcement when combining nanohydroxyapatite, zirconia, and glass fillers, resulting in superior flexural behavior and wear resistance. Collectively, these findings suggest that BA may operate through similar mechanisms, improving the printed resin’s load-bearing capacity. Clinically, this enhancement may translate into longer-lasting restorations, particularly in posterior regions where occlusal forces frequently range from 300 to 800 N, and may exceed 800 N in patients with bruxism45.

However, the differences between the 1% and 2% BA groups and the control were not statistically significant, suggesting a threshold concentration may be required before BA reinforcement becomes effective. This is consistent with Cruz et al., who reported that low nanofiller concentrations failed to improve adhesive cohesive strength due to insufficient particle–matrix interaction46. The limited mechanical benefit observed at lower BA concentrations in this study may therefore reflect inadequate integration of the additive within the resin matrix. In contrast, Kulanthaivel et al.47 demonstrated that incorporating methacrylate-functionalized boron nitride nanosheets (BNNSs) into dental adhesives significantly enhanced flexural strength and Young’s modulus even at concentrations as low as 1 wt%. The discrepancy with the present findings likely arises from differences in filler type, particle size, surface functionalization, and interaction with the polymer matrix, highlighting the importance of chemical compatibility when selecting boron-based additives.

Microhardness is another important parameter for evaluating the clinical performance of restorative materials, as greater hardness correlates with enhanced resistance to wear, scratching, and surface degradation under functional and abrasive forces48. In the current study, BA incorporation resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in Vickers hardness, with the 3% BA group showing the highest mean value. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Similar findings were reported by Asgartooran et al.49, who evaluated boron-doped hydroxyapatite–chitosan nanocomposites and observed significant hardness improvements only at higher boron concentrations, while lower levels produced non-significant increases. In this study, the modest improvements observed suggest that larger sample sizes, optimized particle dimensions, or surface-treated BA fillers might yield more pronounced effects. Clinically, even slight increases in hardness can be beneficial, as they are associated with improved wear resistance, enhanced polish retention, and prolonged restoration longevity50.

In contrast to the positive mechanical outcomes, BA incorporation adversely affected color stability, with the 3% BA group showing the highest ΔE₀₀ values. Clinically, these values exceeded the acceptability threshold (ΔE₀₀ ≤ 1.8), indicating noticeable and esthetically unacceptable discoloration. Color stability was evaluated at 3 time points (T0, T1, T2) to account for the influence of polymerization kinetics during the initial post-curing period. Previous studies have shown that resin-based materials do not achieve complete polymerization immediately after curing, and the degree of conversion continues to increase over time51. The incorporation of BA may have interfered with light transmission or altered polymerization efficiency, thereby contributing to increased discoloration. Factors such as filler type, monomer composition, and the presence of additives may further influence this process. In the present study, BA was incorporated into the composite at different concentrations, which could alter polymerization kinetics through its chemical interactions with the resin matrix. A delayed or modified polymerization process can affect the optical properties and, consequently, the color stability of the material during the early post-fabrication stages. Therefore, color measurements were recorded immediately after post-curing (0 min), after 30 min, and after 60 min to detect any time-dependent variations. It is hypothesized that insufficient or delayed polymerization may increase the susceptibility to discoloration due to residual monomers and incomplete cross-linking in the initial phase52.

The color measurements in this study were limited to the first 60 min after post-curing to specifically evaluate early-stage optical changes related to polymerization kinetics, rather than long-term discoloration caused by external factors. This short time frame was selected to capture potential color shifts associated with ongoing monomer conversion and matrix stabilization immediately after light exposure. Moreover, the transient color variation may also be related to boric acid chemistry, as boric acid can interact with hydroxyl and carbonyl groups within the resin matrix and potentially influence the photoinitiator activity or refractive index during curing24. Such interactions could alter light absorption and scattering behavior, thereby contributing to the early discoloration observed in boric acid–modified resins. Nonetheless, this short-term assessment does not represent long-term clinical behavior, and extended studies incorporating thermocycling, staining challenges, and artificial aging are necessary to fully characterize color stability and durability.

Paravina et al.32 established clinical thresholds for perceptibility and acceptability and highlighted that filler characteristics directly influence light scattering and absorption. Consistent with these findings, Kim et al.53 reported that nanofillers, such as ZnO and CeZr, reduced color stability due to particle agglomeration and reduced surface gloss. Similarly, Verniani et al.54 observed ΔE values exceeding 20 in some 3D-printed resins after prolonged exposure to coffee, confirming that resin composition significantly influences susceptibility to discoloration. The increased discoloration observed after post-curing in the present study aligns with reports demonstrating that post-curing protocols affect both the mechanical and optical properties of printed resins. Kim et al.19 showed that extended post-curing improved hardness and flexural strength but resulted in higher ΔE₀₀ values, surpassing clinical acceptability thresholds. Lim et al.20 further demonstrated that nitrogen-saturated post-curing improved the degree of conversion and reduced ΔE₀₀ compared to air curing, highlighting how curing environments influence optical performance. More recently, Aktug Karademir et al.55 reported that optimized post-curing protocols improved microhardness and conversion while minimizing staining susceptibility. Collectively, these findings suggest that BA incorporation may have disrupted light transmission or altered polymerization kinetics during curing, amplifying early discoloration.

The FE-SEM observations help explain the mechanical and optical behavior of BA-modified 3D-printed resins. At higher BA concentrations, the presence of well-distributed microparticles likely enhanced filler–matrix interactions, contributing to the improved flexural strength observed in the 3% BA group. This reinforcement mechanism is consistent with established particle-induced toughening processes, in which properly dispersed fillers promote stress transfer and crack deflection, thereby delaying catastrophic failure under load56,57. However, the FE-SEM images also revealed localized particle agglomeration and microvoid formation in specimens with higher BA content. These microstructural irregularities may have dual consequences: while isolated clusters can act as stress-absorbing sites improving mechanical performance, excessive agglomeration and voids may increase light scattering and heterogeneity within the polymer network, leading to greater color instability. Thus, the SEM analysis supports the conclusion that the mechanical benefits of BA depend not only on concentration but also on achieving optimal particle dispersion within the resin matrix to minimize adverse optical effects. The relatively high standard deviation observed in flexural strength for the 3% BA group likely reflects microstructural inhomogeneity due to partial agglomeration of BA particles, as suggested by FE-SEM images showing localized clusters. Although the specimens were prepared using prolonged magnetic stirring and ultrasonic homogenization, microscopic particle aggregation may still occur at higher concentrations, affecting uniform stress distribution during loading. Optimization of dispersion—such as applying surface functionalization or coupling agents to improve particle–matrix adhesion—may help minimize this variability in future studies. The use of non-silanized BA prioritizes the evaluation of particulate-mediated effects (e.g., light scattering and stress transfer) but also implies limited interfacial bonding with the polymer matrix, which can facilitate micro-agglomeration and contribute to variability at higher loadings. In conventional resin composites, silanization enhances filler–matrix coupling and mechanical performance; by contrast, the present approach intentionally avoided functionalization to disentangle chemical-coupling effects from optical/mechanical influences of BA particles.

Clinically, the findings highlight the need to balance mechanical reinforcement and esthetic preservation. BA-modified 3D-printed resins demonstrate potential for posterior restorations, where functional performance is prioritized over esthetics. However, their use in anterior restorations may be limited due to concerns about discoloration. Future studies should explore optimizing BA particle size, surface treatment, and concentration, as well as combining BA with translucent stabilizing fillers to enhance color stability. In addition, long-term aging protocols and in vivo evaluations are essential to better understand performance over time. Efforts should also focus on refining post-curing protocols and additive incorporation techniques to achieve a balance between durability and esthetic stability. Given the trade-off between reinforcement and early color change, BA concentrations ≤ 1 wt% may be considered only for posterior applications where mechanical performance is prioritized and minor color shifts are acceptable. Although the 3 wt% BA group exhibited the highest mean flexural strength, this apparent improvement was accompanied by substantial variability and pronounced color instability, likely related to particle agglomeration and optical scattering, which compromise both reliability and clinical applicability. Therefore, ≥ 2 wt% BA is not recommended for esthetic zones due to clinically unacceptable ΔE₀₀ values and unpredictable mechanical behavior. To reduce early discoloration while maintaining conversion, oxygen-limited curing (nitrogen purge or oxygen-barrier gel), a two-stage protocol (standard short cure followed by an extended lower-irradiance/temperature cure under nitrogen), and temperature control (≈40–60 °C) are proposed as optimization pathways for future validation. Overall, the addition of boric acid to photo-curable 3D-printed resins strikes a balance between minor mechanical reinforcement and significant color instability. The clinical applicability of this approach will depend on further optimization of particle functionalization, dispersion uniformity, and post-curing protocols to achieve predictable, esthetically stable outcomes.

The present study has several limitations. First, its in vitro design does not replicate the complex oral environment, where factors such as salivary enzymes, fluctuating pH, and microbial activity could influence both mechanical and optical outcomes. Second, the sample size was limited, potentially reducing statistical power, especially in microhardness testing, where only non-significant trends were observed. Third, only three BA concentrations (1%, 2%, 3%) were evaluated; exploring intermediate or higher levels may help define the optimal threshold for reinforcement. Additionally, only a single type of 3D-printed resin was tested, so the results cannot be generalized to other commercially available systems with different resin matrices or filler compositions. Another limitation is the short-term assessment; long-term simulations involving thermocycling, water storage, and staining challenges were not performed, but are essential to evaluate clinical durability. The potential antibacterial properties of boron derivatives, which could enhance clinical relevance, were also not investigated. It should also be noted that the reinforcement mechanisms discussed in this study—such as enhanced filler–matrix interaction, improved stress transfer, and crack deflection—were inferred based on mechanical outcomes and surface morphology observations rather than direct physicochemical evidence. Future investigations incorporating complementary techniques, such as Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to assess degree of conversion, porosity analysis, and light transmission evaluation, are recommended to confirm the underlying mechanisms responsible for the observed mechanical trends. Additionally, Rheological characterization and direct assessment of photoinitiator content were not performed after prolonged stirring/sonication; therefore, subtle viscosity changes or initiator degradation cannot be excluded. Future work should include rotational rheometry and initiator quantification (e.g., UV–Vis/HPLC) and compare non-silanized versus silanized BA to determine how interfacial coupling modifies dispersion, light transport, and mechanical outcomes. Furthermore, the finishing protocol included sandblasting before the final polish, which may elevate surface roughness relative to a polish-only approach and thereby influence early color measurements; future studies should incorporate unsandblasted and polish-only control groups to isolate surface-preparation effects on color stability, microhardness, and flexural properties. Finally, the influence of alternative post-curing environments was not examined.
