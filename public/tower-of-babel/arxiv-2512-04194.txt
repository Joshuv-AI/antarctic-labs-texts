Probabilistic Safety under Arbitrary Disturbance Distributions using Piecewise-Affine Control Barrier Functions
Matisse Teuwen, Mathijs Schuurmans, Panagiotis Patrinos

Abstract
We propose a simple safety filter design for stochastic discrete-time systems based on piecewise affine probabilistic control barrier functions, providing an appealing balance between modeling flexibility and computational complexity. Exact evaluation of the safety filter consists of solving a mixed-integer quadratic program (MIQP) if the dynamics are control-affine, (or a mixed-integer nonlinear program in general). We propose a heuristic search method that replaces this by a small number of small-scale quadratic programs (QPs), or nonlinear programs (NLPs) respectively. The proposed approach provides a flexible framework in which arbitrary (data-driven) quantile estimators can be used to bound the probability of safety violations. Through extensive numerical experiments, we demonstrate improvements in conservatism and computation time with respect to existing methods, and we illustrate the flexibility of the method for modeling complex safety sets. Supplementary material can be found at mathijssch.github.io/ecc26-supplementary/ .

I
Introduction

In recent years, there has been a tremendous increase in
data-driven and learning-based controller design.
Leveraging recent advances in machine learning, as well as
the ubiquity of data and high-fidelity simulators,
these approaches often achieve remarkable levels of performance,
in particular for complex tasks.
However, oftentimes, these controllers only come with very
limited safety guarantees
[
1
]
.

This has motivated an increased research interest in so-called
safety filters
,
which essentially operate by projecting the control action
of some given policy onto a set of admissible inputs
designed to guarantee specific safety properties of the system.
The key idea is to preserve as much of the baseline controller’s performance as possible,
enforcing safety through only minimal and necessary interventions.

At the core of designing such a safety filter lies the control barrier function.
control barrier functions, originally coined in
[
2
]
for continuous-time systems,
are functions whose 0-superlevel set is a controlled invariant set.
They play a role analogous to control Lyapunov functions
(used extensively in the theory of model predictive control),
but with a focus on ensuring safety rather than stability
[
3
]
.

Over the years, several variants of control barrier functions have emerged, each extending the
original framework to handle different settings
[
4
,
5
]
.
Discrete-time control barrier functions were introduced in
[
6
]
as a
counterpart to continuous-time control barrier functions.
For uncertain systems, robust control barrier functions
[
7
,
8
]
were introduced to handle bounded disturbances. These approaches are suitable
when the support of the disturbance is compact and can be tightly estimated.
Whenever this is not the case, such robust formulations tend to be either inapplicable
(e.g., for unbounded noise) or overly conservative (no tight estimates of the support).
This motivates stochastic and
probabilistic control barrier functions, which aim to bound the probability of leaving the safe set
over a finite horizon (the
N
-step exit probability
).
Although conceptually, these approaches allow us to deal with unbounded noise,
they require very careful design that ensures that the stage-wise exit
probability is tightly bounded, in particular for long horizons
N
,
where conservative bounds may easily lead to vacuous estimates of the
N
-step exit probability.

A common approach to the design of probabilistic control barrier function is to enforce a supermartingale
condition at every time step and leverage concentration inequalities, such as Ville’s

[
9
,
10
]
or Freedman’s
inequalities
[
11
]
. These methods require moment
information and often result in conservative bounds. More recent work attempts to
reduce this conservatism by introducing auxiliary functions that reshape the
expectation condition, though these approaches remain restricted to Gaussian
noise models
[
12
]
. Risk-based
formulations using CVaR
[
13
,
14
]
offer an
alternative but likewise are prone to conservatism.

More recent work has relaxed distributional assumptions entirely,
using data-driven estimators such as conformal prediction or the scenario approach
[
15
]
.
While appealing for its generality, it is very challenging for such an approach
to provide tight guarantees without exploiting any additional problem structure.

Our goal is therefore to develop a probabilistic safety filter that is able to exploit
as much as possible known structure of the probabilistic control barrier function (ensuring tightness of the violation probabilities),
while maintaining sufficient flexibility in the probabilistic control barrier function class to model complex
safe set geometries.
In general, non-smooth control barrier functions (including piecewise-constant control barrier functions
[
16
]
)
have been identified as useful
to balance flexibility and structure
[
17
]
.
In this work, we will restrict our attention in particular to piecewise affine
control barrier function candidates, which can model arbitrary safe sets, potentially even allowing
the use of representations based on neural networks, with ReLU activation functions.

We highlight the following contributions of this work:

(i)

We synthesize a safety filter using a probabilistic PWA control barrier function condition requiring knowledge only
of the disturbance quantiles

(ii)

We introduce a heuristic solution method to speed up evaluation of the safety filter

(iii)

We extend the formulation to the data-driven setting, where the distribution of
the disturbance is not known, but rather estimated from data

(iv)

We present extensive numerical experiments comparing our method with existing approaches,
demonstrating its effectiveness, reduced conservativeness and flexibility.

Informed by the results in

More precisely, given the current state

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\kappa\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu:{\rm I\mathchoice{\hskip-2.0pt}{\hskip-2.0pt}{\hskip-1.75pt}{\hskip-1.7pt}R}^{n}\to{\rm I\mathchoice{\hskip-2.0pt}{\hskip-2.0pt}{\hskip-1.75pt}{\hskip-1.7pt}R}^{m}

,
(

we define the
safety filter policy
as

\kappa(x)\in\Pi(x)

\displaystyle\Pi(x)=\argmin_{u\in U}

\displaystyle\;\lVert u-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\kappa\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(x)\rVert^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle\sttshort

III-B

Search over index

Although (

be a small-scale optimization problem (a convex quadratic program in the case of control-affine
dynamics),
the overall complexity of

is also determined by the number of iterations in the inner loop
(cf. line

Since

exponentially in the number of constituent polyhedra

(one exception being when
p_{i}=1
for all

are halfspaces).

Fortunately, the number of effective trials can be vastly
reduced in practice by checking the most promising candidates first.
We propose the following heuristic for iterating over the set

Under the assumption that

will tend to be close to

we can approximate constraints (

) by
the corresponding sections of the piecewise affine barrier function

(14)

It then stands to reason that if
(
14
)
is satisfied for all
(i,j_{i})_{i\in[d]}

(for some
\mathchoice{\text{\boldmath{$\displaystyle j$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle j$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle j$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle j$}}}=(j_{i})_{i\in[d]}\in J
),
then we also expect
\hat{\Pi}_{\mathchoice{\text{\boldmath{$\displaystyle j$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle j$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle j$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle j$}}}}(x)
to be nonempty.
Thus, the first candidate sequence should consist of the halfspaces of each polyhedron
P_{i}
,
for which constraint violation in the current state

is lowest.
To this end,
let
\sigma_{i}=(\sigma_{ik})_{k\in[p_{i}]}
,
i\in[d]
, denote the permutation of the index set

[p_{i}]
that sorts the sequence
(h_{ij}(x))_{j\in[p_{i}]}

in decreasing order, i.e.,

\hat{h}_{i\sigma_{ik}}\geq\hat{h}_{i\sigma_{ik+1}}\;\forall k\in[p_{i}-1],i\in[d].

In case of infeasibility, we then first proceed by
testing the other halfspaces of the set
P_{i}
that has the largest
violation, since we expect to gain most from swapping a constraint that

already (nearly) violates.
That is, we sort the indices
i\in[d]
in
decreasing order of

(cf. (

resulting in the sequence
\mathchoice{\text{\boldmath{$\displaystyle i$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle i$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle i$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle i$}}}=(i_{k})_{i\in[d]}

We then obtain successive candidate sequences
(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})\in J
by iterating
over the set
\bigtimes_{i\in\mathchoice{\text{\boldmath{$\displaystyle i$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle i$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle i$}}}{\text{\boldmath{$\textstyle i$}}}}\sigma_{i}
in lexicographic order,
ensuring that we check that halfspaces of

(corresponding to the largest violations)
in the innermost loop.

before moving on to the

Despite this greedy search method being a heuristic, we have found empirically
that it allows us to find a feasible collection of indices within one or two
inner iterations. For this reason it is useful in practice to limit
the number of inner iterations to some small amount.

IV
Data-driven extensions

Since the disturbance distribution is often unknown in practice,
requiring the knowledge of its quantile
\operatorname{q}

in (
9
) may be restrictive.
In this scenario, we can replace the quantile in the right-hand side of
condition (
7
) by a data-driven estimator,
to obtain a
probabilistic control barrier function with high-confidence
.
This then allows us to provide the same guarantees as before, at the cost
of some additional conservatism to account for the lack of knowledge of the
distribution. For readability, we defer some longer proofs to
section

-A
.

IV-A

General case

Let

VI
Conclusion and future work

We proposed a safety filter design for
discrete-time stochastic systems, utilizing
piecewise affine probabilistic control barrier functions,
which are highly expressive, yet computationally manageable.
We derived tractable sufficient conditions to ensure
a given upper bound on the
N
-step exit probability
(with both known and unknown noise distributions),
and demonstrated that it is less conservative than comparable methods in the literature.
Furthermore we illustrate the flexibility of the method
for modeling complex safety sets.
