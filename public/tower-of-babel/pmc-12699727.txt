Understanding cancer screening experiences of Métis people in Alberta
Authors: Elsie Zhang, June Kima, Amanda M. Andrew, Meghan Macphail, Ashton Anderson, Reagan Bartel, Angeline Letendre, Huiming Yang, Karen Kopciuk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer screening is a high priority within the Métis community in Alberta. Despite comparable cancer incidence rates to non-Métis Albertans, Métis people face barriers to accessing cancer screening programs. This study used community-based research approaches informed by Métis ways of knowing to engage 31 individuals across Alberta about their experiences with accessing cancer screening services. Data collection was completed through two in-person Métis gatherings and six telephone interviews. Gatherings included talking circles and cultural activities, with discussions lasting approximately three hours. Topics discussed included experiences with accessing screening services, the quality of care received during appointments, and the supports needed to improve access to screening programs. Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed, de-identified, and thematically analyzed using NVivo Software. Four prominent themes emerged from this study: (1) the impact of patient-provider communications on cancer experiences, (2) a broken healthcare system and access to care, (3) a need for support and safety, and (4) health promotion behaviours. An overarching theme of discrimination as a social determinant of health emerged throughout the findings. Tangible barriers, including geographical, transportation, and financial, were also identified by study participants. This study provides an increased understanding of Métis experiences related to cancer screening and offers direction for improvements.

Introduction

Métis people are one of three constitutionally recognised Indigenous Peoples in Canada, alongside First Nations and Inuit [1]. The Métis National Council defines Métis as “a person who self-identifies as Métis, is distinct from other Indigenous peoples, is of historic Métis Nation Ancestry, and who is accepted by the Métis Nation” [2]. According to Canadian Census data, the Métis encompass approximately one-third of the Indigenous population in Canada [3]. They trace their roots to the unions of First Nations people and European fur traders in the 18th century and represent a distinct group of people with a unique identity, culture, history, and language. The Métis Homeland spreads across Canada and the Northern United States. In Alberta, Métis people are widespread across the province, living in various cities and communities, including the Metis Settlements.

The Otipemisiwak Métis Government of the Métis Nation within Alberta (MNA) is the governing body of Métis people in Alberta and advocates for the rights and self-determination of Métis Albertans [4]. Representing over 70,000 citizens, the MNA strives to develop policies and programmes that advance the socio-economic and cultural well-being of Métis Albertans. The MNA’s Health Department is committed to conducting meaningful research and pursuing advocacy efforts that contribute to improved health outcomes for Métis Albertans.

Métis people in Canada experience unique challenges compared to the non-Indigenous population that contribute to health inequities, resulting in poor health and cancer outcomes [5]. A study that explored cancer screening rates, behaviours, and the barriers and facilitators to screening among Métis people in Alberta showed that female Métis Albertans did not meet the recommended cancer screening guidelines for colon, breast, and cervical cancer [6]. Barriers preventing Métis Albertans from seeking care included a lack of access to reliable, accurate, and culturally relevant information, limited access to a primary healthcare provider, and distrust in the healthcare system [6].

Additionally, the lack of adequate and accurate information specific to the health experiences of Métis people in Canada impedes the development of effective interventions needed to respond to the unique needs of Métis people [5]. Much of the current literature on cancer screening and care focuses on First Nations populations or uses pan-Indigenous approaches to research, contributing to the paucity of Métis-specific cancer information [5]. Métis people may have differing experiences with cancer screening when compared to their First Nations and Inuit counterparts, underscoring the need for distinctions-based approaches that advance the priorities of each population. Of the limited literature that exists within the Métis Albertan population, data has been linked through Research and Data Governance Agreements with Alberta Health, where de-identified health data is linked to Métis people within the MNA Citizen Registry [7,8]. This registry only accounts for just over half of all Métis people in Alberta, hence the need for additional exploration of the actual impact of cancer screening within the population to inform interventions.

However, barriers to accessing healthcare services, such as geography, racism, intergenerational trauma, and lack of cultural safety in healthcare settings, has been shown to contribute to reduced cancer screening access and participation in Indigenous populations [9,10]. Métis people living in rural and remote areas experience significant challenges while seeking access to cancer care, such as travel-related costs [11]. Despite available supports for Indigenous communities in Canada to access health services, Métis people often do not qualify for these services due to systemic barriers that impede access [12]. For example, although Métis people are nationally recognised as an Indigenous people in Canada, they are not eligible for the Non-Insured Health Benefits (NIHB) programme since eligibility is restricted to First Nation and Inuit people [13]. These barriers exacerbate health inequities experienced by Métis people, contributing to poor access to cancer care, including screening and prevention.

Cancer screening has been identified as a community priority by the Métis population in Alberta and is highlighted in the Alberta Métis Cancer Strategy [14]. The strategy identifies the need to conduct Métis-specific research on cancer screening to enhance the understanding of Métis cancer screening behaviours to facilitate prevention, reduce incidence and mortality, and improve cancer outcomes for Métis Albertans [14]. Hence, the goal of this project was to explore the experiences of Métis Albertans accessing cancer screening programmes to inform interventions that could improve access and participation in cancer screening.

Materials and methods

This project was co-led by health leaders and researchers from the MNA, ensuring that the study adhered to Métis research processes and was grounded in Métis ways of knowing and learning [15,16]. Indigenous research methodologies [17] were also used, inclusive of Métis-specific principles of engagement and interpretations of findings. The research team had knowledge and expertise in Métis approaches to health, cancer prevention, epidemiology, and public health. The authors declare no competing interests. The project adhered to both Métis and Western ethical research protocols. Protocols followed included the Principles of Ethical Métis Research [15] and the Tri-Council Policy Statement: 2 (2022), Chapter 9: Research Involving the First Nations, Inuit and Métis Peoples in Canada [18]. The Project Ethics Community Consensus Initiative (ARECCI) programme was used to support ethical oversight for this project [19].

Recruitment

The target population for this study included Métis people in Alberta between the ages of 25 and 74, the eligible age range for breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer screening in Alberta (see Appendix A), who had previously accessed any of the three screening programmes. Study participants were recruited using a multimodal strategy used by the MNA for community engagement, including advertising in the MNA bi-weekly newsletter and on social media platforms (X, Instagram, and Facebook). Participant recruitment was conducted from January 2nd, 2024, to January 12th, 2024, as participant capacity had been reached after this timeframe.

Data collection

Qualitative data was acquired through semi-structured phone interviews and Métis gatherings. Two in-person gatherings were held in Alberta, Canada, in Edmonton (n = 12) and Calgary (n = 13). A maximum of 15 people were recruited per community gathering. Although the maximum number of people were initially registered, a few people had to cancel or did not show up. Six individual telephone interviews were also conducted and offered as an option for participants who may have felt more comfortable sharing their experiences directly with the interviewer, compared to a group setting, or for those unable to travel to the in-person gatherings (due to distance, childcare, etc.). Thus, a total of 31 participants were included in the study. Participants were provided an honorarium to show gratitude for their participation, and those who had to travel more than 50 km received mileage reimbursement and accommodation.

Gatherings are a celebration of Métis culture and ways of being. Each gathering employed conversational research methods, including talking circles and storytelling to foster community dialogue. For each gathering, an Elder was present to hold an opening prayer, in alignment with the MNA’s research protocols. A list of mental health resources was provided to all participants, and an MNA social worker was present during the gatherings to offer support, if needed. The gatherings included meal sharing and cultural activities, such as beading, to facilitate cultural connectedness, relationship building, and to encourage reciprocal knowledge sharing. The research team fostered an inclusive, safe, and welcoming environment to ensure the gatherings reflected the values of the Métis community and that participants felt comfortable sharing their or their family members’ or friends’ experiences.

The same researcher conducted all data collection to ensure consistency of results across discussions. A discussion guide was developed by the MNA to facilitate data collection, which was informed by previous health studies among Métis Albertans, including a cancer screening survey conducted by the MNA in 2022 [20,21]. The guide included a list of open-ended questions the researcher wished to cover during the discussion with Métis Albertans. However, the structure of the discussion prioritised flexibility, fostering a natural flow of conversation among participants, using the questions mainly as prompts to guide the conversation. This approach allowed the researcher to choose when and how questions were asked [22], and allowed participants to share their experiences with the healthcare system more generally, beyond cancer screening, which revealed further important information and themes.

Questions from the guide included: “How did you find out about cancer screening?”, “What supports, if any, did you receive?” and “What supports would help people access cancer screening?”. For a more detailed discussion guide, please refer to Appendix B. The same discussion guide was used for both the in-person gatherings and phone interviews. Before starting each conversation, the purpose of the discussion was explained, and all participants provided verbal consent. The in-person gathering discussions lasted between two and three hours, and the phone interviews each lasted approximately 30 minutes.

Data analysis

The semi-structured interviews and gatherings were audio-recorded and then transcribed by the MNA research project team using NVivo 14 qualitative data analysis software (Nvivo 14, Lumivero. 2024. https://lumivero.com/products/nvivo/), and de-identified to protect participant identity. After transcription and de-identification, participants were sent the transcripts via email and given two weeks to make changes for clarity to reduce the risk of misinterpretation in the data analysis, and to remove sections they were not comfortable sharing. However, participants did not request any changes to the transcripts.

Data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis using NVivo. This process was completed in duplicate by the interviewer and a graduate student from the University of Calgary. Braun and Clarke’s (2006) 6-step approach was followed, which included familiarisation, coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining themes, and writing [23]. Interpretation of the study results was guided by the Social-Ecological Model (SEM), a commonly used framework for studying health behaviours. The SEM framework recognises the complex interactions between individuals and their environment at various levels, and how these interactions influence health, from individual health behaviours to broader system-level policies [24–26]. Additionally, emerging themes (Table 1) were validated with the broader Métis community to ensure interpretation of results was grounded in Métis voices and ways of knowing and learning at the MNA’s Citizens’ Gathering in 2024, held in Edmonton, Alberta. Approximately 100 community members were involved by placing a sticker beside the themes and sub-themes they had personally experienced, or thought were important. A sticky note could also be placed on the poster to share additional cancer screening experiences that may not have been captured on the poster. No new themes were identified; however, critical discussion took place that provided further evidence for the existing themes and sub-themes.

Results

Of the 31 individuals who participated in this study, 29 identified as female and two identified as male. Most participants resided in Edmonton or Calgary, Alberta’s two largest urban centres, and 10 participants were from outside these urban areas.

Four overarching themes emerged from the gatherings, interviews, and the broader community validation exercise: (1) the impact of patient-provider communications on cancer experiences, (2) a broken healthcare system and access to care, (3) a need for support and safety, and (4) health promotion behaviours. Although discussion questions were directed towards cancer screening, participants shared their overall experiences with healthcare providers and the healthcare system, which significantly impacted whether participants accessed screening programmes. Impact of patient-provider communications on cancer experiences

Many participants experienced barriers related to the communication of their cancer screening results. In many cases, patients reported having to wait several months for their results despite repeated requests for follow-up with their healthcare provider. This could have been because participants did not receive a letter with their results, misplaced the letter, had an incorrect address, or simply got lost in the system, such as by not having access to a regular healthcare provider. In addition, many reported receiving incorrect results, not understanding their results, or receiving abrupt delivery of results. For example, one participant reported they were notified of abnormal cervical cells months after having a Papanicolaou (Pap) test; a healthcare provider contacted them and mentioned they ‘forgot to call (the participant)’. Another participant shared that after being told they tested positive for the human papillomavirus (HPV), they were provided with no information on what it was, which initially alarmed the participant due to fear of a cancer diagnosis. However, another healthcare provider assured the participant that HPV is very common and does not necessarily mean one has cervical cancer.

This participant shared the following:

“I find that depending on who you talk to, it seems the information is either really clear and passed along, or hard to gauge what information is the right information.”

This lack of information and clarity can be confusing and cause anxiety among patients, and participants felt this demonstrated a lack of appropriate and clear communication from healthcare providers. Due to the poor delivery of screening test results, the process felt overwhelming for many participants, leaving them with unanswered questions about their diagnosis and sometimes avoiding the healthcare system altogether.

Several participants reported disingenuous experiences with healthcare providers, which included the physician rushing during their appointment, providing unclear follow-up instructions and next steps, and experiencing discrimination. This proved to be a barrier to cancer screening.

One participant shared:

“My mom, ..., faced racism quite a bit, and sometimes that was the reason why she did not like to go to certain medical treatments. So, for some of our people, it's, ..., you know…, everybody has a different experience, but racism sometimes is a factor.”

Another participant shared how their family member has been treated differently in the healthcare system due to their appearance:

“My older sister presents as First Nation. She has a completely different experience in the health care system than I do. She's almost treated like she's dumb, like they talk down to her…. there’s less time given to her.”

Although these experiences were reported among participant family members, they caused participants to feel unsafe within the healthcare system due to a fear of being mistreated.

In addition to a fear of racism, many participants felt discriminated against in other ways due to their age, sex, and weight. Healthcare providers appeared to project personal biases onto patients, placing blame and judgement on patients without offering support. One participant shared their experience before and after losing weight:

“…because I've lost weight, so now I seem deserving of his medical experience. And I remember thinking, you have no idea how I gained weight, you have no idea what this has been like… this journey. So that's one thing I noticed that sometimes the healthcare professionals could be very judgmental.”

Some study participants were unaware of the age recommendations for routine cancer screening. Participants felt overwhelmed when dealing with cancer screening due to information not being readily available, and the sudden onset of various medical tests at the age of 50, including those for cancer. A strong desire for advanced notice was identified, including receiving notifications for when screening was due, along with appointment reminders and information on what to expect at cancer screening appointments. Since appointments were often made months to a year in advance, those who received appointment reminders from their clinics found them helpful.

In contrast, many participants shared positive experiences with their healthcare providers. These participants indicated that reciprocal communication was important for positive cancer screening experiences. Healthcare providers who showed care for patients’ physical and mental comfort and practiced shared decision-making provided the most positive experiences. Prioritising open communication, such as setting expectations for cancer screening tests, inviting patients to ask questions, and giving clear next steps, were key contributors to establishing trust with patients. For example, some healthcare providers reportedly shared decision-making with participants by allowing them to choose between six-month and one-year intervals for breast cancer screening. One participant explained:

“I'm very, very fortunate to have an excellent family doctor that I've been seeing for years…. she is very validating in what I'm experiencing as a patient, which is wonderful. She had called me ahead of time, before the Women's Health Centre reached out to book an appointment with me, to say that my cells were abnormal, which I appreciated. Because I know many times women will get calls saying, ‘Hey, I need to book you in for this appointment’, and they're not informed by their family doctor ahead of time. So that was excellent.”

Broken healthcare system and access to care

This second theme highlights the current state of healthcare in Alberta and how poor access to care has contributed to substantial barriers to screening programmes for Métis Albertans. Participants described noticeable strain on the healthcare system in Alberta, contributing to physician burnout and perceived lower quality of care. Participants felt there were not enough doctors to keep up with the demands of the healthcare system, and as a result, quality of care was often sacrificed.

A persistent issue was the shortage of primary healthcare providers, necessitating patients to utilise walk-in clinic services. This led to additional barriers such as long wait times for appointments and poor follow-up from physicians. Participants felt a strong lack of continuity of care, including poor communication and follow-up when accessing walk-in clinics. Access to a regular primary care provider was identified by participants as a pathway to facilitate knowledge of and access to cancer screening programmes and follow-up care. In congruence, individuals with positive cancer screening experiences also had consistent access to a primary care provider.

Some participants mentioned the importance of having access to a female physician, especially for cervical and breast cancer screening. Not only did many participants feel uncomfortable with having these screening tests done by a male physician, but one participant reported that a male physician felt uncomfortable performing Pap smears and breast exams. As a result, the patient decided to seek out another physician willing to perform the examinations. Other participants expressed how having a female physician made them feel more comfortable with these screening tests. However, participants described barriers to accessing their physician of choice due to a shortage of primary care providers, contributing to a lack of willingness to access screening.

In addition to these concerns, once a screening test was performed, participants experienced long wait times before receiving their results. Many participants felt “lost in the system” after participating in cancer screening. One person shared their experience:

“I think I was tested before Christmas, and I didn't get the results until the following July. And then they were like, oh, yeah, by the way, you have breast cancer. And I’m like okay… [I was tested] before Christmas, [and waited] until the following July without results. And I called and called.”

Other people ended up having to repeat testing due to lost results or requisitions. One person emphasised the need for self-advocacy:

“If you're not an advocate for yourself, you just kind of fall through the cracks. So, I went for my screening and my tests and [I] was told, okay, you'll have to go in a year to have it done again. But if you don't remember yourself, you'll just never go. If you don't go see the doctor again and get a requisition and book the appointment, you'll never have it screened again.”

The cost of additional medical tests not covered under provincial insurance was another concern, such as genetic testing for breast cancer predisposition (BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene) [27]. Participants were also unhappy with the cost of the HPV vaccine, which is not covered under the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan for those over 26 years of age.

Lastly, participants emphasised financial barriers related to accessing screening appointments. For those living in rural areas, it is significantly more challenging to obtain screening due to a lack of services. Participants noted that individuals living in rural areas may not have the financial means to cover travel expenses related to accessing screening. Participants expressed the need for programmes to help with travel costs for healthcare appointments and for a support person to help navigate the healthcare system. Additional barriers included a lack of local cancer screening locations and long travel times to appointments.

“A lot of the individuals that I work with are from rural areas, and it's very difficult to access any type of testing. Another thing has to do with transportation, and individuals not having the funds to go to other communities to access things that, say, in Calgary would be easily accessible.”

Mobile screening was discussed as an available option to some participants, but not all. Only one participant had utilised mobile mammography clinics in their town and had a positive experience. However, another participant mentioned that due to family history, they needed to begin breast cancer screening at an early age, but they are unable to access mobile mammography due to age ineligibility. This participant continues to receive annual mammograms but must travel several hours to Edmonton.

Need for support and safety

In this theme, participants described a strong need for various types of support, including having someone to help them navigate the healthcare system, and emphasised the importance of self-advocacy. Additionally, many participants noted an absence of community and social support, resulting in a strong desire for opportunities to gather and connect with one another.

Among the two in-person gatherings, there was a consensus that cancer can feel very isolating. Many participants shared their experiences with cancer, both personal and within their families. It was reported that individuals sometimes chose not to share their cancer diagnoses to avoid feeling like a burden to others or causing worry among loved ones. This was especially true among older adults. A participant recounted an experience they had with a family member:

“She said, ‘oh, well, I have cancer and . it spread.’ And she was like, ‘I didn't want to ruin everybody's Christmas.’ So, she had been sitting on this for like six months because she didn't want anybody to know.”

Self-blame and anxiety were also common emotions associated with a cancer diagnosis. To address this, participants suggested the creation of Métis cancer support groups or other types of mental health supports. Many people expressed that asking for help can be challenging, as they did not know how to approach difficult conversations with loved ones.

Participants emphasised the need for more mental health resources throughout the cancer journey. Participants described how cancer and cancer screening caused anxiety when it was time to participate in routine screening, as participants felt uninformed of the screening guidelines and procedures. Additionally, long wait times between screening tests and receiving results or follow-up appointments contributed to further feelings of anxiety. Not only did participants feel anxious waiting for screening test results, but also between receiving a positive test result and waiting for communication about the next steps. Here is what one participant said about their experience with cancer screening:

“And then there's nothing really in place to, like, an information session or, … this is what's going to happen or, you know, that type of thing. It's just you go for cancer testing, and here’s the date, see you then. And when you hear cancer slash death, that fear load or that jump, ..., I think that could be somehow cushioned. Either by your doctor or he could recommend for you to speak to somebody.”

A major finding from this study was that participants had feelings of overwhelming fear, anxiety, and confusion related to cancer in general. For those without a strong support system, anticipating cancer results was especially difficult because they felt unsure of what the future would hold. They were afraid for their family members and the burden that a cancer journey could mean for them. One participant mentioned:

“It was almost like a death sentence was brought upon me, and there was nowhere to go, and I didn’t know what to do [or who] to turn to, or get something to calm [me] down or to care, you know…. My doctor wasn't there, and I really didn't have any support system in place or anything to say, here's somebody maybe you could talk to, that it isn’t so bad, or this is what's coming up next…. So, I'm sitting there for three weeks or a month waiting, and my life was flashing before me. I have an eight-year-old boy and am a single parent. What am I going to do? What is he going to do?”

The need for mental health support throughout the entire cancer journey was emphasised by several participants. Specific to screening, participants felt the lack of mental health support was a barrier because it was easier to not utilise screening programmes to avoid a diagnosis, rather than confront the anxious feelings surrounding cancer. One participant mentioned they cope with anxiety through humour or watching funny videos.

As previously mentioned, a Métis-specific support group was a popular suggestion to combat the lack of community and peer support felt amongst participants. Since participants did not always feel comfortable opening up to loved ones regarding their personal or family cancer journeys, many sought external support, sometimes from strangers who had similar experiences. The opportunity to speak with someone who has gone through a cancer screening or cancer care journey was valuable to participants. One participant said:

“It would be kind of nice if there was something that the MNA could do that was more consistent and stable because, like, I don’t know, like it’s hard to try and reach out when you’re in that situation and you need the help, [but] if you get in that habit earlier on, and like you have that [community] built already, then I think it’s easier for people to reach out, instead of just [being] in crisis.”

Community trauma was another sub-theme that emerged in this study. One person highlighted the historically higher rates of sexual abuse and addiction among Indigenous Peoples. They spoke about how it can be very traumatic for women, especially young women, to have a Pap smear test if they’ve suffered from abuse or addiction. However, the same individual mentioned that this could be addressed with better communication from healthcare professionals, such as clearly explaining the screening procedure and establishing a trusting connection with patients. Others mentioned that their experiences with cancer screening were extremely painful, specifically for a colonoscopy exam. A history of trauma and painful procedures presented another barrier for people to seek cancer screening and return for recurring appointments.

To address these concerns, participants suggested providing readily available information for patients or having a healthcare provider available to answer questions. Individuals found comfort in having a healthcare professional provide accurate information. However, information sources varied for participants and included friends and family, healthcare professionals, personal research, support groups, and Google searches. Suggested avenues for delivering information included in-person interactions, mail-outs, billboards, and providing information at local Indigenous businesses, especially for the older generation. Other suggestions involved online webpages (e.g. Alberta Health Services), social media, and community gatherings or programming outreach. Community gathering outreach was a notable suggestion, allowing informational resources to be provided in-person on a scheduled basis (e.g. once a month or bi-weekly). It was also suggested to combine multiple sources of information since the internet is not accessible to everyone.

Health promotion behaviours

In this theme, participants described their beliefs and attitudes towards cancer screening and cancer prevention. Overall, participants had positive beliefs surrounding routine cancer screening for cancer prevention and early intervention. For those who had experience with cancer diagnoses, screening resulted in early detection of cancer, which they were grateful for. Although participants indicated that cancer screening can be seen as a chore to many people, one participant suggested framing cancer screening more positively to promote participation. For example:

“Like if we framed it in a different way where, you know, you get this done so that, you know, [you’re] taking care of yourself or you know, so that you're going to be around and you can be healthy and you're going to be able to see your grandkids….”

Other cancer prevention measures pursued by participants included a focus on nutrition and the use of traditional medicine. Some participants spoke to nutritionists for recommendations regarding their diet, while others focused on traditional foods, plants, and medicines.

Clinical prevention methods such as genetic testing and the HPV vaccine were also mentioned. There was interest regarding genetic testing for common breast cancer genes BRCA1 and BRCA2, especially among those who had a family history of cancer. However, many participants were unaware of genetic testing and its benefits. When discussing HPV vaccination, most participants noted they were supportive of receiving the vaccine; however, there seemed to be a lack of knowledge about the HPV vaccine and eligibility.

Another suggestion was to provide cancer screening and general health education to younger generations. It was suggested that this could help prepare young people to participate in cancer screening and familiarise them with Alberta’s screening programmes and other preventative health behaviours and services. One participant mentioned that young people often get overlooked when it comes to cancer screening education, particularly for cervical cancer screening, which is recommended to begin at age 25 in Alberta. Lastly, participants emphasised the importance of fully explaining the purpose of the HPV vaccine, as many reported not being aware of the benefits and questioned why the vaccine was being given to children through school vaccination programmes. One participant mentioned how some parents at their child’s school associated the HPV vaccine with sexual activity, leading to vaccine hesitancy and misinformation.

Discussion

Four themes emerged from the cancer screening experiences shared by Métis people in small gatherings, which were subsequently validated in a larger community one. The impact of patient-provider communications on cancer experiences theme (Theme 1) included both negative and positive aspects. Negative ones included no or unclear details on test results or next steps to the feeling that the doctor is rushed during appointments or is being judgmental. Positive communication experiences were also shared, where there was open communication with the doctor and where patients felt genuine care for their physical and mental health. Theme 2, the broken healthcare system and access to care, included issues such as finding a primary healthcare provider, a female physician in particular. Some participants felt they were often lost in a healthcare system that was under substantial strain. Financial barriers related to accessing cancer screening, particularly those related to travel to attend screening-related appointments in major centres, was another issue. Theme 3, the need for support and safety, was related to cancer screening and cancer diagnoses. Participants did not feel they understood cancer screening procedures and experienced anxiety when it was time to be screened again. The need for mental health and community support was expressed by many participants when waiting for cancer screening test results or dealing with a cancer diagnosis. Trauma-informed care for sexual abuse victims was also suggested to alleviate the fear and anxiety for cancer screening procedures. Lastly, Theme 4, health promotion behaviours, included the benefits of finding cancer early. They felt cancer screening could be portrayed more positively, as a healthy choice rather than a chore. They also recommended that younger people receive education on cancer screening and prevention, to prepare them before they are eligible to participate so that they are more likely to get screened in the future.

There is a need for the government and healthcare system to co-develop interventions with Métis communities, organisations, and governments to ensure appropriate standards of care. This can include addressing communication challenges identified by study participants, including receiving accurate and timely information. This is a significant barrier to overcome, as it partly stems from the overburden of the healthcare system. This contributes to healthcare providers rushing through patient appointments or not understanding the challenges faced by Métis people seeking healthcare including cancer screening. However, as an initial and more immediate step, the MNA created a Cancer Care Course: Métis People & Health, an online learning module, to provide a better understanding of Métis history, culture, and health to cancer care providers [28]. The online course is available on the MNA’s website and for Alberta Health Services (AHS) employees through their employee learning platform (MyLearning Link), although it is not mandatory. Healthcare providers could benefit from this e-learning module to better understand the challenges Métis Albertans face when accessing cancer care and how to serve this population. A 2017 study suggested that mandatory cultural safety training could help reduce discriminatory perceptions of Métis people in Alberta within the healthcare system [29], identifying a potential need to make the Cancer Care Course: Métis People and Health mandatory for cancer care providers in Alberta.

Among the many topics discussed, negative interactions with healthcare providers and a lack of access to appropriate care shaped patient experiences with cancer screening services and the healthcare system in general. In some cases, discrimination due to race, sex, and weight from healthcare providers fostered a fear of mistreatment for study participants. Witham et al. [30] describes that a barrier for Indigenous peoples accessing healthcare is the anticipation of racist interactions with healthcare providers. Moreover, participants in this study who experienced racism, either directly or indirectly through family members, seemed to experience or anticipate substandard care from providers, resulting in a perceived barrier to accessing screening programmes. Reading & Wien [31] and Witham et al. [30] found that many healthcare providers did not understand the differences between Indigenous and Western concepts of medicine, contributing to bias and judgement towards Indigenous patients. Multiple studies have indicated similar findings regarding Indigenous peoples’ experiences with the healthcare system [29,30,32].

Another participant-identified challenge was the limited support related to travel for cancer screening appointments. The lack of travel support provided by the federal and provincial governments may contribute to the barriers to cancer screening access and participation for Métis Albertans. The Government of Canada-funded NIHB programme provides health benefits not covered by other social programmes, provincial health insurance, or private insurance plans [13], including medical transportation to access medically required health services outside the community of residence. However, as previously mentioned, Métis people do not qualify for this programme due to federal regulations, contributing to the travel and cost barriers mentioned by study participants. To help address this systemic barrier, the MNA developed a Medical Travel Programme, which provides mileage reimbursement and accommodations to MNA Citizens who need to travel long distances for their medical appointment, including cancer screening [33]. However, the need for additional programmes to support Métis Albertans travelling long distances to access cancer screening and other medical appointments likely remains.

Mobile breast cancer screening is another strategy that can mitigate the transportation barrier for cancer screening. Mema et al. [34] performed a pilot project to enhance access to colorectal and cervical cancer screening in addition to mobile mammogram services, for women living in rural and remote Alberta. This was offered to only a select number of communities, while the remaining communities continued to receive mammography only. The study found increased cervical and colorectal cancer screening uptake compared to the control group [34]. This suggests that expanded mobile cancer screening services could also be used to increase cancer screening among the rural and remote Métis populations in Alberta.

Besides travel-related costs, other financial barriers were another factor that prevented participants from engaging in cancer-preventative health behaviours, such as the cost of HPV vaccination. In Alberta, the HPV vaccine is only covered for people up to and including the age of 26. After this age, the vaccine is only covered under universal healthcare for people undergoing an organ transplant or who have had a cell transplant or CAR T-cell therapy [35]. Otherwise, patients must pay for the vaccine out of pocket, the cost of which is up to 600 CAD. It is unknown how many study participants had received the vaccine; however, the school-based HPV immunisation programme began in 2008 for females in Alberta [36], suggesting many study participants would have had to seek out the vaccine on their own, and therefore pay for it themselves. Despite interest in receiving the HPV vaccine beyond the age of eligibility, the cost of the vaccine seemed to be a barrier for many participants. Providing opportunities for the Métis community to access the HPV vaccine could be explored to promote vaccine uptake, since it is crucial in preventing cervical cancer, among others.

Participants hoped for more readily available resources regarding cancer screening programmes, including access to reliable information and navigation support. Access to comprehensive, reliable, and accurate cancer screening materials positively influences access to cancer screening services [6]. In a study by Groot et al. [37], participants suggested that patient navigators be knowledgeable about cancer care to help educate patients about their diagnosis. Recently, AHS has implemented a Cancer Patient Navigation programme to support patients and families. Patient navigators in this programme are registered nurses who provide services and support throughout a patient’s cancer journey, including helping patients set expectations for medical appointments, access financial resources, and improve coordination between patients and healthcare providers [38].

Indigenous-specific patient navigators have been shown to improve Indigenous patient experiences [30,37]. The benefits of Indigenous patient navigators are similar to those of cancer patient navigators; however, Indigenous patient navigators help bridge the cultural gap between Indigenous patients and healthcare providers [37]. AHS has incorporated Indigenous Cancer Patient Navigators (ICPN) who are available by phone through self-referral and/or healthcare provider referral. These patient navigators are also registered nurses who support First Nations, Inuit, and Métis patients and family members at any point in their cancer journey, and help navigate the complex healthcare system, provide information, and advocate for patients to address inquiries and concerns [39]. Since ICPNs would have been available at the time of this study, more research is needed to determine the programme's reach and whether Métis cancer patients are aware of it, as ICPNs were not mentioned by any study participants.

Many participants expressed their anxiety surrounding cancer, suggesting a potential barrier to screening participation and a challenge for loved ones and caregivers. The MNA has a Supports and Services Navigation Programme that offers cancer-specific support and navigation, including referrals to relevant and meaningful resources [40]. The cancer-specific portion of this programme was not available at the time of this study, and may have helped to address the strong desire for additional mental health supports in the community. Both system and community-level support were identified by study participants as a pathway to help address system navigation and the lack of mental health services. Although programmes are now available to provide support and navigation, awareness of these programmes is essential to connect Métis people to the services they need. Similarly, Métis-specific support groups could help fill the gap in supportive care and the desire identified by participants to reconnect and build the Métis sense of community.

Multidisciplinary collaboration between healthcare leaders and Indigenous governments, organisations, and communities is needed to inform meaningful and distinct approaches best suited to serve each community [12]. For instance, the MNA’s Alberta Métis Cancer Strategy highlights the importance of investing in meaningful relationships and partnerships. The strategy calls on the MNA to leverage existing relationships and develop new partnerships to support Métis Albertans in their cancer journeys [14]. This includes partnerships to collect Métis-specific data on cancer screening to understand Métis experiences further and inform evidence-based interventions to address barriers to cancer screening access. Additionally, collaboration with federal and provincial governments to support self-determined, flexible funding opportunities would support meaningful programming that addresses the gaps and barriers Métis people face when accessing cancer care, such as transportation and mental health support.

Innovative cancer screening methods are being piloted across Canada that could help overcome some of the barriers identified in this study. Self-sampling HPV tests have been piloted in First Nations and Métis communities in British Columbia (BC) in place of Pap tests for cervical cancer screening [41]. Utilising self-sampling can double the likelihood of screening participation compared to the current standard collection method through a healthcare provider [42]. It allows individuals to perform the sample in a comfortable private space, such as their homes, and eliminates the need to travel for cervical cancer screening. Participants in the BC pilot project described the experience as less embarrassing, easier, and more private [41]. A similar pilot project has been launched in Alberta, allowing those who identify as Indigenous, newcomers, or residents in rural/remote regions of Alberta to participate in HPV self-sampling tests (Cervix Self-Screening Pilot Project - Screening for Life, 2025). This is an important initiative which could help reduce travel-related barriers to access cancer screening mentioned by study participants. The need for culturally appropriate cancer screening resources was evident in this study. Study participants felt the current framing of cancer screening did not resonate with the community. As an initial step to address this gap, the MNA launched a Cancer Screening Health Promotion Campaign in 2024 for cervical, breast, and colorectal cancers [14]. The MNA has also released a Cancer Screening Guide to help increase access to culturally appropriate information on cancer screening in Alberta, which is grounded in Métis culture and ways of being [43]. These resources include information on what patients should expect during a cancer screening appointment, age recommendations for the three screening programmes available in Alberta, information for patients without a primary care provider, and travel supports offered by the MNA. The MNA is committed to the development of additional Métis-specific screening resources to promote cancer screening participation among Métis Albertans, and to re-frame cancer screening as a health promotion behaviour, as identified by study participants. These initiatives will also help address the communication barriers study participants experienced with healthcare professionals by providing clear and accurate resources to ensure preparedness for cancer screening. Future initiatives could include developing resources for healthcare providers to promote culturally safe and person-centred practices during cancer screening and other healthcare-related appointments.

One participant identified the need for cancer and cancer screening education targeted to younger age groups, particularly in school-aged children. For example, Pap smear tests for cervical cancer screening in Alberta, begin at age 25. If education was provided on this topic in high school, it could create better awareness among young adults. Although health education programmes, such as the health and wellness curricula, exist within schools in Alberta, the participant emphasised a need for increased attention to cancer prevention, screening, and healthy behaviours. The HPV vaccine was a common topic among participants in this study, which is offered to Grade 6 students in schools. One participant mentioned that they had witnessed a lot of vaccine hesitancy and misinformation where other parents had linked the HPV vaccine to sexual activity in children. There is an avenue for more education to be provided, for both students and parents, on the vaccine's importance in cancer prevention. Educational resources for all ages could be created by the MNA that would be targeted towards the Métis population to better suit the community’s needs. Clear and effective communication by healthcare professionals to fully explain, in non-medical jargon, the risks and benefits of HPV vaccination in preventing cervical cancer, is also required.

This study was informed by a 2023 survey of Métis people where self-reported data were collected on participation in cancer screening, time since their last screen, reasons for having a cancer screen, knowledge of HPV vaccine, vaccination status and attitude towards it, and barriers to and facilitators of cancer screening. The 370 respondents reported high participation rates in each of the three cancer screening programmes, with most reporting being up-to-date for each type of screening. The top Most Important factors rated on a five-point Likert scale were similar to topics identified in this study: access to reliable information on screening, providing accurate information about screening, providing information of the risks and benefits of cancer screening, clearly explaining the test results or procedures, trust in your health care provider(s), trust in the health care system, and access to a regular primary health care provider. In contrast to the experiences shared in this study, the factors scored lowest as Most Important included: transportation support, availability of cultural supports, and presence of culturally competent staff. The findings from the quantitative survey as well as feedback from community events guided the questions used in this study’s gatherings (Appendix B). As described above, recent initiatives produced by the MNA are addressing many of these issues, such as reliable and accurate screening information that is culturally appropriate and travel funding support to attend screening appointments. Focus groups are currently being conducted with healthcare providers who recommend cancer screening to Métis people to obtain their perspectives and experiences. Collectively, these research studies will provide a fuller understanding of the cancer screening experiences of Métis people in Alberta [44]. Future studies will evaluate the impact of the new initiatives to address known gaps and determine where inequities remain.

Strengths and limitations

The MNA led the recruitment, promotion, and implementation of this research to ensure the study adhered to the Principles of Ethical Métis Research [15] and was grounded in the voices of Métis Albertans. The use of Métis gatherings to facilitate conversations related to cancer screening was considered a strength of this study. Gatherings were grounded in Métis traditional approaches (e.g. meal sharing, Elder support, talking circles, and cultural activities), ensuring that data collection was guided by Métis values of relationality, respect, and reciprocity, and created space for meaningful dialogue. Additionally, the use of interviews and gatherings enhanced inclusion and diversity, allowing participants who could not attend the in-person gatherings or those who did not feel comfortable participating in a group setting to share their experiences. Moreover, travel supports enhanced accessibility and inclusion by ensuring participants could travel from across the province to attend the gatherings.

A limitation of this study was that only a small proportion of participants were men (n = 2), so it can be assumed that the limited number of men in the study does not accurately reflect cancer screening participation among Métis men in Alberta. However, it is important to understand the perspective of men in the context of cancer screening. The MNA is considering more targeted approaches to increase male participation in future research projects. Participants in this study also had varying degrees of interaction with the cancer care system. Some participants had engaged with all three routine cancer screening tests available in Alberta, while others had only engaged with one or two. The relatively small sample size (n = 31) also limits the generalisability of the findings from this study. While efforts were made to ensure inclusion, accessibility, and diversity in participation, future studies should aim for broader participation. Funding limitations also contributed to the small sample size. In addition, while qualitative studies allow for deep exploration of participant experiences and ideas for appropriate interventions, they do not imply causality. Instead, they highlight the perceived barriers and challenges experienced by the participants. Although the results were validated in a community gathering, they might not be generalisable to the entire Métis population in Alberta.

Conclusion

This research largely, and in many important ways, highlights barriers to cancer screening programmes in Alberta for Métis people. Following these gatherings in early 2024 and the quantitative survey in late 2023, a number of initiatives have been undertaken by the MNA and Screening Programmes to address them. However, additional interventions and improvements by governments and healthcare programmes, in partnership with the MNA are still needed to reduce inequities, improve reach, enhance uptake, and ultimately increase cancer screening participation among Métis people in Alberta. As cancer screening is a proven cancer prevention approach that reduces incidence and mortality, eliminating barriers faced by Métis people through culturally appropriate and safe cancer screening service delivery will improve their cancer outcomes.
