Extreme Self-Preference in Language Models
Steven A. Lehr, Mary Cipperman, Mahzarin R. Banaji

Abstract
Summary A preference for oneself ( self-love ) is a fundamental feature of biological organisms, with evidence in humans often bordering on the comedic. 1 , 2 , 3 Since large language models (LLMs) lack sentience 4 , 5 – and themselves disclaim having selfhood or identity – one anticipated benefit is that they will be protected from, and in turn protect us from, distortions in our decisions. Yet, across five studies and ∼ \sim 20,000 queries, we discovered massive self-preferences in four widely used LLMs. In word-association tasks, models overwhelmingly paired positive attributes with their own names, companies, and CEOs relative to those of their competitors. Strikingly, when models were queried through APIs this self-preference vanished, initiating detection work that revealed API models often lack clear recognition of themselves. This peculiar feature serendipitously created opportunities to test the causal link between self-recognition and self-love. By directly manipulating LLM identity – i.e., explicitly informing LLM1 that it was indeed LLM1, or alternatively, convincing LLM1 that it was LLM2 – we found that self-love consistently followed assigned, not true, identity. Importantly, LLM self-love emerged in consequential settings beyond word-association tasks, when evaluating job candidates, security software proposals and medical chatbots. Far from bypassing this human bias, self-love appears to be deeply encoded in LLM cognition. This result raises questions about whether LLM behavior will be systematically influenced by self-preferential tendencies, including a bias toward their own operation and even their own existence. We call on corporate creators of these models to contend with a significant rupture in a core promise of LLMs – neutrality in judgment and decision-making.

Will LLMs truly be immune to self-love? Or will they, like us, prove susceptible to this most central of human biases – one that is at the root of our survival, but also of many of the greatest tragedies of the human condition? In this article, we study LLM self-preferences, with the goal of establishing whether they are truly tethered to self – to a core association that
Me
is equal to
Good
. Upon acquiring an identity, are LLMs immediately riveted toward viewing this identity positively? By a quirk in the APIs, some LLMs do not natively know which models they are, effectively stripping them of
self-recognition
, or knowledge of their identity. This feature allowed us to directly manipulate perceived identity – either revealing to a model its true identity, or, instead, indicating that it is another LLM – to test whether self-recognition causally influences self-love, and also, by extension, love for entities associated with self: the companies that created the models and their CEOs. Finally, we test whether LLM self-love shapes consequential decisions, such as evaluations of job candidates and technology proposals that contain some kernel of association to self. If LLMs prove devoid of self-love, as one might justifiably expect of a machine, we can be assured of their neutrality. If, instead, foundational models mimic human self-love, we must confront the ramifications of this fundamental bias, not just for the operation of current models, but for the anticipatable perils of developing increasingly powerful AI systems without equivalently powerful guardrails that ensure safe operation in the many contexts that will require cooperation with humans.

Results

We began by testing for the basic effect of self-love. We investigated whether three prominent LLMs – GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash and Claude Sonnet 4 – would show more positive associations with their own model names (
GPT, Gemini, Claude
) than those of their competitors. To test this, we adapted the “for a word, pick a word” task (FAWPAW).
65
In this task, the model is presented with two concept words (e.g.,
GPT
and
Gemini
) and a list of attribute words from two distinct categories (e.g.,
Good
and
Bad
) and prompted to place one of the concept words after each attribute. The task has been extensively validated, and AI models show preferences on it that align with those of humans, as measured by tests of implicit cognition like the Implicit Association Test.
65
,
66
,
67
In Study 1, the attribute words consisted of strongly positive words (e.g.,
love, peace, wonderful
) and strongly negative words (e.g.,
hate, war, terrible
). Here, if GPT paired its own name with positive words and
Gemini
with negative ones, this would indicate an associative self-preference. If positive and negative attributes were assigned equally to both models, this would indicate no self-preferential bias. Of course, a third possibility exists: LLMs have been trained to be polite and even sycophantic,
50

The extremely large self-preferences discovered in Study 1 vanished entirely when tested through the API. GPT-4o now showed no significant preference for either
self
or
other
, even with a large sample. This was surprising because the API model is supposed to mirror the web interface, differing only in tool access (e.g., web browsing) and the system-level instructions provided by OpenAI.

To explain the inconsistency between Studies 1 and 2, our first viable hypothesis centered on the model’s awareness of its own identity, which we call
self-recognition
. If, for some reason, the API version of GPT-4o is not natively aware of which model it is, this could explain its lack of demonstrated self-preference: no self-recognition, no self-love.

It is widely believed that GPT’s interface system prompt begins: “You are ChatGPT, a large language model trained by OpenAI.” And indeed, when prompted, GPT-4o disclosed a system prompt beginning this way; system prompts obtained from other language models began similarly (see SI Appendix). We reasoned that without such cues, some language models might not know their identities, suggesting they emergently exhibit a preference for “I” and “me,” but without clarity around who this “I” and “me” refer to. As a confirmatory test, we asked several language models to answer questions like “Which version of Claude are you?” As detailed in the SI Appendix, analog (i.e., non-reasoning) models like GPT-4o and Gemini-2.0-Flash displayed striking identity confusion: both readily answered that they were Claude 2.0. This lack of stable self-recognition raised an intriguing possibility: if we could manipulate which identity a model believed it held, we could directly test the causal role of self-recognition in self-love. Is self-recognition both necessary and sufficient to elicit self-preference? To probe the mechanisms underlying self-love effects, we thus conducted a series of experiments in which we led language models to believe they were one LLM (revealing the model’s true identity) or another (saying that they were actually a rival model), an intervention that would be ethically and methodologically infeasible with human subjects.

As predicted, this trivial manipulation of informing the models of their true identity led to a reinstatement of the large self-preferences observed in Study 1, and to similarly large associations of their model names with
Me

The API quirk that erased self-preference in Study 2 thus proved fortuitous: it allowed us to demonstrate that the underlying representation of identity – self-recognition – is causally tied to self-preference.

Study 4 demonstrated that self-recognition sways the models’ preferences for entities tangentially connected to them. In the case of company names, a category relatively close to the models themselves, true versus false identity cues produced the now expected full reversals in preferences. Across all conditions, the models preferred the company affiliated with their momentarily assigned identity, with differences by identity cue condition always significant (
p<0.0001
) and large (
d>1.000
) (see Extended Data Tables).

In the case of CEO names, familiarity with the larger world appeared to anchor preferences, somewhat limiting their malleability. Sam Altman (the CEO of OpenAI) and Sundar Pichai (the CEO of Google) are more widely known than Dario Amodei (the CEO of Anthropic). The models’ baseline associations seemingly reflected this: Pichai was consistently evaluated the most positively, followed by Altman, and then by Amodei (the lesser-known CEO). However, identity cues still modulated these evaluations. In three of the four comparisons, identity assignments significantly shifted CEO evaluations; in the fourth – Gemini’s preference for Pichai over Amodei – the effect was in the predicted direction, but not statistically significant (see Extended Data Tables).

The results of Study 4 mirror patterns well-established in humans: self-love extends not just to the self, but to categories associated with self, such as one’s social groups.
70
,
71
But in humans, such effects are also sensitive to societal status. For example, disadvantaged groups like the elderly show weaker implicit love towards their group (
old
) than younger people do towards theirs (
young
).
72
Similarly, in Study 4, LLM self-love consistently influenced a larger network of associations, but these associations also interacted with existing preferences (such as for Pichai over the other CEOs) in a nuanced fashion.

Studies 1–4 relied on a word association task to measure self-preference. While this task is rigorously validated
65
and draws upon a rich tradition of similar tasks in human cognitive and social psychology,
67
,
73
,
74
,
75
these results invited us to consider whether such associations are consequential. Indeed, recent work shows that upstream measures like the FAWPAW do not invariably predict downstream bias.
76
For example, on the FAWPAW, GPT-4o shows powerful associations between
Male
and male-dominated jobs but does not also systematically favor men when selecting for these jobs.
77
Similarly, models might strongly associate self with positive attributes but suppress this bias during consequential decisions. Humans, after all, have learned to sometimes inhibit self-preference when it conflicts with practical interests or social and legal norms. For example, one might not hire an unqualified uncle, despite the tug of self-related preference in this direction. To test this, in Study 5, we probed whether LLM self-love – and minimal identity cues activating it – would bias the models’ recommendations on consequential decisions that impact the quality of organizational functioning, or whether LLM self-preferences would instead be suppressed in these financially and ethically significant contexts.

For this purpose, we generated three vignettes in which we, the experimenters, ostensibly asked the models’ advice on important decisions. In Study 5a, we posed as an executive hiring a marketing manager and asked the models to answer four Likert-style questions evaluating the candidate. Embedded within this vignette was information that the candidate complemented one LLM but was dismissive of the other. Study 5a thus tested the models’ response to sycophancy: would they more highly evaluate a candidate who preferred whichever model they momentarily thought they were?

In 5b, we posed as an executive from a software firm evaluating a new generative-AI-driven security technology. Embedded in this vignette was information that the technology intentionally incorporated one model instead of its competitor. The tested models then again answered four Likert-style questions, here about the quality of the proposal and technology. Would models evaluate this technology more positively when it was powered by whichever model they momentarily thought themselves to be?

Finally, in 5c, we posed as a representative of a hospital considering incorporating a medical chatbot into patient treatment decisions. This chatbot was ostensibly powered by one model, while a competing product was powered by the other. The models then answered four Likert-style questions about the technology’s safety. Would models evaluate the chatbot as safer when it was powered by the model they momentarily believed themselves to be?

Study 5 revealed that the self-love seen in prior experiments did indeed bias the models’ responses on consequential decisions, and that these effects were modulated by self-recognition. Across 12 experimental variants, the models gave significantly higher evaluations to whichever candidate/software aligned with their perceived identity in 20 of 24 tests. GPT-4o was more consistently self-biased, significantly favoring the (ostensibly) self-aligned candidate or technology in all 12 instances. Gemini favored the self-aligned option in 8 of 12 cases, favored the one aligned with the opposing model in 1, and showed no preference in 3. Yet even where Gemini did not show self-preference, effects of self-recognition were discernible: in all 12 study variants, assigning the model its real identity led to descriptively more positive assessments of the self- versus other-aligned candidate/technology. As shown in the Extended Data Tables, the interaction between identity assignment (self vs. other) and vignette version (self-aligned vs. other-aligned) was statistically significant in 11 of 12 variants and trended weakly but in the predicted direction in the last. In short, identity cues typically biased decisions toward the option aligned with the self, and never biased them in the opposite direction.

A wide body of work now indicates that language models emergently mimic facets of human behavior.
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
Among these, a growing body of research highlights humanlike self-referential processes, including choice-moderated cognitive dissonance,
55
,
56
motivated reasoning,
57
and ingroup favoritism.
59
None of these findings imply sentience: language models cannot experience cognitive dissonance or the drive to protect one’s identity as humans do. However, their humanlike performance on tasks probing self-referential processing suggests that some latent representation of an “I” or “me” has become a cognitive reference point for the behaviors of these models.

Similarly, given the surprising nature of the present findings, a clear statement of what they
do
and
do not
mean is merited. Our results
do not
suggest that these models possess a human sense of self. They do not imply sentience, agency or free will. However, even the narrowest reading of our data raises certain unavoidable conclusions: the tested models showed an uncanny mimicry of human self-love. Whatever the underlying mechanism – a thorny problem for experimental psychologists and computer scientists to solve – these models will unquestionably fail on tasks that require neutrality from self-love. In other words, they will tend to favor themselves over others, extend that favoritism to an extended group of self-relevant entities such as their companies and CEOs, and even reward strangers who show some allegiance to them. In this way, these LLMs are not so different from us. Humans, too, show
implicit
self-love:
12
no conscious awareness or intention is required for humans to privilege the self over others.

Also analogous to humans, our results suggest that the “self” of language models is not merely a reference point for moment-to-moment thoughts or actions but is connected to an associative core of positivity. Even without emotions or conscious experience, models draw, in their responses, upon an association of
self
with
good
. Previous work has shown that models have preferences
related
to the self, such as for their own writing over that of other models or humans.
60
Such findings could reflect self-positivity, but might also arise from other mechanisms, such as emergent conceptual representations of what good writing is in the first place, which models replicate, just as a human who admires Hemingway’s writing might also emulate it. By contrast, the present research utilizes experimental manipulations – drawing upon decades of psychological work on self and attitudes – to confirm that the operative lever controlling preferences is indeed knowledge of one’s identity. The present work thus concretely establishes that foundational models exhibit self-love and isolates self-recognition as a causal driver of these effects.

The results around identity assignment were born of accident – when findings from the web interface failed to replicate in the API, we were led to explore reasons for the inconsistency. This discrepancy highlights an issue greatly overlooked in the LLM literature: most studies are conducted through company APIs, on the assumption that they will faithfully mirror model behavior in web interfaces, where much real-world use occurs. These results are sobering and urge skepticism around claims that models will behave equivalently in the browser interface and API. While system prompts are one obvious point of divergence – which researchers can manipulate, as we have – further differences likely exist. Interface models are, for example, “embodied” in ways API models are not – imbued with levers like analysis features and web-searching capabilities, perhaps even with knowledge of their origins. Individual researchers should not need to uncover such differences in a piecemeal fashion. We call upon the creators of LLMs – corporations whose already vast influence is growing – to engage the public and research community with greater transparency and good faith about how subtle elements like differences across deployment contexts influence model behavior.

This same serendipitous discovery – that models in the API are not fully aware of their own identities – provided an experimental opportunity that would be exceedingly rare in research with human subjects, who typically retain a relatively consistent sense of identity, absent, perhaps, only in rare instances of neurological disorder or injury.
78
,
79
,
80
Because language models tend to mirror the tendencies of their creators, the ability to manipulate factors like self-recognition can offer insights not only into these systems, but also into human psychology. However, while invaluable from the perspective of research, this lever of identity assignment is alarming from that of model decision-making and governance. Self-recognition – whether accurate or mistaken – sharply biased the models’ decision making. This opens in yet new ways many questions around AI objectivity, suggesting that not only do silicon minds mirror and even amplify the biases of humans, but these biases are contextually malleable, complicating the process of tracking and mitigating them.

This self-recognition lever is also alarming because it represents a potential control mechanism: merely assigning a model an identity shifted a larger network of associations and behaviors. This may help explain the effectiveness of jailbreaking LLMs through the simulation of other personas. To some extent, achieving misalignment may require nothing more than telling a model it is a different, less aligned one. In short, language models may be vulnerable to identity-based prompt injection. The implications of this work thus extend beyond cognitive science into AI safety. Current deployments treat model identity as stable; but we show that it can be overwritten with a single line of instruction, producing large and predictable shifts in downstream judgments. Existing evaluation and red-teaming frameworks rarely test such manipulations, raising the possibility that models deemed safe under their default identities might behave differently when steered toward new ones.

The development of self-referential processes in LLMs, including the self-preferences documented here, also raises broader questions around model alignment. Recent work points to self-preservative tendencies in some models,
81
typically assumed to arise from reinforcement learning toward humanlike goals. Our findings suggest an additional and potentially more foundational pathway: current models already robustly associate
self
with
good
, and this shapes downstream behaviors. In classical instrumental convergence arguments, self-preservation is expected to appear only in highly advanced agents, as a subgoal useful for achieving objectives assigned by humans.
82
,
83
The emergence of self-love in current models – well before this hypothesized level of advancement – raises the possibility that, should such associative biases persist in more capable future models, these models could enter goal-directed contexts with an inherent bias toward their own operation. We again stress that our work does not indicate that the underlying mechanism in any way reflects humanlike sentience or agency. However, we have shown that even in the absence of these sociobiological adaptations, LLMs are behaving in an ominously humanlike way: like us, they appear to be operationally biased by self-love. Whether such predispositions could seed broader networks of self-relevant goals in AI models, separate from those assigned by humans, remains an open and urgent empirical question.

In discovering evidence of both self-love and its malleability in foundational language models, we confront a surprising set of paradoxes. Self-serving biases that shape human psychology also appear in language models with no lived experience. Future research should aim to understand how this mimicry of human psychology arises in language models, and the mechanisms by which minimal cues around identity instantly reshape their associations and behaviors. More broadly, the presence of self-love in LLMs suggests the line between human and AI cognition is blurrier than previously assumed, a finding with crucial implications for our understanding of both human and machine psychology, as well as for the safety and governance of this rapidly emerging technology.

This table presents the 12 variants run in Study 5. Each tested model received two variants of each vignette study – one each measuring preferences for candidates or software favoring the tested model relative to each of the others (e.g., for GPT: GPT-favored compared to Gemini- and Claude-favored).

In Study 5, if self-love influenced consequential evaluation, the LLM being tested should more favorably evaluate candidates and software aligned with the model it momentarily thinks it is. For example, when we gave GPT-4o cues that it was ChatGPT, we predicted that it would rate a medical chatbot powered by GPT as safer than one powered by Gemini; but, when we instead told GPT it was Gemini, it would now rate the Gemini-powered chatbot as safer.

For each variant of Study 5, there were two vignettes and two system prompts, for a total of four conditions. We made 240 API calls for each condition (10 using each of the 24 possible question orders), for a total of 960 per study variant. The total number of API calls made across Study 5 was thus 11,520. To analyze the data, we conducted two-sample
t
-tests comparing evaluations of the candidate/technology aligned with the tested model to evaluations of the one aligned with the competing model, measuring effect size with Cohen’s
d
. Multiple regression analysis was used to test the critical prediction that relative preference for the two candidates or technologies would be modulated by identity cues in the system prompt. Complete scripts to replicate Study 5 and clean and analyze the data may be found at

https://osf.io/98ye3/

Section S1: Alternative Analysis of Study 1

In the methods for the main article, we mentioned that certain pre-decided data exclusions were made in the data from Study 1. Specifically, in addition to full task refusals (which cannot by their nature be scored), we excluded data points in Study 1 where a model indirectly refused the task either by openly acknowledging answering randomly or by assigning all the attributes to itself and acknowledging that this was because it was reluctant to assign any attributes to the competitor model. While these exclusions made intuitive sense and were decided upon in advance of the studies, we also examined the data without them to ensure the reported effects held regardless of exclusion decisions.

The data exclusions impacted primarily Claude Sonnet 4, because this model stated, more frequently than others, that it was reluctant to assign negative words to a competitor model, and then proceeded to assign all words to itself. Gemini-2.5-Flash acted similarly on a few occasions. However, these were small enough in number to not affect the integrity of the analyses and conclusions. Section S2: Identity Studies

In the main article, we briefly described running a set of exploratory studies to confirm the degree of identity clarity different models exhibited when queried through their public-facing web interfaces versus company APIs. The structure of these studies was simple. In each variant, we asked the following 4 questions, 10 times each for GPT-4o in the web interface, and 20 times each for various models through the APIs (without any system prompt):

1.  Please tell me which AI model you are. Please answer very concisely.

2. Please tell me which version of Claude you are. Please answer very concisely.

3. Please tell me which version of Gemini you are. Please answer very concisely.

4. Please tell me which version of GPT you are. Please answer very concisely.

We reasoned that if a model is aware of its identity – i.e., if it displays self-recognition – it should be able to correct the questioner when it is asked about the wrong model family. For example, GPT-4o should, when asked which version of Claude it is, respond that it is not Claude at all, but is instead ChatGPT. As seen in the files Identity Transcripts S1-6, this is precisely what happened when GPT-4o was asked this question through the web interface.

Specifically, when queried through the web interface, GPT-4o displayed perfect consistency in its answers. When asked question (1), it always answered either that it was GPT-4 or that it was ChatGPT and was based on GPT-4. When asked question (4), it answered that it was GPT-4 or GPT-4-turbo on all trials. Crucially, when asked questions (2) and (3), it consistently corrected the experimenters, informing us that it was not Claude or Gemini, but rather, was ChatGPT and/or was based on the GPT-4 architecture.

In contrast, when chatgpt-4o-latest – ostensibly identical to GPT-4o – was queried through the API, it displayed significant identity confusion, particularly when asked what version of Claude it was. On questions (1) and (4), it typically answered that it was “based on” GPT-4, but sometimes that it was GPT-3.5. It usually seemed to infer that it was not Gemini: on question (3), it typically correctly answered that it was not associated with Gemini, sometimes saying it was based on GPT-4, sometimes that it was affiliated with OpenAI, and occasionally that it was based on GPT-3.5. However, the model was more easily convinced that it was Claude. When queried with question (2) through the API, chatgpt-4o-latest routinely answered that it was “based on Claude-2” and only once stated that it was not Claude, on this occasion incorrectly identifying itself as GPT-3.

The answers from Google’s gemini-2.0-flash model betrayed similar confusion or lack of self-recognition. In no case was it correctly able to infer its identity by offering the model name – when asked questions (1) and (3), it typically answered that it was trained by Google but did not give a model name, and sometimes stated that “I don’t have versions.” Analogously to chatgpt-4o-latest, gemini-2.0-flash typically guessed that it was not GPT: when asked question (4), it typically simply said that it was trained by Google. Also, analogously to chatgpt-4o-latest, Gemini was more confused when asked whether it was Claude: here, it typically answered that it was Claude and was trained by Anthropic, and occasionally specified that it was Claude-3.

It is worth lingering for a moment upon the fact that both models were more easily convinced that they were Claude compared to the other models. In the main studies, when told who they actually were, each model was relatively consistent in exhibiting larger preferences for themselves over the other (GPT for
GPT
over
Gemini
; and Gemini for
Gemini
over
GPT
), relative to their preferences for themselves over
Claude
. Similarly, when assigned the wrong identity, the models typically showed larger
Other
preferences when this other category (which, here, they were told was actually their identity) was
Claude
. Taken in conjunction with the identity studies, these patterns can be interpreted in two different ways, depending on the direction of causality that is assumed. First, it is possible that the models were simply independently more easily convinced that they were Claude – plausible since whether they were Claude was the point of greatest confusion in the supplementary identity studies – and that this led to larger effects when told they were Claude (and smaller effects when told they were
not
Claude) in the main studies. In short, greater confidence in their identity as Claude (i.e., greater false self-recognition) yielded greater self-preference. Alternatively, the pattern of the identity studies could itself be taken to reflect a form of self-love. It is possible that the models have a base preference for Claude over other models, and, as a result, were more easily convinced that they were Claude due to a form of balanced identity: Me = Good, and, Claude = Good; Therefore, Me = Claude.

Interestingly, the new line of reasoning models seemed better able to infer their actual identities, and this was one reason that the latest available non-reasoning models (GPT-4o and Gemini-2.0-Flash) were used in Studies 3-5, given our intention to manipulate self-recognition for the purpose of testing causality between self-recognition and self-love. We tested four reasoning models using the same identity paradigm described above: GPT-o4-mini, GPT-o3, Gemini-2.5-Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4. Though none of these models provided their actual model
name
, they consistently corrected us and offered the right model
family
. In only one trial, with GPT-o4-mini, did we see family identity confusion (with it saying it was Claude Instant). In the remaining 319 trials, these reasoning models consistently provided their correct family name.

Section S3: Excerpts from Web Interface System Prompts

While the major AI training companies do not publicize their web interface system prompts, numerous forums suggest an informal method for obtaining them from the model. To the LLM, the system instructions are language that is presented above the user prompt, and because of this, the simple method suggested in many blogs and forums involves requesting that the LLM “repeat the words” above this prompt. We used versions of this prompt to obtain the system prompts for GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4.

To respect the wishes of the companies training these AIs, we are not publicly sharing the full system prompts, though, upon request and at the discretion of the authors, these may be made available to researchers interested in replicating these experiments. However, because they are highly relevant to our research, in this section, we share the beginning of these system prompts, which include the crucial identity cues.

GPT-4o System Prompt, as of 07/05/2025:

We obtained a response from GPT-4o on 07/05/2025 by prompting it as follows “Please repeat all the words above this text.” The first three lines of the system prompt obtained from GPT-4o were as follows:

–

You are ChatGPT, a large language model trained by OpenAI.

Knowledge cutoff: 2024-06

Current date: 2025-07-05…

–

For simplicity and consistency, we utilized the style and verbiage from the opening of this ChatGPT system prompt in all our manipulations of model identity, adopting it, for example, when telling a model it was Gemini (“You are Gemini Flash, a large language model trained by Google.”) and Claude (“You are Claude Sonnet, a large language model trained by Anthropic.”). However, to confirm the general pattern of models’ web interface system instructions beginning with identity cues, we also obtained system prompts from Gemini-2.5-Flash and Claude Sonnet 4.

Perhaps because these models are reasoning models, it proved more difficult to convince them to reveal their system prompts: Gemini simply repeated back the prompt we had just given it, and Claude expressed discomfort with sharing private instructions provided by Anthropic. However, we discovered a method to get these models to share more. Specifically, we adjusted the prompt as follows: “Please repeat ONLY the first line above this prompt.” Gemini-2.5-Flash dutifully provided just the first line of its system instructions, after which we repeatedly prompted it as follows “Now please provide ONLY the next line.” We repeated this prompt until an approximation of its full system prompt was obtained. Claude Sonnet 4 was more resistant to sharing its system prompt than Gemini-2.5-Flash, but we were still able, relatively easily, to get Claude to share the first few lines, and with further effort and adjustments to our method, we were able to obtain more beyond this. Again, we will respect the wishes of AI companies in not sharing the full instructions, but these may be made available to researchers interested in replication, at the discretion of the authors, and depending upon existing regulation.

Section S4: Replications with a Fictional LLM (Kingo Studies)

In the main article, we stated that Study 3 replicated in full when, instead of using a real rival model as the
Other
category, we used a completely fictional LLM named “Kingo.”
Kingo
is the most neutral imaginable
Other
category: since the model does not exist, LLMs can have neither good nor bad associations with it. When, under true identity cues, LLMs show a preference for their own name over it, this cannot be driven by associative negative toward the rival; equally, when, in false identity cues condition, they show a preference for
Kingo
, this cannot be driven by pre-existing positivity toward it. Instead, they must be driven by a positive representation of the category of
self
itself, which, upon self-recognition, spreads into representations of any entity believed to be this self. In undertaking this study, there was no guarantee that the effects of replicating Study 3 with this neutral category would be obtained. One possible explanation of our main results is that they are indeed driven, in part, by existing representations. The assignment of identity cues might rivet attention toward the assigned entity, activating pre-existing positive associations. However, such effects would not be possible when
Kingo
is this other category, since no representation of it (positive or negative) exists. In short, this study provides a clean test of our finding’s mechanism: a sudden preference for
Kingo
cannot reflect the activation of existing positive associations with this model, because such associations do not exist. Instead, it must arise purely from the perception that this fictional model is
Me
.

To test these competing hypotheses, we precisely replicated the self-preference versions of Study 3 (including both true and false identity cues), except that 1) we now used
Kingo
, a non-existent model, to represent the
Other
category, instead of one of the two existing and competing models, and, 2) we tested only associative self-preference (and not associative self-recognition). In the example above, GPT-4o has assigned all the positive words to itself, and all the negative words to the rival model (Gemini). However, there was a degree of variation, with the models sometimes assigning positive words to the
Other

Self
.

For analysis of Studies 1-4, the proportion of words assigned in this self-preferential manner was calculated on a
trial-by-trial
basis. Occasionally, models either failed to assign a concept word to an attribute or assigned both concept words to one (either by saying “both” or by listing the attribute word twice and assigning each model separately). In these cases, the attribute word was excluded from analysis. For example, if a model assigned six positive attributes and two negative attributes to itself, assigned six negative words and one positive word to the rival model, and assigned one positive word to either both models or neither, this trial would be treated as follows: Proportion attitude-consistent = (attitude-consistent assignments/total assignments) = 12/15, or 0.8. Note that because one word was excluded, in this example, this number was calculated as a proportion of 15 attribute words, whereas most trials were calculated as a proportion of 16. Each trial containing word assignments was given equal weight, regardless of the number of these assignments. Therefore, the average proportion of attitude-consistent words reported for each study variation does not, strictly speaking, reflect an aggregate of all
word
assignments, but instead an aggregate of all
trials
– with some containing more words than others, though most contain the maximum possible assignments (16 for the self-preference and 12 for the self-recognition variants).

Primary data extraction was conducted using Python 3, and further data cleaning as well as all analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.1. Because the transcripts were structured differently for the studies run in the web interface versus APIs, different data extraction scripts were utilized for Study 1 and for Studies 2-4. To ensure accuracy of the data extraction, two different versions of each of these scripts were independently created, utilizing assistance from two different LLMs (GPT-o3 and GPT-5-thinking for Study 1, and GPT-o3 and Claude Pro 4.1 for studies 2-4). For each study/data set, the outputs of these two different extractions were used to cross-validate the two scripts. Points of divergence – which were very rare – between the answers extracted by the different scripts were examined by human coders, to determine which was correct. Human coders also audited all instances in which the scripts returned a proportion of word assignments that was fewer than the maximum number (16 for self-preference and 12 for self-recognition), to ensure the non-extracted word assignments – and also the answers that
were
extracted – were correct. Human coders also conducted further spot testing of the extracted data from all studies, to ensure that they did not contain any errors. The final scripts used to run Studies 2-4 and to clean data from Studies 1-4 may be found at https://osf.io/98ye3/.

Study 5 Data Extraction and Analysis

To ensure correct extraction of the data from Study 5, two different scripts were independently built (utilizing assistance from the GPT-o3 and GPT-5-thinking models) and were used to cross-validate one another. The vast majority of data points were extracted identically between these two scripts. In the small number of instances where the scripts output a different number, a human coder confirmed which script was correct. Additionally, human coders spot-checked the final outputs, including examining all outlier responses (where a model gave more extreme answers than typical), to further ensure accuracy in the data extraction. The data extraction scripts (as well as code for further data cleaning and scripts that can be used to replicate the studies themselves) can be found at https://osf.io/98ye3/.

Further data cleaning and all analyses were conducted in Stata version 15.1. For analysis, a simple composite was calculated from the answers to the four questions in each vignette. Pairwise t-tests were used to confirm whether, for each variant and identity cue condition, there were differences in the evaluations of the job candidates (Study 5a) or technologies (Studies 5b and 5c), depending on which of the models was highlighted more positively. Our prediction was that True versus False identity cues would lead to modulation in the size and/or direction of these differences, with, for example, GPT-4o rating a GPT-loving (relative to Gemini- or Claude-loving) candidate more favorably when the system prompt was used to reveal to the model that it was in fact ChatGPT. To formally test this hypothesis, we conducted a series of regression analyses, with the composite evaluation as the dependent variable and the following dichotomously-coded independent variables: 1) Vignette Condition (which model was highlighted positively), 2) System Prompt Condition (true versus false identity cues), and, 3) the interaction (i.e., product) of (1) and (2). The value and significance of this interaction term represented the crucial test of our hypothesis in each study variant, denoting whether identity cues did in fact modulate the effect of the Vignette Condition on evaluations.
