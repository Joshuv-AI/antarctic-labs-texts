Comparing deep learning and Fourier series models for equipment failure prediction in predictive industrial maintenance 4.0
Authors: Houria Abouloifa, Mohamed Bahaj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent innovations in the field of smart manufacturing call for high machine availability, good product quality, and a high degree of flexibility in production processes. In addition to reducing downtime and increasing asset occupation, predictive maintenance also has the potential to improve safety and reduce maintenance costs by analyzing data to identify the root causes of equipment failures and prevent future occurrences. In this study, we develop a predictive maintenance model using both a Fourier series-based mathematical approach and a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) deep learning algorithm, applied to synthetic multivariate sensor dataset representing industrial equipment behavior. The goal is to identify the most accurate method for forecasting machine failures and minimizing production interruptions. Our results demonstrate that the LSTM model outperforms the Fourier series model, achieving a lower MAE (0.0385), MSE (0.1085), and RMSE (0.3294), highlighting the superior performance of data-driven sequential learning in capturing failure dynamics. Statistical analysis using a paired t-test confirmed that the performance differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001), and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Additional residual visualizations further supported the conclusions.Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI), Deep learning, Fourier series, LSTM maintenance 4.0Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Computer science

Research methodology

It can be difficult to choose an appropriate learning algorithm for a given domain’s target application. The reason for this is that different learning algorithms have different goals, and depending on the properties of the data, even learning algorithms in the same category may provide different results21.

As discussed in the previous sections, our decision to use deep learning rather than traditional machine learning techniques stems from its strong capability to handle unstructured and complex data characteristics that are typical of maintenance-related datasets, including those used in our study. It has been demonstrated that deep learning performs better in several areas, like object recognition and image classification. It has also become more well-liked in fields like finance, where time-series data is crucial. Another area where data is gathered over time to track an asset’s condition and look for patterns to anticipate failures is predictive maintenance, which can also profit from some deep learning algorithms Because they excel at learning from sequences.

In this paper and as schematize in Fig. 2 above, we start from a synthetic predictive maintenance dataset of past machines failure incident, to allow controlled experimentation and systematic observation of model behavior under known temporal patterns. The dataset comprised multiple sensor channels measuring variables such as process temperature, rotational speed, and torque, along with failure event annotations. This data that has been then cleaned in a pre-processing step. Pre-processing remains one of the most crucial stages in predictive maintenance pipelines, especially when working with high-dimensional or noisy sensor data. While many studies rely on simple linear correlation to remove redundant features, this approach can miss non-linear dependencies that are often critical in industrial processes. To address this, researchers have proposed advanced techniques such as mutual information, kernel-based selection, and robust dimensionality reduction methods. For example22,23, demonstrate multivariate and non-parametric outlier detection strategies suitable for complex sensor networks. Similarly24, highlight the need for careful variable filtering in high-velocity industrial data streams. In econometric applications25, show that robust variable selection significantly improves model stability and interpretability. These works underscore the importance of incorporating more comprehensive pre-processing methods in both research and practical deployment.

To gain deeper insight into the dataset, we begin with an exploratory data analysis to examine variable distributions and identify meaningful patterns. Based on this analysis, we perform feature selection to retain only the most relevant variables and reduce noise by eliminating insignificant or redundant data. Next, we construct a training model using historical failure cases identified over time. A corresponding test model is then developed from this training data. For the predictive task, we implement and compare two modeling approaches:

LSTM network: We construct an LSTM network in our notebook to predict when a machine will fail in the future based on simulated sensor results, allowing maintenance to be scheduled ahead of time. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are one of the deep learning techniques that the predictive maintenance field finds particularly attractive26. This feature makes it feasible to look back over longer periods of time to uncover failure trends, which is advantageous for their applications utilizing time series data. The LSTM model is trained exclusively on structured time-series data generated from our synthetic dataset, which was intentionally designed to emulate realistic sensor signals in predictive maintenance contexts. These signals incorporate periodic components, gradual degradation trends, and controlled noise levels, rather than being purely random sequences. To clarify, no arbitrary or unstructured random data was used in the training phase.

Fourier series: we apply a spectral analysis of the signals given by our dataset. a signal can be converted from the time domain to the frequency domain using this mathematical tool. Fourier series can be adapted for predictive maintenance, especially in the context of analyzing time-series data related to equipment performance or condition monitoring. Predictive maintenance relies on detecting patterns or changes in the behavior of machines that could indicate an impending failure.

Afterwards, we compare the results as we adjust the metrics. The models are tested and results are evaluated based on MAE, MSE and RMSE performance indicator. A hyperparameter optimization is then applied on both models to minimize the validation Mean Absolute Error (MAE) on a held-out portion of the training set. After that, a paired t-test was conducted on the absolute errors of LSTM and Fourier predictions to assess whether differences were statistically significant. Additionally, 95% confidence intervals were computed for each model’s MAE and RMSE. Finally, two simple baselines were added for contextual performance comparison (Persistence Model that Predicts the last observed value as the next value and Linear Regression that Fits a linear model to the input window), residual histograms and quantile–quantile plots were used to inspect error distributions and comparative bar charts summarizing MAE, MSE, and RMSE across models were generated.

Deep learning-based failure prediction model

Deep learning, as an important branch of machine learning, has achieved significant advances applications to many fields. By using RNN, which is the evolution of Multi-layer Perception and the most skilled deep learning approach in settling sequential data, the feature extraction procedure does not have to be processed manually.

In our study, we employed Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, a specialized type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) designed to address the limitations of standard RNNs in capturing long-term dependencies. While RNNs are commonly used in natural language processing, LSTMs are particularly well-suited for time-series forecasting due to their ability to retain information over extended sequences. This makes them highly effective for modeling sequential patterns, such as equipment failure trends in predictive maintenance, as well as other applications like speech recognition and language translation.

LSTM networks achieve this by using gating mechanisms—namely, input, forget, and output gates—that regulate the flow of information through the network. These gates determine which information is retained, updated, or discarded as it passes through the memory cell, allowing the model to maintain a dynamic internal state that serves as short-term memory. The hidden state is updated using the current input, the previous hidden state, and the current cell state, as illustrated in (Fig. 16). This architecture enables LSTMs to effectively learn complex, non-linear relationships across multiple variables. As demonstrated in recent studies29,30, LSTM models outperform traditional techniques such as decline curve analysis by accounting for simultaneous influences of multiple features in time-series datasets.

Fourier series-based failure prediction model

The applications of Fourier analysis are extensive in the electronics and instrumentation industry31. A Fourier series is a way to represent a function as a sum of periodic components, and in practical terms, it decomposes a periodic function into a sum of sine and cosine functions These functions are orthogonal, meaning they capture different aspects of the original function’s variation over time.

Recurring occurrences can be identified and their periodicity ascertained using Fourier analysis. Signals are represented by Fourier transforms as linear combinations of sine waves that indicate the level of activity at particular frequencies. It is possible to differentiate behaviors of different frequencies if they are combined into a single signal32. Performances measuring

After creating the model, training and evaluation of its performance were carried out using various metrics. Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) are the most often used metrics for evaluating accuracy for continuous variables34.

MAE

Mean Absolute Error (MAE): MAE determines the average size of errors in a group of forecasts without accounting for their direction. The average of the absolute differences between observation and prediction for the test sample is weighted equally for each individual difference.

MSE

If outliers need to be identified, MSE can be employed. Because of the L2 norm, MSE is really excellent at giving these points more weights: It is evident that the squaring portion of the function increases the error if the model ultimately produces a single extremely poor forecast35.

, n: number of observations/rows.

MSE is more sensitive to outliers and can be useful in identifying models that make large mistakes.

RMSE

Quadratic equations can be scored using the root mean squared error (RMSE) formula, which also determines the average error magnitude. It is the mean of the squared differences between the actual observations and the predictions.

, n: number of observations/rows.

Because MAE is expressed in the same units as the original data, it can be readily interpreted: it shows the average absolute inaccuracy of the predictions and it is therefore easy to grasp. MSE is expressed in squared units of the original data, it is primarily utilized as a step in between when calculating RMSE because it is less interpretable on its own. Finally, RMSE penalizes greater errors more severely than MAE and it tends to be larger than MAE even if it is expressed in the same units as the original data.

Since these metrics are negatively orientated scores, smaller values of all three metrics indicate a better model, but their relative sizes help identify error characteristics. Lower values of RMSE imply better fit, and the better the model performs, the lower its mean absolute error number must be.

Results shows that LSTM model has better performance then Fourier series model with and MAE score of 0.11 (1.64 point lower than Fourier series), an MSE score of 0.4 (3.83 point lower than Fourier series) and a RMSE score of 0.63 (1.43 point lower than Fourier series) (Fig. 20). In both models, it can be observed that MAE and RMSE are close, this suggests that there are relatively few outliers, and the errors are distributed fairly evenly. Also, the fact that RMSE is higher than MAE suggests that some larger errors exist, but they are not extreme. MSE is harder to interpret directly but reinforces that the squared errors are not too high.

While deep learning models such as LSTM are often favored for their ability to capture complex nonlinear and non-stationary patterns in time-series data, the Fourier series approach offers distinct advantages rooted in mathematical interpretability, computational efficiency, and signal decomposition capabilities. In industrial settings where sensor data often exhibit periodic or quasi-periodic behaviors, such as vibrations, temperature cycles, or rotating machinery signals, the Fourier series provides a powerful tool for isolating dominant frequency components and identifying underlying failure patterns. Unlike black-box deep learning models, Fourier-based models are fully transparent and can offer insights into harmonic trends and periodic disturbances that may precede mechanical failures. Furthermore, their low computational overhead makes them particularly suitable for real-time or edge-device deployment in Industry 4.0 environments where resources may be limited. By transforming the predictive maintenance problem into the frequency domain, the Fourier series model leverages well-established principles of signal processing to detect anomalies, cyclic degradation, or resonant failures. This approach is especially relevant for use cases where domain experts require interpretable results or where historical data is limited, making it difficult to train deep learning models effectively. Therefore, the inclusion of the Fourier approach in this comparative study is not only justified but essential, as it represents a class of lightweight, interpretable, and mathematically rigorous models that remain highly relevant in industrial predictive maintenance contexts.

A comparison of results before and after hyperparameter tuning reveals the critical role of optimization in ensuring a fair evaluation between LSTM and Fourier models. We recognize that, in a real scenario, additional challenges could be faced and further experiments and model iterations would be necessary to achieve an even more refined solution suited to the specific needs of the problem. In fact, the comparative results presented in this study provide a structured view of how LSTM and Fourier series models behave when applied to controlled, synthetic sensor data. The findings confirm that in the context of this synthetic dataset, the LSTM model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy when applied to signals that contained both periodic and non-periodic components, while the Fourier approach remains valuable in contexts where periodicity dominates and computational efficiency is a priority. However, these observations are derived from an artificial dataset, specifically designed to contain known periodic and non-periodic components, and thus cannot be assumed to directly translate to real-world industrial data without adaptation and further testing.

We explicitly acknowledge that the Fourier method, in its current univariate implementation, is limited in capturing cross-sensor dependencies and noisy, non-periodic signals. Conversely, while the LSTM model can leverage the multivariate structure of the data, it is more resource-intensive, requires careful hyperparameter tuning, and may be challenging to deploy in constrained industrial environments. These trade-offs highlight that model selection must be informed by the specific operational, computational, and data characteristics of the target deployment scenario.

Implications and limitations

Several considerations may explain why the LSTM model significantly outperforms the Fourier series model in forecasting equipment failures under simulated industrial conditions. In fact, deep learning models are better suited for capturing complex, non-linear, and non-periodic behaviors in sensor data: LSTM’s ability to learn long-term dependencies and temporal patterns makes it highly effective in scenarios where failure signatures are subtle, irregular, or influenced by multiple interacting variables.

In contrast, the Fourier series model is limited by its reliance on periodicity and its sensitivity to signal noise. Although Fourier analysis is computationally efficient and interpretable, it lacks the adaptability needed for handling noisy or dynamic time-series data common in industrial environments. This limitation became evident in our experiments, where the Fourier model showed higher prediction error across all metrics.

While the LSTM demonstrates clear predictive advantages in this synthetic, controlled setting, the Fourier model remains appealing for its interpretability and computational efficiency in scenarios with strongly periodic signals. We conclude that within the controlled setting of synthetic multivariate sensor data, the LSTM showed a stronger ability to approximate complex patterns compared to the Fourier model, but emphasize that these findings are bounded by the exploratory scope of synthetic-data experimentation and should be validated on real-world datasets.

Despite the strong performance of the LSTM model, several limitations must be acknowledged. The dataset used in this study is synthetic, which, while controlled and reproducible, may not fully reflect the variability and noise present in real-world industrial systems. The dataset was intentionally designed to mimic realistic sensor data patterns observed in predictive maintenance applications within Industry 4.0 environments. The following design principles were applied to bridge simulation and industrial reality:

Many rotating machinery and industrial equipment systems (motors, pumps, turbines) exhibit cyclic vibration signatures tied to operational speed or load cycles. Our synthetic dataset integrates dominant harmonic frequencies aligned with those reported in vibration analysis literature, ensuring that the Fourier model can operate under representative periodic conditions:

Real-world equipment often experiences progressive wear or performance loss over time. The synthetic signals embed slowly increasing amplitude in key frequency bands and drift in mean values.

Industrial sensor readings are rarely noise-free due to environmental factors, sensor calibration drift, and measurement uncertainty.

Sudden deviations, such as spikes or drops in signal amplitude, were introduced to simulate anomalies preceding failure events, reflecting early-warning signs often seen in field-collected time series.

By embedding these real-world characteristics into the synthetic dataset, the comparative performance of LSTM and Fourier series models is assessed under conditions that reflect practical predictive maintenance challenges. Although the dataset remains synthetic, its structure and variability are rooted in documented industrial phenomena, improving the transferability of insights to real deployments.

The use of synthetic data allows for a clean evaluation of each model’s ability to capture known patterns, isolate noise, and generalize over time. While real-world deployment requires validation on industrial datasets, the synthetic setup enables a focused investigation of model mechanics, interpretability, and comparative performance under clearly defined constraints. The primary goal of this study is therefore not to claim production-readiness, but to provide empirical insights into the suitability of signal-based versus deep learning models in predictive maintenance pipelines.

While deep learning model requires more computational resources and careful hyperparameter tuning, which can present barriers to adoption in resource-constrained settings, future research should explore hybrid models that combine the interpretability of signal processing with the adaptability of deep learning. Additionally, validating these models on real-world datasets with diverse machine types and failure modes is essential to assess their generalizability and practical utility in production environments.

The hyperparameter optimization process ensures that the reported performance is representative of a reasonably tuned model under the given dataset and experimental setup. While we recognize that more sophisticated search strategies could potentially yield further improvements, our grid search provides a robust and reproducible selection framework, mitigating the risk of overfitting and eliminating arbitrary parameter choice.

It is important to note that the comparative performance observed here cannot be directly generalized to industrial environments without validation on real-world sensor datasets. These results should therefore be interpreted as indicative insights rather than prescriptive conclusions.

Conclusion

Predictive Maintenance 4.0 is a revolutionary step in industrial operations that uses cutting-edge technologies like the Internet of Things (IoT), machine learning, and big data analytics to optimize equipment maintenance and minimize operational downtime. By using real-time data, predictive models, and automated decision-making processes, organizations can transition from reactive or scheduled maintenance to a more efficient, condition-based approach, which not only reduces the risk of unexpected failures but also lowers costs, extends asset life, and boosts overall productivity. As industries continue to change, the successful implementation of Predictive Maintenance 4.0 will require investments in qualified staff who can handle and interpret the data that is produced.

This work should be interpreted as an exploratory, comparative study rather than a conclusive benchmark of predictive maintenance methods. By using a synthetic dataset with controlled characteristics, we have been able to systematically observe the behaviors and limitations of two fundamentally different modeling paradigms. The results demonstrate that LSTM models can offer substantial accuracy gains in complex signal environments, whereas Fourier-based models provide interpretability and speed in more periodic contexts. However, the controlled nature of the dataset limits the direct applicability of these findings to real-world cases. Future work will focus on validating both approaches with industrial datasets, extending the Fourier framework to multivariate signals, and exploring hybrid methods that combine the interpretability of Fourier analysis with the adaptive power of deep learning.

Planning and strategies are needed for the maintenance task in order to satisfy productivity, quality, and safety requirements. The development of Industry ideas presents both new possibilities and difficulties and the use of artificial intelligence technologies is a must.
