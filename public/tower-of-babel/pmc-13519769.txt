Infusion rate adjustment in enzyme replacement therapy with pabinafusp alfa for mucopolysaccharidosis II
Authors: Kimitoshi Nakamura, Norio Sakai, Hideaki Hirai, Naoko Takasao, Ryo Ibaraki, Tatsuyoshi Yamamoto, Yuji Sato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsEnzyme replacement therapy (ERT) for mucopolysaccharidosis II (MPS II) requires long‐term, weekly intravenous infusions often lasting over 3 h each time, which can burden paediatric patients and caregivers and negatively affect their quality of life and treatment compliance. The aim of this study was to assess whether shortening infusion duration impacts the long‐term efficacy and safety of ERT.MethodsA post hoc analysis was conducted using 260 weeks of clinical data from 27 Japanese patients with MPS II who received pabinafusp alfa at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg/week during a Phase II/III clinical trial and an extension study. Individual adjustments to the weekly infusion duration were allowed only during the extension study. Safety was assessed through the incidence of adverse events and infusion‐associated reactions, while efficacy was evaluated by measuring heparan sulfate and dermatan sulfate levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), serum and urine, as well as assessing liver and spleen volumes and neurocognitive development scores.ResultsShorter infusion times were not associated with increased infusion‐associated reactions or other adverse events. Heparan sulfate and dermatan sulfate levels in the CSF, serum and urine, as well as liver and spleen volumes were not affected by changes in infusion rates, indicating sustained therapeutic efficacy.ConclusionsInfusion rate adjustments were not associated with notable changes in the safety or efficacy of ERT with pabinafusp alfa in patients with MPS II. Clinicians may safely consider decreasing infusion times on a case‐by‐case basis to improve patients' quality of life and treatment compliance.Keywords: blood–brain barrier, enzyme replacement therapy, infusion rate, mucopolysaccharidosis II, pabinafusp alfa, safety

INTRODUCTION

Mucopolysaccharidosis II (MPS II; Hunter syndrome) is a rare genetic lysosomal storage disorder caused by a deficiency of the enzyme iduronate‐2‐sulfatase (IDS), 1 which leads to a systemic accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), such as heparan sulfate (HS) and dermatan sulfate (DS), resulting in a wide spectrum of somatic and neurological symptoms. 2 , 3 The clinical presentation and prognosis of MPS II can range from the severe (neuronopathic or rapidly progressive) phenotype to the attenuated (non‐neuronopathic or slowly progressive) phenotype. 4 Patients with the severe phenotype typically present with progressive and severe central nervous system (CNS) involvement in addition to somatic symptoms, while those with the attenuated phenotype primarily exhibit somatic manifestations with little or no CNS involvement.

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with weekly infusions of recombinant human IDS (idursulfase) is the current standard of care. While IDS ameliorates somatic symptoms, it does not address the neurological symptoms of MPS II, since the blood–brain barrier (BBB) prevents large molecules, such as enzymes, from reaching the CNS. 5 , 6 To address this limitation, pabinafusp alfa (JR‐141), a fusion protein of IDS and an anti‐human transferrin receptor antibody, was developed. Pabinafusp alfa is able to penetrate the BBB and reach the CNS, allowing it to simultaneously treat both neurological and somatic symptoms. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 It was approved for clinical use in Japan in 2021 13 and is being further studied in an ongoing global Phase III clinical trial (NCT04573023) and its extension study (NCT05594992).

Following diagnosis, early initiation of ERT is vital to minimize the progressive nature of GAG accumulation and resulting organ damage. 14 However, ERT requires long‐term weekly intravenous infusions, which can pose significant challenges in paediatric clinical practice. These challenges include difficulties with repeated venipunctures, leakages from intravenous lines, complications with central venous port placement, 15 and the need to manage infusion‐associated reactions (IARs), 16 which may necessitate slower infusion rates, leading to prolonged infusion times. These issues can lead to frequent extended hospital visits that adversely affect quality of life for paediatric patients and their families and undermine long‐term treatment compliance, despite the critical need for sustained ERT to manage this progressive, life‐threatening disease. 17

To manage IARs, strategies such as shortening infusion times and administering prophylactic medication have been implemented. 18 However, there are concerns that modifying infusion parameters may impact the pharmacodynamics of pabinafusp alfa and potentially compromise the safety and efficacy of ERT.

To assess whether adjusting the infusion rate (and consequently the infusion duration) affects the pharmacodynamics of pabinafusp alfa, we conducted a post hoc comparative analysis of long‐term data from patients with MPS II who received pabinafusp alfa in a clinical trial setting.

METHODS

Study design

We conducted a retrospective post hoc analysis incorporating 260 weeks of clinical data from 27 Japanese patients with MPS II who were administered pabinafusp alfa at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg/week during a Phase II/III trial (JR‐141‐301 study; NCT03568175) 9 and its subsequent extension study (JR‐141‐302 study; NCT04348136). Of the 27 patients, 24 patients had previously received ERT with IDS before being switched to pabinafusp alfa, while three patients were newly diagnosed and started ERT with pabinafusp alfa. During the initial trial (Weeks 1–52), all patients received the same rate of infusion. Following enrolment in the extension phase (Week 53 onward), infusion rates were allowed to be increased on an individual basis at the discretion of the investigator.

For the purpose of this analysis, patients were categorized by infusion speed into two groups:

The “Fast group” comprised patients (n = 18) who had ≥66% of their pabinafusp alfa infusions during the extension period at a rate >33 mL/h (i.e., the duration of more than two‐thirds of infusions was <3 h)

The “Fast group” comprised patients (n = 18) who had ≥66% of their pabinafusp alfa infusions during the extension period at a rate >33 mL/h (i.e., the duration of more than two‐thirds of infusions was <3 h)

The “Slow group” comprised patients (n = 9) who had <66% of their pabinafusp alfa infusions during the extension period at a rate >33 mL/h (i.e., the duration of more than two‐thirds of infusions was >3 h)

The “Slow group” comprised patients (n = 9) who had <66% of their pabinafusp alfa infusions during the extension period at a rate >33 mL/h (i.e., the duration of more than two‐thirds of infusions was >3 h)

Infusion parameters

The study protocol of the Phase II/III trial stipulated that after the patients' safety had been established with the initial dose (8 mL/h), all subsequent doses were administered at a constant infusion rate of less than 33 mL/h (during Weeks 1–52). In the subsequent extension study (Week 53 onward), investigators were allowed to increase infusion rates at their discretion. As investigators attempted to reduce the infusion burden on patients, weekly infusion times tended to shorten as the study progressed.

During the initial trial (Weeks 1–52), the median infusion duration of patients in the Fast group was 3.08 h, with the fastest infusion lasting 2 h and the longest infusion lasting 4.67 h. In comparison, the median infusion duration for patients in the Slow group during Weeks 1–52 was 3.417 h, with the fastest infusion lasting 2.65 h and the slowest infusion lasting 6.77 h. During the extension period (Weeks 53–261), patients in the Fast group had a median infusion duration of 1.967 h, with the fastest infusion taking 0.75 h and the slowest taking 4.78 h. Conversely, patients in the Slow group had a median infusion duration of 3.333 h during the extension phase, with the fastest infusion taking 1.38 h and the longest taking 7.00 h, as shown in Table S1.

Efficacy‐related outcome measures

The concentrations of HS‐ and DS‐associated GAGs, the two major substrates metabolized by IDS, were assessed according to previously published methods. 19 The concentrations of HS‐ and DS‐associated GAGs in the CSF of patients from the Fast (n = 18) and Slow (n = 9) groups from Week 1 (baseline) through Week 105 are shown in Figure 3. Following treatment with pabinafusp alfa, patients in both the Fast and Slow groups demonstrated a marked reduction in HS‐associated GAGs (Figure 3A) and DS‐associated GAGs (Figure 3B) in the CNS from baseline to week 26. This reduction in HS‐ and DS‐associated GAGs was sustained through the last timepoint when HS‐ and DS‐associated GAGs were measured in the CNS (Week 105), suggesting that infusion rate adjustments do not affect the efficacy of pabinafusp alfa in the CNS.

DISCUSSION

This article summarizes the results of a post hoc analysis of long‐term clinical data on Japanese patients with MPS II receiving ERT with pabinafusp alfa. Safety outcomes were similar between the Fast and Slow groups, with no notable differences in AEs, IARs or ADAs, suggesting that infusion rates had little effect on the safety profile of pabinafusp alfa and that faster infusion rates were well tolerated by patients who tolerated standard infusion rates. Efficacy outcomes in both the Fast and Slow groups demonstrated that pabinafusp alfa reduced HS‐ and DS‐associated GAGs in the CNS, maintained the somatic efficacy of prior ERT and may offer neurocognitive benefits, with infusion rate having little impact on CNS or somatic efficacy. Findings from this study suggest that infusion rates can be adjusted in clinical practice without compromising the safety or efficacy of the drug. This flexibility should allow clinicians to optimize weekly infusion rates to improve long‐term treatment compliance and quality of life in paediatric patients.

Although the relationships between infusion rates and changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics have been previously investigated, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 few studies have focused on long‐term ERT in paediatric patient populations. Most previous discussions of infusion rates in children have centred on maximum allowable infusion volumes in infants. 26

ERT for lysosomal storage disorders requires lifelong weekly intravenous infusions, ideally initiated in infancy. In the absence of other forms of treatment, such as gene therapy, 27 discontinuation of ERT usually leads to disease progression with severe and potentially irreversible consequences. Managing infusion‐associated hypersensitivity reactions, which are reported to occur in approximately 30% of patients with MPS II undergoing ERT, 28 is therefore crucial to avoid treatment interruption. Strategies for managing severe hypersensitivity include intensive immune tolerance induction, 29 , 30 but infusion rate reduction and/or the administration of antihistamines or antipyretics are generally effective for milder reactions. However, prolonged infusion times may negatively affect patients' quality of life and treatment compliance and could potentially alter the pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic profiles of the enzyme products used for ERT. Our data suggested that IARs may occur independently of infusion rate and that adjustments to infusion rates do not appear to have an obvious impact on safety or efficacy outcomes in patients experiencing such reactions. This suggests that IARs may be more closely associated with individual patients' specific conditions 31 than with infusion rate. Taken together, the results of this study show that infusion rate adjustments can be made flexibly to respond to actual clinical situations without affecting the safety or pharmacodynamic outcomes of long‐term treatment with pabinafusp alfa.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective nature of this post hoc study means that patients were not randomly assigned to Fast or Slow groups but that investigators had discretion to modify infusion rates in patients, likely based on AEs or IARs experienced during the initial trial (Weeks 1–52). Since only those patients who tolerated the standard infusion rate well were subsequently exposed to faster infusion rates, there is a potential bias that infusion rate adjustments may reflect individual patients' variable tolerability to initial pabinafusp alfa infusion and may have led to lower observed incidences of IARs.

This analysis also relied on a subjective classification of patients into Fast and Slow groups based on the percentage of their infusions that were administered at a rate >33 mL/h. This may have oversimplified the actual variability of infusion conditions over the 260‐week treatment period. A more detailed analysis of the finer differences in infusion rates and corresponding pharmacodynamic parameters might provide deeper insights into the relationship between infusion rates and pharmacodynamic outcomes. Third, due to the practical difficulties involved in frequent pharmacokinetic data sampling in paediatric patients, it was not possible to directly assess the effects of different infusion rates (and times) on plasma drug concentrations. However, a Phase I/II study conducted in Japan 8 showed stable plasma concentrations of pabinafusp alfa across a range of doses (0.01, 0.1, 1.0 and 2.0 mg/kg) administered weekly over a 4‐week treatment period, with no apparent effect on safety or tolerability. This supports the view that variations in the infusion rates of pabinafusp alfa at the same dosage are unlikely to cause any significant changes in pharmacokinetic parameters, including plasma drug concentrations.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that infusion rate adjustments do not significantly affect the safety or pharmacodynamic profile of pabinafusp alfa during long‐term treatment in patients with MPS II. Our findings suggest that clinicians may safely consider shorter infusion durations when using pabinafusp alfa to treat MPS II, with close monitoring and careful management, to accommodate individual patient needs or clinical circumstances, potentially improving quality of life and treatment compliance in paediatric patients.
