“Two‐For‐One”: 4D Cardiac and Pulmonary MR Imaging From a Single Acquisition Using bSTAR
Authors: Pierre Daudé, Jordan Taylor, Joseph W. Plummer, Rajiv Ramasawmy, Anastasia Tsakirellis, Joel Moss, W. Patricia Bandettini, Marcus Chen, Adrienne E. Campbell‐Washburn, Ahsan Javed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo generate 4D (3D+time) images of both the heart and lungs using a single volumetric radial free‐breathing bSSFP dual echo acquisition (bSTAR) with two image reconstructions within a single pipeline deployed inline at 0.55 and 1.5 T.Methods10 healthy volunteers and 12 patients with cardiomyopathy, lung diseases, or pulmonary hypertension were imaged at 0.55 T. Left‐ventricular volumetry was compared to 2D cine images for the cardiac images. Image quality score was measured by two expert readers for cardiac and pulmonary bSTAR images.ResultsCardiac and pulmonary images were reconstructed inline in 18 min 28 s and 8 min 39 s respectively. Ejection Fraction measurements showed no significant difference (mean bias −0.39%, p > 0.05) between bSTAR and 2D cines. Experts scored whole heart bSTAR images fair‐to‐good (image quality score of 3.47 ± 0.58) and whole lung bSTAR images as good (image quality score of 3.94 ± 0.85).ConclusionThe ability to assess both cardiac and lung morphology and function in a single scan with parallel image reconstructions will facilitate the evaluation of the cardio‐pulmonary interplay.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03331380

Introduction

The heart and lungs are two highly coupled organs, and their physiology is closely linked. There are many pathophysiological conditions in which both the heart and lungs are affected. For instance, pulmonary hypertension is a heterogeneous condition that can affect the lung parenchyma, pulmonary vasculature, and cardiac chambers [1]. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing, which measures ECG, blood pressure, and blood gasses during exercise, is a common diagnostic tool to evaluate the heart and lungs together. However, for imaging exams, the current clinical practice is to image cardiac and respiratory systems separately using a multimodal approach that may include echocardiography, chest CT, ventilation‐perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy, and cardiac MRI. The multimodality imaging approach leads to longer diagnostic pathways (scheduling and logistical complexity) and increased cost.

A more integrated imaging workflow would be particularly valuable for patients in whom both cardiac and pulmonary abnormalities are clinically relevant including those with pulmonary hypertension, ischemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure. Mid‐field MRI offers a unique opportunity to perform a comprehensive cardiopulmonary exam with morphological and functional imaging [2]. To reduce examination time, we propose a single scan to obtain a comprehensive evaluation of the cardiac and pulmonary anatomy and function.

Cardiac and respiratory resolved whole heart MR imaging has been recently demonstrated with the free running framework [3]: 3D radial samples using the phyllotaxis trajectory are acquired and binned according to cardiac and respiratory self‐gating, and multidimensional images are reconstructed using compressed sensing. This technology has been demonstrated for anatomical imaging, 4D flow [4], quantitative imaging [5]. However, this innovation is currently difficult to deploy in clinical settings due to time‐consuming reconstruction (several hours [6]), which hampers its clinical adoption.

Recently, a novel sequence entitled bSTAR [7], which is based on 3D half‐radial dual‐echo balanced SSFP (bSSFP) readout, has enabled high resolution pulmonary imaging (e.g., isotropic 0.9 mm3 at 0.55 T [8]). To facilitate the adoption of this sequence, Lee N.G et al. [9] implemented the acquisition in an open‐source vendor‐neutral pulse sequence software (Pulseq [10]). In their work, image reconstruction was done offline with reconstruction times of several hours.

Cardiac and pulmonary imaging are subject to different technical constraints. Cardiac cine MRI requires high temporal resolution to resolve myocardial motion, with SCMR recommendations [11] suggesting temporal resolution of ≤ 45 ms for wall‐motion assessment, and in‐plane spatial resolution between 1.5–1.8 mm2. In contrast, pulmonary imaging places greater emphasis on high spatial resolution, with thoracic CT practice parameters commonly recommending [12] volumetric thin‐section reconstructions below 1.25 mm. These differing requirements motivate reconstruction strategies that are tailored to the target organ and imaging task.

We hypothesize that bSTAR can enable clinically useful 4D (3D+time) isotropic functional cardiac and pulmonary imaging in a single acquisition at 0.55 and 1.5 T, leveraging the bSSFP contrast and ultra‐short echo time. Additionally, Gadgetron [13] can enable low‐latency advanced reconstruction for high resolution cardiac and pulmonary applications [14, 15]. In this work, we propose acquiring a single volumetric bSTAR dataset and reconstructing it through two parallel time‐resolved pipelines, one targeting the heart and the other the lungs, which we deploy inline on the scanner.

Cardiac and Pulmonary Image Reconstruction Workflow

We implemented a low latency image reconstruction using Gadgetron [13]. The reconstruction pipeline is designed as follows (Figure 1): Acquisitions are streamed using MRI raw data (MRD) [18] format from the scanner to Gadgetron using “FIRE” research framework [19] (Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany). Due to gradient imperfections, 3D k‐space trajectories are corrected on‐the‐fly using gradient system impulse response function (GIRF) [20]. To minimize reconstruction wait times, coil sensitivity maps are estimated during the acquisition halfway through the scan time. Cardiac binning was achieved using the recorded ECG. The bSTAR k‐space sampling scheme consisted of half‐radial projections distributed along a wobbled Archimedean spiral trajectory. Because each interleave was rotated by a small random angle, the first spoke in an interleave was not a true superior–inferior projection; consequently, respiratory motion was estimated from the center of the k‐space for each shot [7, 14]. At the end of the scan, the following cardiac and pulmonary image reconstruction run in parallel:

Cardio‐pulmonary image reconstruction pipeline: During the scan, raw data are preprocessed and coil sensitivity map is estimated. At the end of the acquisition, two image reconstructions run in parallel: cardiac motion resolved and respiratory motion‐compensated image reconstruction using TV regularization and respiratory resolved images using Motion compensated Low Rank reconstruction.

For the pulmonary image reconstruction, respiratory resolved images were reconstructed at 1.2 mm3 with eight respiratory states using Motion Compensated low Rank reconstruction (MoCoLoR) [21, 22]. The iterative image reconstruction solved the following optimization problem in five iterations: argminX∑r,nNRNWrFSnXr−yr,n22+λL‖MX‖* where y rn is respiratory motion‐resolved multi‐channel data, X is the respiratory resolved images to be reconstructed, S n is coil‐sensitivity of the nth channel, F is the nonuniform Fourier transform, W r are the density compensation weights for each respiratory states, the regularization term ‖.‖* represents the nuclear norm with its associated low‐rank penalty weights (λL) and M is the estimated motion field across respiratory states. N R is the total number of respiratory states (N r = 8), and N is the total number of channels (N = 10).

For cardiac image reconstruction, cardiac‐resolved images were reconstructed at 1.4 mm3 with 30 cardiac phases using the 4D iterative motion corrected reconstruction [23]. Briefly, data were binned in 30 cardiac phases and 4 respiratory states. The cardiac dimension is collapsed and respiratory motion‐resolved 4D images are reconstructed using compressed sensing with spatial and temporal total variation regularization (iterations = 5, λ s = 0.01, λ t = 0.05). Non‐rigid respiratory deformation fields are then estimated with an optical flow image registration [24] using the end‐expiration state as reference. Finally, the iterative motion corrected image reconstruction is performed by solving the following optimization problem in 15 iterations: argminX∑c,r,nNcNRNWr,cFSnMrXc−yc,r,n22+λsTVs(X)+λtTVt(X) where y crn is respiratory and cardiac motion‐resolved multi‐channel data, X is the end‐expiration cardiac resolved images to be reconstructed, M r is the estimated motion field across respiratory states, S n is the coil‐sensitivity of the nth channel, F is the nonuniform Fourier transform, W r,c is the density compensation weight for each respiratory and cardiac motion states, and TV represents the spatial (TV s) and temporal (TV t) total variation terms with the associated weights (λs = 0.001, λt = 0.05). N c represents the total number of cardiac frames (N c = 30), N R is the total number of respiratory states (N r = 4), and N is the total number of channels (N = 10).

bSTAR is a dual echo technique, each echo was reconstructed separately in parallel, and then complex images from the two echoes were combined to result in echo combined images for cardiac resolved images whereas only the first echo is used for respiratory resolved images. To reduce aliasing artifacts [25], images are reconstructed on a 1.16 times larger field of view. The Pipe and Menon iterative method [26] was used to estimate density compensation weights. Total variation weight terms were optimized using parameter sweep as illustrated in Figure S1.

Image reconstructions were performed using Gadgetron on a computer equipped with 4 GPUs (NVIDIA H200, 144Gb) and 128 CPUs cores (2× AMD EPYC 7H12 64‐core processors).

Gradient System Impulse Response Measurement

The bSTAR sequence is a 3D radial trajectory with bipolar dual echoes and therefore can suffer from distortions due to gradient imperfections that are more important in rapid switching gradients. Previous studies have used an acquisition‐specific gradient measurement approach [27, 28], whereas we implemented a GIRF‐based trajectory correction to reduce distortion. The system‐specific GIRF was measured using two off‐centered slices on a spherical phantom placed at isocenter [20, 29]. The bSTAR sequence gradient waveforms were retrieved from their k‐space trajectories. For each TR, the gradient waveforms containing both echoes were corrected with the GIRF measurements using multiplication in the frequency domain. K‐space trajectory was obtained by temporal integration and then cropped out by echoes to match the acquisition windows. A structural phantom [30] was scanned on the 0.55 T Free.Max using bSTAR with previously described acquisition parameters to evaluate the trajectory correction.

Evaluations Metrics

Apparent Contrast‐To‐Noise Ratio for Pulse Sequence Optimization

Since visualization of both pulmonary and cardiovascular anatomy relies on a single bSTAR acquisition, optimization of the flip angle is required to achieve clinically useful contrast for both cardiac and pulmonary imaging. To find the optimal flip angle for cardiac and pulmonary imaging, apparent contrast‐to‐noise ratios (aCNR) for blood‐myocardium and lung parenchyma‐air were measured for the combined echoes on bSTAR dataset acquired with different flip angles (FA = [25°,45°]) in one healthy volunteer.

Apparent Signal‐To‐Noise Ratio for Dual Image Reconstruction

Apparent signal‐to‐noise ratio (aSNR) was measured as the ratio of the mean signal in the region of interest (lung, myocardium, blood pool) over the standard deviation of the noise only region (the airway) across the study population included at 0.55 T.

Left Ventricle Volumetry and Image Quality Score for Cardiac Imaging

Left Ventricle volumetric measurements were compared between the Cartesian SAX view and 4D SAX reformatted bSTAR. bSTAR 4D volumes were reformatted using an open‐source software 3D Slicer [31] into the SAX cine view, then using a commercial software (suiteHEART software version 5.1.0; NeoSoft LLC, Pewaukee, WI, USA) they were resliced to reproduce the Cartesian scan slice prescription (slice thickness = 8 mm, slice gap = 2 mm), although exact matching of the anatomical slice locations with the Cartesian acquisition was not imposed. Automatic segmentation of the left ventricle was conducted using the suiteHEART and revised by expert with 5 years of cardiac MRI experience. End‐diastolic volume (EDV), end‐systolic volume (ESV) and ejection fraction (EF) were analyzed and compared between bSTAR and the 2D cine images.

Image quality was quantitatively assessed using a 5‐point Likert scale (1, nondiagnostic; 2, poor; 3, fair; 4, good; 5, excellent image quality) along with artifacts quality score (1, severe; 2, strong; 3, moderate; 4, negligible; 5, absence of artifacts) by two experts with more than 35 years of clinical cardiothoracic imaging experience. The 5‐point Likert scores are detailed in (Tables S1 and S2). In addition, image quality was dichotomized based on clinical relevance, with a threshold of ≥ 3 on the Likert scale defined as sufficient for diagnostic confidence. For cardiac imaging, the image quality score was done on the 4D SAX reformatted bSTAR.

Percentage of ECG mistriggering (RR intervals > 1.5 mean RR intervals) during bSTAR acquisition is reported to measure the success of the cardiac binning.

Image Sharpness and Image Quality Score for Pulmonary Imaging

To assess whether the pulmonary images were adequately respiratory resolved, image sharpness was assessed in all subjects acquired at 0.55 T by estimating the slope (in cm) of a sigmoid fitted to a line profile between the liver‐lung interface [32]. A higher slope value signifies a sharper diaphragm boundary. Image sharpness between a pulmonary gridded reconstruction and MoCoLoR reconstruction was analyzed.

Image quality of pulmonary bSTAR images was quantitatively assessed using a 5‐point Likert scale along with artifacts quality score by the same two experts. The 5‐point Likert scores are detailed in (Table S3).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Python. The different metrics' distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the normality of the distribution, Wilcoxon signed rank or paired t‐test was applied for conducting the analyses. In addition, effect size was estimated using Hedges' g value. A linear mixed‐effects model including acquisition method, group (healthy subjects vs. patients), and their interaction as fixed effects, with subject as a random effect, was used to evaluate differences in left ventricle volumetry. Left ventricle measurement agreement was evaluated by Bland–Altman analysis, providing bias and limits of agreement (LOA).

For the image quality scoring and artifact quality scoring statistical analysis, linear mixed‐effects models including acquisition method, expert reader, and their interaction as fixed effects, with subject as a random effect were used. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with (95% confidence interval) was used to determine agreement in image quality and artifact quality scoring between readers. The association between image quality and artifacts quality scores and BMI was evaluated using linear mixed‐effects models, including acquisition method and expert reader as fixed effects and subject as a random effect. Because ECG mistriggering was only available for bSTAR acquisitions, its association with the scoring was evaluated in a separate model restricted to cardiac bSTAR images, adjusting for BMI and reader. BMI was included as a continuous variable, while ECG mistriggering was dichotomized using a threshold of > 1%.

Cardiac Image Reconstruction Pipeline: Volumetric Measurement and Image Quality

Strong agreement was observed in cardiac volume and function measurements between 2D cine and 4D bSTAR. Ejection fraction measurements showed no significant difference between bSTAR and 2D cines (mean bias −0.39%, p > 0.05, Hedges's g = 0.04) with LOA ranging from −9.26% to 8.48%. However, volumetric measurements had significant (p < 0.001) mean bias of 7.1 mL, Hedges's g = 0.14, LOA (−7.67 mL; 18.45 mL) for end‐diastolic volumes and no significant mean bias of 3.6 mL, Hedges's g = 0.10, LOA (−11.32 mL; 21.78 mL) for end‐systolic volumes (Figure 4A). In the mixed‐effects model for left ventricular volumetry, the interaction between acquisition method and group was not significant (EDV: p = 0.53, ESV: p = 0.85, EF: p = 0.75), indicating that the difference in volumetry between reformatted bSTAR and Cartesian SAX cine did not vary between healthy subjects and patients.

Expert reviewers quantitatively assessed cardiac image quality using established 5‐point Likert scoring to determine whether the new framework satisfies clinical image‐quality standards.

Image quality scores were significantly lower for bSTAR cardiac imaging compared with denoised Cartesian cine for both readers (Reader A: 3.48 ± 0.59 vs. 4.36 ± 0.48; Reader B: 3.45 ± 0.60 vs. 4.11 ± 0.50; both p < 0.001; Figure 6A). Mixed‐effects analysis confirmed a significant overall effect of acquisition method (β = −0.65, p < 0.001), with no significant effect of reader (p = 0.131) and no significant interaction between acquisition method and reader (p = 0.309), indicating consistent findings across readers. The proportion of images rated as sufficient for diagnostic confidence (score ≥ 3) was high for both acquisitions across both readers (≥ 95% for cardiac bSTAR, 100% for Cartesian Cine).

Comparison of image quality scoring (A, B) and artifact quality scoring between two readers for Cartesian cine, cardiac resolved bSTAR, and pulmonary resolved bSTAR.

Artifact level scores were also significantly lower for bSTAR cardiac imaging compared with denoised Cartesian cine for both readers (Reader A: 3.18 ± 0.66 vs. 4.31 ± 0.57; Reader B: 3.41 ± 0.67 vs. 4.03 ± 0.50; both p < 0.001; Figure 6C). Mixed‐effects analysis demonstrated a significant effect of acquisition method (β = −0.63, p < 0.001) and a significant interaction between acquisition and reader (β = −0.51, p < 0.05), indicating that the magnitude of the difference between techniques varied between readers. However, no overall systematic difference in artifact scoring between readers was observed (p = 0.079).

Inter‐observer agreement was good for cardiac bSTAR (ICC = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.45–0.90) and moderate for Cartesian cine (ICC = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.14–0.81).

BMI was not significantly associated with image quality scores (β = −0.024 kg/m2, p = 0.28) and with artifact scores (β = −0.032 kg/m2, p = 0.25) for cardiac bSTAR imaging and Cartesian imaging (β = −0.05 kg/m2, p = 0.79, β = −0.023 kg/m2, p = 0.42). ECG mistriggering was low (1.7% ± 3.8%) with skewed distribution (median 0.31%, IQR 0.03%–0.93%, max = 16.4%), with 5 subjects (23%) exhibiting values > 1%. It was not significantly associated with image quality (p = 0.53) or artifact scores (p = 0.54) in cardiac bSTAR images. Video S5 shows the images given the worst Likert score which was cardiac resolved bSTAR acquired on arrhythmic patient (ECG mistriggering = 16.4%).

Therefore, quantitative cardiac volumetry showed agreement with measurements derived from the gold‐standard short‐axis 2D cine series, with diagnostic image quality preserved.

Pulmonary Image Reconstruction Pipeline: Image Sharpness and Image Quality

The sharpness of the liver–lung interface was quantified. The MoCoLoR reconstruction produced a significantly (p < 1e‐6) sharper liver‐lung interface (slope s = 3.75 cm−1) compared to the thoracic gridding image reconstruction (s = 1.78 cm−1). This highlights the robustness of the respiratory binning.

For the pulmonary image reconstruction, image quality scores were 3.86 ± 0.94 for Reader A and 4.02 ± 0.76 for Reader B, Figure 6B. The proportion of images rated as sufficient for diagnostic confidence (score ≥ 3) was high across both readers (Reader A: 86%, Reader B: 95%). Artifact scores were high for both readers (Reader A: 4.86 ± 0.35; Reader B: 4.86 ± 0.32, Figure 6D), indicating minimal perceived artifact burden. Inter‐observer agreement was moderate for image quality (ICC = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.24–0.81) and good for artifact assessment (ICC = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.45–0.90). Higher BMI was associated with lower image quality scores (β = −0.06 per kg/m2, p < 0.05) and lower artifact quality scores (β = −0.03 per kg/m2, p < 0.05). No significant effect of reader was observed (all p > 0.05), and no interaction between BMI and reader was detected (all p > 0.05) for the image quality and artifact quality scores.

Discussion

In this study, both high resolution 4D (3D+time) cardiac and pulmonary imaging were achieved from a single acquisition using a sub 20 min reconstruction. This has the potential to address an unmet need in evaluating patient diseases that affect the cardiopulmonary system, such as pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, and congenital heart disease.

The bSTAR sequence provides a bSSFP contrast which is optimal for visualization of the cardiovascular structure along with an ultra‐short echo time that enables visualization of lung parenchyma. Previously published methods using bSTAR at 0.55 T have used a flip angle of 20° [34] or 25° [9] whereas similar 3D radial bSSFP cardiac sequences [33] have used higher flip angles of 110°. In this study a flip angle of 40° at 0.55 T was found to be a good compromise for maximizing aCNR for both blood‐myocardium and lung parenchyma‐air while staying within the SAR limits.

On this basis, we implemented a single 10‐min cardiopulmonary acquisition rather than separate optimized lung and cardiac scans, together with concurrent reconstruction pipelines tailored to each organ. We believe this approach simplifies workflow, reduces overall scan time, and improves patient comfort, while partially restoring organ‐specific optimization through reconstruction‐specific spatial and temporal resolution. The joint acquisition could be compared to organ‐tailored acquisitions in the future.

To reconstruct diagnostic image quality, k‐space trajectory correction was required as shown in Figure S2. In previous works using the bSTAR sequence [7, 9], two different calibration methods [27, 28] for the correction of the k‐space trajectory were used, they both required a dedicated calibration measurement on a phantom for a specific bSTAR trajectory and acquisition. In this study, we are proposing to use a more general method, gradient system impulse response function [20], currently recognized as the gold standard for k‐space trajectory correction. This method alleviates the requirement of sequence‐specific calibration and can be applied as sequence parameters change (e.g., resolution and field‐of‐view), which facilitates easier dissemination of the technology in clinical settings.

Inline reconstruction was achieved in this study with cardiac and pulmonary images reconstructed in under 20 and 8 min, respectively. This is a significant improvement over previously published several‐hour reconstructions for both respiratory‐resolved pulmonary and cardiac‐resolved whole heart images. We optimized the reconstruction workflow to save time in several reconstruction steps. Specifically, we estimated the coil sensitivity maps at half of the acquisition time, saving time for the parallel cardiac and pulmonary image reconstruction (Figure S3).

Compared to the Free‐Running framework [3] that resolved cardiac and respiratory motion in the same volume for imaging the heart (usually 20 phases per cardiac cycle, four respiratory states [6, 33]), our approach is to divide it into two different specific image reconstructions which enable optimized spatial and temporal resolution for each application. In our implementation, cardiac‐resolved images are reconstructed at lower spatial resolution (1.4 mm isotropic) for optimal SNR, but with high temporal resolution (30 cardiac phases) to ensure accurate evaluation of cardiac function; whereas pulmonary reconstruction generates higher spatial resolution images (1.2 mm isotropic) required for pulmonary vasculature assessment and nodule detection, but with eight respiratory frames sufficient to resolve breathing motion. Each reconstruction pipeline uses a specific iterative reconstruction algorithm. Motion compensated low‐rank reconstruction used for pulmonary imaging is heavily dependent on exact reciprocity of the deformation field, which can be challenging for the cardiovascular structure due to simultaneous motion of fine structures (valves, papillary muscles) in addition to blood flow (jets for instance). Therefore, a 4D iterative motion‐compensated image reconstruction was preferred for the heart. Future work using deep learning motion estimation [36] is envisaged to address this challenge.

In addition to ensuring sufficient image quality, it is essential that quantitative cardiac volumetry aligns with measurements derived from the gold‐standard short‐axis 2D cine series. Similar to the whole heart imaging literature, ejection fraction showed no significant difference with the gold standard 2D cines (mean bias −0.39%), with a negligible effect size (Hedges' g = 0.04) supporting good overall agreement at the group level. However, the LOA were relatively wide (−9.26% to 8.48%), suggesting greater variability at the individual level. These limits were wider than those previously reported for comparable whole heart 3D methods [33] (−5.1% to 2.7%) and for Cartesian cine test–retest analyses [37] (−5.7% to 5.3%), highlighting the need for further investigation to identify and mitigate the sources of this variability, as these discrepancies may have clinically relevant consequences for the classification of patients with reduced versus normal ejection fraction. In addition, end‐diastolic volumes were significantly higher by 7 mL; this presented with a small effect size (Hedges' g = 0.14). The primary hypothesis is that minor variations in anatomical slice positioning between the SAX reformatted bSTAR data and the 2D stack of cines may result in differences in the segmented areas. This variation is consistent with the difference observed in intra−/inter operator measurements of the left ventricle [38] (ΔEDVintra = 5 mL; difference ΔEDVinter = 8 mL).

Although bSTAR cardiac imaging demonstrated significantly lower image quality (Reader A: 3.48; Reader B: 3.45) and artifact level scores (Reader A: 3.18; Reader B: 3.41) compared to Cartesian cine imaging, when applying a clinically motivated threshold for diagnostic usability (score ≥ 3), the majority of images were deemed sufficient for diagnostic confidence for both techniques across both expert readers (≥ 95% for cardiac bSTAR, 100% for Cartesian Cine). Therefore, bSTAR cardiac imaging shows promise as an alternative to standard multi‐slice 2D cine imaging to improve patient comfort, workflow efficiency, and simplicity, but would require validation on a larger patient cohort before clinical adoption.

The impact of patient‐related factors on image quality varied depending on the organ‐specific image reconstruction. ECG mistriggering was not significantly associated with image quality or artifact scores in cardiac bSTAR imaging, likely reflecting its low prevalence and limited variability within the cohort, which limited statistical power to detect an effect. BMI was not independently associated with cardiac image and artifact scores.

In contrast, image quality scores for pulmonary bSTAR imaging were high across both readers (Reader A: 3.86; Reader B: 4.02), which is sufficient for interpretation with low artifact burden (4.86 for both readers), although variability in perceived image quality remains (ICC = 0.59). Despite generally favorable performances, BMI was negatively associated with pulmonary image quality; this effect is likely related to reduced SNR and the presence of streaking artifacts at the lung apex in larger patients. The absence of expert reader interaction indicates that this effect was consistent across observers. The pulmonary bSTAR had a mean aSNR of 5.58 which was within ranges [3.0, 7.9] found in the literature for similar spatial resolution [34, 35].

Isotropic bSTAR cardiac or respiratory resolved volumes provide the flexibility to visualize the cardiac and pulmonary anatomy in different orientations. This added flexibility to measure volumetric information (e.g., lung mass, pericardial effusion) was illustrated in Figure 7 and can help clinical decisions. A representative case of pulmonary hypertension shows the benefit of this new framework for assessing cardiac and pulmonary interplay. Inclusion of a larger cohort of patients with known cardiopulmonary diseases will be pursued in the future to evaluate the diagnostic acceptability ratio metric of the dual‐reconstruction bSTAR technique before clinical adoption.

We also performed proof‐of‐concept testing of the combined cardiac and pulmonary imaging at 1.5 T in five healthy volunteers, without rigorous analysis. As 1.5 T scanners are more widely available and standard for cardiac imaging, our goal was to assess the feasibility of this approach on this more common system. Recently, a similar approach based on free‐running cardiopulmonary imaging has been demonstrated on healthy volunteers at this field strength [39]. However, with an offline reconstruction time of several days and without cardiac clinical validation against gold standard cine [39], cardiopulmonary imaging at 1.5 T still required thorough research before clinical adoption.

We envision this new framework may enable future applications in the quantitative pulmonary domain such as ventilation mapping [40], and 3D MRI spirometry [41] and in the cardiovascular domain such as vascular imaging and 3D feature tracking. A fully 5D reconstruction resolving both cardiac and respiratory motion would be an attractive approach for exploring cardiopulmonary interplay, as respiration has already been shown to influence cardiopulmonary flow [4], but such an approach is limited by currently available computer hardware (Table S4).

Limitations

A major limitation of this work is the requirement of multiple GPU with large memory (> 80 Gb) to reconstruct simultaneously the two time‐resolved volumes. This GPU capability is usually not present in a standard clinical setting. Fortunately, coil‐sketching technique [22, 42] that shows reducing GPU memory consumption for image reconstruction, rapid GPU hardware improvement, or the deployment of cloud‐based reconstruction holds hope for alleviating this problem.

Cardiac waveform extraction is not self‐gated and requires an external ECG recording which could limit its deployment in higher magnetic field strength (> = 3 T) scanners where the ECG signal is less reliable during rapid gradient switching.

This study has been performed on a prototype Free.Max with a prototype gradient coil (gradient peak slew rate: 160 mT/m/s and Gmax: 33 mT/m), which is higher than the commercial versions (gradient peak slew rate: 40 mT/m/s and Gmax: 25 mT/m). Matching bSTAR imaging protocol will result in longer TR (2.94 ms) and overall scan time of 12 min 12 s.

The evaluation of pulmonary bSTAR images in terms of image sharpness, image quality, and artifact scoring would have benefited from including a comparison with another UTE sequence, and this will be pursued in future work.

Conclusions

We demonstrated a new framework to assess both cardiac and lung structure and function in a single scan which may enable evaluation of the cardio‐pulmonary interplay as illustrated in our patient examples. The inline low latency reconstruction ∼20 min for both 4D images can facilitate clinical deployment.
