Quality improvement project for minimising red blood cell unit wastage at a secondary referral hospital in Malawi
Authors: Brany Mithi, Edson Mzumara, Kwame Chisi, Robert Fungurani, Jean Nyondo, Arnold Jumbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoor blood bank stock management can result in excess or understocking of blood units for transfusion. A retrospective analysis of blood bank records from 2020 to 2024 at a secondary referral hospital in Malawi revealed a consistently high number of expired red blood cell units. This quality improvement project (QIP) was implemented to reduce blood unit wastage from over 4% to zero of the total blood units received per month.We developed an intervention package to address gaps in blood bank inventory stock management. The package comprised change ideas such as adjusted blood ordering mechanism, strict adherence to the First Expire First Out (FEFO) strategy, improved coordination and staff coverage for the blood bank. The iterative Plan-Do-Study-Adjust (PDSA) cycle guided the implementation of the change ideas. Key outcomes from the pre-intervention and post-intervention phases were compared to evaluate the effectiveness of the interventions.Overall, five PDSA cycles were completed in the implementation phase. Baseline assessment uncovered multiple non-conforming practices, including non-adherence to standard methods for issuing blood cell units and over-ordering of blood units, a practice driven by long distance and transport logistical challenges. The mean expiry rate of blood units for the pre-intervention period (March–July 2024) was 4.16% (37/890 units), with March recording the highest wastage (5.4%). Following implementation of QIP strategies, a significant decline in wastage of blood units was observed (1% (6/520), p=0.007), with zero-unit expiries recorded from September 2024 to March 2025. The proportion of ward returned blood units declined from 1.1% (8 of 713) to 0.7% (4 of 570) (p=0.45).Our QIP identified and addressed operational gaps contributing to blood unit wastage. Through a step-by-step implementation of a multifaceted strategy, to improve staff coverage, coordination and compliance with blood bank standard practices, blood unit wastage due to expiry was reduced to zero.

Problem

Hospital blood banks play a critical role in the temporary storage and prompt provision of quality blood units for transfusion services in secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities. Red blood cells (RBCs) are the most frequently transfused blood products (whole blood and packed cells) with varying shelf life (21, 35 and 42 days) depending on preservatives used.1 Their processing and storage requirements are less complex compared with other blood products such as platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate.2

Rumphi District Hospital is one of the secondary referral facilities in Malawi that provide emergency and surgical procedures requiring blood transfusion. The 250-bed capacity hospital has a catchment population of nearly 40 000 people and serves as a referral facility to 18 health facilities in the district. The hospital’s blood bank department receives RBC units from Malawi Blood Transfusion Services (MBTS), a regional hub situated in Mzuzu City, approximately 65 km away, with a travel time of at least 1 hour by road. In an emergency, where blood supply is halted by unavailability of blood at MBTS or due to transport logistical challenges, our blood bank technicians often collect blood from family or hospital-recruited blood donors. However, such donors contribute less than 1% of the total blood units issued for transfusions.

RBC unit wastage is a common challenge at Rumphi District Hospital blood bank. A review of blood bank records from 2020 to 2024 uncovered a trend of red cell unit expires, with 2023 alone, registering 48 (9%) expired RBC units of the total blood units recieved from MBTS. In view of that, efforts to reduce blood unit wastage require a clear understanding of the root causes and engagement with key stakeholders in developing strategies to address operational gaps in the entire blood supply chain.

Therefore, we conducted a quality improvement project (QIP) at the blood bank with a goal to reduce wastage of RBC units from over 4% to zero of the total RBC units received per month. The following were our specific objectives:

To identify root causes of the high rate of expiry and wastage of blood units.

To design and implement tailored-strategies to address the operational gaps identified using the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle.

The quality improvement initiative used the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence V.2 guideline to ensure rigorous and transparent reporting of the outcomes.3

Background information

Blood transfusion services are a critical part of healthcare services, associated with benefits, risks and costs.4 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), low blood donation rates affect ready availability of whole blood and packed cells for timely blood transfusion.5 The demand for blood transfusion services in Malawi remains high due to high prevalence of malaria in children, postpartum haemorrhage, road traffic accidents and surgical procedures.6–8 Children and pregnant women use about 75% of the blood supply in sub-Saharan Africa.9

While inadequate blood units supply has been reported as the major challenge in many countries, quarterly review reports in Malawi indicate that secondary referral hospital blood banks tend to experience periodical excess blood units expiries as compared with tertiary facilities where demand for blood components is usually high.10 11 Despite available studies on blood transfusion services in Malawi, there are limited data on quality improvement initiatives focusing on reducing blood unit wastage.12 13 Most of the existing studies have focused on addressing the demand and quality of blood components for transfusion.14–17

In resource-constrained settings, contributing factors for blood unit wastage include poor forecasting and inability to reissue blood units before expiry.18 Oftentimes, blood bank technicians prefer to order excess blood units in order to cover for several days, a practice driven by long distance and transport logistical challenges.19 However, evidence suggest that several strategies can help address common gaps in blood bank stock management. Stanger et al report that good performance of blood bank inventory management largely depends on blood bank laboratory staff, who are committed, skilled and regularly trained, underscoring the importance of good staffing and incentivisation.20

This QIP was implemented primarily to reduce RBC unit wastage at our hospital blood bank while maintaining optimal blood inventory levels, amid the steadily increasing demand for blood transfusion. The quality initiative complements the Malawi National Blood Policy in ensuring that high quality blood products are readily available and given to the right patients in the right quantity at the right time with minimal chances of wastage or transfusion adverse events.

To answer the above questions, the team needed to work on records that provide sufficient data for baseline assessment. Hence, we conducted a retrospective review of blood bank records covering from 2020 to March 2024. Our interest was to see the past trends in blood unit utilisation in respect to the amount of blood ordered, received, issued and expired. One of the major challenges faced during the review process was ‘missing records’ especially the blood order books and delivery notes. Most of the records found were from 2023 and 2024.

Therefore, as a team, we agreed to focus our baseline review on 2024 records, starting from January to July. The blood bank order books and delivery notes provided the required data on the amount of blood units ordered and received, while data on the amount of blood units issued or expired was extracted from electronic records and blood bank registers respectively. This data was analysed using simple descriptive statistics and was presented in tables and graphs. Findings of the baseline analysis indicated high prevalence of RBC unit expires (Jan 0%, Feb 0%, March 5.4%, April 4.5%, May 4.3%, Jun 1.7%, July 4.6%). (figure 1).

To better understand the root causes of the high rate of blood wastage due to expiries and identify long-lasting solutions, discussions were held with staff from the laboratory and the transport section. Several key issues emerged during these discussions. First, it was observed that newly recruited laboratory staff and students were not adequately oriented on blood bank operations. As a result, many were unable to accurately forecast blood units need and did not consistently adhere to standard blood bank practices, including proper sorting of blood units in storage and the use of the First Expire First Out (FEFO) approach when issuing blood.

Both technical staff from MBTS and hospital blood bank were also implicated. firstly, It emerged that some laboratory technicians were deliberately ordering more blood units than required due to the long distance to the MBTS regional centre and the unreliable availability of transport. Additionally, analysis of blood bank inventory records revealed descrepancies between the amount of blood units ordered and received. Excess or fewer blood units were issued at MBTS depending on their stock status. Much as all the practices were intended to minimise the risk of blood shortages, excess supplies with short shelf life span inadvertently contributed to increased blood wastage in some months.

Design

A QIP team comprising four laboratory staff members was set-up to codesign strategies for reducing RBC wastage in the hospital blood bank. Selection was based on their demonstrated competency and prior training in blood safety and blood banking. The QIP team had an average of 4.5 years of professional experience in the laboratory. The laboratory manager was designated as the QIP team lead in order to enhance oversight of blood bank operations.

Following baseline assessment, a quasi-experimental design was used to assess effectiveness of our interventions (change ideas) by comparing indicators from blood bank records, covering the past 5 months (March to July 2024) with QIP findings in the next 5 months (August to December 2024). Three additional months (January–March 2025) of post interventional phase were added to obtain feedback and assess level of integration of change ideas into routine practice.

Based on baseline assessment and engagement with laboratory staff, we identified key areas which we thought were crucial intervention targets for reducing blood wastage: (1) coordination, (2) blood ordering and issuance practices, (3) staff coverage and (4) documentation and records keeping. Each of the thematic areas was mapped with specific change ideas for implementation (figure 2).

Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle 1

During the initial cycle, we engaged laboratory staff to implement two key change ideas: making adjustments in blood ordering system to reduce excess blood and conducting regular sorting of blood units according to date of expiries to ensure compliance with FEFO when issuing blood for transfusion. While there was good level of compliance to FEFO approach during the first month of implementation, we encountered resistance as other laboratory staff feared that adjusting blood orders would lead to understocking of blood units in the blood bank.

Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle 2

In the second cycle, we continued engaging laboratory staff on the importance of adjusting orders based on consumption, highlighting the ethical and cost implications of blood wastage. To ensure full compliance to the adjusted ordering mechanism, the laboratory manager deliberately reassigned three laboratory staff to the blood bank section. Their key responsibility included making orders and at least accompanying the driver during blood collection to verify quantity and crosscheck expires. Not only did this approach enhance blood bank staff coverage during normal working hours, but it also led to improved adherence to standard practices and change ideas being implemented.

Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle 3

In the third cycle, our focus was on managing inventory records to address the challenge of missing records while maintaining the adjusted ordering system and compliance to FEFO approach. Specific tasks included regular checking of the blood bank to ensure blood units are sorted according to blood groups and expiry, creating files for blood delivery notes, filling temperature and maintenance logs for the blood bank. We also enforced documentation of blood grouping and cross-match results and accompanying details of blood units issued in the Integrated Basic Laboratory Information System. Daily updates were also provided to the QIP team on stock level before a decision is made to order additional blood units. We observed high level of compliance to the interventions especially among QIP team.

Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle 4

In addition to change ideas implemented in the previous cycle 3, key interventions in cycle 4 included enhanced coordination between the QIP team with blood bank users as well as MBTS staff. Several tasks were implemented including sharing daily updates with laboratory staff regarding blood bank stock status and consumption. We also conducted a presentation on QIP progress as a way of getting feedback from the general laboratory staff.

Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle 5

Final phase of the QIP centred on refining the change ideas while integrating them into routine practice. QIP team made deliberate efforts to involve other staff in implementation of interventions such as making adjusted blood orders to minimise excess and wastage, recording all tests and blood unit details when issuing as well as ensuring strict adherence to FEFO approach. Overall, there was good adherence as no expiry was recorded the past 4 months (from September to December 2024)

Post intervention phase

Three months post intervention phase focused on assessing the level of integration of strategies into routine practice and determining trends in our key indicators (expires, returns, orders etc.). During this phase, we also solicited feedback from laboratory staff members on the overall performance of the QIP and suggestions for further improvement.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics such as percentages and proportions were computed to summarise and compare data trends before and during implementation phase. The differences in RBC unit wastage between the two phases were calculated in STATA using a t-test with a 0.05 p value set for determining statistical significance. Findings were presented in form of graphs and tables created in Excel sheets.

Qualitative datasets (transcripts) were independently analysed by two individuals: the lead investigator and an expert in qualitative research. The analysis process involved manual coding of transcripts where emerging themes were identified and compared. Emerging themes were presented alongside supporting quotations from selected respondents.

Patient and public involvement

Patient partners (healthcare workers) were involved during the baseline assessment and stakeholder engagements to better understand root causes and formulate relevant research questions to guide the QIP implementation. Along with other health organisations, patient partners were involved in identification of key participants and during progress briefing, to get feedback that helped refine and integrate interventions into routine practice. Some also contributed to review of the paper as co-authors. The QIP findings will be disseminated to all study participants via different platforms including conference presentations and emails.

Results

Red blood cell unit expiry and return trends across study phases

During QIP implementation period (August–December 2024), the number of RBC units (whole blood and packed cells) received from regional distribution centre was relatively low compared with pre-intervention phase. The rate of expired RBC units declined from an average 4.16% (37/890 units) to 1% (6/520 units) between August and December 2024 (p=0.007), although no expires were recoreded in the subsequent 3 months post intervention observatory phase (January–March 2025). RBC unit wastage in form of returns from the wards declined from 1.1% (8 of 713) of issued units in pre-interventional phase to 0.7% (4 of 570) during interventional phase (p=0.45). Impact of external factors on blood unit availability

There was marked variation in the quantity of blood units received across the three phases of the QIP. During the pre-intervention phase (March–July 2024), 890 blood units were received, compared with 520 blood units recieved during the intervention phase (August–December 2024). However, just in 3 months of post-intervention phase (January–March 2025), the blood bank received 552 blood units, which was higher than the total amount received during the 5 months of intervention phase.

Although stringent ordering practices to minimise excess blood units were part of our change ideas, the decline in amount of blood units received in some months during the intervention phase may be a result of insufficient blood collected by MBTS due to countrywide fuel crisis and delayed funding. Additionally, secondary students who constitute a reliable and low-risk blood donor population in Malawi were on extended festive holidays in the month of December, 2024.

Post-interventional phase and staff perspectives

During the last 3 month period (January–March 2025), blood bank received 552 units of RBCs, of which 424 units were transfused. The crossmatch-to-transfusion ratio was 1.0, suggestive of efficient red blood unit utilisation. There were no expires registered other than one unit returned, accounting for a return rate of 0.2% (1 out of 424). Feedback was gathered from laboratory staff to ascertain appropriateness and effectiveness of interventions of the QIP. 8 out of 10 technicians responded to our questionnaire, resulting in 80% response rate. The following below were staff perspectives regarding QIP performance.

Reduced red blood cell unit wastages

Majority of respondents acknowledged the decline in amount of blood unit wastage which they attributed to the implementation of the QIP interventions targeting the blood bank inventory.

Progress for QIP is there because previously there were a number of expired RBC units compared with the current period, I think strategies put in place are really working. (Lab Tech 1)

Yeah! there are improvements that I have observed, the number of expired blood units has gone down to almost zero as compared in the past where we used to have more expired blood units. (Lab Tech 2)

Improved adherence to First Expire First Out

Respondents noted a general improvement in adherence to the FEFO and 5Ss (Set, Sort, Shine,…) principles, citing enhanced cleanliness in the blood bank area, compared with the past when workbenches were often cluttered and blood stains were visible on the inside surfaces of the refrigerator.

There is sanity in blood bank department in terms of cleanliness and free of clutter in benches and this applies to Blood Bank refrigerator. In the past you could see blood spots (spillage) inside the refrigerator and no one seemed concerned about cleaning the refrigerator. (Lab Tech 3)

Blood is well arranged in order of the expiry dates which makes it easier for us to issue even when the guys working at the blood bank are not available. (Lab Tech 4)

Improved blood bank staff coverage

Respondents noted that the reassignment of three laboratory officers to oversee the blood bank significantly enhanced its operations and overall blood bank inventory management.

…… we should maintain those three people, otherwise reducing the number of blood bank technicians may also have an impact on the operations of the blood bank. (Lab Tech 5)

Lessons and limitations

Through a comprehensive review of blood bank records and ongoing engagement with key stakeholders, we identified the root causes of blood unit wastage and further implemented targeted interventions to address managerial, technical and logistical gaps in the blood bank inventory management system. Major gaps included poor forecasting of blood unit needs, non-adherence to the FEFO approach and deliberate ordering of excess blood units to cover for more days in the face of prevailing transportation challenges.

In view of the above key challenges, our approach involved step-by-step implementation and monitoring the effectiveness of tailored strategies, guided by the PDSA cycle which supports continuous improvement through regular evaluation and modification of strategies.21–23 Achieving 0% blood unit wastage in 7 months demonstrated effectiveness and the successful integration of our QIP interventional package.

As a way to first address identified gaps at the blood bank, our initial strategy involved setting up a QIP team of motivated laboratory personnel. These staff members received hands-on training and were subsequently reassigned to the blood bank section. This approach strengthened the oversight of blood bank operations and adherence to standard practices such as daily stock monitoring, blood units sorting and implementation of FEFO approach. Besides, the reassignment of three trained technicians ensured adequate staffing coverage, thereby addressing the operational gaps created when one staff assigned at the blood bank is on leave or engaged. The end result was a significant reduction in wastage of RBC units which are perishable products with a limited shelf life.24 25

Realising the critical role of communication as a key component of team coordination and alignment of efforts to maintain quality standards, we established a WhatsApp group forum, which provided a quicker way to share daily updates on blood ordering logistics as well as resolving any emerging issues.26–28 All key decisions made through this forum were communicated to the rest of the laboratory staff. After completion of the QIP, we deliberately maintained the WhatsApp forum and added some tecnical staff to support the sustainability of progress achieved.

Despite achieving its primary objective of reducing wastage of blood units due to expiry to zero, the QIP team encountered several implementation challenges. First, the blood units inventory management proved to be inherently complex, as supply and demand continuously fluctuated due to a range of logistical factors beyond the control of the hospital blood bank technicians. Consequently, interventions such as stringent blood-ordering practices proved to be counter-productive during such periods when increased ordering was driven by prolonged stockouts at the distribution centre. Therefore, we adopted a balanced and flexible approach to managing blood bank inventory, one that ensured reduction in wastage while maintaining adequate stock levels to ensure uninterrupted blood transfusion services.

Second, the QIP team composition excluded other key professionals, especially nurses, who could have provided valuable insights into the challenges experienced within hospital wards. These are cadres tasked with the collection of blood units from the hospitalblood bank to various hospital wards for transfusion.29 Therefore, their involvement could have contributed to addressing blood wastage resulting from returned units, which remained a challenge. Nevertheless, our QIP addressed most of the outstanding blood bank inefficiencies through an intervention package, implemented for such adequate time period, to allow for series of reviews and gradual improvements.

Conclusion

Our QIP identified logistical and operational gaps contributing to blood unit excess and wastage. Through a step-by-step implementation of a multifaceted strategy, to improve staff coverage, coordination and compliance with blood bank standard practices, blood unit wastage due to expiry was reduced to zero. In order to sustain and build on these gains, we recommend that hospital management revive hospital blood bank committee to facilitate regular Continuous Professional Development sessions (CPDs) focused on transfusion practices and modern blood banking.
