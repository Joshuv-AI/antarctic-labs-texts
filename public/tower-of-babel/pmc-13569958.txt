The Human Subiculum and Its Role in Spatiotemporal Memory and Mental Imagery
Authors: Hye Ja Kang, Maria Jieun Hwang, Ji Sun Kim, Ji Yeon Kim, Marie‐Agathe Crampon de Place, Sang Ah Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraditionally viewed as a mere relay station of the hippocampal formation, the subiculum is emerging as a computational hub of the medial temporal lobe, essential for multiplexed neural representations guiding behavior. While foundational animal research has revealed that its sophisticated cytoarchitecture allows for conjunctive coding of spatiotemporal information, our understanding of how such neural properties translate to human cognitive function remains incomplete. The evolutionary expansion of its anterior region and the enhancement of its anatomical complexity and widespread neocortical connectivity imply that the function of the human subiculum may extend beyond the spatial domain. In this review, we examine how the subiculum may serve as a scaffold for goal‐directed spatiotemporal computations in humans and speculate on its role in supporting abstract cognitive functions such as mental imagery and episodic simulation.

Introduction

With its central role in spatial navigation and episodic memory, the hippocampus and its neural circuitry provide crucial insights into the computations underlying cognition and behavior (Eichenbaum 2017; Ekstrom and Ranganath 2018). The hippocampal formation comprises the hippocampus proper (Cornu Ammonis), the dentate gyrus, and the subicular complex. The subicular complex includes the subiculum proper—hereafter, simply the subiculum—together with the presubiculum and parasubiculum (O'Mara et al. 2001; Ding 2013). While neurons in the hippocampus proper and dentate gyrus have been studied intensively as the core components of the trisynaptic circuit that process spatial and episodic memory (Gilbert and Brushfield 2009; Knierim 2015; Hainmueller and Bartos 2020), the unique functional significance of neurons in the subiculum has been difficult to pin down until recently (O'Mara 2005; Ledergerber et al. 2021).

Traditionally, the subiculum was most widely known as the major output region of the hippocampus and often regarded as a passive gating structure rather than an active information processor (O'Mara et al. 2001; O'Mara 2005; Matsumoto et al. 2019). Emerging evidence from animal models, however, indicates that the subiculum plays a key computational role in memory‐guided behavior, supported by its distinct anatomical and physiological properties (Matsumoto et al. 2019; Cembrowski et al. 2018; Kitanishi et al. 2021). Along its longitudinal axis, the subiculum exhibits pronounced gradients in intrinsic spiking characteristics and connectivity (Witter 2006; Cembrowski et al. 2018). These gradients give rise to axis‐dependent specializations that may contribute to multiplexed representations extending beyond spatial coding and enabling conjunctive tuning across multiple features of experience and memory (Ledergerber et al. 2021; Poulter et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2024).

Although the basic architecture of the hippocampus is broadly conserved across mammalian species, several features of the human hippocampus motivate a renewed focus on the role of the subiculum in higher‐order cognition (Ding 2013; Zeidman and Maguire 2016; Bienkowski et al. 2021). The human hippocampus is distinctively elongated, with a significantly expanded and folded anterior region (homologous with the ventral region in rodents) (Zeidman and Maguire 2016; Bienkowski et al. 2021). This morphological reconfiguration parallels the massive evolutionary expansion of the neocortex and a corresponding reorganization of cortico‐hippocampal networks (Ding 2013; Huang et al. 2021; Dalton et al. 2022). In particular, the human subiculum shows extensive connectivity with widespread cortical areas implicated in scene processing, memory, and cognitive control (Huang et al. 2021; Dalton et al. 2022). Such anatomical properties raise the possibility that it occupies a functionally unique position that bridges the gap between cognitive capacities such as spatial navigation, which are shared by distantly related species, and uniquely human abilities such as elaborate mental simulations and imagery (Byrne et al. 2007; Bicanski and Burgess 2018).

The morphological features of the hippocampus have also posed challenges for investigating its subfields. In particular, the small size of individual subfields and their convoluted structure make the boundaries between them difficult to resolve (Zeidman and Maguire 2016; Bienkowski et al. 2021). Fortunately, recent methodological advances, including high‐resolution structural and functional imaging, have made it possible to explore not only the hippocampus as a whole but also subfield‐specific signals and axis‐dependent organization within the hippocampal formation (Dalton et al. 2019; Maass et al. 2015; Grande et al. 2023; for a summary of key empirical studies, see Table 1). In this paper, we review findings on the human subiculum from the past decade, alongside converging evidence from animal studies, with a particular emphasis on its structural and functional heterogeneity. Building on this synthesis, we propose a framework for a unique role of the subiculum in human cognition.

Part 1. The Subiculum as a Hub: Anatomical and Physiological Signatures

The information processing capacity of a brain region is intimately linked to its anatomical organization. Specific structural and physiological features of the subiculum, which diverge from those of adjacent areas, motivate the view that it functions as an active processor rather than a simple relay station. In this section, we first review the subiculum's intrinsic cytoarchitectural, electrophysiological, and connectivity profiles derived from studies of rodents. Building upon this anatomical framework, we then examine the contributions of the subiculum to navigation and memory, as well as its capacity as a computational hub for complex cognitive processes guiding behavior.

Anatomy (Cytoarchitecture)

The subicular complex lies at the interface between the hippocampus proper and the entorhinal cortex. It comprises the prosubiculum (ProS), subiculum, presubiculum (PreS; the dorsal presubiculum is often referred to as postsubiculum in the rodent literature), and parasubiculum (PaS), arranged along the proximal‐distal axis (Ding 2013; Witter 2006; O'Mara et al. 2001). Despite its spatial contiguity, the subiculum exhibits marked differences in cytoarchitecture and laminar organization relative to its adjacent neighbors, with the CA1/ProS border on the proximal side and PreS/PaS on the distal side.

The subiculum is typically described as comprising three principal layers: a superficial molecular layer, a pyramidal layer, and a deep polymorphic layer. The pyramidal layer contains principal pyramidal cells intermingled with interneurons. Compared to that of CA1, this layer is thicker and its constituent neurons more loosely packed (Ding 2013; O'Mara et al. 2001). In contrast, the PreS and PaS possess a more clearly defined multilaminar structure (5–6 layers, characteristic of the periallocortex) and exhibit a thinner, more densely packed principal cell layer than the subiculum (Ding 2013; Witter 2006; O'Mara et al. 2001). These well‐established cellular and laminar distinctions correspond to functional dissociations across the subicular complex, implying that these regions should not be treated as a homogeneous output structure for the hippocampus.

Intrinsic and Molecular Heterogeneity

Within the subiculum, intrinsic neural firing properties vary along multiple anatomical axes. Subicular pyramidal neurons exhibit activity profiles that are distinguishable from those in adjacent regions and are commonly classified as intrinsically bursting or regular‐spiking neurons based on their intrinsic electrophysiological responses (Staff et al. 2000; Jung et al. 2001). Furthermore, these neurons exhibit cell‐type‐specific synaptic plasticity; for example, when low‐frequency stimulation is applied from the CA1, intrinsic bursting neurons typically undergo long‐term depression (LTD), whereas regular‐spiking neurons exhibit long‐term potentiation (LTP) (Fidzinski et al. 2008). Across the transverse (proximal‐distal) axis, these cell types are distributed in a topographical gradient: regular‐spiking cells are more prevalent in the proximal subiculum (near CA1), whereas intrinsically bursting cells are more prevalent in the distal part (toward PreS) (Greene and Totterdell 1997; Jarsky et al. 2008; Cembrowski et al. 2018). Additionally, the regular‐spiking cells are preferentially localized to the superficial layer, while intrinsically bursting cells are predominantly found in the deep layer (Greene and Totterdell 1997; Matsumoto et al. 2019). Local circuitry within the subiculum is also highly organized. Recurrent excitatory connectivity is present, and physiological and circuit‐mapping studies suggest a prominent directional bias: excitatory transmission flows primarily from regular‐spiking to intrinsically bursting neurons, with relatively limited evidence for the reverse direction (Harris et al. 2001; Böhm et al. 2015).

Although classifications based on ex vivo electrophysiology make it challenging to investigate their connection to behavior, studies provide suggestive evidence that distinct neuronal firing properties reflect different functional roles in neural computations (Kepecs et al. 2002; Naud and Sprekeler 2018). Dunn et al. (2018) showed that the proportion of regular‐spiking neurons was increased in rodents that underwent contextual fear conditioning relative to controls, providing indirect evidence that intrinsic firing properties are related to learning. In vivo recordings showed that sparsely bursting subicular neurons tend to exhibit more spatially selective bursting activity compared to the more widely distributed activity of the dominantly bursting neurons (Simonnet and Brecht 2019).

The subiculum's heterogeneity extends beyond intrinsic neural firing patterns and beyond the proximal‐distal axis. Cembrowski et al. (2018) described the mouse subiculum as a transcriptomic “patchwork,” demonstrating that its pyramidal neurons can be subdivided into multiple, spatially segregated subclasses along the longitudinal, transverse, and superficial‐deep axes based on gene expression profiles. This molecular and physiological segregation maps onto distinct projection motifs: subicular subregions and cell classes form partially separable output channels that selectively target specific cortical and subcortical structures (Witter 2006; Kim and Spruston 2012; Cembrowski et al. 2018).

Connectivity

Consistent with its position as a major hippocampal output node, the subiculum serves as a key interface through which hippocampal information processing is communicated to widespread cortical and subcortical systems (O'Mara et al. 2001; Witter 2006; Gao et al. 2026, this issue). Among its cortical partners, interactions with the entorhinal cortex are particularly prominent. The proximal subiculum preferentially connects with the lateral entorhinal cortex (LEC), which is strongly interconnected with the perirhinal cortex and is typically associated with object‐related information (Tamamaki and Nojyo 1995; Witter et al. 2017; Knierim et al. 2014). In parallel, anatomical evidence demonstrates direct interactions between the subiculum and perirhinal cortex, complementing the LEC‐mediated pathway (Naber et al. 1999; Witter 2006; Agster and Burwell 2013). In contrast, the distal subiculum exhibits stronger coupling with the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC), which is more strongly implicated in spatial/contextual processing, alongside PreS/PaS, retrosplenial cortex, and postrhinal cortex (Witter 2006; Witter et al. 2017).

Subicular neural circuitry is most tightly linked with that of the hippocampus proper, receiving its prominent input from CA1. This CA1‐subiculum projection is topographically organized along the transverse axis: distal CA1 projects preferentially to proximal subiculum, whereas proximal CA1 projects preferentially to distal subiculum (Amaral et al. 1991; Naber et al. 2001; Henriksen et al. 2010). Notably, this organization aligns with the medial‐lateral (MEC‐LEC) gradient: proximal CA1 and distal subiculum are more strongly linked to the MEC, whereas distal CA1 and proximal subiculum relate more strongly to LEC connectivity (Naber et al. 2001; Henriksen et al. 2010). This architecture provides an anatomical basis for partially parallel information streams linking CA1‐subiculum output channels with entorhinal subdivisions and their broader cortical partners, rather than a single uniform hippocampal‐entorhinal route (Witter et al. 2000). Furthermore, anatomical and electrophysiological studies show that the subiculum does not only passively receive incoming CA1 signals but also backprojects to CA1 (Jackson et al. 2014; Xu et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2014, 2019). These backprojecting neurons receive strong input from the visual cortex, suggesting that this feedback pathway conveys distinct information compared to the canonical route (Sun et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2026, this issue). Unlike in CA1, where a single cell projects to multiple targets, neurons in the subiculum exhibit a lower degree of axonal collateralization, typically projecting to only one or a few target areas (Naber and Witter 1998). Collectively, the anatomical properties further support the conceptualization of the subiculum as an active computational module that transforms signals before routing them to downstream regions.

Connectivity also varies along the longitudinal axis. In rodents, the dorsal subiculum (approximately homologous to the posterior subiculum in humans) is more strongly connected with the retrosplenial cortex, anterior thalamic nuclei, mammillary bodies, and related posterior cortical regions. Conversely, the ventral subiculum (approximately anterior in humans) projects densely to subcortical and limbic targets, including amygdala, nucleus accumbens, medial prefrontal cortex, and hypothalamic/extended limbic structures (van Groen and Wyss 1990; Canteras and Swanson 1992). This anatomical divergence is consistent with broader dorsal–ventral functional differentiation of the hippocampal formation.

In sum, rodent studies have shown that the anatomical and physiological features place the subiculum in an optimal position to implement selective output routing and transformation, supporting integrative computations that can combine spatial and non‐spatial signals to guide behavior. However, to better understand its role in human cognition, we must consider the unique properties of the hippocampal formation acquired in the evolution of our own species.

The Structural Uniqueness of the Human Subiculum

While the cytoarchitecture of the subiculum is largely conserved across mammals, its organization and large‐scale connectivity appear to have been substantially modified in non‐human primates and humans. Along with the rotation of the hippocampus from the ventral‐dorsal to anterior–posterior axis and the expansion of the anterior hippocampus (Insausti 1993; Bienkowski et al. 2021), the anatomical organization of the subicular complex and its interaction with broader cortical and subcortical systems appear to have undergone substantial modifications since our divergence from rodents (Ding 2013; O'Mara et al. 2001). Notably, the complex folding and backward bending of the anterior hippocampal pole has given rise to the extensive uncal subiculum (see Figure 1). Situated immediately adjacent to the amygdala, this expanded uncal area facilitates dense, reciprocal interactions with the hippocampo‐amygdalar transition area (HATA). Along with these morphological changes, the commissural projections, which extend along much of the hippocampal longitudinal axis in rodents, are largely restricted to anterior regions in primates (Amaral et al. 1984; Zeidman and Maguire 2016). Although direct anatomical evidence in humans remains limited, the same anteriorly biased organization may also be present in the human hippocampus. These profound structural elaborations and enriched local circuitry, combined with increased connectivity with the expanded neocortex, may have enhanced the capacity of the human subiculum to integrate and transform information, thereby supporting important cognitive functions unique to humans.

In rodents, the cytoarchitectural boundary between the ProS and the subiculum is less defined than in primates. Thus, the ProS is frequently subsumed within the proximal subiculum rather than being treated as an independent region (Aggleton and Christiansen 2015). The ProS is more prominently delineated in humans, aligning with the elongation toward the uncal portion and the overall expansion of the anterior hippocampus (Ding 2013; Insausti 1993). Although some studies group the ProS with the subiculum proper under the general label “subiculum,” the ProS shows distinct cytoarchitectural features, including relatively small pyramidal neurons intermingled with larger neurons compared with the subiculum proper, as well as the presence of a sparse zone (Ding 2013; Rosenblum et al. 2024). These differences argue for treating ProS as a functionally meaningful region of its own rather than a mere extension of subiculum proper. In neuroimaging studies, these histological criteria cannot be readily identified from conventional MRI contrast, limiting the reliable separation of the two regions as separate regions of interest.

The connectivity patterns organized along the anatomical axes within the subiculum are broadly conserved from rodents to humans. In particular, intrinsic connectivity differences along the proximal–distal axis appear to be well preserved within the entorhinal‐hippocampal circuitry (Grande et al. 2022; Maass et al. 2015). Consistent with rodent neuroanatomy, the human proximal subiculum shows relatively stronger functional connectivity with distal CA1 and LEC, and the distal subiculum with proximal CA1 and MEC. Strong functional coupling between subiculum and CA1 using high‐resolution resting‐state fMRI provides indirect support for reciprocal interaction between these regions, potentially including a backprojection from the subiculum to CA1 (Dalton et al. 2019). Along the anterior–posterior axis, which corresponds to the dorsoventral axis in rodents, high‐resolution fMRI and meta‐analysis studies reveal differential network connectivity between the anterior and posterior regions of the subiculum. The anterior subiculum shows stronger coupling with the anterior temporal lobe and the default mode network (Leelaarporn et al. 2024), and the posterior subiculum shows stronger coupling with the posterior medial network (Libby et al. 2012; Chase et al. 2015; Dalton et al. 2019). In particular, the longitudinal dissociation in functional connectivity with perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices was most evident in the subiculum and CA1, but not for CA2, CA3, and dentate gyrus, with the strongest gradient in the subiculum (Libby et al. 2012).

Despite these conserved spatial gradients, the human subiculum exhibits distinct macroscopic and connectional features that extend beyond the rodent model. It has extensive direct connections that bypass the entorhinal cortex, reaching widespread regions including early sensory cortices, the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices, and the posterior cingulate cortex (Huang et al. 2021). The functional significance of the subiculum is also evident in the fact that several major spatial and mnemonic circuits receive most of their hippocampal input from it. In primates, the subiculum provides the principal projection to the retrosplenial cortex, anterior thalamic nuclei, and mammillary bodies, whereas direct contributions from the CA subfields to these regions have been reported to be sparse or absent (Aggleton et al. 2012; Aggleton and Christiansen 2015). The broad functional connectivity of the human subiculum suggests that it may occupy an important position in humans as well (Chase et al. 2015; Zeidman and Maguire 2016). However, whereas these pathways have been demonstrated directly using anatomical tracers in nonhuman primates, evidence in humans comes primarily from functional connectivity, combined with relatively limited support from structural imaging.

Task‐Based Conjunctive Coding of Space and Time

The subiculum's representations of space are distinguishable from those of other hippocampal subfields. Direct comparisons have most often been made with CA1, showing that subicular spatial cells exhibit broader and more stable place fields, less prone to remapping when the geometry of the environment changes (e.g., cylinder vs. square) (Sharp 1997; Sharp and Green 1994). Despite having broader spatial tuning, the intrinsically higher firing rates of subicular neurons make it possible for them to carry comparable spatial information per unit time to CA1 neurons (Kim and Spruston 2012; Kitanishi et al. 2021). Beyond place coding, the subiculum encodes a diverse array of spatial variables, including environmental boundaries (Lever et al. 2009; Stewart et al. 2014; Muessig et al. 2024), geometric corners (Sun et al. 2024), intra‐room landmarks (Poulter et al. 2021), and current axis of travel (Olson et al. 2017).

Among all the spatial variables represented in the subiculum, environmental geometry is particularly prominent, providing a foundational scaffold for spatial mapping. A striking commonality across these representations is their conjunctive nature (Bicanski and Burgess 2020). Subicular neurons often encode not only specific geometric features of the environment (e.g., corners, boundaries) but also the agent‐feature relationship in a vectorial format, such as the allocentric distance or direction to those features (Lever et al. 2009; Stewart et al. 2014; Sun et al. 2024). Lever et al. (2009) recorded neurons in the dorsal subiculum while rats foraged in an arena and identified cells whose firing was anchored to spatial boundaries, not only to the perimeter wall but also to an internal wall placed within the enclosure. Because their activity was systematically modulated by the allocentric direction and distance to boundaries, these neurons were termed boundary vector cells (BVCs). This conjunctiveness contrasts with border cells in the entorhinal cortex (Solstad et al. 2008; Bicanski and Burgess 2020), which primarily respond when an animal is in close proximity to a boundary, serving more as immediate boundary detectors. Notably, the “boundary” represented by these cells is not restricted to an impenetrable physical wall. Subicular BVCs also respond to crossable yet behaviorally meaningful boundaries, such as gaps or drop‐offs that structure the space despite being traversable. This suggests that, for subicular BVCs, a “boundary” may be defined less by its physical form per se and more by its functional relevance for spatial mapping and navigation. Consistent with this broader, structure‐oriented coding, Lee et al. (2018) showed that during a T‐maze spatial choice task, subicular activity was strongly modulated by task‐defined epochs (e.g., pre‐choice vs. post‐choice) rather than by specific locations.

The subiculum's sensitivity to spatial structure extends beyond direct perception to include a mnemonic component. Subicular responses can persist even after physical cues (e.g., obstacles) are removed from the environment, implying a memory trace of prior spatial configurations. The discovery of vector trace cells (VTCs), which continue to fire at the former location of a removed object, exemplifies this property (Poulter et al. 2021). This persistence of neuronal firing indicates that the subiculum does not merely reflect immediate sensory inputs but actively maintains a stable internal model of the spatial context. Although the direct relationship between intrinsic firing properties, connectivity, and single‐cell spatial representations requires further investigation, the dissociable spatial distribution of the representative cells allows us to infer functional associations. For instance, VTCs are predominantly found in the distal part of the dorsal subiculum (Poulter et al. 2021). Although found in both the proximal and distal subiculum, BVCs exhibit a distributional bias toward the distal region, closer to the PreS and retrosplenial cortex, aligning with the spatial connectivity gradient (Lever et al. 2009). This distal bias is further supported by findings that the distal subiculum is selectively required for spatial working memory tasks (Cembrowski et al. 2018).

Conjunctive coding, which is more pronounced in the subiculum than in other hippocampal subfields, may provide an important clue as to its role in integrating information from multiple sources into a more compressed form. Ledergerber et al. (2021) simultaneously recorded CA1 and subicular neurons and showed that although both regions contain place‐selective cells, subicular activity is more spatially dispersed and exhibits stronger task‐dependence, meaning that single neurons encode multiple variables in combination (e.g., position, head direction, and running speed) rather than tuning to a single feature in isolation. Nakai et al. (2024) showed that population activity from CA1 and subiculum may be embedded into low‐dimensional neural manifolds and demonstrated that these ensemble spatiotemporal trajectories carry navigational variables, such as position, speed, and future path, with higher decoding accuracy in the subiculum than CA1. This population‐level advantage also extends to offline activity; for instance, during post‐task sharp‐wave ripples in slow‐wave sleep, reward locations and events were represented more frequently in the subiculum than in CA1. Given the subiculum's role as a major hippocampal output region that interfaces with distributed cortical and subcortical targets, such mixed coding may provide downstream regions with a compact, multi‐feature state representation that is easier to read out for navigation and memory‐guided behavior (Rigotti et al. 2013; Ledergerber et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2022).

Studies using inactivation methods and pathway‐specific manipulation highlight how this circuitry supports multiple components of episodic‐like memory. For example, dorsal subiculum lesions impair memory for spatial locations and for the temporal order of spatial experiences, while sparing non‐spatial objects and odor, suggesting a selective role in spatiotemporal organization of memory (Potvin et al. 2010). CA1‐projecting subicular neurons have been shown to facilitate the association of objects to spatial locations in memory: silencing this feedback pathway during learning impairs object‐place memory, whereas stimulating it enhances memory and modulates place‐selective activity in the CA1 (Sun et al. 2019).

Several lines of evidence suggest that the subiculum is more strongly engaged in memory retrieval than encoding. Roy et al. (2017) reported a clear functional double dissociation between memory formation and retrieval pathways in mice using circuit‐specific optogenetic manipulations and contextual fear‐learning paradigms. They found that memory encoding requires the direct CA1‐to‐MEC projection, whereas memory retrieval critically depends on an indirect pathway passing through the subiculum (CA1 → subiculum → entorhinal cortex). Silencing the subiculum or its connections during recall selectively impaired memory retrieval across multiple hippocampal‐dependent tasks. Conversely, inhibition of the direct CA1–entorhinal pathway during encoding disrupted memory formation but had no effect when applied during recall. Furthermore, the ventral subiculum‐ventral striatum pathway has also been shown to be crucial for spatial memory consolidation and learning‐induced plasticity in the ventral striatum (Torromino et al. 2019).

Anatomical, functional, and physiological studies in nonhuman animal models demonstrate that the subiculum may be specialized for providing compressed, task‐driven, spatiotemporal representations for efficient and adaptive memory retrieval and behavior. However, given the unique anatomical properties of the human subiculum reviewed above, an examination of its potentially unique role in human cognition is also needed.

Part 2. Functional Role of the Subiculum in Human Cognition

Abstract Spatial Representation and Episodic Simulation

Spatial representations in the rodent subiculum translate remarkably well to humans (Herweg and Kahana 2018; Lee et al. 2018). Like other animals, humans use environmental boundaries to estimate metric distances, reorient in space, and correct navigational errors (Hartley et al. 2004; Doeller and Burgess 2008; Bellmund et al. 2020; Zisch et al. 2024; Segen et al. 2025, this issue). Human VR‐based studies have provided evidence that geometric information about environmental boundaries is represented in the subiculum and entorhinal cortex. Using fMRI multi‐voxel pattern analyses (MVPA), Shine et al. (2019) successfully decoded allocentric boundary direction from both the subiculum and entorhinal cortex, with particularly robust representations in the posterior portion of these regions. Notably, anterior regions showed preferential selectivity to goal direction information rather than boundaries, suggesting that the subiculum does not merely represent environmental geometry but may integrate it with goal‐related signals along its anterior–posterior axis. Together with evidence that the entorhinal/subicular region encodes environment‐centered goal direction to simulate future trajectories (Chadwick et al. 2015), these findings indicate that the subiculum possesses the capacity to integrate environmental geometry with goal signals to guide adaptive behavior.

Consistent with electrophysiological evidence of theta‐modulated BVCs in the rodent brain (Lever et al. 2009; Newman et al. 2026, this issue), direct intracranial recordings of the human hippocampal formation have shown boundary‐modulated theta oscillations in the subiculum (Lee et al. 2018; Stangl et al. 2021; Maoz et al. 2023). In Lee et al. (2018), theta power increased significantly in the subiculum, but not in other hippocampal subfields or the entorhinal cortex, when participants navigated toward goal locations near the boundaries of a VR arena. Importantly, this boundary‐proximity effect remained robust even after controlling for confounds such as trial length, speed, movement, and overall performance, indicating that subicular activity reflects the spatial representation of goal locations relative to environmental boundaries on a trial‐by‐trial basis. Furthermore, Stangl et al. (2021) demonstrated that boundary‐modulated theta activity emerges not only during self‐movement but also while observing another person navigating close to the walls of the room. Although these effects were observed broadly across the MTL rather than being specific to the subiculum, they nonetheless provide converging evidence that the subiculum is involved in not only representing one's own proximity to boundaries but also that of other individuals and target locations at a distance.

Recent evidence from our own lab illustrates that the subiculum may be involved in an even higher level of abstraction of spatial information. Using high‐resolution 7T fMRI, Kim et al. (forthcoming) examined spatial chunking behavior and its neural correlates during a multi‐target navigation task. Participants sequentially collected eight targets in a virtual room and, after a delay, navigated to and marked each location in order (Figure 2A). They found that activation of the anterior subiculum was associated with a chunked grid‐like pattern of responses that were anchored to the environmental geometry. For instance, despite the fact that target locations were distributed randomly across a square‐shaped environment, most of the participants' responses clustered around nine nodes in a square array; changes to the boundary geometry (e.g., rectangular room, 2‐wall room) altered this array. This spatial chunking behavior was associated specifically with the anterior subiculum and was functionally dissociable from the posterior dentate gyrus, which was associated with memory of the exact target location. The involvement of the subiculum in spatial chunking suggests that it may integrate geometry‐based spatial information with task demands to support efficient memory‐guided navigation.

(A) In a multi‐target navigation task, participants sequentially collected coins in a virtual room and later recalled their locations. Although the targets were uniformly distributed, most participants exhibited a node‐like chunking pattern in their responses reflecting the boundary layout of the room. For instance, when two of the four surrounding boundaries were replaced with transparent barriers, nodes near them became less evident. Anterior subicular activity increased when participants responded on the chunked nodes and scaled with individual tendency toward chunking bias (Kim et al. forthcoming). (B) An episodic memory task in which participants watched five objects sequentially placed into baskets scattered within a virtual room and reenacted the event sequence (left, bottom). Spatiotemporal binding, reflected in the correct retrieval order of baskets, was well preserved even when the object order was forgotten. The subiculum showed greater engagement in this process than other hippocampal subfields. The enhancement of spatiotemporal association through navigation training (left, top) generalized to improvement in the non‐navigational episodic memory task, with increased activation and pattern reinstatement specifically in the subiculum (but not in CA1 or CA23DG) (Shin et al. 2026).

Subicular memory processing may provide a framework for structuring not only spatial navigation but also broader cognitive functions, including spatiotemporal episodic memory. Of the what, where, and when components of episodic memory, the binding of space (where) and time (when) is thought to provide a scaffold for constructing coherent events (Dickerson and Eichenbaum 2010; Eichenbaum 2017; Ekstrom and Ranganath 2018; Lee 2023; Chandra et al. 2025). Consistent with this view, successful memory of spatial sequences emerges early in human development and is followed by the association of objects to spatial locations to give rise to a fully bound episode consisting of objects bound to their spatiotemporal contexts (Mastrogiuseppe et al. 2019).

A recent study in our lab (Shin et al. 2026) suggests that spatiotemporal binding in episodic memory has primacy even in adulthood and may be supported by high‐dimensional subicular representations (Figure 2B). Using a virtual reality what‐where‐when episodic memory task, we first demonstrated that participants across both Korea and the United States tend to preserve where‐when information even while forgetting the order of objects. Ultra‐high‐resolution 7T fMRI further revealed that the subiculum was selectively engaged for where‐when memory, whereas comparable effects were not observed in CA1 or CA23DG (CA2, CA3 and dentate gyrus). Furthermore, compared with other hippocampal subfields, the subiculum exhibited higher representational dimensionality and complexity. Consistent with evidence from animal models, these findings suggest that multiplexed subicular representations may give rise to task‐related integration of spatiotemporal information.

In the same study, we also explored whether this subiculum‐dependent episodic memory can be enhanced through where‐when binding in navigational training (Figure 2B). Participants completed a two‐week multi‐target virtual navigation training paradigm designed to engage hippocampal spatiotemporal processing. Relative to an active control group, navigation training produced robust improvements in non‐navigational episodic memory; these behavioral improvements were accompanied by increased retrieval‐related activation and stronger encoding‐retrieval neural pattern reinstatement specifically in the subiculum. Because changes in subicular activity were associated with enhanced spatiotemporal memory retrieval, these findings reveal that the subiculum is not only a critical substrate for organizing episodic memories through high‐dimensional spatiotemporal representations, but that the reconstruction of these representations can be adaptively modified to extend more generally to improvements in episodic memory retrieval.

Indeed, evidence from other human studies converges upon findings from rodent models that the subiculum is especially important in episodic memory retrieval. One study showed that the subiculum, particularly its posterior part, exhibits greater activation during recall than during the initial learning or encoding of associative face‐name, word, and object pairs (Suthana et al. 2015). Moreover, in associative memory tasks that distinguished detailed recollection from a feeling of familiarity, subicular activity was found to be more pronounced during retrieval than encoding and for detailed recollection than familiarity‐based recognition (Eldridge et al. 2005).

From a circuit perspective, this preferential role in retrieval likely reflects a division of labor across hippocampal subfields. CA3 is thought to support the storage and retrieval of associative representations through its recurrent network, allowing incomplete retrieval cues to reactivate previously stored patterns through pattern completion. CA1 then compares these retrieved representations with current sensory input, thereby detecting whether the retrieved information is consistent with the present context. The subiculum reconstructs these signals into a high‐dimensional, mixed‐selective code, making them more readily available to downstream regions involved in memory‐guided behavior (Ledergerber et al. 2021; Nakai et al. 2024; Shin et al. 2026).

The subiculum's multiplexed code can concurrently represent multiple features and predictive variables, providing flexibility in reconstructing coherent, detailed episodes and embedding them within ongoing decision‐making processes that ultimately lead to behavior. In line with this view, Molitor et al. (2021) showed that the human subiculum exhibits dual functionality during associative inference with overlapping picture pairs (A–B, B–C). On one hand, like the CA23DG, it supported pattern differentiation by reducing similarity between overlapping memories (A and C); at the same time, higher post‐learning activation pattern similarity between A and C in the subiculum predicted faster and more accurate inferential memory.

Complementary evidence comes from work on predictive coding. Using fMRI and an incidental auditory‐cue‐to‐visual‐shape association task, Aitken and Kok (2022) showed that both CA1 and the subiculum encode prediction errors across learning, with the CA1 exhibiting stronger prediction‐error signals during the early phase. In contrast, once cue‐shape associations were established, the posterior subiculum preferentially represented cue‐predicted shape irrespective of the actual input, and its functional connectivity with the early visual cortex (V1) increased as participants learned to anticipate upcoming visual stimuli. These findings suggest that the subiculum not only serves as the major hippocampal output to the neocortex but also conveys predictive signals to sensory areas to shape perception. However, this remains one of the only studies to directly link subiculum‐neocortex connectivity in predictive learning, and the connectivity analyses were undirected. Future studies employing methods that allow for causal inference will be important to the understanding of information flow from the subiculum to the cortex.

Mental Simulation and Imagery

Given that the subiculum flexibly supports adaptive behavior by leveraging its multiplexed representations to broadcast predictive information, its function may extend beyond memory to support constructive episodic processes that integrate information within and across experiences and contexts. The Scene Construction Theory posits that the hippocampus enables disparate elements to be assembled into a coherent scene within a spatial framework (Maguire and Mullally 2013). Along its anterior–posterior axis, neuroimaging studies have consistently shown stronger involvement of the anteromedial hippocampus, relative to posterior portions, during scene construction (Zeidman and Maguire 2016; Dalton et al. 2018; Barry et al. 2019). Within this anteromedial portion, the subicular complex has been identified as a hub of the scene‐processing network, although some have particularly emphasized the PreS/PaS due to their close links with the retrosplenial cortex, parahippocampal cortex, and posterior cingulate cortex, which are key components of the visuospatial processing system (Dalton and Maguire 2017; Zeidman and Maguire 2016).

As discussed above, the human subiculum maintains both direct and indirect connections with cortical regions involved in visuospatial processing. While structural studies indicate that medial parietal and parahippocampal areas, including the retrosplenial cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and parahippocampal cortex, preferentially connect with the posterior subiculum (Dalton et al. 2022), functional connectivity reveals a more nuanced organization, with systematic differences along the transversal axis (Grande et al. 2022). Specifically, fMRI voxels within the entorhinal cortex that showed higher intrinsic connectivity with the parahippocampal cortex also exhibited higher connectivity to the distal subiculum. On the other hand, entorhinal cortex voxels with higher functional connectivity to the retrosplenial cortex were also connected to the proximal subiculum (i.e., CA1 border).

At the same time, there is some evidence that retrosplenial cortex activity is more strongly coordinated with the anterior than the posterior subiculum (Chase et al. 2015). This anterior‐biased connectivity may be explained, at least in part, by the rotated organization of homologous subicular regions across species. According to the translational model of Bienkowski et al. (2021), the ventral portion of the anterior human subiculum corresponds to the mouse dorsal subiculum, which is closely associated with the retrosplenial cortex. This connectivity pattern is functionally significant, as the retrosplenial cortex has been implicated in transformations between egocentric and allocentric representations (Byrne et al. 2007). Beyond these long‐range cortical projections, the subiculum is also closely interconnected with the PreS/PaS, providing dense outputs while receiving reciprocal inputs (O'Mara et al. 2001; Dalton et al. 2019; Huang et al. 2021).

Functional evidence for the subiculum's involvement in scene processing comes from its specific response to scenic stimuli and its recruitment during tasks involving imagination and construction. The subiculum exhibits preferential activation and greater pattern similarity for scenes compared to objects or faces (Hodgetts et al. 2017; Read et al. 2024), a preference that Grande et al. (2022) found to be biased toward the distal portion. Further support for anterior subiculum involvement in scene construction comes from a retrieval‐based study in which participants first encoded full scenes and later recalled their temporal order from partial cues (object or background) (Hwang and Lee 2024). Within the anterior hippocampus, CA1 and subiculum showed particularly robust neural patterns associated with scene construction, indicating a key role for these subfields in reconstructing coherent scenes from partial cues. Additionally, while the PreS/PaS have been reported to be involved in both imagination and memory retrieval, the anterior subiculum is more selectively recruited during imagination (Zeidman et al. 2015).

Change in Subicular Function and its Effect on Cognition Across the Lifespan

Given its essential role in higher‐level cognition, studying the developmental maturation and age‐related decline of subicular function may be useful in understanding cognitive change across the lifespan.

In rodents, development of the hippocampal formation is accompanied by the progressive maturation of spatial representations and, presumably, the cognitive abilities that depend on them. While principal neurons in the subiculum are generated relatively early in gestation compared to the CA fields, the laminar organization and connectivity of the subiculum continue to undergo substantial postnatal refinement (Bayer 1980; Khalaf‐Nazzal and Francis 2013). This prolonged anatomical maturation is paralleled by the gradual development of spatial representations, particularly for BVCs. In rat pups, head‐direction cells in the PreS/PaS emerge even before eye opening (Bjerknes et al. 2015) and are thought to guide the stabilization of CA1 place fields and boundary‐related representations in the MEC and subiculum during the animal's earliest explorations of the environment (Muessig et al. 2016, 2024). Across the first month of life, conjunctive vector coding in the subiculum continues to mature further, following the development of adult‐like grid representations in the MEC (Muessig et al. 2024). This extended developmental trajectory may further reflect the multiplexed, integrative properties of subicular spatial coding, which may support increasingly complex cognitive processes.

In humans, hippocampal volume increases throughout childhood and early adulthood, but developmental trajectories differ by subregion. Canada et al. (2021) observed an increase in subiculum volume in preschool children, especially between ages 5 and 6. However, this change did not follow a linear trajectory across development. When considering the entire span from childhood through early adulthood, subicular volume appears to increase initially before reaching a plateau. This nonlinear volumetric increase from childhood to adulthood is specific to the subiculum and CA1, compared to other hippocampal subregions (Tamnes et al. 2018). Diffusion imaging‐derived microstructure of hippocampal subfields has further dissociated subicular development from that of CA1, showing a gradual increase in white matter integrity from childhood to adulthood in the subiculum, in contrast with a nonlinear development of CA1 (Vinci‐Booher et al. 2023). Interestingly, one longitudinal study showed that the volumetric gains in the subiculum during childhood are more tightly associated with improvements in spatial memory compared to other subregions (Keresztes et al. 2022). These results suggest that the early structural maturation of the subiculum, and its continual development across young adulthood through increased neuronal density and myelination, may be especially meaningful for cognitive development.

Later in life, the subiculum becomes particularly vulnerable to age‐related degeneration. Based on rodent studies, aging is generally accompanied by hippocampal synaptic loss and volume reduction associated with memory decline (Xu et al. 2018; Hamezah et al. 2017). One study on nonhuman primates reported significantly lower spine density in the subiculum of aged animals than young animals, suggesting an age‐related decline in subicular function (Uemura 1985). In Alzheimer's Disease (AD) mouse models, theta‐gamma coupling was disrupted across hippocampal subregions, including the subiculum, even before the accumulation of amyloid‐beta (Goutagny et al. 2013). Furthermore, another study showed that amyloid‐beta accumulation in the subiculum induced neuronal hyperexcitability (Chen et al. 2025; Hijazi et al. 2023). Based on these findings, we speculate that alterations in subicular activity in humans may lead to an age‐related decline in higher‐order cognitive functions such as mental imagery and episodic simulations.

In humans, age‐related neuronal loss (West et al. 1994) and volume reduction are more pronounced in the subiculum, compared to other hippocampal subregions (de Flores et al. 2015). Among participants in their 90s and older, larger subicular volumes predicted better memory performance than CA1, CA2/3, or the dentate gyrus (Eguchi et al. 2019), suggesting that preservation of subicular integrity may mitigate cognitive decline particularly in late aging. Structural findings further indicate that subicular volume and its associated fornix microstructure are specifically related to free recall performance, a relationship not found in other hippocampal subregions (Hartopp et al. 2019). Along the anterior–posterior axis, age‐related disruption appears especially pronounced in anterior hippocampal connectivity with the parahippocampal cortex, a circuit crucial for memory and spatial processing (Stark et al. 2021), raising the possibility that the anterior subiculum may be a brain region of interest related to individual vulnerability or resilience to cognitive aging.

Beyond the scope of healthy aging, subicular atrophy has emerged as a pivotal indicator of pathological cognitive decline. Several studies point to subicular atrophy as a sensitive marker for AD. For instance, comparisons across healthy controls, individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and patients with AD revealed stepwise hippocampal volume loss overall; but subregional analyses revealed that only the subiculum and PreS consistently differed across all disease stages, underscoring their selective involvement in disease progression (Carlesimo et al. 2015). In terms of functional decline, the association between individual differences in scene construction and hippocampal volume in frontotemporal dementia patients (Wilson et al. 2020) may be attributed to preservation of the subiculum and PreS/PaS, although this has not been shown directly. In contrast, individual differences in scene construction in AD patients were associated with the posterior parietal cortex, presumably due to the common hippocampal dysfunction (including the subiculum) across AD patients.

Complementing this structural evidence, resting‐state functional connectivity between the subiculum and the transentorhinal cortex, a transitional area between the perirhinal cortex and the entorhinal cortex, has been shown to decline with age (Dalton et al. 2019). Given that the transentorhinal cortex is among the first regions to exhibit tau protein accumulation, disrupted subiculum–transentorhinal connectivity may index early network‐level changes associated with both age‐related and pathological memory decline.

The evidence reviewed above suggests that across the human lifespan, the subiculum undergoes prolonged development, is disproportionately affected by aging and AD, and shows strong associations with individual cognitive ability. Given its established contribution to episodic memory and scene processing, in addition to newly emerging evidence for its role in abstract thinking, it is plausible that the maturation and deterioration of the subiculum and its associated networks in development and aging, respectively, play a pivotal role in the gain and loss of cognitive functions that depend on rich, episodic mental constructions.

Conclusion

The scientific study of hippocampal subfield circuitry and function in animal models continues to unveil the fascinating neural processes that give rise to complex cognition and behavior. Indeed, much of human neuroscience thus far has confirmed the existence of similar navigational and memory mechanisms in the human brain, from network activity down to the level of single neurons. Nevertheless, the evolutionarily recent changes in the anterior subiculum and its connectivity to the neocortex present new prospects for exploring its role in giving rise to uniquely human cognitive abilities. We believe that our review marks only the beginning of many exciting discoveries on the human subiculum for decades to come.
