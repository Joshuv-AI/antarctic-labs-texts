Genomic characterization of SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating in the population of Bangui, Central African Republic (CAR) in 2022
Authors: Pulchérie Pelembi, Philippe Colson, Alain Farra, Ornella Anne Sibiro-Demi, Christian Malaka, Aurélia Kwasiborski, Véronique Hourdel, Gilles Landry Ngaya, Romaric Nzoumbou-Boko, Jean-Claude Manuguerra, Emmanuel Ryvalin Nakoune-Yandoko, Guy Vernet, Bernard La Scola, Valérie Caro, Alexandre Manirakiza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWith the ongoing global resurgence of COVID-19, it is crucial to better understand past transmission dynamics to strengthen national preparedness for future surges. Since its emergence in Wuhan, China, in December 2019, SARS-CoV-2 has caused multiple waves of infection worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified several variants of concern, including Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta. At the end of 2021, the Omicron variant emerged in South Africa and rapidly spread across the globe. While many studies have explored the evolution and transmission of these variants, data from the Central African Republic remained limited. This study aimed to identify and characterize SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating in Bangui to support genomic surveillance and COVID-19 prevention strategies. Characterizing circulating variants provides essential information for the early detection of emerging lineages, the anticipation of potential epidemic waves, and the adaptation of public health strategies. MethodologyWe conducted a retrospective, descriptive study in Bangui, Central African Republic (CAR), from January to August 2022. A total of 102 nasopharyngeal samples positive for SARS-CoV-2 (Ct ≤ 30), selected from the biobank of the Pasteur Institute in Bangui, were reanalyzed using multiplex qPCR to confirm their Ct values. Eligible samples were then sequenced using the MinION MK1C (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) platform, following the ARTIC Network protocol at the Institut Pasteur, Paris (CIBU). Bioinformatics processing using reads mapping to a reference genome, followed by consensus sequence generation, included standard steps of basecalling, demultiplexing, quality filtering, genome alignment, primer trimming, consensus generation, and variant calling. The different variants were described using the Nexstrain web application.ResultsThe results revealed that 66.7% of sequenced genomes (n = 68) had high coverage ≥ 80%, all belonging to the Omicron variant. Four sub-lineages were identified according to the Pangolin classification: BA.1 (14.7%), BA.2 (35.3%), BA.4.1 (4.4%), and BA.5.1 (45.6%). These sub-lineages were present in all eight districts, with a predominance in the eighth district, and were most frequently diagnosed in June (fourth wave of the epidemic).ConclusionOmicron was the only variant detected among successfully sequenced samples, although limited sampling and sequencing success restrict broader generalisation. It is crucial to establish continuous surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 to detect any emerging variants in real time, while strengthening sequencing capabilities at the national level. Continuous genomic surveillance is crucial to anticipate potential future COVID-19 waves and to enable timely public health responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03203-4.

Introduction

First identified in Wuhan, China, in December 2019, SARS-CoV-2 rapidly spread worldwide, prompting the World Health Organization to declare a Public Health Emergency of International Concern [1, 2]. However, lockdown measures, health barriers, and adjustments to healthcare systems have been adopted in many countries to deal with this threat [3, 4 ]. These epidemic waves, modulated by population density, international mobility and heterogeneous public health policy responses, have had major economic and social consequences [3], highlighting inequalities in the response to the pandemic [5]. The evolution of the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic [6] thus reveals not only the dynamics of the virus, but also the global challenges in terms of health and international solidarity. At the same time, the emergence of different variants has made the situation even more complex, complicating the pandemic management.

The WHO subsequently designated several variants as being of concern (VOC), including strains B.1.1.7 (Alpha), B.1.351 (Beta), P.1 (Gamma), B.1.617.2 (Delta) and, more recently, B.1.1.529 (Omicron) [7, 8]. These variants accumulated mutations, particularly in the spike protein, which is the primary target of vaccine-induced neutralizing antibodies. Such mutations may modify key antigenic regions and promote partial immune escape, leading to reduced vaccine effectiveness against infection and transmission. Consequently, the ability of vaccines to prevent infection and transmission has been called into question, necessitating adaptations [9, 10]. Genomic surveillance, meanwhile, has proved essential for detecting the emergence of new variants and monitoring their spread and evolution. Understanding the transmission dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 variants is essential to better manage the pandemic, but few data exist concerning their impact in the Central African Republic [10]. Studies in various countries have shown that the Alpha, Delta and Omicron variants exhibit varying rates of transmissibility and levels of infection severity, thus influencing public health strategies [11, 12].

However, the Central African Republic, a country with fragile health infrastructure, recurrent sociopolitical crises, and limited research capacity, faces significant challenges in implementing comprehensive genomic surveillance programs [13, 14]. Alongside these VOCs, variants of interest (VOI) and variants under monitoring (VUM) have also been defined. These variants present heterogeneous profiles with respect to transmissibility, immune evasion, and neutralization capacity. Nevertheless, robust surveillance data for emerging variants remain scarce [12, 15]. The limited data on the SARS-COV-2 variants prevailing in the CAR has hindered the design and implementation of appropriate control strategies. Consequently, it is critical to strengthen research and surveillance capabilities in CAR.

This study aimed to characterize the predominant SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating in the Central African Republic (CAR) and generate genomic surveillance data to support public health decision-making and COVID-19 control strategies.

Materials and methods

Type, location and study population

This is a retrospective, descriptive study conducted from January to August 2022 at the Pasteur Institute of Bangui (IPB) in the Central African Republic. It was carried out as part of the sentinel surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 infection. The study included both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients presenting to the IPB for SARS-CoV-2 testing and aimed to analyze the genomic characteristics of circulating variants. During the study period, 506 SARS-CoV-2 positive cases were identified. A total of 102 samples were selected for whole-genome sequencing based on a cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 30 and availability of stored specimens for several months in the IPB biobank prior to sequencing due to limited sequencing capacity at the time. This selection criterion was applied to maximize sequencing success, as higher viral loads are associated with improved genome recovery using the ARTIC tiled amplicon protocol and Oxford Nanopore sequencing. The sampling strategy was not randomized. Sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were carried out in France at the Institut Pasteur Paris (IPP), specifically at the Emergency Biological Intervention Unit (CIBU). Statistical analysis was performed at the Mediterranean Infection University Hospital Institute (IHU) in Marseille.

Data management

We established an Excel database, containing anonymized socio-demographic data, SARS-CoV-2 exposure history, symptoms profiles, and clinical metadata. This database was updated daily to incorporate newly enrolled participants. Following genomic sequencing, variant designations and lineage information were integrated into the dataset. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.2 for Tables 1 and 2; and Excel (Table 3; Fig. 1) to characterize variants distribution patterns across socio-demographic characteristics, geographic regions and temporal collection periods. The genomic sequences were submitted to the GISAID database under access code EPI_ISL_10854812–10,854,859.

Biological analysis of samples

Viral load quantification

Collected nasopharyngeal swab samples were processed in a laminar flow hood and PCR was performed in two ways: (i) Using the GeneXpert platform (Cepheid Sunnyvale, USA). (ii) Real-time reverse transcription (RT)-PCR using the TaqPath COVID-19 CE-IVD RT-PCR Kit (Thermo Fischer, Waltham, USA), following RNA extraction with the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit - cfDNA/cfRNA Isolation, according to the manufacturer’s procedure (Qiagen San Diego, USA). This kit uses primers and probes targeting the three genome regions (S, N and Orf1a/b) of SARS-CoV-2 (Viana et al. 2022). A total of 102 SARS CoV-2-positive samples obtained in both cases, with a Ct value of 30 or less, were selected for next-generation sequencing with Oxford Nanopore Technologies’.

Sequencing, NGS technology from Oxford nanopore technologie

ONT libraries for SARS-CoV-2 sequencing were performed according to the protocol nCoV-2019 sequencing protocol v3 (LoCost) V.3 [16]. from ARTIC Network. The cDNA synthesis was carried out using LunaScript RT SuperMix (New England Biolabs, NEB) followed by multiplex PCR using ARTIC V4.1 primers (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT). For each sample, two separate PCR reactions were performed, using the two primer pools. The cycling program was as follows: initial step 30 s at 98 °C, followed by 15 s at 98 °C, and 5 min at 65 °C, for a cycle number between 25 and 35 and cooling at 4 °C. PCR amplicons were then purified using 0.8 x bead to sample ratio with AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter) and quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the end repair step, NebNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-Tailing module (NEB) were used, with incubation at 25 °C for 15 min, followed by 65 °C for 15 min. Native barcodes (EXP-NBD104 and 114, ONT) were then ligated to the amplicons using Blunt/TA Ligase Master Mix (New England Biolabs), with the following incubation step: 25 °C for 20 min, 65 °C for 10 min, and cooling at 4 °C for 1 min. All barcoded samples were pooled together and purified using AMPure XP beads. Sequencing adapters were ligated using NebNext Quick Ligation Module (NEB) followed by a final purification with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). A total of 15 ng of the final library was loaded on R9.4.1 flow cell in a MinION Mk1C (ONT) device.

Quality control and inclusion criteria

Genome quality was evaluated based on genome coverage, median sequencing depth, proportion of ambiguous bases (Ns), and consensus genome length. Following ARTIC Network guidelines, reads were filtered, aligned to the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome (NC_045512.2), and used to generate consensus sequences, with ambiguous positions retained as Ns. Genomes with ≥ 80% coverage were retained for downstream analyses, while those below this threshold were excluded.

All laboratory and bioinformatics procedures were performed in alignment with internationally established genomic surveillance standards, including the ARTIC Network framework for tiled amplicon sequencing and consensus quality control, thereby ensuring methodological rigor and global data comparability.

Bioinformatics analysis

The raw sequencing data were generated in FAST5 file format. Basecalling and demultiplexing was performed using Guppy software (version 6.0.7) with the fast-accuracy model to convert FAST5 files into FASTQ format. The bioinformatic pipeline (https://artic.network/viruses/sars-cov-2) was used to generate consensus sequences in fasta format for each sample. Reads underwent quality control and filtering prior to alignment to the reference genome of SARS-CoV-2. Primer sequences were removed using the actual primer trimming tool. During consensus generation, positions with sequencing depth below the pipeline threshold (≥ 20×) were masked as ambiguous bases (Ns). Genome coverage was defined as the proportion of the reference genome covered by confidently called bases. Genomes not meeting this coverage threshold were excluded from further analysis. The downloaded sequences were aligned using the Mafft software [17]. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using a maximum likelihood approach implemented in IQ-TREE2 [18]. Branch support was assessed using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL [19], lineage assignment was performed using the Pangolin tool according to standard recommendations and clade/lineage assignment was performed with Nextclade [20].

Phylogenetic analysis was exploratory and not intended for fine-scale inference.

Results

Characteristics of participants and variants identified

The study involved sequencing 102 SARS-CoV-2-positive nasopharyngeal samples with a Ct ≤ 30, obtained from participants who had given their informed consent. The mean age of the patients was 41 years, with a standard deviation of 11.8. The median age was 39 years [34; 50]. People aged 30 to 39 years were the most affected, with 85.3% (N = 87) of participants being male. Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the total patients versus those with genomic coverage above 80%. Table 2 presents the characteristics (age, sex, collection periods, district, symptoms) according to the variants identified among the 68 samples (66.7%) with high-coverage consensus genome (≥ 80%). All sequences belonged to the Omicron variant, comprising four sub-lineages according to the Pangolin nomenclature. Among the 68 genomes with ≥ 80% coverage, the distribution was as follows: BA.1 (14.7%, n = 10), BA.2 (35.3%, n = 24), BA.4.1 (4.4%, n = 3) and BA.5.1 (45.6%, n = 31). 72% (N = 49) of patients were screened for suspected COVID-19 cases, compared to 28% for international travel purposes. Symptomatic infections (fever, cough, and headache) were observed in more than 70% (n = 48) of participants. Temporal trends were assessed using sample collection dates, as no time-scaled phylogenetic reconstruction was performed.

Sequencing performance and quality metrics

Across all sequenced samples (n = 102), the mean genome coverage was 71%, while the median coverage reached 92% ; the proportion of ambiguous bases (N) was 29% (cf. Table 4).

Among retained genomes (≥ 80% coverage ; n = 68), mean genome coverage was 95% (median: 93.6%), supporting reliable base calling, indicating high genome completeness. The proportion of ambiguous bases (Ns) averaged 4.97%, reflecting overall good sequence quality despite minor unresolved regions. The median sequencing depth reached 93.6×, ensuring reliable consensus generation. Genomic coverage was relatively homogeneous across samples, as evidenced by the very close mean and median values. Genome length (mean, bp) was 29,863, near-complete SARS Cov-2 genome.

Discussions

In resource-limited settings such as the Central African Republic (CAR), genomic surveillance remains challenging. In this retrospective study, the absence of local sequencing capacity required the storage and international shipment of samples, potentially introducing delays, RNA degradation, and variability in sequencing success. Among 102 selected samples, 68 genomes (66.7%) met quality control criteria (≥ 80% coverage) and were included in downstream analyses. These genomes represent 13.4% (68/506) of all confirmed cases, highlighting limited genomic coverage. The low proportion of sequenced samples and Ct-based selection may bias lineage distribution toward high viral load infections. Samples with higher viral loads are more likely to yield high-quality genomes, potentially leading to the overrepresentation of specific variants. In addition, constraints related to sample storage, transport, may have further influenced the composition of the dataset. Consequently, the observed lineage distribution should be interpreted with caution. Despite these constraints, the generated genomes contribute valuable genomic surveillance data from an underrepresented setting and provide insight into circulating SARS-CoV-2 lineages during the study period.

Omicron was the only variant detected among successfully sequenced samples, although limited sampling and sequencing success restrict broader generalisation. Four Omicron sub-lineages were identified: BA.1, BA.2, BA.4.1, and BA.5.1 (Fig. 2). The predominance of BA.5.1 and BA.2 is consistent with regional and global trends observed during 2022 [21], reflecting the rapid replacement dynamics characteristic of SARS-CoV-2 evolution. Omicron had even been identified in 87 countries just three weeks after its appearance, according to the work of Viana and colleagues [22]. Previous studies have highlighted the high transmissibility of Omicron and its sub-lineages, partly associated with mutations in the receptor-binding domain that increase affinity for the ACE2 receptor [23]. Temporal analysis showed a shift in sub-lineage distribution over the study period, with BA.1 detected early on, followed by an increase in BA.2, and subsequent predominance of BA.5.1as shown in Fig. 3. These dynamics are consistent with global observations of successive lineage replacement during the Omicron wave [24]. However, gaps in sequencing data for certain months, due to high Ct values, may have limited the continuity of surveillance and the ability to fully capture variant dynamics. These temporal variations are based on sampling dates rather than on time-calibrated phylogenetic inference. Temporal distribution of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sublineages

Comparison with sequences available in the GISAID database confirmed that Omicron emerged in CAR at the end of 2021 and became the dominant variant throughout 2022, replacing previously circulating variants such as Alpha, Beta, and Delta. This finding correlates with the study by Manirakiza et al. on the assessment of immunity against strains originating in Wuhan and the BA.2 variant among healthcare workers [25]. According to the GISAID database, the Delta B.1.620/640 variant originating in Lithuania [26] was predominant in 2021, followed by the Alpha variant. In 2022, SARS CoV-2 Omicron BA.1.1 and BQ.1.1 were the most prevalent, unlike those found in our study. Differences observed between studies likely reflect variations in sampling periods and the rapid evolution of SARS-CoV-2, which can lead to substantial shifts in variant distribution over short timeframes. SARS-CoV-2 evolves rapidly, and new mutations and variants can emerge over relatively short periods. These dynamics are consistent with findings reported by study [27], highlighting the continual genetic evolution of the virus over time.

The spatial distribution of cases in this study showed a concentration in urban areas, particularly in densely populated districts, notably in the 8th (Fig. 4), district with multiple infrastructures, including one of the largest markets in the city, which may facilitate transmission, as observed at the start of the outbreak at the market in Wuhan, China [28]. The demographic characteristics of the study population showed a predominance of male participants and a relatively young median age (40 years), consistent with the population structure in many African settings [29]. Women represented only a small proportion of the cohort studied (approximately 15%). The lower proportion of SARS-CoV-2 infections observed among women may reflect differences in exposure patterns, as men in the study population were more frequently engaged in occupations or activities associated with higher transmission risk. However, the limited representation of female participants restricts the generalisability of sex-based comparisons. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable genomic data from an underrepresented setting and contributes to the global understanding of SARS-CoV-2 diversity and spread. Moreover, the findings from this study are consistent with those from other countries that have reported a male predominance, suggesting that common contextual factors, such as occupational exposure, mobility patterns, differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, or unequal access to diagnostic testing may contribute to comparable gender distributions in different settings [24, 29]. Further research is needed to better understand the social and epidemiological determinants underlying this trend.

Strengthening local sequencing capacity will be essential to improve the timeliness, representativeness, and public health utility of genomic surveillance in CAR. Although the number of SARS-CoV-2 infections has decreased significantly in recent times, genomic surveillance remains extremely important. An additional outcome of this study is that it has contributed to increasing the number of SARS-CoV-2 genomes from the Central African Republic available on public databases. As a result, CAR now has more than 300 genomes submitted to GISAID, which may support future genomic surveillance and comparative studies.

Conclusion

This retrospective descriptive study reports genomic data on SARS-CoV-2 variants detected in Bangui, Central African Republic, during 2022. Omicron was the only variant detected among successfully sequenced samples, although limited sampling and sequencing success restrict broader generalisation. Although based on a limited number of samples collected from a single laboratory, these findings provide additional genomic information from a setting that remains underrepresented in global SARS-CoV-2 surveillance efforts. The observed distribution of Omicron sub-lineages is consistent with patterns reported in other regions during the same period.

This study underscores the relevance of continued genomic surveillance to monitor SARS-CoV-2 evolution, while also highlighting current constraints related to sequencing capacity. Strengthening local sequencing infrastructure could improve the representativeness and timeliness of genomic data generated in similar resource-limited settings.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small number of samples and the incomplete genome recovery for a subset of sequences may limit the representativeness of the observed variant distribution. Second, samples were collected from a single laboratory in Bangui, which may not reflect the diversity of SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating across the entire country. Third, the retrospective and descriptive design does not allow inference of transmission dynamics or associations with clinical outcomes. Finally, the lack of real-time sequencing capacity during the study period limited the timeliness of variant detection.
