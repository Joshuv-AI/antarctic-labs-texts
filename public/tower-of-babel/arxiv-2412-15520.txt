Logistic Regression Model for Differentially-Private Matrix Masking Data
Linh H. Nghiem, Aidong Adam Ding, Samuel S. Wu

Abstract
A recently proposed scheme utilizing local noise addition and matrix masking enables data collection while protecting individual privacy from all parties, including the central data manager. Statistical analysis of such privacy-preserved data is particularly challenging for nonlinear models like logistic regression. By leveraging a relationship between logistic regression and linear regression estimators, we propose the first valid statistical analysis method for logistic regression under this setting. Theoretical analysis of the proposed estimator confirmed its validity under an asymptotic framework with large noise variances to account for strict privacy requirements. Simulations and real data analyses demonstrate the superiority of the proposed estimators over naive logistic regression methods on privacy-preserved datasets.



Keywords:
privacy, measurement error, generalized linear model, mixture model

1
Introduction

In the age of data-driven innovation, privacy-protected data collection methods have become essential for balancing the interests of organizations and individuals. These methods ensure that personal and sensitive information is collected, stored, and processed in ways that protect user privacy and comply with regulatory frameworks like GDPR, HIPAA, and CCPA. Protecting privacy is not only necessary for legal compliance but also critical to building and maintaining consumer trust. To ensure that data analysis yields valid statistical inferences from privacy-protected data, new statistical methods are required. Here, we propose a statistical inference procedure for logistic regression on data collected using matrix masking combined with noise addition.

Over the years, many privacy-protected data collection methods have been developed. Common approaches include anonymization (removing personal identifiers) and pseudonymization (replacing sensitive information with fictitious identifiers). While widely used, these methods do not offer formal mathematical privacy guarantees. Differential privacy
(
Dwork,, 2006
)
addresses this gap by adding statistical noise to datasets, providing a theoretical, quantitative guarantee of privacy. It has since become one of the most popular privacy-preserving methods, with noise addition to summary statistics enabling privacy-preserving logistic regression studies, such as that of
Zhang et al., (2012)
. However, noise addition at the summary level requires a trustworthy central data manager, which does not protect user privacy from data collectors themselves. To achieve user privacy at the individual level, local differential privacy adds noise to data before it is sent to the data collector.
Local differential privacy schemes have been successfully implemented by companies like Apple
(
Thakurta et al.,, 2017
)
and Google
(
Erlingsson et al.,, 2014
)
to protect individual-level data.

Another privacy protection scheme, matrix masking uses a random orthogonal matrix to scramble raw data and prevent identification of individual records
(
Ting et al.,, 2008
)
.

Ding et al., (2020)
proved that a triple matrix-masking (TM
2
) data collection procedure
(
Wu et al.,, 2017
)
, using multi-party computing concepts, can collect a matrix masked data set while preserving individual-level privacy.
This method, combined with local differential privacy’s noise addition, achieves the same quantitative privacy guarantees with less noise than local differential privacy only scheme
(
Ding et al.,, 2023
)
. The combined scheme is referred to here as the TM²+Noise scheme.

The TM
2
+Noise data presents unique challenges for logistic regression. Although simple noise addition to the covariates results in data that follows a measurement error model, which has been studied extensively, the existing statistical methods for logistic regression models with covariate measurement errors, such as those in
(
Buonaccorsi,, 2010
, Chapter 6)
, are not directly applicable to this setting for the following reason. First, under this TM
2
+Noise scheme, the observed response variable is no longer binary, but a real-valued continuous variable. Secondly, the matrix masking only preserves the first and second sample moments of the data, the presence of a non-linear link function in the logistic regression model (and a non-linear model in general) typically requires other information to conduct statistical inference for the model parameters. This other information, however, is not preserved under matrix masking. Hence, incorporating matrix masking with noise addition requires new statistical methods for non-linear models like logistic regression.

In this paper, we propose the first valid statistical estimator and inference for the logistic regression model under the TM
2
+Noise setting. Building upon the seminal work of
Haggstrom, (1983)
who relate the logistic regression to the linear regression through a Gaussian mixture model, we generalize it to a conditional Gaussian mixture model that allows confounding covariates, and obtain the corresponding form of the logistic regression model. Then, we establish both a point and an interval estimator of this model using only the first and second sample moments of the raw data, whose expectations are preserved under the TM
2
+Noise scheme. We study the properties of this estimator under an asymptotic framework that does
not
assume the variance of added noise to be fixed, demonstrating the tradeoff between statistical accuracy and privacy. Simulation studies and a data application on the relationship between hypertension prevalence and various covariates in a medical database, illustrate the desirable performance of the proposed statistical approach. Our paper also contributes to a growing statistics literature on making inferences from summary statistics
(
Bhowmik et al.,, 2015
;
Whitaker et al.,, 2021
)
.

2
Notations and model setup of logistic regression data collected from TM
2
+Noise scheme

Data is collected from
n
individuals, where for each individual
i=1,...,n
, the data includes a binary outcome variable
y_{i}^{*}\in\{0,1\}
and and a set of
p
covariates represented by a
p
-dimensional row vector
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
.

The logistic regression model defines the relationship between the binary response variable and the covariates as follows:

P(y^{*}_{i}=1\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*})=\dfrac{\exp(\beta_{0}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}_{1})}{1+\exp(\beta_{0}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}_{1})}=\dfrac{1}{1+\exp\left\{-\left(\bm{\beta}_{0}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}_{1}\right)\right\}},

(1)

where
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is the intercept and
\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
is the coefficient vector. The primary goal is to make statistical inferences on the parameters
\bm{\beta}_{1}
based on observed data.

If collected directly, the raw data from the
n
individuals can be represented as the response vector
\mathbf{y}^{*}=(y_{1}^{*},\ldots,y_{n}^{*})^{\top}
and the
n\times p
covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}^{*}
with
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i}
as its
i
-th row. However, for privacy protection, the data is collected using the TM
2
+Noise scheme, so the analyst only has access to

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}^{*}+\mathbf{U},~\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}^{*}+\mathbf{v},

(2)

where
\mathbf{M}
is an unknown
n\times n
orthogonal matrix that preserves row sums, i.e
\mathbf{M}^{\textsc{T}}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
, and
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{v}
are noise terms with dimensions
n\times p
and
n\times 1
, respectively.
The elements of these noise terms
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{v}
are drawn from the normal distribution
N(0,\sigma_{x}^{2})
and
N(0,\sigma_{y}^{2})
respectively, with
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
known to the analyst.
Due to the masking matrix
\mathbf{M}
and added noise, all elements in the masked data
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{y}
are real-valued. The focus is on estimating and making inferences for
\beta_{0}
and
\bm{\beta}_{1}
based on the masked data
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{y}
.

If the raw data
\mathbf{X}^{*}
and
\mathbf{y}^{*}
were directly available, the
conditional maximum likelihood estimator
(cMLE) would typically be used to estimate
\beta_{0}
and
\bm{\beta}_{1}
.
This involves minimizing the conditional negative log-likelihood function
\ell^{*}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1}\mid\mathbf{y}^{*},\mathbf{X}^{*})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log P(y_{i}^{*}=1\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*})
. Equivalently, this estimator solves the score equation

\mathbf{S}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1};\mathbf{y}^{*},\mathbf{X}^{*})=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{*}-\dfrac{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{*}}{1+\exp\left\{-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}\right\}}\right)=\bm{0}.

(3)

Here and in the following, for any matrix
\mathbf{B}
, we let
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}=(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{B})
denote the matrix formed by stacking a column of one to the left of
\mathbf{B}
, and
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1}^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times 1}
denote the combined coefficients vector.

While this cMLE approach works well for raw data, adapting it to masked data is challenging due to the non-linearity in terms of
\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}
within this score equation.
To address this, we first review an alternative estimation method based on the unconditional maximum likelihood for the logistic model (
1
) and then adapt this approach to the masked data setting.

3
Methodology

3.1
Logistic model from mixture normal

A key ingredient of our proposed methodology is to relate the logistic regression model to a normal mixture model, as done in
Haggstrom, (1983)
. Particularly, for the raw observation
(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*},y^{*}_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
with
y^{*}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
Haggstrom, (1983)
models
\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\mid(y^{*}_{i}=j)\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{j},\bm{\Sigma})
and hence the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}
is a normal mixture

\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\sim p_{1}N(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1},\bm{\Sigma})+p_{0}N(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}),

(4)

and
p_{j}=P(y^{*}_{i}=j)
for
j=0,1
, with
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
being mean row vectors,
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
being a positive definite variance matrix, and
p_{0}+p_{1}=1
. To see the connection between the model (
4
) and the logistic model (
1
), the model (
4
) implied

\displaystyle P(y^{*}_{i}=1|\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})=

\displaystyle\frac{P(y^{*}_{i}=1,\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})}{P(y^{*}_{i}=1,\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})+P(y^{*}_{i}=0,\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})}

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1})\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1})^{T}\}}{p_{1}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1})\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1})^{T}\}+p_{0}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})^{T}\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\frac{p_{0}}{p_{1}}\exp\{\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}}{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})^{T}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\ \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1})^{T}\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\exp\{[\log(\frac{p_{0}}{p_{1}})+\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}}{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})^{T}]+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1})^{T}]\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\exp\{-(\beta_{0}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}_{1})\}},

where
\bm{\beta}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})^{T}
and
\beta_{0}=\log(p_{1}/p_{0})-(1/2)(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})\bm{\beta}_{1}
. It is straightforward to see that the last expression has the same form as the logistic model (
1
).

Since the (conditional) logistic model can be viewed as an implication of a normal mixture model, we can estimate the coefficients vector
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1}^{T})^{T}
of the logistic model via estimating parameters of the normal mixture model (
4
). Particularly, the maximum likelihood estimators for
p_{1}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
are respectively

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*},\qquad\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{0}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-y_{i}^{*})\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-y_{i}^{*})},\qquad\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{1}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*}},

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[y_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{1})^{T}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{1})+(1-y_{i}^{*})(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{0})^{T}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{0})].

Hence the maximum likelihood estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{1}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{0})^{\textsc{T}}

(7)

with corresponding quantities from (
6
).

Haggstrom, (1983)
related the estimator in (
7
) to a
linear regression
of
\mathbf{y}^{*}
on
\mathbf{X}^{*}
:

y_{i}^{*}=b_{0}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{b}_{1},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. A special property, as shown by
Haggstrom, (1983)
, is that that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
in (
7
) is
exactly
the same as the maximum likelihood estimator of the mixture model (
4
) for the parameter
\bm{b}_{1}/\tau^{2}
where

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{1}=[\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*})]^{-1}\textrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*},y^{*}_{i}),\ \ \tau^{2}=\textrm{Var}(y_{i}^{*}-b_{0}-\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{b}_{1})\ \ \mbox{and }b_{0}=E(y^{*}_{i})-E(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*})\bm{b}_{1}.

(8)

Here and in the following, for any random row vectors
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
\mathbf{V}_{2}
, we have

\textrm{Cov}(\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2})=E[\left\{\mathbf{V}_{1}-E(\mathbf{V}_{1})\right\}^{\textsc{T}}\left\{\mathbf{V}_{2}-E(\mathbf{V}_{2})\right\}]
, and
\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{V}_{1})=\textrm{Cov}(\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{1}).

The quantities
\bm{b}=(b_{0},{\bm{b}_{1}^{T}})^{T}
and
\tau
in (
8
) can be estimated by the ordinary least squaress (OLS) estimators:

\hat{\bm{b}}=(\hat{b}_{0},\hat{\bm{b}}_{1}^{T})^{T}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*},\qquad\hat{\tau}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{n}\left\{\mathbf{y}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*}\right\}.

(9)

Hence, the estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
defined in (
7
) can be written as
\hat{\beta}_{1}=\hat{\bm{b}}_{1}^{T}/\hat{\tau}^{2}
, which is the sub-vector consisting of the last
p
components of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\bm{b}}/\hat{\tau}^{2}
. Notice that the OLS estimators in (
9
) is from linear regression which can be adapted to matrix masked data easily.
This key property will be used in section 3.3 to develop a consistent estimator and valid confidence interval for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
when the data are masked. Before doing it, we generalize the mixture model (
4
) to allow a more flexible distribution of the raw covariate
\mathbf{X}^{*}
.

3.2
Conditional mixture model

The mixture model (
4
) is restrictive because it assumes the covariate
\mathbf{X}^{*}
corresponding to each class of
y
is multivariate Gaussian, and that the mixture weight is constant. In this section, we extend it to a conditional mixture model

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i}\mid(y_{i}^{*}=j,\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{j}+\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}\mathbf{C},\bm{\Sigma}),

(10)

\displaystyle p_{1}(\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})=P(y_{i}^{*}=1\mid\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})=\dfrac{\exp(\gamma_{0}+\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}\bm{\gamma}_{1})}{1+\exp(\gamma_{0}+\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}\bm{\gamma}_{1})},

(11)

for
j=0,1
and
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}
is a
1\times q
vector and
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
,
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{\gamma}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times 1}
are the matrix of coefficients.

In other words, Model (
10
)-(
11
) extends model (
4
) by assuming the conditional distribution, rather than the unconditional distribution, of
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i}
given
\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}
within each class of
y_{i}
is multivariate Gaussian. Furthermore, the probability belonging to each class can potentially vary as a function of
\mathbf{Z}^{*}
, rather than being a constant. It is straightforward to see that the mixture model (
4
) is a special case of model (
10
)-(
11
) with
\mathbf{Z}^{*}=1
always. For this more flexible model, only part of covariates,
\mathbf{X}^{*}
, follow the mixture model. The covariates
\mathbf{Z}
can be considered as a confounding variable, so accounting for it is essential in estimating the effects of the covariates
\mathbf{X}
on the outcome
\mathbf{y}
.

By a similar argument to the equation (
5
), the conditional mixture model (
10
)-(
11
) implies a logistic model of
y_{i}^{*}
on
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}
, i.e

P(y_{i}^{*}=1\mid\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})=\dfrac{1}{1+\exp(-\beta_{0}-\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i}\bm{\beta}_{1}-\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i}\bm{\beta}_{2})},

(12)

where

\beta_{0}=\gamma_{0}-(1/2)(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}^{\textsc{T}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0}^{\textsc{T}}),\ \ \bm{\beta}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})^{\textsc{T}}\mbox{ and }\bm{\beta}_{2}=\bm{\gamma}_{1}-\mathbf{C}\bm{\beta}_{1}.

(13)

The detailed derivations are provided in Appendix
A.2
.
With this model, we are interested in the estimation and coefficient for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
.

Furthermore, if the raw data
(y_{i}^{*},\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})
are i.i.d from the model (
10
)-(
11
), for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the coefficient
\bm{\beta}_{1}
can also be estimated using ordinary least squares from a linear regression of
y^{*}
on the set of
p+q
covariates
\mathbf{W}^{*}=(\mathbf{X}^{*},\mathbf{Z}^{*})
. Indeed, let
\bar{\bm{b}}=\left[\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{W}_{1}^{*})\right]^{-1}\textrm{Cov}(\mathbf{W}_{1}^{*},y_{1}^{*})=(\bar{\bm{b}}_{1},\bar{\bm{b}}_{2})
, where
\bar{\bm{b}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
and
\bar{\bm{b}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times q}
are the coefficients associated with
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Z}
, respectively, and
\varepsilon_{1}=y^{*}_{1}-\mathbf{W}_{1}^{*}\bar{\bm{b}}
. The following lemma establishes the relationship between
\bar{\bm{b}}_{1}
and
\bm{\beta}_{1}
, whose proof is provided in Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 1

Under the conditional mixture model (
10
)-(
11
), we have
\bm{\beta}_{1}=\left[\textrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})\right]^{-1}\bar{\bm{b}}_{1}
.

Note that in Lemma
1
, there is no relationship between
\bm{\beta}_{2}
and
\bar{\bm{b}}_{2}
; generally, estimation of
\bm{\beta}_{2}
is not possible via ordinary least squares unless further assumption on the distribution of
\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mid y^{*}
is imposed. Nevertheless, Lemma
1
implies that for estimating
\bm{\beta}_{1}
, we can still use the OLS estimators for the linear regression

y_{i}^{*}=b_{0}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bar{\bm{b}}_{1}+\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{*}\bar{\bm{b}}_{2}.

The OLS estimators for the regression coefficients
\bm{b}=(b_{0},\bar{\bm{b}}_{1}^{T},,\bar{\bm{b}}_{2}^{T})^{T}
and error variance
\tau^{2}=\textrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{1})
are

\hat{\bm{b}}=(\hat{b}_{0},\hat{\bm{b}}_{1}^{\textsc{T}},\hat{\bm{b}}_{2}^{\textsc{T}})^{\textsc{T}}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*},\ \ \hat{\tau}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{n}\left\{\mathbf{y}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*}\right\}.

(14)

Then an estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is obtained as
\hat{\bm{b}}_{1}^{T}/\hat{\tau}^{2}
, a sub-vector consisting of
p
-components, from the second to the
(p+1)
-th component, of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\bm{b}}/\tau^{2}
.

To facilitate the derivation of an estimator from the masked data in the next subsection, we justify this estimator through an estimating equation perspective. To this end, let
b_{0}=E(y_{1}^{*})-E(\mathbf{W}_{1}^{*})\bar{\bm{b}}
,
\bm{\theta}=(b_{0},\bar{\bm{b}})^{\textsc{T}}/\tau^{2}
and
\phi=1/\tau^{2}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{*}=(1,\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Consider the two following estimating equations

m_{\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i,\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)=\bm{0}
and
m_{\phi}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i,\bm{\phi}}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)=0,

where

m_{i,\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)=\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{*}y_{i}^{*}-\dfrac{1}{\phi}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{*}\bm{\theta},\quad m_{i,\bm{\phi}}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)=\dfrac{1}{2\phi}-\dfrac{1}{2}y_{i}^{*2}+\dfrac{1}{2\phi^{2}}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{*}\bm{\theta}

(15)

We will derive in the next subsection that, at the true parameter
\bm{\theta}
and
\phi
, the two estimating equations
m_{i,\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)
and
m_{i,\phi}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\phi)
are unbiased. These unbiased estimating equations imply that under regularity conditions, the
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
(and hence
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})
is asymptotically unbiased and normally distributed for
{\bm{\theta}}
(and for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
). This property will be used to derive a valid confidence interval for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
, which we will make precise in the next subsection.

3.3
Estimation and inference from masked data

We first consider the effect of matrix masking but
without
noise addition, i.e
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}^{*}
,
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}^{*}
where
\mathbf{M}
is an
n\times n
orthogonal matrix that preserves row sums, satisfying
\mathbf{M}^{\textsc{T}}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
. In this case, it is straightforward to see that the first and second sample moments of the raw data are preserved, i.e
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}=\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*}
, and
\mathbf{y}^{\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}^{*\textsc{T}}\mathbf{y}^{*}
. Consequently, the estimators
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
and
\hat{\tau}^{2}
defined in (
9
) are invariant to
\mathbf{M}
, hence so is the coefficient
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
.

Thus, any estimator, such as the OLS estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
, that relies on these invariant quantities will yield the same results on data with or without matrix masking. This aligns with the original intention of the matrix masking scheme’s designers, in that linear regression analysis using OLS estimators requires no adjustment on the matrix-masked dataset.

However, it is important to note that the regular conditional maximum likelihood estimator, obtained by solving (
3
), does not possess this invariance property, making it challenging to adapt for masked data. Here, we focus on OLS-based estimators adapted for logistic regression to leverage this invariance.

We now propose a corrected OLS-based estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
for TM
2
+Noise data, utilizing estimating equations that depend solely on the invariant quantities described above. The theoretical analysis of this estimator is significantly simplified by its invariance property, as the estimator yields the same result as if there were no masking by the matrix
\mathbf{M}
. This allows us to perform the analysis of the estimator within an equivalent data model under the assumption of no matrix masking, i.e., setting
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
in (
2
):

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}^{*}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}\mathbf{U},\qquad\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}^{*}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{y}\mathbf{v},

(16)

where
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{v}
are
n\times p
and
n\times 1
matrices with each entry generated from
N(0,1)
distribution, with known
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{y}^{2}
. In this case, we propose an estimator
(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(c)},\hat{\phi}^{(c)})
for
(\bm{\theta},\phi)
to be the solution of the corrected estimating equations

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{m}_{i,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta},\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{\textsc{T}}y_{i}-\dfrac{1}{\phi}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{\textsc{T}}-\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q})\bm{\theta}\right\}=\bm{0},

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i,\phi}(\bm{\theta},\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\dfrac{1}{2\phi}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(y_{i}^{2}-\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{y}^{2}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2\phi^{2}}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i}^{\textsc{T}}-\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q}\right)\bm{\theta}\right\}=0,

where
\mathbf{J}_{p+q}=\text{diag}(\bm{0},\mathbf{I}_{p+q})
.
The explicit formulas for the resulting estimator are

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n}{\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}-n\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{y}^{2}-\bm{y}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-n\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\bm{y}},

(18)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}^{(c)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-n\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\bm{y},

and the final estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{(c)}
for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
is the subvector of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(c)}
consisting of
p
elements, from the second to the
(p+1)
-th element. With this estimating equation approach, we can leverage the theory of estimating equations, see for example
Carroll et al., (1998)
, to establish the asymptotic properties of the proposed corrected estimator.
Since the variances of the added noises,
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{y}^{2}
, determine the level of privacy protection, they can become large under strict privacy requirements. Consequently, we derive the theoretical results under an asymptotic framework where both the sample size,
n
, and the noise variance,
\sigma^{2}=\max(\sigma_{x}^{2},\sigma_{y}^{2})
, diverge to infinity.

For these derivations, we assume that the raw data
(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i},y^{*}_{i})
are independently and identically distributed according to the mixture model specified in (
10
)-(
11
), subject to the following technical conditions:

Condition 1

The marginal expectation and variance-covariance matrix
E(\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})
and
\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})
are finite.

Condition 2

The parameter space for
\bm{\Xi}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1},\mathbf{C},\bm{\Sigma},\gamma_{0},\bm{\gamma}_{1}\}
is compact and the marginal variance-covariance matrix of
\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{W}^{*}_{i})
is not degenerate .

These two mild conditions imply that the marginal covariance matrix of
\mathbf{W}_{i}^{*}=(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{i})
are finite, and condition
2
also implies the parameter space for
(\bm{\theta},\phi)
is compact. To establish the asymptotic properties of the corrected estimator
\bm{\beta}_{1}^{(c)}
, we first show that the corresponding equations are unbiased in the following proposition whose proof is in the Supplementary Materials S1.

Proposition 3.1

At the true parameter
\bm{\theta}
and
\phi
,
E\left\{\bm{m}_{i,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})\right\}=E\left\{m_{i,\phi}(\bm{\theta},\phi)\right\}=\bm{0}
.

We note that when
\sigma=0
, the estimating equations
\bm{m}_{i,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
and
m_{i,\phi}(\bm{\theta},\phi)
become
\bm{m}_{i}^{*}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
and
m^{*}_{i,\phi}(\bm{\theta},\phi)
defined in (
15
), respectively, so Proposition
3.1
also establishes the unbiasedness of these estimating equations when the raw data are available.

Theorem 3.1

Under conditions
1
-
2
, if
\sigma^{4}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
where
\sigma=\max\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}\}
, then the estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{(c)}
is a consistent estimator for
\bm{\beta}_{1}
, and

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{(c)}-\bm{\beta}_{1})\sim N\left(\bm{0},\mathbf{V}\right),

where
\mathbf{V}
is the middle
p\times p
sub-matrix of the matrix
\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{-1}
, with
\mathbf{A}=E_{\bm{\theta},\phi}\left[\nabla\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{\theta},\phi)\right]
, and
\mathbf{B}=E_{\bm{\theta},\phi}\left[\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{\theta},\phi)\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{\theta},\phi)^{\textsc{T}}\right]
.

Proof of the theorem is provided in the Supplementary Materials S1.

Based on Theorem
3.1
, we can compute the standard error of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
from estimating
\mathbf{V}
. Specifically, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
in the formula for
\mathbf{V}
can be estimated by

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{11}&\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{12}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{21}&\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{22}\end{bmatrix},

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{11}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial\bm{m}_{i,\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\bigg|_{(\bm{\theta},\phi)=(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\phi})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n\hat{\phi}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-n\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{12}=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{21}^{\top}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial\bm{m}_{i,\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\phi}\bigg|_{(\bm{\theta},\phi)=(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\phi})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n\hat{\phi}^{2}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-n\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q})\hat{\bm{\theta}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{22}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial\bm{m}_{i,\phi}}{\partial\phi}\bigg|_{(\bm{\theta},\phi)=(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\phi})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\hat{\phi}^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{n\hat{\phi}^{3}}\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\textsc{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-n\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{J}_{p+q})\hat{\bm{\theta}},

and

\widehat{\mathbf{B}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{m}_{i}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\phi})\bm{m}_{i}^{\textsc{T}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\phi}).

Then, a
100(1-\alpha)\%
asymptotic confidence interval for each component
\beta_{1j}
of
\bm{\beta}_{1}
is

\hat{\beta}^{(c)}_{1j}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{v_{j+1,j+1}}.

where
z_{\alpha/2}
denotes the
1-\alpha
quantile of the standard normal distribution, for
j=1,\ldots,p
.

4
Simulation

We conducted simulation studies to demonstrate the performance of the proposed estimation procedure with varying variances
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{y}^{2}
for random noises added to the covariates and responses, respectively.
We set
p=3
and generated the raw data
(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*},y_{i}^{*})
from the mixture normal model (
4
) and
(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{*},y_{i}^{*})
from the conditional mixture model (
10
)-(
11
) for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For the mixture model (
4
), we vary the probability
p_{1}\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
, set the true vector
\bm{\mu}_{1}=(1,1,1)
, and the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
to have an AR(1) structure with autocorrelation
0.5
. We set
\bm{\beta}_{1}=(1,-1,0)^{\top}
and computed
\bm{\mu}_{0}=\bm{\mu}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}
. For the conditional mixture model (
10
)-(
11
), we set
q=2
and generated the true
\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{*}
from a bivariate uniform distribution on
[-1,1]^{q}
, and set the matrix
\mathbf{C}
to be a random
q\times p
matrix with each element from a
\text{uniform}(1,2)
distribution. We set
\gamma_{0}=0
and
\gamma_{1}=(1.5,1)^{\top}
.

After generating the raw data, the masked covariates
\mathbf{X}
or
\mathbf{W}=[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}]
and the masked response
\mathbf{y}
were generated by adding random noises to the raw data matrices and masking with a random orthogonal matrix. That is, we generated
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{y}
according to model (
2
):
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{W}^{*}+\sigma_{x}\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}^{*}+\sigma_{y}\mathbf{v}
, where
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{v}
are
n\times p
matrix and
n\times 1
vector of independent standard normal random variables.
We varied the sample size
n\in\{10^{4},10^{5},10^{6}\}
, and selected the values for
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{y}^{2}
to satisfy three
(\varepsilon,0.01)
levels of differential privacy (DP) with
\varepsilon\in\{5,1,0.5\}
. We provided a brief review of DP and the DP guarantees of the matrix masking and noise addition mechanism in Appendix
A.1
. Since the outcome
y^{*}
has a different range from the covariates
\mathbf{X}^{*}
or
\mathbf{W}^{*}
, different values of
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
were needed to achieve the same
(\varepsilon,0.01)
-DP at the same sample size. The
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
corresponding to the
(\varepsilon,0.01)
-DP were reported in the second and the third columns of Tables
1
and
2
.

On each simulated dataset, we computed several point and interval estimators. First, the naive conditional MLE estimator is defined to be the solution of the estimating equation
\mathbf{S}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1};\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{0}
, obtained by naively substituting the masked data (
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
,
\mathbf{y}
) in place of the raw data (
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
,
\mathbf{y}^{*}
) in equation (
3
). The corresponding confidence intervals for this naive conditional MLE were computed using the asymptotic Wald method.
Next, we computed the naive least squaress estimator (LS) by similarly substitution of masked data in (
9
). Finally, we computed our corrected least squaress (cLS) estimator in (
18
), which accounts for the effects of noise addition and masking.

We evaluated the performance of the point estimators by examining their bias and median squared errors (MSE) and evaluate the confidence intervals based on their coverage probability across
1000
datasets per simulation settings.
When
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y}=0
, corresponding to
\varepsilon=\infty
, the raw data and masked data are identical, making the naive and corrected least squaress estimators equivalent. This setup provides a baseline for comparing the performance of the conditional MLE and the LS estimator on raw data. When noise is added (
\varepsilon<\infty
) for privacy protection, data utility decreases, resulting in a degradation of estimator performance from this baseline. We reported the results for the unconditional mixture model normal distribution (
4
) with
p_{1}=0.5
in Table
1
and the conditional mixture model (
10
)-(
11
) in Table
2
. The results for
p_{1}\in\{0.1,0.9\}
show similar conclusions and are presented in the Supplementary Materials S2.

5
Real data analysis

We illustrate our estimation methods to study the relationship between an individual’s health outcome and three personal characteristics. The raw data was obtained from the
All of US
Registered Tier Dataset v7 database (version R2022Q4R9, released on 04/20/2023). This database contains numerous private information of individuals and cannot be released directly to the public; our access to this database requires a strict license. Hence, the focus of this data analysis is to compare the results of a logistic regression obtained on the raw data versus those obtained on the masked data that may potentially be released to the public.

For this analysis, we were interested in modeling the probability of hypertension as a function of three key factors, including gender, race, and age
(
Chandler et al.,, 2021
)
. The raw dataset includes
169,772
individuals with complete observations for all four variables. Among these individuals,
60,706
had been diagnosed with hypertension, corresponding to a crude prevalence rate of
35.8\%
. There is an imbalance in gender distribution, with
106,897
females, representing
63.0\%
of the population. Additionally,
32,914
individuals (
19.4\%
) identify themselves as Black or African American. The mean age in the dataset is
51.9
years, with a standard deviation of
16.6
years.

For the logistic regression, the response variable
y
represents an individual’s hypertension status (Yes/No). Gender and race are binary covariates, where we set gender = 1 to indicate female and race = 1 to indicate Black or African American. To ensure comparability between the continuous and binary covariates, the age variable was scaled to a range between
0
and
1
. Given that the actual ages in the dataset range from 18 to 88 years, we applied the transformation
x_{age}=(age-18)/70
and used it as a covariate in the logistic model.

We first performed logistic regression analysis on the raw dataset
(
\varepsilon=\infty
, i.e.,
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y}=0
)
, and to establish a baseline for scenarios without privacy protection, we compared the least squares (LS) estimates to standard maximum likelihood estimates (MLE) on this raw data.
Next, we generated pseudo-datasets by adding noise at three levels after applying random matrix masking following (
2
). Since we scaled the age variable resulting in the same range of [0,1] for all the covariates and response for raw data set, we set
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y}=\sigma
, and chose the value of

to achieve
(\varepsilon,0.01)
-DP as described in Section

\varepsilon\in\{5,1,0.5\}
, the corresponding value for
\sigma
is
\{0.59,1.33,1.87\}

Logistic regression was then performed on these pseudo-datasets using our corrected least squaress (cLS) estimator. Comparing the baseline results from the raw data to the cLS results on pseudo-datasets demonstrates the potential impact of privacy-protection mechanisms on analysis outcomes. It is important to note that standard MLE estimates for logistic regression cannot be applied to the pseudo-datasets because the binary response variable
y
becomes real-valued due to the noise addition and matrix masking procedures.

6
Conclusion

As privacy concerns escalate in the era of big data, privacy-preserving statistical analysis has emerged as a critical area of research. While most existing methods focus on differential privacy schemes that perturb sufficient statistics, these approaches generally require a trusted central data manager. In contrast, local noise addition combined with matrix masking facilitates privacy-preserving data collection without relying on a central authority. This paper presents the first valid statistical analysis procedure for logistic regression — a nonlinear statistical model — applied to data collected via noise addition and matrix masking. The method exploits an extended relationship with linear regression-based estimators to ensure valid analysis. Our proposed estimator is proven to be valid in large samples, even under stringent privacy requirements (i.e., as noise magnitude
\sigma\to\infty
). Simulation studies and real data analyses confirm the validity of the proposed procedure and demonstrate its substantial improvement over naive logistic regression methods, which fail under these privacy-preserving settings.

An intermediate future work would be to extend the framework to multinomial regression model where the raw response is categorical with
J>2
categories, i.e
y_{i}^{*}\in\{1,2,...,J\}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. Since
\sum_{j=1}^{J}P(y_{i}^{*}=j\mid\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})=1
, a common approach is to select a baseline category, say
J
without loss of generality, and then model

P(y^{*}_{i}=j\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*})=\dfrac{\exp(\beta_{0,j}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}_{1,j})}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{J}\exp(\beta_{0,k}+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}\bm{\beta}_{1,k})},

(19)

for
j=1,...,J-1
and
P(y_{i}^{*}=J\mid\mathbf{X}^{*}_{i})=1-\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}P(y^{*}_{i}=j\mid\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*})
.
Haggstrom, (1983)
demonstrated that logistic regression (
19
) can also be derived from a mixture of
J
Gaussian distributions over the covariates, thereby linking the regression coefficients to least squares estimators. Therefore, our approach can be extended to multinomial logistic regression under matrix masking and noise addition. However, instead of a single
J
-class response variable
y^{*}
, the raw data must include
J-1
binary indicator variables for classes
k=1,\dots,J-1
. This requirement arises because, after applying matrix masking and adding noise, the class information contained in the resulting single variable
y
cannot be separately utilized in the
J-1
logistic regressions of (
19
). The resulting formulas is more complex, as each logistic regression in (
19
) involves comparisons among
J-1
classes. A detailed treatment of these derivations is left for future work.

A.1
Differential privacy guarantee for the TM
2
+Noise scheme

In this section, we briefly review the concept of differential privacy and how the TM
2
+Noise scheme guarantees different levels of DP. More details can be found in
Ding et al., (2020)
and
Ding et al., (2023)
.

Broadly speaking, differential privacy (DP) quantifies privacy protection by measuring the closeness of the distributions of two pseudo-datasets generated from raw datasets that differ in only one individual
(
DP_0
)
. Two raw datasets,
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}\prime}
, are called
neighbors
if they differ in at most one row (i.e., one individual’s data). Formally, a perturbation mechanism
\mathbf{D}^{\textrm{ps}}(\cdot)
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any measurable set
\mathcal{S}
and any pair of neighbors
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}\prime}
,

e^{-\varepsilon}\leq\frac{P[\mathbf{D}^{\textrm{ps}}(\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}})\in\mathcal{S}]}{P[\mathbf{D}^{\textrm{ps}}(\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}\prime})\in\mathcal{S}]}\leq e^{\varepsilon}.

(20)

Most of the differential privacy literature considers privacy guarantees only for a statistic
T(\mathbf{D}^{\textrm{ps}})
derived from the pseudo-dataset, such as summary statistics, which protects the released statistic but not against data collectors who have access to the full pseudo-data. To protect against the latter, DP must be established at the level of the entire pseudo-dataset
\mathbf{D}^{\textrm{ps}}
, as in Definition (
20
).

Ding et al., (2023)
analyzed such full-dataset DP guarantees under the neighboring definition where datasets differ in exactly one row by at most
1
in
\ell_{2}
-norm. Within that framework, for TM
2
+Noise scheme with independent
N(0,\sigma^{2})
noise for all entries, ensuring
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP requires

\sigma\geq\text{the upper root of }g(\sigma)=\frac{(2\sqrt{d}+1)}{2(n-d)}\gamma_{\delta;2(n-d),d/\sigma^{2}}+\sqrt{d}-\sigma^{2}\varepsilon,

(21)

where
d
is the number of columns in
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}}
and
\gamma_{\delta;df,a}
is the upper
\delta
-quantile of a non-central
\chi^{2}
distribution with
df
degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameter
a
. For a better interpretation, the root of (
21
) implies a more relaxed bound:

\sigma^{2}\geq\frac{2n-d+\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{2(n-d)}\frac{9\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}.

When the sample size
n>\log(1/\delta)
, this requirement scales as
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
for strict privacy (
\varepsilon<1
). In contrast, a pure noise-addition scheme (without matrix masking) requires

\sigma^{2}\geq\frac{1.7\log(\delta^{-1})}{\varepsilon^{2}},

which scales as
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
.

We used (
21
) to compute the
\sigma
values to achieve
(\varepsilon,0.01)
-DP in both the real-data analysis and simulated settings.
Notice that (
21
) is derived for a dataset
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}}
containing both the
n\times 1
raw response vector
\mathbf{y}^{*}
and the
n\times p
covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}^{*}
, i.e.
d=p+1
. Furthermore, (
21
) was derived under the assumption that all entries of
\mathbf{D}^{\text{raw}}
lie in
[-1,1]
, which needs to be converted to
\sigma_{y}
and
\sigma_{x}
according to the observed ranges of
\mathbf{y}^{*}
and
\mathbf{X}^{*}
in the data, respectively. Particularly, for the simulation studies in Section
4
, the raw response variable
y^{*}
and covariates
\mathbf{X}^{*}
have different ranges, resulting in
\sigma_{y}\neq\sigma_{x}
in Table
1
and Table
2
.
For the real data analysis in Section
5
, all the variables were scaled to the range
[0,1]
, thus
\sigma_{y}=\sigma_{x}
is half of the
\sigma
value from the bound (
21
).

\bm{\Sigma}_{zy}

Hence, we can write

\bm{\Sigma}_{zx}&\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}\\

\bm{\Sigma}_{zy}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{b}}_{1}\\
\bar{\bm{b}}_{2}\\

Using the Schur’s matrix inversion formula, we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{xx}&\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{zx}&\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(\bm{\Sigma}_{xx}-\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx})^{-1}&-\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{xx}-\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\\

\displaystyle\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{1}=(\bm{\Sigma}_{xx}-\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx})^{-1}(\bm{\Sigma}_{xy}-\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zy}),

(23)

and

\begin{array}[]{cl}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{2}&=-\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{xx}-\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{xy}+\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zy}\\
&\qquad+\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx}(\bm{\Sigma}_{xx}-\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zx})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}\bm{\Sigma}_{zz}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{zy}\\

(24)

Now we will express
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{1}
in terms of parameters of the mixture model (

)-(

), through finding the expressions for the two factors in (

):

. First, we have

\begin{array}[]{cl}&\ \ \ \bm{\Sigma}_{xx}=\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{*})\\
&=E\left[\textrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{*}\mid y_{1}^{*},\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*})\right]+\textrm{Var}\left[E\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{*}\mid y_{1}^{*},\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*}\right)\right]\\

&=\bm{\Sigma}+\textrm{Var}\left[(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})y_{1}^{*}+\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*}\mathbf{C}\right]\\
&=\bm{\Sigma}+\textrm{Var}[(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})y_{1}^{*}]+\textrm{Var}[\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*}\mathbf{C}]+\textrm{Cov}[(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})y_{1}^{*},\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*}\mathbf{C}]\\
&\qquad+\textrm{Cov}[\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*}\mathbf{C},(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})y_{1}^{*}]\\

&\qquad+\mathbf{C}^{\textsc{T}}\textrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{*},y_{1}^{*})(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{0})\\

Recall that for any two random vectors

\begin{array}[]{cl}&\bm{\Sigma}_{xz}=\textrm{Cov}[\mathbf{X}^{*}_{1},\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{1}]=\textrm{Cov}[E(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{1}\mid\mathbf{Z}^{*}_{1}),\ \mathbf{Z}^{*}_{1}]\\
