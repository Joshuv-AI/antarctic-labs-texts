Moment Expansions of the Energy Distance
Ian Langmore Gridmatic Inc. ianlangmore@gmail.com

Abstract
The energy distance is used to test distributional equality, and as a loss function in machine learning. While 𝒟 2 ​ ( X , Y ) = 0 {\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)=0 only when X ∼ Y X\sim Y , the sensitivity to different moments is of practical importance. This work considers 𝒟 2 ​ ( X , Y ) {\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y) in the case where the distributions are close. In this regime, 𝒟 2 ​ ( X , Y ) {\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y) is more sensitive to differences in the means X ¯ − Y ¯ {\bar{X}}-{\bar{Y}} , than differences in the covariances Δ \Delta . This is due to the structure of the energy distance and is independent of dimension. The sensitivity to on versus off diagonal components of Δ \Delta is examined when X X and Y Y are close to isotropic. Here a dimension dependent averaging occurs and, in many cases, off diagonal correlations contribute significantly less. Numerical results verify these relationships hold even when distributional assumptions are not strictly met.



Keywords:
Energy distance, Distribution distances, Kernel two sample tests, Kernel distance, Maximum mean discrepancy, Proper scoring rules

Introduction

We are motivated by a forecaster tuning forecast
X_{t}
, hopefully distributed the same as ground truth
Y_{t}
.
Only one sample
y_{t}
is available at a time, so many distributional distances, such as Wasserstein, are impossible to compute. The forecaster may however compute the
energy score

[
5
]
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{ES}}(X_{t},y_{t})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left\{\|X_{t}-y_{t}\|\right\}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{X,X^{\prime}}\left\{\|X_{t}-X_{t}^{\prime}\|\right\}.

Above,
X_{t},X_{t}^{\prime}
are
i.i.d.
In practice, the expectation is approximated with the
N
sample estimate,

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{ES}}}(t):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\{\|X_{t}^{(n)}-y_{t}\|\right\}-\frac{1}{2N(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,m=1\\
n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{N}\left\{\|X_{t}^{(n)}-X_{t}^{(m)}\|\right\}.

By averaging over times
t\in\mathcal{T}
we have a goodness of fit or loss function

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in T}\widehat{{\mathcal{ES}}}(X_{t},y_{t}).

(1)

The forecast minimizing (
1
) can be found with stochastic gradient descent
[
2
]
. This strategy has proven successful in machine learning for weather forecasts.
[
10
,
8
]
.

We drop the time dependence in order to focus on moments. The expected, time independent energy score is

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{X,Y}\left\{\|X-Y\|\right\}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{X,X^{\prime}}\left\{\|X-X^{\prime}\|\right\}.

This is a strictly proper scoring rule
[
5
]
, and is minimized just when
X
is distributed the same as
Y
(written
X\sim Y
).

The minimizing forecast does not change if we subtract
(1/2){\mathbb{E}}\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|
. Doing this gives us the (squared)
energy distance

[
13
]

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y):

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\|X-Y\|-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\|X-X^{\prime}\|-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|.

(2)

One can show that, with

\displaystyle F(\omega):

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left\{e^{i\omega\cdot X}\right\},\quad\mbox{and}\quad G(\omega):={\mathbb{E}}\left\{e^{i\omega\cdot Y}\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int\frac{|F(\omega)-G(\omega)|^{2}}{\|\omega\|^{d+1}}{\,\mathrm{d}\omega},

(3)

where

\displaystyle c_{d}:

\displaystyle={\mathrm{Vol}}\left({\mathbb{S}^{d}}\right)=\frac{2\pi^{(d+1)/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)}

Since characteristic functions uniquely define distributions, this shows
{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
is equal in distribution to
Y
, written
X\sim Y
. Rigorous conditions on the kernel under which this occurs have been studied by
[
11
]
.

{\mathcal{D}^{2}}
appears to have been first introduced by
[
12
,
13
]
as an extension of the
Cramér-von Mises distance
. It appears to have been independently discovered by
[
1
]
, who provides an alternate proof.

A practical consideration for learning is, what trade-offs will the minimizing forecast make, in light of the fact that
X\sim Y
generally cannot be achieved? We consider the case where moments of
X-Y
are much smaller than the average scale
\lambda
of
X
and
Y
. Proposition
3.1
will show that, roughly speaking,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\approx C_{1}\frac{m_{1}(\mu)}{\lambda}+C_{2}\frac{m_{2}(\Delta)}{\lambda^{3}},

(4)

where
m_{i}
are functionals of the difference of means
\mu:={\mathbb{E}}X-{\mathbb{E}}Y
and difference of covariances
\Delta:=\mbox{Cov}\left(X\right)-\mbox{Cov}\left(Y\right)
. Since an optimization will tend to balance the terms in (
4
), we expect a trained model to have
m_{1}\sim m_{2}/\lambda\ll m_{2}
. In other words, using the energy distance as a loss leads to matching the mean more precisely than the second moment.
The most explicit expansion comes in the nearly spherical case, where
m_{1}(\mu)=\|\mu\|^{2}
and
m_{2}(\Delta)=2\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}+\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}
, plus a term depending on skew. This is demonstrated numerically for normal and non-normal distributions.

Similar work has considered asymptotic (in sample size) expansions of kernel distances.
In
[
6
]
the maximum distance between
X
and
Y
detectable with
t
samples is shown to be proportional to
t^{-1/2}
. The recent work of
[
14
]
obtains an expansion of the test statistic, which reveals which moments are detectable given a sample size. This is done assuming
X
and
Y
are affine transformations of
i.i.d.
variables. This is in some sense more comprehensive than our work, but this comes at a cost of complexity of presentation and the affine assumption.

Section
2
demonstrates (
4
) in a simplified setting. The moment expansion is presented, discussed, and proven, in Section
3
. Numerical verification is done in Section
4
.

2
Intuition from M-dependent sequences

In this simplified setting of limited correlation length, we will see that off diagonal correlation differences
\Delta_{mn}
for
n\neq m
, appear in a corrector
O(1/d)
times smaller than the leading term in
{\mathcal{D}^{2}}
. This leading term involves only the first two moments of the marginals, with the difference of means
\mu
dominating the difference of variance.

Suppose
\left\{X_{n}\right\}
and
\left\{Y_{n}\right\}
are stationary second order sequences with

X_{n}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_{\ell}
for all
n,\ell
.
As always, we let
\left\{X^{\prime}_{n}\right\}
and
\left\{Y^{\prime}_{n}\right\}
be sequences independent from, but identical in distribution to,
\left\{X_{n}\right\}
,
\left\{Y_{n}\right\}
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}^{2}}_{d}
be the squared energy distance formed from vectors
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
,
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
. That is,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}^{2}}_{d}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left\{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(X_{n}-Y_{n})^{2}}\right\}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(X_{n}-X^{\prime}_{n})^{2}}\right\}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(Y_{n}-Y^{\prime}_{n})^{2}}\right\}.

The sequences are M-dependent if there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that
X_{n}\perp\!\!\!\perp X_{n+k}
for all
|k|>M
. In this case, with

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}:

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-M}^{M}\mbox{Cov}\left(X_{n}-Y_{n},X_{n+k}-Y_{n+k}\right),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nu^{2}:={\mathbb{E}}\left\{(X_{1}-Y_{1})^{2}\right\},

we have a central limit result
[
7
]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{d}(X_{n}-Y_{n})^{2}-\nu^{2}\right]\to\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2}/d).

We are therefore justified approximating (via. the delta method)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{d}(X_{n}-Y_{n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\nu^{2}+\varepsilon}=\nu\left[1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\nu}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\nu^{2}}+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\nu^{3}}\right)\right],

where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2}/d)
.

The leading term
\nu
only involves the marginals.
Since
{\mathbb{E}}\varepsilon=0
, the corrector to the leading term is
\nu{\mathbb{E}}\varepsilon^{2}/(8\nu^{2})=\gamma^{2}/(8d\nu)
. The corrector does involve off diagonal correlations, but is a factor
1/d
smaller than the leading term.

Analysis of the leading terms from all three contributions to
{\mathcal{D}^{2}}_{d}
can be done assuming the
X_{n}
are perturbations of
Y_{n}
. That is,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\{X_{1}-Y_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{Var}\left\{Y_{1}\right\}=\lambda^{2}-\delta^{2}/2,\quad\mbox{Var}\left\{X_{1}\right\}=\lambda^{2}+\delta^{2}/2,

with
\mu_{1}^{2}+\delta^{2}\ll\lambda^{2}
.
In this case, up to a
O(\gamma^{2}/d)
perturbation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\mathcal{D}^{2}}_{d}&\approx\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left\{(X_{1}-Y_{1})^{2}\right\}}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left\{(X_{1}-X^{\prime}_{1})^{2}\right\}}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left\{(Y_{1}-Y^{\prime}_{1})^{2}\right\}}\\
&=\sqrt{\mu_{1}^{2}+2\lambda^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(\lambda^{2}+\delta^{2}/2)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(\lambda^{2}-\delta^{2}/2)}\\
&=\frac{1}{2^{3/2}}\left[\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{\lambda}-\frac{\mu_{1}^{4}}{8\lambda^{3}}+\frac{\delta^{4}}{8\lambda^{3}}+O\left(\frac{\mu_{1}^{6}+\delta^{6}}{\lambda^{5}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(5)

The form of the score ensures that the difference of means arising in
X-Y
have no opportunity to cancel with terms coming from
X-X^{\prime}
or
Y-Y^{\prime}
(each of which is independent of the mean). Thus, so long as we are in the perturbative regime (large
\lambda
), the influence of mean perturbations (here
\mu_{1}
) on the score will dominate the scale perturbations (here
\delta
), irrespective of dimension. On the other hand, the relative influence of on versus off diagonal covariance terms was determined by averaging, and so depends on dimension.

Leading moments of the Fourier representation

3.1
Statement of results

We begin by writing the characteristic functions
F
,
G
as

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=e^{U},\quad\mbox{and}\quad G=e^{V}.

Next, re-write the Fourier representation of
{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)
(

U
and

\displaystyle{\tilde{H}}(\omega):

\displaystyle=\left|\exp\left\{\frac{U(\omega)+V(\omega)}{2}\right\}\right|^{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad W:=U-V,

the squared energy distance is

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)&=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int\frac{{\tilde{H}}(\omega)}{\|\omega\|^{d+1}}\left|e^{W(\omega)/2}-e^{-W(\omega)/2}\right|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}\omega}.\end{split}

(6)

Our fundamental assumptions will be on the decay of
{\tilde{H}}
, which roughly corresponds to relative size of
X
,
Y
versus the difference of distributions. Then, we will use cumulant identities

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\omega_{n}}W\bigg|_{\omega=0}&=i\mu_{n}:=i{\mathbb{E}}\left\{(X_{n}-Y_{n})\right\},\\
\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{{\partial}\omega_{n}{\partial}\omega_{m}}W\bigg|_{\omega=0}&=-\Delta_{mn}:=-\left[\mbox{Cov}\left(X_{n},X_{m}\right)-\mbox{Cov}\left(Y_{n},Y_{m}\right)\right],\\

&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-{\mathbb{E}}\left\{(Y_{m}-{\bar{Y}}_{m})(Y_{n}-{\bar{Y}}_{n})(Y_{\ell}-{\bar{Y}}_{\ell})\right\}\bigg],\end{split}

(7)

where

,

,
then write the Taylor series of the exponentiated

\lambda
.

In what follows,

3.3
Interpretation and applications

Proposition
3.1
sheds light on the energy score in the perturbative regime (large
\lambda
).
First, notice that differences of the mean have leading influence due to the structure of the score, which can be written using Taylor series of cumulants, weighted by a decay of
\|\omega\|^{-(d+1)}
. The order of
\lambda
attached to each term of the series determines the relative influence, and is independent of dimension. Second, the influence of on versus off diagonal members of the covariance is determined by averaging. Specifically, spherical averages result in the term
2\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}+\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}
. This will involve dimension, but the story is nuanced as we will see below.

3.3.1
Multivariate Gaussians and the M-dependent case revisited

We can use the form in corollary
3.2
to obtain the leading terms when
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2}I-\Delta/2)
,
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\lambda^{2}I+\Delta/2)
. Then

\displaystyle{\tilde{H}}(\omega)=H(\lambda\omega)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\omega\cdot\left(\frac{2I}{2\lambda^{2}}\right)\omega\right\}=\exp\left\{-\|\omega/\lambda\|^{2}\right\}

This means
h(r)=\exp\left\{-r^{2}\right\}
and then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h(r){\,\mathrm{dr}}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}h(r)r^{2}{\,\mathrm{dr}}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}.

Using the symmetry of
X
,
Y
about their mean (which implies
\beta\equiv 0
), and
corollary
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)&\sim\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{1}{(8d)^{1/2}}\|\mu\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\frac{1}{8d}\frac{1}{(8d)^{1/2}}\left[2\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}+\mathrm{Trace}\left(\Delta\right)^{2}-\|\mu\|^{4}\right]\end{split}

(8)

To plug in the M-dependent case of Section
2
, let us choose
\Delta
to have
\delta^{2}
on the diagonal and
\rho^{2}
on the
M
sub/super diagonals. Then, for large
d
,

\displaystyle\|\mu\|^{2}

\displaystyle=d\mu_{1}^{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\mu\|^{4}=d^{2}\mu_{1}^{4},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Trace}\left(\Delta\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\delta^{4},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}\sim d\delta^{4}+2dM\rho^{4},

so that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{d}{8}}\mu_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{8}}\left[\frac{\delta^{4}}{8}-\frac{\mu_{1}^{4}}{8}+\frac{\delta^{4}}{4d}+\frac{M\rho^{4}}{2d}\right]

(9)

This more precise result (
9
) agrees with (
5
). In particular, we see that off-diagonal covariance contribution
M\rho^{4}/(2d)
is
\sim 1/d
smaller than the leading diagonal contribution
\delta^{4}/8
. This is due to
\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}
giving an
O(d)
weight to each individual diagonal element, whereas the
\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}
term gives a weight of one to all elements.

3.3.2
Cosine similarity of gradients

In the machine learning setting,
X
is a forecast from a learned model, and
Y
is ground truth. An ideal loss term to encourage correct covariance structure would be

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:

\displaystyle=\|\mbox{Cov}\left(X\right)-\mbox{Cov}\left(Y\right)\|_{F}^{2}=\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\lambda_{n}^{2},

where
\lambda_{n}
is the
n^{th}
eigenvalue of the difference of covariance matrices
\Delta
. This weights every discrepancy in the covariance difference equally, and penalizes the spectrum in the L2 sense. However, when using
{\mathcal{D}^{2}}(X,Y)
as a loss, the leading contribution from covariance is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:

\displaystyle=2\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}+\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}=2\sum_{n=1}^{d}\lambda_{n}^{2}+\sum_{n,m=1}^{d}\lambda_{n}\lambda_{m}.

Since both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{L}
have a minimum at
\Delta=0
, it’s fair to ask how similar
\mathcal{L}
is to
\mathcal{I}
.

Let
\xi
be the model parameters, for example neural network weights. Typically
\xi
is learned by a flavor of gradient descent. Since the
\lambda_{n}
are a function of
\xi
, and so we can express the gradients as

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}\mathcal{L}(X,Y)}{{\partial}\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{{\partial}\lambda_{n}}{{\partial}\xi}\frac{{\partial}\mathcal{L}}{{\partial}\lambda_{n}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{{\partial}\mathcal{I}(X,Y)}{{\partial}\xi}=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{{\partial}\lambda_{n}}{{\partial}\xi}\frac{{\partial}\mathcal{I}}{{\partial}\lambda_{n}}.

We are thus motivated to study the cosine similarity of the gradients
\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}
vs.
\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{I}
. We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}:&=\frac{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}\cdot\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{I}}{\left\|\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}\right\|\,\left\|\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{I}\right\|}=\frac{2+\gamma^{2}}{\sqrt{4+\gamma^{2}(4+d)}}.\end{split}

(10)

where
\gamma^{2}:=\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}/\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}\in[0,d]
is a measure of diagonal importance.

Consider the case of local correlations of size
M
, inside a large system of size
d\gg M\gg 1
(as in Section
2
). This means,
\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}\sim Md
. The diagonal of
\Delta
will have positive or negative elements if
X
systematically over/under represents variance. In this case,
\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}\sim d^{2}
, so
\gamma^{2}\sim d/M
, and then
\mathcal{S}\sim 1/\sqrt{M}
.
On the other hand, if the diagonal is unbiased, we can expect
\mathrm{Trace}(\Delta)^{2}\sim d
, so
\gamma^{2}\sim 1/M
, and then
\mathcal{S}\sim\sqrt{M/d}
.
The effect on learning should be that the componentwise scales of
X
become equal to those of
Y
fairly quickly. Once that happens, the gradients of
\mathcal{L}
become close to orthogonal to those of
\mathcal{I}
, and the off-diagonal correlations may be neglected.

On the other hand, consider a high resolution system
d\gg 1
, and large scale correlation length that is a constant multiple of the system size. That is,
\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}\sim\rho d^{2}
, for
\rho\in(0,1)
. If the diagonal is biased,
\gamma^{2}\sim 1/\rho
, and then

As a result, we expect scale bias to distract from learning large scale correlations with energy distance. Once the scale is unbiased, the learning efficiency will be much better.

First start with
