MIBoost: A Gradient Boosting Algorithm for Variable Selection After Multiple Imputation
Robert Kuchen

Abstract
Statistical learning methods for automated variable selection, such as LASSO, elastic nets, or gradient boosting, have become increasingly popular tools for building powerful prediction models. Yet, in practice, analyses are often complicated by missing data. The most widely used approach to address missingness is multiple imputation, which creates several completed datasets. However, there is an ongoing debate on how to perform model selection in the presence of multiple imputed datasets. Simple strategies, such as pooling models across datasets, have been shown to have suboptimal properties. Although more sophisticated methods exist, they are often difficult to implement and therefore not widely applied. In contrast, two recent approaches modify the regularization methods LASSO and elastic nets by defining a single loss function, resulting in a unified set of coefficients across imputations. Our key contribution is to extend this principle to the framework of component‑wise gradient boosting by proposing MIBoost , a novel algorithm that employs a uniform variable‑selection mechanism across imputed datasets. Simulation studies suggest that our approach yields prediction performance comparable to that of these recently proposed methods.



Keywords:
Gradient boosting, Multiple imputation, Variable selection, Component‑wise gradient boosting, Prediction

1
Introduction

Multiple imputation is widely considered the gold standard for handling missing data. When the main interest lies in causal inference, coefficients and standard errors obtained from each imputed dataset can be easily aggregated using Rubin’s pooling rules
[
1
]
. However, performing variable selection for prediction purposes with multiple imputed datasets remains methodologically challenging, as the selected models can differ substantially across datasets. Many previously proposed methods for obtaining a single unified model are complex and insufficiently implemented in software packages. Simply selecting all variables chosen in any of the datasets often leads to overly complex models
[
2
]
, so many users resort to ad hoc solutions, such as including only variables selected in a minimum proportion (e.g., 50%) of datasets. While this heuristic may yield satisfactory results in some cases, it suffers from strong dependence on an arbitrary threshold and can be unstable in small samples or in the presence of correlated predictors
[
3
]
. To address this problem,
[
4
]
proposed a framework that leverages the popular variable selection methods LASSO and elastic nets
[
5
]
. They suggest a framework comprising two different approaches, which both rely on minimizing a single loss function, ultimately resulting in a unified model. This framework is implemented in the
R
package
miselect

[
6
]
.

Another statistical‑learning method often considered an alternative to LASSO and elastic nets is component‑wise gradient boosting
[
7
]
, which also implicitly results in coefficient shrinkage and variable selection, making it well‑suited for high‑dimensional data. Whereas LASSO and elastic nets are limited to generalized linear models, boosting accommodates diverse loss functions and nonlinear effects, making it highly versatile. However, no method to date ensures uniform variable selection when applying component-wise gradient boosting to multiply imputed data. This paper extends the idea of defining a single loss function across and proposes a novel algorithm for component‑wise gradient boosting that enforces consistent variable selection across all imputed datasets.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose
MIBoost
, a novel boosting algorithm specifically designed for prediction after multiple imputation, which couples base‑learner selection across imputations to improve model stability and interpretability.

•

We develop a cross‑validation strategy for
MIBoost
that avoids data leakage by separating training and validation imputations and aggregates performance across imputations in a principled way.

•

We conduct a comprehensive simulation study assessing the predictive accuracy, variable selection performance, and computational efficiency of
MIBoost
compared to existing boosting‑based and imputation‑based approaches.

MIBoost
algorithm together with its cross‑validation framework,
MIBoostCV
. Section
4
presents a comprehensive simulation study comparing the performance of
MIBoost
with established methods. Finally, Section
5
discusses our findings and outlines future research directions.

2
Background

2.1
Statistical and Component-Wise Gradient Boosting

Statistical gradient boosting
[
8
]
is a machine learning algorithm that minimizes a specified differentiable loss function
\rho
by incrementally constructing an additive model in a stagewise manner
[
9
,
10
]
. Given
n
observations, we define the
empirical loss
as

\hat{R}(\bm{\eta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho\big(y_{i},\eta(\bm{X}_{i})\big),

which quantifies the discrepancy between the additive predictor
\hat{\bm{\eta}}
and the observed responses
\bm{y}
. Boosting achieves minimization of this loss by iteratively descending
\hat{R}(\bm{\eta})
along the direction of its negative gradient. Specifically, at each iteration, we compute

\bm{u}=-\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\hat{R}(\bm{\eta}),

which corresponds to the negative gradient of the empirical loss with respect to the current additive predictor (often referred to as
pseudo-residuals
). In the special case of squared error loss, these pseudo-residuals coincide with the ordinary residuals.

Design Matrix and base-learners

The additive predictor
\hat{\bm{\eta}}
is a function of the design matrix
\bm{X}
, which contains information on a set of
c
original
candidate covariates
(e.g.,
age
,
sex
). Centering these covariates to a mean of zero is recommended for numerical stability and comparability. From these, we derive a collection of
p

candidate functions
(
c\leq p
), so that we can express the design matrix as

\bm{X}=[\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{p}]=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{x}_{n}^{T}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{X}_{j}
denotes the vector of values of the
j
‑th
candidate function
across all observations, and
\bm{x}_{i}
the
i
‑th row containing all candidate function values for observation
i
. Candidate functions may be simple transformations (e.g.,
age
,
age
2
) or more flexible effects, such as penalized splines or random intercepts. Building on these candidate functions, we define
base‑learners
as simple prediction rules (e.g., univariate regressions or smoothers) that estimate their association with the outcome.

In this paper we focus on
component‑wise gradient boosting

[
7
]
, in which each base-learner corresponds only to a single candidate function and thus to a single candidate covariate. Furthermore, at each iteration, only a single base‑learner is selected and updated, while all others remain unchanged. Formally, at each boosting iteration, denoted by
t
in the following, we compute, for each of the
p
base‑learners, the
least‑squares projection
of the current negative gradient vector
\bm{u}^{[t]}
(the pseudo‑residuals) onto the function space of that base-learner:

\hat{h}_{\beta_{r}}^{[t]}=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(u_{i}^{[t]}-h(x_{i,r})\big)^{2},

where
\mathcal{H}_{r}
denotes the function space associated with base-learner
r
. The estimated function
\hat{h}_{\beta_{r}}^{[t]}
is referred to as the
empirical base-learner
for candidate function
r
in iteration
t
.

For a simple linear base‑learner, this corresponds to an ordinary least‑squares regression of the pseudo‑residuals on an intercept and the
r
-th candidate function, yielding the fitted function

\hat{h}_{\beta_{r}}^{[t]}(\bm{X}_{r})=\hat{\beta}_{0,r}^{[t]}\bm{1}+\hat{\beta}_{r}^{[t]}\bm{X}_{r}.

In each iteration, the base-learner that best fits the pseudo‑residuals is selected:

r^{*}=\arg\min_{r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(u_{i}^{[t]}-\hat{h}_{\beta_{r}}^{[t]}(x_{i,r})\big)^{2}.

The additive predictor is then updated by moving a small step
\nu
(typically
\nu=0.1
) in the direction of the selected base-learner:

\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{[t]}=\bm{\hat{\eta}}^{[t-1]}+\nu\hat{h}_{\beta_{r^{*}}}^{[t]}.

Note that the same base‑learner can be selected multiple times. Consequently, with only one base‑learner updated per iteration, a small stopping iteration
t_{stop}
typically leaves some covariates excluded and, due to the small step length, results in coefficient shrinkage. This makes component-wise gradient boosting conceptually related to penalized regression approaches such as the LASSO and elastic nets
[
5
,
7
,
11
]
.

Moreover, unlike black‑box machine‑learning methods, the resulting additive model remains interpretable and can flexibly accommodate different outcome types—including continuous, binary, count, and time‑to‑event data—by specifying an appropriate loss function
\rho
. These properties make component‑wise boosting particularly attractive for high‑dimensional problems that require balancing predictive performance with model sparsity.

The optimal number of iterations, denoted by
t_{stop}^{*}
is determined by identifying the number of iterations that results in the model with the lowest prediction error. Typically, this number is selected using cross-validation (CV).

2.2
Existing Approaches for Model Fitting with Multiply Imputed Data

Several strategies exist for fitting prediction models on multiply imputed data. The simplest is
naïve data stacking

[
12
]
, in which all
M
imputed datasets are combined into one large dataset comprising
M\times n
observations. While easy to implement, this approach artificially inflates the sample size, possibly resulting in overfitting .

An alternative is
estimate averaging

[
2
]
, in which model selection is performed independently in each imputed dataset, and the
M
resulting coefficient estimates are averaged. However, variables selected in one dataset may be omitted in another, leading to a final model with many non‑zero coefficients and thus compromising model parsimony.

To address this,
selection frequency thresholding

[
3
]
has been proposed. In this approach, only variables selected in at least a prespecified proportion of imputations are retained, with a 50% cutoff (“majority vote”) being most common. Other thresholds (e.g., 25% or 40%) can be used to balance sparsity and stability. While this approach leads to sparser model than estimate averaging, the cutoff is arbitrary and relevant variables with unstable signals may be excluded. Moreover, dependencies between covariates are partially ignored, and imputation uncertainty is not fully captured.

Beyond these, more advanced approaches have been proposed.
Bayesian data‑augmentation methods

[
13
]
integrate over the missing‑data distribution within a unified posterior framework, naturally propagating uncertainty, but at the cost of high computational demands and sensitivity to prior choices.
Bootstrapped inclusion‑probability methods

[
14
]
use resampling across imputations to stabilize variable selection but require extensive computation and lack clear thresholds for inclusion.
EM‑based penalized regression

[
15
]
integrates imputation with regularized model fitting, offering efficiency but relying on strong distributional assumptions and custom model‑selection criteria. Finally,
joint modeling approaches

[
16
]
fit a single model across all imputations by combining likelihoods, although these methods can be complex to implement and are less explored in practice.

Since they deemed existing methods unsatisfactory,
Du et al. 2022
proposed a novel post‑imputation variable‑selection framework extending regularization methods in two formulations. In the
stacked
approach, applicable with both LASSO and elastic nets, imputations are combined into a single augmented dataset, with each subject’s observations weighted so that their total weight sums to one, yielding a single final model without inflating the sample size. In the
grouped
approach, applicable only with LASSO, imputed datasets remain independent, resulting in different coefficient estimates, yet coefficients for the same covariate are penalized jointly, enforcing uniform selection across imputations.

In both formulations, the
standard
and
adaptive
versions of LASSO and elastic nets can be applied, resulting in a total of six models. However, since
Du et al. 2022
reported that the stacked, adaptive formulations of LASSO and elastic nets achieved the best performance (denoted
SaLASSO
and
SaENET
), we focus on these in the following.

As these methods, like
MIBoost
, rely on minimizing a single loss function and are implemented in the
R
package
miselect

[
6
]
, we compare our approach with them.

3
Novel Methods

3.1
The Algorithm
MIBoost

The core idea of
MIBoost
conceptually simple: although separate gradient boosting algorithms are run on each of the
M
imputed datasets, base‑learner selection at each iteration is based on the
aggregated loss
across these datasets. This enforces a uniform selection and updating mechanism, while gradient computation and predictor updates remain dataset‑specific. The final model is obtained by averaging the fitted base‑learner functions across imputations, yielding a single combined predictor.

Algorithm
3.2
provides a mathematical formulation of our procedure. Superscripts
(m)
and
[t]
denote the imputation index (
m=1,\dots,M
) and boosting iteration (
t=1,\dots,t_{\mathrm{stop}}
), respectively, where
(m)
indicates that the corresponding quantity is computed on the
m
‑th imputed dataset. Each imputed dataset
m
maintains its own additive predictor
\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{(m)[t]}
, initialized with either the mean of
\mathbf{y}^{(m)}
or zero. The procedure consists of four steps:

(1)

At the start of each boosting iteration, we compute the negative gradient vector (pseudo‑residuals)
\bm{u}^{(m)}
for each imputed dataset, obtained as the negative derivative of the loss function with respect to the additive predictor, evaluated at the previous iteration’s predictor
\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{(m)[t-1]}
, reflecting the direction in which the loss decreases most steeply.

(2)

These pseudo‑residuals
\bm{u}^{(m)}
are then regressed on each candidate base‑learner
h_{\beta_{r}}
(
r\in\{1,\dots,p\}
) independently within each imputed dataset, yielding dataset‑specific fitted base‑learner functions and corresponding residual sums of squares (RSS).

(3)

For each base‑learner, aggregate the residual sums of squares across imputations to obtain a single loss value
L_{r}^{[t]}
. Select the base‑learner
r^{*}
with the lowest aggregated loss for this iteration. This joint selection step ensures that in all imputed datasets the same model component is updated, improving stability and favoring predictors consistently informative across datasets.

(4)

Finally, the additive predictors
\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{(m)[t]}
in each imputed dataset are updated by adding the fitted contribution of the selected base‑learner, scaled by the step length
\nu
(typically
\nu=0.1
), to the previous iteration’s predictor.

(5) Post‑processing:
The above process is repeated for
t_{\mathrm{stop}}
iterations. After boosting concludes, the fitted base‑learner functions are averaged across imputations, producing a single coherent final additive predictor. Algorithm
3.2
summarizes the complete procedure.

3.2
MIBoostCV
- Cross‑validation for
MIBoost

To select the optimal stopping iteration
t_{\mathrm{stop}}^{*}
and evaluate out‑of‑sample performance, we employ
K
‑fold cross‑validation (CV). A detailed mathematical formulation of this procedure is provided in Algorithm
3.2
. The steps of the CV procedure are as follows:

Outer loop over CV folds:

(1)

Split data in training and validation subset:

The full dataset with missing values is first partitioned into
K
disjoint folds. In each CV iteration, one fold serves as the validation subset, and the remaining
K-1
folds form the training subset.

(2)

Multiple imputation:

In each CV iteration
k
, the training subset is multiply imputed
M
times using the chosen imputation procedure, producing
M
complete training subsets. Crucially, to avoid data leakage, the imputation is performed
after
the training–validation split. The associated validation subset is then imputed
M
times using the
M
imputation models learned from the training imputations, ensuring consistency between training and validation imputations. For every imputed training subset, there is thus a corresponding imputed validation subset.

(3)

Centering:

Subsequently, since boosting performs best with standardized inputs, all covariates in the
M
imputed training subsets are centered. The corresponding means from the training subsets are then used to center the covariates in the associated imputed validation subsets.

Inner loop over boosting iterations:

(4.1)

Application of
MIBoost
:

Within each fold, the
MIBoost
algorithm is applied to the
M
imputed and centered training subsets for
t_{\mathrm{stop}}
iterations, with base‑learner selection coupled across imputations as described in Algorithm
3.2
.

(4.2)

Average base‑learner functions across imputations:

At the end of each boosting iteration, the estimated base‑learner functions are averaged across the
M
imputed training subsets, resulting in a single model for that iteration.

(4.3)

Prediction and error computation:

This single model is then used to estimate the response variable in each of the
M
imputed validation subsets using their respective covariate values. The prediction errors from these
M
validations are averaged, yielding one aggregated prediction error per boosting iteration
t\leq t_{\mathrm{stop}}
.

(4.4)

Aggregate per‑fold error:
For each boosting iteration
t
, store the averaged prediction error for fold
k
.

(5)

Average prediction errors across folds:
Since the CV procedure involves
K
iterations, we obtain
K
aggregated prediction errors for each boosting iteration. These are then averaged across CV folds to obtain a single final aggregated prediction error for each iteration.

(6)

Select optimal stopping iteration:
Select
t_{\mathrm{stop}}^{*}
as the boosting iteration that is associated with the smallest final prediction error.

(7)

Final model fitting:
Impute the full dataset with missing values, resulting in
M
complete training datasets, and center the covariates independently within each imputed dataset. Then, run
MIBoost
on these datasets for
t_{\mathrm{stop}}^{*}
boosting iterations to obtain the final model.

Algorithm 1:
MIBoost
–
Multiple‑Imputation Gradient Boosting

The algorithm requires the following to be specified:

•

m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
: index of imputations.

•

i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
: index of observations.

•

t_{\mathrm{stop}}
: stopping iteration.

•

\nu
: step length.

•

\rho
: differentiable loss function.

•

\hat{h}_{\beta_{1}},\dots,\hat{h}_{\beta_{p}}
: base‑learners.

•

\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{(m)[0]}
: initialized predictors.

Iterative procedure (for
t=1
to
t_{\mathrm{stop}}
):

1.

Compute negative gradients:
For each imputation
m
,

u_{i}^{(m)[t]}=-\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta^{(m)}}\rho\big(y_{i}^{(m)},\eta^{(m)}\big)\right|_{\eta^{(m)}=\hat{\eta}_{i}^{(m)[t-1]}},

or in vector form:

\bm{u}^{(m)[t]}=-\nabla_{\bm{\eta}}\hat{R}^{(m)}(\bm{\eta})\big|_{\bm{\eta}=\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{(m)[t-1]}},

with
\hat{R}^{(m)}(\bm{\eta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(y_{i}^{(m)},\eta_{i})
.

2.

Fit base‑learners:
For each
r\in\{1,\dots,p\}
and
m
,

\hat{h}_{\beta_{r}}^{(m)[t]}=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{i}^{(m)[t]}-h(x_{i,r}^{(m)}))^{2}.

3.

Select best base‑learner:

L_{r}^{[t]}:=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{i}^{(m)[t]}-\hat{h}_{i,r}^{(m)[t]})^{2},\hskip 8.50012ptr^{*}=\arg\min_{r}L_{r}^{[t]}.

4.

Update predictors:

\hat{\eta}_{i}^{(m)[t]}=\hat{\eta}_{i}^{(m)[t-1]}+\nu\hat{h}_{i,r^{*}}^{(m)[t]}.

5.

Post‑processing:
Average fitted base‑learners across imputations:

\bar{h}_{\beta_{r}}(x_{r})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{h}_{\beta_{r}}^{(m)[t_{*}]}(x_{r}),

and construct the final additive predictor:

\hat{\eta}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{p}\bar{h}_{\beta_{r}}(x_{r}).

Algorithm 2:
MIBoostCV

The algorithm requires the following to be specified:

•

k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
: index of folds.

•

m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
: index of imputations.

•

\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
: full dataset.

•

\mathcal{I}_{k}
: indices of observations in fold
k
.

•

\mathrm{MIBoost}(\cdot)
: boosting procedure.

•

t_{\mathrm{stop}}
: maximum boosting iteration.

Outer loop: For each fold
k=1,\dots,K
:

1.

Split data in training and validation subset:

\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i\notin\mathcal{I}_{k}\},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{D}^{(k)}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\}.

Multiple imputation:

Impute the training subset
M
times using the chosen imputation model:

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(-k,m)}=\mathcal{I}^{(m)}\!\big(\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\big).

M

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(k,m)}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{trained}}^{(m)}\!\big(\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\big).

\mu_{j}^{(-k,m)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}|}\sum_{i\notin\mathcal{I}_{k}}\tilde{x}_{ij}^{(-k,m)}.

):

4.1.

Application of
MIBoost

Apply
MIBoost
to each imputed and centered training subset:

.

4.3.

Prediction and error computation:

Use
\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{t}^{(-k,\mathrm{avg})}
to predict outcomes for each imputed validation subset:

Compute prediction errors for each imputed validation subset and average across
m
.

Aggregate per‑fold errors:
For each boosting iteration
t

.

4
Simulation Study

4.1
Setup

We compare the performance of our proposed algorithm with established methods for variable selection in multiply imputed datasets. First, we apply traditional component‑wise gradient boosting independently to each imputed dataset and derive a single final model using an estimating-averaging approach. We also apply the
SaLASSO
and
SaENET
algorithms implemented in the
R
package
miselect
. Exact specifications for all methods are provided in the Appendix.

We perform a simulation study comprising 100 simulation rounds, each generating
n=500
observations. In each round, the data with missing values are split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). Within each training set, all methods use 5‑fold cross‑validation, repeatedly partitioning the data into inner training and validation subsets to determine the optimal hyperparameters (number of boosting iterations
t_{\mathrm{stop}}^{*}
and shrinkage parameter
\lambda
, respectively). All computations were done with
R
.

Data‑generating mechanism

The outcome variable is generated using the linear model

\mathbf{y}=\alpha+\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\varepsilon},\hskip 25.0pt\alpha=5,\hskip 25.0pt\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,4^{2}),

where the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
contains
p=55
covariates,
q=5
of which are informative:

\beta_{j}\sim\begin{cases}\mathrm{Unif}(1,3),&j\leq 5\\
0,&j>5.\end{cases}

We partition
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{info}},\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{noise}}]
, where

\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{info}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{q}(\mathbf{0},\Sigma),\hskip 9.24994pt\Sigma_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&i=j\\
\rho&i\neq j\end{cases},\hskip 9.24994pt\rho=0.25,\hskip 25.0pt\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{noise}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Thus, informative covariates follow a correlated multivariate normal distribution (
\rho=0.25
), while the uninformative covariates are independent standard normal.

Missingness mechanism and imputation

We introduce missing values in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{y}
under a missing‑at‑random (MAR) mechanism. The first two covariates (
X_{1},X_{2}
) are fully observed and drive missingness in all other variables (
X_{j},j>2
) and the outcome:

\mathbb{P}(R_{iv}=0\mid X_{i1},X_{i2})=\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\big(\alpha+\gamma_{1}X_{i1}+\gamma_{2}X_{i2}\big),

where
R_{iv}=0
indicates a missing value,
\bm{\gamma}=(0.75,-0.5)^{\mathsf{T}}
, and the intercept
\alpha
is chosen such that the missingness proportion is approximately 30%. Missing values are imputed using
multivariate imputation by chained equations
(
MICE
) with predictive mean matching, generating
M=10
imputed datasets. For each variable, only covariates with a Spearman correlation of at least 0.1 with the target are included in its imputation model.

Discussion

First, it is noteworthy that the boosting algorithms, on average, selected a greater number of covariates than
SaLASSO
and
SaENET
. The tendency of boosting algorithms to include uninformative covariates has previously been report by

]
. Consequently, approaches for deselecting such covariates are under active discussion and could potentially be incorporated into

in future developments
[
18
]
. It should also be emphasized that this behavior is highly dependent on the underlying simulation design, as demonstrated in
[
19
]
.

Despite differences in model complexity, all four methods achieved nearly identical predictive performance. For boosting, this is unsurprising, as prediction accuracy is often only marginally affected by parsimony once key predictors are included
[
2
]
.
SaLASSO
and
SaENET
failed to select all informative variables but included fewer uninformative ones, balancing the effect. Relative performance remains highly dependent on the simulation setting.

However, it should be noted that estimate averaging resulted in the inclusion of substantially more covariates. In practice, such models would require a considerably greater amount of information, potentially entailing a more laborious and costly data collection process. Furthermore, when model interpretability is a priority, increased complexity inevitably reduces transparency and complicates interpretation.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that we introduced
MIBoost
specifically in conjunction with component-wise gradient boosting because the need for obtaining a single interpretable model based on multiply imputed datasets is particularly pronounced in statistical learning settings, in which interpretability is crucial. This choice also facilitates direct comparison with the methods proposed by
[
4
]
Nonetheless, the underlying principle— selecting, across all imputed datasets, the one base-learner that minimizes an aggregated loss function — can be readily generalized to other boosting frameworks. Accordingly, the approach can in the future be extended to likelihood-based boosting, in which an aggregated likelihood can be defined, as well as to non-statistical boosting methods.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed
MIBoost
, a novel approach for applying component-wise gradient boosting to multiply imputed datasets. The method operates by minimizing a total loss function that aggregates the losses associated with each base learner across all imputations. This procedure ensures a uniform variable-selection mechanism across datasets, such that, in every iteration, all gradient boosting algorithms select the same candidate covariate. In a simulation study,
MIBoost
achieved predictive performance virtually identical to that of other established methods designed to ensure uniform variable selection across multiply imputed datasets. Importantly, the underlying principle of the algorithm can be readily generalized to broader boosting frameworks in future work.

First of all, it is important to note that the
miselect
package provides built‑in cross‑validation (CV) functions to find the optimal values for
\lambda
and
\alpha
. However, these functions can only be applied to data that have already been imputed. As elucidated in subsection
3.2
, to prevent data leakage, data should only be imputed
after
being split into the respective training and validation subsets. We therefore wrote our own code to split the data, perform imputation, and carry out 5‑fold CV.

To find the optimal
\lambda
and
\alpha
values, we first needed to define a
\lambda
sequence for cross‑validation. To obtain such a sequence, we initially ran both
SaLASSO
and
SaENET
for 50 preliminary simulation rounds using an automatically generated
\lambda
sequence of length 200 and recorded all optimal
\lambda
values obtained across the CV folds. Based on these results, we constructed a new
\lambda
sequence that was broader than the observed range of optimal values, with a higher density of values in regions in which many optimal
\lambda
values were found.

For
SaENET
only, we applied the same procedure to
\alpha
. Our preliminary simulations showed that the optimal
\alpha
values varied across simulation rounds and CV folds, spanning the full range from
0
(ridge regression) to
1
(LASSO). We therefore defined an
\alpha
sequence consisting of 41 equally spaced values between 0 and 1.

Having determined the
\lambda
and
\alpha
sequences for cross‑validation, we ran our custom CV code. For
SaLASSO
, in each simulation round we selected the
\lambda
value associated with the smallest CV error. For
SaENET
, we performed a grid search over all
41\times 200=8{,}200

(\lambda,\alpha)
pairs and selected the pair with the smallest CV error.

Furthermore, the
miselect
package allows for weighting observations to account for the fact that stacked datasets consist of
M\times n
observations. For both
SaLASSO
and
SaENET
, we chose observation weights of
1/M
, as recommended in
[
4
]
. An alternative weighting approach—scaling each observation by the proportion of missing values across all variables—was also discussed in
[
4

Finally, since we applied not only standard LASSO or elastic net but their adaptive versions, we needed to define adaptive weight vectors for each run of
