DP-RFT: Learning to Generate Synthetic Text via Differentially Private Reinforcement Fine-Tuning
Fangyuan Xu*, Sihao Chen, Zinan Lin, Taiwei Shi, Sydney Graham, Pei Zhou, Mengting Wan, Alex Stein, Virginia Estellers, Charles Chen, Morris Sharp, Richard Speyer, Tadas Baltrusaitis, Jennifer Neville, Eunsol Choi, Longqi Yang

Abstract
Differentially private (DP) synthetic data generation plays a pivotal role in developing large language models (LLMs) on private data, where data owners cannot provide eyes-on access to individual examples. Generating DP synthetic data typically involves a difficult trade-off. On one hand, DP finetuning methods train an LLM as a synthetic data generator with formal privacy guarantees, yet it still requires the raw content of private examples for model training. However, methods that avoid direct exposure to private data are bounded by an off-the-shelf, un-finetuned model, whose outputs often lack domain fidelity. Can we train an LLM to generate high-quality synthetic text without eyes-on access to individual private examples? In this work, we introduce Differentially Private Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (DP-RFT), an online reinforcement learning algorithm for synthetic data generation with LLMs. DP-RFT leverages DP-protected nearest-neighbor votes from an eyes-off private corpus as a reward signal for on-policy synthetic samples generated by an LLM. The LLM iteratively learns to generate synthetic data to maximize the expected DP votes through Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). We evaluate DP-RFT for long-form and domain-specific synthetic data generation, such as news articles, meeting transcripts, and medical article abstracts. Our experiments show that DP-RFT closes the gap between private evolution and DP finetuning methods in terms of the fidelity and downstream utility of the generated synthetic data, while respecting the private data boundary.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable progress in their capabilities through massive-scale training
(
23
;
4
)
.
However, LLM training requires centralized access to its training data. This is typically incompatible with
private data
, where data owners cannot provide eyes-on access to the data due to privacy constraints, regulations, or other reasons
(
6
;
5
;
38
;
7
)
.

Differentially-private
(DP) synthetic data has emerged as a responsible way of circumventing the need for eyes-on data access. The goal is to leverage LLMs to generate a synthetic dataset that is statistically similar to the private dataset
(
22
;
27
;
53
;
55
;
18
)
, while ensuring the privacy guarantee that individual samples in the original data cannot be inferred from the model outputs
(
13
)
. DP synthetic data can be used for downstream model development
with intact privacy guarantee thanks to DP’s post-processing properties
(
14
)
.

Existing DP synthetic data generation methods typically face a difficult trade-off between data quality versus the required level of access to private dataset during data generation.
While DP finetuning methods, e.g. DP-SGD
(
1
)
, provide privacy guarantee when training the synthetic data generator on the private dataset, they still require data custodians to share and expose raw private data samples in the training process
(
30
;
39
)
.
On the other hand,
methods which avoid direct exposure to private data e.g. Aug-PE
(
51
)
, leverage iterative prompting to guide off-the-shelf LLMs to generate data that are more similar to the private distribution. However, as such approaches are bounded by samples generated from a frozen LLM, the outputs often lack domain fidelity.

To get the best of both worlds, we propose
Differentially Private Reinforcement Fine-Tuning
(DP-RFT), a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for training LLMs as DP synthetic data generators without the need to expose individual private examples to LLM during the process. Building upon the reward-based RL post-training techniques for LLMs
(
36
;
56
;
21
;
17
)
, DP-RFT leverages DP-protected nearest-neighbor votes (DP Votes,
30
;
51
) from an eyes-off private corpus as a reward signal for LLM’s on-policy synthetic sample generation. The DP-votes can be calculated within the boundary of the private data and then exposed to LLM with formal privacy guarantee, so that the private data owners do not need to provide access for individual private examples to LLM during training. The LLM learns from reward feedback to generate synthetic data to maximize the expected DP votes through Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO;
43
,
43
).

We evaluate DP-RFT for
long-form
synthetic data generation with different domains and types of text, such as news articles
(
32
)
, meeting transcripts
(
58
)
, LLM-user chat logs
(
57
)
and medical article abstracts
(
52
)
. We train DP-RFT models under different levels of privacy budget, and evaluate the generated synthetic data by intrinsic quality (§
5.2
), as well as downstream utility when the synthetic data is used as training data for another language model (§
5.1
).
Our experiments show that DP-RFT outperforms Aug-PE in terms of the fidelity and downstream utility of the generated synthetic data, and closes the gap between DP finetuning and Aug-PE in terms of downstream utility. Our key contributions are:

1.

New finetuning method for DP synthetic data generation
: We propose DP-RFT , which leverages DP-protected reward function and reinforcement learning to train a LLM against private corpus, without
ever
ingesting private examples directly during the training process and hence satisfying the
eyes-off
constraint.

2.

In-depth experiments and analyses
: We conduct comprehensive evaluation of our methods on four datasets, showing significant improvement on downstream utility, especially for a tight privacy budget and on dataset which are out-of-distribution for the backbone language model. Our qualitative analysis further reveals that DP-RFT is able to better capture lexical and structural similarity of long-form, structural outputs.

Background and Related work

Differential Privacy with LLMs.

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in many real-world cases, preserving the privacy of training and evaluation data has been an active research area. One popular approach is to apply DP-SGD
(
1
)
to train LLMs for classification
(
54
;
26
)
or generation tasks
(
47
;
54
;
26
;
55
;
31
;
24
;
33
;
45
)
. Later work proposed training-free approaches, such as injecting calibrated DP noise into the token-by-token generation process of LLMs
(
46
;
16
;
12
)
, or aggregating multiple LLM outputs in a DP manner
(
50
)
.
While these methods provide theoretical DP guarantees, they all require exposing the private data as input to the LLM. In practice, there could be regulatory constraints that require the private data to be completely
eyes-off
, preventing the adoption of such methods. Private Evolution (PE)
(
30
;
51
;
29
;
48
)
is a newly emerged framework to address this challenge.
It proposes an iterative prompting pipeline which steers the model generation to be more similar to the private data, as measured by embedding similarity.
While these methods avoid private inputs, their reliance on un-finetuned models limits the synthetic data quality. Our DP-RFT aims to take the best of both worlds: adapting model weights to better fit private data without directly inputting it into the model. Recent work
(
34
)
proposes to generate synthetic data via gradient matching, which requires gradient computation on private data. Notably,
20
recently proposed fine-tuning LLMs using embedding similarity as a reward with an offline reinforcement learning algorithm (DPO;
42
,
42
).

Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for LLMs.

We briefly review how reinforcement learning is used to post-train large language models. Let a language model with parameters
\theta
define a policy
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)
over token sequences given a prompt
p
. The goal of RFT is to update
\theta
so that samples
d\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)
have higher task-specific utility, quantified by a scalar reward
R(d,p)
. In modern LLM post-training,
R
can come from a learned reward model (e.g., RLHF,
36
,
36
), or from verifiable signals (e.g., passing tests in code/math), and training proceeds by maximizing the cumulative rewards. Practically, RFT alternates between: (i) sampling on-policy generations from
\pi_{\theta}
for a batch of prompts; (ii) computing scalar rewards for each sample; and (iii) updating
\theta
via a policy gradient method (e.g., PPO,
43
,
43
; or GRPO,
44
,
44
) on those samples. Compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT), where LLM ingests reference texts as input during training, RFT directly optimizes the task metric exposed by
R
and is thus effective when high-quality labels are scarce or when a reward function
R
can be defined. The fact that RFT does not ingest example outputs during training is the key to our DP-RFT algorithm’s design.

DP-RFT: Differentially-Private Reinforcement Finetuning

Given a set of private documents
D
priv
, our goal is to train a language model
M
gen
that generates synthetic documents
D
syn
similar to
D
priv
.
M
gen
takes an input prompt
p
which contains public information about the private corpus and produces a synthetic document
d
. We describe our method, DP-RFT, which leverages reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) to train
M
gen
against a DP-protected reward function
R
. We illustrate the workflow of DP-RFT, as well as its differences from prior approaches, including DP-Finetuning
(
1
)
and Augmented Private Evolution (Aug-PE;
51
) in
Figure 1
.

3.1
Generating input prompts

We use a large language model (LLM) to construct input prompts which contain public information about the target domain.
We construct a diverse set of prompts to encourage diverse generation from
M
gen
following Augmented Private Evolution (Aug-PE)
(
51
)
for training and inference of DP-RFT. We focus on one or both of the two axes of diversity (1) content diversity and (2) length diversity. For content diversity, we generate a set of keywords using an LLM. For instance, for BBC articles, we prompt an LLM to generate a list of topics (e.g. Politics, Sports), along with keywords associated with each topic. We obtain a set of prompts, each with a different set of keywords, which we use as input to
M_{gen}
. For length diversity, we define a possible range of length for the private documents and random sample one length to include in the prompt (e.g. “The generated document should contain around 300 words”). We include the details of the procedure in §
C
in the appendix.

3.2
Rewards

DP voting as reward.

Aug-PE
(
51
)
prompts language models to generate multiple synthetic documents and iteratively generate synthetic documents via a process called
DP voting
: each private document votes for a synthetic document that is most similar to them measured with a similarity function, such as a text embedding model. They find that selecting synthetic documents that receive more votes (i.e. close to more private documents in the embedding space) lead to better synthetic documents and hence downstream performance.
We take inspiration from Aug-PE and propose the following new designs to the DP-voting mechanism and the iterative generation-sampling process:

(1) Using raw similarity scores as votes.
In Aug-PE, each private sample votes only for its closest synthetic document, producing a binary (1 or 0) signal. This discards valuable information in the raw similarity scores, such as
how much
one synthetic sample is better than another. To capture this richer signal, we instead use the raw similarity scores
sim(d,D_{priv,i})
directly as votes from the
i
-th private document
D_{priv,i}
to the synthetic document
d
, where
sim
is a similarity measure (e.g. text embedding similarity).

(2) DP-RFT.

While Aug-PE can promote synthetic documents that are similar to the private documents, the model is untrained, and hence has limited steerability. We instead leverage the votes as the reward in Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) to steer
M
gen
to generate synthetic documents more similar to the private corpus. Concretely, for a synthetic document
d
and a predefined similarity measure
sim
, we define
R_{sim}=\frac{1}{{|D_{priv}|}}\sum_{i=1}^{i=|D_{priv}|}sim(d,D_{priv,i})
as the reward.

Ensuring differential privacy.

To ensure differential privacy, we add Gaussian noise to
R_{sim}
following Aug-PE. For a synthetic example
d
, we first obtain its similarity to each of the private documents
sim(d,D_{priv,i})
and clip the similarity score to a threshold
c
. Given a noise multiplier
\sigma
and a batch of synthetic samples of size
s
, we add Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma c\sqrt{s})
to
\sum_{i}sim(d,D_{priv,i})
, i.e., the sum of similarity between the synthetic document and all private documents. Finally, we obtain the average similarity scores across all private documents. We provide the pseudocode of this procedure in
Figure 2
.
The privacy analysis follows the Private Evolution framework
(
30
)
, which is reproduced in §
D

Mitigating reward hacking.

Although optimizing for
R_{sim}
can lead to synthetic documents similar to the private corpus defined by
sim
, relying only on
R_{sim}
as the sole reward can lead to reward hacking
(
2
;
37
)
. Unlike prior RFT methods which focus on math and coding problems
(
9
;
49
)
where a verifiable reward (e.g. presence of the gold answer string) can be defined, generating documents that are similar to a set of documents is relatively open-ended and unconstrained. Moreover, text embedding models are known to have various biases
(
8
;
15
)
and could assign high similarity scores to dissimilar documents, especially when embedding
long
documents. Thus, in addition to
R_{sim}
, we employ a reward
R_{prompt}
that measures adherence to the input prompt to prevent reward hacking.
1
1

1

While RL algorithms such as PPO includes a KL penalty term to reduce divergence from the reference model, we find that increasing KL penalty does not resolve the issue in our preliminary experiments. Instead, it discourages the model from learning to generate samples closer to the private distribution.
We implement
R_{prompt}
as a combination of LLM-as-a-judge and rule-based checks (§
4
).

To combine the two rewards, we use a threshold approach. We set the reward to 0 if
R_{prompt}
is less than a threshold
\tau
and otherwise return
R_{sim}
. The final reward is defined as:

R=\begin{cases}R_{\text{sim}}&\text{if }R_{\text{prompt}}>\tau\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We train DP-RFT using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO,
43
).

5.1
Downstream evaluation

Table 1
reports the results on downstream performance for GPT-2 and BERT
Small
.

DP-RFT outperforms Aug-PE across datasets and privacy budgets.

Across all datasets and privacy budgets, DP-RFT consistently achieves higher downstream accuracy than Aug-PE on both GPT-2 and BERT
Small
. The gains are particularly pronounced for BERT
Small
, with up to 22%, 35%, 2%, and 47% relative improvement on PubMed, BBC, WildChat, and QMSum, respectively, and are also substantial for GPT-2, reaching up to 10%, 4%, 4%, and 2% on these datasets. These improvements indicate that DP-RFT is able to effectively shift the distribution of the LLM compared to Aug-PE, which iteratively prompts a frozen LLM. In addition, DP-RFT consistently outperforms the un-finetuned QWEN baseline (except for BBC at
\epsilon=2
, where performance is comparable), further demonstrating the benefits of training
M_{\text{gen}}
.

DP-RFT outperforms DP-FT under tight privacy budgets and low-data regimes for BERT
Small
.

While DP-FT operates under a looser privacy constraint (i.e., violating the
eyes-off
setting), its performance for BERT
Small
degrades substantially as the privacy budget decreases, underperforming DP-RFT across all datasets when
\epsilon\neq\infty
. In contrast, for GPT-2, DP-FT consistently outperforms DP-RFT across privacy budgets and datasets. We attribute this difference to the fact that BERT
Small
is converted into a causal language model during fine-tuning, which differs from its pre-training setup. To further investigate this effect, we scale up the amount of private data to the full dataset in §
G
, observing improved downstream performance for DP-FT. Yet, DP-RFT continues to outperform DP-FT for BERT
Small
in low-data regimes (e.g., QMSum with only 700 examples) or under tight privacy budgets, highlighting its practical advantages in realistic privacy-constrained settings.

5.2
Similarity evaluation

Table 2
reports similarity metrics between synthetic and private data. Overall, DP-RFT exhibits higher similarity to private documents than the unfine-tuned QWEN across both instance-level metrics (mean and maximum similarity) and distributional measures (FID), indicating that DP-RFT more effectively captures the underlying data distribution. In addition, DP-RFT outperforms Aug-PE for instance-level metrics in most settings, with the advantage becoming more pronounced under tighter privacy budgets.

We observe dataset-dependent trends in similarity performance. For example, with
\epsilon=\infty
, DP-RFT and Aug-PE achieve comparable embedding similarity on PubMed, whereas DP-RFT yields approximately 20% higher similarity on the other three datasets. We attribute this difference to PubMed being closer to the distribution of the backbone generator
M_{\text{gen}}
, making it easier to steer via prompting. To quantify this, we measure the distance between the target distribution and
M_{\text{gen}}
using perplexity of the private data under
M_{\text{gen}}
, finding that PubMed has lower perplexity (6.75) than BBC (9.11) and QMSum (10.14). Although WildChat also exhibits low perplexity (4.04) as it contains model-generated texts, its long-form and structured nature makes it more difficult to steer through iterative prompting. We note that Aug-PE achieves lower FiD on PubMed and WildChat, which may be attributed to the fact that Aug-PE explicitly optimizes FiD and can therefore perform better on distributions that are more familiar to the base model.

To complement embedding similarity, we conduct an LLM-as-a-judge pairwise similarity evaluation against private document on use of word and tone, finding that DP-RFT is consistently preferred over Aug-PE across all datasets. We describe the setting and results in details in §
H
in the Appendix.

6.1
Qualitative Analysis on the Synthetic Examples

To understand the effect of DP-RFT training and the properties the generated synthetic data, we analyze the distributional similarity between synthetic vs. real on private WildChat conversations.

Lexical Similarity.

We analyze the distribution of Jaccard similarity between each synthetic example and its
most
similar private example, as shown in
Figure 3
. Across privacy budgets, examples generated by DP-RFT exhibit higher lexical overlap with the private data than those produced by Aug-PE. In contrast, the distribution of examples from Aug-PE is more spread out, potentially indicating that Aug-PE outputs are more diverse, despite lower fidelity in terms of lexical similarity. This observation is consistent with the FiD results in §
5.2
, where Aug-PE achieves lower FiD despite worse average embedding similarity. Incorporating more explicit diversity control into DP-RFT is a promising direction for future work.

Structural Similarity.

For structured data, both content and structural similarity of synthetic examples are important.
Figure 3
compares the per-turn word-count distributions of synthetic and real WildChat conversations across privacy budgets. Overall, synthetic data generated by DP-RFT more closely matches the private distribution than Aug-PE across all privacy settings. Interestingly, however, Aug-PE better captures the bi-modal structure of the distribution, reflecting the mixture of short user turns and longer assistant responses. One possible explanation is that Aug-PE employs a generate-then-select strategy over a large candidate set at each iteration
(
51
)
, whereas DP-RFT optimizes over smaller mini-batches of rollouts. Future work can examine the effect of rollout batch size in DP-RFT training more closely.

6.3
Computational Overhead.

Computational cost is an important consideration for practical deployment of synthetic data generation methods. We benchmark the wall-clock GPU hours required by DP-RFT and Aug-PE under our WildChat experimental setting. DP-RFT consists of two phases:
(1) Training
, where 200 optimization steps require approximately 5 hours on 8 A100 GPUs (40 GPU hours total), and
(2) Inference
, where generating 2,000 samples requires roughly 1 A100 GPU hour using vLLM
(
25
)
. In contrast, Aug-PE does not involve model training but incurs substantial cost during iterative generation: producing 2,000 samples per iteration requires approximately 10 GPU hours, resulting in a total of 100 GPU hours over 10 iterations
2
2

2

We use the official implementation at
https://github.com/microsoft/DPSDA
.
. While Aug-PE avoids a training phase, its iterative procedure is computationally expensive, particularly for long-form text generation. In comparison, the training cost of DP-RFT is a one-time expense, after which synthetic data can be generated efficiently.

7
Conclusion

We introduce DP-RFT, a reinforcement learning method for training language models to generate synthetic data aligned with a private corpus
without
ingesting individual private examples. The key idea of our method is to steer the model with reinforcement learning guided by a similarity measure with the private document as reward, while respecting the eyes-off constraint. Through extensive experiments on four datasets spanning diverse domains and structures, we show that DP-RFT consistently improves both fidelity and downstream utility over prior eyes-off baselines such as Aug-PE, and narrows the gap with DP finetuning methods under tight privacy budgets.

Impact Statement

This work advances machine learning by exploring a reinforcement learning–based approach to differentially private synthetic data generation without eyes-on access to private examples. By using DP-protected similarity signals as training feedback, DP-RFT enables large language models to generate synthetic texts better aligned with private data distributions while respecting formal privacy constraints. This reduces the need for direct exposure to sensitive data and may help lower privacy risks in applications where data sharing is restricted. More broadly, our results suggest a path toward more practical and responsible use of LLMs in privacy-sensitive domains.

Appendix D
Privacy Analysis

The privacy analysis of DP-RFT follows the Private Evolution framework
(
30
)
, with modifications only in the sensitivity analysis of the mechanism.

Firstly, we analyze the privacy cost of the procedure in Figure
2
. Each private sample contributes a vector of size
s
. By the clipping operation, each entry of the vector has absolute value at most
c
, so its
\ell_{2}
norm is bounded by
c\sqrt{s}
. Consequently, adding or removing a single private sample changes
R_{\text{sim}}
by at most
c\sqrt{s}
in
\ell_{2}
norm. Adding i.i.d. Gaussian noise with standard deviation
\sigma c\sqrt{s}
then corresponds to the standard Gaussian mechanism
(
14
)
with noise multiplier
\sigma
.

The overall DP-RFT algorithm performs
T
iterations of Figure
2
, which can be viewed as
T
adaptive compositions of the Gaussian mechanism. By Corollary 3.3 in
11
,
3
3

3

See the arXiv version:
https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02383
.
this is equivalent to a single Gaussian mechanism with effective noise multiplier
\sigma/\sqrt{T}
. Therefore, we can apply the tight bounds for Gaussian mechanisms from
3
to analyze the privacy cost. In particular, given any two of
\epsilon
,
\delta
, and
\sigma/\sqrt{T}
, their analysis allows us to solve for the third. For example, for a target privacy budget
(\epsilon,\delta)
, one can compute the required noise multiplier
\sigma/\sqrt{T}
, which in turn specifies the amount of noise
\sigma c\sqrt{s}
to add to
R_{\text{sim}}
.
