The effects of invasive treatment on patients with frailty and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Su-Tao Hu, Yu-Kun Zhang, Tian-Shu Gu, Jing-Kun Zhang, Xue Wu, Xing Liu, Xian Shao, Lin Wang, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Tong Liu, Kang-Yin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The effect of invasive treatment in patients with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) with associated frailty remains undefined. This retrospective cohort study aimed to compare the outcomes of invasive treatment with conservative management in this specific patient population. We used the Tianjin Health and Medical Big Data Superplatform, with data from 82 hospitals in Tianjin, China, from January 2010 to March 2024. Patients with NSTEMI were included and divided into a non-frailty group (Hospital Frailty Risk Score [HFRS] < 5) and a frailty group (HFRS ≥ 5). Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to balance the covariates, and invasive treatment was compared with conservative management. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiac events (MACE), defined as the composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and recurrent myocardial infarction (MI) within 3 years. There were 73,823 NSTEMI patients (median age 66; 32.6% female) included in the analysis: 26,522 patients were in the frailty group, and 47,301 patients were in the non-frailty group. Among NSTEMI patients with frailty, 14,414 patients (54.3%) underwent invasive treatment. Following PSM yielding 6,040 well-matched pairs, frail patients undergoing invasive treatment had a lower risk of recurrent MI (subdistribution hazard ratio [sHR], 0.681; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.614–0.754; P < 0.001), all-cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.447; 95% CI 0.410–0.488; P < 0.001) and MACE (aHR, 0.516; 95% CI 0.481–0.553; P < 0.001), compared to conservative management. Similar results for invasive treatment compared to conservative management were observed in NSTEMI patients without frailty. Regardless of whether patients had associated frailty or not, invasive treatment was related to a decreased risk of recurrent MI and all-cause mortality in NSTEMI patients, when compared to conservative management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25294-9.

Introduction

Frailty is a complex geriatric syndrome defined by diminished physiological reserves and an increased susceptibility to a range of external and internal stressors1. This multifaceted condition reflects a state of reduced resilience, often leading to a cascade of health complications and a decline in functional independence. Cardiovascular diseases remain a leading health burden worldwide, with myocardial infarction (MI) and its complications, such as heart failure and shock, posing a significant threat to patients’ lives and health2. Furthermore, frailty is a risk factor for MI in patients without coronary artery disease (CAD) and is associated with poor prognosis in patients who have already suffered a MI3–9.

Current guidelines recommend an invasive treatment strategy for patients with Non-ST-segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI)10,11; However, there is a paucity of evidence-based management specifically for NSTEMI patients with frailty. Previous studies on the management of NSTEMI patients with frailty have primarily focused on the elderly population, with inconsistent results12–15. Hence, the optimal management of frail patients with NSTEMI remains unknown.

This study aimed to compare the long-term outcomes of invasive treatment with those of conservative medical treatment in patients with NSTEMI complicated by frailty, given the limited evidence for the management of this specific population.

Methods

Data source and participants

The data for this retrospective cohort study were obtained from the Tianjin Health and Medical Big Data Superplatform, comprising a retrospective cohort of patients admitted to and discharged from 82 hospitals in Tianjin, China, from January 2010 to March 2024. Based on this big data platform, treatment information of all patients, such as electronic medical records (EMR), hospital information system (HIS), laboratory information system (LIS), and picture archiving and communication system (PACS) from each hospital were aggregated. Using International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, we included patients who were diagnosed for the first time with NSTEMI (ICD-10 codes I21.40). Endpoints

The primary endpoint was Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE), defined as the composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and recurrent MI (ICD-10 codes I21-I22) within 3 years. The secondary endpoints consisted of the individual components of the primary endpoint and Major bleeding (Supplemental Table 4). The information of all-cause mortality was from Tianjin CDC. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables that are normally distributed were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test. For continuous variables exhibiting a non-normal distribution, data were depicted as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, and group comparisons were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A different statistical modelling technique was used for each outcome. For all-cause mortality and MACE, we used Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier analysis, the strength of associations were expressed as hazard ratios. For recurrent MI and Major bleeding, we used Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models that accounted for the competing risk of death and expressed associations as subdistribution hazard ratios. Additionally, we employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four piecewise points to explore potential nonlinear relationships between the HFRS and the outcomes.

To account for potential confounders, the propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. The covariates adjusted in PSM included age, sex, Killip class, HFRS, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia and smoking), medications at discharge (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, statins, beta blocker and angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blockers [ACEI/ARB]) and laboratory tests (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], triglycerides [TG], hemoglobin concentration [HB], creatinine [Cr] and uric acid [UA]).

Patients in the invasive treatment group were matched 1:1 with those in the conservative treatment group based on their propensity scores using the nearest available pair matching method with a caliper width of 0.01. Multiple imputation by chained reaction was performed to account for missing values to impute variables with missing values (TG, LDL-c, HDL-c, HB, UA, Cr) using the following predictors: age, sex, Killip class, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia and smoke) and medications at discharge (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, statins, beta blocker and ACEI/ARB).

Subgroup analyses of endpoints were conducted based on important characteristics, including age (< 65, 65–85, ≥ 85), KILLIP class (≤ II, > II), sex (male or female), hypertension, diabetes, ischemic stroke, multimorbidity (defined as existence of two or more chronic diseases) and polypharmacy (defined as receiving five or more medications). We also performed mediation analyses to explore the effect of some guideline-directed discharge medications on the association between the invasive treatment and the clinical outcomes. To assess the robustness of our primary findings and to utilize the entire cohort, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). The propensity scores derived from the same logistic model used in the PSM analysis were utilized to calculate the inverse probability weights. Finally, we further investigated the impact of the timing of invasive treatment (within 24 h of admission, 24 to 72 h after admission, and after 72 h of admission) on the prognosis in PSM patients. For all statistical analyses, a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were processed using R version 4.0.4 software.

Results

Patient enrollment

From January 2010 to March 2024, a total of 84,775 NSTEMI patients were identified. After applying the exclusion criteria, 73,823 patients were included in the analysis. Among these, 26,522 patients (35.9%) were classified into the frailty group, while 47,301 were in the non-frailty group (64.1%). In the frailty group, 14,414 patients (54.3%%) underwent invasive treatment, while in the non-frailty group, 36,641 (77.5%) underwent invasive treatment. Baseline characteristics

In patients with NSTEMI, frail patients were older (median age 70 vs. 64), had a lower proportion of males (61.2% vs. 70.9%), a higher proportion with KILLIP class 3/4 (23.4% vs. 9.4%), a lower proportion of receiving invasive treatment (54.3% vs. 77.5%), and a greater proportion of comorbidities compared to non-frail patients (Supplementary Table 5). Baseline characteristics of NSTEMI patients with frailty were presented in Table 1. Compared to conservative group, the invasive group were younger (median age 67 vs. 75), and had a higher proportion of males (68.2% vs. 52.8%), and KILLIP class 1/2 (83.8% vs. 68.1%). The invasive group had a higher proportions of patients discharged with medications such as aspirin, ticagrelor, beta blockers, ACEI/ARB and statins, while the proportion of patients discharged with clopidogrel and oral anticoagulants was lower. The invasive group had fewer comorbidities except dyslipidemia, smoke history, and previous PCI. Patients in the invasive group had higher TG, HB and LDL-C levels compared to the conservative group, while Cr and UA levels were lower. After PSM, the baseline characteristics of the two groups were balanced (Table 2). In NSTEMI patients without frailty, the baseline characteristics between invasive and conservative group were comparable to those of NSTEMI patients with frailty (Supplemental Table 6 and Supplemental Table 7).

Comparison of NSTEMI patients with frailty and those without frailty

After adjusting for gender, age, previous medical histories (including hypertension, diabetes, ischemic stroke and previous PCI), smoking and LDL-C, there were higher risks of all-cause mortality (18.4% vs. 6.4%; adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 2.053; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.955–2.157; P < 0.001), Major bleeding (7.9% vs. 5.6%; subdistribution hazard ratio [sHR], 1.202; 95%CI 1.128–1.282; P < 0.001) and MACE (26.7% vs. 15.6%; aHR, 1.474; 95%CI 1.422–1.528) in the frailty group (Table 3).

Mediation analyses

In mediation analyses, we found that discharged with ticagrelor partly mediated the association between invasive treatment and MACE in frail and non-frail patients, accounting for 4.1% or 7.1% of the proportion mediated, respectively. (Supplemental Table 8 and Supplemental Table 9).

Sensitivity analyses

Among NSTEMI patients with frailty, after applying IPTW, the baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups (Supplemental Table 10). It was observed that the invasive treatment was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality, recurrent MI, and MACE (Supplemental Table 11). Consistent results were observed in NSTEMI patients without frailty (Supplemental Table 12 and Supplemental Table 13).

The effect of timing of invasive treatment on the prognosis in PSM patients

Among PSM patients with NSTEMI and frailty, compared with those who underwent invasive management within 24 h after admission (Supplemental Table 14), individuals receiving invasive therapy between 24 and 72 h showed no difference in prognosis (aHR, 0.862; 95%CI 0.725–1.023), whereas those who had the invasive treatment more than 72 h after admission faced an increased risk of MACE (aHR, 1.192; 95%CI 1.046–1.358). The conservative-management group had the worst outcome (aHR, 1.983; 95%CI 1.824–2.156). In the non-frail cohort, the timing of invasive therapy had little influence on prognosis and the conservative group had the worst outcome (Supplemental Table 15).

Discussion

In this study which evaluated the impact of invasive treatment on the prognosis of NSTEMI patients with and without frailty, our principal findings are as follows: (i) frailty was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and Major bleeding in patients with NSTEMI; (ii) among NSTEMI patients, those with frailty were less likely to undergo invasive treatment compared to non-frail patients; and (iii) invasive treatment significantly reduced the risk of all-cause mortality and recurrent MI in NSTEMI patients, regardless of the presence of frailty.

Consistent with previous studies6,8,9,20–25, our results showed that frailty significantly increased the risk of all-cause mortality and Major bleeding in patients with MI. The poor prognosis in frail patients could be attributed to a higher prevalence of comorbidities, a higher rate of sarcopenia, reduced physiological reserves, and a diminished potential for recovery26. Furthermore, frailty has been associated with high plaque vulnerability27. Therefore, when clinically assessing the prognosis of patients with MI, it is necessary to consider frailty as a risk factor and to improve the management of these patients7,28. It was noteworthy that the RCS analysis indicated that when the HFRS exceeded 9.0, the risk of recurrent MI decreased with the increase in HFRS. A recent study documented similar findings29, showing that patients with high frailty risk had a lower 5-year risk of acute myocardial infarction (10.7% VS. 13.2%) compared to those with intermediate frailty risk. A possible reason for this was that patients with high frailty risk had a significantly increased all-cause mortality rate (88.2%)29, which created a competing risk for MI events. Our study accounted for the competing risk of death when assessing recurrent MI as an endpoint, and the result indicated no significant difference between frail and non-frail group.

We found that a lower proportion of frail patients underwent invasive treatment compared with the non-frail patients, aligned with previous studies16,30. These frail patients, who had a higher burden of comorbidities and more severe conditions, prompted physicians to exercise caution when considering invasive treatment. Indeed, frailty is often associated with multimorbidity and health inequalities31. In clinical practice, frailty has increasingly been recognized as a crucial factor for perioperative assessment in patients undergoing invasive surgery and for determining cardiovascular outcomes32,33. Compared with non-frail patients, frailty was associated with longer hospital stays and higher hospital costs, particularly following invasive treatment16,34,35. Considering the patients’ medical conditions, surgical risks, and hospitalization costs, physicians were more likely to favor conservative treatment approaches.

The optimal treatment strategy for patients with NSTEMI and frailty remains unclear. Previous studies have predominantly concentrated on the elderly population exhibiting frailty, with some investigations revealing that invasive treatment was associated with improved outcomes in elderly patients with MI and frailty13,36,37. Our study uncovered similar findings, indicating that invasive treatment was related to a lower risk of MACE in NSTEMI patients with frailty. The association was observed consistently across both elderly and non-elderly patient subgroups. However, other studies have reported contrasting findings, indicating no difference in outcomes between invasive and conservative groups in elderly NSTEMI patients with frailty15,38. The discrepancies in these findings may be partly attributed to the varying approaches employed to evaluate frailty. Due to data limitations, we used the HFRS to assess frailty, whereas the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) was predominantly utilized in most other studies. The HFRS was an administrative tool derived from ICD-10 diagnostic codes within a patient’s electronic health records. And the CFS was a bedside assessment tool that required clinical judgment to rank patients on a scale from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill). The advantage of using administrative hospital data to identify frailty risk was that it could be calculated using routine data for all patients in hospital and removes the need to apply a manual score39. Although scores such as the CFS could be quick and simple to calculate, they had inter-operator reliability issues and imposed a substantial implementation burden17. The limitation of HFRS was that variation in documentation and coding of diagnoses could contribute to measurement error. For example, routine diagnosis and documentation of conditions such as delirium vary between physicians, departments, and hospitals40. Nonetheless, previous research has confirmed that the HFRS was an effective tool for stratifying frailty and predicting adverse outcomes16,41. Moreover, limitations in sample size and the confounding factors inherent to registries may also contribute to inconsistencies in results across different studies15. Hence, larger, multicenter, randomized clinical trials would be necessary to further investigate the impact of invasive interventions on prognosis of NSTEMI patients with frailty.

Previous guideline recommended an early invasive strategy within 24 h in NSTEMI patients with a Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score greater than 14010. Recently, a study reported that the GRACE tool overestimated the 12-month mortality risk in older patients with MI, and the frailty was an independent predictor of mortality and added significant discrimination to the GRACE 12-month mortality estimate7. Our findings suggested an invasive therapy within 72 h of hospitalization in frail NSTEMI patients, which required further research for validation.

We restricted our study population to patients with NSTEMI rather than including the full spectrum of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), which also encompasses unstable angina (UA) and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) considering the well-established differences in pathophysiology, risk stratification, and clinical management guidelines among these three entities. NSTEMI is specifically characterized by elevated biomarkers in the absence of persistent ST-segment elevation, representing an intermediate level of ischemic risk. By focusing on a relatively homogeneous NSTEMI cohort, we aimed to reduce clinical heterogeneity and enhance the internal validity of our comparisons. Future studies are warranted to explore the impact of invasive treatment in frail populations with UA or STEMI.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective study, although we had attempted to mitigate through PSM, multivariable Cox regression and IPTW to enhance the credibility of our results, the possibility of residual selection bias and unmeasured confounders could not be completely ruled out. Second, due to the data limitations, we were unable to obtain information on the results of coronary angiography during hospitalization and follow-up periods. Consequently, this study did not assess the characteristics of coronary artery lesions in patients with NSTEMI complicated by frailty. Third, The absence of GRACE and TIMI scores precluded further exploration of how frailty might modify the interaction between these established risk scores and adverse outcomes. Whether the impact of invasive treatment on the prognosis of frail patients varied across different risk score subgroups remained unclear. Finally, Consistent with previous large-scale database study29, we used the ICD-10 code (I21.4) to define NSTEMI, which potentially included a subset of patients with type 2 myocardial infarction. These patients typically had a poorer prognosis and were less likely to benefit from revascularization, which might have introduced confounding bias into our results.

Conclusion

Frailty is identified as a risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with NSTEMI. Furthermore, invasive treatment was associated with a reduced risk of recurrent MI and all-cause mortality in NSTEMI patients, regardless of the presence of frailty. Therefore, it is recommended that invasive treatment be considered for NSTEMI patients with frailty.
