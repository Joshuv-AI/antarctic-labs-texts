Determinants of hospital length of stay in older adults after hip fracture: a prospective cohort study
Authors: José Roberto Faria Junior, Luana Letícia Capato, Melise Jacon Peres Ueno, Vitor Roberto Sanchez Teixeira, Vinícius Palma Bois, Gustavo Henrique Pelinson, Álvaro Sundin Foltran, Carmelinda Ruggiero, Daniela Cristina Carvalho de Abreu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate factors associated with hospitalization length in older adults after hip fracture surgery.DesignProspective observational cohort study. A total of 172 older adults (mean age 79.01 ± 8.66 years; 70.30% women) hospitalized for fragility hip fractures. Data were collected within the first 24 hours post-surgery. Primary outcome measuresHospitalization length (in days). Factors investigated for the association with length of stay included sociodemographic, surgical, and laboratory variables, as well as multimorbidity, polypharmacy, self report of pain, cognitive, emotional, nutritional, and functional status. A generalized linear model adjusted for age and sex was used, with a significance level of 5% (p ≤ 0.05). ResultsThe mean hospitalization length was 5.4 days. Cognitive impairment was observed in 86.9% of participants, depressive symptoms in 63.6%, and 68.8% were at risk of malnutrition. Mean handgrip strength was 18.48 kg. Generalized linear model analysis indicated that a longer interval (in days) from fracture to surgery, delayed surgery, higher ferritin and urea levels, lower Cumulated Ambulation Score (CAS). and lower handgrip strength were associated with longer hospitalization (p < 0.05). ConclusionsA longer fracture-to-surgery interval (including delayed surgery), higher urea and ferritin levels, and poorer early functional status (lower CAS and reduced handgrip strength) were independently associated with a longer hospital stay among older adults with hip fracture. These findings support hospital strategies to minimize surgical delays, monitor renal-related and inflammatory markers, and incorporate early functional assessments to identify patients at risk of prolonged hospitalization. Addressing system-level contributors to surgical delay (e.g., access barriers, transportation, bed availability) may also improve the continuum of care.Keywords: Biochemical markers; fragility fracture, Multidimensional assessment, Hospitalization duration

Introduction

As the population ages, the risk of frailty and chronic diseases increases, such as osteoporosis, particularly in community-dwelling older women. These conditions further compromise bone and muscle integrity, making older adults more susceptible and vulnerable to fragility fractures. Among fragility fractures, hip fractures are the most serious, leading to prolonged hospitalizations, secondary complications, and longer rehabilitation periods, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life [1]. Approximately 90% of proximal femoral fractures occur due to falls [2]. Hip fractures in older adults are associated with significant functional decline, increased permanent physical disability and higher mortality rates [3].

Studies have shown that the length of hospitalization is associated with clinical complications and negative outcomes, such as increased insulin resistance, impaired gastrointestinal function, deconditioning of the cardiovascular, respiratory and musculoskeletal systems, a greater predisposition to thromboembolism, and 30-day mortality after discharge [4]. Reducing hospitalization time has become an important goal in hospital management, particularly in the context of a growing older population and the need to decrease costs. Therefore, it is crucial to understand which factors at hospital admission or in the first hours after hip fracture surgery can predict the length of hospitalization and, consequently, guide health strategies to minimize prolonged hospitalization [5].

Evidence from the literature indicates that being female, severe postoperative pain, multiple fractures, history of stroke, presence of osteoarthritis, cognitive deficits, delay to surgery greater than 48 h, and limited early mobilization are associated with longer hospitalization in older adults with hip fracture [6, 7]. Furthermore, advanced age, multimorbidity, cognitive impairment, postoperative complications, and delayed surgery have been identified as predictors of prolonged hospitalization after hip fracture, often defined as more than 11 days [6]. However, the reported length of stay varies considerably across contexts: in the United States, hospital stays typically range from 4 to 8 days [8], whereas in China they remain prolonged, averaging between 11 and 12 days [9]. Such heterogeneity reflects differences in healthcare systems, population characteristics, and clinical pathways, underscoring important knowledge gaps, particularly in low- and middle-income countries such as Brazil, where systemic barriers and resource limitations may contribute to distinct determinants of hospitalization length.

Despite the growing body of international research on predictors of hospitalization length after hip fracture, most studies have been conducted in high-income countries and focus primarily on mortality and postoperative complications. Few investigations have addressed functional, biochemical, and mobility-related factors, and there is a lack of evidence from middle-income settings such as Brazil, where systemic barriers and resource constraints may distinctly influence hospitalization outcomes.

Thus, the objective of this study was to verify the profile of older adults who suffered fragility hip fractures and to examine whether clinical, surgical, biochemical, pre-fracture mobility, basic post-fracture mobility, and overall muscle strength were associated with the length of hospitalization.

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study involving adults aged 60 years and older, of both sexes, admitted for fragility hip fractures at a public hospital in Ribeirão Preto, São Paulo, Brazil, which serves as a referral hospital for hip fracture surgery [10]. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 60 years and having independent ambulation prior to the fracture with or without the use of assistive devices. Exclusion criteria were: previous hip fractures, femoral shaft fractures, open fractures, pathological fractures, polytrauma, malignancies, imminent death from terminal illness, non-surgical treatment due to high surgical risk, and clinical diagnosis of advanced dementia.

Assessments were performed within the first 24 h after hip fracture surgery, with follow-up until hospital discharge. Data for sample characterization, i.e., age, sex, weight, height, cause of fracture, clinical variables (time from fracture to surgery, time from hospital admission to surgery, length of hospital stay), surgical variables (type of fracture, type of surgical procedure and type of anesthesia) and laboratory variables (serum concentration of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, albumin, ferritin, folic acid, calcium and urea) were obtained from medical records.

Health information was collected through questionnaires, including the 10-Point Cognitive Screening for screening for cognitive deficit (cut-off < 8 points), based on years of education [11]; the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), with a cut-off score of ≥ 5, used to determine the presence of depressive symptoms in older adults [12]; self-perception of health assessed with a semi-structured questionnaire [13]; the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), which classifies as well-nourished (24–30 points), at risk of malnutrition (17–23.5 points), or malnourished (< 17 points) [14], and pre-fracture mobility assessed using the New Mobility Score (NMS), with a total score ranging from 0 (unable to walk independently) to 9 (completely independent).

Physical assessment included basic mobility after surgery, assessed using the Cumulated Ambulation Score (CAS), which evaluates independence in three activities, bed transfer, chair transfer, and walking in a confined space, each scored from 0 to 2, with a total score ranging from 0 to 6 [15]. Pain was self-reported using the Numeric Pain Scale (NPS) [16].

Handgrip strength (HGS) was assessed using the Jamar handgrip dynamometer (Sammons Preston, Illinois), with the participant seated at the edge of the bed, using the dominant upper limb [17]. The test was repeated three times to obtain an average value. Considering that handgrip strength differs significantly between sexes [18], sample characterization data are presented by sex.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software, with a significance level of 5% (p ≤ 0.05). To identify factors associated with hospital length of stay (LOS, dependent variable) after hip fracture, we fitted a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with a Gamma distribution and log link. Clinical, surgical, laboratory, health, and physical variables were examined in separate generalized linear models, each adjusted for age and sex. Time-to-surgery variables were analyzed in separate models to avoid conceptual overlap between related time intervals. This specification was chosen to accommodate the strictly positive, right-skewed distribution of hospitalization days and the non-normal error structure, providing a more robust approach to analysing LOS. Effect sizes were expressed as non-standardized Beta (B) coefficients. The study protocol was approved by the local Human Research Ethics Committee (CAAE: 45135021.9.0000.5378) and all participants signed the informed consent form to participate, in accordance with the Helsinki’s declaration procedures for human research studies.

Results

A total of 172 participants were evaluated, with a mean age of 79.01 (8.66) years, of whom 70.3% were women. All fractures (100%) resulted from falls from standing height, and 34.2% of participants had experienced a previous fall prior to the fracture-causing incident. The most common fracture types were intertrochanteric fractures (52.8%) and femoral neck fractures (41%). Regarding surgical management, 60.3% of the older adults underwent osteosynthesis and 39.7% underwent arthroplasty. Among those who received arthroplasty, 39.7% underwent partial arthroplasty.

The mean length of hospitalization was 5.43 (7.25) days. The mean time from fracture to surgery was 5.02 (5.62) days, and the mean time from hospital admission to surgery was 2.49 (2.94) days. Cognitive impairment was identified in 86.9% of participants. Depression screening revealed that 63.6% exhibited symptoms of depression, while 68.8% were classified as having inadequate nutritional status. The NMS was 6.93 (2.64) points, indicating mobility close to the threshold for high pre-fracture mobility. In contrast, the mean CAS was 0.98 (1.18) points, indicating very limited mobility within 24 h post- surgery. Finally, the mean HGS was 18.48 (7.93) kg, with males presenting a mean of 24.43 (3.50) kg and females 14.64 (8.90) kg, both below the reference values proposed by Cruz-Jentoft et al. [18] (Table 1). Discussion

The present study evaluated 172 older adults with hip fractures, with a mean age of 79.01 years, the majority of whom were females (70.3%). All fractures resulted from falls from standing height. Our results indicated a high prevalence of cognitive impairment, depressive symptoms, and nutritional deficits, with an average length of hospitalization of 5 days. The GLM analysis revealed that greater fracture-to-surgery interval, delayed surgery, higher ferritin and urea levels, lower CAS and lower handgrip strength were consistently associated with longer hospitalization.

The characteristics of our participants align with previous studies that also reported a high prevalence of females and an older mean age among populations with hip fractures [19, 20]. However, regarding fracture types, our results showed a greater proportion of peritrochanteric fractures (58%). In contrast, Hartog et al. [19] reported a lower proportion of peritrochanteric fractures in their cohort, suggesting that differences in fracture distribution may be influenced by population characteristics, recruitment settings, or regional epidemiological patterns.

Our results corroborate those of Ráfia et al. [21], who reported an average hospitalization time of 5 days, contrasting with Nikkel et al. [8] and Manosroi et al. [22] where the average was 8.1 (± 8.3) days. Previous studies indicated a prolonged hospitalization time in older males following hip fractures [23], justifying our decision to include sex as a confounding variable in our statistical models.

General health assessment indicated a high prevalence of mild depression symptoms, with over half of the participants (57.02%), while 6.61% presented severe symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous literature. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Heidari et al. [24] reported a high prevalence of depression among older adults with hip fracture, highlighting that depressive symptoms are common and can negatively affect functional recovery and rehabilitation outcomes. Regarding cognitive function, most participants (64.71%) had scores suggestive of a probable cognitive deficit, 22.25% showed possible deficits. Similarly, Smith et al. [25] demonstrated that cognitive impairment is frequent in this population and is associated with delayed recovery, reduced independence in activities of daily living, and increased risk of complications during hospitalization. Together, these studies support the clinical relevance of our findings and underscore the importance of identification and multidisciplinary management of both depressive symptoms and cognitive deficits in older adults with hip fractures to optimize recovery and reduce the burden of post-hospitalization disability. A plausible pathway is that depression (reduced motivation) and cognitive impairment (reduced understanding, memory, and executive control) hinder adherence to mobility advice and participation in physical rehabilitation, thereby worsening functional recovery. These findings underscore the need for early identification and multidisciplinary management of depressive symptoms and cognitive deficits to optimize outcomes after hip fracture.

Nutritional status revealed that the majority faced some degree of nutritional problem, with 57.6% at risk of malnutrition, and 11.2% classified as malnourished. These findings indicate a high prevalence of inadequate nutritional status in our cohort. Similarly, Sourdet et al. [26] highlighted malnutrition as a major contributor to preventable iatrogenic disability during hospitalization of older adults, reinforcing the clinical and public health implications of our results. In this context, our study supports the urgent need for public health policies that promote access to adequate nutrition for older adults, which may optimize recovery, and reduce the burden of disability associated with hospitalization.

Our study demonstrated that delayed surgery from hospital admission is strongly associated with longer hospital stays indicating that for each additional day of surgical delay corresponding to approximately 1.39 additional days of hospitalization. These findings are consistent with Manosroi et al. [22], who identified surgical delay as a strong associated with prolonged hospitalization. Similarly, surgery performed within 48 h was associated not only with reduced mortality and perioperative complications but also with a significantly shorter hospital stay [15]. In their retrospective study, Moja and colleagues reported a median length of stay of 12 days, with patients who underwent surgery within 48 h stayed a median of 10 days, whereas those who had surgery after 48 h remained hospitalized for 4 days longer. Among the latter group, surgical delay was the only factor associated with extended hospitalization, highlighting the critical importance of timely surgical intervention. The waiting time to surgery raises important questions regarding the effectiveness of care models adopted for older adults with hip fractures and organizational issues related to operating room availability. In line with Moja et al. [27], who demonstrated that surgery performed within 48 h is associated with better outcomes, our findings add that minimizing surgical delays is not only clinically relevant but also a key strategy to facilitate earlier discharge and reduce hospital burden. Our findings reinforce the importance of minimizing surgical delays as a key strategy to facilitate earlier discharge and improve overall outcomes.

Additionally, our results showed that a longer interval from fracture to surgery was associated with longer hospitalization time. In the literature, most evidence refers to the association between surgical delay after hospital admission and adverse outcomes, such as increased mortality [28], postoperative complications [29], and, in some cases, longer hospital stay. However, there is limited information on the impact of the time elapsed from fracture to surgery on length of hospital stay, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. Importantly, the fracture-to-surgery interval encompasses a pre-hospital period prior to admission, reflecting structural aspects of the Brazilian healthcare system. In the local care pathway, after the fracture, older adults are typically referred to an emergency care unit, where they undergo initial assessment and subsequently wait for the identification of which surrounding hospital has the capacity to admit the patient, including the availability of beds or operating rooms, before being transferred to the hospital where surgery will be performed.

Regarding biochemical markers, higher serum urea levels were associated with a longer hospital stay (B = 0.07, p ≤ 0.001), suggesting that this simple laboratory marker may reflect underlying clinical conditions contributing to prolonged hospitalization in older adults with hip fracture. Although the study by Chen et al. [30] explored the urea-to-albumin ratio as a factor associated with postoperative mortality and complications, their findings also suggest that alterations in nitrogen metabolism and inflammatory processes may be related to worse clinical outcomes. Although higher serum urea levels explained only about 10% of the variance in hospital length of stay, this unfavorable metabolic profile may partly reflect clinical conditions associated with prolonged hospitalization observed in our sample. Elevated serum urea may reflect increased protein catabolism, systemic inflammation, dehydration, or impaired renal function, conditions that are highly prevalent in older adults after hip fracture [31]. These metabolic disturbances can compromise recovery, delay wound healing, and increase the risk of complications, thereby contributing to longer hospital stays [31]. Therefore, serum urea may represent a potential clinical marker associated with hospital length of stay in older adults following hip fracture, although it should be interpreted alongside other clinical and functional factors identified in this study.

In addition, ferritin was significantly associated with hospital length of stay at the coefficient level. Serum ferritin is considered a dual biomarker of iron status and inflammation [31], and elevated levels may reflect an underlying inflammatory state that could be related to slower recovery after fracture. Moreover, ferritin is frequently elevated in conditions such as obesity, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and dyslipidemia, all of which are associated with chronic inflammation [32, 33]. These results may partly explain the observed association with longer hospitalization among older adults with poorer baseline health.

Our results indicated that greater handgrip strength is associated with reduced length of hospitalization, likely because muscle strength serves as a crucial protective factor against severe health declines [34] and adverse outcomes after hospitalization [35]. In the study by Guo et al. [36], involving older adults with a mean age of 61.6 years (55.6% females), higher handgrip strength was associated with a lower risk of hip fractures and falls over four years of follow-up. Moreover, baseline handgrip strength has been associated with both incident and persistent walking recovery, providing prognostic insight into functional trajectory after hip fracture [37]. In our study, the mean handgrip strength considering both sexes combined was 18.48 kg, which is lower than the 29,3 kg reported by Guo et al. [36], who categorized values into sex-specific quintiles. Therefore, direct comparison with our combined-sex mean is limited. When analyzed separately, mean handgrip strength was 24.43 kg for males and 14.64 kg for females, both still below the respective values reported [36]. This difference may be explained by the demographic profile, as our participants were significantly older (mean age 79 years) compared with 65.7 years in their study. Reduced global muscle strength, as measured by handgrip strength, has been identified as a predictor of falls [38], which is consistent with our findings that all fractures in our cohort resulted from falls from standing height.

Our results demonstrated that higher CAS in the early postoperative period was significantly associated with shorter hospitalization (B = -0.975, p ≤ 0.001). This finding aligns with previous studies highlighting CAS as a robust predictor of clinical outcomes after hip fracture. Foss et al. [15] demonstrated that lower CAS scores in the first postoperative days were associated with longer hospital stay, delayed discharge, higher mortality, and increased postoperative complications. Similarly, Kristensen and Kehlet [39] reported that basic mobility at hospital discharge, assessed using CAS, is an independent predictor of mortality up to five years after surgery. The mean CAS in our cohort was low (0.98 points) within the first 24 h post-surgery, indicating severely limited mobility at this stage. Despite this, even small increments in CAS were associated with a measurable reduction in hospitalization days, supporting the clinical utility of early mobility assessment and targeted interventions. These results underscore the importance of systematic CAS monitoring and the implementation of individualized early mobilization strategies to enhance recovery, reduce hospital stay, and improve functional outcomes in older adults with hip fracture.

Our findings showed no significant associations between hospitalization length and variables such as fracture type, anesthesia, surgical access, multimorbidity, cognitive or emotional status, vitamin D, folic acid, calcium, or pre-fracture mobility. Although relevant to older adults’ health, these factors did not emerge as significant factors in this Brazilian hip fracture cohort. Surgical delay was the strongest factor associated with longer hospitalization, followed by lower CAS, a longer fracture-to-surgery interval, and weaker handgrip strength, whereas ferritin and urea contributed modestly. These results underscore the value of minimizing time to surgery and incorporating early functional and laboratory assessments at admission, while highlighting the need to optimize the care pathway from the moment of fracture through surgical admission. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Postoperative complications, which may have influenced length of hospitalization stay, were not recorded. In addition, the study did not include follow-up on readmissions, which could have provided further insights into discharge quality and the effectiveness of care transitions after hip fracture. Some laboratory variables were unavailable for all participants, as certain tests (including vitamin D, albumin, ferritin, folic acid, and calcium) are not routinely or mandatorily requested in the public hospital setting. This may have reduced statistical power for specific analyses. Furthermore, the cohort comprised only patients who were independently ambulatory prior to fracture and excluded individuals with advanced dementia. Although necessary to ensure the feasibility of functional assessments, this criterion may limit the generalizability of the findings to more frail and cognitively impaired populations.

Conclusion

Our study underscores three priorities for care of older adults with hip fracture: early surgical management, vigilant clinical and laboratory monitoring (particularly renal function), and timely postoperative mobilization, all of which were associated with hospitalization length. Beyond in-hospital processes, results emphasize improving the fracture-to-surgery pathway—shortening time to diagnosis, streamlining ambulance transport and transfers, addressing barriers within the public health system, and ensuring hospital capacity, including the availability of beds and operating rooms—to enable timely surgery as a quality-of-care indicator. Moreover, the association between lower muscle strength and longer stay reinforces the need for policies that broaden access to strength-focused exercise programs for older adults at the population level. Incorporating these elements into multidisciplinary care pathways may help identify vulnerable patients, streamline care, and, potentially, reduce complications while optimizing healthcare resources.
