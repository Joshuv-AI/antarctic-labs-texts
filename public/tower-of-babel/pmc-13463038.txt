External conditioning of data collection enhances the information content from wearable sensors
Authors: James Truslow, Shinichi Goto, Angela Spillane, Huiming Lin, Rahul C. Deo, Calum A. MacRae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart rate variability (HRV) has well-documented associations with cardiometabolic disease. However, HRV is also affected by a wide range of other factors. We hypothesized that the presence of specific passive context or external structured conditions to the collection of physiologic data might enhance the information content of these data. To test this, we analyzed HRV (specifically SDNN) collected during different contexts (“perturbations”) using data from the Apple Heart and Movement Study (AHMS). We measured HRV’s association with seven cardiometabolic diseases using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). We first examined the effect of two passive perturbations that controlled for circadian variation. We then explored the effect of an active perturbation, specifically a mindful-breathing session (mindful HRV). While the passive perturbations did not result in significant increases in information content for any cardiometabolic phenotypes, mindful HRV added discriminant information for all seven cardiometabolic diseases (difference in AUROC 0.026-0.180 compared with non-mindful HRV). After adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, the advantage of mindful HRV remained significant for five diseases. Active perturbations, enhanced the signal for cardiometabolic status and emphasize the utility of real-time triggers for adding information content to ambient recording using wearables. (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04198194, 2019/12/13, Apple Heart & Movement Study).

Introduction

In addition to extending conventional clinical monitoring to the home setting, wearable digital devices have the potential to extend precision health and accelerate precision medicine by enabling the discovery of novel quantitative phenotypes that identify early departures from health or stratify disease1. In unsupervised collection frameworks, the variable context (typically unrecorded) of sensor measurements often renders wearable data challenging to interpret, reducing the power to make rigorous inferences. Classic approaches to understanding complex systems exploit standardized perturbations and measured responses2–4. Integrated sensor and annotation ecosystems, such as Apple Watch and iPhone, may offer the ability to add to the information content of wearable platforms by combining robust data collection with triggered perturbations or the collection of other relevant metadata.

Heart rate variability5,6(HRV) is a physiologic metric that can be evaluated using data recorded from digital devices. In this study, we exclusively analyzed short-term HRV, specifically SDNN from 1-min recordings, to evaluate whether the information content of these brief measurements could be enhanced by conditioning the context of collection. HRV reflects autonomic nervous system function and is associated with various health and fitness states, such as exercise capacity, behavioral health, and cardiometabolic diseases, as well as the risk of sudden cardiac death6. Most studies associating high HRV with health have included modest numbers of patients, and the results have been inconclusive7–9. The most robust associations have reported lower HRV in patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM)10, which has been attributed to diabetic autonomic neuropathy, and heart failure (HF)11, where the mechanisms can be complex. These abnormalities of HRV are typically documented in later-stage clinical evaluation in the context of specific symptoms. Given that HRV is abnormal even in pre-diabetes, specific patterns of HRV might reflect a broader latent cardiometabolic phenotype. Modern wearable devices provide a unique opportunity to investigate HRV in much larger cohorts. More precise understanding of factors conditioning the collection of data from wearables and the exploitation of such external factors to refine the annotation of relevant data types, may broaden the utility of consumer wearables for health maintenance and the pre-medical detection of abnormalities.

HRV is affected by a wide variety of patient and extrinsic factors12, including circadian variation13, the patient’s activity or posture14, sleep status15, and mental factors16. However, it is unclear how these factors influence any signal for cardiometabolic conditions within HRV. In the Apple Heart and Movement study (AHMS)17, many of the potential conditioning factors are recorded by Apple Watch and can be incorporated into analyses of the associations between HRV and various disease entities. Furthermore, users can self-initiate or be prompted to undertake structured maneuvers with specific characteristics, such as a mindful-breathing exercise. We hypothesized that heart rate data collected under certain conditions, either passive or active, would increase the information content of the data types collected.

Results

Controlling for time of the day did not improve cardiometabolic disease signal

HRV displays substantial circadian variation, which, if not accounted for, can introduce noise into the HRV signal5,6,12. Therefore, controlling the time of the day at which HRV is sampled, as a proxy for components of circadian and diurnal entrainment, could reduce such noise and thus may improve the signal for specific disorders. To test this hypothesis, we compared the discrimination of cardiometabolic diseases using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for waking HRV collected at random time points (random HRV) with the AUROC for waking HRVs collected in the evening (evening HRV) when the HRV is empirically observed to be the lowest across the day (see the comparison between evening HRV and random HRV in the Methods section for details of the observation period).

A total of 84,594 participants were included in the analysis, with a mean of ~83 pairs of evening HRV and random HRV per person. Sleeping HRV did not improve cardiometabolic disease signal

The variation of HRV is characterized by a maximum during sleep and a minimum while awake. Given that HRV is a surrogate of autonomic nervous system activity, it is conceivable that those with healthy autonomic nervous systems would exhibit higher sleeping HRV than those with any autonomic abnormalities, which could result in a larger difference in HRV between participants with healthy and impaired autonomic nervous systems at time points with higher baseline HRV. Thus, we hypothesized that the association of HRV with cardiometabolic disease might be more robust when the normal baseline HRV is greatest, as empirically defined from observations within the cohort. To test this hypothesis, we compared the discrimination of cardiometabolic diseases using HRV collected during sleep (sleeping HRV) with those collected while awake (waking HRV).

A total of 83,492 participants were included in this analysis, with a mean of ~136 pairs of sleeping HRV and waking HRV per person. (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). The median sleeping HRV was 46.1 ms, and the median waking HRV was 35.8 ms. The mean intrasubject difference between sleeping HRV and waking HRV was 11.3 ms. We observed no improvement in the discrimination of cardiometabolic diseases by using sleeping HRV compared to waking HRV. Changes in AUROC using sleeping HRV compared to waking HRV were: −0.026 [−0.037, −0.016] for CAD; −0.051 [−0.057, −0.045] for DM; −0.037 [−0.048, −0.026] for ACS; −0.008 [−0.024, 0.008] for HF; −0.040 [−0.044, −0.037] for hypercholesteremia; −0.053 [−0.057, −0.050] for hypertension; and −0.011 [−0.028, 0.006] for PAD. Active perturbation improved the signal for cardiometabolic disease

Another strong factor affecting autonomic nervous activity is the participant’s physiologic and behavioral/mental state at the time of measurement. We hypothesized that variation in physiologic and behavioral/mental state within and across individuals introduces variation in HRV, and that actively controlling this status might improve the information content for any association with cardiometabolic disease. To test this hypothesis, we compared the discrimination of cardiometabolic diseases using HRV collected during brief mindful sessions (mindful HRV) with those collected at a similar time of the day but without mindfulness sessions (non-mindful HRV).

A total of 54,548 participants were included in the analysis (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3), with an average of ~16 pairs of mindful HRV and non-mindful HRV per person. The median mindful HRV was 58.0 ms, and the median non-mindful HRV was 33.2 ms. The mean intraindividual difference between mindful HRV and non-mindful HRV was 27.7 ms. The discrimination of cardiometabolic disease was significantly improved for all seven cardiometabolic diseases when using mindful HRV rather than non-mindful HRV. Improvements in AUROC with mindful HRV compared with non-mindful HRV were: 0.068 [0.055–0.082] for CAD; 0.029 [0.020–0.037] for DM; 0.053 [0.038–0.070] for ACS; 0.175 [0.059–0.319] for HF; 0.026 [0.022–0.031] for hypercholesteremia; 0.035 [0.030– 0.040] for hypertension; and 0.063 [0.037–0.092] for PAD. (Fig. 3). We report these AUROC values to quantify the relative signal strength of HRV in relation to disease under different collection conditions, rather than to establish clinical utility for disease detection; accordingly, the modest increments in AUROC observed for several conditions reflect enhanced information content accruing with simple perturbation, rather than readiness for clinical deployment. To understand how these differences in AUROCs might contribute to useful outputs, we performed analyses to assess the ability of mindful HRV to reclassify individuals with cardiovascular risk factors, along with decision curve analysis and comparison of positive predictive values. This analysis resulted in a net reclassification index (NRI) of 0.650 [0.600–0.701], 0.400 [0.326–0.468], 0.544 [0.466–0.615], 0.537 [0.382–0.673], 0.302 [0.271–0.332], 0.407 [0.376–0.436], and 0.630 [0.515–0.741], respectively. The incremental advantage of mindful HRV remained significant when the analysis was repeated in models adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, with the exception of ACS and hypercholesterolemia (Fig. 4). Decision curve analyses demonstrated greater net benefit for mindful HRV, compared to non-mindful HRV, across clinically relevant threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 4). Mindful HRV also yielded higher precision than non-mindful HRV for all seven diseases, while achieving equivalent recall. At a fixed recall of 60%, the improvement in precision with mindful HRV compared to non-mindful HRV was 0.0064 [0.0061, 0.0113] for CAD, 0.0113 [0.0045, 0.0179] for DM, 0.0048 [0.0023, 0.0072] for ACS, 0.0061 [0.0019, 0.0113] for HF, 0.0215 [0.0155, 0.0277] for hypercholesterolemia, 0.0288 [0.0224, 0.0352] for hypertension, and 0.0035 [0.0013, 0.0057] for PAD. Mindful HRV improves the discrimination of cardiometabolic diseases

To test the utility of our findings, we further analyzed the association of mindful HRV with cardiometabolic diseases by comparing the discrimination of logistic regression models trained using only participant demographic information (a “reduced model” using only age, sex, and BMI), with discrimination yielded by the addition of mindful HRV (a “full model” using age, sex, BMI, and mindful HRV). For all cardiometabolic diseases we observed a significant advantage of the full model compared to the reduced model, as measured by the improvement in AUROC (Fig. 5). Discussion

We show here that the information content for cardiometabolic conditions represented in HRV can be enhanced by triggering specific subject behaviors at the time of measurement. Passive control for circadian or diurnal variation through standardization of the time of heart rate collection, either to times corresponding to the lowest or highest HRV of the day, did not significantly improve the signal for cardiometabolic diseases. However, structured deep breathing through mindfulness sessions significantly improved the signal for all cardiometabolic diseases tested in HRV. Prior work has examined the associations of overall HRV metrics with a range of diseases in epidemiological cohorts. There are also limited studies suggesting that structured deep breathing protocols might improve the spectral resolution of HRV data and enable the identification of discrete patterns of HRV abnormality18. Our data demonstrate a robust association of HRV, when collected in the context of structured deep breathing, with disease states and suggest that the specific context may significantly increase the power of simple HRV metrics in predicting cardiometabolic disorders. The primary strengths of this work are the large sample size, the range of diseases described, and the participant-level matching of mindful and non-mindful measurements. While conventional short-term HRV measured under standardized laboratory conditions remains the established reference for autonomic assessment, our findings demonstrate that standardized, short-term HRV measurements collected via accessible wearable devices preserve robust associations with cardiometabolic disorders and other diseases. This approach bridges the granularity of controlled short-term assessment with the scalability and temporal density of free-living data collection, enabling longitudinal surveillance that is not feasible with episodic laboratory testing alone. The mindfulness session thus functions as a minimal, user-friendly perturbation that enables the collection of short-term, condition-controlled information within a real-world digital health framework

The standard deviation of NN intervals (SDNN), used in the current analysis, is a measure of the variation in time between each heartbeat6. It is known to reflect the activity of the autonomic nervous system19, which regulates many body functions, such as heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, and digestion. The association of HRV with cardiometabolic disease is best established for DM. While less widely appreciated, various reports have shown associations with other cardiometabolic diseases, such as myocardial infarction20, coronary artery diseases21, congestive heart failure22, and hypertension23, albeit in relatively small and selected patient cohorts. Here, we confirm these findings in a much larger and less selected population cohort, which includes a substantial proportion of healthy individuals. These findings suggest that it may be feasible to expand the utility of HRV to a broader set of conditions with relevant conditioning of collection.

HRV is influenced by numerous factors6,12, many of which are accessible within a time-stamped wearable-derived HRV dataset. We hypothesized that controlling for some of these factors during the measurement of HRV would increase signal and/or reduce noise, thus increasing the strength of any associations. We first focused on variation with time of day, in particular with times associated with high sympathetic tone and lower HRV or higher parasympathetic tone and higher HRV. Averaged over many individuals and many days, the lowest HRV is observed in the evening, and the highest is observed in the early morning in a window prior to the initiation of waking. Our dataset includes sensor measurements during the entire day-night period and incorporates annotations on the timing of participants’ bedtime and waking. Though we were able to empirically define these discrete low and high HRV windows, we did not observe any increase in the discriminatory power of HRV for cardiometabolic disorders using these selected collection windows. One potential explanation of these findings includes differential sampling, as the windows for recording HRV may only overlap minimally with the true highest or lowest HRV.

In contrast to the two essentially passive contexts we outline, when using HRV data collected while actively controlling breathing, we observed significant improvements in the signal for cardiometabolic diseases. While the difference in AUROC is modest, the perturbation with mindfulness sessions significantly improved the classification of disease status (NRI: 0.302–0.650). We note that NRI can overstate improvement and should be interpreted cautiously; the supporting analysis of positive predictive values and decision curve analyses jointly support the superior performance of mindful HRV relative to non-mindful HRV. These results suggest that a substantial number of participants were correctly reclassified by even a non-calibrated perturbation-related HRV, though there was only an incremental advance in AUROC. This improved signal/noise ratio may partly be explained by reduced noise from body motion, given that the participants were asked to hold still while being mindful or controlling their breathing. However, this contribution is unlikely to be substantial since sleeping should have had a similar effect. The observed improvement likely reflects, in large part, the relative standardization of measurement conditions—such as respiratory rate, tidal volume, posture, and physical activity—that are known to modulate HRV and are typically uncontrolled during passive or sleep recordings. Consequently, the impact of a mindfulness session is likely to derive primarily from reduced confounding by these factors rather than from any unique physiological effect of mindfulness per se. Controlled deep breathing may introduce non-physiological perturbations, including resonance between respiratory and blood pressure-related HRV oscillations, that could independently organize the measured waveform. There is also a significant increase in the magnitude of HR variation at extremes of intrathoracic pressure. Respiratory rhythm influences heart rate variability in multiple ways, even while resting. Central impulses, both circadian and diurnal, drive discrete levels of parasympathetic activation and sympathetic inhibition. Wide swings in intrathoracic pressure lead to phasic autonomic discharges, which normally couple discrete hemodynamic features, including venous return, contractility, and aortic transmural pressures, to modulate cardiac output. These maneuvers also accentuate the differences between peak sympathetic and peak parasympathetic nerve activation. The mental status of participants may also influence the background noise in HRV12, even if incompletely and for short periods of time, further conditioning the collection of HRV data. In native use, a single minute of triggered deep breathing and HR recording, even with unknown user compliance, resulted in increased information content for the cardiometabolic phenotypes we studied.

The impact of conditioned collection on homeostatic physiologic parameters is anticipated. Specific perturbations offer important insights into the response characteristics of closed-loop feedback systems, both biological and human-made2,4. From a systems biology perspective, observing the response to controlled perturbations reveals more about the state of the biological system and its reserve capacity than observation under basal conditions. Our findings demonstrate that wearable devices can operationalize this principle, when they are appropriately designed by embedding brief, structured perturbations into everyday use, thereby probing physiologic reserve without requiring specialized laboratory settings. The ability of discrete challenges to differentiate various subsets of abnormality or impending failure has not been fully explored for most human physiologic systems, though there are notable exceptions, including the exercise tolerance test and the glucose tolerance test. With the advent of wearable devices, in particular those with the capability for ‘at the wrist’ triggered conditioning, it is possible to imagine a wide range of diagnostic challenges, ranging from simple physiologic manoeuvers to standardized ingestions, designed to surveil and detect early deviations from normal or even the loss of physiologic reserve prior to any overt abnormalities. This ‘pre-medical’ niche may not only complement existing diagnostic and preventative paradigms but also can begin to define discrete trajectories from wellness to disease definition thresholds. Common diseases including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, atherosclerosis, and chronic kidney disease represent aggregates of numerous underlying etiologies with variable outcomes which discrete trajectories to diagnosis may reveal and thus improve the precision of diagnosis.

Several limitations of the study should be highlighted. First, disease status and medication use were obtained by self-report rather than from a confirmed diagnosis or prescription record. Disease severity or duration and drug dosages are not reported. The absence of active linkage to institutional medical records limits our ability to verify the accuracy of these data, and any misclassification of disease status may bias effect estimates toward the null. Second, we could not verify the extent to which participants adhered to the guided breathing prompts during mindful sessions. This uncertainty directly affects the interpretation of mindful HRV, as those who did not follow the protocol may have contributed additional noise to our analysis. However, since all analyses were self-controlled, any non-adherence would have biased our results toward the null rather than creating spurious associations. In addition, the known impact of deep breathing on the organization of HRV suggests that our observations are likely to represent conservative estimates of the impact of external perturbations on the utility of wearable signals. Third, our observational design did not allow us to determine whether the enhanced mindful HRV signal reflects intrinsic physiological regulation or externally imposed modulation by the guided breathing protocol. The standardized breathing pattern may amplify HRV through mechanical and reflex mechanisms that are not readily distinguished from native autonomic physiology in this dataset. Fourth, the cross-sectional nature of this analysis precludes causal inference; while we cannot exclude the possibility that lower HRV predisposes to cardiometabolic disease, it is well known that diabetes mellitus causes low HRV through diabetic autonomic neuropathy, and similar disease-related mechanisms likely apply to other cardiometabolic conditions. The observed associations, therefore, most likely reflect HRV as a consequence of established disease or its associated risk factors rather than a causal antecedent. Fifth, SDNN was measured using 1-minute PPG recordings; while this ultra-short metric may not be directly comparable to standard 5-minute or long-term HRV indices, our findings demonstrate that it is consistently associated with cardiometabolic disease and that its information content can be enhanced by conditioning the collection context. Finally, participants who use the mindfulness feature may represent a distinct subpopulation within the study cohort (e.g., individuals with greater health awareness or interest in wellness behaviors), so that the observed effect sizes may not fully generalize to all wearable users. To characterize this potential selection effect, we compared baseline demographics and clinical characteristics between mindfulness users and the broader study population and did not find any significant differences. Nonetheless, our within-person comparison of mindful versus non-mindful HRV inherently controls confounders associated with this self-selection, thereby preserving the internal validity of the conditional contrast. These findings persist despite the fact that we were unable to specify whether mindfulness sessions used timed breathing or simply abstract mindfulness as the stimulus.

In conclusion, heart rate variability collected under controlled breathing conditions had a significantly stronger association with cardiometabolic disease than data collected opportunistically, and this structured data collection enabled substantial improvements in disease classification. Further work will be required to define the extent to which mindful HRV will be helpful for disease subclassification or longitudinal monitoring in response to intervention. Simple yet systematic approaches to externally conditioning the collection of data from wearable devices have the potential to substantially increase the information content from these widely available tools. The range of potentially informative perturbations is broad, in particular on devices with capabilities for the annotation of activity and the delivery of triggered stimuli.

Methods

Study design

The Apple Heart and Movement Study (AHMS) is a mobile application-based longitudinal cohort study involving the collection of sensor, survey, and health data. Participants were informed about the study through IRB-approved materials, including study websites managed by Apple, the American Heart Association (AHA), and Brigham and Women’s Hospital. Participants are asked to complete a series of surveys and to consent to share data collected from their Health app (which uses the HealthKit framework) and sensor data obtained directly from the iPhone and Apple Watch. The study began enrolling on November 14, 2019, with data collection ending in February, 2025. The study was approved by the Advarra Central Institutional Review Board (PRO00036784) and registered to ClinicalTrials.gov (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04198194, 2019/12/13, Apple Heart & Movement Study). After self-report and self-verification of eligibility, the participants followed the steps for enrollment as delivered by the Research app. They were instructed to read the informed consent form (ICF) and provide an electronic signature if they consented to participate. All analyses were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. There is no compensation for participation. Over 200,000 participants were followed longitudinally in the study. The study provides a unique opportunity to understand the associations of continuous sensor measurements with cardiometabolic conditions at extremely large scales.

Handling of serial enrollments

A particular edge case we considered is one where a participant withdraws from the study, uninstalls the Research App, re-installs, and re-enrolls. In this case, the same participant would be assigned two distinct subject-identifier codes and appear to analysts as two distinct individuals. To reduce the proliferation of identities in this way, we referred to personal identifying information (PII) associated with each enrollment, encrypted so as to blind the analysts to the participants’ actual PII. By matching encrypted PII among multiple subject-identifier codes, we removed a few hundred subject-identifier codes, and their health data, from each of the three cohorts we assembled (See Supplementary Figs. 1–3).

Participant demographics

On enrollment, participants in AHMS responded to the Research Profile Survey within the Research app and to the Demographics Survey; the data included the year of birth, state of residence, race and ethnicity, marital status, employment status, education level, gender identity, sex assigned at birth, and subjective social status. Each enrollment event triggered the creation of an alphanumeric subject-identification code uniquely associated with each participant’s study data (except in rare cases of multiple enrollments—see above). Within the first month after enrollment, participants received a Medical History and Medications survey delivered through the Research app. Participants’ demographic information was collected from these surveys. We also inferred a participant’s use of beta-blockers and calcium-channel blockers from their self-report in the Apple Health app.

Definitions of cardiometabolic diseases

The status for 31 prevalent medical conditions was taken from each participant’s earliest medical history survey. We treated survey responses “prefer not to answer” or “do not know” as missing values. Of the 31 medical conditions, we identified CAD, DM, ACS, HF, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, and peripheral artery disease as cardiometabolic diseases. In the years covered by this analysis, the medical history survey did not discriminate between type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes.

Calculation of heart rate and heart rate variability

Each heart-rate variability measurement in this study was the output of a native Apple Watch HRV algorithm and is defined as the standard deviation of the inter-beat (RR) intervals between normal heartbeats (a method called “SDNN”) in a 1-min recording, using heartbeats acquired by the watch’s photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors. All measurements studied in the current manuscript were of these short-term indices, and we did not include any long-term HRV indices. We note also that SDNN derived from these 1-min recordings may not be directly comparable to SDNN obtained from standard 5-min or 24-h ECG recordings. When available, we used this same sequence of heartbeats to compute the average heart rate during the 1-min HRV measurement. When calculating the inter-beat periods, we ignored periods the watch’s beat-detection algorithm flagged as invalid. We kept an HRV sample only when there was a corresponding recording of a heartbeat-series sample from the same participant. We did not use an HRV measurement if the associated sequence of heartbeats was missing.

Comparison between evening HRV and random HRV

This analysis used participant data collected between 2020-Sep-01 and 2022-Oct-01 (UTC). We divided the diurnal cycle into 16 time bins, relative to the time when the participant woke up. Consistent with previous reports, HRV in our study population reaches its minimum approximately when heart rate reaches its maximum, both of which occur about 12 h after waking, which corresponds to the interval between 11.125–14.625 h after waking in our defined bins (Fig. 6, evening HRV). To compare between HRVs collected randomly and evening HRVs, we paired each evening HRV with a randomly selected HRV sample (obtained within ±24 h of the recording of the evening HRV) from the same participant (random HRV), all of which were collected while the participant was assumed to be awake. Then, we created a balanced set of evening HRVs and random HRVs by randomly selecting a single pair of the evening and random HRVs. Comparison between sleeping HRV and waking HRV

In this analysis, HRV samples and sleep-analysis samples were collected between 2020-08-31 and 2022-09-01. Both data types had to originate from an Apple Watch running watchOS 7 or 8, where the sleep tracking feature was implemented. This feature logs one or more intervals—each resulting in a sleep-analysis sample—each night where it has high confidence that the user is asleep. We define a “night of sleep” in the following way: (1) the time difference between the start of the earliest sleep sample and the end of the last sleep sample exceeded 1 h but was less than 14 h; (2) the total duration of sleep samples had to sum to >15% of the time elapsed between the start of the earliest sleep sample and the end of the last sleep sample; (3) a night of sleep had to start between 5:00 p.m. and 11:00 a.m., local time, and had to end between 1:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. We defined a sleeping HRV to be an HRV measured during one such “night of sleep”. We defined ”waking HRV” as an HRV sample obtained at intervals between two nights of sleep. Note that, regarding criterion #2, across all nights of sleep where HRV was recorded, the fraction of each night that was not logged as “sleeping” by Apple Watch tended to be very small—the median value was 6% of a night. AUROCs for discriminating cardiometabolic diseases were calculated and compared using these HRVs as predictors.

Comparison between mindful HRV and non-mindful HRV

This analysis used participant data collected from the start of AHMS to 2021-Sep-30 (UTC). An HRV sample was denoted “mindful” if it was recorded during the participant’s execution of a mindful session that was recorded on the Apple Watch, providing a guided deep breathing visualization for at least 1 min. The Apple Watch opportunistically turns on the PPG sensor at the start of a session, allowing an HRV sample to be collected. An HRV sample was denoted “non-mindful” if not recorded during a mindful session. We split each day with an HRV into 16 time-of-day bins, and matched mindful HRVs to non-mindful HRVs from the same participant, from the same time-of-day bin, and within ±24 h.

Construction of models, including participant characteristics information

To understand the improvement of the information content of mindful HRV over non-mindful HRV independent of participant characteristics, we constructed logistic regression models including participant age, sex, and BMI with either mindful HRV or non-mindful HRV. We estimated a participant’s age at enrollment using the birth year reported during their enrollment process. If we found more than one birth year listed in the enrollment information for that participant, then we treated their birth year as a missing value. We estimated a participant’s sex at birth using self-report in the demographics survey. We use only the earliest 28 days of a participant’s responses to this survey. Where we found two or more contradictory responses from one participant, we treated their sex as a missing value. We estimated a participant’s BMI using self-reported weight and height in the demographics survey. We treated weights lower than 20 kg or above 600 kg and heights lower than 70 cm or greater than 250 cm as missing values. A logistic regression model was trained to discriminate each cardiometabolic condition, and AUROCs were calculated for comparison. The 95% confidence interval was calculated using the bootstrapped distributions. AUROC was evaluated on the training set itself, since the ratio of positive cases to predictors (25:1 or greater for most medical conditions in our dataset) is large enough to ignore the risk of overfitting. We drew 1000 bootstrapped samples for each distribution, rejecting any bootstrapped sample containing either 100% positive or 100% negative cases.

Sensitivity analyses excluding those on known rate-controlling medications

Beta-blockers and some calcium-channel blockers are known to interfere with heart rate and potentially affect heart rate variability. Furthermore, participants with arrhythmia might have different HRV patterns. To test if our finding is affected by the use of these agents or arrhythmia, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with these conditions. The use of beta-blockers and calcium-channel blockers, and the diagnosis of an arrhythmia, were obtained from self-reports.

Statistical analysis

We used two-level Bayesian hierarchical models to estimate each participant’s mean HRV for a given perturbation. Bayesian modeling was performed using the Python package PyStan 2.19. All models were fit with four parallel chains. Examination of convergence and model fit was performed following Bayesian workflow best practices24.

To quantify the association between any given disease and HRV, we used unpenalized logistic regression models from the Python package scikit-learn 1.0.2. Since the number of observations was much larger than the dimensionality of the model, the risk of overfitting was considered negligible. For the resulting classifiers, we computed AUROC using scikit-learn 1.0.2. Our 95% confidence intervals represent quantiles taken from 1000 bootstrapped samples. When we compare a perturbation to its baseline, for example, mindful HRV compared to non-mindful HRV, we report the difference in AUROC, as in ΔAUROC = AUROCMINDFUL – AUROCNON-MINDFUL. Confidence intervals for ΔAUROC were also derived from the bootstrap, and we consider results to be statistically significant when the 95% CI does not cross 0.

The net reclassification index (NRI), also called net reclassification improvement, is a measure of the improvement of one (“new”) risk model over another (“old”) model. It is the sum of two components: (1) the probability of the new model giving a higher risk score for people with the disease; (2) minus the probability of the new model giving a higher risk score for people without the disease. For historical reasons, this sum is usually multiplied by a factor of 2. We use the continuous formulation25. Since NRI is known to sometimes produce false positive results when used alone26, we report the metric in complement to our primary performance metric of ΔAUROC.
