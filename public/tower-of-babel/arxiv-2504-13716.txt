The number system in rational base 3 / 2 3/2 and the 3 ​ x + 1 3x+1 problem
Shalom Eliahou and Jean-Louis Verger-Gaugry

Abstract
. The representation of numbers in rational base p / q p/q was introduced in 2008 by Akiyama, Frougny and Sakarovitch, with a special focus on the case p / q = 3 / 2 p/q=3/2 . Unnoticed since then, natural questions related to representations in that specific base turn out to intimately involve the Collatz 3 ​ x + 1 3x+1 function. Our purpose in this note is to expose these links and motivate further research into them.

1.
Introduction

The representation of numbers in rational base
p/q
with
p,q
coprime positive integers was introduced in 2008 in
[
1
]
, with a focus on the case
p/q=3/2
to study Mahler’s
Z
-number problem and the Josephus problem.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, its representation in rational base
3/2
is the unique expression of the form

n=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{a_{i}}{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{i}

with digits
a_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
for all
i\geq 0
and
a_{k}\neq 0
if
n\geq 1
. We then denote the corresponding word in
\{0,1,2\}^{*}
by

\langle n\rangle=a_{k}a_{k-1}\cdots a_{0}.

We set
\langle 0\rangle=\epsilon
, the empty word, and we identify any word
0w\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
with
w
.

Denoting
\mathcal{L}=\{\langle n\rangle\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\{0,1,2\}^{*}
, here are two natural questions about
\mathcal{L}
:

(1)

If
w\in\mathcal{L}
, which extensions among
w0,w1,w2
still belong to
\mathcal{L}
?

(2)

Given
k\geq 1
, what is the lexicographically largest word of length
k
in
\mathcal{L}
? Equivalently, what is the largest
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\langle n\rangle
is of length
k
?

Apparently unnoticed so far, answers to these questions turn out to intimately involve the Collatz
3x+1
function, namely
T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by

(1)

T(n)=\begin{cases}n/2&\textrm{ if }n\equiv 0\bmod 2,\\
(3n+1)/2&\textrm{ if }n\equiv 1\bmod 2.\end{cases}

The purpose of this note is to expose these links and motivate further research into them. Its contents are as follows. In Section
2
, we recall basic properties of the numeration system in rational base
3/2
, in particular the recursive construction of the word
\langle n\rangle
in Section
2.1
and the odometer allowing to construct
\langle n+1\rangle
from
\langle n\rangle
in Section
2.2
. We also propose a variant of the Collatz conjecture in Section
2.3
. In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, we address the above questions (1) and (2), respectively. Section
4
concludes the note with two questions and two conjectures closely related to the Collatz conjecture and possibly of the same order of difficulty.

2.
Basics

Following
[
1
]
, every
n\in\mathbb{N}
has a unique expression of the form

n=\frac{1}{2}\big(a_{k}(3/2)^{k}+a_{k-1}(3/2)^{k-1}+\dots+a_{0}\big)

with
a_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
for all
i
and
a_{k}\neq 0
if
n\geq 1
. Accordingly, we associate to
n
the word

\langle n\rangle=a_{k}a_{k-1}\cdots a_{0}\in\{0,1,2\}^{*},

called the
representation of
n
in rational base
3/2
. We set
\langle 0\rangle=\epsilon
, the empty word, and we identify any word
0w\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
with
w
.

By Lemma 4.1 in
[
5
]
, the length of the word
\langle n\rangle
is given by
|\langle n\rangle|=\log_{3/2}(n)+O(1)
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a word
w\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
is
admissible
if
w=\langle n\rangle
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}\subset\{0,1,2\}^{*}
the language of admissible words, i.e.

\mathcal{L}=\{\langle n\rangle\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

As observed in
[
1
]
,
the language
\mathcal{L}
is prefix-closed
, i.e. stable under taking prefixes. That is, every prefix
w^{\prime}
of an admissible word
w=w^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}
is itself admissible.

Conversely, given an admissible word
w\in\mathcal{L}
, which extensions among
w0,w1,w2
remain admissible? The answer involves the Collatz
3x+1
function. See Proposition
3.1
.

2.1.
Construction of
\langle n\rangle

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a recursive construction of
\langle n\rangle\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
may be described as follows. Set
\langle 0\rangle=\epsilon
, the empty word.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Denote
\langle n_{0}\rangle=a_{k}\cdots a_{0}
with
a_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
for all
i
. Then

\begin{cases}\ a_{0}\equiv 2n_{0}\bmod 3,\\
\ \langle n_{0}\rangle=\langle n_{1}\rangle a_{0}\textrm{ where }n_{1}=(2n_{0}-a_{0})/3.\end{cases}

Moreover, we have
a_{0}=2n_{0}-3n_{1}
and
a_{0}\equiv n_{1}\bmod 2
.

Proof.

As
\langle n_{0}\rangle=a_{k}\cdots a_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle n_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(a_{k}(3/2)^{k}+\dots+a_{1}(3/2)+a_{0}\big)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\big(a_{k}(3/2)^{k-1}+\dots+a_{1}\big)\right)\frac{3}{2}+\frac{a_{0}}{2}.

Hence
a_{0}\equiv 2n_{0}\bmod 3
, and setting
n_{1}=(2n_{0}-a_{0})/3
, we have
\langle n_{1}\rangle=a_{k}\cdots a_{1}
. The claims follow.
∎

2.2.
The odometer

The
odometer
constructs the word
\langle n+1\rangle
from the word
\langle n\rangle
. It is described as a finite automaton in
[
1
]
. Here is an equivalent description.

Let
\sigma=(2\ 1\ 0)
be the cyclic permutation
2\mapsto 1\mapsto 0\mapsto 2
. For a word
u\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
, let
u^{\sigma}\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
denote the word obtained by applying
\sigma
to each letter of
u
. For instance,
12012^{\sigma}=01201
, then identified to
1201
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\langle n\rangle=u0v\in\{0,1,2\}^{*}
with
v\in\{1,2\}^{*}
, then
\langle n+1\rangle=u2v^{\sigma}
.

Proof.

See
[
1
, Section 3.2.3, pp. 66-67]
.
∎

The rule applies even if
u
or
v
is the empty word. In particular, if
\langle n\rangle=v\in\{1,2\}^{*}
, then identifying
v
with
0v
, we get
\langle n+1\rangle=2v^{\sigma}

2.3.
The Collatz conjecture

Dating back to the 1930’s, this conjecture deals with iterates
T^{(k)}
of the Collatz function
T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined in (
1
).

Conjecture 2.4
.

Let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
n_{0}\geq 1
. Then there exists
k\geq 1
such that
T^{(k)}(n_{0})=1
.

Here is an equivalent version, as shown below.

Conjecture 2.5
.

Let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
n_{0}\geq 1
. Then the proportion of even numbers in the sequence of iterates
T^{(k)}(n_{0})
tends to
1/2
as
k
tends to infinity.

Proposition 2.6
.

Conjectures
2.4
and
2.5
are equivalent.

We thank the anonymous referee for insisting to formally state Proposition 6 and carefully prove it, as opposed to just a remark with informal arguments as in the original version of this article.

Proof.

If Conjecture
2.4
holds, then at some point the sequence of iterates
T^{(k)}(n_{0})
collapses to
1,2,1,2,1,2,\dots
, whence Conjecture
2.5
.

Conversely, assume Conjecture
2.5
holds. Given an integer
n\geq 1
, let
\Omega(n)=(n,T(n),T^{(2)}(n),\dots)
denote the trajectory of
n
under iteration of
T
. Moreover, given
k\geq 1
, let

\Omega_{k}(n)=(n,T(n),T^{(2)}(n),\dots,T^{(k-1)}(n)),

and let
k_{1}
denote the number of odd integers in
\Omega_{k}(n)
. Assume for a contradiction that Conjecture
2.4
fails. Then there is an integer
m\geq 10
such that
\min\Omega(m)=m
. (Note that
\min\Omega(n)=1
for
n\leq 9
.) We claim that for all
k\geq 1
, we have

(2)

T^{(k)}(m)\leq\frac{(3.1)^{k_{1}}}{2^{k}}m.

The proof is straightforward, by induction on
k
. Indeed, (
2
) holds for
k=1
, since
m
is odd by minimality in
\Omega(m)
and
T(m)=(3m+1)/2=(3+1/m)m/2\leq(3.1)m/2
since
m\geq 10
. Let now
k\geq 1
and assume that
T^{(k)}(m)\leq(3.1)^{k_{1}}/2^{k}m
. Denote
n=T^{(k)}(m)
. If
n
is even, then
T^{(k+1)}(m)=T(n)=n/2\leq(3.1)^{k_{1}}/2^{k+1}m
, as desired. If
n
is odd, then

T^{(k+1)}(m)=T(n)=(3+1/n)n/2\leq(3+1/m)n/2\leq(3.1)n/2

since
n\geq m\geq 10
. Hence
T^{(k+1)}(m)\leq(3.1)^{k_{1}+1}/2^{k+1}m
, as desired. The claim is proved.

Now
(3.1)^{k_{1}}/2^{k}\geq 1
if and only if
k_{1}/k\geq\ln(2)/\ln(3.1)
, and
\ln(2)/\ln(3.1)>0.6
. By Conjecture 5, for
k
large enough, the proportion
k_{1}/k
will fall below
0.6
. Hence
T^{(k)}(m)\leq(3.1)^{k_{1}}/2^{k}m<m
, contradicting the minimality of
m
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

At the time of writing, Conjecture
2.4
has been verified up to
n_{0}\leq 2^{68}
. See
[
2
]
. With this bound, the results of
[
3
]
and its Table 2 on page 54 imply that, besides the trivial cycle
\{1,2\}
under
T
, no other cycle, if any, can have length less than
114,208,327,604
. See
[
4
]
for a general reference on the conjecture, and
[
6
]
for recent strong results about it.

3.
Links with the Collatz function

We now address the two questions about
\mathcal{L}=\{\langle n\rangle\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
asked in the Introduction, and show that in both cases, the answer naturally involves the Collatz function
T
.

3.1.
Extending admissible words

Here is the first question. Given
w\in\mathcal{L}
, which extensions among
w0,w1,w2
still belong to
\mathcal{L}
?

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{L}
, say
w=\langle n\rangle
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\begin{cases}\ w1\in\mathcal{L}\iff n\equiv 1\bmod 2,\textrm{ and then }w1=\langle(3n+1)/2\rangle=\langle T(n)\rangle.\\
\ w0\in\mathcal{L}\iff n\equiv 0\bmod 2\iff w2\in\mathcal{L},\textrm{ and then }w0=\langle 3n/2\rangle\textrm{ and }\;w2=\langle(3n+2)/2\rangle.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
\langle n\rangle 1\in\mathcal{L}
, Proposition
2.2
implies
1\equiv n\bmod 2
and
w1=\langle(3n+1)/2\rangle
. Similarly, if
\langle n\rangle 0\in\mathcal{L}
then
0\equiv n\bmod 2
and
w0=\langle 3n/2\rangle
. Finally, if
\langle n\rangle 2\in\mathcal{L}
then
2\equiv n\bmod 2
and
w2=\langle(3n+2)/2\rangle
. The stated equivalences follow.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be odd. Then
\langle T(n)\rangle=\langle n\rangle 1
.

Proof.

Immediate from the above Proposition.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The map
n\mapsto n/2
for
n
even is straightforward in the base
2
representation of
n
: just remove the last digit,
0
. Symmetrically, Corollary
3.2
shows that the map
n\mapsto(3n+1)/2
for
n
odd is straightforward in the base
3/2
representation of
n
: just add
1
as rightmost digit. By any chance, could it happen that considering the map
n\mapsto T(n)
simultaneously in base
2
and in rational base
3/2
may lead to new insights on the Collatz conjecture?

3.2.
Saturated words

Given a number system
\mathcal{B}
over a finite set of digits, we say that a positive integer
n\geq 1
is
saturated
if its representation
\langle n\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}
in base
\mathcal{B}
is of length strictly less than that of
\langle m\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}
for all
m\geq n+1
. Accordingly, we say that
\langle n\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}
is a
saturated word
. For instance in base
10
, the saturated integers and corresponding words are the
n=10^{\ell}-1=9\cdots 9
.

The digits
1,2
of the saturated words in rational base
3/2
turn out to be unpredictable in some sense and closely related to the
3x+1
problem. Recall that
|w|
denotes the length of the finite word
w
.

Notation 3.4
.

Given
k\geq 1
, we denote by
\sat(k)
the largest positive integer whose representation in base
3/2
is of length
k
. That is,

\sat(k)=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N},|\langle n\rangle|=k\}.

Proposition 3.5
.

For all
k\geq 1
,
\langle\sat(k)\rangle
is the unique admissible word of length
k
with digits in
\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

By the odometer as described in Proposition
2.3
, if
\langle n\rangle
is of length
k
and contains a
0
digit, then
\langle n+1\rangle
is of the same length
k
. This shows that the inequality
|\langle n+1\rangle|>|\langle n\rangle|
happens exactly when
\langle n\rangle
contains no
0
digit except possibly as an irrelevant prefix.
∎

For instance, a look at
\langle n\rangle
for
1\leq n\leq 27
in Table
1
yields
\sat(k)
and the corresponding word for
k\leq 7
in Table
2
below.

\hline\cr 1&1&2\\
2&2&21\\
3&4&212\\

6&17&212211\\

Observe in Table
2
that
\langle\sat(k-1)\rangle
is a prefix of
\langle\sat(k)\rangle
. Indeed, this is true for all
k\geq 1
.

Proposition 3.6
.

For all
k\geq 2
, we have
\langle\sat(k)\rangle=\langle\sat(k-1)\rangle a_{0}
where
a_{0}\in\{1,2\}
and
a_{0}\equiv\sat(k-1)\bmod 2
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is prefix-closed, it follows from Proposition
3.5
that the length
k-1
prefix of
\langle\sat(k)\rangle
equals
\langle\sat(k-1)\rangle
. Thus
\langle\sat(k)\rangle=\langle\sat(k-1)\rangle a_{0}
where
a_{0}\in\{1,2\}
, and
a_{0}\equiv\sat(k-1)\bmod 2
by Proposition
2.2
.
∎

3.3.
The function
U

It turns out that the sequence
(\sat(k))_{k\geq 1}
coincides with the sequence of iterates
U^{(k)}(0)
of a certain function
U
closely related to the Collatz function
T
. See Proposition
3.10
below.

Notation 3.7
.

Let
U:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be defined as

U(n)=\begin{cases}\ (3n+2)/2=T(n)+n+1&\textrm{ if }n\equiv 0\bmod 2,\\
\ (3n+1)/2=T(n)&\textrm{ if }n\equiv 1\bmod 2.\end{cases}

Remark 3.8
.

We have
U(n)\equiv T(n)+n+1\bmod 2
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Starting from any
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, let us consider the sequence of iterates
U^{(k)}(n_{0})
for
k\geq 1
. The following proposition shows that, for the sequence of corresponding words
\langle U^{(k)}(n_{0})\rangle
in rational base
3/2
,
each term is a prefix of the next one and the added digit is either
1
or
2
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\langle U(n)\rangle=\begin{cases}\langle n\rangle 1&\textrm{ if }n\equiv 1\bmod 2,\\
\langle n\rangle 2&\textrm{ if }n\equiv 0\bmod 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

Straightforward from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

A particularly interesting sequence of iterates
U^{(k)}(n_{0})
is for
n_{0}=0
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For all
k\geq 1
, we have
\sat(k)=U^{(k)}(0)
.

Proof.

For
k=1
, we have
\sat(1)=2
and
U(1)=T(1)=2
. For
k\geq 2
, we only need show
\sat(k)=U(\sat(k-1))
. Indeed, it follows from Proposition
3.6
that

\sat(k)=\frac{3\sat(k-1)+a_{0}}{2}

where
a_{0}\in\{1,2\}
and
a_{0}\equiv\sat(k-1)\bmod 2
. Hence
\sat(k)=U(\sat(k-1))
by definition of
U
, as desired.
∎

Consequently, the growth factor of the sequence
\sat(k)
tends to
3/2
.

.

1/2
as

goes to infinity, as supported by numerical evidence? Note that by Proposition
3.9
, the number of digits “0” remains constant along the trajectory of
n_{0}
.

\langle\sat(k)\rangle

Is Conjecture

\langle\sat(k-1)\rangle

\langle\sat(k)\rangle

\langle\sat(\infty)\rangle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle\sat(k)\rangle,

an infinite word over
\{1,2\}

w

\mathcal{B}
of cardinality
|\mathcal{B}|=b
is said to be
normal
if for all
\ell\geq 1
, all
b^{\ell}
words in
\mathcal{B}^{\ell}
occur as subwords in
w[n]
, the length
n
prefix of
w
, with frequency tending to
1/b^{\ell}
as
n\to\infty
. Our stronger conjecture runs as follows.

Conjecture 4.3
.

The word
\langle\sat(\infty)\rangle
is normal.

Our second question is about fully understanding the Collatz map
T
in rational base
3/2
.

Question 4.4
.

For
n
odd, we have seen that
\langle T(n)\rangle=\langle n\rangle 1
. For
n
even, how does one determine
\langle T(n)\rangle
from
\langle n\rangle
? Is any answer susceptible to lead to new insights on the Collatz conjecture?

Note that determining the parity of
n\in\mathbb{N}
from the digits
a_{i}
of the word
\langle n\rangle=a_{k}\cdots a_{0}\in\mathcal{L}
is more cumbersome than in base
2
or
10
. Indeed, the following criterion is straightforward but not quite practical for calculations by hand.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
\langle n\rangle=a_{k}\cdots a_{0}\in\mathcal{L}
. Then

n\equiv 0\bmod 2\ \iff\ \sum_{i=0}^{k}2^{k-i}3^{i}a_{i}\equiv 0\bmod 2^{k+2}.

Proof.

We have
n=\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(3/2)^{i}a_{i})=2^{-(k+1)}(\sum_{i=0}^{k}2^{k-i}3^{i}a_{i})
. Hence
n\equiv 0\bmod 2\iff 2^{k+1}n\equiv 0\bmod 2^{k+2}\iff\sum_{i=0}^{k}2^{k-i}3^{i}a_{i}\equiv 0\bmod 2^{k+2}
.
∎
