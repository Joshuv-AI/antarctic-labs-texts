A Scoping Review of Pediatric Kienböck Disease: What Do We Know?
Authors: James Rex, Eric Taleghani, Melissa Previtera, Kevin Little, Roger Cornwall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeKienböck disease is a rare entity in pediatric and adolescent patients. The available published literature is limited primarily to case series and case reports. A trial of conservative treatment with rigid immobilization has been described as the standard of care in these cases. Our aim was to examine the body of literature regarding Kienböck disease in patients under 18 years of age to determine the quality of current evidence and whether concrete conclusions could be drawn regarding management and outcomes.MethodsLiterature searches were run in the databases PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library with keywords for terms such as osteonecrosis, lunate, and Kienböck disease. The results underwent deduplication followed by title screening, abstract screening, and full-text screening. From the resulting studies, data were extracted regarding patient presentation and diagnosis, interventions, and reported outcomes.ResultsFive hundred twenty-seven studies were screened after removal of duplicates. Further review yielded 69 studies published from 1976 to 2023 with data specific to pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease. In total, 57 studies detailed operative intervention in 142 patients. Sixteen studies reported isolated conservative management in 26 patients. Surgical procedures were highly variable, with radial shortening osteotomy being the most prevalent. Reported outcomes were similarly inconsistent, with pain and range of motion most documented.ConclusionsPublications regarding pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease are limited primarily to case reports and case series with highly variable data collection regarding patient presentation, interventions, and outcome measures. Although conservative management with immobilization has been reported as the gold standard treatment for this condition, most literature reports operatively treated cases. A prospective investigation is needed to delineate the appropriate management and expected outcomes of pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease.Type of study/level of evidenceDifferential diagnosis/symptom prevalence study 3a.Key words: Adolescent, Kienböck disease, Lunatomalacia, Pediatrics

Pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease, also known as lunatomalacia, is a challenging clinical entity to manage because of its rarity and a lack of consensus regarding treatment. Prior literature has reported the prevalence of the disease in the general population ranging from 0.0066% to 1.9% in large radiographic database studies.1, 2, 3, 4 The prevalence of symptomatic Kienböck disease appears to be even lower, only affecting between 63% and 72% of those with radiographic findings.1,2 Most cases described in the literature are in adult patients in the third to fifth decade of life, with the highest burden of disease in patients aged 50–59.5, 6, 7 Kienböck disease is substantially less common in the pediatric and adolescent populations, with an incidence estimated at less than 10 patients per million person years in a database study spanning 26 years.7

In adults, the disease appears to progress despite conservative management.8,9 Conversely, Kienböck disease in younger patients appears to behave differently, with some evidence that the lunate can revascularize and recover its architecture without intervention.10, 11, 12 Thus, it has been recommended that pediatric and adolescent patients should be managed conservatively with prolonged immobilization.13,14 However, this treatment approach is largely based on small case series, and it remains unknown what quality of evidence supports conservative versus surgical treatments in pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease. In order to fill this knowledge gap, we performed a scoping review of the available literature regarding pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease, determining the quality of evidence and whether any conclusions could be drawn regarding specific treatments for this challenging condition in this population.

Materials and Methods

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, search strategies were created and run in the databases PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library (Wiley) from their date of inception to July 3, 2024. The searches were created by a health sciences librarian and included controlled vocabulary and keywords for the terms osteonecrosis, lunate, and Kienböck disease. The improved Cochrane Childhood Cancer Group pediatric search hedge was used as well. Full search strategies can be found in the Appendix S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org. No filters were used. Results were uploaded and deduplicated through Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd).

Abstracts that included reference to Kienböck disease or lunatomalacia were included, unless it was explicitly stated that the population reviewed in the work was greater than 18 years of age. This yielded a subset of publications that underwent final full-text screening. Articles without data specific to the management of Kienböck disease in patients under 18, and those reporting data as an aggregate such that information specific to pediatric and adolescent patients could not be assessed, were excluded. Articles without any available English translation were excluded. From the resulting studies, data were extracted regarding patient presentation and diagnosis, interventions, and reported outcomes. Using the age of 15 as a rough estimate of skeletal maturity, as used by Irisarri et al13 and Lichtman et al,14 the patients aged <15 years of age and patients 15–18 years of age were further analyzed separately.

A total of 527 studies were available after removal of duplicates. After title/abstract screening, 277 publications were indicated for full-text review. An additional 24 papers contained data only on adult patients, whereas 3 studies did not report an age range. Fifty-three studies were basic science papers or reviews without patient data, and 62 studies had no available English translation. This screening yielded 69 studies for the final analysis. Interventions

A wide variety of interventions were described for pediatric and adolescent patients, with 57 of the 69 studies documenting operative treatment. Of the 168 patients described in the literature, only 26 (19%) were treated conservatively with variable durations of immobilization. Looking specifically at the 138 patients with individual data, 32.7% (18/55) of patients younger than 15 and 8.4% (7/83) of patients over the age of 15 were successfully treated conservatively with immobilization alone.10, 11, 12, 13,15,20,26,29,39,43,44,64,67,69 The most reported operative intervention was radial shortening osteotomy, followed by vascularized bone grafting (Fig. 2).

Notably, successful nonsurgical treatment with immobilization was only reported in 13 (19%) of the studies reviewed. Radial shortening osteotomy was the operative treatment of choice for most because of concern for pathologic ulnar negativity. Of historical interest, several studies from the 1970s and 1980s detailed silicone lunate replacement.

Discussion

Our scoping review of pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease illustrates the challenges inherent in the study of rare conditions. The existing literature is largely limited to case reports and small series, most with fewer than 10 patients. Within the published data, inconsistent reporting with respect to staging/disease severity, presenting symptoms, and radiographic, clinical, and functional outcomes prevents meta-analysis. As a result, it is difficult to determine definitive assessment, treatment, and prognosis guidelines from the existing body of published literature. A similar problem exists for Kienböck disease in the adult population. There have been recent advances in diagnostics, classification systems, and surgical options, with optimism that these advances would allow for more precise and individualized treatment plans. However, the quality of the supporting evidence remains poor.81,82

Regarding treatment options for pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease, our analysis of the available evidence reveals a preponderance of studies reporting operative treatment, with only 17% of studies reporting conservative treatments, and only 19% of patients managed conservatively. Of the reported surgical techniques, radial shortening osteotomy was most common, followed by vascularized bone grafting. Reports of conservative treatment were comparatively rare, and no studies compared conservative and operative treatment. Although most published studies focused on surgical treatments, some expert opinions favor conservative management.13,14 For example, Lichtman et al82 recommend at least a trial of observation in patients under the age of 20; however, they note that this recommendation is based on low-quality evidence. Direct, comparative studies of surgical and nonsurgical treatments are needed to determine the relative value of different management strategies for this patient population.

Wagner et al83 recently conducted a systematic review on pediatric and juvenile lunatomalacia, including 37 case series and reports. They found that 36% of patients were treated conservatively, with a younger mean age in the conservatively treated group (12.5 years) compared with the surgical group (15 years). Furthermore, they found that conservative management was generally associated with favorable outcomes, whereas surgical treatment often carried higher complication rates, particularly in younger patients, although detailed information on the complications was not provided. They were unable to carry out robust statistical analyses because of limited and incomplete data, but they advocated for nonsurgical treatment for patients younger than 15 years. In contrast to these findings, our review identified only a 19% rate of conservatively managed patients across a larger body of literature (69 studies vs 37). Several methodological differences may explain this discrepancy. First, our search strategy drew from different databases, including the Cochrane Library rather than the Web of Science. Second, we did not restrict our search to include terms such as “pediatric” or “adolescent,” which resulted in the inclusion of studies where these patients were analyzed alongside adult cohorts. This approach enabled a more comprehensive review of published cases of Kienböck disease in children and adolescents. Additionally, the review by Wagner et al83 was conducted as a systematic review aimed at answering a specific clinical question regarding optimal treatment approaches. In contrast, our review was designed as a scoping review to characterize the existing body of literature, identify knowledge gaps, and evaluate inconsistencies in reporting that currently limit meaningful comparative analysis such as a systematic review or meta-analysis.

Pediatric patients are a dynamic patient population, with treatment responses often varying by age and skeletal maturity. For pediatric Kienböck disease, patients who are skeletally immature may have the ability to remodel and revascularize the lunate even in later-stage disease.10, 11, 12 Such healing represents an important difference from the typical clinical course seen in adult patients, in which progression is common.8,9 However, the existing literature regarding pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease does not identify specific ages or developmental stages for which prognosis or management details may differ. Of the 168 pediatric/adolescent patients described in the literature, only 58 patients had a distal radius physeal state documented.10,11,13,15,17, 18, 19, 20, 21,23,25,26,31,34,39,42, 43, 44, 45, 46,54,56,60,62,69,71,72 Therefore, not enough data exist to determine which of patients may be more capable of spontaneous resolution versus those that will experience a stepwise decline necessitating surgery.

This scoping review identifies clear gaps in the literature regarding pediatric Kienböck disease, providing direction for future research. Studies should clearly define the pediatric and adolescent population by excluding patients >18 years of age and include a proxy for skeletal maturity, such as distal radius physeal state or bone age. If adult patients are included, treatment and outcome data should be stratified by age group or skeletal maturity. Objective clinical outcomes, specifically wrist range of motion and grip strength, should be reported before and after intervention. Patient-reported outcome measures appropriate for the juvenile population should also be collected pre- and postintervention to assess patient perspectives and functional recovery. Baseline assessments are essential for determining initial disease severity and the overall effect of treatment. Direct comparative studies are ideal, and in the setting of rare pathology, multicenter studies are necessary. Establishing a centralized data registry would help overcome limitations in sample size and promote standardized data collection and analysis. An example of this approach can be seen in research on congenital upper-extremity differences. To address the limitations of small sample sizes and retrospective designs, a prospective, multicenter registry for congenital upper limb differences was established. Because its inception, thousands of patients have been enrolled, enabling multiple analyses that have provided stronger data on epidemiology, treatments, and functional impacts of various congenital upper limb differences.84, 85, 86

There are several limitations to this review. Our search strategy was limited to studies published in English, which may have excluded relevant studies in other languages. We did not formally appraise the quality of included studies. Because most available evidence consists of small case series and case reports, some publications may not have been captured in the databases we queried. In addition, we chose to exclude studies that included pediatric patients in the study cohort but did not stratify treatment or outcome data by age. Although obtaining these data would strengthen future meta-analyses, the combination of pediatric and adult cohorts reflects a fundamental challenge in the current literature on pediatric lunatomalacia. The data collected in this study demonstrate that there is insufficient high-level evidence to guide treatment decisions or to predict outcomes for the varying management strategies for pediatric and adolescent Kienböck disease. We identified knowledge gaps and made recommendations for future research, including the use of standardized data collection to generate high-quality evidence to guide care for young patients affected by this troubling clinical problem.
