Low tourniquet pressure has less impact on lower extremity nerve innervation: comparison of different tourniquet pressures used with intraoperative neuromonitoring with a randomized controlled study
Authors: Ahmet Müçteba Yıldırım, Serkan Bayram, Taha Kizilkurt, Nur Canbolat, Mehmet Barış Baslo, Mehmet Aşik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to investigate the compression and ischemic effects of two different tourniquet pressures on tissues during surgery show a clinical difference.MethodsPatients aged 18–65 years who underwent foot and ankle surgery and applied a tourniquet in a single center between September 2022 and November 2023 were included in this prospective randomized study. Accordingly, tourniquet pressures were applied as limb occlusion pressure (LOP) + 50 mmHg in group 1 (12 patients) and LOP + 100 mmHg in group 2 (12 patients). The time point at which the femoral nerve motor evoked potential (MEP) decreased by 50%, the time point at which the MEP decreased by 100% for all nerves (femoral, tibial, and deep peroneal), and the time point at which all responses returned after the tourniquet was deflated were identified as the time points for analysis.ResultsThere were no differences in demographic data (age, body mass index, and sex) between the two groups. The mean tourniquet pressure was 191 ± 16 mmHg in Group 1 and 247 ± 21 mmHg in Group 2 (p < 0.001). A 50% decrease in the femoral nerve MEP value was observed at an average of 47 min in Group 1 and 34 min in Group 2 (p < 0.001). A complete loss of MEP responses for all nerves was observed at an average of 69 min in Group 1 and 56 min in Group 2. After the tourniquet was deflated, all MEP responses returned to baseline values at an average of 8.5 min in Group 1 and 12.6 min in Group 2 (p = 0.007). The results showed that lower limb nerve innervation was affected later and returned to normal earlier after deflation of the tourniquet in Group 1 (low tourniquet pressure group).ConclusionsThe innervations of the lower extremity nerves were affected later in the group in which low tourniquet pressure was applied (average 191 mmHg). Again, in this group (LOP + 50 mmHg), nerve conduction recovered an average of 10 min after deflation and four minutes earlier than in the high tourniquet pressure group.Level of evidenceLevel I, diagnostic study.Trial registrationNCT05926154.Keywords: Tourniquet, Nerve injury, Quadriceps denervation, Neuromonitoring, Quadriceps tendon thickness

Background

Tourniquets are widely used in orthopedic surgeries to improve visualization, reduce blood loss, and facilitate surgical procedures. However, its use is also associated with several complications [1], which in recent years has led to decreased tourniquet use in arthroplasty [2]. Moreover, no consensus has been reached on the optimal lowest tourniquet pressure for reducing these complications. Limb occlusion pressure (LOP) is affected by various parameters such as thigh diameter, tourniquet cuff width, age, and sex. The authors suggested that a reliable interval according to the LOP must be determined to benefit from the positive effects of the tourniquet [3, 4]. Vascular events, such as peripheral nerve injuries, loss of quadriceps muscle strength, and deep venous thrombosis, are the most significant tourniquet-related adverse events. Extended tourniquet duration and elevated inflation pressure are the primary risk factors for these injuries [5]. Previous studies have evaluated tourniquet-related peripheral nerve changes using postoperative electromyography (EMG) [6, 7]. However, no study has investigated how tourniquet time and pressure affect intraoperative peripheral nerve function using intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM). The literature also lacks evidence-based guidelines regarding the duration of reperfusion interval. Studies of quadriceps muscle strength loss have revealed that surgeries performed consistently around the knee create a bias factor.

It is established in the literature that a 15-minute waiting period is necessary for the neurovascular structures to return to their normal state following the deflation of a tourniquet that has been in use for two hours. Our hypothesis was that this waiting time may vary according to tourniquet pressure. We aimed to answer the following questions in our randomized controlled study. (1) Do the compression and ischemia effects of two different tourniquet pressures on tissues during surgery demonstrate clinical differences? (2) Can objective intraoperative electrophysiological data determine ideal tourniquet gap duration? (3) Are the effects of tourniquets on quadriceps tendon morphology affected by tourniquet pressure in the acute period?

Materials and methods

This randomized prospective cohort study included patients who underwent foot and ankle surgery using a tourniquet between September 2022 and November 2023. Institutional Review Board and local ethics committee approval was received before the initiation of this study (08/04/2022-07 2022/620). This study was registered at http://www.clinicalTrials.gov (NCT05926154).

This sample group was selected to evaluate the isolated effects of tourniquet use on the quadriceps. The inclusion criteria were as follows: Patients aged 18–65 years, undergoing lower extremity surgery (below the knee), surgery requiring the use of a tourniquet (including ankle trauma), and consenting to surgery according to the surgical procedure (under general anesthesia). The exclusion criteria were as follows: orthopedic surgery related to the lower extremities and spine; diagnosis of a disease that affects the peripheral nervous system (peripheral neuropathy and cerebrovascular accident); morbidities that prevent ideal tourniquet application (chronic lymphedema, body mass index [BMI] > 35, and acute deep venous thrombosis); diagnosis of a disease that affects the peripheral vascular system (peripheral vascular disease and calcified popliteal artery); an American Society of Anesthesiologists score of ≥ 3; and surgery time of 80 min. The procedures were explained to the patients and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The participants were randomized using a computerized internet-supported randomization tool (http://www.random.org/sequences). According to this random list with a 1:1 allocation ratio, patients were assigned to either the LOP + 50 mmHg group (group 1) or the LOP + 100 mmHg group (group 2). The minimum number of patients required to yield significant results in this study was determined using power analysis. According to the two-tailed independent samples t-test analysis with 95% confidence (1-α), 80% test power (1-β), and a d = 1.25 effect size, each group should include 12 participants, resulting in a total of 24 participants. Demographic data such as patient age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) were collected. Tourniquet pressures were calculated according to the method described by Graham et al. [8]. This was determined by calculating the occlusion pressure of the extremity using the suggested formulation.

LOP = [(systolic pressure − diastolic pressure) × (extremity diameter) / 3 cuff width)] + diastolic pressure The participants were divided into two groups according to tourniquet pressure: LOP + 50 mmHg (group 1) and LOP + 100 mmHg (group 2). The same postoperative analgesia protocol was used for all patients.

Intraoperative neuromonitoring

General anesthesia and the total intravenous anesthesia protocol were employed because IONM would be used during surgery. During induction, midazolam 0.05 mg/kg was administered for amnesia and sedation, fentanyl 2–3 µg/kg for analgesia, and propofol 3 mg/kg for hypnotic anesthesia. Anesthesia was maintained with remifentanil and propofol infusions. As intraoperative neuromonitoring is affected by intraoperative blood pressure changes, temperature drops, and anemia, optimal conditions were ensured using motor-evoked potential (MEP), somatosensory-evoked potentials (SEP), and free-run EMG as IONM modalities. Corkscrew electrodes were placed for transcranial magnetic stimulation. In the lower extremity, needle electrodes were placed in the tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus muscles for the deep peroneal nerve, gastrocnemius muscle for the tibial nerve, rectus femoris muscle proximal and just distal to the tourniquet for the femoral nerve, and tibialis posterior nerve for the SEP (Fig. 1). For IONM, software with five MEP channels and one SEP channel was installed on the NIM Eclipse® (Medtronic, Medtronic Inc., Jacksonville, FL) device. A 6-channel montage was conducted for the purposes of examining the EMG, TcMEP and MEP modalities. Each lead was placed intramuscularly, both those that were active and those that were used as reference points. The impedances of all the recording electrodes were documented at a velocity of 100 m/s. Electromyographic recording of instantaneous electrical responses to TcMEP stimuli was conducted within a bandwidth of 30–1000 Hz, and subsequent TcMEP values were recorded. The time taken for electromyography scans ranged from 50 to 100 ms, whereas TcMEP scans took 100 ms. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) was employed to stimulate MEPs using a non-repetitive anodal stimulation protocol with a pulse duration of 475 volts and an interval of 6 ms. The recordings remained unaffected by anesthesia when responses from the upper extremity were taken as a reference point alongside those from the lower extremity. MEP stimulation intensity was set at 100–600 µV.

Electrode placement for intraoperative neuromonitoring. a Rectus femoris, tibialis anterior, and extensor hallucis longus muscles for the femoral and deep peroneal nerves. b Tibialis posterior nerve for SEP stimulation. c Gastrocnemius muscle of the tibial nerve

A 50% decrease in SEP and MEP amplitude values and > 10% prolongation in SEP latency data were accepted as the threshold values [9]. The baseline values were obtained before tourniquet inflation. Subsequently, warnings for MEP were received at 5-min intervals.

The tourniquet duration was set to a minimum of 80 min. Tourniquet application was discontinued in operations lasting > 120 min (to ensure consistency in the analysis of tourniquet duration, cases with a duration below 80 min or above 120 min were excluded from the study). After tourniquet deflation, the recovery times of MEP data were recorded with minute warnings (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative neuromonitoring. a. Baseline data before tourniquet inflation. b. 50% decrease in MEP in the femoral nerve after tourniquet inflation. c. 100% decrease in all values under the tourniquet. d. All MEP decreases returned after the tourniquet was opened

The primary parameters were MEP values and tourniquet times. The primary parameters were as follows: MEP in the femoral nerve decreased by 50% compared with the baseline; MEP values in three nerves were lost; and all values returned in all nerves after tourniquet deflation. The secondary parameters were as follows: Measurement of Quadriceps tendon thickness, blood level of creatinine kinase, and VAS score.

Quadriceps Tendon Thickness (QTT): Quadriceps denervation is one of the most common and clinically significant adverse effects of tourniquet use. However, no study has evaluated the effect of tourniquets on the quadriceps tendon. Measurements were performed in the B mode using a Clarius Portable L7 HD3 Linear Ultrasound device (13 − 4 MHz). Measurements were performed in the supine position with the knee flexed at 30° and a pillow placed on the popliteal web to prevent anisotropy in the quadriceps. The European Society of Musculoskeletal Radiology performed the QTT measurements. The ultrasonography (USG) probe was placed parallel to the long axis of the femur in the sagittal plane, with the distal part of the patella at the midpoint of the proximal pole. USG was adjusted to a depth of 2 cm. QTT was measured at three levels: 5–10–15 mm from the proximal patella. The first hyperechoic region was identified as the boundary between the superficial and deep quadriceps tendon [10]. Measurements were made three times: preoperatively, on the first postoperative day, and 1 month after the first postoperative assessment (Fig. 3). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to evaluate the midterm effects of the tourniquet. In previous studies, the levels that clinically reflected the actual thickness of the quadriceps tendon were 15 mm and 30 mm proximal to the superior pole of the patella. In this study, QTT was measured separately at the preoperative and third postoperative MRI examinations by taking measurements from 15 to 30 mm proximal to the T1 sequence and averaging them [11, 12] (Fig. 3). Creatinine kinase is a biomarker of muscle damage that can be elevated by tourniquet use. Blood creatine kinase levels were evaluated preoperatively on the first postoperative day and in the first postoperative month. Total blood volume was calculated according to Nadler’s formula, and total blood volume loss was calculated according to the formula proposed by Meunier [13, 14]. Surgical pain scores were recorded using a visual analog scale (VAS). Thigh diameters were measured 10 cm proximal to the midpoint of the proximal pole of the patella. Bilateral lower extremity comparative EMG evaluation of the patients in the first postoperative month was performed by an experienced neurologist (M.B.B). Statistical analysis IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 was used for statistical analysis. The conformity of the quantitative data to a normal distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical analysis. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare quantitative variables that did not follow a normal distribution between the two groups. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for intragroup comparisons of quantitative variables with two follow-ups that did not show a normal distribution, the Friedman test was employed for intragroup comparisons of quantitative variables with three or more follow-ups, and the Dunn–Bonferroni test was employed to evaluate pairwise comparisons. Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare the qualitative data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents the basic demographic data. Both groups showed no significant differences in sex, age, side, and BMI. Moreover, the mean arterial pressure, extreme occlusion pressure (LOP), and tourniquet time measurements were not significantly different between the groups. Group 1 (LOP + 50 mmHg) exhibited a 50% decrease in the femoral nerve MEP value at an average of 47 min, whereas group 2 (LOP + 100 mmHg) demonstrated a 50% decrease at an average of 34 min (p < 0.001). The duration of a 100% decline in MEP for all nerves was significantly longer in Group 1 than in Group 2 (p = 0.007; p < 0.01). The total recovery time of MEP for all nerves was significantly shorter in Group 1 than in Group 2 (p = 0.007; p < 0.01) (Table 2). No clinical differences were detected between the mean surgical time and preoperative blood pressure values.

The two groups had similar preoperative and first postoperative day and month VAS scores (Table 4). The changes in VAS scores for patients on the first postoperative day and month compared with preoperative values were also not statistically significant between the groups (p > 0.05). The preoperative thigh diameter on the operated side was similar in both groups. However, on the first postoperative day, group 1 had a significantly smaller increase in thigh diameter than group 2 (0.46 ± 0.31 cm/1.47 ± 0.67 cm, p = 0.001; p < 0.01) (Table 5). Additionally, the two groups had similar total blood volumes, total estimated blood loss measurements, and preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin measurements (Table 5).

Discussion

Longer tourniquet use increases the risk of neurological complications, whereas the reperfusion interval reduces it. However, the literature should include evidence-based studies on the ideal reperfusion interval duration. In this study, we used the time to 100% return of MEP after tourniquet release as a parameter to indicate the reperfusion interval. Patients with low tourniquet pressure had a significantly shorter MEP return time than patients with high tourniquet pressure (group 1 mean, 8.67 ± 2.81 min; group 2 mean, 12.67 ± 3.14 min p = 0.007; p < 0.01). Based on this recovery time, a minimum reperfusion interval of 15 min was required to restore the limb and neural functions.

Olivecrona et al. conducted a prospective randomized controlled study to evaluate the effect of two different tourniquet pressure values on neurological complications in total knee arthroplasty surgeries using an electrophysiological study [6]. They included 18 patients who underwent electromyography (EMG) and quantitative sensory testing (QST) on the third postoperative day and the second postoperative month. The average tourniquet cuff pressure was 237 mmHg (LOP group, 223 mmHg; control group, 251 mmHg) and the average tourniquet time was 81 min. EMG detected denervation findings in only one patient. Motor response amplitudes in the peroneal nerve were not significantly lower in the operated leg than in the opposite extremity in the third and second postoperative months. The authors found no statistically significant difference in the motor and sensory NCVs and QST in the operated leg compared to the non-operated leg. The authors concluded that the risk of nerve injury is low when low tourniquet cuff pressure is used. Similar to other studies, they demonstrated through QST that tourniquets induced less damage to unmyelinated or small myelinated nerves. In the present study, no denervation was detected in the EMG evaluation performed during the first postoperative month. Consistent with the literature, no significant difference was observed in the amplitudes, NCVs, and latencies of the sural and superficial peroneal nerves between the operated side and the opposite side. These findings were related to the tourniquet affecting large, thick myelin-sheathed nerves, which is consistent with the existing data [15]. Moreover, no significant difference was detected in the tibial and peroneal nerve amplitudes, NCV, and latencies between the operated and opposite sides in the EMG tests performed during the first postoperative month. However, the tibial NCV on the operated side in group 1 was significantly higher than that in group 2 (p = 0.014; p < 0.05). This result indicates that a lower tourniquet pressure may be more beneficial than a higher pressure for nerve function.

Although the change in VAS score was lower in the low tourniquet pressure group on the first postoperative day, no significant difference was observed (group 1 VAS change, 1.58 ± 2.19; group 2, 2.83 ± 0.83, p = 0.068). According to this result, a lower tourniquet pressure may reduce postoperative pain slightly better than a higher pressure. Pinsonsark et al. compared the effects of different tourniquet pressures on postoperative pain scores and complications in a double-blind, randomized controlled study including 150 patients who underwent unilateral primary total knee arthroplasty [16]. In terms of applied pressure values, the patients were divided into three groups: systolic blood pressure (SBP) + 75 mmHg (group I), SBP + 100 mmHg (group II), and SBP + 150 mmHg (group III). The tourniquet area and surgical site pain scores were significantly lower in Group I at 24 h, 48 h, and 2 weeks after the operation than in Groups II and III.

The authors noted that an increase in thigh diameter did not differ significantly among the three groups at the 24th postoperative hour (group I, 0.90 ± 0.38; group II, 0.70 ± 1.63; group III, 0.86 ± 1.27 cm). [16] Unlike the previous study, the tourniquet effects on the quadriceps with the sample group were isolated, and a significant difference in thigh diameter change was observed between the two groups on the first postoperative day.

The hemogram change and total estimated blood volume loss were lower in the LOP + 50 mmHg group than in the LOP + 100 mmHg group; however, this did not result in a significant difference between the two groups. In a previous study, a significant difference was found among the three groups in terms of hemoglobin change and total blood volume loss at the 48th postoperative hour (hemogram: group I, 0.63 ± 0.54; group II, 0.56 ± 0.44; group III, 0.53 ± 0.44; total blood volume loss group I, 458.9 ± 38.4; group II, 462.8 ± 32.3; group III, 454.5 ± 26.4 mL) [16]. Low tourniquet pressure resulted in less blood volume loss; hence, the surgery was performed with controlled hemostasis, and microvascular structures were spared by the tourniquet.

The effect of tourniquet application on the quadriceps tendon has not been evaluated in existing literature. In a study on quadriceps tendon morphology, Klich et al. evaluated the thickness and stiffness changes in the quadriceps and patellar tendons in 15 male elite sprinters and 15 male elite endurance track cyclists using ultrasonography and a myotonometer [17]. QTT was measured from the proximal part of the superior patellar pole to 5–10–15 mm before and after the race. Participants in both sprint and endurance categories showed a significant increase in QTT by approximately 6–7% (p < 0.05). This increase was explained by the increased interfiber edema secondary to tendon damage and deterioration of the microfiber structure. We used a similar evaluation in our study, in which we evaluated the acute effect of the tourniquet on the quadriceps tendon using USG. A statistically significant increase in QTT was detected in the high tourniquet pressure group on the first postoperative day compared with the other groups (preoperative to first postoperative day QTT change). This increase is related to an increase in interfiber edema due to the acute trauma effect of the tourniquet.

D. De Boer et al. examined tendon morphology after immobilization. In this study, 17 male patients were immobilized using a single lower-extremity sling [18]. The muscle and tendon adaptation mechanisms were evaluated and compared with those in the control group. The knee extensor mechanism torque decreased by 21.0% ± 7.1% in the third week (p < 0.001). When the quadriceps muscle cross-sectional area was evaluated in the third week, a significant decrease of 10% was observed compared to the baseline and control groups (p < 0.001). In the third week of immobilization, when tendon morphology was evaluated, no significant changes in the tendon cross-sectional area or tendon length were observed. Tendon stiffness decreased by 29.3% (p < 0.005) after 23 days. The maximum tendon strength decreased significantly by 29.3% (p < 0.005) in the third week after immobilization. The authors showed that, while the elasticity and Young’s modulus of the tendon increased in the third week after immobilization, parameters such as tendon thickness and length did not change significantly, unlike the muscles. In this study, we performed to evaluate the long-term effects of a tourniquet on the quadriceps tendon. Evaluations conducted during the preoperative period and at the third-month follow-up indicated that the QTT did not change significantly, and no significant difference was found between the groups in which two different tourniquet pressures were applied.

This study has some limitations. Given the lack of precedent in the field, our study was designed with a power analysis that included the lowest number of patients possible, with the aim of examining the relationship between neuromonitoring data and tourniquet pressures and durations. Nevertheless, the relatively small sample size is a potential limitation of this study. The analyzed patients underwent surgery because of fractures, which may have affected tendon measurements owing to increased postoperative immobilization. Patients who experienced trauma were also included in the study; hence, the blood test (creatine kinase and hemoglobin) values may have been affected. Finally, the pain scores specific to the tourniquet area and other morphological features of the quadriceps tendon were not evaluated.

Conclusion

This is the first prospective randomized controlled study to demonstrate the effects of tourniquets with IONM. USG and thigh diameter measurements demonstrated that the application of low tourniquet pressure had a significantly lower negative effect on quadriceps tendon morphology in the acute period. In the group in which low tourniquet pressure was applied, the results revealed that the innervations of the lower-extremity nerves were affected later. Nerve conduction recovered at an average of 10 min after deflation in the low tourniquet pressure group, which was 4 min earlier than that in the high tourniquet pressure group.
