Microleakage of luting cements in CAD/CAM pediatric zirconia crowns: an in vitro study
Authors: Sukitta Iampinitkul, Pisaisit Chaijareenont, Wannapa Chinadet
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe durability of pediatric zirconia crowns for primary teeth is influenced by the choice of luting cement, with the effectiveness of the cement being directly correlated to its ability to reduce microleakage. This in vitro study aimed to assess and compare the microleakage of custom-made zirconia crowns (CZCs) and prefabricated zirconia crowns (PZCs) on primary maxillary incisors when luted with self-adhesive resin cement, resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC), and bioactive cement. Sixty primary maxillary incisors were prepared and allocated into two groups, each corresponding to the two types of crowns. These groups were further divided into three subgroups each to test the different luting cements. Following the cementation process and thermocycling, the specimens were immersed in a 2% methylene blue solution for microleakage evaluation. The analysis involved sectioning the teeth and examining them under a stereomicroscope. Statistical analysis, using two-way ANOVA and post hoc Dunnett T3 tests (p < 0.05), revealed significant differences in microleakage among the cements. The study found that PZCs luted with RMGIC showed the highest level of microleakage, whereas those luted with bioactive cement exhibited the lowest, positioning bioactive cement as the preferable choice for minimizing microleakage. This finding illustrates the critical importance of selecting appropriate luting cements to optimize the clinical outcomes of zirconia crown restorations in pediatric dentistry, focusing on reducing microleakage to ensure the restoration’s durability and success.Keywords: Computer-aided design, In vitro, Microleakage, Prefabricated zirconia crowns, Primary teethSubject terms: Health care, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Full coverage restoration for primary teeth differs from the techniques used for permanent teeth. It is frequently achieved with prefabricated crowns, which are offered in sets with diverse sizes and shapes to facilitate adaptation and reduce time requirements1,2. Zirconia, a widely utilised material for fabricating crowns on permanent teeth, has been increasingly adopted in pediatric dentistry as prefabricated crowns3. Prefabricated zirconia crowns (PCZs) are increasingly accepted as an aesthetic restoration for primary tooth due to their high aesthetic appearance, superior colour stability, reduced likelihood of fracture or chipping, great biocompatibility, high wear and corrosion resistance4–6. Prefabricated zirconia crowns showed significantly less plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation when compared to conventional stainless-steel crowns4,7,8. Moreover, the oral health–related quality of life of preschool children that were restored with PZCs was significantly better than that of those who received resin-bonded composite strip crowns9,10. However, concerns about the retention of PZCs have arisen due to their limitations. As these crowns are non-adjustable and not amenable to contouring, they exhibit wider margins than other restoration options, exposing the luting cement to the oral environment. This exposure increases the risk of cement degradation and dissolution, potentially leading to shrinkage and microleakage. Primary maxillary incisors, with their smaller bonding surfaces and tapered, non-square shape, present unique challenges for achieving a precise marginal seal, unlike posterior teeth which typically have larger, flatter, and more square-shaped surfaces that facilitate bonding. This anatomical difference makes the choice and performance of luting cement particularly critical for anterior teeth to ensure effective retention and minimize microleakage. Therefore, PZCs’ marginal seal relies heavily on the performance of the luting cement. However, limited evidence about which luting cements exhibit the least microleakage between PZCs and primary teeth exists3,11.

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) and resin modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) can be used to cement zirconia crowns, particularly in situations where ease of cementation and moisture tolerance are priorities. It is commonly chosen due to its ability to adhere under suboptimal isolation conditions, and its fluoride-releasing properties provide additional benefits in pediatric patients. However, they have high water solubility and weaker bonding strength compared to resin-based cements, which can lead to a compromised marginal seal and increased microleakage over time5,12–14. Resin cements are highly recommended as luting agents for ceramic restoration in permanent teeth due to their impressive strength, reduced microleakage, and ability to reinforce the final restoration, bond to various substrates, and enhance retention15,16. However, conventional resin cements require dental adhesives to enhance their bonding to enamel and dentin, which entails additional steps and time and limits their usage in pediatric dentistry. To address this issue, self-adhesive resin cement was developed, simplifying the cement application process. It combines the ease of application found in standard luting materials with the enhanced mechanical properties and bonding abilities of conventional resin cements. These self-adhesive cements are gaining popularity for use on permanent teeth, but evidence concerning their application on primary teeth is limited17,18.

Recently, bioactive cements designed specifically for pediatric dentistry have been introduced. According to the manufacturers’ claims, these cements present an alternative to GIC because they have superior physical and chemical attributes. These include the ability to release fluoride, calcium, and phosphate, thereby promoting hydroxyapatite formation, ensuring good biocompatibility, reducing microleakage, and enabling bonding to zirconia. However, the evidence for their utilisation with PZCs remains insufficient12,19.

Microleakage, the clinically imperceptible ingress and egress of fluids, bacteria, and ions at the tooth–cement interface, is a significant concern in the success of dental restorations. It can lead to secondary caries, post-operative sensitivity, the development of pulpal pathology, and even the failure of the restoration. Therefore, understanding and minimising microleakage in the context of pediatric zirconia crowns is of paramount importance for the longevity and stability of these restorations19,20. This is particularly relevant when using prefabricated crowns, which are not custom-made and may result in open margins, making the microleakage of the luting cement even more critical.

While PZCs have gained traction in pediatric dentistry, their prefabricated nature and the high associated costs have raised concerns among dental professionals. Due to their non-customized design, PZCs often exhibit variability in cement space and marginal adaptation, which can affect retention and increase the risk of microleakage. Custom-made zirconia crowns (CZCs), which are designed to each tooth using CAD/CAM technology, address these challenges by providing a more precise fit and uniform cement thickness across all areas. This precise fit is hypothesized to reduce microleakage compared to PZCs. Agreeing with this perspective, Hanafi et al.21 introduced locally fabricated PZCs using CAD/CAM systems, which provided a cost reduction of approximately 50%. Moreover, their study demonstrated no significant differences in gingival index, plaque index, oral hygiene index–simplified, or bleeding on probing between commercial and locally fabricated PZCs at 6-month follow-ups. Consequently, CAD/CAM PZCs seem promising as an alternative restoration method for primary maxillary incisors. However, no studies have compared CAD/CAM PZCs with CZCs to directly evaluate their performance, particularly regarding marginal adaptation and microleakage.

This in vitro study aimed to assess and compare the microleakage of CZCs and PZCs on primary maxillary incisors when luted with bioactive cement (BioCem®), self-adhesive resin cement (RelyX™ U200), and RMGIC (RelyX™ Luting2). Given the lack of studies comparing microleakage across these crown and cement combinations in pediatric dentistry, our investigation is designed to fill this gap in the literature. The study was guided by two null hypotheses: first, that there would be no significant difference in microleakage between CZCs and PZCs, and second, that there would be no significant difference in microleakage among the three luting cements used.

Materials and methods

Design

This study has followed the standardized Checklist for Reporting in vitro Studies (the CRIS guidelines) for in vitro studies as discussed in the 2014 concept note22. All methods were conducted in accordance with the CRIS guidelines as well as all ethical relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University (CMU), Thailand (approval: 22/4/2022, No.14/ 2022).

Sample collection

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: human primary maxillary central and lateral incisors that had been extracted due to dental caries, prolonged retention, or trauma. The exclusion criteria were unrestorable teeth, in which less than three-quarters of the crown remained. Written informed consent was obtained from each legal guardian before tooth extraction. The sample consists of 60 primary maxillary incisors, each presenting a minimum of three-quarters of the crown and at least 1.0 mm of root surface surrounding the cementoenamel junction. After extraction, the teeth were cleansed of any persistent gross organic debris, cleaned with normal saline, and then preserved in a 0.1% thymol solution. A power calculation was conducted to determine the optimal number of teeth for each group based on a significance level of 0.05 and an error limit of 10%. To account for dropouts during the experiment, the sample size was increased by 10% to reach a minimum of at least 10 samples per group.

Sample preparation

Each tooth was wiped with sterile gauze before any further preparation. For teeth with carious lesions, all caries were thoroughly removed to ensure a consistent and reliable bonding surface. In the case of pulp exposure during tooth preparation, Vitrebond™ (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied as a liner to protect the pulp exposure site. It is important to note that no pulp treatment was performed in this study, as the teeth were extracted primary teeth. Each tooth was securely mounted in a custom-made block designed to hold the tooth in place for preparation. The mounting process involved the application of self-cured clear-coated epoxy resin (RUNGART, Bangkok, Thailand). To ensure a standardized protocol, each tooth was embedded in the epoxy resin 1 mm below the cementoenamel junction and held in the long axis position. The epoxy resin was allowed to polymerize completely over a 24-hour period, and any samples that did not meet these criteria were excluded from the analysis. The teeth were prepared according to standard guidelines for pediatric zirconia crowns, which align with the preparation recommendations for NuSmile® crowns, a widely used brand in pediatric dentistry: The initial preparation was performed by removing 1.5–2.0 mm from the incisal edge with an egg-shaped diamond bur (FG379, Intensiv, Montagnola, Switzerland) and 0.6–0.8 mm per side for proximal reduction with a pointed cone diamond bur (FG858; D2 Intensiv, Montagnola, Switzerland). The bur was replaced for each tooth. Then, the entire clinical crown was reduced by 20–30% (or 0.5–1.25 mm) on all planes of the tooth. The reductions followed the natural contours of the clinical crowns and met in a thin, tapered incisal edge with feather-edge margins. Finally, all sharp angles were rounded.

To ensure uniformity and consistency of tooth reduction, depth cuts were made using depth-calibrated burs to ensure that the amount of reduction was accurate and consistent across all samples. A survey was conducted to verify that the teeth were properly aligned for the path of insertion, confirming that the prepared teeth would accept the crowns without interference. Additionally, all teeth were prepared by the same experienced operator to maintain a consistent technique throughout the study.

The study involved a total of 60 primary maxillary incisors, which were divided into two main groups:

Laboratory work

Group 1: custom-made zirconia crowns (CZCs)

The prepared teeth were scanned with a digital intraoral scanner (CEREC PrimeScan, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA). Subsequently, a CAD design software program (CEREC PrimeScan 5, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA) was utilised to design the CZCs. The cement space was set at 80 μm and the zirconia crown thickness was set at 1.0 mm. The finished design was then transferred to CAM software (CEREC inLab Software, Dentsply Sirona, New York, NY, USA), and milled from a zirconia disc (Katana® Zirconia HT, Kuraray Noritake, Tokyo, Japan) on a milling machine (Inlab MC X5, Dentsply-Sirona, New York, NY, USA). After the milling procedure, the crowns were fired in a furnace (CEREC SpeedFire, Dentsply-Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) for crystallisation and glazing according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, until the final restoration crowns were completed.

Group 2: prefabricated zirconia crowns (PZCs)

NuSmile® ZR Zirconia Anterior crowns (NuSmile Ltd., Houston, TX, USA) were used as a design model for fabricating the prefabricated zirconia crowns (PZCs) in this study. NuSmile® Try-in crowns were first used to determine the appropriate size by achieving a passive fit to the prepared tooth. After the correct size was selected, the corresponding NuSmile® ZR crown was scanned with a digital oral scanner (CEREC PrimeScan 5, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA) to generate an STL file. This digital file was used to mill the PZCs from the same zirconia disc material (Katana® Zirconia HT, Kuraray Noritake, Tokyo, Japan) used for the custom-made crowns (CZCs) in Group 1, ensuring material consistency across groups. Cementation

Before cementation, all the teeth were immersed in distilled water for 24 h. Then, the crowns were cemented onto the prepared teeth using the appropriate cements for the subgroup. The operator was blinded to the type of cement used. All procedures were performed according to the relevant manufacturer’s instructions. In this study, no surface treatment was applied to the zirconia crowns. This decision was made to evaluate the performance of the luting cements under standard clinical conditions, where surface treatments may not always be performed, particularly in pediatric dentistry, where simplicity and efficiency are prioritized.

To ensure a stable position until the cement had set, finger pressure was applied axially for 10 min after the cement mixture was initiated and light cured with a dental curing light (EliparTM, 3 M Deutschland GmbH, Neuss, Germany). The excess cement was manually removed and allowed to bench set for 1 h before being placed in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h. Afterwards, artificial aging was conducted by subjecting the teeth to 500 cycles in a thermocycling device (HWB332R, CWB332R, TC301) between 5°to 55 °C water baths, in accordance with the latest International Standardization Organization specifications (ISO/TS11405:2015). Each cycle involved a dwell time of 30 s and a transfer time of 5 s.

Dye penetration test

After thermocycling, all teeth were removed from the self-cured clear-coat epoxy resin. The root surface beneath the crown margin was sealed for 1.0 mm with two layers of nail varnish. Then, the teeth were immersed in 2% methylene blue aqueous solution dye for 24 h at 37 °C. After immersion, the teeth were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to eliminate excess dye, and then, they were left to air-dry. Subsequently, the teeth were embedded in self-cured clear-coat epoxy resin (RUNGART®, Bangkok, Thailand) and sectioned twice in the buccolingual direction to create one 1 mm-thick slice using an Isomet 1000 precision saw. The saw was fitted with a 6-inch diameter, 0.7 mm-thick diamond wheel and cooled with water throughout the cutting process.

Microleakage measurement

The sectioned slices, which were 1 mm thick, were examined on both sides using a stereomicroscope (SZX7; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a digital camera (Canon EOS 6D, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) at 16X magnification. Linear dye penetration was assessed at four measurement points per tooth, with two points on the labial surface and two points on the palatal surface. The 1 mm sectioning allowed for detailed measurement at these points. The extent of dye penetration was measured as the distance (µm) from the external surface of the crown to the point where no dye could be observed. The average of the two measurements from each surface (labial and palatal) was taken to ensure accuracy and consistency. Two examiners performed this measurement independently using ImageJ software (Image J, MA, USA).

Data analysis

The mean of all four measurement points was computed for each tooth, and the results are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs). To assess reproducibility, both the intra- and inter-examiner intraclass correlations (ICCs) were calculated. Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS 23.0 software (IBM SPSS Science, Chicago, IL, USA). A two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was conducted, followed by a Dunnett T3 post hoc test, to evaluate the statistical associations among the groups. Throughout the study, a significance level of 0.05 was adopted.

Results

A total of 60 primary maxillary incisors were included in this study, with a distribution between CZCs and PZCs. The sample consisted of 27 male and 33 female subjects, with an age range of 3 to 13 years (mean age: 8.21 ± 2.35 years). The teeth were distributed among central and lateral incisors on both the right and left sides. The primary causes of extraction were caries, prolonged retention, and a small number due to trauma, with caries being the most common reason. Statistical analyses, including Chi-square and One-way ANOVA, confirmed that there were no significant differences between the groups regarding gender, age, tooth type, or cause of extraction.

Before conducting the experimental measurements, two examiners underwent calibration. The inter-examiner agreement showed a high ICCs of 0.983, while the intra-examiner agreement was even higher, at an ICC of 0.996. Marginal microleakage was observed in all experimental groups. Descriptive statistics are presented as the mean and standard deviation of the average microleakage for the various combinations of crown and luting cement that were tested. Among these combinations, the greatest microleakage was found in PZCs cemented with RMGIC (4,123.93 ± 839.47 μm), while the least was observed in PZCs cemented with bioactive cement (1,797.77 ± 1,453.44 μm). Following, in descending order, were CZCs cemented with bioactive cement (2,116.47 ± 1,548.13 μm), CZCs cemented with self-adhesive resin cement (2,933.07 ± 1,519.19 μm), CZCs cemented with RMGIC (3,139.65 ± 1,265.31 μm), and PZCs cemented with self-adhesive resin cement (3,262.69 ± 2,010.30 μm).

The two-way ANOVA test revealed statistically significant interactions between the types of crowns and cement used and statistically significant differences in microleakage results among all materials (p = 0.000). CZCs cemented with RMGIC exhibited significantly higher microleakage than CZCs cemented with bioactive cement (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference in microleakage was observed when CZCs were cemented with self-adhesive resin cement. Meanwhile, PZCs cemented with RMGIC and self-adhesive resin cement showed significantly higher microleakage than PZCs cemented with bioactive cement (p = 0.000, p < 0.01, respectively). Additionally, PZCs cemented with RMGIC displayed significantly higher microleakage than CZCs cemented with RMGIC (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2).

The crowns cemented with self-adhesive resin cement and bioactive cement, whether PZCs or CZCs, exhibited microleakage at the cement–tooth interface. The crowns cemented with RMGIC exhibited microleakage at both the cement–tooth and the cement–crown interfaces (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate microleakage in CAD/CAM PZCs and CZCs when luted with various types of cement. The results indicate that while the crown type whether custom-made or prefabricated had no significant overall effect on microleakage when the same type of cement was used, the type of cement played a more critical role in influencing microleakage across both crown types. Each group exhibited varying degrees of marginal microleakage, suggesting that complete prevention remains elusive, regardless of the crown type or cement used. Bioactive cement consistently resulted in the lowest microleakage in both CZCs and PZCs, while RMGIC showed higher levels, particularly in prefabricated crowns. This finding emphasizes that the type of cement is a more crucial factor than the crown type itself in minimizing microleakage.

Microleakage is especially important in pediatric dentistry due to the nature of prefabricated zirconia crowns. Unlike custom-made crowns used in permanent dentition, prefabricated crowns are not specifically designed for each tooth, which may result in a less precise fit and potentially more open margins. This inherent gap increases the risk of microleakage over time, making the choice of luting cement critical to the long-term success of these restorations. Studies like Pengpue et al.14 and Chinadet et al.13​ have shown that luting cement plays a significant role in the retention and overall performance of zirconia crowns. Bioactive cement was found to enhance the tensile bond strength between PZCs and primary maxillary incisors, compared to RMGIC and resin cements, which is consistent with the findings in this study where bioactive cement demonstrated the lowest microleakage.

To expand on this, the two-way ANOVA results further clarified the interactions between crown types and cements, revealing the complex dynamics that influence microleakage. CZCs cemented with RMGIC exhibited more pronounced microleakage compared to those luted with bioactive cement. However, when CZCs were paired with self-adhesive resin cement, the difference in microleakage levels was smaller. In contrast, PZCs demonstrated more pronounced microleakage when cemented with both RMGIC and self-adhesive resin cement, compared to bioactive cement. These findings suggest that microleakage is influenced not just by the crown or cement type alone, but by the specific combinations of crowns and cements used.

Furthermore, the reduced microleakage observed in crowns cemented with BioCem® bioactive cement in our study is likely attributable to its unique properties, particularly those designed for use in pediatric dentistry. BioCem® creates a strong cement–dentin interface through the formation of hydroxyapatite tags, enhancing adhesion and minimizing gaps that could allow fluid ingress. This, along with the release of beneficial ions like calcium and phosphate, contributes to a more durable bond and reduced microleakage over time. Chinadet et al.13 also found that applying surface treatments to pediatric zirconia crowns improved bond strength, which supports the idea that surface treatments paired with bioactive cements could further enhance the long-term stability of pediatric zirconia restorations. While our findings are consistent with those of Stepp et al.19, where BioCem® demonstrated better performance compared to traditional cements such as Ketac Cem® in permanent teeth, it is important to acknowledge the lack of studies specifically focused on microleakage in primary teeth. Primary teeth have higher organic content and more permeable dentin, which can affect how cements perform in terms of bonding and microleakage. The limited available literature indicates the need for more comprehensive studies on the behavior of luting cements in primary teeth, particularly in the context of long-term clinical performance and caries prevention. Such research could provide valuable insights into optimizing crown retention and minimizing microleakage in pediatric dentistry.

In this study, crowns cemented with RMGIC manifested pronounced microleakage, likely stemming from intrinsic material properties, such as its high water solubility and diminished bonding capabilities to both tooth structures and zirconia. A discrepancy in the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) between RMGIC and natural teeth can induce stresses, which, when compounded by 500 thermocycles to simulate the aging of restorative material, generate discernible gaps at both the crown–cement and cement–tooth interfaces23–25. The presence of more noticeable dye and cracks in RMGIC than in resin cements (Fig. 3), implying a potential comparative structural weakness24,26. Conversely, a self-adhesive resin cement, manifested suboptimal sealing, likely due to its acidic monomers, which derive from phosphoric acid methacrylates interacting with inorganic fillers, struggling to penetrate the dentin’s smear layer without additional preparatory step. This resulted in inconsistent hybrid layers, which were further exacerbated by factors such as acid-resistant mineral deposits blocking adhesive systems from enamel rods and residual moisture impeding the resin’s penetration into dentin collagen. Moreover, the presence of tertiary amines in certain cements can hinder polymerisation, thus reducing bond strength26–29.

The dye penetration test was chosen for this study due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ability to provide clear, visual quantification of microleakage along the crown-tooth interface23–25. It is one of the most widely used methods in dental research for evaluating marginal leakage26. However, this method has certain limitations. It measures linear penetration rather than volumetric leakage, which may not fully capture the extent of microleakage observed under more clinically dynamic conditions. Additionally, the test relies on visual assessment, which introduces some subjectivity to the results. Although the test is a widely accepted standard for in vitro microleakage studies, it does not replicate the full complexity of oral conditions, such as thermal changes, saliva interaction, or mechanical stresses. Despite these limitations, the dye penetration test remains a valuable tool for comparative in vitro studies, providing reproducible and consistent results.

Another distinguishing feature of the two crown sets evaluated was the cementation space, both in terms of thickness and uniformity. Cement thickness, or the space between the restoration and the prepared tooth, significantly influences the effectiveness and longevity of ceramic restorations15,30. Increased resin cement thickness tends to compromise bond strength, primarily due to challenges such as incomplete cement curing and the implications of polymerisation shrinkage26,31,32. While reducing luting space is advocated to improve restoration fit, a consensus on the ideal dimension remains elusive. A study by Martinez-Rus33 suggested that a cement space from 50 to 100 μm is ideal for resin cement performance. However, narrow cement spaces do not always guarantee a superior seal; for instance, a study by Nayyef and Ibraheem26 found that a marginal cement space thickness of 25 μm resulted in less microleakage than a 0 μm cement space.

For this investigation, an 80 μm cement space was used to assess CZCs, a decision that was guided by our preliminary analyses. The results showed no marked differences in microleakage between CZCs and PZCs, except for crowns cemented with RMGIC. While bond strength is not directly tied to microleakage, the thickness of the cement space can influence the bond strength, with effect varying depending on the type of cement used. Significantly, CZCs with RMGIC exhibited almost double the microleakage as PZCs cemented with Bioactive cement. Further research is needed to understand how varying cement space thicknesses might also influence microleakage.

However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this in vitro study. Although thermocycling was employed to simulate clinical conditions, these controlled settings cannot fully replicate the dynamic complexity of the oral environment, such as varying pH, saliva composition, and mechanical forces. Additionally, while this study focused on microleakage, the storage of specimens in distilled water for 24 h does not allow the bioactive properties of the cement, such as fluoride release or ion exchange, to be fully expressed. Future studies should consider longer aging periods in clinically relevant storage media, such as simulated body fluid (SBF) or artificial saliva, to evaluate these bioactive effects. Further research could also expand on this work by exploring a wider range of luting cements and investigating the impact of surface treatments, such as sandblasting or the use of a 10-MDP-containing primer, on microleakage. Finally, extending the investigation to include primary molars, in addition to anterior teeth, would provide a broader understanding of microleakage across different tooth types.

Conclusion

This study aimed to address the gap in literature comparing microleakage between CZCs and PZCs luted with various cements. The null hypotheses stated that there would be no significant differences in microleakage between crown types or among the three luting cements. The findings led to the rejection of the second null hypothesis, as bioactive cement consistently demonstrated significantly lower levels of microleakage compared to self-adhesive resin cement and RMGIC. However, the first null hypothesis was accepted, as no significant differences were observed in overall microleakage between CZCs and PZCs when the same cement was used. An exception was noted with RMGIC, where CZCs exhibited lower microleakage than PZCs. These results suggest that bioactive cement is a more favorable choice for minimizing microleakage, particularly in PZCs. In contrast, RMGIC may be less suitable for either crown type due to its higher microleakage.
