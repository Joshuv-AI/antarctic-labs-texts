Measuring and understanding geographic inequities in physician distribution in the Philippines: a multi-method study protocol
Authors: Veincent Christian F Pepito, Ryan Gabriel C Molen, Julius R Migriño, Theo Prudencio Juhani Z Capeding, Ann Rose D Payumo, Hazel Ivy M Jeremias, Karl R Ubial, William C Tiu, Manuel M Dayrit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Philippines faces physician shortages, exacerbated by their propensity to practise in urban centres. Despite government placement programmes, recruitment and retention of physicians in many areas throughout the country remain difficult. This study aims to: (1) describe the distribution of general physicians and board-certified specialist physicians and (2) identify the individual, work environment, local environment, national environment and international environment factors that may influence physicians to establish their practice in areas with below-optimal physician-to-population ratios in the Philippines.Methods and analysisTo describe the distribution of general physicians and board-certified specialist physicians, we will map Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth)-accredited physicians at the regional and at the provincial level, superimposing medical schools and corresponding training institutions. To identify the individual, work environment, local environment, national environment and international environment factors that may influence physicians to establish their practice in areas of the Philippines with below optimal physician-to-population ratios, we will conduct three rounds of data collection: (1) an online survey of current Philippine Department of Health (DOH)-deployed physicians; (2) online or face-to-face focus group discussions and key informant interviews with a subset of DOH-deployed physicians, senior general and specialist physicians practising in Metro Manila and outside and DOH personnel involved in the implementation of these physician placement programmes at the national, regional and local level; and (3) a stated preference experiment administered to resident physicians of the specialties under study to determine which government interventions can encourage them to establish their practice in areas with lower than optimal physician-to-population ratios in the Philippines.Ethics and disseminationWe have obtained ethics clearance from the Ateneo de Manila University School of Medicine and Public Health Research Ethics Committee. We will disseminate our findings in online or face-to-face dissemination sessions with the DOH, Philippine Health Insurance Corporation, Association of Philippine Medical Colleges, the Philippine Statistics Authority, the Professional Regulation Commission Board of Medicine, the Commission on Higher Education and partner specialty societies. We will also publish our results in peer-reviewed medical or public health journals.Keywords: Health Workforce, Geographical mapping, Physicians

Introduction

The shortage of physicians and their maldistribution is a barrier towards Universal Health Coverage in the Philippines. More physicians practise in Metro Manila, the country’s capital, than in other regions likely due to better economic opportunities.1–3 This maldistribution exacerbates the shortage of physicians in the country, contributing to widely different levels of access to both primary and specialty care across the Philippines.4 This disparate levels of access to care explain why health outcomes of Filipinos in regions outside the Metro Manila are worse than Filipinos living in the capital.5

Aware of the maldistribution of physicians in the country, the Philippine Department of Health (DOH) has launched programmes to address this issue. In 1993, the Philippine National Rural Physician Placement Program, more commonly known as the Doctors to the Barrios (DTTB) Program was established to deploy physicians to areas which lack community physicians.3 The passage of the country’s Universal Health Coverage Act ushered in the establishment of the National Health Workforce Support System (NHWSS) to support local government units in improving health targets such as the population to health workforce ratios.6

One enabling mechanism of the NHWSS is the recently established Post-Residency Placement Program (PRPP), formerly known as the Post-Residency Deployment Program.7 8 The PRPP aims to address the inequitable distribution of medical specialists and limited access to specialty care in many Filipino communities. This is done by deploying recent graduates of paediatrics, internal medicine, general surgery, obstetrics and gynaecology and anaesthesiology residency training programmes of DOH-retained hospitals in areas in the country with shortages of these specialty physicians for 1 year,7 which was recently expanded to include recent graduates of emergency medicine, orthopaedics, ophthalmology, otorhinolaryngology, psychiatry and radiology.8

Another enabling mechanism of the NHWSS is the Espesyalista Para Sa Bayan (EPSB, literally Specialists for the Nation) Program which aims to address the inequitable distribution of medical specialists and limited access to specialty care. The programme deploys board-certified family medicine physicians, general surgeons, anaesthesiologists, obstetricians and gynaecologists, paediatricians, internists and psychiatrists (the EPSB specialties) for 2 years to priority Level 1 and 2 health facilities in areas of the country with limited access to specialist care.9 This is meant to help the country reach optimal physician-to-population ratios (table 1).

The EPSB Program was initially established in June 2022, while its interim guidelines were only released in December 2023. Due to its nascency, there are still no studies focusing on the government programme or on interventions encouraging specialists to practice in areas with few specialists. However, initial implementation challenges have been observed, particularly in encouraging long-term practice in areas with fewer than optimal number of specialists-to-population in the country.9 There are also plans to expand the EPSB Program to include other specialties, such as pathologists and radiologists.

As the shortage and maldistribution of physicians is a global phenomenon, previous studies in other countries and in the Philippines have used novel methodologies to study this phenomenon. Mapping studies have been conducted to map the distribution of dermatologists and surgeons in the Philippines.10 11 Qualitative studies have also been done to study barriers to universal health coverage implementation in the country2 and in retention of physicians in the DTTB Program.3 Stated preference experiments have previously been used to study enrolment and retention issues in human resources for health research in both developing and developed countries. In this review, non-financial incentives were found to strongly influence job preference, sometimes more than financial incentives. In Indonesia, stated preference experiments found that job preference among medical students varied across type of medical school, sex, hometown and socio-economic background.12 In the Philippines, stated preference experiments have been used in studying work preferences for rural health job postings among medical interns.13

However, the last published study on DTTB retention predates two major developments3: The COVID-19 pandemic and Republic Act No. 11 509 or the Doktor Para sa Bayan (literally Doctor for the Nation) Act. This scholarship programme aims to provide generous scholarships in partner state universities and private medical schools, training physicians who will eventually become DTTBs.14 There are also no studies assessing the retention and unintended consequences of the novel physician placement programmes of the DOH, which can serve as a lesson for other countries as they develop their programmes to address shortages and maldistribution of physicians and other health workers.

This research programme aims to: (1) describe the distribution of general physicians and board-certified specialist physicians in the Philippines and (2) to identify the individual, work environment, local environment, national environment and international environment factors that may influence physicians to establish their practice in areas of the Philippines with lower than optimal physician-to-population ratios.

Methods and analysis

This multi-methods research programme is split into three work packages to answer its two research objectives. The study will run for 2 years from 1 January 2025 to 31 December 2026.

Work package one: mapping physician distribution

The first part involves the estimation of physician-to-population ratios for all physicians, general physicians and the following medical specialties: internal medicine, paediatrics, family medicine, psychiatry, obstetrics and gynaecology, general surgery, anaesthesiology, pathology and radiology. Because of the country’s thrust towards universal health coverage and the positioning of the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) as the sole purchaser of health services in the country,2 15 the numerator will be PhilHealth-accredited physicians. We will download the May 2025 list of PhilHealth-accredited physicians from their website.16 Personally identifying information such as names, home addresses and contact details will be redacted and sex, age and location of practice will remain as data for analysis and mapping. Total enumeration of all PhilHealth-accredited physicians will be used to ensure thoroughness and accuracy.

The denominator of all physician-to-population ratios will be the country’s 2025 mid-year population data, disaggregated to age groups and by regional and provincial levels. These data will be obtained from the Philippine Statistics Authority. We will then use these data to estimate the ratio of physicians (disaggregating per specialisation) to their target population at the national, regional and provincial levels (eg, paediatricians to number of children, obstetricians and gynaecologists to number of women, etc). We will transform these physician-to-population ratios into choropleths showing these distributions at the provincial level for all physicians, general physicians and the specialties to be studied. While highly urbanised cities in the Philippines are independent from provincial governments, this mapping study counts these cities under their province.

To further understand supply-side dynamics of physician maldistribution, we will also map as overlays to the choropleths a spot map of medical schools as of March 2025, which was obtained from the Association of Philippine Medical Colleges and residency training programmes, obtained from the partner medical societies. For all physicians and general physicians choropleths, the spot map overlays are the medical schools, while for the specialty society choropleths, the spot map overlays are their corresponding residency training programmes. We will also estimate regional and national level Gini coefficients for each physician cadre to quantify the degree of inequality of physician distribution for each cadre. In addition, we will also estimate how many of each physician type is needed to meet the ‘optimal’ physician to population ratio at the regional and the provincial level (table 1).

Work package two: assessing retention in DOH physician placement programmes

The second work package is a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study involving a survey of physicians in the three DOH physician placement programmes (ie, DTTB, PRPP and EPSB) as of 2025 using the Modified Stayer’s Questionnaire (MSQ).3 The MSQ is an instrument to measure perceptions on practising medicine in areas with very few physicians for use in Philippine settings. The participants’ answers will further characterise the individual, work environment, local environment, national environment and international environment factors influencing the physicians’ choice of establishing their practice in areas of the country with below optimal physician to population ratios. The DOH Central Office will cascade a link to the online MSQ to all the physicians of the placement programmes. The research team will supplement this cascade through social media dissemination. The survey will run from March 2025 to December 2025. Survey data will be analysed descriptively for each placement programme (DTTB, PRPP and EPSB). There are 739 deployed DTTBs, 1266 deployed PRPPs and 78 deployed EPSBs in 2025 who were eligible to accomplish the MSQ (Tiu and Ubial, personal communication).

The data from the MSQ will be supplemented and contextualised with data from online key informant interviews (KIIs) with selected MSQ respondents from the different placement programmes who are willing to share their experiences and perspectives. KIIs and focus group discussions (FGDs) will also be carried out with DOH personnel involved in the design and implementation of these physician placement programmes at the national, regional and local levels. To get the perspectives of specialty societies, we will also conduct KIIs and FGDs with senior consultants with a minimum of 10 years of practice who are practising in Metro Manila and another set of senior consultants with similar years of experience who are practising outside Metro Manila. The specialists to be interviewed may be referred to by the partner societies according to whether they can give insights on the questions covered by the interview. The interview will cover questions on their decision-making process in choosing their field of specialisation, the factors considered in choosing the location of their practice, the nature of their practice (eg, typical patient demographics, time spent in private practice vs public service what factors influence enrolment and retention in the DTTB and EPSB programmes and recommendations to improve such in the two placement programmes. KIIs and FGDs will run from April 2025 to January 2026 and were carried out in Filipino or English. KIIs and FGDs will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Qualitative interview and focus group discussion respondents will be recruited until saturation is reached.

All KIIs and FGDs will be coded deductively using the modified socio-ecological framework of attraction and retention of rural health workers, in the original language of the interview before being translated (when needed) during manuscript writing. The attraction and retention of rural health workers is affected by a host of individual environment, work environment, local environment, national environment and international environment factors. International environment factors typically include higher salaries, better working conditions and better career opportunities in other countries. National environment factors include policy environment and political climate, labour policies, salary levels, etc. Local environment factors include living conditions, social environment, peace and order, etc. Work environment factors include relationship with colleagues, leadership within the facilities, opportunities for personal and professional development, availability of infrastructure, medicines and consumables. Lastly, individual factors work at the level of the physician, including origin, age, gender, marital status etc.17 Unintended consequences of the programmes and recommendations for improvement will be coded inductively.

Work package three: stated preference experiment

The results of the second work package, supplemented by key-informant interviews with current or recently graduated resident physicians of each of the specialty programmes under consideration, will be used to design a stated preference experiment to assess the effect of different incentives on attracting current resident physicians to join the EPSB programme once they are board-certified. In our interviews with current or recently graduated resident physicians, we showed them our initial attributes and levels from our interviews from the previous work package and solicited their feedback on the attributes to be included (or excluded) and the specific levels for each attribute. In choosing which attributes to include, we included attributes and levels that can possibly be used as a policy lever to improve recruitment (eg, salaries and availability of equipment) and have excluded attributes or factors that cannot be acted on by the DOH (eg, safety and peace and order situation). We have consulted with end users at the DOH on the final attributes and levels prior to the circulation of the finalised stated preference experiment tool. We carried out interviews with current or recently graduated resident physicians and the finalisation with the DOH from October 2025 to February 2026.

The stated preference experiment will use a d-efficient design. Assuming this design and the following: 16 choice tasks per respondent, 12 parameters, and 150 choice tasks, and 10% non-response, we will need 125 physicians according to the rule of thumb (ie, equation 6) of Assele’s paper.18 To enable specialty-specific subgroup analyses with adequate power, (n=125 per specialty), we will recruit 1125 residents across nine specialties.

To help disseminate the stated preference experiment tool, we will partner with specialty societies. They have agreed to cascade the online survey link to their resident physicians in training. To maximise the participation of each residency training programme and because of the recommendations of the partner specialty societies, we will recruit residents in training through a total enumeration method. Here, all residents in training will receive a link to the survey from their training officers (who are members of the specialty societies) and the residents will accomplish the stated preference experiment survey. If we see that some specialisations have less than 125 respondents, we will request the society or the training officers of these facilities to encourage their residents to accomplish the stated preference experiment. If after two follow-ups response rates remain suboptimal, we will pay a visit to the facilities and conduct the survey face-to-face. The stated preference experiment survey will run from February to May 2026.

Data from the stated preference experiment will be analysed descriptively first, then if possible, overall, then per specialisation or per specialist groups: (1) diagnostic, which includes pathologists and radiologists; (2) surgical, which includes general surgeons, obstetricians and gynaecologists and anaesthesiologists; (3) non-surgical, which includes internists, paediatricians, psychiatrists and family physicians. These specialist groupings reflect similar training pathways and practice environments, allowing for detection of patterns specific to diagnostic, surgical or non-surgical specialties. The DCE data will be analysed using mixed-effects multinomial logit regression.19

Reflexivity

The author team is a mix of early career, mid-career and senior researchers, and programme implementers with varying experience in qualitative and mixed-methods study designs and implementation of health workforce deployment programmes. VCFP, RGCM, ARDP, HIMJ and TPJZC are early career researchers who have conducted a few mixed methods studies on health workforce and the Philippine health system. JJRM is a mid-career researcher with previously published work on health workforce.13 KRU and WJCT implement health workforce placement programmes for the Philippine Department of Health. Lastly, MMD is a senior researcher with almost 50 years of experience in government, academe and international development, having served as a secretary of the Philippine Department of Health and director for Human Resources for Health at the WHO. All authors are native speakers of the languages used in the interviews and/or focus group discussions (ie, English and/or Filipino) and are based in the Philippines.

Ethics and dissemination

We have received ethics clearance from the Ateneo de Manila University School of Medicine and Public Health Research Ethics Committee (Protocol ID: AdMUREC-ASMPH_24_003).

The first work package uses publicly available data. Nevertheless, we will still redact personally identifying information of physicians. Written and oral informed consent will be requested from KII and FGD participants. For the second and third work packages, opt-in informed consent will be put on the landing pages of the online surveys so that respondents can make the decision to participate in the surveys. Personally identifying information from the transcripts of the KIIs and FGDs will be redacted to maintain anonymity. Recordings, transcripts and datasets arising from the study will be stored in a cloud storage platform accessible only by password-protected computers to maintain confidentiality. In the event of emotional distress, the participants may be referred to the Ateneo Bulatao Center for Psychological Services.

The tools for data collection and analysis may be published online to enable the DOH, PhilHealth and other researchers to monitor physician placement programmes. Results will be disseminated in online or face-to-face dissemination sessions with the DOH, PhilHealth, Philippine Statistics Authority, Association of Philippine Medical Colleges, the Professional Regulation Commission Board of Medicine, Commission on Higher Education and partner specialty societies. The results may also be presented in local and/or international conferences and published in peer-reviewed journal articles to help guide other countries in developing their own programmes to address the maldistribution and shortages of physicians in their own settings.
