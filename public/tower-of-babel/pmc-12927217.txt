Does income-related inequity exist in the utilization of home and community-based services among older adults in China? A cross-sectional study based on the China health and retirement longitudinal study 2018
Authors: Songjia Zhang, Chishuang Wang, Lizheng Ge, Yuyang Zhu, Haojie Jin, Zhiming Xie, Rujia Zhang, Qingren Yang, Xiaoyi Wang, Xiangyang Zhang, Chun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith escalating population aging, the significance of ensuring equitable access to elderly care services is becoming increasingly prominent. This study explored whether inequity exists in home- and community-based service (HCBS) use among older adults in China, assessed the extent of inequity, and analyzed the factors influencing the utilization of HCBSs and the resulting inequities.MethodsThis study focused on older adults (aged 60 and above) using data from the 2018 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Variables from previous studies and the Anderson Behavioral model were classified into need and non-need factors for the analysis. Ultimately, 2823 participants were included. We used the concentration curve, concentration index (CI), and horizontal inequity (HI) to measure inequity in HCBS utilization. Through CI decomposition analysis, we examined the contribution of each factor to the overall inequity.ResultsIn the older adult population, with income levels ranging from low to high, service utilization showed an initial rising trend, and then a decline. Middle-income older adults used HCBSs the most under both actual and need-standardized scenarios, accounting for 24.12% and 25.08% of the total utilization, respectively. Low- and high-income older adults had lower HCBS utilization than middle-income older adults. The CI and HI for inequity in HCBS utilization among older Chinese adults were 0.085 (p < 0.01) and 0.064 (p < 0.01), respectively. A slight inequity in HCBS use among older adults in China was found in favor of higher-income users. The contribution of non-need factors to inequity in HCBS use was 450.93%, of which the contribution from income was 464.69% (some non-need factors contributed negatively to inequity). Differences in income significantly exacerbated the issue of fairness in HCBS usage, with low-income older individuals potentially more susceptible to experience inequities.ConclusionsGovernment-issued policies to promote the utilization of HCBSs by vulnerable older adults have achieved certain results. Middle-income older adults are more likely than those with higher incomes to use HCBSs. However, there is still inequity in service utilization, with low-income older adults showing less service utilization, which requires further government intervention. We propose recommendations for future policy improvements.Keywords: Inequity, Concentration index, Home and community-based services, Older adults, Service utilization, China

Background

Equity has long been a topic of international concern [1]. Much research has explored inequity in service utilization, especially regarding health services, such as inpatient or outpatient service utilization, among older adults, patients, and ordinary residents [2–5]. In these cases, equity has been understood to mean that services should be distributed according to need rather than social privileges [6]. In the context of China’s aging population, our research focuses on the growing population of older people. Equity in elderly care service utilization for older adults is a vital aspect of healthcare service utilization and merits further study.

As is the case worldwide, most older adults in China prefer aging in their own homes. In the context of the home-based care model, it is crucial for families to take on a central role in the process of eldercare, while communities also need to provide additional support. Against this background, the Chinese government initially considered constructing home- and community-based services (HCBSs) in 2000, with additional clarification occurring in 2008, after which various policies were proposed to promote the development of HCBSs [7]. HCBSs refers to services provided by the community and social forces under the leadership of the government for elderly individuals living at home. The services provided can be broadly classified into three categories: [1] medical care services, such as regular physical examination, health education, and rehabilitation training; [2] daily care services, such as housekeeping, senior canteen, and grocery shopping; [3] social support services, such as education, psychological guidance, legal counsel, and entertainment [8, 9]. While respecting the emotional and psychological needs of older adults, HCBSs can effectively enhance their quality of life and address China’s pressing issue of elderly care [10]. Therefore, in this context, HCBSs are the key future development direction for elderly care services in China [10–13].

With the continuous progress of the country’s targeted poverty alleviation policy, China’s overall poverty rate has continued to decline. According to the China Household Finance Survey, the poverty rate of older adults in China dropped from 17.7% in 2014 to 14.5% in 2018, but the poverty problem of older adults is still serious and needs attention. The Chinese government considers that HCBSs should act as a “safety net.” Therefore, in the process of HCBS development, the Chinese government has repeatedly stressed the importance of paying attention to older adults living in poverty. By 2023, China has issued a total of 25 national guiding policies related to HCBSs, of which 13 documents (52%) pay attention to poverty or low-income older adults. In addition, six national policies on old-age care also highlight the importance of focusing on older individuals living in poverty. The description related to equity in these policies is presented in Table 1. These policies reflect the Chinese government’s concerns and efforts to improve equity in HCBS utilization. According to these policies, the service needs of older adults living in poverty should be focused on and prioritized. Meanwhile, with the support of government earmarked funding, impoverished older adults can receive nursing subsidies and elderly care service subsidies, and the places where they live will be arranged for accessibility improvements. When using HCBSs, older adults with financial difficulties can receive preferential treatment or even free HCBSs.

However, in practice, several studies have pointed out that the utilization of HCBSs among older adults in China continues to vary, depending on their economic conditions. Lin et al. used data from the Party Committee and Government Office of Foshan City, Guangdong Province, and found that older adults’ use of community-based health services was significantly correlated with income and major expenditure items [14]. Similarly, Li et al.’s study showed that older adults’ financial strain was significantly related to their use of HCBSs [7]. Another study, based on the 2018 wave of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey, found that low-income older adults, especially those living in rural and western areas of China, faced difficulties in HCBS utilization [8].

The above studies preliminarily reveal possible income-related inequities in the utilization of HCBSs among older adults. This is contrary to what the policy is intended to achieve. However, whether there is inequity in the utilization of HCBSs among the entire elderly population in China has yet to be measured.

Therefore, this paper attempts to answer the question: Does inequity in the utilization of home and community-based services exist among older adults in China due to income? First, we will use the concentration curve to intuitively show the distribution of HCBS utilization by older adults in different economic situations. Second, the concentration index (CI) and the horizontal inequity index (HI) will be used to quantify the degree of inequity, as well as help draw a conclusion about whether income-related inequity exists. On the basis of proven inequity, the CI decomposition will be carried out to further explore the extent to which income contributes to inequity. Finally, through the above analysis, we hope to put forward policy suggestions to facilitate the building of the basic elderly care system and help ensure that basic elderly care is accessible to the entire elderly population.

Methods

Analytical approach

A concentration curve was used to describe the distribution of utilization. The CI and HI were used to evaluate whether there was inequity in the utilization of HCBSs among older adults with different income levels [15]. CI decomposition was used to calculate the degree of contribution of each influencing factor to overall inequity.

Concentration curve

The concentration curve plots cumulative shares of the variable of interest “y” against cumulative shares of the population as ranked by socioeconomic status [16]. The horizontal axis of the concentration curve represents the cumulative percentage of the population ranked from the lowest to highest income levels. The vertical axis represents the cumulative percentage of HCBS utilization. If service utilization is more distributed among low-income individuals, the concentration curve will be above the absolute equity line, and vice versa.

Horizontal inequity index

Horizontal equity means that people with the same needs enjoy the same service, regardless of their ability to pay (income). Factors affecting service utilization can be divided into two categories: need and non-need factors [15]. Need factors refer to variables that reflect the health status of the sample, such as sex, age, and health status. Non-need factors refer to variables other than need factors such as income. In the case of horizontal equity, need factors should be the decisive factors in service utilization. The HI reflects the distribution of service utilization determined by non-need factors after eliminating differences in need factors.

Data and variables

The data used in this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The CHARLS aims to collect a set of high-quality microdata representing families and individuals aged 45 years and above in China to analyze the aging population in China and promote relevant academic research. The CHARLS uses a multistage stratified sampling design and probability proportional to size sampling technique, covering 450 communities or villages in 150 counties and 17,000 people in approximately 10,000 households. This study used data obtained from the CHARLS 2018 wave. Older adults were defined as those aged 60 and above in this study. Ethical approval for the CHARLS was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University, and informed consent was obtained from respondents for participation in the survey.

The corresponding question concerning HCBS utilization in the CHARLS questionnaire was: “Have you ever received the following home and community care services? (Circle all that apply.).” In this question, respondents were asked about their use of HCBSs in terms of medical care services (nursing homes, regular physical examinations, family beds, community nursing, and health management), daily care services (daycare centers and senior dining tables), and social support services (onsite visits, entertainment, and other services). We considered that respondents had not used HCBSs if the answer “None of the above” was chosen for this question.

For the inclusion of independent variables, we referred to the Anderson Health Services Utilization model [20], which is widely used to analyze the influencing factors on service utilization. We divided the included independent variables into two categories: need factors and non-need factors (including enabling factors and predisposing factors).

Need factors encompass demographic and health characteristics, including variables like gender, age, diagnosed chronic diseases (such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes), activities of daily living (ADLs), and self-reported health status. ADLs involved older adults’ dressing, bathing or showering, eating, getting into or out of bed, using the toilet, controlling urination, and defecating on their own. In relation to these items, those who answered that they had no issues with any of them were considered to have “No difficulty,” while the remaining cases were defined as “Have difficulty.”

The non-need factors included socioeconomic characteristics, such as income, education, geographical area, residence type, health insurance, marital status, number of living children, and living arrangements. Considering the uncertainty of the income sources of older adults and the level of mutual economic assistance operating in Chinese families, this study used per capita annual household income to represent the income of older adults, which is equal to the total annual household income divided by the number of people living with them in the household. The total annual household income included wage income (main respondent, spouse, and other household members), transfers (individual- and household-based), household agricultural income, and self-employed income. For income statistics, samples with a key variable of zero were excluded because the zeros were either true (i.e., no income at all) or falsely reported (i.e., respondents were unwilling to tell the truth), and we could not tell whether the responses were true or false. Education for older adults was divided into three levels: illiterate, primary school, and middle school and above. Geographical area was defined as the location of the respondent and classified into the three official economic belts (according to the principle of combining the level of economic development and geographical location, mainland China is divided into the eastern, central, and western economic belts). The eastern belt includes Beijing, Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hebei, Jiangsu, Liaoning, Shandong, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Zhejiang (a total of 11 regions). The central belt includes Anhui, Henan, Heilongjiang, Hubei, Hunan, Jilin, Jiangxi, Inner Mongolia, and Shanxi (a total of nine regions). The western belt includes Gansu, Guizhou, Qinghai, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Xinjiang, Yunnan, and Chongqing (a total of eight regions). Finally, people who stated that they were “married and live with their spouse” or who answered affirmatively to “have a partner living with you as a couple (cohabiting)” were considered to be living with their spouse. After excluding all missing values, the final sample size was 2823 respondents. The variable selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Variable selection

Actual, need-predicted, and need-standardized utilization were used to determine the distribution of HCBS utilization. Utilization refers to the actual utilization reported by the interviewees. Need-predicted utilization refers to utilization predicted according to the need factors after standardizing the non-need factors in the probit regression equation. Need-standardized utilization refers to utilization determined by non-need factors after indirect standardization.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The basic characteristics of the samples and the use of HCBSs are listed in Table 2. The overall HCBS use rate in older adults was 20.12% (568/2823). There were statistically significant differences in HCBS utilization among older adults of different ages (p < 0.001), incomes (p < 0.01), education (p < 0.001), geographical areas (p < 0.001), and chronic diseases (p < 0.001). The use of HCBSs increased with age. Utilization was higher in older adults with chronic diseases (25.32%) than those without chronic diseases (16.91%). The higher the income, the higher the HCBS utilization, except for the second highest income (20%) group (19.14%). The rate of utilization was highest among older adults with primary school education (23.38%) and was similar among those who were illiterate (17.32%) and those with higher education (17.10%). The western region had the highest utilization rate (23.57%), followed by the eastern (21.76%) and central (15.94%) regions.

Inequity in HCBS utilization

Table 4 shows the distribution of HCBS utilization among older adults in China after they were divided into five low-to-high-income groups. In terms of actual utilization, the middle-income group had the highest utilization level (24.12%), while the bottom income quintile had the lowest utilization level (17.61%). The need-predicted utilization of older adults in the middle- and lower-income groups was higher than that in the high-income group, indicating that the need for HCBSs decreased with increased income. Actual utilization and need-predicted utilization differed. The actual utilization of the middle-income and richest 20% groups was higher than the need-predicted utilization, while the actual utilization of the lower-income group was lower than the need-predicted utilization. After standardizing the need factors, the utilization of HCBSs in older adults with different income levels differed significantly. The utilization of HCBSs in older adults with varying income levels, from low to high, first increased and then decreased, with the middle-income group having the highest utilization rate (25.08%).

Decomposition of the concentration index (CI)

The contribution of various factors to inequity in home- and community-based service (HCBS) utilization

Probit regression analysis was conducted using three models. The crude model included only income as the independent variable. The partially adjusted model further incorporated need factors, including gender, age, chronic disease, activity of daily living, and self-reported health status. Beyond need factors, the fully adjusted model accounted for non-need factors, including education, geographical areas, residence, health insurance, marital status, number of living children, and living arrangement (living alone). Results showed that income was a significant determinant of HCBS utilization in both the crude, partially adjusted and fully adjusted models. Specifically, older adults in the middle-income and richest 20% income quintiles were significantly more likely to use HCBSs compared to those in the poorest 20% quintile.

CI decomposition results showed that the contribution rate of non-need factors was 450.93%, indicating that non-need factors were the main reason for inequity in HCBS use in older adults. Among them, income contributed the most (464.69%), suggesting that it was the primary factor leading to inequity in HCBS utilization among older adults. The non-need factors of geographical area (15.29%), medical insurance (11.14%), and education (8.47%) also contributed to inequity in favor of the high-income group.

Discussion

This article confirmed the existence of income-related inequity in HCBS utilization among older adults in China. In other words, even though the existing policy pays attention to equity, the implementation effect of the policy is still unsatisfactory in reality. However, it must be noted that income-related inequity in the utilization of services by older people is apparent in different countries and services. Several studies have identified this inequity using the CI and HI, for example, Canada’s physician services [21], Portugal’s primary care services (general practitioner visits) [22], and India’s healthcare services [23]. However, most of these studies focused on healthcare services. Equity in community-based elderly care service utilization has been poorly investigated. Unlike the conclusion that healthcare utilization is generally concentrated in high-income populations, the utilization of HCBSs is most concentrated in the middle-income older adults in China. On the positive side, while low-income older adults still face inequities, the fact that HCBSs are mainly used by middle-income older adults, rather than among high-income older adults, suggests that HCBS policies play a role in limiting the inequity of service utilization. However, on the negative side, our result indicated that Chinese older adults’ HCBS utilization still exhibits inequity, and appropriate government intervention is needed.

Through further analysis, we found that the largest contributor to inequity in HCBS utilization is income. For low-income older adults, economic weakness directly leads to their weakness in purchasing power, which is also an important reason why the utilization of services that require fees, such as medical services, generally favors the rich. Deng’s study showed that the use of free, community-based, regular diabetes examination was concentrated among low-income older adults, suggesting that offering free services may increase the use of HCBSs among low-income older adults [24, 25]. According to the policy, for the older adults in financial difficulty, most of the HCBSs are free or low-cost. However, our results show that low-income older adults do not make much use of HCBSs, indicating that, on the one hand, there are few vulnerable older adults who can actually enjoy free services, and on the other hand, low charges may be the threshold that prevents some vulnerable groups from using services. In addition, low-income older adults may also be disadvantaged in accessing information, as they have less awareness about HCBSs and whether such services are available in their community [26]. Moreover, it is common for low-income older adults to think that accepting free services is a nuisance to others, and it is difficult for them to become accustomed to being served. A strong sense of self-reliance, a preference for traditional care from family members [27, 28], and a misunderstanding of what HCBSs involve [29] may isolate older adults from the services they need [30, 31].

For high-income older adults, a good economic foundation provides them with more ways to support themselves later in life. Wang et al. found that use of institution-based care was concentrated among rich older adults in China [32]. For these individuals, services from the hospital, professional institutions, or nursing assistants can better cater to their diverse needs [33]. Therefore, HCBSs that can only provide some basic services and focus on meeting the basic needs of the majority of older adults will not be their best choice.

For middle-income older adults, they have more advantages in service accessibility than low-income older adults, and they do not have a strong willingness to pay for institutional care or private care workers; therefore, HCBSs are the best choice for them.

To address this inequity in HCBS utilization among older adults, we still need to firmly focus on low-income older adults. As already discussed, for low-income older adults, making services free is the most direct way to promote utilization. However, under tight fiscal pressure caused by the aging problem, it is impossible to maintain social justice by the power of the government alone. Thus, on the one hand, it is necessary to expand the channels of funding. HCBS centers, especially the senior dining tables, can be opened to all members of society, to increase the possibility of profit. While making profits on their own, HCBS centers should also extensively absorb social donations to accumulate more opportunities for public welfare projects. On the other hand, this money should be invested in the right places. First, the types of services provided to low-income older adults need to be targeted. Given the fact that older adults with a low economic status often suffer from poor health [7, 34], they may urgently need life-saving care and certain basic healthcare services that HCBSs can provide. Other services provided by HCBSs, such as education, psychological counseling, and entertainment, are not the most desired or urgently needed. The services provided by HCBSs should meet these individuals’ actual needs and provide services that can effectively help them improve their quality of life, which would in turn improve their willingness to use HCBSs and the possibility to do so. Second, there needs to be a clearly and reasonably defined list of which categories of older adults can enjoy free services, and communities are required to regularly update the list of older adults in their jurisdictions who can enjoy services for free. Finally, related publicity work is equally important. Residents in the community should know what HCBSs are, what HCBSs have to offer, and whom HCBSs are for. It should be transparent and easy to understand when services will be provided, what services will include, and how much services will cost.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, since we used a public database, there are unavoidable limitations, such as the variety of services. There are many kinds of HCBSs, and only a few representative services were included in our study. In addition, health status and economic status were self-reported by the respondents. However, referring to previous studies, where self-reports were also common methods of data collection, we believe that this deficiency will not affect the final conclusion.

Second, this paper uses the cross-sectional data of 2018, so it can only reveal the inequity in HCBS utilization and find some factors related to the inequity, and cannot explore the research results based on causality. We look forward to longitudinal studies to further explore the causal relationship and mechanism between socio-economic factors and inequity in HCBSs utilization, as well as the impact of the inequity on the quality of life and health status of older adults.

Conclusions

Based on the study findings, the following conclusions can be drawn. First, there was a slight trend in favor of higher-income groups in terms of inequity in the use of HCBSs among older adults in China, while middle-income older adults benefited the most from HCBS utilization. Second, need factors, including age, chronic disease, and ADLs, as well as non-need factors, including income, education, and geographical area, affected the utilization of HCBSs among older adults. Third, non-need factors had a significant influence on the utilization of HCBSs, with varying income levels playing a significant role in terms of generating inequity. Therefore, some practical recommendations have been given to promote HCBS utilization among low-income older adults.
