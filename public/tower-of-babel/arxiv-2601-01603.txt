Matrix Kloosterman Sums, Random Matrix Statistics, and Cryptography
Tianshuo Yang

Abstract
This paper presents a comprehensive study of matrix Kloosterman sums, including their computational aspects, distributional behavior, and applications in cryptographic analysis. Building on the work of [ 7 ] , we develop algorithms for evaluating these sums via Green’s polynomials and establish a general framework for analyzing their statistical distributions. We further investigate the associated L L -functions and clarify their relationships with symmetric functions and random matrix theory. We show that, analogous to the eigenvalue statistics of random matrices in compact Lie groups such as S ​ U ​ ( n ) SU(n) and S ​ p ​ ( 2 ​ n ) Sp(2n) , the normalized values of matrix Kloosterman sums exhibit Sato–Tate equidistribution. Finally, we apply this framework to distinguish truly random sequences from those exhibiting subtle algebraic biases, and we propose a novel spectral test for cryptographic security based on the distributional signatures of matrix Kloosterman sums.



1
Introduction

Kloosterman sums are fundamental objects in number theory, algebraic geometry, and representation theory. Classically, they are defined as exponential sums of the form

K(a,b;p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}\\
xy=1\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi i(ax+by)/p},

and they exhibit exhibit deep connections with modular forms, automorphic representations, and
\ell
-adic cohomology
[
4
]
. Their natural extension to matrix groups—known as matrix Kloosterman sums—has been investigated in recent work of Erdélyi and Tóth
[
1
]
, and independently by Zelingher
[
7
]
. These developments have opened new avenues in the study of random matrix statistics, equidistribution phenomena, and applications to cryptographic analysis.

Matrix Kloosterman sums are defined for matrices in the general linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and incorporate both multiplicative characters (through determinants) and additive characters (through traces). Their significance stems from several perspectives:

1.

Algebraic Structure
: Matrix Kloosterman sums are intimately connected with the representation theory of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and symmetric functions through Green polynomials (
[
3
,
5
]
, and
[
7
]
).

2.

Random Matrix Statistics
: Deligne’s equidistribution theorem
[
4
]
suggests that normalized Kloosterman sums should be distributed like traces of random matrices in compact Lie groups. This connection between number theory and random matrix theory has profound implications for understanding the statistical properties of these sums.

3.

Cryptographic Applications
: The randomness properties of matrix Kloosterman sums make them suitable for testing cryptographic algorithms. By comparing the distribution of matrix Kloosterman sums computed from cipher outputs with theoretical random matrix distributions, one can assess the quality of cryptographic randomness.

In this paper, we develop algorithms for computing matrix Kloosterman sums using Green polynomials and classical Kloosterman sums, building on the foundational work of Zelingher
[
7
]
. We design and implement two complementary algorithms: Algorithm I, which computes matrix Kloosterman sums for Jordan-type matrices, and Algorithm II, which extends the computation to arbitrary matrices via their Jordan decomposition.

We investigate the
L
-functions associated with matrix Kloosterman sums and establish their connections with symmetric functions and random matrix statistics. Through extensive numerical experiments, we confirm the Sato–Tate distribution behavior of normalized matrix Kloosterman sums and demonstrate their equidistribution in compact Lie groups. Using these insights, we propose a novel statistical test for cryptographic randomness based on the distribution of matrix Kloosterman sums and show its effectiveness in detecting algebraic or structural deviations from true randomness.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the necessary background on conjugacy classes and representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, symmetric functions, classical Kloosterman sums, and matrix Kloosterman sums. Section 3 presents the computational algorithms. Section 4 investigates the associated
L
-functions. Section 5 explores the connections with random matrix theory. Section 6 describes cryptographic applications and numerical experiments. Section 7 concludes with directions for future research.

2.1
Conjugacy Classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}

q

q

p

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Definition 2.1

(Regular Elliptic Element)
.

An element

\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Such an element has
n

Theorem 2.2

(Conjugacy Classification)
.

[
3
]

For any
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

s\geq 1

d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\geq 1

y\sim\mathrm{diag}\left(J_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(y_{1}),\ldots,J_{\bm{\mu}_{s}}(y_{s})\right),

where
J_{\bm{\mu}_{j}}(y_{j})
denotes the Jordan block matrix associated with partition
\bm{\mu}_{j}
and regular elliptic element
y_{j}
.

The Jordan block
J_{(\mu)}(y)
for a single part
\mu
and regular elliptic element
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is defined as:

J_{(\mu)}(y)=\begin{pmatrix}y&I_{d}&&\\
&y&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&I_{d}\\
&&&y\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d\mu}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),

Symmetric Functions and Green Polynomials

We recall essential facts about symmetric functions and Green polynomials

Definition 2.4

(Partitions)
.

A partition
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})

\bm{\lambda}\vdash r

|\bm{\lambda}|=\sum\lambda_{i}=r

\ell(\bm{\lambda})=\ell
, and

.

For a partition
\bm{\lambda}
, define
z_{\bm{\lambda}}=\prod_{i\geq 1}i^{m_{i}}m_{i}!

We use

to denote the power-sum symmetric function associated to the partition

P_{\bm{\mu}}(x;t)

\bm{\mu}

Definition 2.5

(Green Polynomials)
.

The Green polynomials
Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(q)
are defined by:

Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(q)=q^{n(\bm{\mu})}X_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(q^{-1}),

where

p_{\bm{\lambda}}(x)=\sum_{\bm{\mu}}X_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(t)P_{\bm{\mu}}(x;t).

Green polynomials satisfy important properties

]

•

and vanish unless
|\bm{\lambda}|=|\bm{\mu}|
.

•

Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{(r)}(t)=1
for all partitions
\bm{\lambda}
of
r
.

•

\sum_{|\bm{\lambda}|=r}z_{\bm{\lambda}}^{-1}Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(q)=1
.

•

{\rm deg}\left(Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(t)\right)\leq n(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(i-1)\mu_{i}

For tables of Green polynomials, see Green
[
3
]

[

]

2.4
Classical Kloosterman Sums and Sheaves

Let
\psi:\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a nontrivial additive character and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
with
\alpha_{i}:\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
multiplicative characters.

Definition 2.6

(Hyper-Kloosterman Sum)
.

The rank
k
Kloosterman sum is:

\mathrm{Kl}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi)=(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}^{\times}\\
t_{1}\cdots t_{k}=\xi\end{subarray}}\alpha_{1}(t_{1})\cdots\alpha_{k}(t_{k})\psi(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{k}).

Katz
[
4
]
studied Kloosterman sheaves, which are
\ell
-adic sheaves on the multiplicative group
\mathbb{G}_{m}
whose Frobenius traces give Kloosterman sums. For a rank
k
Kloosterman sheaf
\mathrm{Kl}_{k}^{\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi)
, the Frobenius eigenvalues at
\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}^{\times}
are denoted
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
, satisfying
|w_{j}|=q^{d(k-1)/2}
.

2.5
Matrix Kloosterman Sums

We recall the definition of matrix Kloosterman sums and their properties from
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
.

2.5.1
Definition

Definition 2.7

(Matrix Kloosterman Sums)
.

Let
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
\psi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
a nontrivial additive character, and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
multiplicative characters of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. The matrix Kloosterman sum is defined as:

\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\\
\prod_{j=1}^{k}g_{j}=y\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\mathrm{Det}(g_{i}))\right)\psi\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}\right)\right).

This definition generalizes classical Kloosterman sums in two directions: from scalars to matrices, and from products to both products (through the condition
\prod g_{j}=y
) and sums (through the trace in the additive character).

2.5.2
Reduction Formulas

Matrix Kloosterman sums exhibit multiplicative properties under direct sums of matrices with disjoint eigenvalues.

Theorem 2.8

(Direct Sum Decomposition)
.

[
1
]

Let
y_{1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
y_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n_{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
have no common eigenvalues over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Then:

\mathrm{Kl}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\mathrm{diag}(y_{1},y_{2}))=q^{(k-1)n_{1}n_{2}}\mathrm{Kl}_{n_{1}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y_{1})\mathrm{Kl}_{n_{2}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y_{2}).

For regular elliptic elements, matrix Kloosterman sums reduce to classical Kloosterman sums.

Theorem 2.9

(Regular Elliptic Reduction)
.

[
7
]

Let
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be regular elliptic with eigenvalues
\{\xi,\xi^{q},\ldots,\xi^{q^{n-1}}\}
,
\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}^{\times}
. Then:

\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)=(-1)^{(k-1)(n+1)}q^{(k-1)\binom{d}{2}}\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi),

where the right side is the classical Kloosterman sum in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
.

2.5.3
Jordan Block Formulas

The most intricate case involves Jordan blocks. Let
n=dr
and
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be regular elliptic with eigenvalues
\{\xi,\xi^{q},\ldots,\xi^{q^{d-1}}\}
.

Theorem 2.10

(Jordan Block Formula)
.

[
7
]

For any partition
\bm{\mu}\vdash r
:

\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,J_{\bm{\mu}}(y))=(-1)^{(k-1)n}q^{(k-1)\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\vdash r}\frac{Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(q^{d})}{z_{\bm{\lambda}}}(-1)^{(k-1)\ell(\bm{\lambda})}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\bm{\lambda})}\mathrm{Kl}_{d\lambda_{j}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi).

Let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
be the Frobenius eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Kl}_{k}^{\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi)
at
\xi
, and define normalized eigenvalues
\widetilde{w}_{j}=q^{-d(k-1)/2}w_{j}
. Using the identity

p_{\bm{\lambda}}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})=(-1)^{(k-1)\ell(\bm{\lambda})}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\bm{\lambda})}\mathrm{Kl}_{d\lambda_{j}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi),

One obtains a symmetric function formulation:

Corollary 2.11
.

\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,J_{\bm{\mu}}(y))=(-1)^{(k-1)n}q^{(k-1)\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\vdash r}\frac{Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(q^{d})}{z_{\bm{\lambda}}}p_{\bm{\lambda}}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}).

This reveals that matrix Kloosterman sums are essentially evaluations of symmetric functions at the Frobenius eigenvalues of Kloosterman sheaves, with Green polynomials as coefficients.

2.5.4
General Formula

Combining Theorems
2.8
and
2.10
, one obtains a complete formula for arbitrary matrices.

Theorem 2.12

(General Formula)
.

[
7
]

Let
y=\mathrm{diag}(J_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(y_{1}),\ldots,J_{\bm{\mu}_{s}}(y_{s}))
, where
y_{j}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d_{j}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
are regular elliptic with mutually disjoint eigenvalues, and
\bm{\mu}_{j}\vdash r_{j}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{s}d_{j}r_{j}=n
. Let
\eta_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{d_{j}}}^{\times}
correspond to eigenvalues of
y_{j}
, and
w_{j1},\ldots,w_{jk}
be Frobenius eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Kl}_{k}^{\mathbb{F}_{q^{d_{j}}}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi)
at
\eta_{j}
. Then:

\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)=(-1)^{(k-1)n}q^{(k-1)\binom{n}{2}}\times\prod_{j=1}^{s}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\vdash r_{j}}\frac{Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}_{j}}(q^{d_{j}})}{z_{\bm{\lambda}}}p_{\bm{\lambda}}(w_{j1},\ldots,w_{jk}).

In the special case
\bm{\mu}=(r)
, i.e.,
J_{\bm{\mu}}(y)
is a regular element, we have that
Q^{(r)}_{\bm{\lambda}}(t)=1
for all
\bm{\lambda}\vdash r
and therefore

\sum_{\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})\vdash(r)}\frac{Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{(r)}(t)}{z_{\bm{\lambda}}}p_{\bm{\lambda}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=h_{r}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\vdash r}m_{\bm{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

is the complete symmetric polynomial of degree
r
.

3
Algorithms for Computation

3.1
Normalization

For statistical analysis, we work with normalized matrix Kloosterman sums:

\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)=q^{-(k-1)\frac{n^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{Kl}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y).

By Green
[
3
]
,
{\rm deg}\left(Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(t)\right)\leq n(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(i-1)\mu_{i}
. For all partition
\bm{\mu}\vdash r
, the maximum
n(\bm{\mu})
is
\binom{r}{2}
, achieved by
\bm{\mu}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
. We have that
|\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)|\leq q^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{d_{j}r_{j}(r_{j}-1)}{2}}
. In particular, for
\bm{\mu}=(r)
, we have
Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{(r)}(t)=1
for all partitions
\bm{\lambda}
of
r
. Hence for Jordan block
J_{(r)}(y)
, where
y
is a regular elliptic element, the normalized sums are bounded independently of
q
.

3.4
Complexity Analysis

The computational complexity depends on several factors:

•

Jordan Decomposition
: Over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the rational and Jordan canonical forms of an
n\times n
matrix can be computed in polynomial time. In particular, standard algorithms (e.g., Frobenius form followed by primary decomposition) run in
O(n^{3})
field operations.

•

Green Polynomials
:
Green polynomials
Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(t)
for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

can be computed effectively via their interpretation as the transition
coefficients between the Hall–Littlewood and power-sum bases in the
ring of symmetric functions
[
5
]
.
Recursive formulas, vertex-operator constructions, and computer algebra
implementations allow one, for fixed
n
, to compute an individual

Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(t)
in time polynomial in the
size of the partition data.

However, in applications to matrix Kloosterman sums, one typically needs
all Green polynomials of a given size. For

y\sim\operatorname{diag}(J_{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(y_{1}),\ldots,J_{\bm{\mu}_{s}}(y_{s}))
, computation of

\widetilde{\operatorname{Kl}}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)
requires the
values
Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}_{j}}(t)
for all
partitions
\bm{\lambda}\vdash|\bm{\mu}_{j}|
and

1\leq j\leq s
. Since the partition number
p(n)
grows exponentially by
the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic, obtaining the full set

\{Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\bm{\mu}}(t):|\bm{\lambda}|=|\bm{\mu}|\leq n\}

poses a significant computational challenge. It is therefore desirable
to develop more efficient algorithms for computing all Green polynomials
of bounded size.

•

Classical Kloosterman Sums
:
The naive computation of
\operatorname{Kl}_{d}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi)

requires
O(q^{d/2})
operations, but can be accelerated using
character-sum techniques and structural decompositions, yielding
substantial improvements in practice.

For practical applications with moderate
n
and
q
, these algorithms are feasible.

4
L
-Functions and Symmetric Functions

4.1
L
-Functions of Classical Kloosterman Sums

For a fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}^{\times}
, consider the generating series of normalized classical Kloosterman sums:

L(T)=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{dn}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi)\frac{T^{n}}{n}\right).

Theorem 4.1
.

(See for example
[
2
]
)

L(T)^{(-1)^{k}}
is a polynomial of degree
k
:

L(T)^{(-1)^{k}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-\widetilde{w}_{i}T),

where
\widetilde{w}_{i}=q^{-d(k-1)/2}w_{i}
are normalized Frobenius eigenvalues.

4.2
Coefficient Formulas

Expanding the product gives expressions in terms of elementary symmetric functions:

L(T)^{(-1)^{k}}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}e_{r}(\widetilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{w}_{k})T^{r}.

Using Newton’s identities, the coefficients can be expressed in terms of the Kloosterman sums
K_{j}=\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{dj}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The coefficient
C_{r}
of
T^{r}
in
L(T)^{(-1)^{k}}
is:

C_{r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\geq 0\\
1s_{1}+\cdots+rs_{r}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{K_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots K_{r}^{s_{r}}(-1)^{k(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{r})}}{s_{1}!\cdots s_{r}!1^{s_{1}}\cdots r^{s_{r}}}.

This can be computed efficiently using partition enumeration.

4.3
Matrix Kloosterman Sums and Generating Functions

We have the following result about the generating function of matrix Kloosterman sums for the partition
(n)
:

Theorem 4.3
.

For regular elliptic
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, one has

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{(k-1)dn}\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{dn}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,J_{(n)}(y))T^{dn}=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-\widetilde{w}_{j}T^{d})}.

Proof.

When
\bm{\mu}=(n)
, we have
Q_{\bm{\lambda}}^{(n)}(t)=1
for all
\bm{\lambda}\vdash n
. Thus:

\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{dn}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,J_{(n)}(y))=(-1)^{(k-1)dn}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}\vdash n}\frac{1}{z_{\bm{\lambda}}}p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\widetilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{w}_{k})=(-1)^{(k-1)dn}h_{n}(\widetilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{w}_{k}),

where
h_{n}
is the complete symmetric function. The generating function for complete symmetric functions is
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h_{n}T^{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-\widetilde{w}_{j}T)^{-1}
.
∎

This establishes a direct link between matrix Kloosterman sums and symmetric functions.

4.4
Connection with Symmetric Power
L
-Functions

The generating function in Theorem
4.3
is essentially the
L
-function of the symmetric powers of the Kloosterman sheaf. Recall that for a sheaf
\mathcal{F}
, the
L
-function of its

L(\mathrm{Sym}^{\ell}(\mathcal{F}),T)=\prod_{x\in|X|}\prod_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{k}=\ell}(1-\alpha_{1}(x)^{i_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{k}(x)^{i_{k}}T^{\deg x})^{-1}.

For any
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, let

\pi_{1}(\xi),\ldots,\pi_{k}(\xi)
be all the eigenvalues of the
geometric Frobenius element
{\rm Fr}_{\xi}
on
{\rm Kl}_{k}^{\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi)_{\xi}
. By Theorem 4.3, we have the following result:

Corollary 4.4
.

The generating function of matrix Kloosterman sums for partitions
(n)
is related to the
L
-function of the first symmetric powers of the Kloosterman sheaf:

L(\mathrm{Sym}^{1}(\mathrm{Kl}_{k}^{\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi)),T)==\prod_{\xi\in\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}^{\times}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\pi_{j}(\xi)}T^{{\rm deg}(\xi)}}

=\prod_{[y]\text{ regular elliptic}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\operatorname{Kl}}_{{\rm deg}(y)\cdot n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,J_{(n)}(y))(-1)^{(k-1){\rm deg}(y)\cdot n}T^{{\rm deg}(y)\cdot n},

where the right side is interpreted as a generating function for symmetric power
L
-functions.

5
Random Matrix Statistics

5.1
Deligne’s Equidistribution Theorem

Deligne’s work on the Weil conjectures, extended by Katz, provides the theoretical foundation for the random matrix behavior of Kloosterman sums.

Theorem 5.1

(Deligne–Katz Equidistribution)
.

[
4
]

Let
\mathrm{Kl}_{k}^{\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}}(\bm{\alpha},\psi)
be a Kloosterman sheaf of rank
k
with geometric monodromy group
G_{\rm geom}
. As
q\to\infty
(or as we vary parameters), the Frobenius conjugacy classes
{\rm Fr}_{\xi}
at points
\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{d}}^{\times}
become equidistributed in the space of conjugacy classes of the compact group
G_{\rm geom}
, with respect to Haar measure.

For classical Kloosterman sums (the case
k=2
), Katz proved that the geometric
monodromy group of the associated Kloosterman sheaf is
\mathrm{SL}(2)
(equivalently,
its compact form
\mathrm{SU}(2)
). For higher-rank Kloosterman sums (
k>2
), the
geometric monodromy groups are typically the symplectic groups
\mathrm{Sp}(2k)
(for
even
k
) or closely related classical groups, depending on the parity of
k

and the characteristic. Katz’s classification shows that, except for a few small
exceptional cases, the monodromy is as large as allowed by the symmetries of the
sheaf.

5.2
Sato–Tate Distributions

For
k=2
, the normalized Kloosterman sums
\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{d}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi)
lie in
[-2,2]
. Writing
\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{d}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,\xi)=2\cos\theta
with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, the Sato–Tate law states:

Theorem 5.2

(Sato–Tate Distribution)
.

[
4
]

As
q\to\infty
, the angles
\theta
for classical Kloosterman sums (
k=2
) are distributed according to the density:

f_{ST}(\theta)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sin^{2}\theta,\quad\theta\in[0,\pi].

Equivalently, the normalized sums
x=2\cos\theta
have density:

f_{trace}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}},\quad x\in[-2,2].

This is exactly the distribution of traces of random matrices in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
with respect to Haar measure.

5.3
Matrix Kloosterman Sum Distributions

For matrix Kloosterman sums, the distribution depends on the conjugacy class of
y
and the rank
k
.

Conjecture 5.3
.

Let
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be a fixed regular elliptic element. As
q\to\infty
(or as we vary
\bm{\alpha}
), the normalized matrix Kloosterman sums
\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)
are distributed like traces of random matrices in a compact Lie group determined by the monodromy group of the associated Kloosterman sheaf and the partition type of
y
.

For
y=J_{(n)}(y_{0})
with
y_{0}
regular elliptic, Theorem
4.3
shows:

\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{dn}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,J_{(n)}(y))=(-1)^{(k-1)dn}h_{n}(\widetilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{w}_{k}).

Thus, the distribution of these sums is determined by the distribution of complete symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues
\widetilde{w}_{j}
, which are themselves distributed like eigenvalues of random matrices in the monodromy group.

5.4
Monodromy Groups for Matrix Kloosterman Sums

Based on Katz’s work and our formulas, we expect:

•

For
k=2
: The monodromy group is
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, and matrix Kloosterman sums follow
\mathrm{SU}(2)
statistics.

•

For general
k
with trivial
\bm{\alpha}
: The monodromy group is
\mathrm{Sp}(2k)
or a similar symplectic group.

•

For general
k
with nontrivial
\bm{\alpha}
: The monodromy group may be a unitary group
\mathrm{U}(k)
.

The precise monodromy group depends on the characters
\bm{\alpha}
and the additive character
\psi
.

6
Experimental Analysis and Numerical Results

We now turn to the empirical validation of the theory. The core hypothesis, founded on the Katz–Sarnak density conjectures and Deligne’s equidistribution theorem, is that as
p\to\infty
, the normalized values of matrix Kloosterman sums become equidistributed with respect to the Haar measure on an appropriate compact Lie group.

Matrix Kloosterman sums thereby provide a novel statistical tool for assessing cryptographic randomness. The basic idea is as follows:

1.

Convert the cipher output into matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
via a prescribed encoding mechanism.

2.

Compute matrix Kloosterman sums for these matrices.

3.

Compare the empirical distribution of normalized sums against the theoretical random matrix distributions.

4.

Detect statistical deviations, which serve as indicators of non-random behavior.

6.1

We propose the following test statistics:

1.

Trace Distribution Test
: Compare the histogram of
\Re\bigl(\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y)\bigr)

f_{\mathrm{trace}}(x)
.

2.

Angle Distribution Test

k=2
, compute
\theta=\arccos\bigl(\Re(\widetilde{\mathrm{Kl}}_{n}(\bm{\alpha},\psi,y))/2\bigr)
and compare with the Sato–Tate density

7
Conclusion and Future Work

7.1
Summary of Contributions

We have investigated the matrix Kloosterman sums and their connections with

•

The representation theory of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
through Green’s classification.

•

Symmetric functions and Green polynomials.

•

L
-functions and symmetric powers of Kloosterman sheaves.

•

Random matrix statistics through Deligne’s equidistribution theorem.

We provided algorithms and demonstrated cryptographic applications through extensive experiments.

The pivotal application of this paper is the translation of the above theory into a practical test for cryptographic randomness. The security of modern stream ciphers relies on the sequence being indistinguishable from random; however, many “random-looking” sequences can harbor hidden algebraic structures.

7.2
Concluding Remarks

Matrix Kloosterman sums form a remarkable bridge between algebraic number theory, representation theory, random matrix theory, and cryptography.
Their explicit expressions in terms of Green polynomials and classical Kloosterman sums render them computationally accessible, while their relationships with
L
-functions and equidistribution theorems furnish a robust theoretical framework for understanding their statistical behavior.

The cryptographic application introduced in this work—assessing randomness through the distributional properties of matrix Kloosterman sums—demonstrates the potential of these objects as a sensitive and mathematically principled testing mechanism.
As cryptographic systems grow in complexity and as post-quantum cryptography continues to develop, tools grounded in deep arithmetic and representation-theoretic structure may become increasingly important in evaluating and strengthening security guarantees.
