Algorithms for zero-sum stochastic games with the risk-sensitive average criterion This work was supported by the National Key Research and Development Program of China (2022YFA1004600)
Fang Chen, Xianping Guo, Xin Guo, Junyu Zhang

Abstract




Abstract:
This paper is an attempt to compute the value and saddle points of zero-sum risk-sensitive average stochastic games. For the average games with finite states and actions, we first introduce the so-called irreducibility coefficient and then establish its equivalence to the irreducibility condition. Using this equivalence, we develop an iteration algorithm to compute
\varepsilon
-approximations of the value (for any given
\varepsilon>0
) and show its convergence. Based on
\varepsilon
-approximations of the value and the irreducibility coefficient, we further propose another iteration algorithm,
which is proved to obtain
\varepsilon
-saddle points in finite steps.
Finally, a numerical example of energy management in smart grids is provided to illustrate our results.

Key words:
Risk-sensitive average criterion; stochastic game;
\varepsilon
-approximation of the value;
\varepsilon
-saddle point; iteration algorithm.

Introduction

Stochastic games, introduced by Shapley
[
24
]
, are an important class of stochastic optimality models and have been widely studied due to their rich applications
[
19
,
20
]
. Traditional stochastic game theory primarily focuses on the expected criteria
[
14
,
15
,
23
,
26
]
, where all players are assumed to be risk-neutral.
However, it is well known that players may be risk-sensitive in real-world applications
[
11
]
. Therefore, risk-sensitive stochastic games, in which the risk-sensitivity and reward of players are considered simultaneously through the exponential utility function and risk-sensitive parameters, have garnered sustained research interest
[
3
,
5
,
7
,
12
,
6
,
8
,
4
,
13
,
16
,
25
]
.

In this paper, we focus on zero-sum discrete-time stochastic games with the risk-sensitive average criterion, and thus only describe the existing works on this aspect.
Basu and Ghosh
[
3
]
established the existence of saddle points for risk-sensitive average discrete-time stochastic games with countable states and bounded costs. This result was extended to the case of Borel state and action spaces by Bäuerle and Rieder
[
5
]
.
For risk-sensitive average discrete-time stochastic games with unbounded costs, Ghosh et al.
[
12
]
employed the nonlinear Krein-Rutman theorem to prove the existence of saddle points.
Cavazos-Cadena and Hernández-Hernández
[
7
]
showed that an appropriate normalization of risk-sensitive discounted value functions converges to the risk-sensitive average value function as the discount factor tends to
1
in discrete-time stochastic games.
For semi-Markov games, Bhabak and Saha
[
6
]
addressed the case of finite states and proved the existence of the value and saddle points.
Chen and Guo
[
8
]
studied semi-Markov games with compact state and action spaces, proved that the value and a saddle point exist, and provided an algorithm to compute
\varepsilon
-approximation of the value.

As can be seen in
[
3
,
5
,
7
,
12
,
6
,
8
]
, for the risk-sensitive average games the existence of saddle points has been established under various conditions, whereas their computation has not been addressed even for the finite-state case. Since it is desirable and important in practical applications to compute saddle points, in this paper we will make an attempt on the computation. Precisely, we study a discrete-time risk-sensitive average stochastic game with finite states and actions, and aim to provide algorithms for computing (or at least approximating) the value and saddle points.

First, we introduce the so-called irreducible coefficient, give its efficient calculation from the data of the game, and establish the equivalent relationship between the irreducibility condition and the irreducible coefficient (see Proposition
3.2
). Second, under the irreducibility condition we construct an increasing sequence and a decreasing one, whose limits are proved to equal the value. Based on these two sequences, we propose an iteration algorithm and its stopping rule for computing
\varepsilon
-approximations of the value (see Algorithm
1
and Theorem
3.1
). Third, using
\varepsilon
-approximations of the value and the irreducible coefficient, we further construct an approximate solution to the Shapley equation, and prove that any mini-max selector of the approximate solution is an
\varepsilon
-saddle point (see Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
4.1
). Subsequently, we present an algorithm for computing
\varepsilon
-saddle points, where the number of iteration steps is explicitly given (see Algorithm
2
). To the best of our knowledge, Algorithm
2
developed in this paper is the first algorithm to compute
\varepsilon
-saddle points of risk-sensitive average stochastic games. Finally, we give an example of energy management in smart grids to illustrate the applications and effectiveness of our results.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the model of the stochastic game and introduce the risk-sensitive average criterion. Our main results on approximating the value and saddle points are presented in Sections 3 and 4, respectively. Finally, to illustrate our results, we give an example in Section 5.

2
The game model

A two-person zero-sum risk-sensitive stochastic game is defined as

G(\theta):=\{\theta,E,A,B,(A(i),B(i),i\in E),P(j|i,a,b),c(i,a,b)\},

(2.1)

where
\theta\in(0,\infty)
is a risk-sensitive parameter,
E
is the state space, and
A
and
B
are the action spaces of players 1 and 2, respectively. The spaces
E
,
A
, and
B
are assumed to be finite. For each
i\in E
, the nonempty set
A(i)\subset A
(resp.
B(i)\subset B
) denotes the set of admissible actions of player 1 (resp. player 2) at state
i
.
The stochastic kernel
P
on
E
given
\mathbb{K}:=\{(i,a,b)|i\in E,a\in A(i),b\in B(i)\}
is the transition function.
Finally, the real-valued function
c
on
\mathbb{K}
is the cost/reward function to player 1/player 2.
Since
E
,
A
, and
B
are finite,
c
is bounded.
Without loss of generality, assume that
c
is nonnegative.

Now we introduce the concept of a policy. To do so, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, let
H_{n}
denote the space of admissible histories of the game up to the
n
th decision epoch, i.e.,
H_{0}=E
,
H_{n}=\mathbb{K}^{n}\times E(n\geqslant 1)
.

Definition 2.1
.

(a)

A (randomized history-dependent) policy for player 1 is a sequence
\pi=\{\pi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of stochastic kernels
\pi_{n}
on
A
given
H_{n}
satisfying
\pi_{n}(A(i_{n})|h_{n})=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
h_{n}=(i_{0},a_{0},b_{0},\ldots,i_{n-1},a_{n-1},b_{n-1},i_{n})\in H_{n}
.

(b)

A policy
\pi=\{\pi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
for player 1 is called Markov if for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a stochastic kernel
\varphi_{n}
on
A
given
E
such that
\pi_{n}(\cdot|h_{n})=\varphi_{n}(\cdot|i_{n})
for all
h_{n}=(i_{0},a_{0},b_{0},\ldots,i_{n-1},a_{n-1},b_{n-1},i_{n})\in H_{n}
. We write such a policy as
\pi=\{\varphi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

(c)

A Markov policy
\pi=\{\varphi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
for player 1 is said to be stationary if
\varphi_{n}
are independent of
n
. In this case, we write
\pi
as
\varphi
for simplicity.

(d)

A stationary policy
\varphi
for player 1 is called deterministic stationary if there is a map
f:E\to A
satisfying
\varphi(\cdot|i)=\delta_{f(i)}(\cdot)
for all
i\in E
, where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x
. We denote such a deterministic stationary policy by
f
for simplicity.

Denote by
\Pi_{1}
,
\Pi_{1}^{m}
,
\Pi_{1}^{s}
, and
\Pi_{1}^{sd}
the sets of all randomized history-dependent, Markov, stationary, and deterministic stationary policies for player 1, respectively.
Moreover, the sets
\Pi_{2}
,
\Pi_{2}^{m}
,
\Pi_{2}^{s}
, and
\Pi_{2}^{sd}
of all randomized history-dependent, Markov, stationary, and deterministic stationary policies, respectively, for player 2 are defined similarly, with
B
and
B(i)
in lieu of
A
and
A(i)
, respectively.

Given any initial state
i\in E
,
\pi=\{\pi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}\in\Pi_{1}
, and
\sigma=\{\sigma_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}\in\Pi_{2}
,
by the Ionescu Tulcea theorem
[
17
, Proposition C.10]
, there is a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}
on
((E\times A\times B)^{\infty},\mathcal{B}((E\times A\times B)^{\infty}))
satisfying that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}(X_{0}=i)=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}(A_{n}=a,B_{n}=b|Y_{n}=h_{n})=\pi_{n}(a|h_{n})\sigma_{n}(b|h_{n})\quad\forall a\in A,b\in B,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}(X_{n+1}=j|Y_{n}=h_{n},A_{n}=a_{n},B_{n}=b_{n})=P(j|i_{n},a_{n},b_{n})\quad\forall j\in E,

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h_{n}=(i_{0},a_{0},b_{0},\ldots,i_{n-1},a_{n-1},b_{n-1},i_{n})\in H_{n}
,
a_{n}\in A(i_{n})
, and
b_{n}\in B(i_{n})
, where for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega=(i_{0},a_{0},b_{0},\ldots,i_{n},a_{n},b_{n},\ldots)\in(E\times A\times B)^{\infty}
,

\displaystyle X_{n}(\omega):=i_{n},\ A_{n}(\omega):=a_{n},\ B_{n}(\omega):=b_{n},\ Y_{n}(\omega)=(i_{0},a_{0},b_{0},i_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},b_{n-1},i_{n}).

(2.2)

Let
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}
denote the expectation operator with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}
. Then, the risk-sensitive average cost for player 1 is defined by

\displaystyle J(i,\pi,\sigma):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\theta n}\ln\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}\left[e^{\theta\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c(X_{k},A_{k},B_{k})}\right],\quad i\in E,(\pi,\sigma)\in\Pi_{1}\times\Pi_{2}.

(2.3)

The upper and lower value functions are given by

\displaystyle\overline{J}(i):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{1}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Pi_{2}}J(i,\pi,\sigma),\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{J}(i):=\sup_{\sigma\in\Pi_{2}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{1}}J(i,\pi,\sigma),\quad i\in E,

respectively.
Moreover, if
\underline{J}(i)=\overline{J}(i)
for all
i\in E
, the common function is called the value of the game and is denoted by
J^{*}(i)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose that the value of the game exists and fix any
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
.

(a)

A policy
\pi^{\varepsilon}\in\Pi_{1}
for player 1 is called
\varepsilon
-optimal if

\displaystyle J(i,\pi^{\varepsilon},\sigma)\leqslant{J}^{*}(i)+\varepsilon\quad\forall i\in E,\sigma\in\Pi_{2}.

(b)

A policy
\sigma^{\varepsilon}\in\Pi_{2}
for player 2 is called
\varepsilon
-optimal if

\displaystyle J(i,\pi,\sigma^{\varepsilon})\geqslant{J}^{*}(i)-\varepsilon\quad\forall i\in E,\pi\in\Pi_{1}.

(c)

If both
\pi^{\varepsilon}\in\Pi_{1}
and
\sigma^{\varepsilon}\in\Pi_{2}
are
\varepsilon
-optimal, then the policy pair
(\pi^{\varepsilon},\sigma^{\varepsilon})
is said to be an
\varepsilon
-saddle point. In particular, a
0
-saddle point is called a saddle point.

(d)

A constant
r\in\mathbb{R}
is called an
\varepsilon
-approximation of the value if
\max_{i\in E}|J^{*}(i)-r|\leqslant\varepsilon
.

The aim of this paper is to develop algorithms for computing (at least approximating) the value and saddle points.

3
On the computation of
\varepsilon
-approximation of the value

In this section, we provide an algorithm to approximate the value.
To this end,
we recall the Shapley equation for the risk-sensitive average stochastic game, which is given by

\displaystyle\lambda h(i)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A(i))}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(B(i))}\sum_{a\in A(i)}\sum_{b\in B(i)}\sum_{j\in E}e^{\theta c(i,a,b)}h(j)P(j|i,a,b)\mu(a)\nu(b)\ \forall i\in E,

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{P}(A(i))
and
\mathcal{P}(B(i))
denote the sets of probability measures on
A(i)
and
B(i)
, respectively,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
h
is a function defined on
E
, and the pair
(\lambda,h)
is called a solution to the Shapley equation (
3.1
).

Next, we prove that the value exists by a solution to the Shapley equation. For convenience, we introduce some notation.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be the space of all real-valued functions
h
on
E
with the norm
\|h\|:=\max_{i\in E}|h(i)|
.
Note that
(\mathcal{M},\|\cdot\|)
is an ordered Banach space where
h\geqslant g
means that
h(i)\geqslant g(i)
for all
i\in E
. Denote

\mathcal{M}_{+}:=\{h\in\mathcal{M}|h(i)\geqslant 0,i\in E\},\quad\mathcal{M}_{+}^{o}:=\{h\in\mathcal{M}|h(i)>0,i\in E\}.

(3.2)

Clearly,

\mathcal{M}_{+}^{o}\subset\mathcal{M}_{+}\subset\mathcal{M}
.
Now, we define an operator
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{M}_{+}
as

\mathcal{L}h(i):=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A(i))}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(B(i))}\mathcal{L}^{\mu,\nu}h(i),\quad h\in\mathcal{M}_{+},i\in E,

(3.3)

where

\mathcal{L}^{\mu,\nu}h(i):=\sum_{a\in A(i)}\sum_{b\in B(i)}\sum_{j\in E}e^{\theta c(i,a,b)}h(j)P(j|i,a,b)\mu(a)\nu(b).

(3.4)

It follows from (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) that
\mathcal{L}
maps
\mathcal{M}_{+}
into itself. Therefore, for each
n\geqslant 0
, we can define an operator
\mathcal{L}^{n}
on
\mathcal{M}_{+}
by

\mathcal{L}^{0}h=h
and
\mathcal{L}^{n}h=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}^{n-1}h)
for each
h\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given any function
h\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
, a stationary policy pair
(\varphi,\psi)\in\Pi_{1}^{s}\times\Pi_{2}^{s}
is called a mini-max selector of
h
if

\mathcal{L}h(i)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(B(i))}\mathcal{L}^{\varphi(\cdot|i),\nu}h(i)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A(i))}\mathcal{L}^{\mu,\psi(\cdot|i)}h(i)=\mathcal{L}^{\varphi(\cdot|i),\psi(\cdot|i)}h(i)\ \forall i\in E.

(3.5)

Since
E
,
A
, and
B
are finite, the following result directly follow from
[
2
, Section 2.4]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
i\in E
and
h\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
,
the following linear program (Primal LP)
and its dual program (Dual LP) admit optimal solutions, denoted by
(w_{h}(i),\varphi_{h}(\cdot|i))
and
(v_{h}(i),\psi_{h}(\cdot|i))
, respectively.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\textup{Primal LP:}\\
&\min_{w,\mu}\,w\\
&\mbox{s.t.}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w\geqslant\mathcal{L}^{\mu,\delta_{b}}h(i)&\forall b\in B(i),\\
\sum_{a\in A(i)}\mu(a)=1,\\
\mu(a)\geqslant 0&\forall a\in A(i);\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} &\textup{Dual LP:}\\
&\max_{v,\nu}\,v\\
&\mbox{s.t.}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v\leqslant\mathcal{L}^{\delta_{a},\nu}h(i)&\forall a\in A(i),\\
\sum_{b\in B(i)}\nu(b)=1,\\
\nu(b)\geqslant 0&\forall b\in B(i).\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

(3.6)

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}h(i)=w_{h}(i)=v_{h}(i)
for all
i\in E
and
h\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
, and
(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})
is a mini-max selector of
h
.

To ensure the existence of the value and a saddle point, we require the following irreducibility condition, which is commonly used for risk-sensitive average game
[
3
,
7
,
12
]
.

Assumption 3.1

(Irreducibility condition)
.

Given any
(f,g)\in\Pi_{1}^{sd}\times\Pi_{2}^{sd}
, the Markov chain
\{X_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is irreducible under the policy pair
(f,g)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the following statements hold.

(a)

The limit

\lambda_{*}:=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}
exists, where
{\bf 1}
represents the constant function one.

(b)

There exists a function
h^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{o}
such that
\lambda_{*}h^{*}=\mathcal{L}h^{*}
, i.e., the Shapley equation (
3.1
) has a solution
(\lambda_{*},h^{*})
.

(c)

The value of the game exists and satisfies

J^{*}(i)={\theta}^{-1}{\ln\lambda_{*}}
for all
i\in E.

Moreover, the game admits a stationary saddle point
(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})\in\Pi_{1}^{s}\times\Pi_{2}^{s}
.

Proof.

(a) From (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}h_{1}\geqslant\mathcal{L}h_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}rh_{1}=r\mathcal{L}h_{1}\quad\forall h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{+},h_{1}\geqslant h_{2},r\geqslant 0.

(3.7)

This, by
[
22
, Lemma 2.0.7]
, yields that
the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\sup_{h\in\mathcal{M}_{+},\|h\|\leqslant 1}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}h\|)^{\frac{1}{n}}
exists. Furthermore, using an induction argument, we obtain that

\mathcal{L}^{n}h\leqslant\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
with
\|h\|\leqslant 1
, which implies
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{M}_{+},\|h\|\leqslant 1}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}h\|=\|\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}\|
. Therefore, the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}
exists and

\displaystyle\lambda_{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\sup_{h\in\mathcal{M}_{+},\|h\|\leqslant 1}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}h\|)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

(3.8)

(b) Since
c
is nonnegative, we have
\mathcal{L}{\bf 1}\geqslant{\bf 1}
. Thus, by the monotonicity
of
\mathcal{L}
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}^{n+1}{\bf 1}\geqslant\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}\geqslant{\bf 1}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.9)

This, together with (
3.8
), implies
\lambda_{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathcal{L}^{n}{\bf 1}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}\geqslant 1.

On the other hand, by (
3.4
) and (
3.3
) we derive that

\|\mathcal{L}u-\mathcal{L}v\|=\max_{i\in E}|\mathcal{L}u(i)-\mathcal{L}v(i)|\leqslant e^{\theta||c||}\|u-v\|\quad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{M}_{+},

(3.10)

with
||c||:=\max_{(i,a,b)\in\mathbb{K}}c(i,a,b)
, which implies that the set
\{\mathcal{L}u|u\in\mathcal{M}_{+},\|u\|\leqslant r\}
is bounded for any
r\geqslant 0
. Then, by the finiteness of
E
, we have that the set
\{\mathcal{L}u|u\in\mathcal{M}_{+},\|u\|\leqslant r\}
is relatively compact, which together with (
3.10
) implies that
\mathcal{L}
is a compact operator.
According to the nonlinear Krein-Rutman theorem
[
22
, Proposition 3.1.5]
,
the compactness of
\mathcal{L}
, (
3.7
), and the inequality
\lambda_{*}\geqslant 1
guarantee that
there exists a function
h^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
satisfying
\lambda_{*}h^{*}=\mathcal{L}h^{*}
and
||h^{*}||\neq 0
.

Next, we prove
h^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{o}
.
By Lemma
3.1
, there exists
(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})\in\Pi_{1}^{s}\times\Pi_{2}^{s}
such that
\mathcal{L}h^{*}(i)=\mathcal{L}^{\varphi^{*}(\cdot|i),\psi^{*}(\cdot|i)}h^{*}(i)
for all
i\in E
. This, together with the nonnegativity of
c
and (
3.4
), yields that

\mathcal{L}h^{*}(i)\geqslant\sum_{a\in A(i)}\sum_{b\in B(i)}\sum_{j\in E}h^{*}(j)P(j|i,a,b)\varphi^{*}(a|i)\psi^{*}(b|i)
for all
i\in E
.
Then, using the equality
\lambda_{*}h^{*}=\mathcal{L}h^{*}
, we can prove by induction that

h^{*}(i)\geqslant\lambda_{*}^{-n}\mathbb{E}^{\varphi^{*},\psi^{*}}_{i}[h^{*}(X_{n})]\quad\forall n\geqslant 0,i\in E.

(3.11)

From
h^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
and
||h^{*}||\neq 0
, there is a state
j\in E
with
h^{*}(j)>0
. On the other hand, under Assumption
3.1
, for each
i\in E
, there is a positive integer
N_{i}
such that

\mathbb{P}^{\varphi^{*},\psi^{*}}_{{i}}(X_{N_{i}}=j)>0
. Therefore, using (
3.11
) we obtain that

h^{*}(i)\geqslant\lambda_{*}^{-N_{i}}\mathbb{E}^{\varphi^{*},\psi^{*}}_{{i}}[h^{*}(X_{N_{i}})]\geqslant\lambda_{*}^{-N_{i}}h^{*}(j)\mathbb{P}^{\varphi^{*},\psi^{*}}_{{i}}(X_{N_{i}}=j)>0\quad\forall i\in E,

which implies
h^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{o}
.

(c) The desired result follows by combining
[
6
, Theorem 1]
and part (b).
∎

Proposition
3.1
shows that under the irreducibility condition the game has the value and a saddle point. Next, we introduce the irreducibility coefficient and establish the relationship between it and the irreducibility condition, which is used to propose an algorithm for computing
\varepsilon
-approximations of the value.

Definition 3.2
.

The irreducible coefficient is defined as

\displaystyle\gamma:=\min_{(i,j)\in E^{2}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{1}}\inf_{\sigma\in\Pi_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}(\tau_{j}\leqslant|E|),

(3.12)

where
\tau_{j}:=\inf\{n>0\mid X_{n}=j\}
denotes the first return time to
j\in E
with
\inf\emptyset:=+\infty
, and
|E|
is the cardinality of the state space
E
.

Proposition 3.2
.

(a)

For each
i,j\in E
, define

\displaystyle V_{0}^{j}(i):=1\quad\text{and}\quad V_{k+1}^{j}(i):=\max_{a\in A(i)}\max_{b\in B(i)}\sum_{i_{1}\neq j}P(i_{1}|i,a,b)V_{k}^{j}(i_{1}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.13)

Then, the irreducible coefficient
\gamma
satisfies
\gamma=1-\max_{(i,j)\in E^{2}}V_{|E|}^{j}(i)
.

(b)

Assumption
3.1
holds if and only if
\gamma>0
.

Proof.

(a) Given any
k\geqslant 1
,
\pi=\{\pi_{n},n\geqslant 0\}\in\Pi_{1}
,
\sigma=\{\sigma_{n},n\geqslant 0\}\in\Pi_{2}
, and
i,j\in E
, it follows from (
3.13
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi,\sigma}(\tau_{j}\leqslant k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{i_{0}\in E}\delta_{i}(i_{0})\sum_{a_{0}\in A(i_{0})}\sum_{b_{0}\in B(i_{0})}\sum_{i_{1}\in E\setminus\{j\}}P(i_{1}|i_{0},a_{0},b_{0})\pi_{0}(a_{0}|h_{0})\sigma_{0}(b_{0}|h_{0})\cdots\sum_{a_{k-1}\in A(i_{k-1})}

\displaystyle\sum_{b_{k-1}\in B(i_{k-1})}\sum_{i_{k}\in E\setminus\{j\}}P(i_{k}|i_{k-1},a_{k-1},b_{k-1})\pi_{k-1}(a_{k-1}|h_{k-1})\sigma_{k-1}(b_{k-1}|h_{k-1})

In this section, we introduce an example of energy management in smart grids.

Example 5.1

There is a prosumer

and renewable energy generators, such as wind turbines, tidal turbines, or solar panels.
The stored charge in the storage unit may be reduced due to self-discharge resulting from internal chemical reactions

]

Additionally, since the amount of energy generated by renewable energy generators depends on natural environments, the energy harvested from renewable resources is random. Denote by

(

[n,n+1)

[

Prosumer

When the storage level at the beginning of period

may purchase
b_{n}^{p}

(0\leqslant b^{p}_{n}\leqslant N_{p})

(0\leqslant b^{c}_{n}\leqslant(i_{n}+b_{n}^{p})\wedge N_{c})

N_{p}
and

represent the maximum purchased energy and the maximum consumable energy, respectively.
Then, the storage level at the beginning of period
[n+1,n+2)
will be
\min\{N_{s},\max\{0,G_{n}+i_{n}+b_{n}^{p}-b_{n}^{c}\}\}
.

On the other hand, since the energy price set by the utility company depends on total demands of the electricity market, the cost of purchasing energy for prosumer

and

Moreover, let

Then the payoff of prosumer

Since the energy demand of the other prosumers is unknown for prosumer

we suppose that Prosumer

We now formulate this energy management problem as a risk-sensitive stochastic game, where player 2 and player 1 are prosumer

and the other prosumers in the electricity market, respectively.
Let
E=\{0,1,\ldots,N_{s}\}
be the state space. The action spaces of player 1 and player 2 are

,

and

\displaystyle P(j|i,a,b)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-(i+b^{p}-b^{c})}Q(k)&j=0,\\
Q(j-(i+b^{p}-b^{c}))\ &0<j<N_{s},\\
