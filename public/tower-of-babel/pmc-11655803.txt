Differentiating clinically significant prostate cancer from clinically insignificant prostate cancer using qualitative and semi-quantitative indices of dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI
Authors: Tsutomu Tamada, Mitsuru Takeuchi, Hiroyuki Watanabe, Atsushi Higaki, Kazunori Moriya, Akihiko Kanki, Yoshihiko Fukukura, Akira Yamamoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the utility of qualitative and semi-quantitative evaluation of DCE-MRI for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer (csPC).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 307 lesions in 231 patients who underwent 3.0T MRI. Experienced radiologists assessed PI-RADS v 2.1 assessment category, qualitative contrast enhancement (QCE), contrast enhancement pattern (CEP: type 1, 2, 3), tumor contrast ratio, and tumor size of PC lesions in consensus. Mean and 0–10th-percentile ADC value of the lesion (ADCmean and ADC0−10) were calculated. Specimens obtained from MRI–ultrasound fusion-guided prostate biopsy were used as the pathological reference standard.ResultsIn assessment of tumor aggressiveness, PI-RADS assessment category, QCE, tumor size, and ratio of CEP 2 + 3 were significantly higher in PC with Gleason score (GS) ≥ 3 + 4 (n = 256) than in PC with GS = 6 (n = 51) (P ≤ 0.001). Tumor ADCmean and tumor ADC0−10 were comparable between PC with GS ≥ 3 + 4 and PC with GS = 6 (P = 0.164 to 0.504). Regarding diagnostic performance of csPC in 45 PI-RADS 3 transition zone lesions, only ratio of CEP 2 + 3 was significantly higher in PC with GS ≥ 3 + 4 (n = 31) than in PC with GS = 6 (n = 14) (P = 0.008).ConclusionQualitative DCE-MRI indices may contribute to PC aggressiveness and improve detection of csPC in PI-RADS assessment category 3 lesions.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is classified into clinically significant prostate cancer (csPC) that requires curative therapy such as radical prostatectomy, and clinically insignificant prostate cancer (cisPC) that is indicated for active surveillance using prostate-specific antigen (PSA). Prostate multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) with T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) plays an important role in the detection of csPC [1–4]. The imaging acquisition, evaluation, and reporting of prostate mpMRI are standardized in the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS). In the latest version (v2.1) [5], DCE-MRI has a minor role in detecting csPC and plays only a small part in diagnosing lesions in the peripheral zone [1]. However, DCE-MRI has a role as a backup sequence for DWI in the case of degraded image quality due to susceptibility artifacts, low spatial resolution, and low signal-to-noise ratio [5]. Furthermore, DCE-MRI increases confidence in the clinical diagnosis of csPC because it has been shown that more aggressive lesions tend to show strong early enhancement [6]. In detection of csPC, it is crucial to accurately assess pre-treatment risk stratification of PC, for example as Gleason score 6 (GS = 6) (cisPC) versus GS ≥ 3 + 4 (csPC) [7]. A systematic review of 39 studies with 2457 patients has reported a weakly to moderately negative correlation between tumor GS and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) obtained from DWI [8]. However, the substantial overlap of ADCs between GS groups limits its suitability for clinical application [9]. In quantitative DWI, non-Gaussian and bi-exponential fitting models, which are expected to improve the accuracy of assessing water molecule diffusivity compared to standard monoexponential ADC has only comparable discrimination ability for assessing tumor aggressiveness in PC [9]. However, it has been reported that even with monoexponential ADC, histogram analysis such as ADC 0–10th-percentile value (ADC0−10) is desirable in PC, for the reasons that ADC0−10 indicates aggressive regions more clearly than does mean ADC and because PC is characterized by tissue heterogeneity within the tumor [9]. The diagnosis of tumor GS is most accurately determined in specimens from radical prostatectomy. However, the number of PC with GS = 6 in clinical studies is limited because radical prostatectomy is often performed in patients with csPC. Therefore, it is not necessarily easy to compare GS = 6 and GS ≥ 3 + 4 lesions. As a recent study of developments in prostate biopsy methods has reported that MR-guided biopsies such as MRI-ultrasound fusion-guided prostate biopsy (MRGB) can diagnose tumor GS more accurately than conventional systematic biopsy [3], we decided to collect cases diagnosed by MRGB and conduct a clinical study of tumor aggressiveness in PC. It is problematic in daily clinical practice that the detection rate for csPC lesions in PI-RADS assessment category 3 is reported to be approximately 30% [10, 11]. The majority of PI-RADS assessment category 3 lesions diagnosed as benign conditions or as cisPC are in the transition zone (TZ). This insufficient diagnostic performance can lead to unnecessary biopsies. The purpose of this study is to investigate the diagnostic performance of clinical information, PI-RADS category, quantitative DWI, and qualitative and semi-quantitative DCE for diagnosis of csPC.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kawasaki Medical School (Application No. 5490-02) and was performed in accordance with principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent was waived for this study because of its retrospective design.

Study population

Eligible for inclusion in the study were 419 consecutive male patients with elevated PSA levels or suspected PC who underwent 3.0T prostate multiparametric MRI, including T2-weighted imaging, DWI, and DCE-MRI as well as MRGB of lesions suggestive of PC between October 2019 and March 2023. Of the 419 patients, 188 were excluded for the following reasons: diagnosis of benign prostatic condition by MRGB (n = 174); previous therapy including radiation therapy, radical prostatectomy, or bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) administration for bladder cancer (n = 13); and prostatic neuroendocrine tumor (n = 1). After applying these exclusion criteria, 231 patients were included in this study.

Imaging technique

All prebiopsy prostate mpMRI examinations were performed using a 3.0T MRI scanner (Ingenia 3.0T CX Quasar Dual or Ingenia Elition 3.0T; Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) equipped with a 32-channel digital coil for signal reception. As a measure against motion and magnetic susceptibility artifacts, scopolamine butylbromide or glucagon was administered intramuscularly immediately before the MR examination to reduce intestinal peristalsis. The mpMRI protocol comprised the following sequences: axial T2WI, axial DCE-MRI, axial DWI, coronal T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), and 3D T2WI. The coronal T1WI sequence was not evaluated in the present study. Table 1 lists the MR imaging parameters in detail. DCE-MRI was obtained using a three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted gradient echo sequence with fat suppression, acquired every 9.8 s for 177 s (18 phases). DCE-MRI acquisition began simultaneously with the start of intravenous injection of a gadolinium-based contrast medium at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg body weight at a rate of 1.5 mL/s (gadobutrol; Gadovist; Bayer AG, Germany) followed by a 30-mL saline flush at a rate of 1.5 mL/s. In DWI, ADC map was obtained with b-values of 0 and 2000 s/mm2 using a non-Gaussian, mono-exponential fitting model.

MR-guided biopsy

MRGB was performed using the UroStation system (Koelis; Grenoble, France), which features a workstation for segmentation of the prostate and the lesion, elastic image fusion technology between MR images and trans-rectal US (TRUS) images, and real-time 3D organ-tracking technology. Thin-slice 3D T2WI was used in MRGB to ensure accuracy of fusion with the TRUS images. To avoid inter-reader diagnostic bias as much as possible, a fellowship-trained abdominal radiologist with 25 years of experience in prostate MRI (T.T.) determined all target lesions for MRGB immediately prior to prostate biopsy using PI-RADS v2.1 and conventional overall multiparametric MRI assessment (Likert scale) [5, 12]. At MRGB, the same radiologist performed prostate MRI segmentation in all patients and identified lesions for biopsy. MRI cross-sectional images that included lesions were captured and saved in a PowerPoint file. At least two cores were obtained from each targeted lesion. The experienced urologists responsible for the biopsy determined the need for additional cores based on lesion size, lesion location, and confidence in targeting accuracy.

Clinical and histopathologic analysis

A study coordinator (H.W.) recorded each patient’s age, PSA level (ng/mL) at the time of the MRI examination, and the PI-RADS assessment category and tumor GS of each PC lesion. Specimens obtained from MRGB were stained with hematoxylin-eosin. All specimens were assessed and assigned a tumor GS by board-certified attending pathologists in accordance with the 2014 International Society of Urological Pathology Modified Gleason Grading System [13]. The minimum tumor diameter of the targeted lesion for MRGB was 5 mm. CsPC was defined as GS 7 or greater. If a patient had multiple targeted lesions, the GS was determined individually.

Image analysis

Analysis of prostate mpMRI was performed by two fellowship-trained radiologists with 16 and 25 years of experience in prostate MRI (A.H. and T.T., respectively) who assessed the images of each patient in consensus, using a dedicated workstation (Synapse EX; Fujifilm Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The PC lesions of each patient were confirmed by referring to the PowerPoint file showing MR images of the target lesion of prostate biopsy and the pathology report showing the biopsy results, which were prepared in advance of the MRGB implementation.

First, for each PC lesion, DCE-MRI was assessed for qualitative enhancement effect (QCE), contrast enhancement ratio (CER), and contrast enhancement pattern (CEP). A time–intensity curve (TIC) was generated for each lesion using a dedicated workstation (Synapse EX). QCE was determined visually using a 5-point grading scale (1 = none, 2 = weak, 3 = mild, 4 = moderate, 5 = strong) in the phase with the highest contrast effect in the TIC. The radiologists measured the pre-contrast signal intensity (SIpre) and post-contrast maximum signal intensity (SIpost) of the target lesion by setting a circular or oval region of interest (ROI) with reference to the TIC. CER was calculated using the following formula: CER = (SIpost-SIpre)/SIpre. Each ROI was as large as possible. QCE and SIpost were defined as the maximum SI until 90 s after contrast administration. The tumor CEP was classified as one of three types using semi-quantitative kinetics with TIC [14]. Type 1, delayed enhancement with a gradual increase in SI over time; type 2, early enhancement followed by plateau; and type 3, early enhancement followed by wash-out. Plateau was defined as a change in SI of within ± 10% after early enhancement, wash-out as a 10% or greater decrease in SI from early enhancement, and early enhancement as a maximum signal intensity at 60–90 s after contrast injection that was at least twice the pre-contrast signal intensity.

The same radiologists then performed quantitative analysis of the DW images. Mean ADC value (ADCmean (×10–3 mm2/s) and ADC0−10 (×10–3 mm2/s) of the tumor were calculated by histogram analysis on a dedicated workstation (Synapse EX) using the ROI placement technique. The ROI was as large a circle or oval as possible in the slice showing the largest diameter of the tumor. The ROIs were placed to avoid the periprostatic venous plexus, neurovascular bundle, and urethra. The area of the ROI (mm2) was defined as the size of the tumor. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP v11.0.0 software (SAS, Cary, NC) and SPSS for Windows v24.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL). Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare PI-RADS assessment category, QCE, CER, tumor size, ADCmean, and ADC0−10 between PC with GS = 3 + 3 and PC with GS ≥ 3 + 4. The frequency of each type of CEP (type 1 versus type 2 versus type 3, and type 1 versus Type 2 + 3) was compared using Fisher’s exact test or the χ2 test. Discrimination ability between PC with GS = 3 + 3 and PC with GS ≥ 3 + 4 for each index was calculated as area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, using receiver operator characteristic curve (ROC) analysis. These comparisons were performed for all PC (peripheral zone (PZ) + TZ), only PZ tumor, only TZ tumor, and PI-RADS assessment category 3 in TZ. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Included in the study were 231 patients with 307 lesions (age range, 50–89 years; mean age, 72.1 years). Median PSA level at the time of initial MRI was 7.82 ng/mL (range, 1.46–160.23 ng/mL). The mean interval between MRI examination and MR-guided biopsy was 39 ± 36 days (range, 2–276 days; median, 28 days). Of the 307 lesions, 175 were located in the peripheral zone, 127 in the transition zone, and 5 in both the peripheral zone and transition zone. These lesions were pathologically diagnosed as GS = 3 + 3 in 51, GS = 3 + 4 in 140, GS = 4 + 3 in 65, GS = 4 + 4 in 31, GS = 4 + 5 in 19, and GS = 5 + 5 in 1. Mean tumor size (maximum area) of the 307 lesions was 44.8 mm2 ± 44.9 mm2 (range, 6.4–481 mm2). In 307 lesions, 45 TZ lesions and 17 PZ lesions were diagnosed as PI-RADS assessment category 3 prior to MRGB.

Discrimination ability between csPC and cisPC

In discrimination between GS = 3 + 3 lesions (n = 51) and GS ≥ 3 + 4 lesions (n = 256) in all lesions (PZ + TZ), PI-RADS assessment category, QCE, and tumor size were significantly lower in GS = 3 + 3 lesions than in GS ≥ 3 + 4 lesions (P = 0.01 to P < 0.001) (Table 2). The ratio of CEP with pattern 1 was significantly higher in GS = 3 + 3 than in lesions GS ≥ 3 + 4 lesions (all P < 0.001) (Table 2). PSA, tumor ADCmean, tumor ADC0−10, and CER were comparable between PC with GS ≥ 3 + 4 and PC with GS = 3 + 3 (P = 0.105 to 0.649). The AUC, sensitivity, and specificity for all indices were 0.520–0.674, 31.6–92.2%, and 25.5–90.2%, respectively (Table 2). Representative cases are shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

Discussion

In this study, we used MRGB as a reference standard for pathology to evaluate the tumor aggressiveness of PC and the discriminatory ability of csPC and cisPC in PI-RADS assessment category 3 lesions using clinical findings, quantitative DWI, and qualitative and semi-quantitative DCE-MRI.

First, in the assessment of tumor aggressiveness in all PC, i.e., in the discrimination between cisPC (GS = 3 + 3 lesions) and csPC (GS ≥ 3 + 4 lesions), PI-RADS assessment category and tumor size were higher in csPC than in cisPC, but PSA was comparable. Thus, larger sized PI-RADS rated category 4 lesions are highly suspicious for csPC. In contrast, PSA indicates suspicion of prostate cancer but is not useful for distinguishing whether or not the cancer is clinically significant [5, 6]. In the present study, the quantitative DWI indices showed no significant difference between csPC and cisPC. Some previous studies have reported significant differences in ADCmean and 25th percentile ADC in csPC and cisPC [2, 15], whereas others have found a wide range of correlation coefficients between GS and mean ADC obtained by DWI at 3T, suggesting unreliability for clinical application. (ρ = − 0.313 to − 0.645) [2, 16, 17]. Variation in correlation coefficients between studies may be due to several factors, including differences in the distribution of GS in PCs, differences in the number of patients in each study, and the presence of specific histologic types (cribriform pattern in Gleason pattern 4) in high-grade PC that may increase ADC. In addition, one meta-analysis (13 studies with 1107 tumor foci in 705 patients) concluded that although ADC values show moderate accuracy in separating high-risk from low-risk PCa, important biases may overestimate performance and unexplained sources of heterogeneity probably exist [18]. In contrast, there was a significant difference between csPC and cisPC in QCE and CEP in DCE-MRI in the present study. In other words, compared to csPC, cisPC tended to show a weaker contrast enhancement effect in the early phase and a more delayed enhancement pattern, resembling the findings of normal prostatic tissues. The qualitative indices provide additional information that has great potential for clinical application in evaluation of tumor aggressiveness because they are easy to assess in routine clinical practice and require little time for evaluation. The semi-quantitative indicator, CER, was comparable between the two groups. The T1 value of csPC was lower than that of cisPC [19]; i.e., SIpre of csPC was higher than that of cisPC, which may have reduced the difference between the two groups in CER ((SIpost-SIpre)/SIpre). In a previous study that used the quantitative DCE-MRI index (permeability MRI), Kep and Ve had significant differences in their comparisons [15]. However, the DCE-MRI quantitative indexes are subject to strict imaging procedures and evaluation is complicated, which limit its widespread use in clinical practice.

Second, with respect to discrimination between csPC and cisPC in PZ lesions, results were similar for all lesions in terms of PSA, PI-RADS assessment category, tumor size, ADC value, CER, and CEP. QCE was higher for csPC than for cisPC, but there was no significant differences (P = 0.090). As with all lesions, CEP tended to show significantly delayed enhancement pattern in cisPC and early enhancement pattern in csPC.

Finally, in TZ lesions, there was a significant difference between csPC and cisPC in terms of PI-RADS assessment category, QCE, and CEP. Woo et al. reported no significant difference in tumor ADC between csPC and cisPC when PZ or TZ lesions were evaluated individually, unlike in whole lesions [20]. The present results suggest that PI-RADS assessment category and CEP may provide clinically useful information for differentiating csPC from cisPC in both the peripheral and transitional zones, whereas the quantitative indices of DCE-MRI and DWI failed to show any valuable effect on their discrimination. However, in assessment of tumor aggressiveness in all lesions, in PZ lesions, and in TZ lesions, values of AUC, sensitivity, and specificity for each index did not indicate high discrimination between csPC and cisPC (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Therefore, consideration should be given to whether they should be used as reference information for assessment of tumor aggressiveness, and whether they should be used to help develop clinically applicable methods such as machine learning models that combine these useful indices. Moreover, considering the advancements in automated image analysis, such as the fully-automated deep learning-powered system for DCE-MRI analysis, the study could benefit from exploring the integration of such technologies to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of DCE-MRI analysis in csPC detection. This would not only improve the clinical workflow efficiency but also potentially reduce the impact of inter-observer variability.

PI-RADS v2.1 has the problem in routine clinical practice that only about 30% of Category 3 TZ lesions are csPC, and many unnecessary biopsies are performed on category 3 lesions [10, 11]. The drawback with making PI-RADS assessment category 3 eligible for MR-targeted biopsy is that it increases the number of unnecessary biopsies. In addition, the above csPC cancer detection rate is based on evaluation by experienced radiologists, and is expected to be even lower if assessment is performed by inexperienced radiologists. In the present category 3 TZ lesions, only CEP showed a significant difference between csPC and cisPC. Of note, DCE-MRI is not used to diagnose TZ lesions in PI-RADS v2.1 [5]. This is probably due to the fact that benign prostatic hyperplasia nodules show a strong contrast effect, as do PCs. However, displaying TIC on lesions diagnosed as category 3 in PI-RADS v2.1 and evaluating their patterns may enable a reduction in unnecessary biopsies if the diagnostic performance of csPC is improved. Furthermore, evaluation of the CEP pattern has the advantage of being easily assessed by inexperienced radiologists. Based on our study results, biopsy of CEP type 2 or type 3 PI-RADS 3 lesions avoids 36% of unnecessary biopsies (5 lesions (true negative)/14 lesions (true negative + false positive)) compared to biopsy of all PI-RADS 3 lesions.

Our study had several limitations. First, this was a single-institutional retrospective study with consensus evaluation in two experienced radiologists. In addition, the small number of GS = 3 + 3 lesions in this study (n = 51) could potentially affect the statistical results. It is necessary to validate the present results in future prospective multi-institutional clinical studies with an inter-observer variability using multiple readers and a larger number of a separate cohort patients. Second, in this study, there were only 17 PZ lesions (GS = 3 + 3, 3 lesions and GS ≥ 3 + 4, 14 lesions) in the PI-RADS category 3, so we excluded them from the analysis regarding PI-RADS category 3 lesions. Finally, the pathological evaluation did not use specimens after total prostatectomy. However, matching a post-total prostatectomy specimen to lesions on MRI images is not always easy, and pathologic evaluation in MRGB, which allows direct biopsy of MRI visible lesions, has gained a fair amount of credibility [3, 21]. We also believe that the accuracy of MRI evaluation and biopsy accuracy is somewhat assured because all MRI image contouring and lesion definition in the present study was performed by experienced radiologists, and all targeted biopsies were performed by experienced urologists. However, it was not possible to evaluate MRI invisible lesions in this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CEP may contribute to assessment of prostate cancer aggressiveness and improve detection of csPC in PI-RADS assessment category 3 lesions.
