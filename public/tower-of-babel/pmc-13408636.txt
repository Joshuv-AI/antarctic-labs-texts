Mechanical and durability performance of gypseous soils stabilized with zeolite and lime
Authors: Shahram Ghasemi, Mashalah Khamehchiyan, Mohammad Reza Nikudel, Erfan Sadeghi, Ahmad Zalooli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGypseous soils create engineering challenges because of their unpredictable behavior when exposed to moisture. In practice, traditional stabilizers such as cement and lime are widely used but are often costly and less sustainable. This study investigates natural clinoptilolite zeolite as an eco-friendly stabilizer for gypseous soils (10–40% gypsum content) compared to lime. Zeolite was activated by sodium hydroxide (mol/L) and in combination with lime. Stabilizers were applied at 3–10% dosages by dry weight. Mechanical performance (UCS, P-wave velocity) and durability under wetting–drying and freeze–thaw cycles were evaluated, supported by SEM/EDAX, XRD, and MIP analyses. Results show that zeolite stabilization outperforms lime across all gypsum contents in terms of strength and durability. For soils containing 40% gypsum, treated with 10% zeolite resulted in a 406% increase in UCS after 28 days of curing, compared to only a 129% increase for lime-treated samples. SEM/EDAX, XRD analyses show that zeolite addition suppresses ettringite formation, unlike lime-stabilized samples. When a small portion of lime was used with zeolite, the overall strength increased, although some ettringite still appeared. During wetting–drying cycles lime-stabilized soils failed before 9 cycles while zeolite-stabilized samples mostly maintain their integrity after 9 cycles. Post-cycle MIP revealed only 1–2% porosity increase for zeolite versus 4–5% for lime after wetting–drying cycles. The better performance of zeolite stabilization over lime is attributed to the absence of ettringite, as well as its ability to limit gypsum dissolution and leaching during wetting–drying cycles. Although zeolite shows slightly higher moisture sensitivity under freeze–thaw, it offers significant advantages over conventional calcium-based stabilization for sulfate-rich soils.Keywords: Gypseous soil, Zeolite, Lime, Wetting-drying, Freeze-thaw, EttringiteSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Soils containing calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O) are known as gypseous soils. These soils are usually found in arid and semi-arid climates, such as North Africa, the Middle East, Eastern Europe, Australia, and North America1. In such environments, low rainfall limits the leaching of gypsum, allowing it to accumulate within the soil profile. Gypsum may occur naturally in soils or, be added in limited quantities to improve geotechnical characteristics2,3. However, high gypsum contents can create serious problems in engineering and agricultural applications4. Gypsum content strongly controls the physical and mechanical characteristics of these soils. Generally, soils containing more than 2% gypsum are classified as gypseous5, and contents above 10% noticeably affect engineering performance6.

Gypseous soils are considered problematic due to their erratic and unpredictable behaviors when came into contact with moisture, mainly due to dissolution, erosion, and swelling7,8. These soils can adversely affect buildings and infrastructure through sulfate attack on concrete, reinforcement corrosion, and excessive soil swelling. In dry conditions gypsum acts as a natural cement that gives the soil favorable engineering properties. However, these properties deteriorate when moisture is introduced9. Gypsum dissolution during wetting weakens interparticle bonds and may cause collapse and settlement10. In contrast, when the anhydrous form of calcium sulfate (anhydrite) is present, hydration and volumetric expansion can increase swelling pressures11. These issues are particularly important in developing arid regions experiencing rapid urbanization and industrial growth on gypsum-bearing soils4,12.

The performance of gypseous soils can be improved through mechanical and chemical stabilization methods. Mechanical techniques, including compaction, soil reinforcement using geosynthetics or natural fibers, and pre-wetting can enhance soil properties4,13–15. However, the benefits of these methods are often short‑term and may not provide acceptable resistance under prolonged wetting-drying cycles. Chemical stabilization improves soil mechanical behavior by modifying mineralogical composition and interparticle bonding. This approach typically increases bearing capacity and resistance to volumetric changes, while reducing plasticity, compressibility, and excessive settlement16,17. Cement and lime are the most widely used stabilizers because of their availability and proven effectiveness. Nevertheless, their application has raised concerns regarding sustainability, as their production is energy intensive, emits large amounts of CO₂, and may introduce risks such as heavy metal release18,19. Harris, et al.20 observed an increase incidence in pavement failures attributed to the formation of expansive secondary minerals, predominantly ettringite (Ca6Al12(SO4)3(OH)12·26H2O) and/or thaumasite (Ca3Si(OH)6(CO3)(SO4)·12H2O) when calcium-based stabilizers were used for soil stabilization21,22. Ettringite generally forms through reactions between calcium-based additives and clay minerals in the presence of sulfate ions derived from gypsum23–26. The formation of these expansive minerals induces soil heave, cracking, and a significant loss of mechanical strength.

Although lime and cement can provide short-term improvements, their durability and environmental impacts remain important drawbacks. Stabilized gypseous soils are often susceptible to degradation under natural weathering processes, including cyclic wetting–drying, freeze–thaw action, and groundwater fluctuations. Durability under environmental cycling is crucial for gypseous soils, as short-term strength may not accurately reflect long-term field performance. Aldaood et al.23 reported that repeated wetting–drying cycles reduce both UCS and P-wave velocity in lime-treated gypseous soils, and deterioration intensified as gypsum content increases. This behavior was mainly attributed to moisture ingress and crack propagation during durability cycles, gypsum dissolution during wetting, and the formation of ettringite. Aldaood et al.24 showed that freeze–thaw deterioration in lime-treated gypseous soils is strongly controlled by gypsum content and moisture availability during cycling.

Despite these limitations, the development of sustainable alternatives remain essential for gypseous soil stabilization. Calcium-based industrial by-products including, GGBFS, fly ash, calcium carbide residue, cement kiln dust, and steel slag27–30 have been widely investigated. However, at higher gypsum contents, the availability of calcium ions promotes ettringite formation and swelling, thereby exacerbating soil deterioration31–33. To mitigate the adverse effects of calcium-based stabilizers, partial replacement of lime and cement with materials, such as fly ash has been suggested as a practical approach for stabilizing gypseous soils34–36. Although these materials have somewhat improved soil properties, exposure of the treated soil to moisture often results in rapid ettringite formation and subsequent damage. An alternative strategy involves stabilizing gypseous soils using non‑calcium‑based binders. Stabilizers such as crude oil37,38, acids, polymers and enzymes22,39–41, nanomaterials42, calcium-free metakaolin-based geopolymers43 and barium based compounds44 show inconsistent performance, toxicity and disposal concerns. These drawbacks restrict their large-scale applicability when resources and financial capacity are limited. Consequently, there remains an urgent need for cost‑effective and environmentally sustainable stabilizers for gypseous soils.

Natural zeolite can address many of the aforementioned challenges owing to its pozzolanic aluminosilicate composition, reacting with cement Ca(OH)₂, thereby enhancing strength, durability45,46. From an economic standpoint, natural zeolite is widely available in many regions worldwide and commonly occurs in thick, easily accessible zeolitic tuff deposits, improving its cost‑effectiveness as a stabilization material. Compared with many industrial binders, zeolite requires relatively low processing energy47. It can also be incorporated into soils using conventional mixing and compaction equipment routinely employed in stabilization projects, supporting large-scale implementation. In addition, natural zeolite exhibits pozzolanic activity comparable to that of fly ash and silica fume, making it a technically competitive supplementary material48. Numerous studies have reported the beneficial effects of incorporating zeolite in combination with cement for soil stabilization. Turkoz and Vural49 showed that replacing 6–10% of cement with zeolite reduced swelling and dispersivity of cohesive soils. ShahriarKian et al.50 investigated cement–zeolite mixtures in low‑plasticity silty and clayey sands and observed notable improvements in strength and durability. Mola-Abasi et al.51 reported that partial substitution of cement with zeolite significantly enhanced the strength of cement-stabilized sands. Chenarboni, et al.52 investigated the mechanical behavior of stabilized expansive soil and found that replacing 30% of cement with zeolite resulted in maximum compressive strength. Öncü and Bilsel53 observed an 85% reduction in swell potential when expansive soils were treated with 50% zeolite. Furthermore, Sadeghi et al.54 presented encouraging evidence that combination of zeolite and cement can effectively improve the properties of natural loess soil. While natural clinoptilolite zeolite offers several advantages, it also has certain limitations. Although its pozzolanic activity can be comparable to that of fly ash under appropriate conditions, its reactivity is generally lower than conventional cementitious binders, resulting in slower strength development. When used alone, zeolite does not significantly improve soil strength, as its pozzolanic behavior requires activation; therefore, it is commonly combined with cement. In addition, its performance depends on mineralogical purity, which may cause variability in efficiency.

Building on insights from previous studies, the use of calcium-free stabilizers has been an ongoing topic of investigation as a potential solution for mitigating the inherent problems of gypseous soils. Therefore, natural clinoptilolite zeolite was selected due to its widespread availability, low calcium content, high cation exchange capacity, and porous structure, which can limit ettringite formation, reduce sulfate mobility, and improve the performance of gypseous soils under environmental cycling. The novelty of this study lies in addressing a key gap in the stabilization of sulfate-rich gypseous soils, where zeolite has mainly been used as a partial replacement for cement in non-sulfate systems. In contrast, this work evaluates natural clinoptilolite zeolite as a sole, calcium-free stabilizer for highly gypseous soils (10–40% gypsum) under severe environmental conditions, including wetting–drying and freeze–thaw cycles. In this context, the chemical composition and microporous structure of zeolite are expected to play a key role in suppressing ettringite formation and limiting sulfate mobility, thereby targeting the primary failure mechanisms associated with calcium-based stabilizers under environmental cycling. Unlike conventional lime-based treatments that may induce sulfate-related deterioration, this study highlights the potential of zeolite to mitigate such effects and provides a direct comparison with lime and lime–zeolite mixtures. These findings offer new insights into sustainable and calcium-free stabilization strategies for problematic gypseous soils. In all cases, the stabilizing agents were incorporated into the soil at dosages ranging from 3% to 10% by dry weight. The stabilizing efficiency was subsequently evaluated under freeze–thaw and wetting–drying cycles using unconfined compressive strength (UCS) tests, P‑wave velocity measurements, X‑ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy‑dispersive X‑ray analysis (EDAX), and mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP).

Because natural soils often exhibit highly variable or uncontrolled gypsum contents, gypseous soils were synthesized by mixing predetermined amounts of gypsum with locally sourced fine-grained soils from Tehran, Iran. Sample preparation and mix design

To ensure accuracy and minimize the influence of external factors, all samples were systematically prepared under controlled laboratory conditions. Accordingly, mixtures were produced with gypsum contents of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% by dry weight of soil. The soil was thoroughly mixed with predetermined amounts of gypsum under dry condition until a homogeneous blend was achieved. Subsequently, each mixture was subjected to a standard Proctor compaction test in accordance with ASTM D69856 to determine the Maximum Dry Density (MDD) and Optimum Moisture Content (OMC). Moreover, the effect of different gypsum contents on Atterberg limits were investigated following ASTM D4318-17e1 suggested method57.

The complementary substitution technique was adopted to examine the influence of different stabilizers (lime and zeolite) on gypseous soils. The stabilizers were incorporated at dosage of 3%, 5%, 7%, and 10% by dry weight of soil. The selected zeolite and lime contents were based on previous studies demonstrating that this dosage range provides effective mechanical improvement in gypseous soils9,23,54. After thorough dry mixing, water equivalent to the OMC was added, and mixing was continued until a uniform moisture distribution was achieved. The resulting wet mixtures were then sealed in plastic bags. The treated and untreated soils were allowed to mellow for 1 and 24 h, respectively.

When zeolite is used alone, no chemical reaction occurs; therefore, an increase in soil alkalinity is required to activate its pozzolanic activity58,59. In this study, two different approaches were employed to increase the soil pH, and thereby initiate the desired chemical reactions. In the first approach, a sodium hydroxide solution was prepared using NaOH flakes with 95% purity and was used in place of water during sample mixing. In the second approach, lime was incorporated as an alkaline agent to raise the pH of the soil. In this method, a portion of the zeolite was replaced by lime at two substitution levels, corresponding to 20% and 40% of the zeolite content by weight.

The prepared soil specimens were compacted in single-split cylindrical PVC molds with an internal diameter of 50 mm and a height of 100 mm. To maintain the mold integrity during compaction and prevent deformation, each PVC mold was placed within a rigid metal frame, as shown in Fig. 4a. Based on extensive preliminary trials, a compaction load of 5 kN was selected to achieve dry densities comparable to those obtained from the standard Proctor test for the cylindrical molds employed in this study. All specimens were compacted in three equal layers at this pressure to ensure reproducibility and consistency with the determined MDD and OMC. The effect of under-compaction was also considered in the sample preparation and the height of each layer. Each layer was carefully scarred on the top surface to ensure proper interlocking between the layers. After compaction, the specimens were gently removed from both the metal frame and the PVC molds (Fig. 4b), wrapped in two layers of plastic film, and sealed in double plastic bags to minimize moisture loss (Fig. 4c–d). The specimens were then stored at room temperature and cured for periods of 7, 14, 28, 60, and 365 days. The mix designs investigated in this study are summarized in Table 3, including gypsum contents ranging from 10 to 40%, lime and zeolite dosages of 3–10%, and zeolite–lime mixtures.

Experimental procedures

Measurement of UCS

After each curing period, the specimens were carefully unwrapped from their plastic coverings, and their dimensions were measured with precision. The UCS tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM D510260. A constant strain rate of 0.1 mm/min was applied until failure of the specimens. The reported UCS values correspond to the average of three independent tests.

Evaluation of durability

To assess the durability of stabilized soils under wetting–drying conditions, specimens containing the lowest (10%) and highest (40%) gypsum contents were selected for testing. After a 28-day curing period, soil samples treated with lime and zeolite were subjected to repeated wetting–drying cycles in accordance with modified procedure based on ASTM D55961. Each cycle consisted of 48 h of complete immersion in distilled water at room temperature, followed by 48 h of oven drying. The wetting duration was extended to provide a more realistic evaluation of gypseous soil behavior under prolonged exposure to moisture, considering the relatively high solubility of gypsum. A 48-hour immersion period was sufficient to achieve quasi-saturation of the specimens and to allow substantial gypsum dissolution. Similarly, the extended drying period (48 h) was adopted to permit moisture redistribution, evaporation and potential recrystallization processes during drying. During the drying phase of each cycle, the samples were kept at 60 °C to prevent gypsum dehydration. Although higher drying temperatures may induce partial gypsum dehydration (CaSO4⋅2H2O to CaSO4⋅0.5H2O), the temperature was limited to 60 °C to remove free water while minimizing sulfate phase transformation and microstructural alteration, in accordance with established practices in gypsiferous soil stabilization studies23,54. After each cycle, P-wave velocity and UCS were measured. P-wave velocity and UCS were determined in the wet state during odd-numbered cycles and in the dry state during even-numbered cycles. The wetting–drying cycles were continued for up to nine cycles. Beyond this point, a significant number of specimens experienced complete structural failure, while the remaining samples exhibited severe cracking, spalling, and loss of integrity, rendering reliable UCS and P-wave velocity measurements impractical. In this study, failure was defined as the occurrence of extensive cracking, significant disintegration, or collapse of the specimen that prevented mechanical or ultrasonic testing. Accordingly, the durability assessment was terminated after completion of the ninth cycle. The procedure for the wetting–drying durability test is illustrated in Fig. 5.

The stages of wetting-drying test (a) Submerged samples, (b) Samples removed from the pool for surface drying, (c) Samples dried in the oven.

Freeze–thaw durability tests are generally conducted using open and closed systems. In the open system, samples absorb water from the felt pad through capillary action during thawing phase. Conversely, the closed system ensures no moisture exchange with the environment, making it suitable for soils with very low permeability. In this study, the closed freeze–thaw system was adopted and the tests were performed in accordance with ASTM D-560 standard62. After a the 28-day curing period, samples were placed in a freezer at -24 °C for 24 h while remaining sealed within plastic wrap and double plastic bags (Fig. 6). Subsequently, samples were removed from the freezer and allowed to thaw at laboratory room temperature for an additional 24 h, completing one freeze–thaw cycle. After each cycle, P-wave velocity and UCS were determined. The freeze–thaw test was continued for up to 15 cycles. At this stage, several specimens exhibited complete structural deterioration. Upon removal from the plastic wrapping, these samples exhibited behavior similar to that of uncemented soil, indicating severe internal damage. In such cases, UCS and P-wave velocity measurements were no longer feasible due to the complete breakdown of specimen integrity. Although some specimens showed intact appearance after 15 cycles, they exhibited significant degradation in mechanical and ultrasonic properties compared with their initial condition. Given the extensive deterioration observed and the inability to reliably test a portion of the samples, the freeze–thaw durability assessment was terminated after completion of 15 cycles.

Samples in the freezing stage of the freeze-thaw test.

Measurement of P-wave velocity

Prior to conducting the UCS and durability tests, the P-wave velocity of the stabilized soil specimens was determined using a Pundit Lab ultrasonic device equipped with 40 mm diameter transducers operating at 54 kHz. The reported P-wave velocity values correspond to the average of three independent measurements.

Microstructural and chemical tests

The stabilized soils treated with zeolite and lime were characterized in terms of their microstructure and chemical composition using SEM, XRF, XRD, and MIP. These investigations were carried out to evaluate the influence of the stabilizers, the impact of environmental conditions, and the potential formation of ettringite. All analyses were performed on specimens cured for 60 days and on samples obtained after completion of the durability cycles. Mineralogical characterization was conducted using a Philips X’PERT MPD diffractometer equipped with a Cu-Kα tube, operating at 30 mA and 40 kV, with a wavelength of 1.54056 Å. Prior to XRD and XRF testing, samples that had failed in the UCS tests were oven-dried at 60 °C for 48 h, then pulverized and sieved to produce homogeneous powders suitable for analysis.

SEM/EDAX tests were performed to investigate the microstructure changes and to detect ettringite formation. SEM was conducted using a ZEISS-ƩIGMAVP microscope (Germany), while EDAX was performed using an X-Mas Oxford instrument. A cubic soil sample with dimensions of 1 cm was prepared from the soil samples that had been cured for 60 days and from the samples subjected to durability testing. To preserve the soil fabric, the specimens were impregnated with epoxy resin and subsequently coated with a thin gold layer prior to SEM examination.

Pore size distribution was evaluated using a Thermo Finnigan mercury porosimetry Pascal 440. In the MIP analysis, a mercury surface tension of 480 dyn/cm and a contact angle of 141.3° were adopted, with an applied pressure range of 0.1–155 MPa. A cubic soil sample with dimensions of 10 mm was prepared in a dry state for each mix design. The mercury porosimeter is capable of measuring pore diameters in the range of 0.01 to 10 μm.

Results

Effect of gypsum on soil properties

The presence of gypsum in soils notably affects their behavior as construction materials. Therefore, it is crucial to thoroughly assess the properties of the soil at any construction site, considering both the soil type and the specific requirements of the project. This evaluation ensures that the soil is capable of maintaining adequate adequate load-bearing capacity and long-term stability during the service life of the structure63. In this section, the effect of adding 10, 20, 30, and 40% gypsum into CL soil were investigated. The study focuses on changes in Atterberg limits, compaction characteristics, and UCS, as presented in Fig. 7.

Effect of gypsum content on soil properties; (a) atterberg limit, (b) compaction (c) UCS.

As shown in Fig. 7a, liquid limit and plastic limit exhibit a clear decreasing trend with increasing gypsum content. The reduction in the LL is more pronounced than in the PL, resulting in an overall decrease in the PI. These observations are consistent with the findings of other studies64–66. These findings are attributed to the replacement of plastic clay minerals by non-plastic gypsum67, as well as cation exchange processes where calcium ions from gypsum interact with clay particles, increasing flocculation.

Regarding soil compaction characteristics, existing literature presents varied findings68,69. The results from the standard compaction tests (Fig. 7b) indicate an initial increase in the MDD at low gypsum contents, followed by a decreasing trend at higher concentrations. Conversely, the OMC consistently decreases with increasing gypsum content. The initial rise in MDD at low gypsum levels can be explained by gypsum’s role as a filler material and its lower tendency to absorb water70. At higher gypsum concentrations, the subsequent decrease in MDD is likely due to the replacement denser soil particles with low-density gypsum and changes in soil grading resulting from ion exchange and flocculation.

The UCS of the soil is highly sensitive to the gypsum concentration. As illustrated in Fig. 7c, the incorporation of 10% gypsum results in an initial strength improvement, which is attributed to enhanced internal friction and cohesion68,71,72. However, as gypsum content exceeds 10%, the UCS follows a declining trend. At a gypsum content of 30%, the soil’s UCS becomes lower than that of the untreated soil, reaching its lowest value at 40% gypsum with a significant 43% reduction compared to the soil with 10% gypsum. This strength degradation at higher concentrations is associated with a reduction in the internal friction angle and cohesion64,73, weakening of inter-particle bonding due to the replacement of clay minerals, and substitution of soil particles with low-density gypsum.

Lime stabilization

In this study, soils with gypsum content of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% were stabilized using 3%, 5%, 7%, and 10% lime. The samples were subjected to UCS tests after curing periods for 7, 14, 28, 60, and 365 days, and the corresponding results are presented in Figs. 8 and 9. These results demonstrate that soils containing 10% gypsum exhibited the highest strength, and as the gypsum content in the soil increased, the strength of the stabilized soil progressively decreased. According to the results illustrated in Fig. 10, the specimens treated with 5% lime exhibit the highest UCS after 28 days of curing. However, beyond this point, the trend reverses, and further increases in lime dosage lead to a reduction in soil strength.

The strength measurements obtained at 60 and 365 days of curing, as shown in Figs. 8 and 9, indicate that the maximum UCS at both curing periods was observed in the soil containing 10% gypsum stabilized with 7% lime. In contrast, after 28 days of curing (Fig. 10), the highest strength was obtained in the soil stabilized with 5% lime. Nevertheless, at higher gypsum contents, soils stabilized with 5% lime consistently exhibited superior strength compared with mixtures containing larger lime dosages. These results further indicate that increasing the lime content beyond the optimum level does not enhance the final strength of the gypsum-bearing soils.

Zeolite stabilization

When zeolite is mixed with soil alone, no chemical reactions occur. To initiate the pozzolanic activity, the soil’s pH needs to be raised. Therefore, sodium hydroxide was employed in this study to raise the pH and activate the zeolite. To determine the optimal concentration of sodium hydroxide, two soil samples with gypsum contents of 10% and 40% were selected. In this case, instead of using water the samples were stabilized using 1, 2, and 4 mol/L of sodium hydroxide, along with 5% and 10% zeolite. The samples were then subjected to a UCS test after 28 days of curing. The results are shown in Fig. 11.

Determination of the optimum concentration of sodium hydroxide for soils containing 10 and 40% of gypsum stabilized with 5 and 10% zeolite cured for 28 days; (a) 10% gypsum, and (b) 40% gypsum.

The results presented in Fig. 11 show that increasing the sodium hydroxide concentration up to 2 mol/L enhances the strength of gypseous soils. However, further increases in concentration lead to a decline in specimen strength. However, a 4 mol/L solution creates a highly alkaline environment (Table 4). Such elevated alkalinity may raise environmental and economic concerns and increase the risk of corrosion. It may also pose potential safety hazards during handling and field application. As shown in Table 4, the pH values of all NaOH-treated soils are lower than those of the lime-treated samples. In addition, a reduction in pH is observed after 28 days compared with the initial measurements. This decrease can be attributed to pozzolanic reactions and the formation of silicate and aluminate gels during curing. The initial increase in strength can be attributed to enhanced dissolution of aluminosilicate phases under higher alkalinity, which promotes the release of Si and Al species and facilitates the formation of binding gels. This process leads to a denser microstructure and improved interparticle bonding. However, at concentrations above 2 mol/L, excessive alkalinity likely causes over-dissolution, non-uniform gel precipitation, and increased microcracking due to shrinkage and residual alkalis (as illustrated in Fig. 12). These mechanisms ultimately lead to a reduction in UCS. The consistent peak strength observed at 2 mol/L across all compositions confirms that this concentration provides the optimal balance between dissolution and geopolymerization reactions, making it the most effective and technically justified activator concentration for soil improvement.

Wetting-drying test

Gypseous soils tend to lose strength over time under environmental stresses, particularly wetting-drying cycles74 and freeze-thaw cycles24,75. Lime-stabilized gypseous soils also exhibit significant durability reduction during wetting–drying cycles, especially at higher gypsum contents. This strength loss is mainly associated with increased moisture, gypsum dissolution, and crack propagation within the soil matrix7,20. Due to the poor performance of samples stabilized with 3% lime and zeolite, these results are excluded from this section. Therefore, the discussion focuses only on stabilization using 5%, 7%, and 10% lime and zeolite.

As shown in Fig. 18, repeated wetting–drying cycles caused a progressive decline in both UCS and P-wave velocity of the samples, regardless of the stabilizing agent used. The reduction was more pronounced in soils with higher gypsum content. As cycles progressed, noticeable changes in specimen appearance occurred, and by the sixth cycle some samples were completely degraded.

Freeze-thaw test

Dempsey and Thompson76 suggested that UCS of specimens’ can be used as an indicator of soil durability under freeze–thaw cycles. Simonsen, et al.77 also reported the adverse effects of freeze-thaw cycles on soil strength properties. Khoury and Brooks78 attributed the effect of freeze-thaw cycles on UCS and elastic modulus to the rate of chemical reactions. Aldaood, et al.24 reported a significant reduction in the UCS of lime-stabilized gypseous soil subjected to freeze-thaw cycles. They identified gypsum dissolution, water penetration, crack formation due to freezing and thawing, and ettringite formation as the main factors contributing to soil degradation. In addition, these researchers attributed soil deterioration during freeze–thaw cycles primarily to alterations in soil structure and mineralogy. Their comparison of open and closed-system freeze-thaw tests further demonstrated that the availability of moisture strongly influences specimen durability.

The effect of freeze–thaw cycles on the UCS and P‑wave velocity of gypseous soils stabilized with lime and zeolite is presented in Fig. 19. The results show a sharp reduction in both UCS and P‑wave velocity during the first cycle, followed by a more gradual decline in subsequent cycles. Throughout the test, there was a minor change in the external appearance of the specimens compared with the wetting-drying cycles (Fig. 20). Small surface cracks developed during the tests and gradually expanded as the number of cycles increased. The relatively unchanged external appearance of the specimens, despite the substantial reduction in UCS and P‑wave velocity, suggests severe internal structural damage and a consequent loss of structural integrity. Notably, the reduction in P-wave velocity and UCS is more pronounced in samples stabilized with zeolite than in lime‑stabilized ones. Overall, the lime-stabilized specimens demonstrated better performance compared to zeolite-stabilized specimens.

Discussion

Effetiveness of stabilization methods

Lime is widely used in various soil stabilization and ground improvement projects due to its ability to improve the mechanical behavior of gypseous soils. Previous studies have shown that both lime content and curing time play critical roles in determining soil strength. In the early stages, strength gain is mainly attributed to pozzolanic reactions and the formation of ettringite. However, prolonged curing may lead to a reduction in strength9. Soils with low gypsum content typically exhibit negligible formation of expansive minerals such as ettringite and thaumasite31. In contrast, lime stabilization is often less effective in soils with high gypsum content and may result in unsatisfactory performance22. In this study, the effect of lime on the UCS of gypseous soils was investigated. Figure 21 illustrates the variation in UCS at different lime contents after a curing period of 28 days.

Variations of UCS at different gypsum and lime contents; (a) UCS variation and (b) corresponding percentage strength variation of gypseous soils containing 10–40% gypsum stabilized with different lime contents after 28 days of curing. Standard deviations for all data are less than 10%.

The results presented in Fig. 21a show that, for soils with a constant gypsum content, the strength initially increases with the addition of lime up to 5%, after which it begins to decrease. This trend is also clearly visible in Fig. 10. Furthermore, when the lime content is kept constant, the UCS decreases as the gypsum content increases, indicating that the effectiveness of lime stabilization diminishes with increasing gypsum content. For instance, the use of 3% lime increased the UCS of soil containing 10% gypsum to 1830 kPa, whereas the UCS decreased to 1140 kPa when the gypsum content increased to 40%. However, the analysis of percentage changes in UCS reveals a different trend. As illustrated in Fig. 21b, at a lime content of 3%, the rate of strength increase shows a positive trend with increasing gypsum content. Although the final strength remains lower due to the lower initial strength of the soil, the relative improvement is greater. In contrast, at lime contents higher than 5%, the rate of strength improvement decreases as gypsum content rises. This indicates that higher lime contents become less effective at elevated gypsum levels. Therefore, lime performs more effectively as a stabilizing agent in soils with lower gypsum content, whereas the use of higher lime percentages in soils with high gypsum content should be approached with caution. For example, 3% lime resulted in strength increases of 167.5% and 188.6% in soils containing 10% and 40% gypsum, respectively. In contrast, the use of 10% lime produced a strength increase of 181.9% in soil with 10% gypsum but only 129.4% in soil containing 40% gypsum.

According to the data presented in Table 4, the addition of lime as a stabilizing agent to the soil increases the pH to values exceeding 13. This highly alkaline environment promotes the dissolution of calcium into the pore water. Simultaneously, pozzolanic reactions occur, facilitating the reaction between the dissolved calcium and the silica (SiO4) and alumina (AlO6) naturally present in the soil matrix. This pozzolanic reaction will persist as long as both high pH and available calcium are maintained. These reactions lead to the formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium aluminate hydrate (C-A-H) phases due to the availability of Ca2+, OH−, SiO4, and AlO6 ions23. The development of these binding phases significantly enhances the strength and durability of the lime-stabilized soil. However, when the lime and gypsum content in the soil exceeds an optimal level, a reduction in strength is expected due to the formation of amorphous reaction products and the disruption of the crystalline structure of the soil matrix9. This behavior is clearly reflected in the results shown in Fig. 21.

To assess the effect of zeolite on the strength of gypsum-bearing soils, the UCS results for samples stabilized with various zeolite contents after 28 days of curing were calculated and are presented in Fig. 22.

Variations of UCS at different gypsum and zeolite contents; (a) UCS variation and (b) corresponding percentage strength variation of gypseous soils containing 10–40% gypsum stabilized with different zeolite contents activated with 2 mol/L NaOH, after 28 days of curing. Standard deviations for all data are less than 10%.

The results presented in Fig. 22 exhibited trends different from those observed in the lime-stabilized soil. Based on the data in Fig. 22a, increasing the zeolite content leads to an increase in soil strength across all gypsum contents. For example in soil containing 10% an 40% gypsum the UCS values were 1708 kPa and 1530 kPa, respectively, when stabilized with 3% zeolite. However, after stabilization with 10% zeolite, the UCS increased to 2300 kPa and 1999 kPa, for the respective gypsum contents. Additionally, at a constant zeolite content, an increase in the gypsum content up to 20% resulted in higher soil strength. However, beyond 20% gypsum content, the soil strength begins to decrease, reaching its minimum values at 40% gypsum. For instance, with 3% zeolite, the UCS reached its maximum value of 1740 kPa at 20% gypsum content and subsequently decreased to 1530 kPa at 40% gypsum content. Based on the results of Fig. 22b, the reduction in strength for higher gypsum content can be attributed to the lower initial strength of untreated soils. The percentage variation in UCS indicates two key trends. First, increasing the zeolite content while keeping the gypsum content constant results in higher UCS values. Second, the effectiveness of zeolite becomes more pronounced as the gypsum content increases. For example, in soils containing 10% gypsum stabilized with 3% and 10% zeolite, the UCS increased by 149% and 236%, respectively. In contrast, in soils containing 40% gypsum, the strength increased by approximately 287% and 406% when stabilized with 3% and 10% zeolite, respectively.

Based on the results presented in Table 4, the use of 2 mol/L NaOH increases the pH of the soil-zeolite mixture to values above 12. This highly alkaline environment facilitates the dissolution of aluminosilicate frameworks, leading to the formation of C-S-H and C-A-H gels, which improve the mechanical performance of the stabilized soil. Alkaline activation of zeolite involves the dissolution of its alumino‑silicate structure, allowing the formation of a three‑dimensional alumino‑silicate gel network known as Sodium Alumino‑Silicate Hydrate (N-A-S-H) under high pH conditions54. These chemical reactions result in the formation of cementitious N-A-S-H and C-A-H gels that bind soil particles together and significantly enhance strength parameters over time. Increasing the zeolite content provides a greater supply of silica and alumina for cementitious compound formation, which leads to higher UCS values, as shown in Fig. 22.

The results presented in Fig. 23a show that, for soils with a constant gypsum content and a fixed stabilizer content, increasing the lime proportion within the stabilizer mixture from 20% to 40% leads to a corresponding increase in soil strength. This trend is clearly observed in soils containing both 10% and 40% gypsum. For example, in soil containing 10% gypsum stabilized with 5% total stabilizer, increasing the lime proportion from 20% to 40% raises the UCS from 2090 kPa to 2393 kPa.

Variations of UCS at different gypsum content and zeolite+lime mixtures; (a) UCS variation and (b) corresponding percentage strength variation of gypseous soils containing 10% and 40% gypsum stabilized with mixtures of lime and zeolite (80Z+20 L and 60Z+40 L), after 28 days of curing.

The rate of change in UCS shown in Fig. 23b indicates similar trend like zeolite stabilization methods. The results show that as the amount of the stabilizing agent and the lime proportion within it increases, the UCS growth rate becomes more pronounced in soils with higher gypsum content. In other words, combining zeolite and lime effectively stabilizes soils with high gypsum content and remove the negative effect of the lime stailization method. When the lime proportion in the stabilizing mix is higher, the environment becomes more alkaline, enabling a greater amount of zeolite to participate in the reaction. This promotes the formation of additional cementitious compounds, thereby enhancing the soil strength. However, insufficient lime in the stabilizer limits the zeolite’s reactivity, leaving a fraction of zeolite inactive and causing a lower enhancement of soil strength.

As previously mentioned in the introduction, zeolite is a naturally occurring material whose pozzolanic reactivity is comparable to that of fly ash and siliceous fumes. When combined with lime, the free calcium ions released from the lime react with the silica and alumina present in both the zeolite and the soil matrix, thereby promoting the formation of C-S-H and C-A-H phases. Given that zeolite contains significant amounts of reactive silica and alumina, it significantly enhances the production of these cementitious compounds during the stabilization process. Consequently, the availability of Ca2+ becomes the primary limiting factor in the reaction kinetics. As the lime content increases, greater amounts of free calcium become available, which further accelerates the pozzolanic reactions and leads to a corresponding increase in strength. This trend is clearly evident in Fig. 23.

Figure 24 compares the maximum UCS values achieved after 28 days in soils containing 10% and 40% gypsum, stabilized using 5% and 10% of lime, zeolite, and their mixture. The results show that the sample stabilized with 10% lime–zeolite mixture yielded the highest strength for soils with 10% (3190 kPa) and 40% (3010 kPa) gypsum content. This mixture proves particularly effective for soils with 40% gypsum content, achieving a 660% increase in strength compare to th untreated soil. In this scenario, its performance surpasses that of lime alone (129.4% increase) and zeolite (406% increase) due to lime’s role as an intrinsic activator.

Comparison of UCS variations across all soil treatment methods after 28 days of curing.

The presented results clearly indicate that the combined performance of zeolite and lime stabilization is superior to that of zeolite or lime used individually. Furthermore, since this combination eleminates the necessity for activators, such as sodium hydroxide, it significantly mitigates environmental and safety concerns. However, it must be noted that the primary challenge in gypeous soils is the formation of ettringite. Therefore, a comprehensive chemical investigation of these stabilization methods is needed prior to drawing conclusions. Accordingly, the subsequent section will focus on the chemical analysis of these techniques.

Chemical analysis

Ettringite is a calcium aluminate sulfate hydrate (C-A-S-H) mineral whose formation represents a critical concern in the stabilization of gypseous soils, thus requiring careful evaluation. The characteristic diffraction peaks for ettringite were identified via XRD analysis at approximate 2θ values of 9.1, 15.8, 22.9, and 31 (as shown by red arrows in Fig. 25). Given that ettringite is a secondary or minor reaction product in the stabilized soil systems, its corresponding peak intensities are naturally lower compared to the primary phases like gypsum or stabilized soil matrix components. Nevertheless, the presence of these distinct peaks confirms the formation of ettringite relative to the untreated soil. XRD results demonstrated that soils containing 10% and 40% gypsum developed ettringite when stabilized with 5% and 10% lime. The formation of ettringite was also confirmed through the EDAX analysis (Fig. 26), evidenced by the presence of needle-like minerals and key constituent elements, specifically Ca, Al, S. Furthermore, the amount of ettringite increases as both gypsum and lime contents rise, as observed in SEM/EDAX analysis. This formation leads to notable volumetric expansion, with the degree of swelling directly correlating with the levels of gypsum and lime in the soil. The needle-like morphology of ettringite exerts substantial stress on the soil matrix, resulting in pore enlargement and potential microcracking. These structural changes weaken the integrity of the soil and ultimately reduce its strength. The increase in sample volume during both the 28-day curing and one-year curing period is illustrated in Fig. 27. Consequently, the considerable reduction in soil strength observed with increasing gypsum content and lime addition can be well justified by this mechanism.

XRD analyses of the zeolite-stabilized samples (Fig. 28) revealed no evidence of ettringite formation, except for the peak at 2θ ≈ 31, which overlaped with the gypsum reflection. Furthermore, SEM observations showed no evidence of needle-like crystalline morphology in the stabilized soils. To obtain a more accurate measure of crystalline ettringite formation, a subtraction-based semi-quantitative analysis was employed for both zeolite and lime stabilization. First, the integrated intensities of the identified ettringite peaks were summed after normalization. The total intensity was then corrected by subtracting the integrated intensities of the corresponding background peaks associated with gypsum and the soil matrix. The resulting net crystalline intensity values are presented in Table 5. To ensure analytical accuracy, the peak at 2θ ≈ 31 was excluded from the calculations because of its significant overlap with the dominant gypsum reflection. This approach ensured that the calculated net intensity was derived from the least affected characteristic ettringite reflections. The results in Table 5 demonstrate the negative net intensity values obtained for the untreated soil and gypsum samples confirm the absence of ettringite in these materials. A direct comparison of the calculated net crystalline intensity in lime-stabilized soils revealed that all lime-treated samples exhibited significantly higher ettringite formation than those stabilized with zeolite. These findings demonstrate that lime promotes ettringite formation in the presence of gypsum, whereas zeolite stabilization effectively suppresses or inhibits the formation of this expansive phase.

Wetting-drying test

Repeated wetting–drying cycles altered the shape and volume of the samples due to moisture absorbion, progressively damaging the soil structure and leading to a consistent reduction in UCS and P‑wave velocity, as illustrated in Fig. 18. The reduction in UCS and P‑wave velocity in the zeolite‑stabilized samples is less severe than that observed in the lime‑stabilized samples from the early stages of the wetting–drying cycles. The observed variations in P‑wave velocity correspond closely with the changes in UCS as the number of cycles increases, which is further supported by the statistical analysis presented in Table 6. For all samples, the Pearson correlation coefficients (r) ranged from − 0.946 to -0.993, indicating a strong negative correlation between the number of wetting–drying cycles and both UCS and P‑wave velocity. This relationship reflects the progressive degradation and loss of mechanical properties caused by repeated wetting–drying exposure. The coefficients of determination (R² = 0.895–0.986) indicate that approximately 90–99% of the variation in UCS and P-wave velocity can be explained by the number of wet–dry cycles, confirming a highly consistent linear degradation trend. Both lime and zeolite-treated soils demonstrate similar degradation trends, although G40-L5 and G10-L10 mixtures exhibit slightly higher R² values, suggesting greater structural susceptibility under wetting-drying cycles.

Damage to the samples under wetting–drying cycles primarily results from processes such as gypsum dissolution, crack propagation, and ettringite formation. Similar observations have been reported by Aldaood, et al.23 and Cuisinier, et al.79. As discussed in Sect. 4.2, increasing the gypsum and lime contents in lime‑treated samples leads to a higher concentration of ettringite within the soil matrix. Due to its needle‑like morphology and expansive nature, ettringite increases soil porosity and reduces strength. As these cycles progress, both porosity and water penetration rise, causing the ettringite to become volumetrically unstable36. This mineral may partially dissolve and be leached from the soil, further enlarging pore spaces. Consequently, as illustrated in Fig. 30, the soil exhibits a progressive reduction in strength.

During the drying stage, soil specimens develop cracks because of their low tensile strength and the shrinkage caused by moisture loss. As drying progresses, matric suction80 and horizontal net stress within the samples gradually rise, producing tensile stresses parallel to the soil surface which generate new cracks. These cracks typically initiate on the specimen surface of the specimen, where external layers are more affected than the core, resulting in surface degradation, and flaking of the outer sections, as depicted in Fig. 31d. With additional cycles, partially closed or unsealed cracks act as preferential pathways for water infiltration. Therefore, as the wetting–drying cycles continue, damage accumulates, leading to a marked decline in strength and overall specimen deterioration.

The presented results in Table 7 indicate that zeolite-treated samples exhibit higher bulk density, lower porosity, and smaller pore size than lime-treated samples at the end of the cycles, demonstrating better performance in wetting-drying cyclic conditions. The improved performance of zeolite can be attributed to two primary factors. First, stabilization with zeolite does not promote ettringite formation, as discussed in Sect. 4.2. Second, the molecular structure of zeolite contributes significantly to its stabilizing effect. Zeolites belong to the tectosilicate group and possess an open three‑dimensional framework composed of silica and alumina tetrahedra. This structure contains exchangeable cations that balance the negative charge of the framework. These interconnected voids and pores serve as hosts for cations and water molecules. The internal surface area within these channels has been recorded to span several hundred square meters per gram of zeolite, showcasing zeolites’ exceptional efficiency in facilitating processes of ion exchange. Hence, zeolite can adsorb and retain sulfate ions within the soil81 and limiting their mobility and reducing sulfate dissolution. This mechanism helps control the increase in soil porosity during wetting–drying cycles. Therefore, compared with lime stabilization, zeolite stabilization provides superior performance, particularly for gypseous soils exposed to repeated wetting–drying conditions.

Freeze-thaw test

The effect of freeze–thaw cycles on UCS varied with gypsum content. At higher gypsum contents, the plastic behavior of the soil decreased due to the increased presence of free gypsum, as discussed in Sect. 3.1. This condition weakened the soil’s elastic response and its ability to bond and retain soil particles. Consequently, soils with higher gypsum contents exhibited more rapid deterioration under freeze–thaw cycles owing to their less coherent structure.

Despite their seemingly intact external appearance (Fig. 20), the substantial reduction in strength indicates that repeated ice lens formation during freeze–thaw cycles has compromised the structural integrity and the cohesive and adhesive bonds established by the stabilization agents. Essentially, the expansive forces generated by ice lens formation have overwhelmed the pozzolanic reactions. This is primarily because the rate of these chemical reactions significantly diminishes at temperatures as low as -24 degrees Celsius. Consequently, upon removal from their protective plastic casings, the specimens disintegrate and exhibit behavior similar to uncemented soil. In this state, the primary source of strength is suction, which aids in preserving the sample’s integrity.

The more pronounced strength reduction observed in zeolite‑treated specimens is primarily attributed to their higher moisture content. As shown in Table 8, the average moisture content of zeolite‑treated specimens is higher than that of lime‑treated specimens. In contrast, the lower moisture content in lime‑treated soils can be explained by water consumption during ettringite formation. Because moisture availability is the dominant controlling factor in freeze–thaw processes, zeolite‑treated specimens experienced more severe freeze–thaw damage. As illustrated in Fig. 32, a strong positive linear correlation exists between the initial moisture content and specimen degradation, quantified by reductions in both UCS and P‑wave velocity. Linear regression analysis (Fig. 32a) indicates a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.70 between initial moisture content and the percentage reduction in UCS, while an even stronger correlation (R² = 0.93) is observed for the percentage reduction in P‑wave velocity. These high R² values provide compelling evidence that higher initial moisture content significantly accelerates material degradation during freeze–thaw cycling. Furthermore, MIP results (Table 7) indicate that zeolite‑treated specimens exhibit higher porosity at the end of the freeze–thaw cycles compared with lime‑treated specimens, corroborating the observed deterioration trends.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the effectiveness of natural zeolite as a stabilizing agent for gypseous soils through a comprehensive experimental program that included mechanical testing, chemical characterization, SEM/EDAX observations, and durability assessments. The performance of zeolite stabilization was systematically compared with that of lime stabilization as well as with combined zeolite–lime stabilization. Based on the experimental findings, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The incorporation of zeolite as a soil stabilizer leads to a significant increase in soil strength, with the improvement becoming more pronounced as the zeolite content increases. In contrast, lime stabilization enhances soil strength only up to an optimal content of approximately 5%, beyond which further lime addition adversely affects the mechanical performance.

The results indicate that zeolite can significantly enhance the engineering properties of gypseous soils, offering distinct benefits compared to traditional lime stabilization, which is less sustainable and environmentally friendly.
