Insights into the activity, production, and signaling circuits of homoserine lactones in mammalian cells
Authors: Sepand Tehrani Fateh, Sahand Tehrani Fateh, Seyed Ali Ziai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEmerging evidence suggests that acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs), bacterial signaling metabolites, also play signaling roles in eukaryotic organisms, including human cells. Through a meta-analysis of prior investigations, we mapped the effects of AHLs and their signaling roles. Given their evolutionary significance and potential physiological roles, we further asked whether human cells possess the machinery to perceive and produce AHLs or similar compounds. AHLs commonly regulate pathways involved in cell-fate decisions, with RXRs and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) identified as potential upstream AHL receptors. Structural specificity was supported by the observation that both the γ-butyrolactone ring and the acyl tail contribute to receptor affinity. Furthermore, human glycine/glutamine acyltransferases showed structural and functional homology to bacterial AHL synthases using in silico analyses. Moreover, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry of conditioned medium from two human cell lines suggested the production of potential AHLs. Together, this study proposes the possibility of an AHL-driven signaling circuit in human cells.

Introduction

Cellular communication and collective behaviors, resembling those observed in multicellular organisms, have been well documented in bacteria as quorum sensing.1,2 This process allows population-level gene regulation in response to autoinducers, secondary metabolites that serve as signaling molecules. Among these, acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs or acylated HSLs), which consist of a 5-membered cyclic ester (γ-butyrolactone) linked to an acyl chain through an amide bond, are particularly well studied and versatile, facilitating both inter- and intra-species communication. AHL-induced gene regulation mediates cooperative behaviors, including bioluminescence3,4 and biofilm formation.5 Additionally, by regulating enzymes involved in core metabolic pathways, such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the metabolism of glucose, nucleotides, and amino acids, AHLs help optimize bacterial resource utilization.6

The influence of AHLs, however, extends beyond the bacterial domain and affects cellular functions in eukaryotic cells.7 In fact, these molecules may have interacted with eukaryotic, particularly metazoan, cells over a long period, whether through the natural symbioses with microbiomes8,9,10,11 or in the context of pathogenic bacterial colonization.12 Despite this long-standing exposure, our understanding of AHLs’ effects on human and animal cells has only recently advanced through extensive investigations.

AHLs, however, demonstrate variable and inconsistent effects on cellular behavior, particularly regarding cell fate decisions. They can decrease cell viability, induce cell-cycle arrest, inhibit cellular differentiation, or drive cells toward the opposite fate. Their influence on bioenergetics is equally unpredictable. Additionally, in some cells, AHLs trigger inflammatory responses and stress pathways. The variable response is also reflected at the molecular level, as the status of different components, such as their expression levels, phosphorylation, and subcellular localization, differs across experimental conditions. Multiple targets and receptors, ranging from cholesterol-rich microdomains in cellular membranes13 to intracellular and surface receptors like IP3,14,15 bitter receptors (TAS2R3816,17,18,19 and TAS2R13820), and members of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), RXR, and (retinoid acid receptor) RAR families,20,21,22,23,24 have been suggested to mediate these diverse effects.

Despite this ancient exposure, which potentially suggests the co-evolution of perception and signaling mechanisms, and despite numerous yet scattered investigations, a comprehensive understanding of their receptors, effects, and modes of action remains elusive. Furthermore, given the critical roles of AHLs in bacteria and their effects on human and animal cells, a significant question arises: do human and animal cells produce AHLs, and is there a signaling circuit involving AHLs within these cells?

This study seeks to answer these questions by providing a meta-analysis of 31 unique AHL molecules and their effects on 99 distinct cell types. We investigate the underlying mechanisms of action, identify upstream targets, and explore potential receptors, drawing on both experimental and computational findings. In addition, we present preliminary mass spectrometric evidence suggesting that human cells may produce AHLs, with evolutionarily conserved acyl-transferases being identified as potential AHL synthases in the human proteome. These findings support the hypothesis that human cells are capable of producing AHLs and that a signaling pathway may exist to mediate these effects. Our work helps bridge gaps in AHL biology and sets the stage for future research into their therapeutic potential and broader implications for human health.

Results

AHLs of certain structure impose both general and cell-specific effects on cellular physiology through common molecular pathways

Extensive investigations on AHLs have tested 31 unique molecules across 99 cell types of diverse origins (normal, cancerous, and stem cells), encompassing 338 AHL-cell pairs reported in 103 studies (Figure 1), which were identified through a systematic search and review (Figure S1). The data extracted from these 103 studies (Data S1), standardized with respect to AHL and cell nomenclature, experimental details, and corresponding results, provide a detailed overview of AHLs’ effects and constitute a comprehensive database for guiding future research. Among the tested AHLs, N-(3-oxododecanoyl)-homoserine lactone (3-oxo-C12-HSL) is the most extensively studied, followed by C4-HSL. Notably, 3-oxo-C12-HSL, 3-oxo-C10-HSL, and 3-oxo-C14-HSL showed the most consistently strong biological effects across studies, suggesting structure-specific activity for these molecules. The cellular responses to AHLs vary based on concentration and exposure duration. Higher concentrations and longer exposure durations often trigger programmed cell death, stress responses, and immune activation. In contrast, lower concentrations and shorter treatments influence cell-fate decisions, metabolism, morphology, migration, and tissue homeostasis. The response to AHLs would also depend on the cell type, treatment duration, and other experimental conditions (e.g., culture dimension and oxygen saturation), which further complicates the landscape of AHL effects.

Molecular network reconstruction based on proteins and genes altered by AHL exposure reveals consistent involvement of core signaling pathways, including AKT, JAK-STAT, NF-κB, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), as evidenced by changes in gene/protein expression, phosphorylation status, subcellular localization, and activity (Figure 2). Additionally, members of the chaperone network, HSP90AA1, HSPA5, DNAJB1, and DNAJB9, may shape AHL-responsive signaling states. However, given their established roles in stabilizing signaling clients and in modulating ER-stress and proteostasis pathways, together with their mixed directions of regulation across datasets, these proteins are better interpreted as context-dependent signal transducers, because their effects appear to vary substantially with cell type, dose, and exposure time. Further downstream, comparative analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across four datasets show distinct expression profiles, with only 33 genes shared among all four datasets and 320 genes among 3 (Figure S2). The direction of gene expression changes is inconsistent, even among shared genes. Moreover, enrichment profiles exhibit limited overlap, and these inconsistencies expand when categorizing genes into upregulated and downregulated groups.

Nevertheless, incorporating functional enrichment analysis revealed a greater level of convergence across datasets. In this regard, DEGs form a highly interconnected network, bridging distinct gene sets into a unified and cohesive landscape. The cellular responses induced by 3O-C12-HSL, as revealed by the enrichment analysis of DEGs, varied across the four datasets, with each exhibiting a unique set of Gene Ontology (GO) terms (Figures 3A and 3B). Despite variations, these genes also converge on some shared pathways and cellular functions. More specifically, enrichment analysis reveals that DEGs are commonly involved in essential physiological processes such as the cell cycle, programmed cell death, regulatory mechanisms, developmental programs, cellular differentiation, and metabolism (Figures 3C–3E). Additionally, semantic analyses indicate relationships between seemingly unrelated, uniquely enriched GO terms, highlighting their functional proximity (Figure 3D). PPAR and RAR/RXR families possess superior affinity toward AHLs with distinct structural preferences

AHLs’ effects could be explained by receptor-independent or dependent mechanisms. Candidate receptors were initially screened based on signaling pathway involvement, transcription factor enrichment in gene sets (Figures 4A and 4B), reconstructed networks (Figures 4C and 2), and previous literature. Although the PPAR and RAR families, also suggested by prior studies, did not rank highest in terms of enrichment and network properties, their transcriptional regulatory roles suggest they may mediate AHLs’ effects by influencing upstream transcription factors (Figures 4B and 4C). Blind docking, a global search for the most stable accessible binding sites, revealed that RARA, RXRα, RXRβ, PPARγ, PPARα, and PPARδ exhibited binding affinities for 3O-C12-HSL comparable to that of LasR, its cognate receptor, and were higher than those observed for the control proteins (Figure 5A). Notably, while the binding affinity of 3O-C12-HSL for these proteins was lower than that of native receptor-ligand interactions, it surpassed the affinity of the native 8-oxo-ETE ligand for the PPAR family. Further molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on RXRα, RXRβ, PPARγ, and PPARα, the receptors showing the highest affinity for 3O-C12-HSL, were used as a short-timescale stability screen to assess whether the docked ligand remained accommodated and whether the complexes exhibited rapid dissociation or gross structural instability during the 10 ns production runs. Within this interval, 3O-C12-HSL remained associated with all four receptors, and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) and B-factor analyses did not indicate drastic conformational changes in either receptor family (Figures 5B and 5C). Nevertheless, their early dynamical behavior differed, with RXR complexes showing greater apparent stabilization and PPAR complexes displaying more pronounced fluctuation and ligand-state heterogeneity. This pattern is consistent with the broader nuclear-receptor literature, in which PPARγ can adopt multiple ligand-dependent conformational states,25 whereas agonist-bound RXRα can adopt a more stabilized ligand-binding pocket.26 Such conformational differences may still be mechanistically meaningful, as subtle variations could influence downstream programs and partially contribute to the context-dependent effects of ligands observed in other receptor systems.27 However, the present simulations are not sufficient to infer receptor functionality or allosteric effects within RXR/PPAR heterodimers, which would require longer simulations together with structural and functional studies.

To further investigate the specificity of these receptors for AHLs, we conducted comprehensive molecular docking analyses on multiple AHLs with varying acyl chain lengths, both with and without 3-OH or 3-oxo functional groups, as well as their fatty acid (FA) counterparts (i.e., the acyl tail without the HSL moiety).

Our analyses demonstrated that AHLs exhibited superior affinity for the receptors compared with either the γ-butyrolactone moiety or their FA counterparts, a trend consistent across all receptors (Figures 5D–5F). Pairwise statistical analyses of each AHL and its corresponding FA counterpart further supported this finding, except for a few cases within the RXR protein family (Supplemental information). This indicates that both the γ-butyrolactone moiety and the acyl tail contribute to AHL-receptor affinity and subsequent bioactivity.

Additionally, an increase in acyl tail length was associated with higher receptor affinity, with AHLs containing 13 or more carbon atoms displaying the highest affinity in our study (Figures 5D and 5G). AHLs with hydroxyl or carbonyl functional groups exhibited slightly greater affinity than those without functional groups, with a statistically significant difference observed only for PPARα (Figure 5H). The hydroxyl and carbonyl groups are chemically distinct and can, in principle, engage in different polar interactions within the ligand-binding domain. However, in our system, these differences may be subtle relative to the stronger contributions of the lactone ring and acyl tail length, and the docking approach may not have had sufficient sensitivity to resolve them consistently across receptors.

Acyltransferases homologous to bacterial AHL synthases in human cells could potentially synthesize endogenous AHLs

AHLs are primarily produced by prokaryotic organisms through members of the AHL synthase family, such as LuxI, EsaI, and LasI,28,29,30 with conserved amino acid sequence and protein structure. We wondered if some proteins from the eukaryotic domain, specifically from the human proteome, exist which exhibit homology to the AHL synthases. Basic sequence alignment tools proved ineffective due to the evolutionary distance between bacterial species and Homo sapiens. These glycine acyltransferases catalyze the transfer of not only acyl chains of varying lengths, degrees of saturation, and functional groups, carried by coenzyme A (CoA) but also benzoyl-CoA, phenylacetyl-CoA, or indolacetyl-CoA to the NH3+ group of glycine and also to L-glutamine and L-asparagine.31,32,33 This enzymatic process closely resembles the reactions catalyzed by bacterial AHL synthases (Figure 6B). Hypothetically, the acylation of S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) by versatile glycine acyltransferases with a broad range of substrates, followed by lactonization, could result in the synthesis of AHL molecules. The affinity of SAM for these enzymes (data not shown), which is comparable to its affinity for bacterial AHL synthases, suggests the possibility of the acylation of SAM as their secondary reaction.

Mass-spectrometric evidence on the production of potential AHLs by human cells

To further investigate the production of AHLs by human cells, our analysis of conditioned medium extracts from the HL-60 and KG1a cell lines using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) provided evidence that these cells produce small molecules that release daughter ions at m/z 102, a characteristic feature of AHLs. In HL-60 cells, we identified 2 major peaks (m/z 246 and 378), while KG1a cells exhibited 3 major peaks (m/z 246, 350, and 378), all of which were unique compared to the uncultured medium used as the control blank sample (Figures 6C and S3). A peak at m/z 256 was observed in the control blank sample, likely originating from fetal bovine serum present in the uncultured complete medium. However, at this stage, we are not confident in predicting the molecular structure of these ions and we interpret these signals cautiously as potential AHL-like molecules, as our current screening method does not provide sufficient information. Nevertheless, further purification and analysis will be required for confirmation and structural elucidation.

Discussion

AHLs have traditionally been studied as bacterial quorum-sensing molecules, but increasing evidence indicates that their biological relevance extends beyond bacteria. In this study, we integrated systematic literature review, transcriptomic re-analysis, molecular network reconstruction, molecular docking and dynamics simulations, homology analysis, and preliminary LC-MS/MS screening to investigate whether AHLs may participate in signaling processes in mammalian cells. This study provides an overview of the signaling pathways through, which AHLs may mediate their effects, identifies candidate receptors, and provides preliminary evidence for their potential production in human cells.

Cellular responses to AHLs are variable and sometimes inconsistent, depending on AHL structure, concentration, duration of exposure, cell type, and experimental context. Despite this apparent contradiction, AHLs may act through a shared set of signaling pathways, including AKT, JAK-STAT, NF-κB, and MAPK, whose downstream outcomes depend on the internal physiological state of the cells.

Part of AHLs’ effects can be explained by receptor-independent mechanisms, which involve general responses to stress and stimuli, typically observed at high concentrations or after prolonged exposure. Conversely, the regulation of fundamental physiological processes, such as the cell cycle, cell-fate decisions, and metabolism, which recur across cell types under varying conditions but are reflected in distinct gene expression and enrichment profiles, might suggest the presence of upstream receptors that recognize AHLs and mediate their effects. Although a few targets have previously been proposed as receptors mediating AHLs’ effects, a comprehensive analysis of reconstructed molecular networks and transcriptomic gene set enrichment offers an opportunity to identify regulators that could consistently explain the molecular and physiological changes observed across diverse experimental conditions.

Multiple candidate receptors were selected through network and upstream-regulator analyses and were further evaluated computationally. Among these candidates, AHLs demonstrated superior predicted affinity toward members of the RXR and PPAR families. The cytotoxic effects of AHLs are most likely a consequence of high treatment concentrations, which depend on the cellular conditions. In contrast, physiological effects could be mediated through the RXR and PPAR families at much lower concentrations, as suggested by previous studies.34 A PPARγ ligand screening assay demonstrated that C12-HSL, at a low concentration (4.60 μmol/L), binds to the PPARγ ligand-binding domain and interferes with the binding of the native PPARγ ligand in the PolarScreen PPARγ competitor assay.35 Furthermore, 3O-C12-HSL can transactivate Peroxisome Proliferator Response Element (PPRE) reporter expression through PPARγ-dependent signaling at concentrations between 100 nM and 10 μM. However, its net effect via PPARγ may depend on various factors, including the presence of other ligands and the activation state of interacting pathways.36 Additionally, the predicted affinity of AHLs toward these receptors increased with acyl-tail length, which aligns with previous experimental evidence suggesting that AHLs with longer or functionalized acyl tails tend to exhibit enhanced bioactivity.

The identification of the RXR and PPAR families as potential receptors of AHLs suggests that these molecules may play a role in regulating cell-fate decisions, potentially intertwined with lipid metabolism during development or malignant transformation.37,38 Although few studies currently support this effect,39,40 these molecules could be further tested for their role in developmental programs as well as their potential application in differentiation therapy for cancers. Moreover, the apparent structural specificity of AHL bioactivity raises the possibility that structurally related endogenous molecules may exist in human cells.

The range of organisms capable of producing AHLs, as well as the ecological roles of these molecules, is expanding. While AHLs were initially attributed primarily to Gram-negative bacteria, recent reports suggest that some Gram-positive bacterial species also produce these molecules.41,42,43 Given the ability of AHLs to regulate mammalian cellular physiology, together with the identification of potential mammalian receptors, we next asked whether AHLs or AHL-like molecules could also be produced endogenously by metazoan cells, particularly human cells.

Lactone moieties with diverse ring sizes are prevalent in biomolecules, where they contribute to functional properties and are synthesized through various mechanisms.44 AHLs and similar molecules containing γ-butyrolactone (5-membered) or delta-valerolactone (6-membered) rings can be generated either chemically under acidic conditions45 or enzymatically, for example, via the lactonization of hydroxylated FAs catalyzed by the paraoxonase enzyme family.46,47 In bacterial species, AHLs are produced through a 2-step reaction involving acylation and lactonization, catalyzed by members of the AHL synthase family, such as LuxI, EsaI, and LasI.28,29,30 During this process, acylation of SAM occurs through a nucleophilic attack on the 1-carbonyl of acyl bound to acly carrier protein (acylACP) by the free electron of the amine group of SAM. This is followed by the formation of a lactone ring through a nucleophilic attack of the gamma-carbon of SAM on the carboxylate oxygen, ultimately leading to the production of AHLs. These biosynthetic principles provided a rationale for searching for mammalian enzymes with the capacity to catalyze analogous acylation and lactonization-related reactions.

Several glycine acyltransferases demonstrated not only sequence and structural homology but also functional similarity to AHL synthases. Co-localization of some glycine acyltransferases with acyl-CoA/ACP and SAM within the mitochondria might further support the potential production of AHLs through this enzyme family.31 More interestingly, lipid metabolism, including acyl chain synthesis, is regulated by members of the RXR and PPAR protein families,48 which were identified here as potential AHL receptors. This raises the possibility of a regulatory circuit linking AHL-like molecule production, lipid metabolism, and nuclear receptor signaling.

Further mass spectrometric analysis of human cell line-conditioned medium provided preliminary evidence for the presence of AHL-like molecules. The molecular weights of the potential AHL-like molecules identified in this study, which may reflect the lengths of their acyl chains, align with the chemical properties of stable and functional AHLs. In mammalian physiological conditions, AHLs can undergo lactonolysis due to temperature and acid-base balance. However, longer acyl chains reduce the likelihood of spontaneous lactonolysis in mammalian systems, thereby enhancing AHL stability. It has been suggested that AHLs require a minimum acyl chain length of four carbon atoms to maintain their functional activity.45 However, this stability is further influenced by the degree of saturation and the presence of functional groups such as 3-oxo or 3-hydroxy.49 The physiological functionality of AHLs is also dictated by their acyl chain length, as longer chains decrease diffusion efficiency and membrane permeability while increasing intermolecular interactions, such as London dispersion forces, and enhance binding affinity to hydrophobic receptor pockets.50 Notably, HSLs and similar compounds other than AHLs could also be considered when solving the structures of the identified ions. AHLs may feature more complex acyl chains, including methyl branching and double bonds.51 Moreover, HSL may be found with other tails, as seen in p-coumaroyl-HSL52 or cinnamoyl-HSL.53

No prior study has conclusively demonstrated the endogenous production of AHLs or of structurally similar molecules in human or other mammalian cells. However, data from the Human Metabolome database54 indicate the presence of multiple AHLs, including C4-HSL, C8-HSL, 3OH-C8-HSL, C12-HSL, and 3OH-C12-HSL, as well as HSL (HSL without an acyl chain), in blood and urine, although these compounds are currently annotated as imidic acids.55,56,57 The symbiosis of bacterial species capable of AHL biosynthesis and their release into biological fluids precludes ruling out an exogenous contribution, yet our sterile in vitro studies provide preliminary evidence for an endogenous origin. Consistent with this finding, a previous study reported baseline luciferase activity in genetically engineered cells expressing chimeric LasR proteins with a downstream luciferase reporter in the absence of exogenous AHLs, potentially indicating endogenous AHL production in human cells.58

In conclusion, this study provides an integrative framework for understanding AHL activity in mammalian cells. The findings suggest that AHL responses are structurally selective, biologically context-dependent, and partially convergent on pathways controlling cell fate, metabolism, inflammation, and stress. The identification of RXR and PPAR family members as potential receptors, together with the discovery of candidate human acyltransferases and preliminary detection of AHL-like molecules in conditioned medium, supports the hypothesis that mammalian cells may possess signaling systems mediated by AHL-like molecules.

Limitations of the study

This study provides primary answers to the following questions: what are the general and specific responses to AHLs, and are there specific receptors and common molecular pathways mediating AHLs’ effects? Extensive investigations into the effects of AHLs on cellular physiology have presented an opportunity to address these questions. Furthermore, this study serves as a proof of concept for the question of whether human cells (and metazoans in general) are capable of producing AHLs. Therefore, this study should not be viewed as providing comprehensive answers but rather as a collection of evidence supporting the validity of these questions for further investigation.

Nevertheless, the study comes with inherent limitations. These include inconsistencies across experimental designs of the included studies and transcriptomic datasets, as well as inconclusive LC-MS/MS screening without confirmation of the chemical structure of potential AHLs. In addition, the present study does not provide quantitative information on the production, release, turnover, or accumulation of potential AHLs, which will be necessary to assess whether these molecules can reach physiologically relevant signaling levels. Other limitations are rooted in the nature of the methods used. For example, enrichment analysis does not account for the direction of changes in gene expression, while molecular docking and dynamics simulations involve substantial approximations that require experimental confirmation.

Complementary investigations may further expand the functional analogy between these molecules and bacterial systems, where AHLs function as population-level gene regulators that mediate intercellular communication, potentially regulating tissue-, organ-, or system-level activities in multicellular organisms. Additionally, the in vivo production of AHLs, as well as their effects in vivo, requires further investigation. In particular, the biodistribution, stability, and effective tissue concentrations of AHLs under in vivo conditions remain insufficiently characterized and may substantially influence their biological relevance.

Future research will focus on overcoming these limitations, providing further evidence for the existence of AHL-based signaling pathways and elucidating their physiological roles. Our current efforts are aimed at the purification and chemical analysis of AHLs, as well as confirming their synthesis and perception mechanisms through enzyme kinetics, knockout models, AHL production dynamics and fluxes, expression analysis, coupled with luciferase assays and selective antagonism, and treatment with synthetic Homo sapiens-derived AHLs. Additionally, their clinical significance in various diseases, particularly cancer, should be explored, given their potential roles and the notable effects of bacterial AHLs on cancer cells.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human cell lines

HL-60 cells are promyeloblast cells derived from the peripheral blood of a 36-year-old woman with acute myeloid leukemia in 1976. KG1a cells are of acute promyelocytic leukemia origin and were established from the bone marrow of a 59-year-old man with erythroleukemia that had progressed to acute myeloid leukemia. HL60 and KG1a cells were obtained as authenticated cell lines from the cell banks of the Razi Vaccine and Serum Research Institute and the Pasteur Institute of Iran, with no additional in-house authentication performed. The cell lines were initially screened and routinely monitored for mycoplasma contamination by PCR using in-house designed primers. Cells were cryopreserved at −196°C in cryopreservation medium consisting of 7% DMSO and 93% FBS, and thawed as needed. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA, 10% and 20% v/v for HL-60 and KG1a, respectively), 100 IU/mL penicillin (Gibco, USA), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and were routinely passaged when they reached a density of 0.4–0.5×106 cells/ml.

Method details

Systematic review, data extraction, and synthesis

We asked how Acyl Homoserine Lactones (AHLs) affect the physiology of human and animal cells and systematically reviewed original articles describing cell treatments with AHLs. The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines,83 where applicable to in vivo studies. PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, and Outcome) framework, eligibility criteria, databases, search strategies, filters, and data extraction methods were as follows.

Population

Homo sapiens or animal cells (metazoan) – both primary or cell lines – normal or diseased – Studies with genetically manipulated cells in any way will be excluded (except for luciferase assay for evaluation of gene expression and similar methods) – Cells of single type or origin are of interest. For instance, co-cultures, PBMCs, or solenocytes were considered unacceptable due to the existence of cells of various kinds.

Exposure

Acyl-homoserine lactones – Purified and chemically characterized natural compounds or their synthetic counterpart. Crude extracts, exposure with bacteria or bacterial infection, new analogs or modified compounds, AHLs in combination with other compounds or with each other (for instance for evaluation of protective, antagonistic, or synergistic, are not acceptable for this study.

Comparison

Negative control in the form of treatment with the vehicle or untreated groups.

Outcome

Any response evident in cellular physiology, biological functions and pathways, morphology, molecular functions and landscapes (e.g., proteome, transcriptome, and metabolome). Any form of cellular perception or detection of the AHLs are considered acceptable.

A preliminary search was conducted to obtain keywords for a systematic search. Scopus, Web of Science, and MEDLINE were searched on 23rd of April, 2024 – 9-10 am UTC. No limitations were set in terms of date, but the language of the results was limited to English.

The search strings were as follow.

(TITLE-ABS-KEY(“Acyl Homoserine Lactone” OR “Acylated Homoserine Lactone” OR “Homoserine Lactone”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (Animal∗ OR Human∗ OR Mammal∗ OR cell∗ OR neur∗ OR cancer∗ OR tumor∗ OR ∗cell OR ∗cells OR ∗cyte OR ∗cytes OR ∗blast OR ∗blasts OR ∗glia OR ∗glias) AND ALL(“In vitro” OR “In-vitro” OR “In-vivo” OR “In vivo” OR “Cell culture” OR culture OR cultured OR “animal model” OR “animal study” OR “cell line” OR donor OR host OR patient OR sample OR tissue OR treatment OR treating)).

The extracted studies from the databases were first screened for the key components of the question of the review (population, exposure, comparison, and outcome) by their title and abstract by two authors independently. Similarly, the included studies from the last stage were assessed by their full text by two authors independently. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or a third reviewer if they remained.

The extraction of data was conducted by two authors independently. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or a third reviewer if they remained. The following data were extracted: testing compound, treated cell and details on origin, concentration, and age, testing concentration, testing duration, general response, molecular alterations, methods of evaluation, and any proposed models or pathways for the effect or response.

The included studies were screened and extracted for AHL-Cell pairs, with the associated conditions of each treatment, i.e., concentration and duration, general response to AHLs (e.g., changes in cellular viability, morphology, and migration), response at the molecular level, methods used, and any further notes. Both AHLs and cells were categorized to provide an overview of their types and frequencies, as well as treatment concentrations and their associated effects. Our primary focus was on the physiological effects induced by AHLs and the underlying molecular mechanisms. All molecular components affected by AHLs, including changes in gene/protein expression, phosphorylation status, subcellular localization, and structure, were extracted from individual studies, pooled, and integrated into a network using the STRING database63 via Cytoscape (Version 3.8.0).64 This approach allowed for the identification of functional relationships and the core signaling pathways involved in cellular responses to AHLs.

Transcriptomics datasets

Six studies identified in the systematic search assessed the effects of 3O-C12-HSL on gene expression through transcriptomic analyses. We were able to obtain the raw transcriptomic data or primary DEGs from four of these studies for further evaluation.84,85,86,87 DEGs from studies examining the effects of 3O-C12-HSL on Aortic Endothelial cells and Lymphoma cell lines (i.e., BCBL-1 and BCP-1) were calculated directly using the algorithm provided on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Enrichment analysis

To investigate the mechanism of action of AHLs and subsequent cellular responses, we conducted enrichment analysis of the DEGs from datasets S1-S4 through over-representation analysis at two levels: GO and Upstream Transcriptional Regulator/Pathway. GO enrichment was used first to compare heterogeneous datasets at the level of downstream biological functions, whereas upstream regulator analysis was used subsequently to evaluate whether these functional patterns could be linked to common regulatory drivers. Since each dataset used different gene nomenclature, comparison of expression and enrichment profiles across datasets was challenging. To address this, the DEGs from each dataset were mapped to standard gene symbols using the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) database59 for compatibility. Only transcripts indexed as “Approved Symbol” were retained; in cases where no approved symbol was available, the “Alias Symbol” was used, followed by manual inspection of the gene function and description. Transcripts that could not be matched were excluded from the analysis. All subsequent analyses were performed on upregulated, downregulated, or all genes.

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed using the g:Profiler web server’s g:GOSt functional profiling module,65 with default settings. To compare the top enriched pathways in each dataset, GO terms were clustered to eliminate redundancy using the Revigo web server.66 For comparisons of common enriched terms, however, all terms were included in the analysis. To further enrich the comparison of enrichment profiles across datasets, the proximity of the top GO terms in the semantic space, based on the genes included in each term, was calculated and visualized using Go-Figure.67

We used the SPEED2 algorithm68 and the TRRUST database60 to identify upstream transcriptional pathways or regulators of DEGs. The results were evaluated for consensus across multiple datasets and for regulatory relationships, specifically between transcription factors. Common genes, enriched GO terms, and transcriptional regulators were extracted, and their attributes (e.g., frequency, principal component, adjusted p-value, gene ratio, or intersection size) were calculated and visualized using R software. Scripts for data extraction, calculations, and visualizations are available in the Supplemental information.

Molecular docking

Ligands

The two-dimensional structures of homoserine lactone, thirty-four AHL molecules with varying acyl chain lengths and modifications (e.g., carbonyl, hydroxyl, and unsaturated bonds), as well as their fatty acid counterparts (names and SMILES available in Tables S1 and S2), along with the natural ligands of RXR, 9-cis-retinoic acid (9-cis-RA) and all-trans-retinoic acid (ATRA), and PPAR ligands, 8-oxo-eicosatetraenoic acid (8-oxo-ETE) and prostaglandin J2 (PTG), were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) as SDF files. The geometry of each molecule was optimized (force field: MMFF94, steps: 5000, algorithm: Steepest Descent, convergence: 10e−7) using Avogadro (Version 1.2.0)69 and exported as a PDB file. The molecules were then processed using AutoDockTools (Version 1.5.6), where rotatable bonds were identified, Gasteiger charges were added, and the files were subsequently exported in PDBQT format.

Primary blind molecular docking screening

To identify potential AHL receptors, blind molecular docking was performed to rank and categorize proteins based on their binding affinity to 3O-C12-HSL, a bioactive AHL, and C4-HSL, an ineffective AHL. In particular, to investigate potential AHL receptors in human cells, eleven proteins were selected for molecular docking and molecular dynamics studies. These proteins were chosen based on prior literature, protein network analysis, and enrichment analysis of upstream regulators. Additionally, actin (as an intracellular structural protein), keratin (as an extracellular structural protein), pyruvate kinase (as a metabolic enzyme with a specialized binding pocket), albumin (as a carrier protein with general affinity for various molecules), and LasR (as the cognate receptor for C4-HSL and 3O-C12-HSL in Pseudomonas aeruginosa) were included in the primary molecular docking screening to provide a robust benchmark for evaluating binding affinities.

To uncover potential receptors for AHLs, we chose to use AlphaFold models61,62 over experimentally resolved structures to assess the proteins as a whole in an unbiased manner. Notably, when experimentally resolved structures were available, we compared them with the AF models to ensure accuracy. Structure preparation was performed using AutoDockTools, where polar hydrogen atoms and Kollmann charges were added, and the structure was exported as a PDBQT file. The coordinates of the protein centers and the dimensions of the search box, encompassing the entire protein, were determined using the editconf package of GROMACS (Version 2024.2) and finalized with AutodockTools, with an additional 1 nm padding in each direction. The AF models were placed in water and ions and subjected to energy minimization using the CHARMM36 force field, the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model, and the Steepest Descent algorithm (5000 steps and stopped at a maximum force under 1000 kJ/mol/nm) in GROMACS. The models were then evaluated for drastic conformational changes both visually via superposition in PyMOL (Version 2.3.5) and quantitatively by calculating RMSD (RMSDs and superpositions of natural, model, and minimized proteins are reported in the supplementary materials). The energy minimization process resulted in changes to the side chain orientations, while the protein backbones remained unaffected.

The ligand-protein docking process was performed using Autodock Vina (Version 1.1.2). Our initial assessment revealed that both the size of the search space and the complexity of the protein influenced the molecular docking results, particularly in terms of binding energy. To ensure comparability across results, exhaustiveness values, determining the number of sampling points from the search space, and thereby the thoroughness of the search, need to be adjusted accordingly. In this regard, preliminary docking of the natural ligand to PPARγ was performed with sequential exhaustiveness values (10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320) in triplicate to identify the convergence point in both binding site and energy. Based on these findings, the exhaustiveness values for docking with other proteins were calculated and adjusted according to the search box volume for each protein. Blind docking was carried out in six replicates per protein. The best model from each replicate set was selected for comparison of the binding affinities of 3O-C12-HSL and C4-HSL to the different proteins.

Secondary docking

To obtain the most favorable poses and binding affinities of 3O-C12-HSL to the top six proteins from the PPAR and RXR/RAR families, a secondary docking study was conducted with the search space limited to the binding site of the top-scoring pose from the primary blind docking. The search space was defined as 30 Å in each direction, and the exhaustiveness value was set to 80. Molecular docking was performed in triplicate, and the best pose with the lowest binding energy was selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to gain a better understanding of the interactions between ligands and potential receptors, their stability, and ligand-induced structural changes in proteins. The simulations were conducted using the GROMACS package,73 on a Linux Ubuntu 24.04 operating system (x86_64) running on an Intel Core i9-8950HK CPU and an Nvidia Quadro P2000 GPU. The CHARMM3682,88 and CHARMM General Force Field89 were selected as the parameter set for the MD studies, as they are well-suited for proteins, drug-like small molecules, and the estimation of meaningful interaction energies, while providing open-source algorithms and workflows.

The conformation of 3O-C12-HSL with the lowest binding energy, obtained from secondary docking, was extracted from the output PDB files. 3O-C12-HSL was then hydrogenated using Avogadro, as CHARMM36 is an all-atom force field. Bond orders in the output file were further adjusted to ensure the construction of a correct topology with matching coordinates. The ligand was initially parametrized using the CGenFF webserver,70,71 and the charges were optimized using FFparam72 according to MP2 quantum mechanical calculations with the 6-31G∗ basis set using Gaussian (Version 16). The ligand’s topology, based on the newly parameterized moieties, was consistent with that obtained from quantum mechanics calculations. The CGenFF output was converted to formats compatible with GROMACS using the cgenff_charmm2gmx.py script provided by Lemkul Lab.

AlphaFold models of RXRβ (Uniprot: P28702), RXRα (Uniprot: P19793), PPARγ (Uniprot: P37231), and PPARα (Uniprot: Q07869) were subjected to MD simulations based on their superior binding affinity to 3O-C12-HSL. Protein-specific termini (“NH3+” and “COO−”) were selected for each protein. All systems were simulated using the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model, with a distance of 1.0 nm between the protein surface and the edge of the water box. Counterions were added to ensure overall neutrality.

The systems were initially minimized using 1000-step energy minimization (Steepest Descent algorithm, maximum force <10.0 kJ/mol, with a maximum of 50,000 steps). The ligand and protein were then coupled and simultaneously restrained during NVT (V-rescale, 100 ps, 50000 steps, 300 K reference temperature) and NPT (C-rescale, 100 ps, 50000 steps, 1 bar reference pressure) MD simulations. Long-range electrostatics were handled by the Particle Mesh Ewald method,90 with a real-space cutoff of 1.0 nm, and a 1.2 nm cutoff for van der Waals interactions. All covalent bonds between heavy atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm.91 The well-equilibrated systems were then subjected to 10 ns of production MD after releasing the position restraints. The system was re-centered and rewrapped within the unit cell before further analysis. One-dimensional and pairwise RMSD analyses, as well as RMSF and B-factor calculations, were performed using the GROMACS package and MDAnalysis toolkit (Version 2.8.0) to evaluate the system’s stability, ligand binding, and conformational changes.74,75 Detailed steps, related scripts, and parameters are provided in the Data S1.

Structure-activity analysis molecular docking

To investigate the structural determinants of AHL binding to potential receptors and identify molecules with optimal affinity, we performed molecular docking studies. Homoserine lactone, thirty-four AHLs with varying acyl tail lengths (C2 to C20) and modifications (i.e., hydroxyl and carbonyl groups), as well as their fatty acid counterparts (acyl tails without the lactone ring, C4 to C20), were docked into the identified binding pockets of RXRβ, RXRα, PPARγ, and PPARα. The docking simulations were conducted with a search space of 30 Å in each direction and an exhaustiveness value of 80 and were performed in triplicate. AHL binding affinities were compared based on acyl tail length, modifications, and the presence of the γ-butyrolactone ring.

Preparation of leukemic cell lines conditioned medium crude extract

The human acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell line HL-60 (Doubling time: 27 h) and the acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) cell line KG1a (Doubling time: 31.5 h) were cultured at 60,000 and 100,000 cells/ml, respectively, and were incubated for 48 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The cultures were frequently monitored for bacterial colonization and confirmed to be contamination-free, because certain species also produce AHLs, which could compromise the study’s objectives.

Three liters of conditioned medium from each cell line was collected after centrifugation (2,000×g, 20 min) and extracted with ethyl acetate at a 2-fold volume ratio. During extraction, an interphase containing foam-like material formed between the aqueous and ethyl acetate phases. After sedimentation, the upper organic phase was carefully collected, and the clarification step was repeated as needed to ensure removal of the interphase. The ethyl acetate phase was evaporated to dryness using a rotary evaporator at 30°C under reduced pressure, reconstituted in 2 mL of HPLC-grade methanol, and filtered through 0.22 μm Nylon syringe filters for HPLC-MS/MS analysis. Fresh uncultured complete medium subjected to the same extraction protocol served as the control blank sample.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry screening for AHLs

The HPLC-MS/MS setup consisted of an Agilent Zorbax HPLC system (SB-C18 150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm column) coupled with an Agilent G6410 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer and a positive-mode ESI interface. The HPLC conditions were as follows: a mobile phase of acetonitrile:water (0.1% formic acid), which was sonicated, degassed, and filtered. The gradient used was 45% acetonitrile for 20 min, followed by 90% acetonitrile for 3 min, 100% acetonitrile for 7 min, and 45% acetonitrile for 10 min, with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 25°C, and a 20 μL sample was injected via an autosampler.

Mass spectrometer settings included a capillary voltage of 4500 V, a mass range of 120–450 amu, a dwell time of 500 ms, a fragmentor voltage of 120 V, and a collision energy of 7. A characteristic daughter ion of AHLs has previously been reported at m/z 102, corresponding to the γ-butyrolactone ring.42,92,93

For qualitative assessment of potential AHLs, each sample extract was first analyzed using the precursor-ion scan mode to identify molecules producing the characteristic daughter ion at m/z 102. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) of each medium extract was then examined for prominent masses, with individual masses further analyzed using extracted ion chromatograms (EIC). To further evaluate the detected ions, the presence of additional daughter ions at m/z 56, 74, or 84 was evaluated using the product ion method. All data were processed and analyzed using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis software.

Investigating potential AHL synthases in human cells

Potential AHL synthases in human cells, homologous to bacterial enzymes, were identified through sequence and structural similarity analyses using HMMsearch94 and FoldSeek76 web servers. Six curated AHL synthases with confirmed evidence at the protein or transcript level served as input for FoldSeek against the AlphaFold/Proteome v4 database, with taxonomy restricted to Homo sapiens (Mode: 3DiAA – iterative search). Hits from the six searches with a probability value of one were pooled, and unique entries were extracted for further analysis.

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed using MUSCLE (Version 5.1.osx64)77 on thirty-four curated bacterial and eighteen automatically annotated eukaryotic AHL synthases obtained from UniProt, alongside FoldSeek hits containing proteins under 500 amino acids. Protein structures were generated using ColabFold (Version 1.5.5)78 with the following settings: MMseqs2 (UniRef+Environmental), num_models: 1, num_recycles: 3, PLDDT score threshold: 100, num_relax: 0, relax_max_iterations: 200, and Multiple Structural Alignment (MSTA) was conducted using FoldMason.79

The resulting MSA and MSTA of the 82 proteins were utilized to construct a phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE (Version 2.3.6)80 with default parameters. Q.pfam+R5 and PMB+F+G4 were identified as the best-fit models for the MSA and MSTA, respectively, by the ModelFinder95 and a Maxiumum-likelihood tree was constructed. Visualization and annotation of the phylogenetic tree were carried out using iTOL (Version 7).81

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, including Student’s t test for binding affinity comparisons, and data visualizations were carried out using R software. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with p ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. The exact number of replicates (n) for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. In this study, n denotes the number of independent technical replicates in the in silico analyses. Detailed statistical information, including sample size, statistical tests, and significance values, is provided in the relevant figure legends and results sections.
