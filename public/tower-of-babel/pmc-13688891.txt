School-related internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors and their associations with subjective well-being among Chinese children and adolescents: a network analysis
Authors: Meihua Wang, Xinrui Yin, Wendi Kong, Xiangyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInternalizing and externalizing problem behaviors frequently co-occur among children and adolescents and are associated with their subjective well-being (SWB). However, the symptom-level interactions underlying this co-occurrence remain unclear. Grounded in a network analysis framework, this study investigated the conditional associations among these school-related problem behaviors and their associations with SWB. Data were drawn from 2,049 students in the 2022 wave of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS). We estimated indicator networks to identify functionally central and bridging indicators, and constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) via Bayesian networks to explore probabilistic dependencies. Additionally, network comparison methods were utilized to evaluate structural invariance across gender, urban–rural residency, and educational stages. We employed flow functions to trace direct and indirect associational links with SWB. The results revealed that IPB5 “worry about homework”, IPB4 “worry about performance”, and IPB6 “worry about playmates” emerged as the central symptoms within the network. Furthermore, EPB1 “argue with peers”, IPB2 “fear of exams”, and EPB3 “difficulty completing” acted as key bridge symptoms connecting the two domains. While the overall network structure remained stable across genders, significant structural disparities were observed between urban and rural students. Across educational subgroups, the relative prominence of core and bridge symptoms varied. These findings offer a descriptive characterization of symptom co-occurrence patterns within the Chinese educational context. Introduction

Childhood and adolescence constitute critical periods for psychological and behavioral development and are also characterized by a high prevalence of internalizing problems (e.g., depression, anxiety, and withdrawal) and externalizing problems (e.g., aggression and delinquent behaviors) [1]. A survey of children and adolescents aged 6 to 16 across 15 Chinese cities revealed a 17.5% prevalence of behavioral problems [2]. Evidence from existing meta-analyses and large-scale surveys indicates that the prevalence of externalizing problems among Chinese adolescents exceeds 35% [3], while the prevalence of depression during the high school stage reaches 28% [4, 5]. Both clinical practice and empirical research consistently demonstrate a high degree of co-occurrence between internalizing and externalizing problems [6], and this co-occurrence pattern is associated with deterioration in subjective well-being (SWB), a key indicator of quality of life and positive psychological functioning [7].

Importantly, the school context constitutes a primary developmental setting in which internalizing and externalizing problems manifest and interact among children and adolescents. Within the school environment, emotional and behavioral difficulties are closely intertwined with academic demands and peer relational dynamics. Viewed through the lens of control-value theory [8], the highly competitive, examination-oriented norms in China often elicit profound worry and anxiety. These serve as cognitive-motivational responses to a perceived lack of control in high-value academic situations. Consequently, school-related internalizing problems prominently include academic worry, performance anxiety, loneliness, and social concerns. Simultaneously, school-related externalizing problems encompass peer conflict, classroom disruption, and difficulties completing academic tasks. According to developmental cascade models [9, 10], these internalizing and externalizing domains are not isolated; rather, problems in one domain reliably spread to undermine functioning in the other, particularly through the mechanisms of academic struggles and peer conflicts. These school-specific manifestations may follow different co-occurrence patterns than those observed in clinical or broadband symptom assessments, yet they have received comparatively limited attention at the symptom level. Relying solely on aggregate scores may obscure the specific symptom-level associations that underlie co-occurrence within school contexts and their relationship with well-being.

Previous research has predominantly examined internalizing and externalizing problems through latent variable models, which assume that observed symptoms are passive indicators of underlying latent traits. This perspective overlooks dynamic associations at the symptom level and cannot readily explain why certain specific symptoms are more strongly associated with comorbidity than others [11]. Network theory offers a complementary framework, conceptualizing mental health problems as systems of mutually associated symptoms rather than as manifestations of a single underlying cause [12]. Within this framework, symptoms are treated as nodes and their associations as edges [13]; bridge symptoms refer to nodes that connect distinct psychopathological domains. Applied to school-related emotional and behavioral problems, this symptom-level perspective enables a fine-grained characterization of how specific school-situated concerns are conditionally associated with one another and with SWB.

Network analysis was initially developed within clinical samples; however, its extension to general and community populations is well-supported. Contemporary dimensional models of psychopathology conceptualize internalizing and externalizing problems as existing on a continuum from subclinical to clinical levels [14, 15], and sub-threshold problem behaviors are prevalent among general child and adolescent populations with significant functional consequences [16]. When applied to general population data with a domain-specific item set, network analysis instead characterizes the conditional dependence structure among the assessed items, and interpretations are accordingly scoped to those specific items [17]. The present study adopts this interpretive frame explicitly: the included items were designed to capture school-related emotional and behavioral difficulties, and findings therefore pertain to conditional associations among these school-situated symptoms rather than to the full clinical architecture of internalizing and externalizing psychopathology. This school-specific focus is a deliberate conceptual choice, as it enables precise examination of symptom co-occurrence within the ecologically meaningful context of schooling. Prior studies have successfully employed item-level network analysis with non-clinical youth samples (e.g., [18, 19]), providing empirical precedent for the current approach.

In recent years, network analysis has increasingly been applied to child and adolescent psychopathology. Sánchez-Hernández et al. [18] employed network analysis to identify central symptoms underlying internalizing and externalizing problems in children and adolescents, but primarily constructed separate network models without systematically examining bridge symptoms within a single integrated network. Existing studies also remain limited in their coverage of Chinese children and adolescents. Prior research has largely concentrated on specific subpopulations—such as children with dyslexia or those exposed to adverse childhood experiences [20–22]—with few large-scale investigations using nationally representative general population samples. Furthermore, few studies have incorporated SWB into network models, limiting understanding of which specific school-related symptoms are most strongly associated with diminished well-being in this population.

Drawing on data from the 2022 wave of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), this study uses network analysis to investigate the conditional associations among school-related internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors in Chinese children and adolescents. Specifically, we address three research questions. First, we estimate a symptom-level network to identify central symptoms and bridge symptoms connecting the internalizing and externalizing subnetworks, and further estimate a Bayesian network (directed acyclic graph, DAG) on the same indicators to explore the probabilistic directional dependencies among symptoms, thereby deepening the structural characterization. Second, we conduct network comparison tests to examine whether network structure and global strength differ across gender, educational stage, and urban–rural residence. Third, we incorporate subjective well-being (SWB) into an extended network and use flow networks to visualize how individual symptoms are directly and indirectly associated with each indicator.

In accordance with control-value theory [8], which identifies worry and anxiety as central cognitive-motivational responses to perceived lack of control in high-value academic contexts, we expect worry-related internalizing symptoms to emerge as the central nodes within the school-related symptom network. Furthermore, consistent with developmental cascade evidence demonstrating that externalizing problems reliably spread across domains to undermine both academic functioning and internalizing adjustment [9, 10], we expect externalizing behaviors to function as key bridge symptoms linking the two domains. Regarding SWB, symptoms impairing social functioning and attentional capacity are expected to exhibit negative associations [23, 24]. Since, to the best of our knowledge, evidence regarding gender, urban–rural, and educational stage disparities remains mixed in this specific context, no specific hypotheses can be formulated for these subgroups, and an exploratory approach is adopted.

Methods

Participants

Data were drawn from the 2022 wave of the CFPS, the most recent data available at the time of this study. The CFPS is a nationally representative survey administered by the Institute of Social Science Survey of Peking University across 25 provinces and covering more than 10,000 households. The CFPS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-14010). Written informed consent was obtained prior to each interview, with participants aged 15 years and older providing consent themselves and legal guardians providing consent for participants aged 15 years and younger.

Participants aged 9–18 years who completed assessments of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors were eligible for inclusion. Of the 2,858 individuals who met the age criterion and completed both assessments, 108 cases were excluded due to missing symptom data resulting from proxy reporting. To ensure independence of observations, one child was randomly selected from households with two or more school-aged children. The final analytic sample consisted of 2,049 children and adolescents.

Measures

Internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors

Internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors were assessed using a modified version of the Behavior Problems Index (BPI; [25]), originally developed based on the Achenbach Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; [26]). This measurement has been adapted and validated in the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) to evaluate the psychological and behavioral well-being of Chinese children and adolescents. It has acceptable reliability with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the internalizing problem dimension as 0.65, and 0.64 for the externalizing problem dimension. The original 14-item scale was psychometrically evaluated by the survey team, and two items (one internalizing and one externalizing) were removed due to inadequate factor structure. The structural validity of the remaining 12 items was supported by a two-factor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The model demonstrated acceptable fit to the data, χ2(53) = 790.18, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.082 (95% CI [0.077, 0.088]), CFI = 0.939, TLI = 0.924, and SRMR = 0.044. All standardized factor loadings were significant (p < 0.001), ranging from 0.513 to 0.751 for internalizing problem behaviors and from 0.548 to 0.709 for externalizing problem behaviors. Accordingly, the present study used the remaining 12 items, including 7 items assessing internalizing problem behaviors and 5 items assessing externalizing problem behaviors.

The internalizing items included: IPB1 (angry at difficulties, “I get angry when encountering learning difficulties”), IPB2 (afraid of exams, “I am afraid of exams”), IPB3 (feel lonely, “I often feel lonely”), IPB4 (worry about performance, “I worry about not performing well at school”), IPB5 (worry about homework, “I worry about not finishing my homework”), IPB6 (worry about playmates, “I worry about not having playmates at school”), and IPB7 (ashamed of mistakes, “I feel ashamed when making mistakes at school”). The externalizing items included: EPB1 (argue with peers, “I often argue with peers”), EPB2 (difficulty concentrating, “I have difficulty concentrating”), EPB3 (difficulty completing, “I have difficulty completing assignments”), EPB4 (trouble for disturbing, “I get into trouble for talking and disturbing others”), and EPB5 (trouble for fighting, “I get into trouble for fighting with peers”). These indicators capture school-related emotional concerns (internalizing) and classroom behavioral difficulties (externalizing), and are analyzed as an indicator network rather than a comprehensive symptom-level clinical network. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating more severe problem behaviors. In the present study, the overall internal consistency reliability of the scale was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.79).

Subjective well-being

SWB was assessed using two indicators, life satisfaction and subjective happiness, which capture its fundamental dimensions. While multi-item scales are prevalent in psychological research, prior methodological studies have robustly demonstrated that single-item measures for life satisfaction and happiness exhibit adequate reliability and validities, making them highly appropriate and commonly employed in large-scale national surveys like the CFPS [27, 28].

In the current study, life satisfaction was measured with a single self-report item (“How satisfied are you with your life?”), rated on a scale from 1 (very unsatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied). Subjective happiness was assessed using another single item (“How happy do you think you are?”), rated on a scale from 0 (lowest level of happiness) to 10 (highest level of happiness).

Covariates

To account for potential confounding effects, a set of socio-demographic, family, and health covariates known to influence youth psychopathology and well-being were incorporated into the analyses [29–32]. Specifically, socio-demographic indicators comprised age (in years), gender (1 = male, 0 = female), Educational stage (1 = primary school, 2 = junior middle school, 3 = high school), and place of residence (1 = urban, 0 = rural). Family-level characteristics encompassed left-behind status (1 = yes, 0 = no) and average household income, with the latter log-transformed to adjust for skewness. Physical health status was included as a health-related indicator. In the analytical procedure, each core variable was first regressed on the above control variables. The residuals from these regressions were then extracted and used as input for subsequent network analyses, thereby minimizing the potential influence of demographic characteristics on the associations among the core variables.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 12.0, including the calculation of means, standard deviations, minimum values, and maximum values. Network and correlation analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2). Specifically, the bootnet package [33] was used for network estimation and stability assessment, the qgraph package [34] for network visualization and the flow function to characterize the relationships between internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors and subjective well-being, and the bnlearn package [35] for Bayesian network modeling and analysis. Additionally, network comparisons were conducted based on gender, urban/rural residence, and educational stage using the NetworkComparisonTest (NCT) package in R [36]. Missing data were minimal, ranging from 0% to 0.34% across all study variables. Missing data were handled using Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) in the bootnet package for the network analysis and the locally complete observations approach implemented in the bnlearn package for the Bayesian network analysis.

Network structure estimation was conducted using the estimateNetwork function in the bootnet package. Variables were represented as nodes, and partial correlations between variables were represented as edges, with edge thickness indicating edge weights. To reduce spurious associations, the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Glasso) was applied to obtain a sparse edge-weight matrix. The optimal level of regularization was determined using the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC).

Strength centrality and bridge strength/expected influence were computed using the networktools package. Nodes with higher strength and bridge strength/expected influence were considered more critical connectors in the network. Network accuracy and robustness were evaluated using the bootnet package. A case-dropping bootstrap procedure without replacement (1,000 iterations) was conducted to assess the stability of strength centrality, expected influence, bridge strength centrality, and bridge expected influence. The correlation stability coefficient (CS-Coefficient) was calculated, with values above 0.25 indicating acceptable network stability and values above 0.50 indicating high stability and limited dependence on the specific sample. In addition, a non-parametric bootstrap with replacement (1,000 iterations) was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals for edge weights. Narrow confidence intervals that did not include zero were interpreted as indicating more stable parameter estimates and more accurate edge weights.

Bayesian networks were estimated using the hill-climbing algorithm implemented in the bnlearn package in R. Bayesian networks are probabilistic graphical models that represent conditional dependencies among random variables through directed acyclic graphs and conditional probability tables. To improve network stability, the sample was resampled 1,000 times, during which edges were added, removed, or reversed to optimize the network score. An averaged network was then generated using the averaged.network function based on the resampling results, yielding a parsimonious and more stable directed network. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, edge directions were interpreted as probabilistic dependencies rather than causal effects.

It is important to clarify the analytic role of SWB. Because life satisfaction and subjective happiness reflect positive psychological functioning rather than problem-behavior symptoms, they were not treated as constituent nodes of the symptom network. Accordingly, the centrality, bridge centrality, Bayesian network, and network comparison analyses were estimated on the 12 problem-behavior indicators. To characterize how this system relates to well-being, life satisfaction and subjective happiness were subsequently added as additional nodes separately, and the flow function was used to trace the direct and indirect associations between problem-behavior symptoms and SWB indicators.

Network comparisons across gender, educational stage, and urban–rural residency

The Network Comparison Test was conducted to examine similarities and differences in network characteristics across subgroups. As per supplement Table 7, at the global network structure level, no significant differences were observed across gender or educational stages; however, significant structural disparities emerged between urban and rural students,. Regarding global strength (i.e., the sum of absolute edge weights), no significant differences were found across any groups, indicating that the overall connectivity between internalizing and externalizing symptoms remained similarly tight across populations. In terms of network characteristic similarity, edge weight correlations were strong (r > 0.5) across gender, educational stage, and residency, suggesting high consistency in network connectivity patterns among all groups (see supplement Table 8).

For centrality indices, the rank order of both strength and bridge centrality was highly similar across gender and residency (r > 0.6). However, significant differences in centrality rankings were observed among different educational stages. Specifically, pairwise comparisons revealed no significant correlations in bridge centrality rankings between primary, junior middle, and high school. Furthermore, strength centrality rankings showed no significant correlations between primary and junior middle school, nor between primary and high school.

The profiles of core symptoms differed distinctly across the educational stage subgroups. In terms of strength centrality, the core symptoms among primary school students were IPB6 (worry about playmates; strength = 0.78) and IPB5 (worry about homework; strength = 0.64). In the junior middle school and high school groups, symptoms associated with academic pressure were more prominent; specifically, IPB5 (worry about homework) was the primary core symptom for both the junior middle school (strength = 0.76) and high school (strength = 1.01) subgroups, while IPB4 (worry about performance) also exhibited a notable weight in the junior middle school sample. Regarding bridge centrality, the key bridge symptom in the primary school subgroup was EPB3 (difficulty completing). In contrast, the primary bridge symptom among junior middle school students was EPB4 (trouble for disturbing), whereas in the high school subgroup, the key bridge symptoms were IPB3 (feel lonely) and IPB6 (worry about playmates).

Discussion

Although previous studies have documented the co-occurrence of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors among children and adolescents, the symptom-level associations underlying this co-occurrence—and how individual symptoms relate to SWB—have received comparatively limited attention in the Chinese context. The present study applied a network analysis framework to data from the 2022 China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) to examine the conditional associations among school-related internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors. Specifically, we identified central and bridge symptoms within the network and explored variations in network architecture across gender, educational stage, and urban–rural subgroups. We used flow networks to examine how problem behaviors were directly and indirectly associated with life satisfaction and subjective happiness. These findings offer a descriptive characterization of indicator co-occurrence patterns within the Chinese educational context, providing empirical support for our expectations while also generating new exploratory insights.

At the overall network level, the core symptoms of school-related internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors were IPB5 (worry about homework), IPB6 (worry about playmates), and IPB4 (worry about performance). All three nodes represent internalizing problem behaviors, and excessive worry constitutes a shared feature of these core symptoms. However, it is crucial to interpret this finding in light of the scale's composition, which predominantly captures school and peer-related concerns. Within this specific measurement context, the results suggest that emotional distress—particularly anxiety-related processes—may occupy a central position in the co-occurrence of internalizing and externalizing problems. One possible explanation lies in the highly competitive educational context and the collectivist cultural background. Within a merit-oriented value system, strong parental expectations for children’s success may be associated with self-worth and self-identity that are more contingent upon external evaluations [37]. For students in primary and secondary education, homework and academic achievement may be especially salient concerns, which is consistent with the central position of the corresponding nodes in the network. Meanwhile, the emphasis on relational harmony inherent in collectivist culture encourages students to prioritize group belonging, rendering worries about peer relationships another salient component linked to both internalizing and externalizing problems. These findings align with previous network analyses of adolescent depression and anxiety, which similarly identified excessive worry as a core bridging feature [38].

Regarding bridge symptoms, EPB1 (argue with peers) emerged as bridge symptom linking internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors within this network. Previous research has indicated that externalizing behaviors may be associated with internalizing problems through intermediary processes [39]. One plausible explanation is that classroom misconduct may be associated with teacher punishment and peer exclusion, and such adverse social experiences may also be associated with feelings of rejection and negative affect [40, 41]. Meanwhile, EPB3 (difficulty completing) also functioned as a bridge symptom, potentially reflecting both cognitive and emotional processes through which academic difficulties co-occur with internalizing distress. Specifically, repeated academic failures may be linked to frustration and diminished self-efficacy, while persistent difficulties can correlate with heightened worries about school performance. In addition, IPB2 (afraid of exams) also emerged as a bridge symptom connecting internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors. One plausible explanation is that exam-related fear may co-occur with reduced attentional resources, which may be associated with disengagement from academic tasks and difficulty sustaining classroom behavior [42]. Moreover, students with heightened fear of evaluative failure may adopt avoidance-oriented strategies such as task disengagement, through which anxious affect may co-occur with broader externalizing manifestations [43].

The DAG analysis revealed a structured pattern of conditional dependencies among symptoms, with IPB5 (worry about homework), IPB4 (worry about performance), and IPB6 (worry about playmates) occupying upstream positions in the network. These patterns suggest that academic worry and social concern may be associated with broader symptom configurations within this item set. In contrast, IPB3 (feel lonely) and EPB2 (difficulty concentrating) were positioned downstream, indicating conditional dependence on other symptoms within the network.

The network comparison analysis indicated that the overall network structure and global strength did not differ significantly across genders, consistent with prior evidence for structural stability of internalizing symptom networks among children and adolescents [19]. Significant structural differences were observed between urban and rural subgroups, a pattern that may reflect differences in educational resources, family structures, and social support systems, though causal interpretation is not warranted from these cross-sectional data.

Regarding educational stages, although the overall network structure remained stable, the relative connectivity of specific symptoms varied across educational subgroups. For primary school students, the core symptoms were primarily related to peer interactions and initial academic tasks. In secondary school subgroups, academic-related worries (e.g., IPB5 “worry about homework”) were prominent as the most central symptoms. This distinct pattern aligns with the increased academic demands and competitive educational environment characteristic of higher educational stages, particularly in the Chinese educational context [44].

Furthermore, the characteristics of bridge symptoms exhibited distinct profiles across educational stages. In the primary and junior middle school subgroups, externalizing problems (e.g., EPB3 “difficulties completing” or EPB4 “trouble for disturbing”) served as the primary bridge symptoms connecting the two sub-networks. In contrast, for high school students, internalizing symptoms, such as IPB3 (feel lonely) and IPB6 (worry about playmates), emerged as the key bridge nodes. This stage-specific difference is in line with literature indicating that late adolescence is characterized by heightened sensitivity to peer relationships and social belonging [45], contexts in which internal emotional distress may exhibit stronger concurrent associations with other behavioral issues.

Overall, these findings elucidate the complex conditional interplay of school-related internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors. The prominence of academic and peer-related worries underscores their importance in the co-occurrence of behavioral issues among Chinese youth. Similarly, the varying prominence of bridge symptoms across educational levels highlights the necessity of stage-specific perspectives in understanding youth mental health dynamics. While these descriptive patterns map the conditional associations within this specific context, they also point to critical avenues for future inquiry.

The analysis of subjective well-being (SWB) indicated that symptoms directly associated with life satisfaction and subjective happiness included IPB3 (feeling lonely), IPB6 (worry about playmates), EPB2 (difficulty concentrating), and EPB4 (trouble for disturbing others), highlighting the potential relevance of emotional, social, and attentional difficulties for children's positive affective experiences. Consistent with prior research, internalizing problems were associated with poorer evaluations of life, whereas certain externalizing problems appeared associated with reduced well-being, possibly through social exclusion mechanisms [46]. Notably, IPB7 (ashamed of mistakes) was positively associated with subjective well-being, diverging from some findings in Western samples [47]. This discrepancy may reflect cultural differences in the meaning of shame; within the Confucian cultural context, moderate shame is often considered indicative of moral internalization and self-regulation [48], and may be associated with more adaptive behavioral adjustment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. Most notably, this study relies on a highly abbreviated version of the Behavior Problems Index comprising only 12 items, which poses fundamental constraints on the interpretability of the network findings. Network analysis treats each item as an autonomous node, and it is well established that network structure, centrality estimates, and bridge symptom identification are sensitive to the composition of the included item set [49]. Within the internalizing subscale, five of seven items concern academic performance worries and peer relationship concerns. Coverage of externalizing psychopathology is similarly narrow, with limited representation of oppositionality, impulsivity, irritability, and aggression. As a result, the structural prominence of academic worry observed in the present network may be influenced by this specific item selection. These findings should therefore be interpreted as a characterization of school-relevant behavioral indicators rather than a comprehensive mapping of general psychopathology. Future research should employ more comprehensive and psychometrically validated instruments to provide a broader and more representative sampling of symptom content. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference about temporal ordering or causal direction among symptoms. Although DAG analysis produces directional graphs, these reflect conditional dependence structures in the data and do not establish that any symptom precedes or causes another. Future research should employ longitudinal designs, such as cross-lagged panel network analysis, to investigate the temporal dynamics of symptom co-occurrence.

Conclusions

Utilizing data from the 2022 CFPS, this study examined conditional associations among school-related internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors and subjective well-being within a network analytic framework. Our findings reveal that academic and peer-related worries exhibit the highest connectivity within this symptom network. Furthermore, argue with peers, fear of exams, and difficulties completing schoolwork serve as bridge symptoms connecting these two domains. Network comparisons demonstrated overall structural stability across genders, alongside notable structural disparities between urban and rural students, as well as distinct connectivity patterns across educational stages.

By mapping these co-occurrence patterns within a specific item set, this study provides a valuable descriptive characterization of youth mental health dynamics within the Chinese educational context. While the cross-sectional design precludes inferences regarding developmental trajectories or causal directions, these insights lay a robust empirical foundation for hypothesis generation. Future longitudinal research employing more comprehensive clinical measures is essential to further unravel the directional development of these interconnected behaviors and translate these symptom-level findings into targeted clinical interventions.
