Heart rate management using ivabradine in acute myocardial infarction: a propensity score-matched single-center study
Authors: Meixian Chen, Peiqi Zhong, Chao Li, Qingfeng Gao, Zhurong Luo, Daqian Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe therapeutic role of ivabradine in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains clinically debated. This study used real-world data to evaluate the rehospitalization risk associated with ivabradine treatment for heart rate control. Among 408 patients with AMI, 76 (18.6%) received ivabradine. Kaplan-Meier analysis, log-rank test, Cox proportional hazards models, and propensity score matching (PSM) were used to assess the rehospitalization risk. Additionally, an interaction test evaluated ivabradine’s impact on the rehospitalization risk across relevant risk factors. Multiple imputation was employed to handle missing data. Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that ivabradine therapy was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause rehospitalization in patients with AMI before PSM (HR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.44–0.91, P = 0.015), but not after (HR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.50–1.24, P = 0.310). Subgroup analysis demonstrated a decreased rehospitalization risk following ivabradine therapy in females (HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.22–0.89, P = 0.023), and patients with heart rate > 70 bpm (HR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.40–0.89, P = 0.011), Killip classification > 1 (HR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.39–0.99, P = 0.046), anterior wall myocardial infarction (HR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.26–0.93, P = 0.029), > 1 diseased vessel (HR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.38–0.93, P = 0.023), and no chronic kidney disease (CKD) (HR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.46–0.98, P = 0.040) or clopidogrel therapy (HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.32–0.90, P = 0.019). Target-range heart rate at first discharge was linked to a lower rehospitalization risk (HR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.57–0.96, P = 0.025). Multivariable analysis indicated that ivabradine reduced the rehospitalization risk over a 12-month follow-up (adjusted HR: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.66–0.96, P = 0.017). In conclusion, ivabradine therapy during hospitalization was associated with a lower risk of all-cause rehospitalization in patients with AMI. Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a cardiovascular disease that poses serious threat to human health and is a major cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults worldwide1,2. The incidence of AMI has been increasing in recent years3. Recurrent myocardial infarction, heart failure (HF), and arrhythmia are the main causes of rehospitalization after AMI, imposing considerable psychological and economic burdens to these patients4–6. While the development of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) technology has significantly improved myocardial infarction treatment, many people still experience adverse events due to associated complications7. Therefore, identifying drugs that can reduce rehospitalization rates among patients with AMI is particularly important.

Numerous drug treatment strategies exist for AMI, including lowering blood pressure, controlling heart rate, antiplatelet therapy, and inhibiting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)8. Among these, heart rate (HR) control is an important approach, as it can limit sympathetic influence on cardiac remodeling9. Beta-blockers are representative drugs for heart rate control and have been shown in many studies to reduce the incidence of heart failure after AMI9. Furthermore, they have become a cornerstone of drug therapy for heart failure10. However, beta-blockers can reduce muscle strength, lower blood pressure, and cause other negative effects, rendering them unsuitable for some patients with AMI, such as those with hypotension. Consequently, alternative heart rate control drugs are needed to address the limitations of beta-blockers.

Ivabradine is an HR-lowering drug that inhibits the hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channel, an important inhibitor of the cardiac pacemaker current (If) in the sinoatrial node. Importantly, it does not adversely affect myocardial contractility and cardiac conduction11,12. Ivabradine has been shown to effectively reduces the risk of hospitalization and mortality in patients with chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) by maintaining sinus rhythm13. The SHIFT trial [Systolic Heart Failure Treatment with the (If) Inhibitor Ivabradine Trial] demonstrated an 18% relative risk reduction in cardiovascular death and hospitalization for worsening heart failure with ivabradine compared with placebo14. Furthermore, the safety of ivabradine for HR control in patients with AMI have been confirmed15,16. To date, no randomized controlled trials or large-scale real-world studies have explored whether heart-rate reduction with ivabradine improves outcomes in the acute MI setting. In this prospective study, we aimed to investigate the association between ivabradine therapy and the risk of rehospitalization in patients with AMI.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

The final analysis included 408 patients with AMI: 76 in the ivabradine group and 332 in the ivabradine-free group. Before PSM, the ivabradine group had a significantly higher age, heart rate, length of initial hospitalization (LOIH), and a higher proportion of patients with Killip classification > 1 compared to the ivabradine-free group. Furthermore, more patients in the ivabradine group received spironolactone and clopidogrel (including five patients who were switched from ticagrelor to clopidogrel due to bleeding or dyspnea) compared to the ivabradine-free group (all P < 0.05; Table 1). Following PSM, the groups were statistically comparable in all baseline characteristics, laboratory findings, and angiographic results (Supplementary Table S2).

Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses of ivabradine therapy and rehospitalization

The univariate analysis for the entire cohort (Table 2) indicated that ivabradine therapy insignificantly reduced the risk of rehospitalization by 28% (HR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.51–1.01, P = 0.059). Furthermore, the LOIH (HR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.52–0.86, P = 0.002) and the prescription of RAAS inhibitors (RAASi) (HR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.56–0.95, P = 0.018) or ticagrelor (HR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.50–0.83, P < 0.001) were independently associated with a lower risk of rehospitalization. Based on both clinical and statistical reasons (see Supplementary Table S1), the following confounding factors were included in the multivariable analysis: age, heart rate, Killip classification, LOIH, and spironolactone therapy (Table 3). Compared to the ivabradine-free group, the ivabradine group was similarly associated with the risk of rehospitalization in the non-adjusted model (HR = 0.72; 95% CI: 0.51–1.01, P = 0.061) but showed a protective effect in model I (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.47–0.93, P = 0.018) and model II (aHR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.44–0.91, P = 0.015) (Table 4) .

Stratified analysis

To further explore the effect of ivabradine on rehospitalization in patients with AMI, we controlled for all covariates in the stratified analysis (Table 5). The results showed that ivabradine therapy was associated with a lower risk of rehospitalization in patients who met the following criteria: female (HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.22–0.89, P = 0.023), heart rate > 70 bpm (HR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.40–0.89, P = 0.011), Killip classification > 1 (HR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.39–0.99, P = 0.046), anterior wall myocardial infarction (HR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.26–0.93, P = 0.029), more than one diseased vessel (HR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.38–0.93, P = 0.023), and without chronic kidney disease (CKD; HR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.46–0.98, P = 0.040) or clopidogrel therapy (HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.32–0.90, P = 0.019). However, none of the intergroup interaction tests reached statistical significance.

Sensitivity analysis

A multivariable Cox regression analysis after multiple imputation (MI) revealed that ivabradine treatment remained associated with a lower risk of rehospitalization (Table 4), with an aHR of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.66–0.96, P = 0.017) for the Multi - State model (MSM). Following PSM, the ivabradine and ivabradine-free groups (both n = 74) were statistically comparable (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S), and the ivabradine therapy was not significantly associated with a decreased risk of rehospitalization (HR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.50–1.24, P = 0.310).

Target heart rate analysis

The result (Table 6) indicated that ivabradine therapy did not significantly affect the proportion of patients with AMI achieving the target heart rate (55–70 bpm) at discharge (not achieving vs. achieving: 41.3% [n = 137] vs. 34.2% [n = 26], P = 0.257). However, having a target heart rate at first discharge was associated with a reduced risk of rehospitalization (HR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.57–0.96, P = 0.025). Furthermore, stratified analysis revealed that patients who did not achieve the target heart rate at first discharge experienced a significantly lower rehospitalization risk if their treatment included ivabradine than if it did not (Odds Ratio (OR): 0.68; 95% CI: 0.57–0.81, P < 0.001). However, this effect was insignificant among patients who did reach the target heart rate (OR: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.62–1.03, P = 0.083).

Discussion

This study found that continued in-hospital treatment with ivabradine was significantly associated with a lower risk of rehospitalization in patients with AMI. Specifically, ivabradine was shown to reduce the risk of rehospitalization in patients who met the following criteria: female; heart rate > 70 bpm; Killip classification > 1; anterior wall myocardial infarction; more than one diseased vessel; or patients without CKD or clopidogrel therapy. However, prospective clinical trials with a larger sample size are still required to confirm these findings.

An increased heart rate is a widely recognized independent predictor of mortality in various cardiovascular diseases, including AMI, stable coronary disease, chronic heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. In these conditions, a higher heart rate may indicate increased sympathetic excitability, suggesting an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand17. Therefore, heart rate control is a key goal in AMI treatment. Our study found that patients who achieved the target heart rate at first discharge had a lower risk of all-cause rehospitalization. However, we did not continue to monitor the heart rate after discharge or record it at rehospitalization, making it impossible to determine if a persistently controlled heart rate affected the rehospitalization rate.

Beta-blockers are a primary choice for heart rate reduction among the anti-anginal treatments in patients with AMI, as they decrease myocardial oxygen demand, improve left ventricular function, and alter cardiac remodeling18. While a previous heart failure study found that ivabradine reduced heart rate regardless of beta-blocker use19, our study found no significantly influence of ivabradine on the proportion of patients with AMI who achieved the target heart rate at first discharge. This appears to contradict the other study. Our findings may be explained by the fact that the heart rate in our ivabradine group only decreased from 88.5 to 75.6 bpm by the first one-month follow-up, which was still higher than our target. Additionally, the other study had a much larger cohort of 1,003 patients, which could account for the differing results.

Rehospitalization is a key metric for evaluating drug efficacy. A previous study found that ivabradine treatment had a similar rehospitalization incidence to beta-blocker treatment in patients with inferior wall myocardial infarction20. This suggests ivabradine may have a comparable potential to beta-blockers in reducing the risk of rehospitalization, a known benefit of beta-blockers in patients with AMI21. In contrast, a recent study found that ivabradine did not affect the 12-month hospitalization incidence for heart failure or cardiovascular death in post-discharge patients with AMI and poorly controlled heart rate, even after titration of beta-blocker dosing22.

In our study, ivabradine therapy was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause rehospitalization, but this conclusion was limited to univariate and multivariable analyses of the entire cohort. Ivabradine did not significantly affect the risk of rehospitalization after PSM. This may be due to confounding factors related to treatment effects; however, the conclusion that ivabradine therapy was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause rehospitalization in the overall cohort remains valid. Our subgroup analysis demonstrated that patients with heart rate > 70 bpm, Killip classification > 1, or more than one diseased vessel could benefit from ivabradine. This indicates that patients with more serious cardiac injury were the primary beneficiaries. These findings are similar to those from a heart failure study where inpatients with a lower baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) showed a greater increase in heart rate after ivabradine treatment compared to outpatients19.

Ivabradine is a highly selective If inhibitor that suppresses the sinus node rhythm and thereby slows the heart rate. Importantly, it reduces the heart rate without negatively affecting the atrioventricular conduction time or myocardial contractility11,12. In addition to its rate-lowering effect, many studies have reported that ivabradine has other potential roles in heart disease. For example, a randomized controlled study demonstrated that ivabradine can improve left ventricular systolic function and cardiac remodeling23. Furthermore, studies in animal models have shown that ivabradine can improve cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction24–26. In our study, while ivabradine did not significantly improve the attainment of the target heart rate at first discharge, patients who achieved the target had a lower rehospitalization risk. For those who did not reach the target, ivabradine may still reduce the risk of rehospitalization, suggesting potential non-heart rate-dependent mechanisms like improving myocardial fibrosis and reducing oxidative stress27.

Our study had several limitations. First, as a real-world, single-center prospective cohort study with a relatively small sample size, our results may have limited external validity and statistical power. Second, the initial cohort of 612 eligible patients was reduced to 408 due to non-consent, withdrawals, and missing follow-up data, which may have introduced selection bias. Third, patients in the ivabradine group had higher baseline risks since ivabradine was primarily used in high-risk populations with inadequate response or intolerance to beta-blockers. Fourth, while known confounders were controlled for using multivariable Cox models and PSM, residual confounding from clinical decision bias and unmeasured variables remains a possibility. Fifth, the narrow caliper width used in PSM may have excluded potential matches, reducing the sample size and statistical power of the matched cohort. Sixth, the MI model included many variables for a dataset of 408 rows, which may have introduced an overfitting risk. Despite the consistency of our results across analytical approaches (the complete cohort, PSM, MSM, and MI), future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate our conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ivabradine therapy during hospitalization was associated with a lower risk of all-cause rehospitalization in patients with AMI. However, the robustness of our findings requires further validation.

Methods

Study population

This prospective, real-world cohort study was conducted at a single academic medical center. We consecutively enrolled 408 patients diagnosed with AMI, including ST-segment and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarctions. These patients were admitted to the 900th Hospital of the PLA Joint Logistics Support Force between January 2018 and December 2020. Of 612 patients who met the study inclusion criteria, 473 consented to participate. Subsequently, 29 withdrew during the study, and 36 patients were excluded due to more than 30% missing follow-up data. Ultimately, the analysis included 408 patients with complete data. Patients were divided into two groups based on ivabradine therapy: the ivabradine group (n = 76) and the ivabradine-free group (n = 332).

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the 900th Hospital of the PLA Joint Logistics Support Force (Approval number: 2018-001). All patients or their legal representatives provided informed consent and voluntarily agreed to participate. The research data are retained by the hospital’s ethics committee.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for this study if they met the following criteria: age 18 to 95 years; sinus rhythm; confirmed AMI diagnosis; paroxysmal atrial fibrillation that reverted to sinus rhythm during hospitalization; and willingness to provide informed consent and actively participate. We excluded patients with: persistent atrial fibrillation; other ectopic rhythms; irreversible atrioventricular block; sinus pauses of at least 3.0 s; and a permanent pacemaker or likelihood of requiring pacemaker implantation. Additionally, contraindications to antithrombotic therapy, systemic diseases (e.g., hematological disorders or malignancies with a life expectancy of under one year), general weakness, severe underweight (BMI < 16 kg/m2), and refusal to participate in clinical intervention or follow-up were also grounds for exclusion.

Data collection

We recorded patient demographics (sex, age, smoking, blood pressure, heart rate, body mass index [BMI]), infarction site, culprit vessel, number of diseased vessels, Killip classification, hypertension, diabetes, and other comorbidities. Additionally, we documented laboratory data at baseline and during follow-up, which included hemoglobin, platelet count, creatinine, urea nitrogen, electrolytes, high-sensitivity troponin I/T, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), electrocardiography, coronary angiography, and PCI status.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of rehospitalization. Secondary endpoints included: all-cause mortality, LOIH, occurrence of heart failure, cumulative rehospitalization, the percentage of patients achieving the target heart rate of 55 to 70 bpm at first discharge, and the time to first rehospitalization.

All-cause rehospitalization was defined as admission to any hospital for any reason after discharge. To ascertain rehospitalization events, we retrieved data from our institution’s hospitalization information system and conducted in-person interviews during follow-up. For patients unable to attend in-person follow-up, telephone inquiries were made. Due to data collection limitations, we could not distinguish between cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular causes of rehospitalization.

Treatment protocol and follow-Up

All patients received treatment according to the 2016 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Myocardial Infarction28. This included dual antiplatelet therapy, statins, RAAS inhibitors (RAASi), β-blockers (β-Bs), and anticoagulant and Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPI) therapy as necessary. Nitrate drugs were used as appropriate based on the patient’s clinical condition.

Patients in the ivabradine-free group were treated according to the guideline-recommended protocol without ivabradine during hospitalization.

Patients with sinus rhythm and a heart rate ≥ 70 bpm during hospitalization were eligible for inclusion in the ivabradine group. They received ivabradine if they had experienced angina refractory to standard treatment or had a high risk of developing, or had already developed, acute heart failure. This was particularly relevant when β-blockers were contraindicated or could not be up-titrated in the short term. Two experienced cardiologists jointly assessed these patients. With their or their legal representatives’ consent, ivabradine (ivabradine hydrochloride tablets from Les Laboratoires Servier Industrie, H20150217) was prescribed at a dose of 2.5–7.5 mg BID, aiming to achieve a heart rate of 55–70 bpm.

Patients were followed up 1-, 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-discharge, with repeated measurements of laboratory parameters and echocardiography as clinically indicated. If rehospitalization occurred during follow-up, an immediate visit was arranged to document the event.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Empower (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and R software (version 3.4.3). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed or median with interquartile range when non-normal. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro - Wilk test, and normally distributed variables were compared by Student’s t-test while non-normal variables were compared by the Mann - Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. Groups were compared for categorical variables using the χ² test. Time to first all-cause rehospitalization was estimated using the Kaplan - Meier method, with group comparisons performed using the univariate log-rank test.

Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses were used to investigate the association between ivabradine use and the risk of rehospitalization. Covariates were included in the multivariable model if their addition to or removal from the model induced a change greater than 10% in the regression coefficient for ivabradine. Results from both unadjusted and fully adjusted models are reported. The unadjusted model did not include covariates and Model I was adjusted for patient age in years and sex. Model II was further adjusted for baseline heart rate, Killip classification, LOIH and spironolactone use. All covariates were selected a priori on the basis of clinical relevance.

The multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted for age, sex, heart rate, Killip classification, LOIH, and spironolactone. Variables such as β-Bs use, dual antiplatelet therapy, and revascularization status were not included in the Cox regression model. This decision was based on a pre-analysis that showed these variables were evenly distributed between groups (i.e., no significant differences) and had an impact of less than 10% on the regression coefficient of ivabradine.

Subgroup analyses were performed using stratified Cox proportional hazards models, and interactions between subgroups were assessed using likelihood ratio tests.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis employed three approaches to evaluate the primary results.

To minimize potential bias, the outcomes were compared after performing PSM. One-to-one matching (ivabradine vs. ivabradine-free) without replacement was performed using the nearest neighbor match on the PSM logit. This logit was derived from variables including sex, age, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, BMI, heart rate, LOIH, Killip classification, site of myocardial infarction, culprit vessel, number of diseased vessels, CKD, and spironolactone, and clopidogrel use (all P < 0.05 in Table 1). The caliper width was set to 0.02 times the standard deviation of the PSM logit.

We also utilized MI to maximize statistical power and minimize bias that may arise from excluding confounders with missing data. MI was based on five replications and employed the Markov chain Monte Carlo method in R to address missing data on the following variables: BMI, site of myocardial infarction, culprit vessel, number of diseased vessels, heart rate at discharge, LOIH, and β-Bs, RAASi, spironolactone, clopidogrel, and ticagrelor use. The MI cohort was used for both sensitivity analysis and a complete-case analysis.

We performed a multi-state model (MSM) analysis to characterize the dynamic progression from hospital discharge to rehospitalization. We defined two states, post-discharge and rehospitalization, and estimated transition hazards by fitting separate Cox proportional hazards models for each transition. Each model included adjustment for baseline age, sex, resting heart rate, Killip classification, LOIH, and spironolactone. We computed state-occupation probabilities using the Aalen-Johansen estimator and verified the proportional hazards assumption by examining Schoenfeld residuals. All analyses were conducted in R software (version 3.4.3) with the mstate package, and robust standard errors were applied to address potential informative censoring. This multi-state framework enabled us to capture the temporal complexity of patient trajectories and to quantify how ivabradine administration alters both the timing and likelihood of rehospitalization.
