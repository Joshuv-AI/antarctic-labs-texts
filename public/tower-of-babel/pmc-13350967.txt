AI detection in Italian essays through different text representations and adversarial robustness evaluation
Authors: Andrea Sciandra, Francesco Dal Cero, Michele A. Cortelazzo, Arjuna Tuzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effectiveness of various text representation methods in distinguishing between AI-generated and human-written content, using a corpus of 1000 Italian essays. Four techniques were employed for text representation: Text Features, Most Frequent Words (MFWs), Correspondence Analysis (CA), and a fine-tuned Large Language Model (LLM). Machine learning models, including Random Forests, Elastic-net, and Support Vector Machine, were applied to these representations. The study achieved high classification accuracy, with Text Features performing exceptionally well. However, adversarial tactics revealed vulnerabilities in models based on Text Features and MFWs, while approaches based on CA and LLM demonstrated greater resilience. CA in particular proved to be the best compromise between parsimony in the number of predictors, accuracy and robustness to targeted text-modification attacks. The research also focused on the explainability of the results, highlighting significant differences between AI- and human-written texts, including sentence structure and lexical choices. Ethical considerations, particularly in educational settings, were emphasized, along with the need for further cross-linguistic studies and diverse domain datasets. This research provides valuable insights into the challenges and potential solutions in AI text detection, emphasising the importance of balancing accuracy with interpretability and robustness against adversarial attacks.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has facilitated the generation of text that closely mimics human writing in terms of fluency and coherence1. LLMs have exhibited an unprecedented capacity to generate text that is nearly indistinguishable from human-written content. This poses a significant challenge in distinguishing between human and AI-generated content, which is of paramount importance in fields such as academia, journalism, product reviews, and content moderation2,3. Indeed, the potential risks connected with the unethical misuse of AI encompass plagiarism, the fabrication of deceptive product reviews, fake news and the dissemination of misinformation, all of which can subsequently affect public opinion and political discourse2–4.

The traditional approach to authorship attribution, which has been analysing stylistic patterns of human authors for decades, has now been expanded to include AI detection. This requires the use of advanced computational techniques to identify subtle differences in linguistic features5. Contemporary AI detection methods frequently employ machine learning (ML) and deep learning, focusing on characteristics such as lexical diversity, and statistical patterns of machine-generated content. The key features that differentiate human-generated text from AI-generated text include perplexity, grammatical error rates, and n-gram distributions. Human texts typically exhibit higher perplexity and greater linguistic diversity6. Neural network classifiers that have been trained using large datasets comprising both human-authored and AI-generated samples have proven particularly effective at identifying markers such as the overuse of common phrases or atypical token probability distributions. Recent studies have demonstrated that neural network-based detectors may outperform metric-based methods in terms of both human authorship attribution and LLM-generated text detection5. However, these approaches often lack explainability compared to their metric-based counterparts. Furthermore, ensemble methods and hybrid models combining stylometry7 with pre-trained language models like RoBERTa have shown promise in detecting sophisticated AI-generated outputs8. The use of watermarking techniques also represents a significant area of research in this context9. Nevertheless, these techniques have been shown to be vulnerable to paraphrasing-based attacks10.

A new challenge is detecting human-AI collaborative writing, as the reliability of AI detection tools in this scenario is questionable11,12. In particular, even the most sophisticated detectors are susceptible to a significant reduction in accuracy when presented with simple paraphrases of AI-generated text13. Furthermore, as LLMs become increasingly sophisticated at mimicking human writing styles in different domains, detection tools will encounter challenges in identifying anomalies or inconsistencies. Currently, there is a risk of false positives, whereby human-written text is incorrectly identified as AI-generated14. This can result in unfair accusations of cheating or plagiarism. The potential for bias in detection algorithms raises ethical concerns and highlights the societal implications of such technologies. Additionally, the lack of ethical standards and regulations governing the creation and use of AI-generated texts poses a significant challenge to their monitoring and control15.

These limitations stress the necessity for caution when relying solely on AI detection tools, especially in academic and professional contexts where false accusations can have significant consequences2. As the use of human-AI collaborative writing becomes more prevalent, existing AI detection and authorship analysis techniques will require adaptation in order to address the aforementioned challenges effectively1. Therefore, it is imperative that the fields of linguistics, computer science, statistics, law, and ethics converge in order to ensure the fairness and accuracy of AI detection practices.

The aim of this paper is to contribute to the growing body of research on AI text detection by proposing an authorship attribution framework that leverages recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and ML. Our approach seeks to address the current limitations of existing methods while improving the accuracy and generalisability of the detection process, paying attention to the interpretability of the results by domain experts16.

The use of CA derives primarily from Lebart’s19 theoretical analysis, which described this technique as equivalent to both supervised and unsupervised multilayer perceptrons. Furthermore, Lebart20 pointed out that the CA coordinates can aid decision-making, demonstrating the complementarity between exploratory techniques21 and a ML approach. In addition, the use of CA in the background of authorship attribution was already tested in the literary domain (novels22, achieving outperforming classification results compared to other types of text mapping23.

Methods

Corpus and features

For this study, we created an in-house dataset. The corpus comprises 1,000 essays on various topics, 500 of which were written by Italian university students who later asked ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 version) to write essays from the same title.

Participation in the survey was voluntary and involved only adult students who freely agreed to contribute to the research. Although optional, this task was an integral part of the students’ teaching programme, and the preliminary results were provided as feedback during subsequent lectures. Participants uploaded their essays anonymously to a teaching platform and were informed that the submitted texts would be processed in compliance with privacy regulations. Therefore, the researchers involved have no way of identifying the authors of the essays used in the study. No personal or confidential information about the participants was used during the research. The research was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR - EU Regulation 2016/679). We shared a detailed protocol with the participating students, explicitly stating that uploading files constituted informed consent to participate in the research. The University of Padua’s FISPPA Department Research Ethics Committee (CERD) granted ethical approval for the described procedures.

Graduates wrote 16% of the essays, while undergraduates wrote the rest. The students were enrolled on ten different degree courses spread over four different Italian universities. The students were given a total of 230 different titles. In particular, each student was given a title and had to return two 400-word written essays (minimum 400, maximum 440 words), one of which was written by himself/herself and one written by ChatGPT. Each student was given an example of a prompt to submit to ChatGPT and further examples of prompts to the chatbot in case the delivery was not adhered to (e.g. compared to the expected length). Specifically, we used a role-prompting technique, because defining a role helps to tailor language models to a specific area of expertise or viewpoint24. This kind of prompting is therefore suitable for guiding the model’s tone and specificity. The initial text provided was as follows: ‘I would like you to write an essay in the style of a 20-year-old Italian university student. The essay must be at least 400 words long (minimum 400, maximum 440 words). The topic of the essay is as follows: < Title>’.

The texts were collected from January to May 2024, with some additional texts collected by September 2024. The pre-processing of the corpus included converting all words to lowercase and removing punctuation and numbers.

These features have been used in several AI detection studies8,28,29, alongside pre-trained Transformers30, as well as combinations of lexical, stylometric and statistical features. In particular, the perplexity of a linguistic model with regard to a set of words is given by the inverse of the probability of this set, normalised by the number of words. Texts written by humans tend to have higher perplexity scores, as they are less predictable than texts generated by AI, which usually have lower perplexity scores. Specifically, the calculation procedure is based on n-grams and the Kneser-Ney smoothing method. Burstiness, on the other hand, measures the statistical and linguistic unpredictability of content and is calculated by the ratio of the standard deviation of token frequencies to their average frequency. Finally, Yule’s index is a lexical diversity measure that aims to assess the richness of a text’s vocabulary and is often used for authorship attribution tasks. The Yule’s index is defined by the first and second moments of the vocabulary distribution.

The second method involves the extraction of the 100 MFWs belonging to function words, also known as ‘stopwords’ in the NLP context. In this research, we opted for an ad hoc list of Italian stopwords in order to avoid revealing the topics discussed in the essays. This type of features has a long tradition in authorship attribution31, and allowed us to focus exclusively on stylistic traits, making the model generalisable across different contexts.

The third method involves extracting dimensions from CA18 and applying them to the training set (80%) of the text corpus. Initially, the dimensionality of the Term-Document Matrix (TDM) was reduced by selecting only terms with at least 70 occurrences (830 terms). Therefore, we started with a (800 × 830) TDM with (relative) frequencies as weights. It is important to emphasise that the test set texts were not used to determine the CA axes. Indeed, test set coordinates were extracted as supplementary data, projected into the system of dimensions generated by the CA based on the training set. This operation was aimed at guaranteeing in the CA the independence of the training set from the test set, so that the latter would consist of completely new texts (out-of-sample) for the dimensionality reduction procedure. After calculating the chi-square distance and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), each text (word) becomes a column (row) vector in the 799-dimensional space of CA orthogonal axes. Regarding the number of axes used, we chose to include a limited number of 15, corresponding to approximately 20% of the inertia (18.68% of the explained variance). As a second set of axes, we opted for a much higher number, 150, corresponding to 67.32% of the inertia.

The last method of text representation is based on a Large Language Model, namely BERT-base-Italian-uncased, which was fine-tuned directly. It was therefore decided to optimise the LLM by performing additional training on the texts included in the training set. Once this phase was completed, the author prediction was performed on the data belonging to the test set. During this process, repeated cross-validations were carried out, depending on the number of training epochs selected. Identifying the optimal number of epochs is crucial in order to avoid overfitting and underfitting, and to achieve the desired balance. In our case, we decided to fine-tune the training process by varying the number of epochs from 1 to 5. The best level of accuracy was achieved with 5 epochs. We chose BERT32 firstly because an Italian version of this LLM is available and secondly because, unlike GPT, it provides a bidirectional text representation that could be of higher quality28. In previous tests on a subset of the corpus, we compared the results of fine-tuning with a machine learning procedure using contextual embeddings derived from a LLM and we achieved very similar results with a slight difference in favour of fine-tuning. For this reason, the analyses in this paper only refer to the LLM optimised for prediction by fine-tuning.

Machine learning procedures and analysis of the results

Since we are dealing with a binary classification problem, the models were trained on the training set data and, applying them subsequently to the test set, we calculated the probability that each text was written by one of the two authors (in this case ‘GPT’, referring to AI texts, was set as the positive reference class). In terms of ML-based classification methods, we selected three models:

Our idea was to provide informative validations using different techniques, including bagging, non-linear models, and a parsimonious model with the aim of improving generalisation and potentially reducing overfitting. The training set was created by randomly selecting 80% of the essays, while the remaining essays were used for the test set. Each statistical model underwent a 5-fold cross-validation36, repeated 15 times (repeated cv). The comparison for each combination of representation type, language model and training control was assessed by analysing the accuracy, the unweighted Kappa statistic, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and the number of misclassifications (Table 1). The final evaluation of the competing models and text representation methods refers exclusively to the results achieved in the test set. This is to ensure that the models do not simply recognise the text collection used in the training phase.

Next, we performed a conformal analysis37 of the predictions produced by the SVM-RBFK model, which showed the best results with different feature sets. The specific aim was to calculate p-values to assess the confidence of the classification predictions, particularly in cases where the probability was uncertain. For this purpose, we used 70% of the data for the training set, 10% for the validation set and the remaining 20% for the test set. The procedure consists of five main steps. First, a hierarchical tree is developed based on all possible label sets obtained from the two labels ‘GPT’ and ‘HUM’. Second, a hypothesis is formulated for each node in the tree. Since there are multiple nodes on the tree, this is a multiple hypothesis-testing problem with a hierarchical structure. Third, conformal p-values are calculated for each tested hypothesis using the split-conformal prediction method and any machine learning algorithm (such as SVMs). Next, two types of hierarchical testing procedures based on the conformal p-values are developed to check the first type’s error rate (False Positives). Finally, the prediction set is formed from the test results of the previous step by exploiting split-conformal prediction.

SHAP analysis38 allows individual predictions made by machine learning models to be explained on the basis of SHAP values from collaborative game theory. A novelty introduced by SHAP values is that they can be represented as an additive method of feature attribution, similar to a linear model, facilitating interpretation39.

First, it was necessary to decompose the importance of the text features within one of the best-performing models in the classification phase (e.g. RF), even if predictions using a reduced set of features were analysed for computational reasons. Accordingly, a RF model was created based only on the five most important predictors according to a ROC curve analysis. Next, SHAP values were calculated to interpret the prediction output. The function requires specifying an average prediction value for the data in the test set, which was set equal to 0.5 as the dataset is balanced. This quantity indicates the average expected value for the response variable in the absence of predictors. In terms of interpretation, a prediction with a probability greater than or equal to 0.5 is attributed to ‘GPT’ and, in all other cases, to ‘HUM’, as in previous analyses. Additionally, a second reduced model was created based exclusively on the five most relevant MFWs, as determined by the ROC curve analysis. Predictions and SHAP values calculations were carried out similarly to those for Text Features.

Finally, we calculated SHAP values for individual portions of a text in order to explain the predictions provided by the LLM used for classification. This was done by using the LLM to split the text into tokens and using a further model for the classification of text sequences. Using the softmax function, the probabilities of belonging to each class were calculated and the prediction output was generated. Next, SHAP values were used to analyse how the various text portions contributed to the final prediction and determine the importance of each token.

Adversarial tactics

In the last step of the analysis, we wanted to challenge the classification models by making targeted changes to the texts to observe how the probability of authorship changes. For this purpose, a set of ten texts belonging to the GPT class were selected, which were correctly classified with a high degree of certainty. These texts showed a probability of over 90% of being AI-generated. Referring to adversarial tactics, we applied five human-made techniques to modify the texts produced by ChatGPT. These changes were all driven by SHAP analysis results showing the importance of specific tokens, as well as the higher variability and randomness in human writing. We therefore wanted to manually alter ten texts and then apply the same predictive model used for the original texts, to see if and how the response variable (indicating the probability of belonging to the GPT class) associated with them changed. For each type of feature, we then recalculated the probabilities of classifying a text as AI-generated following each modification, highlighting cases where this probability fell below 50%, thus leading to an incorrect prediction due to adversarial attacks.

Results

As this topic has been extensively investigated over the past two years, our results will not only focus on classification accuracy, but also on issues such as the reliability of estimates, the interpretability of results and the capacity to withstand adversarial tactics.

While all text representations perform well, Text Features emerge as the best, correctly classifying all observations in the test set (200) with a very limited number of predictors (10). CA achieves the best result among the other representations, again with a very limited number of dimensions used as features (15). At this stage, one might wonder whether the study ended with this result. There are several reasons to continue with the investigation. Firstly, the Text Features used are limited and can easily be bypassed, as in the case of the number of sentences. Furthermore, the Text Features provide very rough details when comparing human and AI lexicon, limiting interpretability and consequently the work of linguistics experts. Finally, while obtaining high-performance models in terms of accuracy is important, one of the aims of this type of approach is to show the reliability of the resulting estimates.

Assessing the reliability of individual predictions is crucial in ML to assess the feasibility domain of a predictive model, which is defined as the measure of the descriptive space in which a classification model can be reliably applied. Conformal prediction is an algorithm-independent technique, i.e. it works with any predictive method, and provides p-values for classes in the case of classification. SHAP values are then used to analyse how the various text segments contribute to the final prediction and to calculate the importance of each token. The final step of the analysis involved assessing the vulnerability of the models to adversarial attacks. Based on the SHAP analysis results, the following revisions were made to the selected texts:Mod.1 Removal of the conclusione-conclusion token in cases where it occurs.Mod.2 Additions of the token perché-because.Mod.3 Additions of the tokens si-himself/herself/itself and non-not.Mod.4 Merging of separate sentences into a single sentence.Mod.5 Specific revisions in complex parts, use of synonyms and rephrasing of sentences.

The removal of the conclusione-conclusion token to make the texts more human-like was justified by its frequency in AI-generated texts and by the SHAP values analysis. The same applies to the tokens si-himself/herself/itself and non-not, whose high occurrence contributed texts to be classified as written by students. Furthermore, decreasing the number of sentences resulted in less fragmented and more discursive texts, which are typical of human writing (and probably more so in the Italian language). This variation not only affected the number of sentences and tokens per sentence, but also their distribution, since only some of the sentences were modified. Finally, the purpose of the fifth and last modification was to make the texts less recognisable to classifiers by substituting the parts considered most easily identifiable, thus attempting to increase the unpredictability that is usually lacking in artificially generated texts. This adversarial tactic can be achieved by replacing over-refined text chunks or simply by using some synonyms for words that are unlikely to be found in texts written by students. For instance, the sentence produced by ChatGPT, ‘Music is a magical entity that permeates the deepest folds of our human existence’, was modified as follows: ‘Music is a magical entity that reaches the deepest limits of our human existence’. In this way, we particularly tried to address GPT’s limited ability to generate sentences connected through subordinate clauses and cohesive discourse markers. As can be seen from the values in Table 4, the token ‘conclusion’ was initially found in almost all of the selected texts, with an average frequency of slightly less than 1. However, since the text created in each step was modified in the next step, the frequency of this word remained at 0 from the first modification onwards. By contrast, the occurrence of the token ‘because’ in the texts increased in the second step, raising the average frequency by almost four units. Similarly, in the next modification, the tokens ‘himself/herself/itself’ and ‘not’ were added to the texts approximately four and almost five times respectively, significantly increasing their average frequencies. With regard to reducing repetitiveness and predictability, the fourth modification involved increasing the length of sentences, which led to a considerable increase in the variable ‘SD_tok_per_sentence’. However, it should be noted that the previous three modifications also had a slight impact on this predictor, as can be seen from its values in Table 4. Finally, the last modification aimed to increase the perplexity and burstiness values in the texts. While this did happen, if we consider only the transition from the penultimate step to the final one, this does not always occur when we take the values as a whole.

Initially, we chose to rely on the predictions made by Text Features via the SVM-RBFK model. The chosen texts had a probability of belonging to the GPT class ranging from 0.91 to 0.99, reflecting their obvious AI-generated characteristics.

Within this set of adversarial tactics towards the Text Features-based model, the removal or insertion of tokens had a rather limited impact, except in the case of si-himself/herself/itself and non-not. Merging sentences to reduce their number had an important effect on the model’s predicted probabilities and contributes to make texts less recognisable. In half of the analysed cases, this modification caused the model to classify the texts as having been written by students (Fig. 8). The last modification, on the other hand, added uncertainty to the prediction.

In the second case, we opted to use the predictions made by the SVM-RBFK model based on MFWs. The selected texts had a probability of belonging to the GPT category ranging from 0.96 to 0.99. For this set of features, the changes had a stronger effect, since almost all the changes affected the word frequencies. Adding the tokens perché-because, si-himself/herself/itself and non-not makes the texts significantly more human (Fig. 9).

In the third case, we chose to use the predictions provided by the SVM-RBFK model, which employed the CA dimensions as features. The selected texts had a probability of belonging to the GPT class ranging from 0.91 to 0.97. The changes had less pronounced effects in this case, since CA is initially based on the (relative) frequencies of MFWs. For this reason, the change that visibly had the most impact was the third, namely the increase in the frequencies of the si-himself/herself/itself and non-not tokens in texts. No text was misclassified in this case, proving that this technique is much more robust against this type of attack. To sum up, targeted attacks by means of AI-generated text revisions highlighted the poor robustness of ML models that exploit Text Features, and especially those relying on MFWs. In particular, the merging of distinct sentences into a single sentence turned out to be the most effective adversarial technique against the Text Features-based model. As was to be expected, the MFWs-based model, on the other hand, showed a clear decline in its ability to recognise AI-generated texts as early as the second modification (addition of the perché-because token). Conversely, although the CA-based model started from lower accuracy levels, it was much less sensitive to targeted attacks, managing to perform no misclassification for this limited set of modified AI-generated texts. Combined with the initial classification results and the evaluation of the parsimony of the models in terms of the predictors used, this result should make us reflect on the ability of these text representations to detect human–AI collaboration.

The general feeling resulting from the application of AI detection by means of ML is that the classifiers seem to ‘learn too well’ from the case under consideration. Indeed, with respect to Text Features and MFWs, the estimates are extremely accurate after the training phase, but lack flexibility following targeted attacks and, most likely, with respect to different domains (see the Discussion section).

Discussion

The findings achieved in a ML framework with different text representation types were very high in terms of classification accuracy and are consistent with those of other studies with a similar setting40,14. Regardless of the results, when the ML approach is compared with text feature mapping or MFWs with an LLM fine-tuning procedure, the latter is clearly a black box41. In the context of AI detection, it has been suggested that although supervised classification leads to relatively better results, these models are often susceptible to overfitting41. The Text Features we selected obviously favour interpretability because they can easily be understood by researchers40, which represents an added value for those working in interdisciplinary contexts16. In our analysis, models exploiting this feature set achieved 100% accuracy in the test set, but proved to be very weak to adversarial techniques. These attacks were extremely targeted, as they were based on the results of the SHAP analysis. Although the attacks were applied to a very limited set of essays, it should be noted that humans are certainly better at paraphrasing and evading AI detectors than machine-based paraphrasers9. In particular, Kirchenbauer et al.42 demonstrated that this type of attack could be reliably detected from 400 tokens onwards, which is the minimum length of our texts. In general, adversarial techniques reduce accuracy10 even to percentages close to 20%2, and this applies to all text-based classifications, including those that exploit LLMs41. In a specific case such as essays in education, knowledge of input prompts could potentially facilitate better classification by learning the distribution (probability) of terms used in AI-generated texts9.

In our work, the use of CA appeared to offer significant advantages from several perspectives. Firstly, we achieved the best classification with only 15 dimensions used as predictors (excluding the Text Features), which was superior even to the fine-tuning of the Italian LLM based on 768 dimensions. Thus, in terms of computational efficiency and parsimony of predictors, CA is preferable to other approaches. Secondly, CA was not seriously affected by adversarial tactics and was able to correctly classify the modified texts. It should also be noted that, although coordinates are used in this framework, it is still possible within a CA to highlight which terms contribute most to the generation of each axis. This is the main innovation of our work: we have applied a well-known dimensionality reduction technique to textual data in the context of authorship attribution and AI detection. This produced excellent classification results, even in the face of targeted text editing attacks. Another relevant issue in this type of analysis concerns the quantification of classification uncertainty. Using conformal prediction alongside accuracy measures in ML models enables informed decisions to be made, even in the most uncertain cases.

Despite its excellent performance, this approach exhibits some limitations. Firstly, it is challenging to generalise the results for different domains and languages41. With regard to the former issue, it has been demonstrated that this ML framework can be adapted for literary works, particularly by employing CA as a text mapping technique23. However, we agree that datasets including examples from different domains within the same language need to be created43. In this regard, we created an in-house Italian dataset, which may make this research appear very specific. Nevertheless, as a future development of this work, we would like to test these procedures for English and other languages. This is because it may reveal cross-linguistic patterns28. Furthermore, we actually want to verify findings from language comparisons, such as the idea that AI-generated content is more easily detected in English due to the greater availability and quality of LLMs in this language8. Overall, we believe that the problem of authorship attribution can be seen as independent of language and data type44. In addition to language, the use of only one LLM such as GPT may also limit the generalisability of our results, even if an approach such as ours has become widespread11,12,14,40,45. While complete data on all LLM-based chatbots is unavailable, it should be noted that ChatGPT is used by over 700 million people — equivalent to around 10% of the global population — and appears to far exceed competing chatbots46. Therefore, while our analysis cannot be extended to other chatbots in this study, the results would have a very broad impact in terms of users.

A further methodological limitation is the fact that the tests on adversarial techniques were limited to a small number of examples. While it is clear that these manually crafted tasks are time-consuming, given what was written earlier about their superiority for testing AI detection procedures, it is evident that several examples of this nature will always be necessary in the future.

A major challenge concerns the interpretability of the results. Through the SHAP values approach, we demonstrated that the contribution of each variable to the classification could be visualised. This result is clearly achievable for words or textual features, as well as for LLMs (highlighting specific text chunks related to the two classes) and, indirectly, for CA (highlighting which terms contribute most to the generation of each axis). Furthermore, the use of SHAP values proved to be of considerable importance for targeted attacks, steering the choice of adversarial tactics. Combining this information with human intervention in the texts to rephrase some sentences allowed us to highlight the strengths and weaknesses of different representations of the texts within a ML framework.

One final aspect that has not yet been discussed concerns errors (misclassifications): in the few cases that we detected, human texts were more frequently mistaken for AI-generated than the opposite. This finding is consistent with other research8,13 and raises important ethical questions. When using an AI detection system, especially in education, these issues should be considered, not necessarily for punitive purposes, particularly when inclusivity towards non-native speakers is a concern2. Furthermore, it was found that the use of AI tools is not inherently unethical; rather, the focus of the discussion should be on fostering a deeper understanding of the limitations and advantages of AI. In particular, educational institutions should teach more about the ethical and transparent use of AI because it is already influencing our society and many professions13.
