Slit-slide-sew bijections for oriented planar maps
Jérémie Bettinelli, Éric Fusy, Baptiste Louf

Abstract
We construct growth bijections for bipolar oriented planar maps and for Schnyder woods. These give direct combinatorial proofs of several counting identities for these objects. Our method mainly uses two ingredients. First, a slit-slide-sew operation, which consists in slightly sliding a map along a well-chosen path. Second, the study of the orbits of natural rerooting operations on the considered classes of oriented maps.



1
Introduction

1.1
Growth bijections for trees and maps

The main theme of this paper is that of
growth bijections
for combinatorial structures: in simple words, given a combinatorial family
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
indexed by a size parameter
n
, a growth bijection is a procedure that bijectively constructs all the objects of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
from those of
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
, using only a slight modification. For this to be possible, the objects of the smaller size (
n-1
) bear some additional markings, and those of the larger size (
n
) also bear different additional markings. Such growth bijections are in general inspired by pre-existing simple recursive counting identities linking the cardinalities
|\mathcal{F}_{n}|
and
|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}|
.

A famous instance of a growth bijection is
Rémy’s bijection

[
Rém85
]
(see also
[
Rod38
]
), which provides a proof of the identity

(n+1)\,\mathrm{Cat}_{n}=2\,(2n-1)\,\mathrm{Cat}_{n-1},

(1)

where
\mathrm{Cat}_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}
is the
n
-th Catalan number, which, in particular, counts binary trees on
n+1
leaves (and thus with
2n+1
edges). As a result, this identity (
1
) can be interpreted as an equinumerosity between the sets of

\diamond

binary trees on
n+1
leaves with a marked leaf;

\diamond

binary trees on
n
leaves with a marked edge, as well as a parameter either
\mathrm{left}
or
\mathrm{right}
.

Consequently, there exists a bijection between these two sets and the idea is to find a simple constructive one, in the sense that the modification needed to pass from one set to the other one is as minimal as possible. In this example, the bijection is quite straightforward to find and we invite the reader unknowing of it to find it as a warm-up exercise.

For plane forests and planar maps, a family of growth bijections relying on
slit-slide-sew
operations has been introduced by the first author
[
Bet14
,
Bet20
,
BK26
]
. Such an operation also appears (under the name “cut-and-slide”) in a work by the third author
[
Lou19
]
that extends Rémy’s bijection to all planar maps, and in a work of Schabanel
[
Sch25
]
on bipartite maps via a bijection of Schaeffer
[
Sch97
]
. We must also mention that growth bijections have inspired
probabilistic growth schemes
both for trees and planar maps
[
LW04
,
AB14
,
Fle24
]
.

1.2
Families of oriented maps

In the present paper, we will focus on three specific classes of oriented planar maps, namely:

\diamond

bipolar oriented quasi-triangulations;

\diamond

bipolar oriented maps;

\diamond

Schnyder woods.

We will show that slit-side-sew bijections can be successfully applied to these families, yielding a simple proof to a counting formula in each case (Propositions
1
,
2
and
3
). However, contrary to previous bijections of this type, an additional ingredient is needed here: we will have to compute the probability that a given edge possesses a certain “good” property. This will be done bijectively by averaging over orbits of a natural rerooting operation.

Bipolar oriented maps.

The considered maps are all specific classes of bipolar oriented maps; for Schnyder woods it reduces to such a class via a known easy bijection. First, a
planar map
is an embedding of a finite connected graph (possibly with multiple edges and loops) into the sphere, considered up to orientation-preserving homeomorphisms. Then a
bipolar oriented map
is a planar map whose edges are all oriented as follows. One oriented edge is distinguished and called the
root edge
. Its tail and head are respectively called the
South Pole
and
North Pole
, and denoted by
\mathrm{S}
and
\mathrm{N}
. The South Pole is a
source
, which means that all the edges incident to it are
outgoing
, that is, oriented away from it; the North Pole is a
sink
, which means that all the edges incident to it are
incoming
, that is, oriented toward it; every non-pole vertex is neither a source nor a sink, that is, is incident to at least one incoming and one outgoing edge. Finally, there are no directed cycles. See Figure
1
.

The
external face
is taken as the face on the left of the root edge. In figures, we will always draw the external face as the unbounded component of the plane, in white; we will use a yellowish coloring for internal faces. The vertices incident to the external face make up the
boundary
of the map and are called
external vertices
. The other vertices are called
internal vertices
.

Figure 1:
Left.
A bipolar oriented map. The poles are in purple; the root is in red; the internal vertices are in green.
Right.
A bipolar oriented quasi-triangulation: all the internal faces have degree
3
.

Bipolar oriented quasi-triangulations.

A
quasi-triangulation
is a rooted map where the external face has simple contour, of any degree larger than or equal to
2
, and the internal faces all have degree
3
. A
bipolar oriented quasi-triangulation
is a bipolar map whose underlying map is a quasi-triangulation.

Local rules.

It is well known (see e.g.
[
dFdMR95
]
) that a bipolar oriented map satisfies the following local properties, illustrated in Figure
2
.

\diamond

Every non-pole vertex has its incident edges partitioned into a nonempty group of consecutive incoming edges and a nonempty group of consecutive outgoing edges.

\diamond

Every internal face has its contour partitioned into two directed paths sharing the same extremities: a left lateral path (having the face on its right) and a right lateral path (having the face on its left). The
left length
(resp.
right length
) of an internal face is the length of its left (resp. right) lateral path.

\diamond

The path formed by the right outer boundary is a directed path from the source to the sink.

Moreover these local properties easily guarantee acyclicity, hence actually characterize bipolar oriented maps.

Figure 2:
The local rules around the vertices and faces of a bipolar oriented map. Here, the right length of the face is
2
.

Schnyder woods.

We consider a triangulation
\mathbf{t}
, that is, a planar map whose faces are all of degree
3
, with a distinguished face, drawn as the unbounded face in the plane. We denote by
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
,
\rho_{3}
the incident vertices, in clockwise order. A
Schnyder wood

[
Sch89
]
of
\mathbf{t}
is a partition of its internal edges into three trees
\mathbf{t}_{1}
,
\mathbf{t}_{2}
,
\mathbf{t}_{3}
satisfying the following two conditions. See Figure
3
.

(1)

For every
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the tree
\mathbf{t}_{i}
spans all the internal vertices and the external vertex
\rho_{i}
, which is its root.

(2)

If we orient the trees toward their roots, one has the local rule depicted on the right side of Figure
3
around each internal vertex, namely: in clockwise direction, one always sees the outgoing edge of
\mathbf{t}_{1}
, the incoming edges of
\mathbf{t}_{3}
, the outgoing edge of
\mathbf{t}_{2}
, the incoming edges of
\mathbf{t}_{1}
, the outgoing edge of
\mathbf{t}_{3}
, and finally the incoming edges of
\mathbf{t}_{2}
. For each tree, the number of incoming edges might be null.

Figure 3:

A Schnyder wood on
9
vertices. The edges of three trees are oriented toward the respective tree roots.
Right.
The local rule around an internal vertex.

It is well known
[
FPS09
]
that Schnyder woods are in bijection with bipolar oriented maps such that every internal face has right length
2
. The bijection is recalled and illustrated in Figure

.

Figure 4:
Left.
To obtain the bipolar oriented map from the Schnyder wood, remove the edges of
\mathbf{t}_{1}

\rho_{1}

\rho_{1}
, revert the edges of
\mathbf{t}_{2}
, set
\mathrm{S}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=\rho_{2}
,
\mathrm{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=\rho_{3}
, and the root as the remaining external edge, oriented from
\mathrm{S}
to
\mathrm{N}
. Observe that the degree of
\rho_{1}
becomes the degree of the external face.

The internal vertices of the Schnyder wood naturally correspond to the faces of the bipolar oriented map and these all have right length
2
.

1.3
Enumeration

Counting formulas.

We consider the following counting coefficients:

\diamond

the number
T_{k,j}
of bipolar oriented
quasi-triangulations
with
k
internal vertices and an external face of degree
j
, for any
k\geq 0
,
j\geq 2
;

\diamond

the number
B_{k,\ell,j}
of bipolar oriented
maps
with
k
internal vertices,
\ell
internal faces, and an external face of degree
j
, for any
k\geq 0
,
\ell\geq 1
,
j\geq 2
;

\diamond

the number
S_{k,j}
of Schnyder woods on
k+j+1
vertices and such that
\rho_{1}
has degree
j
, for any
k\geq 0
,
j\geq 3
. Equivalently, by the bijection of Figure
4
,
S_{k,j}
is the number of bipolar oriented maps with
k
internal vertices, an external face of degree
j
, and such that all internal faces have right length
2
.

Note in particular that
B_{k,\ell,2}
is the the number of bipolar oriented maps with
k+2
vertices
and
\ell
faces (by merging the left boundary of length
1
to the root edge), and that
S_{k,3}
is the number of Schnyder woods on
k+3
vertices (by removing the degree
3
external vertex
\rho_{1}
and its incident edges, and then taking the incident internal vertex as the new vertex
\rho_{1}
).

These coefficients are given by the following explicit formulas:

\displaystyle T_{k,j}

\displaystyle=j\,(j-1)\frac{(3k+2j-4)!}{k!\,(k+j-1)!\,(k+j)!}\,

k\geq 0
,
j\geq 2
;

(2)

\displaystyle B_{k,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=j\,(j-1)\frac{(k+\ell-2)!\,(k+\ell+j-2)!\,(k+\ell+j-3)!}{k!\,(k+j)!\,(k+j-1)!\,\ell!\,(\ell-1)!\,(\ell-2)!}\,

k\geq 0
,
\ell\geq 1
,
j\geq 2
;

(3)

\displaystyle S_{k,j}

\displaystyle=j\,(j-1)\,(j-2)\frac{(2k+2j-4)!\,(2k+j-3)!}{k!\,(k+j)!\,(k+j-1)!\,(k+j-2)!}\,

k\geq 0
,
j\geq 3
.

(4)

The first two formulas are obtained in
[
BM11
]
via a recursive decomposition of bipolar oriented maps and an application of the obstinate kernel method. They can also be obtained from known bijections: in
[
Bor17
,
KMSW19
]
for the first formula, and in
[
AP15
,
FPS09
]
for the second one. The third one can be obtained from the bijection in
[
BB09
]
. See Section
5.2
for more details on how these bijections yield the formulas.

Combinatorial identities.

From the above formulas, we easily obtain the following “growth identities”, for which we will give a bijective interpretation in the present work.

Proposition 1

(Bipolar oriented quasi-triangulations)
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
j\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle\mathllap{k\,T_{k,j}}

\displaystyle=\mathrlap{\left(1-\frac{2}{j+1}\right)\big(3k+2j-4\big)\,T_{k-1,j+1}\,.}\hskip 156.49014pt

(5)

Proposition 2

(Bipolar oriented maps)
.

Let
k\geq 1
,
\ell\geq 1
, and
j\geq 2
. Then the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\mathllap{k\,B_{k,\ell,j}}

\displaystyle=\mathrlap{\left(1-\frac{2}{j+1}\right)\big(k+\ell-2\big)\,B_{k-1,\ell,j+1}\,.}\hskip 156.49014pt

(6)

Proposition 3

(Schnyder woods)
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
j\geq 3
. Then the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\mathllap{k\,S_{k,j}}

\displaystyle=\mathrlap{\left(1-\frac{3}{j+1}\right)\big(2k+j-3\big)\,S_{k-1,j+1}\,.}\hskip 156.49014pt

(7)

Bijective interpretation.

The three combinatorial identities (
5
), (
6
), (
7
) all possess the same structure (seeing the third one as counting bipolar oriented maps with internal faces of right length
2
). The structure is as follows:

\diamond

On the left-hand side, there is the coefficient with parameters
k
and
j
, together with the prefactor
k
, which counts internal vertices.

\diamond

On the right-hand side, there is the coefficient with parameters
k-1
and
j+1
, together with two prefactors:

(
a
)

the first one has the form
\big(1-\frac{\lambda}{j+1}\big)
, where
j+1
is the external face degree;

(
b
)

the second one counts (most) edges.

We will use a unified framework. To fit this framework, we will need to adopt some slightly different points of view depending on the class of maps into consideration. This bears the consequence that the prefactor
(b)
has a slightly different interpretation for each class.
More precisely, using Euler’s characteristic formula, one easily obtains that the maps counted by
B_{k-1,\ell,j+1}
all possess
k+j+\ell-1
edges. Furthermore, in the particular case of quasi-triangulations, one obtains that the maps have
\ell=2k+j-3
internal faces, and thus
3k+2j-4
edges. In the case of bipolar oriented maps corresponding to Schnyder woods, the condition on the right lengths yields that the number of edges is
2\ell+1
, and thus
2k+2j-3
. As a result, the prefactor
(b)
counts

\diamond

all the edges
in (
5
) on quasi-triangulations;

\diamond

all but
j+1
edges, that is,
edges having internal faces on both sides
in (
6
) on general bipolar oriented maps;

\diamond

all but
j
edges, that is,
edges having an internal face on their right
in (
7
) on bipolar oriented maps corresponding to Schnyder woods.

The prefactor
(a)
will be seen as a probability that an edge from the class-specific proper set of edges satisfies an extra property called
boundary-reaching
and whose definition will be given in Section
2.2
. It will be shown that the set of maps we consider can be partitioned into orbits of a rerooting operation, and that the probability given by
(a)
holds over each orbit. This is an instance of the so-called
homomesy
phenomenon: see Remark
1
.

Summing up, in the proper class of bipolar oriented maps, the left-hand side counts maps carrying a distinguished internal vertex and the right-hand side counts maps with the same number of vertices in total but one more on the boundary, carrying a distinguished edge satisfying proper constraints.

1.4
Organization of the paper

The aim of the present paper is to exhibit a bijective interpretation of the identities (
5
), (
6
), (
7
). We start by presenting in Section
2
our bijection and its specializations to the three specific classes of bipolar oriented maps we consider here. In Section
3

(a)
that edges are boundary-reaching.

Since our method provides alternate proofs to the identities (
5
), (
6
), (

.

2
Slit-slide-sew bijection

In this section, we only consider bipolar oriented maps. As a consequence, all the edges are oriented. For an edge
e
, we respectively denote its tail and head by
e^{-}
and
e^{+}
.

Paths.

A
path
from a vertex
v
to a vertex
v^{\prime}
is a finite sequence
\bm{\gamma}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
of edges such that
e_{1}^{-}=v
, for
1\leq i\leq k-1
,
e_{i}^{+}=e_{i+1}^{-}
, and
e_{k}^{+}=v^{\prime}
. Beware that a path is only made of edges oriented in the underlying orientation; they cannot be used “backward”. Also, since we consider here acyclic orientations, paths are
simple
, in the sense that the vertices they visit are all distinct.

Among all the paths from a vertex
v
to the North Pole
\mathrm{N}
, one will be of particular interest. Recall that, around a given non-pole vertex, the edges are partitioned into a consecutive group of incoming edges then a consecutive group of outgoing edges. The
rightmost path
from
v
to
\mathrm{N}
is the path that takes at each vertex the rightmost outgoing edge, that is, the first outgoing edge in counterclockwise order after the group of incoming edges. If
v
is the South Pole
\mathrm{S}
, then the first edge of the path is the one having the external face to its right.

Note that, as soon as the rightmost path
\bm{\gamma}
from a vertex
v
to
\mathrm{N}
reaches the boundary of the map, it stays on it until the Pole
\mathrm{N}
. Thus,
\bm{\gamma}
is made of an
internal part

\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}
, followed (once the boundary is reached) by an
external part
, which is included in the boundary. Clearly, the internal part has length
0
if and only if
v
is external. We call
external index
with respect to
v
the length of the external part.

2.1
The construction, from maps with a distinguished internal vertex

Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of bipolar oriented maps, and
\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}
denote the set of bipolar oriented maps carrying a distinguished internal vertex, that is, the set of pairs
(\mathbf{m},v)
, where
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{B}
and
v
is an internal vertex of
\mathbf{m}
. For such a pair, the
marked face
is defined as the internal face
f
at the right of
v
, that is, the one at the right of the rightmost outgoing edge at
v
. The
external index
, denoted by
\delta
, is the external index with respect to
v
.

Let
(\mathbf{m},v)\in\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}
. We break down the process into the following steps. See Figure
5
(from top left to top right in counterclockwise order).

Figure 5:
The slit-slide-sew bijections
\Phi
,
\Psi
between bipolar oriented maps with a marked internal vertex and bipolar oriented maps with a marked right-internal boundary-reaching edge. The little arrows show at which corner to enter in the slitting step. The marked face is highlighted in red and sees its left length decrease or increase by one.

A. Sliding path

We consider the internal part
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
of the rightmost path from
v
to
\mathrm{N}
. We let
\ell_{0}
be the edge following
e_{1}
in clockwise order around
v
. Since
v
is internal and
e_{1}
is the rightmost outgoing edge of
v
, the edge
\ell_{0}
is incoming at
v
, with the marked face
f
at its right.

B. Slitting, sliding, sewing

We slit along
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}
, entering at
v
from the corner in
f
and exiting through the external face. This doubles the path
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}
, making up two copies:
\bm{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})
to the left, and
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})
to the right.

We then sew back
\bm{\ell}
onto
\mathbf{r}
after
sliding down
the right side by one unit, in the sense that we match
\ell_{i-1}
with
r_{i}
, for every
1\leq i\leq k
. For further reference, we denote by
\ell_{i-1}\,{\Join}\,r_{i}
the resulting edge.

C. Output

In the resulting map
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
, we set
\tilde{e}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=\ell_{0}\,{\Join}\,r_{1}
. The output of the construction is the pair
\Phi(\mathbf{m},v)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{e})
.

Before presenting the reverse bijection, let us first see what properties the output must satisfy.

2.2
Edge constraints

An edge may satisfy the following properties, which are crucial for our purposes.

Boundary-reaching edge.

An edge
e
in a bipolar oriented map
\mathbf{m}
is
boundary-reaching
if the rightmost path from
e^{+}
to the North Pole
\mathrm{N}
reaches the boundary of
\mathbf{m}
before
\mathrm{N}
, that is, if the external index with respect to
e^{+}
is strictly positive. Equivalently, by planarity,
e
is
not
boundary-reaching if all the paths from
e^{+}
to
\mathrm{N}
reach the boundary at
\mathrm{N}
. See Figure
6
.

Figure 6:
The thick blue edge is boundary-reaching whereas the thick green one is not. The rightmost paths from their ends to
\mathrm{N}
reach the boundary at the vertices with corresponding color.

Right-internal edge.

An edge
e
in a bipolar oriented map
\mathbf{m}
is called
right-internal
if the face to its right is an internal face. Note that, except in the trivial case of a map with no internal faces, the root edge is always right-internal. In fact, all the edges are right-internal, except those on the right part of the boundary.

Proposition 4
.

Let
(\mathbf{m},v)\in\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}
, of external index
\delta\geq 0
, and let
\Phi(\mathbf{m},v)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{e})
. Then
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
is a bipolar oriented map, with
\tilde{e}
a right-internal edge. Moreover, the external index of
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
with respect to
\tilde{e}^{+}
is equal to
\delta+1
, so that, in particular,
\tilde{e}
is boundary-reaching.

Proof.

We rely on the characterization of bipolar oriented maps by the local rules given in Section
1.2
. The local condition at any internal faces still holds, the left length and right length being preserved, except for the marked face
f
, whose left length decreases by
1
. Note in passing that
f
is at the right of
\tilde{e}
, so that
\tilde{e}
is right-internal. The local condition for the external boundary also still holds.

The only nonstraightforward property remaining to check for
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
is that the local rules still hold at vertices along the sliding path. Note that the edges to the right of
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}
are directed toward
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}
(by definition of rightmost paths). Hence, for each vertex along
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{i}}
, we replace, between an incoming and an outgoing edge, a (possibly empty) consecutive group of incoming edges with an other (possibly empty) consecutive group of incoming edges.

Besides concluding the proof that
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
is a bipolar oriented map, this property also ensures that the (possibly empty, in the case where
k=1
) path
(\ell_{1}\,{\Join}\,r_{2},\dots,\ell_{k-1}\,{\Join}\,r_{k})
is prefix to the rightmost path from
\tilde{e}^{+}
to
\mathrm{N}
in
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
.
These edges preserve the property of having an internal face on each side, hence they form a path of internal edges in
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
, ending at the origin of
\ell_{k}
, which becomes an external edge after the sewing operation. Hence,
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{i}}:=(\ell_{0}\,{\Join}\,r_{1},\dots,\ell_{k-1}\,{\Join}\,r_{k})
is made of
\tilde{e}
followed by the internal part of the rightmost path from
\tilde{e}^{+}
to
\mathrm{N}
, while the edge
\ell_{k}
has been transferred to its external part. The external index of
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
with respect to
\tilde{e}^{+}
is thus
\delta+1
, as claimed.
∎

3.1
Rerooting operator for bipolar oriented maps

In this section, we focus on (
9
) and (
10
). Since these obviously hold when
j=2
, we will restrict ourselves to the case
j\geq 3
.

Rerooting operator.

For a planar map
\mathbf{m}
and a marked oriented edge
\vec{\rho}=(u,v)
of
\mathbf{m}
, a
bipolar orientation of
\mathbf{m}
rooted at
\vec{\rho}
is an orientation of all the edges of
\mathbf{m}
in such a way that the resulting oriented map is a bipolar oriented map with root edge
\vec{\rho}
. It is known
[
LEC67
]
that
\mathbf{m}
admits a bipolar orientation rooted at
\vec{\rho}
if and only if
\mathbf{m}
is 2-connected, that is, such that the deletion of any single vertex does not disconnect
\mathbf{m}
.

Moreover, for another marked directed edge
\vec{\rho}\,^{\prime}=(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
of
\mathbf{m}
, it is known
[
dFdMR95
]
that the bipolar orientations of
\mathbf{m}
rooted at
\vec{\rho}
are in bijection with the bipolar orientations of
\mathbf{m}
rooted at
\vec{\rho}\,^{\prime}
. Consequently, the number of bipolar orientations of
\mathbf{m}
does not depend on the choice of the marked directed edge. In the special case – the one we will use in the present work – where
u^{\prime}=v
, the bijection, called
rerooting operator
, works as follows: given a bipolar orientation of
\mathbf{m}
rooted at
\vec{\rho}
, an edge is flipped (in the sense that its orientation is reverted) if and only if there does not exist a directed path from it to
v^{\prime}
. See Figure
7
.

Figure 7:
Rerooting operator in the case where the tail of the new root edge is the head of the former root edge. In this context, we represent North poles with red squares and South poles with blue disks. The 4 edges to be flipped are circled in green.

Orbits.

A
rooted map
is a planar map with a marked oriented edge, called the
root edge
, all the other edges not being oriented. As above, the
external face
is the one on the left of the root edge, and is always drawn as the unbounded component of the plane.

For a 2-connected map
\mathbf{m}
with root edge
\vec{\rho}
, and external face degree
j\geq 3
, let
\vec{\rho}=\vec{\rho}_{0}
, …,
\vec{\rho}_{j-1}
be the oriented edges in clockwise order around the external face. We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{i}(\mathbf{m})
the set of bipolar oriented maps on
\mathbf{m}
rooted at
\vec{\rho}_{i}
, and set
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=\bigcup_{i=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{B}_{i}(\mathbf{m})
.

The rerooting operator from the previous paragraph yields a bijection
\sigma
from
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
, specializing into a bijection from
\mathcal{B}_{i}(\mathbf{m})
to
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}(\mathbf{m})
for every
0\leq i<j
. Note that the bijection
\sigma
amounts to flipping the edges that are not boundary-reaching, and take
\vec{\rho}_{i+1}
as the new root edge.

An
orbit
of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
is a cyclic sequence
\mathchar 525
of distinct elements in
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
such that for any pair
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
of successive elements of
\mathchar 525
, one has
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathbf{x})
. See Figure
8
for an example. Note that the length of any orbit has to be a multiple of
j
, since the index
i
of the root edge goes up by
1
(modulo
j
) from one orbit element to the next one.

Figure 8:

Boundary-reaching submap.

Given a bipolar oriented map
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
, we define its
boundary-reaching submap

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
as the set of all boundary-reaching edges of
\mathbf{x}
. It is easy to see that it forms a connected submap that contains all the vertices of the boundary of
\mathbf{x}
except the North pole. Since we assume that
j\geq 3
, the North pole has two neighbors on the boundary of
\mathbf{x}
: the South pole and another vertex
v
. We define the
separating path

P
as the leftmost path that is incoming at
v
and starts from the South Pole, or, equivalently, the unique directed path from
\mathrm{S}
to
v
such that any edge
e
on
P
is the leftmost incoming edge at
e^{+}
. Note that all the edges of
P
are boundary-reaching; moreover by planarity,
P
actually separates
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
from its complement in
\mathbf{x}
, in the sense that the boundary-reaching edges of
\mathbf{x}
are exactly those on
P
or to its right. See Figure
9

One can then rephrase the rerooting operation in terms of these objects: to obtain
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
from
\mathbf{x}
, flip all edges of
\mathbf{x}\setminus\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
and set the new root edge as the one succeeding the original one along the boundary in clockwise order, that is, the one from the original North pole to the tip of the separating path.

Orbit property.

Let us now compute the average probability over a given orbit that a given edge is boundary-reaching.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathbf{m}
be a rooted 2-connected map of external face degree
j\geq 3
. Let
\mathchar 525
be an orbit of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
. Then, for any edge
e
of
\mathbf{m}
, there is a proportion
2/j
of elements in
\mathchar 525
for which
e
is not boundary-reaching.

Proof.

For a fixed orbit
\mathchar 525
, the
multiplicity
of an edge
e
of
\mathbf{m}
is the number of elements in
\mathchar 525
such that
e
is not boundary-reaching, that is, is to be flipped in the transition to the next element of
\mathchar 525
. Letting
a\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=|\text{$\mathchar 525$}|/j
, we thus have to prove that every edge has multiplicity
2a
. The property is very easy to check for edges incident to the external face. Indeed, for
0\leq i<j
, the elements in
\mathchar 525
such that the (nonoriented) edge corresponding to
\vec{\rho}_{i}
is not boundary-reaching are exactly those where the root edge is either
\vec{\rho}_{i-1}
or
\vec{\rho}_{i}
.

We now show that the property propagates step by step to any edge by showing that two edges
e
,
e^{\prime}
consecutive in clockwise order around a vertex
v
have the same multiplicity. This clearly allows to conclude the proof since, for every edge
e
, one can find a sequence of edges starting with an external edge (for which the property is known), ending at
e
, and where two successive edges in the sequence are consecutive around a vertex. We may assume that the corner
c
between
e
and
e^{\prime}
is in an internal face since, otherwise, it means that
e
and
e^{\prime}
are in the external face and we already know that they have same multiplicity.

We introduce a bit of terminology. For any orientation of the edges of
\mathbf{m}
, the corner
c
between
e
and
e^{\prime}
is called
extremal
if
e
and
e^{\prime}
are either both incoming or both outgoing, and is called
lateral
otherwise. We call
special
the elements of
\mathchar 525
for which
e
and
e^{\prime}
have a different status (boundary-reaching or not boundary-reaching). Let
\mathbf{x}\in\text{$\mathchar 525$}
; the following holds.

\diamond

If
\mathbf{x}
is not special, then
e
and
e^{\prime}
both belong to the same submap
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
or
\mathbf{x}\setminus\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
. So, they are either both flipped or both left unchanged by the rerooting operation. As a result,
c
has the same status (lateral or extremal) in
\mathbf{x}
and in
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
.

\diamond

If
\mathbf{x}
is special, then there are two possibilities, shown on the right of Figure
9
.

Top case.

If
e
is not boundary-reaching (and thus
e^{\prime}
is boundary-reaching) in
\mathbf{x}
, then it means that
e
is just on the left of
P
and
e^{\prime}
is on
P
, both
e
and
e^{\prime}
going out of
v
. Then
c
is extremal in
\mathbf{x}
and lateral in
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
.

Bottom case.

If
e
is boundary-reaching (and thus
e^{\prime}
is not boundary-reaching) in
\mathbf{x}
, then it means that
e
is on
P
and
e^{\prime}
is just on the left of
P
(possibly,
e
being the last edge on the right boundary of
\mathbf{m}
), with
e
incoming and
e^{\prime}
outgoing at
v
. Then
c
is lateral in
\mathbf{x}
and extremal in
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
.

Monitoring the status of
c
along the orbit
\mathchar 525
, we see that the special elements in
\mathchar 525
alternate between the top case and the bottom case; the number of these special elements is thus evenly spread among the two cases. This entails that
e
and
e^{\prime}
have the same multiplicity.
∎

Remark 1
.

Lemma
10
is an instance of the
homomesy
phenomenon
[
PR15
]
, which has been actively studied in the context of the rowmotion operator
[
Str15
,
DHPP23
]
.
Namely, for a rooted 2-connected map
\mathbf{m}
of external degree
j
, and for an edge
e
of
\mathbf{m}
, Lemma
10
exactly states that the indicator function for the event that
e
is not boundary-reaching is
\frac{2}{j}
-mesic on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
under the rerooting operator.

As reviewed in
[
PR15
, Section 2.2]
, a well-known example where homomesy explains the prefactor in a counting formula is the cycle lemma for counting positive words – words such that each non-empty prefix has more
1
’s than
0
’s – in
\mathfrak{S}(1^{b}\,0^{a})
(for some fixed
b>a
). These are counted by
\frac{b-a}{a+b}\binom{a+b}{a}
, where the prefactor
\frac{b-a}{a+b}
gives the proportion of positive words in each orbit under the shift operator.
The situation for orbit sizes is rather simple in this case: they are of the form
(a+b)/d
, where
d\,|\,(b-a)\land(a+b)
. In contrast, in our case, simulations show that the orbit sizes in
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{m})
vary a lot, and some are much larger than the number of edges of
\mathbf{m}
.

3.2
Rerooting operator for Schnyder woods

We now turn to (
11
). The overall strategy is the same as in the previous section. We will, however, work directly with Schnyder woods in a first step, considering the rerooting operator on so-called 3-orientations. In a second step, we will return back to bipolar orientations, right after Equation (
12
).

3-orientations and rerooting mapping.

For a Schnyder wood on a triangulation
\mathbf{t}
, we convene that the root edge of
\mathbf{t}
is the external edge
(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
. Similarly to bipolar orientations, the number of Schnyder woods of a simple planar triangulation
\mathbf{t}
does not depend on the choice of the root edge. In order to establish this claim, we use so-called
3-orientations
, that is, orientations of the internal edges such that every internal vertex has outdegree
3
and the
3
external vertices
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
,
\rho_{3}
have outdegree
0
.

It is known
[
Fel04
]
that, for each 3-orientation of
\mathbf{t}
, there is a unique Schnyder wood whose underlying orientation is the given 3-orientation. It is obtained by “propagating the colors” according to the local rule depicted on the right of Figure
3
. More precisely, for any internal edge
e
, one considers the so-called
straight path

P_{e}
of
e
, which is the unique directed path starting at
e
, ending at one of the external vertices
\rho_{i}
and having one outgoing edge on each side at every vertex it passes by; then the color assigned to
e
is that of
\rho_{i}
in the sense that
e\in\mathbf{t}_{i}
. Consequently, once a root edge of
\mathbf{t}
is fixed, its 3-orientations bijectively correspond to its Schnyder woods.

Let
(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
be a directed edge of
\mathbf{t}
, let
\rho_{1}
be the other vertex incident to the face on the left of
(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
, and let
z
be the other vertex incident to the face on the left of
(\rho_{1},\rho_{3})
. See the left column in Figure
10
. There is a simple bijection between the 3-orientations of
\mathbf{t}
rooted at
(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
and those rooted at
(\rho_{3},z)
, which proceeds as follows. We revert the straight path
P
of the unique outgoing edge of
z
not leading to
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{3}
, we discard the orientations of
(z,\rho_{1})
and
(z,\rho_{3})
, and we orient the edges
(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
and
(\rho_{2},\rho_{1})
outward of
\rho_{2}
(note that
P
corresponds to the unique path of
\mathbf{t}_{2}
from
z
to
\rho_{2}
). See the middle column in Figure
10
. The claim follows.

from

Similarly as above, the average probability over a given orbit that a given face is in the right region can be computed.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathbf{q}
be a rooted simple quasi-triangulation of external face degree

\mathchar 525

\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{q})

\mathchar 525

Proof.

For a fixed orbit
\mathchar 525

multiplicity
of an internal face

is the number of elements in
\mathchar 525
such that
f
is in the right region. Letting

\mathbf{q}
has multiplicity

We proceed in two steps similarly as in Lemma

f
adjacent to the external face and then propagating it to any internal face. So let

be incident to an external edge
\vec{\rho}_{i}
(and possibly a second external edge). Precisely, we will show that for any

\mathbf{x}_{0}

Proposition 14
.

For

, the sets

and
\mathcal{S}_{0,j}
are in bijection with rooted binary trees having

T_{0,j}=S_{0,j}=\mathrm{Cat}_{j-2}=\frac{(2j-4)!}{(j-1)!(j-2)!}.

is in bijection with rooted plane trees having

B_{0,\ell,j}=\mathrm{Nar}_{\ell,j-1}=\frac{(\ell+j-2)!(\ell+j-3)!}{\ell!(\ell-1)!(j-1)!(j-2)!}.

Proof.

We call
dissection
a rooted planar 2-connected map
\mathbf{m}
where all the vertices are incident to the external face. We claim that any dissection admits a unique bipolar orientation. Indeed, if we order the vertices around the external contour in counterclockwise order as
v_{0}
, …,
v_{j-1}
with
v_{0}
the origin of the root edge, then the condition for the right external boundary of bipolar oriented maps ensures that every edge
\{v_{p},v_{p+1}\}
has to be directed from
v_{p}
to
v_{p+1}
for
0\leq p\leq j-2
. And the acyclicity implies that every internal edge
\{v_{p},v_{q}\}
, with
p<q
, has to be directed from
v_{p}
to
v_{q}
. Hence, dissections are in bijection with bipolar oriented maps with no internal vertices.

In particular
\mathcal{T}_{0,j}
is in bijection with dissections of external face degree
j
and internal faces of degree
3
, and
\mathcal{B}_{0,\ell,j}
is in bijection with dissections with external face degree
j
and
\ell
internal faces. By duality, these respectively correspond to rooted binary trees with
j-1
leaves, and to rooted plane trees with
\ell
nodes and
j-1
leaves, which are respectively counted by
\mathrm{Cat}_{j-2}
and by
\mathrm{Nar}_{\ell,j-1}
.

In order to deal with
\mathcal{S}_{0,j}
, it is convenient to observe that a bipolar oriented map has no internal vertices if and only if every internal face has left length
1
. Indeed, if there is an internal vertex
v
, then the internal face
f
on the right of the rightmost outgoing edge of

has its left lateral path passing by

, hence of length at least

has its left lateral path of length at least

then this path has to visit a vertex

As a consequence, for bipolar oriented maps with no internal vertex, having all internal faces of degree

5.1
Growth bijections for the base cases

Catalan.

Note that Rémy’s bijection
[
Rém85
]
, which interprets the identity (
1
), yields a growth bijection for maps in
\mathcal{T}_{0,j}
through the bijection via dissections from the previous section. Hence, there is a complete growth process for maps in
\mathcal{T}_{k,j}

to
\mathcal{T}_{0,k+j}
, then use our bijection (Corollary

)
k

\mathcal{T}_{0,k+j}
to
\mathcal{T}_{k,j}
. The same holds for
\mathcal{S}_{k,j}
.

Narayana.

Regarding
\mathcal{B}_{0,\ell,j}
, we have not been able to find in the literature a growth bijection for structures counted by Narayana numbers. Recall that
\mathrm{Nar}_{a,b}
counts rooted plane trees with
a
nodes and
b
leaves, or, alternatively, rooted binary trees with
a
left and
b
right leaves.

For the sake of completeness, we briefly present such a bijection here. One easily obtains from the formula for
\mathrm{Nar}_{a,b}
the following simple identity

(a+1)\,a\ \mathrm{Nar}_{a+1,b}=(b+1)\,b\ \mathrm{Nar}_{a,b+1},

(13)

which, together with the base case
\mathrm{Nar}_{1,b}=1
yields the formula for
\mathrm{Nar}_{a,b}
. Our bijection for (
13
) relies on cutting/joining operations similarly to
[
BM14
,
Mar23
]
. We find it more convenient to describe it on rooted binary trees counted by left and right leaves (though it can also be described for rooted plane trees counted by nodes and leaves).

For a rooted binary tree, we call
inner edges
those connecting two nodes, and
leaf edges
those incident to a leaf. An edge is called
left
or
right
according to whether it connects a parent to its left child or to its right child. In this terminology,
\mathrm{Nar}_{a,b}
counts rooted binary trees with
a
left leaf edges and
b
right leaf edges. Such trees also have
a-1
right inner edges and
b-1
left inner edges. Hence, if we let
\mathcal{F}_{a,b}
(resp.
\mathcal{G}_{a,b}
) be the set of rooted binary trees with
a
left leaf edges,
b
right leaf edges, and having a marked left leaf edge and a marked right inner edge (resp. a marked right leaf edge and a marked left inner edge), then (
13
) reads
|\mathcal{F}_{a+1,b}|=|\mathcal{G}_{a,b+1}|
.

The bijection between
\mathcal{F}_{a+1,b}
and
\mathcal{G}_{a,b+1}
is actually the specialization of an involution
\chi
on the set of rooted binary trees with a marked leaf edge of some type (left or right) and a marked inner edge of the other type. It is illustrated in Figure
14
and proceeds as follows. We cut the (red) marked inner edge and attach to the (blue) marked leaf edge the cut tree that does not contain the marked leaf edge. We keep the markings in the process.

Figure 14:
The involution
\chi
interpreting (
13
). In the intermediate state, the two marked leaf edges may be in the same tree (top row), or in the 2 trees (bottom row). In this example, the original tree has

Observe that, in the intermediate state, we obtain a pair of rooted binary trees with a marked leaf edge of each types. In the end, the number of leaf edges of the type of the marked inner edge increases by one (in the cutting step), while the number of leaf edges of the type of the marked leaf edge decreases by one (in the attachment step). All in all,
\chi

\mathcal{F}_{a+1,b}
and
\mathcal{G}_{a,b+1}

Apply

Take the corresponding element in
\mathcal{B}_{0,\ell,k+j}
through the classical bijections.

Grow from

to

5.3
Random generation

For the sake of completeness, we now discuss random generators obtained by our slit-slide-sew bijections. As we will see, the complexity, while polynomial, is quite high due to rejection induced by the boundary-reaching constraint. We discuss in details the case of
\mathcal{T}_{k,j}
.
Let
\Gamma T_{k,j}
be the random sampler on
\mathcal{T}_{k,j}
specified as follows.

\diamond

If
k=0
, draw a uniformly chosen random binary tree with
j-2
nodes (using e.g. Rémy’s procedure) and return the oriented map in
\mathcal{T}_{0,j}
associated with it.

\diamond

If
k\geq 1
, repeat:

–

\tilde{\mathbf{m}}\leftarrow\Gamma T_{k-1,j+1}

–

e\leftarrow
uniformly chosen random edge in
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}

until
e
is boundary-reaching. Open
e
into a face
f
of degree
2
, and let
\tilde{e}
be the representative of
e
having
f
on its right. Let
(\mathbf{m},v)=\Psi(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{e})
. Return
\mathbf{m}
.

It is easily checked by induction on
k
that
\Gamma T_{k,j}
is a uniform random sampler on
\mathcal{T}_{k,j}
. Let
\Lambda T_{k,j}
be its expected complexity. Since the cost of computing the rightmost path from a given vertex in
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{T}_{k-1,j+1}
is at most
k-1
, we have, for
k\geq 1
and
j\geq 2
,

\Lambda T_{k,j}\leq\Lambda T_{k-1,j+1}+(k-1)+\frac{2}{j+1}\Lambda T_{k,j}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\Lambda T_{k,j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{j+1}{j-1}\Big(k-1+\Lambda T_{k-1,j+1}\Big)\,,

so that, after iterating,

\displaystyle\Lambda T_{k,j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(j+m-1)(j+m)}{(j-1)j}(k-m)+\frac{(j+k-1)(j+k)}{(j-1)j}\Lambda T_{0,j+k}

hence, bounding each summand by
\dfrac{(j+k-1)(j+k)}{(j-1)j}k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda T_{k,j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(j+k-1)(j+k)}{(j-1)j}\Big(k^{2}+\Lambda T_{0,k+j}\Big)\,.

Finally,
\Lambda T_{k,j}=\mathcal{O}\Big((1+k/j)^{2}(k^{2}+j)\Big)
. Note that the complexity is linear only in the regime
k=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{j})
, while for
j=\mathcal{O}(1)
it is of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{4})
.

Similar uniform random samplers can be obtained for
\mathcal{B}_{k,\ell,j}
and
\mathcal{S}_{k,j}
, of respective complexities
\mathcal{O}\Big((1+k/j)^{2}(k^{2}+\ell+j)\Big)
and
\mathcal{O}\Big((1+k/j)^{3}(k^{2}+j)\Big)
.

More efficient alternatives are provided by the recursive method of sampling, see e.g.
[
BM03
,
BB19
]
. With no rejection involved, they only necessitate simple (rational in terms of the parameters) formulas for the ratio of adjacent coefficients, without actually requiring a positive bijective proof of such formulas. Also, they usually necessitate one more parameter. For instance, letting
\mathcal{Y}_{a,b,c}
be the set of standard Young tableaux on the 3-line diagram
(a,b,c)
, we have

\mathcal{Y}_{a,b,c}\simeq\mathbf{1}_{a>b}\mathcal{Y}_{a-1,b,c}\cup\mathbf{1}_{b>c}\mathcal{Y}_{a,b-1,c}\cup\mathbf{1}_{c>0}\mathcal{Y}_{a,b,c-1}.

The induced recursive random sampler
\Gamma Y_{a,b,c}
thus chooses whether to call

\Gamma Y_{a-1,b,c}
or
\Gamma Y_{a,b-1,c}
or
\Gamma Y_{a,b,c-1}
with probabilities given by the ratios of the counting coefficients, which are simple rational expressions in
a
,
b
,
c
, due to massive cancellations of the hook lengths. This gives a linear-time random sampler for
\mathcal{Y}_{a,b,c}
and thus for
\mathcal{Y}_{k,j}\simeq\mathcal{T}_{k,j}
.
