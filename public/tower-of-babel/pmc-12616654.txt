Feasibility and patient perspectives on telerehabilitation for COPD in Greenland
Authors: Sigurd Gregersen, Henrik Hansen, Christina Nielsen, Maja Hykkelbjerg Hald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presents a significant health burden in Greenland. Pulmonary telerehabilitation (PTR) provides an evidence-based, feasible approach to deliver care to the geographically dispersed population. This mixed-method study evaluates the feasibility and acceptability of PTR for individuals with COPD in Greenland, combining quantitative evaluation of adherence, satisfaction, and clinical outcomes with qualitative interviews and a field log. Seven participants were enrolled in the 10-week program; four completed it. Satisfaction was high and clinical outcomes improved. Participants reported increased self-efficacy and perceived the intervention as effective for disease management. However, dropout rate was high, technical issues were frequent, and some found online sessions less engaging, expressing a desire for more peer support and structural incentives to sustain motivation beyond the program. In this small sample, PTR appeared to be an acceptable and valuable addition to COPD care in Greenland. However, questions remain about the suitability of this approach for the broader population, given Greenland’s telehealth infrastructure and digital readiness. Future efforts should focus on strengthening peer support and sustained engagement beyond programs. Considering digital health literacy and user readiness is essential for telerehabilitation efforts in Greenland.Keywords: Telerehabilitation, COPD, Greenland, feasibility, acceptability

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global health concern, contributing to high morbidity, increased healthcare utilization, and reduced quality of life [1]. The primary risk factor for COPD is tobacco smoking, though air pollution and certain occupational hazards can contribute [2]. In Greenland, smoking rates have declined in recent decades, yet 52% of the population still smokes daily [3]. As a result, COPD remains a significant public health issue in Greenland, which is expected to persist in the future due to the high smoking rates. Based on data extracted from electronic medical records, 2.2% of the adult population in Greenland has a diagnosis of COPD. However, this number is likely underestimated, primarily due to general challenges in the Greenlandic healthcare system hindering diagnostic coding [4].

Standard COPD management includes smoking cessation, pulmonary rehabilitation (PR), vaccination, and inhaler medication [2]. PR is a core component of COPD management, which consistently demonstrates improvements in quality of life, exercise capacity, and dyspnea [1]. Its efficacy is well–established, to the extent that further trials solely comparing PR to conventional care are no longer recommended [5]. There is currently no structured PR program available for individuals with COPD in Greenland and thus it remains completely underutilized. Location and transport to PR centers are well described barriers for patients to enroll and adhere to PR programs [6]. These challenges are likely to be even more prevalent in remote regions like Greenland where extreme weather, geographic isolation, and limited healthcare infrastructure further complicate and hinder reasonable and safe access to structured PR programs. Furthermore, healthcare providers suitable to deliver PR are scarce in Greenland, and in less populated settlements there are usually no staff with formal healthcare training.

Pulmonary telerehabilitation (PTR) has emerged as an evidence–based solution to these barriers, enabling remote delivery of care [1,7]. PTR has previously been shown to be a valid alternative to center–based PR achieving the same outcomes, with some data indicating increased adherence rates [7]. Both individual and group–based telerehabilitation models have shown potential in different healthcare settings, including studies from Denmark and Australia [8,9].

Successful implementation of PTR depends largely on factors such as patients' digital readiness and access to technology [10,11]. These factors may vary widely across different populations, making it essential to evaluate the feasibility and transferability of such interventions before full–scale implementation [12].

A key aspect of assessing feasibility is patient acceptability. Whether patients find the intervention acceptable can influence their engagement, clinical benefits, and long–term adherence [13,14]. This is particularly relevant when evidence–based interventions, such as PTR, are introduced in a new cultural setting, where tailoring to fit local context may be necessary [15]. In Greenland, traditions of community and collective support, linguistic diversity, and digital readiness can shape expectations and acceptance of group–based PTR. Greenland is the world’s largest island and has about 57,000 inhabitants dispersed in towns and small settlements along the coast, with no road connections and reliance on air and sea transport. These geographical and infrastructural conditions challenge healthcare delivery within the publicly financed system, which includes one national hospital in Nuuk, regional hospitals, and local clinics. Nuuk, the capital with around 20,000 residents, serves as the political and administrative center and hosts the national referral hospital [16]. Given this sociocultural and geographical context, a robust focus on acceptability is crucial for successful implementation of PTR. To assess participant acceptability, this study applies the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA), which conceptualizes acceptability as a multifaceted construct reflecting participants' and providers’ cognitive and emotional responses to an intervention [17].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a PTR program for individuals with COPD in Nuuk, Greenland.

Materials and methods

The research team

The first author is a healthcare professional working in the Greenlandic healthcare system where the intervention was delivered, and the last author has a background in public health science. The two co–authors are Danish healthcare professionals and researchers with experience in telerehabilitation, primarily in Danish hospital and community settings. All authors have Western, Danish backgrounds. We acknowledge that these professional and cultural backgrounds may influence our perspectives, and we have reflected critically on our positions throughout the research process.

Study design

This feasibility study utilized a mixed methods design. Quantitative measures included adherence rates, satisfaction questionnaires, and clinical outcomes. Qualitative data were collected through a semi–structured focus group interview. This mixed–method approach allowed a comprehensive evaluation by assessing feasibility with quantitative measures and acceptability through qualitative insights into participants’ experiences.

Participants, study setting and recruitment

The study was conducted at Queen Ingrid’s Hospital (QIH) in Nuuk. Participants were identified by a patient coordinator through an extraction from electronic medical records and subsequently contacted by telephone. Criteria for the data extraction from electronic medical records were a diagnostic code of COPD and a registered address in Nuuk. Recruitment continued until a full training group of 6−8 participants had agreed to join. Recruitment took place between October 7 and October 16, 2024.

Inclusion criteria were: 1) residency in Nuuk to allow baseline assessment, 2) access to a computer/phone and the internet, 3) able to participate in a Danish dialog, as the physiotherapist delivering care was not fluent in Kalaallisut, 4) willing to participate in online training, 5) post–bronchodilator Forced Expiratory Volume in the first second (FEV1)/Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) ratio below 0.7, and FEV1 < 80% of predicted value, 6) Medical Research Council (MRC) dyspnea scale score ≥ 2.

Exclusion criteria were: 1) unstable angina pectoris, 2) hemodynamically significant aortic stenosis, 3) physical or psychological conditions preventing use of video consultation, e.g. hearing, speech or visual impairments, 4) exacerbation within the past six weeks.

Baseline assessment and intervention protocol

Preparation and baseline assessment

Prior to initiating the PTR program, participants underwent baseline assessment of BMI, spirometry, smoking status and MRC–score at QIH. During this visit, all participants provided written informed consent and received the program materials, which included: the exercise program, a description of the required workout equipment, the BORG CR10 scale (perceived exertion scale), and a COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score sheet. All materials and the consent form were provided in Danish. In addition, they were given “Livsstilshåndbogen”, a booklet providing standardized patient education on lifestyle–related diseases in Kalaallisut and Danish. Participants were introduced to Puisa, the video consultation platform used for consultations and training sessions throughout the program. They received a brief guide to Puisa written in Kalaallisut and Danish and were shown how to connect to video consultations via a link sent by text message or e–mail.

Intervention procedures and protocol

Within the first week of recruitment, participants attended an initial video consultation. The primary focus of this session was SMART–inspired goal setting, assessment of motivation, prior experience with exercise, and participants’ perceptions of their condition. The Borg CR10 scale and the exercise program were introduced, followed by a brief 5-minute practice session to test the exercises in the participants’ home environment. Participants then completed a 1-minute sit–to–stand test (1-min STS) and the CAT questionnaire.

A mid–intervention consultation was conducted in week five to evaluate progress, revisit goals and support the therapeutic alliance. At the end of the program, a final consultation was held during which clinical outcome measures were reassessed. The initial, mid–intervention, and final consultations were all conducted individually, in one–to–one sessions with a physiotherapist.

Each session comprised a warm–up (5 minutes), resistance training (35 minutes), and patient education (20 minutes), with a total duration of one hour. All sessions were group–based, during which participants and the physiotherapist could both see and hear each other in real time.

The exercise protocol was inspired by a study group from the Respiratory Department at Hvidovre Hospital in Denmark and adhered to Danish guidelines for PR [18,19]. It involved high repetitive time–based muscular endurance training. The protocol focused on progressively increasing exercise volume and intensity. Participants used simple home equipment (e.g. chairs and water–filled bottles) to perform time–based exercises, starting with 20 seconds of work per set, progressing to 40 seconds in later weeks. Training load was individually adjusted by the physiotherapist by increasing load or slightly modifying exercises. Every sixth session a maximum repetition test was conducted to ensure the training load used by the participant was sufficient. The structure and exercises used are presented in Table 1.

The patient education covered key topics commonly addressed in pulmonary rehabilitation, such as disease understanding, exacerbation management, inhalation technique, and breathing exercises. To enhance engagement and contextualize learning, participants were involved through small assignments and reflective questions, encouraging them to connect the educational content with their daily lives and personal goals. For instance, during the exercise module participants were asked to note a personal barrier to being active (e.g. “sidewalks too slippery” or “lack of time”). They were then asked to come up with concrete strategies to overcome this barrier and then share their reflections with the group.

Consultations and training sessions were conducted via Puisa, a video consultation platform developed for use in the Greenlandic healthcare system. The platform is designed to be both secure and user–friendly. Participants accessed the virtual meeting room by entering a link or code sent via text message or email, using a PC, smartphone, or tablet.

Training sessions were held on Tuesday and Thursday mornings from October 29, 2024, to January 14, 2025. During late December 2024, some sessions were rescheduled or postponed due to holiday vacations, which affected participant attendance.

Feasibility study

Data collection

Feasibility was addressed by assessing evaluation outcomes, including adherence rates, satisfaction and clinical outcome measures collected pre–and post–intervention. To supplement these outcomes, a brief evaluation of the implementation process was provided to contribute an instructor’s perspective.

Feasibility evaluation outcomes

Adherence

Adherence was defined as participants attending the video consultation for either the training session, the patient education session, or both. Attendance was tracked along with reasons for non–participation, such as illness, technical difficulties, or other barriers.

Satisfaction

Satisfaction was assessed using an 11-item questionnaire (see Appendix 1), distributed electronically via SurveyMonkey after the final training session. The questionnaire was sent via e–mail to all participants, including those who did not complete the program. It was sent out immediately after the last training session and was closed for replies after two weeks.

The questionnaire was developed by the research team for this feasibility study and was also designed as a simple, practical tool for systematic evaluation of future rehabilitation efforts in Greenland. We first identified key areas to be assessed such as overall satisfaction, training volume and intensity, session scheduling, and the usability of the video consultation platform. Items were then created to cover these areas and categorized into five categories: The intervention (3 items), time and duration (3 items), team setting (2 items), usability of the platform (2 items), and recommendation (1 item). The questionnaire was in Danish.

Response formats varied depending on the question. Some items used a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Very satisfied” to “Very dissatisfied”, while others used a 3-point format such as “Too hard”, “Appropriate”, or “Too easy”. For the final item participants were asked whether they would recommend the program to a friend with COPD, with response options being “Yes, definitely”, “Yes, maybe”, “Yes, maybe” and “No”.

Participants were given the opportunity to expand their answers in a free–text field in relation to some questions. Additionally, they were able to provide general feedback and suggestions in a free–text field at the end of the questionnaire.

The responses from all items within each category were grouped into satisfaction and dissatisfaction responses. For questions using a 5-point Likert scale, responses indicating satisfaction included “Very satisfied” and “Satisfied,” while dissatisfaction was indicated by “Dissatisfied” and “Very dissatisfied”. Neutral responses were categorized separately. For questions using a 3-point scale, only the response “Appropriate” was categorized as an indicator of satisfaction. For the final recommendation question, responses of “Yes, definitely” and “Yes, maybe” were classified as indicators of satisfaction.

Clinical outcomes

1-min STS

The 1-min STS is a validated measure for assessing the lower limb muscle endurance and functional exercise capacity of individuals with COPD in remote settings [20,21]. The test was chosen for its simplicity, requiring only a chair and easy–to–follow instructions, making it well–suited for remote functional assessment. Participants performed as many repetitions of a sit–to–stand motion as possible in one minute, with their arms folded across their chest, from a regular household chair. The total number of completed stands was recorded. Chair height was not standardized across participants, as ideal chair height (43–45cm) could not be confirmed by video consultation. Before testing, participants were instructed in safety and proper technique. Participants were allowed only one attempt at the assessment. To account for the inherent delay in video communication, the test was slightly modified: timing began when participants initiated the first rise, rather than on a verbal countdown. At the end–of–program consultation, it was ensured that participants used the same chair for the assessment.

CAT score

CAT score is an eight–item questionnaire quantifying the burden of COPD symptoms on a scale from 0−5 [22]. All scores are added up to give a total score of 0−40 (high scores = greater symptom burden). The questionnaire format was explained thoroughly prior to the assessment. At the initial consultation, participants filled out a paper form CAT score sheet provided as part of the program materials. At the end–of–program consultation, the CAT score was collected remotely by showing each item in large format via screen share and noting participants’ responses. Both assessments were conducted via video consultation.

Evaluation of the implementation process

The physiotherapist kept a field log to document and reflect on events related to implementation. This was included as part of the evaluation outcomes to provide an instructor perspective and to address areas not covered by other data sources. Events were recorded immediately after each patient consultation or group training session. Subjects in the field log included: the flow of the introductory phase (e.g. onboarding, goal setting), ease of use of the video consultation software and supervision challenges from the therapist’s perspective, technical incidents including a thorough description of the characteristics of the problem, and other events that either facilitated or caused friction in the implementation process.

Log entries were continuously evaluated and discussed with colleagues at Steno Diabetes Center Greenland. Events that were repeated or particularly relevant for implementation were included in the results.

Acceptability study

Acceptability

Sekhon’s theoretical framework of acceptability was applied to conceptualize findings. The framework defines acceptability as: “a multi–faceted construct that reflects the extent to which people delivering or receiving a healthcare intervention consider it to be appropriate, based on anticipated or experienced cognitive and emotional responses” [17].

The original framework includes seven component constructs: affective attitude, burden, perceived effectiveness, ethicality, intervention coherence, opportunity costs, and self–efficacy. Sekhon emphasizes that a key aspect of the framework is the distinction between prospective and retrospective assessment of acceptability, as some constructs are best assessed before, during, or after the intervention period. To ensure accurate use of the framework and avoid misinterpretation, we focused on the constructs that were most relevant to participants' retrospective evaluations of the telerehabilitation intervention. Specifically, we explored opportunity cost, perceived effectiveness, and self–efficacy.

Interview and analysis

A semi–structured focus group interview was conducted with a subset of participants to explore their beliefs and experiences with participation in the program. In total, four participants took part in the interview. The interview was conducted in Danish, which was a common language of the participants and interviewer. Since all participants were highly proficient in Danish, this choice ensured that all could comfortably participate and that the conversation would have a natural flow without the need for an interpreter. The interview guide was designed to explore the before–mentioned aspects of TFA, which we wanted to assess: opportunity cost, perceived effectiveness, and self–efficacy. Only participants who completed the program were invited to the interview. Participants who dropped out did not get sufficient exposure to the full intervention such as important aspects of the patient education, exercise progression, and review of their goals. They were therefore deemed unable to comment meaningfully on most interview topics. The interview took place at QIH one week after the last training session on January 23, 2025.

The focus group interview used a Sharing Circle format, which is recognized as an inclusive and commonly used method for collecting qualitative data with Indigenous people in Greenland [23]. The interview took place in a conference room where chairs were equally spaced and arranged in a circle. A small bean bag served as a “talking stick” and was passed around the circle allowing only one person to speak at a time. To begin the session, everyone in the circle introduced themselves and shared a personal value or something meaningful to them, which served as an icebreaker. Afterward, the interviewer asked questions based on the interview guide and passed the talking stick around the circle.

The interview was initially planned to be conducted by a member of the research team who was not directly involved in the intervention delivery. However, due to unforeseen circumstances, the primary researcher conducted the interview instead. In an effort to mitigate potential social desirability bias, participants were encouraged to provide frank and candid feedback in order for the research team to improve the intervention protocol for future use.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim using a transcription key and the data were analyzed using systematic text condensation [24]. First, the transcription was read from start to finish while noting impressions and identifying initial themes. In this step, the first author (SG) bracketed preconceptions and theoretical assumptions. These were set aside as best as possible, emphasizing an inductive approach to remain open to participants’ unfiltered perspectives. Given SG’s dual role as both intervention provider and analyst, particular attention was paid to approaching the data with openness in this step. The initial impressions were grouped into six themes related to participants’ perceived acceptance of the intervention.

During the coding process, we prioritized flexibility, allowing themes to be merged or redefined if they overlapped substantially or were difficult to define. After completing the coding process, the six initial themes were refined into three themes with underlying subthemes. All meaning bearing units in each subtheme were unified into a single condensate written in first–person format to ensure participants’ own wording. The descriptions and concepts in the analysis were developed from these condensates. Quotes were included to illustrate perspectives. To contextualize the findings, we connected them to the component constructs defined in TFA. Lastly, the full transcription was reviewed once more to look for data that would contradict the findings.

To broaden perspective and ensure reliability, the results were reviewed by the last author, who had extensive experience in qualitative analysis.

Results

A total of 12 individuals were invited to participate in the study, of whom 7 agreed to join. Reasons for declining were due to practical challenges (e.g. lack of time, technical issues) or personal preferences (e.g. not comfortable with group training). Although not specifically planned, all 7 enrolled participants identified as Inuit. Two individuals with Danish backgrounds were unable to participate due to lack of internet access or a suitable device. Feasibility study

Adherence

Seven participants initiated the program, of whom four completed the program. One participant withdrew with two sessions remaining due to illness unrelated to the intervention, and two were lost to follow–up for unknown reasons, despite repeated outreach via phone and email. Out of 140 planned training sessions, 93 were completed, resulting in an overall attendance rate of 66.4%. The four participants who completed the program had an average attendance rate of 76%, while the three who did not complete the program attended between 50–65% of sessions prior to withdrawal. Known reasons for non–attendance included illness, personal or family matters, soreness or lack of sleep, technical problems (internet connection issues or missing invitation links), vacations, and preference for other community activities. Implementation process

The field log kept by the physiotherapist revealed several factors which influenced the implementation process. The most notable negative aspects were technical difficulties, some of which stemmed from user error and lack of digital skills, while others were due to limitations of the videoconferencing platform.

From an instructor’s perspective, the platform lacked certain features. There was no ability to change the in–call layout or mute participants. In some cases, participants' screens were hidden behind the instructor’s own video feed, making it difficult to ensure proper supervision and exercise execution. The optimal group size on the platform appeared to be 4−5 participants. Larger groups made supervision impractical.

Invites and reminders via text led to issues with communication. One participant complained about the excessive amount of reminders and invitations, and another had the invites being filtered as spam in week six. This led to difficulties joining the sessions, and the problem had to be resolved with an in–person meeting at QIH.

Despite these challenges, there were also positive aspects. The participants were generally positive and grateful to be invited to a COPD rehabilitation program, which made recruitment easier. The startup process was smoother for individuals who found it easy to navigate the Puisa platform. The virtual format allowed easy and efficient mid–program evaluations and brief check–ins. The startup process, including initial consultation, handing out materials, and goal setting, was intuitive for the instructor to follow.

Acceptability study

Interviews

Three participants completed the focus group interview. Three themes emerged from the analysis of the interview, reflecting the participants’ experiences with the telerehabilitation program.

New insights and perspectives on disease

Participants agreed that gaining knowledge and new perspectives on their condition was valuable. One participant highlighted how the group setting helped them reflect on the severity of their own condition and the potential consequences of not taking action. The program enabled them to take responsibility for their health and understand the importance of exercise in managing COPD. One participant’s words captured this perspective:

"I think it has been an eye-opener. As I’ve said before, I didn’t really care about my disease. I just had to take my medication every day, and that was it. I didn’t observe myself the way I do now after going through the program.” (P1)

The quote highlights how the program changed the participant’s perception of their disease. Previously, they saw treatment as simply taking medication, but now they have become more aware of their condition and the importance of self–care.

The overall positive experience with the program may have been reinforced by the participants’ accounts of how their initial COPD care after diagnosis felt fragmented and impersonal. Two participants described receiving medication without clear instructions, leaving them with little understanding of their condition. Their contact with the healthcare system felt incohesive, partly due to seeing different clinicians at every visit. Participant 3 gave a clear example and described:

“I was just given some medication and then, ‘That was it.’ I had two types of medication, and I thought, 'When am I supposed to use the other one?' No one told me… It was only during this program that the instructor explained things with illustrations and all that. What COPD is, how it works, and what it means. That was the first time I got a proper explanation.” (P3)

The quote illustrates how the usual COPD care in Greenland felt insufficient for this participant. The patient education aspect of the telerehabilitation program ensured that this participant had a proper understanding of their disease. This perspective highlights how patient education delivered in the program could be a valuable addition to usual care.

Perceived support and supervision in the virtual setting

Participants valued the support they received from the instructor and felt encouraged throughout the virtual program. Participant 1 described how she felt supported by her workplace, which allowed her to integrate the training session into her workday, as if she were working from home. She also felt genuinely cared for by the instructor, and felt the exercises were tailored to her needs. Verbal encouragement, such as reminders to increase effort and progress exercises, contributed to a sense of supervision in the virtual setting.

However, while some participants appreciated this support, some also experienced limitations with the virtual setting of the program. One participant, in contrast to Participant 1’s experience of personalized care and supervision, felt that the lack of physical presence made it difficult to receive precise corrections on exercises. He perceived online training as the next best option compared to an in–person group setting and explained why with the following remark:

“This online thing, it’s not the best. It’s the second–best option. If you know what I mean?. The best thing is to be together. To help each other. If we have that opportunity, then we stick together and meet again next time. You don't just stay away.” (P2)

This quote highlights the importance of group context and cohesion within the group for the participant. Meeting in a physical space was perceived as more supportive, strengthening group cohesion and motivation.

Another participant highlighted how open–ended questions during sessions sometimes led to awkward silence. It was unclear whether someone else in the videoconference was about to answer the question, which led to hesitation.

Improving telerehabilitation programs and community support

Participants shared their suggestions for improving the program, particularly concerning scheduling and maintaining the exercise habit. Some felt that earlier sessions would better fit into their daily routines, while others felt that the intensity of the exercises in the early stages was too easy.

One participant highlighted the abrupt transition at the end of the 10-week program. He wished for a structured way to maintain the exercise routine and preserve a sense of community. Another participant agreed and suggested a virtual training space where participants could continue exercising together, even without an instructor. She suggested:

“I would like to agree with Participant 2 on that. That maybe, even without a trainer, there could be a link where we could continue together.” (P1)

The idea proposed by Participant 1 points to a desire for continued group engagement. Additionally, it was proposed that meeting online or in–person before the program started could have helped build a stronger sense of community from the get–go. These suggestions reflect a shared wish to sustain exercise habits and peer support beyond the program.

Discussion

This feasibility study explores the first PTR program conducted in Greenland. Feasibility indicators showed generally high satisfaction rates and improvements in clinical outcomes among those who finished the telerehabilitation program. Despite the overall positive indicators of acceptability and satisfaction, dropout rates were high. A review of the implementation process from the instructor’s perspective identified technical barriers in the video consultation platform. These challenges complicated communication and, in some cases, hindered proper supervision of training sessions. Qualitative interviews revealed that participants who completed the program gained self–efficacy, valued the more in–depth and personal education compared to usual care, and generally perceived the intervention as effective. Some participants did, however, find online sessions less engaging than in–person formats. They also suggested changes to strengthen group cohesion and structural incentives to support ongoing exercise after the program ended.

Feasibility

Notes from the implementation process and questionnaire feedback indicated that the video consultation platform could be improved. The telehealth literature emphasize that poor digital infrastructure can hinder proper clinical supervision and negatively impact overall program success [25,26]. Poor telehealth infrastructure might be especially relevant for Greenland’s remote regions, where internet access is more likely to be unstable. These findings are important considerations for expanding telerehabilitation more broadly. Challenges should be expected, especially in remote parts of the country.

Regarding adherence, average attendance was relatively high among completers at 76%. However, the dropout rate of 43% was notably high as well. Two participants withdrew late in the program despite prior satisfactory attendance. It was not possible to obtain direct feedback from these participants despite several attempts. Reasons for dropouts in our study are likely multifaceted. One pattern is attendance declining during the holiday season in December and never fully recovering. Some participants traveled abroad, while others found it difficult to attend sessions due to holiday obligations. Questionnaire and interview feedback indicated that the program’s schedule did not align well with participants’ daily routines, possibly influencing dropouts. Keating et al. (2011) similarly describe how convenience and scheduling flexibility are crucial for attendance in PR [6]. Future PTR initiatives in Greenland may benefit from more flexible scheduling and additional support to address the personal and seasonal factors that impact attrition and uptake.

Another factor, which might have influenced attendance, was the digital health literacy of participants. During the intervention, text message invites and reminders were sometimes missed, filtered as spam, or considered to be excessive. This highlights how minor technical challenges can hinder continuous attendance. These challenges are often more significant for older adults, those unfamiliar with technology, or those with a lower level of education, which are common barriers in adopting telehealth programs [27]. In contrast, participants who found it easier to navigate the videoconferencing platform experienced a much smoother startup. Importantly, this study took place in Nuuk, Greenland’s capital, which generally has higher living standards, better access to technology, and higher average level of education compared to other regions [28,29]. Digital health literacy is presumably lower in remote areas, potentially limiting transferability and scaling of telerehabilitation elsewhere in Greenland.

One important aspect we did not assess regarding feasibility was user readiness, which includes participants’ ability and willingness to adopt telehealth services, as well as broader organizational support [26]. Even when the need for telehealth is clear, the intentions are good, and the technology is available, telehealth programs can still fail if patients, clinicians, or administrators are not fully prepared to use them. Assessing user readiness might have identified participants requiring additional support, allowing us to know how and for whom to adapt the intervention. Evaluating readiness systematically in future projects could strengthen the overall feasibility of telerehabilitation in Greenland, ensuring that program rollouts align with local infrastructure, funding, and patient capacities across the vast geography.

The small sample size in our study obviously limits the strength of any clinical outcome findings. Nevertheless, this is the first time a telerehabilitation program has been conducted in a primarily Inuit population in Greenland, so even suboptimal measures of effectiveness can be a relevant indicator for the future. Participants showed improved clinical outcomes in CAT scores and 1-min STS, similarly to what is commonly regarded as a clinically important difference [30,31]. While these results should be interpreted with caution, they indicate that the intervention protocol may have positive clinical impacts in a Greenlandic population similar to those seen in a Danish population [9].

Acceptability

Our findings suggest that participants generally perceived PTR as acceptable. Most notably, they described gaining new knowledge and a deeper understanding of life with COPD, indicating an increased sense of self–efficacy and perceived effectiveness.

Participants did not express a substantial opportunity cost, even when asked directly during the interview. However, several participants noted that changes in scheduling would have made attendance easier, suggesting that taking part in the program did in fact require some participants to disrupt daily routines to attend sessions.

A scoping review by Wallace [32] on telehealth for chronic musculoskeletal pain highlights how telerehabilitation can enhance intrinsic motivation, autonomy, and self–efficacy by fostering a sense of ownership of own care [32]. This perspective can also be seen in a recent qualitative study that explored participants’ experiences of an online delivered exercise and education intervention for patients with knee osteoarthritis in Denmark, called TeleGLA:D®. They found that patients felt more in control of their treatment as they were responsible for setting up their exercise space themselves [33]. Likewise, our participants who improvised with homemade weights and established a training space in their own homes may have similarly boosted self–efficacy. Compared to traditional in–person rehabilitation, where an instructor usually manages equipment and machine settings, telerehabilitation could foster autonomy by encouraging patients to set up and manage their own exercise spaces. This helps meet some of the major goals of PR: cultivating autonomy, knowledge, and self–management that last beyond the program.

Our findings align with the findings of the TeleGLA:D® study in another aspect. In TeleGLA:D®, patients noted that motivation and individualized guidance could suffer without in–person interaction. This point was echoed by our participant, who described the online format as “the second–best option”. This perspective relates to another component of TFA: ethicality. While most participants in our study felt well–supported, this specific comment suggests that the ethicality of the intervention might not match the value system or preferences of all individuals with COPD in Greenland.

Another TFA construct, “burden”, was indirectly assessed. Participants who completed the program did not report feeling overwhelmed, but recruitment barriers (lack of access to technology) suggest that burden might have affected those who declined participation. Acquiring hardware or relying on help to engage with technology creates considerable burden. This may reflect limited digital readiness among some individuals in Greenland. To lessen friction in PTR uptake, minimizing participant burden in COPD rehabilitation in Greenland is a key consideration going forward.

Participants highlighted a need for continued group engagement to maintain motivation. The transition from structured sessions to maintaining an exercise routine on their own was perceived as abrupt. One participant proposed continuing the sessions unsupervised, indicating a desire for a structural incentive to maintain peer support and motivation beyond the program. This observation aligns with findings by Midthun et al. [34], who reported that patients in a home–based PTR program also wished for an improved transition after the program, monthly check–ins, and keeping their study–provided tablet to stay motivated [34]. Similarly to our participants, this shows a desire for a less abrupt end to the intervention period and a wish for some modality of continued structure or support going forward. Furthermore, the emphasis on community support in our study reflects a well–known non–exercise benefit of PR, which is establishing social support networks to foster motivation and reduce social isolation among individuals with COPD [35].

Future rehabilitation programs could encourage community–building components. A pragmatic solution could be the creation of groups on messaging apps (e.g. WhatsApp or Messenger), which could strengthen group cohesion during and beyond the program. While an online group may boost motivation and peer support, it could also risk excluding individuals who are unable or unwilling to join. Therefore, if such an incentive is incorporated into rehabilitation programs in Greenland, it must be clear that it serves only as a supplement to the formal program. Another option to provide ongoing structure and support is to introduce a maintenance program for those who have already completed PTR. Nielsen et al. [36] outline a 65-week, once–weekly group–based maintenance program which could help consolidate the important habit of regular exercise for individuals with COPD [36]. Furthermore, a program conducted over a longer timespan allows room to introduce additional modes of exercise, such as balance exercises and eccentric loading. The relevance of implementing maintenance programs in Greenland likely rests on two points. First, patients should find such a program meaningful and beneficial to their quality of life. Second, the added cost of prolonged care should be offset by participants’ reduced use of healthcare services in the future. If both conditions are met, offering maintenance programs may be a feasible way to extend the benefits of PR in Greenland.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the acceptability–focused design and an interview approach using a Sharing Circle format adapted to the local context. This enriched our understanding of participant experiences. Unlike some studies, we predefined relevant aspects of acceptability and aligned the interview guide accordingly.

However, the acceptability evaluation could have been strengthened. Using a validated acceptability questionnaire (e.g. Sekhon et al., [37]) could have added structured quantitative data to complement our qualitative findings and strengthen the overall acceptability evaluation [37]. Furthermore, collecting responses using a validated questionnaire would have enabled comparison with other intervention studies. That said, using such a tool would require translation and cultural adaptation to the Greenlandic context to ensure relevance. Including the physiotherapist’s perspective would have captured provider–level acceptability, a key factor in implementation success. Also, data collected before, during, and after the intervention–as recommended by the TFA–would have provided a fuller picture.

Cultural context matters when interpreting acceptability in Greenland. A limitation of our study is the absence of an Inuit partner, which may have restricted the degree of cultural anchoring and perspective in the research process. We acknowledge that conducting the study in Nuuk and primarily in Danish, with limited formal co–design with Inuit partners, may limit the transferability of our findings to community settings outside Nuuk.

Another notable limitation was the lack of data from dropouts. Their input is critical for understanding feasibility, and the absence of their perspectives may introduce survivorship bias. Future studies should improve dropout follow–up strategies.

Finally, having the same researcher lead all stages of the study may limit credibility due to potential bias. For instance, participants may have felt inclined to provide more favorable responses (social desirability bias) because the physiotherapist who delivered the intervention also conducted the interview. Involving external researchers could enhance validity in future rehabilitation studies based in Greenland.

Conclusion

This study is the first to explore the feasibility and acceptability of PTR for COPD care in Greenland. PTR was generally well–received by participants who completed the program, enhancing self–efficacy, COPD self–management, and disease understanding compared to usual care. However, concerns remain regarding its suitability for the broader population, given Greenland's digital readiness and infrastructure.

Feasibility was limited by technical issues, high dropout rate, and varying digital literacy. Participants emphasized the need for stronger peer support and continuity beyond the intervention period. Future efforts should improve infrastructure, scheduling flexibility, and include provider perspectives and evaluation of acceptability across different phases of the intervention. Larger studies are needed, especially in Greenland’s remote areas.

Ethical considerations

The study was initiated after approval from the Scientific Ethics Committee (2024−20410). All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study and for the results to be used for research purposes. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration [38]. All data were handled anonymously. The study adhered to principles for ethical research with Inuit in Greenland [39]. Participants’ perspectives and local knowledge were actively incorporated, and all data were de–identified to protect anonymity in a small community. The research team was mindful of the historical context of research in Greenland and engaged participants respectfully. Collaboration with local health professionals was maintained throughout, and study results will be shared with the community to support culturally relevant healthcare services. Due to practical constraints, it was not possible to involve Inuit partners in this pilot study. Nevertheless, we conducted the research in accordance with ethical principles for work with Inuit, including respectful engagement, protection of anonymity, and culturally sensitive data collection. Future studies will prioritize active collaboration with Inuit partners throughout all stages of the research process.
