Constructing solutions of simplex equations from polygon equations
Serban Matei Mihalache, Tomoro Mochida

Abstract
. We study polygon equations and their connections to simplex equations, which generalize the pentagon and Yang–Baxter equations, respectively. First, we show that certain “commutative” pairs of solutions of (dual) polygon equations give rise to solutions of higher polygon equations. Next, we define an explicit compatibility condition between solutions of the n n -gon and dual n n -gon equations and use it to construct solutions of the ( n − 2 ) (n-2) - and ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -simplex equations. This extends earlier work by Kashaev–Sergeev and Dimakis–Müller-Hoissen.



1.
Introduction

The Yang–Baxter equation
[
28
,
2
]
,

\displaystyle R_{12}R_{13}R_{23}=R_{23}R_{13}R_{12},

originally introduced in the study of integrable models in statistical mechanics, is now fundamental to mathematical physics, topology, and many other areas of mathematics. A.B. Zamolodchikov
[
29
,
30
]
considered a three-dimensional analog of the Yang–Baxter equation, known as the tetrahedron equation.
Later, a higher-dimensional analog of these equations, called the simplex equations, was defined in
[
3
]
, which includes the Yang–Baxter equation as the 2-simplex equation, Zamolodchikov’s tetrahedron equation as the 3-simplex equation, and so on. See, for example,
[
11
,
20
,
4
,
7
,
19
,
1
]
for more details.

Another important equation with a flavor similar to the Yang–Baxter equation is the pentagon (5-gon) equation,

\displaystyle T_{12}T_{13}T_{23}=T_{23}T_{12},

which again appears as a fundamental equation in mathematical physics and topology. Subsequently, A. Dimakis and F. Müller-Hoissen
[
7
]
defined the class of polygon equations. Just as simplex equations generalize the Yang–Baxter and tetrahedron equations, polygon equations generalize the pentagon equation. In
[
21
]
, Müller-Hoissen explored (set-theoretic) polygon equations, investigating the relationship between neighboring polygon equations and how a solution of the
k
-gon equation lifts or descends to a solution of the
(k\pm 1)
-gon equation. Also, in topology, polygon equations play a key role in constructing state sum invariants of manifolds from their triangulations
[
18
,
14
,
17
]
. In particular, they guarantee invariance under a certain Pachner move.

As for the relationship between simplex equations and polygon equations, there have been several attempts to construct solutions of simplex equations from solutions of polygon equations. In
[
15
]
, R.M. Kashaev and S.M. Sergeev showed that whenever a solution of the pentagon equation and a solution of its dual satisfy a certain compatibility condition, they give rise to solutions of the tetrahedron (3-simplex) equation and the 4-simplex equation. See also
[
16
]
. Later in
[
7
]
, Dimakis and Müller-Hoissen associated simplex equations with higher Bruhat orders and polygon equations with higher Tamari orders. Then, by considering the “three color decomposition”, which is a decomposition of the higher Bruhat order into the higher Tamari order, its dual, and what they called the mixed order, they constructed a solution of the
(n-1)
-simplex equation from a compatible pair of solutions of the
n
-gon equation and its dual. This generalizes Kashaev and Sergeev’s construction of a solution of the 4-simplex equation from solutions of the pentagon equation and its dual. However, as pointed out in
[
7
, Section 6]
, they were unable to fully explore the relationship between solutions of the 3-simplex equation and those of the pentagon equation. Also, the compatibility condition between solutions of the
n
-gon equation and its dual was not made explicit, by which we mean that although one can write the compatibility condition for each
n
individually, it is still unclear how to express it uniformly. A similar study, particularly for the class of odd-gon equations, also appears in Dimakis and I.G. Korepanov’s work
[
6
]
.

In this paper, we study polygon and simplex equations. We begin by exploring polygon equations within a general framework. We show that if two solutions of (dual) polygon equations are “commutative” in the sense of Section
4
, their (partial) composition and tensor product yield a solution of a higher polygon equation. In the second part, we explore the relationship between simplex equations and polygon equations. We explicitly define the compatibility condition above for solutions of the
n
-gon and dual
n
-gon equations and observe how these two solutions give rise to a solution of the
(n-2)
-simplex equation and, for completeness, to a solution of the
(n-1)
-simplex equation. We provide direct proofs of these connections for both even- and odd-gon cases.

There is also an observation of the relationship between the pentagon equation and the Yang–Baxter (2-simplex) equation in
[
12
,
25
,
8
]
. It is natural to ask for a generalization of this result, namely the relationship between the
n
-gon equation and the
(n-3)
-simplex equation, and we leave this for future work.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we develop graphical techniques for representing linear maps. We employ them throughout the paper to visualize simplex and polygon equations and to prove most of the theorems and propositions. In Section
3
, we define polygon and dual polygon equations using both multi-index notation and graphical representations. We then prove some properties of solutions of (dual) polygon equations. In Section
4
, we consider constructing a new solution by (partially) composing two solutions of lower polygon equations that are commutative in a certain sense. As a corollary, we prove all the conjectures posed in
[
21
]
. Examples of solutions of polygon equations are given in Section
5
. In Section
6
, we define the mixed relation between solutions of the
n
-gon and the dual
n
-gon equations. Out of such a mixed pair of solutions, we construct solutions of the
(n-1)
- and
(n-2)
-simplex equations. This generalizes the construction of solutions for both 3-gon and 4-gon equations given by Kashaev and Sergeev
[
15
]
. In Appendix
A
, we explain the relationship between Pachner moves in PL topology and polygon equations. Diagrammatic constructions of (non-constant) polygon and simplex equations are presented. Appendix
B
considers a generalization of the mixed relation and Theorems
6.7
and
6.8
in the non-constant setting.

Throughout the paper, we fix a field
\Bbbk
, and vector spaces, linear maps, tensor products, duals, etc., are considered to be over
\Bbbk
unless otherwise stated. Note that many of the arguments in this paper can be generalized to polygon and simplex equations in a symmetric monoidal category.

2.
Representing linear maps

In this section, we describe how a linear map indexed by a multi-index acts on the tensor product of vector spaces and present a graphical representation of these linear maps. Then, we define two operations on linear maps: partial composition and partial trace. This setup will be used throughout the paper to define pentagon and simplex equations and to prove most of our statements.

Let

\displaystyle F\otimes\operatorname{id}_{V}^{\otimes{(n-k)}})\tau_{k,a_{k}}\cdots\tau_{2,a_{2}}\tau_{1,a_{1}},

3.
Polygon equations

In this section, we define the polygon and dual polygon equations. The Polygon and dual polygon equations are a family of equations indexed by an integer
n\geq 3
, where the equations associated to a specific
n
are called the
n
-gon and dual
n
-gon equations.

Originally, the definitions were given in
[
7
]
in terms of the higher Tamari order.
Instead, we define them in tensor notation, namely by directly specifying the factors on which each operator appearing in the equation acts. We also give definitions in graphical representation. See Appendix
A
for a geometric background of these equations.

Definition 3.1

(
Odd-gon equation
)
.

For an integer
k\geq 1
, the
(2k+1)
-gon equation for a linear map
T\colon V^{\otimes k}\to V^{\otimes k}
is an equation in
\operatorname{End}(V^{\otimes\frac{k(k+1)}{2}})
of the form:

T_{a_{1}}T_{a_{2}}\cdots T_{a_{k+1}}=T_{b_{k}}T_{b_{k-1}}\cdots T_{b_{1}},

where
a_{i}=[\,a_{i,1},a_{i,2},\ldots,a_{i,k}\,]
and
b_{j}=[\,b_{j,1},b_{j,2},\ldots,b_{j,k}\,]
are multi-indices indicating which factors of
\frac{k(k+1)}{2}
the operator
T
acts on and are given by the following matrices
A_{2k+1}
and
B_{2k+1}
, respectively:

A_{2k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bNiceArray},\quad B_{2k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\\
\vdots\\
b_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bNiceArray},

with
A_{3}=[\,1,1\,]^{T}
and
B_{3}=[\,1\,]
, where
[\,r\,]_{i,j}
denotes the
i\times j
matrix whose entries are all
r
.

(2k+1)
-gon equation can be defined graphically by

We can also define the dual version of Definition

Definition 3.2

(
Dual odd-gon equation
)
.

For an integer
k\geq 1
, the dual
(2k+1)
-gon equation for a linear map
T\colon V^{\otimes k}\to V^{\otimes k}
is an equation in
\operatorname{End}(V^{\otimes\frac{k(k+1)}{2}})
of the form:

T_{a_{k+1}}T_{a_{k}}\cdots T_{a_{1}}=T_{b_{1}}T_{b_{2}}\cdots T_{b_{k}},

where the multi-indices
a_{i}
and
b_{j}

.

Since the dual version is given by just reversing the order of compositions, the graphical representation of the dual
(2k+1)
-gon equation is given by reversing the composition arrows in the diagram of the odd-gon equation, or, in other words, reading the diagram from top to bottom.

Definition 3.3

(
Even-gon equation
)
.

For an integer
k\geq 2
, the
2k
-gon equation for a linear map
T\colon V^{\otimes k-1}\to V^{\otimes k}
is an equation in
\text{Hom}(V^{\otimes\frac{k(k-1)}{2}},V^{\otimes\frac{k(k+1)}{2}})
of the form:

T_{a_{1}}T_{a_{2}}\cdots T_{a_{k}}=T_{b_{k}}T_{b_{k-1}}\cdots T_{b_{1}},

where the multi-indeces
a_{i}=[\,a_{i,1},a_{i,2},\ldots,a_{i,k-1}\,]
and
b_{j}=[\,b_{j,1},b_{j,2},\ldots,b_{j,k-1}\,]
are given by the folloing matrices
A_{2k}
and
B_{2k}
, respectively:

A_{2k}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k}\end{bmatrix}:=A_{2k-1},\quad B_{2k}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\\
\vdots\\
b_{k}\end{bmatrix}:=\text{$B_{2k+1}$ with the last column deleted.}

Similarly, we can define the dual version.

Definition 3.4

(
Dual even-gon equation
)
.

For an integer
k\geq 2
, the dual
2k
-gon equation for a linear map
T\colon V^{\otimes k}\to V^{\otimes k-1}
is an equation in
\text{Hom}(V^{\otimes\frac{k(k+1)}{2}},V^{\otimes\frac{k(k-1)}{2}})
of the form:

T_{a_{k}}T_{a_{k-1}}\cdots T_{a_{1}}=T_{b_{1}}T_{b_{2}}\cdots T_{b_{k}},

where the multi-indices
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
are the same as in Definition

Again, since the dual version is given by just reversing the order of compositions, the diagram of the dual

We have defined each type of polygon equation in two ways: tensor notation and graphical notation. Comparing these, the tensor notation arranges the input vectors of the (dual) polygon equation linearly and specifies the action of each
T
by a multi-index, whereas the graphical notation places these input vectors on the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
plane and connects them according to the action of
T
. This not only makes the structure of the equation much easier to understand but also, as will be seen later, allows us to treat the odd-gon and even-gon cases in a unified way. For this reason, graphical methods will be heavily used in arguments involving polygon (and simplex) equations throughout the paper.

Remark 3.1
.

In
[
7
, Section 4.2]
, the authors gave two equivalent definitions of polygon equations: the non-hatted and hatted versions. Our definition of polygon equations essentially corresponds to the “hatted version”. Concretely, in the odd-gon case, it is the same as the hatted version, whereas in the even-gon case, it coincides with the dual of the hatted version, and vice versa.

The translation between our definition and the non-hatted version (which is also used in
[
21
]
) is as follows:
For a positive integer
k
, define a permutation
\bar{\sigma}_{k}
by

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{k}:=\sigma_{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}+1\rfloor,k-\lfloor\frac{k}{2}+1\rfloor}\cdots\sigma_{2,k-1}\sigma_{1,k},

where
\sigma_{i,j}
is the transposition that flips the
i
-th and
j
-th factors if
i\neq j
, i.e.,
\sigma_{i,j}(\cdots\otimes x_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{j}\otimes\cdots)=\cdots\otimes x_{j}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{i}\otimes\cdots
, and is identity if
i=j
. Then the two definitions of the
(2k+1)
-gon equation differ in the composition of the permutation
\bar{\sigma}_{k}
. That is,
T
satisfies the non-hatted
(2k+1)
-gon equation if and only if
T\bar{\sigma}_{k}
satisfies the
(2k+1)
-gon equation under our convention. As for the
2k
-gon equation, one needs to additionally dualize the convention and compose with
\bar{\sigma}_{k}
. That is, if
T
satisfies the non-hatted
2k
-gon (resp. dual non-hatted
2k
-gon) equation if and only if
T\bar{\sigma}_{k}
(resp.
T\bar{\sigma}_{k-1}
) satisfies the dual
2k
-gon (resp.
2k
-gon) equation under our convention.
For example, the non-hatted pentagon equation is
T_{12}T_{23}T_{12}=T_{23}P_{12}T_{23}

(
P
is the flip), whereas our case, i.e., the hatted version, is
T_{12}T_{13}T_{23}=T_{23}T_{12}
.

This approach also provides a systematic construction of the mixed relation, from which we obtain non-constant versions of Theorems
6.7
and
6.8
.

B.1.
Mixed relation

Let
\{T(\partial_{0}\Delta^{n+1}),\ldots,T(\partial_{n+1}\Delta^{n+1})\}
be a (non-constant) solution of the
(n+2)
-gon equation and
\{S(\partial_{0}\Delta^{n+1}),\allowbreak\ldots,S(\partial_{n+1}\Delta^{n+1})\}
be a (non-constant) solution of its dual. They are labeled with the
n
-simplices of
\Delta^{n+1}
as before. Then one side of the mixed relation is obtained by placing, from top to bottom, the maps

S(\partial_{0}\Delta^{n+1}),\;T(\partial_{1}\Delta^{n+1}),\;\ldots,\;\begin{cases}S(\partial_{n+1}\Delta^{n+1})&\text{if $n$ is odd,}\\
T(\partial_{n+1}\Delta^{n+1})&\text{if $n$ is even,}\end{cases}

composing them along the matching labeled outputs and inputs, and arranging the remaining free outputs and free inputs, each in reverse lexicographic order. The other side is obtained by placing, from bottom to top, the maps

T(\partial_{0}\Delta^{n+1}),\;S(\partial_{1}\Delta^{n+1}),\;\ldots,\;\begin{cases}T(\partial_{n+1}\Delta^{n+1})&\text{if $n$ is odd,}\\
S(\partial_{n+1}\Delta^{n+1})&\text{if $n$ is even,}\end{cases}

and repeating the same procedure. Examples are shown in Figure
6
.

Non-constant versions of Theorems

Now, given a mixed pair of (non-constant) solutions
\{T(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\}_{i=0}^{n+1}
and
\{S(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\}_{i=0}^{n+1}
above, one can construct (non-constant) solutions of the
(n+1)
- and
n
-simplex equations as follows:

For
i=0,1,\ldots,n+1

R(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\coloneq\sigma\circ(S(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\otimes T(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1}))\circ\tau,

where
\sigma
permutes the labeled outputs of
S(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\otimes T(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})
to the labeled outputs of
R(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})
, and
\tau
permutes the labeled inputs of
R(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})
to the labeled inputs of
S(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\otimes T(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})
, i.e.,

\sigma=\begin{pNiceMatrix},\quad\tau=\begin{pNiceMatrix}.

(See Figure

\{R(\partial_{i}\Delta^{n+1})\}_{i=0}^{n+1}
is a solution of the
(n+1)
-simplex equation.

Q(p)

n

Let
q=[v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}]

(n-1)

\Delta^{n}

Q(q)
is labeled. We set
q^{\prime}\coloneqq[0,v_{1}+1,v_{2}+1,\ldots,v_{n-1}+1]

n

\Delta^{n+1}

R(q^{\prime})

\partial_{1}q^{\prime}\mapsto\partial_{0}q,\;\partial_{2}q^{\prime}\mapsto\partial_{1}q,\ldots,\;\partial_{n}q^{\prime}\mapsto\partial_{n-1}q

V^{\otimes n}\to V^{\otimes n}

Q(q)

n
