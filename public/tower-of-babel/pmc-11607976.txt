Ventricular premature beats in a pregnant woman originating from trabeculae carneae in the right ventricle treated with radiofrequency ablation without X-ray guidance: a case report
Authors: Tao Li, XianYue Li, LiRong Zhou, Muhammad Arslan Ul Hassan, Zhen Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPremature ventricular beats increase the cardiac load in pregnant women, leading to symptoms, such as palpitations. In severe instances, they may reduce placental perfusion, potentially causing intrauterine distress and developmental delays in the fetus, which could result in miscarriage or stillbirth. Fluoroscopy-less ablation offers significant advantages for pregnant women, including reduced radiation exposure, enhanced treatment precision, and a lower incidence of postprocedure complications.Case PresentationThis case describes how radiofrequency catheter ablation was used on a pregnant Chinese woman who was identified with frequent ventricular premature beats originating from the high septal margin trabeculae carneae of the right ventricle. The rarity of this premature beat focus poses challenges for electrophysiology mapping and ablation; however, the ablation ultimately succeeded. In this case report, the clinical manifestations, electrocardiogram, ablation procedure, and patient prognosis are reported and discussed.ConclusionX-ray free ablation clearly reduces radiation exposure while maintaining a high success rate and safety of the ablation procedure.

Background

Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) is an effective treatment for ventricular arrhythmias, but traditional procedures rely on X-ray fluoroscopy, which carries radiation risks. RFCA without X-ray guidance technology uses an advanced three-dimensional calibration system, reduces radiation exposure, and has unique application value, especially in pregnant patients [1]. Pregnant women are more sensitive to X-ray radiation because of their special physiological state, and premature ventricular beats may affect the health of mothers and children, requiring timely and effective treatment [2]. Ventricular premature beats originating from right ventricular trabeculae are more difficult to ablate because of the complexity of the anatomical structure [3]. The application of X-ray-free guidance provides a new treatment option. This case report shares the experience of RFCA without X-ray guidance in treating pregnant women with premature ventricular beats originating from the right ventricular trabeculae carneae, with the aim of providing a reference for clinicians and advancing the development of this technique. With the advancement of technology, X-ray-free guided RFCA is expected to become a safer and more effective treatment in the future.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old Chinese woman was hospitalized with a history of intermittent palpitations for 1 year and one episode of syncope. At 1 year prior, she experienced palpitations, chest pain, dizziness, headache, and amaurosis without apparent cause, and no diagnosis or treatment was provided. Then, 1 month prior, the patient experienced an episode of dizziness, syncope, and incontinence without any obvious reason. The 24-hour Holter Monitor at the local hospital revealed a total of 49,420 premature ventricular beats (PVBs), accounting for 32% of the total heart beats. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed an enlarged left atrium (anteroposterior diameter of 40 mm) and left ventricle (end-diastolic diameter of 55 mm), and a normal ejection fraction (65%). Upon admission, the patient exhibited overall fair health and was in the 21st week of gestation. The other medical history, including personal history, family history, allergic history, and trauma history, was normal. The examination at admission revealed an enlargement of the heart boundaries toward the lower left, an irregular cardiac rhythm, and the absence of any murmur. Frequent PVBs not only significantly affected the patient’s quality of life but also potentially represented a risk to the future delivery. Since onset, the patient had neither taken nor expressed willingness to take any medication to suppress ectopic beats. Thus, RFCA was recommended. A multidisciplinary team including obstetricians also recommended RFCA, and obstetricians participated in the whole process of ablation. The patient and her family signed informed consent for RFCA.

The surface electrocardiogram (ECG) indicated that the QRS waveforms were positive in leads II and aVF, negative in lead III, and QS pattern in V1-2. In addition, the R wave transition of the chest leads was between leads V4 and V5. All of these ECG features supported the deduction that the PVBs are likely to originate from the tricuspid annulus (Fig. 1). During the procedure, lidocaine was administered as a local anesthetic, after which a guidewire was inserted under the guidance of ultrasound through the femoral vein to the superior vena cava (SVC), after which a right 0 long sheath was delivered into the right atrium. Under the guidance of the Carto system, the tricuspid annulus was first mapped, and the earliest local electrogram was noted around the tricuspid annulus, which was only 15 ms earlier than the initial point of the QRS waveforms in the body surface ECG. The catheter was subsequently inserted further into the high septal region, and the local electrogram at the site of trabeculae carneae was found to be 33 ms earlier than the body surface QRS, with a unipolar signal of QS type. Radiofrequency was delivered at a power of 35 W, and a temperature limit of 43 °C. Then, 5 seconds after radiofrequency delivery, the PVCs disappeared, and the ablation was continued for 240 seconds (Fig. 2). No PVCs were detected during the 30-minute observation period. Discussion

Studies have demonstrated that frequent PVBs can contribute to the development of cardiomyopathy and heart failure, as well as the progression of preexisting cardiomyopathy. However, these conditions are often reversible with timely treatment [4]. Additionally, PVBs can increase the burden on a pregnant woman’s heart or even lead to heart failure [5]. The young woman in this case had a history of syncope. TTE revealed left ventricular enlargement, indicating the early stage of dilated cardiomyopathy. However, the specific pathophysiological process behind the development of left ventricular dysfunction caused by PVBs remains unclear. This may be secondary to the asynchronous contraction of ventricles and ventricular remodeling produced by PVBs.

In this case, the operator prioritized minimizing or completely avoiding intraoperative radiation exposure. Ultrasound guidance was employed to advance the guidewire into the superior vena cava, enabling secure placement of the catheter into the right atrium. Simultaneously, the Carto system facilitated accurate reconstruction of the cardiac chambers, with particular emphasis on the regions of interest. A fetal radiation dose under 50 mGy is considered safe and does not result in adverse effects [6]. The procedure was conducted without X-ray exposure, demonstrating the feasibility of catheter ablation for PVBs in pregnant women without fluoroscopy. This method adheres to the strict safety standards for both the mother and the fetus, highlighting the feasibility of non-fluoroscopic catheter ablation in pregnant patients.

As mentioned above, the patient’s preoperative surface electrocardiogram initially suggested that the PVB site was likely located around the tricuspid annulus, but no ideal ablation target was found around it. The catheter continued to extend to the ventricular site and was found to be a suitable and effective target at the septum edge of the trabeculae carneae, indicating that the body surface electrocardiogram could be used only as a rough guiding tool rather than a definitely error-free approach to determine the originating focus and ablation target of the PVBs. Owing to the high anatomical variability of the intracavitary structure of the right ventricle (similar to the intracavitary structure of the left ventricle), precise PVB localization via surface electrocardiograms is often difficult, especially because the incidence here is relatively rare [7]. Finally, accurate intraoperative mapping was required to identify the exact ablation target.

Limitations remain in this case. Owing to the high cost, the patient refused to undergo intracardiac echocardiography (ICE). It is a powerful imaging modality for guiding electrophysiology and structural heart interventions. Real-time ICE imaging has an expanding role in providing uninterrupted guidance for catheter-based ablation for cardiac arrhythmia [8]. However, in this case, ablation was successfully completed at the trabeculae carneae mapped by Carto without the use of ICE.

For further reference, the use of ICE can significantly lower risks, shorten procedure time, reduce complications, and increase success rates for patients with arrhythmia requiring ablation. ICE has increased in importance in cardiac interventions in recent years because of its significant advantages in improving procedural safety, reducing patient discomfort, lowering costs, and minimizing radiation exposure [9].

This case report differs from previous literature primarily in its focus on the specific population of pregnant women, employing a multidisciplinary approach involving obstetrics and other departments to minimize fetal impact, an aspect not heavily emphasized in previous studies. Additionally, the right ventricular septal trabecular anatomy is complex, yet the patient underwent successful ablation surgery without the use of ICE owing to financial constraints, which is rare. However, we strongly advocate for the use of ICE, as it is beneficial for the success of the procedure and the reduction of postoperative complications [10]. We believe that ICE is effective in surgery because of its greater safety and efficacy, enabling real-time dynamic observation of cardiac structures, especially in complex anatomical areas. Furthermore, ICE can improve surgical success rates and reduce complications by displaying subtle cardiac anatomical landmarks. Finally, ICE can shorten the surgical time, reduce radiation exposure, and be more beneficial for special populations, such as pregnant women.

Conclusion

This case report describes the successful application of X-ray-free guided RFCA for the treatment of premature ventricular beats of the right ventricular trabecular carneae in a pregnant woman. The technique demonstrated the clear advantage of reducing radiation exposure while maintaining a high success rate and safety. The patient recovered well from the procedure without complications, confirming the clinical value of this approach in specific clinical situations. This successful experience supports the prioritization of X-ray-free guided radiofrequency ablation as a viable option for the treatment of ventricular arrhythmias in pregnant women or other radiation-sensitive patient populations. Future studies should focus on evaluating the long-term efficacy and generalizability of this technique to further optimize arrhythmia treatment strategies.
