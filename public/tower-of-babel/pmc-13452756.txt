Pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma (involving skin, bone and soft tissue): a case report with long-term follow-up and review of literature
Authors: Maysam Moghaddam Amin, Ákos Sükösd, Imre Antal, Zoltán Sápi, Zsuzsanna Pápai, Miklós Szendrői
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma is rare, usually indolent vascular tumor characterized immunohistochemically by SERPINE1-FOSB fusion, presenting as multiple bone and soft tissue lesions, affecting males in their 3rd–4th decades.Case presentationWe present the case of a 28-years-old male patient with the history of pain in the left lower extremity hindering him in the daily activities. Radiological examinations showed well-defined lytic lesions of the femur, patella, tibia, fibula, talus and calcaneus with low metabolic activity on PET-scan. MR revealed multiple soft tissue involvements of the affected extremity. Multiple skin lesions were located on the extensor surface of the leg. Molecular analysis confirmed the pathognomonic gene fusion. Patient was treated with epirubicin as an eight-cycle locoregional intraarterial monotherapy, achieving clinical control of the disease and substantial improvement in complaints for over 10 years. The patient’s complaints decreased substantially, his bony lesions, however, are persisting for over ten years of his follow-up.DiscussionThe molecular characteristics and differential diagnostics of the disease is well known, however, due to the rarity of the disease, the wide array of treatment methods and short-term follow-up intervals, there is still no consensus over the most efficient treatment plan. Many chemotherapeutic agents and treatment regimens were utilized throughout the literature, with acceptable outcomes as far as reported but self-limiting character and spontaneous regression of the disease have also been described according to our experience during the very long follow-up period of our patient.Keywords: Pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma, Epithelioid sarcoma-like hemangioendothelioma, Vascular tumor, Bone lesion, Epirubicin, Treatment options

Introduction

Pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma (PMHE) is an extremely rare neoplasm of vascular origin arising from bones and soft tissues. Al-Qaderi et al. collected only 82 reported cases from the literature until 2019 [1]. According to the 5th edition of WHO Classification of Tumors of Soft Tissue and Bone (2020), PMHE is a distinctive, rarely metastasizing endothelial neoplasm that occurs mostly in young adult males (average age is around 30 years with a male to female ratio of 4:1) [2, 3]. It usually arises as multiple (in half of the cases very painful) nodules with a tumor size between 1–2,5 cm in the bone (about 25%), soft tissue and skin in one of the lower extremities (60%) [4, 5]. Histological diagnosis is often challenging, and the long-term prognosis is unclear. Due to the rarity and various clinical presentation of PMHE, there is still no consensus concerning the optimal treatment until present time. The follow-up time of the presented cases in the literature differs, are usually short or midterm. The aim of this study is to present a case of PMHE with bone, soft tissue and skin involvement treated by short term chemotherapy and a long-term follow-up for over ten years. We also present the different treatment possibilities and their efficacy based on the literary data.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male patient without any relevant medical anamnesis was referred to our Hungarian tertiary center for bone and soft tissue sarcomas in January 2014 with an 8-months history of swelling and worsening pain of the left lower extremity making him unable to walk without support. Major analgesics were needed to tolerate the constantly prevailing pain. In the skin of the extensor surface of the leg brownish maculo-papulous nodules were seen with a size of 0.3 to 1 cm (Fig. 1). Plain radiographs and CT-scan revealed multiple well defined lytic lesions without a sclerotic rim in the distal metaphysis (18 × 10 × 6 mm) and medial condyle (20 × 20 × 30 mm) of the femur, in the patella (15 × 10 × 15 mm) and the proximal meta- and diaphysis (7 × 9 × 16 mm) of the tibia and fibula, and smaller lesions in the talus and calcaneus (Fig. 2). Some lesions were located intracortically, occasionally penetrating into the surrounding soft tissues. The lungs and abdominal organs were not affected. MRI examination revealed nodules in the same places signaling low on T1 weighted and inhomogeneously signaling high on T2 weighted images and STIR sequences especially following gadolinium administration. Additionally, multiple nodules were observed on the MR images intracutaneously and in the gastrocnemius muscles. The other leg and abdominal organs showed no sign of disease. PET-CT findings presented low isotope uptake in the identical places and provided no additional information to MRI (Fig. 3).

Based on the findings by the imaging techniques, we considered different diseases for differential diagnosis. Clinically, brown tumor caused by hyperparathyroidism was ruled out by a negative parathormone test. Metastatic bone disease and multiple myeloma were considered unlikely because of the distant unilateral localization, the negative laboratory tests and negative PET-CT result for an underlying primary cancer. More likely, a vascular tumor came into consideration with bone, soft tissue and skin manifestation. Open biopsy was performed from the subcutaneous nodules, muscle and bone. Macroscopically the soft tissue nodules were firm and appeared brownish gray on the cut surface. Microscopically the tumor tissues originating from different tissues shared the same appearance: they were composed of sheets, loose bundles and fascicles of elongated spindle cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm often resembling rhabdomyoblasts. The tumor cells contained large vesicular nuclei with pronounced small nucleoli. In some areas, neutrophil granulocyte infiltration was also present (Fig. 4a). A minor component of cells with epithelioid cytomorphology could also be found. The mitotic activity and cellular atypia were low.

Using a broad spectrum of immunochemistry panels, tumor cells were negative for S-100, CD34, SMA, desmin, H-caldesmon, Myf-4, EMA, CAMTA1 and stained strong positively for vimentin, Pan-CK and for the vascular markers CD31, ERG and CK AE1/AE3, and FOSB. The positive staining for SMARCB1/INI1 were retained. The results of immunochemistry especially the FOSB positivity, underlined the diagnosis pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma (Fig. 5).

From the surgical point of view only an above-knee amputation could have been taken into consideration because of the wide dissemination of the tumor (distal femur, leg and tarsal bones), which option was refused by the patient, and we considered it as the last resort as well. Therefore, according our onco-team decision, the patient received an eight-cycle locoregional intraarterial monochemotherapy. 80 mg epirubicin was administered eight times in two week-interval into the femoral artery without any complication. At the time of treatment (2014), no established systemic therapy for pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma existed because of the rarity of the disease. Epirubicin was selected because anthracyclines represented one of the most commonly used cytotoxic agents for soft tissue sarcomas, with a toxicity profile considered more favorable than that of doxorubicin. Regional intra-arterial administration was chosen to maximize local drug exposure while reducing systemic toxicity.

During the next 6 months following the chemotherapy, the pain and swelling of the leg and ankle decreased substantially and the nodules in the skin diminished. Control MRI pictures revealed cystic lesions in same size and number in the bones (Fig. 3) and slight shrinking of the nodules in the muscles and no lesions in the skin.

Our patient returned to daily activities using nonsteroid antiphlogistic drugs only occasionally. Walking support was not anymore necessary. In the remaining 9 years of follow up, MRI was performed two times yearly and staging CT-scan once a year. According to the CT and MRI reports, the lesions in the bone and soft tissue are still present in same size and number as before. There are no distant metastases in the lung and abdomen, and the other lower extremity is still unaffected.

Discussion

The first possible description of a pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma (PMHE) occurred by Mirra et al. in 1992 who published 5 morphologically similar tumors which were considered a fibroma-like variant of epithelioid sarcoma with indolent behavior [6]. Billings et al. introduced the name epithelioid sarcoma-like hemangioendothelioma (ESLH) based on 7 cases, where the tumors had an epithelioid character histomorphologically, showed, however, signs of vascular differentiation with the use of different immunohistochemical stains [7]. Hornick and Fletcher introduced first the name pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma in 2011 based on a clinicopathological study of 50 patients to emphasize its myoid-like histopathologic appearance, pointing out, however, at the same time the vascular differentiation of the tumor by immunohistochemical methods [2]. Now, there is a consensus that ESLH and PMHE are the same entities.

The immunohistochemistry panel for PMHE is well described in the literature and was also characteristic in our case (positive staining for CD31, ERG and CK AE1/AE3, and FOSB) [8, 9]. Of particular interest is the fusion of the SERPINE1 and FOSB genes which seems to be pathognomonic for PMHE and was also detected in our case [10–12]. There are, however, other myogenic or vascular tumors which can mimic the histomorphology of PMHE or have an overlapping immunohistochemistry. We could exclude epithelioid hemangioendothelioma where the nuclear expression of CAMTA1 is very characteristic, in our case was lacking, as CD 34 which is usually positive in benign hemangiomas [13, 14]. The SMARCB1/INI1 are inactivated (negative staining) in epithelioid sarcoma and rhabdoid sarcomas, in our case, however, was retained. It is important to exclude the frankly malignant epithelioid sarcoma (ES) with high tendency for local recurrence and metastasis, and a reported 10-year survival rate between 17 and 55% [15]. Both PMHE and ES affect young patients and have a predilection for distal extremities. PMHE is different, however, as it affects mostly one site and appears in multiple nodules often affecting different tissues (soft tissue, bone, skin). Both tumors show histologically epithelioid and spindle cell morphology, diffuse expression of keratins, some epithelioid sarcomas are positive for FLI1 and ERG and CD34 and typically negative for FOSB. Epithelioid sarcoma shows grater cell atypia, mitotic activity and sometimes necrotic areas are also present.

PMHE should also be distinguished from myogenic tumors because of the histological similarity in most cases. The negative staining for smooths muscle markers, such as caldesmon, desmin and SMA and positive staining for vascular markers (CD31, FLi1, ERG) and FOSB helped us in the differential diagnosis.

According to the literature, the treatment of PMHE varies in a broad spectrum from amputation through chemotherapy to simple observation. Reason for it is the extreme rarity of the disease and the very different symptoms from case to case [16–18]. In half of the cases the tumor nodules are painless, and the patient can walk with full weight bearing. In our case, the patient needed major analgesics and was unable to weight his swollen left lower extremity.

Maximen et al. reported a case of spontaneously regressive multifocal PMHE that showed signs of regression after 1 year and was stable throughout the following year [19].

Otani et al. and Pasricha et al. used receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) inhibitor denosumab to prevent bone destruction caused by PMHE. Although denosumab did not show a definitive anti-tumor effect, it helped restrain bone destruction and relieve clinical symptoms [20, 21]. Since reactive osteoclast-like giant cells have been seen in more than 60% of PMHE cases [16], they are believed to be activated by FOSB-positive tumor cells and are responsible for the lytic lesions [20]. Brance et al. utilized pamidronate and reported clinical improvement of complaints [22].

Ozeki et al. and Huang et al. treated PMHE using mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor everolimus and were able to achieve total metabolic regression without any significant adverse effects at a daily dose of 5 mg and the disease remained well controlled at 10 and 21 months [23, 24]. Gabor et al. and Danforth et al. reported the utilization of another mTOR inhibitor sirolimus, the latter combined it with zoledronic acid, also with appropriate results [25, 26]. Alhanash et al. utilized tyrosine kinase inhibitor pazopanib achieving favorable clinical and radiological response [27]. Van IJzendoorn et al. reported the use of telatinib, another tyrosine kinase inhibitor, and were able to achieve complete remission [28].

Mirra et al. reported 5 cases from 1973 to 1988 named the fibroma-like variant of epithelioid sarcoma (which today is considered the same as PMHE): one patient received multidrug chemotherapeutic regimen consisting of doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine and methotrexate achieving complete regression of the disease during a follow-up period of 13 years [6]. Another patient received doxorubicin combined with radiation therapy, after surgical excision [6]. A retrospective multicenter study by Cai et al. analyzed the use of gemcitabine and docetaxel and reported satisfactory efficacy and acceptable safety with a 1-year progression-free survival rate of 80%, and disease control rate of 100% [29].

Davis et al. utilized radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for treating the bone lesions with the surgical excision of the skin nodules which made the patient tumor-free for the 2-years’ observation period [30].

Hornick et al. examined reviewed the cases of 50 patients, 46 patients were treated with local excision even in patients with multiple lesions, and four patients went through amputation for extensive and multifocal disease. Surgical margins were positive in 23 patients that undergone excision. Eight patients received postoperative radiation therapy, and six patients received chemotherapy [2] (Table 1).

In summary, the histopathological characteristics and thus the differential diagnosis of PMHE is well documented, however, given the rare occurrence, diverse treatment modalities and relatively short follow-up intervals reported, it is still challenging to define an ideal treatment regimen. Our patient received epirubicin eight times intraarterially. This drug was not reported to be used for the treatment of PMHE by other in papers published so far. Although the complaints decreased and the intraosseous lesions were stabilized thereafter, we cannot unambiguously state that all this was the effect of epirubicin. Our patient is still not free of disease, although the skin lesions have diminished, the bone lesions are stable but still persisting.

According to the published cases in the literature, pseudomyogenic hemangioendothelioma can appear as a less serious disease which needs only observation to more expressive form with intensive clinical symptoms where even amputation was taken into consideration. However, being aware of the usually indolent behavior and at least the good outcome with conservative treatment, amputation is not recommended. Surgical excision of the skin nodules tends to be a safe approach in most of the cases. The role of chemotherapy in treatment of PMHE is not clarified yet, further studies are required for evaluating the need of systemic chemotherapeutic treatment, since their positive effect is not proven beyond any doubt. Considering that spontaneous regression has also been reported in the literature, we cannot state securely that achieving control over disease was directly related to the effect of chemotherapy. Biological therapy (denosumab) and bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid) seem to be efficient in relieving symptoms caused through bone destruction and might be a safer, less aggressive agent than chemotherapeutic regimens. Moreover, minimally invasive surgical techniques, such as radiofrequency (RF) ablation of bone lesions tend to be a promising and safe alternative of pharmaceutical management.

Given the potential self-limiting property of PMHE, the long-term disease control observed in this case cannot independently confirm a causal therapeutic effect of epirubicin. Additional evidence-based data should be collected from more comparable patients with prominent clinical symptoms who refuse aggressive interventions in future studies.

Since the treatment of PMHE remains undefined, other agents including doxorubicin-based chemotherapy, gemcitabine/docetaxel, mTOR inhibitors (everolimus, sirolimus), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (pazopanib, telatinib), denosumab and bisphosphonates have all been reported with encouraging results in isolated cases or small series. Therefore, no standard first-line chemotherapeutic regimen can currently be recommended.

The present report should be interpreted in light of its inherent limitation as a single-case study. Although the patient was followed for more than 10 years with serial MRI and CT examinations, the findings cannot be generalized to the broader PMHE population. In addition, imaging assessment was based on routine clinical follow-up rather than standardized quantitative response criteria or volumetric measurements, which limits objective evaluation of subtle changes in tumor burden. Further multicenter studies with standardized imaging protocols are needed to better define the optimal management of this rare neoplasm.
