Association of sensory impairment severity and assistive device use with prevalent and incident motoric cognitive risk syndrome
Authors: Fengjuan Hu, Gongchang Zhang, Hu Liu, Tingting Huang, Shuli Jia, Wanyu Zhao, Ning Huang, Chenying Fu, Birong Dong, Meiling Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is growing evidence that the sensory impairments in visual and/or hearing are associated with dementia, however, the association with relationship between sensory impairments, assistive device use, and motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR) remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between sensory impairment and MCR, and to assess the impact of sensory impairment severity and assistive devices.DesignA prospective cohort study.MethodsParticipants aged ≥ 60 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) in 2011 and 2015 waves were included in the current study. Sensory impairments were assessed from self-reported questionnaire. MCR was defined as subjective cognitive complaints combined with slow gait speed. Participants were categorized by sensory function and assistive device use. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between sensory impairments, assistive device use, and MCR risk.ResultsAmong 4882 participants, the overall prevalence of MCR was 11.92%, with 80.66% having visual impairment and 63.03% having hearing impairment. Cross-sectionally, visual and hearing impairments were associated with 1.59-fold (95% CI: 1.06–2.38) and 1.62-fold (95% CI: 1.17–2.24) higher odds of MCR, respectively. Longitudinally, severe sensory impairments significantly increased MCR risk (visual: OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.20–3.86; hearing: OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.29–3.04). Sensory impairment participants without assistive device showed increased risk of new-onset MCR (visual: OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.09–3.71; hearing: OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.30–2.96) but no significant association was detected in participants with visual assistive device.ConclusionsThis study highlights the significant association between sensory impairments and MCR in older Chinese adults, with severity of impairment and lack of assistive device use exacerbating risk. The findings underscore the importance of early detection and intervention for sensory deficits to mitigate cognitive decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06829-1.Keywords: Sensory Impairment, Motoric Cognitive Risk Syndrome, Assistive Device, China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)

Introduction

The rapid acceleration of global population aging has significantly increased the burden of dementia. By 2050, it is projected that the number of individuals with dementia worldwide will reach 152.8 million [1]. Dementia not only imposes a heavy burden on individuals and their families but also presents substantial challenges to healthcare systems [2]. In recent years, motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome has emerged as a novel pre-dementia syndrome that has garnered increasing attention. MCR is defined as the presence of subjective cognitive complaints and slow gait speed in individuals without dementia or mobility disorders [3]. Research indicates that MCR affects approximately 9% of older adults globally [4] and is significantly associated with cognitive decline, dementia, falls, disability, and increased mortality risk [5]. Notably, about 30% of individuals with MCR may progress to dementia [6]. Therefore, MCR serves as a simple and cost-effective screening tool with important clinical applications.

Sensory impairments, particularly visual and hearing impairments, are prevalent among older adults and have been identified as significant risk factors for cognitive decline and dementia [7]. Age-related hearing loss (ARHL) is one of the most common sensory impairments in older adults [8]. ARHL involves multiple pathological mechanisms, including degeneration of cochlear hair cells (sensory presbycusis), reduced function of the stria vascularis (metabolic presbycusis), and auditory nerve degeneration (neural presbycusis), often presenting as a mixed pathology [9]. ARHL leads to communication difficulties, social isolation, and cognitive decline. Recent evidence highlights that ARHL is one of the most modifiable risk factors for dementia [10]. The strain of listening effort in challenging situations or with impaired hearing systems consumes cortical resources, potentially altering brain responses to cognitively demanding tasks [11]. Similarly, visual impairments can lead to reduced participation in social activities, lower physical activity levels, and increased risk of falls, all of which contribute to cognitive decline [12].

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of sensory impairments in cognitive decline, studies specifically examining the relationship between sensory impairments and MCR in older Chinese adults remain limited. This study aims to investigate the association between sensory impairments (visual and hearing) and MCR in older adults using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). By examining the severity of visual and hearing impairments, we investigate how the severity of these sensory impairments influences MCR prevalence and assess the potential of assistive devices in reducing MCR risk. Our results will contribute significant insights into the impact of sensory impairments on MCR and offer practical recommendations for early intervention and prevention strategies for older adults.

Methods

Study population

The data for this study were derived from the CHARLS, a longitudinal research project aimed at investigating health and economic adaptations in response to China’s rapidly aging population. CHARLS has been described in detail elsewhere [13]. In brief, we utilized wave1 as baseline and wave3 as follow-up data to examine the relationship between sensory impairment and the incidence of MCR [14].

We included an initial sample of 4882 participants aged 60 years and older at baseline in 2011. Participants with pre-existing physical disabilities, mental retardation, or memory-related disease at baseline were excluded from the study [14]. Furthermore, individuals identified as having MCR at baseline, those who were lost to follow-up, and those who did not complete the MCR assessment in 2015 were also excluded from the longitudinal analyses. Consequently, the final sample for the longitudinal analysis consisted of 2995 participants. The detailed selection of study population is illustrated in Fig. 1. In addition, the prevalence of sensory impairments and the use of assistive sensory devices were evaluated during the baseline survey and the follow-up surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, and 2018.

The study flow

MCR syndrome

MCR was defined as the coexistence of subjective cognitive complaints and slow gait speed in the absence of a history of dementia or mobility disability [3]. Subjective cognitive complaints were assessed by asking “How would you rate your memory at the present time?” Respondents were classified as having cognitive complaints if they answered “fair” or “poor” out of five options (excellent, very good, good, fair or poor). Slow walking speed was defined as one standard deviation or less of the mean of two 2.5-m walking speeds stratified by gender and age (< 75 years, ≥ 75 years). Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the slow walking speed cutoff values for specific gender and age groups in wave1 and wave3, which were similar to previous studies [14, 15].

Sensory impairment

Sensory impairment was identified by self-reported visual and hearing functions. Visual function was assessed using 2 questions: “How good is your eyesight for seeing things at a distance?” and “How good is your eyesight for seeing things up close?” Responses are scored on a five-point scale ranging from “excellent” to “poor”. Data on hearing functions were also collected using the question: “Is your hearing excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” Respondents were classified as having sensory impairment if they answered fair or poor vision (for either long distance or near vision) or hearing. Dual sensory impairment (DSI) was defined as the concurrent presence of both visual impairment and hearing impairment. The methodology for evaluating and classifying these sensory impairments in our study aligns closely with protocols established in prior research within the same framework [16, 17].

The use of sensory assistive device was assessed using 2 questions “Do you ever wear a hearing aid?” (yes/no) and “Do you usually wear glasses or corrective lenses?”. For visual assistive device use, three choices were given in wave1 including (1) Yes; (2) Legally blind; (3) No, while another choose (4) Sometimes was added in wave2-wave4. Participants who answered legally blind were not further assessed visual function. Respondents were classified as using assistive devices if they answered Yes or Sometimes in wave2-wave4 [18]. Then we categorized respondents as I: normal sensory function without assistive devices; II: normal sensory function with assistive devices; III: sensory impairments with assistive devices; IV: sensory impairments without assistive devices.

Covariates

Covariates of this study included age, gender (male/female), body mass index (BMI), residence (urban/rural), marital status (married or partnered/spouse absent/never married), education (illiterate/unfinished or finished primary school/middle school/high school and above), smoking status (never/former/current smoke), drinking status (never/former/current drink), physical activity level (active/less active/sedentary), and chronic disease number (0/1–2/≥ 3) [19–21].

The spouse absent status in marital status refers to divorced, separated, widowed statuses or married but not living with spouse temporarily. For physical activity level, we calculated total physical activity volume (PAV) by metabolic equivalent (MET), PAV = 8.0 × weekly vigorous physical activity duration + 4.0 × weekly moderate physical activity duration + 3.3 × weekly low intensity physical activity duration [22, 23]. Physical activity level was classified by total PAV score as active (≥ 1500 MET-minutes/week), less active (600–1499 MET-minutes/week), and sedentary (< 600 MET-minutes/week) according to previous studies [24, 25]. The number of chronic diseases was calculated using the following self-reported conditions: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer of malignant tumor, chronic lung diseases, liver disease, heart problems, stroke, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive disease and arthritis or rheumatism.

Statistics

Characteristics are presented as number (percentage) for categorical variables, median (Interquartile range, IQR) for abnormally distributed variables. Baseline characteristics of sensory impairment and covariates were compared using Mann-Whitney U test for abnormally distributed variables and Pearson’s Chi-squared test for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association of sensory impairment and MCR. Subgroup analyzes stratified by sensory impairment level and sensory assistive device use were performed. Two models were conducted in all regression analyses. Model 1 adjusted for age, gender, BMI. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for residence, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity level, and chronic disease burden. The results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX), and two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Forest plot and bar graphs were drawn using the “forestploter” and “ggplot2” R package.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study population

The baseline characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. Among the 4882 participants, 3938 (80.66%) had visual impairment and 3077 (63.03%) had hearing impairment, revealing significant differences across several key characteristics. Individuals with either visual or hearing impairments were more likely to reside in rural areas (p < 0.05), have lower educational levels (p < 0.001), and suffer from multiple chronic diseases (p < 0.001). There was a significant gender difference in the prevalence of visual impairment, with a higher proportion of females affected (p < 0.001). Participants with hearing impairment were significantly older (p < 0.001) and showed different patterns in smoking and drinking habits (p < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in marital status or physical activity levels between those with visual and hearing impairments. Participants with sensory impairment had a significantly higher MCR prevalence than those with normal sensory function (visual impairment: 12.65% vs. 8.90%; hearing impairment: 13.68% vs. 8.92%).

Cross-sectional associations between visual impairment, hearing impairment and MCR

Table 2 presents the associations between sensory impairments and MCR in the cross-sectional study. After adjustment of all covariates, visual and hearing impairments were associated with 1.59-fold and 1.62-fold higher odds of MCR, respectively (both p < 0.05). In Model 1, the prevalence of MCR varies depending on the severity levels of visual and hearing impairments, with further increases observed as these impairments worsen from “fair” to “poor” (both p < 0.05). After adjusting for additional variables in Model 2, the increased prevalence of MCR follows a similar trend: “poor” visual impairment was associated with 1.88-fold higher odds (95% CI: 1.22–2.90, p < 0.01), “fair” hearing impairment with 1.53-fold higher odds (95% CI: 1.08–2.16, p < 0.05), and “poor” hearing impairment with 1.84-fold higher odds (95% CI: 1.22–2.76, p < 0.01) of MCR. Compared to participants with no sensory impairments, those with DSI had significantly higher odds of MCR (OR 2.25, 95% CI: 1.29–3.90, p < 0.01) after full adjustment in Model 2. In contrast, neither visual impairment alone nor hearing impairment alone showed statistically significant associations with MCR. Associations between baseline covariates and MCR are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Cross-sectional associations between sensory assistive device use and MCR

Figure 2 presents the associations between sensory assistive device use and MCR. For visual impairment, compared to the reference group, only group IV (visual impairment without assistive devices) showed a significantly increased risk of MCR (model 1: OR 1.44, 95% CI: 1.11–1.86; model 2: OR 1.57, 95% CI: 1.02–2.41; both p < 0.05). For group II/III (normal or visual impairment with assistive devices), no significant differences in MCR prevalence are observed in either model 1 or model 2. For hearing impairment, we combined group II and III due to the small sample size of MCR patients, and the prevalence of MCR was not significantly different compared with the reference group. Group IV (hearing impairment without assistive devices) experienced a significantly increased odds of MCR in both models (model 1: OR 1.55, 95% CI: 1.28–1.88; model 2: OR 1.60, 95% CI: 1.16–2.22; both p < 0.01).

Longitudinal associations between sensory impairments and MCR

The 4-year incidence of MCR was 11.72%. As shown in Table 3, both visual and hearing impairment at baseline significantly increased the risk of developing MCR during the follow-up period (visual impairment: OR 1.81, 95% CI: 1.04–3.15, p < 0.05; hearing impairment: OR 1.94, 95% CI: 1.29–2.92, p < 0.01). In the analysis of severity, after full adjustment, individuals with fair or poor hearing had a higher MCR risk compared to those with normal hearing (fair: OR 1.85, 95% CI: 1.09–3.16, p < 0.05; poor: OR 1.98, 95% CI: 1.29–3.04, p < 0.01). In contrast, a significant association was observed only for poor visual impairment (OR 2.15, 95% CI: 1.20–3.86, p < 0.05). DSI was significantly associated with an increased risk of MCR (OR 2.18, 95% CI: 1.11–4.26, p < 0.05), whereas neither visual impairment alone nor hearing impairment alone showed a significant association. Furthermore, given that sensory impairment is a time-varying exposure, we performed an additional analysis examining the association between sensory impairment transitions (“wave1→wave2” and “wave1→wave3”) and incident MCR at wave 3. The results indicated that, compared to participants with normal hearing throughout, those with persistent hearing impairment had significantly higher odds of incident MCR (persistent hearing impairment (wave1→wave2): OR 3.05, 95% CI: 1.60–5.82, p < 0.01; persistent hearing impairment (wave1→wave3): OR 4.37, 95% CI: 2.05–9.31, p < 0.001). In contrast, no consistent or significant associations were observed for transitions in visual impairment (Supplementary Table 4).

Longitudinal impact of assistive devices use on MCR

The longitudinal associations between sensory assistive devices use and MCR over 4-years of follow-up are presented in Fig. 3. After adjusting all covariates, individuals with sensory impairments who do not use assistive devices (category IV) exhibit a significantly increased risk of MCR (visual impairment: OR 2.01, 95% CI: 1.09–3.71, p < 0.05; hearing impairment: OR 1.96, 95% CI: 1.30–2.96, p < 0.01). For participants with visual assistive devices (category II/III), no significant odds ratio was detected compared with those without visual impairment. Due to the insufficient sample size for MCR (only 1 out of 15 participants) in categories II/III, the analysis for hearing impairment was not conducted. These results underscore the importance of assistive devices in mitigating MCR risk for individuals with sensory impairments.

Prevalence of sensory impairments and utilization of assistive devices

Figure 4 illustrates the prevalence of sensory impairments and the utilization rates of assistive devices among participants aged ≥ 60 years in the CHARLS study. Due to the absence of sensory impairment assessments in 2020, we analyzed data from wave1 (2011) to wave4 (2018). The results show that, despite no significant increase in the mean age of the cohort (wave1: 68.30 ± 6.89, wave2: 68.44 ± 7.02, wave3: 68.50 ± 7.06, wave4: 69.16 ± 7.15, years), the prevalence of single sensory impairment and DSI increased annually (Supplementary Table 5). However, the utilization rates of assistive devices remain relatively low, with visual assistive devices ranging from 18.2% to 38.4% and hearing assistive devices from 0.8% to 1.5% across the four survey waves. Among individuals with DSI, assistive device utilization was consistently low, ranging from 15.1% to 34.4% across the study waves.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide important insights into the association between sensory impairments, assistive device use, and MCR syndrome among older adults in China. Our analyses reveal that both visual and hearing impairments were significantly associated with increased prevalence and risk of MCR, with the severity of these impairments playing a critical role. Notably, DSI and persistent hearing impairment demonstrated a particularly strong association with MCR in our current study. Furthermore, the use of assistive devices appears to mitigate the risk of MCR, underscoring the potential benefits of early intervention and the importance of addressing sensory deficits in aging populations.

In our study, the baseline prevalence of self-reported visual (80.66%) and hearing (63.03%) impairments was high, consistent with prior research conducted among older adults in China, highlighting the substantial burden of sensory deficits in this population [16]. Against this backdrop, the overall prevalence of MCR at baseline was 11.92%. Our results also reveal significant demographic and socioeconomic disparities among older adults with sensory impairments. The higher prevalence of visual impairments among females and hearing impairments in older age groups may reflect biological and age-related vulnerabilities, as supported by previous research [26]. Additionally, the prevalence of functional sensory impairments increases with age, with 1 in 7 adults aged 90 years or older reporting functional DSI [27]. Furthermore, the overrepresentation of individuals with sensory impairments in rural areas and those with lower educational attainment underscores the role of socioeconomic barriers in accessing timely healthcare and assistive technologies. This aligns with findings from another study [28], which noted that rural residents often face limited healthcare infrastructure and financial constraints, exacerbating the burden of sensory impairments. Additionally, the association between sensory impairments and multiple chronic diseases suggests a potential bidirectional relationship, where sensory deficits may exacerbate the management of chronic diseases, and vice versa [29]. However, while our findings are consistent with existing literature, some studies have reported conflicting results regarding gender disparities in hearing impairments, possibly due to variations in occupational noise exposure or cultural factors [30]. These discrepancies highlight the need for further investigation into the interplay between biological, environmental, and socioeconomic determinants of sensory health in aging populations.

Visual and hearing impairments are significantly associated with an increased risk of MCR. This finding aligns with multiple international studies, which suggest that sensory impairments affect cognitive health through multiple mechanisms [16]. Sensory deficits may increase cognitive load, as individuals must expend greater effort to process impoverished auditory or visual signals. This increased cognitive demand may manifest as subjective cognitive complaints, which are a key component of MCR [31]. The impact of visual impairment on cognitive function may be mediated through the following pathways: First, the decline in visual function directly impairs an individual’s ability to perform basic activities of daily living, such as reading, using smartphones and computers, and preparing meals can result in person-environment misfit, causing difficulty in handling daily tasks even at familiar environment [32]. Second, the accumulation of allostatic load over time may exacerbate cognitive decline by affecting neuroendocrine system function [33]. Third, visual impairment is often accompanied by behavioral changes, such as reduced physical activity, increasing fall risks and reducing social engagement which collectively contribute to cognitive deterioration [34]. These mechanisms are supported by previous work which highlighted the role of sensory deficits in accelerating cognitive aging [35]. Regarding auditory function, we found that hearing loss is also significantly associated with an increased risk of MCR. This finding echoes the conclusions of prior research which identified hearing loss as a modifiable risk factor for dementia [36]. Globally, 65% of adults over 60 years have hearing loss, and epidemiologic studies have linked hearing loss to nearly two times greater risk of dementia [37]. Hearing loss diverts greater cognitive resources to processing degraded auditory signals, leaving fewer resources available for other cognitive functions like memory and executive function [38]. The increased cognitive load, along with structural changes in the brain including reduced gray matter volume in regions important for memory and attention, contributes to the development of MCR [39]. The mediating role of brain structural changes and tau pathology in the relationship between hearing impairments and cognitive decline provides a novel perspective for understanding this complex association [40]. For individuals with DSI, these pathways may interact synergistically, creating a particularly challenging environment for maintaining cognitive health. These findings underscore the importance of early identification and intervention for sensory impairments, particularly in underserved populations. Future research should focus on developing effective strategies to improve the accessibility and adherence to assistive devices, as well as designing integrated interventions for individuals with multiple sensory impairments. Furthermore, the co-occurrence of visual/hearing impairments and cognitive decline may also relate to conditions like susac syndrome, an autoimmune vasculopathy causing these symptoms, which should be considered clinically [41]. Moreover, previous research has highlighted the significant impact of combined sensory and cognitive impairments on functional limitations. Individuals with both DSI and dementia experience higher levels of functional limitations, particularly in self-care and household activities [42]. Co-occurrence of multiple sensory and motor impairments may further exacerbate cognitive decline, highlighting the importance of considering their cumulative impact on cognitive health [35].

Studies have demonstrated that individuals who use assistive devices for sensory impairments can experience significant improvements in their quality of life and cognitive function. For instance, a previous study found that hearing aid users exhibited better cognitive performance compared to non-users, likely due to enhanced auditory input and reduced cognitive load [43]. Similarly, research on vision correction has shown that the proper use of glasses or other visual aids can improve daily functioning and reduce the risk of falls and injuries, which indirectly supports cognitive health [44]. Despite these potential benefits, the utilization rates of assistive devices remain starkly low. Globally, only about 17% of individuals with hearing loss use hearing aids, a proportion that is even lower in low- and middle-income countries [45]. In parallel, fewer than 45% of individuals with visual impairment use appropriate glasses or other visual aids [46]. Our study found that the utilization rates for visual assistive devices ranged from 18.2% to 38.4%, while those for hearing assistive devices ranged from 0.8% to 1.5% across the four survey waves. Among participants with DSI, assistive device usage also remained suboptimal, fluctuating between 15.1% and 34.4% during the study period. Notably, in wave2 (2013), an additional “Sometimes” option was introduced for assessing visual assistive device use, which may explain the higher usage rate observed in 2013 compared to 2011. However, from 2013 to 2018, there was no significant increase in the utilization rates of visual assistive devices. This stagnation underscores the urgent need to enhance awareness and accessibility of these devices, particularly among rural and less educated populations. A community health worker–delivered hearing care intervention, including the provision of low-cost amplification devices, significantly improved self-perceived communication function among older adults with hearing loss [47]. Public health actions to improve sensory care access, such as increasing the availability of affordable hearing aids and expanding medicare coverage for sensory rehabilitation services, may help reduce MCR risk among older adults with sensory impairments [36].

This study uses of a large, nationally representative sample from the CHARLS study, the longitudinal design provides robust evidence for the temporal relationship between sensory impairments and MCR. However, several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, sensory impairments and assistive device use were based on self-report, which may introduce reporting bias; future studies would benefit from objective measures of sensory function [48]. Second, as our study focused on older Chinese adults, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited. Third, the low utilization rate of hearing aids restricted our ability to robustly evaluate their potential to mitigate MCR risk, highlighting the need for larger studies with more comprehensive device usage data. Finally, despite adjusting for a range of covariates, residual confounding could persist due to variations in assistive device use patterns and baseline characteristics among participants with sensory impairments.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a strong association between sensory impairments and MCR in older Chinese adults, with severe impairments and lack of assistive device use significantly increasing the risk. Addressing sensory health through early detection, timely interventions, and improved access to assistive technologies is crucial for mitigating cognitive decline in this population. Future research and policy efforts should focus on enhancing sensory health to promote better cognitive outcomes in older adults.
