Refractory nausea in an 86-year-old patient after surgery

An 86-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with 12 days of persistent nausea, poor oral intake, and weight loss after an elective left total knee arthroplasty. Her medical history included Crohn disease (diagnosed 3 years previously, well controlled on vedolizumab), 2 remote episodes of Clostridioides difficile colitis, hypothyroidism, psoriasis, and prior laparoscopic left colon resection and cholecystectomy. Her home medications included levothyroxine (125 μg daily), amitriptyline (50 mg daily), doxepin (20 mg nightly), and vitamin supplements. She had a 10 pack-year history of smoking but had quit 26 years ago. She had no history of alcohol or substance use.

The total knee arthroplasty had been performed under adductor canal and spinal block with conscious sedation using intravenous propofol. The patient was given intravenous dexamethasone (2 mg) and ondansetron (4 mg) intraoperatively. Postoperatively, she ambulated well, tolerated food, and was discharged home the following day. Postoperative analgesia included oral acetaminophen (650 mg every 6 h), celecoxib (100 mg twice daily), and hydromorphone (1 to 2 mg every 4 h as needed); she received 4 doses of oral hydromorphone for a total of 6 mg before discharge. After discharge, she used minimal opioid doses and subsequently discontinued hydromorphone within the first few days because of nausea.

On postoperative day 3, the patient had 2 episodes of nonbilious, nonbloody emesis, followed by persistent nausea without further vomiting. She had been having regular bowel movements since surgery, and had no sick contacts, travel history, vertigo, or fever. She had no previous history of persistent postoperative nausea. By postoperative day 15, she had substantial difficulty maintaining oral intake, was losing weight, and presented to hospital. In the emergency department, her vital signs were normal apart from mild tachycardia. The physical examination, including abdominal and digital rectal examinations, was normal. She received intravenous fluids, ondansetron (4 mg), and metoclopramide (10 mg) without any improvement. Her laboratory investigations showed a hemoglobin of 105 (reference 120 to 160 g/L) g/L, down from 124 g/L preoperatively; normal electrolytes and renal function; mildly elevated ketone (1.5 [reference ≤ 0.5] mmol/L), C-reactive protein (37 [reference ≤ 11] mg/L), alkaline phosphatase, and γ-glutamyl transferase levels; normal lipase levels; and 2 negative troponin results. Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 107 beats/min. Stool testing for C. difficile and cultures were negative. Urinalysis showed ketones and leukocytes without nitrites. Subsequent cortisol testing at 8 am was 923 (reference 101 to 536) nmol/L, which ruled out adrenal insufficiency. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis showed an unremarkable hepatobiliary system, no bowel obstruction, no substantial fecal loading, uncomplicated diverticulosis, and diffuse, mild pancolonic wall thickening without acute inflammatory changes. Head CT and magnetic resonance imaging of the patient’s brain showed no acute intracranial pathology or posterior circulation infarction.

The patient was admitted to our internal medicine service for management of her persistent nausea. A gastroenterologist saw her in consultation and suggested postoperative gastroparesis, functional dyspepsia, anesthetic-related nausea, or medication effects from opioids or antidepressants as possible causes. We treated her with scheduled intravenous ondansetron (4 mg, 3 times daily), intravenous metoclopramide (10 mg, 3 times daily), oral doxylamine–pyridoxine (10 mg each, twice daily, as needed) intravenous dimenhydrinate (25 mg every 6 h), a single dose of intravenous dexamethasone (4 mg, as needed) and oral haloperidol (1 mg every 6 h), alongside dietary modification and upright positioning with small meals. Despite these interventions and extensive work-up, her nausea did not improve over 11 days of admission, and she lost 7.25 kg in total.

Through a review of case reports, we identified postdischarge nausea and vomiting (PDNV) as a possible cause of the patient’s symptoms and that aprepitant is sometimes an effective medication for managing PDNV. On day 12 of admission, we administered a single dose of aprepitant (80 mg orally). Within 4 hours, she experienced a marked improvement in nausea and was able to eat a full dinner. We administered 2 additional doses of aprepitant over the next 2 days, after which she resumed normal oral intake, and we discharged her home without needing further antiemetic prescriptions. When we saw her 2.5 weeks later in follow-up at the internal medicine outpatient clinic, her weight had remained stable, her appetite had normalized, and her nausea had not recurred.

Discussion

Postdischarge nausea and vomiting can occur anywhere from 24 hours to 1 week after hospital discharge, often occurs after surgery, and can vary in severity.1 Although immediate postoperative nausea and vomiting is well described, less is known about PDNV, even though it can occur in 10% to 45% of outpatients.1–3 In general, postoperative nausea and vomiting is often considered to occur within 24 hours after surgery, while PDNV occurs after 24 hours, sometimes termed “late PDNV” or “delayed PDNV.”1 Nausea in isolation can be a challenging presentation to diagnose, given the wide variety of underlying causes, but differential diagnosis can be simplified systematically into neurologic, gastrointestinal, infectious, medication-related, or metabolic causes. Apfel and colleagues developed a risk score for PDNV based on 5 risk factors: female sex, age younger than 50 years, history of nausea or vomiting after previous anesthesia, opioid administration in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU), and nausea in the PACU.5 One point is assigned for each risk factor, yielding a total score from 0 to 5. Corresponding PDNV risks for scores of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 are 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%, 60%, and 80%, respectively.2,5 This patient had only 2 risk factors according to this scoring system (female sex, postoperative opioids), giving her an estimated 30% risk of PDNV.5 Interestingly, although this patient had a history of smoking, nonsmoking status is considered a risk factor only for postoperative nausea and vomiting and not an independent risk factor for PDNV (Table 2).1 A separate study found that risk factors for PDNV developing on days 3 to 7 after discharge were different than those in the first 3 days, with history of postoperative nausea and vomiting, operating room time, use of ondansetron, and pain as the strongest predictors of PDNV between days 3 to 7.3 The use of ondansetron, however, is likely to be associated with nausea in the PACU, and not itself a contributor to PDNV. Regardless, our patient would have been classified as low risk even with this modified schema.

In postoperative nausea and vomiting, the focus is on prevention and prophylaxis of nausea in the postoperative setting, while current, although limited, evidence for PDNV supports the use of multimodal antiemetics.1,2,5 A recent review found better results with combined antiemetic therapy than monotherapy.6 Aprepitant combined with ondansetron has shown greater efficacy than ondansetron alone in several clinical studies.2,5–7 A systematic review and meta-analysis found no difference in the incidence of PDNV when comparing propofol to inhalational anesthesia, highlighting a unique difference between postdischarge and postoperative nausea and vomiting.8 When a patient develops nausea and vomiting early in the postoperative period (i.e., postoperative nausea and vomiting), the prophylactic management of PDNV on discharge is guided by the presence of risk factors for PDNV.1 For patients with no risk factors, a first- or second-generation 5-hydroxytryptamine-3 (5-HT3) antagonist such as ondansetron is recommended.1 For patients with moderate risk (1 to 2 risk factors), a first- or second-generation 5-HT3 antagonist such as ondansetron or a neurokinin-1 (NK-1) antagonist such as aprepitant can be prescribed.1 Lastly, if a patient has 3 or more risk factors, they are deemed to be high risk and should receive an NK-1 antagonist (e.g., aprepitant 40 mg orally for 1 dose).1 This guidance is intended to reduce the risk of continued nausea and vomiting after discharge (i.e., PDNV); however, no consensus guidelines for the management of delayed PDNV exist.1

Generally, PDNV is thought to be caused by postoperative opioid administration and subsequent μ-opioid pathway activation, which increases signalling through substance P and NK-1 receptors, amplifying the brain’s emetic response.1 Aprepitant is a selective NK-1 receptor antagonist with long-lasting effects owing to its slow receptor dissociation rate,9 which may explain its effectiveness in this clinical scenario, when other antiemetics had no response. Similarly, a case report by Tran and colleagues described that an intravenous NK-1 antagonist, fosaprepitant, was effective in resolving refractory postoperative nausea and vomiting on postoperative day 1 after ineffective trials of ondansetron, metoclopramide, and prochlorperazine.10 Neurokinin-1 antagonists for postoperative nausea have a unique mechanism of action at one of the final steps of the nausea pathway. This highlights the importance of trialling antiemetics acting on different nausea pathways when managing refractory nausea.

We describe a presentation of intractable nausea requiring readmission to hospital more than 2 weeks after total knee arthroplasty under regional anesthesia and propofol sedation. Clinicians must rule out potentially life-threatening causes of nausea but also consider PDNV if a patient presents several days after surgery. Treatment principles should be based on trialling antiemetic agents that target different nausea pathways. Furthermore, appropriate patient education and postoperative follow-up to assess for nausea are essential to early detection and management. Given its prevalence and substantial impact on patients, more research is needed to investigate the prevention and management of PDNV.
