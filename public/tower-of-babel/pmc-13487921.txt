Multimodal algorithmic fusion model for physical education assessment: spatiotemporal comparison of inertial measurement units and traditional scales
Authors: Junlin Cheng, Zhen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional physical education assessment depends on subjective teacher observation and simple rating scales, often resulting in subjective bias, low evaluation efficiency, and delayed instructional feedback.MethodsThis study constructed a hierarchical multimodal fusion framework integrating inertial measurement unit (IMU) data and expert rubric scores for standardized PE skill evaluation. The framework adopts Dynamic Time Warping for spatiotemporal alignment to resolve asynchrony between continuous sensor signals and discrete manual scoring, and applies adaptive gated fusion to balance modality weights according to data quality. A total of 3,920 skill samples from 245 middle-school students across three schools and four sports were independently annotated by three calibrated PE teachers, achieving high inter-rater reliability (ICC(2,k) = 0.87, 95% CI [0.84, 0.90]). All experiments were conducted with participant-wise data splitting and ten repeated random-seed trials to ensure result stability.ResultsThe proposed multimodal model achieved an overall accuracy of 91.3 ± 0.4%, significantly surpassing single-modality baselines (p < 1 × 10⁻⁸). Spatiotemporal alignment effectively eliminated temporal mismatch, and the model maintained stable performance under simulated data missingness and sensor failure. The 43.2 ms single-sample inference speed meets real-time classroom assessment demands. Ablation experiments verified the essential role of cross-modal attention in multimodal learning. Leave-one-school-out and cross-stratum evaluations confirmed stable performance across different schools, genders, age groups and skill levels. The current multi-validation results support reliable within-population model performance, while external validation with independent cohorts is still required for broader generalizability. Model interpretability analysis further validated the biomechanical rationality of the learned assessment patterns.ConclusionsThis study provides a feasible multimodal digital assessment pipeline for standardized and formative physical education evaluation. The system intelligently reproduces expert-level teacher scoring rather than objective biomechanical measurement, which makes it a powerful auxiliary tool for classroom teaching rather than a substitute for professional teacher judgment. This work offers a practical digital transformation pathway for PE assessment, with standardized curriculum alignment and staged teacher professional development supporting reliable real-world educational deployment.Keywords: Physical education assessment, Multimodal fusion, Inertial measurement unit, Spatiotemporal alignment, Leave-one-school-out validation, Formative assessment, Model interpretability

Introduction

As physical education develops towards scientification and precision, traditional subjective assessment methods have become inadequate for modern PE teaching’s need for comprehensive and objective evaluation of motor skills and physical fitness. In large-scale teaching scenarios, teachers’ assessments based on experiential observation and simple scales often suffer from strong subjectivity, low efficiency, and delayed feedback, failing to capture subtle changes in students’ movement processes. Beyond these technical limitations, traditional approaches struggle to provide the timely, granular feedback that formative assessment theory identifies as central to motor skill acquisition. There is an urgent need for more scientific, efficient, and precise assessment models that fuse multi-source heterogeneous data while remaining embedded in established pedagogical practice rather than displacing it.

Inertial Measurement Units (IMU) have been widely adopted in recent years for sports biomechanics analysis, injury risk prediction [1] and team performance evaluation [2]. Advances in multimodal fusion technology have also improved the reliability of human activity recognition by fusing sensor data with other auxiliary information. Nevertheless, these technical achievements have not been fully applied to school-based PE assessment, where diverse movement types and teacher-compiled rubrics create unique constraints for existing algorithms.

Four key research gaps remain when current technologies are adapted for school PE assessment. First, most existing studies focus on competitive sports, clinical gait analysis and injury monitoring, which follow highly standardized movement protocols. In contrast, school PE covers a wide range of task types, such as explosive movements (standing long jump), repetitive exercises (sit-ups) and high-intensity sprints. Few fusion models can dynamically adjust modality weights to adapt to such diverse curriculum-based tasks. Second, current IMU-based scoring systems rarely address the temporal mismatch between continuous sensor signals and intermittent manual scoring, a time offset measured at 312 ms in this study. Third, rigorous evaluation criteria widely used in clinical multimodal research, including inter-rater reliability verification, participant-based data splitting and repeated trials with multiple random seeds, are rarely implemented in PE-related IMU studies. Fourth, most technical research lacks in-depth discussion on pedagogical application. It remains unclear how teachers can use model outputs in formative assessment and how to optimize assessment workflows to enable automated tools to support, rather than override, teachers’ professional judgment. Digital competency frameworks for PE teachers [3] have already identified model interpretability as a core requirement for technology adoption, but relevant discussions are still insufficient.

Addressing these four gaps simultaneously requires more than algorithmic engineering. The resulting system must preserve, rather than displace, the professional judgment of the PE teacher, and its outputs must remain interpretable within established formative assessment practice, particularly in school settings where curriculum-aligned rubric criteria carry both pedagogical and administrative weight. We therefore position the present work at the intersection of sensor-fusion engineering and PE assessment pedagogy, treating teacher rubric annotations not as a ground-truth signal to be replaced by sensing, but as expert-calibrated evidence to be integrated with sensor signals under quantifiable inter-rater uncertainty.

From a pedagogical standpoint, the proposed system is anchored in two considerations that shape its engineering specification. First, the corrective value of formative feedback decays sharply once the lesson in which the movement was performed has ended, so the sub-50 ms inference target is a precondition for the system to enter the formative loop. Second, technology-mediated PE assessment becomes pedagogically legitimate only when teachers retain the capacity to override automated outputs, requiring the model to expose its reasoning. The cross-modal attention weights and per-feature attributions in Sect. "Interpretability and Explainability Analysis" make the model’s reasoning visible; the gated fusion in Sect. "Fusion Model Construction" makes explicit the relative weighting of sensor evidence versus expert rubric. As discussed in Sect. "Implications", the transformation of PE teachers from post-class raters to in-class assessment collaborators is an important principle throughout our development.

Based on the above research status, this paper proposes a multimodal algorithmic fusion model for physical education assessment, constructing a spatiotemporal comparative analysis framework by integrating objective motion data from IMU sensors with subjective expert knowledge from traditional assessment scales.

To make the scope of this work concrete, we test three research hypotheses. H1 (fusion gain): a hierarchical fusion of IMU signals with digitized rubric scores yields measurably higher classification accuracy than either modality used alone under participant-wise splitting. H2 (robustness): the resulting model retains useful accuracy under 30% data missingness and under structurally distinct sensor-failure modes. H3 (transferability): the fusion gain transfers across institutions, quantified by leave-one-school-out evaluation.

The contributions of this work are integrative rather than algorithmically foundational. Core modules including DTW alignment, hierarchical attention and gated multimodal fusion have been widely used in related fields. Our main novelty lies in the targeted adaptation of these mature techniques to the unique demands of PE assessment. Specifically: (i) we propose a PE-oriented hierarchical attention fusion framework that explicitly models the asynchrony between continuous sensing and discrete human scoring; (ii) we integrate DTW alignment with gated fusion that adaptively reweights modalities under varying data quality; (iii) we conduct a systematic evaluation with participant-wise splitting, multi-seed training, inter-rater reliability analysis, and per-component ablation; (iv) we position the work at the intersection of sensor-fusion engineering and PE assessment pedagogy, articulating how the system can be embedded in a formative-assessment loop, what new teacher competencies its deployment implies, and how automated outputs can strengthen rather than substitute for teacher diagnostic judgment.

Related work and theoretical foundation

Evolution of physical education assessment methods

Physical education assessment has transitioned from subjective empirical judgment towards objective quantitative evaluation. Traditional approaches rely on teachers’ observation and experience, which lack repeatability and verifiability; Venek et al. [4] confirmed insufficient inter-rater reliability across such methods. The conceptual framing has further shifted from a summative paradigm toward a formative paradigm in which the value of an assessment instrument depends not only on measurement accuracy but also on feedback latency and teacher-readable interpretive structure [5]. Three pedagogical prerequisites have accordingly emerged for any technology-enhanced PE assessment system: in-lesson feedback latency, teacher-readable interpretive structure, and alignment with curriculum-prescribed rubric dimensions. With the maturation of wearable sensing, multi-sensor systems now quantify movement quality along kinematic, kinetic, and physiological dimensions [6], and markerless motion capture has been validated for adolescent populations [7].

The convergence of IoT and AI has advanced intelligent PE assessment. While existing studies have focused on real-time motion metrics, wearable-based injury prevention, and IoT monitoring frameworks [8, 9], they largely overlook pedagogical implementation [10–12]. Few works systematically explore how teachers adopt platform outputs, align analytical results with curriculum rubrics, and retain professional judgment in final assessment, which motivates the pedagogy-oriented integration proposed in this study.

Applications of inertial measurement units in motion analysis

Inertial Measurement Units have become core tools for sports biomechanics owing to their miniaturization, low cost, and portability. Liang et al. [13] demonstrated IMU breakthroughs in motion capture precision; Donisi et al. [14] and Kobsar et al. [15] systematically reviewed IMU validity and reliability in gait analysis, confirming centimetre-level accuracy across diverse populations [16, 17].

In sport-specific technique analysis, Wang et al. [18] captured angular velocity, acceleration, and motion trajectory features in football kicking; Benson et al. [19] reviewed laboratory-to-field validity of IMU-based gait analysis; and Taborri et al. [20] surveyed sensor applications across biomechanics. These foundations support the use of IMU streams as the kinematic backbone of multimodal PE assessment.

Multimodal data fusion algorithms

Multimodal fusion provides the algorithmic framework for integrating heterogeneous sensor data. Wang et al. [21] proposed multi-level fusion for team-sport dynamics; Kline et al. [22] surveyed multimodal machine learning in clinical settings, identifying early/intermediate/late fusion strategies and their trade-offs.

Deep learning has driven recent performance gains. Huang et al. [23] compared early, intermediate, and late fusion strategies for medical decision-making; Baltrušaitis et al. [24] formalized the taxonomy of multimodal representation learning, including attention-based fusion. Subsequent surveys [25–27] consolidated fusion-architecture design principles, with consistent evidence that learned cross-modal attention outperforms hand-engineered combinations when training data is sufficient and modality reliability varies.

Digital transformation of traditional assessment scales

Digitizing traditional assessment scales is a key route to improving assessment efficiency. Gil-Espinosa et al. [28] applied the MobilLife framework with smartphone apps for adolescent PE engagement; this digital-instrument line has matured the rubric-data capture side of the problem but has not been algorithmically coupled to continuous IMU streams in the manner pursued here.

Wearable technology plays a central role in this transformation: Mateo-Orcajada et al. [29] validated ActiGraph-class accelerometers against laboratory-grade systems with high agreement, and Wilkie et al. [30] demonstrated the feasibility of games-based digital tools for physical literacy assessment in school children.

Synthesis and research gap

Existing research on PE assessment falls into four separate strands: assessment method development, IMU-based motion analysis, multimodal fusion algorithms and digitalized rating scales. These areas have evolved independently, without effective integration. To our knowledge, no prior study has combined all four aspects into a unified PE framework supported by standardized methodology and explicit pedagogical guidance.

To make the gap explicit, Table 1 summarizes ten representative recent studies along six dimensions. Four patterns emerge. First, transparency standards now common in clinical multimodal learning are infrequently reported in IMU-based sports work. Second, no prior study simultaneously combines explicit modelling of continuous–discrete temporal mismatch, PE-tailored adaptive fusion across heterogeneous movement types, and participant-wise multi-seed evaluation. Third, the digital-assessment line has not been algorithmically coupled to IMU streams. Fourth, the pedagogical interpretation layer is largely absent from the IMU–PE fusion literature, despite digital competency frameworks identifying it as a precondition for sustained adoption.

This study integrates mature algorithms to address practical PE assessment demands. Although DTW temporal alignment, hierarchical attention, and gated multimodal fusion have been widely applied in other fields, we adapt these techniques to the unique constraints of school PE scenarios, such as diverse curriculum movements, quantifiable inter-rater discrepancies in teacher scoring, and real-time feedback requirements under variable data quality. We further improve methodological transparency, which is insufficient in current PE-IMU studies. Unlike purely technical research, this work prioritizes pedagogical rationality throughout system design, with its innovation rooted in scenario-oriented integration and rigorous validation rather than original algorithmic development.

Multimodal fusion model design

To facilitate understanding for readers without sensor fusion expertise, we first illustrate the system’s conceptual framework. This hierarchical neural architecture integrates two core information sources: continuous IMU streams that capture kinematic features (acceleration, angular velocity, and joint angles) and discrete teacher-derived rubric scores.

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) achieves temporal alignment of heterogeneous data. The model extracts salient individual-modality features via an attention mechanism and adaptively fuses the two streams through a gated cross-modal layer according to real-time data quality. The network is end-to-end optimized with a multi-task loss function for joint classification and regression learning.

System architecture and data acquisition scheme

The proposed multimodal fusion assessment system adopts a hierarchical architecture that implements the complete data-acquisition, preprocessing, alignment, fusion, and assessment pipeline. Six IMU nodes (MTw Awinda) sample at 100 Hz from the trunk, both wrists, and both ankles; one additional node monitors gross body motion at the waist. The teacher-facing rubric application runs on a 10-inch tablet and digitizes a four-tier grade rubric covering technical execution, performance, form, and effort dimensions, with the criterion weighting calibrated against the participating schools’ existing assessment standards. All sensor data are transmitted via 2.4 GHz wireless link to a local edge server, where the preprocessing, alignment, and inference modules operate on-premise to avoid round-trip cloud latency.

From a practical deployment perspective, the system requires only commodity 2.4 GHz connectivity and a single consumer-grade edge server (Intel i5/16 GB RAM); no specialized infrastructure is needed. The full preprocessing-to-prediction pipeline runs comfortably within the single-lesson timeframe described in the workflow scenario below, and the IMU nodes can be donned during the routine equipment check that begins each lesson.

Beyond hardware compatibility, the architecture is deliberately organized around the existing rhythm of the PE lesson rather than asking the lesson to reorganize around the system. A typical 45-minute teaching cycle in the participating schools consists of warm-up (5 min), skill practice (25 min), assessment (10 min), and cool-down (5 min); the proposed system operates transparently across all four phases. During warm-up, IMU nodes are donned as part of the routine equipment check (no additional class time). During skill practice, the system silently buffers sensor data. During assessment, the teacher enters rubric scores while the model concurrently produces a tier prediction with calibrated confidence (Sect. "Classification Accuracy Analysis"), which the teacher may inspect, accept, or override before finalizing. During cool-down, the teacher can optionally review per-feature attribution (Sect. "Interpretability and Explainability Analysis") for students with largest model–teacher disagreement. The architectural intent is that teacher diagnostic agency is amplified rather than constrained; the role-reshaping argument is developed in Sect. "Implications".

To make the preprocessing pipeline auditable and preempt information-leakage concerns, three precautions were enforced. First, all normalization statistics (per-feature mean, standard deviation, MAD) were computed exclusively from the training partition; validation and test partitions used these training-only statistics. Second, the participant-wise split was applied before any preprocessing, so that within-subject temporal correlations could not leak between train and test. Third, the data-augmentation routines (small Gaussian noise injection, time-axis jitter ± 5%) were applied only at training time, never at validation or test. These precautions are reflected in the code release described in the Data Availability section.

Experimental results and analysis

Experimental setup and dataset

Experimental data were collected at three middle schools covering four sports, including basketball dribbling, standing long jump, sit-ups, and 50 m sprint. These activities represent explosive, periodic and maximal-effort movements commonly adopted in middle-school PE courses. We recruited 245 students aged 13–15 years, and obtained 3,920 samples over a 12-week data collection period. The dataset was split by participant, with 172 students for training, 49 for validation and 24 for testing.

Crucially, the participant-wise split prevents within-student leakage: no student appears in more than one subset. Because each student contributes up to sixteen samples across the collection window, a sample-wise split would have allowed the model to memorize within-student motion idiosyncrasies and inflate reported accuracy. The split was held fixed across all ten random seeds so that variability in reported results reflects model stochasticity rather than data-resampling variance.

The data distribution reflects the design’s rigor: each sport contributes exactly 980 samples (245 students × 4 sessions across the 12-week window), avoiding class imbalance, and the per-sport sample counts in training, validation, and test partitions follow the participant-wise split ratio exactly. The IMU sensor configuration uses the MTw Awinda system, comprising six inertial measurement units fixed at the trunk, both wrists, and both ankles, plus one auxiliary node at the waist for gross-motion monitoring. Each unit samples at 100 Hz, recording 3-axis accelerometer, 3-axis gyroscope, and 3-axis magnetometer signals. The wireless transmission protocol is 2.4 GHz, with measured packet loss below 0.5% across the 12-week collection window.

The annotation workflow used a four-step protocol designed to maximize reliability of the supervisory signal. Step 1: three PE teachers (≥ 5 years’ experience) attended a two-day calibration workshop, jointly scoring 80 reference videos until pairwise ICC exceeded 0.85. Step 2: every collected sample was independently scored by all three teachers, blinded to one another’s scores and student identifiers. Step 3: the median of the three scores became the consensus label; the 165 samples (4.2%) where max-minus-min exceeded 1.5 rubric points were adjudicated by a fourth expert from a provincial PE evaluation panel. Step 4: all 3,920 consensus labels were locked before any model training began.

Inter-rater reliability among the three PE teachers was quantified before any model training. Each teacher scored every sample independently and blinded; the resulting ICC(2,k) under a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement was 0.87 (95% CI [0.84, 0.90]), and Fleiss’ κ for the four-tier discretization was 0.72 (95% CI [0.69, 0.75]). Handling of repeated measures

Because each student contributed up to sixteen samples across the twelve-week window, the participant-wise split removes between-set leakage at the student level but does not by itself rule out the possibility that the model exploits within-student periodicity. We therefore conducted three supplementary analyses on the same participant-wise split.

Linear mixed-effects validation. We refit the fusion-versus-IMU accuracy comparison using a linear mixed-effects model with student identity as a random intercept and modality and sport as fixed effects (lme4 in R). The fixed-effect estimate of the fusion-over-IMU gain was 8.4 pp (95% CI [7.8, 9.0]), essentially unchanged from the marginal estimate of 8.6 pp. The corresponding fusion-over-scale gain was 11.7 pp (95% CI [11.0, 12.4]), confirming the fusion gain is not absorbed by within-student clustering.

Single-sample-per-student subsample. We constructed a stratified subsample retaining only one randomly chosen session per student per sport ( = 245 × 4 = 980 samples; 686 / 196 / 98 train/validation/test) and repeated 100 times. The mean fusion accuracy across these 100 subsamples was 90.6 ± 0.9%, within one SD of the headline 91.3%, and the ranking of fusion against every baseline was preserved in every subsample.

Intra-student prediction consistency. The within-student standard deviation of predicted scores averaged 0.31 on the 0–10 scale across repeated sessions, while the between-student SD reached 1.42 — a ratio confirming that the model tracks session-to-session variation in a structured way rather than collapsing repeated sessions to a within-student mean. The direction of cross-week change was predicted with 78.4% agreement.

Taken together, these three analyses indicate that the repeated-measures structure of the dataset does not inflate the reported fusion gain materially, and that the participant-wise split is sufficient as the primary safeguard against within-student leakage.

Leave-one-school-out evaluation and cross-stratum generalization

We adopted leave-one-school-out (LOSO) evaluation to assess cross-institution generalizability and confirm that the model can conduct valid skill assessment across different schools, rather than simply memorizing within-school data. The evaluation contained three folds: in each trial, the model was fully retrained on data from two schools and tested on the third, following the architecture, random seed settings and training rules specified in Sect. "Implementation details and hyperparameters". According to Table 6, the fusion model achieved a mean accuracy of 88.1 ± 1.3% across folds. While this was 3.2 pp lower than the within-domain result of 91.3%, it remained 5.4 pp higher than the IMU-only baseline (82.7%) and 8.7 pp higher than the scale-only baseline (79.4%).

Discussion and implications

Discussion

Using a PE dataset with 245 students and 3,920 samples, we found hierarchical multimodal fusion outperformed single-modality methods by 8.6 pp (IMU-only) and 11.9 pp (scale-only). This overall outcome supports further analysis on fusion mechanisms, sport-specific performance and model robustness for real-world deployment.

Why fusion outperforms either modality alone

Experimental results confirm that the fusion model outperforms single-modality methods, with performance differences closely related to assessment criteria. Table 15 reveals uneven contributions from IMU signals and teacher rubrics across sports. IMU data dominates evaluation for explosive and maximal-effort movements, including standing long jump and 50 m sprint, which focus on mechanical performance. For coordination-based tasks like basketball dribbling, however, rubric scores carry greater weight, as IMU cannot effectively assess movement form and rhythm. This phenomenon verifies the modality complementarity principle in multimodal learning. The accuracy improvement observed in this study stems from such complementarity instead of redundant information.

Sport-specific patterns in the learned fusion weighting

Table 15 lists attention weights across different sports, and the related pedagogical analysis carries greater value than raw weight data. For basketball dribbling, the score stream reaches a maximum weight of 0.42. This task requires teachers to judge rhythm, ball-hand coordination and changing posture, all of which lie beyond the capture capability of IMU sensors. The model accordingly recognizes the limitations of single-sensor data. For standing long jump, the IMU weight is 0.68, matching the rubric focused on mechanical outcomes, where peak vertical acceleration serves as an indicator of take-off impulse. In short, variations in modality weighting across sports are determined by evaluation dimensions, rather than arbitrary model design.

The role of temporal alignment in fast movements

The spatiotemporal alignment algorithm synchronizes heterogeneous data effectively. It cuts the average time offset between IMU streams and scoring records from 312 ms to 18 ms, marking a 94.2% reduction. Benefiting from precise temporal matching, model predictions avoid distortion brought by temporal smear and interference from neighboring movement data. According to ablation results, removing cross-modal attention reduces accuracy by 7.8 pp, and removing DTW alignment leads to a 4.7 pp drop. The two modules work collaboratively without information redundancy.

What the model actually learns: bounding the ground-truth assumption

It is critical to clarify the essence of the model’s training labels. We adopted consensus scores from three calibrated PE teachers as supervision signals, rather than independent biomechanical or motor skill benchmarks. Two important implications are drawn from this setting.

First, the model can inherit inherent biases in manual scoring, such as gender stereotypes, campus-specific scoring habits and age-related differences in rubric execution. While ICC (0.87) and Fleiss’ κ (0.72) quantify inter-rater inconsistency, these indicators cannot identify collective biases among teachers. The cross-stratum analysis in Sect. "Leave-one-school-out evaluation and cross-stratum generalization" supplements relevant evidence by evaluating model performance across subgroups.

Second, the system is designed to streamline expert assessment, rather than provide absolute ground truth. In practical large-scale deployment, regular validation with professional biomechanical equipment is required. Additionally, the rater calibration activities in Sect. "Experimental setup and dataset" need to be maintained on an ongoing basis, instead of being conducted only once.

Robustness and its scope conditions

Model robustness test results are consistent with the expected behaviour of a multimodal system under data-quality variation. The fusion model retained 85.6% accuracy under 30% data missing while IMU-only dropped to 65.3%; this 20.3-pp gap illustrates the contribution of multi-source redundancy to system stability. Under structurally distinct missingness types (Table 11), fusion accuracy remains 81.4% under whole-sensor-node failure, the most adversarial of the four failure modes. Beyond data-quality robustness, the cross-axis robustness profile reported in Sect. "Leave-one-school-out evaluation and cross-stratum generalization" extends the picture along an orthogonal dimension. The 3.2-pp average cost under leave-one-school-out evaluation, the absence of a detectable gender-conditional gap (boys 91.4 ± 0.5%, girls 91.2 ± 0.5%, = 0.62), the flatness of accuracy across age subgroups (90.9–91.6%, ANOVA = 0.14), and the modest 3.0-pp gradient across baseline-skill tertiles together indicate that the framework’s generalization is not concentrated along any single source of heterogeneity. We avoid framing this multi-axis profile as a substitute for external validation; it should be read as quantitative evidence that the model is not over-fitted to any single demographic, institutional, or task-specific artifact, and as a calibrated baseline against which future external-validation results can be interpreted.

We nonetheless treat this robustness as bounded by the missingness mechanisms we could simulate: failure modes such as systematic single-node mis-calibration accumulating across the 12-week window, or correlated multi-node dropout from electromagnetic interference, remain outside the scope of the present evidence. The robustness reported here should be read as evidence that the architecture is not catastrophically brittle within a broad range of input degradations and across demographic and institutional strata, not as a guarantee of operational reliability under every plausible deployment hazard.

Implications

Reshaping the PE teacher’s role

Traditional assessment and modern sensing technology work best as complements instead of substitutes. Exclusive use of objective sensor data or subjective teacher scoring will lead to drawbacks. Sensor signals fail to reflect subjective aspects like movement aesthetics, whereas manual scoring is affected by insufficient observation accuracy and personal bias. This multimodal fusion method enables seamless integration of expert judgment and quantitative sensing results in a single pipeline.

The character of this role shift can be illustrated through the 45-minute lesson workflow described in Sect. "System architecture and data acquisition scheme". Under the traditional rubric workflow, a PE teacher typically spent 6–8 min of class time on score entry alone for a cohort of 35–40 students, with most scoring postponed to the lesson break. Under the proposed system, the teacher receives a tier prediction with a calibrated confidence value concurrently with rubric entry, and can re-allocate the saved 6–8 min to a targeted intervention for the small subset of students whose model–teacher disagreement is largest (typically 3–5 students per class of 40, given the 91.3% fusion accuracy). The teacher’s interpretive authority is preserved: model output is presented as a decision-support overlay that the teacher can accept, adjust, or override before the score is finalized.

Two specific competencies become more important under this reshaped role, both mapping onto components of the DigCompEdu framework identified as decisive for technology-enhanced PE. The first is data-interpretation literacy: reading the model’s confidence output, per-sport attention weights, and sensor contribution map to decide whether to accept, adjust, or override a tier assignment. The second is rubric-design competence: because the model’s learned representation is rubric-anchored, teachers who design or revise rubrics now influence what the model attends to.

Embedding the system in a formative-assessment loop

Section "Time complexity comparison" reports a single-sample inference latency of 43.2 ms, which brings notable pedagogical benefits. The system can generate ratings and confidence scores instantly during PE classes, supporting on-site formative assessment rather than only end-of-term evaluation. In contrast, traditional scoring usually releases results in the next class, and this delay largely reduces the instructional value of feedback for motor skill correction. We consider real-time in-lesson feedback the most promising way to boost learning outcomes, and further controlled teaching studies are required to confirm its actual effects.

According to motor learning research, feedback can be classified into three types by timing: concurrent feedback during movement, terminal within-session feedback, and cross-class delayed feedback. For adolescent students, terminal within-session feedback contributes most to movement consolidation, while delayed feedback only helps learners grasp rubric rules without improving kinaesthetic ability. Our system operates in the terminal within-session mode, with a latency of 43.2 ms that is well below the standard 1–5 min window. Traditional paper scoring relies on delayed feedback. This progress marks a fundamental change in teaching practice, not merely an efficiency upgrade.

Teacher professional development pathways

Section "Reshaping the PE teacher’s role" defines two core teacher competencies: data-interpretation literacy and rubric-design competence. Cultivating these skills requires specialized continuing professional development (CPD). Unlike generic digital literacy training, the proposed pathway strictly follows the DigCompEdu progression rather than providing one-size-fits-all technical instruction.

We establish a three-stage CPD system aligned with DigCompEdu standards. Basic Operation lasts 4 contact hours and covers sensor placement, tablet-based rubric use and routine maintenance for users at the Newcomer and Explorer levels. The second stage, Output Interpretation, takes 8 h. It trains teachers to understand confidence results, attention weights and feature attribution maps, and to recognize out-of-distribution data. This component strengthens data literacy for rational override decisions and corresponds to the Integrator and Expert levels. The third stage is Rubric Co-design, with 16 contact hours arranged throughout a term. It guides collaborative revision of rubrics and model training data, fitting the Leader and Pioneer levels. Two design choices distinguish this pathway from generic technology-training modules. First, the pathway is built around the system’s actual interpretive outputs (confidence, attention, attribution) rather than abstract digital literacy, keeping training tightly coupled to classroom decision-making. Second, Stage 3 explicitly elevates the teacher from system user to system co-designer, recognizing that the rubric-anchored learned representation makes teacher-authored rubric revision the primary mechanism for any meaningful change to assessment criteria.

Integration into PE curriculum standards

The pedagogical legitimacy of any technology-mediated assessment system in Chinese middle-school PE depends on its alignment with prevailing curriculum standards. Two features of the contemporary PE curriculum framework are directly relevant: the now-explicit emphasis on process-based formative evaluation in addition to summative certification, requiring continuous evidence across a learning unit; and the core-literacy orientation that treats motor-skill development, physical fitness, and healthy behaviour as interlinked competencies not reducible to a single end-of-term score.

The proposed system supports both curriculum features rather than substituting for them. With respect to process-based formative evaluation, the in-lesson latency analysed in Sect. "Embedding the system in a formative-assessment loop" enables one tier prediction and one continuous quality score per student per session across a 12-week unit, yielding 8–16 evaluation points per student per sport rather than a single end-of-unit summary. The intra-student consistency analysis in Sect. "Handling of repeated measures" (within-student SD = 0.31 vs. between-student SD = 1.42) confirms this trajectory carries genuine signal. With respect to core-literacy orientation, the per-sport interpretability outputs in Sect. "Interpretability and explainability analysis" distinguish kinematic, performance, and form dimensions, supporting diagnostic feedback at the granularity at which the framework operates.

Two integration caveats accompany this alignment claim. First, the rubric criteria embedded in the model’s training labels are those of the participating schools’ interpretation of prevailing curriculum standards, not of the standards themselves; institutions with materially different rubric interpretations would need to re-calibrate the rubric component before deployment. Second, the system supplements rather than displaces the broader range of assessment evidence the curriculum framework recognizes, including peer assessment, self-assessment, and project-based assessment.

Equity, bias, and validity considerations

As the model is trained on teacher-generated scores, it could carry over systematic scoring discrepancies related to student gender, school background and physical maturity. The cross-stratum analysis in Sect. "Leave-one-school-out evaluation and cross-stratum generalization" evaluates potential biases quantitatively. Results show a marginal accuracy difference of 0.2 pp between genders (p = 0.62) and a variation below 0.7 pp across age groups (p = 0.14). A 3.0 pp performance gap exists between the top and bottom tertiles of baseline skills, which is caused by the rubric-floor effect instead of inherent model bias. The present work confirms concurrent validity relative to expert ratings. Follow-up studies can build on this research to address remaining limitations.

Conclusion

This study addresses insufficient objectivity and limited efficiency in physical education assessment by proposing a multimodal algorithmic model that fuses IMUs with traditional assessment scales. Using a participant-wise split of 3,920 samples from 245 middle-school students across four sports, three calibrated PE teachers (ICC(2,k) = 0.87, Fleiss’ κ = 0.72) provided the supervisory signal. The fusion model achieved 91.3 ± 0.4% accuracy, 8.6 pp above IMU-only and 11.9 pp above scale-only baselines (p < 1 × 10⁻⁸); spatiotemporal alignment reduced average time offset from 312 ms to 18 ms; the model retained 85.6 ± 0.5% accuracy under 30% data missing; and 43.2 ms inference supports immediate feedback.

Four main outcomes are reported in this study. First, leave-one-school-out evaluation returned a mean accuracy of 88.1 ± 1.3%, representing a 3.2 pp performance decline across different schools and verifying basic cross-institution generalizability. Cross-stratum results show steady performance across gender (91.4% vs. 91.2%, p = 0.62) and age groups (90.9–91.6%, p = 0.14). The 3.0 pp difference among students with varied basic skills stems from the rubric-floor effect. These findings support the model’s robustness within the research population, while external validation with new cohorts remains necessary. Second, the model’s attention and feature attribution results conform to well-recognized biomechanical rules. Third, this system replicates expert PE assessment results rather than measuring objective biomechanical indicators, so it should assist rather than replace teachers’ professional judgment. Fourth, Sect. "Implications" covers teacher role transformation, formative assessment application, three-stage CPD schemes and curriculum alignment. This framework fully integrates technical innovations with practical pedagogical design.

This study has several limitations. The dataset covers four individual sports across three schools, so its applicability to other age groups, campuses and team contact sports needs to be confirmed via external tests. While LOSO and cross-stratum analyses support the model’s robustness, validation on fully independent cohorts remains absent. Only three teachers participated in scoring. Even with standard calibration and review by a fourth expert, inter-rater reliability is still constrained. Our missing data tests included random loss and four structured missing patterns at 30%, but did not cover systematic issues like gradual calibration drift of individual sensor nodes.

For future work, we will recruit more participants and expand sport categories, and incorporate computer vision to enrich multimodal inputs. External validation against biomechanical reference tools will also be performed. Moreover, we will launch two controlled studies. One explores the link between real-time formative feedback and motor learning outcomes. The other assesses the proposed three-stage CPD pathway against the DigCompEdu standards.

In its present form, the proposed framework offers a reference methodological pathway for coupling sensor data with expert-derived rubric scoring in school PE assessment, not a finalized technical solution. The participant-wise evaluation protocol, inter-rater reliability reporting, leave-one-school-out and cross-stratum analyses, pedagogical positioning, and interpretability analyses serve as a methodological template for future PE-IMU studies.
