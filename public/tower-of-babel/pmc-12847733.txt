Integration of human ERKs and DUSPs into the yeast cell wall integrity pathway
Authors: Beatriz Lavilla-García, Teresa Fernández-Acero, María Molina, Humberto Martín
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Saccharomyces cerevisiae Cell Wall Integrity (CWI) pathway responds to cell wall stress and is composed of MAP3K Bck1, MAP2Ks Mkk1 and Mkk2 and MAPK Slt2. Although human ERK5 has been considered the functional orthologue of Slt2, our results indicate that human ERK1 and ERK2 exhibit a much greater ability than ERK5 to replace Slt2 under various cell wall stresses. ERK5 is only able to slightly complement an slt2Δ mutant phenotype in the presence of tunicamycin, and the constitutively active truncated version ERK5ΔCt did not improve this complementation ability. Like Slt2, ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5ΔCt are concentrated in the nucleus, and show higher phosphorylation than ERK5 upon CWI pathway stimulation. Expression of a hyperactive version of the human MAP2K MEK5 leads to specific ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt phosphorylation, leading to a partial replacement of the Mkk1/2-Slt2 function under cell wall stress. Expressed in yeast, the human Dual Specificity Phosphatases DUSP3 and DUSP6 reduce the level of ERK5 phosphorylation to a similar extent, whereas DUSP6 shows higher activity than DUSP3 on ERK1 or ERK2. Our results show the different degree of integration of human ERKs and DUSPs into the yeast CWI signalling circuit, which can be exploited for functional analysis or pharmacological screenings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33816-8.

Introduction

Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) cascades are widely conserved signalling systems that regulate essential cellular processes in eukaryotic organisms. They are organized in three consecutive levels in which the upstream protein called MAPKKK (MAP Kinase Kinase Kinase), MAP3K or MEKK, phosphorylates and activates a MAPKK (MAP Kinase Kinase), MAP2K or MEK, which in turn activates the MAPK by dual phosphorylation in the activation domain at conserved threonine and tyrosine residues (TXY motif), where the amino acid X is distinct for subsets of MAPKs. The relevance of these signalling pathways lies in the fact that they couple a particular stimulus with a physiological response, by controlling the activation of a set of substrates through their phosphorylation by the MAPK. Among them, transcriptional regulators are an important group since they mediate essential changes in gene expression to respond to the stimulus. These cascades are tightly regulated through specific interaction motifs, scaffold proteins, dynamic subcellular localization and negative regulators, especially protein phosphatases1.

In mammals, there are four families of conventional MAPKs that become activated by extracellular stimuli: p38 MAPK, c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK), Extracellular signal Regulated Kinase (ERK) 1/2, and ERK5. They all regulate critical cellular functions and thus are related to many pathological conditions. The p38 and JNK MAPKs transduce typical stress signals while the ERK kinases respond to mitogenic signals such as growth factors and hormones. ERK1 and ERK2 are two similar kinases activated when such signals stimulate GPCR or tyrosine-kinase receptors that in turn stimulate the small GTPase Ras. This results in the activation of the MAP3K RAF, which activates the MAP2K MEK1 and MEK22. These kinases activate ERK1 and ERK2 by phosphorylating their TEY motif. Despite the purported redundancy of ERK1 and ERK2 for cellular proliferation and differentiation, and the large number of substrates that they share3, they also play specific roles depending on the cellular context. ERK5 is activated by both mitogens and stress signals, such as osmotic, oxidative, and fluid shear stress. It is different from the rest of the members of conventional MAPKs in its extremely large size and the presence of an autoinhibitory carboxy-terminal tail. The activation of ERK5 takes place when MEK5, previously activated by MEKK2 and MEKK3, phosphorylates its TEY motif, which subsequently triggers multiple autophosphorylation events in its carboxyterminal tail that are required for maximal ERK5 transactivator activity4. Moreover, ERK5 is known to be a transcriptional co-activator independently of its kinase activity5.

MAPK activity is mainly downregulated by Ser/Thr phosphatases, Tyr phosphatases or Dual Specificity Phosphatases (DUSPs), which can eliminate phosphates on both threonine and tyrosine. Mammalian cells possess 25 DUSPs, and several of them are specialized in dephosphorylating MAPKs in the TXY activation domain. They are generally classified as typical, if they contain a KIM (Kinase Interaction Motif) that interacts with the common docking domain of MAPKs, and atypical, if they lack such interaction domain. Most of the typical DUSPs are also called MKPs (MAP Kinase Phosphatases), such as DUSP6, also known as MKP3. However, there are some atypical DUSPs that can dephosphorylate MAPKs but are not named MKPs, such as DUSP3, also known as VHR6.

Regarding their localization, in general, MAPKs exert crucial activities in the nucleus, such as the regulation of gene transcription. For example, ERK1 and ERK2 are localized in the cytoplasm of resting cells and their activation promotes its translocation to the nucleus where they phosphorylate many transcriptional regulators. However, they also phosphorylate cytoplasmic substrates, meaning that they can also remain active in the cytoplasm. In the case of ERK5, although its cytoplasmic localization has been reported in many resting cells, it is localized mainly in the nucleus or all over the cell. Upon stimulation, MEKK2 translocates to the nucleus, where phosphorylates nuclear MEK5 and subsequently nuclear ERK5. Whereas ERK1/2 are shuttled by Importin-7 via the nuclear pores, different mechanisms have been described to regulate the localization of ERK57,8. The mammalian MAPK cascades play a significant role in cancer. Gain-of-function mutations in the upstream components of the pathway, namely receptors or RAS, and in the MAP3K, MAP2K/MEK or MAPK itself, such as RAF, MEK1/2 and ERK1/2, have been established as hallmarks of several types of cancer in humans9. MEK5/ERK5 overexpression has also been found in several types of tumours. This pathway also presents a critical role in chemotherapy resistance to MAPK inhibitors. Thus, combinations of MEK1/2, ERK1/2 and MEK5 and ERK5 inhibitors are under trials as chemotherapy10,11. In addition, DUSPs also play vital roles in cancer progression12.

The budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has served as a model for studying the structure and function of MAPK pathways due to the high conservation of these signalling routes with those of higher eukaryotes. Yeast cells contain five MAPKs belonging to the Cell Wall Integrity (CWI), High Osmolarity Glycerol (HOG), mating, pseudohyphal-invasive growth and sporulation pathways13. The CWI responds primarily to cell wall damages, but also to other kind of stressful conditions14. Consequently, the MAPK module of this pathway, which is constituted by the MAP3K Bck1, the redundant MAP2Ks Mkk1 and Mkk2 and the MAPK Slt2, is essential for cell survival under exposure to these kinds of stresses. The value of the yeast model is reflected in the functional complementation that several components of mammalian MAPK pathways exert on yeast mutants affected in the corresponding orthologs15. In particular, various works have shown that CWI MAPK Slt2 can be functionally replaced in yeast by mammalian ERK1 or ERK216,17. However, other authors have proposed ERK5 as the mammalian functional ortholog of Slt2, also showing the inability of ERK2 to complement an slt2Δ mutant18. In any case, none of the reports collectively and comparatively analyse the capacity of the different ERKs to replace the function of Slt2. In this work we have studied to what extent ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5, are able to integrate into the yeast CWI pathway, in terms of MAPK phosphorylation, localization and functionality. In addition, we have explored the utility of yeast cells to analyse the activity and specificity of mammalian DUSPs on ERKs.

Results

Human ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5 substitute the function of Slt2 to varying degrees

To explore the functionality of human ERKs within the CWI pathway, we first compared the protein sequences of ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5 with that of yeast Slt2. Primary amino acid sequence analysis indicated that Slt2 shared a higher percentage identity with ERK1 and ERK2 than with ERK5 (Fig. S1). Next, we compared their predicted 3D structure. ERK1 and ERK2 present an obvious structural similarity to each other and to Slt2, particularly in the C-lobe of the kinase domain, localized in the N-terminal region (Fig. 1A). As mentioned above, ERK5 is larger and more complex, and contains a disordered C-terminal autoinhibitory tail, as does Slt2. Next, we cloned the open reading frame encoding each of these three human MAPKs into a yeast centromeric expression plasmid under the control of the constitutive TDH3 promoter and in N-terminal fusion to EGFP in order to study their ability to functionally replace Slt2 and mimic its cellular localization in the yeast cell. As shown in Fig. 1B, upon exposure to several CWI pathway activators, such as the anionic detergent SDS, the cell wall damaging agents calcofluor white (CFW) and Congo red (CR), the TORC1 inhibitor caffeine and the glycosylation inhibitor and ER stressor tunicamycin14, the slt2Δ mutant was unable to grow due the inability to trigger an adequate stress response to maintain cell wall integrity. As expected, the constitutive expression of SLT2 from a plasmid fully restored the wild type phenotype. None of the human ERKs allowed yeast cell growth to the same extent as Slt2 under any of the conditions tested. However, ERK1 and ERK2 partially rescued yeast growth in the presence of SDS, CR, CFW and tunicamycin, but not in caffeine. In this latter condition, the growth of ERK2-expressing cells was even worse than that of slt2Δ control cells bearing the empty vector, suggesting a detrimental role for this MAPK under this stress (Fig. 1B).

Interestingly, ERK5 failed to restore growth of slt2Δ cells in any of the conditions tested except in the presence of tunicamycin, where it did so very slightly (Fig. 1B). Therefore, we decided to generate a construct of ERK5 lacking the last 416 amino acids, ERK5ΔCt, devoid of the Ct autoinhibitory tail. This version had been shown to exhibit constitutive activity when expressed in mammalian cell cultures4, but as with ERK5, it only slightly recovered the growth of slt2Δ cells in the presence of tunicamycin. Similar results were obtained in a different genetic background (Fig. S2A), indicating that this is not a specific behaviour of this strain.

The EGFP-tagged proteins were confirmed to be correctly expressed in yeast cells, showing an equivalent level of expression (Fig. 1C). We also evaluated the functional complementation of the untagged MAPKs (Figure S2B). Removal of EGFP did not increase their ability to complement the slt2Δ phenotype, indicating that the presence of this tag did not interfere with MAPK function and stability.

When these EGFP-tagged MAPKs were observed by fluorescence microscopy, we noticed that both ERK1 and ERK2 localized at the cytoplasm but mainly at the yeast nucleus in non-stimulated cells, a pattern reminiscent of that of Slt2 (Fig. 2). In contrast, ERK5 does not appear to be concentrated in the nucleus. However, as shown for ERK5ΔCt, removal of the C-terminal tail of ERK5 led to its nuclear accumulation, as demonstrated by DAPI staining (Fig. S3), indicating that this region was involved in the exclusion of this MAPK from the yeast nucleus.

Human ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5ΔCt are phosphorylated in response to yeast CWI pathway activation

Next, we wanted to study the level of phosphorylation at the activation loop of human MAPKs expressed in yeast to establish a possible correlation between their phosphorylation status and their degree of complementation of the slt2Δ mutant. As shown in Fig. 3A, B and C, in the absence of stimuli, ectopically expressed EGFP-tagged Slt2, ERK1 and ERK2, but neither ERK5 nor ERK5ΔCt, exhibited basal phosphorylation. In response to CR and tunicamycin, phosphorylation of Slt2, ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5ΔCt increased, but not that of ERK5 (Fig. 3A and B). The expression of the hyperactive allele MKK1S386P leads to a potent hyperactivation of the CWI pathway19. Expression of this version of Mkk1 under the control of the GAL1 promoter led to a strong phosphorylation in the MAPK versions expressed, allowing the detection of full-length ERK5 phosphorylation (Fig. 3C).

These experiments suggest that although removal of the C-terminal domain of ERK5 facilitates its activation, ERK1 and ERK2 are more easily integrated than ERK5 into the CWI MAPK module to become activated upon yeast CWI pathway stimulation. In this strain, both in the presence (Fig. 4A) and the absence (Fig. 4B) of wild-type MEK5 under basal and CWI pathway-inducing conditions, phosphorylation of Slt2, ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5ΔCt was almost negligible. However, when we expressed a hyperactive version of MEK5, bearing the phosphomimetic mutations S311D and T315D20, we observed a strong and specific phosphorylation on ERK5, both the wild-type and the C-terminally truncated version, but not ERK1, ERK2 or Slt2, indicating a high specificity for its ERK5 substrate (Fig. 4B).

To gain insight into the structural basis underlying the specificity of the MEK5-ERK5 interaction, we analysed in silico the putative interaction of MEK5 with the different MAPKs using AlphaFold2-Multimer. MEK5 has two domains in its structure (Fig. 5A), which are essential for binding and activation of its substrate MAPK, ERK521. First, in its Nt region, MEK5 has a PB1 domain (Phox and Bem1 domain) that facilitates its interaction both with the MAP3Ks that activate it (MEKK2/MEKK3) and with ERK5, ensuring the specificity of interaction and facilitating the rapid phosphorylation of the MAPK once the pathway is activated. Second, in its Ct region, MEK5 has a catalytic domain with serine/threonine kinase activity, responsible for ERK5 phosphorylation10,22. The AlphaFold2-Multimer prediction of the interaction between MEK5 and ERK5 (Fig. 5B) shows how the Nt and Ct domains of MEK5 acquire an open conformation that allows the optimal positioning of ERK5 for its activation. The Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) obtained in this prediction suggests that the interaction might be occurring through the Nt domain of ERK and both the Nt region of MEK5, where the PB1 domain is located, and the Ct, where the phosphorylation reaction takes place. This can be observed in the PAE of this interaction (Fig. 5B), where the area representing the distance between MEK5 and Nt domain of ERK5 appears in blue, indicating a small error in the relative positioning of both kinases.

When predicting the interaction of MEK5 with ERK1 (Fig. 5C), ERK2 (Fig. 5D), and Slt2 (Fig. 5E), the spatial distribution obtained was very similar to that observed with ERK5, although in these cases the disordered region of MEK5, which connects its Nt domain with the Ct, seems to adjust the positioning of the MAPK worse, being slightly detached, probably due to conformational differences between its natural substrate, ERK5, and the other MAPKs. In these models, the interaction of these MAPKs with MEK5 seems to be occurring through the Nt but not the catalytic Ct of MEK5, as revealed by the corresponding PAEs (Fig. 5B-E, lower left boxes). This is consistent with the negative results observed for phosphorylation of MAPKs other than ERK5 by MEK5 in yeast cells.

Expression of hyperactive MEK5 allows ERK5 to partially complement a triple mutant mkk1Δmkk2Δslt2Δ

Since MEK5 expression allowed phosphorylation of ERK5 in yeast, we asked whether phosphorylated ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt might be able to complement an slt2Δ mutant. To this end, we expressed either the wild-type or the hyperactive version of MEK5 (MEK5S311D/T315D) together with ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt, as well as with ERK1, ERK2 and Slt2, in a mkk1Δmkk2Δslt2Δ mutant, and exposed these cells to Congo red. As shown in Fig. 6, whereas the expression of ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt alone or with MEK5 wild type did not restore yeast growth under cell wall stress, co-expression of hyperactive MEK5 slightly complemented the phenotype of the triple mutant. This means that the phosphorylation achieved by MEK5S311D/T315D on ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt enables them to partially replace the function of Slt2. In contrast, co-expression of MEK5S311D/T315D with ERK1 and ERK2 did not recover yeast growth in the absence of yeast Mkk1 and Mkk2 when compared with the empty vector (Fig. 6). These results confirm the specificity of MEK5 over ERK5 among human MAPKs, and the need of phosphorylation by MEK5 for ERK5 and by yeast MEKs for ERK1 and ERK2 to allow functional complementation in the absence of Slt2. The expression of Slt2 from an equivalent plasmid partially supported cell growth, but irrespective of the absence or presence of MEK and MEK5S311D/T315D (Fig. 6). Together with the lack of Slt2 phosphorylation by MEK5, this result confirms the high specificity of MEK5 over ERK5. Expression of a hybrid kinase containing the constitutively active version Mkk1S386P fused to Slt2 fully restored cell growth of the mkk1Δmkk2Δslt2Δ mutant both in the absence and presence of Slt2 expressed from the plasmid, due to the Slt2 activation by Mkk1S386P in the hybrid protein (Fig. 6).

To study whether the phosphorylation of ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt exerted by MEK5S311D/T315D triggered nuclear translocation in yeast cells, as reported in human cells23, we monitored their cellular localization under fluorescence microscope. Co-expression of MEK5S311D/T315D with EGFP-ERK5 or EGFP-ERK5ΔCt did not alter the localization previously observed under non-stimulating conditions, indicating that protein phosphorylation did not apparently increase their nuclear import (Fig. S4). This result could explain the partial complementation produced by ERK5 and ERK5ΔCt in the presence of MEK5S311D/T315D despite the strong phosphorylation achieved. We also verified that the activity of MEK5 or MEK5S311D/T315D did not interfere with yeast growth nor replace the function of Mkk1/2, Pbs2 or Ste7, the MEKs of the CWI, HOG or mating pathways (Fig. S5), which again reflects the high specificity of MEK5.

Human phosphatases DUSP3 and DUSP6 dephosphorylate ERK1, ERK2 and ERK5 in yeast

As mentioned above, DUSPs counteract MAPK phosphorylation in mammalian cells. We decided to clone and express two human DUSP to evaluate whether their phosphatase activity could be reproduced in yeast cells, as well as to test their specificity towards the different ERKs expressed. We chose DUSP6, a typical DUSP known to dephosphorylate ERK1/2, and DUSP3, an atypical DUSP, which has been shown to dephosphorylate not only ERK1/2 but also JNK and p3824,25.

We first studied the effect of overexpressing DUSP3 and DUSP6 on ERK1 and ERK2. In these cells, either under basal conditions or upon ERK1/2 activation by CR treatment, DUSP6 significantly reduced the phosphorylation of both MAPKs (Fig. 7A). This reduction is due to the catalytic activity of DUSP6 since a phosphatase-dead version bearing the mutation C293S was unable to reduce the phosphorylation of both ERKs (Fig. 7B). However, cells carrying this mutant version still showed some reduction in ERK1 phosphorylation only under inducing conditions, which might be due to some interference with activating Mkks. DUSP3 overexpression also reduced ERK1 and ERK2 phosphorylation, although to a lesser extent and, unlike DUSP6, this effect was only found statistically significant under CWI stimulating conditions (Fig. 7A). When we explored the effect of these phosphatases on ERK5 and its truncated Ct version ERK5ΔCt after phosphorylation with hyperactive MEK5, we observed that they similarly reduced the phosphorylation of both proteins (Fig. 7C). Thus, our system reproduces the reported activity of DUSP6 and DUSP3 on ERK1 and ERK2 and shows that ERK5 can be dephosphorylated by both DUSPs. Interestingly, DUSP3 showed a slightly higher dephosphorylating activity than DUSP6 on ERK5, but not on ERK5ΔCt.

Expression of human MAPK phosphatases DUSP3 and DUSP6 counteracts Slt2 phosphorylation

To test whether DUSP3 and DUSP6 were capable of dephosphorylating yeast ERK-type MAPKs, we made use of a yeast strain lacking Itc1, a chromatin regulator involved in the repression of a cell type specific genes26. The itc1Δ mutant constitutively produces a-factor and, as a consequence, exhibits hyperactivation of mating MAPKs Fus3 and Kss1, as well as the CWI pathway MAPK Slt227. Thus, this mutant serves as a tool to evaluate the activity of DUSP3 and DUSP6 on these three MAPKs simultaneously. As shown in Fig. 8, overexpression of both DUSP3 and DUSP6 significantly reduced Slt2 phosphorylation in both wild-type and itc1Δ mutant strains, but not that of Fus3 or Kss1. The effect of DUSP6 on Slt2 phosphorylation was stronger than that of DUSP3.

Discussion

Here we have shown that human ERK1 and ERK2 integrate into the yeast CWI pathway much better than ERK5 under different CWI pathway-triggering stresses. First, ERK1 and ERK2 are phosphorylated by yeast MEKs Mkk1/Mkk2 more efficiently than ERK5 in response to cell wall stress. Under these conditions, only a slight phosphorylation of ERK5 was detected when its C-terminal inhibitory domain was removed or when hyperactive human MEK5 was expressed simultaneously. Expression of a hyperactive version of Mkk1 led to a high phosphorylation of all MAPKs, including full length ERK5, suggesting that although ERK5 might be phosphorylated by Mkk1, its integration into a naturally stimulated CWI MAPK module is very limited. Second, ERK5 shows very little ability to complement the phenotypes of an slt2Δ mutant. We have only detected some functionality of ERK5 to supplant Slt2 under tunicamycin treatment. This suggests a specific response of the CWI pathway to this compound, known to induce endoplasmic reticulum stress by inhibiting N-linked protein glycosylation14, to which ERK5 could provide much more efficient performance than against other types of stress.

In resting human cells, ERK1/2 are localized in the cytosol. After stimulation, they are phosphorylated by MEK1/2, which exposes a non-canonical NLS for its phosphorylation by Casein Kinase 2 (CK2), and they are further translocated into the nucleus by importin 728. However, the localization of ERK1/2 in yeast cells resembles that of Slt2, which is predominantly localized in the nucleus even in resting state. Thus, ERK1/2 seem to be recognized by the same mechanism of nuclear transportation than Slt2, which also lacks a canonical NLS29. This would allow ERK1/2 to phosphorylate nuclear substrates of Slt2 and thus replace its function. In human cells, ERK5 is distributed throughout the cytoplasm and the nucleus in both stimulated and resting cells, but stimulating conditions cause a further increase of nuclear ERK58. Cytoplasmic ERK5 is imported into the nucleus by importin-α/β through a classical NLS7. In our yeast model, unlike ERK1/2, ERK5 does not seem to be concentrated in the nucleus. Therefore, it could be speculated that, together with its low level of phosphorylation, this could be the cause of the lack of functionality of ERK5 within the CWI pathway. As described in human cells4, elimination of the Ct tail of ERK5 favours its nuclear translocation also in yeast, although to a much lesser extent than ERK1/2. However, this situation does not enhance the functionality of ERK5, implying that factors other than its cellular localization are impeding its function in yeast. Moreover, the fact that co-expression of hyperactive MEK5 leads to high phosphorylation and increased competence of both ERK5 and its truncated Ct version to replace Slt2 points to impaired activation of ERK5 by yeast MAP2Ks as the main reason for its low complementation capacity. Notably, MEK5 was only able to activate ERK5 when constitutively activated, indicating that, like ERK5, it is unable to integrate into the yeast CWI pathway.

Our results differ in some respects from previous reports on the ability of mammalian MAPKs to complement an slt2Δ mutant. On one hand, Levin-Salomon et al. (2009) reported that only ERK2, but not ERK1, partially restore yeast growth in response to caffeine17, whereas Truman et al. (2006) did not find ERK2 complementation under this stress18. As ERK1 and ERK2 were expressed from other vectors and were tagged differently from our work, a direct comparison cannot be made. However, we not only observe a similar phenotypic complementation of slt2Δ cells for both ERK1 and ERK2 against most of the tested CWI stimuli, but even a negative specific effect of ERK2 expression on caffeine, as cells grow even worse than those bearing an empty vector. In view of our results, ERK2 but not ERK1 might interfere with some stress pathways necessary for the yeast response to caffeine. This points to some differential roles of each human ERK in yeast, as has been reported in higher eukaryotes30. Levin-Salomon et al. (2009) noticed that phosphorylation of ERK1 and ERK2 occurs spontaneously even in the absence of MAP2Ks Mkk1 and Mkk2, as we also observed here, and even in the absence of any yeast MAP2K, pointing to its autophosphorylation. However, we observe that it does not seem to be sufficient for its full activation, as it does not allow growth recovery of a triple mutant mkk1Δmkk2Δslt2Δ exposed to cell wall stress.

On the other hand, in contrast to our results, Truman et al. (2006) showed that ERK5 was able to replace Slt2 function in response to caffeine and CFW18. The lack of complementation of our untagged version of ERK5 of an slt2Δ mutant under both conditions rules out the possibility that the EGFP tag is what impedes the functionality of this protein. The discrepancy with previous results may be due to differences in the expression level, as these authors used a different promoter and vector. In any case, we show that the amount of ERK5 is comparable to that of Slt2 in our experiments. In addition, they did not study the behaviour of ERK2 in response to stresses other than caffeine, nor did they express ERK1, and thus it is difficult to compare the behaviour of the three MAPKs. Nevertheless, our work shows that ERK1 and ERK2 exert a greater capacity to substitute for Slt2 in response to a high number of stresses, although none of the expressed ERKs completely replaces Slt2 function enough to define either of them as a true functional orthologue.

The existence of specificity in the binding of proteins to their substrates is of great importance to avoid spurious crosstalk with other pathways. For example, the activation of ERK5 by human MEK5 appears to be very specific, as it has been observed that MEK5 is not able to activate MAPKs other than ERK531. In line with this, a lack of interaction of ERK5 with other MAP2Ks, such as MEK1 or MEK2, has been described32. This specificity between MEK5 and ERK5 was also observed in our yeast model, where we have shown that both wild-type MEK5 under CWI pathway stimulation conditions and its hyperactive mutant version are not only unable to phosphorylate ERK1 and ERK2, but also the yeast MAPK Slt2. These results indicate that yeast is a very valuable tool to study the specificity in the interaction of the different components operating in mammalian MAPK pathways.

The emergence of AlphaFold has been a turning point in biomedical research, since it offers the possibility of predicting with considerable confidence the folding of proteins whose tertiary structure is unknown33. This impact became even greater with the release of AlphaFold-Multimer, which additionally allows the prediction of protein-protein interactions34. These tools may also be useful for the prediction of interactions between proteins of different species35. However, there are currently not many studies in which these predictions have been validated in vitro or in vivo. In this work, we have modelled the interaction between human MAP2K MEK5 and human MAPKs ERK1, ERK2 or ERK5 and yeast MAPK Slt2, using AlphaFold2-Multimer. Our results confirm the specificity between MEK5 and ERK5 with a high degree of certainty, with the interaction taking place through both the Nt region, where the PB1 domain is located, and the Ct region, involved in the phosphorylation reaction, of MEK5. In the other MAPKs, only the interaction through the Nt of MEK5 was predicted with an acceptable level of confidence. This suggests that, although the PB1 domain has been described as playing a very important role in the specificity of the MEK5-ERK5 interaction, the MEK5 catalytic domain would also be involved. Moreover, this model fits with the specific phosphorylation of ERK5 by MEK5 that we observed in yeast. Therefore, these results support the use of AlphaFold2-Multimer as a tool to anticipate which interactions are most likely to occur, even between proteins of different species.

Our yeast model has also proved useful for testing the competence of mammalian DUSPs to dephosphorylate different ERKs. In this regard, very little is known about which phosphatases act on ERK5. Some studies have suggested that ERK5 could be a substrate of the DUSP6 protein36,37. However, other works suggest that this phosphatase dephosphorylates ERK1 and ERK2, having no significant activity towards ERK538,39. Our study not only shows that ERK5 could be regulated by DUSP6, but also by DUSP3. DUSP6, but not DUSP3, contains the typical kinase interaction motif (KIM), which determines the specific binding and thus substrate selectivity of the MKP for the MAPK40, suggesting that this could be the reason why DUSP6 appears to be more active than DUSP3 on ERK1 and ERK2 in our yeast model. Since both phosphatases act on ERK5, binding to ERK5 does not seem to require a KIM domain. DUSPs have been considered as very promiscuous enzymes41. In this respect, we have observed that both DUSP6 and DUSP3 are able to act on the three human ERKs but have a very limited effect on yeast MAPKs, acting only slightly on Slt2 and not on Fus3 or Kss1. This indicates that these phosphatases are quite specific to human MAPKs and retain such specificity on their natural substrates when expressed in yeast.

Yeast models have been successfully used to study the molecular mechanisms involved in a wide variety of human diseases, as well as for pharmacological screening42–44. In this regard, our model holds the potential to be employed for conducting functional analyses of expressed ERKs and MEK5, assessing, for instance, the effect of gain-of-function mutations found in diseases. Moreover, the activity of compounds inhibiting the activity of these human proteins in the yeast CWI pathway, of interest as potential cancer chemotherapeutic agents, could also be tested in our yeast model. In sum, the results obtained in this work demonstrate that heterologous expression of components of human MAPK pathways in the yeast S. cerevisiae serves as a platform to gain further insight into the molecular mechanisms that regulate their activity. As these signalling pathways play critical roles in numerous pathological conditions, this strategy could help to improve therapeutic approaches.

Preparation of yeast extracts and immunodetection by Western blotting

Cultures were centrifuged at 4 °C and collected cells immediately frozen. Then, cells were disrupted in cold lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) pH 8, 50 mM NaF, 50 mM glycerol phosphate, 5 mM Na2P2O7, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate and 3 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)] and Pierce Protease Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by vigorous shaking with 0.75–1 mm glass beads in a FastPrep-24 (MP Biomedicals), at 5.5 m/s for 20 s, twice. Samples were then centrifuged at 4 °C, 13,000 rpm for 10 min to separate lysates from cell extracts and glass beads. Protein concentrations were determined by measuring samples at OD280 (Beckman DU640 spectrophotometer or Thermo Fisher Scientific Nanodrop 2000c), and extracts were normalized with lysis buffer to a final volume of 20 µL and boiled at 99 °C for 5 min with 20 µL of loading buffer (125 mM Tris HCl pH 6.8, 25% glycerol, 250 mM DTT, 5% SDS, and 0.2% lactophenol blue). Proper amount of protein samples was fractionated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using 10% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to 0.45-mm nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham™ Protran®) for 1 h at 110 V and for 2 h at 90 V for ERK5 blots. All gels were loaded with a prestained broad molecular weight 10–245 kDa protein ladder (Abcam). Then, membranes were blocked by 1 h incubation in 5% non-fat milk diluted in 0.1% Tween 20 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at room temperature, and probed with the following primary antibodies: 1:1000 rabbit monoclonal anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK-ERK1/2-Thr202/Tyr204 (Cell Signaling) for the detection of phosphorylated ERK1, ERK2, ERK5, ERK5ΔCt, Slt2, Kss1 and Fus3, 1:1000 mouse monoclonal anti-GFP (JL-8) (Clontech, Takara) for EGFP fusion proteins, 1:250 mouse monoclonal anti-DsRed2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for mRFP fusion proteins, 1:250 mouse monoclonal anti-MEK5 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for the detection of MEK5, 1:1000 mouse polyclonal anti-GST (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for GST fusion proteins, and 1:50000 rabbit polyclonal anti-glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (Sigma-Aldrich) for the detection of the protein G6PDH, encoded by the yeast gene ZWF1, as a loading control. It has been previously demonstrated that the total amount of this protein does not vary under conditions of CWI pathway stimulation50,51. The membranes were then washed five times in 0.1% Tween 20-PBS for 5 min and incubated in the dark for 1 h with a 1:5000 dilution of the secondary IRDye-680 or IRDye-800 anti-rabbit or anti-mouse goat polyclonal antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences). After repeating the previously described membrane washes, the fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies were detected using Odyssey CLx infrared (LI-COR Biosciences) or ChemiDoc™ MP (BioRad) imaging systems. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ or ImageLab. Data normalization was performed by dividing the protein band intensity by that of the G6PDH loading control. Standard deviation was calculated from three different experiments. The t-test for independent samples was applied to express the statistical significance of the results [p-value ≤ 0.05 (represented as *)/0.01 (**)/0.001 (***)].

Microscopy techniques and image processing

For in vivo fluorescence microscopy analysis, cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 1 min and examined on the Leica DMi8 automatic inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems) with the K8 camera and a filter for the visualization of cell morphology and size by Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) and the appropriate filter for the visualization of fluorescent epitope EGFP (Enhanced Green Florescent Protein). Images were captured using LasX (Leica Microsystems) and processed with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

AlphaFold

Protein-protein interaction predictions were carried out using ColabFold52 (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). This free software is executable in the Google Collaboratory environment and allows the prediction of protein folding and protein complex formation by integrating the MMSeqs2 algorithm (many-against-many sequence searching, capable of searching and clustering a high number of sequences) with AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold2-multimer33,34. Predictions were performed using ColabFold v1.5.5. version, selecting PDB100 as template mode and setting the recycle number (the number of times that the predicted structure will be fed back for refinement) as 3. All other parameters were left at their default values. After evaluating the prediction quality parameters, only predictions with an overall pLDDT (predicted Local Distance Difference Test) greater than 50, and a pTM (predicted Template Modelling score) and ipTM (interface predicted Template Modelling score) greater than 0.3 were considered. Of the results obtained, only the top-ranked (rank 1) predictions were used in this study. Structures were analysed using ChimeraX.
