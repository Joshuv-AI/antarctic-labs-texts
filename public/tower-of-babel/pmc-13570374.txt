Risk Factors for Loss of Hospital-Based Obstetric Care in Rural and Urban Hospitals
Authors: Julia D. Interrante, Caitlin Carroll, Kevan O’Hanlon, Emily C. Sheffield, Katy B. Kozhimannil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhich factors are associated with obstetric services loss via unit or hospital closure for rural and urban hospitals?FindingsThis cohort study of 3108 hospitals that provided obstetric services in 2010 found those with lower birth volumes had higher predicted probabilities of unit closure and higher predicted probabilities of hospital closure by 2023; unprofitable rural hospitals had higher predicted probability of unit closure, and urban predicted probabilities were higher for unit and hospital closure. Among hospitals maintaining obstetrics through 2023, 41% had high-risk factors in 2023 for future closure.MeaningResults of this study suggest that some factors differ for obstetric unit and hospital closure in rural and urban communities, suggesting distinct policy approaches.

Introduction

Between 2010 and 2022, 537 US hospitals discontinued obstetric services because the hospital stopped operating its obstetric unit or closed entirely.1 Service losses occurred in both rural and urban communities; by 2022, 52% of rural and 36% of urban hospitals lacked obstetric services.1 When rural communities lose access to obstetric services, residents may encounter barriers to accessing other perinatal services, including prenatal care,2,3 doula services, and perinatal mental health care.4,5 Evidence on impacts of rural obstetric services loss on maternal and infant health outcomes is mixed, finding lower cesarean delivery rates,2,6 but also increases in preterm births.7 Many urban residents also face access challenges after obstetric services loss, including longer travel times, limited options, and institutional mistrust.8,9

In surveys, rural hospital administrators have reported financial stressors and workforce shortages as factors affecting obstetric unit closure decisions.10,11 Rural hospitals that experienced obstetric services loss were smaller, more often for-profit, and were in higher poverty areas and places with fewer family physicians.12,13,14 In urban areas, obstetric services loss was more common among hospitals serving high proportions of publicly insured or uninsured patients15 or of racially minoritized patients.8

However, it is not known whether the factors associated with obstetric services loss differ for unit closure vs hospital closure. Additionally, variables associated with obstetric loss have not been compared for rural and urban areas. Doing so may highlight hospitals at risk of future loss and inform policies to maintain access.

The objectives of this analysis were to assess which hospital characteristics were associated with obstetric services loss and whether those characteristics differed for unit closure vs hospital closure and assess whether factors differed for rural and urban US hospitals between 2010 and 2023. Further, this analysis examined how hospital characteristics changed between 2010 and 2023 among hospitals that maintained obstetric services, and whether these hospitals may be at risk for future obstetric loss.

Methods

Data Sources and Study Population

We applied a validated method to identify obstetric services availability at all hospitals in the US using 2010 to 2023 data from American Hospital Association Annual Surveys, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ Provider of Services Files, and primary data from hospital websites and news articles.16 The data used in this study did not constitute human participant research and was exempt from requirement for informed consent, as determined by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board. This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. We linked data on hospital-level obstetric availability to annual hospital cost reports.17 Annual county characteristics were added from the Area Health Resources Files.18

This study included all hospitals that provided obstetric services in 2010. The main outcome was measured in 2023 in mutually exclusive categories indicating whether each hospital (1) maintained obstetric services in each year between 2010 and 2023, (2) remained open but closed their obstetric unit, or (3) closed entirely. Hospitals that had obstetric services in 2010 and 2023 but experienced a period between 2011 and 2022 when their obstetric unit was closed were excluded from analyses.

Hospital Characteristics

The following hospital characteristics were included because they have been associated with obstetric unit or hospital closures in prior literature12,13,19,20,21: distance to the nearest obstetric hospital, annual birth volume, adjusted average daily census (ADC), nurse-hours per patient day (eg, average nursing care a patient receives in a day), hospital ownership (government, not-for-profit, for-profit), Critical Access Hospital (CAH) status, profitability, and county unemployment rates. Variable measurements details are included in eMethods in Supplement 1. Distance, birth volume, and ADC were binned in rounded cutoff values approximating quartiles. Nurse-hours per patient day and county unemployment were dichotomized at approximate median values. Profitability was dichotomized as unprofitable (negative total margin) or profitable (otherwise). Medians and quartiles were calculated separately for rural and urban hospitals based on 2010 values. Rural or urban hospital location was based on the US Office of Management and Budget 2023 delineations of core based statistical areas.22 Counties in metropolitan statistical areas were considered urban; all others were considered rural.

Statistical Analysis

We first described characteristics of rural and urban hospitals in 2010 across outcome categories (maintained services, unit closed, hospital closed). We used Wald tests to assess statistical differences in characteristics among hospitals with unit or hospital closure by 2023 compared with hospitals that maintained services.

Second, we fit separate rural and urban multinomial logistic regression models to estimate adjusted predicted probabilities with 95% CIs of obstetric unit closure and hospital closure. We also estimated average marginal effects as percentage-point (pp) differences of each hospital characteristic on the probability of obstetric unit and hospital closure. Adjusted models included all described variables; unadjusted included a single variable. We conducted several sensitivity analyses: fitting a single model with rurality interaction, including state fixed effects, using different county-level predictors, including an extended set of variables, fitting separate binomial logistic regression models, and measuring outcomes in 2019 (prior to COVID-19).

Next, we examined whether key hospital characteristics–unprofitability and birth volume or ownership–interacted with closure rates. Specifically, we estimated adjusted predicted probabilities and average marginal effects of unprofitability at each birth volume quartile and by ownership category on obstetric unit and hospital closure using interaction models.

Finally, we assessed whether hospitals that maintained services between 2010 and 2023 experienced changes in predicted probability of obstetric loss in 2023 relative to predicted probability in 2010. Using estimates from the multinomial model, we calculated the predicted probability of obstetric loss for each surviving hospital using their 2023 characteristics and compared it with the same hospital’s probability of loss based on their 2010 characteristics. Among these hospitals, we also compared hospitals with low (below 75th percentile) vs high (75th percentile and above) predicted risk of closure in 2010, observing changes in characteristics over time to examine changes in risk profiles. Analyses were conducted with Stata/MP version 19 (StataCorp). Two-sided P < .05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 3108 hospitals were included in the analysis. Among the 1126 rural hospitals with obstetric services in 2010, 239 (21.2%) closed their obstetric unit and 42 (3.7%) closed entirely by 2023 (Table 1). Among 1982 urban obstetric hospitals, 250 (12.6%) closed their obstetric unit and 92 (4.6%) closed entirely by 2023. Hospital characteristics differed by obstetric category. Among rural hospitals, there were large differences in distance to the nearest obstetric hospital (<10 miles: 4% for hospitals maintaining service, 6% for unit closure, 21%, for hospital closure) and nursing care for patients (<7 nurse-hours per patient day: 55% for hospitals maintaining service, 70% for unit closure, 64% for hospital closure). In both rural and urban areas, the plurality of hospitals that maintained obstetric services were in the highest birth volume quartiles (rural, 31%; urban, 37%). Approximately one-third of obstetric hospitals that closed had for-profit status (rural, 29%; urban, 38%), and many obstetric units that closed were in CAHs (rural, 58%; urban, 11%). Unprofitable hospitals had higher unit closure (rural, 41%; urban, 30%) and hospital closure (rural, 43%; urban, 50%). Hospital characteristics descriptions are provided in eMethods 1 and eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

Predicted Probability of Obstetric Losses

Table 2 examines adjusted predicted probabilities of obstetric unit and obstetric hospital closure for rural and urban hospitals. Rural hospitals had 6-pp higher predicted probability of unit closure and 3-pp higher probability of hospital closure compared with urban hospitals (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Low birth volume and unprofitability had higher marginal effects associated with obstetric loss in both rural and urban hospitals. As birth volume decreased, the probability of unit closure increased. Among rural hospitals, probabilities ranged from 5% (95% CI, 2%-8%) in the highest birth quartile to 36% (95% CI, 30%-43%) in the lowest. Similarly, among urban hospitals, these probabilities ranged from 2% (95% CI, 1%-3%) to 32% (95% CI, 25%-39%). Urban hospitals in the lowest birth quartile also had 10% (95% CI, 7%-14%) probability of hospital closure. Unprofitable rural hospitals had a 30% (95% CI, 25%-35%) probability of unit closure, 12 pp higher than their profitable counterparts. In the adjusted model, being unprofitable was not a significant independent factor in rural hospital closure. Among unprofitable urban hospitals, probability of unit closure was 15% (95% CI, 12%-19%) and probability of hospital closure was 10% (95% CI, 7%-13%).

Proximity to another obstetric hospital and for-profit ownership were also significant factors associated with obstetric loss, both through unit and hospital closure. Rural obstetric hospitals within 30 miles of another obstetric hospital had greater than 7-pp higher probabilities of unit closure; this was similarly true for urban hospitals within 10 miles. Rural hospitals within 10 miles of another obstetric hospital had a higher probability of hospital closure (11%; 95% CI, 5%-18%). In rural areas, for-profit hospitals had a 13-pp higher probability of unit closure and 6-pp higher probability of hospital closure compared with government-owned hospitals. Further, rural for-profit hospitals had 16-pp higher probability of unit closure than urban for-profit hospitals (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Other factors (ADC, nurse staffing, unemployment, CAH status) were associated with some, but not all, loss categories. Lower ADC was associated with hospital closure, especially in urban areas but not with unit closure. Having fewer nurses and being in a county with higher unemployment were associated with unit closure only in rural areas. Conversely, being a CAH was protective against hospital closure in both rural and urban counties, with 5-pp lower predicted probability for rural hospitals and 4-pp point lower probability for urban hospitals; but it was not a significant factor associated with unit closure.

Given the importance of unprofitability, birth volume, and ownership, we examined effects of unprofitability in obstetric loss at different birth volumes and ownership categories (Figure 1; eTables 3-4 in Supplement 1). Unprofitability was a significant factor associated with obstetric loss when birth volume was lower. Among profitable rural hospitals, the probability of unit closure ranged from 3% (95% CI, 1%-6%) in the highest birth volume quartile to 32% (95% CI, 24%-40%) in the lowest; among their unprofitable counterparts, probabilities ranged from 11% (95% CI, 2%-19%) in the highest birth volume quartile to 47% (95% CI, 36%-58%) in the lowest. Unprofitable rural hospitals had a 15-pp higher probability of unit closure than profitable rural hospitals in all but the highest birth volume quartile. Patterns were similar among urban hospitals, but profitability differences were not statistically significant. Among urban hospitals, probabilities of hospital closure were higher for unprofitable than profitable hospitals in the lower birth volume quartiles. Unprofitable urban hospitals in the lowest birth volume quartile had a 24% (95% CI, 15%-34%) probability of hospital closure.

Changes in Hospital Characteristics and Future Risk of Obstetric Loss

Among hospitals that maintained obstetric services through 2023, mean probabilities of unit closure among rural and urban hospitals increased slightly from 2010 to 2023 but with wide variation. For rural hospitals, the mean pp change in probability of unit closure from 2010 to 2023 was 1.0 (IQR, −5.7 to 8.6). For urban hospitals, the mean pp change from 2010 to 2023 was 1.0 (IQR, −1.3 to 4.4) (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Hospital characteristics changed as birth volume consolidated into fewer, higher-volume hospitals that were farther apart (eTable 12 in Supplement 1).

Hospitals at high risk of any obstetric services loss in 2010 either continued to be at high risk or reduced closure risks by 2023 (Figure 2). Among rural high-risk hospitals, the share within the lowest birth volume quartile decreased from 41% in 2010 to 35% in 2023. Conversely, rural low-risk hospitals experienced an increase in the proportion at the lowest birth volume quartile (7% vs 16%). Temporal shifts in birth volume among urban hospitals were smaller and more consistent across baseline risk categories.

Larger magnitude 2010 to 2023 changes were found in the proportion of rural and urban hospitals that were unprofitable. Among all rural hospitals that maintained obstetric services, 21% were unprofitable in 2010, increasing to 35% in 2023. While only 10% of the low-risk rural hospitals were unprofitable in 2010 (vs 35% of high-risk), this difference decreased over time, such that the proportion of rural hospitals that were unprofitable by 2023 was similar in both risk groups (34% of the low-risk group and 37% of the high-risk group). Similar patterns emerged for urban hospitals, with 28% unprofitable across all risk groups by 2023.

Discussion

As obstetric care access declined in communities across the US from 2010 to 2023, lower birth volume and hospital unprofitability were factors associated with obstetric services loss, as were proximity to another obstetric hospital and for-profit hospital ownership. The importance of factors differed from obstetric loss due to unit or hospital closure and whether the hospital was in a rural or urban county. This study also highlights how hospitals that maintained obstetric services between 2010 and 2023 may be at risk for future obstetric loss. Approximately 1 in 5 obstetric hospitals were unprofitable in 2010, and approximately 40% of unprofitable hospitals closed their obstetric units by 2023. In 2023, more than 30% of remaining obstetric hospitals (1 in 3) were unprofitable, implying potential continued declines in obstetric care access.

Relative to existing literature,10,11,12,13 this analysis distinguished risk factors for obstetric unit closure and hospital closure and extended assessments of risk factors to urban hospitals. For example, lower birth volume had a larger marginal effect in unit closure than hospital closure. Unprofitability was a factor associated with unit closure but not of hospital closure among rural hospitals, while it was associated with both types of closure in urban hospitals. Conversely, for-profit ownership had a 13-pp and 6-pp higher predicted probability of unit and hospital closure among rural hospitals but was only associated with hospital closure in urban hospitals. Meanwhile, being a CAH was protective against hospital closure in both rural and urban hospitals but was not a factor in unit closure.

Prior literature on the role of unprofitability in rural hospital closure, regardless of obstetric services availability, is nuanced. Some found unprofitability was associated with rural hospital closure.20,23 Others found less than 10% of unprofitable rural hospitals closed19,20; those that did were smaller and in counties with high unemployment,24,25 factors adjusted for in this study. In all sensitivity analyses we conducted, most of the CI for unprofitability on rural hospital closure was on the positive side, and unprofitability was significant in unadjusted multinomial and adjusted binomial models, suggesting some potential complex role of unprofitability in rural obstetric hospital closure that may be intertwined with other variables.

Lower birth volume was a consistent factor associated with obstetric services loss, congruent with current payment systems in the US that link revenue with volume-based reimbursement (fee-for-service, global fee, value-based or pay-for-performance).10,26 Payment rates for obstetric care are lower than other clinical services and infrequently cover the fixed costs of providing this care.10,12,27,28,29 This is reflected in our findings on the birth volume-unprofitability interaction, and to different degrees in rural (where birth volume is lower30) and urban hospitals. The probability of rural unit closure was over 30 pp higher at the lowest compared with the highest birth volume quartiles, and risks were generally higher for unprofitable than profitable hospitals. Rural hospitals with less than 150 annual births were operating at similar risk (over 30%) of unit closure as urban hospitals with less than 500 births. Further, unprofitability had a greater marginal effect in unit closure in rural (12 pp) than urban (4 pp) hospitals. The rural and urban differences found in this study may be consistent with distinct challenges faced by these hospitals.

Financial strain is often named in announcements of obstetric unit closures, and some hospitals have implemented strategies such as resource sharing across service lines to help reduce financial pressures.10,11 However, obstetric unit margins are not always comprehensively assessed when evaluating service line value and impact on hospital finances, and financial decisions around obstetric unit closure may be made on incomplete information.10,31 Emergent financing policies and opportunities, such as the Transforming Maternal Health Model32 being implemented in 15 states and the Rural Health Transformation Program,33 may influence future decisions around operating obstetric units. While it is not realistic or appropriate to have a hospital in every community or for every hospital to have obstetric services, access to risk-appropriate care is essential to efforts to reduce maternal morbidity and mortality, and patients with clinically complex conditions in rural areas face barriers to accessing risk-appropriate care.34

Limitations

Obstetric unit and hospital closures are difficult to predict, and the model developed for these analyses was based on available data. Decisions around closures are nuanced,11 and some factors that influence closure decisions cannot be observed in administrative data. Qualitative research on this topic, for example, has highlighted how hospital leadership can champion maintaining obstetric services.10 In addition, many rural hospitals may continue operating obstetric services below financially viable birth volume thresholds because of community need.11

Baseline characteristics were measured in 2010 to estimate closure by 2023. Thus, we were unable to adjust for specific factors that may have impacted decisions around obstetric losses since 2010, such as Medicaid expansion and the COVID-19 pandemic. Similarly, some hospital closures may have been preceded by unit closures during the study period, and some unit closures in 2023 may result in future hospital closures, reducing our ability to distinguish between unit and hospital closure. However, observing changes over a longer period provided greater power to answer questions of interest, and in sensitivity analyses accounting for state effects, overall conclusions were consistent.

Conclusions

The results of this study suggest that some factors differ for obstetric unit and hospital closure in rural and urban communities, suggesting distinct policy approaches. Obstetric service losses may continue as more hospitals fall into categories associated with increased risk of obstetric loss, including unprofitability or low birth volume. In fact, 41% of hospitals that maintained services from 2010 to 2023 had high-risk factors in 2023 for future obstetric loss. However, the 915 hospitals at high risk in 2010 that were able to maintain services either reduced or maintained their risk level. Understanding what these hospitals did to maintain services may be important to developing strategies to ensure access to obstetric care. Assessing factors driving obstetric services availability in the US is important for identifying specific policies and programs needed to maintain access to critical health services during pregnancy and childbirth.
