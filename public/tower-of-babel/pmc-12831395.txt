Hepatitis C virus genotype 4 cluster with suspected sexual transmission in Chicago, IL

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is an infection that can cause inflammation of the liver and is estimated to have 50 million chronic cases and 1 million new infections per year globally [1]. HCV is split into seven confirmed genotypes (GT), which have a heterogenous geographic distribution throughout the world [2, 3]. Within the United States, 75% of HCV cases can be attributed to GT 1, followed by 20–25% of cases GT 2 and 3, and very few cases of GT 4, 5, 6, 7 [4]. HCV GT 4 globally accounts for around 12–15% of all HCV infections, with the highest prevalence of cases in Northern Africa, Central Africa, and the Middle East [3].

HCV transmission has most commonly been associated with direct blood exposure via blood transfusions, injection drug use (the most common transmission route in the United States), and mother-to-child transmission [5, 6]. Less common routes of transmission can include unsafe tattooing and piercings, sharing of personal items such as razors, and sex with a person infected with HCV [5, 6]. While the prevalence of GT 4 is low in the United States, a retrospective study between 1999 and 2002, noted that the most common risk factor for GT 4 in the United States was via injection drug use [7]. During 2018 and 2022, the number of newly diagnosed HCV cases in Chicago decreased by 47.9% from 1,836 cases in 2018 to 956 cases in 2022. Of the 956 cases in 2022, 84 (8.9%) were acute HCV and 59 (6.2%) were coinfected with HIV [8].

On July 25, 2024, the Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH) was notified by the Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH) of a potential cluster of HCV cases due to an increasing number of GT 4 HCV infections reported in Chicago. At the time of this initial detection of the cluster, there were 16 total cases (9 acute and 7 chronic). We conducted a thorough investigation to characterize demographics, clinical, behavioral characteristics, social networks, and identify core transmission patterns.

Methods

Case investigation

We defined a cluster case as HCV GT 4 infection in a person diagnosed during March 6, 2023 –October 21, 2024, who either resides in Chicago, IL, has visited Chicago, or resides in nearby counties and that was reported via the Illinois National Electronic Disease Surveillance System (INEDSS). Laboratory confirmation was conducted using HCV RNA or genotype testing [9]. Cases were classified as acute if they had evidence of recent seroconversion, a negative anti-HCV test followed by a subsequent positive test within a 12-month period [9], or elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels without other known causes of liver inflammation. Chronic cases were defined as individuals with a history of HCV infection or no evidence of recent seroconversion but a detectable HCV RNA test.

We abstracted information on prior HCV history, clinical presentation, HIV status, syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, and mpox history in Chicago via medical records, local, state, and national surveillance systems, such as enhanced HIV/AIDS reporting system (eHARS) for HIV, Chicago Health Information Management System (CHIMS) for syphilis, and INEDSS for gonorrhea, chlamydia, and mpox and matched to HCV cluster cases. We examined reported risk factors, including injection drug use and sexual activity, to characterize potential transmission networks.

We attempted to interview all patients who met the case definition for acute and chronic stage of disease to assess clinical symptoms, risk behaviors, social networks, and healthcare access. Risk behaviors included sexual activity and location, recent tattoos, dentist appointments, medical procedures, including med spas, non-injection drug use and injection drug use. Initial outreach was conducted via phone calls, followed by field visits for patients who could not be reached.

Partner investigations

We elicited contacts via case interviews and attempted to interview all contacts and link to testing sites. Contacts were defined as those who were sexual or needle sharing partners. Contact outreach was conducted via phone calls only by highly trained staff.

Social network

Social networks were analyzed by a directed, binary network graph connecting nodes with edges. Nodes are defined here as cases, partners, common locations, or geosocial apps. Edges are defined as a connection that is self-reported from the cluster cases.

Statistical analysis

We examined descriptive statistics of demographic, behavioral, and clinical characteristics using SAS version 9.4.

Ethics

This work was classified as public health surveillance activity under the 45 CFR 46.102(l)(2) regulation and therefore was a non-research activity and informed consent was not needed. It was conducted consistent with U.S. Federal law and CDPH policy.

Results

Case investigation

During March 6, 2023-October 21, 2024, a total of 23 acute and 7 chronic GT 4 HCV cases were reported to the CDPH (Fig. 1). Most cases were males (30, 100%) who lived on the Northside of Chicago (24, 80%) (Table 1; Fig. 2). The median age was 37 years (IQR = 32–44 years) and most identified as White, non-Latino (12, 40%), followed by Latino (10, 33%). Nineteen (63%) cases were among persons with HIV (PWH); of those, 27 (90%) had well-controlled HIV (CD4 count > 200 cells/mm³ and viral load < 200 copies/mL). Among the 19 PWH, 16 (84%) were acute HCV cases and 3 (16%) were chronic HCV cases. Moreover, 89% of PWH had a transmission risk of men who have sex with men (MSM) at the time of their HIV diagnosis. Of the 11 HIV-negative persons, 5 (45%) reported being on HIV Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP). Twenty-four (80%) patients had a history of a sexually transmitted infection (STI) in Chicago prior to HCV diagnosis; 23 (77%) patients had an STI ≤ 12 months prior to HCV diagnosis accounting for 96% of all prior STIs, with 13 (43%) of those being concurrent infections.

Among the 30 cluster cases, 18 (60%) were fully (n = 13) or partially (n = 5) interviewed, 9 (30%) unable to locate (UTL), and 3 (10%) declined interview. Of the18 patients who were interviewed, 15 (83%) reported male sex partners within the prior year, 1 (6%) reported female sex partners within the year, and 2 (11%) declined to report sex of sex partners. Twelve (67%) reported anonymous sex, 2 (11%) reported engaging in group sex via sex parties, and 1 (6%) reported chemsex (intentional use of drugs to enhance sexual activity) within the year prior to HCV diagnosis. No patients interviewed reported attending med spas. Two (11%) of those interviewed reported injection drug use and 4 (22%) reported non-injection drug use, but type of drug varied between patients. At time of interview, 15 (83%) reported receiving hepatitis C treatment, with 6 (40%) of patients having finished treatment. Twelve patients knew the name of the treatment they were taking, with 6 cases taking glecaprevir/pibrentasvir and 6 were taking sofosbuvir/velpatasvir. Of the 3 interviewed cases not receiving any treatment at time of interview, one was waiting for insurance to approve the medication, one had an appointment in the coming weeks to start treatment, and the third was UTL after the first partial interview.

Partner investigations

Among the 185 sex partners named by HCV cluster cases, only 8% (14) were initiable or able to be contacted with the rest of the partners being anonymous (Table 2). We attempted 30 total phone contacts with the range of 1–5 contacts per partner. Three (21%) of the initiable partners were successfully contacted and fully or partially interviewed and 11 (79%) were UTL. Of the 3 interviewed partners, 1 (33%) was linked to HCV testing and tested negative and 2 (67%) self-reported regular STI testing that includes HCV testing and tested negative within the past two weeks of partner interview. One named partner subsequently became a case within our cluster, but he was UTL in both partner and case investigations.

Social network analysis

Many of the cases reported meeting partners on geosocial apps. Among those cases who were interviewed (n = 18), 5 (28%) reported meeting sex partners on Grindr, 4 (22%) reported meeting on Sniffies, and 1 (6%) reported Scruff. While there was only one named partner that subsequentially became a cluster case, two cluster cases also named the same partner. This partner was deemed UTL. Additionally, 3 (17%) reported attending the same bathhouse in the year prior to their HCV diagnosis.

Discussion

For this HCV genotype 4 cluster in Chicago, IL, we have concluded that the suspected route of transmission was predominately through sexual transmission. We did not identify a common potential exposure location. Moreover, only two of those interviewed (n = 18) reported injection drug use (11%). It worth to note, that patients within this cluster that were interviewed (n = 18) had high number of multiple sex partners (median = 5, IQR =2–8.5.5, with a range of 1–60), and reported to have anonymous sex partners Among all cluster patients (n = 30) 80% (n = 24) had a prior history and/or concurrent STIs and 63% (n = 19) were among PWH.

While GT 4 HCV is relatively uncommon in the United States, studies in Europe have shown that GT 4 and GT 1 have large networks of transmission among MSM, including PWH and persons without HIV [10–12]. Like our cluster, the vast majority of these cases denied participating in injection drug use. Condomless anal sex, fisting, HIV infection, and concurrent STIs are associated with increased risk of HCV [13, 14]. Permucosal transmission has been shown to be a likely route of transmission among MSM with intra-rectal exposure to semen during anal sex [13, 15]. Studies have shown that the increased risk of HCV among MSM has coincided with an increase of other trends including the use of geosocial apps and the association with STIs [16].

Among our cluster patients interviewed (n = 18), 83% (n = 15) had received treatment. Studies in the United States reported that only 35% of individuals received treatment [17]. Additional analyses on provider type and insurance status is warranted to understand the high treatment rate within this cluster.

This investigation has multiple limitations. First, as majority of the GT 4 HCV cases were found retrospectively, we were unable to perform whole genome sequencing to see the similarities of the genetic sequences of each sample. Second, some data could only be collected via self-report and may have been subject to recall bias and social desirability bias concerning drug use and sexual activity. We were unable to interview all patients due to patients declining interview or being unable to locate. Partner contact tracing was low among cluster cases due to a high proportion reporting anonymous sex, we were unable to interview all named partners due to partners declining interview or being unable to locate. Additionally, some behavioral questions among those partially interviewed (28%) may not be complete. Finally, per the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and the Infectious Diseases Society of America HCV genotype testing is no longer required for all individuals due to pangenotypic treatment, and not all of Chicago HCV cases receive genotype testing and therefore this cluster could be larger [18].

Conclusions

While HCV transmission via sexual activity is still relatively low, there has been an increase in the past two decades [19]. To reduce the risk of STIs, including HCV that is sexually acquired, persons can use condoms during sex, ask about their partner’s STI status, and get tested for STIs regularly. HCV re-infection rates among MSM are a major concern as it has been observed to occur in higher rates than primary infection among MSM in Western Europe and NYC [19, 20]. In the future, sequencing of these rare GTs should be considered due to the potential of unusual subtypes, which could cause unresponsiveness to current treatment regimens [21]. There was a high rate of STI co-infection (77%), therefore prevention tools such as condom use remains strongly recommended for individuals with concurrent STI and HIV infection, those with multiple or anonymous partners, and individuals participating in sexual networks where HIV transmission has been documented. Regular STI screening is essential as co-existing infections increase susceptibility to HCV acquisition through genital or rectal mucosal inflammation. In summary, while sexual transmission of HCV remains relatively uncommon in the general population, certain behaviors and co-existing conditions such as STIs and HIV substantially increase the risk. There is a need for tailored counseling that will incorporate individual risk factors. Using a syndemic approach that holistically supports patients experiencing HCV, HIV, STIs including gonorrhea, chlamydia, and syphilis, is crucial to support people with HCV infection that may have been sexually transmitted.
