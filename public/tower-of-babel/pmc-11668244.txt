How Do Variants of Residues in the First Coordination Sphere, Second Coordination Sphere, and Remote Areas Influence the Catalytic Mechanism of Non-Heme Fe(II)/2-Oxoglutarate Dependent Ethylene-Forming Enzyme?
Authors: Midhun
George Thomas, Simahudeen Bathir Jaber Sathik Rifayee, Christo Z. Christov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The ethylene-forming enzyme (EFE) is a Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate (2OG) and l-arginine (l-Arg)-dependent oxygenase that primarily decomposes 2OG into ethylene while also catalyzing l-Arg hydroxylation. While the hydroxylation mechanism in EFE is similar to other Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases, the formation of ethylene is unique. Various redesign strategies have aimed to increase ethylene production in EFE, but success has been limited, highlighting the need for alternate approaches. It is crucial to incorporate an accurate and comprehensive description of the integrative and multidimensional effects of the protein environment to enhance the redesign strategy in metalloenzymes, particularly in EFE. This involves understanding the role of the second coordination sphere (SCS) and long-range (LR) interacting residues, correlated motions, electronic structure, intrinsic electric field (IntEF), as well as the stabilization of transition states and reaction intermediates. In this study, we employ a molecular dynamics-based quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics approach to examine the integrative effects of the protein environment on reactions catalyzed by EFE variants from the first coordination sphere (FCS, D191E), SCS (A198V and R171A) and LR (E215A). The study uncovers how substitutions at different positions in EFE similarly impact the ethylene-forming reaction while posing distinct effects on the hydroxylation reaction. Results predict the effect of the variants in controlling the 2OG coordination mode in the Fe(II) center. Specifically, the study suggests that D191E uniquely prefers transitioning from an off-line to an in-line 2OG coordination mode before dioxygen binding. However, studies on the 2OG flip in the presence of off-line approaching dioxygen and dioxygen binding in the D191E variant indicate that the 2OG flip might not be feasible in the 5C Fe(II) state. Calculations show the possibility of a hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)-assisted oxygen flip in EFE and its variants (other than D191E). MD simulations elucidate the characteristic dynamic change in the α7 region in the D191E variant that might contribute to its increased hydroxylation reaction. Results indicate the possibility of forming an in-line ferryl from the IM2 (Fe(III)-partial bond intermediate) in the D191E variant. This alternative pathway from IM2 may also exist in WT EFE and other variants, which are yet to be explored. The study also delineates the impact of substitutions on the electronic structure and IntEF. Overall, the calculations support the idea that understanding the integrative and multidimensional effects of the protein environment on the reactions catalyzed by EFE variants provides the basics for improved enzyme redesign protocols of EFE to increase ethylene production. The results of this study will also contribute to the development of alternate redesign strategies for other metalloenzymes.

Introduction

Ethylene is widely used in various industries as a crucial building block for manufacturing plastics, fibers, textiles, and other chemicals.1,2 It is primarily produced from fossil fuels,3 leading to significant environmental challenges such as resource depletion and the release of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere,4 implying the need to develop new strategies for ethylene production with reduced environmental impact. An important alternative is enzymatic ethylene synthesis by certain bacteria that contain an ethylene-forming enzyme (EFE).5 EFE is a Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate (2OG) dependent oxygenase that also requires l-arginine (l-Arg) and catalyzes two main reactions (Figure 1A).5−11 The minor reaction involves the oxidative decarboxylation of 2OG to succinate, followed by l-Arg hydroxylation. Hydroxylated l-Arg rapidly decomposes to form 1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C). The hydroxylation mechanism catalyzed by EFE is similar to that of many other members of Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases.12−18 In contrast, the major reaction catalyzed by EFE is a unique chemical transformation that involves the decomposition of 2OG into three molecules of CO2 and ethylene.5,8,9,19−22 Recent studies by Bollinger et al. have also demonstrated the formation of 3-hydroxypropionate (3-HP) in trace amounts (Figure 1A).7,9 Although enzyme-catalyzed ethylene production by EFE releases CO2, it is relatively more sustainable than the significant greenhouse gas emissions generated during industrial ethylene production. Additionally, recent studies have demonstrated that expressing EFE in cyanobacteria enables light-driven CO2 fixation, further mitigating its environmental impact.23−25

In the active site of EFE, the Fe(II) center is positioned within the antiparallel β-barrel sheet and coordinated by two histidine residues (H189 and H268) and the side chain carboxylate oxygen of aspartate residue (D191) (Figure 1B).8,22 This coordination motif, called the facial triad, is a general feature of Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases.26,27 Crystallographic8,11,22 and spectroscopic8,10,11,22 studies of the Fe(II)/2OG enzymes confirmed that in their resting states, in addition to the facial triad, Fe(II) coordinates three water molecules to form a six-coordinated (6C) state.28−31 Two of these water molecules are displaced by 2OG via a ligand-exchange reaction, which does not change the coordination state.28−30 The remaining water is displaced upon binding of the main substrate to the active site (without coordination to the Fe), thus creating a five-coordination (5C) state with an open coordination space for binding the dioxygen molecule.20,29,30

The catalytic mechanism of EFE has been intensively studied recently.5,7−11,19−21,32−34 Experimental and computational studies have shown that the l-Arg is necessary for ethylene formation.8,10,20 Computational studies have elucidated the role of the protein environment in directing and binding the dioxygen to the Fe(II)-center to form the Fe(III)-superoxo (Fe(III)·OO•–) complex.32 Further, a study has illustrated the role of the second coordination sphere (SCS) residues in controlling the 2OG coordination mode in the Fe(II)-center.32,33 Studies have also demonstrated that the two reactions catalyzed by EFE branches from the off-line EFE·Fe(III)·OO•–·l-Arg complex (in off-line orientation, the C1 carboxylate oxygen of the 2OG is trans to H268).9,19−21 The hydroxylation reaction proceeds with dioxygen activation through the nucleophilic attack of the distal oxygen (Od) of metal-bound superoxide on the C2 of 2OG, leading to decarboxylation and Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide intermediate formation (Figure 2). This intermediate undergoes O–O bond cleavage to form an off-line ferryl intermediate (EFE·Fe(IV)=O·l-Arg). Initially directed away from l-Arg, the ferryl oxygen undergoes a ferryl flip to form an in-line EFE·Fe(IV)=O·l-Arg complex, directing the ferryl oxygen toward l-Arg. The in-line ferryl intermediate abstracts hydrogen from the substrate, generating a Fe(III)–OH intermediate and a substrate radical. The hydroxyl ion from the Fe(III)–OH intermediate rebounds, leading to the Fe(II) state and hydroxylated l-Arg (Figure 2).19,21

Proposed reaction pathway for the divergent reactions catalyzed by WT EFE. The hydroxylation pathway is shown in blue arrows, and the ethylene and 3-HP formation is in magenta. Here, R represents the substrate l-Arg.

In contrast, during ethylene formation, instead of forming a Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide intermediate, dioxygen activation results in the formation of a Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate intermediate by inserting dioxygen between the C1–C2 bonds of 2OG (Figure 2). This intermediate forms Fe(III)-carbonate and a succinyl radical, which releases a CO2 molecule to form a propanoate-3-yl radical. This propanoate-3-yl decomposes to produce ethylene and two CO2 molecules (Figure 2).19 Later, experimental studies validated the formation of Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate intermediate for ethylene formation, confirming our computational predictions.9

More recent studies have further demonstrated that the propanoate-3-yl radical recombines with Fe(III)-bicarbonate, restoring the Fe(II) state to yield the Fe(II)-2-(1-carboxyethyl) carbonate complex (Figure 2).7,9 A Grob-like polar concerted fragmentation on the formed intermediate is proposed to yield ethylene and two CO2 molecules. Experimental evidence also suggests that the formation of 3-HP likely diverges from the Fe(II)-2-(1-carboxyethyl) carbonate complex due to partial fragmentation (Figure 2).7,9

Recently, intensive studies have been aimed at increasing enzymatic ethylene production.8,10,11,22,33,34 Several SCS and long range (LR) variants have been experimentally studied for ethylene and P5C production.7−9,11,33 Most variants have been shown to reduce ethylene and P5C formation.8,9 However, a few variants have been shown to retain the capacity for hydroxylation to various extents (A198V (60%), F283A (20%)).8 Further experimental and computational studies have been performed to produce ethylene using alternate substrates8,10,22 (l-canavanine, d-Arg, NG-Me-Arg, agmatine). Interestingly, the substitution of the Fe-coordinated aspartate to glutamate (D191E) is the only variant that has constructively increased the hydroxylation reaction while reducing the ethylene-forming reaction.11 Recent studies demonstrated how the inactive l-Arg analog l-canavanine and two inactive EFE variants, R171A and Y306A, affect the EFE structure and LR-correlated motions.33l-Canavanine fails to switch the coordination mode of D191,33 the SCS variant R171A influences the l-Arg binding and the Fe(II) chelation, and the LR variant Y306A leads to the formation of an interior cavity, which triggers multiple local and distal structural changes.33 However, how these and other variants influence the reaction mechanism remains unexplored. Moreover, a crucial missing point in the current redesign strategies and protocols is the lack of comprehensive and reliable incorporation of the integrated protein environment through its dynamic effects,35−38 correlated motions,39−42 SCS/LR interactions,43−47 and electric field effects.48−50 The incorporation of these effects might increase the efficiency of the redesign strategies.

Studies on other Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases have demonstrated that SCS and LR substitutions can significantly affect enzyme activity.46,47,51−53 Research on the TET2 enzyme has revealed that even distant substitutions from the Fe(II) center can reduce enzyme activity.54,55 Experimental and computational studies on KDM2A, KDM4A, and KDM7B have also shown how substitutions impact active site conformations and demethylation activity.14,46,53,56,57 Experimental studies have indicated that SCS and LR substitutions in nonheme enzymes can lead to nonstandard reactions, as in the nitrene transfer reaction in EFE.31,58 Additionally, studies on Fe(II)/2OG-dependent halogenases, such as AdaV and BesD, have highlighted the effect of substitutions on competing hydroxylation and halogenation reactions.59−61 Experimental studies on penicillin-forming oxidase, which is structurally similar to 2OG-dependent oxygenases, revealed that SCS substitutions can induce rapid motions in the enzymes.62−64 Therefore, it is crucial to understand how substitutions in EFE affect its catalytic mechanism by considering their impact on the integrative protein environment.

The present study aims to elucidate this missing knowledge by investigating four experimentally characterized EFE variants: A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A (Figure 1B).8,11 Revealing how these variants influence the complex effects of the protein environment on the EFE catalysis will provide guidance on how in reverse to make more effective EFE variants that enhance ethylene production.

The residue A198 contributes to a hydrophobic cage surrounding the EFE active site. Experimental findings indicate that substituting A198 to valine results in 60% of the hydroxylation activity relative to the wild-type (WT) enzyme, with minimal ethylene formation (less than 5%).8 Conversely, variant D191E enhances the hydroxylation rate compared to the WT EFE while significantly reducing ethylene production.11 The other selected variants, E215A and R171A, abolish hydroxylation and ethylene-forming reactions. E215 is distal from the active site, while R171 forms a hydrogen bonding interaction with the nonbonding C1 carboxylate oxygen of 2OG.8 Overall, two variants exhibit hydroxylation reactions, while the others are less efficient at catalyzing the reactions. These variants can be categorized based on the position: D191E as a first coordination sphere (FCS) variant, A198V and R171A as SCS variants, and E215A as an LR variant (Figure 1B).

The current study utilizes a molecular dynamics (MD)-based quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) study to elucidate the role of four experimentally characterized EFE variants (Figure 1B) on the reaction mechanism of l-Arg hydroxylation in EFE by means of (i) effects on the geometries and energies of the key transition states (TSs) and reaction intermediates (RIs) along the reaction path; (ii) effects on the contributions of the SCS/LR residues and correlated motions; (iii) effects on the electronic structure and key molecular orbitals; (iv) effects on the intrinsic electric field (IntEF). The study reveals how substitutions in EFE in the FCS, SCS, and remote areas (RAs) affect the transformation between the off-line/in-line 2OG/ferryl coordination modes of the Fe center. The results support experimental findings that substitutions at different positions in EFE—FCS, SCS, and LR—can exercise a sensitive effect on the catalyzed reactions by EFE. By understanding the multidimensional atomistic effects of the EFE variants (which block the ethylene formation), we can selectively design new variants that have the opposite/reverse effects on the integrative protein environment and, in this way, improve the ethylene production by EFE.

Methods

Model Preparations

The initial structures for the selected EFE variants (A198V, D191E, and E215A) were prepared using the same parameters and protocols as in our previous studies of WT EFE19 and the R171A variant.33 To model the off-line EFE·Fe(III)·OO•– complexes of the A198V and E215A variants, we utilized the crystal structure of the WT EFE complex with Mn(II), 2OG, and l-Arg (PDB: 5V2Y) as the starting point.8 The Mn(II) in the crystal structure was replaced with Fe(II), and dioxygen was coordinated to the Fe(II) center in trans to H189. The in-silico substitutions for A198V and E215A were implemented using the Leap module of AMBER 18 software.65 Hydrogen atoms were then added based on their protonation states using Amber routines.66 In EFE and its variants A198V and E215A, the protonation states of the histidine residues were identified as follows: H116, H169, H189, H233, and H268 were protonated at the δ position, while H168 and H305 were protonated at the ε position. Additionally, histidine residues H284, H309, and H335 were found to be doubly protonated.

The starting structure of the D191E variant was accessed using the crystal structure of the variant using the accession code PDB ID: 6VP5.11 A similar procedure was adopted as with A198V and E215A for modeling the off/in-line EFE·Fe(III)·OO•– complex of D191E. In the D191E variant, all histidine residues were found to be singly protonated. H116, H169, H189, and H268 were protonated at the δ position, while H168, H233, H284, H305, H309, and H335 were protonated at the ε position.

Topology files for nonstandard residues such as 2OG and dioxygen were generated using the Antechamber module with the GAFF tool.67 Parameters for the Fe(III) center (high spin (HS) S = 2, M = 5 after antiferromagnetic coupling between the iron and dioxygen),68−73 coordinating with 2OG, H189, D/E191, H268, and dioxygen, were generated using the Metal Center Parameter Builder (MCPB v3.0).74 The rest of the proteins were parameterized using the FF14SB force field implemented in Amber.75 The system was solvated by a 10 Å water box, which extended from the residue’s farthest point, and counterions (Na+) were added to achieve neutralization.

Molecular Dynamic Simulations

The MD simulation procedure followed the same protocol as in our previous WT EFE19 and R171A variant33 studies. A two-stage minimization at the MM level was conducted before the simulation. In the initial stage, solvent molecules were minimized with a restraint of 100 kcal/mol Å2 on the solute. Subsequently, complete relaxation was allowed during the second stage of minimization. The system underwent 5000 steps of the steepest descent and 5000 steps of the conjugate gradient for energy minimization.

The minimized system was gradually heated from 0 to 300 K in the NVT ensemble using a Langevin thermostat, with a 50 kcal/mol Å2 restraint on the solute molecules for 250 ps.76 Subsequently, the heated system underwent constant heating with a weak restraint on the solute molecules at 300 K for 1 ns in an NPT ensemble to maintain constant density. The system was then equilibrated for 50 ns at 300 K and 1 bar in the NPT ensemble without restraints. From the well-equilibrated system, MD production runs were conducted for 1 μs in an NPT ensemble with 1 bar pressure. Constant pressure was maintained using the Berendsen Barostat,77 and hydrogen atoms were constrained by applying the SHAKE algorithm.78 Long-range interactions within a cutoff of 10 Å were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method.79 All simulations were performed using the GPU version of Amber-PEMEMD, and periodic boundary conditions were applied in all simulations.80

The MD trajectory was analyzed using various software,81,82 and hydrogen bonding analysis was conducted using CPPTRAJ.83 Dynamic Cross-Correlation Analysis (DCCA), Differential DCCA (d-DCCA), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) were carried out using Bio3d on the backbone Cα atoms.84

Dioxygen Diffusion Dynamics

The dioxygen diffusion pathway for the D191E variant was investigated using a protocol similar to that employed in our previous studies of WT EFE.32 A total of 100 dioxygen molecules were randomly distributed around the protein using the PACKMOL software.85 The diffusion dynamics were performed following the procedure described in Section 2.2.

QM/MM Calculations

QM/MM calculations were conducted following a methodology similar to that implemented in the WT EFE studies.19 Snapshots from the well-equilibrated regions of the MD simulations were chosen for the QM/MM studies. Excess water molecules within 12 Å from the protein were initially truncated in the selected snapshots.

The QM/MM calculations were performed using ChemShell software,86 with the QM part treated by Turbomole87 and DL_POLY88 for the MM part. The QM/MM boundary was defined by hydrogen link atoms using the charge shift model.89 The QM region comprised the FCS residues such as Fe(III), 2OG, H189, D/E191, H268, dioxygen, and l-Arg substrate (Figure 1B). The residues of the facial triads were truncated between the Cα–Cβ bond, including only the side chains of H189, D/E191, and H268 in the QM region as in the WT studies. The QM region consists of 75 atoms for the WT and its variants A198V, E215A, and R171A of EFE. In the D191E variant, the QM region contains 78 atoms in total. The overall charge of the QM region for the WT and its variants is neutral (0).

The MM region consisted of residues within 8 Å of the QM region residues. The QM region was treated with the unrestricted B3LYP (UB3YP) functional,90 while MM regions utilized Amber force fields (FF14SB),75 and the region beyond the MM region was fixed.

Both experimental and computational studies have demonstrated that dioxygen activation and substrate oxidation happen at the HS quintet state (S = 2 and M = 5).91−93 Therefore, all mechanistic studies were performed in the quintet state. Geometry optimizations and frequency calculations were performed using the UB3LYP functional90 and def2-SVP basis set94,95 [QM(B1)/MM], as demonstrated in WT EFE19 and many other 2OG enzymes.14−16,46,55,96−100 A relaxed scan from the optimized geometries was performed to identify the TS along the reaction coordinate. The dimer method was used to optimize the highest energy geometry for the TS from the potential energy surface without any constraints.

All optimized geometries underwent vibrational frequency calculations to confirm the minima and TS using harmonic approximations with the Hessian matrix, as implemented in Turbomole. Energy corrections were applied through an improved single-point calculation with UB3LYP functional and def2-TZVP basis set [QM(B2)/MM].95 The final reported energies of all geometries include zero-point energy (ZPE) corrections as [QM(B2 + ZPE)/MM]. The absolute energies of the QM/MM optimization at the B1, B2, and B3 levels are provided in the Supporting Information (SI) with the coordinates. The accuracy of the results was tested by performing additional calculations using (i) an extended QM region that includes the SCS residue R277, increasing the total number of QM atoms to 101 with a QM charge of +1, and (ii) single-point calculations using Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction. The results of these test calculations are provided in the SI (Tables S1–S3).

Energy Decomposition Analysis (EDA) was utilized to identify residues that stabilized TS through nonbonded interactions.101,102 TITAN was employed to compare the reactants’ and intermediates’ IntEF, as performed in the EFE WT studies.19,103 Natural Orbitals (NO) and Spin Natural Orbitals (SNO) were calculated using Gaussian.104

Results and Discussion

Conformational Dynamics of the Reactant Complexes (EFE·Fe(III)·OO•–·l-Arg) in EFE Variants

To elucidate the effect of substitutions on conformational flexibility of the active site, substrates, the overall enzyme as well as the LR-correlated motions, we performed 1 μs MD simulations for the D191E, A198V, and E215A EFE variants and compared them to the published data for the WT19 and R171A33 proteins (Figures S1–S4).

Similar to the simulations for the WT enzyme, simulations for all variants show that the C5 oxygens of 2OG are stabilized by a salt bridge interaction with R277 (Figure S5), while the noncoordinating C1 oxygen of 2OG forms a hydrogen bond with R171 in all variants except the R171A protein (Figure S5).8,11,19,22,33 Specifically, in the D191E variant, R171 also interacts with the nonbonding C1 carboxylate of 2OG through a water-mediated hydrogen bond (Figure S5). Although there are slight changes in the orientations of the Fe-coordinating ligands, the overall geometry of the Fe center is similar to that of the WT enzyme (Figure S6). Further, in the A198V and E215A variants, the noncoordinating oxygens of D191 form hydrogen bonds only with the η2NH2 group of the l-Arg substrate (Figure S7). In contrast, in the D191E variant, the noncoordinating oxygen interacts with both the η2NH2 and η1NH2 groups of l-Arg, which may influence the orientation of the guanidinium group of l-Arg within the active site of these variants (Figure S7). Additionally, our previous studies on the R171A variant suggested that the interaction of the nonbonding carboxylate oxygen of the D191 with l-Arg is absent in the R171A variant, where it was shown that the guanidinium group flips between the C1 carboxylate oxygens of 2OG and D191 (Figure S7).33

Experimental and computational studies of WT EFE have demonstrated that l-Arg also interacts with residues E84, V85, T86, and R316.8,19,22 The studies of the EFE variants confirm that the side chain of E84 forms a strong hydrogen bond with the εNH of l-Arg, and the amino group of l-Arg interacts with T86 in the A198V and E215A variants; however, these interactions are absent in D191E, where the carboxylate group of E84 is oriented toward the ηNH2 of l-Arg to form a weak hydrogen bond and T86 interacts with εNH of l-Arg through hydrogen bonding (Figure S8). Additionally, the backbone oxygen of V85 forms a hydrogen bond with the amino group of l-Arg in the A198V and D191E variants, as observed in previous studies of the R171A variant (Figure S8).33 Overall, there is a substantial change in the orientation of l-Arg in the EFE variants compared to the WT enzyme (Figure 3); a similar observation was made in a previous study on the R171A variant, where l-Arg extended further away from the Fe center.33 In this study, the A198V and E215A variants show a similar orientation for l-Arg, which is more squeezed than what is seen in the WT enzyme but maintains the same direction. The most significant change in l-Arg orientation is observed in the D191E variant, where the backbone of l-Arg is in a similar position to that in the WT enzyme, but the guanidinium group extends toward E84 (Figures 3 and S8).

Orientation of the l-Arg substrate in WT (yellow), A198V (red), D191E (green), E215A (magenta), and R171A (blue) versions of EFE.

Furthermore, the crystal structure of WT EFE shows a twisted peptide bond involving residues D191 and Y192 (dihedral angle of 146.8° for D191-Cα, D191-C, Y192–N, and Y192–C).8 MD simulations of WT EFE19 and the EFE variants showed a relaxed twisted peptide conformation. Further, simulations indicated that the side chain of Y192 orients toward the l-Arg in the MD simulation of A198V and E215A variants; however, it points away from l-Arg in the D191E and R171A variants (Figure S9).

Substitutions of residues influence not only the local site, but also the integrative protein environment, and one of the ways of doing this is via perturbation of its correlated dynamics.35,36,39,40 We, therefore, explored the correlated motions of the EFE variants in their EFE·Fe(III)·OO•–·l-Arg states in comparison to the WT EFE by performing DCCA and PCA. Previous studies identified three flexible regions in WT EFE: Region (1) Residues 80–93 (β3 and β4 and connecting loop), Region (2) β11 and connecting loops (residues 211–245), and Region (3) Residues 291–303 composing β15 and α7 with connecting loops (Figure S10).19 Prior studies had shown that Region 1 is involved in the stabilization of the l-Arg in the active site, Region 2 protects the active site from solvent exposure, and Region 3 is involved in the formation of a unique hydrophobic region around the active site of EFE.19 This study shows that the substitution of resides in FCS, SCS, and LR affects the overall pattern of flexibility in EFE. For instance, Regions 2 and 3 are flexible in the E215A variant but not Region 1. Similarly, Region 3 is flexible in the A198V and D191E variants but not Regions 1 and 2. Apart from these three regions, no other flexible regions were observed in the A198V and E215A variants (Figure S11). However, in the D191E variant, the α7 region (residues 303–320) was also found to be flexible. This unique flexibility in the α7 region of the D191E variant might contribute to its increased rate of hydroxylation (Figure S11).

WT EFE studies had also shown that the three flexible regions in EFE undergo interdependent motions (Figure 4A),19 suggesting that Region 3 is dynamically anticorrelated with Regions 1 and 2.19 DCCA of the variants shows no evidence of these interdependent motions since the substitutions have affected their flexibility. The unique flexible region (α7) observed in the D191E variant is shown to have a positive correlation with Region 3 and anticorrelated motion with Region 1, which might affect the reaction catalyzed by the D191E variant (Figure 4).

The positive values shown in cyan represent the positively correlated motions. The negative values in pink represent anticorrelated motions. The identified flexible regions in the WT EFE are shown in circles.

Our earlier MD study on R171A33 shows the impact of substitution on the active site, particularly on substrate orientation and LR-correlated motions, indicating that the LR correlation of the region α7–9 and its connecting loops (residues 305–340) that interact with the R171 residue disrupts the variant protein thus affecting the substrate stability in the active site.33

Overall, the complex networking of the different regions in the EFE is affected by single residue substitutions. Further, the results hypothetically suggest that the absence of the interdependent correlated motions of the three flexible regions might have contributed to the poor ethylene-forming activities for the variants. Additionally, the unique flexible region observed in the D191E variant might have contributed to its greater hydroxylation reactivity. This study suggests that regardless of the location of the substitution in the FCS, SCS, or RA, it can induce a major effect on the substrate binding and the network of the correlated motions, thus ultimately influencing the catalysis in EFE. Importantly, the results indicate that the closeness or remoteness of the site of substitution cannot suggest the strength of its impact on catalysis and, specifically, that substitutions of residues in RA can profoundly influence enzyme reactivity, consistent with other experimental observations in EFE.7,8,11,33

Dioxygen Activation from the EFE·Fe(III)·OO•–·l-Arg Complex in the EFE Variants

To explore the mechanistic pathways in the selected EFE variants, we employed QM/MM calculations. The initial structures were derived from MD simulations of the variants and were subsequently optimized (RC-A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A). Previous computational studies have indicated that different snapshots from MD simulations can show variations in the activation barrier. In this study, the selected snapshots were taken from well-equilibrated regions of the MD simulations, where they closely resemble the average structure. Nonetheless, attention should be taken because different snapshots could lead to energy barriers varying by several kcal/mol. The optimized geometries of the reactants in these variants exhibit similar bond lengths for Fe-proximal oxygen (Op) (ranging from 2.03 to 2.09 Å) and Op-distal oxygen (Od) (1.27 Å) (Figure S12). The calculated spin densities of Fe (ranging from 4.18 to 4.22) and dioxygen (ranging from −0.65 to −0.77) correspond to those of the Fe(III)·OO•– complex, as observed in WT EFE19 and many other 2OG-dependent enzymes (Tables S4–S7).15,16,56,105

The reaction coordinates are the same as in our study of the WT EFE.19 The l-Arg hydroxylation and ethylene formation reactions begin with the attack of the Od on C2 of 2OG to form the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide and Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate intermediates (IM1-A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A). The energy barrier for the decarboxylation reaction is 8.4/8.6/6.6/8.1 kcal/mol for A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A, respectively, which is comparable to the WT enzyme, where the barrier was 11.4 kcal/mol (Figure 5).19 The resulting TS1 exhibits the elongation of the Op–Od and C1–C2 bonds and the shortening of the Fe–Op and Od–C2 bonds. The elongation of the C1–C2 bond reflects the partial release of the CO2. Our studies of WT EFE show that there is almost no difference between the TS leading to the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide intermediate (that leads ultimately to the formation of the ferryl species and l-Arg hydroxylation) and the one leading to the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate intermediate (leading to ethylene formation).19 Although the TS1 looks very similar for the four EFE variants (Figure 6), they show some differences when compared to this state of the WT enzyme, specifically in the SCS and LR residues that stabilize TSs and RIs, IntEF, as well as the electronic structure, which are subsequently discussed. Such features might be important for understanding how the l-Arg hydroxylation favored over ethylene formation, as shown for the D191E variant.11

The relative energies are shown in kcal/mol. The ChemDraw images for the corresponding reactants and intermediates are provided below the potential energy surface.

Orientation of the TS1 complex during dioxygen activation in WT (yellow), A198V (red), D191E (green), E215A (magenta), and R171A (blue) versions of EFE.

The intermediate IM1 formed during l-Arg hydroxylation in all the variants is the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide species, which defines the hydroxylation pathway (Figure 5). The oxidative decarboxylation is found to be exothermic in all variants, with reaction energies of −25.9/–33.3/–17.9/–48.5 kcal/mol for A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A, respectively, compared to −24.4 kcal/mol for the WT EFE (Figure 5).19 The exothermicity of the reaction likely results from the release of the CO2 molecule, as observed in studies of other Fe(II)/2OG enzymes.15,16,19,105,106 The decarboxylation is highly exothermic in variants D191E and R171A, which could be due to the difference in the interaction of the C1 carboxylate oxygen of the 2OG with the guanidinium group of the l-Arg, which is discussed in the Section 2.2. Although the activation energies of the variants are relatively close, the stabilization of the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide intermediate varies broadly within nearly 20 kcal/mol, indicating the differential influence of the variations on the kinetic and thermodynamic properties of the highest energy step of the dioxygen activation (formation of IM1). Thus, the dioxygen activation studies suggest that all variants follow the hydroxylation pathway, which agrees with the experimental studies.8,11 The geometric analysis of the RCs of the EFE variants and the TS structures indicate that the orientation of the substrate and its interaction with the C1 carboxylate of 2OG might be the reason for not following the ethylene pathway.

Electronic Structure of the Fe(III)-OO•– Intermediate in the EFE Variants

We explored the influence of the variations in EFE on the electronic structure of the EFE Fe(III) center that initiates both the hydroxylation and ethylene pathways. For all cases, the Fe-coordinated dioxygen molecule binds in an off-line fashion; therefore, the axes are labeled as in the WT EFE studies: The y-axis is directed along the Fe–O2 bond, whereas the z-axis and x-axis are along the Fe–O1 and Fe–O2 bonds (O1 is part of the C1 carboxylate group of 2OG, O2 is the keto oxygen of the 2OG; Figure S13).19 Our studies show that in the EFE variants, the dioxygen radical is antiferromagnetically coupled with the Fe center in the HS Fe(III)·OO•– complex (S = 5/2) in agreement with the WT EFE19 and other Fe(II)/2OG enzymes.14−16,21,56,105,107,108 The electronic configuration of the reactant complex in the WT EFE study is identified as ↑dxy↑dxz↑dyz↑dx2–y2↑dz2↓π*⊥↑↓π*||. The decarboxylation reaction shifts the electron pair in the σC1–C2 bond to the Fe orbitals, resulting in the electronic configuration ↑dxy↑↓dxz↑dyz↑dx2–y2↑dz2↑↓π*⊥↑↓π*||, here the α electron is transferred to the π*⊥ and the β electron is transferred to the dxz orbital. The subsequent formation of the partial bond intermediate and breakage of the partial bond to yield an off-line ferryl complex involved the transfer of two electrons from the Fe orbitals to the σ*Op–Od. The resulting off-line ferryl complex has an electronic configuration of ↑dxy↑dxz↑dyz0dx2–y2↑dz2. Previous studies on the WT EFE have illustrated that the unpaired β electron present in the superoxo anion radical can also be present in the π*|| as an implication of the exchange repulsion with F283, resulting in the electronic configuration ↑dxy↑dxz↑dyz↑dx2–y2↑dz2↑↓π*⊥↓π*||.19

Orbital analysis of the variants structure (RC-A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A) showed that the unpaired β electron of the Fe(III)·OO•– complex is present in the π*⊥ orbital (Figure 7). This result might be because F283 is positioned further away compared to that in the WT EFE, eliminating the repulsive interaction between the anion radical and F283 observed in the WT enzyme. All variants show the same electronic configurations for the reaction complex as in the WT EFE (↑dxy↑dxz↑dyz↑dx2–y2↑dz2↓π*⊥↑↓π*||). In the A198V variant, however, the dioxygen radical antiferromagnetically couples with dx2–y2, whereas in the other variants and in the WT enzyme, they are coupled with the degenerate dxz/yz orbitals. This could be because the substitution of A198 with valine might disturb the unique hydrophobic environment that surrounds the active site of EFE, resulting in an unusual antiferromagnetic coupling in this variant. This difference indicates the ability of variants to induce changes in the orbital interactions and finely tune the electronic structure of the Fe center in EFE. Although the starting electronic configuration differs in the A198V variant from the other chosen variants, the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide intermediate that results from the decarboxylation reaction shows the same electronic configuration as in the other variants, and in the WT enzyme, i.e., ↑dxy↑↓dxz↑dyz↑dx2–y2↑dz2↑↓π*⊥↑↓π*||.

How Do Residues from the Second Coordination Sphere and Beyond Stabilize TS for the Formation of Fe(II)-Succinyl-Peroxide Intermediate?

Previous studies indicate the role of SCS and LR residues in stabilizing the TS in the WT EFE.19 Understanding the differences in the roles of SCS and LR residues in stabilizing the TS1 during dioxygen activation in the EFE variants is critical to delineate the complexity of the effects of the substitutions on the integrated enzyme protein environment and, ultimately, on the EFE mechanism. In all variants (but D191E), the Fe(III)-nonbonding carboxylate group of D191 (or E191) interacts with the guanidinium, C4, and C5 carbons of l-Arg, potentially stabilizing the substrate orientation. Like the situation for WT EFE, in the A198V and R171A variants, the nonbonding carboxylate group of D191 interacts with the ηNH2 groups of l-Arg.19 In contrast, in the D191E and E215A variants, the nonbonding carboxylate oxygens of E191 and D191 form weak van der Waals interactions with the C4 and C5 hydrogens of l-Arg, respectively (Figure S14). These variations in substrate interactions with the Fe-coordinating D/E191 residue may influence the TS1 stabilization within the active site, potentially impacting the reaction mechanism because of the observed different orientations of the substrate. Further, similar to the WT EFE, the SCS hydrophobic residues such as V196, F250, A281, and F283 also might contribute to the steric stabilization of the active site in the variants.19

We further explored if there are differences between the EFE variants and the WT protein in the SCS/LR residues that energetically stabilize/destabilize the TS1 with respect to RC (Figures 8, S15, and S16). Residues such as R184, K269, and E285 stabilize the TS1, while D91 destabilizes it in all variants and the WT protein. Similarly to the WT enzyme, R171 stabilizes the TS1 in the A198V and E215A variants; however, this residue does not contribute to the TS1 in the D191E variant. In R171A EFE, the substitution abolished the residue’s stabilizing role, and instead, R277 energetically stabilizes the TS1. Additionally, we compared the similarities/differences in the TS-stabilizing residues of WT EFE that lead to Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate (ethylene pathway) and Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide (hydroxylation pathway) intermediates (Figure S16).19 Although there are a lot of similar contributions (such as E84, D91, R171, R184, and R277), there are some significant changes in the magnitude of stabilization and destabilization of the TS1 by residues R171 and E84. Therefore, these residues might play a role in the bifurcation of the reactions of ethylene formation and l-Arg hydroxylation.

Energetically stabilizing (orange) and destabilizing (violet) residues during the dioxygen activation reaction in the (A) A198V, (B) D191E, (C) E215A, and (D) R171A EFE variants are shown in stick mode.

Exploring the network of correlated/anticorrelated motions of the SCS and LR residues that stabilize the TS provides an understanding of the integrated networking in the EFE that contributes to catalysis and might be important for the design of EFE variants with increased ethylene production by varying LR partners of the SCS residues. Substrate interacting and TS1 stabilizing R171 positively correlates with the residues in the regions β3–7 and loops connecting them (79 to 100) and loop connecting β13 and β14 (278 to 288) in all variants (but not R171A). Energetically stabilizing residues R184 and K269 show a similar correlation in all the variants; for instance, R184 positively correlates with β3, β4, and the connecting loop regions (residues 85 to 94, Region 1). The stabilizing K269 shows a positively correlated motion with the β8–19 and loop connecting regions (residues 184 to 211). However, K269 shows anticorrelated motion with the α8 region (residues 311 to 334) only in the R171A variant. The similarities observed in the EDA residues and their LR correlations in these variants might have hindered the formation of Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate intermediate, preventing ethylene formation in these variants. The existence of variant-specific LR-correlated dynamics suggests the diverse effect of each variant on the integrative network of the protein environment in EFE.

Mechanism for the Formation of Off-Line Ferryl Intermediate from the Fe(II)-Succinyl-Peroxide Intermediate

From the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxide intermediate, partial cleavage of the O–O bond leads to a half-bond intermediate (IM2-A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A). In IM2, the distance between the Op–Od bond increases and varies from 2.12 to2.27 Å in the variants, consistent with the WT EFE.19 The formation of IM2 from IM1 requires a relatively low energy barrier in agreement with the WT EFE (1.1 kcal/mol),19 which can vary between the variants (TS2-A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A is −2.0/1.2/–2.3/0.60 kcal/mol, Figure 5). For the A198V and E215A variants, the formation of IM2 becomes barrierless only after including the ZPE correction, which is consistent with observations from similar computational studies.15,105 The partial bond between the Op-Od in IM2 completely breaks, forming an off-line ferryl intermediate (IM3-A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A) with a Fe–Op distance of 1.6 Å. The Fe ion’s spin density further indicates the ferryl intermediate’s formation (Tables S4–S7). The formation of IM3 is highly exothermic (in agreement with the WT EFE where IM3 is stabilized by −50.9 kcal/mol),19 and the overall reaction energy is −46.0/–60.4/–39.9/–60.1 kcal/mol, respectively, for the A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A variants (Figure 5). A comparison of the reaction energies shows a broad variation of around 30 kcal/mol, suggesting the potential of the single variation in the FCS, SCS, and LR to significantly modulate the stabilization of the active oxidant (the ferryl). The optimized geometries of all the stationary structures during the dioxygen activation of the variants are provided in Figures S17–S20. Overall, studies show that no variant indicated the insertion of dioxygen between the C1–C2 bond of 2OG, implying that these variants are not prone to ethylene-forming reactions, as observed in experimental studies.8,11

Influence of the Intrinsic Electric Field in the Dioxygen Activation Reaction

Recent studies have shown that changes in the IntEF can influence enzyme selectivity and catalysis.49,55,98,109−111 Earlier, we proposed that applying an external electric field to EFE can switch the competing hydroxylation and ethylene-forming reactions.34 In this study, the IntEF along the Fe–O bond is calculated in the variants as a function of FCS, SCS, and LR variations compared to the WT (Table S8).

The calculated IntEF in RC of WT EFE is −0.0333 atomic units (a.u.).34 The variation of the FCS for the D191A variant decreased the IntEF value by −0.0014 au with respect to the WT enzyme. Other variants in the SCS and LR regions show less negative IntEF in comparison to the WT protein: A198V (−0.0307 au), E215A (−0.0320 au), and R171A (−0.0306 au). Therefore, the less negative IntEF values observed in the A198V, E215A, and R171A EFE variants might have influenced the lowering of their activation barrier during the decarboxylation reaction in comparison to the WT enzyme.

In IM3 (off-line Fe(IV)=O intermediate), the IntEF of the A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A variants is −0.0337, −0.0361, −0.0330, −0.0311 au respectively (Table S8). The IntEF along the Fe–O bond decreases over the conversion from Fe(III)·OO•– complex to ferryl complex, which suggests that the IntEF changes along the reaction path for the formation of the ferryl intermediate and the more negative IntEF might be needed for the substrate oxidation reaction.

Can the Off-Line to In-Line Ferryl Flip Occur during the HAT Reaction?

Earlier computational studies have proposed that the ferryl intermediate can be energetically and structurally feasible as distinct off-line and in-line isomers that can be catalytically productive in different Fe(II)/2OG enzymes.15,105,112−115 Several possible mechanisms for conversion between the two forms have been proposed using computational methods. For example, in PHF8, AlkB, and ALKBH2, the transformation was predicted to happen at the 5C state of the Fe(II) center before the dioxygen binding,15,105 while in WelO5, the transformation from in-line to off-line was suggested to occur during dioxygen activation.107,112 Computational studies of PHF8, AlkB, and ALKBH2 have proposed that the off-line ferryl oxygen (Fe(IV)=O) might undergo a water-mediated ferryl flip to form an in-line ferryl complex.15,105 Recent computational studies on the HgIS enzyme predict that the hydrogen bonding network of SCS residues facilitates the ferryl flip.99 Although computational studies predicted that the off-line/in-line forms can be structurally and energetically feasible, experimental evidence regarding their existence is limited. In the WT and variant forms of EFE, the orientation of the l-Arg substrate requires an in-line ferryl for the hydroxylation reaction.8,11,19,22 However, the crystallographic structures of the WT and variants of EFE show the off-line orientation8,11,22,33 and the QM/MM calculations on the dioxygen activation also suggest the formation of off-line ferryl.19,21 The 2OG flip at the 5C state in WT EFE was shown to be unfavorable.19,32 Earlier, it was proposed that the formed off-line ferryl would undergo a ferryl flip;8,11,19,21 however, the plausible mechanism for the ferryl flip in EFE remains unclear. Therefore, in this study, we explored the possibility of hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)-assisted oxygen flip in the WT enzyme and EFE variants, where the oxygen flip happens as a consequence of the HAT reaction to form an in-line ferryl. The QM/MM potential energy scans (PES) for the HAT reaction were performed from IM3 obtained during dioxygen activation (Figure 9A). The calculations show that in both WT EFE and its variants, the ferryl oxygen shifts from an off-line to an in-line orientation. This oxygen flip is initiated by the HAT reaction and is completed before the actual hydrogen transfer begins (IM4). However, the oxygen flips are characterized by different activation and reaction energies (Figure 9A). Specifically, in the A198V variant, the energy barrier is only 1.21 kcal/mol. In the WT enzyme, the energy barrier is 9.54 kcal/mol, which is similar to the required barrier in the E215A variant (9.02 kcal/mol), while in the R171A variant, the oxygen flip requires a higher barrier (15.9 kcal/mol); however, the highest barrier is calculated for FCS variant D191E (22.2 kcal/mol). The reaction is exothermic in all other variants except for the D191E protein. The reaction energies vary broadly between −4.61 and 12.3 and 0.60 kcal/mol in the WT enzyme. The observed differences in activation barriers and reaction energies might be related to the different substrate positioning discussed in the Section 2.2. Importantly, the high energy barrier of the oxygen flip in the D191E protein (22.2 kcal/mol) suggests that this FCS variant might utilize an alternate strategy for forming an in-line ferryl complex.

Alternate Strategy for the Formation of In-Line Fe(III)·OO•– Complex

Earlier studies of other Fe(II)/2OG enzymes, such as KDM7B, KDM2A, AlkB, and ALKBH2, suggest that the transformation of 2OG from off-line to in-line orientation can occur with a low barrier at the 5C state of the Fe(II) center before the dioxygen binding.15,56,105 Earlier computational studies of WT EFE have shown that 2OG binds to form an off-line coordination mode, which is in agreement with crystallographic studies.8,32 We, therefore, performed QM/MM PES studies of the EFE variants to assess the transformation of the 2OG from off-line to in-line coordination mode at the 5C state of the ES complexes (before dioxygen binding) (RC’). The PES scan was performed for the variants as in the WT EFE studies19 utilizing the dihedral NH189–Fe–O2–O1 (here, N is the Fe-bound nitrogen of the H189, O2 is the oxo oxygen, and O1 is Fe-bound carboxylate oxygen, Figure S13). The study showed that the 2OG rotation is only possible for the D191E variant apart from the WT enzyme, as illustrated in Figure 9B. In the other variants, the 2OG rotation did not lead to the formation of the in-line product due to the Fe–O2 bond breaking, possibly because of the increasing angle strain. The energy barrier for the formation of the in-line 5C state in the D191E variant (14.2 kcal/mol) is lower than that observed in the WT EFE (21.8 kcal/mol). Importantly, the activation energy for the 2OG rotation at the 5C state is lower than the barrier of the HAT-assisted oxygen flip (22.2 kcal/mol), suggesting that uniquely for the D191E variants, this transformation could happen at the 5C state in contrast to the other EFE variants and the WT enzyme. Therefore, the D191E variant might undergo a 2OG rotation at the 5C state to form an in-line Fe(III)·OO•– complex.

The investigation of the SCS residues suggests that they might influence the conformational change of the 2OG. Previously, WT EFE studies have proposed that the high barrier for the 2OG rotation at the 5C state is due to the interaction of 2OG with R171.19 A similar interaction is present in all the variants other than in the R171A protein but with a different strength, which might have affected the 2OG rotation. For instance, in E215A, this interaction is stronger, which hinders the rotation of 2OG (Figure S21). Further, the substitution of A198 with valine sterically hinders the in-line coordination mode of the 2OG. In the R171A variant, the guanidium group of the l-Arg sterically hinders the 2OG rotation (Figure S21). In contrast, in the D191E variant, the nonbonding C1 carboxylate oxygen makes a water-mediated hydrogen bond, which might have aided the transformation of the 2OG from off-line to in-line coordination mode (Figure S21).

The experimentally observed second-order rate constant for the dioxygen addition in the D191E variant is higher than that of the WT enzyme,11 which indicates that in the D191E variant, the 2OG flip might not be feasible as it requires 14.2 kcal/mol and the dioxygen addition in the WT EFE proceeds in an off-line manner (no energy barrier for the 2OG rotation is needed). Further, both the forward (off-line to in-line) and the reverse (in-line to off-line) scan for the 2OG flip confirm the endothermicity of the in-line 2OG conformation, indicating that off-line 2OG is more stable than the in-line 2OG conformation in the D191E variant, which is in agreement with the experimental findings.11 Our previous studies suggest that the dioxygen molecules present in the vicinity of the Fe(II)-center, as well as the binding process of the dioxygen to the Fe(II)-center, can influence the 2OG flip.32,56 To explore the effect of dioxygen molecules around the Fe(II) center and the dioxygen binding process in D191E, we first needed to simulate the process of dioxygen diffusion from the protein surface to the Fe(II) center. We, therefore, performed a 1 μs MD simulation with 100 dioxygen molecules. The simulation predicts that several dioxygen molecules come from the exterior of the protein and remain in the close vicinity of the Fe(II) for 100 to 400 ns, localizing the dioxygen in the off-line position (trans to H189) (Figures S22–S24). The simulation also predicts the very rare in-line positioning of dioxygen. Only two in-line approaching dioxygen molecules (trans to H268, Figure S24) were observed, and they exhibited a residence time of approximately 25 ns (Figures S22 and S23), which is significantly shorter than the residence time of off-line approaching dioxygen molecules.

After simulating the dioxygen diffusion to the vicinity of the Fe(II)-center, we explored the effect of 2OG flip from off-line to in-line in the presence of dioxygen. For this purpose, a snapshot obtained from the MD simulation having off-line dioxygen at the vicinity of 5C Fe(II)-center was optimized. PES of the off-line to in-line 2OG rotation revealed that the activation energy for this process in the presence of dioxygen is 24.0 kcal/mol, which is much higher than that observed in the absence of dioxygen (14.2 kcal/mol) (Figure 9). Further, the study explored the reaction path for the dioxygen binding in the quintet and septet surface for the formation of the off-line Fe(III)-OO•– complex of D191E (Figure 10). The activation barrier at the B1 level shows a low barrier of 4.4 kcal/mol at the quintet surface and 2.5 kcal/mol at the septet surface to form an off-line Fe(III)-OO•– complex. Eventually, the inclusion of the single-point energies and zero-point energy correction (B3) predicts that the off-line dioxygen binding is barrierless. The transition from the septet to quintet state occurs immediately after forming the Fe(III)-superoxide in agreement with the WT EFE and PHF8 computations (Figure 10).32 Previous computational studies on KDM2A and PHF8 suggest that in-line dioxygen binding facilitates the 2OG flip from off-line to in-line to form an in-line Fe(III)-OO•– complex.15,56 Therefore, we also explored the in-line dioxygen binding (trans to H268) utilizing snapshots with very rarely observed in-line approaching dioxygen in the MD (Figure S22). The calculations of in-line binding of the dioxygen to the Fe(II) center did not lead to a stable product (in-line Fe(III)-OO•– complex), similar to the predicted results for the WT EFE.32

QM/MM potential energy profile for the off-line dioxygen binding in the Fe(II)-center of the D191E EFE in the septet (red) and quintet (black) surfaces. The calculated energies are at the B1 level, and the relative energies are shown in kcal/mol.

The results suggest that while the 2OG rotation in the absence of dioxygen might be energetically feasible in the D191E variant in comparison to the HAT-assisted oxygen flip, the second-order rate constants for O2 addition in both WT and D191E, along with computational predictions on 2OG rotation in the presence of dioxygen and the off/in-line dioxygen binding in D191E, indicate that 2OG flipping is not be viable in the D191E variant. Nonetheless, for comparative purposes, we also calculated the dioxygen activation mechanism starting from the in-line Fe(III)-OO•– complex to predict the potential geometric differences in the off/in-line dioxygen activation mechanisms (SI, pages S30–31 and Figures S25–S32).

The question of when the off-line to in-line change occurs remains still open. To probe whether this transformation could happen during the dioxygen activation, namely during the transformation of the partial bond Fe(III) intermediate (IM2) to ferryl (IM4), we performed PES utilizing C3–C2–Fe–O dihedral (Figure S13) as the reaction coordinate. Here, C3 and C2 are the carbons of succinate, and O is the Fe-bound oxygen. The activation barrier for forming in-line ferryl was 1.89 kcal/mol, making this step a potentially plausible candidate for this transformation (Figure S33). Similar to the D191E variant, a conversion from IM2 to IM4 may also be feasible in the WT EFE and other variants, though further computational studies are needed to explore this proposal. Importantly, although computational studies can predict the feasibility of this transformation, experimental evidence is necessary for a higher degree of certainty about the nature of the off-line to in-line transformation.

Hydrogen Atom Transfer Mechanism in the EFE Variants

HAT shows the highest energy barrier of all the chemical reaction steps (not from the entire catalytic cycle) in many116 of the Fe(II)/2OG hydroxylation reactions,14,15,55,56,71,96,98,102,106,117,118 including the WT EFE.19 To be cautious, HAT can be considered the highest activation energy barrier (rate-limiting) step for the chemical transformation (the dioxygen activation and substrate oxidation). Experimental studies, however, demonstrated that for the overall catalytic cycle in some of the Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases, the product release is the rate-determining step.119,120 Although experimental studies are missing, computational studies on the HAT reaction in the variants help to explore the potential capacity of the SCS and LR-substitutions to influence different aspects of the HAT, such as electronic structure, molecular orbitals, IntEF, and effects of SCS and LR residues in stabilizing the TS, thus providing guidance for further experiments. To explore the hydroxylation mechanism, we performed QM/MM minimum energy reaction path calculations from IM4′ of each variant (Figure 11). The calculated activation energies are 15.8/13.5/24.3/36.3 kcal/mol for the A198V/D191E/E215A/R171A variants, respectively (Figure 11). The activation energy barrier for the HAT reaction in the WT enzyme is 15.7 kcal/mol. Although the activation energies for the dioxygen activation were relatively closer among the WT enzyme and the EFE variants (varying between 6.60 and 11.4 kcal/mol, Figure 5), there are much larger variations between the activation barriers observed for HAT (13.5 to 36.3 kcal/mol, Figure 11), indicating the stronger impact of the variations on the hydroxylation process than on the dioxygen activation. The energy barrier also shows that similar to the WT EFE,19 the HAT reaction is the rate-determining step in the hydroxylation reaction catalyzed by EFE variants, as in many other Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenases.16,55,71,96,102,106 The experimental studies demonstrated that variants R171A and E215A do not undergo ethylene-forming reaction while they show minimal activity for hydroxylation reaction (less than 5%),8 whereas the A198V variant undergoes hydroxylation at 60% of the extent with respect to the WT enzyme8 and the D191E variant is 3 times more active for hydroxylation than the WT protein but still does not perform the ethylene-forming reaction.11 The high barriers for HAT in the E215A (24.3 kcal/mol) and R171A (36.3 kcal/mol) variants compared to WT EFE (15.7 kcal/mol) suggests the impact of these substitutions on the HAT reaction (Figure 11). The activation barriers of A198V (15.8 kcal/mol) and D191E (13.5 kcal/mol) variants relative to the WT enzyme (15.7 kcal/mol) suggest similar energetic influences on the hydroxylation activity (Figure 11). It is important to note that the computational studies represent a modeled picture of the potential most feasible way of influence that these variants exercise on the HAT, and future experimental studies are needed to validate this prediction. Previous studies have determined the rate constants for the HAT reaction in WT EFE (16 s–1) and the D191E variant (42 s–1).11 By applying the Eyring equation, calculated rate constants correspond to energy barriers of 16.08 kcal/mol for WT EFE and 15.5 kcal/mol for the D191E variant. Despite the D191E variant exhibiting a rate constant 2.6 times higher than WT EFE, the energy barrier difference remains under 1 kcal/mol, which falls within the usual error for the QM/MM methods. Nonetheless, the calculated energy barriers align with the experimentally observed trends in the rate constants for WT EFE and the D191E variant. Further, reverse PES calculations of the HAT from IM5 to IM4′ also show a very similar activation barrier to that of the forward PES reaction path of the HAT. Importantly, the unique correlated motion between the α7 region and Regions 1 and 3 (discussed in the Section 2.2) in the D191E variant may contribute to the observed 3-fold increase in hydroxylation, which is absent in the A198V variant.

The formed Fe(III)–OH intermediate (IM5) is slightly exothermic in the WT enzyme, with a reaction energy of −0.3 kcal/mol.19 The reaction energy for the HAT reaction varies as follows for the variant proteins: 0.3 kcal/mol for A198V, −0.05 kcal/mol for D191E, −5.2 kcal/mol for E215A, and 7.5 kcal/mol for R171A (Figure 11). In comparison to the WT enzyme, these values range from nearly isothermic in WT EFE to exothermic and endothermic, indicating the sensitivity of the reaction to these variations.

How Do the Variations Influence the MO Mechanism of the HAT Reaction?

The HAT reaction involves the transfer of the C5 hydrogen of the l-Arg to the ferryl oxygen, which leads to the elongation of the Fe–Op bond to form the Fe(III)–OH intermediate. Previous computational studies have predicted two possible electron pathways for the HAT reaction, i.e., the σ-channel and the π-channel pathways (Figure S34).15,16,28,55,56,69,105,106,121−126 The σ-channel involves the transfer of an α electron from the substrate (σCH) to the antibonding σ*z2 orbital along the Fe–O bond. In contrast, the π-channel entails the transfer of the β electron from the σCH of the substrate to the π* orbitals (π*xz and π*yz). The spin densities of the Fe in the TS4 for the A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A variants are 4.01, 3.99, 3.96, and 3.96, respectively (Tables S4–S7), indicating the σ-channel pathway, which is similar to that observed in the WT EFE (where the Fe spin state is 3.92) studies.19 Additionally, the Fe–Op–H angle at the TS is one of the other key factors that define the type of electron transfer pathway during the HAT reaction. If the Fe–Op–H angle is close to 180°, it leads to the σ-channel, whereas if the Fe–Op–H angle is close to 120° it leads to the π transfer pathway (Figure S34).125,127 The observed angle at the TS4 is 158.94, 133.66, 150.91, and 149.47° for the A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A variants, respectively, and in the WT EFE, it is 156.41°.19 Although the angle at the TS is slightly less than 180°, they all undergo the σ-transfer, which is consistent with many other computational studies.46,55 The electron transfer in the HAT reaction is further verified by performing NO-SNO analysis, as provided in Figure 12, which confirms that in all cases, only σ-transfer is followed. Consistent with other experimental and computational studies,16,46,69,100,125 frontier molecular orbitals (FMO) also revealed the preference for σ*z2 over π*xz/yz orbitals (Figure S35). Although the EFE variations influence the rate of the HAT step, it looks like this effect is done without dramatic perturbation of the orientation of the reactive parts, which maintain the same σ-molecular orbital mechanism from the HAT.

Influence of Residues from the SCS and Beyond in the HAT Reaction of the EFE Variants

Our previous studies have elucidated that the SCS and LR residues T190, H309, and R321 primarily stabilize the TS of the HAT (TS4) in the WT EFE.19 The steric stabilization of the TS4 by the hydrophobic residues, as well as SCS residues interacting with the C4 oxygens of the succinate, is similar to that of the dioxygen activation (Figure S5). However, delineating which SCS and LR residues that energetically stabilize/destabilize the TS might not be evident from observations of the structure only. Instead, EDA analysis101,102 reveals the MM residues that energetically stabilize/destabilize the HAT TS (TS4) during the reaction (Figures 13, S36, and S37). In the two variants that undergo the hydroxylation reaction (A198V and D191E EFEs), SCS residue E84 strongly destabilizes the TS4; the destabilization of the TS by E84 might have a role in the HAT reaction mechanism of these variants. Apart from E84, E83 weakly destabilizes the HAT TS in the A198V and D191E variants. Similarly to that observed in the EDA of dioxygen activation, R171 stabilizes the HAT in the A198V (−1.48 kcal/mol), D191E (−2.81 kcal/mol), and E215A (−6.83 kcal/mol) variants, and the stabilization of the variant R171A by R277 is also present in the HAT TS (Figure S37). We further compared EDA residues for stabilizing/destabilizing the HAT TS in the WT EFE (Figure S37).19 Residue E84, which was identified to have a destabilizing effect on the variants, does not have much stabilizing/destabilizing role in the WT enzyme. Further, R171 contrastingly destabilizes the HAT TS in the WT enzyme in comparison to the EFE variants. Previous experimental studies illustrated that the substitution of E84 with alanine prevents ethylene formation and reduces the hydroxylation activity (less than 10%);8 therefore, the EDA residue E84 might be important in controlling the hydroxylation reaction. The studies elucidate the differential influence of the EFE variations on the key residues that energetically stabilize the TS of the rate-limiting step of EFE. This result supports that although these variations can be positioned in very different locations of the enzyme—FCS, SCS, and RA—they exercise a sensitive, definite, and diverse effect on the integrative effect of the protein environment, which affects the EFE reaction mechanism.

Influence of Intrinsic Electric Field on the Ferryl HAT

Our earlier studies have shown that IntEF along the Fe–O bonds shows changes in off/in-line conformations of the Fe(III)·OO•– complex.34 Therefore, we analyzed the change in the IntEF between the off/in-line ferryl complexes. The IntEF values are increased along the Fe–O bond during the HAT-assisted ferryl flip (from IM3 to IM4) by 0.0022, 0.0026, 0.0023, and 0.0045 au in the A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A variants, respectively (Table S8). However, IntEF varies considerably between the variants at IM4 (in-line ferryl complex). The observed IntEF in the IM4 is relatively less negative for E215A and R171A. The observed IntEF in E215A (−0.0307 au) and R171A (−0.0266 au) variants might contribute to their high activation barriers for the HAT reaction; in contrast, A198V (−0.0315 au) and D191E (−0.0335 au) variants have relatively lower barriers for HAT with higher negative values for IntEF.

Mechanism of Rebound Hydroxylation in the EFE Variants

The mechanism of rebound hydroxylation reaction in the chosen EFE variants is explored from IM5 and compared with the one in the WT enzyme (Figure 11).19 The activation energy barriers for the rebound reaction are 1.82, 1.51, 11.9, and 14.78 kcal/mol for the A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A variants, respectively (Figure 11). The activation energy for the rebound reaction in the WT enzyme is 6.8 kcal/mol. A comparison of the activation energies suggests that the rebound reaction might occur rapidly in the A198V and D191E variants, in contrast to the E215A and R171A variants. Similarly to the HAT, the activation energy of the rebound step varies, largely indicating the sensitive effect of the variants on the kinetics of this reaction step. Additionally, further test calculations for dioxygen activation, HAT, and rebound hydroxylation were carried out using an extended QM region that includes the SCS residue R277. Single-point calculations incorporating Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction were also performed on the A198V variant to validate the activation barriers and spin densities. The results show a consistent trend in activation barriers, relative energies, and spin densities compared to the standard system (Tables S1–S3).

Formation of the hydroxylated product (PD) led to the reduction of Fe(III) to Fe(II), as observed in WT EFE19 and other 2OG-dependent enzymes.16,19,56,106 The observed spin densities at the Fe center are 3.78, 3.79, 3.78, and 3.81 for the A198V, D191E, E215A, and R171A variants, respectively, indicating a Fe(II) HS oxidation state (Table S4–S7). The overall reaction energies indicate that the formed hydroxylated product is stable; however, the variants significantly differ in their reaction energies, as shown in Figure 11, suggesting that the variations of EFE residues can largely influence the stabilization of the final hydroxylated product.

Do SCS and LR Residues Stabilizing the Rebound Reaction Differ from that Observed in Dioxygen Activation and HAT?

The hydroxyl group in the hydroxylated product (l-Arg-OH, PD) forms a hydrogen bond with either nonbonding carboxylate oxygens of D/E191 or with succinate, which might contribute to the low barrier during the rebound reaction. The absence of this interaction might have led to the high rebound barrier in the R171A variant (Figure S38). Further, the other SCS residues sterically stabilizing the TS5 are similar to those found during the dioxygen activation (TS1) and HAT (TS4) reaction.

Comparing the energy-stabilizing residues of TS5 with TS1 and TS4 (based on the EDA) revealed that R171 stabilizes TS4 in all variants except R171A. This stabilizing effect is also present in TS1 for the A198V and E215A variants but is absent during the rebound hydroxylation reaction (Figures S16 and S37). Notably, the stabilizing role of R171 is only observed in TS5 for the A198V variant. In the other two variants (D191E and E215A), R171 was shown to destabilize the TS5 (Figures S39 and S40). Furthermore, the stabilizing/destabilizing role of the SCS residue E84 in HAT is similarly observed during the rebound reaction in all variants but not the D191E variant (Figures S36 and S40). These observed distinct and unique roles of the SCS residues further reveal the complex influence of the residue variations in EFE on the integrative enzyme environment and, ultimately, on catalysis. Conclusions

Redesigning metalloenzymes with reliable efficiency and selectivity remains challenging, and for the improvement of the redesign strategies, it is vitally important to ensure an accurate and comprehensive description of the integrative and multidimensional effects of the enzyme environment. Therefore, understanding the effects of the FCS/SCS/LR interactions, correlated motions, electronic structure, IntEF, and the stabilization of TSs and RIs is crucial.

Our MD-based QM/MM studies suggest the effects of the integrative protein environment on the reactions catalyzed by EFE variants from the FCS (D191E), SCS (A198V and R171A), and LR (E215A). Different substrate orientations in the EFE variants and their interactions with the SCS were obtained from the MD simulations. The study establishes that the absence of the unique correlated motions in the variants in comparison to the ones observed in the WT EFE and the distinct correlated motion involving the α7 region in the D191E variant might contribute to the observed altered reactivities in the EFE variants in comparison to the WT EFE.

QM/MM reaction path studies of the dioxygen activation mechanism show that no EFE variants form the Fe(II)-succinyl-peroxycarbonate intermediate that leads to ethylene formation, supporting their preference for the hydroxylation reaction. In WT EFE and EFE variants (excluding the D191E protein), the studies revealed a HAT-assisted oxygen flip to switch from the off-line to in-line ferryl before the hydroxylation reaction. Uniquely, the D191E variant undergoes a 2OG switch from off-line to in-line coordination mode before the dioxygen binding at a five-coordinated (5C) state in contrast to all other studied EFE variants that perform HAT-assisted oxygen flip. However, dioxygen diffusion dynamics, 2OG rotation in the presence of oxygen, and off/in-line dioxygen binding studies in the D191E suggest that the 2OG flip at the 5C Fe(II) center is not feasible in the D191E, which is in agreement with the experimental findings.11 Results of the study indicate the possibility of forming an in-line ferryl from the Fe(III)-partial bond intermediate in the D191E variant, thus demonstrating the diverse effects of the variants on the catalytic strategy of EFE. A similar mechanistic pathway for the formation of in-line ferryl from Fe(III)-partial bond intermediate might be feasible in the WT EFE and other variants, which needs to be further explored. The results elucidate the atomistic, energetic, and electronic structural differences between the hydroxylation reactions of the different EFE variants.

The results imply that perturbations of the hydrophobic environment that surrounds the active site of EFE (the A198V variant) could influence the electronic structure of the Fe(III)·OO•– complex. Importantly, the less negative IntEF along the Fe–O bond in the in-line ferryl of the EFE variants contributes to the increase in the activation barrier of the HAT reaction.

Overall, the study shows that the studied EFE variants exercise distinct, diverse, and sensitive effects on the integrative protein environment of EFE. By understanding the complex and interdependent atomistic changes resulting from SCS residue substitutions that abolish ethylene production, we can deduce alternative/reverse atomistic changes in the integrative enzyme environment that, in contrast, will lead to an increase in ethylene production. Then, we can design new SCS variants that lead to such integrative atomistic changes and ultimately increase ethylene production. The results of this study, along with other experimental evidence, can be used to selectively design new variants that have the opposite/reverse effects on the integrative protein environment than the variants studied in the present study, thus improving the ethylene production by EFE.
