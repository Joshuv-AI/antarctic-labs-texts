The Long road to AI–physician partnership starts with learners: a map for AI in medical education
Authors: Nikhil Patel, Andre Kumar, Jeffrey Chi, Kevin Keet, John Kugler, Jason Hom, Poonam Hosamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical students and residents are already using generative artificial intelligence (AI) to draft notes, summarise records and generate differential diagnoses, often informally and without institutional oversight. Adoption has outpaced policy and pedagogical processes, which makes restriction an unrealistic response. The practical question is how to integrate these tools so that clinical skills are not replaced as they are being built. We aim to give medical educators a practical guide for judging which uses of generative AI support skill formation at each stage of training and which displace it. Three distinct harms are at stake: deskilling, the erosion of acquired competencies; never-skilling, the failure to develop foundational skills when AI performs them before learners do; and mis-skilling, the absorption of AI-generated errors and biases as accurate practice. To calibrate integration, we propose a framework built on cognitive displacement, the degree to which a tool takes over the reasoning that training is designed to build. Appropriate use depends on a task’s position on that spectrum and the learner’s stage. Applied across the training continuum, the framework locates each risk where it has the greatest potential for harm. Never-skilling threatens preclinical students who have not yet built the competencies that AI could erode. Deskilling threatens residents, whose judgement is still consolidating. Mis-skilling arises wherever biased tools meet trainees making unsupervised decisions. Because the framework’s stage-specific predictions remain largely untested, we set out an agenda for measuring these risks prospectively. The principle is calibration, not prohibition. The open question is who designs that integration, and when.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) can now generate differential diagnoses, summarise patient records, draft clinical notes and answer specialty-specific questions in real time.12 By 2024, roughly one in three hospitals had integrated generative AI with the electronic health record (EHR), and clinicians are not limited to what their institutions deploy.3 Two thirds of hospitalists surveyed at one academic centre reported using AI without institutional endorsement, most often for clinical questions and differential diagnoses.4 Learners are not waiting for curriculum committees, accrediting bodies or specialty societies to grant permission. They already use these tools across all phases of training, often informally and without institutional oversight.56 The question facing medical educators is no longer whether AI belongs in clinical training but how to integrate it in ways that protect the formation of clinical skills it could otherwise replace.

Such concerns are not new. When René Laennec introduced the stethoscope, critics warned that listening through an instrument would separate the physician from the patient and weaken bedside examination skills.7 What counts as an essential skill shifts as tools change, and as Lea describes, a tool’s effect is not fixed at its release but follows from the choices a field makes in adopting it.8

There are three categories of risk that describe what is at stake with generative AI expansion into healthcare: deskilling, never-skilling and mis-skilling. Deskilling, an established concept from labour economics, describes the erosion of competencies already acquired when automation displaces the practiced cognitive work that maintains them.9 Never-skilling describes the failure to develop foundational competencies when AI performs those functions before trainees engage with the effortful work that builds them.1011 Mis-skilling describes the absorption of AI-generated errors or biases as accurate practice, internalised as if learnt from a more experienced colleague.1012

Given how widely generative AI is already used across training, a strict ban is neither enforceable nor sensible and would only deepen the divide between learners and educators. What learners need is deliberate exposure, calibrated both to how much reasoning a given use takes over and to where the learner sits in training. We propose a framework based on cognitive displacement, the spectrum from AI that automates data access without displacing interpretation to AI that produces assessments and plans the learner has not constructed. This article sets out to specify when a given generative AI use is developmentally appropriate, and to show how the framework’s stage-specific predictions can be tested. We define the displacement spectrum and place it against each stage of training through the transition to independent practice.

A framework for calibrated AI integration

Not all AI integrations carry equal risk to skill formation, and we situate them along a spectrum of cognitive displacement defined by where a tool enters the reasoning chain and what it supplies. At low displacement, the tool automates access to information without displacing its interpretation. Encounter transcription, prior lab organisation and guideline retrieval supply the inputs to reasoning, but the learner remains responsible for interpreting the information and deriving a conclusion. At moderate displacement, the tool drafts work the learner would otherwise compose, such as patient histories, discharge summaries and interprofessional communications, leaving the learner to verify and revise. At high displacement, the tool supplies the differential diagnoses, assessments and plans that clinical reasoning training is designed to build. This distinction is determined not by how much work the tool does, but whether its output can be verified. Retrieved data can be verified against its source, while a generated conclusion can only be verified by someone who could have reached it independently.

Cognitive displacement is defined relative to the reasoning a learner is still developing, which sets it apart from several previously proposed constructs. It is distinct from automation in the sense of Parasuraman and colleagues, whose scale measures how much of a task a machine takes over, from gathering information to carrying out an action.13 That scale describes the machine whereas displacement describes the learner and asks what a tool removes from someone who has not yet built the competency. It is also distinct from cognitive offloading, the delegation of effort to an external aid to reduce working-memory load.14 Offloading is often useful. What matters for skill formation is whether the offloaded work is the reasoning a given stage exists to build, where offloading has been linked to weaker critical thinking.15 Finally, displacement differs from Abdulnour and colleagues’ centaur and cyborg behaviours, in which a clinician either divides a task with AI or intertwines with it throughout.10 These describe how a user works with a tool whereas displacement describes what the tool supplies. A common instruction to learners is to verify AI output before trusting it, which might seem to make any use low-displacement so long as the learner checks the result.10 However, verification presupposes a standard to verify against, and a learner who could not have produced the output can only accept it.16 The same use that displaces reasoning for a novice informs it for someone further along, which is why displacement is shown against training stage. The framework specifies when that independent standard can be assumed.

Other recent frameworks have made a similar argument about how the timing of AI introduction affects training. Abdulnour et al, whose DEFT-AI framework structures the teaching conversation after an observed AI interaction, warn that AI introduced too early may prevent learners from acquiring essential skills, but limit its scope to clinical reasoning alone.10 Ong et al, building from principles in aviation, suggest restricting access to AI until basic competency has developed.17 Ke et al come closest to what we propose, using a three-phase sequence, with each phase gated on documented competency. Their framework confines itself to LLMs in diagnostic reasoning while setting documentation, administration and scientific discovery aside by name, and it stops at residency.16 All three establish that timing matters without giving any single use at any single stage its own designation.

A second group of frameworks addresses AI in training without making a stage-specific timing judgement. AI-PACE, built on Bloom’s taxonomy, maps competencies across the cognitive, psychomotor and affective domains and distributes them along undergraduate, graduate and continuing education, but it treats these as content to be taught at each stage rather than uses to be permitted or deferred.18 Wolthusen et al offer a three-question heuristic on capability, learning-strategy fit and ethics for an educator weighing a single use case, applied per decision rather than per stage.19 Boman et al argue that learning with generative AI cannot be separated from learning about it. They identify productive struggle as the aim, but locate progression on Miller’s pyramid rather than on the training continuum.20 The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) principles set institutional values rather than curricular sequence, among them a commitment to equal learner access that we discuss below.21

A framework that defers AI usage invites two questions: whether the deferral can be enforced, and whether it is fair. Learners carry devices with constant access to these tools and monitoring their use is not feasible.16 What is feasible is assessment that makes independent reasoning visible: oral examinations, observed encounters and written cases that require a learner to show how a conclusion was reached rather than only that one was produced.16 Enforcement therefore rests less on restricting access than on how competency is assessed, alongside transparency about which uses are appropriate at each stage and clear accountability for AI output. Fairness is a separate concern. The AAMC asks that all learners have equal access to AI and warns against disparities driven by institutional resources.21 A calibration keyed to training stage does not create that kind of disparity, because it applies to every learner at a given stage. This will safeguard against a workforce stratified by who learnt to reason without AI and who did not.16

We developed this framework as a narrative synthesis. Relevant literature was identified through non-systematic targeted searching of PubMed for peer-reviewed sources. Because most of the research on this topic is recent, we did not impose a formal date restriction. The two axes of this framework were specified in advance from a governing idea grounded in our collective experience as clinicians and educators. Each author first proposed uses and placements independently, which were reviewed by the group and reconciled. The first author drafted the initial cell designations, which all authors reviewed and approved. All authors are based at a single academic medical centre in the USA, and our perspective reflects that setting.

The role of AI in preclinical education and the risk of ‘never-skilling’

Never-skilling is a primary concern for preclinical students, who may fail to develop foundational skills when generative AI performs that work before they have practiced it. A recent example comes from the surgical literature on robotic-assisted surgery, which enabled attending physicians to operate alone or with reduced assistance, resulting in fewer opportunities for authentic practice for surgical trainees.22 The same erosion of unassisted practice threatens the core competencies preclinical training exists to build.

Building clinical reasoning

Among the domains at risk in preclinical education, clinical reasoning demands the greatest concern. As we have previously argued, deploying generative AI in educational settings opens a Pandora’s box. The same capabilities that make these systems useful for generating illness scripts and summarising evidence also offer an attractive shortcut around the cognitive work students most need to practice.2 The concern is that students who outsource this reasoning risk losing the probabilistic intuition and iterative hypothesis-testing that define expert clinical thought, which require repeated practice with feedback under a high cognitive load. High-displacement tools remove those repetitions, leaving early learners fewer opportunities to build that intuition.23

A second concern remains underappreciated by students: these models are trained on published literature whose study populations, inclusion criteria and biases are opaque to the user. A student who accepts an AI-generated differential without interrogating the evidence base is learning to trust a black box rather than practicing clinical reasoning.2425 Randomised evidence shows that assistance from generative AI does not uniformly improve diagnostic reasoning performance, with quality depending heavily on how the clinician engages with the tool.1 AI engagement at the preclinical stage must therefore be deliberate, explicitly scaffolded and carefully overseen.

Medical knowledge

AI’s contribution to medical knowledge acquisition is the most straightforward. Generative AI can identify knowledge gaps from assessment performance, generate targeted study materials and calibrate explanations to individual learning needs in ways previously only available through intensive tutoring.26 Educators must ensure AI-supported knowledge acquisition is paired with synthesis tasks (case write-ups, oral presentations, concept mapping) that demand the connected understanding assessments alone do not reveal.

The role of AI in clerkship education

The next phase of medical education, the clerkship years (the supervised clinical rotations that follow the preclinical phase), presents a more complex learning environment as students will now reason about messy clinical encounters as compared with the curated cases of preclinical years. We must balance the training of students by giving them the skills to adapt to new AI-powered tools and workflows being applied in real time in clinical environments while keeping the work of clinical reasoning protected.

Clinical data searching and summarising

One common challenge among clerkship students is navigating voluminous EHRs and parsing critical information. Students may also need to learn how to navigate different EHRs at different clinical sites, which increases cognitive load. One study found that EHR-related tasks consumed the majority of clinical time during medicine clinical rotations, often at the cost of time that might otherwise be spent seeing or thinking about patients.27 Generative AI can compress this work as it now matches or exceeds clinician performance on clinical text summarisation and generation.28 The act of prompting a tool to retrieve data requires cognitive work to identify what is clinically relevant. The student can then focus their time and energy on the interpretation and synthesis of this data into their clinical case.

Applying clinical reasoning

Clerkships were designed to put clinical reasoning skills into practice. Students apply the frameworks they built during preclinical years to actual cases: building differentials, weighting probability against severity and forming plans for individual patients in context. AI can scaffold this learning when used at the correct point in the reasoning loop. A model that surfaces a missed diagnosis after the student has committed to their own differential, much like a senior resident suggesting an alternative diagnosis, provides an optimal framework. But the same tool replaces the work entirely when used as a primary differential generator rather than a check. Generative AI now matches or exceeds unassisted clinicians on this task, so a student who asks for a differential before forming their own receives an output that feels like the answer.28–32 Repeated across a clerkship, the pattern becomes a student who cannot reason unaided by AI.

Clinicians have felt this before when EHR outages occurred. Care must continue and clinicians who retained the underlying skills are least impeded by its absence. Aviation confronted a similar problem without removing the automation, encouraging pilots to maintain manual flying skills on routine flights when workload and weather permit. Ong et al argue medicine should follow, proposing minimum unaided practice and benchmarking of clinician performance without AI, alongside simulation that recreates AI failure.17 These proposals are addressed to practicing clinicians but importantly translate to clerkships as this is where habits are forming and the level of supervision is at its peak.

Physicianship and professionalism

Professionalism has most often been taught implicitly, through the modelling of relationships between students and patients, families and other healthcare professionals. As generative AI increasingly mediates those exchanges, there is a risk that students never internalise the ethos those acts are meant to embody. Identity and morality begin forming preclinically and take shape on clerkships, where students put into practice the clinical skills they had learnt in the theoretical format. Each application introduced at these stages warrants asking whether it accelerates short-term learning at the cost of competencies that outlast any current tool, and what it does to identity formation.

The role of AI in residency education and the risk of deskilling

Up to 92% of residents (physicians in postgraduate specialty training) report using AI for clinical decision-making or education.33 Unlike earlier phases of training where curricular decisions shape first exposure, graduate medical education faces a different problem: adoption has outpaced institutional policy. The question is no longer whether AI will enter clinical training but whether programmes will manage it when it does. When programmes do not, residents face both erosion of competencies they have begun but not consolidated and arrested development of skills still forming. These risks are distinctive to residency: misguided AI use is harder to detect and correct as clinical responsibility grows.

Documentation as formative practice

Ambient scribing, the use of AI to capture audio and provide a written note, is the most visible point of AI adoption in residency. One health system documented aggregate savings exceeding 15 700 clinician hours over 1 year, and a separate academic deployment reported reduced documentation time among participating physicians.3435 However, the gains in efficiency may be offset by educational costs. In a qualitative study, family medicine residents describe documentation as cognitively valuable and worry that ambient scribing may erode the reflective learning that note-writing supports.36 Few specialty societies have issued residency-specific guidance for these tools.

This is a pattern that medical training has seen before. The EHR, introduced as an efficiency tool, sharply reduced time spent in direct patient care, including for trainees.3738 Residents now spend less than 18% of their time in direct patient care.39 Ambient scribing’s appeal is that documentation time can be reallocated to the bedside. However, this can come at a cost to residents’ educational development. Note writing is not transcription; it is also the act through which we organise an encounter into a problem representation and commit to a plan.40 When a note is drafted by a model and edited by the resident rather than constructed, that synthesis risks being lost.

Diagnostic reasoning and deskilling

The first real-world clinical evidence of AI-induced deskilling appeared in 2025. In a multicentre observational study, 19 experienced endoscopists with a mean of 28 years of practice each saw their adenoma detection rate fall from 28.4% to 22.4% after AI exposure.41 A similar pattern appears in diagnostic reasoning. In a multicentre randomised trial of attendings and residents in family, internal and emergency medicine, physicians given GPT-4 plus conventional resources showed no improvement in diagnostic accuracy over those using conventional resources alone, while GPT-4 alone outperformed the physician control group by 16 percentage points.1 Because most published evidence comes from mixed or attending-only populations, whether integration failures are worse for residents still consolidating the same reasoning competencies their supervisors have built remains an open question.

Mis-skilling and calibration of trust

Mis-skilling is a problem of trust. Residents form independent judgement while making decisions overnight and on rotations with intermittent attending supervision, and what appears routine at this stage becomes practice. The question is not whether a resident knows that AI makes errors, but whether they can tell when the error has occurred.

The first failure is deference. Clinical training runs on hierarchy and trainees learn to weight a senior resident’s, fellow’s or attending’s assessment above their own when considering whether to voice disagreement. An AI system that answers fluently and without hedging occupies a similar position, yielding the same habit.4243 Automation bias describes what follows. Clinicians favour automated recommendations, even when contradictory evidence is available to them.44 In a multicentre randomised trial, exposure to systematically biased AI reduced clinician diagnostic accuracy, an effect that was not mitigated by image-based explanations of the model’s reasoning.45

The second failure is bias, systematic error in an AI system’s outputs, whether arising from its training data, its design or the human judgements embedded in either, that a learner may absorb as correct when it is presented fluently.46 A widely used commercial risk-stratification algorithm assigned lower risk scores to Black patients at equivalent illness severity because it was trained to predict healthcare costs rather than health needs, encoding structural inequality into its target variable.47 The model performed well against its own design criteria, and the disparity was visible only through subpopulation analysis.

Trust can be miscalibrated in either direction. The dominant concern under a displacement framework is over-trust, but the opposite failure, discounting a correct recommendation because it came from an algorithm, is also real. This has been described in the decision literature as algorithm aversion, and McGrath et al name it among the competencies AI-PACE is meant to address.1848 Assessment that surfaces a learner’s own reasoning should catch both.

While residents do need to understand what AI can and cannot do, the evidence that such training corrects automation bias is weak. In a randomised trial of physicians who completed a 20-hour AI literacy course covering model capabilities and critical evaluation of AI output, exposure to deliberately erroneous model recommendations still reduced diagnostic reasoning scores by 14% relative to error-free recommendations.49 Automation bias literature places the primary defenses in system design and accountability rather than in the vigilance of the user. Framing the problem as a deficit in resident scrutiny risks making residents culpable for failures they had little power to prevent.50 Education is one layer among others, such as subpopulation testing before deployment and naming accountability for who owns the output of the AI model.

The role of AI postresidency

The end of training does not end skill formation, nor does it confer immunity from deskilling. Though experience consolidates skills from earlier stages of training, it does not reduce the risk of erosion when cognitive work is displaced. However, deferring high-displacement use is neither feasible nor appropriate for independent physicians, who both rely on these tools and hold the judgement to catch many of their errors. The open question in independent practice is not whether to permit high-displacement use, but how to sustain the unaided skills that keep it safe.

Conclusion

The principle is calibration, not prohibition. Higher-risk integrations warrant deliberate review and attention to developmental stages. As AI handles more of the cognitive load that training once required learners to carry unaided, the educator’s role shifts from doing the work to designing how the work gets done. An instructor-in-the-loop pattern recurs across teaching, policy, and assessment.

In teaching, AI can deliver formative feedback against rubrics at scale. In policy, institutional guidelines need to name accountability for AI output, specify permitted uses by training stage and address automation bias directly. In assessment, AI grading is most defensible in well-defined applications such as Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) rubric scoring and written clinical reasoning exam grading, with explicit escalation paths for borderline cases. Competencies have been defined and have demonstrated feasibility, yet implementation remains scarce.51–53

Strengths and limitations

The framework put forth in this article suggests that we calibrate generative AI use to cognitive displacement and to training stage, and locate each of three risks, never-skilling, deskilling and mis-skilling, at the stage where it does the most harm. Its intended contribution is a shared vocabulary and testable, stage-specific guidance for programmes that are already deploying these tools. The case for calibrated rather than prohibitive integration has been made before, most directly by Ke et al, who named the need for stage-sensitive integration without specifying the calibration.16 Indexing that judgement to displacement and stage turns a general principle into an actionable structure a programme can apply and test.

This framework is derived from consensus and a reading of the literature rather than from prospective validation. Other educator groups might reasonably draw some cell boundaries differently. Enforcement depends on assessment and supervision rather than on controlling access, so the framework’s protections are only as strong as a programme’s ability to detect false proficiency, competence that appears intact until the tool is removed.16 The evidence base is also skewed towards North American settings and a few specialties.6 Education, finally, is necessary but not sufficient. The main defenses against absorbing biased or erroneous outputs are accountable deployment and system design, with training as one layer.

A prospective research agenda

This framework’s predictions are testable. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) offers a useful precedent. Educators have established how to measure POCUS skills against a defined competency standard and track their decay once practice lapses.54 That template transfers to the three risks. Deskilling, never-skilling and mis-skilling each predict a specific harm at specific cells of figure 1, measurable at intervals long enough for skill changes to appear. Building on the longitudinal design put forth by Ke et al, a prospective study would follow stage-matched cohorts, establish baseline unaided performance against defined competencies, anchor each measure to a specific cell and repeat those measurements as tools are introduced. The framework’s sharpest prediction is that introducing a high-displacement use earlier than the map allows will produce a measurable decrement on the matched unaided outcome. The published evidence remains thin and weighted towards attitudes rather than skill outcomes, a gap that recent reviews of deskilling also identify.65556

As AI tools shift from text boxes learners query to agents that prepopulate notes and propose plans, critical evaluation yields to passive approval, and the consequences compound the longer the integration goes unmanaged. The choice is whether the integration is designed with the same care that medicine applies to procedures, formularies and curricula, or left to take whatever shape adoption produces. What remains open is who designs that configuration and whether educators and institutions act before it is settled for them.
