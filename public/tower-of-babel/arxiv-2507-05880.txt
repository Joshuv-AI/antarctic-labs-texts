Zeyuan Meng, Zixuan Yi, Iadh Ounis

Abstract
. A recent Large language model (LLM)-based recommendation model, called RecRanker, has demonstrated a superior performance in the top-K recommendation task compared to other models. In particular, RecRanker samples users via clustering, generates an initial ranking list using an initial recommendation model, and fine-tunes an LLM through hybrid instruction tuning (listwise, pairwise, pointwise) to infer user preferences. However, the contribution of each core component—user sampling, initial ranking list generation, prompt construction, and hybrid instruction tuning strategy—remains underexplored. In this work, we inspect the reproducibility of RecRanker, and study the impact and role of its various components. We begin by reproducing the RecRanker pipeline through the implementation of all its key components. Our reproduction shows that the pairwise and listwise methods achieve a performance comparable to that reported in the original paper. For the pointwise method, while we are also able to reproduce the original paper’s results, further analysis shows that the performance is abnormally high due to data leakage from the inclusion of ground-truth information in the prompts. To enable a fair and comprehensive evaluation of LLM-based top- k k recommendations, we propose RecRankerEval—an extensible framework that covers five key dimensions: user sampling strategy, initial recommendation model, LLM backbone, dataset selection, and instruction tuning method. Using the RecRankerEval framework, we show that the original results of RecRanker can be reproduced on the ML-100K and ML-1M datasets, as well as the additional Amazon-Music dataset, but not on BookCrossing due to the lack of timestamp information in the original RecRanker paper. Furthermore, we demonstrate that RecRanker’s performance can be improved by employing alternative user sampling methods, stronger initial recommenders, and more capable LLMs. Our RecRankerEval framework offers a flexible platform for deploying and evaluating various configurations of RecRanker, paving the way for a further in-depth analysis of LLM-based recommenders.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
Shi et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
have recently gained significant attention in the field of recommender systems (RS) for their strong reasoning capabilities and
their
ability to model user intent through natural language.
Existing LLM-based recommendation models
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
typically formulate recommendations as a prompt-based task, allowing LLMs to infer and reason about user preferences, thereby improving
the
recommendation performance.
To better align LLMs with
the
recommendation tasks, these models are often fine-tuned on domain-specific datasets through instruction tuning—a technique that uses task-specific prompts to guide the model during training
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2023b
)
.
A prominent example is RecRanker
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
, one of the first LLM-based recommendation models to apply instruction tuning for top-
k
recommendation.
Specifically, RecRanker first applies clustering-based user sampling to select representative users for prompt construction.
It then employs
an
initial recommendation model
to generate
an
initial ranking

enriching the

Finally, it fine-tunes the LLM using a hybrid of three instruction tuning strategies—pointwise, pairwise, and listwise—to improve its ability to generate accurate top-
k
recommendation lists.
Given its early influence, novel design, and reported effectiveness, RecRanker serves as a strong foundation for LLM-based recommenders.
However, reproducing RecRanker’s results remains challenging, as the released repository lacks essential data processing scripts and training code, preventing a full replication of its experimental pipeline.
Furthermore, the impact of RecRanker’s core components—user sampling, initial ranking list generation, prompt construction, and hybrid instruction tuning strategy—on
the
recommendation performance has not been thoroughly investigated
in previous studies
.

2.
Related Work

Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been increasingly explored for their potential in recommender systems, due to their strong contextual reasoning and generalisation capabilities
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2025
;
Yi and Ounis 2025
;
Yi et al. 2023a
)
.
Two main paradigms have emerged for applying LLMs to recommendation tasks
–
non-tuning and tuning
–

which differ in how they adapt pretrained models to downstream
tasks
use
(
Wu et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2022
;
Yi et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2023c
;
Meng et al. 2024
;
Yi and Ounis 2024
;
Meng et al. 2024
;
Yi and Ounis 2025
)
.
The non-tuning paradigm leverages the zero- and few-shot reasoning capabilities of LLMs by

constructing

prompts that encode user–item interactions.
For instance, Zhang et al.
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
used a pretrained model for conversational recommendation without any task-specific training.

However, due to the limited input token length of LLMs, zero-shot prompts can only include a small number of items, which may negatively impact the recommendation performance
(
Geng et al. 2022
;
Shin et al. 2021
)
.

To address this limitation,

later LLM-based recommendation models proposed
identifying
the most relevant textual information of items and users as input to the LLMs, thereby improving the recommendation performance
(
Cui et al. 2022
)
.

For example, NIR
(
Wang and Lim 2023
)
first identifies candidate items that are most relevant to the user based on interaction history or item features, effectively reducing the input space, and then applies a three-stage prompting strategy for sequential recommendation.

Few-shot methods further improve performance by including demonstration examples in the prompts
(
Wang 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
.
Dai et al.
(
Dai et al. 2023
)
found that using
demonstration
templates based on pointwise, pairwise, and listwise ranking strategies achieved better results than zero-shot prompts.

Despite these advances, few-shot methods struggle to align LLMs with the specific objectives of recommendation tasks, due to their lack of task-specific supervision
(
Petrov and Macdonald 2023
;
Wang and Lim 2023
)
.

To bridge this gap, tuning-based methods fine-tune LLMs using natural language instructions, allowing models to learn domain-specific patterns
(
Bao et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2024
)
.
InstructRec
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)

was
the first
model
to adopt this approach, designing instruction formats for tasks such as sequential recommendation and product search.
However, existing tuning methods often rely on randomly sampled interactions and a single tuning strategy, limiting their ability to fully exploit LLMs’ potential

(
Geng et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2024
)
.

Unlike prior tuning-based methods,

RecRanker
(
Luo et al. 2023
)

addresses these limitations and

is the first LLM-based framework explicitly designed for the top-
k
recommendation task.
It addresses

the limitations of random user sampling and single tuning strategy

by using user sampling to construct three instruction tuning prompts—listwise, pairwise, and pointwise—and fine-tuning Llama2
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
accordingly, which enhances alignment

between the LLM and
the top-
k
recommendation task,
thereby improving
performance.

Despite its promising design, RecRanker still presents critical reproducibility challenges.

The original implementation lacks essential data processing and training scripts, and its instruction tuning relies on full fine-tuning with 16 NVIDIA A800 80GB GPUs—posing a significant barrier for resource-constrained environments.
Moreover, the original paper provides limited evaluation of its four core components,
leaving open questions about their individual impact on performance.

To address these limitations, we reimplement RecRanker and introduce RecRankerEval—a flexible evaluation framework that supports controlled reproduction and targeted analysis.

Rather than treating RecRanker as a monolithic design, RecRankerEval enables systematic inspection of its architecture, offering insights into design effectiveness and opportunities for further optimisation.

3.
RecRanker Model

To support our reproduction study and examine the effectiveness of LLM-based top-
k
recommendation, we first focus on RecRanker, the first LLM-based model explicitly designed for this task.
RecRanker has gained increasing attention for its novel integration of LLMs with structured user sampling and prompt design, yet its implementation details and component-level contributions remain underexplored.
RecRanker includes four core components—user sampling,
initial ranking list generation, prompt construction, and hybrid instruction tuning strategy.

These components work together to reformulate the top-
k
recommendation task into a natural language format, making it suitable for processing by large language models.

In the following subsections
(Sections
3.1
–
3.4
)
, we describe each component
of RecRanker
as the foundation for our reproduction and extension in RecRankerEval.

3.1.
User Sampling

User sampling in RecRanker aims to select
a
representative and diverse
set of
users for constructing high-quality prompts to fine-tune the LLM.

To achieve this,

RecRanker employs three strategies for user sampling:

(1) Importance-aware sampling
:

RecRanker
prioritises users with more interactions by assigning each user
u
a sampling probability proportional to their interaction-based importance score.
Specifically, the probability of selecting user
u
is given by:

p_{u,\text{importance}}=\frac{\ln(q_{u})}{\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\ln(q_{v})}
,
where
q_{u}
denotes the number of interactions for user
u
,

\mathcal{U}
is the full user set, and
\ln
represents the natural logarithm.

The resulting sampled set is denoted as
\mathcal{U}_{1}\sim p_{u,\text{importance}}
;

(2) Clustering-based sampling
:

RecRanker applies K-means
(
Hartigan and Wong 1979
)
to cluster users based on their interaction embeddings, then samples users proportionally from each cluster to ensure diversity across intent groups.

Each user
u
is assigned to a cluster
k_{u}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
and the sampling probability is set proportional to the cluster size,
i.e.,
p_{u,\text{clustering}}\propto\left|\{v\in\mathcal{U}:k_{v}=k_{u}\}\right|
.
The sampled user set is denoted as
\mathcal{U}_{2}\sim p_{u,\text{clustering}}
;

(3) Penalty for repeated selection
:
reduces repeated sampling of the same users.
Given the merged multiset
\mathcal{U}_{3}=\mathcal{U}_{1}\cup\mathcal{U}_{2}
,
this strategy introduces a penalty weight

\psi_{u}=C^{M_{3}(u)}
,
where
C\in(0,1)
is a constant and
M_{3}(u)
is the number of times user
u
appears in
\mathcal{U}_{3}
.
The probability of selecting user
u
is denoted as

\mathcal{U}_{3}\sim p_{u,\text{penalty}}
.

Together, these three sampling methods ensure that the selected users provide diverse and informative training signals to the LLM, forming a solid foundation for subsequent prompt construction and instruction tuning.

3.2.
Initial Ranking List Generation

The second component of RecRanker is the initial ranking list generation, which differs between the training and inference phases.
During training, RecRanker forms a training ranking list for each user
u
by combining interacted items (
I_{u}^{+}
) and negatively sampled items (
I_{u}^{-}
), drawn from the set of unobserved items
\mathcal{I}\setminus I_{u}
:

R_{u}^{\text{train}}=I_{u}^{+}\cup I_{u}^{-}
.
Here,
R_{u}^{\text{train}}
typically includes 10 items, with negative samples incorporated based on the assumption that unobserved items are more likely to be preferred than those explicitly disliked.

This strategy enables the model to distinguish between relevant and non-relevant items during training.

During inference, RecRanker uses an initial recommendation model—such as MF
(
Koren et al. 2009
)
or LightGCN
(
He et al. 2020
)
—to assign scores to all unseen items using a prediction function
f_{\text{init}}(u,i)
. The top-
k
items are selected to construct the inference ranking list:

R_{u}^{\text{infer}}=\text{TopK}_{i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus I_{u}}f_{\text{init}}(u,i)
.
In both phases, RecRanker

typically

selects a ranking list of 10 items as input to the subsequent prompt construction component.

3.3.
Prompt Construction

The third component of RecRanker is prompt construction,

which generates natural language inputs to guide the LLM during instruction tuning and inference.

RecRanker

devises

three ranking prompt types—listwise, pointwise, and pairwise—aimed at translating the recommendation task into a format suitable for language model processing.
As shown in Table
1
, each prompt consists of four parts:

(1) Task description
: specifies that the goal is to perform a top-
k
recommendation
task
;

(2) User interaction history
: includes both liked and disliked items. During training, interactions are selected through user sampling; during inference, they are randomly sampled from each user’s

historical interactions.

(3) Ranking context
:
all three prompt types use the top-
k
results from the initial recommendation model, but format these items differently using method-specific natural language templates

(see Table
1
for examples).

(4)
Initial ranking hints
:

each prompt incorporates information from the initial ranking list generated by the recommendation model—such as predicted item ranks or scores—to guide the LLM’s ranking decisions.

3.4.
Hybrid Instruction Tuning Strategy

The final component of RecRanker is
the
hybrid instruction tuning strategy.
RecRanker performs full fine-tuning by feeding prompts into Llama2, aligning the LLM with the top-
k
recommendation task.

Specifically, RecRanker fine-tunes the pretrained LLM by minimising the cross-entropy loss
(
Zhang and Sabuncu 2018
)
.

This tuning process enhances the LLM’s ability to capture user intent and preference patterns more effectively.

After tuning, the LLM uses the three prompt types—pointwise, pairwise, and listwise—for inference, generating individual recommendation outputs for the target users.

RecRanker then applies a hybrid ranking strategy that integrates the outputs from the pointwise, pairwise, and listwise prompts to produce the final top-
k
recommendation list.

In the pointwise setting, each item’s utility score is computed as:
U_{pointwise}=P+U_{retrieval}
, where
U_{retrieval}=-m\cdot C_{1}
, with
P
being the LLM-predicted relevance score,
m
the item’s rank in the initial recommender output, and
C_{1}

is
a predefined constant.

In the pairwise setting, each item receives a constant utility score:
U_{pairwise}=C_{2}
, where
C_{2}
is a constant.

In the listwise setting, the utility score depends on the item’s position in the LLM-generated ranking:
U_{listwise}=-m^{\prime}\cdot C_{3}
, where
m^{\prime}
is the rank assigned by the LLM and
C_{3}
is a constant.

RecRanker computes the final hybrid score for each item by linearly combining the above scores:

U_{hybrid}=\alpha_{1}U_{pointwise}+\alpha_{2}U_{pairwise}+\alpha_{3}U_{listwise}
,

where
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=1
.

RecRanker is the first model to integrate LLMs into top-
k
recommendation, but as previously mentioned reproducing its results presents three challenges:

\bullet

The RecRanker codebase lacks both processed datasets and the necessary data processing scripts.

It also omits critical scripts for generating intermediate files—such as the user embedding
files
and item prediction score
files—required for prompt construction.

This gap makes it difficult to reproduce RecRanker’s third component;

\bullet

For the fourth component, RecRanker adopts full fine-tuning on Llama2 using 16 NVIDIA A800 80GB GPUs.
This setup requires substantial computational resources, making the original
RecRanker

training stage impractical for resource-constrained environments;

\bullet

The original RecRanker paper lacks sufficient ablation study or component-level analysis of its four key modules–user sampling, initial ranking list generation, prompt construction, and hybrid instruction tuning strategy–leaving their individual contributions to the overall performance unclear.

To address these challenges, we reimplement RecRanker within our RecRankerEval framework and verify its reported performance.
Beyond reproduction, RecRankerEval enables a deeper investigation into the model’s design decisions by supporting controlled comparisons, alternative configurations, and targeted analysis of potential issues-such as component sensitivity and data leakage
-as discussed in Sections
5.4
to
5.7
.

4.
RecRankerEval

In this section, we start by providing an overview of the RecRankerEval framework (Section
4.1
). Next,
Sections

to

introduce the five core components of the framework: user sampling strategy, initial recommendation model, LLM backbone, dataset selection, and instruction tuning method.

4.1.
Framework Overview

RecRankerEval is
an extensible framework built upon RecRanker to enable
the
reproducible and systematic evaluation of LLM-based top-
k
recommendation models.

One key objective of
RecRankerEval
is
to support modular experimentation and
a
comparative analysis
of different alternative choices when deploying an LLM-based recommender such as RecRanker. Such choices pertain to five key components: user sampling strategy, initial recommendation model, LLM backbone, dataset selection, and instruction tuning method
.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, RecRankerEval offers standardised interfaces, evaluation protocols, and support for diverse configuration options.

The current framework implementation includes three user sampling strategies (random, K-means, DBSCAN), three initial recommendation models (MF, LightGCN, XSimGCL), three LLM backbones (Llama2, Llama3, GPT-3.5), four datasets (ML-100K, ML-1M, BookCrossing, Amazon-Music), and four instruction tuning methods (pointwise, pairwise, listwise, hybrid).

Beyond these built-in modules, the framework is designed for extensibility—allowing researchers to integrate new models, datasets, and LLMs with minimal effort.
This makes RecRankerEval a generalisable platform for controlled experimentation and in-depth analysis of LLM-based top
-
k
systems

that follow or extend the RecRanker paradigm.

4.2.
User Sampling Strategy

To reproduce the user sampling component described in Section
3.1
, RecRankerEval implements all three strategies used in the original RecRanker: importance-aware sampling, clustering-based sampling, and repetition penalty.

While retaining the importance-aware and repetition penalty mechanisms, we explore alternatives specifically for the clustering-based method, which originally used K-means
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
.

Indeed, in addition

to K-means
, our framework
also
introduces two alternative sampling strategies—random sampling and DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise)
(
Wang et al. 2015
)
sampling—to enable
for a
broader investigation into how different user selection mechanisms impact final recommendation performance.
For random sampling, we randomly select a subset of users for training:

U_{\text{random}}\sim\text{Uniform}(\mathcal{U})
,
where
\mathcal{U}
is the full user set.
For DBSCAN-based sampling, we cluster user embeddings and perform denoised sampling by excluding noise points and selecting users proportionally from valid clusters.
The probability of selecting a user
u
is proportional to the size of their cluster:

p_{u,\text{DBSCAN}}\propto|\{v\in\mathcal{U}:k_{v}=k_{u},\,k_{u}\neq-1\}|
,
where
k_{u}
denotes the cluster label assigned to user
u
by DBSCAN, and
k_{u}=-1
indicates a noise point.

This clustering-based strategy helps reduce the influence of outliers by discarding users labelled as noise (
k_{u}=-1
), which are typically far from any dense user group in the embedding space.

When replacing K-means with the DBSCAN-based sampling, we keep the remaining components of RecRanker’s original three-part sampling strategy unchanged. In contrast, the random sampling strategy is used as a standalone alternative, where training users are selected uniformly at random without incorporating importance or repetition considerations.
This setup enables a systematic investigation into how alternative clustering methods affect the diversity and representativeness of the sampled users, and their downstream impact on LLM-based top-
k
recommendation performance.

4.3.
Initial Recommendation Model

RecRankerEval reproduces the second component of the original RecRanker pipeline by providing initial recommendation models that generate an initial ranking list for each user.
This ranking list serves as the basis for constructing prompts, enabling the LLM to operate within a user-specific recommendation context during instruction tuning.

As shown in Figure
1
,

our current RecRankerEval implementation includes

three initial recommendation models: Matrix Factorisation (MF)

(
Koren et al. 2009
)
and LightGCN

(
He et al. 2020
)
,

both
used in the original RecRanker paper, and XSimGCL

(
Yu et al. 2023
)
,
a recent self-supervised graph-based model shown to improve recommendation accuracy under sparse interactions.
We include XSimGCL to expand the evaluation scope and test RecRankerEval under more advanced

initial recommendation model settings.

This allows to examine
how different initial recommendation models

affect the initial ranking lists used in the prompt construction, thus indirectly influencing the effectiveness of subsequent instruction tuning and the final recommendation performance.

To ensure reproducibility, we implement the complete pipeline for each initial recommendation model, including the generation of intermediate outputs such as the user embeddings and the ranked item lists, which are essential for prompt construction but were not released in the original RecRanker implementation.

It is worth noting that additional recommendation models can be seamlessly integrated into RecRankerEval with minimal effort.

4.4.
Large Language Model (LLM) Backbone

To systematically investigate the role of LLMs in top-
k
recommendation, our RecRankerEval framework supports both instruction-tuned and zero-shot settings, covering a range of model types and usage modes.
Specifically, we categorise LLM integration into two paradigms: instruction-tuned LLMs, which are

open

models capable of being fine-tuned on prompt data to better align with the recommendation task; and zero-shot LLMs, which are

closed

models that cannot be fine-tuned and are instead used directly via prompting.

As shown in Figure
1
,

RecRankerEval supports Llama2 and Llama3 as

open

models, both of which are fully integrated into the
RecRankerEval
training pipeline
and used in the instruction-tuning setting.
Our framework also incorporates GPT-3.5 as a

closed

model,

and applies it in a zero-shot setting, since it is only accessible via API and does not support fine-tuning.

By supporting these diverse configurations in RecRankerEval, we can explore how the top-
k
recommendation performance varies across different LLMs (open vs. closed), adaptability settings (tunable vs. non-tunable), and application modes (instruction-tuned vs. zero-shot).

4.5.
Dataset Selection

As shown in Figure
1
, we evaluate RecRanker
and its variants
on four widely used public datasets.

Results on the ML-100K and ML-1M are directly reproduced from the original RecRanker’s
supplied resources
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
; Results on the BookCrossing dataset are reproduced based on the descriptions in the RecRanker’s original paper
(
Ziegler et al. 2005
)
; while we also experiment with the additional Amazon-Music dataset to assess the generalisability of the
RecRanker and its variants
across different domains.

More specifically, while for
ML-100K and ML-1M, we reproduce the dataset configurations based on the original RecRanker paper
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
,
since the original implementation does not provide processing scripts for BookCrossing, we
reproduce
the full data preparation pipeline according to the descriptions in the paper.
In addition, we
also similarly process the additional

Amazon-Music dataset
.
To ensure reproducibility, we provide complete data processing scripts for

all datasets,

allowing researchers to recreate the experimental datasets from raw sources.
Detailed preprocessing
descriptions
for all datasets are provided in Section
5.1
.

4.6.
Instruction Tuning Method

As shown in Figure
1
, our RecRankerEval framework implements
the
four instruction tuning methods

introduced in the original RecRanker paper namely the three, pointwise, pairwise and listwise methods as well as a hybrid strategy that combines all three.

For each

method, we fine-tune the
open LLMs—specifically Llama2 and Llama3—using prompts

that correspond to the method’s ranking objective as described in Section
3.3
.

These prompts are constructed to match the ranking objective

of each

instruction tuning method

and guide the LLM in learning to rank items accordingly.
Once fine-tuned, the

LLMs

generate top-
k
recommendation lists based on the given prompts.

To achieve a fair comparison across the various tuning
methods,
RecRankerEval provides standardised output formats and evaluation templates.
This addresses a key limitation of the original RecRanker implementation, which did not release standardised output processing scripts or formatting conventions.

In addition, we provide scripts that automatically process the raw model outputs—such as item-score pairs or ranked lists—into final recommendation results suitable for evaluation.
This automation streamlines the pipeline and ensures a consistent evaluation across different instruction tuning setups.

5.2.
Baselines

We
report the performance of
the
initial recommendation models as baselines for evaluating the effectiveness of RecRanker.
To reproduce the results
of RecRanker’s original paper
, we leverage MF
(
Koren et al. 2009
)
and LightGCN
(
He et al. 2020
)
,
both of
which
were

originally used in RecRanker.

We also
incorporate

another

state-of-the-art model, XSimGCL
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
, to

further
investigate the impact of
the
initial recommendation models on
the
recommendation performance.
We describe below these models
:

\bullet

MF
(
Koren et al. 2009
)
:
Matrix Factorisation (MF) is a

well-established
collaborative filtering model that decomposes the user-item interaction matrix into low-dimensional matrices, capturing user preferences and the latent features of items;

\bullet

LightGCN
(
He et al. 2020
)
:

LightGCN is a graph-based collaborative filtering model that simplifies prior GNN-based recommender architectures (e.g., NGCF
(
Wang et al. 2019b
)
) by removing feature transformations and non-linear activations;

\bullet

XSimGCL
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
:
XSimGCL

leverages

noise-based embedding augmentation and contrastive learning to improve the consistency of node representations in user-item interaction graph, enabling a more effective capture of user interaction similarities.

5.3.
Evaluation Protocol and Implementation Details

We adopt the same evaluation metrics as used in the original RecRanker paper
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
, namely Hit Ratio (HR) and Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG), to measure top-
k
recommendation performance.
Following common practice in recent LLM-based recommendation work
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Luo et al. 2023
)
, we set
k
to 3 and 5, due to the input token length limitation of the LLMs.

As mentioned in Section
4.4
, we use Llama-2 (7B)
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
to reproduce RecRanker’s original results, and additionally include Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
and GPT-3.5 Turbo
(
Ye et al. 2023
)
for an extended evaluation.

To reduce training cost, we train all LLMs using a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 48GB GPU, in contrast to RecRanker’s original setup of 16 A800 GPUs.
We also apply LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
for efficient fine-tuning.

For
a
fair comparison, we follow RecRanker’s hyperparameter configurations

for both Llama2 and Llama3
: a learning rate of 2e-5, a context length of 2048, batch size of 2, and gradient accumulation steps of 64.
We train the models using a cosine learning rate scheduler with 50 warm-up steps.

During the inference stage, we follow RecRanker’s paper and use the vLLM
(
Kwon et al. 2023
)
framework, setting

the temperature to 0.1, top-
k
sampling to 40, and nucleus sampling (top-
p
) to 0.1.

To investigate this result, we re-examine the prompt construction process described in
Section

, particularly focusing on the use of hint scores in pointwise prompts.
We find that in the original design,
the
pointwise prompts include explicit scores derived from user ratings: interacted (i.e., ground-truth) items are assigned high scores, while non-interacted items receive low or zero scores.
This introduces a risk of data leakage, as the model effectively receives direct

signals

about the correct answers within the input prompt.

Such leakage can artificially boost the model’s performance by reducing the learning burden and enabling shortcut-based predictions.

To verify this hypothesis, we construct a corrected variant,
RecRanker_{pointwiseFix}
, by removing the hint scores from
the
pointwise prompts.

Table

presents the updated results across four datasets and three initial recommendation models (MF, LightGCN, XSimGCL).

Compared to
RecRanker_{pointwise}
, the performance of
RecRanker_{pointwiseFix}
drops significantly, aligning more closely with
RecRanker_{pairwise}
and
RecRanker_{listwise}
.

In this corrected setting,
RecRanker_{listwise}
emerges as the most effective variant across most datasets and initial recommendation models.

These findings show that the original pointwise variant’s strong performance was likely the result of prompt leakage, and suggest the need for careful prompt design when evaluating instruction tuning strategies.

To further investigate the generalisability of RecRankerEval, Table
4
also includes results on two additional datasets
in relation to the original RecRanker paper

On BookCrossing, all RecRanker variants underperform relative to their respective initial recommendation model baselines.

Although we follow the original paper’s preprocessing procedure, the lack of

actual
timestamps requires
the simulation of
interaction times for sequential partitioning, which may introduce noise and hinder training.

In contrast, Amazon-Music provides real interaction timestamps, allowing for a more realistic sequential split.

On this dataset, the RecRanker variants deliver more competitive results, with
RecRanker_{listwise}
achieving the best overall performance and significantly outperforming both the baseline and other variants.

These results suggest that

RecRanker

can generalise to new domains, but the quality of temporal and interaction data plays a critical role in enabling effective LLM-based training and evaluation.

To investigate the impact of different initial recommendation models on the performance of

RecRanker,

Table
4

Across all three initial recommendation models,

we observe consistent trends across
the
datasets.

RecRanker_{listwise}
and
RecRanker_{hybridFix}
generally outperform
RecRanker_{pairwise}
and
RecRanker_{pointwiseFix}

RecRanker_{listwise}

This effect is particularly pronounced under XSimGCL, where
RecRanker_{listwise}
achieves the highest scores across all four datasets, such as an H@3 of 0.0585 on ML-100K and 0.0443 on Amazon-Music.

a
higher quality initial ranking list (as XSimGCL does), the LLM benefits more from
the

Comparing
results
across
the

a

better overall performance for all tuning variants.

For example, on Amazon-Music,
RecRanker_{listwise}

an

However, we observe that
the
RecRanker variants based on classical initial recommendation models (e.g., MF) benefit more significantly from instruction tuning, suggesting that LLM-based approaches can effectively enhance recommendation performance when the base model provides
a
limited ranking quality.
In summary, our findings demonstrate that both the choice of initial recommendation model and the design of the instruction tuning strategy jointly affect the final recommendation quality.
Stronger initial recommendation models benefit more from expressive prompt structures such as
listwise
and
hybrid
, while weaker
base
models gain more from the LLM’s ability to refine coarse initial rankings through fine-tuning.

To evaluate the effect of different user sampling strategies on
RecRanker’s
performance, we compare three approaches introduced in Section

: KMeans (used in the original RecRanker

), DBSCAN (a density-based clustering method), and Random sampling.
Each sampling method is evaluated under four instruction tuning methods–

the

We adopt LightGCN to ensure consistent comparison with prior work and because of its stable performance across different datasets.

We exclude the BookCrossing dataset due to its limited timestamp information, which may interfere with a consistent sampling-based evaluation.

We begin by comparing results across different sampling methods under the same tuning strategy.

In the
listwise
setting,
DBSCAN_{listwise}
achieves the best results on all four metrics on the ML-1M and Amazon-Music datasets, and performs competitively on ML-100K.

Similarly, in the
hybridFix

on ML-100K and Amazon-Music, and

achieves

In the

KMeans_{pairwise}

In summary, we can conclude that DBSCAN is the most effective sampling strategy across the used datasets and tuning methods, while KMeans and Random show competitive but less consistent performances.

6.
Conclusions

In this paper, we
proposed
RecRankerEval, a reproducible and extensible framework

built upon RecRanker,

designed to support the systematic evaluation of
RecRanker’s components as well as
LLM-based top-
k
recommender systems
that follow a similar paradigm
. Our experimental results show that
we could successfully reproduce
the main results reported in the original RecRanker,
while also evaluating the impact of different fine-tuning, user sampling strategies, initial recommendation models and backbone LLMs on the overall performance of the model
.

We also identified and corrected

a data leakage issue in the pointwise method caused by prompts containing ground-truth information.

Beyond reproduction, RecRankerEval indeed enables comparative analysis across model design choices.
We observed that stronger initial recommendation models and more capable LLMs (e.g., Llama3) achieve consistent performance improvements.
Among the instruction tuning methods, the listwise variant achieves the most effective results across datasets.

Furthermore
, we
found
that user sampling strategies influence
the
recommendation performance, with methods like DBSCAN performing better than random and

KMeans-based approaches.

Overall, RecRankerEval offers a practical framework for reproducible experimentation and systematic analysis of LLM-based top-
k
recommendation models across key design choices.
