Disposal of Unused and Expired Medicines in a Take‐Back‐Unwanted‐Medicines (TBUM) Site in Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Sylvester Nyameaye, Victoria Anima Fordjor Luther, Jeffrey Asomaning, Gafaru Mohamed, Baaba daCosta Vroom, Harriet Affran Bonful
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimMedicines form a major component of Ghana's healthcare delivery system. However, stockpile of unwanted medicines in many Ghanaian homes and lack of proper disposal system results in indiscriminate disposal of unwanted medicines. Improper disposal affects the environment and economy. The study aims to assess disposal of unwanted medicines within the past 12 months, methods of disposal, and associated factors.MethodsA cross‐sectional analytic survey involving 426 randomly selected households in the Ga Central municipality was conducted using structured questionnaires. The primary outcome was disposal of expired/unused medicines within the past 12 months measured categorically as yes disposal versus no disposal. The secondary outcome was type of disposal method, measured as home disposal versus organized disposal. Prevalence of expired/unused medicines at home, knowledge, attitude and awareness of disposal programs were assessed. Descriptive, logistic regression and post‐estimation analyses were performed using Stata 16 at an alpha of 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of respondents was 29.5 years (+/−11.9), 226 (53.1%) were females and 37.1% (95% CI: 32.48–41.87) disposed of expired/unused medicines, out of which majority (91.8%; CI: 86.34–95.54) disposed of their expired/unused medicines at home. The prevalence of unwanted medicines at homes was 56.6% (95% CI: 51.7–61.3). Knowledge of disposal practices (14.5%; 95% CI: 11.34–18.27) and awareness of Take‐Back‐Unwanted‐Medicine (9.4%; 95% CI: 6.79–12.57) were low. Attitude (67.1%; 95% CI: 62.5–71.6) towards good disposal was high. Elderly individuals in households (AOR = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.29–0.84), adverse drug reactions (AOR = 2.03; 95% CI: 1.05–3.9), knowledge (AOR = 3.01; 95% CI: 1.53–6.30), and awareness (AOR = 2.91; 95% CI: 1.17–7.24) were associated with expired/unused medicines disposal.ConclusionMajority disposed of unwanted medicines at home, thus Food and Drugs Authority should create awareness on safe medicines disposal through community outreach, integration into healthcare training programs and scaling up of the TBUM program to other districts.Keywords: disposal, expired medicines, Ghana, Take–Back–Unwanted–Medicines (TBUM), unused medicines

What Is Known?

Improper medicine disposal affects health and the environment.

What Is New?

The prevalence of expired/unused medicines in homes of GCM, a TBUM implementing district of Ghana was 56.6%. About 37.1% of the participants disposed of expired/unused medicines within the past 12 months out of which 8.2% of them disposed of their unused/expired medications through the TBUM program.

Clinical implication

Knowledge of expired/unused medications stockpile and factors that contribute to their disposal will help promote safe disposal and safeguard the environment from potential harm.

Introduction

Globally, the number of people using pharmaceuticals is rising [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 50% of these consumers do not take their medications as prescribed, resulting in leftover, unused, or expired medications [2]. These unwanted medications (leftover, unused, or expired medications) should be disposed of appropriately as medical waste, per WHO's recommendation [3]. Improper disposal of these unwanted medicines ends up in the ecosystem with harmful impacts on health, economy and the environment.

In Ghana, over 50% of the participants had expired medicines at home, and about 75% of them disposed of their unused medicines in normal waste bins [4]. A study in the Sunyani Municipality of Ghana detected significant amounts of antibiotics such as amoxicillin, doxycycline, and ciprofloxacin in dumpsites and rivers [5], resulting in antibiotic resistance [6]. These may lead to a significant increase in healthcare costs [7], contamination of the aquatic system [8] and loss of soil fertility [9].

To address the issue of unused/expired medication stockpiles at home and careless disposal, the Food and Drug Authority (FDA) of Ghana launched drug take‐back initiatives dubbed Take‐Back‐Unwanted‐Medicine (TBUM) in 2020 [10]. The Ga Central Municipality (GCM) was one of the districts in the Greater Accra Region where this program was piloted. However, little is known about the disposal of unwanted medicines within the past 12 months, the methods of disposal, and the associated factors within the TBUM implementing district. Furthermore, the majority of research on the topic is descriptive.

This study evaluated the extent of disposal of expired/unused medicine in the municipality within the past 12 months, methods of disposal and associated sociodemographic and other related factors.

Methods

Study Design, Setting and Population

A cross‐sectional community‐based survey was employed. Pretested structured questionnaires were used to interview 426 randomly selected participants from the Ga Central Municipality, Accra. The study was conducted from October 2021 to January 2022. The study population included residents from selected households within GCM.

The Ga Central Municipality (GCM) was purposively selected for this study due to its status as one of the most densely populated districts in Accra, comprising 28,936 households [11], in addition to being a TBUM district. A household is defined as an individual or a group of individuals who reside together in the same house or compound, share common housekeeping arrangements, and acknowledge one individual as the head of the household [12]. The municipality consists of five principal communities: Ablekuma with 4,265 households, Anya with 11,777 households, Ablekuma‐Anya with 4,768 households, Santa‐Maria with 2,930 households, and Sowutuom with 5,196 households [11].

The Take‐Back‐Unwanted‐Medicine (TBUM) program, which was piloted by the FDA in 2020, had two major pharmacies in the GCM serving as permanent collection points for the program [13]. The selected households were geographically close to these pharmacies, within 5–10 km. The common method for disposal of solid waste in the municipality is by burning (43.3%), that for liquid waste is by pouring into compounds (46.5%), onto streets (18.6%), and into gutters (14.2%) [11].

Inclusion Criteria

Residents from selected households (>=18 years) in the GCM from 5 communities: Ablekuma, Ablekuma‐ Anya, Anya, Santa‐Maria and Sowutuom.

Exclusion Criteria

The study excluded residents with mental problems and those who refused to comply with COVID‐19 safety protocols.

Sample Size

This was calculated using the formula n = [z2*p(1 − p)]/1 + e2[z/*p(1 − p)]/e2*N]2

Using a household population (N) of 28,936 according to the Ghana Statistical Service, 2014 [11], a population proportion (p) of 0.5, margin of error (e) of 0.05 and z score of 1.96, the sample size was 384. For a nonresponse rate of 10%, the final sample size for this study was 423 households or participants.

Sampling

The sampling frame consisted of households within the GCM. This data was obtained from the Ghana Statistical Service [12]. A stratified sampling technique was used to achieve the highest form of representation from each major community. Sampling was done in stages. The first stage of sampling was done by selecting the required number of households from each of the five communities. This was achieved using proportionate sampling as (number of households in selected community/total number of households in the district)*sample size. The results were 172 households from Anya, 63 households from Ablekuma, 70 households from Ablekuma‐Anya, 43 households from Santa‐Maria and 75 households from Sowutuom. The second stage was done by selecting households from each community using a systematic sampling technique with a sampling interval of 68 (Sampling interval = N/n, thus 28936/423 = 68). The first household was selected based on random sampling balloting. A randonly selected participant from each selected household was then interviewed.

Data Collection

Data were collected through direct interviews of participants utilizing structured questionnaires in KoboCollect, an electronic data collection tool. The questionnaires were organized into sections addressing socio‐demographic factors, health system factors, individual‐level factors, and storage and disposal factors. The primary outcome was the disposal of expired or unused medicines within the past 12 months, measured categorically as either yes disposal or no disposal. The secondary outcome was the method of disposal, categorized as either home disposal (via waste bins, flushing, and dumpsites) or organized disposal (returning expired or unused medicines to collection points in pharmacies). Four question items, adapted from Egan et al. [14], were employed to assess and categorize these outcomes. The question items covered socio‐demographic factors, health system factors and disposal practices.

Health system factors were factors associated with the healthcare delivery system such as source of medicines, excess medicine supply, drug disposal information, wrong/unclear labeling, adverse medicine reaction, and long prescription duration [15, 16].

For the purpose of this study, individual level factors was used as collective term for level of knowledge, attitude and awareness.

Five (5) question items were employed to evaluate the level of knowledge, adapted from Kahsay et al. [17]. Responses were recorded on a nominal scale with categories of ‘no’ or ‘yes.’ The responses were coded as 0 and 1, where 1 indicated ‘yes.’ Individuals who achieved a knowledge score of at least three ‘yes’ responses out of the five questions were classified as possessing ‘good knowledge.’

Five (5) question items were utilized to assess the level of attitude toward disposal practices, adapted from the study by Kahsay et al. [17]. This was recorded on a 5‐point Likert scale (Strongly agree, Agree, Neutral, Disagree, Strongly Disagree), coded as 5, 4, 3, 2, and 1, respectively. The total attitude score was calculated by summing all scores, and the mean attitude score was determined by dividing the total attitude score by the number of study participants. Participants with attitude scores below the mean score were categorized as having unfavorable attitudes, while those with scores equal to or above the mean were categorized as having favorable attitudes [18, 19].

Two question items were used to assess and categorize the level of awareness of the disposal program, adapted from Egan et al. [14]. The responses were recorded as 1 and 2, where 2 indicated ‘yes’ and represented ‘Awareness,’ and 1 indicated ‘No’ and represented ‘Not Aware.”. Individuals with at least one ‘yes’ were assigned aware [14].

Pre‐testing was carried out, and the feedback was used to refine the tools before using for the final study.

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS‐ERC 045/09/21). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Analyses

Data analysis was conducted using STATA, Version 16. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the data, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables expressed as means with corresponding standard deviations. Bivariate associations between explanatory variables and the disposal of expired or unused medicines within the preceding 12 months were examined using the Chi‐square test, with statistical significance defined at p < 0.05. Simple logistic regression was then applied to explore the crude associations between independent variables and the outcome of interest, using a 90% confidence level. Variables demonstrating statistical relevance were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model, assessed at a 95% confidence level, to determine the adjusted strength of associations. Post‐estimation analyses were conducted to assess associations between categorical variables and the outcome.

Associations

Chi‐square tests were used to test the association between independent variables and disposal of expired/unused medicines within the past 12 months at p < 0.05. This revealed that marital status, education level, being elderly, disposal information, long prescription duration, excess medicine supply, wrong/unclear labelling, adverse reaction, medicine source, level of knowledge, and level of awareness were significantly associated with disposal of unwanted medicines.

Simple and multiple logistic regression analyses were then performed to determine the association and strength of association. After adjusting for all factors in multiple logistic regression, the strength of association of the factors associated with disposal of expired/unused was determined. The factors that remained significantly associated with disposal of expired drugs (Table 4) are: Elderly individuals were 50% less likely to dispose of expired drugs (AOR = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.29–0.84). Discussion

Prevalence of Expired/Unused Medicines in GCM

This study investigated the prevalence of unused and expired medications, disposal practices for expired drugs, and their associated determinants. Within the Ga Central Municipality (GCM), the prevalence of unused and expired medications was 56.6%. Although substantial, this estimate was markedly lower than the 98% prevalence (n = 500) reported in Konongo‐Odumasi, a community in Ghana's Ashanti Region [20]. The comparatively reduced prevalence observed in GCM may be attributed to the implementation of the “Take Back Unwanted Medicines” (TBUM) program, which is absent in Konongo‐Odumasi. In contrast, a significantly lower prevalence of 13.8% (n = 507) was reported in Awi, Ethiopia [21], likely reflecting differences in public awareness and accessibility of safe disposal mechanisms across settings.

Despite ongoing interventions in GCM, the persistence of unused and expired medications at the household level highlights continued risks to public health and safety. The findings underscore the need for strengthening disposal infrastructure and community engagement. Specifically, the government of Ghana and the Ministry of Health, through the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA), should expand the network of TBUM collection points within the district and intensify public awareness campaigns to promote safe and appropriate disposal practices.

Impact of TBUM Program

In the present study, only a small proportion of participants (8.2% of the 37.1% who disposed of unused or expired medications within the past 12 months) reported utilizing organized disposal program This prevalence is higher than the 1% (n = 500) reported by Abruquah et al. [20] in Konongo‐Odumasi, located in Ghana's Ashanti Region, where the absence of a formal take‐back program likely accounts for the lower uptake. Similarly, a study conducted in Nairobi found that 4.88% (n = 164) of respondents returned unused or expired medications through an organized take‐back program [22], a figure lower than that observed in Ga Central Municipality (GCM). This discrepancy may reflect variations in the design, coverage, and implementation of disposal initiatives across the two settings.

By contrast, studies from high‐income countries report significantly higher rates of organized disposal. For example, in Kentucky, United States, 36.7% (n = 324) of participants reported using organized disposal programs [14]. The markedly higher prevalence observed in this context may be attributed to stronger public health infrastructure, widespread community awareness campaigns, and higher literacy levels compared to low‐ and middle‐income settings.

Determinants of Disposal Practice in GCM

Disposal of expired/unused medicines within the past 12 months in GCM was associated with factors including elderly individuals, adverse medication reactions, awareness, and knowledge.

The results of the study are consistent with the findings of a study in the United States of America that found a relationship between disposal practices and awareness [14]. Another study conducted in South Africa revealed that awareness differed according to residency, educational attainment, and work status [23]. In the United States of America, awareness of take‐back programs or disposal opportunities was associated with disposal practice [14]. In some low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), a study reported limited knowledge among the residents on the proper disposal of unwanted/expired drugs [17]. Another study in an LMIC reported that the majority of the participants had a positive attitude to good disposal practice [24]

Taking medications for chronic conditions is common among the elderly [25]. They may have more difficulties keeping track of unwanted medications due to their increased medication use. Compared to younger people, elderly people are more likely to have multiple chronic medical conditions [26]. Hence, they tend to use drugs more frequently. Households with elderly members were 50% less likely to dispose of unwanted medicines than in households without elderly, suggesting that households with elderly accumulate or fail to dispose of more unwanted medications. This is consistent with research from India and a systematic review of studies conducted across multiple continents, which found a significant correlation between age and the amount of unused medication accumulated in homes [27, 28]. This suggests that the accumulation of medications can be influenced by age. Hence, healthcare professionals should focus more on the elderly when providing advice on medication disposal.

Post‐estimation tests indicated that occupation had an overall impact on disposal of unwanted medicines within the past 12 months in GCM. Certain professions necessitate the continuous use of medications to mitigate and/or lessen the impact of health hazards related to the workplace. There is conflicting evidence in literature regarding the relationship between the disposal of expired medications and occupation. According to a Tanzanian study, occupation had no discernible impact on how unwanted medications were disposed of [29]. As noted in the current study, a different study conducted in Lebanon found a correlation between occupation and the disposal of unwanted medications [30]. This could be due to continuous educational training and ethics, and accessibility to disposal materials in these geographical areas. We suggest that policymakers should consider an individual's work when addressing the issue of expired drugs, as it may have an impact on their accumulation.

In terms of adverse events, when patients encounter uncomfortable side effects, they are more likely to stop taking, hold onto, or discard their medications. We found that, in comparison to those who had not experienced any side effects after taking a medication, those who reported adverse events had twice the likelihood of discarding unwanted medications. This aligns with an Ethiopian study that showed that storing unwanted medications at home is related to medication adverse effects [31]. This suggests that adverse events may impact the decision to discard or store medications. Therefore, pharmacists should emphasize the potential negative effects of medicines dispensed more and instruct patients on how to properly handle drugs following adverse events.

Environmental and Health Implications of Inappropriate Disposal Practice

To address related health risks, unwanted medications must be disposed of properly. When medications leak into water bodies and pipelines, leaving behind leftover pharmaceuticals in fish and aquatic ecology, disposing of unwanted medications in trash cans or flushing them down the toilet can be hazardous [32]. Antibiotic resistance to strains of microorganisms found in sewage can increase when expired pharmaceuticals are present, as these microbes can mutate into resistant and deadly pathogens [6]. Exposure to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in the environment has been linked to renal failure in vultures [33].

Policy Implications

The study's findings regarding the factors linked to the disposal of unwanted medications can be explored to mitigate the potential health risks associated with these medications. We suggest that the Ministry of Health (MOH) should focus its health education programs on the elderly and those in workplaces to raise awareness and knowledge levels among community members. Secondly, the MOH and the FDA should give priority to raising awareness through community outreach with churches, mosques, market centers and durbars. In addition, disposal awareness programs should be integrated into continuous education programs (CPDs) for pharmacists, nurses and other health workers.

Finally, officials overseeing the TBUM disposal program should expand the number of collection locations throughout the municipality to enhance the collection and increase public awareness through community campaigns. Encouraging the safe disposal of unwanted medications at home will safeguard the environment from potential harm.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This could be the first study assessing disposal of expired drugs practices in a TBUM implementing district, providing indirect information on the level of uptake of the TBUM program. This study used quantitative research methods to ensure representativeness of the large population in GCM and inferential statistical analysis of disposal practice, as previous studies in the region were largely descriptive. Future studies can assess the impact of the TBUM program on disposal practices using rigorous experimental study designs, involving comparison districts without the TBUM program as controls.

Like all cross‐sectional studies, the association between the independent and the dependent variables may not be causal. Though the study was vigorously conducted to help minimize bias, the findings should be cautiously interpreted in light of the study setting.

Conclusion

The prevalence of expired/unused medicine in the GCM was high compared to similar settings in low‐and middle income countries. The majority of unwanted medicines were disposed of at home via waste bins and flushing. Few community members returned their expired drugs to pharmacies for disposal under the TBUM program. The level of knowledge on disposal practice and awareness of the TBUM program was low compared to other LMICs; however, the level of attitude towards good disposal practice was high compared to other LMICs. Elderly people in the household, adverse reactions, knowledge of disposal practice, and awareness of the TBUM program were associated with the disposal of unwanted medicines within the past 12 months in GCM.

Recommendations

Health Institutions should increase awareness of drug take‐back programs through the use of social media and public awareness campaigns. Healthcare providers should be given regular training by the Ghana Health Service through continuous education programmes and in‐service training to increase knowledge of good disposal practices and drug‐take‐back programs. Also, the TBUM initiative should be scaled up to other municipalities by the Ministry of Health.
