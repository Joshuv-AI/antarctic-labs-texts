Short communication: Fluorescence preservation of protoporphyrin IX in fresh frozen glioma tissue
Authors: Matthias Demetz, Kevin Buckley, Alexander Miller-Michlits, Yelyzaveta Miller-Michlits, Aleksandrs Krigers, Johannes Kerschbaumer, Claudius Thomé, Adelheid Wöhrer, Christian F. Freyschlag

ABSTRACT

5-Aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA)-guided fluorescence surgery is widely used to improve the extent of resection in patients with glioblastoma. By visualizing tumor intraoperatively, this technique enhances the neurosurgeon's ability to distinguish tumor tissue from surrounding healthy brain (Gandhi et al., 2019; Certo et al., 2020). The underlying principle relies on the selective accumulation of Protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), a fluorescent heme precursor that is induced in glioma cells following oral administration of 5-ALA (Johansson et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2003). Under violet-blue light, PpIX emits a strong red fluorescence, which can be visualized intraoperatively using modified surgical microscopes.

Several studies have demonstrated that 5-ALA-guided resection significantly improves progression-free survival and extent of resection in glioblastoma, and it is now integrated into the standard of care for eligible patients (Eatz et al., 2022; Baig et al., 2021). However, one important limitation of this approach is that fluorescence is typically assessed only visually, in real-time during surgery. Quantitative spectroscopic approaches have previously been proposed as a complementary method to conventional fluorescence microscopy, enabling the detection of subvisual PpIX accumulation in glioma tissue (Dablouk et al., 2025)−10. Moreover, for research purposes, it is crucial to determine whether PpIX remains stable and detectable in tumor tissue that has been resected and stored as fresh frozen biobanked samples, which represent a valuable resource for molecular and translational studies.

To date, it remains unclear whether PpIX fluorescence persists in frozen tumor samples, and if so, whether it can be reliably detected using commonly used imaging tools. This question has significant implications for translational neuro-oncology research, especially for projects aiming to analyze 5-ALA positive tumor regions with spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, or other advanced molecular techniques.

In this prospective study, we sought to determine whether PpIX fluorescence remains detectable in previously frozen glioblastoma tissue and to compare the sensitivity of standard surgical microscopy with a novel spectroscopic prototype. The prototype was designed to detect PpIX at low concentrations through a fiber-optic sensor using optimized excitation and emission parameters11. The spectroscopic prototype has previously been validated on non-fluorescent tissue, demonstrating minimal autofluorescence signal. We hypothesized that this device might detect subvisual fluorescence even when conventional fluorescence guidance fails.

Between February 2024 and December 2024, we prospectively collected 5-ALA positive tumor tissue from patients undergoing routine surgery for radiologically suspected glioblastoma at our institution. All included cases represented de novo tumors with radiological features suggestive of glioblastoma on preoperative imaging. All patients received oral 5-ALA preoperatively as per standard clinical protocol. During surgery, areas of visible fluorescence were identified using the Zeiss Kinevo surgical microscope. Tissue samples were obtained from areas demonstrating strong visible fluorescence intraoperatively under the surgical microscope. In addition, samples without detectable fluorescence served as internal negative controls. Tissue samples from fluorescent regions were resected, snap frozen immediately, and stored at −80°C in our institutional biobank. Representative sections of each sample were reviewed by an experienced neuropathologist using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, confirming tumor-rich glioblastoma tissue in all cases. Samples with insufficient tumor content or predominantly necrotic tissue were excluded from further analysis. All cases were histopathologically confirmed as glioblastoma (CNS WHO grade 4). A total of 46 tissue specimens were obtained from 34 patients. Twenty-two patients contributed one sample, while 12 patients contributed two samples obtained from different regions of the tumor; no patient contributed more than two samples.

In April 2025, after careful thawing, all fluorescence positive (n = 34) and negative (n = 12) samples were evaluated using both the Zeiss Kinevo surgical microscope and the spectroscopic prototype. Frozen tissue samples were removed from −80°C storage and allowed to thaw at room temperature immediately prior to analysis. Spectroscopic and microscopic fluorescence assessments were initially performed on the thawed tissue. The specimens were subsequently embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, after which both spectroscopic and microscopic evaluations were repeated. All analyses were performed directly after thawing. The goal was to assess whether fluorescence signals could still be detected after months of storage and whether any difference in sensitivity existed between the two devices.

The results were clear and consistent across all tested fluorescence positive samples. The prototype successfully detected a distinct PpIX fluorescence signal in every case, confirming that the compound remains stable and measurable in frozen glioblastoma tissue. In contrast, the microscope failed to detect any visible fluorescence in all samples tested post-thaw, despite having been used successfully to identify fluorescence at the time of surgery. Storage duration ranged between 5 and 14 months; however, no apparent differences in fluorescence detectability were observed across samples with varying storage times. Negative control samples (tumor tissue without intraoperative fluorescence) showed no detectable signal in the spectroscopic prototype despite histopathological confirmation of tumor presence on H&E staining. These findings demonstrate a marked difference in sensitivity between the two methods and suggest that fluorescence intensity decreases below the threshold of visual detection following freezing and thawing, while remaining detectable by sensitive spectroscopic analysis. Our findings have multiple implications. The primary finding of this study is that PpIX remains detectable in fresh-frozen glioblastoma tissue after prolonged storage. This observation is relevant for translational research because many molecular studies rely on archived fresh-frozen tissue. Demonstrating that PpIX remains detectable after freezing enables rapid verification and localization of fluorescent tumor regions within biobanked specimens prior to downstream molecular analyses, thereby facilitating studies investigating the molecular basis of visible and subvisual fluorescence in gliomas. Given that many translational research projects rely on biobanked fresh-frozen tissue, the ability to verify PpIX within archived specimens represents a practical advantage for future molecular investigations. While the higher sensitivity of spectroscopic detection compared with conventional microscopy has been reported previously12, our data extend these observations by showing that spectroscopic detection remains feasible even after long-term biobank storage. However, the mechanisms underlying the loss of visible fluorescence after freezing and thawing remain incompletely understood. Potential explanations include freeze-thaw-related alterations of tissue architecture and optical properties, resulting in changes in light absorption and scattering. In addition, tissue-specific factors such as blood content, necrosis, and other microenvironmental characteristics may affect fluorescence visualization. Future studies are warranted to systematically investigate the relative contribution of these factors.

In summary, this proof-of-concept study demonstrates that Protoporphyrin IX fluorescence persists in fresh frozen glioblastoma tissue and can be reliably detected using a dedicated spectroscopic prototype. Standard surgical microscopy, in contrast, failed to detect post-thaw fluorescence in all tested cases. These results provide a strong rationale for using biobanked 5-ALA positive samples in future translational studies and highlight the importance of sensitive detection technologies when working with archived tissue. Future research will focus on molecular profiling of these regions to further explore the biological and clinical relevance of PpIX fluorescence in glioblastoma.
