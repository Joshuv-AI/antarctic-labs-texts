Amelioration of the neurological phenotype in PKU mouse model emphasizes the potential of base editing for metabolic diseases
Authors: Timo Keskinen, Mervi E Hyvönen
Published: 2026

Main text

Gene editing has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for inborn errors of metabolism. New techniques enable the correction of disease-causing gene variants in living cells, or even in vivo in animal models or human individuals. Several metabolic diseases have already been targeted with lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated base editing that efficiently enters the liver (Figure 1). However, the clinical phenotype often includes other organs; specifically, neurological symptoms are common, even if the defect in enzyme function is in the hepatocytes. Gautier et al.1 describe successful reversion of phenylalanine accumulation in the central nervous system, together with the amelioration of movement disorder, in humanized phenylketonuria (PKU) mouse model treated with adenine base editor (ABE), correcting the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) P281L (c.842C>T) gene variant in the liver. These results reinforce the potential of gene editing for treating intoxication-type metabolic diseases.

LNP-mediated base editing in liver hepatocytes to cure metabolic diseases

Base editor encoding mRNA and single guide RNA are synthesized and encapsulated within lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations. The formulations are infused into the bloodstream, where they rapidly absorb apolipoprotein E (ApoE) onto the surfaces of the particles. Upon reaching the target organ, the liver, the LNP-ApoE complexes bind to the LDL receptors on the surfaces of the hepatocytes. The LNPs enter the hepatocytes via receptor-mediated endocytosis. The low pH environment in the endosomes induces the escape of the base editing RNA cargo to the cytosol. Next, the base editor mRNA is translated into functional base editor protein, which binds the single guide RNA and forms a ribonucleoprotein complex that enters the cell nucleus. After binding the target protospacer locus in the genome, the deaminase domain of the editing complex corrects the defective DNA base, which results in a corrected gene that is transcribed and translated into functional gene product, such as a liver enzyme. PKU is the most common disorder of amino acid metabolism, leading to neurotoxic levels of phenylalanine and, if untreated, severe developmental delay and cognitive impairment. The enzyme PAH is predominantly expressed in hepatocytes, where it catalyzes the conversion of phenylalanine into tyrosine. As shown in this study and previous work,2,3,4 only a minority of hepatocytes need to be corrected to significantly lower blood phenylalanine levels. In one of the ABE-treated mice, the editing efficacy in the liver was substantially lower (10%) than those in the rest of the group (52%–71%). The authors assume this to be due to technical issues in the injection. However, as the plasma phenylalanine level in this particular mouse decreased from over 2,500 μmol/L to below 200 μmol/L, this could still be considered a good treatment response. In human children with PKU, the treatment target is to maintain plasma phenylalanine levels in the range of 120–360 μmol/L according to both the American and European guidelines; the American guidelines recommend the same target for lifelong treatment, whereas the European guidelines allow phenylalanine levels of 120–600 μmol/L after 12 years of age. Considering safety in new therapies, the smallest effective dose should be used.

The article aims to address the important question of the reversibility of the neurological phenotype of PKU by gene editing. Biochemical analyses from brain tissue showed that the phenylalanine levels in ABE-treated PKU mice were similar to those in heterozygous littermates. This reflects the change in plasma phenylalanine concentration well. Phenylalanine competes for transport through the blood-brain barrier with other large neutral amino acids such as tyrosine and tryptophan, which are needed for the formation of neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine. Brain tyrosine and tryptophan levels increased in ABE-treated PKU mice, and neurotransmitter concentrations were also normalized in the mice. Neurotransmitter alterations are considered to play a central role in neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as inattention, hyperactivity, depression, and anxiety, affecting adults with PKU,5 even if they have survived their childhood without intellectual impairment by adhering to strict dietary management.

Assessing the neurocognitive symptoms in a mouse model is challenging, but the behavioral tests conducted for the PKU mice showed improvement in movement skills of PKU mice treated with ABE at 4 weeks of age. However, gene editing did not completely correct the behavior, as indicated by the open-field and elevated zero maze tests. This probably reflects the age of the mice at the time of the treatment. The juvenile PKU mice have already been exposed to high phenylalanine for the first weeks of their life. In humans with PKU, it is well known that early initiation of dietary treatment is crucial to prevent neurological damage. For this reason, screening for PKU in newborns is being conducted for decades in many countries. The timely diagnosis also supports the possibility of gene editing therapy in the future. Treatment early in infancy or childhood would be the most effective to prevent neurological complications, but it might also be beneficial later, considering the reversal of neurotransmitter deficiency in the mouse model.

LNP-mediated base editing has several advantages compared with gene addition strategies, especially for pediatric-onset metabolic liver diseases. The effect of gene correction is stable even in proliferating cells, whereas the effect of gene addition via non-integrating viral vectors is diluted in the proliferating liver in infancy. LNPs efficiently transport nucleic acids into the liver by binding to low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptors on hepatocytes.6 Delivering the editor enzyme as rapidly degrading mRNA will prevent continuous genome editing and, thus, improve the safety of the potential treatment. LNPs are less immunogenic than viral vectors, suggesting that the treatment could be repeated, but the potential immunogenicity of the components of the gene editing machinery has to be taken into consideration.

Base editors do not cause double-strand breaks in DNA, but possible off-target editing needs to be assessed. Off-target analysis for the ABE8.8 enzyme with the selected guide RNA targeting the PAH P281L (c.842C>T) variant was performed previously,7 showing very low-frequency editing in an intron of SPAG17 encoding a ciliary structural protein. We agree with the authors that this is unlikely to cause any significant risk. For future development of base editing therapies targeting different mutations, adequate and appropriate methods of off-target analysis still need to be evaluated.

In addition to PKU, many other intoxication-type metabolic diseases, such as urea cycle defects and organic acidemias, impose a heavy burden on patients and families and often cause severe, life-threatening complications. In these situations, gene editing can offer an alternative to liver transplantation, which carries the risks of operation and requires a suitable donor and lifelong immunosuppression. Notably, the first personalized gene editing for an infant suffering from a severe urea cycle defect, carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS1) deficiency, was published 2025.8

Most of the clinical trials applying base editing are not aiming to restore the defective enzyme function, but rather to silence or disrupt a gene in a pathway. This is rational, as the corrective editing needs to be targeted to each mutation, and the development is expensive and laborious. The PAH P281L (c.842C>T) variant is a known and relatively common pathogenic mutation, accounting for approximately 10% of classic PKU phenotypes in Europe, either in a homozygous genotype or in a compound heterozygous genotype.9 Many of the inborn errors of metabolism are even rarer and are caused by a variety of mutations. Therefore, platform or umbrella trials have been suggested as a solution for developing personalized base editing or prime editing for rare disease-causing gene variants.10 LNP production is affordable, and when the LNP product is validated for safety, new mutations could be targeted after testing the guide for editing efficacy and off-target effects in vitro. This approach could make the therapies accessible to larger patient populations.
