Whole exome sequencing in pediatric hyperammonemia: significant diagnostic yield and identification of three novel variants
Authors: Reza Hajati, Fatemeh Hashemian, Shadab Salehpour, Arezou Sayad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHyperammonemia, not a disorder, but an outcome of a disorder marked by elevated ammonia levels in the blood, is a serious medical condition that makes accurate diagnosis based on clinical and laboratory findings challenging. In this scenario, employing advanced genetic tests like whole exome sequencing can provide crucial insights that help clinicians better manage the disease. Also, identifying disease-causing variants provides conditions for genetic counseling and subsequent preventive measures, including prenatal diagnosis.MethodsThis study was conducted at the Comprehensive Genetic Services Center (CGSC) of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (SBMU), Iran, from 2020 to 2024, involving 20 pediatric patients with clinically suspected hyperammonemia due to inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs). All patients underwent biochemical testing, including plasma amino acid (PAA). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed for molecular diagnosis, and identified variants were interpreted according to ACMG/AMP guidelines. The identified variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing in all the probands. No additional in vitro functional assays were performed.ResultsAmong the twelve genetically confirmed cases, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in genes related to urea cycle disorders (CPS1, OTC, ASS1), organic acidemias (MMUT, PCCB, HMGCL), amino acid transport defects (SLC7A7), and mitochondrial disorders (PDHA1). Patients with CPS1 variants (P2 and P3) presented with hyperammonemia, seizures, and developmental delay, with distinct biochemical profiles reflecting different degrees of enzyme deficiency. The patient with MMUT variant (P10) showed marked metabolic acidosis, consistent with methylmalonic aciduria. Variants in OTC (P4, P5) and ASS1 (P6) were associated with urea cycle dysfunction with hyperammonemia, but the elevated plasma citrulline level was a significant biochemical finding in the patient (P6). Patients with HMGCL (P7), SLC7A7 (P8), and PCCB (P11) variants exhibited organic aciduria patterns compatible with their respective enzymatic defects. In addition, a hemizygous variant in PDHA1 (P12) was identified in a male patient with increased lactate and microcephaly. Detailed clinical, biochemical, and molecular features of all confirmed cases are summarized in Table 3.ConclusionThis study highlights the efficacy of whole exome sequencing in diagnosing genetically heterogeneous conditions like hyperammonemia, with implications for treatment optimization and genetic counseling. The identification of novel variants enhances understanding of the genetic landscape in populations with high rates of consanguinity. Future research should focus on expanding genetic databases and exploring the functional impacts of newly discovered variants.Keywords: CPS1, MMUT, Novel variants, Hyperammonemia, Whole exome sequencing, IEM

Introduction

Hyperammonemia is characterized by the elevated level of a toxic nitrogenous waste named ammonia in the blood. After the catabolism of protein in our body or dietary protein breakdown, ammonia is produced in the colon and intestine, and it is then transferred to the liver for detoxification by the urea cycle as the main pathway of ammonia detoxification (Fig. 1) [1, 2]. Ammonia plasma concentration is variable in different ages (Healthy term infants: 45 ± 9 micromol/L, Preterm infants: 71 ± 26 micromol/L, Children older than 1 month: less than 50 micromol/L and Adults: less than 30 micromol/L). It normally decreases from neonate to adulthood, but as a general rule, the excess amount of this toxic substrate, especially for a long period, can cause severe neurologic damage with seizure, cerebral palsy, cognitive impairment, coma, and even death when it exceeds 300 µmol/L and is left untreated in time. The clinical and genetic heterogeneity of metabolic disorders with hyperammonemia makes the diagnosis challenging, so we need a precise and powerful technique for the diagnosis and management of the disease. Although tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) in newborn screening programs has high sensitivity and specificity for many inborn errors of metabolism, confirmatory tests such as plasma amino acids (PAA) and urine organic acids (OA) are not always available or performed in all patients. In this context, next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based methods, including whole exome sequencing (WES), provide a comprehensive approach for accurate diagnosis, especially in populations with high rates of consanguinity. It also helps clinicians to be able to use the most appropriate treatment approach for these patients and reduce unnecessary and time-consuming treatments [3]. Since most metabolic disorders, such as those with hyperammonemia, have autosomal recessive inheritance, in the field of genetic counseling, especially in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage, identification of disease-causing variants in the affected person provides the conditions for screening other at-risk family members [4]. The previous reports in this field in the Iranian population are limited to a few cases (Table 1). The present study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic utility of whole exome sequencing (WES) in pediatric patients with hyperammonemia suspected of having inborn errors of metabolism and to identify both known and novel genetic variants contributing to the disease. This research was conducted as a retrospective cohort study involving 20 Iranian pediatric patients referred to the Comprehensive Genetic Services Center (CGSC) at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. The study further aimed to assess the relevance of genetic findings for clinical management, including treatment optimization and genetic counseling, particularly in a population with a high prevalence of consanguineous marriages. By analyzing this regional cohort, the study sought to expand understanding of the genetic landscape of hyperammonemia in the Iranian pediatric population and to provide insights into the identification of novel variants that may inform future preventive strategies, such as prenatal diagnosis.

Methods

Case presentation

This research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with the approval of the ethics board of the Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences(SBMU). All participants or their responsible guardians consented to be informed of the nature of the research and signed the informed consent form to participate in the study. The research ethics committee of SBMU approved the proposal in July 2024 (Approval ID: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1403.269).

Twenty Iranian pediatric patients with hyperammonemia, including eight boys and twelve girls, aged five days to fifteen years, in the Comprehensive Genetic Services Center (CGSC) of SBMU, underwent a genetic examination. The pediatric endocrinologist and metabolic specialist confirmed that all participants have hyperammonemia associated with a metabolic abnormality based on clinical examination, laboratory findings, and maternal pregnancy history. Increased ammonia levels are interpreted according to age-specific reference ranges (Healthy term infants: 45 ± 9 micromol/L, Preterm infants: 71 ± 26 micromol/L, Children older than 1 month: less than 50 micromol/L and Adults: less than 30 micromol/L). Any cases that were suspected of secondary hyperammonemia due to any reason other than metabolic disorders, like sepsis or liver failure, were ruled out. Molecular test

After evaluation of patients by specialists, a peripheral blood sample was collected from the patients and their parents. Following DNA extraction using the salting-out method, paired-end exome sequencing was performed with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, utilizing the SureSelect Human All Exon V7 kit to enrich exonic and flanking exon-intron boundary regions. The sequencing process generated raw data in FASTQ file format. Subsequently, a series of bioinformatics analyses were conducted. These included assessment of read quality using FastQC, alignment of the short (100 bases) reads to the reference genome sequence GRCh38 (hg38) with the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA), conversion of SAM files into BAM files using SAMtools, removal of adapter sequences, and elimination of PCR duplicates using Picard(broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Variant calling was performed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK; software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/best-practices). The output of this secondary analysis was a VCF file. This VCF file was further analyzed using various software and online tools, such as wANNOVAR (wannovar.wglab.org). Variants identified in the affected individual were filtered based on factors including disease characteristics, inheritance pattern, gene functions, type of genetic change, genomic regions involved, and data from large databases. Common variants were excluded based on population allele frequency using public databases, including gnomAD (gnomad.broadinstitute.org), ExAC, 1000 Genomes, and ESP. A population frequency cutoff of < 1% (minor allele frequency, MAF < 0.01) was used to retain rare variants potentially associated with disease. Nonsynonymous (missense, nonsense), splice-site, frameshift, and small insertions/deletions (indels) were prioritized. Additionally, in silico algorithms, such as SIFT, PolyPhen, and MutationTaster (mutationtaster.org), were employed to predict variant pathogenicity. Variants that remained after filtering were evaluated according to ACMG guidelines to determine their pathogenicity. For the candidate variant clinically associated with the disease, primers were designed using tools such as Primer3Plus (primer3plus.com/index.html), NCBI Primer-BLAST, and OligoAnalyzer Tool (idtdna.com/pages/tools/oligoanalyzer). PCR reactions and Sanger sequencing (Applied Biosystems 3500XL DNA Sequencer, CA, USA) were subsequently performed to confirm the variant and assess its segregation in the probands and their parents.

Results

After genetic analysis of pediatric patients affected by hyperammonemia using whole exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing confirmation, in twelve out of twenty patients (60%), disease-causing variants were identified, and in eight patients (40%), we could not find any genetic results. Among the twelve patients with positive genetic results, we could find three novel variants in two known hyperammonemia-related genes, CPS1(MIM:608307) and MMUT (MIM:609058), which cause Carbamoylphosphate synthetase I deficiency(MIM:237300) and Methylmalonic aciduria, mut(0) type(MIM:251000), respectively. The novel variant in the CPS1 gene (NM_001875.5:c.1931G > T) is a missense variation that changes Cysteine (TGT) to Phenylalanine (TTT) in exon 17 of 38. During the patient’s annual laboratory evaluations before genetic testing, repeated plasma amino acid profiling revealed consistent abnormalities, including elevated alanine and glutamine levels alongside reduced citrulline—a biochemical pattern suggestive of CPS1 deficiency. These findings, in conjunction with the identification of a pathogenic variant in the CPS1 gene, support the variant’s role in disease pathogenesis. By the ACMG guidelines, this variant meets the criteria for classification as likely pathogenic. However, further case reports and functional studies are warranted to establish its pathogenicity definitively.

The novel variant in the CPS1 gene (NM_001875.5:c.3161T >C) is a missense variation that changes Isoleucine (ATA) to Threonine (ACA) in exon 26 of 38. Based on ACMG guidelines, this variant is classified as VUS [9]. It is anticipated that with the accumulation of more data related to this variant and other VUS, including information from this study, it will likely be reclassified as a likely pathogenic variant in the future. AlphaMissense is a machine-learning system that can categorise a missense genetic variant as likely to be pathogenic or benign. It utilizes variant population frequency databases, evolutionary conservation, and the structural alterations induced by missense mutations to evaluate the potential pathogenicity of variants of interest. The algorithm’s prediction indicates that the VUS in our study falls within the category of likely pathogenic (Fig. 2) [10].

The novel variant identified in the MMUT gene (NM_000255.4:c.309_327del) is a pathogenic frameshift variant that has not been reported in genetic databases. In a 2006 study involving 160 patients with methylmalonic acidemia due to methylmalonyl-CoA mutase enzyme deficiency, mutations were found across all exons, with the majority located in exons 2, 3, 6, and 11.

Patient P2, carrying the c.1931G > T (exon 17) missense variant, exhibited a milder disease course, with symptoms that were effectively controlled using standard UCDs therapy, including sodium benzoate, L-carnitine, and L-arginine. This patient achieved normal developmental milestones, though learning difficulties—manifesting as disinterest in learning and school underperformance—were noted. Biochemically, the plasma amino acid profile revealed elevated alanine 723.5µM (144–557µM) and glutamine 1292.5µM (329–976µM) alongside reduced citrulline 3.9µM (11–45 µM), but the urinary organic acids and acylcarnitines profile was normal.

In contrast, patient P3, harboring the c.3161T > C (exon 26) variant, presented with a catastrophic neonatal-onset form of CPS1 deficiency, characterized by lethargy, seizures, coma, and early death at three years of age. The biochemical profile was notably more severe, with marked ketosis (evidenced by elevated pyruvic acid, lactic acid, propionylglycine, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, adipic acid, suberic acid, and epoxytetradecanedioic acid) and a plasma amino acid pattern that, while sharing some features with P2 patient (e.g. elevated alanine 813.2µM (139–474µM) and glutamine 2439.4µM (316–1020µM) alongside reduced citrulline 4.9µM (9–38µM), also included nonspecific elevations of multiple other amino acids (tyrosine, proline, phenylalanine, ornithine, methionine, lysine, and asparagine).

Discussion

In this study, whole-exome sequencing (WES) enabled a definitive genetic diagnosis in 12 out of 20 pediatric patients with hyperammonemia, yielding a diagnostic rate of 60%. This rate is consistent with previous reports on inherited metabolic disorders, which generally range from 50% to 70% [11, 12]. However, the detection rate of various diseases using exome sequencing techniques can vary widely, ranging from less than 10% to nearly 100%, depending on the genetic nature of the disease and its inheritance pattern [13, 14].

Given the predominantly autosomal recessive inheritance of most metabolic disorders, studying populations with high rates of consanguinity, such as in Iran, increases the likelihood of identifying rare or novel disease-causing variants. Prior reports on Iranian patients with hyperammonemia were mostly limited to isolated cases, highlighting the need for comprehensive genetic studies. In our cohort, most positive findings occurred in families with consanguineous backgrounds, consistent with previous observations, and emphasizing the value of targeted genetic investigations in such populations.

In our study, we identified two novel variants in the CPS1 gene and one novel variant in the MMUT gene for the first time. These variants are associated with Carbamoylphosphate synthetase I deficiency and Methylmalonic aciduria, mut(0) type, respectively (Table 3). Carbamoylphosphate synthetase I deficiency is an autosomal recessive inherited metabolic disorder of the urea cycle pathway, resulting in hyperammonemia (Fig. 1).

The identification of two distinct variants in the CPS1 gene in this study highlights the spectrum of clinical severity observed in UCDs and underscores the critical relationship between genotype and phenotype. The contrasting presentations of patient P2 (mild phenotype) and patient P3 (severe neonatal-onset form) provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CPS1 deficiency and its clinical management.

The broader aminoacidemia, along with the severe clinical course, suggests a near-complete loss of CPS1 function, leading to profound metabolic dysregulation. The familial recurrence of lethal neonatal disease in two siblings of patient P3 (two brothers died at 10 and 15 days after birth with seizure and coma) further supports the severity of this variant. The milder phenotype in patient P2 likely reflects residual enzyme activity, permitting some ammonia detoxification. In contrast, the exon 26 variant in patient P3 appears to cause a near-total enzymatic blockade, resulting in uncontrolled hyperammonemia and its devastating consequences. These observations demonstrate that missense variants in CPS1 can exhibit a wide range of functional impacts, depending on their location and effect on protein stability or catalytic activity. A key limitation of this study is the lack of functional data on the two CPS1 variants. Further studies, such as enzyme activity assays or protein stability analyses, would help confirm the hypothesized mechanisms of pathogenicity. Additionally, the absence of detailed biochemical and genetic information from patient P3’s siblings limits the ability to fully assess familial genotype-phenotype correlations. The limited number of patients in this cohort was mainly due to the high cost of genetic testing procedures.

Patient 9 was a female infant born to consanguineous parents with a positive family history, as her older brother had died at the age of three years due to severe hyperammonemia of unknown genetic cause. During the early months of life, she presented with recurrent seizures and elevated plasma ammonia levels. Given the positive familial history and biochemical findings, she was referred to the Comprehensive Genetic Services Center for further evaluation with a preliminary suspicion of a urea cycle disorder. Subsequent genetic testing identified a pathogenic homozygous variant in the MMACHC gene, consistent with cobalamin C (cblC) deficiency. Although hyperammonemia is not a typical feature of cblC deficiency, the clinical and biochemical presentation in this patient suggests that secondary impairment of the urea cycle function due to metabolic decompensation may have contributed to elevated ammonia levels.

In this study, no specific genetic variant was identified in 8 patients (40%). It is suggested that, in addition to re-analyzing exome data and using updated CNV analysis pipelines, if the results remain negative, particularly in families where the patient is the product of a non-consanguineous marriage and exhibits other symptoms such as developmental delay, additional genetic techniques such as whole-genome sequencing (WGS) should be employed.

Conclusion

Whole exome sequencing has proven to be a transformative tool in diagnosing genetically heterogeneous conditions like hyperammonemia. In this study, we identified the underlying genetic causes in 60% of cases (12/20), highlighting the diagnostic yield of this technology. Identification of three novel variants in the CPS1 and MMUT genes underlines the significance of population studies with high rates of consanguinity marriage, such as Iran. These findings not only help in better patient management but also make it possible for other family members to receive genetic counseling based on the variant detected in the family. Despite the success of genetic diagnosis in 12 cases, 40% of patients (8/20) remained without a genetic diagnosis, showing the need for other genetic techniques and data interpretation pipelines, and even re-analysis of exome data. Future studies will be pivotal and help better interpret the identified variants, especially the VUS. The integration of these efforts can further enhance diagnostic yield and pave the way for more comprehensive management strategies in hyperammonemia and other inherited metabolic disorders.
