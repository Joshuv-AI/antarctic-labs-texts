Determining adverse childhood experiences and their association with substance use among young adults in universities and colleges in Mombasa County, Kenya
Authors: Merab Odero, Richelle W. Kihoro, Phoeby W. Mbogo, Safari Agure, Erastus Muniu, James Muttunga, Zackary Kwena, Susan Njoroge, Collins Okoyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubstance use and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) remain major risk factors for health, education, and social relationships, with the effects of ACEs persisting into adulthood. This study aimed to investigate the association between substance use (alcohol, tobacco and tobacco products, and illicit drugs) and ACEs among young adults in higher learning institutions in Mombasa County, Kenya.MethodsThe analytical cross-sectional study collected data on having ever experienced any form of ACEs and substance use (alcohol, tobacco and tobacco products, and illicit drugs). A total of 849 participants were randomly sampled from 11 higher leaning institutions in the county. Questionnaires collected information on ACEs and substance use, with ACEs scores used to assess association with the three substances. Descriptive statistics were used for exploratory data analysis, while Chi-square tests and logistic regression assessed the relationship between ACEs and the use of the three substances.ResultsThe overall ACEs prevalence was 88.1% (95%CI: 86.0%-90.3%). Among the three substances, alcohol had the highest prevalence at 45.6% (95%CI: 42.4%-49.1%). Exposure to physical abuse (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.8 (95% CI: 1.0-3.1), p = 0.044), emotional neglect (aOR = 1.6 (95% CI: 1.2–2.2), p = 0.002), emotional abuse (aOR = 2.0 (95% CI: 1.1–3.5), p = 0.019), sexual abuse (aOR = 2.2 (95% CI: 1.3–3.8), p = 0.003) and parent/guardian alcoholic (aOR = 1.8 (95% CI: 1.3–2.5), p < 0.001) was significantly associated with higher prevalence of alcohol use. Exposure to emotional neglect (aOR = 1.5 (95% CI: 1.0-2.1), p = 0.039) and collective violence (aOR = 2.0 (95% CI: 1.3–3.2), p = 0.001) was significantly associated with higher prevalence of tobacco and tobacco products use. The use of illicit drugs was significantly associated among those who had experienced emotional abuse (aOR = 1.9 (95% CI: 1.1–3.3), p = 0.019) and those who had a household member imprisoned (aOR = 1.6 (95% CI: 1.1–2.3), p = 0.024).ConclusionThis study found that most young adults in colleges and universities had experienced at least one ACE and reported substance use, with prevalence higher among those exposed to ACEs. This emphasizes the need for targeted approaches within higher learning institutions supporting early detection and prevention of ACEs and their associated consequences such as adult substance use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13011-026-00734-5.

Introduction

Substance use is defined as the consumption of psychoactive substances including alcohol, tobacco, prescription medication or illicit drugs which may alter the functioning of the body and mind [1]. Illicit drugs refer to the non-medical use of a variety of drugs banned by international law or drug treaty’s as their use may pose a threat to the health and well-being of users [2]. These broadly include opioids, cocaine, cannabis and amphetamines [3].

The use of psychoactive substances continues to pose a significant public health concern, affecting approximately one in every 17 people [4]. According to global estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO), 2.5 billion people above 15 years old use alcohol [5]. In 2025, 316 million people used psychoactive drugs with cannabis use the most popular at 244 million users [6].In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the lifetime prevalence of alcohol use among adults is 34.9%, with estimates highest in western SSA at 48.8% and lowest in eastern SSA at 8.1% [7]. In 2023, approximately 10% of adults aged 15–64 years in Southern, West and Central Africa reported using cannabis within the past year [6]. In Kenya, approximately 17.5% of adults aged 15–65 years report use of at least one psychoactive drug [8]. The most commonly used drug is alcohol with 11.8% reporting use, followed by tobacco (8.5%) and cannabis (1.9%) [8].

Substance use has been and remains a major public health concern associated with several short- and long-term adverse health consequences as well as developmental, economic, and social costs [5, 9, 10]. A significant proportion of global deaths are attributed to alcohol use, with the African and European regions having the highest burden [11]. Consumption of alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs has been associated with numerous adverse health consequences including; cardiovascular diseases, liver damage, cancers, cognitive deficits, psychiatric conditions, intentional and unintentional injuries, homicides and suicides as well as premature death caused by traffic accidents [5]. Further to the direct health risks, substance use is also correlated with negative social, economic, and behavioral outcomes such as risky sexual behavior, loss of employment, high costs of medical treatment for the family, and interpersonal violence [12–15].

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) refers to some of the most intensive and frequently occurring sources of stress or trauma that children suffer early in life, usually before the age of 18 years [16]. The term ACEs became widely known after a large study involving more than 17,000 participants explored the impact of childhood abuse, neglect and household challenges on long-term well-being [17]. ACEs are generally grouped into three; abuse, neglect and household dysfunction and they include several categories such as emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, violent treatment towards mother, household substance abuse, household mental illness, parental separation or divorce, and having an incarcerated household member [18].

Prolonged stress in childhood leads to life-long health consequences as it can disrupt early brain development and compromise the functioning of the nervous and immune systems [19, 20]. ACEs have been associated with a wide range of health and behavioral problems such as, early initiation into and continued consumption of alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, mental health illnesses such as depression and anxiety, fetal death, ischemic heart disease, cancer, liver disease, risk of intimate partner violence, having multiple sexual partners, sexually transmitted diseases, suicide attempts, unintended pregnancies, and eating disorders [21–24]. However, child exposure to ACEs has not received much attention from researchers, especially in Africa, and even where attention has been paid, the prevalence is grossly underreported. In Kenya there is limited data and research on ACEs, hindering a full understanding of their impact and where research has been conducted they have primarily focused on the prevalence of ACEs and their association with mental health issues [25–28]. Furthermore, ACEs receive inadequate policy attention and intervention despite their serious health and social consequences.

The lack of data on substance use and abuse and its association with ACEs among college and university students has serious implications on the success of any intervention aimed at reducing this problem. We conducted this study in Mombasa County in Kenya, because it is an urban and coastal area with a relatively higher prevalence of substance use compared to other regions in Kenya [8]. This study was set to establish the prevalence of substance use and ACEs, and establish the association between substance use and ACEs among young adults in institutions of higher learning in Mombasa County, Kenya.

Methods

Study design

This was an analytical cross-sectional study conducted from 22nd September to 30th October 2019, in which both retrospective (history of ACEs and substance use) and prevalence (current status of substance use) data was collected. A total of 849 participants were randomly sampled from 11 institutions of higher learning in Mombasa County, Kenya.

Study site

This study was conducted in Mombasa County, located in South Eastern Kenya along the coast of the Indian Ocean. The 2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census reported a total population of 1,208,333 for Mombasa County, with approximately 60% below the age of 30 years [29]. Mombasa city, the capital of the county and the second largest city in the country experiences a significant influx of both domestic and foreign migrants. The major economic activity is tourism. There are 23 private and public universities, colleges, and campuses in the county. At the time of the study Kenyan education institutions followed the 8-4-4 system that structures education as 8 years of primary, 4 years of secondary and 4 years of university. However, courses like medicine or engineering courses may be longer than four years due to regulatory requirements.

Study population

The participants for this study were students attending 11 universities and colleges in Mombasa County. The study participants comprised of students enrolled in certificate, diploma and undergraduate programs. The study excluded students enrolled in postgraduate programs, those who did not consent and those of unsound mind.

Where n is the minimum sample size, z - standard deviation units for the desired level of confidence (95%), z value of 1.96, p – prevalence of substance use this was assumed at 50%, q = 1-p, DEFF- design effect due to cluster sampling, set at 2, d - is the degree of accuracy, assumed at 0.05. Thus, the minimum sample size was taken as 769. Adjusting the sample size by 10% due to refusals/non-response, a minimum sample of 846 was required.

Sampling procedures

A two-stage cluster random sampling procedure was used where the first stage involved sampling higher learning institutions (clusters) and the second stage participants from the sampled institutions.

The first stage involved listing all institutions of higher learning in Mombasa County, Kenya which constituted the sampling frame, which included 9 universities and 14 colleges. The institutions were stratified by status into two categories namely: university and college. The number of institutions to be sampled per category was determined using the proportionate allocation method. Eleven institutions were sampled using simple random sampling technique. The number of the institutions sampled from each category was determined by dividing the required number (11) by the total (23) and multiplied by the number in the category. That is, for example, the number of colleges to be included was (11/23) *14 = 6.6 rounded off to 7, and for universities (11/23) *9 = 4.3 rounded off to 4. Simple random sampling method was used to randomly draw 7 colleges and 4 universities from a list of all colleges and universities.

In the second stage the number of participants sampled per institution varied based on population size of the institution and was randomly sampled from a list of student registration numbers, not names, provided by the institution administration. A stratified systematic random sampling method was employed to sample the students per institution, this sampling method accounted for students’ varying education levels and gender distribution within the institutions. Within each participating institution, students were first grouped according to their academic level and gender. Lists were obtained from institutional registers, after which systematic random sampling was applied within each stratum using a fixed sampling interval until the required number of participants per institution was achieved.

Data collection and instruments

Two standardized data collection tools, Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) and WHO Model Core Questionnaire, were used to collect information on ACEs and substance use [31, 32]. Sixteen research assistants were recruited and trained on how to conduct the interviews.

The ACE-IQ tool captured data on the identification of the study participants and screened for the following ACEs: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, emotional neglect, household member treated violently, household member who was a substance abuser, household member with mental illness or suicidal, incarcerated household member, peer violence (bullying), community violence and collective violence.

The WHO Model Core Questionnaire is a researcher-administered tool used to screen for substance abuse. It consists of sections with questions on tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, amphetamine, inhalants, sedatives, hallucinogens, opioids, and other drugs. Data were collected on the use of alcohol, tobacco and tobacco products, as well as illicit drugs, which included cocaine, heroin, amphetamines, sedatives, hallucinogens, opiates, tranquilizers, cannabis, and glue. This questionnaire also collected data on socio-demographic aspects of the students and frequency of substance use.

Data management and analysis

Data collected was double entered into a password-protected Microsoft Access database. On completion of data entry, data validation was carried out. The ACE-IQ was first categorized based on the frequency. It involved scoring 1 if the participant answered in the affirmative that is whether “many times”, “a few times”, “yes” or “once” to the ACE questions. This was not the case for the emotional neglect questions where “rarely” or “never” denoted a score of 1. An overall ACE score was then generated scoring 1 for any participant who had ever experienced any ACEs and 0 if otherwise. ACE-IQ data were analyzed following guidelines recommended by the WHO [33]. Exploratory data analysis employed frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and measures of central tendency and dispersion for continuous variables, to summarize socio-demographic characteristics, participants’ history of ACEs, and patterns of substance use. Frequency tables were used to describe exposure to different ACEs, ever use and age at first use of alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs among participants. Prevalence was calculated by substance use type and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained using binomial logistic regression model taking into account clustering by institution. Chi-square test and logistic regression were used to determine the association between ACEs and use of alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 16.1 (STATA Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Overall, 849 students from 11 higher learning institutions in Mombasa County, Kenya participated in this study, 440 (51.8%) were male and 409 (48.2%) were female. The mean age of the participants was 23.0 years (SD = 3.7, range = 17–43). Most of the participants were in their 1st academic year 283 (33.3%) and only 2 (0.2%) were in the 5th academic year. Majority of the participants were single 780 (91.9%) while few were married 69 (8.1%) as shown in Table 1.

Patterns and frequency of substance use

Majority of the participants admitted that they have ever used any drug 480 (56.5%), out of which 300 (62.5%) were male and 180 (37.5%) were female. A total of 387 (45.6%) participants said that they have ever had an alcoholic drink, where most were male 249 (64.3%) and 138 (35.7%) were female. The mean age of first alcohol use was 17.4 years (SD = 3.5, range = 6–35). Among the participants who had ever taken an alcoholic drink, 106 (58.6%) took 1–2 drinks on a weekly basis and 67 (36.8%) took 1 drink daily on the days they drank alcohol.

With regards to tobacco and tobacco products; 111 (13.1%) of all participants admitted to have ever smoked cigarettes, 89 (80.2%) being male and 22 (19.8%) females. The mean age of first tobacco and tobacco products use was 16.7 years (SD = 3.7, range = 6–25). 43 (38.7%) and of those participants who had ever smoked cigarettes did it once or twice, and most 85 (76.6%) smoked less than one cigarette per day in the last 30 days.

On illicit drugs; sedatives were the most used drug registering 203 (23.9%) of all participants. The mean age of when one first took any of the illicit drugs ranged between 10 and 19.6 years as shown in Table 2.

Respondents who had experienced physical abuse (OR = 2.4, 95%CI: 1.5–3.9, p < 0.001), emotional neglect (OR = 2.0, 95%CI: 1.4–2.8, p < 0.001), emotional abuse (OR = 2.5, 95%CI: 1.6-4.0, p = 0.001), bullying (OR = 1.9, 95%CI: 1.2–3.2, p = 0.011), community violence (OR = 1.4, 95%CI: 1.0–2.0, p = 0.044), sexual abuse (OR = 2.0, 95%CI: 1.2–3.2, p = 0.005), parent/ guardian alcoholic (OR = 1.9, 95%CI: 1.3–2.7, p < 0.001), household member imprisoned (OR = 1.7, 95%CI: 1.2–2.6, p = 0.006), household member mentally ill (OR = 1.6, 95%CI: 1.1–2.5, p = 0.016) and collective violence (OR = 2.5, 95%CI: 1.7–3.7, p < 0.001) all showed higher odds of use of tobacco and tobacco products. Participants who had experienced physical abuse (OR = 2.7, 95%CI: 1.7–4.3, p < 0.001), physical neglect (OR = 2.1, 95%CI: 1.1–4.1, p = 0.029), emotional neglect (OR = 2.1, 95%CI: 1.6–2.9, p < 0.001), emotional abuse (OR = 2.8, 95%CI: 1.8–4.4, p < 0.001), domestic violence (OR = 1.7, 95%CI: 1.3–2.2, p < 0.001), bullying (OR = 1.8, 95%CI: 1.2–2.9, p = 0.010), sexual abuse (OR = 3.1, 95%CI: 1.9–4.9, p < 0.001), parent/ guardian alcoholic (OR = 2.4, 95%CI: 1.8–3.3, p < 0.001), household member imprisoned (OR = 1.8, 95%CI: 1.3–2.6, p = 0.001) and collective violence (OR = 1.6, 95%CI: 1.1–2.3, p = 0.011) showed higher odds of use of alcohol. Discussion

Multiple studies in Kenya have underscored the severity of substance use among university students [34–37]. Few studies have described the prevalence of substance use in relation to ACEs in this population. To our knowledge, this is the first study in Kenya to provide evidence on the exposure to ACEs and their link to alcohol, tobacco and tobacco products, and illicit drugs use among young adults in higher learning institutions in Mombasa County, Kenya. The findings from this study will help inform strategies to prevent the occurrence of ACEs and associated consequences, such as substance use in adulthood.

The overall prevalence of ACEs was high at 88.1% among young adults in the study area. This could be due to high poverty levels, cultural and gender norms and increased vulnerability to sexual exploitation linked to the county being a tourist destination, all of which leading to a higher likelihood of exposure to ACEs. More than three-quarters of the study participants had experienced at least one form of ACE, this is consistent with findings from a multi-country survey conducted in five low- and middle income countries in Africa which included Kenya that showed multiple experiences of ACEs over a lifetime [38]. This finding is also similar among university students in developed countries across America and Asia as well as in African countries [39–42]. Exposure to ACEs has been shown to affect the health and well-being of an individual through influencing brain development and disrupting the endocrine and immune systems [43].

More than half of our study population had ever used drugs, this is higher than the national prevalence of drug and substance use in Kenya which is at 17.5% [8]. Our results are consistent with another study conducted among college students in Western Kenya that found a similarly high prevalence of substance use of 69.8% [35]. The elevated use of drugs in our study when compared to the national prevalence may likely reflect the coastal position of Mombasa county as a tourist hub which may increase local drug availability and use. Additionally, the high prevalence in our study could be explained by peer influence and prevailing college culture that normalizes experimentation, both of which have been shown to increase substance use among university students [44, 45]. Our findings found that the most commonly used substance was alcohol, this is plausible as it is more readily available and may be cheaper compared to other substances such as illicit drugs or tobacco. It is not only the most commonly used drug among university students but also the general population and also among psychiatric patients [35, 46, 47].

Males were more likely to use substances than women in all categories, this has been previously explained by cultural norms restricting females from use, as highlighted in a study conducted in four counties in Kenya [48]. These results suggest potential implications for developing substance use interventions that address gender norms such as traditional views of masculinity. Therapeutic interventions for men with substance use that address destructive masculinity beliefs and support exploring healthier and unique expressions of masculinity have been shown to be effective [49]. Digital technologies such as use of computer- assisted interventions for the prevention and treatment of substance use disorders have been shown to be beneficial for both males and females [50]. Males also had an earlier initiation into alcohol than females, a pattern that has been associated with increased likelihood of polysubstance use, specifically cannabis [51].

The age group with the highest prevalence of substance use across all categories were those aged 21 to 25 years, this may be due to greater exposure to peer pressure and tendency to engage in experimentation in this age bracket. Students in fourth academic year were also more likely to abuse alcohol and illicit drugs, this raises important policy considerations for sensitization of students in their senior years on the substance abuse. Sensitization efforts should emphasize the documented academic consequences of substance use including longer time to graduation and declining academic performance [52].

The observed significant difference in prevalence of substance use between those who had experienced ACEs and those who had not is consistent in literature [53–55]. This finding supports the results of a four- year longitudinal study where exposure to traumatic events such as ACEs led to elevated risk of progression to moderate to heavy alcohol use compared to those with no traumatic events [56]. It has been suggested that substance use is utilized as a coping mechanism or a form of self- medication to deal with various forms of ACEs [57]. Positive childhood experiences such as strong parental-child relationships, high family cohesion, quality relationships, and parental monitoring have been shown to be protective against substance use [58, 59]. Important protective factors to consider is that not all people who experience ACEs report substance use and this can be due to factors such as genetic predispositions, environment influences or adaptive coping styles [54].

Our findings show a significant association between emotional neglect and tobacco and alcohol use. A possible explanation for this is that university students may use the pleasure and relief effects that come from inhaling nicotine or consuming alcohol as a solution to their problems and worries which they perceive their parents, guardians or friends cannot understand [60]. Lack of supervision during childhood may persist into adulthood, leading to emotional neglect and increasing university students’ risk of repeated nicotine exposure and increased alcohol consumption [60]. While initially perceived as a stress reliever or mood modulator, continued tobacco use can result in tolerance which can lead to addiction over time [61].

Notably, our study revealed a significant association between emotional abuse and alcohol and illicit drug use. It has been shown specifically for cannabis use that exposure to emotional abuse increases the likelihood of cannabis use during adulthood, with externalizing behavior in adolescents mediating this association [62]. Similarly, a study conducted among pregnant women found that those who had experienced emotional abuse as children may continue to experience it as adults which could influence or worsen illicit drug use during pregnancy [63]. Our finding is contrary to a previous study that identified emotional abuse as a significant predictor of tobacco use and not alcohol and illicit drug use [46]. However, this previous study was conducted among inpatients in a psychiatric referral health facility.

In our study, participants who had parents or guardians who were alcoholic were more likely to use alcohol. Previous studies have reported that families with parents or guardians who are alcoholics tend to have an unpredictable home environment, more conflict, burden of secrecy surrounding the alcoholism and deal with inconsistencies in parenting behavior [64]. This leads to use of alcohol as a means of coping. Children in these families are also vulnerable to other forms of abuse including sexual and physical abuse which were significantly associated with alcohol use. That said, there is evidence that some young adults with history of family’s alcoholism have made conscious efforts to not repeat their parent’s mistakes thus do not engage in alcohol use [65].

Our results show that young adults in higher learning institutions with household members imprisoned were more likely to use illicit drugs. The illicit drugs used the most in this study were sedatives at 23.9% followed by cannabis at 16.0% and amphetamines at 12.3%. A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that there were weak to zero associations between parental incarceration and illicit drug use and recommends the need for randomized and longitudinal studies to determine causality [66].

Study limitations

This study relied on ACEs that were self-reported and not physically visible, hence the likelihood of recall or social desirability bias leading to over or under-reporting of symptoms should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, the study’s scope was restricted to college and university students in Mombasa County, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations with different age groups, education levels or in different geographic areas. Further, this study was cross-sectional in design, which limited causal inferences between ACEs and substance use, we only assessed for the associations between ACEs and substance use at a single time point. Another limitation to consider is that the study did not collect information on all confounding variables such as mental health disorders or other psychosocial factors which could lead to spurious associations between ACEs and substance use. It is also important to acknowledge that the study did not measure the severity, frequency or duration of the ACEs, thus the findings should be interpreted in light of this limitation.

Conclusion

The results of this study show that most of the young adults in colleges and universities had experienced at least one form of ACEs and had used drugs. The significant difference in prevalence of substance use between those who had experienced ACEs and those who had not points to the need to come up with interventions to address ACEs as a way of curbing substance use in adulthood. To alleviate the high burden of substance use and ACEs we recommend, (1) screening of ACEs and substance use in health centers within the institutions, (2) trauma-informed approaches into substance use prevention programs, (3) establishment of outreach programs through community based organizations to promote appropriate coping strategies other than substance use within these higher learning institutions and (4) curriculum integration of life skills, stress management, coping skills and substance use and abuse awareness.
