Managing paraneoplastic GABABR-related limbic encephalitis in a patient with a small cell lung cancer receiving an Immune-Checkpoint Inhibitor (ICI) therapy: description of a case report
Authors: Mathilde Cezar, Rachele Belletto, Sara Polidori, Lisa Soive, Marco Campitiello, Pierre-Olivier Legros, Julie Egea, Francesca Plastino, Marie Benaiteau, Louise Tyvaert, Giampaolo Tortora, Raffaele Longo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGABAB receptor (GABABR)-related paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis (PLE) is frequently associated with cancer, particularly small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and shows several clinical presentations often accompanied by distinctive MRI and EEG findings. It can be exacerbated by immune-modulating therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and needs a multidisciplinary approach.Case reportThis case report describes a 67-year-old male with a diagnosis of SCLC who developed a paraneoplastic LE. The patient’s clinical presentation included amnesia, apraxia, and mesial temporal lobe seizure. MRI showed bilateral hippocampal involvement, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed elevated protein levels and GABAB-R antibodies confirming the diagnosis of PLE. Despite the initial treatment with high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulins, the patient’s neurological symptoms persisted, prompting the addition of plasmapheresis and rituximab that led to a slight clinical improvement. Considering this modest activity and the elevated IL-6 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid, we added a treatment by tocilizumab that allowed a full neurological recovery.Discussion and conclusionThis case highlights the difficulty and complexity of the diagnosis and management of this rare entity, particularly in cancer patients receiving ICIs. This report suggests also the potential efficacy of combining rituximab and tocilizumab and clearly demonstrates that a multidisciplinary approach, tailored to individual patient’s response, is crucial for optimizing treatment outcome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04483-z.

Introduction

Immune-mediated encephalitis, an inflammatory process of the brain tissue associated with the presence of antineuronal antibodies, includes paraneoplastic and autoimmune encephalitis (AE) [1]. Several neurological manifestations have been described, such as limbic encephalitis (LE), Morvan syndrome, seizures or cerebral ataxia depending on the type of autoantibody [2]. After the exclusion of the more common causes, such as other encephalitis, neoplasms, vascular conditions, and post-seizure alterations [1–4], autoimmune LE should be considered in any cancer patient with acute or sub-acute neuropsychiatric manifestations and a documented brain inflammation [1–6].

The gamma-amino butyric acid B receptor (GABABR), a G-protein coupled receptor involved in the GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission in the central nervous system and widely distributed in the hippocampus, thalamus, and cerebellum, can be an autoantigen found in LE [7–11]. Many tumors, particularly small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC), can abnormally express synaptic proteins, including GABABRs that can trigger an immune response with production of anti-GABABR IgG antibodies [11]. Clinically, patients present with mesial temporal lobe seizure, which is the most prominent and early neurological symptom, memory disturbance, and a variety of neuropsychiatric symptoms [4]. Patients with pre-existing neurologic autoimmune diseases can present a fulminant encephalitis after starting immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [12, 13]. Despite their clinical benefits, because of their immune-activating mechanism of action, ICIs can trigger various immune-mediated toxicities [13–20]. The symptoms of the ICI-related LE are usually steroid-responsive [14–16]. Moreover, patients with GABABR related LE have been reported to potentially respond to antibody-depleting immunotherapies and tumor therapy [11], also rituximab may have an added benefit and a potentially faster onset of action [21, 22]. However, there are no data in the literature supporting a specific treatment if symptoms are refractory to rituximab, particularly in the case of a paraneoplastic syndrome exacerbated by ICIs. Paraneoplastic GABABR-related LE is usually associated with a poor prognosis, in terms of survival rate and quality of life, particularly in the presence of seizure [1–4].

We report the case of a 67-year-old man with a diagnosis of paraneoplastic GABABR- related LE associated with a metastatic SCLC.

Case report

Clinical presentation

A 67-year-old, Caucasian, male with a medical history of hypertension, an ischemic cardiopathy with a past myocardial infarction, a chronic stage IIIA renal insufficiency, and active smoking (> 50 pack-years) was hospitalized in November 2024 for a severe and symptomatic hyponatremia at 120 mmol/L(136 < normal range > 145 mmol/L), associated with dizziness and sweating, caused by a syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

Diagnostic workup

Whole body computed tomography scan and the 18F-FDG (18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose)_PET scan found a tumor lesion of the left lung hilum and multiple metastases of the adrenal glands, diaphragmatic lymph nodes, lungs, and peritoneum (Fig. 1 A, B). The fibroscopy documented a budding tumor lesion of the anterior tracheal wall and of the left, intermediate, bronchial trunk. Histology showed an extensive tumor invasion by sheets of small, hyperchromatic cells with a fine chromatin without nucleoli (Fig. 2 A). At the immunohistochemistry, tumor cells were positive for TTF-1 and chromogranin-A (Fig. 2B) confirming the diagnosis of SCLC. The proliferation index (Ki-67) was elevated at 90%. In December 2024, after the first cycle of a chemo-immunotherapy by carboplatin/etoposide/atezolizumab, the patient progressively developed anterograde and retrograde amnesia, particularly for short-term memories, and apraxia, then he presented an episode of a right mesial temporal lobe seizure, confirmed by the electroencephalogram (EEG) (Fig. 3 A). We started an antiseizure treatment by Lacosamide at 100 mg twice daily that led to the complete regression of the epileptic activity as documented by another EEG (Fig. 3B). Clinically, he was conscious, and he did not present any other neurological symptoms. However, after a few days, he developed a tremor of the extremities and a quickly neurological deterioration, initially manifesting as aggression, then by confusion and aggravation of the amnesia. The score of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) test was 15/30, consistent with a moderate neurological damage. Blood analyses were in the normal range. A new EEG suggested the presence of a metabolic, toxic or drug-induced, diffuse encephalopathy (Fig. 3 C). Brain magnetic resonance images (MRI) revealed bilateral, hippocampal involvement with a discrete reduction in the diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) sequences associated with a cortical contrast enhancement and a T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensity signal consistent with the diagnosis of LE (Fig. 4 A). The lumbar punction showed an elevated protein level (0.66 g/dL; normal range < 0.4 g/dL) associated with the presence of a slightly intensity of monoclonal IgG without serum equivalence, confirming its intrathecal production. There was not any evidence of tumor cells in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The molecular analysis of Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 and 2 genome was negative. The antineuronal GABABR IgG were also documented both in the CSF and the serum. A second lumbar punction confirmed the presence of antineuronal GABABR IgG and elevated levels of interleukin (IL)−6 (73 pg/mL; normal range < 2.5 pg/mL), supporting the diagnosis of a paraneoplastic LE (PLE). The dosage of antineuronal GABABR and IL-6 was centrally assessed in the laboratory of Lyon and Mulhouse, respectively. Treatment

At the beginning, the patient received high-dose corticosteroid therapy (1 g/day, d1-5) and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) (0.4 g/kg/day, d1-5) without any improvement of the neurological symptoms. Despite a regression of the hippocampal hyperintensity at the brain MRI (Fig. 4B) and the absence of residual temporal lobe seizures at the EEG (Fig. 3B), the patient did not show any clinical improvement of the neurological symptoms, justifying, after a multidisciplinary discussion, a plasmapheresis treatment, which was prematurely discontinued after 2 sessions for its clinical ineffectiveness. After the discovery of the antineuronal GABABR antibody, a combined treatment with high-dose corticosteroids (1 g/day) and rituximab (IV 1 g, days 1,15, then every six months) was administered on January and February 2025. Considering the potential exacerbation of the neurological symptoms by ICIs, we stopped the immunotherapy and continued the chemotherapy alone by carboplatin/etoposide for a total of 6 cycles. This treatment was well tolerated without any important toxicity. Given the elevated intrathecal IL-6 levels and the patient’s limited clinical improvement, we also administered tocilizumab at a dose of 8 mg/kg every four weeks. The patient showed a slight but progressive improvement of the amnesia with a reduction of the extremity tremors. The 18F-FDG_PET scan, performed after 4 cycles of chemotherapy, revealed a complete tumor response (Fig. 5A-B). Outcome

The patient is now alive, in clinical good conditions and he has fully recovered from his neurological symptoms. The latest brain MRI of Avril 2025 (Fig. 4 C) revealed a decreased bilateral, hippocampal and para-hippocampal gyrus, hypersignal, as well as of the hippocampal volume, suggesting a slight improvement of the PLE and a progression towards an hippocampal atrophy.

Discussion and conclusion

The etiological classification of AE presently distinguishes idiopathic, paraneoplastic, post infectious, and iatrogenic AE (often associated with immunomodulatory/suppressive drugs and ICIs) [1]. The serum and/or CSF autoantibodies can target either intracellular (Group I) [anti-Hu, anti-Ma/Ta, anti-GAD (glutamic acid decarboxylase), anti-Yo, and anti-CV2/CRMP5 (collapsin response mediator protein 5)] or cell surface neuronal antigens (Group II) [anti-LGI (leucine-rich glioma inactivated)1, anti-CASPR2 (contactin-associated protein-like 2), anti-GABAAR, anti-GABABR, anti-NMDAR (N-methyl D-aspartate receptor), and anti-AMPAR (α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor)] [2]. The group I AE, more often associated with an underlying malignancy, is characterized by a worse prognosis and an irreversible tissue damage and usually shows a worse treatment response [1–3]. Even if the group II includes more commonly non-paraneoplastic conditions [1], the GABABR is frequently associated with SCLC [4]. The different physiopathogical mechanism of these two groups can explain the different prognosis, response to treatment, and recover of the tissue damage [1–4]. In the group I, antitumoral immune response cross-reacts with neural antigens causing a lymphocyte-mediated neuronal killing and loss. On the contrary, in the group II, autoantibodies bind to cell-surface epitopes, typically belonging to ion-channels, and result in a neuronal dysfunction that is often reversible after the immunosuppressive treatment [1–4].

Clinically, most AE patients present with a subacute onset and progression (< 3 months) of various neurological symptoms, mainly related to limbic disfunction, including memory deficits, psychiatric symptoms, seizures, and altered mental status [5]. Even if other neurological symptoms, such as movement disorders, ataxia, autonomic or sleep disorders may be present, particularly when the extratemporal structures are involved, LE is one of the most common forms of AE [6]. The most frequent tumor types associated with paraneoplastic AE include: SCLC (anti-Hu and multiple other group I AE), ovarian tumors (anti-NMDAR and anti-Yo), testicular tumors (anti-Ma/Ta), Hodgkin lymphoma (Tr/DNER and multiple other group II AE) [1, 6]. Recently, the Graus criteria clearly define two new entities, including the seronegative AE in the absence of neuronal antibodies and the “antibody-negative but probable AE” in patients not fulfilling criteria for LE [5]. The existence of seronegative AE is probably due to the presence of autoantibodies that are not detected by commercial assays or to a different pathogenic mechanism such as a cell-mediated injury without any associated autoantibody [5, 6]. Depending on the anatomical sites involved, AE can present with radiological characteristics mimicking other diseases, including other encephalitis, neoplasms, vascular conditions, and post-seizure alterations [1–4]. A correct differential diagnosis is crucial for the patient’s management and the administration of an active therapy [1].

LE represents the typical presentation of AE and was first described in the 1960 s as a clinico-pathological entity with mesial temporal lobe symptoms caused by an inflammatory process of the limbic system [7]. In the presence of typical neurological symptoms, MRI plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of LE even in the absence of neuronal antibodies and it is crucial to make a differential diagnosis from other non-immune disorders potentially showing an unilateral involvement, such as seizures, herpes simplex virus encephalitis or gliomas [8]. The 18F-FDG_PET can be useful in identifying metabolic alterations in mesial temporal regions when the MRI is negative [9]. In 2006, Urbach et al.. summarized the typical essential features and progression pattern of LE as follows: unilateral or bilateral hyperintensity and swelling of the mesial temporal lobe structures within ~ 3 months from the clinical onset, regression of the swelling within ~ 9 months, and gradual atrophy starting within ~ 1 year [10]. However, this timing is extremely variable as in some patients the swelling is absent, persisted for over one year, or atrophy appeared within few months [8]. In about two-thirds of all cases of GABABR related LE, MRI reveals a volume increase and hyperintense signals in one or both mesial temporal lobes on T2 and FLAIR-weighted sequences suggesting temporo-mesial encephalitis [11]. The EEG usually shows a slowing and epileptic activity in one or both anterior temporal lobes [1–4]. Autoantibodies can be detected in serum, CSF, or both and recognize an extracellular domain of the B1-subunit of the GABABR [11].

Many recent data showed that ICI treatment could reveal or trigger paraneoplastic syndromes, including PLE [12–20]. In our case, the PLE was probably precipitated or exacerbated by atezolizumab, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between ICIs and autoimmune responses in individuals with pre-existing malignancies.

The management of LE is complex and based on a multidisciplinary and combined approach of immunotherapy and specific anticancer treatment. Different immunomodulator treatments, such as corticosteroids, plasma exchange, immune adsorption or IVIGs, have been reported in the literature with variable results [18]. The anticancer treatment plays also a pivotal role and should always be used in combination with the other treatments as this combined approach clearly appears more effective [18].

In our case, the initial therapeutic interventions with high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG followed by plasmapheresis provided only a partial improvement. Consequently, plasmapheresis was discontinued after only 2 sessions even if there is a possibility that its reduced effectiveness was related to its administration few days after the IVIG treatment. However, despite the absence of any important clinical improvement, the brain MRI showed a regression of the hyperintensity in T2/FLAIR images (Fig. 4B 3E). This clinical/radiological discordance might be linked to the early efficacy of the treatment on the hippocampus inflammatory swelling anticipating any clinical improvement of the neurological symptoms. The persistence of neurological symptoms prompted a shift in management to rituximab, which yielded only a modest improvement of the anterograde amnesia and tremors. The patient’s elevated IL-6 levels in the CSF further suggested the theoretical utility of the association of rituximab and tocilizumab. IL-6 promotes the differentiation of CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, which contributes to cytotoxicity-induced neuronal damage [20]. Tocilizumab is a humanized anti IL-6 receptor monoclonal antibody, which blocks IL-6-mediated signal transduction that has a crucial role in stimulating both B and T-cells in autoimmune processes [21]. Tocilizumab has shown its efficacy in various autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus [21, 22]. Given that the cytokines or antibody-producing plasma cells are not influenced by the activity of rituximab, tocilizumab could potentially represent a good strategy for LE with insufficient response to rituximab, by inhibiting IL-6 and thus improving autoimmune-related neuronal damage through different mechanisms [21, 22]. To date, there is a lack of studies specifically evaluating the combined use of tocilizumab and rituximab in PLE. However, the combination of this therapeutic strategy and its potential efficacy has been demonstrated by a clinical trial in systemic sclerosis [21, 22]. However, as the last 18F-FDG_PET scan documented a complete tumor response (Fig. 5A-B), we can also hypothesize that chemotherapy contributed to the control of PLE by its anticancer effect. Consequently, it is very difficult to well establish the exact role of each treatment in this case. Recently, the inhibitors of the Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling (SOCS)-Janus kinase (JAK)-Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription (STAT) signaling and SOCS mimetics represent promising therapeutic strategies by modulating abnormal immune responses and should be evaluated in further preclinical and clinical investigations [23]. Tofacitinib, a JAK3/1 inhibitor penetrating the brain blood barrier, showed a potential efficacy in treating refractory immune-mediated diseases, including AE [24]. However, the exact role of these drugs in the treatment strategy of AE patients should be validated in larger prospective trials [23, 24].

This case highlights the critical importance of the early identification of PLE in patients undergoing immune-modulating therapy for cancer. It also exemplifies the complexity of managing such conditions, necessitating the use of a combination of treatments, including high-dose corticosteroids, plasmapheresis, IVIG, rituximab, and potentially tocilizumab, with treatment adjustments tailored to the patient’s clinical response.
