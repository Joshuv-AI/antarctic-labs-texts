An inducible co-stimulator ligand (ICOSL)-based peptide therapy for proteinuric kidney diseases targeting αvβ3 integrin
Authors: Alexis P Jimenez-Uribe, Yan Xu, Yanxia Cao, Ryan Spear, Kwi Hye Koh, Adrian P Mansini, Ariana G Alcantar, Felix Kliewe, Nicole Endlich, Jochen Reiser, Steve Mangos, Eunsil Hahm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysregulated αvβ3 integrin activation in podocytes contributes to proteinuric kidney disease by disrupting podocyte architecture and the glomerular filtration barrier. However, no treatments currently exist that therapeutically target this integrin. We previously identified inducible co-stimulator ligand (ICOSL) as an endogenous antagonist of αvβ3 integrin that protects kidney function through its arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) motif, independent of its canonical role in immunity. Leveraging the therapeutic advantages of small peptides and the renoprotective function of ICOSL, we used a structure-guided approach to design a 19-amino acid human ICOSL-derived peptide (hICOSL-19) containing the RGD motif and evaluated its therapeutic potential for proteinuric kidney disease. hICOSL-19 exhibited high-affinity binding to αvβ3 integrin, similar to full-length ICOSL, and retained its αvβ3-antagonistic activity. PEGylation of hICOSL-19 (PhICOSL-19) extended its circulating half-life while preserving its antagonistic function. In murine models of acute and chronic proteinuric kidney disease, including immune-mediated glomerulonephritis and diabetic nephropathy, PhICOSL-19 treatment significantly reduced proteinuria, limited glomerular injury, and preserved podocyte architecture. In vitro and in vivo safety assessments further showed no detectable podocyte cytotoxicity, T-cell activation, or anti-drug antibody response. Introduction

Integrins play crucial roles in maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier, partly by regulating podocyte adhesion to the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) and by modulating foot process stability [1, 2]. Accordingly, alterations in their expression or activation patterns can lead to altered podocyte function and kidney filtration impairment. Notably, the activation of αvβ3 integrin has been identified as a hallmark of nephritis in mice [3] and is implicated in the development of proteinuria in glomerular diseases, mainly in focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and diabetic nephropathy (DN) [4, 5], but has also been implicated in other conditions such as Fabry nephropathy [6] and IgA nephropathy [7].

The αvβ3 integrin not only recognizes ligands that contain arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD)-motifs such as vitronectin, fibronectin, and osteopontin [8, 9], but it can also be activated by other non-RGD containing ligands associated with kidney disease such as triiodothyronine (T3 hormone) [10], angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3) [11], advanced glycation end-products [12], and soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) [4, 5, 12–14]. Moreover, some early experimental evidence has shown that blocking αvβ3 integrin reduces proteinuria in DN and venom-induced glomerulonephritis models [15–17]. Despite its relevance, there are currently no clinically approved therapies targeting this integrin for kidney disease [18]. This is likely due to several confounding factors, including a high risk of immunogenicity of antibody-based treatments [19], the limited specificity of small molecule inhibitors [20], and the dose-dependent paradoxical effects observed with peptidomimetic antagonist [21–24].

Inducible co-stimulator ligand (ICOSL), also known as ICOSLG and CD275, is an endogenous co-stimulatory molecule primarily expressed by immune cells that is associated with T cell activation through its receptor ICOS [25]. Interestingly, ICOSL expression is upregulated in the kidney during crescentic glomerulonephritis [26] and LPS-induced kidney injury in mice [27]. However, contrary to what is expected from a participant in immune activation, the loss or blockade of ICOSL aggravates kidney dysfunction, suggesting a role in renoprotection [26, 28]. Recently, it was discovered that this renoprotective effect is independent of ICOSL’s immune activity and instead depends on interactions with αvβ3 integrin through ICOSL’s RGD-motif. Additionally, it was shown that ICOSL preferentially binds to the active form of αvβ3 integrin and acts as an endogenous antagonist of αvβ3 integrin [28].

By leveraging the advantages of peptide-based therapeutics [29], and the endogenous renoprotective function of ICOSL, we aimed to develop an ICOSL-based peptide targeting αvβ3 integrin for the treatment of proteinuric kidney disease. Proteinuria is a common manifestation of glomerular injury across both immune-mediated and metabolic kidney diseases, including glomerulonephritis and diabetic kidney disease [30], where podocyte dysfunction and disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier contribute to disease progression. We first confirmed that human ICOSL (hICOSL) binds αvβ3 integrin with greater affinity than other RGD-binding integrins. Guided by the hICOSL structure, we then designed a 19-amino acid RGD-containing peptide, hICOSL-19, which retained high-affinity αvβ3 binding and antagonistic activity comparable to full-length hICOSL. PEGylation of hICOSL-19 (PhICOSL-19) extended its plasma half-life while maintaining its antagonistic activity in vitro. We next evaluated peptide safety by assessing podocyte cytotoxicity and potential immunogenicity in vitro and in vivo. Finally, we tested the therapeutic efficacy of PhICOSL-19 in two complementary murine models of proteinuric kidney disease: a nephrotoxic serum (NTS)-induced glomerulonephritis model which recapitulates immune-mediated glomerular injury [31], and progressive diabetic nephropathy in BTBR ob/ob mice [32, 33]. In both models, PhICOSL-19 treatment reduced proteinuria and glomerular injury and preserved podocyte architecture.

Results

Human ICOSL (hICOSL) selectively binds to αVβ3 integrin over other RGD-binding integrins

Previously, we demonstrated that hICOSL preferentially binds to αvβ3 integrin in its MnCl2-induced active conformation and binds minimally when the integrin is in its inactive form [28]. To evaluate ICOSL’s binding specificity across different RGD-binding integrins, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) in the presence of MnCl2 (Fig. 1a). hICOSL exhibited low binding affinities toward αvβ1 (KD = 1.03 μM), αvβ6 (KD = 0.49 μM), αvβ8 (KD = 0.87 μM), and α5β1 (KD = 2.61 μM) (Fig. 1b), consistent with previous reports showing no detectable binding to αIIbβ3 or αvβ5 [28]. In contrast, αvβ3 integrin bound hICOSL with high affinity (KD = 18.6 nM), comparable to the binding affinity reported for the cognate ligand vitronectin (KD = 8.2–12.3 nM) [34], suggesting that αvβ3 is ICOSL’s preferred integrin target.

To assess hICOSL-αvβ3 interactions at the cellular level, we performed proximity ligation assays (PLA) (Fig. 1c) in immortalized human podocytes treated with MnCl2. As expected, MnCl2-treated podocytes displayed robust orange punctate fluorescence, indicating a close proximity between hICOSL and αvβ3 integrin (Fig. 1d). Next, we used the same approach to test kidney biopsies from patients with DN. Despite a heterogeneous staining pattern, an interaction was evident throughout the kidney, with more pronounced localization within the glomerulus (Fig. 1e and Fig. S1). Collectively, these results corroborated previous findings and highlighted hICOSL’s high binding affinity and specificity for αvβ3 integrin at the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels.

Design and functional characterization of a hICOSL-based peptide

Based on our previous work and current SPR data, we sought to determine whether a hICOSL-based peptide could be developed as a therapeutic agent. Using the UniProt database [35] (entry IDs: O75144 and Q9JHJ8), we confirmed the presence of the RGD motif in both mouse (residues 237–239) and human (residues 93–95) ICOSL (Fig. S2a). Using the recently published hICOSL crystal structure [25] (PDB ID: 6X4G), we designed a 19-amino acid hICOSL-derived peptide (hICOSL-19) containing its RGD motif (Fig. 2a, b). Structural and functional mapping studies have shown that the canonical ICOS-binding interface of ICOSL is located within the membrane-distal IgV domain and involves residues clustered along the GFCC′ face, including Y51, Y53, Q55, L114, L116, and F122, as well as residues within adjacent loops [25, 36]. We therefore selected an RGD-containing region that was spatially distinct from this structurally defined ICOS-binding interface. Given that approximately 60% of peptides in clinical development are between 2 and 20 amino acids in length [37], we chose a 19-residue sequence (amino acids R83 to F101) that contained the RGD motif, had adjacent α-helical and β-sheet regions, but lacked the ICOS-binding site. The resulting hICOSL-19 peptide was estimated to have a molecular weight of approximately 2.3 kDa (Fig. 2b).

SPR analysis in the presence of MnCl2 showed that the hICOSL-19 peptide binds human αvβ3 integrin with high affinity (KD = 25.4 nM), comparable to full-length hICOSL (KD = 18.6—23.3 nM) (Figs. 1b and 2c, d). Both the full-length protein and the peptide exhibited strong binding to mouse αvβ3 integrin (KD = 37.4 ± 0.4 nM and KD = 44.9 ± 0.4 nM, respectively) (Fig. S2b, c). In contrast, a scrambled control peptide (also 19 amino acids long) showed no binding to either human or mouse αvβ3 integrins (Fig. 2e and Fig. S2d). The importance of the RGD-motif in mediating the interaction between hICOSL-19 and αvβ3 integrin was confirmed using an interaction inhibition assay with the cyclic pentapeptide cyclo-Arg-Gly-Asp-D-Phe-Val (cRGDfV, also known as cilengitide), a competitive blocker of RGD-binding integrins. As expected, cRGDfV effectively competed with both full-length hICOSL and the hICOSL-19 peptide for binding to αvβ3 integrin, thereby hindering the interaction (Fig. 2f, g). Steady-state affinity fitting revealed reduced binding affinity (KD = 342 ± 155 nM) of hICOSL-19 to αvβ3 integrin in the presence of cRGDfV, characterized by rapid association and dissociation kinetics (Fig. 2g). Similar results were obtained with mouse αvβ3 integrin (Fig. S2e, f). Together, these data demonstrate that the designed hICOSL-19 peptide binds active mouse and human αvβ3 integrin with high affinity in an RGD-dependent manner, exhibiting binding kinetics comparable to full-length hICOSL while structurally lacking the key residues for ICOS-binding.

hICOSL-19 peptide targets αvβ3 integrin on podocytes

Given that αvβ3 activation in podocytes induces structural and functional alterations [38], we investigated whether the hICOSL-19 peptide could directly target αvβ3 integrin in immortalized podocytes [39, 40] (Fig. 3a). MnCl2-induced activation of αvβ3 integrin promoted podocyte adhesion to surfaces coated with either full-length hICOSL or hICOSL-19 peptide, at levels comparable to the canonical αvβ3 ligand vitronectin, whereas adhesion to albumin was minimal (Fig. 3b, c). This interaction was RGD- and αvβ3-dependent, as pre-treatment with cRGDfV or an anti-β3 integrin antibody markedly reduced adhesion in both human (Fig. 3b-d) and mouse (Fig. S3a-c) podocytes. In competitive adhesion assays, pre-incubation with hICOSL-19 or cRGDfV diminished podocyte binding to wells coated with vitronectin (Fig. 3e and Fig. S3a), full-length hICOSL (Fig. S3b), or hICOSL-19 (Fig. S3c). This suggests that hICOSL-19 effectively competes with vitronectin for αvβ3 integrin binding and may participate in a competitive, potentially reversible, mechanism of integrin antagonism.

We next examined whether hICOSL-19 could antagonize αvβ3 integrin-mediated signaling by assessing focal adhesion kinase (FAK) phosphorylation, a key downstream event linked to cytoskeletal alterations and foot process effacement in podocytes [38]. As expected, pre-treatment with either cRGDfV or hICOSL-19 peptide prior to MnCl2/vitronectin-induced integrin activation significantly reduced FAK phosphorylation in podocytes (Fig. 3f), confirming that the hICOSL-19 peptide functionally antagonizes αvβ3 integrin signaling.

These findings prompted us to investigate the physiological role of ICOSL in the kidney, particularly under disease conditions. Building on our prior observation that ICOSL is transiently upregulated during early glomerular injury but reduced in advanced disease states [28], we examined its expression in different glomerular cell types. Inflammatory stimulation with interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced a rapid and robust increase in ICOSL expression in podocytes compared to glomerular endothelial or mesangial cells (Fig. S4a). In contrast, sustained mechanical stress [41–43] led to a marked decline in ICOSL expression in both human and mouse podocytes (Fig. S4b, c). Mining Nephroseq datasets [44] confirmed that ICOSL expression is significantly reduced in glomeruli across multiple late-stage chronic kidney diseases, including diabetic nephropathy, FSGS, IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, and membranous nephropathy [44, 45] (Fig. S4d). These findings suggest that early inflammation transiently boosts ICOSL as a protective response, but persistent injury drives its downregulation, supporting the idea that hICOSL-19 peptide therapy could be beneficial to restore glomerular protection.

PEGylation enhances plasma half-life of hICOSL-19 while retaining function

A primary limitation of peptide-based therapeutics is their low bioavailability due to a short half-life. To address this, we first determined the half-life of hICOSL-19 peptide by performing plasma pharmacokinetic analysis following a single injection of fluorescein (FITC)-labeled hICOSL-19 peptide. The peptide exhibited a two-phase clearance profile (Fig. S5a), with an initial clearance (t1/2 fast = 1.3 min) followed by a terminal-phase plasma half-life (t1/2 slow = 14.96 ± 1.2 min). To improve this, hICOSL-19 peptide was modified by PEGylation at its N-terminus using 12 polyethylene glycol (PEG12) moieties, a common chemical modification to enhance bioavailability [46]. The PEGylated hICOSL-19 (PhICOSL-19) peptide, with an approximate molecular weight of 2.73 kDa, exhibited a slower initial clearance (t1/2 fast = 2.6 min) and a significantly extended terminal-phase plasma half-life (t1/2 slow = 46.74 ± 1.45 min), representing a three-fold increase in its half-life as compared to the unmodified peptide (Fig. S5b).

We next assessed whether PEGylation affected the ability of hICOSL-19 to antagonize αvβ3 integrin-mediated signaling in podocytes. Adhesion assays revealed that PhICOSL-19, like unmodified hICOSL-19, supported robust podocyte adhesion, whereas neither the scrambled control peptide nor the PEGylated RGD-mutant peptide (PmutPep; AAA substitution) promoted adhesion (Fig. 3g). Adhesion was significantly inhibited by pre-treating cells with either cRGDfV or anti-β3 integrin antibody (Fig. 3g, h, and Fig. S5c), confirming that the interaction remained RGD- and αvβ3-dependent following PEGylation. In competitive adhesion assays, pre-incubation with PhICOSL-19 peptide reduced podocyte adhesion to hICOSL-19-, PhICOSL-19-, and vitronectin-coated wells (Fig. 3g, i), indicating that PEGylation preserved αvβ3-binding capability. Consistent with these findings, PhICOSL-19 inhibited MnCl2/vitronectin-induced FAK phosphorylation in podocytes (Fig. 3j). Together, these results demonstrate that PEGylation enhances the pharmacologic properties of hICOSL-19 while maintaining its αvβ3 integrin antagonistic abilities.

PhICOSL-19 peptide treatment reduces proteinuria and glomerular damage in acute and chronic kidney injury

We next evaluated the in vivo efficacy of PhICOSL-19 peptide in nephrotoxic serum [NTS; sheep anti- glomerular basement membrane (GBM) antibody]-induced glomerulonephritis. In the passive NTS model, which mimics acute glomerulonephritis, we used Icosl-KO mice that lack endogenous ICOSL expression to avoid host-related confounding effects (Fig. S6a). PhICOSL-19-treated mice showed a significant reduction in proteinuria compared with saline-treated controls after 24 and 48 h of treatment (Fig. S6b, c). To assess therapeutic potential in a more severe and persistent setting, we next employed an active NTS model that recapitulates chronic, immune-mediated kidney disease (Fig. 4a). Wildtype BALB/c mice were pre-immunized with sheep IgG and complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) prior to NTS injection to trigger a secondary immune activation. One day after NTS administration, mice developed severe proteinuria compared with healthy mice (Fig. S7a). The following day, proteinuric mice were treated with either PhICOSL-19 or the PEGylated RGD-mutant control peptide (PmutPep) for five consecutive days. One day after the final treatment, PhICOSL-19-treated mice showed a significant reduction in urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) when compared with the PmutPep group (Fig. 4b). Histological analysis demonstrated attenuated glomerular injury (Fig. 4c, d, and Fig. S7b), and ultrastructural analysis by electron microscopy (EM) revealed reduced podocyte foot process effacement, reversing the marked effacement observed in PmutPep-treated diseased mice (Fig. 4e, f).

Finally, we tested the efficacy of PhICOSL-19 peptide in a chronic, metabolically mediated model of kidney disease using BTBR ob/ob mice (Fig. 4g), which develop obesity and progressive diabetic nephropathy [32]. At 5 weeks of age, BTBR ob/ob mice showed markedly elevated UACR and 24-h urinary albumin excretion (UAE) when compared to age-matched BTBR WT controls (Fig. S7c, d), indicating significant proteinuria before treatment initiation and consistent with the original characterization of this model [32]. Daily administration of PhICOSL-19 for 6 weeks significantly reduced both UACR and UAE compared with PmutPep-treated mice (Fig. 4h, i), without affecting body weight (Fig. S7e). These functional improvements were accompanied by reduced glomerular injury (Fig. 4j, k, and Fig. S7f), decreased podocyte foot process effacement (Fig. 4l, m), and normalization of GBM thickness (Fig. 4l, n), a characteristic structural feature of diabetic nephropathy.

PhICOSL-19 demonstrates no detectable cytotoxicity or immunogenicity

To evaluate the safety and potential immunogenicity of PhICOSL-19 following repeated administration in vivo, WT BALB/c mice received PhICOSL-19 (500 μg/kg, i.v.) daily for 4 weeks (Fig. 5a). PhICOSL-19 administration did not significantly affect body weight during the treatment period (Fig. S8a), and no gross abnormalities were observed upon macroscopic organ examination. Spleen weight and length were also comparable between PhICOSL-19-treated and untreated control mice (Fig. S8b, c). Flow cytometric analysis of splenocytes showed similarly low percentages of CD69+ cells among CD3+ T cells in PhICOSL-19-treated and control mice (Fig. 5b, c), indicating no detectable increase in splenic T-cell activation. Serum antibody reactivity against PhICOSL-19 was further assessed by indirect ELISA using PhICOSL-19-coated plates. Although a modest increase in optical density was observed in serum from PhICOSL-19-treated mice, this difference was not statistically significant compared with untreated controls (Fig. 5d). Anti-PEG and anti-ICOSL antibodies were included as positive controls to confirm assay performance (Fig. S8d). Together, these findings indicate that repeated PhICOSL-19 administration was well tolerated and did not induce detectable cellular or humoral immune responses.

We next assessed the potential immunostimulatory activity of PhICOSL-19 in vitro. Jurkat T cells were exposed to different concentrations of PhICOSL-19 for 24 h, and T-cell activation was evaluated by CD69 expression, with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin serving as a positive control. PhICOSL-19 did not significantly increase the percentage of CD69 + cells compared with vehicle treatment, whereas robust activation was observed with PMA/ionomycin (Fig. 5e). Consistent with these findings, PhICOSL-19 treatment of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) did not significantly increase CD69 + T cells compared with vehicle-treated cells (Fig. 5f).

Finally, to assess potential cytotoxicity in the therapeutically relevant target cells, human podocytes were treated daily with different concentrations of PhICOSL-19 for 96 h. PhICOSL-19 did not alter F-actin organization (Fig. 5g), as reflected by no significant changes in cell number (Fig. 5h), relative cell area (Fig. 5i), or mean fluorescence intensity-to-area ratio (Fig. 5j). PhICOSL-19 also did not reduce cell viability as assessed by ATP content (Fig. 5k) and NAD(P)H-dependent dehydrogenase activity (Fig. 5l), nor did it increase extracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity (Fig. 5m). In contrast, adriamycin (ADR), used as a positive control for podocyte injury, induced clear cytotoxic effects (Fig. 5g-j). Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro findings support a favorable preliminary safety profile for PhICOSL-19, with no detectable immunogenicity or podocyte cytotoxicity.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the designed ICOSL-based peptide and its PEGylated form bind and antagonize αvβ3 integrin on podocytes. Across acute and chronic kidney disease models driven by immunologic and metabolic injury, PhICOSL-19 treatment consistently reduced proteinuria, ameliorated established glomerular pathology, and preserved podocyte architecture. Combined with its favorable preliminary safety profile, these findings support the therapeutic potential of PhICOSL-19 for treating αvβ3 integrin-mediated kidney disease (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Proteinuric kidney diseases encompass a wide range of renal pathologies with diverse underlying etiologies, including various forms of glomerulopathy. Currently available therapies often rely on immunosuppressive agents such as glucocorticoids, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, and calcineurin inhibitors [47]. These treatments help reduce inflammation or the activation of immune cells that arise because of the primary insult. However, due to the complexity of their etiology, these diseases often involve additional complicating factors, including the generation of autoantibodies, complicated genetic predispositions, and the production of nephrotoxic molecules [47, 48]. Hence, successful management of these diseases is often difficult.

Activation of αvβ3 integrin has been implicated in the pathophysiology of FSGS and DN and is associated with elevated levels of circulating suPAR [49, 50], an immature myeloid cell-derived soluble factor [51] that triggers podocyte foot process effacement through αvβ3 integrin activation [12, 13, 38, 49]. Consistent with its role in podocyte injury, blockade of αvβ3 integrin in experimental models of glomerular disease ameliorates glomerular dysfunction and reduces proteinuria [4, 15, 16]. Furthermore, αvβ3 integrin activation seems to be restricted to the kidney during glomerular injury [3], making this integrin an attractive target for treating glomerular disease. However, αvβ3 integrin-targeting therapies for kidney disease currently do not exist.

Efforts to target αvβ3 integrin in other diseases have led to the development of small molecules, peptides, and antibodies. One of the most extensively studied agents was the modified pentapeptide cRGDfV, commonly known as cilengitide, which was developed as an αvβ3 integrin antagonist for cancer. Peptide-based drugs can offer advantages such as high target specificity and relatively low immunogenicity [29]. Unfortunately, cilengitide failed in a phase III clinical trial, in part because its antagonistic activity could shift toward agonism at lower concentrations [21, 52]. These observations underscore the challenge of therapeutically modulating αvβ3 integrin and highlight the need for alternative approaches that restrain pathological integrin activation without promoting unintended signaling.

Peptide-based therapeutics inspired by endogenous molecules have become an important class of pharmacological agents, with more than 40 peptides approved over the past two decades for diverse applications, including hormone analogs, growth factors, ion channel ligands, and antimicrobial agents [29]. In nephrology, however, peptide-based therapies remain relatively limited. Examples include semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP1-RA) [53–55], and pegmolesatide, a synthetic PEGylated erythropoietin (EPO)-mimetic peptide that activates the erythropoietin receptor for the treatment of CKD-associated anemia [56]. Beyond their established metabolic effects, GLP-1RAs have demonstrated cardiovascular and kidney benefits in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD, including reductions in albuminuria and the risk of adverse kidney outcomes [53–55]. These examples illustrate how endogenous biological molecules and pathways can serve as templates for the development of peptide-based therapeutics with optimized pharmacological properties [57, 58].

Podocytes themselves express several molecules traditionally associated with immune or other cellular systems that can acquire distinct local regulatory functions. For example, CD80, a costimulatory molecule that typically binds to CD28 in T cells, can interact with Neph1 in podocytes and disrupt the slit diaphragm [59]. Another example is the cell surface receptor tissue factor (TF). It has been reported that TF is expressed by podocytes and interacts with the type I interferon receptor (IFNAR) to restrain its activation in a renoprotective manner [60], deviating from its canonical role as part of the extrinsic coagulation pathway. Similarly, we previously described an endogenous renoprotective role for ICOSL, where it can antagonize αvβ3 integrin activation through its RGD-motif, a function entirely independent of its canonical role in immunity [28].

Based on the importance of αvβ3 integrin in glomerular injury, the endogenous protective activity of ICOSL, and the advantages of peptides as therapeutics, we developed a peptide based on the crystal structure of human ICOSL. This short 19-amino-acid peptide retains the RGD-motif and excludes all amino acids known to be important for ICOSL’s immune activity. In the present study, hICOSL-19 bound αvβ3 integrin with an affinity comparable to that of full-length ICOSL and antagonized αvβ3 integrin at the cellular level. These findings demonstrate that the noncanonical podocyte-protective function of ICOSL can be functionally separated from the intact immune molecule and reproduced within a short peptide sequence, providing a pharmacologically tractable means of harnessing this endogenous protective mechanism.

A major challenge for peptide therapeutics is rapid degradation and short systemic half-life [57, 58]. PEGylation increased the circulating half-life of hICOSL-19 approximately three-fold, from 15 to 47 min, while preserving its biological activity. Importantly, despite the relatively short circulating half-life of PhICOSL-19, once-daily administration was sufficient to produce significant therapeutic effects in the mouse models tested. Because dosing frequency was not directly compared, however, it remains possible that more frequent administration or more sustained peptide exposure could further enhance efficacy. Future studies will therefore evaluate dosing frequency together with additional strategies to prolong peptide stability and systemic exposure, including further PEGylation, lipidation, amino acid modification, cyclization, or albumin/Fc-binding approaches [61–63].

Most importantly, the molecular and pharmacological properties of PhICOSL-19 translated into functional kidney protection in vivo. PhICOSL-19 reduced proteinuria and glomerular injury across complementary models representing acute and chronic immune-mediated injury as well as chronic metabolic kidney disease. Notably, therapeutic benefit was observed in models in which proteinuria was established before treatment initiation, supporting a therapeutic rather than solely preventive effect. The consistency of these findings across mechanistically distinct disease settings suggests that αvβ3 integrin-mediated podocyte injury may represent a common downstream pathway that can be therapeutically targeted despite differences in the initiating insult. PhICOSL-19 may therefore have potential in glomerular diseases associated with pathological αvβ3 integrin activation and elevated circulating suPAR, including FSGS and DN, as well as other conditions associated with proteinuria, such as sepsis [64] and viral infection [65]. Given the involvement of αvβ3 integrin in other pathological processes, including various forms of cancer [23, 46], the potential applications of this strategy may extend beyond kidney disease.

The preliminary safety profile of PhICOSL-19 further supports the feasibility of this therapeutic approach. PhICOSL-19 peptide did not induce detectable podocyte cytotoxicity or T-cell activation, and antibody reactivity was comparable between control and PhICOSL-19-treated animals. Antibody reactivity detected in both groups may reflect pre-existing anti-PEG antibodies [66, 67]. Because PEG-containing compounds are widely present in plastics, detergents, foods, and ointment formulations, environmental exposure may contribute to pre-existing anti-PEG reactivity, and exposure to highly PEGylated molecules has also been reported to induce antibody production [68]. Although more extensive long-term toxicology and immunogenicity studies will be required, these findings provide initial evidence that PhICOSL-19 retains its desired biological activity without detectable cytotoxic or immunostimulatory effects.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The present study was designed primarily to establish proof-of-concept therapeutic efficacy rather than to optimize dosing, pharmacokinetics, or formulation. Only a single dose of PhICOSL-19 was evaluated, and dose–response relationships and dosing frequency were not systematically examined. In addition, the BTBR ob/ob efficacy study was conducted with a relatively limited sample size, and confirmation of these findings in larger, adequately powered cohorts will be important. All peptide treatments were administered intravenously, and alternative routes of administration will need to be evaluated for long-term therapy. Because CKD is typically managed with combination therapy rather than a single agent, future studies should also determine whether PhICOSL-19 provides additive or complementary benefit when combined with established therapies, including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), tacrolimus, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, GLP-1RAs, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and other clinically relevant treatments [69]. Such combinations may be particularly attractive because PhICOSL-19 directly targets αvβ3 integrin-mediated podocyte injury, whereas currently available therapies largely act through distinct immunologic, metabolic, or hemodynamic pathways. Finally, although our studies focused primarily on podocyte αvβ3 integrin, other renal cell types, as well as infiltrating immune cells, may express αvβ3 integrin during kidney injury and could also be targeted by PhICOSL-19. Further studies will be required to define the contribution of these additional cellular targets to its therapeutic effects.

In summary, this study establishes that the noncanonical αvβ3 integrin-regulatory activity of ICOSL can be isolated from the full-length immune molecule and recapitulated within a short ICOSL-derived peptide while remaining functionally distinct from canonical ICOSL immune signaling. Pharmacological optimization by PEGylation preserved this activity and enabled meaningful protection against established glomerular injury across mechanistically distinct immunologic and metabolic models, with a favorable preliminary safety profile. Together, these findings provide proof-of-concept evidence for converting an endogenous podocyte-protective mechanism into a pharmacologically tractable therapeutic strategy and establish a new approach for directly targeting αvβ3 integrin-mediated proteinuric kidney disease.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Conditionally immortalized mouse and human podocytes [39, 40] were cultured and differentiated as previously described [39, 40, 70]. Podocytes were maintained at 33 °C for proliferation and differentiated at 37 °C for 10–14 days. Unless otherwise indicated, experiments were performed using differentiated podocytes. Human podocytes were cultured in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, 1 X insulin-transferrin-selenium, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Mouse podocytes were cultured on collagen I-coated flasks in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and recombinant mouse IFN-γ (10 U/mL) at 33 °C, and differentiated at 37 °C in medium containing 2% FBS without IFN-γ. Human renal mesangial and glomerular endothelial cells (ScienCell) were cultured in the manufacturer-recommended media at 37 °C. Jurkat T cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR studies were performed using a Biacore T200 as previously described [28]. Full-length hICOSL [28] and custom-synthesized hICOSL-19, PhICOSL-19, and scrambled control peptides (GenScript) were immobilized on CM5 sensor chips by standard amine coupling. Human or mouse integrins were perfused at concentrations of 0–160 nM in HEPES buffer containing 2 mM MnCl2. For competition studies, αvβ3 integrins were preincubated with cRGDfV (30 μM). Sensorgrams were double-referenced, and binding kinetics and equilibrium affinity constants were determined using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

In situ proximity ligation assay (PLA)

PLA was conducted utilizing the Duolink in Situ Orange Mouse/Rabbit kit (Sigma Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MnCl₂-treated differentiated human podocytes and de-identified frozen kidney biopsy specimens from individuals with early diabetic nephropathy (n = 7) were analyzed. Biopsy specimens were provided by Dr. David Cimbaluk, Rush University Medical Center, and their retrospective use was approved by the Rush University Medical Center IRB (no. 14051401). Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked, and incubated overnight with rabbit anti-ICOSL [28] and mouse anti-β3 integrin antibodies (10 μg/mL each). Matched normal IgGs served as negative controls. PLA probe ligation and amplification were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and signals were visualized by confocal microscopy.

Podocyte adhesion assay

For direct adhesion assays, hICOSL-19, PhICOSL-19, full-length hICOSL, vitronectin, or BSA were used to coat 384-well plates. Differentiated human or mouse podocytes were pretreated with the indicated peptides, cRGDfV, anti-β3 antibody, control IgG, or vehicle, followed by MnCl2 (250 μM), and then seeded onto coated plates for 2 h at 37 °C. Nonadherent cells were removed, and adherent cells were fixed, DAPI-stained, imaged, and quantified. Competitive adhesion assays were performed on vitronectin-coated wells following pretreatment with hICOSL-19, PhICOSL-19, cRGDfV, or vehicle. Relative adhesion was expressed as a percentage of maximal adhesion to vitronectin. Detailed concentrations are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Western blot

Human podocytes were pretreated with vehicle, hICOSL-19 (100 μg/mL), PhICOSL-19 (100 μg/mL), or cRGDfV (10 μg/mL) for 20 min and then plated on vitronectin-coated plates in the presence of MnCl2 (250 μM) for 45 min. Total FAK and phosphorylated FAK (Tyr397) were detected by Western blot using specific antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology). Signals were visualized using a LI-COR Odyssey system, quantified using ImageJ, and expressed as the phospho-FAK/total FAK ratio normalized to vehicle-treated controls.

Animals

Icosl-KO mice backcrossed onto the BALB/c background [71] were obtained from Dr. Alison Finnegan (Rush University Medical Center, Chicago, Illinois, USA). BALB/c, BTBR ob/ob, and BTBR WT mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Rush University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol no. 23–066). Mice were housed in the animal facility at Rush University Medical Center under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Animal rooms were maintained at approximately 72 °F and 45% relative humidity. Sample sizes were chosen based on previous experience and publications in our laboratory. Animals within each experiment were age- and sex-matched, and the number of animals used in each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legends. Animals were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation or isoflurane anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation, according to the approved IACUC protocol, and tissues were subsequently collected for analysis. For all in vivo efficacy studies, PhICOSL-19 was administered at 500 μg/kg body weight. This dose was selected for initial proof-of-concept studies based on our previous efficacy studies using full-length ICOSL [28] and published dosing ranges of integrin-targeting peptides in mice [72, 73].

In vivo evaluation of PhICOSL-19 therapeutic efficacy

PhICOSL-19 efficacy was evaluated in three complementary models of proteinuric kidney disease. In the acute passive anti-GBM nephritis model, 15-week-old female Icosl-KO mice received nephrotoxic serum (NTS; 1 mL/kg, IV) followed by PhICOSL-19 or saline at 1 and 24 h after challenge. Proteinuria was assessed daily for 2 days. In the chronic active NTS model, 10-week-old female BALB/c mice were pre-immunized with sheep IgG in complete Freund’s adjuvant and challenged with NTS 4 days later. Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was measured before treatment to confirm proteinuria, after which mice were randomized to daily IV PhICOSL-19 or PEGylated mutant control peptide (500 μg/kg) for 5 days. Proteinuria and renal histopathology were assessed 1 day after the final treatment. In the metabolic kidney disease model, 5-week-old male BTBR ob/ob mice with established proteinuria were randomized to daily IV PhICOSL-19 or PEGylated mutant control peptide (500 μg/kg) for 6 weeks. UACR and 24-h urinary albumin excretion (UAE) were assessed after treatment, and kidneys were collected for histopathological analysis.

Proteinuria measurements

Proteinuria was assessed using spot urine samples in the passive and active NTS models and 24-h metabolic-cage urine collections in BTBR ob/ob mice. Urinary albumin and creatinine were measured using commercial assays, and UACR was calculated as urinary albumin divided by urinary creatinine. For 24-h collections, UAE was calculated from urinary albumin concentration and total urine volume.

Histology

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded kidneys were sectioned at 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS). Glomerular injury was scored in 20–50 glomeruli per sample using a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 4 based on hypercellularity, matrix expansion, sclerosis, and crescent formation. Histological evaluation was performed by Dr. David Cimbaluk, Director of the Renal Pathology Laboratory at Rush University Medical Center.

Electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy was performed as previously described [28]. Kidney samples were fixed, post-fixed with osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, embedded in Epon resin, sectioned, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before imaging. Three mice from each peptide-treated group and two naïve control mice were analyzed, with three glomeruli examined per mouse and multiple images obtained per glomerulus.

In vivo safety and immunogenicity

Eight- to nine-week-old WT BALB/c mice (female, n = 5; male, n = 5) received PhICOSL-19 peptide (500 μg/kg) by daily tail-vein injection for 4 weeks, while age-matched untreated WT BALB/c mice (female, n = 5; male, n = 5) served as controls. Body weight was monitored at baseline and at 2 and 4 weeks. At study completion, splenocytes were analyzed by flow cytometry for T-cell activation using CD45, CD3, and CD69, and serum was analyzed for antibodies reactive to PhICOSL-19 using an indirect ELISA. Anti-PEG and anti-ICOSL antibodies served as positive controls. Serum antibody reactivity was compared between PhICOSL-19-treated and untreated mice.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed utilizing Prism 10.2.1 software unless stated otherwise (GraphPad). Significance was determined with a threshold of p ≤ 0.05, denoted as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001, and referenced accordingly in the figure legend. T-tests were employed to compare differences between two groups. Differences among multiple groups were assessed using one-way ordinary or two-way ANOVA according to each experiment. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD).
