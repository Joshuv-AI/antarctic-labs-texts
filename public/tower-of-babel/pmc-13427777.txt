Dexmedetomidine versus propofol for sedation in awake neurosurgery: respiratory and hemodynamic safety
Authors: Charlotte Levé, Fabrice Vallée, Sylvie Aubrun, Séverine Levavasseur, Mickael Rajaona, Kanssaa El Ayeb, Emmanuel Mandonnet, Cyril Touchard, Joaquim Mateo, Jérôme Cartailler, Jona Joachim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMonitored anesthesia care (MAC) with continuous sedation for awake craniotomy allows rapid recovery but increases risks of hypoventilation and hypoxemia. In April 2019, our center replaced propofol-based sedation with dexmedetomidine-based protocols to improve respiratory safety. We evaluated the impact of this protocol change on respiratory and hemodynamic outcomes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective before-after observational study analyzed all adult patients undergoing awake craniotomy between April 2017 and July 2021. The propofol group (April 2017-March 2019) received propofol-remifentanil sedation; the dexmedetomidine group (April 2019-July 2021) received dexmedetomidine-based sedation with supplemental low-dose propofol. Primary endpoints were hypoxemia (SpO2 < 92%) and hypercarbia (PaCO2 and transcutaneous PCO2). Secondary endpoints included hemodynamic stability and adverse events.ResultsWe included 103 patients (51 propofol, 52 dexmedetomidine). Desaturation below 92% occurred more frequently in the propofol group (40% vs. 17%, p = 0.04). PaCO2 levels were significantly lower with dexmedetomidine across all sedation periods (period 1: 52 ± 0.9 vs. 64 ± 2 mmHg, p < 0.0001; period 2: 52 ± 1 vs. 66 ± 2 mmHg, p = 0.0002; period 4: 48 ± 0.8 vs. 51 ± 1 mmHg, p = 0.03). Mean arterial pressure was higher and less variable in the dexmedetomidine group (p < 0.01). Blood loss exceeding 500 mL was more common with propofol (41% vs. 19%, p = 0.027). Mapping conditions were excellent in both groups (96%).ConclusionsIn this single-center retrospective study, transitioning from propofol-based to dexmedetomidine-based sedation for awake craniotomy was associated with improved respiratory and hemodynamic safety.Keywords: Awake craniotomy, Dexmedetomidine, Procedural sedation, Per operative hemodynamic, Neuro-anesthesia

Introduction

Awake craniotomy is the gold standard for low-grade glioma resection, enabling cortical mapping to preserve functional areas while optimizing tumor removal [6, 9]. This procedure requires specialized anesthetic management maintaining hemodynamic stability, controlling ventilation to prevent hypoxemia, and ensuring patient comfort through management of pain, anxiety, and nausea/vomiting. Despite widespread adoption, no standardized anesthetic protocols exist. Two main techniques are commonly used: monitored anesthesia care (MAC) with continuous sedation and spontaneous ventilation, and asleep-awake-asleep (AAA) with general anesthesia. Both have proven effective [7, 16], with technique selection depending primarily on institutional experience [2].

Our center uses MAC with continuous sedation, which enables rapid recovery and superior hemodynamic stability compared to AAA, but increases risks of hypoventilation and hypoxemia [5]. Hypnotic drug selection significantly influences these adverse events. Dexmedetomidine, an alpha-2 agonist, demonstrates comparable efficacy to propofol while better preserving spontaneous ventilation [17]. In drug-induced sleep endoscopy, dexmedetomidine causes less airway obstruction and desaturation with superior hemodynamic stability across sedation depths [14]. Additionally, dexmedetomidine provides anxiolysis and improves pain management [23].

In April 2019, our center replaced propofol-based sedation with dexmedetomidine-based protocols to improve respiratory safety while maintaining comfort.

Current evidence regarding dexmedetomidine in awake craniotomy remains conflicting. Several studies support its use based on favorable safety profiles, particularly reduced upper airway obstruction and respiratory adverse events [8, 11]. However, two recent meta-analyses found no significant benefit, especially in awake craniotomy [1].

We designed this retrospective study to evaluate the impact of our protocol change on respiratory and hemodynamic stability.

Methods and materials

Study design

This single-center retrospective observational study analyzed data from all adult patients undergoing awake craniotomy between April 2017 and July 2021 at Hôpital Lariboisière, Paris, France. In April 2019, our center’s standard sedation protocol changed from propofol-based (“Pro” group, April 2017-March 2019) to dexmedetomidine-based (“Dex” group, April 2019-July 2021) for clinical reasons unrelated to this study. This before-after design examines the association between protocol change and patient outcomes. Data were recorded through automated clinical systems and analyzed retrospectively.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

Human Ethics approval was obtained from the French Ethics Committee for Research in anesthesia and intensive care (CERAR) under IRB number 00010254–2025-020 in February 2025, with waiver of informed consent in accordance with Helsinki Declaration. Patients were notified by mail and could opt out. Data were extracted from the hospital Patient Data Management System (IntelliSpace Critical Care and Anaesthesia-ICCA, Philips, Eindhoven, The Netherlands).

Inclusion criteria were age > 18 years, elective awake craniotomy for brain tumor, absence of severe comorbidities, and no opposition to data collection.

Anesthesia protocol

All patients were evaluated preoperatively by the anesthesiologist, nurse (hypnosis session), and speech therapist. All procedures were performed by the same neurosurgeon (EM).

In both groups, standard monitoring included five-lead ECG, SpO2, invasive arterial blood pressure, urinary catheter with temperature sensor. Airway patency was monitored clinically (visual chest excursion) and via thoracic impedance signal. Hematosis monitoring included arterial blood gases and non-invasive gas exchange assessment by transcutaneous PCO2 (PtcCO2, TCM5, Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark) also used to prevent bradypnea. The PtcCO2 sensor was placed at the clavicle, heated to 42 °C, with a 6 mmHg metabolic constant applied [13]. Sedation depth was assessed using the Observer Assessment of Alertness/Sedation scale (OAA/S, range 0–5).

Propofol and remifentanil were administered via target-controlled infusion (TCI) with effect-site targeting; dexmedetomidine was given by continuous infusion. Pain management included remifentanil TCI, intravenous paracetamol, scalp and temporal muscle blocks with 0.2% ropivacaine. After craniectomy, the surgeon performed dural infiltration with 0.2% ropivacaine.

In the Pro group, propofol targets were 1–4 mg/kg/h. In the Dex group, dexmedetomidine loading dose was 1 μg/kg over 20 min, followed by 0.1–1 μg/kg/h infusion, with low-dose propofol added as needed.

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was maintained > 70 mmHg or > 80% of baseline using dilute noradrenaline. Hypoxia was treated with high-flow nasal oxygen (AIRVO 2, Fisher & Paykel) and oropharyngeal airway, chin-lift or jaw-thrust maneuvers as needed. Osmotherapy (mannitol 20%, 0.25 g/kg) was administered before dural opening when indicated. Anti-epileptic medications were continued; intraoperative seizures were treated with cortical cold saline. Antiemetic prophylaxis included dexamethasone (8 mg) and ondansetron (4 mg).

Surgical periods

Four distinct periods were defined:—Period 1 (preparation): Light sedation (OAA/S 2–3) for line placement, then awakening for positioning—Period 2 (incision to craniectomy): Deep sedation (OAA/S 1–2)—Period 3 (awake mapping): Full alertness (OAA/S 5) for neuropsychological testing and brain mapping—Period 4 (resection and closure): Light to deep sedation (OAA/S 2–3).

Data collection and endpoints

Data extracted included patient demographics (age, sex, weight, height), comorbidities, ASA score, tumor location and histology, preoperative symptoms, drug dosages (propofol, remifentanil, dexmedetomidine), vital signs (SpO2, PtcCO2, MAP, heart rate, temperature), arterial blood gases (PaCO2), fluid balance, procedure duration, mapping quality, tumor resection extent, and neurological deficits.

Adverse events included desaturation, need for intubation, antihypertensive or vasopressor use, atropine administration, bradycardia, bleeding, transfusions, seizures, intraoperative pain or nausea/vomiting, and postoperative pain assessed using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) ranging from 0 to 10.

The primary endpoint was respiratory homeostasis, comprising hypoxemia (SpO2 < 92%) and hypercarbia (elevated PaCO2 and PtcCO2). Secondary endpoints included hemodynamic stability (MAP and heart rate variability across periods) and adverse events.

Statistical analysis

Data were anonymized and analyzed using R (version 3.2.3) and Prism (version 8). Continuous variables are reported as median (IQR) or mean ± SD and compared using Student’s t-test after Shapiro–Wilk normality testing. Categorical variables are reported as n (%) and compared using Chi-square test. Variability in PaCO2, MAP, and heart rate were calculated as the difference between maximum and minimum values across all periods.

Results

Population and tumoral characteristics

Over the study period, 103 patients were included, with 51 in the Pro group from April 2017 to April 2019 and 52 in the Dex group from April 2019 to July 2021. The cohort mainly consisted of young patients (44 [34.5–55.5] years), and there was no significant age difference between the Dex (46 [33.75—56.25] years) and Pro groups (41 [35.5—54.5] years) (p = 0.6). The population was 40% female, with no difference between the two groups, and no major comorbidities; all patients had an ASA score of 1 or 2, with a similar distribution between the groups. The main preoperative symptom was the occurrence of an epileptic seizure n = 58 (56.3%), and the lesion was a glial tumor in 85% (n = 86) of cases, with 16% (n = 16) being high-grade and 68% (n = 70) low-grade. The most common tumor location was frontal (48% of cases), with a higher distribution in the Dex group (n = 30, 58%) compared to the Pro group (n = 19, 38%), followed by temporal location in 22% of cases.

Intraoperative data

The mean total operative time was 425 ± 8 min across the population, with 429 min in the Dex group and 421 min in the Pro group (no difference in duration, p = 0.6). The mean wake-up time before mapping was significantly longer in the Dex group compared to the Pro group (13 ± 2 vs. 8 ± 0.5 min, p = 0.02). Regarding intraoperative medications, total remifentanil consumption was significantly lower in the Dex group (836 µg) than in the Pro group (1137 µg, p = 0.002). Median propofol consumption was also lower in the Dex group (597 mg vs. 1360 mg, p < 0.0001). Mapping conditions were rated as good in 96% of cases, with no significant difference between the Dex and Pro groups.

Primary end point: intraoperative haematosis assessment

Desaturation below 92% was observed in 27% (n = 28) of patients and occurred more frequently in the Pro group (40%, n = 19) compared to the Dex group (17%, n = 9; p = 0.04). The maximum PaCO2 level reached during sedation was significantly lower in the Dex group across all sedation periods, as shown in Fig. 1A: period 1: 52 ± 0.9 vs. 64 ± 2 mmHg, p < 0.0001; period 2: 52 ± 1 vs. 66 ± 2, p = 0.0002; and period 4: 48 ± 0.8 vs. 51 ± 1, p = 0.03. The variability of PaCO2 values was significantly lower in the Dex group compared to the Pro group, with an amplitude of variation of 7 ± 5 mmHg versus 18 ± 12 mmHg, respectively (p < 0.001). Non-invasive PtcCO2 measurements were significantly different only in period 2, with a lower level in the Dex group (60 ± 2 vs. 74 ± 3, p < 0.003), as illustrated in Fig. 1B. A good correlation was observed between PaCO2 and transcutaneous PtcCO2 measurements with r2 > 0.5 in all periods. Secondary endpoints

Hemodynamic stability

The mean MAP was significantly higher in the Dex group compared to the Pro group across all periods under sedation (Fig. 2A). The use of vasopressors to control blood pressure did not differ significantly between the two groups, being required in approximately 50% of patients (p > 0.9). Hypertension during the awakening phase, necessitating antihypertensive treatment, was observed only in the Pro group, affecting 12% of patients (n = 6, p = 0.03). The heart rate was consistently lower in the Dex group across all four periods (Fig. 2B), with bradycardia (HR < 45 bpm) occurring more frequently, though not significantly (8% in the Dex group vs. 2% in the Pro group, p = 0.370), and without the need for atropine treatment. The variability of MAP and HR values was significantly less pronounced in the Dex group. The amplitude of variation was significantly lower across all periods for MAP (18 ± 10 mmHg vs. 25 ± 9 mmHg, p = 0.001) and HR (17 ± 9 vs. 22 ± 12 bpm, p = 0.02) in the Dex group compared to the Pro group.

Adverses events

In the overall cohort, the most common adverse event was blood loss exceeding 500 ml, occurring in 30% of patients (n = 31). Epileptic seizures occurred in 4% of patients, nausea/vomiting in 12%, pain requiring treatment in 11%, and postoperative neurological deficits in 22%. When comparing the two groups, blood loss over 500 ml was more frequent in the Pro group than in the Dex group (n = 21 (41%) vs. n = 10 (19%), p = 0.027). There was no significant difference between groups in the occurrence of epileptic seizures or nausea/vomiting; however, postoperative pain intensity was higher in the Pro group, with a median VAS score of 2/10 compared to 0/10 in the Dex group (p = 0.028).

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study evaluated respiratory and hemodynamic effects following a sedation protocol change for awake neurosurgical tumor resection. The dexmedetomidine-based protocol was associated with fewer episodes of hypoxemia and significantly lower CO2 levels throughout the procedure.

Our findings align with several studies demonstrating dexmedetomidine’s respiratory safety. Goettel et al. [8] found respiratory adverse events (mostly obstructive apnea) occurred more frequently with propofol-remifentanil than dexmedetomidine (20% vs 0%, p = 0.021). Other studies confirmed similar safety profiles [11].

However, recent meta-analyses present conflicting evidence. Viderman et al. [21] found no significant difference in intraoperative adverse events between propofol and dexmedetomidine, though surgeon satisfaction was higher with dexmedetomidine. Akavipat et al. [1] similarly found no significant differences in conversion to general anesthesia (RR 0.45, 95% CI 0.05–3.83) or respiratory complications (RR 0.4, 95% CI 0.12–1.27), though intraoperative seizures were more common with non-dexmedetomidine protocols (RR 4.26, 95% CI 1.49–12.16).

Regarding studies in healthy volunteers, the results do not show any superiority of dexmedetomidine on respiratory function. Although increasing concentration of dexmedetomidine was associated with minimally changed respiratory variables and no effect on hematosis [4], another study on airway collapsibility found significative modification similar to propofol at moderate and high level of sedation and suggest no specifical “protective effect” of dexmedetomidine [12].

In contrast, several clinical studies in drug-induced sleep endoscopy studies show dexmedetomidine’s benefits with lower level of upper airway obstruction compare to propofol [3, 10, 20]. A randomized-control study in patients with moderate sleep apnea who underwent surgery under sedation and regional anesthesia showed fewer respiratory adverse events under dexmedetomidine compared to propofol [15]. In practice, surgical patients experience moderate to intense nociceptive stimuli that is absent in healthy volunteers. The arousable sedative effect of dexmedetomidine might explain this reduced impact on respiratory function, since the level of consciousness can fluctuate more frequently. Our cohort maintained deeper sedation (OAA/S 1–3) than other awake craniotomy series (OAA/S 2–4) [8, 11]. This practice enhances patient comfort, though this increases respiratory and hemodynamic risks. Despite deep sedation, obstructive sleep apneas were easily treated, CO2 levels remained acceptable with dexmedetomidine, awakening was rapid, brain relaxation and mapping conditions were good.

Hemodynamically, dexmedetomidine provided superior stability. Mean arterial pressure was higher during sedation and remained controlled during awakening, whereas 12% of propofol patients required antihypertensive treatment during awakening. Heart rate was lower and less variable with dexmedetomidine without adverse effects. This stability likely limits blood loss and preserves cerebral perfusion, consistent with findings by Molina and al. [17] and Tian and al meta-analysis [19]. Other adverse events, such as pain, nausea/vomiting, and seizures, occurred at rates similar to previous reports [11]. Seizures were rare due to continued anti-epileptic medications. Higher blood loss occurred primarily in the propofol group (41% vs 19%, p = 0.027) and may reflect hypertensive event during awake period or hypercarbia’s effects, though osmotherapy administration was unchanged. It should be noted that blood losses are often difficult to assess and this observation needs to be reevaluated in a dedicated study.

Our study has important limitations. As a before-after design, results are subject to temporal confounding. Improvements in the dexmedetomidine group may reflect the protocol change, but could also result from increasing team experience, evolving surgical techniques, or unmeasured changes in patient selection or management. Observed improvements should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects; randomized trials are needed to establish causality. Additionally, respiratory rate, anxiety levels, pain intensity during awakening, and patient satisfaction were not systematically collected. High level of sedation and multiple sedation adjuncts (remifentanil) may have compounded adverse events.

Transcutaneous CO2 monitoring showed good correlation with arterial measurements, consistent with prior studies [22], and may reduce need for blood gas analysis while enabling intracranial pressure management through capnia control. However, this method does not detect apnea and must be paired with respiratory rate monitoring or end-tidal CO2 to identify obstructive apnea promptly.

Dexmedetomidine represents a valuable alternative for deep procedural sedation during awake craniotomy, offering superior respiratory and hemodynamic control, key parameters for effective sedation. A recent review highlight dexmedetomidine’s unique profile of “anxiolysis, sedation, and mild analgesia coupled with little or no respiratory depression” for neurosurgical patient [18]. However, enhanced monitoring capabilities, particularly continuous respiratory rate and end-tidal CO2 measurement, remain necessary to ensure safety and prevent apnea in procedural sedation.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective study, transitioning from propofol-based to dexmedetomidine-based sedation for awake craniotomy was associated with improved respiratory and hemodynamic safety while providing very satisfactory working conditions (brain relaxation, mapping conditions). These findings, generated in a real-world clinical context, support the biological plausibility of dexmedetomidine benefits during deep procedural sedation. Prospective randomized trials are warranted to confirm these observations and to disentangle the contribution of individual components of the care bundle.
