Association of uric acid-to-HDL cholesterol ratio and coronary atherosclerotic heart disease in patients with and without CKD
Authors: Chang-Hao Sun, Xin-Yu Zhu, Zhi-Long Wang, Zhen-Lin Liu, Xiao-Tong Wang, Jin-Qiu Wei, Teng-Teng Zhu, Yong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ratio of uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (UHR) is a novel comprehensive indicator related to dyslipidemia. However, the association between UHR and coronary artery disease (CAD) risk in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains unclear.MethodsAfter matching based on age and gender propensity scores, 2124 subjects were included and divided into the CKD group (708 cases) and the non-CKD group (1416 cases). The predictive performance of UHR for CAD was evaluated by the area under the curve (AUC), and the independent association between UHR and the risk of CAD onset was analyzed using a multivariate logistic regression model. The correlation and dose-response relationship between the ratio of uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (UHR) and the risk of CAD were analyzed using LOESS fitting and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis.ResultsAfter matching, the multiple lipid-related indices (Triglycerides (TG), Remnant Cholesterol (RC), Atherogenic Index (AI), Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), Triglyceride Glucose Index (TyG), Lipoprotein Composite Index (LCI), Triglyceride to High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio (TG/HDL-C), Total Cholesterol to High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio (TC/HDL-C), Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol to High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio (LDL-C/HDL-C), UHR) in the CKD group were significantly higher than those in the non-CKD group. The AUC analysis showed that HDL-C, AIP, TG/HDL-C, and UHR had strong predictive performance in the overall cohort and the non-CKD group, while in the CKD group, HDL-C, AI, and TC/HDL-C are better predictive indicators. After adjusting for all confounding factors, multivariate regression analysis revealed that HDL-C, apolipoprotein A-1 (APOA-1), and the APOA-1/APOB ratio were independent protective factors for CAD in the entire cohort. Among them, the protective effect of HDL-C was the most stable (overall population aOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.17–0.39, p < 0.001), and it was significantly in both the CKD (aOR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.09–0.40, p < 0.001) and non-CKD subgroups (aOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.18–0.52, p < 0.001). In CKD, UHR is significantly correlated with CAD (aOR = 6.23, 95% CI: 1.89–20.60, p = 0.003), and the association was more significant in the non-CKD group (aOR = 15.15, 95% CI: 4.20–54.72, p < 0.001). CKD status significantly modified the association between UHR and CAD (P for interaction = 0.015). LOESS fitting suggested that UHR was positively correlated with the probability of CAD occurrence (the correlation was more significant at low UHR, and it slowed down when UHR > 0.5, r = 0.2, p < 0.001), and negatively correlated with eGFR (r = -0.38, p < 0.001). RCS analysis confirmed a significant nonlinear association between UHR and CAD (overall P < 0.001, nonlinear P = 0.002), and the risk of CAD increased when UHR was > 0.41 in CKD patients.ConclusionsUHR is an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease, with higher adjusted OR values and more significant independent risk effects in non-CKD populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05838-1.Keywords: UHR, Coronary artery disease, Lipid, CAG, CKD

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a widespread global health issue and a primary factor contributing to mortality and disability, closely linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Globally, an estimated 788 million (95% uncertainty interval 743–843 million) adults aged 20 years and older were affected by CKD in 2023, resulting in 1.48 million (1.30–1.65 million) deaths [1].

Multiple pieces of evidence indicate that hyperuricemia is closely associated with CKD. In elderly diabetic patients, elevated SUA exhibits a linear correlation with CKD [2]. Uric acid can form monosodium urate crystals in renal tubules and interstitium, directly triggering local inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and fibrosis. Even in the absence of crystal formation, soluble uric acid can enter cells and activate multiple harmful pathways. It stimulates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), leading to vasoconstriction and hypertension; inhibits nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, causing endothelial dysfunction; and directly stimulates intracellular reactive oxygen species production, aggravating oxidative stress and inflammation [3].

These mechanisms are key drivers of atherosclerotic plaque formation. A prospective study in children with CKD revealed that as glomerular filtration rate (GFR) declines, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (HDL-C) levels decrease correspondingly in non-glomerular-origin CKD [4]. Not only that, under inflammatory conditions, HDL-C may even lose their cardioprotective effects and show a tendency toward increased risk of adverse events [5]. This is because dysfunctional HDL accumulates substances such as symmetric dimethylarginine, which disrupt the vascular endothelial glycocalyx via activation of Toll-like receptor 2 and matrix metalloproteinase-9, thereby promoting atherosclerosis and abnormal leukocyte rolling, ultimately exacerbating vascular injury [6].

Recently, the uric acid to HDL-C concentration (UHR) has been reported as a novel positive marker for ischemic heart disease and metabolic syndrome, and has shown better predictive value for moderate coronary artery stenosis than UA or HDL-C alone [7, 8]. Among patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis, higher UHR levels are associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [9]. Hence, we hypothesize that UHR may serve as a superior indicator for the association with coronary artery disease (CAD) in CKD patients. In this study, we aim to investigate the association between UHR and CAD in individuals with chronic kidney disease.

Methods

Study population

From December 2022 to January 2025, a total of 15,215 patients underwent a coronary artery angiography (CAG) examination at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University & Shandong Provincial Qianfoshan Hospital. According to the exclusion criteria: Patients with malignant tumors; Patients with thyroid function disorders (hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism); Acute kidney injury; The complete traditional lipid indicators (TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C) are lacking. After propensity score matching based on age and gender, this study included 2124 hospitalized patients, 708 patients with CKD, and 1416 control subjects. Laboratory assessments

During the collection of clinical data, the research subjects underwent height and weight measurements by professionals after fasting, and their body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on these measurements. Their systolic and diastolic blood pressures were recorded in a resting state. All blood samples were collected from the patients upon admission and before coronary angiography (CAG), and peripheral venous blood was obtained from the patients for sample collection. The samples were stored and transported under low-temperature anticoagulation conditions to ensure that all biochemical tests were completed at the Shandong First Medical University First Affiliated Hospital & Shandong Provincial Qianfoshan Hospital Laboratory Center within 24 h after sampling. The results were obtained using an automatic analysis device (COBAS 8000, Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland).

Meanwhile, the study extracted the clinical and demographic characteristics of the patients from the medical record system, including age, history of hypertension and diabetes, smoking status, as well as relevant laboratory test results, which specifically covered urea nitrogen, creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), UACR, uric acid, HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, bilirubin, and comprehensive lipid indicators: TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, APOA-1, APOB, and small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL).

Definition

CKD: Firstly, patients with professional “renal insufficiency” diagnosis records in their medical records are selected. According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) clinical practice guidelines, any of the following conditions is sufficient to diagnose CKD: (1) The glomerular filtration rate estimated based on the CKD-EPI creatinine formula remains below 60 mL/min/1.73 m². (2) The ratio of urinary albumin to creatinine is higher than 30 mg/g.

This study defines and assesses other related clinical conditions based on the following criteria: Hypertension: refers to a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg at rest, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes: defined as current use of hypoglycemic drugs or insulin, or fasting blood glucose level higher than 7.0 mmol/L. Smoking status: indicates continuous smoking for more than 10 years. Use of lipid-lowering drugs: specifically refers to regular and long-term use of statin drugs. All patients undergo coronary angiography to confirm. Angiography is completed using the Judkins method via the radial artery or femoral artery approach, and the images are independently analyzed by two experienced physicians who are unaware of the study’s groupings. The degree of main coronary artery stenosis ≥ 50% is defined as CAD lesion [10]. The control group consists of patients without typical angina symptoms and with a main coronary artery stenosis rate lower than 50%. CAD severity assessment: quantified using the modified Gensini scoring system, which calculates the score by multiplying the stenosis degree of a specific vessel by its anatomical location weight coefficient [11].

Computational formula

The calculation unit of the composite lipid index is uniformly set as mmol/L. Uric acid concentrations were measured in µmol/L and HDL-C concentrations in mmol/L; to align the units for ratio calculation, uric acid values were converted to mmol/L (1 µmol/L uric acid = 0.001 mmol/L uric acid) prior to computing UHR. The calculation formula is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The following statistical methods were employed to analyze and process the data in this study: For continuous variables that adhere a normal distribution, they were represented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Comparisons among groups were conducted using the independent sample t-test; if the variables did not conform to a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were employed for analysis. For categorical variables, they were represented by frequency and percentage, and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test. To assess whether there is multicollinearity among the independent variables, the variance inflation factors (VIF) of each variable were calculated and presented in Supplementary Table 2. The preliminary analysis revealed that the bilirubin-related indicators (TBIL, DBIL, IBIL) had a complete linear relationship (TBIL = DBIL + IBIL), resulting in a VIF value of 83.643 for TBIL. Additionally, there was a strong correlation among the renal function indicators (BUN, Cr, eGFR). To address the issue of multicollinearity, we excluded DBIL, IBIL, and Cr. After screening, the VIF values of all included variables were less than 5. BMI, Hypertension, Smoke, HR, SBP, DBP, BUN, eGFR, HbA1c, Albumin, TBIL, and Lipid-Lowering Drugs were identified as covariates. For missing data, the specific missing situations of each variable are detailed in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Table 3). To handle the missing covariates, the study employed a multiple imputation method based on the chain equation to reduce selection bias or information loss caused by data missing. This method generates multiple complete data sets and conducts integrated analysis to make the results more representative. It should be noted that variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% were not imputed, so the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was not included in the subsequent statistical analysis. The LOESS fitting analysis was conducted to explore the association between UHR and the predicted probability of CAD as well as eGFR. Firstly, the binary classification results of CAD were transformed into the occurrence probability based on UHR using a logistic regression model. Then, this probability was used as a continuous variable for LOESS fitting, with the smoothing parameter selection as follows: the default smoothing parameter (span = 0.75) was adopted. The continuous variables were non-parametrically fitted using the LOESS algorithm to obtain the smooth trend line. To assess the diagnostic efficacy of the indicators, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated. And the DeLong test was used to compare whether there was a significant difference in the area under the ROC curve between the two groups. Multivariate Logistic regression analysis was used to explore the association between lipid parameters and the risk of coronary artery disease, and the four-node restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to test whether there were nonlinear relationships between the variables. In the RCS analysis, we selected four nodes, which are located at the 5th percentile, the 35th percentile, the 65th percentile and the 95th percentile of the UHR distribution. Based on the multivariate logistic regression model, the UHR×CKD interaction term was included (Wald test) to evaluate the effect modification. Subgroup analysis was conducted to calculate the OR and 95% CI. The interaction plot was drawn using the interactions package. The results were expressed as the adjusted odds ratio (OR) corresponding to each standard deviation increase in lipid indicators and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI).

All analyses utilized R software (version 4.4.1) and SPSS statistical software (version 27). A difference was deemed statistically significant when the P value was below 0.05.

Results

After propensity score matching, a total of 2,124 subjects were included in the analysis. The overall cohort had a mean age of 63.79 ± 11.80 years and included 1,513 males. Between the 708 patients with CKD and the 1,416 non-CKD subjects, no significant differences were observed concerning age, sex, proportion of smokers, or systolic blood pressure. As shown in Table 1, patients with CKD exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and CAD, along with higher heart rate, BUN, creatinine, UA, HbA1c, FBG, and Gensini score. Regarding lipid profiles, CKD patients had significantly higher levels of TG, RC, AI, AIP, TyG, LCI, TG/HDL-C ratio, TC/HDL-C ratio, LDL-C/HDL-C ratio, and UHR.

The association between various lipid parameters and the risk of incident CAD, following adjustment for multiple covariates, is presented in Table 3. The multivariate logistic regression model was adjusted for BMI, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, HR, SBP, DBP, BUN, creatinine, eGFR, albumin, TBIL, DBIL, IBIL, and the use of lipid-lowering drugs. The analysis identified HDL-C, APOA-1, and the APOA-1/APOB ratio as significant protective factors against CAD across all cohorts. Among these, HDL-C demonstrated the most robust and consistent protective effect, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 0.26 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.17–0.39, p < 0.001) in the overall population. This association remained strong within both the CKD (aOR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.09–0.40, p < 0.001) and non-CKD (aOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.18–0.52, p < 0.001) subgroups. Conversely, the uric acid to HDL-C ratio (UHR) emerged as a significant independent risk factor, showing a positive association with CAD risk. This relationship was particularly pronounced in individuals without CKD, where a markedly elevated risk was observed (aOR = 15.15, 95% CI: 4.20–54.72, p < 0.001). It indicates that for every 1 unit increase in UHR, the risk of CAD occurrence increases by a factor of 15.15. A sensitivity analysis was conducted for Non-CKD, including re-fitting the Logistic regression model after excluding the top 5% of UHR distribution and the bilateral 5% of the samples (Supplementary Table 4). The results showed that although the OR value slightly decreased, it still maintained statistical significance (after excluding the unilateral 5%, OR = 14.09, 95% CI: 3.12–63.56,p = 0.001; after excluding the bilateral 5%, OR = 10.13, 95% CI: 2.07–49.62,p = 0.004)), indicating that the association result between UHR and CAD was relatively robust. Furthermore, the results of the interaction test indicate (Fig. 2) that the CKD status has a significant effect-modifying effect on the association between UHR and CAD (P for interaction = 0.015).

Test for interaction between CKD status and UHR on CAD risk

According to the LOESS fitting analysis, Fig. 3 demonstrates significant associations between subjects’ UHR and both the predicted association of CAD and eGFR. It can be observed that as UHR values increase, the probability of CAD occurrence shows a gradual upward trend. The LOESS fitting curve indicates that when UHR is less than 0.5, the slope below it becomes steeper. When UHR exceeds 0.5, the upward trend slows down but remains weakly positively correlated (r = 0.2, p < 0.001). With increasing UHR values, eGFR values continue to decline, suggesting that UHR may be associated with decreased renal function (r = -0.38, p < 0.001).

Loess curve fitting of UHR for CAD and eGFR in the total population

Figure 4 displays the results of the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, indicating a statistically significant overall association (P < 0.001) and a significant nonlinear relationship (nonlinear P = 0.002) between UHR levels and outcome events. As the UHR level increased, the fitted OR values exhibited a nonlinear upward trend. Unlike the overall cohort, in CKD and non-CKD patients, the OR values increase linearly, with the risk ratios for each group being CKD: OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.07–2.84, P = 0.003; Non-CKD: OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.34–2.51, P < 0.001; and the overall effects of both groups are statistically significant. Additionally, in non-CKD patients, the risk of coronary artery disease begins to increase when UHR only increases to 0.28 (OR = 1), while for CKD patients, the risk of coronary artery disease increases when UHR rises to 0.41.

Discussion

In this clinical study, we evaluated the association between UHR and CAD in CKD patients. In this cross-sectional study, we found that UHR was significantly higher in CKD patients. Moreover, UHR demonstrated better predictive ability for CAD compared to other lipid indicators in both the overall and non-CKD populations. Furthermore, after adjusting for confounding factors, elevated UHR emerged as the most significant risk factor for CAD, with this risk being more pronounced in the non-CKD population. Additionally, the association between UHR and CAD exhibited a consistently non-linear strengthening pattern. Notably, our results indicated that CKD increases the threshold at which UHR affects CAD risk and attenuates the pathogenic effect of UHR.

The uric acid-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (UHR) represents a promising novel lipoinflammatory marker that integratively encapsulates three interwoven pathological pathways driving atherogenesis: oxidative stress, HDL functional dysfunction, and systemic low-grade inflammation [3, 12, 13]. Unlike a simple composite metabolic ratio reflecting only hyperuricemia and reduced HDL-C concentrations, UHR captures the net biological balance between pro-lipoinflammatory stimuli and impaired anti-atherogenic defense in the vasculature. Uric acid acts as a potent pro-oxidative and pro-inflammatory mediator, triggering reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, and promoting endothelial dysfunction by upregulating pro-atherogenic cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) [3]; conversely, HDL-C exerts critical cardioprotective effects beyond reverse cholesterol transport, including antioxidant activity, suppression of leukocyte adhesion to the vascular endothelium, and modulation of systemic inflammatory responses [12]. In the setting of metabolic dysregulation, HDL particles undergo qualitative dysfunction—including oxidative modification and loss of cholesterol efflux capacity—abrogating their anti-inflammatory properties and even converting them into pro-atherogenic moieties [14]. In CKD patients, pathological conditions such as uremic toxicity, chronic inflammation, and dyslipidemia may amplify these processes, creating synergistic effects that accelerate the progression of atherosclerosis and CAD [15–17]. An analysis based on the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) revealed that CKD patients with concurrent hyperuricemia had a 65% higher risk of developing ischemic heart disease compared to those without these conditions, further substantiating the compounded harm of their coexistence [17]. Notably, UHR integrates uric acid and HDL-C into a single indicator, demonstrating superior association strength compared to individual measurements. For instance, the Kailuan study revealed that cumulative UHR outperformed baseline UHR in the association with CKD progression and cardiovascular events, with adjusted hazard ratios of 1.484 (CKD presence) and 1.643 (low eGFR) in the highest quartile group [16], suggesting that prolonged elevation of UHR serves as a significant driver of cardiovascular complications in CKD patients. The clinical value of UHR is manifested not only in risk stratification but also in its capacity to improve existing association models. After incorporating it into the Framingham Risk Score (FRS), the ability to discriminate 10-year cardiovascular risk in patients with metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) was significantly enhanced, with a nonlinear threshold effect observed [18]. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that UHR had stronger predictive power in males and obese individuals, potentially related to sex hormone-mediated differences in uric acid metabolism and obesity-associated insulin resistance [15, 18]. This evidence supports integrating UHR monitoring into routine cardiovascular risk assessments for CKD patients to enable early intervention through urate-lowering therapy or HDL-C modulation. However, the causal relationship between UHR and CAD in CKD patients remains unclear currently. Future Mendelian randomization studies may clarify whether UHR is a potentially modifiable risk factor or merely a marker of metabolic dysfunction [19]. Additionally, more clinical research is needed to validate the effects of UHR-lowering interventions (such as xanthine oxidase inhibitors or HDL-C-elevating therapies) on CAD outcomes in CKD patients [20, 21].

Our most intriguing finding—that CKD attenuates the association strength of UHR with CAD and elevates the UHR threshold required for CAD risk elevation—can be mechanistically contextualized by CKD’s role as a systemic inflammatory amplifier within the bidirectional cardio-renal inflammatory continuum [22–25]. CKD is a multifaceted disorder defined not only by impaired renal excretory function but also by a state of persistent chronic low-grade inflammation, excessive oxidative stress, and profound qualitative HDL dysfunction—hallmark features that redefine the biological meaning of traditional lipid-based metrics such as UHR [6, 14, 26]. In the uremic microenvironment of CKD, HDL particles undergo extensive oxidative modification, lose their cholesterol efflux capacity (CEC), and accumulate pro-inflammatory moieties (e.g., symmetric dimethylarginine), which directly abrogate their anti-inflammatory and anti-atherogenic properties and even convert HDL into a pro-atherogenic particle [6, 14]. This decoupling of quantitative HDL-C levels from qualitative HDL function blunts the lipoinflammatory signal encoded by UHR in CKD patients. Furthermore, the pervasive systemic inflammation inherent to the cardio-renal inflammatory continuum creates a “background inflammatory load” that drives atherogenesis independent of traditional metabolic lipid markers [27], obscuring the incremental predictive value of UHR for CAD risk. This phenomenon parallels well-documented observations in other high-risk metabolic populations where traditional lipid metrics incompletely capture vascular damage due to disease-specific metabolic and inflammatory remodeling. In patients with type 2 diabetes, for example, the SCORE2-Diabetes risk assessment model—tailored to the unique metabolic, inflammatory, and vascular context of diabetes—has been shown to far better identify preclinical vascular injury than general population risk scores [28], highlighting how disease-specific pathophysiological contexts reshape the expression, interpretation, and predictive value of cardiovascular risk markers. Therefore, our findings emphasize the need for cautious interpretation when applying UHR for CAD risk stratification in the CKD population. This suggests that future risk assessment requires a paradigm shift from single-biomarker approaches to multimodal integrated models. A more promising strategy would be to combine UHR with biomarkers that directly reflect cardiac stress and injury (such as NT-proBNP and hs-cTnI) as well as functional HDL metrics (like CEC) [14, 26, 27]. Through this approach, we may overcome the current limitations of risk association and provide more precise management strategies for this high-risk population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study, although multiple confounding factors were adjusted for, residual confounding may still influence the results, and a direct causal relationship between UHR and CAD cannot be inferred. Second, the single-center design and single UHR measurement may limit the generalizability of the conclusions and the reflection of long-term dynamic risk changes. Third, although CAD diagnosis was based on clinical criteria, misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis may still exist, and the study did not thoroughly investigate the contributions of specific functional subtypes of uric acid and HDL-C. Finally, due to the missing rate of urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) exceeding 20%, it was not included in the final analysis. This might result in the underdiagnosis of CKD cases with normal eGFR but elevated albuminuria, which could affect the association between UHR and CAD. The true impact of CKD on the relationship between UHR and CAD might be biased. Future studies should include albuminuria data to verify our findings.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, the UHR demonstrated a stronger independent association with the presence of CAD compared to other conventional lipid parameters. The risk of CAD exhibited a nonlinear increasing trend with elevated UHR levels. Furthermore, while UHR itself showed a certain association strength with CAD, this association strength was significantly attenuated in the presence of CKD.
