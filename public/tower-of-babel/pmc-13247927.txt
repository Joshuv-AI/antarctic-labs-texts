Stress MRI assessment of partial anterior cruciate ligament injury based on three-dimensional analysis
Authors: Wojciech Klon, Bogusław Sadlik, Marcin Domzalski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate diagnosis of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries, particularly partial tears, remains paramount for effective clinical management. This study investigates the diagnostic potential of stress magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) employing a dedicated device to induce anterior translation, conducted twice with the device positioned alternately under the thigh and under the tibia. The analysis involved a comprehensive three-dimensional (3D) assessment of displacement factors to correlate the injury type with the actual findings in the arthroscopic examination. The study aims to determine if 3D displacement analysis via stress MRI can precisely localize and characterize specific ligament bundles affected by ACL injuries, correlating these findings with arthroscopic examination results.MethodsThirty patients with ACL injuries and 17 controls with acute knee pivoting injuries less than 3 weeks old underwent stress MRI examinations. Results were compared against arthroscopic findings to evaluate diagnostic accuracy.ResultsDistinctive 3D displacement patterns were observed for various ACL injuries. Partial tears of the anteromedial bundle were characterized by “2x Lateral Displacement + External Rotation” (P=0.048), yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.2. Conversely, injuries to the posterolateral bundle (PLB) were indicated by “Lateral Displacement + 3x Internal Rotation” (P=0.047), achieving an AUC of 0.82, sensitivity of 85%, and specificity of 75%. The utilization of 3D displacement analysis enabled precise localization and characterization of specific ligament bundles affected by injury. The agreement between arthroscopic images and MRI results was assessed using the Sherman, Lo, and Henle scales.ConclusionsThe application of stress MRI allows for distinguishing between injuries to individual bundles in cases of partial ACL damage. The greatest sensitivity and specificity in diagnosis were observed in the group where PLB injury was identified. This study demonstrates the potential of stress MRI with 3D displacement analysis for accurately diagnosing and characterizing partial ACL tears, thereby improving clinical management of ACL injuries.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), stress magnetic resonance imaging (stress MRI), partial tear, ligament bundles

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries represent a significant challenge in orthopedic medicine due to their prevalence and long-term consequences such as instability and early osteoarthritis (1). Partial ACL tears pose particular diagnostic difficulties because their three-dimensional (3D) nature may preserve morphological continuity despite mechanical dysfunction (2). Clinical evaluation frequently remains inconclusive due to ambiguous laxity patterns.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has become a key diagnostic tool for ACL assessment (3), yet conventional imaging techniques may fail to detect subtle fiber disruption, even in the sagittal-oblique plane (4). tress MRI has gained attention because it allows evaluation under controlled loading, reflecting actual biomechanical behavior and improving the detection of partial tears (5). Technological progress in MRI, including high resolution and 3D analysis, has further enhanced diagnostic accuracy (6).

The ACL is shaped like a ribbon (7), it comprises two main bundles: the anteromedial bundle (AMB) and the posterolateral bundle (PLB), each contributing differently to knee stability (8). Their biomechanical behavior differs throughout the range of motion (ROM). The AMB acts as the primary stabilizer in flexion, restraining anterior translation and influencing rotational control due to its longer and more isometric course (9). In contrast, the PLB tightens in extension and internal rotation, limiting hyperextension and playing a greater role in rotational stability (9). Injury to the AMB typically results in increased anterior tibial translation predominantly in knee flexion, whereas damage to the PLB leads to increased anterior translation in both extension and flexion, along with increased internal and external rotation at 45° flexion (10).

Nevertheless, characterizing the ACL solely as a two-bundle structure oversimplifies its complex 3D fiber architecture, which provides multidirectional support beyond a simple mechanical model (9). The ligament’s tensile strength reaches 2,160 N (11), but its rotational course weakens resistance in twisting trauma. With a length of 21–35 mm and a cross-sectional area of only 31 mm2, forces are distributed over a small structure, making it the most frequently ruptured ligament in the human body (12).

Partial ACL injuries may involve up to 75% of fibers, yet still appear continuous on arthroscopic examination (2). Fiber elongation during trauma may reach 107% of the original length (13), causing functional instability despite preserved morphology. Additionally, vascular impairment at the rupture site can lead to necrosis of previously intact fibers (14) and complete injury to one bundle may result in permanent elongation of the remaining bundle (9).

Both MRI and arthroscopy may produce false-negative findings. Overlapping healthy and damaged fibers may hinder MRI interpretation, while scar tissue adhered to surrounding structures can suggest continuity during arthroscopy (15). Diagnostic criteria therefore rely on combined findings, including asymmetrical Lachman test results (16), negative Pivot-Shift under anesthesia (16), low values in KT-1000 or Rolimeter tests (<3 mm) (17), and identifiable ligament damage on arthroscopic evaluation (2). However, the specificity remains low and diagnostic criteria for partial ACL injuries continue to be ambiguous.

Given the biomechanical complexity and diagnostic limitations, improving functional assessment methods for partial ACL injuries is crucial. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze a comprehensive 3D assessment of displacement factors to correlate the injury type with arthroscopic findings. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2498/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted in Bielsko-Biała from November 2018 to September 2019 after obtaining IRB approval from the Ethics Committee of the Beskid Medical Chamber in Bielsko-Biała (Komisja Bioetyczna Beskidzkiej Izby Lekarskiej w Bielsku-Białej; approval No. 2017/02/16/5). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 175 patients after single knee injuries were initially enrolled. Inclusion criteria for the study were: age between 18–60 years old, body mass index (BMI) <30 kg/m2, acute knee pivoting injury (less than 3 weeks), positive Lachman or Pivot-Shift test, adequate ROM of the knee to perform MRI examination. Exclusion criteria included systemic and chronic disorders, claustrophobia, previous injuries to the index knee (including any injury that required medical evaluation or treatment), inability to undergo MRI examination (e.g., metal implants, pacemakers), lateral collateral ligament (LCL) or posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries, and joint hypermobility. Joint hypermobility was assessed using the Beighton score, with a score of ≥5 indicating hypermobility.

Among patients diagnosed with ACL rupture (mean time from injury 7.5±18 days), 30 underwent expedited arthroscopic ACL reconstruction or augmentation, with ACL rupture confirmed intraoperatively (reference standard). The control group comprised 17 participants without ACL injury based on high-resolution MRI interpreted by an experienced musculoskeletal radiologist and supported by clinical examination. Arthroscopy was not performed in controls due to the absence of clinical indication. Although this introduces potential differential verification bias, the high diagnostic accuracy of MRI combined with clinical assessment reduces the likelihood of misclassification (Figure 1).

Variables

In this study, we focused on a range of independent and dependent variables to assess the diagnostic potential of stress MRI with 3D displacement analysis for ACL injuries.

Independent variables

Participant characteristics

The study included various characteristics of the participants such as age, gender, and the specific knee that was injured. Additionally, the time since the injury occurred was documented, with all injuries occurring within three weeks prior to the examination.

MRI examination parameters

During the MRI examination, several key parameters were standardized. The knee flexion angle was set to 30 degrees and verified using a goniometer to ensure consistency. An anterior force of 80 N was applied to the calf, controlled using a calibrated air pump system and measured with a pressure gauge. The position of the Arthroholder device was also a critical parameter, ensuring that the force was applied perpendicular to the tibia.

Dependent variables

3D displacement parameters

The primary outcomes measured were the 3D displacement parameters, including lateral displacement, medial displacement, anterior displacement, posterior displacement, internal rotation, and external rotation. These were quantified in millimeters for displacements and degrees for rotations, providing a detailed assessment of the knee’s response to the applied forces. The values were obtained based on the landmark registration process explained below.

Arthroscopic findings

The presence and type of ACL injury were determined during arthroscopy. Detailed assessments, including damage to individual ACL bundles, Sherman (18), Lo (19), Henle (20), and Crain (21) scales, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the ligament’s condition.

Diagnostic accuracy metrics

The diagnostic accuracy of the stress MRI was evaluated using several metrics. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to assess the overall diagnostic performance. Sensitivity, the ability of the test to correctly identify patients with ACL injuries, and specificity, the ability to correctly identify patients without ACL injuries, were also determined. The diagnostic accuracy of stress MRI will be evaluated in comparison with both standard MRI and arthroscopy, with each modality considered as the gold standard.

Potential confounders and effect modifiers

Several potential confounders and effect modifiers were considered in this study to ensure the reliability of the results. Patient-related factors such as previous knee injuries, regular physical activity or sports participation, and BMI were noted as they could influence the knee’s response to stress and the outcomes of the MRI. Technical factors, including the calibration of the MRI machine and the experience of the MRI technician, were also considered, as variations could impact the quality and consistency of the imaging results. Furthermore, measurement-related factors, such as the accuracy of point marking on MRI images and the performance of software algorithms for landmark registration and displacement calculation, were acknowledged. Agreement of assessments was calculated to ensure consistency.

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnostic criteria for ACL injuries were clearly defined for both MRI and arthroscopy. For MRI, the criteria included visualization of the ACL, assessment of its continuity or disruption, abnormal signal intensity within the ACL for each bundle, and associated findings like bone contusions or meniscal tears (22). Arthroscopic criteria involved direct visualization of the ACL during surgery, confirmation of ligament integrity or identification of tears for each bundle, and the use of standardized scales (Sherman, Lo, Henle, Crain) for detailed ligament assessment.

By thoroughly defining and measuring these variables, this study aimed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the diagnostic potential of stress MRI with 3D displacement analysis for ACL injuries. The careful consideration of potential confounders and effect modifiers, along with the use of standardized diagnostic criteria, helped to ensure the robustness and accuracy of the findings.

Stress MRI examination

The term stress MRI comprises two parts of MRI Examination: standard MRI followed by MRI with applied force to produce tibia transformation (translation and rotation).

All subjects underwent MRI using the Arthroholder prototype (IDD Medical Startup, Poland). This device stabilizes the knee and applies force to the calf, perpendicular to the tibia, with force magnitude controlled via a calibrated pneumatic system.

After placing the Arthroholder on the MRI table, knee flexion was set to 30° and confirmed with a goniometer. The limb was secured at 75° relative to the ground using a wide strap attached to the base. The first scan was performed with the knee resting in the device, without contact between the tibial support and the skin. Subsequently, without repositioning the patient, a second scan was acquired while applying an 80 N anterior force to the calf. The force vector was oriented at 75° to the ground (Figure 2). This load was generated by a piston connected to a pump and regulated by a calibrated pressure gauge.

Positioning of the Arthroholder device before examination: 1, hinge for adjusting the angle of the thigh; 2, thigh-locking strap; 3, air piston; 4, magnetic resonance imaging coil.

The MRI protocol for analysis was based on axial and oblique sagittal planes aligned with the ACL. All sequences were acquired in proton density (PD) weighting using a Philips Ingenia 3.0T system with a dStream Flex L coil (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Point marking and registration

Point marking and registration procedures were conducted using specialized software and techniques. The PD weighted sagittal and axial series were examined utilizing Osirix 10.0 software (Pixmeo, Switzerland). Landmarks were identified and marked according to a predefined set (6). Each examination involved marking six points (Figure 3).

Landmark registration points (black dots) overall 3D view.

Following marking, the selected points underwent redefinition utilizing Slicer 4.10 Landmark Registration with small region SimpleITK v4.13.3 automatic registration algorithms developed by the Insight Software Consortium in the USA. The coordinates of these points, based on the XYZ coordinate system, were exported for subsequent analysis.

Points exported from MRI examinations conducted without force application served as reference points, while those exported from MRI examinations conducted with force application were considered the aligned set of points.

Subsequently, the femoral points were registered and precisely aligned with each other, enabling the accurate calculation of tibial translation in the following step. To accomplish this, proprietary software developed in JavaScript using the ThreeJS framework version 146dev was employed (Figure 4). The software conducted calculations based on the singular value decomposition (SVD) algorithm. It provided continuous control over translation based on the MRI image and allowed for visualization of the displacement on a 3D model of the tibia. The obtained data of translation in millimeters and rotation in degrees underwent further analysis. The average deviation for translations using MRI is ±0.86 mm and for rotations ±0.95°, with very high agreement [intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) ~0.96–1.00] (6).

A graphical interface based on JavaScript and ThreeJS is used for the calculation and evaluation of tibial displacement and rotation after registration, specifically designed for StressMRI following alignment of the thigh. Calculation of combined parameters

In order to find the optimal parameter for assessing damage to individual bundles, parameters of translation and rotation were combined, performing simulations aimed at determining the most effective parameter for each bundle’s damage assessment. The visualization model was presented (Figure 5).

Composite kinematic parameters were investigated to capture relationships between instabilities across different planes and axes. This approach is biomechanically justified, as joint instability is inherently multidirectional and mechanically coupled, with translational and rotational components typically occurring together rather than in isolation.

Because the optimal combinations of parameters are not established, this study had an exploratory objective: to identify combinations most strongly associated with injury to specific ligament bundles. Simple summation with equal weighting was used as a heuristic approach, providing a way to combine heterogeneous parameters. Translational (mm) and rotational (°) components were summed directly without normalization to a common scale, reflecting the chosen methodology for emphasizing raw contributions of each motion type.

Among the large number of tested combinations, the optimal parameters were selected based on the strongest statistical association with bundle-specific injury.

Evaluation of scales in MRI examination

The MRI images were evaluated by two experienced orthopedists and one radiologist to assess the extent of the ACL injury using Sherman (18), Lo (19), Henle (20), and Crain (21) scales.

Arthroscopy

All patients in the study group underwent subsequent arthroscopy. During arthroscopy, the extent of the ACL injury was reassessed using the Sherman, Lo, Henle, and Crain scales by two orthopedic surgeons who performed all procedures. During the arthroscopic examination, each patient was examined under general anesthesia in the supine position with the knee placed in a leg holder. The examination was performed with the knee flexed between 20 and 90 degrees.

Statistical analysis

For repeatable measurements, the arithmetic mean and standard error of the measurement were calculated. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Lilliefors test. In cases where normal distribution was not observed, results were presented by describing the median and interquartile range. The Wilcoxon test was utilized for dependent group comparisons, while the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for independent group comparisons. Correlations were calculated using the Spearman rank correlation test. Significance of paired measurements for interval scales was determined using the Cohen’s Kappa test, and for nominal scales, the Cohen’s Kappa test for significance was applied. Agreement of assessments was assessed using the (ICC. Sensitivity and specificity tests were determined based on the ROC curve. All calculations were performed at a confidence level of P<0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted using PQStat v1.6.6 software (PQStat Software, Poland).

Bias

Selection bias

Patients were selected based on recent rotational knee injuries (within 3 weeks), which may not represent all ACL injury cases. Exclusion criteria led to the elimination of patients with PCL injuries or technical difficulties during MRI, potentially introducing selection bias. Additionally, patients with ACL injuries who had a negative physical examination were not included in the study.

Measurement bias

Differences in MRI interpretation could introduce bias; using multiple software tools (Osirix, Slicer) helps mitigate this. Inter-surgeon variability in arthroscopic assessment was addressed by using standardized scales and involving multiple surgeons.

Performance bias

The precision of the Arthroholder device and the MRI machine could vary, impacting the consistency of force application and image quality. Calibration and standardization protocols were followed to minimize these effects.

Results

The mean age of patients in the study group was 37±9.8 years, comprising 17 right knees, 13 left knees, 22 males, and 8 females. A total of 29 patients have a positive Lachman test, and 20 have a positive Pivot-Shift test. The control group had a mean age of 36±9.4 years, including 8 right knees, 9 left knees, 12 males, and 5 females. A minor meniscal tear was observed in four patients, and a low-grade medial collateral ligament (MCL) tear in two patients. Due to the minor severity of the injuries, these patients were not excluded from the study. All participants provided informed consent.

Basic differences were observed between the ACL injury group and the control group for parameters such as anterior translation (P=0.002) and internal rotation (P=0.005); however, this analysis was not the main subject of this publication. Control group

In the control group, no ACL injuries were observed, and no types of injuries were noted on the Sherman, Lo, Henle, or Crain scales; therefore, no differential analysis was performed.

Discussion

Our study shows that stress MRI allows better visualization of isolated posterolateral ACL bundles injuries with a sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 75%. In all cases of partial ACL tears, anterior translation was observed, but the amount of the translation did not allow differentiation between two types of AMB and PLB lesions. Through a comprehensive analysis of transformation parameters derived from MRI data, we were able to identify significant differences in the displacement and rotation patterns associated with injuries to the AMB and the PLB of the ACL. This highlights MRI as a non-invasive tool for assessing the integrity of specific ACL bundles, thereby aiding in treatment planning and prognosis determination.

The strongest evidence for differentiation between AMB and PLB type injuries was found in the PLB ACL injury group. In this group an increase in internal rotation and lateral displacement of the tibia were observed with an AUC value of 0.8 which describes highest sensitivity and specify at level of 85% and 75%, respectively. Previously published studies has shown lower sensitivity (at a level of 25–67%) and specificity (50–64%) for this type of ACL lesion (8,23).

In the case of anterior-medial bundle (AMB) injury, an increase was observed in the combination of lateral displacement and external rotation, with an AUC of 0.2, which was statistically significant, but the sensitivity and specificity for this finding were modest.

The observed transformations: increased external rotation in AMB injury and internal rotation and lateral displacement in PLB injury were in accordance to previously published biomechanical studies (9,24), where different rotation patters were observed, based on each bundle injuries. It also has been observed that hyperextension and internal rotation place the PLB at greater risk for injury (9).

An essential component of knee assessment is its rotation relative to individual axes. It is a challenging parameter to assess and capture during conventional knee joint examination. However, it is worth considering that the use of calculation methods allows for a detailed assessment of rotation parameters, which play a significant role in assessing knee instability (25). In particular, internal rotation has a significant impact on secondary damage to joint structures in patients with PLB injuries (26). The commonly used Pivot Shift test, which indirectly assesses the rotation parameter, although it has high repeatability and sensitivity (7) is not a quantitative test. Authors analyzing knee joint biomechanics have repeatedly emphasized the importance of a precise analysis of internal rotation (9,14,17,26), however, the lack of diagnostic devices with low measurement error that do not require patient anesthesia (27) makes effective imaging difficult.

Initially, agreement assessments for ACL injury classification and damage parameter assessments were conducted. However, the agreement parameters of the analyzed classifications were relatively low. The highest agreement values were observed after applying force, particularly in the Henle classification (Sheath Violation), where the Kappa coefficient reached κ=28%. The agreement between classification assessments across methods remained limited, with Kappa coefficients for all not exceeding 50%. This underscores the limited diagnostic value of these classifications for partial ACL injury assessment. Although agreement indices were higher after stress imaging, the assessment of morphology based on the analyzed classifications appears insufficient.

The tibial transformation analysis used 3D algorithms (28), leveraging 4D calculations (29) to simplify movement analysis. Matrix computations (30) enabled 3D displacements to be represented in 2D (31), facilitating relationship identification and basic statistics. However, rotation and translation should ideally be assessed in 3D, as knee joint movement is inherently 3D and involves both rotation and displacement (32). While the 2D model offers a simplified, accurate intermediary, it’s only a partial view of true knee movement (27).

A simplified model was created to evaluate multiple parameters as a single outcome, focusing on three key aspects, such as combining twice the external rotation with one proximal displacement. Of 4,950 calculations, only 14 (0.28%) were statistically significant.

It is also worth noting that the knee assessment considered muscle force, which influences the effectiveness of knee stabilization. Previous biomechanical studies of knee transformation were performed under general anesthesia (27) or with unfixed preparations (1). Muscle force plays a significant role in both the course of ACL injury and the progression of secondary changes (33). Taking into account muscle tension is particularly important in the examination of knees with ACL injuries (34).

The presented results do not allow for the formulation of unequivocal guidelines or diagnostic criteria for cutoff points for the damage to individual ligament bundles. Still, they demonstrate the use of a diagnostic tool that can illustrate the image of ACL damage in terms of 3D transformation. Particularly noteworthy is the clear difference in rotation for AMB and PLB bundle injuries. The results also align with the cited biomechanical concept by DeFranco and Bach (9).

Although the small study group may be considered a limitation of this study, inclusion criteria and a standardized study protocol were applied to reduce overall bias. This approach led to the exclusion of a substantial number of participants—104 out of the initial 175 due to chronic injury duration, with an additional 7 excluded before MRI and 17 afterward. Several P values were close to the 0.05 threshold and should be interpreted with caution. Measurement and performance biases could have influenced the results, although standardized MRI protocols, calibration procedures, and consistent assessment methods were applied to minimize these effects [the average MRI measurement deviation for this method was ±0.86 mm for translations and ±0.95° for rotations, indicating high measurement reliability (6)].

Conclusions

The application of stress MRI allows better visualization for types of partial ACL injuries. In PLB group internal rotation and lateral displacement were observed, while in AMB group external rotation and lateral displacement were noted. Sensitivity and specificity were significantly higher for the PLB group.
