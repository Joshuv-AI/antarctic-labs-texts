Revisit the relationship between spread complexity rate and radial momentum
Peng-Zhang He

Abstract
This article discusses the relationship between the boundary spread complexity rate and the radial momentum in the bulk within the framework of AdS/CFT. We demonstrate that the radial momentum of a freely falling particle, as measured by a stationary observer in the bulk, is equal to the spread complexity rate of the boundary conformal field theory. This relationship has been discussed in two previous works, but their formulations appear distinct. However, this paper demonstrates their consistency. While earlier studies focused on particles with large mass and massless particles, we show that the relationship formally holds for particles of any mass. Additionally, we provide a simple method for obtaining spread complexity from radial momentum using optical geometry.

2
Spread complexity

In this section, we will briefly review the concept of spread complexity
Balasubramanian et al. (2022)
. For a quantum system described by a time-independent Hamiltonian
H
, the evolution of the state
\left|\psi(t)\right>
satisfies the Schrödinger equation

i\partial_{t}\left|\psi(t)\right>=H\left|\psi(t)\right>.

(1)

The solution of this equation is

\left|\psi(t)\right>=e^{-iHt}\left|\psi(0)\right>=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}\left|\psi_{n}\right>,

(2)

where
\left|\psi_{n}\right>=H^{n}\left|\psi(0)\right>
. The evolution of the state
\left|\psi(t)\right>
over time can always be described by a space spanned by
\{\left|\psi_{n}\right>\}
. By employing the Lanczos algorithm

Viswanath and Müller (1994)
, we can obtain an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{K}=\{\left|K_{n}\right>:n=0,1,2,\cdots\}
for this space, sometimes call Krylov basis. This basis satisfies

\displaystyle\left|K_{0}\right>=\left|\psi(0)\right>,

(3)

\displaystyle\left|A_{n+1}\right>=(H-a_{n})\left|K_{n}\right>-b_{n}\left|K_{n-1}\right>,\qquad\left|K_{n}\right>=b^{-1}_{n}\left|A_{n}\right>.

(4)

Lanczos coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are defined by

a_{n}=\left<K_{n}\right|H\left|K_{n}\right>,\qquad b_{n}=\left<A_{n}|A_{n}\right>^{1/2},

(5)

and
b_{0}\equiv 0
. The Lanczos algorithm (
4
) demonstrates that

H\left|K_{n}\right>=a_{n}\left|K_{n}\right>+b_{n+1}\left|K_{n+1}\right>+b_{n}\left|K_{n-1}\right>.

(6)

We can expand
\left|\psi(t)\right>
in this basis as follows

\left|\psi(t)\right>=\sum_{n}\psi_{n}(t)\left|K_{n}\right>.

(7)

Spread complexity is defined as

C_{K}(t)=\sum_{n}n|\psi_{n}(t)|^{2}.

(8)

For more detail, it is recommended to refer to
Balasubramanian et al. (2022)
.

3
Spread complexity and radial momentum

Recently, in
Caputa et al. (2024a)
and
Fan (2024)
, it has been independently proposed that the spread complexity on the boundary is equal to the radial momentum in the bulk in the framework of AdS/CFT. However, the formulas conjectured by the two articles seem to be different. In this section, we will restate these two articles and demonstrate that they are consistent.

3.1
Spread complexity and the radial momentum of massive particles

In
Caputa et al. (2024a)
, the author considers the evolution of locally excited states by primary operators in holographic CFTs. The excited state is given by

\left|\psi(t)\right>=\mathcal{N}e^{-iHt}e^{-\epsilon H}\mathcal{O}(x_{0})\left|\psi_{\beta}\right>,

(9)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant,
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature,
\mathcal{O}(x_{0})
is primary operator with scaling dimension of
\Delta
. Here
x_{0}
is the spatial position.
\left|\psi_{\beta}\right>
is a thermofield-double state or vacuum state. In (
9
),
\epsilon
represents an ultraviolet cutoff, which ensures that the energy is finite. This can be seen from the following operation

\displaystyle\left|\psi\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\mathcal{N}e^{-iHt}e^{-\epsilon H}\left|n\right>\left<n\right|\mathcal{O}(x_{0})\left|\psi_{\beta}\right>=\sum_{n}\mathcal{N}e^{-\epsilon E_{n}}e^{-iHt}\left|n\right>\left<n\right|\mathcal{O}(x_{0})\left|\psi_{\beta}\right>,

(10)

where
\left|n\right>
is the eigenstate and
E_{n}
is the eigenvalue of the energy. Due to the presence of
e^{-\epsilon E_{n}}
, the contributions from states with very high energy are suppressed and
\left|\psi\right>
has finite energy

E_{\text{CFT}}=\int dx\left<\psi(t)|T_{00}(x)|\psi(t)\right>=\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon}.

(11)

Such states have already been studied in AdS/CFT
Nozaki et al. (2013)
. The evolution of state (
9
) corresponds to placing a static particle at the asymptotic boundary of AdS at the initial time, which undergoes free fall in the bulk.

Consider the simplest case, which is
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, implying that
\left|\psi_{\beta}\right>=\left|0\right>
. This is a vacuum state on a plane, and its dual description is given by Poincaré AdS
3

ds^{2}=\frac{-dt^{2}+dx^{2}+dz^{2}}{z^{2}}.

(12)

Here, the AdS radius has been set to be
1
. At
t=0
, inserting the operator
e^{-\epsilon H}\mathcal{O}(x_{0})
corresponds to placing a particle with zero velocity at
x=x_{0},z=\epsilon
. For
\Delta\gg 1
and
\epsilon\ll 1
, the mass of the particle is
m\simeq\Delta
. For such a particle, it follows a timelike geodesic in the bulk

z(t)=\sqrt{t^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}.

(13)

This geodesic can be derived from the particle action

S=-m\int dt\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}}\equiv\int dt\mathcal{L}

(14)

through variation. The radial momentum is:

P_{z}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{z}(t)}=\frac{mt}{\epsilon\sqrt{t^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}}.

(15)

However, this result is different from the spread complexity rate of CFT. Specifically, at finite temperature, the spread complexity is
Caputa et al. (2024a)

C_{K}(t)=\frac{\Delta\beta^{2}}{2\pi^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\pi t}{\beta}\right),

(16)

and its rate is

\dot{C}_{K}(t)=\frac{E_{0}}{\epsilon}\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\sinh\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\beta}\right).

(17)

For
\beta\rightarrow\infty
,

C_{K}(t)=\frac{E_{\text{CFT}}t^{2}}{2\epsilon},\qquad\dot{C}_{K}(t)=\frac{E_{\text{CFT}}}{\epsilon}t.

(18)

This is clearly different from (
15
). Reference
Caputa et al. (2024a)
indicates that by letting
z=e^{-\rho}
, then

P_{\rho}=-\frac{m}{\epsilon}t\Rightarrow\dot{C}_{K}(t)=-\frac{P_{\rho}}{\epsilon}.

(19)

P_{\rho}
is called proper momentum. The metric used to calculate this radial momentum is

ds^{2}=d\rho^{2}+e^{2\rho}(-dt^{2}+dx^{2}).

(20)

\dot{C}_{K}(t)=-\frac{P_{\rho}}{\epsilon}
is the main conclusion of
Caputa et al. (2024a)
, for a more detailed discussion in various cases, see
Caputa et al. (2024a)
.

3.2
Spread complexity and the radial momentum of massless particles

Section
3.1
follows the approach of
Caputa et al. (2024a)
and discusses the spread complexity in the context of a massive particle in AdS space freely falling from the asymptotic boundary at zero velocity, where the spread complexity rate is exactly the proper momentum of this particle. Another article
Fan (2024)
discusses the radial momentum of a massless particle starting from the asymptotic boundary. The author argues that the spread complexity in the boundary field theory is directly equal to the radial momentum observed by the stationary observer
2
2

2

A stationary observer is referred to as an observer whose worldline coincides with the integral curves of the Killing vector field
Wald (1984)
;
Liang and Zhou (2023)
. For any observer, an proper coordinate system can be defined to record events near the worldline. If the spacetime is four-dimensional, this coordinate system can be determined by the observer’s worldline and a tetrad along the worldline.
in the bulk for this massless particle

\frac{dC_{K}}{dt}=P_{r}.

(21)

Their verification is quite straightforward and simple; this section will reorganize the process.

The specific procedure is to determine
P_{r}
and then integrate over time
t
along the geodesic of the massless particle. If the outcome aligns with
C_{K}(t)
on the boundary, then the conjecture is satisfied. Consider the general static spherically symmetric metric in a
d
-dimensional spacetime

ds^{2}=g_{tt}dt^{2}+g_{rr}dr^{2}+\cdots=-h(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+\cdots

(22)

We have omitted the coordinates other than time and radial, which does not affect the discussion. These omitted coordinates are collectively denoted as
\{y^{\alpha}\}
.
Assume the tangent vector of the massless particle’s worldline, or wave vector of the photon, is
K^{a}
. Its dual vector satisfies

K_{a}=-\mathcal{E}(dt)_{a}+\mathcal{P}_{r}(dr)_{a},

(23)

and

0=K^{a}K_{a}=g^{tt}(-\mathcal{E})^{2}+g^{rr}\mathcal{P}_{r}^{2}\Rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{r}=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{h(r)f(r)}},

(24)

where
\mathcal{E}
is invariant along the geodesic of photon
Townsend (1997)
. Assume
p
is a point between the asymptotic boundary and the event horizon of the black hole (if it exists), and there is a stationary observer
G
at this point. This observer can record the radial momentum of the photon in his/her proper coordinate system. In the problem we are considering, we only need to find such a proper coordinate system, the basis vectors of which are
Z^{a}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\right)^{a}
and
N^{a}=\lambda\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{a}
, and we are not concerned with the other basis vectors.
\tau
is the proper time of the observer and
\lambda
is normalization constant. One can check that

Z^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g_{tt}}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{a},\qquad N^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{rr}}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{a},

(25)

and

K^{a}=\alpha Z^{a}+\beta N^{a}.

(26)

\alpha
is the energy of the photon as measured by the observer, and
\beta
is the radial momentum of the photon as measured by the observer. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}\equiv\alpha=-Z^{a}K_{a}=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{h(r)}},

(27)

\displaystyle P_{r}=\beta=N^{a}K_{a}=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{h(r)}}.

(28)

The energy of CFT is the energy measured by the stationary observer on the asymptotic boundary, taking the metric of the AdS spacetime as

ds^{2}=-r^{2}dt^{2}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}dr^{2}+\cdots.

(29)

Using (
27
), we get the energy of CFT

E_{0}\equiv\mathscr{E}|_{\text{boundary}}=\epsilon\mathcal{E},\qquad\epsilon=\frac{1}{r}.

(30)

It is worth noting that the boundary CFT energy
E_{\text{CFT}}
defined in Section
3.1
(which scales as
\Delta/\epsilon
) is mathematically equivalent to the bulk conserved energy
\mathcal{E}
of the massless particle in Section
3.2
. For the remainder of this paper, we will adopt the notation convention consistent with Fan
Fan (2024)
, where
\mathcal{E}
represents the bulk conserved energy and the holographic complexity relations are evaluated accordingly. If the geodesic of the photon is
r=r(t)
and

C_{K}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{r}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{h(r(t^{\prime}))}}dt^{\prime}

(31)

is satisfied, our task is completed. Due to the existence of a timelike Killing vector field, we can easily rewrite the metric into ingoing coordinates

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=-h(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+\cdots

(32)

\displaystyle=-h(r)\left[dt^{2}-\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)h(r)}\right]+\cdots

\displaystyle=-h(r)\left(dt^{2}-dr_{\ast}^{2}\right)+\cdots

\displaystyle=-h(r)dv^{2}+2\sqrt{\frac{h(r)}{f(r)}}dvdr+\cdots,

where
v=t+r_{\ast}(r)
and
r_{\ast}=-\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{\sqrt{h(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})}}
is the tortoise coordinate. The geodesic of an ingoing photon is given by
v=\text{constant}
. Without loss of generality, let
v=0
be the geodesic we consider for the photon. Then we have

t+r_{\ast}(r)=0\Rightarrow\frac{dt}{dr}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{h(r)f(r)}}.

(33)

The integral in (
31
) can be written as

\int_{0}^{t}P_{r}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}=\int_{r(t)}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{f(r^{\prime})}h(r^{\prime})}.

(34)

As a simple example, consider the AdS vacuum (
29
), we get
h(r)=f(r)=r^{2}
and

r_{\ast}=-\frac{1}{r}.

(35)

The geodesic
t+r_{\ast}(r)=0
becomes

r=\frac{1}{t}.

(36)

Using (
31
) or (
34
), we get

\int_{0}^{t}P_{r}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}=\frac{\mathcal{E}t^{2}}{2}=\frac{E_{0}t^{2}}{2\epsilon}.

(37)

Comparing with (
18
), this is precisely the spread complexity
C_{K}(t)
on the boundary.

3.3
Discussion on consistency

In fact, the expressions in
Caputa et al. (2024a)
and
Fan (2024)
are completely consistent with each other. When calculating the radial momentum of photon, (
28
) is actually derived from the transformation relationship of vector components between two coordinate systems

A^{\prime}_{\mu}=\frac{\partial x^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}}A_{\nu},

(38)

where
A_{\mu}=A_{a}(dx^{\mu})_{a},A^{\prime}_{\mu}=A^{a}(dx^{\prime\mu})_{a}
. If we re-express
\mathcal{P}_{r}
as
P^{\prime}_{r}
, according to (
28
) and (
38
), we have

P^{\prime}_{r}=\sqrt{g_{rr}}P_{r}\Rightarrow dr^{\prime}=\frac{dr}{\sqrt{f(r)}}.

(39)

Therefore,
P_{r}
is actually the radial momentum in the following metric

ds^{2}=-h(r)dt^{2}+dr^{2}+\cdots.

(40)

Such radial momentum is precisely the proper momentum discussed in
3.1
(with a definitional difference of a factor
\epsilon
). Equation (
19
) differs from equation (
21
) by a negative sign, which arises from the use of the transformation
z=e^{-\rho}
instead of
z=e^{\rho}
when deriving (
19
). In principle, both transformations are permissible, and the difference in the negative sign is avoidable.

3.4
The proper momentum of a particle of any mass is the spread complexity rate

Reference
Caputa et al. (2024a)
discusses the case of particles with large mass. This section will use the method from Reference
Fan (2024)
to examine the radial momentum of particles with any mass value. When
t=0
, the particle is placed on the asymptotic boundary, and our results will reproduce the previously discussed outcomes. This indicates that for massive particles, regardless of their mass, the radial momentum can always determine the spread complexity rate of the boundary CFT.
It is worth pointing out that when the condition
\Delta\gg 1
fails to hold, the particle picture ceases to be valid, yet the geometrical optics approximation remains applicable. Consequently, we can still utilize the geodesic framework to perform the derivation.

Here, we only consider the case where the bulk is a three-dimensional AdS and use the method from Section
3.2
for the calculation. Consider a three dimensional AdS space in the Poincaré coordinate

ds^{2}=\frac{dz^{2}-dt^{2}+dx^{2}}{z^{2}}.

(41)

Assume a particle is placed at the position
z=\epsilon,x_{i}=0
in the bulk at time
t=0
. The geodesic of the particle is
Nozaki et al. (2013)

z(t)=\sqrt{t^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}.

(42)

The momentum of the particle is

P_{a}=-\mathcal{E}(dt)_{a}+\mathcal{P}_{z}(dz)_{a},

(43)

and it satisfies

g^{ab}P_{a}P_{b}=-m^{2}\Rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{z}=\frac{\sqrt{z^{2}\mathcal{E}^{2}-m^{2}}}{z},

(44)

where
m
is the mass of the particle. The time direction and radial basis vectors for the static observer are denoted as
Z^{a}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\right)^{a}
and
N^{a}=\lambda\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{a}
, specifically:

Z^{a}=z\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{a},\qquad N^{a}=z\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{a}.

(45)

Then we have

P^{a}=\rho Z^{a}+\delta N^{a},

(46)

where
\rho
is the energy measured by the observer, and
\delta
is the radial momentum measured by the observer, and

\mathscr{E}\equiv\rho=-Z^{a}P_{a}=z\mathcal{E},\qquad P_{z}\equiv N^{a}P_{a}=z\mathcal{P}_{z}=\sqrt{z^{2}\mathcal{E}^{2}-m^{2}}.

(47)

At
t=0
, the energy of the particle observed by the stationary observer at that time and location is

E_{0}=\epsilon\mathcal{E},

(48)

and the radial momentum (
44
) should be zero, therefore

m=\epsilon\mathcal{E}.

(49)

Substituting back into equation (
47
) yields

\displaystyle P_{z}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{z^{2}\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}-m^{2}}

(50)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(t^{2}+\epsilon^{2})\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}-m^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{mt}{\epsilon}=\frac{E_{0}t}{\epsilon}.

This result aligns with Section
3.1
, but our discussion here applies to particles of
any mass
, whereas Section
3.1
specifically addresses particles with
large mass
. The spread complexity in the dual field theory is:

C_{K}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{mt}{\epsilon}dt=\frac{mt^{2}}{2\epsilon}.

(51)

Similarly, the spread complexity rate for the finite-temperature CFT on the boundary can also be derived from the radial momentum of a particle in gravity, as shown in Appendix
A
.

4
Calculate spread complexity using optical geometry in AdS spacetime

In this section, we will present a method to calculate the spread complexity on the asymptotic boundary of AdS without needing to know the particle geodesics. Considering a general static spherically symmetric spacetime (
22
) and assuming that a photon starts from the asymptotic boundary, we can always consider that there is velocity only in the radial direction in space. Note that a photon travels along null geodesics, and
spatial projection of the null geodesics yields the
light rays, and these are geodesics of the optical metric

Gibbons and Werner (2008)
;
Ono et al. (2017)
.

Since we are not considering photons with velocities in spatial directions other than the radial direction, we can take the coordinates in these directions as constants. Optical geometry is given by
ds^{2}=0

Abramowicz et al. (1988)
, resulting in

dt^{2}=\frac{dr^{2}}{h(r)f(r)}.

(52)

Where
dt
is precisely the affine parameter of the light ray in this Riemannian space of optical geometry. Then we have

C_{K}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{r}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}=-\int_{\infty}^{r(t)}\frac{P_{r}}{\sqrt{h(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}=\int_{r(t)}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{f(r^{\prime})}h(r^{\prime})},

(53)

where we have used (
28
). The negative sign following the second equality comes from the fact that when
dt>0
,
dr
should be less than 0. This result is exactly (
34
).

5
Conclusion

In this article, we have discussed the relationship between the spread complexity on the boundary and the radial momentum in the bulk within the framework of AdS/CFT. The results indicate that in AdS/CFT, the spread complexity rate in the boundary conformal field theory is equal to the radial momentum of particles observed by a stationary observer in the bulk.

Upon re-examining and comparing the correspondence between spread complexity and radial momentum as proposed in references
Caputa et al. (2024a)
and
Fan (2024)
, we have discovered that they are fundamentally consistent, even though their formulations appear to be different. For the case of massive particles, we have demonstrated that regardless of the mass, the spread complexity on the boundary can always be obtained through the radial momentum in the bulk. We also propose a simple method based on optical geometry that allows for the calculation of spread complexity on the boundary of AdS space without the need to know the particle’s geodesic. Our research findings not only deepen our understanding of the AdS/CFT correspondence but also provide new perspectives on the connections between quantum gravity and quantum information.

There are still many questions worth exploring here. If this correspondence truly holds in AdS/CFT, then how should it be proven? AdS/CFT is just one realization of a holographic duality; are there such or similar dualities in other holographic theories?
