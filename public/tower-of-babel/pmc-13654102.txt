Real-time three-dimensional echocardiographic assessment of the association between aortic diameter and left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony in patients with bicuspid aortic valve
Authors: Huan Liu, Xueting Guo, Kunxuan Liu, Qinlin Li, Caiyun Zhang, Kuican Liu, Wenrong Shi, Shanshan Dong, Wenjuan Qin, Lei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is frequently accompanied by aortic dilation (AD), which may impair left ventricular (LV) function. However, research on the relationship between aortic diameter and left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony (LVMD) is limited. This study aimed to investigate the association between the body surface area (BSA)-indexed aortic size index (ASI) and the three-dimensional systolic dyssynchrony index (3D-SDI) as assessed by real-time three-dimensional echocardiography (RT-3DE) in patients with BAV.MethodsThis study retrospectively included 79 patients with BAV and 40 normal individuals. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) was used to measure the internal diameters of the sinus of Valsalva and the ascending aorta in patients with BAV. These measurements were indexed to BSA to calculate the sinus of Valsalva size index (SOVSI) and the ascending aortic size index (AASI), respectively. Patients with BAV were divided into two groups based on the SOVSI and AASI, respectively: the non-dilated group (≤2.1 cm/m²) and the dilated group (>2.1 cm/m²). RT-3DE was used to measure the standard deviation (SD) and maximal difference in the time to minimum systolic volume (Tmsv) across 16, 12, and 6 LV segments. These parameters were expressed as percentages of the cardiac cycle (Tmsv16/12/6-SD% and Tmsv16/12/6-Dif%, respectively). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the associations between the study variables and Tmsv16-SD%. Multiple linear regression analysis was then performed to identify the factors independently associated with Tmsv16-SD%.ResultsCompared with the controls, patients with BAV exhibited LVMD, as indicated by increased Tmsv16-SD%, Tmsv12-SD%, Tmsv6-SD%, Tmsv16-Dif%, Tmsv12-Dif%, and Tmsv6-Dif%. This increase was more pronounced in the dilated group than in the non-dilated group (P<0.001); however, no significant difference in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was observed between these two subgroups. Among patients with BAV, those with dilation of the sinus of Valsalva or the ascending aorta also had lower E/A (E, peak early diastolic mitral inflow velocity; A, peak late diastolic mitral inflow velocity) ratios and higher E/e’ (e’, peak early diastolic mitral annular velocity) ratios. In a multiple linear regression analysis, Tmsv16-SD% was independently associated with SOVSI (β=4.447, P<0.001), AASI (β=3.388, P<0.001), and E/e’ (β=0.727, P<0.001).ConclusionsPatients with BAV exhibit LVMD, which is more pronounced in those with concomitant AD. Both SOVSI and AASI are independently associated with LVMD. The study findings provide further evidence of an association between AD and greater LVMD in patients with BAV.Keywords: Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony (LVMD), real-time three-dimensional echocardiography (RT-3DE), aortic size index (ASI)

Introduction

Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is one of the most common congenital heart diseases {Aliabadi, 2025 #2}(1). A key pathological feature of BAV is aortic dilation (AD), which is often accompanied by abnormal left ventricular (LV) hemodynamic loading and may subsequently affect ventricular function (2). The aortic cross-section may have an elliptical or irregular shape. Because two-dimensional echocardiography is limited by the imaging plane, it usually provides only a single-plane diameter and may therefore underestimate the actual size of the aorta. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) provides high spatial and temporal resolution and enables clear delineation of the anatomical structures of the aorta. Combined with three-dimensional (3D) multiplanar reconstruction (MPR), CCTA enables measurement of the maximal aortic diameter on a standardized cross-sectional plane perpendicular to the long axis of the aorta, thereby improving the accuracy of aortic morphological and quantitative assessment, while overcoming the limitations of two-dimensional echocardiography (3).

In the morphological assessment of the aorta, the traditional method of measuring aortic diameter alone is susceptible to the effects of differences in individual body size. In contrast, the aortic size index (ASI), which standardizes measurements by body surface area (BSA), is recommended by guidelines as a more precise indicator for evaluating aortic remodeling (4). Previous studies have shown that patients with BAV, particularly those with AD, may exhibit impaired LV function (5). However, research focusing on left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony (LVMD) in this population remains limited. LVMD refers to the temporal incoordination of contraction among different myocardial segments of the left ventricle and is a critical factor affecting ventricular contraction efficiency (6).

Real-time three-dimensional echocardiography (RT-3DE) represents an important advancement in non-invasive cardiovascular imaging. This technique enables comprehensive acquisition of full-volume dynamic LV images. Without relying on geometric assumptions, RT-3DE can accurately quantify the three-dimensional systolic dyssynchrony index (3D-SDI), providing an objective and reproducible quantitative measure of systolic synchrony (7). Its clinical utility has been validated in conditions such as heart failure and cardiomyopathies (8,9).

Against this background, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate LVMD in patients with BAV using RT-3DE, investigate the associations of the sinus of Valsalva size index (SOVSI) and the ascending aortic size index (AASI) with 3D-SDI, and identify the factors independently associated with LVMD. These findings may provide additional imaging evidence for the assessment of ventricular function and clinical management of patients with BAV. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0762/rc).

Methods

Patients

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University (No. KJ2025-626-01). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Initially, 79 consecutive patients with BAV who were evaluated at The First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University between June 2025 and January 2026 were enrolled in this retrospective study. All patients met the diagnostic criteria for BAV established by the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) (10). The BAV phenotype was defined according to the classification proposed by Sievers and Schmidtke (11). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) below the normal reference range (<52% for males, <54% for females); (II) comorbidities, including coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, cardiomyopathy, other congenital heart diseases, or severe pulmonary disease; (III) a history of open-heart surgery; (IV) poor-quality echocardiographic images that precluded reliable measurement or analysis; and/or 5) arrhythmia during the examination. Among the 96 patients with BAV initially screened, 12 (12.5%) were excluded because of suboptimal image quality, and 5 (5.2%) were excluded because of arrhythmias during the examination. Ultimately, 79 patients with BAV were included in the final analysis. Based on previous studies, patients with BAV and an ASI >2.1 cm/m² were allocated to the dilated group, while those with an ASI ≤2.1 cm/m² were allocated to the non-dilated group (12). During the same period, 40 age- and sex-matched healthy individuals were enrolled as controls.

Measurement of the diameters of the aortic sinus and ascending aorta

Prospective electrocardiography-gated computed tomography (CT) scanning was performed using a second-generation dual-source CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens, Germany). The scanning range extended from approximately 2 cm above the carina to just above the diaphragm, excluding the entire aortic arch. Tube voltage was selected according to the patient’s body mass index (BMI): 80 kV for BMI ≤20 kg/m², 100 kV for 20 kg/m² <BMI <30 kg/m², and 120 kV for BMI ≥30 kg/m². The individualized tube current-time product ranged from 330 to 400 mAs. Automatic bolus tracking was applied with the region of interest placed in the ascending aorta. The trigger threshold was set at 150 Hounsfield units, followed by a 3-second delay before scanning.

The main scanning parameters were as follows: detector width, 0.6 mm, gantry rotation time, 280 ms per rotation, pitch, 0.20–0.50, slice thickness, 0.6 mm, and slice interval, 0.4 mm. The field of view for the original image reconstruction was 200–250 mm. The following three-phase contrast medium injection protocol was used: 50–60 mL of undiluted contrast medium (iopromide injection, iodine concentration 370 mg/mL, Bayer), followed by 30 mL of a contrast medium-normal saline mixture (30:70 ratio), and 30 mL of normal saline as a chase bolus. The injection flow rate was 4–5 mL/s for all phases.

Image postprocessing was performed on a GE ADW 4.6 workstation (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). MPR was performed using the cardiac module to display cardiovascular structures and their spatial relationships in multiple planes and orientations. Cardiac images were reconstructed across 0–100% of the R-R interval, and end-diastolic images (70–75% of the R-R interval) were used for diameter measurements to ensure stability. The diameters of the aortic sinus and ascending aorta were measured at the level of the maximum inner diameter of the sinus of Valsalva and at the level of the ascending aorta at the pulmonary artery bifurcation, respectively (Figure 1).

Two radiologists, with 5 and 7 years of experience, respectively, independently measured the diameters at the two specified levels three times each in a blinded manner. The mean of the two radiologists’ measurements was used for the final analysis. The sinus of Valsalva size index (SOVSI) and AASI were calculated as follows: SOVSI = aortic sinus diameter/BSA; AASI = ascending aortic diameter/BSA.

Echocardiography

All patients underwent comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography using a Philips EPIQ CVx ultrasound system (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA) equipped with S5-1 and X5-1 transducers, with a frequency range of 1–5 MHz. Examinations were performed according to the recommendations of the ASE (13), with patients in the left lateral decubitus position and simultaneous electrocardiographic recording. Echocardiographic data from four consecutive cardiac cycles were stored at a frame rate of >50 frames per second. Simpson’s biplane method was used to calculate two-dimensional left ventricular ejection fraction (2D-LVEF) (14).

The peak early diastolic velocity (E) and peak late diastolic velocity (A) of the mitral valve were measured from the apical four-chamber view, and the E/A ratio was calculated. The early diastolic excursion velocity (e’) of the mitral annulus was measured at the septal and lateral sides of the mitral annulus in the apical four-chamber view. The average e’ of the septal and lateral sides was used to calculate the E/e’ ratio.

The severity of aortic stenosis (AS) and aortic regurgitation (AR) was graded according to current echocardiographic guidelines using an integrated multiparametric approach (15,16). AS severity was determined primarily based on peak transvalvular velocity, mean transvalvular pressure gradient, and aortic valve area. AR severity was comprehensively assessed based on jet width, vena contracta width, pressure half-time, diastolic flow reversal in the descending aorta, and LV size and function. When individual grading parameters were discordant, two experienced cardiovascular echocardiographers performed an integrated assessment based on all relevant findings.

LV strain and 3DE image acquisition and analysis

Workstation software (QLAB 13.0, Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA) was used to perform offline analysis of cine-loop dynamic echocardiographic images. Apical two-chamber, three-chamber, and four-chamber views were acquired, and global longitudinal strain (GLS) was automatically calculated using the AutoStrain LV module. The X5-1 transducer was used for RT-3DE. Patients were instructed to hold their breath during image acquisition. Full-volume 3D datasets encompassing the entire left ventricle were acquired from the apical four-chamber view and stored for offline analysis. Postprocessing was performed using the 3DQ ADV analysis module in QLAB 13.0 software. The endocardial border was automatically traced, with manual adjustments for regions with suboptimal automatic tracking. The software generated the three-dimensional left ventricular ejection fraction (3D-LVEF) and the 3D-SDI. The 3D-SDI was derived from the R-R interval corrected standard deviation (SD) and maximal difference (Dif) in the time to minimum systolic volume (Tmsv) for 16, 12, and 6 LV segments. These parameters were expressed as Tmsv16-SD%, Tmsv12-SD%, Tmsv6-SD%, Tmsv16-Dif%, Tmsv12-Dif%, and Tmsv6-Dif% (Figure 2).

Reproducibility analysis

Ten patients with BAV were randomly selected for the reproducibility analysis. For the RT-3DE parameters, measurements were obtained twice by the same experienced echocardiographer at an interval of at least 2 weeks to assess intra-observer reproducibility. A second experienced echocardiographer independently repeated the measurements using the same protocol to assess inter-observer reproducibility. For the CT-derived measurements of the aortic sinus and ascending aortic diameters, the same experienced radiologist repeated the measurements after an interval of at least 2 weeks, while a second radiologist independently performed the measurements using the same protocol. All observers were blinded to the measurements obtained by the other observers and to the relevant clinical information of the participants. Intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility for each parameter were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range (IQR)], and categorical variables as n (%). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables and the chi-square test (χ²) or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Correlations were analyzed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify the factors independently associated with Tmsv16‑SD%, with multicollinearity defined as variance inflation factor >10 or tolerance <0.1. The data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with a two‑tailed P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The 79 patients with BAV (of whom 59 were male) had a mean age of 51.2±13.4 years and a mean BSA of 1.76±0.17 m². The aortic sinus diameter was 35.90±4.90 mm, corresponding to an SOVSI of 2.05±0.32 cm/m². The ascending aortic diameter was 37.04±5.44 mm, with an AASI of 2.11±0.32 cm/m². Based on the site of AD, 13 patients had isolated aortic sinus dilation, 18 had isolated ascending AD, and 19 patients had concomitant dilation of both the aortic sinus and ascending aorta. The 40 healthy controls (of whom 26 were male) had a mean age of 55.78±12.04 years and a mean BSA of 1.71±0.24 m². The mean systolic blood pressure was 118.05±9.40 mmHg, and the mean diastolic blood pressure was 74.22±8.21 mmHg.

Comparison of clinical and echocardiographic parameters according to AD

Comparisons of clinical and echocardiographic parameters stratified by SOVSI are presented in Table 1, and those stratified by AASI are presented in Table 2. Compared with the control group, both the aortic sinus dilation and the ascending AD groups demonstrated significantly lower E/A ratios (P=0.05 and P=0.02, respectively) and higher E/e’ ratios (P=0.04 and P<0.001, respectively). In relation to the LV synchrony and deformation parameters, both the aortic sinus dilation group and the ascending AD group exhibited significantly higher GLS values and higher Tmsv16-SD%, Tmsv12-SD%, Tmsv6-SD%, Tmsv16-Dif%, Tmsv12-Dif%, and Tmsv6-Dif% values compared with both their respective non-dilated groups and the control group (all P<0.001). Further, even the non-dilated BAV subgroups for the aortic sinus and ascending aorta showed higher GLS values and higher Tmsv indices compared with the control group (all P<0.001) (Figure 3).

Relationship between aortic dimensions and LVMD

Tmsv16‑SD% was selected as the representative index of 3D‑SDI because of its superior reproducibility and lower susceptibility to variations in image quality (17). Correlation analysis revealed moderate correlations between Tmsv16-SD% and SOVSI (Spearman’s r=0.545, P<0.001), AASI (Spearman’s r=0.649, P<0.001), and GLS (Spearman’s r=0.657, P<0.001). A weak correlation was observed between E/e’ and Tmsv16-SD% (Spearman’s r=0.352, P=0.001). However, no significant correlation was observed between E/A and Tmsv16-SD% (Figure 4).

Reproducibility analysis

The intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility of Tmsv16-SD%, Tmsv12-SD%, Tmsv6-SD%, Tmsv16-Dif%, Tmsv12-Dif%, and Tmsv6-Dif% was good, with intra-observer ICCs of 0.87, 0.87, 0.95, 0.91, 0.84, and 0.85, respectively, and inter-observer ICCs of 0.94, 0.84, 0.91, 0.88, 0.83, and 0.86, respectively. Measurements of the aortic sinus diameter and ascending aortic diameter also demonstrated good reproducibility, with intra-observer ICCs of 0.95 and 0.93, respectively, and inter-observer ICCs of 0.94 and 0.95, respectively.

Discussion

The main findings of this study are as follows: (I) patients with BAV exhibit LV systolic dyssynchrony, which is more pronounced in those with AD; and (II) both SOVSI and AASI are independently associated with LVMD in patients with BAV.

BAV is closely associated with AD. Previous studies have shown that the progression of AD is more rapid in patients with BAV than in those with tricuspid aortic valve, and that a small subset of BAV patients exhibit an exceptionally fast rate of aortic root dilation (18). AD may impair LV systolic function through ventriculo-arterial coupling (19), a process that may also be accompanied by LVMD. RT-3DE enables the assessment of LVMD by quantifying the 3D-SDI; however, its application in patients with BAV has not been fully elucidated.

LV dyssynchrony in patients with BAV

LV dyssynchrony in BAV patients was a central focus of this study. Previous research has established that GLS is more sensitive than LVEF in the detection of early myocardial functional changes (20). However, GLS cannot capture the mechanical effects of dyssynchronous myocardial contraction and relaxation; that is, LVMD. Traditionally, dyssynchrony has been considered a hallmark of heart failure and is closely associated with worsening cardiac function (21). However, in the present study, patients with BAV exhibited clear LV systolic dyssynchrony despite maintaining normal LVEF. This finding indicates that impaired cardiac systolic synchrony may occur subtly in patients with BAV before the development of overt LV systolic dysfunction, as reflected by a decline in LVEF. This early-stage dyssynchrony may originate from long-standing hemodynamic abnormalities and subsequent adaptive myocardial remodeling in patients with BAV. Specifically, aortic valve stenosis or regurgitation, which is often associated with BAV, subjects the left ventricle to chronic pressure or volume overload (22). This persistent overload can first trigger early myocardial fibrosis and abnormalities in sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium handling, which in turn disrupts the temporal coordination of electrical signal conduction and contractile protein activation, ultimately leading to dyssynchronous contraction across myocardial segments (23).

Concurrently, asymmetric LV remodeling, characterized by uneven wall thickness distribution and disorganized myocardial fiber alignment, is common in patients with BAV (24). This structural abnormality further exacerbates electromechanical delay. Moreover, the congenital valvular malformation itself may be accompanied by underlying developmental abnormalities in cardiomyocytes (25), resulting in inherent heterogeneity in myocardial electrophysiological properties. Under the influence of acquired factors such as hemodynamic load, this heterogeneity tends to manifest early as contraction dyssynchrony, thereby establishing it as a significant early marker of subclinical myocardial dysfunction.

This study also revealed that the E/e’ ratio, which reflects LV filling pressure and diastolic function, is an important factor associated with LV systolic synchrony. An elevated E/e’ ratio indicates impaired LV diastolic function and increased filling pressure. This condition may impair systolic synchrony through two primary pathways: first, uneven distribution of intracavitary pressure may directly interfere with electrical signal conduction and local myocardial contractile properties (26); second, diastolic dysfunction is often accompanied by progressive myocardial interstitial fibrosis, which may impair the efficiency of cardiomyocyte electromechanical coupling. Together, these processes can further exacerbate LV systolic dyssynchrony (27).

Association between aortic diameter and LV dyssynchrony

In patients with BAV, the incidence of AD is as high as 60% to 80% (28). Previous studies have suggested an association between aortic structure and myocardial function. For example, using non-invasive LV pressure-strain myocardial work analysis, Tan et al. found that aortic diameter in patients with BAV was correlated with global wasted work and global work efficiency (29). The present study further revealed that patients with BAV and concomitant AD exhibited significantly aggravated LV systolic dyssynchrony. The underlying mechanisms may involve several pathways. First, AD may reduce aortic root compliance, leading to abnormally increased LV afterload and an uneven distribution of ventricular wall stress (30), disrupting the spatiotemporal coordination of myocardial contraction. Second, AD in patients with BAV is often accompanied by hemodynamic abnormalities of the aortic valve (31), which may induce dual overload of pressure and volume on the left ventricle, promoting localized mechanical remodeling of the myocardium. This remodeling process may result in dyssynchrony in the timing of contraction initiation and propagation between the interventricular septum and the ventricular free wall, as well as among different ventricular segments, ultimately exacerbating LV systolic dyssynchrony.

Aortic lesions and LVMD are important prognostic factors for patients with BAV (32). RT-3DE enables a more accurate evaluation of cardiac structure and function via 3D imaging, overcoming the limitations of geometric assumptions inherent to conventional two-dimensional echocardiography, and has demonstrated good agreement with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (33). RT-3DE has been widely applied in a variety of cardiovascular diseases, including heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and coronary heart disease (34). Therefore, RT-3DE may serve as a valuable diagnostic and prognostic indicator for different cardiovascular disorders.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, the findings may be influenced by the characteristics of the specific population, thereby limiting the generalizability of the conclusions. Larger, multicenter studies need to be conducted to validate the study findings. Second, RT-3DE is a sensitive tool for assessing LVMD; however, its accuracy is highly dependent on image quality. The exclusion of 12 participants (12.5%) due to suboptimal image quality in this study may have introduced selection bias. Third, this study did not include long-term follow-up; consequently, we were unable to evaluate temporal changes in the ASI and LVMD or determine their progression over time. Future prospective longitudinal studies are needed to further validate our findings and elucidate the longitudinal relationship between ASI and LVMD.

Conclusions

The study showed that: (I) LVMD is more pronounced in patients with BAV and AD than in those without AD; and (II) the 3D-SDI measured by RT-3DE can sensitively assess LV dyssynchrony. Additionally, the statistical analysis confirmed that AD is independently associated with LVMD.
