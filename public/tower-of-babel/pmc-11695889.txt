Efficacy and Safety of Continuous vs Intermittent Linezolid Infusion in Critically Ill Patients with Septic Shock
Authors: Ahmed M Albadry, Hend Y Zakaria, Mai M Elhefny, Ibrahim M Elsherif
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe synthetic antimicrobial agent Linezolid effectively penetrates many tissues and exhibits effectiveness against drug-resistant Gram-positive bacteria. This agent's pharmacokinetic qualities cast doubt on the need for intravenous treatment in cases of serious illness. For its time-dependent action to have an impact, serum levels must stay above the minimum inhibitory concentration throughout the dosage interval. According to our research, Linezolid infusions have been proposed to be given as continuous infusions to maintain adequate tissue and serum levels without trough concentration fluctuations. This will optimize the drug's effects and protect against toxicity and drug resistance. In critically ill individuals experiencing septic shock in the ICU, this study sought to validate the safety and efficacy of continuous Linezolid infusion in comparison to the conventional regimen. A prospective, randomized, controlled research involving 140 individuals suffering from septic shock who were older than 18 was carried out. Two groups of patients were randomly assigned. With an average treatment duration of 9.83 ± 2.537 in the intermittent infusion group and 7.39 ± 1.653 in the continuous infusion group, the first group obtained IV linezolid 600 mg twice daily as an intermittent infusion (II) over 60 minutes, whereas the second group obtained 300 mg IV as a loading dosage, and then continuous infusion of 900 mg/day in the first day and 1,200 mg/day in the subsequent days. There was a significantly high clinical cure and less ICU and hospital stay in the continuous infusion group (p < 0.001). Moreover, there was a lower incidence of developing thrombocytopenia in patients with renal impairment who got continuous infusion. Introduction

Vasodilatory or distributive shock includes septic shock. It is identified as sepsis with irregularities related to the metabolism, cells, and circulatory system, that are linked to a higher risk of death compared to sepsis alone.1 Gram-negative bacteria were first believed to be the main species responsible for bacterial sepsis. However, it has been demonstrated over the previous 25 years that Gram-positive bacteria are the most frequent source of sepsis, accounting for nearly 50% of cases of sepsis.2

Within the class of oxazolidinones, a new category of synthetic antimicrobials with action vs most mycobacteria and Gram-positive bacteria, the only licensed member is linezolid. When it comes to most strains of streptococci, oxazolidinones are bactericidal, while for Staphylococci and Enterococci, they are bacteriostatic. Within the framework of glycopeptide resistance, oxazolidinones have emerged as an essential substitute for vancomycin therapy, which is often linked to nephrotoxicity.3,4

Time-dependent antimicrobial drugs include linezolid. The most suitable pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) measures for illustrating its activity are the time at which blood concentrations are greater than the minimum inhibitory concentration (T > MIC) and the area under the serum concentration-time curve/minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC/MIC) ratio.5

In seriously ill septic patients, several drugs’ pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics may be markedly changed. Antibiotics pose a risk of insufficient medication exposure or potentially harmful side effects in these patients.5,6 Thus, studies comparing the linezolid's pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics infused intermittently or continuously in severe cases have indicated that the latter is preferable to the former, especially when it comes to establishing great levels of unbound antibiotics in infected tissue.7,8

This study aims to validate the safety and efficacy of linezolid infused continuously or intermittently in seriously ill patients in intensive care units.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

In July 2023 and April 2024, this prospective randomized controlled trial was carried out at the Critical Care Department, Ain Shams University Hospitals, Cairo, Egypt, after the ethical research was approved and written informed consent from the patients was obtained. There is a registration number for the study protocol on clinicaltrials.gov (registration number).

Patients

One hundred and forty adult patients aged >18 years old with septic shock (diagnosed by absent hypovolemia with a vasopressor support to maintain a mean arterial blood pressure of ≥65 mm Hg and serum lactate level >2 mmol/L (>18 mg/dL). In addition to the previous criteria, it was necessary to confirm positive culture susceptibility to linezolid.

Patient's Randomization and Protocol

Depending on the clinical criteria, each patient was first assessed for shock caused by sepsis. Microbiologic sampling, including blood culture, was obtained from all cases.

Moreover, sampling for cultures was obtained from an anticipated source of infection (sputum culture in cases of suspected pneumonia and aspirate or swab in cases of soft tissue infection). Third-generation cephalosporin was administered empirically according to our hospital protocol until culture results.

Only cases with confirmed linezolid-sensitive gram-positive cultures were included in the study and were randomized to receive linezolid in a 1:1 ratio in either group A or B.

Group A continuous infusion (CI): There were 70 patients. They received an intravenous loading dose of 300 mg Linezolid for 60 min, followed by continuous infusion of 900 mg/day on the first day then 1,200 mg/day in the subsequent days (50 mg/h).

Group B intermittent infusion group (II): There were 70 patients. They obtained 600 mg of linezolid IV every 12 hours for 1 hour.

Linezolid was received for 8–10 days, in both groups with the length of time varying according to the patient's clinical response. Using a random number set provided by the ICU coordinator, patients were assigned at the start of the study using a simple randomization method. Every patient received a unique randomization code, which consisted of two numerals representing the randomization sequence and two letters representing the critical care unit.

Patient Follow-up and Outcome Measures

Signs and symptoms, weaning off of vasopressor support, total leukocyte count, and negative blood C&S were employed to assess the clinical response. Anticipated mortality rates in both groups were calculated at the study entrance based on the simplified acute physiology score ii (SAPS II) score and the sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score.

On day seven after starting linezolid, a clinical cure was considered the main objective outcome. Hemodynamic stability, TLC < 10,000/mL, and normal body temperature were indicators of a clinical cure.

The duration of hospital stay, ICU stay, linezolid utilization, and 30-day mortality were studied as secondary objective outcomes.

Moreover, platelet counts were measured every two days throughout linezolid treatment and compared between the two groups to determine the incidence of platelet count reduction by linezolid (LIT) to validate the safety between the two groups. A platelet count reduction of at least 50% from baseline or a platelet count decrease of less than 100 × 103/mm3 was considered thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, the relationship between renal impairment and thrombocytopenia risk was evaluated. A creatinine clearance (CrCl) of less than 60 mL/min, as determined by the Cockcroft–Gault formula, was considered a renal impairment.

Discussion

The present study showed that the administration of linezolid continuously had a significantly greater effect on patient cure with a shorter period of treatment and less time of hospital admission compared to intermittent management in cases with septic shock secondary to chest infection or soft tissue infection.

Since linezolid has a time-dependent activity, thus its intake continuously is a better method of treatment to keep its effective plasma concentration above the MIC, especially in septic patients where there is an increase in the volume of distribution of the drug.9

Our study findings were consistent with those of El-Gaml et al.10 who compared three groups of critically ill patients in which linezolid was administered, the first group with the usual intermittent dose, the second with continuous infusion, and the third with continuous infusion in addition to loading dose. The percentage of patients who improved varied significantly among the three treatment groups. Patients receiving continuous infusion of linezolid showed a higher rate of improvement because this mode of administration significantly lessened the significant fluctuations in plasma levels that were observed when linezolid was given at intermittent intervals.

Additional research by De Pascale et al.11 supported the use of linezolid continuously as a better method, particularly in seriously sick obese cases. This study demonstrates that continuous infusion delivery can safely improve the linezolid pharmacokinetic profile, but intermittent treatment is linked to suboptimal plasma concentrations in obese seriously ill individuals with ventilator-associated pneumonia. Obesity and a seriously ill state did not significantly alter pulmonary distribution in this trial, although continuous infusion led to a higher alveolar penetration ratio.

Additionally, Abou Warda et al.12 showed that in patients with pneumonia acquired by ventilator and hospital, improved by continuous infusion delivery of linezolid than intermittent treatment.

Moreover, Taubert et al.13 and Soraluce et al.14 recommended continuous infusion because the standard linezolid dosage did not lead to optimal clinical recovery.

Group A demonstrated a trend toward improved clinical recovery, but the death rates between group A and group B were not different with no statistical significance. This is most possibly owing to immune suppression, chronic inflammation, and a hypercatabolic state developed via sepsis, all of which were linked with detrimental consequences.15

Our results were in agreement with those of Abou Warda et al.,12 but not in agreement with El-Gaml et al.10 and De Pascale et al.,11 who noted a significant decline in ICU mortality rate in the continuous infusion groups.

The present research studied the percentage of patients who had thrombocytopenia and compared the results between both groups. The incidence in CI and II infusion groups was 18.6% and 28.6%, respectively but without statistically significant difference between both groups. The development of thrombocytopenia is most likely due to the biological effects of the drug rather than the mode of administration, as linezolid may affect its metabolism owing to inhibition of protein synthesis in mitochondria, resulting in high plasma concentrations and a greater likelihood of non-negligible toxicities.16

Renal and non-renal processes are both involved in the removal of linezolid. About 30% of linezolid is excreted unchanged by the kidney in people with normal renal function, and around 65% of linezolid is eliminated non-renally. Linezolid clearance is significantly reduced in the presence of compromised renal function, and the major linezolid metabolite accumulates. This accumulation may increase the likelihood of adverse effects, such as thrombocytopenia.17–21

In the current study, it was shown that renal impairment was significantly linked with a greater risk for LIT in group B but not in group A.

Conclusion

Linezolid is still an essential antibiotic for treating infections caused by resistant gram-positive organisms, but it must be used carefully.

Intermittent infusion of linezolid is linked with a high risk of treatment failure and prolonged intensive care unit stay and hospital stay. Moreover, cases with renal impairment who receive intermittent infusion are at a greater risk of developing thrombocytopenia. Nevertheless, these limitations can be conquered by continuous infusion modality which showed an advantage in terms of enhancing clinical efficacy in seriously ill patients with septic shock secondary to gram-positive bacterial infection.
