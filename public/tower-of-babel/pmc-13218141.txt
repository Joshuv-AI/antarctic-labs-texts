Individual and spousal weight trajectories and their associations with health-related quality of life: a longitudinal population-based cohort from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (2001–2018)
Authors: Parisa Naseri, Parisa Amiri, Leila Cheraghi, Amirali Zareie Shab Khaneh, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the association between obesity and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is well-documented, studies on the association between weight trajectories and HRQoL among spouses are limited. This longitudinal study aimed to characterise distinct body mass index (BMI) trajectories and their association with HRQoL at individual and spousal levels.MethodsThe total sample of 773 couples was followed for an average of 15 years. Univariate group–based trajectory models and multi-trajectory group–based models were used to identify latent classes of individual and couple BMI trajectories, respectively. Linear regression analyses were applied to investigate the associations between identified BMI trajectories and HRQoL at the individual level and at the spousal level, where spousal HRQoL refers to the association between both partners’ BMI trajectories and each individual’s HRQoL.ResultsBased on BMI changes over the life course, four trajectories were identified in wives and husbands at both individual and couple levels, ranging from stable healthy weight to progressively increasing overweight and obesity, with all trajectories showing an overall rise in BMI with age. In the fully adjusted model, compared with wives in the mild progressive overweight group, the mean score of physical HRQoL in women in the progressive obesity group (β=−2.14, p=0.02) and severe progressive obesity group (β=−2.55, p=0.03) was significantly lower. Moreover, compared with husbands with stable healthy weight status, those in the progressive obesity group had lower physical HRQoL (β=−2.67, p=0.02). At the spousal level, lower physical HRQoL was observed in individuals whose BMI trajectories, together with their partner’s trajectory, indicated higher risk (eg, wives with severe progressive obesity and husbands with stable overweight) compared with couples with both partners in lower-risk BMI trajectories (β=−3.61, p=0.01). A similar effect was observed only in severe progressive obese women whose husbands were of stable healthy weight during their lifespan. None of the BMI trajectories at either individual or spousal levels affected mental HRQoL.ConclusionOur findings indicate a significant effect of individuals’ BMI patterns on the physical domain of HRQoL. Spousal HRQoL associations reflect how both partners’ BMI trajectories are jointly associated with individual HRQoL. Clinically, these results highlight the potential importance of early weight management, particularly in women, in relation to long-term physical HRQoL and possible benefits for both partners. Further investigation is required to assess the role of potential confounders in BMI–HRQoL associations.

Background

Overweight and obesity have become an increasing public health concern during decades, and its prevalence is rising globally.1 During the last two decades, overweight and obesity rates have tripled in Iran as a developing country.2 According to the WHO report, more than half of Iranian adults are overweight or obese.3 Although a large body of evidence shows the association between obesity and many physical health problems such as diabetes mellitus (DM), cancers and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs),4 data indicate the substantial impact of obesity on mental and social aspects of health as well as individuals’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL).5 Numerous factors including psychological state, sex, low income, level of education, seriousness of illnesses and age may lead to a reduced HRQoL.6 7 Chronic diseases of many types may have different effects on different aspects of HRQoL.8 Moreover, some studies have addressed the effect of lifestyle factors such as physical activity and chronic diseases on HRQoL.810

HRQoL, as an important health outcome, is the perception of individuals regarding their health, which helps policies in evaluating potentially useful prognostic tools for treatment and preventive intervention efforts and understanding disease burden.11 Several cross-sectional studies have shown adverse associations between overweight and the physical domain of HRQoL12 13; however, limited evidence indicates this association in both physical and mental aspects of HRQoL.14 15 Recent studies in Iran also showed that physical HRQoL was affected by overweight and obesity.16 However, this association may be affected by sex. Some evidence showed that body mass index (BMI) is negatively related to HRQoL in the physical domain, emotional domain, or both,12 14 whereas another study reported that men with higher BMI have higher HRQoL than women with higher BMI values; this association was opposite at lower BMI.17 However, another study reported a significant association between obesity and HRQoL in women, but not in men.18

Although the association between BMI and well-being or HRQoL has been widely investigated, little is known about the well-being effect of others’ BMIs. Spousal concordance, which is defined as similar behaviours and associated health statuses between a husband and a wife, has been well documented.19 Most cross-sectional studies have investigated spousal concordance in relation to chronic diseases such as hypertension (HTN),20 21 CVD,22 cancer23 and type 2 DM,24 which is explained by the shared environment.19 A previous cross-sectional body of literature has indicated the positive relationship of BMI in spouses.22 However, evidence on the association between weight status and HRQoL in spouses is limited. In addition, regarding the dynamic nature of BMI, recent studies in Iran and other countries have investigated BMI trajectories over the lifespan. However, outcomes were different from the results of our study, as they assessed the association between BMI changes with cardiometabolic risk factors.2527

Although previous studies have documented spousal resemblance in health attributes, questions remain regarding the longitudinal resemblance of spouses’ BMI and the possible formation of couple-level BMI trajectory patterns. A study conducted in the United States investigated BMI trajectories in association with only physical health aspects in couples.28 This study showed that husbands’ high BMI levels resulted in adverse physical health outcomes. Since the socio-environmental factors in West countries vary, their results may not be generalisable to East countries’ populations. Moreover, the aforementioned studies of the effect of BMI on HRQoL in the Iranian population were conducted often at a certain time point, regardless of BMI changes over time. In this study, group-based trajectory models as a dynamic statistical approach have been applied for estimating longitudinal patterns in BMI.29

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report in the Middle East that aimed to (1) examine the longitudinal trajectory of couples’ BMI, (2) address the association of BMI trajectories with HRQoL at the individual level and (3) assess whether spouses’ BMI trajectories could predict couples’ HRQoL in both physical and mental domains.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), as a large-scale family-based cohort study with long-term follow-up in Iran, assessed cardiovascular risk factors among a sample population in Tehran (the capital of Iran) in District No. 13. The TLGS was divided into two parts: a cross-sectional study of the prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and their associated risk factors and a prospective follow-up study. Phase 1 (cross-sectional phase) began in 1999 and was completed in 2001, and phase 2 began in 2001, was completed in 2005 and was then repeated every 3 years until 2018. A total of 15 005 individuals aged ≥3 years who were residents of District No.13 and were under the coverage of three medical health centres were selected using a multistage cluster random sampling method. All members of each family, including those not having risk factors, were invited for baseline measurements and followed every 3 years. District No. 13 is one of the 22 districts of Tehran metropolitan city. It is located in the eastern part of the city, covers the area of approximately 13 km2 and is under coverage of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences and Health Services. Moreover, three medical health centres of overall 20 medical health centres in the district were selected. A complete demographic database of all families in three centres, including family dimension, sex, age, postal address and telephone numbers was extracted from the data banks of these centres. Based on the given data, every family was contacted, invited and then recruited to participate in the study.30 For the present study, a total of 2556 couples were selected from phase 1 and followed for 15 years (till phase 6). After the exclusion of 330 couples with reported widowhood or divorce, 2226 remained, and from these, 310 couples did not participate in phase 6. Moreover, spouses with at least four missing BMI measurements were excluded (n=870). From the remaining 1046 couples, 273 were excluded due to missing HRQoL values in either the wife or husband. The final sample included 773 couples/1546 individuals. The study design is shown in figure 1.

Flowchart of the study population. BMI, body mass index; HRQoL, health-related quality of life.

The TLGS is an ongoing population-based cohort study that received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the National Research Council of the Islamic Republic of Iran and the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences (RIES), Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrolment in the TLGS. The present study represents a secondary analysis of existing TLGS data. Ethical approval for this specific analysis was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1398.119).

The analysis used de-identified individual- and couple-level data that were originally collected as part of the TLGS. Access to the data was granted by TLGS data custodians, and all analyses were conducted in accordance with the original informed consent provided by participants. The study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Measurements

Using pre-tested questionnaires, expert interviewers collected sociodemographic data such as age, sex, marital status, educational level, employment, smoking status and behavioural variables. Weight and height were measured with participants minimally clothed and barefoot, using digital scales (Seca 707) and a tape metre, respectively. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²).31

After 15 min of rest, blood pressure (BP) was measured twice on the right arm by a physician using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer, and the average was recorded for systolic and diastolic BP. Fasting blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 9:00 to measure triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and 2-hour post-load plasma glucose (PLPG) at the TLGS laboratory.30 Physical activity level was assessed using Lipid Research Clinic (LRC) questionnaire32 at phase 1. Since the LRC questionnaire assesses physical activity level qualitatively and the questions are fully subjective, the derived data were not accurate. Hence, from phase 2, physical activity was measured using the Modifiable Activity Questionnaire in three leisure time, job and household activities in the past year based on metabolic equivalent of task-minutes/week forms.33 HRQoL data in the physical and mental dimensions were collected using the reliable and validated Iranian version of the short-form 12-item health survey version 2 (SF-12v2).34 SF-12v2 contains 12 questions summarised into eight subscales, that is, four physical health-related subscales that include physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health problems, bodily pain and general health and four mental health-related subscales that include vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems and mental health. The physical and mental subscales comprised the physical component summary (PCS) and the mental component summary (MCS), respectively.35 The score of each subscale ranges from 0 to 100 indicating the worst and the best conditions of health, respectively.

Definitions

HTN was defined as having mean systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg or mean diastolic BP≥90 mm Hg or taking antihypertensive drugs,36 and DM was defined as FPG ≥7 mmol/L or 2-hour PLPG ≥11.1 mmol/L37 or use of glucose‐lowering medications. Chronic disease was defined as having one of these conditions: HTN, DM, cancer, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and a history of CVD or coronary heart disease. Education level was classified into primary, secondary and higher. Smoking status was classified as smoker (current smokers) and non-smoker (past or never smokers). An individual who smokes cigarettes or uses other tobacco products daily or occasionally was considered a current smoker.

Statistical methods

Socio-demographic and behavioural variables and chronic disease of wives and husbands from baseline to the last wave were summarised. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) and categorical variables as the frequency (percent). The independent samples t-test was used to compare crude means of the HRQoL between wives and husbands at the last follow-up.

The univariate group-based trajectory models (GBTMs) and the written programme ‘Traj’38 were used to identify subgroups with similar underlying BMI trajectories during lifespan. GBTM is a statistical methodology for analysing developmental trajectories: the evolution of an outcome over age or time.38 GBTM is a specialised application of finite mixture models using maximum likelihood estimation and allows us to analyse developmental trajectories and to describe and explain changes over a relatively long period.39 It identifies latent subgroups following similar trajectories and summarises individual differences in developmental progression. Trajectories are typically modelled using polynomials of different orders (linear, quadratic and cubic) to capture simple or more complex patterns of change over time, with the optimal order chosen based on model fit and interpretability.

Without any priori hypothesis, the possible number of latent trajectories was investigated. Accordingly, a series of models considering several polynomials (cubic and quadratic) and linear specifications of BMI as a function of participant’s age are fitted separately for husbands and wives. This began with a single model consisting of one group, with the number increasing until the number of trajectories that best fit the data was identified using the value of the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), average posterior probability (APP) of group membership and the odds of correct classification (OCC). The optimal model was selected as a lower BIC, a higher posterior probability >0.70 and OCC >5. For each participant, the model provides the probability of belonging to each of the identified trajectory groups and assigns the participant to the trajectory group based on the highest chance.38 Wives’ and husbands’ BMI trajectories were defined as two separate categorical latent variables. In addition, a group-based multi-trajectory model was used to identify latent classes of couple BMI trajectory.39 This analytical approach allowed us to identify latent groups of couples following similar trajectories across husband’s and wife’s BMI trajectories simultaneously. Further, the couple’s BMI trajectories were determined as one categorical latent variable. After determining husband’s, wife’s and couple’s BMI trajectories, the associations with physical and mental HRQoL were assessed using separate linear regression models for each set of trajectory groups (ie, one model each for wives, husbands and couples). The physical and mental HRQoL were considered as dependent variables, and the BMI trajectory groups of wives, husbands and couples were included as independent variables. Some crude and adjusted models were fitted. Adjusted variables were age, employment status, education level, physical activity, smoking and history of chronic disease (DM, HTN, CVD and cancer). Trajectory analysis was conducted in STATA version 16, and other analyses were run in IBM SPSS Statistics version 26; two-sided P-values<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Based on the goodness of fit criteria in online supplemental table 1 and appendix, the most parsimonious model (the four-group model, quadratic) in wives and husbands was used separately. The BICs for the four-group models were −10278.80 and −9529.97 in wives and husbands, respectively. For this model, all the APP values were higher than 0.94 for both husbands and wives. Among wives, groups 1, 2, 3 and 4 had OCC values of 58.38, 19.65, 74.34 and 6693.50, respectively, and corresponding values among husbands were 212.02, 23.87, 34.22 and 249.81. Moreover, class sizes were >5% in both wives and husbands and were close to 5% in the last group of wives’ trajectories. After adjusting for all potential confounders and compared with wives who were in the mild progressive overweight group, the mean score of the physical HRQoL among wives in the progressive obesity group (β=−2.14, p=0.02) and severe progressive obesity group (β=−2.55, p=0.03) was significantly lower. However, mental HRQoL was not statistically significant in various weight trajectory groups in both sexes.

Discussion

This longitudinal study is the first attempt to determine BMI trajectories in Iranian couples during 15 years of follow-up and address the effects of the explored individual and spousal BMI trajectories on HRQoL. Using a group-based trajectory and multi-trajectory models, the main BMI patterns were examined through four groups at the individual and spousal levels, respectively. At the individual level, wives and husbands who had progressive overweight and obesity over time evaluated their physical health as poorer than their counterparts with healthy weight or with low progressive weight gains. At the couple level, these were observed among wives whose physical health status was affected by their own and their spouse’s weight status. The findings of this study did not show any relationship between self-assessment of mental health and weight groups at individual or couple levels.

In general, no significant fluctuation between different groups based on BMI trajectories was observed, and groups remained almost steady over time in both sexes. Considering sex differences, our findings indicated a higher proportion of husbands in the healthy weight group, whereas wives had predominantly overweight and obese categories. Across all couple trajectory classes, wives generally had higher BMI at baseline and throughout the follow-up, and no trajectory class showed opposing BMI trends between spouses (ie, one spouse decreasing while the other increased). This concordance may reflect shared household environments, behaviours and social norms and clinically supports the potential value of couple-based or family-centred lifestyle interventions rather than individual-only approaches.40 41

Our findings suggest that progressive weight gain among wives is more strongly associated with lower physical HRQoL than in men, highlighting the potential importance of targeted weight management interventions for women who could also benefit their spouses. Overall, compared with wives, a lower proportion of husbands reported reductions in physical HRQoL, indicating sex-specific differences in the association between BMI trajectories and QoL.42 43 Furthermore, the observed associations at the couple level appear largely driven by wives’ BMI trajectories; stratified analyses show significant effects for wives but not for husbands, in which associations with HRQoL became non-significant. This suggests that couple-level interventions might need to prioritise wives’ weight management while considering that husbands’ HRQoL appears less sensitive to spousal weight trajectories.44 45

It is somewhat difficult to compare our results with previous literature mainly because of the scarcity of studies on couples and sex differences in trajectories of body weight and the different labelling of BMI patterns. Although some existing studies point to a higher prevalence of overweight in men46 and a higher prevalence of obesity and higher variability in body weight in women,47 others find no such differences.48 Regarding the comparison of BMI trajectories at the couple level, sex differences are observed in husbands’ and wives’ trajectories. The slightly increasing BMI patterns were observed for wives, and the steady pattern was obtained for husbands, which is in line with the results of a previous study.49

This study indicated that regardless of sex, progressive overweight and obesity trajectory groups reported significantly lower PCS than healthy weight trajectory groups. These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies in this context, including a meta-analysis that used data from six countries50 and a cross-sectional survey among the Italian population.51 Since numerous studies worldwide have repeatedly cited obesity as the leading cause of chronic diseases such as HTN, DM, CVD and cancers,4 52 lower PCS in individuals with obesity is not surprising. The results are more controversial in the mental domain of QoL. Although our results revealed no significant difference in MCS across all trajectory groups, a class I obese (BMI 25–29.9) group of a Chinese population reported significantly higher mental HRQoL.13 This disparity might have some psychosocial reasons. For instance, the ideals of women’s body shape are not the same in various cultures, such as Malaysia and Austria, as well as among people exposed to Western media.53 Moreover, weight bias internalisation is a determining factor strongly related to the mental domain of QoL.54 Several factors must contribute to turning excessive weight into a negative mental burden for the individual and reduce their mental QoL. Apparently, this was not observed in the current population.

Based on the results of the current study, couples’ BMI affects their wives’ PCS, which is in line with a longitudinal study examining changes in BMI and obesity risks in married couples over 25 years and showed that having a spouse who gained weight and became obese almost doubled the chance of one’s becoming obese.55

Although based on our findings, wives’ weight is not directly associated with their husbands’ QoL, and it seems important to consider the association between individuals’ weight status and their spouses’ well-being. As previous studies confirmed the weight correlation of couples and social contagion effects in weight,56 being the spouse of a person who is obese could lead to excessive weight gain and ultimately reduce the physical HRQoL. In addition, a review emphasised the mutual influences that couples have on each other’s health, and these impacts are affected by various factors throughout the years couples live together.57

This study has several notable strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal investigation that attempted to identify individual and spousal BMI trajectory groups and examine their associations with HRQoL at both the individual and couple levels in an Iranian population. The TLGS provided a large, well-characterised, population-based sample with repeated anthropometric measurements over more than 15 years of follow-up, which minimises recall bias and allows for robust assessment of long-term weight trajectories. In addition, the application of group-based trajectory and multi-trajectory modelling enabled the identification of distinct BMI patterns over time and the simultaneous consideration of partners’ weight trajectories, offering a more comprehensive understanding of within-couple dynamics. The inclusion of both physical and mental components of HRQoL further strengthens the study by capturing multiple dimensions of well-being.

Another important consideration is the study’s inclusion criteria. Participants were exclusively married adults living in Tehran who were part of the TLGS. While this allowed for high-quality longitudinal assessment, it also limits the generalisability of the findings to unmarried individuals, couples in rural areas or populations with different cultural, socio-economic or lifestyle characteristics. Future studies including more diverse populations and relationship types would help determine whether these BMI trajectory patterns and their associations with HRQoL are consistent across different settings.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although BMI is a widely used and practical indicator of adiposity in epidemiological studies, it does not distinguish between fat mass and lean mass and may not fully reflect body composition. Second, HRQoL is a multifactorial construct influenced by a range of psychosocial, behavioural and environmental factors; however, residual confounding may remain because some relevant variables such as marital quality, dietary patterns, physical activity, psychological stress and weight-related stigma were unavailable or not fully accounted for. Third, despite the longitudinal design, causal inferences cannot be definitively established. In addition, while GBTM is useful for identifying distinct trajectory groups, it has some limitations. The number and shape of trajectories can be sensitive to model specification, and it assumes that all individuals within a group follow the same trajectory pattern, which may oversimplify individual variability. Alternative approaches, such as latent growth curve modelling or growth mixture models, offer greater flexibility in capturing individual differences and can account for random effects.

Conclusions

At the individual level, our findings indicated lower physical HRQoL in men who are progressively obese than in those with stable healthy weight status. However, compared with women with mild progressive overweight, those with progressive overweight and severe obesity were less satisfied with their physical health. No significant difference in mental HRQoL was observed in different trajectory groups for both sexes. At the spousal level, physical HRQoL was associated with spousal weight only in women. However, subjective HRQoL in couples was independent of couple weight trajectories. Accordingly, to indicate the priority of planning for weight control interventions at both individual and spousal levels, examining tailored structural models to explore indirect relationships among other potential factors rather than BMI, which could affect HRQoL, seems essential.
