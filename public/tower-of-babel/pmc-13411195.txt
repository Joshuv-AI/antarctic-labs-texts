Recruiting Rural Patients for Clinical Research: Lessons From a Colorectal Cancer Screening Trial
Authors: Gina A Keppel, Brooke Ike, C Holly A Andrilla, Allison M Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe Colonoscopy Outreach for Rural Communities (CORC) study is a randomized controlled trial testing the effectiveness of a remotely‐delivered patient navigation program to increase colonoscopy completion for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening among rural and underserved patients. This study assesses CORC's success in recruiting rural patients and outlines approaches for including rural residents in clinical research.MethodsWe conducted descriptive analyses of rurality designations for enrolled patients based on their ZIP code using the Federal Office of Rural Health Policy (FORHP) definition and Rural‐Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes. The primary care organization (PCO) partnership was based on Rural‐Urban Continuum Codes (RUCCs). To recruit patients, CORC partnered with two PCOs in urban counties (RUCC = 3) and four PCOs in rural counties (RUCC >3).FindingsOf 527 participants, 75% (n = 394) lived in rural areas overall; 43.3% resided in large rural areas, 38.3% in small rural areas, and 18.4% in isolated rural areas. Nearly all (97.3%) participants recruited from the four PCOs located in rural counties lived in FORHP‐designated rural areas, whereas urban PCOs contributed both rural (53.3%) and urban participants.ConclusionsPartnering with rural PCOs in a pragmatic clinical trial led to successful recruitment of rural patients. Working with PCOs in urban counties also contributed to successful rural patient recruitment. Several key approaches included partnering with primary care to reach patients in their communities, using multiple strategies to understand PCO's populations served, and tailoring recruitment to rural patients’ unique needs. These strategies may help researchers increase the representation of rural populations in clinical research.Keywords: clinical trials, colorectal cancer screening, practice‐based research, rural patient recruitment, rural primary care

Introduction

As of 2020, an estimated 20% of the United States (U.S.) population lived in rural areas [1, 2]. Rural residents face more health challenges and disparities than urban residents [3, 4] and have higher incidence and mortality for many cancers, including colorectal cancer (CRC) [5, 6]. However, U.S. rural communities are underrepresented in health research and clinical trials [7, 8, 9]. Participant, community, and research factors [10] contribute to this underrepresentation. While rural residents are willing to participate in research [11], they face barriers to participation including lack of knowledge about clinical trials [12, 13], mistrust of research and researchers [13, 14], lack of transportation and long travel times [13, 14, 15, 16, 17], and inaccessibility of clinical trial sites [12], which are typically clustered in urban areas [18, 19]. Researchers also face barriers to including rural residents in studies, including little research funding focusing on rural populations [20], the perception that rural patients are difficult to recruit [21, 22], and a lack of infrastructure to conduct trials in rural communities [18, 19, 21]. Operationally, there are challenges to conducting research in rural communities, including low population density, which reduces the efficiency of recruitment and enrollment and may incur greater travel time and cost for research staff. Additionally, researchers may lack experience in addressing the issues unique to conducting research in rural communities, such as overcoming a lack of trust and understanding cultural issues that affect research engagement [23, 24, 25]. Successfully recruiting rural patients to health research studies generally, and clinical trials in particular, is an important goal to ensure representation of the entire U.S. population in health research discoveries, and to help understand and address rural health disparities in the U.S.

Primary care is a critical element of patient health care delivery in rural regions [26, 27]. Primary care providers often maintain strong and lasting relationships with patients [28], making them potentially important partners for clinical researchers in the engagement and recruitment of patients in rural communities [29]. One of the most important methods to facilitate research participant recruitment is provider recommendation, both by specialists and primary care providers in general [13, 30, 31]. Working with practice‐based research networks (PBRNs) is an effective approach that allows researchers to connect with rural communities through collaboration with primary care practices [32, 33, 34]. Other strategies, such as collaborations with community leaders and community‐based organizations (CBOs) [14, 35, 36] and using digital technology to remotely engage rural patients [35, 37], have also demonstrated success in reaching rural populations.

The Colonoscopy Outreach for Rural Communities (CORC) randomized controlled trial, funded through a mechanism focused on rural populations [38], evaluated the effectiveness of a patient navigation (PN) program delivered remotely by a single‐site CBO for increasing colonoscopy completion for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening among rural patients in the northwestern U.S. This paper describes the CORC study's approach to recruiting rural patients and its enrollment results, and shares lessons learned that can inform future efforts to enroll rural participants in clinical research.

Methods

Setting and Approach

The research team collaborated with the WWAMI region Practice and Research Network (WPRN), a PBRN serving Washington, Wyoming, Alaska, Montana, and Idaho, and two state Primary Care Associations (PCAs) to engage primary care organizations (PCOs) to partner on the CORC study. The WPRN includes more than 140 primary care practices across the five‐state region that collaborate on research. The partnering PCAs support federally qualified health centers to develop and sustain the healthcare workforce, provide training and technical assistance, and implement quality improvement initiatives. PCO partnership criteria included rurality of location, being a federally qualified health center or having a majority of patients with Medicaid or Medicare insurance, and the PCO's baseline CRC screening rates and patient volume, as described elsewhere [39]. We categorized PCO rurality for partnership criteria using county‐level 2013 Rural‐Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) [40], defining PCOs in counties with codes 4 or above as rural, and PCOs in counties with a code of 3 that self‐identified as serving rural patients, as rural‐serving urban. We specifically included rural‐serving urban PCOs with the intention of reaching rural patients throughout the county served by the PCO.

To enroll adequate numbers of participants, we aimed to partner with at least four and up to eight PCOs. WPRN and PCA staff shared information about the study with their member PCOs; the research team met with each of the 39 PCOs who responded with interest to this outreach to discuss the detailed logistics and benefits of the study partnership. Six PCOs met study partnership criteria and agreed to collaborate on the CORC study. The six CORC PCO partners were located in Idaho (n = 1), Oregon (n = 1), and Washington (n = 4). Two PCOs were located in rural‐serving urban counties known to serve substantial rural populations; four PCOs were located in rural counties (Table 1). Each PCO had multiple primary care clinics (range 2–10 clinics) spread across a single county or multiple counties. The CORC trial was approved by an Institutional Review Board and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov.

Patient Recruitment and Enrollment

Patient recruitment took place over 21 months with the goal of enrolling 480 participants. PCOs identified patients aged 45–76 years old with a new referral for colonoscopy for primary CRC screening or follow‐up after an abnormal stool test. Patients who provided verbal informed consent were randomized to one of two study arms: the remotely‐delivered CORC PN program or the standard of care mailed brochure. The CORC PN program is a 4‐topic program adapted from the New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program [41] and is delivered centrally by a lay navigator at a CBO unaffiliated with CORC partner PCOs and located geographically outside their communities. The CORC study protocol is described elsewhere in detail [39].

The CORC study included a pretrial needs assessment and co‐adaptation [42] of research procedures and the patient navigation intervention. Leaders, clinicians, and staff at partner PCOs, and leadership and staff at the CBO, participated in the co‐adaptation process. The research team worked with partner PCOs to tailor research procedures to their settings, including focusing on specific needs related to their rural populations. One of the primary needs raised by PCO partners was rural patients’ potential lack of trust in outsiders. To address this for recruitment, we framed the study as part of the PCO's efforts to improve CRC screening care. All recruitment materials were co‐branded from the CORC study and the patient's primary care provider, and were available in English and Spanish. We also adapted recruitment procedures to incorporate using multiple communication methods to overcome common difficulties reaching rural patients and used a study phone number based outside of the research team's metropolitan area that encompassed three of the PCOs’ local area codes. Research staff mailed an initial study introduction letter and then conducted outreach via phone, text, and email. Screening and enrollment occurred via phone and included a survey about general health and health behaviors, healthcare use, cancer and screening history, technology use, and demographics.

Participant and PCO Rurality

To identify patient rurality, we used the ZIP code associated with the patient's home address. For enrolled participants, we used the ZIP code they provided at enrollment unless they provided a P.O. Box. For these participants, we used the address shared by their PCO, and if not available, used their P.O. Box ZIP code. For nonenrolled individuals, we used the ZIP code provided by the PCO. We categorized patients as rural or not rural using the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) Federal Office of Rural Health Policy (FORHP) definition of rural [43], which combines several classification systems and is used to determine eligibility for federal Rural Health Grants. We used the FORHP rural eligibility definition available in 2023. To describe gradations of rurality for the subset of rural participants that resided in a FORHP‐designated rural area, we further classified individuals using the 2010 primary Rural‐Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) [44] codes for ZIP code areas. RUCA codes provide sub‐county detail on rural and urban areas, uniquely incorporating U.S. Census commuting flow data. We created three rural categories based on primary RUCA codes: rural large town (codes 4–6), rural small town (codes 7–9), and isolated rural community (code 10). Individual PCO clinic location was classified by the FORHP and RUCA codes for ZIP codes as described above. We conducted descriptive analyses using STATA version 19 SE [45].

Results

Table 1 describes PCO characteristics. PCOs in rural‐serving counties included a mix of rural and urban clinic locations. Individual PCO clinics were located across a wide range of areas, including urban areas, rural large towns, small rural towns, and isolated rural areas.

PCOs identified 1761 patients eligible for recruitment; 77.8% of recruitment‐eligible patients lived in FORHP‐designated rural areas. The clinical trial enrolled 527 patients (29.9%) across the three states, exceeding its overall study recruitment goal. Approximately three‐quarters (394, 74.8%) of enrolled patients lived in rural areas. Table 2 describes the rural patients enrolled in CORC. Rural participants were primarily white (96.7%), and 15.6% identified as Hispanic or Latino. Over half (55.0%) were female; 44.5% had private, 31.6% had Medicare, and 20.6% had Medicaid insurance; 68.2% were aged 45–64 years old, and 31.8% were aged 65–76 years. Almost all rural participants had working cell phones (96.9%) and internet access through mobile devices or internet in their home (99.7%). Rural participant characteristics were similar to nonrural CORC participants (data not shown). CORC enrolled rural patients from each partner PCO; the proportion of enrolled participants classified as rural varied from 16.7% to 100% across PCOs (Table 3).

Discussion

The CORC clinical trial successfully met overall and rural recruitment goals by partnering with multiple PCOs known to serve rural populations. Three‐quarters of participants in the CORC clinical trial resided in a rural community. Furthermore, CORC participants lived in varied types of rural areas, with over half of the participants residing in more remote rural areas, such as small towns and isolated rural communities. Our experience can provide guidance to clinical trial researchers seeking to address known barriers to rural research and increase enrollment of rural participants. Based on the experience of the CORC study, we suggest the following approaches for rural patient recruitment: (1) partner with primary care clinics to reach patients in their communities by harnessing existing relationships and local knowledge, (2) assess the rurality of communities that PCOs serve using a variety of designations, (3) consider the diversity of types of rural patients served by PCOs, (4) choose partnerships that provide economies of scale, (5) tailor approaches to rural communities, and (6) consider remote study procedures to increase accessibility.

Partner With Primary Care: Harness Existing Relationships and Local Knowledge

Centering research recruitment in primary care practice leverages the expertise and collaboration of PCO staff and providers on the ground in rural communities and is an important way to engage patients and facilitate research participation [29, 31]. To build partnerships with PCOs for research recruitment, the CORC study engaged primary care associations (PCAs) [46] and a PBRN. PCAs have existing relationships with federally qualified health centers and a mission to support and improve access to care that may align with research initiatives. PBRNs have infrastructure that cultivates and sustains long‐term trusted relationships with PCOs [34], and have demonstrated success in facilitating the conduct of research in community practices outside of academic medical centers [47, 48]. Collaborating with PBRNs is a practical way to engage PCOs in research and expand the reach of clinical trial participation to rural patients.

Assess the Communities That PCOs Serve, Using Multiple Strategies

The CORC study took a targeted approach to selecting research partnerships by assessing potential PCO partners’ service areas and individual clinic locations. The research team identified whether PCOs served rural patients through discussions with PCO staff and by assessing multiple rural classification schemes. Our enrollment results show that the CORC study's partnerships with PCOs located in rural counties universally achieved recruitment of rural patients. Moreover, we also found that partnerships with PCOs in urban‐designated counties that serve rural communities also led to substantial rural patient recruitment. Identifying PCOs serving rural populations using only county‐level definitions may result in excluding PCOs that serve rural residents, particularly in the western U.S., where counties are large geographic units [49]. Examining the location of each clinic within a PCO, as well as reviewing multiple rural definitions, may be informative for considering potential PCO partnerships for recruiting rural patients. This is exemplified by CORC's partnership with PCO 5, whose two clinic locations in a single county, approximately a one‐hour drive apart, recruited vastly different proportions of rural patients. Even though PCO 5's county is designated as urban via commonly used county‐level RUCCs, 98% of participants enrolled from community A were categorized as residing in rural areas by the FORHP sub‐county designation. We also found that at all PCOs, enrolled participants were equally or more rural than the individual clinics. A detailed understanding of PCO's service areas and identifying towns and communities served may assist in identifying PCOs likely to serve rural patients.

Consider the Diversity of Types of Rural Patients Served by PCOs

The geographic diversity of PCO partners’ service areas allowed CORC to achieve participant representation from a variety of types of rural areas. Notably, our sample includes patients from very rural areas, with more than half of CORC participants residing in areas in or near small towns with populations of fewer than 10,000 people or in even more isolated rural communities. Careful assessment of PCO patient populations can also help researchers reach a diversity of rural patients. Although the use of the dichotomous categorization of rural vs. urban is ubiquitous [50], it is an insufficient description of people residing in the U.S. The geography, culture, populations, and health outcomes of rural communities are not homogenous [51, 52], and being able to engage a diverse cross‐section of rural residents is critical to the goal of ensuring representation of rural populations in research [24, 36] and enhancing the generalizability of research results.

Choose Partnerships That Provide Economies of Scale

One challenge in recruiting rural research participants is the low population density in rural communities, which can make adequate sample sizes difficult to achieve [24]. Partnerships with a PCO system rather than single practices or individual clinics within a PCO can help ensure economies of scale to meet recruitment goals. Our experience demonstrates that partnering with rural PCOs with sizable patient panels ensured sufficient volume of colonoscopy referrals to achieve CORC's recruitment goals. Assessing eligible patient volume as PCO partnerships are developed and established can help ensure study success. Additionally, working with PCO systems that have multiple clinic locations across their geographic service area, like CORC's partners, could decrease the travel time required for their patients to access care and alleviate one of the most common barriers to rural research participation [13, 35].

Tailor Approaches to Rural Communities

In addition to the locations of CORC's partner PCOs, there may be features of the CORC study that enhanced recruitment of rural patients. Existing literature demonstrates the importance of tailoring to the local context [29]. The CORC study design included time and resources to tailor recruitment strategies and the study intervention to meet the needs of rural patients and rural PCOs, leveraging the expertise of the PCOs [42]. For example, to address rural patients’ lack of trust in outsiders [14], we ensured all materials were co‐branded with the PCO and the study intervention incorporated rapport‐building strategies with participants, including reserving time for the navigator to engage in small talk to build a connection during phone calls. To overcome challenges in reaching patients, we used their preferred communication methods and collected information about alternate contacts. Additionally, the tailoring process was appreciated by PCOs and helped establish strong partner relationships, which were a critical precursor to being able to successfully recruit patients and conduct the study.

Consider Remote Study Procedures to Increase Accessibility

Finally, one of the integral parts of this study was its fully remote conduct. All screening, enrollment, data collection, intervention delivery, and outcome data collection were performed virtually. This design removed transportation and access to trial sites as potential barriers to participation, which are important considerations for rural patient recruitment [35, 53]. Consolidation of physicians into larger health systems and rural hospital closures have increased over the last few decades [54]. As healthcare delivery continues to evolve, the trend toward consolidation may add to the already increasing distance individuals in rural communities travel for healthcare [55]. Research studies that require in‐person data collection or intervention delivery, even in local clinical sites, could become even more difficult for remote rural populations to access. Continued research is needed on using connected health approaches that provide healthcare services remotely [56] and make clinical trials more accessible to rural communities [37, 57].

Limitations

There are several potential limitations to this analysis. There is no single definition of rural that captures all individuals who experience rural life. There are multiple rural categorization schemes [58], and using alternative definitions leads to different groupings of rural patients. We selected two commonly used schemes for rural classification that fit the areas CORC worked with, including RUCA codes, which are reported to be the most commonly used definition in health services research [59], and that are possible to use from our available participant data at the ZIP code level. Since the FORHP and RUCA rural definitions do not perfectly align, we were unable to assess details about rural residence for some participants. Additionally, the suggested strategies for recruitment outlined herein are based on the experience of the CORC study, and additional evaluation is needed to understand whether these strategies apply more broadly across rural research studies.

Conclusion

Partnering with PCOs that serve rural patients in a pragmatic, practice‐based research study led to the successful recruitment of rural patients, including substantial representation of patients from small towns and isolated rural communities. This success included taking a targeted approach to partnerships that assessed PCO's service areas and rural reach and recruiting from rural counties as well as urban counties known to have and serve substantial rural populations. The CORC study's findings suggest the following strategies for successful rural research recruitment: centering recruitment in primary care practice, leveraging existing relationships by partnering with PBRNs, assessing the communities PCOs serve, considering the diversity of types of rural patients served by PCOs, choosing partnerships based on economies of scale, tailoring approaches to rural communities, and employing remote study procedures when possible. Increasing rural representation in clinical research is an important strategy for overcoming persistent rural health disparities, and employing these recruitment approaches may allow researchers to successfully recruit and enroll rural patients while balancing pragmatic study needs.
