Associations between number of steps and health outcomes in children and adolescents: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Stefan Stojanović, Olena Andrieieva, Nebojša Trajković
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToday’s public health discourse prioritizes the health and well-being of children and adolescents. As step counts include both light and moderate-to-vigorous physical activities, and monitoring steps has become a popular method for assessing daily physical activity, it is critical to gain a better understanding of how measuring daily steps may contribute to overall health in children and adolescents. The aim of this study was to thoroughly review the studies that investigated the associations between daily number of steps (DNoS) and health outcomes (HO) in children and adolescents.MethodsElectronic searches were conducted in PubMed, Google Scholar, Cochrane, MEDLINE, Web of Science, SPORTDiscus (2000 –January 2024.). Key terms such as number of steps, daily steps, average steps, physical activity, health outcomes were used. The primary outcomes were (1) body composition/obesity risk factors; (2) cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF); and (3) cardiovascular risk factors. Secondary outcomes were psychological/psychosocial parameters (sleep habits, i.e. quality of life). We assessed the methodological quality of each trial using AXIS quality assessment tool. However, due to a dearth of studies and conflicting results from current studies, no inferences can be drawn about the correlations between DNoS and sleep quality, quality of life, or cardiovascular risk factors (i.e., blood pressure). Across most of the studies, the average correlation between the DNoS and HO reflected a small, but significant, association with a small effect size.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of daily physical activity, as evidenced by the association between the number of steps and health outcomes in children and adolescents.Trial registrationPROSPERO # CRD42024523729.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20835-9.Keywords: Relationship, Average daily steps, Health outcomes, Children, Youth

Background

The health and well-being of children and adolescents are paramount concerns in contemporary public health discourse [1]. It is well established that physical activity (PA) has numerous benefits, including enhancing cardiovascular fitness, decreasing body fat, and lowering risk factors for diabetes [2]. Regular PA is vital for promoting and maintaining good health in children and adolescents [3]. However, recent trends show that children and adolescents are not meeting the recommended levels of physical activity [4], leading to a decline in physical fitness and an increase in health-related issues. This decline in PA levels among children and adolescents is a global concern, with significant implications for their overall health outcomes (HO) [5].

Several studies have highlighted the positive effects of PA on the physical, psychological, and cognitive health of children and adolescents [6–12]. For example, research has shown that children and adolescents who engage in higher levels of PA tend to have better cardiovascular health profiles as they transition into adulthood [13, 14]. In this regard, there is a study [15] that shows a cut-off point for the benefits that daily DNoS contributes to the health of children and adolescents. Namely, children and adolescents should have 11,700–13,000 DNoS per day to meet PA guidelines and have health benefits. On the contrary, low levels of PA have been associated with higher rates of obesity and other health-related issues among children and adolescents [16, 17]. Given the importance of PA for children and adolescents as well as the growing interest in wearable technology, there is a spreading interest in examining the relationship between the DNoS and various health outcomes in children and adolescents. While PA is acknowledged as a cornerstone of healthy development, there is a need for a nuanced exploration of the specific impact of step count on diverse health outcomes in children and adolescents.

Research on the association between the DNoS and health outcomes in children and adolescents has gained substantial attention in the last 20 years [18–35], yet significant gaps persist in our understanding of this relationship. Although most studies have shown positive associations between DNoS and at least one segment of HO [18, 20, 21, 23–28, 30–33, 35], there are studies that have not reported associations between DNoS and HO [19, 22, 29, 34]. Therefore, the evidence is mixed, which implies the need to systematise the results of the mentioned studies. Additionally, while existing studies have explored the general correlation between PA and health in young populations, a limited number have specifically focused on the nuanced impact of step count on various health outcomes. Additionally, there is a lack of consensus regarding the optimal daily step count for different age groups and the specific health parameters influenced by step accumulation. Furthermore, the majority of current research predominantly relies on self-reported measures or utilises pedometers [35], neglecting the potential benefits of advanced wearable technologies that can provide more accurate and comprehensive data. Addressing these gaps is crucial for informing evidence-based interventions and policies aimed at promoting PA in children and adolescents to optimise their overall health and well-being.

Despite the fact that vigorous PA is considered the strongest predictor of health outcomes [36] there is a growing interest in understanding the relationship and specific association between the DNoS and health outcomes in young populations. As step counts encompass both light and moderate-to-vigorous physical activities [37], and monitoring steps has become a widely used approach to evaluating daily physical activity, it is crucial to gain a deeper understanding of how measuring daily steps might contribute to overall health in children and adolescents. Given this, this study aimed to systematically summarise the studies examining the associations between DNoS and HO in children and adolescents.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [38]. The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (CRD42024523729).

Search strategy

Electronic database searches were conducted in PubMed, Google Scholar, Cochrane, MEDLINE, Web of Science and SPORTDiscus databases. The search was limited to papers published in English from 2000 until the 30th of January 2024. With the understanding that number of steps is the most often used yet there are other terms that are present in the scientific literature, we identified articles that assessed DNoS with the following search words: „number of steps“ OR „daily steps“OR „weekly steps“ OR „average steps“ OR „step count“OR „pedometer step count“ OR „physical activity level“, highlighting that descriptions with no link or affinity to the focus and objective of the research were excluded. Considering health outcomes we identified articles with subsequent key words: “health outcomes” OR “body composition” OR “body weight” OR “body fat percentage” OR “waist circumfurence” OR “obesity risk factors” OR “cardiorespiratory fitness OR “maximal oxygen uptake - VO2max” OR “cardiovascular risk factors” OR (MeSH) “blood pressure” “sleep habits” OR “quality of life”. Considering population we identified articles with subsequent key words: “young” OR “kids” OR “children” OR “adolescents” OR “youth”. Articles were included if they (1) were original articles containing empirical data applying correlation/regression statistics, (2) focused on the identification of relations between DNoS and health outcomes in children and adolescents.

Study selection/eligibility criteria

All the studies that were published in journals in English were included if: (a) examined a group of healthy individuals aged < 19 years; (b) included an objective measurement of DNoS; (c) tested at least one measure of health outcomes (body composition/obesity risk factors, CRF, cardiovascular risk factors, psychological/psychosocial parameters). Studies with the following characteristics were excluded: (b) only patient groups or populations with adverse health events, (c) review papers, (d) studies with no correlation between variables and data. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts of potentially relevant studies (SS, NT). If still potentially relevant after initial screening, two authors independently screened full texts (SS, NT) to examine eligibility. Any incongruities identified in the selection process were addressed through collaborative deliberation with co-authors.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included (1) body composition/obesity risk factors (i.e. body mass index (BMI), body weight, body fat percentage and waist circumfurence); (2) cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) [i.e. maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max)]; and (3) cardiovascular risk factors (i.e. blood pressure) measured using standard techniques.

Secondary outcomes included psychological/psychosocial parameters (sleep habits, i.e. quality of life) and objectively measured physical activity and sedentary behaviour derived from accelerometers or pedometers.

Extraction of data

Two authors extracted and synthesised data into tables (SS, NT). Studies were coded for: first author, year of publication, participant characteristics (age, sex, number of participants), country, daily number of steps (DNoS) measurement, health outcomes measurements, and main findings (associations). Study characteristics were manually extracted into custom Excel workbooks.

Assessment of methodological quality of included studies

Two authors (SS and NT) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using the Appraisal RoB tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) [39]. Each of the twenty criteria had independently graded values of „Yes“, „No“ or „Don’t know/comment“ for each single question in the form. Disagreements were resolved through discussions between the authors. Based on the score on the AXIS form, articles were, respectively, considered ‘poor’, ‘fair’, ‘good’, and ‘excellent’.

Data synthesis and analysis

In instances where data emanated from two or more trials, a meta-analysis was undertaken. For outcomes characterized by an insufficiency of data amenable to pooling, a qualitative presentation of results ensued. It is noteworthy that all incorporated outcome data maintained a continuous nature. Because the current review was focused on papers including associations between DNoS and HO, thus ignoring potentially hundreds of clinical trials of longitudinal design, we performed a meta analysis for associations between DNoS and HO. Effect size (ES) was defined as small, medium and large EF according to [40]. In this connection, the software CMA (Comprehensive Meta Analysis Version 3.0) was used.

Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flowchart of the search and eligible study selection. After duplicates were removed, the search resulted in 787 articles published up to January 30th, 2024. Title and abstract screening resulted in 168 articles of interest, of which 19 were identified for inclusion in systematic review and 18 in meta-analysis, since one did not contain homogenous continous data to be included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment

AXIS quality assessment indicated that of the 19 studies, 11 were of high quality (57.8%) [19–24, 26, 30, 31, 35], 5 were of moderate quality (26.3%) [18, 20, 27, 29, 32], and 3 were of low quality (15.7%) [25, 28, 33]. More than half of the studies proved to be high-quality cross-sectional studies, with a moderate to large sample size, and most of them were published in peer-reviewed journals. The highest scores were obtained concerning the reliability of the measured outcomes and the clear description of aims, measured outcomes, reported findings, and appropriate methods. The results of the AXIS quality assessment of each study can be found in Additional File 1: Material A (AXIS quality assessment scores for each study).

Measures of daily number of steps

Table 1 summarises the number of studies examining the association between DNoS and HO in children and adolescents. Most of the included studies used pedometers or accelerometers to record daily step count data. Ten of the studies used pedometers (DW-200, Yamax, SW200, Yamax, New Lifestyles 1000, Omron hja-310, built-in pedometer in smartwatch and smartphone, Yamax Digi-Walker CW 700), while five of the studies used accelerometers (ActiGraph accelerometer, wGT3X-BT8417, GT3X accelerometer). Only two studies use a different methodology, however. They collected the number of daily steps using a smartwatch and the Misfit Shine 2 wristband, validated in a population of adolescents [41].

Measures of health outcomes

Body composition - Table 1 shows that 18 out of the 19 studies established BMI using standard formula [42]. Additionally, body weight, body fat percentage and waist circumference were measured according to the protocols of the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry [43]. CRF was assessed using a multistage 20-meter shuttle run test [44] in all the presented studies that covered the measurement of the mentioned variable (Table 1). Cardivascular risk factors - Blood pressure was measured using a standardised method and was classified using percentile grids of arterial pressure values for children aged 3–6 years and 7–18 years [45] in both meta-analysis included studies. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index in one study and a questionnaire for caregivers in another study. Quality of life was assessed using the KIDSCREEN-52 in one study and the AUQEI questionnaire in another study.

We observed that most of the included studies [18, 20, 21, 23–28, 30–33, 35] found significant associations between DNoS and at least one aspect of HO in children and adolescents, while there were no significant associations in the rest of the analysed studies [19, 22, 29, 34]. Namely, most of the studies showed associations between DNoS and BMI [19–21, 24–28, 30, 32, 35], showing a negative association, indicating that a greater number of steps is associated with lower BMI values, while some studies [18, 22, 34] did not report associations between the mentioned variables. Furthermore, a small number of studies [25–27] reported associations between DNoS and WC. Moreover, few studies [21, 26, 30] reported associations between DNoS and CRF. In addition, two studies [23, 31] showed associations between DNoS and blood pressure in children and adolescents. Also, one study [28] showed associations between DNoS and quality of life, while one study did not [29]. Some studies have reported significant associations between DNoS and physical fitness [21]; and with body fat percentage [30, 35]. Looking at the results presented in Table 1, it can be seen that different DNoS values were reported. The majority of studies reported a DNoS range of 13,000–16,000 [18, 20, 24, 26, 31, 34], while other studies reported a DNoS range of 10,000–13,000 [22, 25, 27, 33]. On the contrary, there were studies that reported < 10,000 DNoS [19, 21, 23, 28–30, 32, 35]. Looking at the results, it can be seen that there were no significant differences in the associations between DNoS and HO variables based on the daily number of steps, considering that some studies with 10,000 DNoS [25, 27, 33] and studies with > 13,000 DNoS [24, 26, 31] reported positive associations. On the other hand, studies were presented that did not report an association between the variables DNoS and HO and were in a different range of average steps: <10,000 [19]; >10,000 [22]; >13,000 [34]. For this reason, a meta-analysis was performed to determine associations based on the sublimated results of multiple studies for all possible HO variables.

The interpretation of correlation, so called correlation coefficient, was done as follows: with the size of the correlation 0.90 to 1.00 (− 0.90 to − 1.00) associations/correlations were marked as having very high positive (negative) correlation; 0.70 to 0.90 (− 0.70 to − 0.90): - High positive (negative) correlation; 0.50 to 0.70 (− 0.50 to − 0.70) - Moderate positive (negative) correlation; 0.30 to 0.50 (− 0.30 to − 0.50) - weak positive (negative) correlation; 0.00 to 0.30 (0.00 to − 0.30)- correlation [46].

Discussion

This systematic review with a meta-analysis aimed to systematically summarise the studies examining associations between daily step count and health outcomes in children and adolescents. Our findings revealed that increased DNoS is associated with many HO in children and adolescents. Across all studies and effect sizes, the average correlation between the DNoS and HO reflected a small, but significant, association. Specifically, an increased number of DNoS is associated with better HO in children and adolescents, showing improvements in BMI, body weight, waist circumference, body fat percentage and CRF (i.e. VO2max). On the other hand, it is not possible to draw conclusions related to associations between DNoS and quality of sleep, quality of life or cardiovascular risk factors (i.e. blood pressure) due to the lack of studies and inconsistent results of existing studies.

So far, various methods have been employed to assess PA in children and adolescents. These methods include self-reporting tools like questionnaires, activity logs, and diaries, as well as objective approaches such as direct observation, heart rate monitoring, accelerometers, and pedometers [15]. While the DNoS is a commonly used measure to assess PA in paediatrics, it may not fully capture the variety and intensity of activities that children engage in. Unlike adults, children’s physical activity is often more diverse, including activities such as jumping, climbing, and other non-locomotor movements that are not adequately measured by step counts alone. Evidence [47] highlights that triaxial accelerometers are better suited to capturing the full spectrum of children’s and adolescents’s activity, as they can detect movements in multiple planes, providing a more comprehensive understanding of PA.

There is existing evidence that shows the positive effects of PA on health outcomes in children and adolescents [48, 49] and adults [50, 51]. Additionally, few studies [52–54] have reported significant associations between DNoS and HO in the adult population. The results of this study, although they do not look at the causal effect, but rather the associations, are in line with other prementioned studies. The study’s findings highlight that increased PA levels, in this case conventional walking or potentially running, given that these devices detect multiple types of activity, can improve HO among children and adolescents. Walking has been shown to reduce obesity rates, particularly BMI, among adolescents [55, 56]. Additionally, research has shown that active commuting to school, such as walking, is an effective way to boost physical activity levels in youth and decrease the prevalence of obesity [57, 58]. Considering the above, increased PA can be characterised as one of the protective factors for the prevention of obesity.

Since the majority of the included studies [18–22, 24–28, 30, 32, 34, 35] evaluated associations between DNoS and BMI of children and adolescents and showed significant associations between the mentioned variables we can conclude that a higher daily step count contributes to lowering obesity risk factors among children and adolescents. It is worth noting that most studies had a low magnitude of effect, however. weak [18, 24–26, 28, 30, 32] to moderate [20, 21, 30, 33] association. Although these studies determined associations rather than causality, they showed a positive association of DNoS with BMI in children and adolescents. Also, the aforementioned studies [48, 49] have shown that walking has a positive effect on the BMI of children and adolescents. However, few studies [19, 22, 34] indicated no association between DNoS and BMI. Best to our knowledge, there is no data that shows a negative impact of walking (e.g., physical activity) on the BMI rates of adolescents. A large number of studies were mentioned that showed that a higher level of PA, in this case DnOS, has a positive effect on the reduction of obesity rates, i.e., BMI [18, 20, 21, 24–28, 30, 33, 35]. In this regard, it is not surprising that walking has a positive effect on waist circumference [59, 60] among adolescents. Although the included studies [25–27] in this systematic review showed a weak correlation, we observed that associations are at a significant level. Physical activity’s impact on better values of WC was also observed in the adult population as well [61]. PA has also been shown to be a good strategy for increasing CRF values ​​(VO2max) in adolescents [62, 63] and adults [64]. In particular, one study showed that physical activity of high or low intensity, which includes walking, has the effect of increasing CRF [65]. In this regard, since studies included in this review and analysis showed weak but significant associations [21, 26, 30], with small [26, 30] to medium effect [21] and we observe that our findings are consistent with previous results showing a positive effect of physical activity, i.e., walking, on CRF. Although it was once thought to be a condition primarily affecting only adults and the elderly, hypertension now affects children and adolescents in the modern world [23] and sufficient PA level is becoming a good strategy in hypertension therapy [66]. Study [67] showed that PA can increase vascular function (flexibility and responsiveness of blood vessels, improving endothelial function), therefore, it can lead to better blood flow and lower resistance in the arteries, contributing to reduced blood pressure. A review consisting of 73 [67] studies reported that PA can benefit the blood pressure rates of adults, as well as among adolescents. Our findings are in line with previous research, showing low [31] to high [23] associations between DNoS and blood pressure in children and adolescents. However, physical activity, especially an organised PA program, is more difficult to implement than conventional walking. In this regard, there is a need to conduct a larger number of studies that will deal with the impact of walking, i.e., the daily number of steps, on the blood pressure of children and adolescents. As mentioned earlier, no general conclusions can be drawn based on these results due to the small number of studies included in this meta-analysis.

This meta-analytic evidence showed a positive association between DNoS and HO in children and adolescents. Conducting organised physical activity or participating in sports activities is a challenge for children, adolescents and their parents in today’s age. In this regard, it is necessary to raise awareness among adolescents or their parents about the importance of any activity for overall health. Given that parents act as models of their children’s behaviour, by implementing a higher level of physical activity, i.e.,higher DNoS, they can indirectly influence the increase in PA level of children and adolescents, which is ultimately associated with overall health outcomes. The advantage of this study is the consistency in the results that show the associations of the mentioned variables, considering that the sample is composed of the population of five continents (North America, South America, Europe, Asia and Australia). However, the association effects, although significant, were weak, which makes it difficult to generalise the results, so the results should be interpreted carefully.

This study had a few limitations worth acknowledging. Firstly, the average association/correlation between the DNoS and HO of children and adolescents was significant, but small with regards to the magnitude of the effect. This implies that the implications in terms of their application should be evaluated judiciously. Second, all of the studies implemented a cross-sectional design; therefore, causal effects cannot be drawn. Hence, it is not possible to conclude whether the increased step count affected HO or the other way around. Another limitation of this study is the relatively small number of included studies (n = 19) and inconsistent data for HO variables. The next limitation is the small number of studies individually for the variables of HO, which prevented us from performing a meta-analysis on a larger scale for certain HO in children and adolescents. Although there were 18 included studies in total, no homogeneous data were available to perform a meta-analysis of multiple variables from primary and secondary outcomes. At last, a limitation of this study is the use of step counts as the sole measure of PA in paediatric populations. Step counts may not fully capture the diverse range of activities children engage in, such as jumping or climbing, which are better detected by triaxial accelerometers. Therefore, it is recommended to complement step counts with additional methods that can more accurately assess the full spectrum of children’s physical activities.

Conclusion

The results of this systematic review highlight that increased DNoS is associated with different HO in children and adolescents. Across most of the studies, the average correlation between the DNoS and HO reflected a small, but significant, association, with a small effect size. Additionally, we can conclude that higher DNoS in children and adolescents is associated with having better HO, as evidenced by improvements such as reduced body weight, BMI, waist circumference andbody fat percentage, and increase of cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max). However, there is not enough data to draw firm conclusions about the relationships between DNoS and sleep quality, quality of life or cardiovascular risk factors (blood pressure), and the few studies that have been conducted have produced inconsistent results.
