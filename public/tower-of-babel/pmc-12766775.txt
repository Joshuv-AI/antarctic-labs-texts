Mapping implementation barriers and facilitators of a physical activity consultation in the Portuguese primary healthcare: a qualitative approach combining the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases and Behaviour Change Wheel frameworks
Authors: Catarina Santos Silva, Marlene N Silva, Jorge Encantado, Sofia Franco, Bruno Avelar Rosa, Romeu Mendes, Pedro J Teixeira, Cristina A Godinho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite evidence of the cost-effectiveness of physical activity (PA) promotion interventions in healthcare settings, translating them into practice remains challenging. This study aimed to identify implementation barriers and facilitators of a Portuguese PA consultation programme implemented in primary healthcare of the Portuguese National Health Service. Additionally, it sought to inform future implementation strategies, using a theoretically based approach.DesignQualitative interview study, using both deductive and inductive approaches.SettingPrimary healthcare units across all health administration regions of mainland Portugal.ParticipantsTwenty-eight participants (six medical doctors, five exercise professionals and 17 patients) from all health regions of the country, involved in the implementation of the Portuguese PA prescription consultation.ResultsFifty-three categories of determinants were identified, using the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases framework. Key barriers included ineffective referral processes to the consultation, challenges in integrating the intervention with existing healthcare demands and insufficient local/regional prioritisation of PA promotion. Key facilitators included high intervention acceptability, diverse community PA resources and good interpersonal skills of implementers. Drawing on the Behaviour Change Wheel, theoretically based inputs to design strategies addressing each barrier were provided.ConclusionsThe implementation of PA consultation was influenced by a broad range of determinants. The most frequently reported barriers are primarily structural and opportunity-related, suggesting system-level implementation strategies are most appropriate. Future strategies should consider implementing clinical standards/orientations for PA promotion, providing institutional incentives based on the attainment of PA indicators, expanding consultation coverage and diversifying referral strategies, reinforcing health system-community partnerships and strengthening training opportunities for implementers. These findings offer relevant insights for enhancing the future implementation of PA consultations, for scaling them up and, ultimately, to increase their effectiveness.

Introduction

Promoting physical activity (PA) in healthcare settings is cost-effective against physical inactivity,1 a significant risk factor for non-communicable diseases and premature deaths.2 PA counselling and prescription are well-suited to primary care3 and strongly endorsed by international authorities, such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development.1 4 5 Primary healthcare offers broad population access across their life course, continuity of care and opportunities to address PA and potential links to community resources.6 7 Medical doctors are viewed as pivotal agents in encouraging patients to increase PA. They are considered a credible and trusted source, with ongoing patient contact, capable of influencing patients’ behaviours.8

Despite growing evidence supporting the effectiveness of primary care PA counselling and prescription under controlled conditions in improving patients’ PA levels,911 translating them into practice poses significant challenges.1214 In 2021, only 40% of countries had implemented a national protocol for PA promotion in healthcare settings,15 despite WHO’s Global Action Plan for Physical Activity 2018–2030 identifying this as a priority.4

In Portugal, the Directorate-General of Health under the Portuguese Ministry of Health introduced a national model in 2017 for promoting PA that included—besides PA assessment and counselling—the creation of a consultation solely dedicated to PA prescription and referral.16 The intervention components are evidence-based and align with the core elements of the Swedish Physical Activity on Prescription model, including (a) person-centred individualised counselling, (b) written prescriptions, (c) evidence-based PA recommendation, (d) follow-up and (e) supporting environment, community-based network.5 Piloted in the National Health Service, the PA consultation faced implementation challenges, underscoring the need for context-specific assessments of barriers and facilitators for better implementation.

Understanding contextual barriers and facilitators has been recommended, due to their critical role in developing tailored implementation strategies aimed at refining interventions’ implementation.1214 Implementation science emphasises this process to bridge the gap between research and practice. Barriers to PA prescription include individual factors (eg, health professionals’ knowledge or trust in referring patients to external resources), organisational issues (eg, competing time demands and challenges in coordinating with other sectors) and sociopolitical obstacles (eg, lack of financial incentives). However, few studies have applied framework-based methods to identify these determinants,17 which could provide a more comprehensive and systematic understanding of implementation context and better support applied health policy research specific aims.18 Moreover, there is limited implementation research on consultations dedicated exclusively to PA prescription and referral. Also, since some implementation barriers and facilitators can be context-specific and previous studies have focused on other countries, it is important to further investigate whether certain implementation determinants are applicable across different contexts or remain context-dependent.

This study specifically aimed to (a) identify implementation barriers and facilitators of the Portuguese PA prescription consultation and (b) explore future implementation strategies for improving these consultations and supporting the long-term maintenance and scaling-up of this and other similar interventions, using a theoretically based approach.

Methods

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive approach19 and was reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research20 (see online supplemental table 1). Data presented was collected in 2019, during the first implementation phase of the PA consultation.

Study approach

This study used a qualitative approach for identifying implementation barriers and facilitators of the PA consultation. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with both deliverers and receivers (ie, medical doctors, exercise professionals and patients) on their experiences and perspectives with the PA consultation implementation. Within a postpositivism paradigm,21 both deductive (framework-driven) and inductive (considering new emerging data not reflected in the framework) approaches were employed.

Two frameworks were used, according to study goals. To identify implementation barriers and facilitators of the Portuguese PA prescription consultation (study goal I), the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases (TICD) framework was used. The TICD framework is particularly notable for its comprehensive approach and for its focus on healthcare contexts and professional practices. It constitutes a screening tool for identifying determinants of practice and is intended to facilitate implementation studies and quality improvement initiatives. Based on 12 checklists, it comprises 57 determinants across seven domains: guideline factors, individual health professional factors, patient factors, professional interactions, incentives and resources, capacity for organisational change, and social, political and legal factors.22

To explore future implementation strategies (study goal II), the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) was applied. The BCW links behaviour change determinants to intervention options and policies, guiding how to shape and improve implementation behaviours. It offers a practical method to select appropriate intervention and policies based on identified behavioural/implementation determinants. It comprises three layers: (1) the ‘COM-B system’ at the core, composed of three interacting conditions necessary for a behaviour to occur: Capability (physical and psychological), Opportunity (physical and social) and Motivation (reflective and automatic); (2) nine intervention functions, around the COM-B axis: education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modelling and enablement; (3) seven policy categories, around the intervention functions (a policy corresponds to any action from authorities that influences interventions’ occurrence): communication/marketing, guidelines, fiscal, regulation, legislation, environmental/social planning and service provision. Definitions of intervention and policy types are provided by Michie et al.23

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The first author is a female researcher, with a background in nutrition sciences and PA. She has 9 years of working experience in trials with mixed-methods evaluation approaches, having had mentoring and observational trainings in qualitative methodologies. Other authors’ backgrounds are psychology, PA and nutrition sciences, all having expertise in qualitative research. The authors were members of the National Physical Activity Promotion Programme team at the Directorate-General of Health when this study was conducted. This work is part of a PhD in human kinetics, focused on PA and health.

The Portuguese PA consultation

The implementation of the PA consultation involved 14 health units nationwide,16 from the five Administrative Health Regions of mainland Portugal. The implementation was overseen by the Directorate-General of Health and comprised 27 implementers (14 medical doctors, 13 exercise professionals) and 217 patients. Targeting insufficiently active patients with chronic conditions (type 2 diabetes and/or depression, for pilot purposes), eligible patients were referred by their family doctor to a three to four face-to-face consultations programme with 6 months duration (figure 1). It included evidence-based guidelines,24 patient-centred counselling incorporating behaviour change techniques25 and a PA prescription, which can involve a referral and community-based support. It was implemented by a multidisciplinary team—a medical doctor graduated in Sports Medicine and an exercise professional, preferably with a master’s degree in exercise and health. Exercise professionals were sourced from local municipalities or universities, while medical doctors were specialist or intern family doctors within the health units, with an endorsed reduction in usual care to accommodate the PA consultation. All professionals attended training sessions (8 hours for medical doctors, 16 hours for exercise professionals) before implementation, covering PA prescription in type 2 diabetes and depression, behaviour change techniques, consultation protocols and local implementation planning.

In each consultation, patients’ eligibility and health status were assessed by the medical doctor, followed by a tailored PA prescription by the exercise professional (individualised exercise or a referral to a community programme). Patients could also be referred to other primary care professionals as necessary (nutritionist, psychologist, physiotherapist, etc). Follow-up calls were made by exercise professionals to assess whether adjustments to the prescription were needed. Implementation was monitored with weekly calls by the research team, to support local implementation. The pilot study included process evaluation of the PA consultation implementation (barriers and facilitators) and outcome evaluation of its impact on patients PA levels and health status. Therefore, patients underwent some assessment procedures on health indicators before each consultation at the health unit that were not related to the aims of the present study. Implemented in 2019, the pilot project was suspended in 2020 due to COVID-19 and resumed 2 years later.

Sampling

A purposeful sampling of implementers (medical doctors and exercise professionals) was conducted, according to the following procedure: (a) all medical doctors and exercise professionals from participant health units from all the five geographic health administration regions with a minimum 6-month implementation period were invited; (b) invitations were performed by phone call and all aims and procedures of the study were presented and questions clarified.

To ensure a comprehensive understanding of the implementation context, perspectives from receivers (patients) were also collected. Patients were identified through a snowball sampling technique,26 according to the following procedure: (a) medical doctors and exercise professionals who agreed to be interviewed were invited to identify patients that they considered to be information-rich cases, capable of providing detailed insights into their experience and who had received the intervention for at least 3 months in their health unit; (b) patients were then approached by the medical doctors/exercise professionals and agreed to be contacted by the research team; (c) the research team contacted the identified patients by phone call to present all study aims and procedures, invite them to participate and clarify any questions regarding the study. Participation was voluntary (no incentives provided). The final sample size was determined by data saturation.26

We used a purposeful sampling with maximum variation approach (eg, diverse geographic regions; diverse roles in the implementation, such as implementers and receivers; ages; habilitations; etc), to access different perspectives on the same subject and gain a comprehensive understanding of the global implementation phenomenon.

Data collection

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted in the local language, from 28 July 2020 to 26 July 2021. Due to COVID-19 restrictions, medical doctors and exercise professionals were interviewed via videoconference. Patients with sufficient digital skills also used videoconference, while others were interviewed by phone (n=9). We considered a videoconference format more suitable for individual interviews than for focus groups, given that for participants with lower digital literacy, phone-based focus groups would not be technically feasible. Individual interviews would also allow for more in-depth exploration of personal experiences and perspectives and allow individual participants more privacy in their responses.

Each interview was audio-recorded. CSS and JE, both members of the National Physical Activity Promotion Programme but not directly involved in the consultation implementation, conducted the interviews. Topic guides, developed from weekly monitoring notes and the TICD framework,22 explored nine theoretical domains, including recruitment, consultation model, implementation experiences, implementation fidelity (deliverers’ interviews), supporting materials and resources (deliverers’ interviews); results; COVID-19 interruption and future perspectives; intervention sustainability (deliverers’ interviews) and behaviour change maintenance (patients’ interviews) (online supplemental table 2).

Sociodemographic information, including age, pathology group (for patients), years of professional experience—both total and specific to implementing PA promotion interventions (for implementers), was also collected for sample characterisation purposes. Implementers verbally provided this information at the end of the interviews, while patients characteristics were retrieved from self-reported questionnaires in the pilot study. No adaptations to the interview guides were made during the interview period. Additionally, written records on recruitment, barriers, facilitators and local solutions were kept during weekly monitoring.

Data analysis

Recordings were transcribed verbatim using NVivo Transcription software, with accuracy checking against the audio by SF. NVivo Windows (Release 1.7.1) and Excel 365 were used for coding and data analysis. Content data analysis followed a framework approach,18 27 primarily deductive via the TICD framework,22 supplemented by an inductive approach to capture new insights. The analysis comprised five phases18 27:

Data familiarisation and initial notes: this phase was conducted by CSS and JE. Both coders read all interview transcripts and took detailed notes on six of them to immerse themselves in the data, focusing on key sections relevant to the study’s aims. Notes were taken only for a subset of the transcripts because coders were also the interviewers and were already familiar with the overall content of the interviews.

Capability-related determinants

Health professionals’ skills of interpersonal behaviour

Within psychological capability, health professionals’ skills of interpersonal behaviour were universally seen as facilitators among implementers. Positive interactions with patients during PA consultations, such as providing support, care, encouragement and clear instructions, improved patients’ motivation and engagement. For instance, an exercise professional noted:

(…) knowing how to listen to people, it is very important to know how to listen to them and know how to empathize. (EP5)

No determinants were identified for physical capability.

Opportunity-related determinants

Four determinants from the physical environment influencing intervention implementation and, thus, related to physical opportunity construct, were frequently reported by interviewees, all regarding incentives and resources.

Assistance tools and materials

Implementers unanimously highlighted the facilitating role of assistance tools and materials. Training focused on local procedures and behaviour change, comprehensive and clear manuals and devices for step counting were emphasised. Providing a written PA prescription to patients was seen as paramount for patient engagement. An exercise professional described:

I made a sheet that is very similar to a medical doctor’s prescription, isn’t it? And therefore, I wrote here, I don’t know, walking or something… the dose, let’s say, you know? And these were small strategies that I noticed that… (…) these facilitating elements that we discover throughout the process are very interesting and can actually contribute… (…) I gave you this example of making the plan layout as a medical doctor’s prescription. So I think things actually went very well in many cases. (EP4)

Information system

The PA consultation feature in the electronic information system was deemed useful but faced constraints, such as patient referral errors and scheduling issues, leading to time-consuming procedures, complicated by data protection rules preventing access to clinical data for exercise professionals. A medical doctor suggested simplifying data sharing:

(…) [the exercise professional] must type everything that’s in the document, which he receives on paper… that’s it, and… maybe it could be a little easier… I don’t know… just add a little button where, in fact, the patient accepts sharing the information (…) it goes to the exercise professional’s display. (MD5)

An exercise prescription feature within electronic medical software was also proposed:

I think that it would be perfect to have something identical to the [electronic medical software] with a set of suggestions for physical exercise and that we could also add what we have in the community, to meet people’s needs. (…) This would be interesting. (…). in an automated way and then it [the exercise prescription] either went to the patients’ cell phone and they consulted it there, or we printed it and delivered it directly to the person. (EP5)

Health facilities

Having adequate health facilities, specifically dedicated medical offices for PA consultations (a pre-requisite for the implementation) was crucial for implementation.

Human resources

Municipal provision of exercise professionals was valuable, but challenges included insufficient human resources to meet demand and reliance on a limited number of professionals. Participants suggested involving multiple professionals and advocated for political action to integrate exercise professionals into the National Health Service:

(…) it doesn’t have to be centred on one person only. I think it makes sense for two-three people, depending on the size of the Health Centre Group and eventually to be able to have this consultation (…) (MD5)

(…) efforts to have professionals hired by the National Health Service for the health centres (…) So, from a political point of view, I think this must be thought through (…). (EP1)

Several determinants linked to social opportunity, reflecting contextual factors enabling or constraining implementation behaviours, were frequently reported. This encompasses social norms and cultural factors shaping attitudes and behaviours.

Intervention characteristics

Intervention characteristics emerged as the most frequently mentioned determinant, garnering consensus among all participants. Both implementers and recipients widely accepted the consultation format and characteristics, suggesting alignment with existing societal norms, beliefs and values. Key facilitators included multidisciplinarity, patient-centredness, consultation length and frequency, follow-up calls, comprehensive assessments and flexible prescription options (individual or community-based). Biannual follow-up consultations were suggested for enhancement:

What needed to be improved was the so-called monitoring, perhaps every sixsix months (…) (P10)

Participants recommended enhancing implementation sustainability by revising the criteria for medical doctors in the consultation team and implementing a mentee format. This would involve supervising new team members without a Sports Medicine background under a qualified doctor’s guidance. One participant suggested:

We shouldn’t rely solely on one colleague… There should be a colleague with Sports Medicine training, but you can have another colleague, right? That is being supervised by them. (MD5)

Referral strategy

The referral strategy to the PA consultation was unanimously mentioned as a barrier. While patients responded positively to referrals from their family doctors, PA consultation medical staff reported a lack of referrals from colleagues within the health unit, limiting patients’ numbers significantly:

(…) referrals did not exist. (MD2)

Participants emphasised the need for implementing strategies to boost patients’ numbers, including diversifying referral sources beyond family doctors. For example, one exercise professional described a locally adopted approach:

(…) we held a meeting with all the medical doctors in the unit, where I was introduced, where it was explained the whole project, the inclusion criteria, the exclusion criteria as well… and from then on, we gave them homework: within their patients, to identify those who met the criteria to be referred. We made available an Excel file, same shared file for everyone on the network, and they began adding names in there (…). (EP4)

PA opportunities or resources for practice

The variety of PA opportunities or resources, from individual to group activities, was emphasised as crucial to meet diverse patients’ needs and preferences. Implementers universally mentioned it and patients highly appreciated it. For example, two patients with different PA preferences noted:

Being in a group setting is completely different, people make new friends and get to know each other… it’s different… For me, it’s more positive. (P17)

I would participate again, because, well, I could do it at home. (P14)

However, scheduling conflicts during working hours were cited as a common barrier to group PA programmes, limiting patient participation.

Networks or partnerships

Establishing networks between the health sector and community, particularly municipalities, emerged as a universal determinant among professionals interviewed, acting both as barrier and facilitator. It enhanced communication and enabled better patient follow-up and problem-solving. For example, one exercise professional noted:

It was very interesting, because I was not just confined within four walls, I went out into the field to see how things were working and find the best solutions, so that the connections could really work. (EP5)

Partnerships offered benefits like access to neighbourhood PA resources maps and reduced or no costs for referred patients. However, they occasionally complicated organisational procedures, causing delays and coordination issues between parties. Another exercise professional mentioned:

What took the longest was this partnership with [the Municipality enterprise on physical activity], which still took a few months, and, when it was set, there were some obstacles because patients arrived there with the letter we gave them and whoever was there had no knowledge of the partnership. (…) There was some difficulty getting started, but then from the moment that happened, it went well. (EP2)

Prioritisation of PA promotion

Local and regional prioritisation of PA consultation implementation varied among organisations. According to professionals, some health units prioritised PA promotion, receiving strong support from management, while others struggled to gain traction. During the COVID-19 pandemic, efforts shifted to pandemic control, resulting in the suspension of PA consultations, due to safety concerns from health authorities for at-risk patients visiting health units. This raised concerns among implementers about potential setbacks and challenges in programme resumption. For example, one exercise professional expressed concerns:

Well, we haven't been in the health unit since… since March 13th. Our contact is by telephone with Doctor [MD4]. We are discussing, (…) trying to figure out what to do, whether to keep going or wait. This kind of thing really gets to me, because we’re losing. We’ve lost. We are losing them [the patients referred to the PA consultation] (…) We’re losing them and it’s going to be tough. (EP3)

Intervention factors

Regarding intervention factors, two characteristics were frequently highlighted by all participants. First, the observed benefits of the intervention in improving patient health outcomes were frequently mentioned as a major facilitator. Second, concerns were raised by medical doctors and exercise professionals regarding the feasibility and compatibility of the intervention with routine care. To enhance compatibility with primary care demands, there was emphasis on formally allocating working hours to the PA consultation and addressing concerns about time constraints for all assistance activities. For instance, one medical doctor stated:

The problem is everything that comes before that, isn’t it? All the needs that the health units are asked to respond to, to reach certain objectives (…). (MD2)

This also underscores why intern medical doctors were considered more suitable for PA consultation implementation, given their flexible working hours and lack of a fixed patient list.

Team processes

PA consultation team processes were frequently mentioned as crucial, with positive team climate and alignment towards the same goals enhancing implementation. This alignment was potentiated when teams were well organised and everyone had a role, felt involved and was well-informed. However, deviations in initial consultation-related tasks and work distribution, requiring adjustments, posed barriers. Under team processes, communication processes were emphasised by all professionals at both community and intrateam levels. Establishing effective communication channels, like routine updates between the health team and community PA coordinators (facilitated by formal networks with municipalities), was considered a significant facilitator. Formal weekly case discussions within the health team were also suggested for better coordination. One exercise professional mentioned:

(…) if something is to be implemented consistently, having at least two moments a week where the team meets would be important. (EP2)

Assessment procedures

Assessment procedures associated with the pilot study negatively impacted implementation. The assessment questionnaires were deemed excessively time-consuming and disruptive for patients, affecting intervention processes and team organisation:

The questionnaire, taking an hour or more, was difficult to manage… even patients felt it was too long, becoming tired by the end of it… (EP5)

Organisational regulations, rules or policies

Although only a medical doctor cited a determinant within organisational regulations, rules, policies category, it would probably constitute a pivotal facilitator within the primary healthcare context. He mentioned the existence of contractual indicators for PA prescription in health units:

If at a national level a contractual indicator for prescribing PA is implemented, it [the implementation] definitely increases. It’s obvious. (MD2)

This participant briefly highlighted that the setting of delivery, namely the health region to which the health unit belongs, can also positively or negatively influence implementation. One exercise professional (EP3) also mentioned that the health setting, in general, felt somewhat unfamiliar compared with more informal contexts.

Motivation-related determinants

Motivation of receivers and implementers

Patients’ and professionals’ motivations were very frequently mentioned regarding the sustainability and success of the implementation. Particularly, professionals’ proactivity regarding implementation and the referral of motivated patients were highlighted as pivotal. One exercise professional explained:

People were not motivated and sometimes said ‘yes’ to the family doctor, but then missed the appointment. (EP5)

Implementers’ cognitions and attitudes

Although not the most frequently mentioned, professionals’ positive cognitions and attitudes towards PA and the intervention may have played a significant facilitating role among implementers. Valuing PA for its positive health benefits, practising PA, identifying with the intervention’s principles and viewing the PA consultation as a valuable intervention nationwide were motivational factors for medical doctors and exercise professionals to implement the intervention.

Receivers’ expectations

Patients’ expectations regarding the intervention were less frequently reported either, but they may have significantly contributed to patients’ commitment and retention. Positive expectations regarding the health benefits of PA and the perception that the PA consultation would provide additional assistance in improving quality of life were commonly mentioned facilitators by patients. However, a few were disappointed not to engage in PA during the consultation.

The identified implementation barriers and facilitators were generally shared among medical doctors, exercise professionals and patients, indicating broad agreement. However, some barriers were perceived differently among subgroups. Only medical doctors reported team communication issues, suggesting they experienced more coordination challenges. Implementers (doctors and exercise professionals) cited feasibility, resource shortages and system constraints, but these factors were not noted by patients, implying they primarily affected service delivery rather than patient experience.

Discussion

This study investigated barriers, facilitators and future strategies for implementing a PA prescription consultation in Portuguese primary care, using the TICD and BCW frameworks. The PA consultation was a pioneering intervention in the national context. While some research has examined similar initiatives, this is the first study to focus specifically on the Portuguese healthcare system. It is also the first to include perspectives from both implementers and patients. A key finding is that the most frequently reported barriers are primarily structural and opportunity-related, suggesting that system-level strategies are most appropriate to enhance future implementation (figure 2).

Main findings of the study. C, Capability-related; M, Motivation-related; O, Opportunity-related.

Top barriers included an ineffective referral process, a conflict with existing healthcare demands and lack of organisational prioritisation. Main facilitators pertained to high intervention acceptability, diverse community PA resources and strong interpersonal skills among implementers. Although most themes were commonly reported by all participants, challenges related to service delivery—such as issues with feasibility, resource shortages and constraints within the informatic system—primarily impacted service delivery more than patient experience.

Barriers and facilitators spanned all seven TICD domains, highlighting its value as a comprehensive tool for designing and improving intervention implementation. Regarding intervention characteristics, in 52 studies Mino et al29 identified the impact of PA referral schemes’ components on PA levels, scheme uptake and adherence. In their review, screening, a person-centred approach and brief counselling were the only positive predictors of adherence. Integrating their findings with our multidimensional perspective can provide more comprehensive insights for optimising intervention design and implementation.

At the resources’ level, offering diverse PA options and establishing formal protocols supported patient access to community-based services. Although some units experienced delays in setting up these protocols, partnerships with municipalities and universities were key facilitators, echoing findings from a recent systematic review.17 However, only one study specifically analysing PA prescription interventions applied a qualitative design. Using semi-structured group interviews, it explored general practitioners’ and nurses’ self-perceptions regarding the implementation of PA on prescription interventions in primary care centres.30 However, it did not evaluate the implementation of a real-world intervention (instead, it focused on general perspectives) nor the perspectives of patients and exercise professionals. Even so, participants pointed to a lack of knowledge among health staff about external PA resources available for referrals. The study emphasised the value of building community and municipal networks for improving patient access to PA resources (eg, through economic incentives linked to healthcare prescriptions) and for involving specialists in the care of patients with specific conditions.30 These findings align with recommendations from WHO4 and the European Commission,31 which advocate for promoting PA through intersectoral collaboration and community programmes.

While the intervention’s multidisciplinarity, patient-centredness and follow-up calls were praised, concerns were raised about its integration into primary care routines. Staffing shortages and heavy workloads in the Portuguese National Health Service,32 mirroring global healthcare trends,33 often undermined plans to reduce doctors’ caseloads to accommodate PA consultations. This frequently led participants to identify feasibility and compatibility as barriers. Patients’ referral to the PA consultation was also challenging, increasing the burden on implementers. In some units, interns helped alleviate burden, but their high turnover after specialisation threatened sustainability. Previous systematic reviews similarly identified lack of time, misalignment with local routines and high workloads as barriers to implementation.17 34 35 This underscores the need to adapt interventions to contextual characteristics. Two interview-based studies evaluating PA on prescription models indicated that primary care staff and management generally agree the intervention is time-consuming and may constrain other healthcare activities.30 36 However, neither included patients, and one did not assess a directly experienced intervention, limiting the scope of comparison. At the individual level, despite literature highlighting implementers’ lack of knowledge and skills as significant barriers,17 34 35 these factors were infrequently cited. The requirement for implementers to possess specific qualifications and pre-implementation training likely reduced the prominence of this barrier. Still, some participants noted that behaviour change training could be strengthened, suggesting that certain knowledge gaps remain.

A relevant topic to explore is why implementation barriers are mainly structural, whereas facilitators tend to emerge at individual and group levels. This points to a mismatch: even proactive intervention adopters and positive group dynamics can be hindered by unsupportive systems. Individual and system-level factors coexist and interact, highlighting the need for future implementation strategies to clearly address system policies, besides individual or team-based factors only. This aligns with the view of Chater and Loewenstein,37 which contrasts individual-focused interventions (i-frame) with system-focused policies (s-frame). They argue that overemphasis on individual interventions, without addressing system issues, has been ineffective and diverts attention from more impactful systemic changes. They emphasise the role of behavioural research in supporting system-level solutions, noting that s-frame policies can be more effective when informed by i-frame research.37 The European Union Policy Lab has also recently recommended integrating behavioural insights early in the policy cycle to better shape complex systems and enhance policy effectiveness.38

Using the BCW, we identified strategies to address common barriers, which typically emerged at system level. Environmental restructuring, regulation and service provision policies could target opportunity and automatic motivation-related barriers. Suggested actions include expanding consultation coverage across the Health Centre Group to serve a greater number of citizens living in the area, as well as diversifying referral strategies to enhance referrals to the consultation (eg, creating an online file for family doctors to identify patients, reminder materials of inclusion criteria). Allowing direct patient booking could also be considered to increase referrals from family doctors. Restructuring regulations/legislation could improve integration into routine care. This may involve introducing national clinical standards or orientations regarding PA promotion, allocating human resources, creating additional paid time slots for PA consultations in professionals’ schedules or providing institutional incentives linked to the attainment of PA indicators, under a value-based healthcare model. Such measures would enhance the intervention’s compatibility with routine tasks and strengthen its prioritisation within organisations. Improving software features (ie, sharing clinical data with exercise professionals, preset prescription form) and training would support implementation. Strengthening team support at service provision level, particularly in task management, would enhance team processes. Table 4 outlines additional strategies.

Studies investigating implementation strategies to enhance the implementation of PA consultations in primary care are lacking. Gustavsson et al39 tested the feasibility of six implementation strategies in the Swedish PA on prescription model delivered in primary care. Management support, educational lectures, local champions and external facilitation by a researcher were the most impactful. However, these strategies were insufficient to ensure the sustainability of the PA intervention. These findings, alongside ours, provide guidance in translating similar interventions into primary care.

Strengths and limitations

This research assessed implementation determinants based on how frequently participants reported them, which may not reflect the objective importance of each factor. It relied on subjective perceptions and individuals’ retrospective experiences, which could lead to recall and social desirability biases, potentially overlooking key determinants. Additionally, women represented only one-third of the sample and no patients were recruited from the Algarve Health Region. The videoconference and phone call format of the interviews may have also limited engagement compared with in-person interviews, potentially resulting in less detailed responses.

A significant strength of the study is its practical applicability to real-world settings. The findings provide clear inputs on enhancing the implementation of PA prescription-based consultations in primary care, making them valuable to policymakers and adaptable to similar contexts. Additionally, the use of validated frameworks and models (TICD, COM-B and BCW) to collect and interpret data increases confidence in the results. These frameworks recognise multiple levels of influence and interconnectedness of various determinants, offering a holistic understanding of the system. Considering its individual components, as well as the relationships among them, contributes to a better understanding of the integrated whole system.40 The qualitative approach employed is also beneficial. Unlike relying solely on survey questionnaires, which could bias results by restricting determinants’ selection,40 the qualitative method provides a broader and more nuanced understanding. Using diverse data sources, such as interview transcripts and monitoring notes, enabled data triangulation, thereby reducing observer bias.

Future research

Incorporating methods such as Intervention Mapping, concept mapping, conjoint analysis and system dynamics modelling can enhance the systematic design, selection and tailoring of implementation strategies by addressing key contextual factors.41 Although it remains uncommon to test the mechanisms through which strategies exert their effects, understanding these causal pathways would help to clarify these complex implementation processes. This approach goes beyond the identification of barriers and facilitators, enabling the design of more effective, multilevel implementation strategies tailored to specific settings. Another important area for future research is the economic evaluation of implementation strategies to identify the most cost-effective options.41 Considering our results, investigating the cost-effectiveness of financial incentives, such as contractual indicators for PA consultations, could be particularly relevant. This would enable comparison between the required investment and the potential healthcare savings associated with increased PA levels among patients.

Conclusion

The PA consultation programme faced several implementation barriers, primarily structural and opportunity-related. Barriers included low referrals from family doctors, integration challenges with existing healthcare demands, lack of organisational prioritisation, insufficient human resources, time-consuming assessments, software limitations and issues in teamwork distribution. Using the BCW, system-level strategies were proposed to address these barriers, such as incentives based on PA indicators attainment, expanding consultation coverage and diversifying referral strategies, increasing human resources and improving the information system. These findings contribute to understanding the complexities of implementing PA consultations in primary care and provide valuable insights for enhancing similar programmes.
