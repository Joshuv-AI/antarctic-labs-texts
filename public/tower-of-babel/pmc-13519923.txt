Development of Oral pH-Sensitive Eudragit L100-Functionalized Bombyx mori Silk Fibroin Nanoparticles Containing Amphotericin B: Formulations, Characterizations, In Silico Molecular Investigations, and Antifungal Activity Evaluation
Authors: Bui Thi Phuong Thuy, Phuong T. M. Ha, Ngoc Yen Nguyen, Nguyen Trong Tuan, Huynh Vu Thanh Luong, Quyen Thi Bich Tran, Trieu Phu Hau, Duy Toan Pham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amphotericin B (AmB) is a poorly water-soluble drug with low oral bioavailability and high nephrotoxicity. Thus, this study developed oral AmB-loaded pH-sensitive Eudragit L100-functionalized silk fibroin nanoparticles (SF/EL-AmB) to enhance its oral bioavailability and reduce its renal toxicity. The particles were formulated using two different methods of desolvation and adsorption, followed by thorough characterizations regarding particle size, polydispersity index, zeta potential, chemical interactions, AmB entrapment efficiency, specific surface area, drug release in simulated oral conditions, and in vitro antifungal activity. The optimal formula (SF:EL ratio of 1:3 and initial AmB amount of 0.5 mg) yielded particles with average size of 577.9 nm, zeta potential of −47.9 mV, drug loading efficiency of 77.99 ± 2.35%, specific surface area of 38.969 m2/g with mostly mesopores, and moderate in vitro antifungal activity on Candida albicans with IC50 of 4.09 ± 0.21 μg/mL and MIC of 32.0 μg/mL. Analytical techniques (XRD, DSC, and FTIR) and in silico modeling confirmed that AmB was entrapped within the SF/EL nanoparticles in an amorphous molecular dispersion state via hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions between AmB and charged residues of SF, notably Lys82, Glu98, and Lys62. Interestingly, the SF/EL nanoparticles could protect AmB through the stomach acidic condition and control its release rate in the small intestine based on the formulation methods (desolvation or adsorption). Lastly, the SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles could reduce AmB aggregation forms, which theoretically links to a decrease in AmB nephrotoxicity. In conclusion, the SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles could be further investigated to become a novel product for oral AmB delivery.

Introduction

Amphotericin B (AmB) is a potent antifungal medication commonly used to treat both local and systemic fungal infections. − AmB exhibits amphiphilic property due to the polar and nonpolar sides of the lactone ring, as well as amphoteric property due to the presence of ionizable carboxyl and amine groups. Accordingly, under physiological pH conditions, AmB readily undergoes self-association, with a critical aggregation concentration of approximately 0.2 μg/mL, which in turn results in extremely limited aqueous solubility, typically below 1 μg/mL. AmB, classified as group IV by the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS), is poorly water-soluble and has low oral bioavailability. Therefore, for parenteral administration, AmB is frequently coupled with sodium deoxycholate, resulting in a clear micellar colloidal complex in which only a portion of AmB is dissolved. Consequently, the use of intravenous AmB is frequently associated with toxicity from infusion-related responses and, especially, renal damage. , The rate of AmB renal toxicity is high, and the mechanism of toxicity is linked to direct cell membrane activation that increases permeability. Despite its high toxicity, AmB has highly powerful antifungal capabilities, making its use in the treatment of systemic fungi required. Therefore, to lower the toxicity of AmB, enhance solubility and bioavailability, and make it more convenient for patients, oral AmB formulations are a good approach.

Regarding AmB oral formulations, AmB dissolves in pH < 2, making it less stable in the digestive tract and resulting in reduced drug bioavailability. Thus, it requires a carrier to protect AmB, control its release rate, and enhance its oral bioavailability. In this present work, to fabricate the nanocarrier for AmB, we combined a natural polymer fibroin (SF, a protein extracted from Bombyx mori silk that has been approved by US FDA as a biomaterial for drug delivery) for biocompatibility and controllable drug release purposes, − and Eudragit L100 (EL, also an FDA-approved polymer for drug delivery) for protecting AmB through the acidic environment. Thus, this work could give more insights into this potential combination for AmB oral delivery.

Nanobased drug delivery systems have increasingly been investigated. , To this end, SF-based nanoparticles have garnered significant attention in biomedical research due to their unique physicochemical properties, making them highly suitable for various medical and cosmetic applications. ,, These nanoparticles exhibit biodegradability, as they are naturally degraded by enzymatic activity within the body. Furthermore, their biocompatibility ensures minimal toxicity and immune response, rendering them safe for medical use. Additionally, their capability for controlled drug release allows for sustained and targeted therapeutic delivery, enhancing efficacy. − Another crucial characteristic is their surface modifiability, which enables functionalization for specific biomedical applications. Moreover, the synthesis of SF-based nanoparticles can be achieved through various techniques, each influencing the final properties of the nanoparticles. The desolvation method involves the addition of organic solvents, such as ethanol or acetone, to an aqueous SF solution, leading to nanoparticle formation. Alternatively, ionic gelation utilizes cross-linkers like polyethylene glycol and chitosan to facilitate nanoparticle synthesis. − Emulsion-based methods, which rely on water-in-oil emulsions, offer a controlled environment for SF-based nanoparticle formation. Furthermore, functionalization the SF-based nanoparticles with other polymers such as Eudragit, PVA, and PEG significantly enhances SF’s ability to deliver different drugs/extracts and increases its versatility. − Although SF-based nanoparticles have been explored for oral drug delivery, most studies use only the silk fibroin, which hinders the versatility of the system. , Thus, in this work, we functionalized the SF nanoparticles with EL as a novel system to orally deliver AmB.

Eudragit is a synthetic polymer formed by the copolymerization of acrylic acid and methacrylic acid, or their esters, which is frequently utilized in pharmaceutical purposes because of its pH-sensitive ability. L100 and S100 are two commonly utilized Eudragit polymers for the gastrointestinal tract. EL is soluble at pH > 6 and is ideal for loading medications that must be released in the small intestine. Furthermore, EL is a polyanion that has been combined with chitosan to yield EL100-coated chitosan-based nanoparticles for oral drug administration. In our previous work, we were successful in developing Eudragit E100-functionalized SF particles for oral delivery of AmB that possessed particle sizes of approximately 800 nm and AmB encapsulation efficiencies of more than 70%. The particles could protect AmB in the gastric acidic condition and sustain AmB release in the small intestine, as well as demonstrate moderate antifungal activity. Nevertheless, the release profiles of those particles were not precisely controlled and were somehow fluctuated in the intestine (as Eudragit E100 is not a polymer mainly used for intestinal release since it is dissolved in pH 1–5). Moreover, the previous work did not fully evaluate the chemical interactions between the carrier SF and the encapsulated drug AmB via in silico simulations. Therefore, to further optimize oral formulations for AmB, the combination of EL (an intestinal-sensitive polymer) with SF is an efficient approach.

Accordingly, in this study, we first developed novel SF/EL nanoparticles, varying different SF and EL ratios, and encapsulated the drug AmB, using the simple desolvation method. Then, to evaluate the interactions between the carrier materials and AmB, an AmB adsorption study and in silico molecular docking/dynamics simulation were conducted. The optimal formula was physicochemically characterized in terms of size, ζ-potential, morphology, surface area, thermal behavior, crystallinity, and chemical interactions. Next, the AmB release profile in simulated oral conditions was studied, and the AmB antifungal activity was conducted in an in vitro model. Finally, the preliminary renal toxicity of AmB in SF/EL nanoparticles was assessed via the AmB aggregation state.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Bombyx mori silkworm cocoons were purchased from Ninh Binh, Vietnam. Fibroin was extracted from the cocoons following a standardized two-step method (sericin removal and fibroin desolvation), structural confirmation by FTIR, and freeze-dried (Heto PowerDry LL3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at −60 °C and 0.1 mbar for 48 h for all investigations. EL was supplied by Evonik Chemicals. AmB was bought from Sigma-Aldrich, Singapore. Other common chemicals/reagents/solvents were supplied by Xilong Chemicals, China.

Preparations of SF/EL, SF/EL-AmB Nanoparticles

The nanoparticulate system was formulated using the desolvation approach. The blank particles (SF/EL) were formulated alongside the AmB-loaded particles (SF/EL-AmB). To this end, a 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 (v/v) ratio of 1% SF solution to 1% EL ethanolic solution was utilized. For the blank particles, the SF solution was added dropwise into the EL solution, and the mixture was thoroughly stirred for 1 h. The nanoparticles were formed spontaneously. The solvents were removed by centrifugation (Mikro 220R, Hettich, Germany, 31,514g, 20 min), and the particles were collected at the bottom of the centrifuge tubes. The particles were then washed thrice with water by redispersing in water and centrifugation. Finally, the particles were freeze-dried (Heto PowerDry LL3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at −60 °C and 0.1 mbar for 48 h.

For the AmB-loaded particles, AmB was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide to a concentration of 10 mg/mL. Then, 50 μL (0.5 mg AmB) or 100 μL (1 mg AmB) were mixed with the polymer solution, and the mixture was gradually dropped into the fibroin solution. The mixture was then stirred for 1 h, and the SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles were recovered by centrifugation (Mikro 220R, Hettich, Germany, 31,514g, 20 min), washed, and freeze-dried (Heto PowerDry LL3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at −60 °C and 0.1 mbar for 48 h. The supernatant after centrifugation was utilized to determine the unloaded AmB quantity using UV–Vis spectroscopy (V-730, Jasco, Japan) at a wavelength of 408 nm with the standard curve equation y = 0.0866x – 0.0026, R 2 = 0.9995. The blank sample for UV–vis measurement was also conducted similarly to that of the AmB-loaded particles, without the presence of AmB. Adsorption Study of AmB onto the SF/EL Nanoparticles

To further evaluate the interactions of AmB and SF/EL nanoparticles, the adsorption study was conducted. For this reason, 0.03 g of the blank SF/EL nanoparticles were dispersed in AmB solutions (10 μg/mL, 25 mL) at pH 2 (the pH of AmB cationic form) or pH 11 (the pH of AmB anionic form). The mixtures were shaken at 150 rpm for 4 h. At 30 min intervals, 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn and refilled with 1 mL buffer to determine the AmB concentration using the UV–vis method (V-730, Jasco, Japan; pH 2, λmax = 384 nm, standard curve y = 0.0158x + 0.0069, R 2 = 0.9924; pH 11, λmax = 384 nm, y = 0.0411x + 0.0013, R 2 = 0.9991). The adsorption efficiency (%H) was calculated using eq (). 2 %H=m0−mm0×100%

where m o is the initial amount of AmB (μg) and m is the residual AmB amount at each time point (μg).

To elucidate the AmB and SF/EL interactions, the adsorption process data were then fitted by various adsorption kinetics models, including the pseudo-first-order kinetics (PFO), pseudo-second-order kinetics (PSO), and the Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R) isotherm.

Molecular Docking and Dynamics Simulations

To deeply understand the molecular interactions between AmB and SF and/or EL, we performed the in silico docking and dynamics simulations study. Accordingly, the fibroin protein N-terminal structure (3UA0) was recovered from protein data bank (https://www.rcsb.org/structure/3UA0). EL was represented by oligomeric segments to model the polymeric carrier environment and to assess its influence on AmB (ligand) binding behavior. All molecular structures were protonated at pH 7.0, supplemented with hydrogen atoms, and energy-minimized prior to docking. Docking was performed using a two-step protocol consisting of geometry-based ligand placement followed by rigid-receptor refinement with implicit solvent energy evaluation. Docking results were ranked based on docking score (DS ≤ −4.0 kcal/mol), clustered using a root-mean-squared deviation (RMSD) threshold of <2.0 Å, and filtered according to geometric plausibility. Hydrogen bonds, π-related interactions, and van der Waals forces between ligands and active-site residues were subsequently identified for interaction analysis.

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS, with the CHARMM27 all-atom force field selected for SF parametrization and a compatible CHARMM-based topology applied for AmB. The systems were solvated in an explicit TIP3P water model, as recommended for CHARMM27, and supplemented with 0.15 M NaCl to reproduce physiological ionic strength, with protonation states corresponding to pH 7.0. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all of the directions. Energy minimization was performed to remove steric clashes, followed by equilibration in the NVT and NPT ensembles with positional restraints on heavy atoms. Production molecular dynamics simulations were carried out for 200 ns under constant-temperature (300 K) and -pressure conditions (1 atm). Trajectory analyses were focused on RMSD to evaluate global conformational stability and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) to characterize residue-level flexibility of SF during the simulation.

Characterization Methods

Particles Sizes and Zeta Potentials

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) combined with phase analysis light scattering (PALS) techniques was employed to determine the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the SF/EL nanoparticles. Measurements were carried out using a DLS machine (SZ-100, Horiba, Japan) at 25 °C. The samples were diluted 100-fold relative to the original concentrated sample. Then, 2 mL of the diluted sample was transferred into a polystyrene cuvette for measurement. The zeta potential measurements were conducted at the neutral pH of 7.0. In addition, for the discussion on the interactions between AmB and the particles, the particle zeta potentials were also measured at pH 2 and pH 11.

Morphology

The SF/EL nanoparticles were morphologically characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JCM 7000, JEOL, Japan). A dilute nanoparticle dispersion was prepared in deionized water and briefly sonicated for 5 min. A 10 μL aliquot was drop-cast onto a clean aluminum SEM stub premounted with conductive carbon tape. Samples were air-dried at room temperature and sputter-coated with a thin conductive layer of gold using a sputter coater. SEM imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage (accelerating voltage) of ∼3–10 kV and a working distance of ∼5–10 mm.

Surface Area

The specific surface area of the SF/EL nanoparticles was determined by using nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms based on the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) theory. Measurements were carried out at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K) using a surface area analyzer (Nova, Anton Paar, Austria), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Thermal Behaviors

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed using a Netzsch DSC 200 F3, Germany, to evaluate the thermal behavior of the SF/EL nanoparticles. Approximately 3 mg of each dried sample was accurately weighed and sealed in an aluminum pan, with an empty pan used as the reference. The measurement was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidative degradation. The samples were heated from 30 to 280 °C at a constant heating rate of 1 °C/min.

Crystalline Structure

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to analyze the crystalline structure and phase composition of the SF/EL nanoparticles. The measurements were performed using a D8 Advance (Bruker, USA) diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 5°–50° with a step size of 0.2° and a scan rate of 1°/min.

Chemical Interactions

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted to identify the functional groups and chemical interactions present in SF/EL nanoparticles. The spectra were recorded using a Thermo Nicolet 6700 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) spectrometer in the range of 4000–400 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–1. The samples were prepared by using the KBr pelleting method. Approximately 1–2 mg of the finely ground sample was mixed with about 100 mg of dry KBr powder. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized and pressed into the pellets. The prepared pellets were then placed in the sample holder, and the spectra were recorded under the ambient conditions.

AmB Release Study

To investigate the release profiles of AmB in the gastrointestinal tract, the AmB-loaded nanoparticles (20 mg) was dispersed in a simulated gastrointestinal environment, at 37 °C, including a gastric medium at pH 1.2 (HCl + 1% Tween 80, 10 mL) for 2 h and an intestinal medium at pH 6.8 (PBS + 1% trypsin +1% Tween 80, 40 mL) for 6 h. To mimic the real oral tract, the sink condition was not utilized in this case. The drug release in both media was determined by collecting buffer samples (1 mL) at 30 min intervals, centrifugation (Mikro 220R, Hettich, Germany, 31,514g, 2 min), and measuring the supernatant absorbance using UV–vis spectroscopy (V-730, Jasco, Japan). The maximum wavelength of AmB in pH 1.2 was 382 nm, y = 0.0111x + 8.10–5, R 2 = 0.9997, while in pH 6.8 was 412 nm, y = 0.0547x + 0.0033, R 2 = 0.9998. Antifungal Activity

The antifungal activities of the pure AmB, the blank SF/EL nanoparticles, and the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles were evaluated using the agar diffusion and broth microdilution assays against the reference strain Candida albicans (ATCC 10231) by an external service of the Institute of Chemistry, Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology, Ha Noi, Vietnam. The antifungal effect was quantified using the 50%-Inhibitory concentration (IC50) and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values. The culture media used included Sabouraud dextrose broth (SDB, 2% dextrose) and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SA, 4% dextrose). Test samples were serially diluted in the culture medium to obtain 4–10 concentrations for activity evaluation. The pure AmB was first dissolved in DMSO and subsequently diluted with the culture medium for experimental tests. The final concentration of DMSO for all tested concentration was less than 1%, which did not significantly affect the fungi behaviors. For the assay, the reference strain (C. albicans, passage 5) was stored at −80 °C and reactivated in SDB prior to testing and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 103 CFU/mL. Then, 10 μL of each test sample at the desired concentration was added to a 96-well microplate, followed by 190 μL of the activated fungal suspension. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and UV–vis was measured at 600 nm. The percentage of inhibition was calculated by eq () (the positive control was the wells treated with the standard drug nystatin, and the negative control was the wells with no treatment). The MIC value is defined as the lowest concentration of the tested substance that completely inhibits the growth of C. albicans. AmB Aggregation State

AmB has, at least, three main molecular states, including the monomeric form (i.e., nonaggregated form), the water-soluble oligomers (also known as the dimeric form, the most renal toxic form), and the nonwater-soluble aggregates (polyaggregated form). The aggregation state of AmB could be simply assessed using UV–vis spectroscopy (V-730, Jasco, Japan). Thus, the pure AmB and AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles were dispersed in solution at various pH (for pH 6.8, 1% Tween 80 was also added to solubilize the AmB) and vortexed for 15 min. Following dispersion, the samples were centrifuged (18000g, 5 min), and the supernatants were analyzed using a Jasco V-730 UV–vis spectrophotometer in the wavelength range of 250–550 nm. The aggregation state of AmB was determined based on the absorbance intensity of characteristic peaks (obtained from the numerical values of the spectra Excel data files), specifically peak I (325–340 nm) and peak IV (405–408 nm). The ratio of peak I to peak IV (I/IV) was used to evaluate the aggregation state, of which an I/IV ratio <0.25 indicates monomeric AmB, a ratio between 0.25 and 2 suggests partial aggregation, and a ratio >2 indicates superaggregated AmB.

Statistical Analysis

All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t test were used for statistical comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussions

In this work, we developed AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles for oral antifungal activity. First, the particles were physicochemically characterized. Then, the interactions between AmB and SF/EL were elucidated through adsorption experiments in combination with in silico molecular simulations. Furthermore, the release behavior of AmB under the simulated gastrointestinal conditions was investigated. Then, the antifungal efficacy of the AmB-loaded nanoparticles was assessed. Finally, the AmB renal toxicity was preliminarily assessed and compared with various products.

Physicochemical Characterizations

Particles Sizes and Zeta Potentials

The particle sizes varied significantly when the SF:EL ratio was increased from 1:1 to 1:3 (Table ). Fibroin’s amphiphilic property allows it to self-organize into particles when the solvent changes, via hydrogen bonds formed between the light/heavy chains’ components. The nanoparticle system was formed simultaneously with EL polymer chains dissolved in ethanol, resulting in particles with a structure that contains both fibroin and EL. It is worth noting that the polydispersity indexes (PDI) were quite high for these particles, yet the final product possessed a PDI of ≈0.3, which is acceptable for nanoparticles in drug delivery. ,

For the blank SF/EL nanoparticles, increasing the EL amounts (from SF:EL ratio = 1:1 to 1:3) resulted in a marked decrease in particle size, from the micrometer range (≈1058 nm) to submicron nanoparticles (≈ 268 nm). This trend suggests that EL acts as an effective electrosteric stabilizer, promoting tighter polymer packing and partly suppressing SF self-aggregation. At low EL content (1:1), SF-SF hydrophobic interactions and β-sheet formation dominate, leading to extensive aggregation and large particle sizes. In contrast, higher EL ratios introduce more carboxylate groups, enhancing interpolymer repulsion and stabilizing smaller particles. This phenomenon was also observed in SF functionalized with other polymers like PVA, PEG, or PVP. ,, Furthermore, the particle zeta potentials was approximately −30 mV, which was due to the negatively charged SF at neutral pH of 7.0, indicating colloidal stability. These values were indifferent when the SF:EL ratio was increased from 1:1 to 1:3, suggesting that the effects of EL on the particles’ zeta potentials were not significant. Conclusively, the formula with an SF/EL ratio of 1:3, with the smallest particle size and PDI, was selected to encapsulate the AmB.

To this end, AmB was successfully loaded into the SF/EL nanoparticles with a high %EE (≈76–78%) for both 0.5 and 1 mg initial AmB loads, highlighting strong drug-matrix affinity. This high EE can be attributed to hydrophobic interactions between AmB and SF domains, combined with ionic and hydrogen-bonding interactions involving EL carboxyl groups, which collectively reduce drug diffusion into the external phase during nanoparticle formation. These interactions are discussed in detail in Section . Importantly, increasing the AmB initial amount from 0.5 to 1 mg did not significantly improve %EE, suggesting that the SF/EL matrix approaches a loading saturation threshold, beyond which additional drug contributes primarily to particle growth rather than increased encapsulation efficiency. , Additionally, upon AmB incorporation, particle size increased substantially compared to the corresponding blank nanoparticles. This size expansion indicates that AmB, a big molecule, could induce matrix swelling and alter SF β-sheet formation and partial interparticle bridging. Last but not least, the zeta potential became more negative after AmB loading (≈−48 to −51 mV), reflecting the contribution of AmB’s ionizable groups and possible enrichment of EL carboxylate moieties at the particle surface. Although the literature commonly shows that the smaller the nanoparticles sizes, the better their oral bioavailability, a particle size of approximately 500 nm (as in this study) is acceptable for most oral nanoparticles for drug delivery, including average mucus penetration and intestinal uptake. Specifically, in our previous work, the diclofenac-loaded PVP-functionalized fibroin nanoparticles, with sizes of 400 to 800 nm, showed 20% to 30% higher anti-inflammatory effect than the pure diclofenac in an in vivo mouse model.

Ultimately, due to the smaller particle size and PDI, the 0.5 mg-initial-AmB-loaded SF/EL (ratio 1:3) nanoparticles were chosen for further investigations.

Morphology and Surface Area

SEM and BET were employed to examine the morphology and surface characteristics of the SF/EL nanoparticles before and after loading with AmB (Figure ). Before drug loading, the particle surfaces exhibited numerous folds and irregularities. Following AmB loading, the particles displayed a more spherical morphology, which may be attributed to AmB occupying the surface folds. Furthermore, the blank SF/EL nanoparticles exhibited a specific surface area of 38.969 m2/g, with 57.78% of the pore volume corresponding to mesopores (2–50 nm) and 42.22% to macropores (>50 nm). Therefore, AmB, with a molecular surface area of approximately 148 Å, could be easily adsorbed onto the particle surface, and could penetrate into the particle structure through the pores.

Thermal Behaviors and Crystalline Structure

The particles’ crystallinity and thermal behaviors were evaluated using XRD and DSC, respectively (Figure ). Regarding the XRD graph, the pure AmB shows multiple sharp, intense Bragg peaks (notably in the ∼10–25° 2θ region), with the peak locations in agreement with the literature, confirming that the raw drug is highly crystalline. The blank SF/EL nanoparticles present distinctive peaks of fibroin silk-II structure at 2θ locations of ∼24°, corresponding to the crystallization peaks of the β-sheet structure, , suggesting a success transformation of silk-I amorphous structure in the solution to silk-II crystalline structure in the particles. Moreover, EL is an amorphous polymer; thus, its peaks are not sharp in the XRD graph, and it might shift the baseline of the whole system. Interestingly, the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles retained the main peaks of SF/EL, while the characteristic sharp crystalline peaks of AmB were strongly suppressed or disappeared. This indicates that AmB was no longer present as a separate crystalline phase (i.e., physical mixture with SF), but was instead molecularly dispersed and/or presented in an amorphous state within the SF/EL matrix. This fact indicates successful AmB encapsulation and strong drug-polymer interactions that hinder AmB crystal lattice formation. The AmB-SF interactions are discussed in depth in Section .

In terms of the DSC graphs, the broad peak at 50 to 110 °C contributed to the water evaporation in the particles. The blank SF/EL nanoparticles exhibited a glass transition temperature (Tg) at ∼145 °C, in accordance with a previous report. When incorporating AmB, the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles showed a lower Tg (∼139 °C), which was consistent with a plasticization effect and/or reduced polymer chain packing induced by AmB. In other words, AmB likely disrupts interchain SF-SF and SF-EL interactions (i.e., hydrogen bonding/ionic interactions among SF and EL functional groups), increasing chain mobility. Moreover, the AmB-specific melting peak at ∼174 °C was absent in the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles, which further supports the AmB amorphous encapsulation rather than crystalline AmB dispersed in the SF matrix.

Overall, the XRD and the DSC measurements indicated that AmB was successfully entrapped within SF/EL nanoparticles, likely as an amorphous or molecularly dispersed drug stabilized by SF/EL interactions rather than as free crystalline AmB. This fact significantly affected the AmB aggregation state, as discussed in Section .

Chemical Interactions

To elucidate the AmB and SF/EL interactions, FTIR spectroscopy was conducted (Figure ). For comparison purposes, besides the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles made by the desolvation method, we prepared two other formulas of AmB adsorbed onto the blank SF/EL nanoparticles at pH 2 (most AmB stays as cations, abbreviated as SF/EL-AmB 2) and pH 11 (most AmB stays as anions, abbreviated as SF/EL-AmB 11). Accordingly, the blank SF/EL nanoparticles (Figure A,B) confirm the successful formation of a physically associated SF-EL hybrid matrix. In the 1300–1100 cm–1 region, the SF/EL nanoparticles exhibited broadened bands compared with the individual polymers, arising from overlapping C–O/C–O–C stretching vibrations of EL and amide III/C–N contributions of SF. The loss of distinct polymer-specific features indicates strong intermolecular interactions rather than simple physical mixing. In the 1800–1500 cm–1 region, the characteristic amide I (∼1650 cm–1) and amide II (∼1520 cm–1) bands of SF became broadened and partially shifted in the SF/EL nanoparticles, suggesting hydrogen bonding between SF amide groups and EL carboxyl/carbonyl moieties.

FTIR spectra of (A,B) the blank SF/EL nanoparticles: (A) peaks at 1300–1100 cm–1 and (B) 1800–1500 cm–1; and (C–E) AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles (SF/EL-AmB: the formula made by the desolvation method; SF/EL-AmB 2 and 11: the formula made by AmB adsorption onto the blank SF/EL nanoparticles at pH 2 and pH 11): (C) peaks at 1100–980 cm–1, (D) 1800–1450 cm–1, and (E) 3000–2800 cm–1.

Upon AmB loading (Figure C–E), additional spectral changes confirmed successful drug incorporation and revealed clear differences between desolvation-based encapsulation and pH-dependent adsorption. In the 1100–980 cm–1 region, AmB-loaded formulations displayed bands attributable to C–O stretching of the AmB polyol and mycosamine moieties, which were most pronounced for the adsorption samples (SF/EL-AmB 2 and SF/EL-AmB 11). This was due to the fact that AmB was adsorbed on the particles’ surfaces; thus, some of its signals were still visible in the FTIR spectrum. In contrast, the desolvation-prepared SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles showed more subdued AmB features, again, indicating that the drug was embedded within the polymeric matrix via molecular dispersion state. In the 1800–1450 cm–1 region, AmB incorporation induced noticeable changes of the SF amide I/II and EL carbonyl groups, consistent with drug-polymer interactions involving hydrogen bonding. The 3000–2800 cm–1 region, corresponding to aliphatic C–H stretching, further confirmed the presence of AmB in all loaded systems, with stronger intensities observed for the adsorption samples compared to the formulas made from the desolvation method.

Conclusively, the FTIR results demonstrate that SF and EL formed a hydrogen-bonded, electrostatically stabilized hybrid matrix and that AmB was successfully incorporated in an amorphous molecular dispersion state, rather than as mere physical adsorption as observed in the SF/EL-AmB 2 and SF/EL-AmB 11.

Adsorption Study of AmB onto the SF/EL Nanoparticles

The main purpose of the adsorption study was to assess the different behaviors of AmB on particle loading in different ionization states. Thus, the adsorption experiments were carried out in two environments, pH 2 and pH 11 (Figure ). AmB has pK a of 3.58 and 9.11; thus, at these 2 selected pH, most AmB molecules were either in cationic form (at pH 2) or anionic form (at pH 11). At pH 2, the adsorption process started quickly in the first 30 min and then gradually rose until it was nearly saturated at 150 min. Because AmB has a positive charge in a pH 2 environment, which is opposite to the zeta potential of the blank SF/EL nanoparticles (which possessed a zeta potential of −12.5 ± 1.3 mV at pH 2), the electrostatic interactions between AmB and the particles were strong, making the adsorption rate high. In contrast, at pH 11, AmB and the SF/EL nanoparticles possessed the similar negative charge (the zeta potential of the particles at pH 11 was −52.7 ± 5.9 mV); thus, the adsorption process was mostly due to the hydrogen bonding between AmB and the particles, making it slower. Additionally, at pH 11, a certain amount of EL can be dissolved (EL dissolves in solution with pH > 6), further reducing the adsorption capacity of the SF/EL system.

AmB adsorption profiles of the blank SF/EL nanoparticles at pH 2 and pH 11.

Thermodynamically, the D–R model is designed to evaluate whether an adsorption process is physical or chemical on the basis of its adsorption energy. Accordingly, the adsorption energy finding indicated that at both pH 2 (E = 4.28 kJ/mol) and pH 11 (E = 0.58 kJ/mol), the AmB mainly interacted with SF/EL nanoparticles via physical adsorption processes (i.e., E < 8 kJ/mol). This reconfirmed that the chemical interactions were mostly electrostatic, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonding. Last but not least, kinetically, the adsorption of AmB onto the blank SF/EL nanoparticles was PSO kinetic, for both pH (at pH 2, y = 0.062x + 0.7287, R 2 = 0.9967; at pH 11, y = 0.0716x + 4.9308, R 2 = 0.9845), again, indicating that the adsorption was not controlled solely by diffusion but through chemical interactions between AmB and SF/EL. These interactions are discussed in depth in Section .

Molecular Docking and Dynamics Simulations

To deeply understand the interactions between AmB and SF/EL nanoparticles, we conducted in silico docking studies (Figure ). The docking results indicated that the ligand (AmB) exhibited a strong predicted binding affinity toward SF (protein 3UA0), with a docking score of −7.478 kcal/mol and a low RMSD value of 1.535 Å. The interaction network of AmB was characterized by multiple hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions involving charged residues of SF, notably Lys82, Glu98, and Lys62 (Table ). Specifically, two hydrogen-bond acceptor interactions with Lys82 at distances of 2.97 Å and 2.83 Å display highly favorable interaction energies (−14.60 and −15.60 kcal/mol, respectively), acting as key anchoring points that stabilize the AmB-SF complex. Additional hydrogen bonds with Glu98 and Gln65 further reinforce polar interactions within the binding pocket.

In contrast, EL showed a moderate predicted affinity toward SF, with a docking score of – 6.919 kcal/mol and an RMSD of 1.501 Å, indicating a consistent docking pose with limited structural deviation. EL formed a single hydrogen bond (H-donor) with Asp25 at a distance of 2.67 Å, associated with a low interaction energy (−1.80 kcal/mol). Although EL did not contribute a strong binding on its own, its presence becomes relevant since the SF/EL nanoparticles were a composite system. Thus, by occupying a peripheral region near Asp25, EL may locally modulate electrostatic distribution and subtly reshape the pocket surface, thereby reducing peripheral fluctuations and indirectly facilitating a more favorable microenvironment for AmB, which engages deeper residues such as Glu98 and Lys82.

Additionally, the RMSD profile of AmB throughout the 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation demonstrated high structural stability. An initial equilibration phase (0–40 ns) showed moderate fluctuations, followed by a stable plateau between 40 and 150 ns (∼0.2–0.3 nm), and a slightly increased fluctuation range during 150–200 ns (∼0.3–0.5 nm). For most of the investigation time, the AmB RMSD remained within 0.2–0.8 nm, indicating minimal displacement relative to SF once the complex was established. Therefore, AmB maintained a stable binding orientation with limited reorientation with SF, in agreement with the strong docking profile of AmB, possibly due to the ionic and hydrogen-bond interactions with Glu98, Lys82, and Lys62 of the SF chains. RMSF analysis further supports this observation. Residues in the vicinity of the binding site (approximately residues 1220–1320 of SF) exhibited low fluctuation amplitudes (typically 0.03–0.10 nm), indicating restricted local mobility and a structurally stable binding region.

Regarding SF-EL interactions, the RMSD profile remained within a narrow range (∼0.2–0.4 nm), while RMSF values for residues in the 1220–1300 region remained low (∼0.025–0.15 nm). These data indicated that the SF-EL association was stable but comparatively weaker, consistent with its moderate docking affinity. Thus, when EL was incorporated into the system, the functional groups derived from acrylic/methacrylic acid units of EL were capable of forming hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with polar groups on AmB as well as with fibroin residues. Overall, AmB simultaneously interacted with both SF and EL through hydrophobic, hydrogen-bonding, and electrostatic interactions, thus sustaining AmB release in the oral tract. Moreover, the pH-dependent behavior of EL supports its role as a protective polymeric matrix at low pH, with gradual loosening of interactions as pH increases (discussed in Section ). This behavior was consistent with the progressive relaxation of the interaction network observed in dynamics simulations.

AmB Release Study

Next, we investigated the AmB release behaviors in simulated oral conditions, comparing 3 formulas of the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles formulated by the desolvation method (SF/EL-AmB) and by physical adsorption at pH 2 (SF/EL-AmB 2) and at pH 11 (SF/EL-AmB 11) (Figure ). First, in the stomach acidic pH, all formulations exhibited limited AmB release, which can be directly attributed to the chemical characteristics of SF and EL. EL is a methacrylic acid/methyl methacrylate copolymer with carboxylic acid groups (−COOH) that remain protonated and un-ionized at pH 1.2, rendering the polymer insoluble and compact. This suppressed polymer swelling and diffusion pathways for AmB. Hence, AmB was protected through the stomach condition.

AmB release profiles from the AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles in the simulated oral condition (SF/EL-AmB: the formula made by the desolvation method; SF/EL-AmB 2 and 11: the formula made by AmB adsorption onto the blank SF/EL nanoparticles at pH 2 and pH 11).

Second, in the small intestinal pH, the amount of AmB released differed significantly between formulations. The SF/EL-AmB formulation reached near-complete release (reaching 95.13% after 480 min). This behavior is fundamentally driven by EL ionization chemistry. At pH 6.8, the carboxylic acid groups of EL become deprotonated (−COO–), leading to polymer swelling, electrostatic repulsion, and partial matrix relaxation, thereby facilitating AmB diffusion. SF, on the other hand, acts as a hydrophilic protein scaffold that allows water penetration once the EL phase swells. Moreover, AmB progressively shifts to a negative potential at pH 6.8, generating a repulsive force in the system that facilitates the AmB release. In contrast, the SF/EL-AmB 2 exhibited the slowest release, even in PBS. This was due to the fact that under acidic adsorption conditions, AmB was predominantly protonated in cationic forms, and formed strong hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions with negative SF and partially protonated EL (high adsorption energy of 4.28 kJ/mol (Section )), resulting in persistent binding that was not easily disrupted at intestinal pH. Conversely, SF/EL-AmB 11 showed intermediate release, reflecting weaker electrostatic interactions formed under alkaline adsorption conditions (E = 0.58 kJ/mol).

Third, by fitting the release data into mathematical models, both the SF/EL-AmB and SF/EL-AmB 11 well followed the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, with R 2 > 0.9 (Table ). The SF/EL-AmB 2 did not fit into any models, possibly due to the limited release profile (<10%). Additionally, both SF/EL-AmB and SF/EL-AmB 11 possessed a release exponent (n) that falls within the range of 0.45 < n < 0.9, indicating a release mechanism governed by a combination of drug diffusion and polymer erosion. This mechanism was consistent with the pH-sensitive property of the EL polymer, which dissolves slowly in environments with pH > 6.

Additionally, when compared to the SF/EL-AmB with the previously developed Eudragit E100/SF hybrid nanoparticles, the AmB release rates in the intestinal condition were better controlled and sustained. Antifungal Activity

Regarding the in vitro antifungal activity, the blank SF/EL nanoparticles exhibited no detectable antifungal activity (IC50 and MIC > 1024 μg/mL), confirming that SF and EL are biologically inert carriers with respect to C. Albicans (Table ). The free AmB exhibited extremely low IC50 and MIC values (<0.02 μg/mL), reflecting its well-known high membrane-disruptive activity via ergosterol binding in fungal cell membranes. On the other hand, the SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles exhibited an IC50 of 4.09 ± 0.21 μg/mL and an MIC of 32.0 μg/mL, indicating clear antifungal activity, although at higher concentrations than the free AmB. This reduced apparent potency could be due to two reasons. The first one was that the IC50 value of the SF/EL-AmB nanoparticle system was determined for the entire nanoparticle system, including the carrier (SF/EL) and the AmB active ingredient loaded into the nanoparticle system. The second reason is the strong interactions of AmB and SF (Section ) and the sustained release of AmB (Section ). As only the released AmB has an antifungal effect, the overall actions of the system in the limited test time are reduced. Nevertheless, the delayed and sustained AmB oral availability could be beneficial since it is sufficient to inhibit C. albicans growth, while potentially reducing peak drug concentrations that are responsible for AmB-associated toxicity (high potency of free AmB is also associated with significant systemic toxicity). This toxicity issue is discussed in Section .

AmB Aggregation State

AmB self-aggregates in aqueous media, and the aggregate-rich forms are more strongly linked to nephrotoxicity than monomeric AmB. , In UV–vis spectra, AmB typically exhibits four characteristic bands (I–IV), and the I/IV absorbance ratio is used as a practical indicator of aggregation, where lower I/IV ratio means more monomeric AmB (generally “safer”) and higher I/IV ratio means more aggregated AmB (generally “more nephrotoxic”) (Figure and Supporting data (the raw UV–vis data)).

SF/EL-AmB: formula made by the desolvation method; SF/EL-AmB pH 2 and pH 11: formula made by AmB adsorption onto the blank SF/EL nanoparticles at pH 2 and pH 11; AmB-DMSO: pure AmB dissolved in DMSO; AmB pH 2, 6.8, and 11: pure AmB dissolved in the respective pH.

To this end, we compared the aggregation state of the pure AmB, AmB dissolved in various solvents (DMSO, buffer pH 2, 6.8, and 11), and AmB-loaded SF/EL nanoparticles using desolvation or adsorption methods (at pH 2 and pH 11). First, the I/IV ratios of the completely dissolved AmB were low (in the case of AmB dissolved in DMSO, pH 2 (mostly in cationic form), and pH 11 (mostly in anionic form)), indicating that at these conditions, AmB was solubilized/ionized and was not aggregated. Nevertheless, these “safe” conditions could not be employed in the real biomedical applications since DMSO is a toxic solvent and the human cells do not support extreme pH 2 or pH 11 environments.

On the other hand, the pure AmB at physiological pH 6.8 showed the highest I/IV ratios (i.e., highest nephrotoxicity), likely due to its self-aggregate tendency upon entering the bloodstream, a phenomenon that is difficult to control in injectable formulations. It is also worth noting that for the spectrum of pure AmB at pH 6.8, peaks at wavelengths of <320 nm were observed, contributing to the Tween 80 signals. Interestingly, when being incorporated into the SF/EL nanoparticles, either by the desolvation or the adsorption method, AmB showed a reduced aggregation state. The calculated I/IV ratios for the pure AmB in pH 6.8, the SF/EL-AmB, the SF/EL-AmB 11, and the SF/EL-AmB 2 were 2.85, 2.41, 2.36, and 1.58, respectively, indicating the relative nephrotoxicity in the order of pure AmB in pH 6.8 > SF/EL-AmB > SF/EL-AmB 11 > SF/EL-AmB 2. This order linked well with the release profiles of these formulas (Section ). Accordingly, the SF/EL-AmB prepared via the desolvation method demonstrated relatively rapid drug release and also showed aggregation behavior similar to that of free AmB. Notably, SF/EL-AmB 2, which exhibited slower and more sustained release, showed reduced aggregation and a lower I/IV ratio, suggesting that slow release contributes to reduced toxicity. Moreover, all developed formulas showed less AmB aggregation compared to the commercial product Fungizone, which has an I/IV ratio of 3.7. Taking these data into account, we proposed a mechanism in which the AmB-loaded nanoparticles could reduce AmB nephrotoxicity (Figure ). Briefly, AmB induces nephrotoxicity primarily through its interaction with cholesterol present in the membranes of renal tubular cells. Because of its amphipathic nature, the hydrophobic face of AmB readily engages in hydrophobic interactions with membrane cholesterol, particularly within the tubular epithelium. This interaction leads to the formation of aberrant membrane pores, resulting in abnormal ion exchange, electrolyte imbalance, and depletion of intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In the case of SF/EL nanoparticles, the AmB molecules strongly interacted with SF via hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions (confirmed by the in silico docking data, Section ), and thus, the whole systems showed reduced I/IV ratios and less toxicity (Section ). Only the free AmB released from the formulation could interact with the renal tubules (confirmed in Section ). Therefore, compared to the conventional formula that usually bursts to release the AmB, these nanoparticles could potentially control the AmB release rate, as well as its nephrotoxicity.

Although the UV–vis spectral analysis provided useful preliminary evidence that the formulation reduced the proportion of highly aggregated AmB, which is commonly associated with nephrotoxicity, this approach does not directly evaluate mammalian cell viability. Therefore, future studies using kidney-relevant mammalian cell lines, such as HK-2 or HEK293 cells, are necessary to further confirm the renal safety and cytocompatibility of the developed SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed pH-sensitive Eudragit L100-functionalized silk fibroin nanoparticles (SF/EL) as an oral delivery platform for AmB to address its limitations of poor oral bioavailability and high nephrotoxicity. The optimal formula of an SF:EL ratio of 1:3 and an initial AmB loading of 0.5 mg yielded stable nanoparticles with high entrapment efficiency, strong negative surface charge, suitable physicochemical characteristics, and moderate in vitro antifungal activity. The loading strategy strongly influenced AmB release behavior in the simulated intestinal condition: the desolvation method made the particles moderately release AmB, whereas the physical adsorption at different pH values resulted in more surface-associated drug and slower, incomplete release. Spectroscopic analyses (XRD, DSC, and FTIR), together with in silico molecular docking, confirmed that AmB was incorporated into the SF/EL matrix in an amorphous, molecularly dispersed state stabilized by hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions with key amino acid residues of SF (notably Lys82, Glu98, and Lys62). These interactions effectively suppressed the AmB crystallization and self-aggregation. Further investigations could focus on (1) TEM/AFM analysis (to observe the EL coating layer), (2) the docking and MD of the whole fibroin structure (whenever applicable), not just the 3UA0 protein (N-terminal domain of fibroin), as well as at different pH values of pH 2 and pH 11, and (3) the in vitro toxicity of the particles on the kidney-relevant mammalian cell lines, such as HK-2 or HEK293 cells. Overall, SF/EL-AmB nanoparticles could be a promising oral AmB delivery system.
