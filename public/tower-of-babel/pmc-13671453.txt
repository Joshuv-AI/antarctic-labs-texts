Safety signals of tocilizumab in pediatric patients: A real-world pharmacovigilance analysis based on the FAERS database with network pharmacology insights
Authors: Gang Wu, Xueqing Ma, Bin Zhu, Ben Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTocilizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting the interleukin-6 receptor, is widely used in immune-mediated diseases, yet its real-world safety in pediatric populations remains insufficiently characterized. This retrospective pharmacovigilance study analyzed adverse event (AE) reports from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database (2010–2025), focusing on pediatric cases where tocilizumab was the primary suspect drug. After data cleaning, 1,485 reports were included. Four classical disproportionality methods – reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multiple gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) – were used to identify safety signals. Time-to-onset, stratified, and Weibull analyses were applied to characterize adverse event patterns, and network pharmacology was used to explore potential underlying mechanisms. Females accounted for 58.9% of reports, and adolescents aged 12–17 years represented the largest group. A total of 109 safety signals were identified. The most frequently reported AEs included juvenile idiopathic arthritis (ROR = 28.62, 95% CI: 24.30–33.72), haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (ROR = 15.83, 95% CI: 13.18–19.02), and decreased white blood cell count (ROR = 12.14, 95% CI: 9.90–14.88). Potential unlisted AEs, such as sudden hearing loss (ROR = 48.11, 95% CI: 16.74–138.26) and psoriasis (ROR = 11.44, 95% CI: 8.81–14.85), were also detected. Most AEs occurred within 30 days, with a median onset of 14 days, and showed an early failure pattern. Network pharmacology suggested psoriasis may involve JAK-STAT and Th17/Th1 pathways, though further validation is needed. Overall, this study provides real-world evidence to support improved safety monitoring in pediatric patients.KEYWORDS: Adverse drug reactions, FAERS, network pharmacology, pediatrics, tocilizumab

Introduction

Tocilizumab is a humanized recombinant monoclonal antibody targeting the interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6 R), which blocks IL-6 signaling by binding to both soluble and membrane-bound IL-6 R and thereby suppressing IL-6-mediated inflammatory responses.1 IL-6 is a multifunctional cytokine involved in immune regulation, inflammation, and hematopoiesis, and its dysregulated production contributes to the pathogenesis of various autoimmune and inflammatory diseases.2 Tocilizumab was initially approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe active rheumatoid arthritis in adults and has subsequently been approved for systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) and polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis (pJIA).3 In pediatric rheumatic diseases, tocilizumab has become an important biologic therapy for refractory sJIA, showing rapid improvement in fever, rash, arthritis, and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and serum ferritin.4–6

With the emergence of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), tocilizumab has also been widely investigated and used for patients with severe disease because of its ability to inhibit excessive IL-6-mediated inflammatory responses.7,8 However, although several studies have suggested potential clinical benefits, including possible reductions in mortality among selected patients, the survival benefit of tocilizumab in severe COVID-19 remains inconsistent and may depend on factors such as disease severity, timing of administration, and concomitant treatments. Previous clinical experience has emphasized the importance of individualized patient selection when considering tocilizumab therapy.9 In pediatric populations, tocilizumab has additionally been applied in multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) associated with COVID-19, further expanding its clinical use in children.10

Children represent a special population in whom immune development, body composition, and pharmacokinetic characteristics differ substantially from adults. Therefore, the safety profile of tocilizumab in children may differ from that observed in adult populations. A systematic review involving 15 studies reported that the most frequently observed adverse reactions associated with tocilizumab included infections, respiratory disorders, injection site reactions, neutropenia, leukopenia, elevated liver enzymes, and hypercholesterolemia, while serious adverse events (AEs) included severe infections, macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), lymphoma, intestinal perforation, and death.11 In addition to these recognized adverse reactions, rare but clinically significant events have continued to emerge, such as reversible leukoencephalopathy characterized by progressive cognitive impairment that improves after discontinuation of treatment.12 These findings highlight the importance of continuous pharmacovigilance, particularly in children receiving long-term biologic therapy.

Given that the risk of AEs in children varies with age, body weight, underlying diseases, disease severity, concomitant immunosuppressive therapies and previous infection history, individualized safety assessment is emphasized throughout tocilizumab treatment. According to current clinical practice, complete blood counts with differential analysis, liver function tests and lipid profiles are routinely performed, while screening for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B and hepatitis C virus infections is conducted when clinically indicated. In this context, identification of pediatric‑specific safety signals may provide referential information for further optimizing clinical risk prevention and management strategies.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is one of the largest spontaneous reporting databases for post-marketing drug safety surveillance worldwide, containing extensive real-world safety information over more than two decades.13 Compared with conventional clinical studies, FAERS provides broader population coverage and enables detection of rare or unexpected AE signals that may not be identified in randomized trials or small pediatric cohorts.14 However, systematic evaluation of the safety profile of tocilizumab in pediatric patients remains limited. Therefore, this retrospective pharmacovigilance study aimed to characterize AE patterns associated with tocilizumab use in children based on FAERS data, identify potential pediatric-specific safety signals, and provide evidence to support clinical risk assessment, monitoring strategies, and individualized treatment optimization.

Materials and methods

Data sources and processing

Data for this study were retrieved from the FAERS database, including AE reports submitted from the first quarter of 2010 to the fourth quarter of 2025. Pediatric cases were defined as patients aged 0–17 years and were further stratified into three subgroups based on developmental stage and clinical relevance: 0–5 years, 6–11 years, and 12–17 years. To enhance the attributional specificity of drug-AE signal detection, only reports listing tocilizumab as the primary suspect drug were included in this study. Drug names were uniformly converted to uppercase format, with dosage form and strength specifications removed. The terms “Tocilizumab,” “Atlizumab,” “Actemra,” “Roactemra,” and “BAT1806” were mapped to the RxNorm concept set. For entries that could not be precisely matched, fuzzy matching was performed using the Jaro-Winkler algorithm with a threshold set at 0.90. All matching results were manually reviewed by two researchers; discrepancies were resolved by consensus involving a third researcher.

Data extraction and deduplication were performed in strict accordance with FDA specifications: the PRIMARYID, CASEID, and FDA_DT fields were extracted from the DEMO table. For multiple reports sharing the same CASEID, the record with the most recent FDA_DT was retained. If FDA_DT values were identical, the entry with the largest PRIMARYID value was selected. After deduplication, the DRUG table was linked to the REAC table via PRIMARYID. AEs in the REAC table were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 27.0) and classified at both the preferred term (PT) and system organ class (SOC) levels. Each observation corresponded to one MedDRA PT. A single report could include multiple PTs, but the same PT was counted only once per report. Upon completion of data table linkage, each drug-PT pair was treated as an independent observation unit and included in subsequent disproportionality signal analysis (Figure 1(A)).

Data analysis

Four established algorithms, namely reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS), were employed for disproportionality analysis of AEs. Among them, ROR and PRR are frequentist algorithms with excellent overall sensitivity: ROR can effectively correct for reporting bias when the number of AE reports is low, while PRR is less affected by underreporting of cases. BCPNN and MGPS are Bayesian algorithms that offer notable advantages in signal detection specificity, result stability, and false positive control. To ensure the robustness of signal detection results, a positive signal was defined as a PT for which all four algorithms simultaneously met their respective preset thresholds; PTs that did not meet this criterion were classified as negative signals. All algorithms were constructed based on 2 × 2 contingency tables (Supplementary Table 1). The calculation formulas and signal detection thresholds for each algorithm were detailed in Supplementary Table 2.15,16

Time to onset (TTO) of AEs was defined as the interval from the initiation date of tocilizumab treatment to the date of AE occurrence. Prior to analysis, data cleaning was performed to exclude cases with missing treatment or event dates, invalid date formats, or temporal inconsistencies (TTO < 0). The distribution of TTO was summarized using the minimum, median, maximum, and interquartile range (IQR). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to characterize the latency distribution based on complete sequential reports, and the log-rank test was applied to compare differences in TTO distributions between groups. A Weibull distribution was fitted to the dataset with complete TTO data. Based on the shape parameter β and its 95% confidence interval (CI), the temporal patterns of AEs were classified into three categories: early failure pattern, random failure pattern, and wear-out failure pattern.17 All tests were two-tailed, and a P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Data management was performed using Microsoft Excel 2019, and all statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3).

It should be noted that spontaneous reporting databases, such as FAERS, have inherent limitations. The number of reports does not represent the actual incidence or the number of independent patients and cannot establish causal relationships. Potential confounding factors, including underlying diseases, comorbidities, and concomitant medications, may also influence the observed associations. Therefore, the detected signals should be interpreted with caution.

Network pharmacology analysis

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying tocilizumab-associated psoriasis, a network pharmacology analysis was performed. First, an IL-6-related protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database to identify potential targets of tocilizumab. Psoriasis-related targets were then retrieved from the GeneCards database, and overlapping targets between the two datasets were identified using a Venn diagram as potential toxicity-related targets. A PPI network of these toxicity-related targets was subsequently constructed using STRING and visualized in Cytoscape 3.7.2 to identify core targets. Finally, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms.18

Results

Adverse events and population characteristics

A total of 763,787 pediatric adverse event reports involving patients aged 0–17 years were initially screened from the FAERS database between the first quarter of 2010 and the fourth quarter of 2025. After deduplication and inclusion of reports in which tocilizumab was recorded as the primary suspect drug, 1,485 reports were included in the final analysis. Female patients accounted for 875 (58.9%) reports, whereas male patients accounted for 568 (38.3%) reports (Table 1). Regarding age distribution, adolescents aged 12–17 years represented the largest subgroup, accounting for 644 (43.4%) reports, followed by children aged 6–11 years with 468 (31.5%) reports and children aged 0–5 years with 373 (25.1%) reports. Healthcare professionals were the primary reporters, with physicians contributing 819 (55.2%) reports. Among reported outcomes, missing outcome information accounted for 936 (63.0%) reports. Among reports with documented serious outcomes, hospitalization was the most frequently reported outcome, occurring in 375 (25.3%) reports. Geographically, the United States contributed the highest number of reports (527, 35.5%), followed by Japan (211, 14.2%) and Canada (160, 10.8%) (Figure 1(B)). The annual number of pediatric tocilizumab-associated AE reports generally increased after 2010 and remained at a relatively high level after 2017. The highest annual number of reports was observed in 2020 (153 reports) (Figure 1(C)).

Safety signal detection of tocilizumab-associated adverse events

At the SOC level, the most frequently reported AE categories were general disorders and administration site conditions (1,423 reports), investigations (1,323 reports), musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (976 reports), injury, poisoning and procedural complications (822 reports), and infections and infestations (810 reports) (Figure 1(D)). Regarding disproportionality strength, musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders showed the highest SOC-level signal intensity (ROR = 4.78), followed by social circumstances (ROR = 3.17) and investigations (ROR = 2.91) (Figure 1(E), Supplementary Table 3).

The combined analysis of four disproportionality algorithms identified 109 positive PT-level signals (Figure 1(F), Supplementary Table 4). The most frequently reported PTs included juvenile idiopathic arthritis (159 reports), haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (122 reports), white blood cell count decreased (97 reports), arthritis (90 reports), and arthralgia (84 reports) (Table 2). Among all identified PT-level signals, macrophage activation showed the strongest disproportionality signal (ROR = 300.65, 95% CI: 60.67–1489.82), followed by white blood cells urine (ROR = 171.82, 95% CI: 50.29–587.04), blood pressure diastolic abnormal (ROR = 123.85, 95% CI: 51.35–298.72), hypocomplementaemia (ROR = 109.34, 95% CI: 34.81–343.44), and granulocytosis (ROR = 107.40, 95% CI: 38.68–298.23) (Figure 2). Notably, psoriasis (59 reports, ROR = 11.44, 95% CI: 8.81–14.85) and sudden hearing loss (4 reports, ROR = 48.11, 95% CI: 16.74–138.26) were identified as potential safety signals that are not currently listed in the product label.

Stratified analysis

Ten core AE signals remained consistently detected across different sex and age subgroups, including arthritis, infusion-related reaction, neutrophil count decreased, white blood cell count decreased, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, joint swelling, rheumatoid arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, C reactive protein increased, and platelet count decreased (Figure 3(A–D)). In the sex-stratified analysis, the most frequently reported AEs among male pediatric patients were white blood cell count decreased (53 reports), juvenile idiopathic arthritis (49 reports), and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (48 reports). Hypocomplementaemia showed the strongest signal among male patients (ROR = 351.60, 95% CI: 94.37–1309.97), followed by blood pressure diastolic abnormal (ROR = 159.82, 95% CI: 50.86–502.18) (Figure 3(A), Supplementary Table 5–6). Among female pediatric patients, juvenile idiopathic arthritis (109 reports), dizziness (72 reports), and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (70 reports) were the most frequently reported AEs. Granulocytosis showed the strongest signal among female patients (ROR = 368.43, 95% CI: 88.03–1542.04), followed by dactylitis (ROR = 147.40, 95% CI: 52.45–414.24).

(A) Comparison of adverse event signals between male and female patients; (B) Signal distribution in patients aged 0–5 years; (C) Signal distribution in patients aged 6–11 years; and (D) Signal distribution in patients aged 12–17 years.

Age-stratified analysis revealed distinct signal patterns among different developmental stages (Figure 3(B–D), Supplementary Table 7–9). In children aged 0–5 years, granulocytosis showed the strongest signal (ROR = 644.51, 95% CI: 118–3520.39), followed by hypocomplementaemia (ROR = 322.25, 95% CI: 80.55–1289.22). In children aged 6–11 years, Stenotrophomonas infection showed the strongest signal (ROR = 82.68, 95% CI: 26.65–256.53), followed by antinuclear antibody positive (ROR = 65.65, 95% CI: 40.06–107.59). Among adolescents aged 12–17 years, macrophage activation showed the strongest signal (ROR = 485.08, 95% CI: 81.03–2903.89).

Time-to-onset analysis of adverse events

Among the included reports, 627 patients had complete and valid information regarding treatment initiation and AE occurrence dates. The median TTO was 14 days (95% CI: 10–15 days). Most AEs occurred within the first 30 days after tocilizumab initiation, accounting for 383 (61.7%) reports (Figure 4(A–C)). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significant differences in TTO distributions among different age groups (log-rank test, P = .008). However, no significant differences were observed between sex groups or between serious and non-serious AE groups (Figure 4(D–F)). The Weibull distribution model demonstrated an early failure pattern for several major PTs, including haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (β = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.47–0.78), white blood cell count decreased (β = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.54–0.88), neutrophil count decreased (β = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.42–0.74), septic shock (β = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.29–0.91), and platelet count decreased (β = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.47–0.85). Similar early failure patterns were observed at the SOC level (Figure 4(G,H)).

Network pharmacology analysis

Psoriasis was identified as a potential safety signal not currently included in the tocilizumab product label. To further explore the possible biological mechanisms underlying this association, network pharmacology analysis was performed. By integrating tocilizumab-related targets with psoriasis-associated genes, 171 overlapping potential targets were identified (Figure 5(A)). Protein–protein interaction analysis revealed several key targets, including IL6, IFNG, IL10, JAK1, and IL2 (Figure 5(B)). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these targets were mainly involved in cytokine-mediated receptor signaling, JAK/STAT-related signaling, leukocyte and lymphocyte activation, adaptive immune responses, and cytokine or chemokine receptor binding (Figure 5(C)). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in immune- and inflammation-related pathways, including the JAK–STAT signaling pathway, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, Th17 cell differentiation, Th1/Th2 cell differentiation, IL-17 signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and PI3K–Akt signaling pathway (Figure 5(D)).

Discussion

This study utilized the FAERS database to systematically evaluate adverse‑event signals and latency‑distribution characteristics associated with tocilizumab among children and adolescents aged 0–17 years from 2010 to 2025. Furthermore, network pharmacology was adopted to explore the mechanism underlying the off‑label psoriasis signal identified in our analysis. It should be noted that FAERS is a spontaneous reporting database; the number of reported cases cannot be equated with the number of unique real‑world patients. Signals detected from this database only indicate statistical associations between drugs and adverse events and cannot confirm direct causal relationships. Meanwhile, network pharmacology merely generates hypotheses regarding potential molecular mechanisms. These findings lack experimental evidence, and further validation via in‑vivo and in‑vitro basic experiments is required in future studies.

The safety signals observed in this study were generally consistent with the established safety profile of tocilizumab. Tocilizumab has been widely used in pediatric rheumatic and immune-mediated diseases, including systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis and polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Previous randomized controlled trials, long-term extension studies, and real-world studies have demonstrated its clear efficacy and generally manageable safety profile in pediatric populations.19–22 Infections, hematologic abnormalities, elevated liver enzymes, and infusion-related reactions have been repeatedly reported in previous studies, which is broadly consistent with the signals detected in the present study, including decreased white blood cell count, decreased neutrophil count, decreased platelet count, infection, and infusion-related reactions.20,23,24 In addition, an analysis of pediatric clinical trial data by Pardeo et al. showed that neutropenia may occur during tocilizumab treatment, but it is not necessarily directly associated with an increased risk of infection.24 Therefore, the present study does not challenge the relatively favorable safety profile of tocilizumab in children. Rather, from a pharmacovigilance perspective, it further highlights key areas for clinical monitoring and provides supplementary evidence for adverse reaction recognition and risk management during treatment.

In this study, PTs such as juvenile idiopathic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and arthritis ranked high in both reporting frequency and signal strength. These signals are highly likely to stem from indication bias, as tocilizumab is primarily approved in the pediatric population for the treatment of systemic and polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis.25,26 A large number of patients receive the drug for this underlying condition, leading to over-reporting of primary disease symptoms as suspected adverse events. Therefore, these PT signals do not represent new adverse reactions to tocilizumab but rather reflect the disease context of drug use and should not be interpreted as additional safety concerns. In clinical practice, detailed medical history collection, analysis of the treatment timeline, and assessment of objective inflammatory markers are necessary to differentiate disease activity from drug-related events.

Furthermore, macrophage activation exhibited a very high signal strength (ROR = 300.65) in this study, which warrants cautious interpretation. Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) is a severe and life-threatening complication of sJIA, with pathogenesis involving excessive activation of the innate immune system and cytokine storm.27 Given the widespread use of tocilizumab in sJIA, a large number of patients with concomitant MAS or at high risk for MAS are exposed to the drug. Consequently, the “macrophage activation” signal in the database largely reflects a serious complication of the underlying disease rather than a pure adverse drug reaction. Notably, previous studies have indicated that tocilizumab may alter the typical clinical and laboratory features of MAS, making fever less prominent and resulting in significantly lower levels of ferritin, triglycerides, and C-reactive protein compared to patients not receiving tocilizumab, potentially leading to missed or delayed diagnosis of MAS.28 Moreover, case reports have described life-threatening MAS following tocilizumab infusion in children with sJIA, rapidly progressing to hypoxemia and hemodynamic instability, ultimately requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support.29 Therefore, it remains difficult to determine the relative contributions of “disease progression per se” vs. “drug-related excessive immunomodulatory dysregulation” to the macrophage activation signal.

Beyond known safety events, this study also identified positive signals for sudden hearing loss and psoriasis, which are not listed in the drug label. For sudden hearing loss, the number of reports was limited, and the FAERS database does not provide complete information on disease course, concomitant medications, baseline disease activity, or diagnostic confirmation. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as suggestive pharmacovigilance signals rather than evidence of a definitive causal relationship. By contrast, the psoriasis signal has greater clinical relevance for further discussion. Previous case reports and case reviews have described psoriasis or psoriasiform eruptions during tocilizumab treatment.30 After summarizing cases of tocilizumab-associated psoriasiform eruptions, Nielly et al. suggested that although such events are uncommon, they should be recognized and followed up in clinical practice.30

Sex- and age-stratified analyses identified several adverse event signals that remained consistently detected across different subgroups, including infusion-related reactions, neutrophil count decreased, white blood cell count decreased, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), C reactive protein increased, and platelet count decreased. The persistence of these signals across multiple demographic strata suggests that these events may represent clinically relevant safety concerns during pediatric tocilizumab therapy. However, it should be emphasized that FAERS-based disproportionality analysis reflects differences in reporting patterns rather than differences in absolute risk. Therefore, the presence of a signal within a specific subgroup does not indicate that the event is restricted to that population, nor does it establish a causal relationship with tocilizumab exposure. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence requiring further validation.

In younger children aged 0–5 years, granulocytosis and hypocomplementaemia showed relatively strong disproportionality signals compared with other age groups. These observations may be associated with developmental differences in immune maturation, hematopoietic regulation, underlying disease characteristics, or treatment patterns.31 Previous studies have suggested that IL-6 participates in hematopoietic regulation and immune homeostasis; therefore, IL-6 receptor blockade may theoretically influence these processes.32 However, because FAERS does not provide detailed immunological profiles or longitudinal laboratory measurements, the biological mechanisms underlying these signals remain uncertain and require experimental confirmation.

Among children aged 6–11 years, Stenotrophomonas infection and antinuclear antibody positivity showed relatively prominent signals. Stenotrophomonas infection is generally associated with immunocompromised conditions and healthcare exposure.33 The observed signal may reflect altered host defense during IL-6 inhibition, but may also be influenced by disease severity, hospitalization history, concomitant immunosuppressive therapies, and reporting behavior. Similarly, antinuclear antibody positivity may indicate immune modulation or preexisting autoimmune background; however, the clinical significance of isolated antibody positivity cannot be determined from FAERS data. Further longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether these findings represent transient laboratory abnormalities or clinically meaningful immune alterations.

In adolescents aged 12–17 years, macrophage activation and increased disability assessment scale scores showed relatively strong signals. Given that sJIA is a major indication for tocilizumab in children, macrophage activation-related events may partly reflect the underlying disease spectrum and severity rather than a direct adverse effect of the drug.34 In addition, functional score changes may be influenced by disease activity assessment and clinical reporting practices. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, with attention to distinguishing disease progression, treatment response evaluation, and potential drug-associated events.

Overall, the age- and sex-stratified analyses provide additional insight into the distribution of reported adverse events but should not be interpreted as evidence of subgroup-specific toxicity. Future studies incorporating detailed clinical characteristics, medication exposure information, and longitudinal follow-up are needed to determine whether these observed differences represent true biological susceptibility or variations in disease characteristics and reporting patterns.

The present study analyzed the temporal distribution of tocilizumab-associated adverse events using cases with complete and valid treatment initiation and event occurrence dates. Among these cases, the median TTO was 14 days, and 61.7% of reported events occurred within the first 30 days after treatment initiation. These findings suggest that, among reports with available temporal information, adverse events associated with pediatric tocilizumab exposure were frequently reported during the early treatment period. However, it should be noted that TTO analysis was restricted to a subset of FAERS reports with complete date information. Because missing treatment or event dates were common and may not have occurred randomly, the observed temporal pattern may be affected by selection bias and should not be directly extrapolated to all pediatric patients receiving tocilizumab.

Weibull distribution analysis demonstrated an early failure pattern for several frequently reported PTs and SOCs, indicating that the proportion of reported events was relatively higher during the early treatment period and decreased over subsequent reporting intervals. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously because it reflects reporting dynamics rather than the true time-dependent incidence of adverse events. Several factors may contribute to this observation, including early clinical surveillance after treatment initiation, preferential reporting of events occurring soon after drug exposure, discontinuation of treatment among patients experiencing early adverse events, and differences in follow-up duration. Therefore, the observed early failure pattern does not necessarily indicate the development of biological tolerance or a definitive reduction in individual patient risk over time.

The underlying mechanisms contributing to the observed temporal distribution remain uncertain. From a pharmacological perspective, the initiation of IL-6 receptor blockade may coincide with substantial immunological changes, and early exposure may theoretically be associated with acute events such as infusion-related reactions or hematological abnormalities.33,35 However, because FAERS lacks detailed pharmacokinetic data, immune profiling, and longitudinal clinical follow-up, these explanations remain hypothetical and require confirmation in prospective studies.

The signal for psoriasis constitutes one of the relatively novel findings of the present study, with 59 retrieved reports and an ROR of 11.44. Although previous case reports of tocilizumab-associated psoriasis are limited in number, they have suggested that psoriasiform skin lesions may occur during IL-6/IL-6 R blockade.30 The present study further detected this signal in pediatric FAERS reports, thereby expanding the pharmacovigilance relevance of this adverse event in pediatric populations. It should be emphasized that ROR reflects reporting disproportionality rather than true incidence. Therefore, the psoriasis signal should be regarded as a safety clue requiring clinical attention and further validation, rather than as a definitive causal conclusion.

Network pharmacology analysis provided preliminary mechanistic support for the psoriasis signal. In this study, IL6, IFNG, IL10, JAK1, and IL2 were identified as key targets in the potential toxicity network of tocilizumab-associated psoriasis. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses mainly involved JAK-STAT-mediated receptor signaling, leukocyte and lymphocyte activation, adaptive immune responses, cytokine receptor binding, Th17 cell differentiation, Th1/Th2 cell differentiation, and IL-17 signaling. Previous studies have established psoriasis as an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory skin disease, in which the IL-23/Th17/IL-17 axis and the JAK-STAT pathway play central roles in disease development and progression.36–39 After IL-6 receptor blockade by tocilizumab, the cytokine network may undergo rebalancing, thereby affecting Th17/Th1-related immune axes and keratinocyte inflammatory responses. This inference is consistent with the network pharmacology enrichment results of the present study; however, it remains an indirect mechanistic explanation and requires validation through in vitro experiments, animal models, or patient-derived samples.

In summary, this pharmacovigilance study characterized the reported adverse event spectrum and temporal patterns associated with tocilizumab exposure in pediatric patients based on FAERS data. The identified signals were generally consistent with the known safety profile of tocilizumab, including hematological abnormalities, infections, and infusion-related reactions. Potential signals involving psoriasis and sudden hearing loss were additionally detected and warrant further investigation. However, age- and sex-stratified analyses, time-to-onset assessment, and network pharmacology findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence rather than confirmation of subgroup-specific toxicity, causal relationships, or underlying mechanisms.

Several limitations should be considered. First, FAERS is a spontaneous reporting system for safety signal detection and cannot provide incidence estimates or establish causality. Report frequency may be influenced by disease severity, clinical awareness, reporting practices, and other external factors. Therefore, identified signals should be regarded as potential safety concerns requiring further validation. Second, this study was based solely on FAERS without integration of other international pharmacovigilance databases, such as EudraVigilance or WHO VigiBase. Differences in population characteristics, healthcare systems, and reporting patterns may affect the reproducibility and generalizability of the findings. In addition, FAERS lacks detailed clinical information, including complete disease history, laboratory trends, diagnostic confirmation, and treatment background. Thus, some events may reflect underlying disease activity, complications, or monitoring effects rather than direct drug toxicity, particularly in pediatric rheumatic diseases. Finally, incomplete information regarding concomitant therapies, treatment duration, and exposure history may introduce residual confounding. Missing date information may also influence time-to-onset analyses, as these analyses were limited to cases with complete temporal data. Despite these limitations, the combined application of multiple disproportionality methods and complementary analyses provides useful pharmacovigilance evidence and generates hypotheses for future clinical and mechanistic studies.

Conclusion

This study characterized reported adverse event signals associated with tocilizumab exposure in pediatric patients using FAERS data from 2010 to 2025. Consistent signals involving cytopenia, infection, infusion-related reactions, and HLH/macrophage activation-related events were identified, while psoriasis and sudden hearing loss emerged as potential signals requiring further investigation.

However, these findings are based on a single spontaneous reporting database and represent reporting associations rather than confirmed causal relationships or absolute risks. Because FAERS reports are predominantly derived from the United States and include a high proportion of individuals of Caucasian ancestry, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and may not be directly generalizable to other ethnic populations or healthcare settings. Further validation using multinational databases and prospective studies is required.
