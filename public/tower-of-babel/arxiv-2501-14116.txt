Domain-Factored Untrained Deep Prior for Spectrum Cartography
Subash Timilsina, Sagar Shrestha, Lei Cheng, Xiao Fu

Abstract
Spectrum cartography (SC) aims to estimate the radio power map of multiple emitters over space and frequency using limited sensor data. Recent advances leverage learned deep generative models (DGMs) as structural priors, achieving state-of-the-art performance by capturing complex spatial-spectral patterns. However, DGMs require large training datasets and may suffer under distribution shifts. To address these limitations, we propose a training-free SC approach based on untrained neural networks (UNNs), which encode structural priors through architectural design. Our custom UNN exploits a spatio-spectral factorization model rooted in the physical structure of radio maps, enabling low sample complexity. Experiments show that our method matches the performance of DGM-based SC without any training data.



I
Introduction

This letter revisits the
spectrum cartography
(SC) task in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
The goal is to construct a radio frequency (RF) interference propagation map over space and frequency from limited measurements sent by sparsely deployed sensors.
Earlier works proposed to use certain handcrafted structural constraints of the radio map (e.g., low-rank, sparsity, and smoothness) to regularize this clearly ill-posed inverse problem; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
10
,
3
]
. To handle more complex RF environments, particularly where heavy shadowing happens,
deep generative models (DGMs) were used to learn the structural characteristics of radio maps; see, e.g.,

[
11
,
4
,
12
,
13
]
.
Specifically, a deep neural network (DNN)-based representation is learned using offline training data. Then, the DNN is used as a structural constraint in the inverse problem solving process of SC.
Due to DNN’s expressiveness, such “learned priors” can capture detailed and complex characteristics of radio maps, offering state-of-the-art performance of SC
[
4
,
12
]
.

However, learning a DGM consumes a large number of training data, which is not always available.
In addition, mismatches between the training data and actual radio map environments could be detrimental to the SC performance, which is particularly problematic when the environment is dynamic or fast changing.

Contributions.

We propose using the
untrained neural network
(UNN) approach
[
14
]
for SC.
While UNNs have shown promise in vision tasks by leveraging architectural bias without training, directly applying them to radio maps leads to high model and sample complexities.
To address this, we design a custom UNN based on a spatio-spectral factorization model, motivated by the approximate uncorrelatedness between spatial and spectral components over narrow bands
[
15
,
2
,
3
,
16
,
10
]
.
This factorization enables efficient representation of emitter-specific spatial components.
We further exploit shared statistical patterns among emitters to reduce complexity.
Simulations demonstrate that our method performs comparably to learned DGM-based SC approaches—despite requiring no training data.

II
Problem Statement and Background

We consider an SC scenario where
R
emitters share
K
frequency bins; see, e.g.,
[
10
,
11
,
17
,
3
,
12
,
2
,
13
]
.
The spatial region is divided into
I\times J
grid cells, and
N
sensors are deployed in this region. Power propagation of the
R
emitters in the region over
K
frequencies forms an
I\times J\times K
radio map tensor
\underline{\bm{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J\times K}
, where each entry of tensor
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k)
is the the
power spectral density
(PSD) of signal received at location
(i,j)
and frequency
k
; see Fig.
1
(left).
Let us denote
\bm{\varOmega}=\{(i,j)~|~i\in[I],~j\in[J]\}\subseteq[I]\times[J]
as the set of sensor locations, where
|\bm{\varOmega}|=N\ll IJ
. The sensor at
(i,j)
collects
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)
, i.e., the PSD at location
(i,j)
, and transmits it to a fusion center.
The goal of SC is to recover
\underline{\bm{X}}
from
\{\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)\}_{(i,j)\in\bm{\varOmega}}
; see Fig.
1
(right).

SC was tackled using many classical inverse problem solving techniques, e.g., spatial smoothness-based kernel interpolation
[
1
,
10
]
, representation sparsity-based dictionary learning
[
18
]
and compressive sensing
[
19
,
20
]
,
and low-rank based matrix/tensor completion
[
3
,
8
]
.
However, the effectiveness of these methods decreases in complex RF environments, e.g., those with heavy shadowing. This is because assumptions such as spatial/spectral smoothness or low-rank structures of radio maps could be grossly violated in such cases
[
4
,
12
]
.

More recently, deep learning-based SC methods have been proposed to tackle these challenges (e.g.,
[
11
,
4
,
12
,
13
]
). These approaches learn a deep generative model (DGM) to represent radio maps as
\underline{\bm{X}}={\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
, where
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a latent code and
{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}
is trained on data reflecting the target environment. DGMs can capture complex radio map structures, even under severe shadowing. However, they require large amounts of training data
[
4
]
, and performance suffers when there is a domain gap—e.g., due to environmental changes. In such cases, retraining may be needed, limiting their practicality for
in situ
sensing.

III
Proposed Approach

III-A

Preliminaries of UNNs

A way to circumvent training DGMs yet retaining representation expressiveness is to use UNNs.
UNNs were first introduced in the computer vision literature
[
14
]
. It was observed that the inherent structure of a convolutional neural network, when carefully designed, can act as a strong prior for natural images; i.e., the expression
\underline{\bm{X}}={\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
holds for unknown
\bm{\theta}
and a certain
\bm{z}
under careful construction of
{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}
—and
\bm{\theta}
can be learned to adapt any given data
\underline{\bm{X}}
. Using UNNs, the data recovery problems can be formulated as

\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{\theta}}~\left\|\underline{\bm{M}}\circledast\left(\underline{\bm{X}}-{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})\right)\right\|_{\rm F}^{2},

(1)

where
\bm{z}
could be fixed, e.g., a Gaussian/uniform variable
[
14
,
21
]
or a learnable term
[
22
]
.
The UNN-based approach attracted a lot of attention in vision, as it bypasses the need for training data while keeping impressive representation accuracy for complex image data across disciplines
[
14
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
21
,
22
]
.

III-B

UNN Design for Radio Maps

Directly applying existing vision-based UNNs (e.g.,
[
14
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
21
,
22
]
) to represent the
I\times J\times K
radio map
\underline{\bm{X}}
via (
1
) is challenging—
\underline{\bm{X}}
is often too complex to model compactly. For instance, as noted in
[
4
]
, placing just
R=5
emitters in a
100\times 100
grid yields
8\times 10^{17}
combinations, not accounting for other parameters like power or frequency. Capturing such complexity may require a highly overparameterized UNN (i.e.,
|\bm{\theta}|\gg IJK
, where
|\bm{x}|
denotes the cardinality of
\bm{x}
) to satisfy
\underline{\bm{X}}={\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
for arbitrary
\underline{\bm{X}}
drawn from the distribution
{\cal D}
. As a result, solving (
1
) can demand an impractically large number of sensor measurements.

We design a custom UNN structure based on the classical spatio-spectral factorization model of radio maps
[
10
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
12
]
. Under this model, for a region with
R
emitters, we have

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{X}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}\circ\bm{c}_{r},

(2)

where
\circ
denotes the outer product, and
\bm{c}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
and
\bm{S}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J}
are the PSD and the
spatial loss field
(SLF) of emitter
r
, respectively. The SLF captures the spatial power propagation pattern of an emitter.
The model is considered valid if the bandwidth of interest is not too wide compared to the central frequency
[
16
,
15
,
10
,
2
]
; see Fig.
2
.

As argued in
[
4
,
12
]
, the modeling difficulty of radio maps mainly lies in its SLFs. Hence, instead of using a large UNN to represent
\underline{\bm{X}}
, our idea is to use a “frugal” UNN to represent
\bm{S}_{r}
for all
r
— converting a 3D tensor modeling problem to a 2D SLF modeling one. Note that each
\bm{S}_{r}
corresponds to only one emitter, and thus it also has a much smaller “state space” relative to
\underline{\bm{X}}
(only
10^{4}
emitter positions—as opposed to
8\times 10^{17}
—exist with a single
\bm{S}_{r}
in the previous example).
Therefore, if one designs UNNs to represent
\bm{S}_{r}
’s, it is expected to be more parameter-efficient than using existing UNNs like those in
[
14
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
21
,
22
]
to model the entire
\underline{\bm{X}}
.

III-C

Architecture of UNN for SLFs

To model
\bm{S}_{r}
succinctly, the UNN should have as few parameters as possible, enabling SC with fewer samples. At the same time, it must be expressive enough to capture complex RF environments—striking this balance is nontrivial.

Notice that it is reasonable to assume that
\bm{S}_{r}
for
r=1,\ldots,R
follow the same distribution.
Therefore,
\bm{S}_{r}={\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{r})
for all
r
can share the same generative model
{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}
and only distinguish by their latent codes
\bm{z}_{r}
(see
[
4
,
12
]
).
This already reduces model complexity substantially—another benefit of the factorization model in (
2
).
To design
{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}
,
we use a decoder architecture inspired by
[
23
]
.
The deep decoder structure
[
23
]
often uses fewer parameters than other UNN architectures, e.g., the U-net based one in
[
14
]
—which, together with our parameter-sharing design, further reduces the sample complexity for SC.
The architecture at layer
i+1
(except for the final layer) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{Z}^{i+1}={\rm Cn}({\rm ReLU}(\bm{U}^{i}\bm{Z}^{i}\bm{\Theta}^{i})),

(3)

where
\bm{Z}^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{i}^{2}\times k_{i}}
represents the output of
i
th layer of the UNN, with
D_{i}^{2}
and
k_{i}
representing the spatial and the channel dimensions, respectively.
Similarly,
{\bm{\Theta}}^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{i}\times k_{i+1}}
represents the parameters of UNN in the
i
th layer, and we have
{\bm{\theta}}=\{{\bm{\Theta}}^{0},\dots,{\bm{\Theta}}^{L}\}
.
In implementation,
\bm{Z}^{i},\forall i
is a third-order tensor of shape
D_{i}\times D_{i}\times k_{i}
, and
{\bm{\Theta}}^{i}
represents the parameters of convolution operation.
However, we use the tensors’ matrix unfolding with a size of
D_{i}^{2}\times k_{i}
for the ease of analysis (see
[
27
]
for details of unfolding).
The input to the UNN is
\bm{Z}^{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{0}^{2}\times 1}
(i.e., the matrix unfolding version of
\bm{z}
), and the output is an SLF, i.e.,
\bm{S}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J}
generated as

\bm{S}={\rm mat}({\rm Sigmoid}(\bm{Z}^{L}\bm{\Theta}^{L})).

This implies that
I=J=D_{L}
and
k_{0}=k_{L+1}=1
.
Fig.
2
shows the domain-factored and deep decoder-shared UNN designed for radio maps.
Note that directly modeling 3D tensors with a “naive UNN” with the deep decoder architecture without using the structure in (
2
) could lead to significantly more complex UNNs.

III-D

Objective Function and Recoverability

In this section we denote the ground-truth radio map by
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}
.
Each sensor at
(i,j)
measures
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}(i,j,:)
and transmits

\bm{Y}(i,j,:)=h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}(i,j,:))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
to the fusion center, where

h(x)=\log(x+a)
with
a>0
compresses the dynamic range and mitigates numerical issues
[
12
]
.
With our UNN, radio map recovery is posed as

\displaystyle\underset{{\bm{\theta}},\bm{Z},\bm{C}\geq\bm{0}}{\minimize}

\displaystyle~\Bigg\|\underline{\bm{M}}\circledast\left(\underline{\bm{Y}}-h\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\cal G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z}_{r})\circ{\bm{c}}_{r}\right)\right)\Bigg\|_{\rm F}^{2}

(4)

where
\bm{Z}=[\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{R}]
are latent codes and

\bm{C}=[\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{R}]
are PSDs (hence non-negative).
For quantized data, replace (
4
) by the MLE of
[
12
]
(see supplementary material).

The formulation in (
4
) provably recovers
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}
up to bounded error. To see this, we define a solution set
{\cal X}^{\rm UNN}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J\times K}
that can be represented by our
domain-factored UNN in Fig.
2

as
{\cal X}^{\rm UNN}=\{\underline{\bm{X}}~|~\underline{\bm{X}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{r})\circ\bm{c}_{r},~{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{r})\in\mathcal{H}^{(\gamma)},\bm{C}\in\mathcal{C}\},

where
\mathcal{H}^{(\gamma)}=\{{\cal G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z})~|~\|{\cal G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{r})\|_{\rm F}\leq\gamma,~{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M},\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}\}
,
\mathcal{C}=\{\bm{C}~|~\bm{C}\geq\bm{0},\|\bm{c}_{r}\|_{2}\leq\kappa,\forall r\in[R]\}
, and

\mathcal{M}=\{{\bm{\theta}}=[\bm{\Theta}^{0}\ldots\bm{\Theta}^{L}],\bm{\Theta}^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{i}\times k_{i+1}}~|~\|\bm{\Theta}^{i}\|_{\sigma}\leq s,\|\bm{\Theta}^{i}\|_{2,1}\leq b\}
and
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{0}^{2}}~|~\|\bm{z}\|_{2}\leq a\}
.
We characterize
{\cal X}^{\rm UNN}
’s complexity by the following lemma:

Lemma 1

The covering number of the
\epsilon
-net of
{\cal X}^{\rm UNN}
, denoted by
{\sf N}({\cal X}^{\rm UNN},\epsilon)
, can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\log({\sf N}({\cal X}^{\rm UNN},\epsilon))\leq\nicefrac{{R^{3}(\kappa+\gamma)a^{2}b^{2}P\log(2W^{2})s^{2L-2}L^{3}}}{{\epsilon^{2}}}

\displaystyle+RD_{0}^{2}\log\left(\nicefrac{{6RPa(\kappa+\gamma)}}{{\epsilon}}\right)+RK\log\left(\nicefrac{{3R\kappa(\kappa+\gamma)}}{{\epsilon}}\right).

(5)

The proof is in the supplementary material.
Using the above, we show:

Proposition 1

Assume that
\bm{\varOmega}
is uniformly sampled from
[I]\times[J]
without replacement. Suppose that
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\star}\in{\cal X}^{\rm UNN}
and that
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\ast}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}{{\cal G}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}}(\bm{z}_{r}^{\ast})}\circ{\bm{c}}_{r}^{\ast}
, where
(\bm{\theta}^{\star},\{\bm{z}_{r}^{\star},\bm{c}_{r}^{\star}\}_{r=1}^{R})
is any optimal solution of (
4
).
Then, the following holds with probability of at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\frac{\|\underline{\bm{X}}^{\star}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\rm F}}{\sqrt{IJK}}\leq{\sf O}\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{(\log({\sf N}({\cal X}^{\rm UNN},\epsilon)))^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\sqrt{K}N^{1/4}}\right)+\nu^{\rm UNN},

where
\nu^{\rm UNN}=\min_{{\underline{\bm{X}}}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rm UNN}}\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\infty}
.

The proof is obtained by inserting Lemma
1
into the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 2]
, thereby omitted.
Our UNN keeps
L
,
s
, and
b
small, tightening (
5
) and improving sample complexity.
Bounds on
\bm{c}_{r}
,
\bm{z}_{r}
, and
\bm{\Theta}^{i}
are enforced with regularization; see the supplementary material for the quantized case.

V
Conclusion and Future Direction

This letter addressed the SC problem using a custom-designed untrained neural network (UNN) as a deep prior.
By leveraging physical properties of radio maps, our architecture reduces parameter count while maintaining expressiveness, leading to improved sample efficiency.
The proposed method performs competitively with state-of-the-art approaches, such as learned DGM-based models, but avoids issues like training-testing mismatch.
As a future direction, incorporating advanced propagation effects (e.g., multipath, diffraction, scattering) into the UNN architecture or loss in a differentiable way could enhance modeling accuracy and interpretability in complex settings.
