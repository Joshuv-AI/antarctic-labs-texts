Renormalized Perturbation Theory at Field-level: the LSS bootstrap in GridSPT
and Atsushi Taruya

Abstract
We present a first step toward field-level cosmological inference beyond the standard Λ \Lambda CDM model, focusing on optimizing precision tests in the nonlinear regime of large-scale structure (LSS). As an illustrative case, we study the model-independent “bootstrap” coefficient of the second-order perturbation theory (PT) kernel for matter in real space, which we use as a proxy for new physics effects in the nonlinear sector. We discuss in details the ultraviolet (UV) cutoff dependence induced by discretizing fields on a grid, which requires proper renormalization to eliminate grid artifacts. We formulate a Wilsonian perturbative framework in which the evolution from a UV theory defined at a high cutoff Λ uv \Lambda_{\rm uv} down to lower cutoffs is computed analytically, even beyond the validity of a derivative expansion. Within this framework, we develop an extended version of the GridSPT code incorporating the bootstrap parameterization and demonstrate how cutoff-independent predictions can be achieved through the inclusion of appropriate counterterms. We validate our approach at third- and fifth-order in PT, emphasizing the importance of higher-derivative contributions for unbiased parameter extraction. Our framework is readily extendable to biased tracers and redshift-space distortions.

Introduction

The large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe offers a critical testing ground for our understanding of fundamental physics. In order to fully exploit data from surveys like
DESI

[
1
]
and

Euclid

[
2
,
3
]

it becomes increasingly important to develop methods that optimize the extraction of cosmological information from LSS, particularly in ways that are sensitive to physics beyond the standard
\Lambda
CDM model. Various “new physics” scenarios have been suggested, but currently there is no theoretical basis to select one over the others. Additionally, in most plausible cases, the predicted signatures are quite elusive, underscoring the need for a model-independent framework that can identify minor anomalies. To this end, a “LSS bootstrap” approach was formulated in
[
4
]
, and further developed in
[
5
,
6
]
. It is a symmetry-based approach in which no specific form for the Lagrangian and the equations of motion is assumed. The most general form for the dark matter (DM) density and velocity fields, as well as for biased tracers (galaxies, DM halos, …) compatible with the assumed symmetries, is derived at each order in perturbation theory (PT) in terms of the linear field and a finite number of free time-dependent coefficients. The detection of deviations of these coefficients from their
\Lambda
CDM values would then be a signal of new physics.

While state of the art analyses frequently concentrate on low-order correlators like the power spectrum
[

,

,

(for a review, see
[

). A natural progression in this sequence includes conducting inference directly at the field level, thus preserving all accessible informational content in an uncompressed form.
This approach, which has been developed in

has the potential of providing an ideal setting for the exploration of both linear and non-linear manifestations of new physics.

In this work, we pursue the construction of a field-level perturbative framework for model-independent cosmological inference, emphasizing its application to precision constraints on departures from
\Lambda
CDM.

To this end, we build upon the
GridSPT
code
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, an implementation of Eulerian PT (for a review, see
[
43
]
) on discretized grids, and modify it to include the LSS bootstrap parametrization, in order to systematically explore deviations from the standard model at the nonlinear level.

Working at the field level on a grid necessarily introduces an ultraviolet (UV) cutoff. The linear fields which form the building blocks of higher PT orders contain wavenumbers only up to the Nyquist frequency of the grid, or up to even lower values, as we will see when discussing the need to avoid spurious effects such as
aliasing
. A meticulous treatment of this artificial cutoff-dependence is therefore essential to obtain physically meaningful, cutoff-independent, results.

We take a Wilsonian perspective (for a similar philosophy, see
[
44
]
and, more recently,
[
45
,
46
]
, where also biased tracers and non-Gaussian initial conditions were considered): the starting point is the definition of the perturbative model at a high momentum cutoff scale,
\Lambda_{\rm uv}
. The model is built from the linear field coarse-grained at the scale
\Lambda_{\rm uv}
, and is given by a truncated PT series plus a set of “counterterms”. As in the Effective Field Theory of LSS (EFTofLSS)
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
, the form of the counterterms is determined by the symmetries of the system (rotational invariance, Extended Galilean Invariance (EGI), and, for the matter and velocity fields, mass and momentum conservation). Their number, on the other hand, is determined by the order of the approximation, both in the PT expansion and in the expansion on derivatives of the fields. The coefficients of the counterterms encode physics beyond the cutoff, that is, from scales
q>\Lambda_{\rm uv}
not described by the model, and therefore are not computable. Their values must be fixed by
renormalization conditions
, provided by the comparison between the computed observables and the measured ones. On the other hand, the
running
of these parameters, that is, their evolution as the cutoff scale is lowered to
\Lambda<\Lambda_{\rm uv}
, is computable in PT. Therefore, the requirement that physical observables are cutoff-independent provides the connection between the UV model and any other model defined at a lower scale
\Lambda
. We perform this renormalization explicitly up to fifth order in perturbation theory, achieving properly renormalized field-level models suitable for the determination of the bootstrap parameters. This procedure allows us not only to maintain theoretical consistency but also to work efficiently with smaller grids in practical computations.

In particular, we highlight the crucial role of higher-derivative terms in achieving precision inference. These terms, which emerge naturally in the renormalization procedure, can be computed analytically within our framework. Including them is essential to accurately capture small-scale physics and to ensure the robust determination of bootstrap parameters necessary for model-independent tests of cosmology.

A

B

Field level bootstrap

In this section we review the perturbative model that we will implement in this analysis, based on the “LSS bootstrap” approach; for more details, see
[
4
,

5

2.1
First order

We will express perturbations in terms of the density contrast,
\delta({\bf{x}},a)
, and of the rescaled velocity divergence,

\theta({\bf{x}},a)\equiv\partial_{i}u^{i}({\bf{x}},a)\,,

(2.1)

where

u^{i}({\bf{x}},a)\equiv-\frac{v^{i}({\bf{x}},a)}{faH(a)}\,,

(2.2)

v^{i}({\bf{x}},a)
is the peculiar velocity,

f=d\log D_{+}/d\log a
, is the growth function, and
D_{+}(a)
the linear growth factor, that we will assume to be scale-independent.
We then expand the fields according to the standard perturbative Ansatz
[
51
]
,

\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}({\bf{x}},a)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta^{(n)}({\bf{x}},a)\,,\quad\theta^{[N],{\rm PT}}({\bf{x}},a)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\theta^{(n)}({\bf{x}},a)\,.

(2.3)

In the following, we will indicate with lowercase letters inside parentheses (e.g. like in
\delta^{(n)}
) the perturbative contribution at a given order, and with capital letters inside square brackets (e.g. like in
\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}
), the order of truncation of the PT series, that is, the sum of all the
m
-th order contributions with
1\leq m\leq N
.

The first order solutions on the growing mode are

\delta^{(1)}({\bf{x}},a)=\theta^{(1)}({\bf{x}},a)\equiv\varphi({\bf{x}},a)\,,

(2.4)

with
\varphi({\bf{x}},a)=D_{+}(a)\varphi_{0}({\bf{x}})
the linear density field, a Gaussian realization whose variance is given by the linear power spectrum,
P(k)
, of the chosen cosmology.

2.2
Second order

In Fourier space, the second order fields can be expressed in terms of convolutions,

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}({\bf{k}},a)

\displaystyle={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2}}F_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2};a)\varphi({\bf{q}}_{1},a)\varphi({\bf{q}}_{2},a)\,,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\theta^{(2)}({\bf{k}},a)

\displaystyle={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2}}G_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2};a)\varphi({\bf{q}}_{1},a)\varphi({\bf{q}}_{2},a)\,,

(2.6)

where

{\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n}}\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}q_{1}\cdots d^{3}q_{n}}{(2\pi)^{3(n-1)}}\delta_{D}({\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}_{1}\cdots-{\bf{q}}_{n})\,,

(2.7)

and the convolution kernels are,

\displaystyle F_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2};a)

\displaystyle=\beta({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})+\frac{a_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)}{2}\,\gamma({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})\,,

(2.8)

\displaystyle G_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2};a)

\displaystyle=\beta({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})+\frac{d_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)}{2}\,\gamma({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})\,,

(2.9)

with the mode-coupling functions defined as

\displaystyle\beta({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\bf{q}}_{1}+{\bf{q}}_{2}|^{2}{\bf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\bf{q}}_{2}}{2q_{1}^{2}q_{2}^{2}}\,,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\gamma({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{({\bf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\bf{q}}_{2})^{2}}{q_{1}^{2}q_{2}^{2}}\,.

(2.11)

The coefficients of the
\beta({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})
couplings in Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), are fixed to unity by EGI. On the other hand, the bootstrap coefficients
a^{(2)}_{\gamma}
and
d^{(2)}_{\gamma}
are not constrained by symmetries, but assume different, in general time-dependent, values in different models.
As an example, in
\Lambda
CDM the equations for the second order bootstrap coefficients are
[
4
]

\displaystyle\frac{d\,a_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)}{d\ln{a}}=f\left(2-2a_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)+d_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)\right)\,,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\frac{d\,d_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)}{d\ln{a}}=f\left(\frac{3}{2}\frac{\Omega_{m}(a)}{f^{2}}\bigg(a_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)-d_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)\bigg)-d_{\gamma}^{(2)}(a)\right)\,.

Deviations from the solutions of these equations parameterize deviations from
\Lambda
CDM in a model-independent way.
The standard approach in PT is to use the ‘‘Einstein-de Sitter (EdS) limit’’ for the kernels,
1
1

1

For analyses going beyond the EdS approximation, see
[
52
,
53
,
50
]
.
obtained by setting
\Omega_{m}/f^{2}=1
, in the above equations, which then give the time-independent values
[
51
]

\quad a_{\gamma,\;{\rm EdS}}^{(2)}=\frac{10}{7}\,,\quad d_{\gamma,\;{\rm EdS}}^{(2)}=\frac{6}{7}\,.

(2.13)

These values provide the initial conditions for the equations in all models that reduce to EdS at early times.

The bootstrap approach can be extended beyond second order, with an increasing number of cosmology-dependent coefficients. For the explicit expressions up to third order in the Eulerian formalism, see Appendix
A
and
[
4
,
50
]
, and for those up to sixth order in the equivalent Lagrangian formalism see
[
5
]
. Moreover, the program can be extended from the matter and velocity fields to the density contrast for biased tracers. In this case, mass and momentum conservation are not imposed, therefore the kernels contain an higher number of free parameters, corresponding to those of the perturbative bias expansion (see, for instance,
[
54
]
). In this paper, we will restrict the analysis to the matter density field in real space. Moreover, we will set all coefficients of the kernels of order higher than second to their EdS values, and will leave only the second order bootstrap coefficient,
a_{\gamma}^{(2)}
, free to vary.

In configuration space the second order density field is given by,

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}({\bf{x}})

\displaystyle=\varphi^{(2)}_{\beta}({\bf{x}})+\frac{a_{\gamma}^{(2)}}{2}\varphi^{(2)}_{\gamma}({\bf{x}})\,,

(2.14)

where we have omitted the time dependence and have defined the second order fields,

\displaystyle\varphi^{(2)}_{\beta}({\bf{x}})

\displaystyle\equiv\partial_{i}\varphi({\bf{x}})\left(\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)+\left(\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\left(\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\,,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\varphi^{(2)}_{\gamma}({\bf{x}})

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\left(\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\,.

(2.16)

3
GridSPT
and bootstrap

3.1
The
GridSPT
code

Our numerical implementation of the bootstrap perturbative approach is based on the grid-based Eulerian PT code called
GridSPT

[
40
,
41
,
42
]
. Specifically, the code performs standard PT calculations at the field level, and generates numerical realizations of higher-order density and velocity fields at each grid point. Notably, by applying a novel
n
EPT (
n
th-order Eulerian PT) scheme, the field-level calculation with
GridSPT
also yields improved predictions for summary statistics, outperforming those from the conventional order-by-order PT calculations
[
55
,
56
]
.

The core of the
GridSPT
algorithm is a real-space recursion relation under the
EdS approximation. For
n\geq 2
,
the
n
-th order Eulerian density and velocity fields are constructed in configuration space from their lower-order counterparts as follows
[
42
]
:

\begin{pmatrix}\delta^{(n)}\\
\\
\theta^{(n)}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{2}{(2n+3)(n-1)}\begin{pmatrix}n+\frac{1}{2}&1\\
&\\
\frac{3}{2}&n\end{pmatrix}\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{i}\delta^{(m)}\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\theta^{(n-m)}+\delta^{(m)}\theta^{(n-m)}\\
&\\
\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}\left(\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\theta^{(m)}\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\theta^{(n-m)}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.1)

Given the linear fields
\delta^{(1)}({\bf{x}})
and
\theta^{(1)}({\bf{x}})
on grids as in Eq. (
2.4
), the code computes the nonlinear source terms given on the right-hand side of Eq. (
3.1
), making use of the Fast Fourier Transform.
The differential operations involved in the source terms are performed via expressions such as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})

\displaystyle=-i\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i{\bf{k}}\cdot{\bf{x}}}\,\frac{k_{i}}{k^{2}}\varphi({\bf{k}})\,,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i{\bf{k}}\cdot{\bf{x}}}\,\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}\varphi({\bf{k}})\,.

(3.3)

3.2
Bootstrapping
GridSPT

The fields computed from
GridSPT
by solving Eq. (
3.1
) rely on the EdS approximation for the PT kernels. In order to compute the
n=2
bootstrap density field, we write Eq. (
2.5
) as

\delta^{(2)}({\bf{x}})=\delta^{(2)}_{\rm EdS}({\bf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\gamma}\,a^{(2)}_{\gamma,{\rm EdS}}\,\varphi^{(2)}_{\gamma}({\bf{x}})\,,

(3.4)

where the parameter

\varepsilon_{\gamma}\equiv\frac{a_{\gamma}^{(2)}}{a_{\gamma,{{\rm EdS}}}^{(2)}}-1\,,

(3.5)

represents the relative deviation from the EdS limit.
Notice that, at
z=1
and for the
\Lambda
CDM model considered in our analysis, we have
\varepsilon_{\gamma,\Lambda{\rm CDM}}\simeq-7.8\cdot 10^{-4}
.

The field
\delta^{(2)}_{\rm EdS}({\bf{x}})
will be taken from the output of
GridSPT
, while the field
\varphi_{\gamma}^{(2)}
, defined in Eq. (
2.16
), will be built from the same linear field
\varphi({\bf{x}})
as
\delta^{(2)}_{\rm EdS}({\bf{x}})
.
In order to reduce the order of the tensor structures involved, we write Eq. (
2.16
), as

\varphi_{\gamma}^{(2)}({\bf{x}})=\left(\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\left(\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\right]+\left(\frac{\partial_{i}}{\partial^{2}}\varphi({\bf{x}})\right)\partial_{i}\varphi({\bf{x}})\,.

(3.6)

Since in this analysis we will focus on the
a_{\gamma}^{(2)}
parameter, we will set all the other bootstrap parameters to their EdS value, and therefore, we will compute all the fields at orders higher than
n=2
with
GridSPT
, that is,

\delta^{(n)}({\bf{x}})=\delta^{(n)}_{\rm EdS}({\bf{x}})\,,\qquad(n>2)\,.

(3.7)

Actually, this is not completely consistent, as the relations found in
[
4
]
imply that all higher order PT kernels depend also on the second order coefficient
a_{\gamma}^{(2)}
. However, we checked explicitly that this extra dependence is largely subdominant and that taking it into account in the third order field does not change our results in any appreciable way, see Appendix
A
.

Field on the grid: regularization and renormalization

Simple illustration of how aliasing works. A detector samples a signal at each unit interval in a domain of length
L=10
, so
N_{g}=10
, meaning that the Nyquist frequency is
k_{\rm Nyq}=\frac{\pi N_{g}}{L}=\pi
. A wavemode at the Nyquist frequency (pink) is correctly sampled, however frequencies at
k=(2n+1)k_{\rm Nyq}
(e.g., blue and brown) cannot be resolved and appear to the detector as
k=k_{\rm Nyq}
as well. The result is that the measured signal at
k_{\rm Nyq}
is the sum of all the contributions for
n>0
(black).

4.1
The need for a momentum cutoff

As discussed in
[
57
]
, when performing perturbative computations on a grid, one should take care in avoiding spurious “aliasing” effects on the resulting fields. These originate from products of linear fields on the same grid point or, using a field-theoretical language, from
composite operators
. On a finite cubic grid of volume
L^{3}
and
N_{g}^{3}
grid points a mode
{\bf{k}}
is indistinguishable from modes of the form
{\bf{k}}+2\,{\bf{n}}\,k_{\rm Nyq}
, where
k_{\rm Nyq}=\pi N_{g}/L
is the Nyquist frequency and
{\bf{n}}=(n_{x},n_{y},n_{z})
, with
n_{x,y,z}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Modes with
{\bf{n}}\neq\mathbf{0}
, being not supported by the grid, are observed at lower, aliased, modes, see Fig.
1
. In Fourier space,
N
-th order PT fields are given by convolutions involving up to
N
linear fields at all possible modes. Therefore, in order to avoid that the resulting Fourier modes of the composite fields exceed the Nyquist limit, we will low-pass filter the linear fields at the
N
-dependent scale
\Lambda
, given by the Orszatz rule
[
58
,
42
]
,

k\leq\Lambda=\frac{2}{N+1}k_{\rm Nyq}=\frac{2}{N+1}\frac{\pi N_{g}}{L}\,.

(4.1)

In the following we will apply a isotropic top-hat filter in Fourier space, and will indicate explicitely the
\Lambda
-dependence of the fields. In particular, for the linear fields,
\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
will indicate the Fourier transform of

\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=\varphi({\bf{k}})\,\Theta\left(\Lambda-k\right)\,,

(4.2)

while,
\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
and
\theta^{(n)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
, for
n>1
, will indicate
n
-th order fields composed by
n
linear fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
.

Even ignoring aliasing, a cutoff scale is ultimately provided by the grid step, that is, the Nyquist frequency. Therefore, in field-theoretical language, the theory on the grid is unavoidably
regularized
by the presence of an unphysical cut-off, and the cut-off dependence must be carefully taken into account. One needs to understand how
\Lambda
-independent quantities can be defined from
\Lambda
-dependent fields and couplings, and how these quantities can be related to physical observables. That is, one needs to discuss the
renormalization
of the theory. Moreover, on a practical point of view, controlling the cut-off dependence of the theory allows using lower cut-off values, and then smaller number of grid points, increasing numerical efficiency.

4.2
Integrating out UV modes

Figure 2
:
Schematic illustration of the relevant scales and fields defined in the paper. The theory is defined at a scale
\Lambda_{\rm uv}\gg k_{\rm nl}\gg k_{\rm max}
, as the sum of the PT contribution, Eq. (
4.5
), and the UV counterterms, Eq. (
4.7
). By considering a scale
\Lambda<\Lambda_{\rm uv}
, we can define a new theory, again as the sum of a PT part (expressed in terms of the new IR fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}
) and UV counterterms. The latter are given by the sum of the UV counterterms of the original theory and the new contributions of Eq. (
4.13
), obtained by integrating out the
\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}
fields.

In a Wilsonian approach
[
59
,
60
]
, the field theory is initially defined at a UV scale,
\Lambda_{\rm uv}
, and then related to theories at lower cutoff scales
\Lambda
by integrating out fluctuations of wavenumbers between
\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{\rm uv}
. In the present cosmological context, we assume the hierarchy (see Fig.
2
),

k_{\rm max}\ll k_{\rm nl}\ll\Lambda_{\rm uv}\,,

(4.3)

where
k_{\rm max}
is the largest mode for which we will compute the density field, while
k_{\rm nl}
is the mode above which the fluid description on which PT is based breaks down.

The UV theory is based on the EFTofLSS
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
. We will consider PT models at
N
-th order, which in Fourier space, and omitting the time dependence, can be expressed as the sum of two types of contributions,

\delta^{[N]}({\bf{k}})=\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,.

(4.4)

The first term is the standard PT series truncated at order
N
,

\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.5)

with the
n
-th order contribution given by the convolution,

\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n}}F_{n}({\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n})\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{q}}_{1})\cdots\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{q}}_{n})\,.

(4.6)

The
F_{n}
’s are the usual symmetrized PT kernels
[
51
]
(given at
n=2
in Eq. (
2.5
)) and the filtered linear fields
\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{q}})
have support only for wavenumbers
q\leq\Lambda_{\rm uv}
, see Eq. (
4.2
).
The second contribution in Eq. (
4.4
) sums up the UV
counterterms
up to order
n=N
,

\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Delta\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,.

(4.7)

The counterterms are built with the same fields
\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
, and feed the effect of “UV modes” with
q>\Lambda_{\rm uv}
on the modes
k
, in such a way that the PT expansion is well behaved and converges to the truth (e.g. a high resolution N-body simulation or, eventually, observational data). Because PT fails for scales
q>k_{\rm nl}
, the convergence is achieved up to corrections
O(k^{2}/k_{\rm nl}^{2})
, whose coefficients are not computable in PT but must be fixed via
renormalization conditions
(see sect.
4.5
).
The quadratic decoupling of the nonperturbative corrections is a consequence of momentum conservation. Thanks to the hierarchy (
4.3
), cutoff dependent contributions of
O(k^{2}/\Lambda_{\rm uv}^{2})\ll O(k^{2}/k_{\rm nl}^{2})
are negligible in the UV theory.

We then consider a lower value for the cutoff,
\Lambda<\Lambda_{\rm uv}
as shown in Fig.
2
, and derive the model, defined at the new scale
\Lambda
, such that the full field is
\Lambda
-independent, that is, imposing the condition,

\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})=\delta^{[N]}({\bf{k}})\,.

(4.8)

We split the UV field as,

\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{q}})=\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})+\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})\,,

(4.9)

where
\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})
has support on
\Lambda<q<\Lambda_{\rm uv}
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{q}})\Theta(\Lambda-q)=\varphi({\bf{q}})\Theta(\Lambda-q)\,,

\displaystyle\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{q}})\Theta(q-\Lambda)=\varphi({\bf{q}})\Theta(\Lambda_{\rm uv}-q)\Theta(q-\Lambda)\,.

(4.10)

The model at the scale
\Lambda
will be expressed in terms of the fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})
, whereas the new UV fields
\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})
will be “integrated out”, see below.
Implementing Eq. (
4.9
) in Eq. (
4.6
) gives

\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})=\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.11)

where the first term is the standard PT contribution expressed in terms of the fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})
,

\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n}}F_{n}({\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n})\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\cdots\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{n})\,,

(4.12)

while the second term contains the couplings between IR and UV fields,

\Delta\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})=\sum_{p=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
p\end{array}\right){\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n}}F_{n}({\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{n})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\cdots\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{p})\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{p+1})\cdots\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{n})\,.

(4.13)

Defining the standard PT contribution in Eq. (
4.8
) analogously to Eq. (
4.5
),

\delta^{[N],{\rm PT}}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.14)

we get the counterterms at the new scale
\Lambda
,

\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.15)

where

\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Delta\delta^{(n)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}}).

(4.16)

It is important to notice the difference between the two contributions in Eq. (
4.15
) (see also Fig.
2
). The second term,
\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
, is not computable in PT, but can only be fixed by imposing renormalization conditions, obtained by measurements on simulations or on real data. On the other hand, the second term vanishes for
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rm uv}
and can be computed in PT at any
\Lambda
, according to Eq. (
4.13
).

4.3
Structure of the counterterms

In order to understand the structure of the counterterms, we can inspect in details the lowest orders, from the definition (
4.13
).
At
n=1
we have

\Delta\delta^{(1)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})=\int d^{3}q\,\delta_{D}({\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})=\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=0\,,

(4.17)

which vanishes because
k<\Lambda
. For the same reason, at
n=2
we have just the contribution involving two UV fields,

\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2}}F_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{2})\,.

(4.18)

Only the IR fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})
are described deterministically by the PT model at the scale
\Lambda
. On the other hand, the
\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})
fields are integrated out, that is, we take the expectation values of their products. This gives,

\langle\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\rangle={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2}}F_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})\langle\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{2})\rangle=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}F_{2}({\bf{q}},-{\bf{q}})P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)=0\,,

(4.19)

where we have defined
P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)
as

\langle\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{2})\rangle\equiv(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}({\bf{q}}_{1}+{\bf{q}}_{2})P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q_{2})\,,

(4.20)

that is,

P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)\equiv P(q)\Theta(\Lambda_{\rm uv}-q)^{2}\Theta(q-\Lambda)^{2}\,.

(4.21)

The vanishing of Eq. (
4.19
) comes from momentum conservation, which implies the following decoupling behavior of the second order kernel,

F_{2}\left(\frac{{\bf{k}}}{2}+{\bf{q}},\frac{{\bf{k}}}{2}-{\bf{q}}\right)=O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{q^{2}}\right)\,,\qquad{\rm for}\;k\ll q\,.

(4.22)

Moreover, for Gaussian initial conditions, the stochastic field is uncorrelated to the linear field,

\langle\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}}^{\prime})\rangle={\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2}}F_{2}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2})\langle\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{2})\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}}^{\prime})\rangle=0,

(4.23)

and has a power spectrum given by,

\langle\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(-{\bf{k}})\rangle^{\prime}=P^{22}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(k)\,,

(4.24)

where the prime indicates that the factor
(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}(0)
has been omitted from the average, and,

P^{22}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(k)\equiv 2\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}F_{2}({\bf{q}},{\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}})^{2}P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(|{\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}|)\simeq\frac{127}{294}k^{4}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)}{q^{2}}\right)^{2}\,,

(4.25)

with the last equality holding in the
k\ll\Lambda
regime.

In summary, at lowest order in
k/\Lambda
, we can write the second order counterterm field as,

\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\,\epsilon^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}},

(4.26)

where the stochastic coefficient
\epsilon^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
is uncorrelated to the linear field and has constant variance, given by

P^{\epsilon}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}=\lim_{k/\Lambda\to 0}\frac{k^{4}_{\rm nl}}{k^{4}}P^{22}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(k)\,.

(4.27)

All its higher order cumulants can be obtained by analogous computations in PT.

At order
n=3
two new contributions arise,

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle=\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{(3),b}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.28)

with

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle\equiv 3\,{\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2},{\bf{q}}_{3}}F_{3}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2},{\bf{q}}_{3})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{2})\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{3})\,,

(4.29)

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(3),b}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle\equiv\,{\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2},{\bf{q}}_{3}}F_{3}({\bf{q}}_{1},{\bf{q}}_{2},{\bf{q}}_{3})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{1})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{2})\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}}_{3})\,,

(4.30)

whereas the contribution with only one
\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}
vanishes, again, by momentum conservation.
The stochastic field
\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
has vanishing expectation value and is correlated with the linear field,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\rangle=0\,,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\varphi_{\Lambda}(-{\bf{k}})\rangle^{\prime}=3P_{\Lambda}(k)\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}F_{3}({\bf{k}},{\bf{q}},-{\bf{q}})P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\left(\frac{61}{630}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)}{q^{2}}\right)\,k^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{q^{2}}\right)\right)P_{\Lambda}(k)\simeq-\,c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}P_{\Lambda}(k)\,,

(4.31)

where, again, the last line corresponds to the
k\ll\Lambda
limit,
P_{\Lambda}(k)
is the IR linear power spectrum,

P_{\Lambda}(k)\equiv\langle\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{q}})\varphi_{\Lambda}(-{\bf{q}})\rangle^{\prime}\,,

(4.32)

and we have defined the coefficient

c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\equiv\frac{61}{630}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)}{q^{2}}\,,

(4.33)

which gives the perturbative contribution at third order to the speed of sound of the EFTofLSS
[
49
]
.
The power spectrum of the
\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
field is given by

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(-{\bf{k}})\rangle^{\prime}=P_{\Lambda}(k)\left(3\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}F_{3}({\bf{k}},{\bf{q}},-{\bf{q}})P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+2\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[F_{3}({\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}+{\bf{p}},{\bf{q}},-{\bf{p}})\right]^{2}P_{\Lambda}(|{\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}+{\bf{p}}|)P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(p)

\displaystyle\simeq\left[\left(c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\right]\frac{k^{4}}{k^{4}_{\rm nl}}P_{\Lambda}(k)+O\left(\frac{k^{6}}{k^{6}_{\rm nl}}\right),

(4.34)

In other terms, we can write,

\Delta\delta^{(3),a}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\simeq-\left(c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}+\eta^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\right)\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.35)

where
c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
is defined in (
4.33
), and the stochastic coefficient
\eta^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
is scale independent, has vanishing expectation value and variance
\sigma^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
, which can be read from the small
k
limit of the second contribution in Eq. (
4.34
).

The term
\Delta\delta^{(3),b}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
, at small
k
, gives a contribution similar to that obtained at second order, Eq. (
4.26
),

\Delta\delta^{(3),b}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\,\epsilon^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\,,

(4.36)

where
\epsilon^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
has zero average, is uncorrelated to the linear field, and has a power spectrum given by the two-loop UV integral obtained by squaring Eq. (
4.30
).

4.4
Quadratic higher derivative terms

At
n>3
, counterterms of the type (
4.13
) with
n-p=2
contain contributions quadratic in the IR linear fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}
. These terms correlate with
\varphi_{\gamma}^{(2)}
, and therefore enter in the determination of the bootstrap coefficient in Eq. (
3.4
), as we will discuss in Sect.
5.3
. In order to extract
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
reliably, these counterterms should be taken into account. In particular, at
n=4
, integrating out two linear fields
\delta\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
one obtains contributions of the following form, in configuration space,

\displaystyle c^{(n)}_{\varphi^{2};\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}\varphi_{\Lambda}^{2}({\bf{x}})\,,\quad c^{(n)}_{\varphi_{\beta};\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}\varphi^{(2)}_{\beta,\Lambda}({\bf{x}})\,,

\displaystyle c^{(n)}_{\varphi_{\gamma};\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}\varphi^{(2)}_{\gamma,\Lambda}({\bf{x}})\,,\quad c^{(n)}_{\tilde{\gamma};\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}\,\varphi^{(2)}_{\tilde{\gamma},\Lambda}({\bf{x}})\,.

(4.37)

The quadratic operator
\varphi_{\Lambda}^{2}({\bf{x}})
is just the square of
\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
,
\varphi^{(2)}_{\beta,\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
and
\varphi^{(2)}_{\gamma,\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
have been defined in Eqs. (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), respectively, while
\varphi^{(2)}_{\tilde{\gamma},\Lambda}({\bf{x}})
is the Fourier transform of

\varphi^{(2)}_{\tilde{\gamma},\Lambda}({\bf{k}})\equiv{\cal I}_{{\bf{k}};{\bf{p}}_{1},{\bf{p}}_{2}}\,\tilde{\gamma}({\bf{p}}_{1},{\bf{p}}_{2})\,\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{p}}_{1})\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{p}}_{2})\,,

(4.38)

with

\tilde{\gamma}({\bf{p}}_{1},{\bf{p}}_{2})\equiv\frac{{\bf{p}}_{1}\cdot{\bf{p}}_{2}}{|{\bf{p}}_{1}+{\bf{p}}_{2}|^{2}}\gamma({\bf{p}}_{1},{\bf{p}}_{2})\,.

(4.39)

The
\Lambda
dependence of the coefficients can be obtained by a perturbative computation, analogously to what discussed for the “speed of sound” coefficient,
c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
, see Eq. (
4.33
). At fourth order, we get,

c^{(4)}_{X;\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}=\frac{2096}{33957\,\pi^{2}}\,C_{X}\,k_{\rm nl}^{2}\int^{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}_{\Lambda}P(\Lambda^{\prime})d\Lambda^{\prime}\,,

(4.40)

where
X=\{\varphi^{2},\,\varphi_{\gamma},\,\varphi_{\beta},\,\varphi_{\tilde{\gamma}}\}
,
and

C_{\varphi^{2}}=1\,,\quad C_{\varphi_{\gamma}}=\frac{4223}{10480}\,,\quad C_{\varphi_{\beta}}=\frac{32879}{20960}\,,\quad C_{\tilde{\gamma}}=\frac{1503}{1310}\,.

(4.41)

All these terms are “higher derivatives”, that is, suppressed by
O(k^{2}/k_{\rm nl}^{2})
in Fourier space. The absence of contributions
O(1)
is again a consequence of momentum conservation.

4.5
Running and renormalization

The
\Lambda
-dependence (the
running
) of the counterterms in
\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})
is determined by that of
\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
, see Eq. (
4.15
),

\Lambda\frac{d}{d\Lambda}\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=\Lambda\frac{d}{d\Lambda}\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})\,,

(4.42)

and therefore, as we have discussed, it can be computed in PT.
To fully define the model, one has to impose renormalization conditions, that is, boundary conditions for the equations for the running of
\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})
at a given scale
\Lambda=\bar{\Lambda}
, and for a given external momentum
{\bf{k}}=\bar{\bf{k}}
. One possibility is to give the renormalization conditions at
\bar{\Lambda}=\Lambda_{\rm uv}
. Assuming the hierarchy (
4.3
),
the form of the counterterms is fixed by the available symmetries, that is, mass and momentum conservation, rotational invariance and EGI, which, up to third order and at leading order in
k
, give

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\,\epsilon^{(2)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\,,

(4.43)

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\,\epsilon^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}-\left(c^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}+\eta^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}\right)\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\varphi_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})+O\left(\frac{k^{4}}{k_{\rm nl}^{4}}\right)\,.

(4.44)

Notice that these structures are the same as those for the pertrubative counterterms
\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
and
\Delta\delta^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}({\bf{k}})
from Eqs. (
4.26
), (
4.33
), and (
4.36
).
Symmetry arguments enforce the same expansion for the counterterms holds at any lower scale
\Lambda
, as long as the hierarchy
k\ll k_{\rm nl}\ll\Lambda
holds,

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(2)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\,\epsilon^{(2)}_{\Lambda}\,,

(4.45)

\displaystyle\Delta\delta^{(3)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\,\epsilon^{(3)}_{\Lambda}-\left(c^{(3)}_{\Lambda}+\eta^{(3)}_{\Lambda}\right)\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})+O\left(\frac{k^{4}}{k_{\rm nl}^{4}}\right)\,.

(4.46)

Focusing on the parameter
c^{(3)}_{\Lambda}
, from Eq. (
4.15
), we get

c^{[3]}_{\Lambda}=c^{(3)}_{\Lambda}=c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}+c^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}.

(4.47)

It runs with
\Lambda
as (see Eq. (
4.33
)),

\Lambda\frac{d}{d\Lambda}c^{(3)}_{\Lambda}=\Lambda\frac{d}{d\Lambda}c^{(3)}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}=-\frac{61}{1260\,\pi^{2}}\Lambda\,k_{\rm nl}^{2}P(\Lambda)\,,

(4.48)

and therefore it can be expressed, at any
\Lambda
, as

c^{(3)}_{\Lambda}=c^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}+\frac{61}{1260\,\pi^{2}}\,k_{\rm nl}^{2}\int_{\Lambda}^{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}d\Lambda^{\prime}P(\Lambda^{\prime})\,,

(4.49)

where the UV boundary condition,
c^{(3)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}
, has to be fixed by the renormalization condition.

At
N=5
, the sound speed coefficient gets a new contribution,

\Delta\delta^{[5]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=\Delta\delta^{(3)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})+\Delta\delta^{(5)}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})=-c^{[5]}_{\Lambda}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}\varphi_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})+\cdots\,,

(4.50)

where we have defined

\displaystyle c^{[5]}_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv c^{(3)}_{\Lambda}+c^{(5)}_{\Lambda}\,.

(4.51)

The running of
c^{(5)}_{\Lambda}
, obtained analogously to (
4.48
), is given by the two-loop contribution,

\displaystyle c^{(5)}_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=c^{(5)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}-\int_{\Lambda}^{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}d\Lambda^{\prime}\,\left(\frac{d}{d\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\Lambda^{\prime}}^{(5)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle=c^{(5)}_{\Lambda_{\rm uv}}+15\,\lim_{{\bf{k}}\to 0}\frac{k^{2}_{\rm nl}}{k^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}F_{5}({\bf{k}},{\bf{q}},-{\bf{q}},{\bf{p}},-{\bf{p}})P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(q)P_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}(p)\,.

(4.52)

A possible, physically motivated, renormalization condition is to require that the zero momentum limit of the cross-correlator between the
N
-th order field (after the subtraction of the linear contribution) and the linear field,

\lim_{{\bf{k}}\to 0}\left(\frac{\langle\delta^{[N]}({\bf{k}})\varphi_{\Lambda}(-{\bf{k}})\rangle^{\prime}}{P_{\Lambda}(k)}-1\right)\frac{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}{k^{2}}=\lim_{\Lambda\to 0}\lim_{{\bf{k}}\to 0}\frac{\langle\Delta\delta^{[N]}_{\Lambda}({\bf{k}})\varphi_{\Lambda}(-{\bf{k}})\rangle^{\prime}}{P_{\Lambda}(k)}\frac{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}{k^{2}}=-c^{[N]}_{\Lambda=0}\,,

(4.53)

coincides with the measured value for every
N
,

\bar{c}\equiv-\lim_{{\bf{k}}\to 0}\left(\frac{\langle\bar{\delta}({\bf{k}})\varphi(-{\bf{k}})\rangle^{\prime}}{P(k)}-1\right)\frac{k_{\rm nl}^{2}}{k^{2}}\,,

(4.54)

where
\bar{\delta}({\bf{k}})
is the data field (e.g. from N-body simulations).
According to Eqs. (
4.49
) and (
4.52
) imposing
c^{[3]}_{\Lambda=0}=c^{[5]}_{\Lambda=0}=\bar{c}
,
gives the following relation,

c^{[3]}_{\Lambda}-c^{[5]}_{\Lambda}=-\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\Lambda^{\prime}\left(\frac{d}{d\Lambda^{\prime}}c^{(5)}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(4.55)

where the difference at the RHS is given by the two-loop integral in Eq. (
4.52
).

On the other hand, the purely non-perturbative contributions to the sound speed would formally correspond to the
N\to\infty
limit of the coefficient in the
\Lambda\to\infty
limit,

c_{s}^{2}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}c^{[N]}_{\Lambda}\,.

(4.56)

By taking the derivative of Eq. (
4.40
) with respect to
\Lambda
we obtain the running of the coefficients of the higher derivative operators,

\Lambda\frac{d}{d\Lambda}c^{(4)}_{X;\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rm uv}}=-\frac{2096}{33957\,\pi^{2}}\,C_{X}\,\Lambda\,k_{\rm nl}^{2}P(\Lambda)\,.

(4.57)

5
Setting up the analysis

5.1
Likelihood and data

In the following, we will compare the bootstrap-PT model with
\Lambda
CDM simulations, and will test the reliability of our setup in extracting the non-linear bootstrap parameter
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
, as a first step towards more realistic searches for physics beyond
\Lambda
CDM from real data. We will assume a Gaussian likelihood of the form,

\log P_{N}[\delta|\delta_{in}](k_{\rm max};\Lambda,\{\alpha^{i}_{\Lambda}\})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bf n}^{n_{\rm max}}\left[\frac{\left|\delta({\bf{k}}_{\bf n})-\delta^{[N]}({\bf{k}}_{\bf n})\right|^{2}}{L^{3}\,p_{\epsilon}(k_{\bf n})}\right]+{\rm const}\,,

(5.1)

where
{\bf{k}}_{\bf n}=\frac{2\pi}{L}{\bf n}
,
n_{\rm max}=k_{\rm max}\,L/(2\pi)
and we assume constant noise

p_{\epsilon}(k_{\bf n})=\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\,,

(5.2)

where
\bar{n}
is the number density of sources. We indicate with
\{\alpha^{i}_{\Lambda}\}
the set of parameters of the model
\delta^{[N]}({\bf{k}}_{\bf n})
, that is,
\{c^{[N]}_{\Lambda},\varepsilon_{\gamma},\cdots\}
, where dots may include stochastic and higher derivative terms.
The data
\delta({\bf{k}}_{\bf n})
are computed from the overdensity field of dark matter particles measured from a
N
-body simulation with the same initial conditions

\delta^{[N]}({\bf{k}}_{\bf n})
from
GridSPT
. That is, both the
N
-body simulations and the
GridSPT
codes share the same initial random seeds and adopt identical cosmological parameters.

We use a cosmological

-body simulation performed as a part of the ongoing

project (Nishimichi

in prep.). The simulation employs

[

7
Discussion and conclusions

In this work, we have taken a first step toward implementing field-level inference beyond the standard
\Lambda
CDM model, with a particular focus on optimizing precision tests in the nonlinear regime of LSS. As a concrete example of how model-independent constraints on new physics can be derived, we focused on the bootstrap coefficient of the second-order PT kernel for matter in real space.

A central aspect of our analysis is the detailed treatment of the UV cutoff dependence inevitably induced by discretizing the field on a grid. Field theories on a lattice are well studied in other areas of physics, where the challenge of defining a meaningful continuum limit is well recognized and established methods to address it have been developed. Discretization naturally introduces a UV cutoff
\Lambda
, associated either with the removal of spurious aliasing effects or, ultimately, with the Nyquist frequency. The field theory must therefore be properly renormalized to ensure that physical predictions are independent of grid-induced artifacts.

Throughout this work, we restricted our analysis to the perturbative regime, considering only modes with wavenumbers
k<k_{{\rm max}}\ll k_{{\rm nl}}
, where
k_{{\rm nl}}
denotes the nonlinear scale. The UV theory is defined at a cutoff scale
\Lambda_{{\rm uv}}\gg k_{{\rm nl}}
, ensuring that higher-derivative corrections are controlled by a single physical scale,
k_{{\rm nl}}
, while
O(k^{2}/\Lambda_{\rm uv}^{2})
terms are “irrelevant”, since the values of their coefficients affect the models at lower cutoff
\Lambda
only via contributions suppressed by powers of
\Lambda^{2}/\Lambda^{2}_{\rm uv}
. The evolution from the UV down to lower cutoff scales
\Lambda<\Lambda_{{\rm uv}}
can then be computed perturbatively, and therefore it does not necessarily rely on the validity of a derivative expansion in powers of
k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}
. At any given
\Lambda
, the model is expressed in terms of cutoff fields
\varphi_{\Lambda}
, and consists of a truncated PT series supplemented by a set of counterterms. The impact of modes with
k>\Lambda
is encoded in the counterterm coefficients, while modes with
k<\Lambda
are treated using
GridSPT
, which we extended to implement the bootstrap parameterization. Lowering the value of
\Lambda
in a controlled way is instrumental in reducing the number of grid points, and consequently, in reducing the computational cost.

We studied two renormalized models: one including terms up to
N=3
and another up to
N=5
, where
N
denotes the perturbative order. For both cases, we derived the corresponding
k_{{\rm max}}
values, finding them to be consistent with those obtained in standard EFTofLSS analyses for correlators
[
76
]
. In the
N=5
case, we demonstrated that including the contributions from higher-derivative terms quadratic in the linear fields is essential for achieving
\Lambda
-independent results and for obtaining an unbiased extraction of the bootstrap coefficient.

In
[
77
]
a non-renormalization theorem for the coefficients of galileon operators was proven. As
\varphi_{\gamma}
belongs to this category, requiring renormalization might seem, at first sight, in contraddiction with this theorem. Nevertheless, the theorem is inapplicable to the coefficients of higher derivative terms like
(\partial^{2}/k_{\rm nl}^{2})\varphi_{\gamma,\Lambda}
, such as those examined in this study, therefore there is no contraddiction.

The methodology developed here is readily extendable to more realistic scenarios. Incorporating biased tracers is conceptually and practically straightforward within this framework, requiring only the addition of further operators to the
GridSPT
setup, similarly to what we implemented here with the bootstrap operator. Bias operators are in general not protected by momentum conservation, so the renormalization of bias parameters starts at
O(k^{0})
and not at
O(k^{2})
as for the bootstrap parameters
[
77
,
45
,
46
]
. Furthermore, redshift-space distortions, already treated perturbatively within the
GridSPT
framework in previous work
[
42
]
(for other field-level approaches at field level in redshift space see also
[
78
,
79
]
), can be included, although modeling Fingers-of-God (FoG) effects accurately remains a challenging aspect, as discussed extensively in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
80
,
81
,
82
]
).
Finally, the application to real data will require a full sampling over the cosmological parameters and marginalization over the amplitudes and phases of the linear fields. As shown in
[
33
]
this task can be accomplished via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and other advanced sampling techniques
[
83
,
84
,
85
]
.

Appendix B
On injecting shot noise in a continuous field

In cosmology, the noise associated with samples of observed galaxies or other cosmological probes follows a Poisson distribution, because galaxies do (excluding small scale hydrodynamical physics and other non-gravitational effects) sample the underlying matter density distribution
\rho({\bf{x}})
.
In principle this type of noise can be mimicked by starting from the number count:

\tilde{N}({\bf{x}})\equiv\rho_{m}({\bf{x}})\;\Delta^{3}x=\left(1+\delta_{m}({\bf{x}})\right)\bar{n}\;\Delta^{3}x\,,

(B.1)

where
\delta_{m}({\bf{x}})\equiv\frac{\rho_{m}({\bf{x}})}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle}-1
is the density contrast of dark matter and
\Delta^{3}x
is a finite volume element.
This number can be used as the position-dependent average of a Poisson distribution from which to sample an actual number of particles. If the density at
{\bf{x}}
is high then more particles are likely to be sampled, and viceversa:

N({\bf{x}})\sim P(N\;|\;\tilde{N})=\frac{\tilde{N}^{N}e^{-\tilde{N}}}{N!}\,;

(B.2)

note how the distribution of
N
is conditional on
\tilde{N}
, because the latter is also a random variable. Given
N({\bf{x}})
, the noisy density contrast
\delta({\bf{x}})
can be obtained by simply rescaling the counts by their mean over every position
{\bf{x}}
so that
\langle\delta({\bf{x}})\rangle=0
.
It is known that in galaxies or other tracers, the noise is independent from the signal and additive. To show that this is the case here as well, one just needs to prove the following:

\left\langle\delta_{m}({\bf{x}})\left[\delta({\bf{x}}^{\prime})-\delta_{m}({\bf{x}}^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}0\,.

(B.3)

Using the definitions of expected value and the axioms of probability (and omitting the dependence on
{\bf{x}}
for the sake of clarity):

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\frac{\rho_{m}}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle}-1\right)\left(\frac{\rho}{\bar{n}}-1\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\frac{\rho_{m}}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle}-1\right)\left(\frac{\rho_{m}}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle}-1\right)\right\rangle

(B.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle\bar{n}}\frac{1}{V^{2}}\langle\tilde{N}\;N\rangle-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle^{2}}\frac{1}{V^{2}}\langle\tilde{N}\;\tilde{N}\rangle-1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\iint{\rm D}\tilde{N}\;{\rm D}N\;\tilde{N}NP(\tilde{N},\,N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle\rho_{m}\rangle}\int{\rm D}\tilde{N}\;\tilde{N}^{2}P(\tilde{N})

\displaystyle\int{\rm D}\tilde{N}\;\tilde{N}\left[\int{\rm D}N\;NP(N\;|\;\tilde{N})\right]P(\tilde{N})

\displaystyle=\int{\rm D}\tilde{N}\;\tilde{N}^{2}P(\tilde{N})\,.

Notice that
\langle\rho_{m}\rangle=\bar{n}
follows naturally from equation (
B.1
). To complete the proof, the integral within square brackets must be equal to
\tilde{N}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{N}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm D}N\;NP(N\;|\;\tilde{N})

(B.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}N\frac{\tilde{N}^{N}e^{-\tilde{N}}}{N!}

\displaystyle=\tilde{N}e^{-\tilde{N}}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{N}^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,,

where the
N=0
term vanishes. The sum is the Taylor series of the exponential
e^{\tilde{N}}
, which shows that the noise is indeed additive and uncorrelated when the field is sampled this way. This result has also been tested numerically by sampling ten realizations of the noise and displaying the Fourier analogue of the two terms on the left and right of equation (
B.4
) in figure
8
. The individual realizations tend to scatter around the theoretical expectation at large scales, while they converge to it when the number of Fourier modes increases, possibly an effect similar to cosmic variance. Plotting the difference between the power spectra of the noisy field and the starting dark matter field, one expects that its value is constant and goes as
1/\bar{n}

[
86
]
. Indeed figure
9
shows that this is the case. Again, the same behavior as for the previous figure is seen, however at very small scales the noise shows a systematic increase in power compared to theoretical expectation, likely due to aliasing induced in
\delta_{m}
when interpolating the N-body dark matter particles onto a regular grid.
