Azide-Engineered Nanocrystals Enable Direct Grafting of Conjugated Polymers via Click Chemistry
Authors: Wooseok Jin, Jaehan Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organic–inorganic hybrid nanocomposites comprising conjugated polymers in close contact with inorganic nanocrystals (NCs) were crafted via a catalyst-free click reaction. It highlights simple, robust, yet effective sodium azide treatment to effectively detach all of the insulating aliphatic ligands, including covalently bonded ones, from NC surfaces as well as to introduce covalently bonded azide functional sites, yielding azide-terminated NCs for click coupling. Subsequently, ethynyl-terminated organics (e.g., 1-octyne and ethynyl-terminated poly­(3-hexylthiophene) (–P3HT)) were tethered with azide-functionalized NCs via catalyst-free azide–alkyne cycloaddition, yielding semiconducting organic–inorganic nanocomposites (e.g., 1-octyne–CdSe/ZnS quantum dots and P3HT–CdSe tetrapod nanocomposites). The success of the click reaction was substantiated by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopies. A significant reduction in the ethynyl proton signal after the click reaction and decreased azide absorption in FTIR spectra strongly support the anchoring of ethynyl-terminated organics (e.g., 1-octyne and –P3HT) with azide-functionalized NCs. This strategy for the preparation of functional organic-tethered nanocrystals enables the direct introduction of reactive azide sites onto bare NCs, eliminating the need for redundant organic bifunctional ligands or tedious multistep procedures, and may offer a promising route to functional nanocomposites for optoelectronic applications.

Introduction

Colloidal nanocrystals (NCs) have garnered lots of attention for use in electronic, optoelectronic, and thermoelectric applications due to their size- and shape-tunable optoelectronic properties as well as their solution processability. − Conjugated polymers (CPs) have drawn considerable attention for large-scale manufactured applications in light-emitting diodes, solar cells, lasers, and sensors owing to their advantageous attributes such as lightweight and flexibility. , Thereby, semiconducting hybrid nanomaterials composed of CPs and NCs have emerged as promising building blocks for optoelectronic devices, exploiting the complementary advantages of both components.

However, physical blended CP/NC mixtures often suffer from microscopic phase separation, hence limiting the maximum utilization of their nanoscale architectures. Moreover, the insulating aliphatic surfactants typically used to synthesize monodispersed NCs prohibit electronic interactions between NCs and CPs. In this context, various strategies for CP/NC nanocomposites have emerged to achieve uniform dispersion of NCs in the CP matrix, thereby fully exploiting the advantages of their nanoscale features. One common approach is ligand exchange, where desired ligands are introduced onto the NC surface after displacing original ligands through pyridine or amine refluxing. However, such strategies usually resulted in agglomeration of NCs in the CP matrix, leading to microscopic phase separation due to weak interfacial interaction between NCs and CPs. To overcome these limitation, chemical tethering techniques, where end-functionalized CPs were synthesized and grafted onto the NC surface, have been introduced. , Nonetheless, these chemical tethering routes still relied on conventional ligand exchange processes, which are often time-consuming and inefficient and frequently fail to completely remove the insulating aliphatic ligands. Moreover, it is also worth noting that the bifunctional ligands commonly required in conventional chemical tethering methods for crafting CP/NC nanocomposites often result in reduced packing density of NCs within the CP matrix. This limitation is particularly detrimental in applications such as lasing, optical gain media, and solar cells, where a high NC loading and uniform dispersion are crucial. Therefore, the development of simple and efficient strategies for synthesizing CP-tethered NCs with a high packing density remains highly desirable.

The recently developed ligand exchange strategy by inorganic ligands (e.g., molecular metal chalcogenide complexes, chalcogenide ions, and halide ligands) has emerged as a simple and effective method for completely removing the original insulating aliphatic organic ligands from NC surfaces. − In stark contrast to conventional ligand exchange methods (e.g., pyridine or amine refluxing), inorganic ligand treatments offer a distinct advantage: they are capable of displacing both covalently bonded ligands (i.e., X-type) and dative bonded ligands (i.e., L-type) from the NC surface. , Although several studies have demonstrated the grafting of CP onto NCs by inorganic ligand treatment, they still rely on bifunctional molecular linkers. However, to the best of our knowledge, direct grafting of CPs onto inorganic ligand-treated NC without the use of bifunctional likers has not yet been reported.

Herein, we report a facile synthetic strategy for crafting semiconducting organic–inorganic nanocomposites by placing organic materials in intimate contact with inorganic nanocrystals via click chemistry following inorganic ligand treatment with sodium azide. In this approach, inorganic NCs (e.g., CdSe/ZnS QDs and CdSe tetrapods) were first treated with sodium azide to effectively remove both X-type and L-type capping surfactants and to introduce covalently bound azide moieties on their surfaces, forming azide-functionalized NCs. These azide groups subsequently served as reactive sites for coupling with organic components. Meanwhile, ethynyl-terminated poly­(3-hexylthiophene) (–P3HT) was synthesized via a quasi-living Grignard metathesis (GRIM) method. Finally, intimately contacted organic–inorganic nanocomposites (i.e., 1-octyne-grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs and P3HT-grafted CdSe tetrapods) were prepared via a catalyst-free click reaction (i.e., azide–alkyne cycloaddition) between ethynyl moieties of organic compounds (i.e., 1-octyne and –P3HT) and azide functionalities of inorganic nanocrystals (i.e., N3–CdSe/ZnS QDs and N3–CdSe tetrapods).

The success in the tethering of P3HT onto CdSe NC surfaces was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements. The absorption and photoluminescence studies further suggested the formation of strongly coupled interfaces between the CP and the NCs. Notably, this approach enables the formation of highly intimate organic–inorganic interfaces by grafting polymers directly onto NCs functionalized with a single atomic layer of azide groups, circumventing the need for redundant bifunctional linker molecules. Given the broad compatibility of this method with various nanocrystals capable of undergoing pseudohalide treatment (e.g., NaN3), this strategy offers considerable promise for the scalable fabrication of strongly coupled hybrid nanomaterials for diverse optoelectronic applications.

It is noteworthy that the ligand exchange through inorganic ligands, such as chalcogenide ions, halide ligands, and metal chalcogenide complexes, is a simple and effective approach to completely displace both covalently bonded ligands (X-type) and dative bonded ligands (L-type) on the NC surfaces, which is a stark contrast to the conventional ligand exchange technique where X-type ligands cannot be completely detached regardless of several ligand refluxing procedures. Specifically, oleic acid (OA) capped-CdSe/ZnS QDs (i.e., OA-QD) in nonpolar (i.e., hexane phase) were vigorously stirred with sodium azide in the polar solvent (i.e., NMF), resulting in complete removal of OA ligands yielding N3-functionalized CdSe/ZnS QDs (i.e., N3-QD). The transfer of QDs from the hexane phase to the NMF phase clearly suggests the successful removal of OA as well as the introduction of azide-functionalities onto NC surfaces. Subsequently, 1-octyne was coupled with N3-QDs via a catalyst-free click reaction, yielding organic–inorganic nanocomposites (i.e., 1-octyne-grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs). Notably, as the desired organic materials are directly tethered on the NC surfaces with no need for anchoring bifunctional organic ligands, not only were the dispersion and solubility of the resulting nanocomposites largely facilitated in a variety of organic solvents but also the packing density of QDs in desired organic matrices can be greatly improved.

To synthesize CdSe/ZnS QDs, we employed a facile one-pot method for well-dispersed and compositional gradient core/alloyed-shell QDs (i.e., CdSe/Cd1–x Se1–y Zn x Sy, hereafter termed CdSe/ZnS) with an average size of 8.0 ± 0.7 nm. The optical properties of CdSe/ZnS QDs after functionality modifications were investigated using UV–vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopies (Figure ). The measurements were performed for OA-capped QDs (black curve), azide-functionalized QDs (blue curve), and 1-octyne coupled QDs (red curve). The UV–vis absorption spectra show that the absorption spectra remained unchanged across all stages, indicating that the photophysical properties were preserved throughout the functionalization process. Similarly, the PL spectra reveal that the QDs maintained their emission characteristics, suggesting that chemical modifications did not negatively impact their optical stability. Collectively, the UV–vis and PL studies demonstrate the robustness of the CdSe/ZnS QDs’ photophysical properties throughout the functionalization process, confirming the successful and stable synthesis of functionalized QDs.

The emergence of a broad absorption peak in the 2100–2000 cm–1 region, attributed to the stretching and vibrational modes of N3 – in sodium azide, confirms the successful attachment of N3-moieties onto the QD surface after treatment with sodium azide in NMF. , The broad peak at 2051 cm–1 corresponds to N3 – ligands covalently bound to positively charged Zn or Cd sites on the QD surface. In contrast, a sharp absorption peak at 2036 cm–1 is attributed to N3 – ligand dissociated from the QD surfaces and 2160 cm–1 is residue NaN3. Notably, the characteristic aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations from the alkyl chains of OA, observed at 2956 cm–1, 2923 cm–1, and 2852 cm–1 (asymmetric C–H stretching vibration in −CH3, –CH2, and the symmetric C–H stretching vibration in −CH2, respectively), nearly disappeared after NaN3 treatment. This suggests a successful ligand exchange from OA to N3. − Subsequently, the N3 characteristic peak significantly diminished after N3-QDs were coupled with 1-octyne, indicating the successful grafting of 1-octyne onto the N3-QDs via catalyst-free azide–alkyne cycloaddition. To further confirm the formation of the triazole structure on the surface of the QDs, the click-reacted QDs were decomposed in a polar solvent under vigorous stirring and an elevated temperature. The surface-bound ligands were then extracted for subsequent analysis. Figure b compares the spectral features of the pristine 1-octyne and the ligand detached from the octyne-grafted QDs. Newly emerged signals at 3061 cm–1 and 3026 cm–1 are attributed to the C–H stretching vibrations of the aromatic triazole ring, indicating the formation of the triazole structure. In addition, the characteristic peaks at 3310 cm–1 and 2094 cm–1, corresponding to C–H and CC stretching vibrations of the alkyne group, respectively, were markedly diminished following the click reaction, signifying successful coupling. Further supporting this observation, distinct signals observed at 1602 cm–1, 1493 cm–1, and 1261 cm–1 are associated with the triazole ring, corresponding to aromatic CC stretching, NN, and C–N bonding vibrations, respectively. , These newly appearing signals, along with the reappearance of alkyl peaks originating from 1-octyne, collectively confirm the successful conjugation of the triazole moiety onto the QD surface.

In addition, 1H NMR spectroscopy clearly demonstrated the occurrence of coupling between the ethynyl moiety of 1-octyne and the azide functionality of N3-QDs. 1H NMR spectra of 1-octyne-grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs (red line), N3-functionalized CdSe/ZnS QDs (blue line), OA-capped CdSe/ZnS QDs (black line), and pristine 1-octyne (green line) are shown in Figure a. Notably, the disappearance of the proton signal at 5.2–5.4 ppm, assigned to the double bond in OA, clearly indicates the effective removal of OA ligands via sodium azide treatment. Furthermore, the N3-QDs exhibited no observable proton signals aside from those arising from the solvent and residual water, indicating a nearly complete ligand exchange. Importantly, the distinct ethynyl proton signal of 1-octyne, typically observed at 1.93 ppm, was absent in the spectrum of the 1-octyne-grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs, further substantiating the occurrence of a catalyst-free azide–alkyne cycloaddition. These results collectively validate the successful and efficient surface functionalization of QDs with triazole moieties via click chemistry.

Figure b shows a detailed comparison between the 1H NMR spectra of 1-octyne-grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs and those of pristine 1-octyne. The proton signals at 1.94 and 2.17 ppm, assigned to the terminal ethynyl proton and its adjacent proton (marked as a and b in the inset of Figure b), disappeared following the click reaction. This clearly confirms the successful consumption of the alkyne group and the efficient surface functionalization of the QDs via azide–alkyne cycloaddition. In addition, the signals originating from the alkyl chain protons of 1-octyne (denoted as c and d) appear as broadened and slightly shifted peaks (denoted as H2 and H3), reflecting changes in the local chemical environment upon grafting to the QD surface. Notably, the emergence of new aromatic proton signals at 6.93 and 7.45 ppm (labeled H5) is consistent with the formation of 1,2,3-triazole rings, providing direct evidence for the formation of 1,4- and 1,5-disubstituted triazole isomers as a result of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction. These spectral features collectively support the successful and specific formation of triazole structures on the QD surface.

Furthermore, as a proof of concept, P3HT–CdSe tetrapod nanocomposites were synthesized by capitalizing on NaN3 treatment and subsequent click coupling between as-prepared N3-functionalized NCs and ethynyl-terminated CPs. It is worth noting that semiconducting branched nanostructures, such as CdSe tetrapods, carry many advantageous attributes over QDs or quantum rods, especially for use in photovoltaics as their peculiar architecture facilitates the charge separation owing to their large interfacial area and enables a continuous charge transport pathway regardless of their orientation. In this study, poly-(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT)a widely studied CP known for its tunable electrochemical properties and excellent charge transport characteristicswas employed to serve as a polymeric matrix and to couple with azide-functionalized NCs. To enable coupling, the native OA ligands on the CdSe tetrapod surface were removed and replaced with azide groups via treatment with sodium azide (Scheme ). Subsequently, ethynyl-terminated P3HT (–P3HT) was grafted onto N3-functionalized CdSe tetrapods through click coupling, resulting in the formation of P3HT-grafted CdSe tetrapod nanocomposites.

Synthetic Route to P3HT–CdSe Tetrapod Nanocomposites by Capitalizing on Sodium Azide Treatment and Click Coupling

Similarly, the success of the coupling between ethynyl–P3HT and N3–CdSe tetrapods was confirmed by 1H NMR and FTIR measurements. The FTIR spectra of N3–CdSe tetrapods, –P3HT, and P3HT–CdSe tetrapod nanocomposites are shown in Figure . Clearly, the two absorption peaks at 2092 cm–1 and 2048 cm–1 that are ascribed to ethynyl and azide functionalities of –P3HT and CdSe tetrapod surface, respectively, were greatly reduced after coupling between N3-functionalized CdSe tetrapods and –P3HT via catalyst-free azide–alkyne cycloaddition. Additionally, as shown in Figure b, the fingerprint region of –P3HT was maintained after the click reaction, substantiating the successful conjugation of –P3HT onto CdSe tetrapods. In order to further signify the successful grafting between –P3HT and N3–CdSe tetrapods, 1H NMR measurement was performed. Figure compares the 1H NMR spectra of pristine –P3HT, a simple mixture of –P3HT and CdSe tetrapods before the click reaction, and the P3HT–CdSe tetrapod nanocomposites after click coupling, respectively. A clear decrease in the ethynyl proton signal at 3.53 ppm, which is present in both pristine –P3HT and the prereaction mixture, was observed in the spectrum of the nanocomposites. This reduction is indicative of the consumption of the ethynyl functionality via a click reaction with azide groups on the CdSe tetrapod surface. Quantitative analysis of the thiophene to ethynyl proton ratio further supports this observation. Before the click reaction, the ratio was 77.0:1, which increased to 104.4:1 after the reaction, indicating a substantial decrease in the ethynyl proton signal. Meanwhile, the hexyl side-chain proton signals of P3HT remained unchanged across all spectra, confirming that the backbone of the polymer was preserved. It is important to note that the prereaction mixture of –P3HT and CdSe tetrapods exhibited no noticeable change in the ethynyl proton intensity compared to pristine –P3HT, further demonstrating that physical mixing alone does not affect the spectral features, thus reinforcing that the observed signal reduction is due to chemical coupling. The residual signal at 3.53 ppm in the nanocomposite spectrum is attributed to unreacted –P3HT, which can be subsequently removed through the appropriate purification. In sum, these spectroscopic results provide strong evidence for the successful surface grafting of P3HT onto CdSe tetrapods via catalyst-free azide–alkyne cycloaddition.

Conclusion

In summary, semiconducting organic–inorganic nanocomposites comprising CPs in intimate contact with inorganic nanocrystals were crafted via a catalyst-free click reaction. The as-synthesized OA-capped NCs were first treated with sodium azide to completely and effectively displace all the insulating fatty ligands including the X-type surfactant as well as to introduce covalently bonded N3 – moieties onto NC surfaces, forming azide-functionalized NCs. Finally, ethynyl-terminated organics were tethered to azide-functionalized NCs via catalyst-free azide–alkyne cycloaddition, yielding intimate semiconducting organic–inorganic NC nanocomposites (e.g., 1-octyne-grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs and P3HT–CdSe tetrapod nanocomposites) without the presence of any insulating ligands. Importantly, sodium azide treatment is a robust and simple yet effective strategy not only to displace insulating aliphatic ligands but also to introduce coupling agent (i.e., covalently bonded N3) directly onto NC surfaces. The success of click coupling of ethynyl-terminated organics onto N3-functionalized NCs was substantiated by FTIR and 1H NMR measurements. Clearly, the click-reaction strategy enabled by exploiting sodium azide treatment that detaches all the insulating ligands of the NC surface as well as introduces a covalently bonded azide moiety may be readily extended to produce hybrid nanocomposites for a wide range of applications in optoelectronic materials and devices.

Experimental Section

All chemicals including cadmium oxide (CdO), zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn­(acetate)2·2H2O), sulfur powder (S), selenium powder (Se), sodium azide, dodecanethiol, tri-n-octylphosphine (TOP, 90%), 2,5-dibromo-3-hexylthiophene, Ni­(dppp)­Cl2, tert-butylmagnesium chloride (2 mol/L in THF), and ethynylmagnesium bromide (0.5 mol/L in THF) from Sigma-Aldrich; N-methylformamide (NMF) and hexane from Alfa Aesar; 1-octadecene (ODE, 90%), OA (97%), 1-octyne, and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) from TCI were used as received. THF (Thermo Fisher, 99%) was dehydrated by molecular sieves.

Synthesis of CdSe/ZnS QDs

CdSe/ZnS QDs were synthesized by following the reported synthetic procedure. 1 mmol CdO, 2 mmol Zn­(acetate)2·2H2O, 5 mL of OA, and 25 mL of 1-octadecene (ODE) were heated to 150 °C and evacuated for 30 min. After that, the mixture solution was heated to 300 °C. At 300 °C, 0.2 mL of TOP dissolving 0.2 mmol Se (TOPSe) was rapidly injected into the reaction vessel. After 15 min, 0.3 mL of dodecanethiol was dropwisely added (1 mL/min) into the reactor. The reaction mixture was kept at 300 °C for 20 min. Then, 1 mL of 2 M S-TOP was additionally injected into the reactor, and the reaction was kept for 10 min.

Preparation of CdSe Tetrapods

CdSe tetrapods were synthesized via the seed-mediated method by following the reported synthetic procedure. Cadmium oleate (Cd­(OA)2) solution was prepared by mixing 10 mmol CdO, 7.8 mL of OA, 6.2 mL of ODE, and 1 mL of TOP at 280 °C under Ar for 30 min. Once the solution became clear, the reaction was cooled down to 60 °C and 5 mL of 2 M TOPSe solution and CTAB (0.14 mmol) and was then added and stirred for 10 min. Subsequently, zincblende CdSe QDs were prepared as follows. 1 mmol Se and 10 mL of ODE were placed in a three-neck flask and heated up to 300 °C under Ar. After the solution became transparent, 2 mmol CdO, 2 mL of OA, and 2 mL of ODE solution were injected to initiate the nucleation and allowed to grow at 270 °C for 15 min. The heating mantle was then removed to stop the reaction, and 6 mL of ODE was added once the temperature reached 70 °C. CdSe tetrapods were synthesized as follows. 0.15 μmol CdSe zincblende seeds with 0.75 mL of TOP, 1.25 mL of OA, 10.5 mL of ODE, and 0.21 mmol CTAB were mixed and then heated to 260 °C. Subsequently, the precursor solution was injected at the rate of 0.4 mL/min for 50 min. The reaction was stopped, and 5 mL of hexane was added at 70 °C. The excess amount of acetone was added and centrifuged to purify the resulting solution.

Synthesis of Ethynyl-Terminated P3HT

Ethynyl-terminated P3HT (i.e., –P3HT) was synthesized by a quasi-living GRIM method. , 2,5-Dibromo-3-hexylthiophene (0.815 g, 2.5 mmol) and 5 mL of THF were stirred under Ar. 1.25 mL of 2 M tert-butylmagnesium chloride was then added. The mixture was stirred for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, it was diluted to 25 mL with THF, followed by the addition of Ni­(dppp)­Cl2 (11.2 mg, 0.020 mmol). The resulting solution was stirred for 10 min at room temperature, producing intermediate P3HT. The reaction was terminated with ethynylmagnesium bromide (2 mL, 1 mmol) in THF for 30 min. The product, P3HT–, was obtained by precipitating the reaction mixture in methanol, filtering it in an extraction thimble, and washing by Soxhlet extraction with methanol, hexane, and chloroform sequentially. It was recovered after the chloroform evaporated. The regioregularity of P3HT was greater than 97%, as determined by 1H NMR. The average molecular weight and polydispersity index (PDI) of P3HT were 14 kDa and 1.4, respectively, as measured by gel permeation chromatography. Yield: 41.2%. The degree of ethynyl functionalization was estimated by quantitative 1H NMR analysis. The thiophene to ethynyl proton ratio was approximately 79.6:1, indicating that the polymer chains were successfully ethynyl-functionalized, with –P3HT being predominantly monofunctionalized.

Preparation of N3-QDs

The ligand exchange process was performed under an inert atmosphere in a N2-filled glovebox. OA-capped CdSe/ZnS QDs are purified with acetone three times and redissolved in hexane (∼10 mg/mL). 5 mL of CdSe NC solution in hexane (∼10 mg/mL) was mixed with 5 mL of NMF containing excess sodium azide (0.25 g) by vortex mixing for 30 min. The bottom phase was separated and rinsed with fresh hexane three times. This process was repeated several times to clearly detach the OA ligand from the CdSe/ZnS QDs surface. The N3-capped NCs were precipitated with acetone and then redispersed in NMF.

Preparation of Nanocomposites by the Click Reaction

The resulting N3-capped NCs were then precipitated with methanol. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 1-octyne (or 50 mg of –P3HT) and 20 mg of N3-capped CdSe/ZnS QDs were mixed in 5 mL of CF and kept at 65 °C under Ar for 2 days. The final product (i.e., 1-octyne or P3HT grafted CdSe/ZnS QDs) was cooled to room temperature.

Characterization

The morphology of CdSe/ZnS QDs and P3HT–CdSe/ZnS nanocomposites was studied by using low-resolution and high-resolution transmission electron microscopes (JEOL 100cx and Tecnai F30). The photophysical properties of the nanocomposites were explored by using an UV–vis spectrometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu). The 1H NMR and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded by using a Varian VXR-400 spectrometer.
