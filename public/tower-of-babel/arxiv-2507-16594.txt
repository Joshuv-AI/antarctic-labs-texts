An Experimental Study of Split-Learning TinyML on Ultra-Low-Power Edge/IoT Nodes
Zied Jenhani, Mounir Bensalem, Jasenka Dizdarević and Admela Jukan

Abstract
Running deep learning inference directly on ultra-low-power edge/IoT nodes has been limited by the tight memory and compute budgets of microcontrollers. Split learning (SL) addresses this limitation in which it executes part of the inference process on the sensor and off-loads the remainder to a companion device. In the context of constrained devices and the related impact of low-power, over-the-air transport protocols, the performance of split learning remains largely unexplored. TO the best of our knowledge, this paper presents the first end-to-end TinyML + SL testbed built on Espressif ESP32-S3 boards, designed to benchmark the over-the-air performance of split learning TinyML in edge/IoT environments. We benchmark the performance of a MobileNetV2 image recognition model, which is quantized to 8-bit integers, partitioned, and delivered to the nodes via over-the-air updates. The intermediate activations are exchanged through different wireless communication methods: ESP-NOW, BLE, and traditional UDP/IP and TCP/IP, enabling a head-to-head comparison on identical hardware. Measurements show that splitting the model after block_16_project_BN layer generates a 5.66 kB tensor that traverses the link in 3.2 ms, when UDP is used, achieving a steady-state round-trip latency of 5.8 s. ESP-NOW presents the most favorable RTT performance 3.7 s; BLE extends battery life further but increases latency beyond 10s.



I
Introduction

The next frontier for an efficient AI is to run deep learning inference on ultra-low power resource-constrained edge/IoT devices, with the aim at improving data locality and privacy, while lowering bandwidth utilization, and enhancing the real-time processing capabilities
[
1
]
. Significant advancements towards this goal have emerged with the paradigm shift towards on-device ML computing, enabled by modifications and optimizations of deep learning (DL) models
[
2
]
. Herewith, the most promising alternative to classic DL models has been the use of Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) models, as they provide lightweight inference algorithms that can run on the resource-constrained hardware in edge/IoT context
[
3
]
. In terms of overcoming processing power and memory size limitations, Split Learning (SL) in particular partitions the deep learning network: the early layers run on the sensor whereas the remaining layers execute on a
companion device, such as another microcontroller, a gateway or a nearby edge
server
[
4
]
. Only intermediate activations are exchanged, so most privacy and
bandwidth benefits of on-device inference are preserved.

Although SL has been
analyzed on smartphones and single board computers
[
5
]
, there is a notable lack of empirical evidence on constrained microcontrollers, and, crucially, on the role that low power wireless protocols play in the overall latency energy budget. Especially the performance measurements and evaluations are missing in the context of latency incurred, such as in terms of processing time, including network and communication setup, ML inference, and the transmission of intermediate activations (neural network layer outputs) between devices. In addition, Round Trip Time (RTT) and delay associated with the transfer of intermediate activations and prediction data delay between devices is still unknown, which requires new studies. Finally, the impact of over-the-air communication modes on system performance, such as when using different protocols is an open issue. Communication networks based on WiFi, ESP-NOW, and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) are all expected to yield different performance.

In this work, we examine the potential of implementing the SL machine learning approach in resource-constrained environment, enabling on-device distribution of the ML computational load. For running ML models on hardware with limited resources, we opted for the TinyML framework solution. Although there are multiple TinyML frameworks available for the implementation of neural networks on different microcontrollers, we opt for TensorFlow Lite (TFLite), as it comes with sufficient developer support. The model is prepared (partitioned and quantized) in an edge server for which we use a Desktop PC, from which the firmware is then remotely deployed to the IoT devices using Over-the-Air (OTA) firmware updates without requiring physical access to the device. The selected IoT devices are based on low-cost open-source ESP32 microcontroller boards that can run the software necessary for TinyML applications.

The measurements reveal that placing the split after

block_16_project_BN
layer yields the most attractive trade-off between the evaluated split points: the resulting 5.66 kB intermediate activations crosses the link in only 3.2 ms for UDP, producing a steady-state round-trip time (RTT) of 5.8 s, more than 20x as fast as sending the raw
image to a remote server for full inference. ESP-NOW eliminates the need for
WiFi infrastructure and lowers the radio energy per bit by a factor of four
while keeping the RTT below 3.7 s, whereas BLE, although the most energy-
efficient, stretches the RTT beyond 10 s because of its limited data
rate and 512 B MTU.

Related Work

TinyML represents an increasingly important subcategory of ML frameworks, focused on deploying AI models on extremely resource-constrained IoT and edge devices, including MCU units that operate with less than 1 MB of flash memory

1

This integration of TinyML with the heterogeneous IoT and edge platforms, has been enabled by developing new and optimizing existing ML models, algorithms, and software libraries, with an extensive summary of TinyML software provided in
[
6
]
. For the compatibility with the chosen hardware, here we have opted for the utilization of TFLM software for developing proposed image classification application.

While the use of TinyML ensures lower latencies and power consumption as it can run local ML inference in the IoT and edge, it can be further leveraged when integrated with the complementary paradigm of Split Learning. This has been particularly useful for the next generation applications with significant privacy concerns (e.g., healthcare)
[
7
,
8
]
, and as a support for rapid increase of ML across large IoT networks
[

. In our work, we close an unaddressed gap in the literature. As previous SL studies on microcontrollers evaluate a single WiFi configuration
[
7

, we instead present a latency analysis of four protocol options in wireless networking including transport protocols UDP and TCP, as well as ESP-NOW and BLE. Second, in our work we evaluated a practical computer vision model (MobileNetV2), after quantization, and demonstrated that the model can be split and executed within 512 KB SRAM + 8MB PSRAM budget of the ESP32. Third, we released the source code used in our experiments, providing a reproducible baseline and actionable design guidelines for future TinyML + SL deployments.

III
System Architecture

Figure 1:
SL-TinyML framework architecture and communication processes between IoT devices

Fig.
1
shows the system architecture for SL-TinyML integration framework solution for the better utilization of computing resources distributed across edge/IoT nodes for an image classification application. In the proposed framework, computation and processing tasks are separated in two contextual layers, IoT and the edge, with the edge server side used for model training, model partitioning, post-training quantization, firmware generation and local over-the-air updating of firmware images for the IoT devices, across which the inference is distributed.

As seen in Fig.
1
, the initial step is deployment of a pre-trained ML model in the edge server. This model is then divided into multiple parts depending on the application, while preserving the original structure without any modifications. To further optimize the split models for later deployment across the IoT constrained hardware, post-training quantization is applied with a representative dataset consisting of real world images used to calibrate the quantization process, minimizing potential accuracy degradation. This sets up the following conversion to a TinyML-compatible format.
In the step of TinyML conversion, TensorFlow library can be used to covert any pretrained model into a TFLite model format, as this reduces the split models sizes and improves their inference speed.
Next is the generation of firmware images, where the previously split and quantized models prepared
for deployment across heterogeneous platforms, are integrated into complete firmware scripts and converted into binary firmware images. As a last step in edge server, the local OTA server is used for remote deployment of firmware images across IoT devices, without requiring physical access. This is enabled since the Edge/IoT nodes are connected within a Wireless Local Area Network (WLAN), as shown in Fig.
1
(WiFi).

The IoT layer consists of a set of highly constrained hardware devices (microcontrollers), across which a split learning model prepared at the edge server is distributed in the form of firmware images. The process begins on the first IoT node which interfaces the camera sensor, by the acquisition of sensor data, that is used as the input to the DNN model, e.g., MobileNetV2. This is followed by the same node running a part of the model for inference. After that, the first IoT node plays the role of server for the other IoT nodes in the network, while other nodes assume the client roles. These devices exchange intermediate layer outputs wirelessly to execute their assigned partitions of the split neural network, with the resulting intermediate output of one device transmitted to the next one using one of the supported communication protocols. This process continues through the TinyML inference pipeline until a prediction result is obtained and returned to the IoT node 1 as feedback. As seen in Fig.
1
, proposed framework solution supports different paradigms for IoT communication and transmission of the intermediate layer outputs, including TCP client/server, UDP client/server, ESP-NOW peer-to-peer, and BLE client/server communication. This allows a uniform evaluation environment for the framework across different supported communication solutions.

Finally, in the proposed framework, a network manager can decide to add a new node or a new pre-trained ML model in the edge server, and then the processes of model splitting, quantization, conversion, and firmware generation, needs to be executed again. IoT devices are periodically connecting to the edge server in order to check for the availability of new firmware updates, through a Wifi connection. The local OTA server will then transfer the new firmware versions to the IoT nodes. Once a new firmware is sent, the IoT node performs a verification step to ensure data integrity often through checksum or hash validation. If the firmware is deemed valid, it is written to the device’s flash memory. The device then reboots to activate the newly installed firmware. After rebooting, a post-installation validation process confirms the success of the update and ensures that the device is operating correctly under the new software version.

IV
TinyML and Split Learning Experimental Setup and Measurements

This section presents the proposed testbed setup and design choices, where a neural network model is optimized for TinyML. The model is divided into two parts and is deployed in two IoT devices, with intermediate layer output of the model transmitted via a wireless channel.

IV-A

Testbed Settings

As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the proposed TinyML + SL testbed solution consists of one edge server and two IoT devices. For the edge server, which is used as the development environment for the ML application, we use a Desktop PC (Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-14900 CPU, 64 GB RAM). For IoT devices we use ESP32-S3 microcontroller boards (240 MHz, Flash memory 16MB, SRAM 512 KB, PSRAM 8MB). These hardware development boards were chosen because they have been analyzed in different testbeds and can run TinyML models
[
3
]
, as well as the presence of software libraries that support inference such as TensorFlow Lite Micro (TFLM)
[
6
]
.

In addition, these boards provide a flexible partition table system that allows developers to define custom memory allocation for code, models, and intermediate activations, which is a critical feature when deploying embedded ML models. One of the microcontrollers interfaces a camera sensor (Arducam OV2640 Mini), which is used to capture images for an image classification application.
The ML model used is the MobileNetV2 architecture
[
10
]
, a lightweight convolutional neural network pretrained on the ImageNet dataset for image classification.
The full MobileNetV2 model is loaded with a width multiplier of 0.35 to reduce computational complexity, a common approach for balancing accuracy and efficiency in edge applications. A randomly picked an internal layer (
block_16_project_BN
) as the split point. The model is divided into two parts: Part 1 includes all layers up to the selected split layer, and Part 2 constructs the remaining layers sequentially.

To reduce the size of the model, we use quantization through TensorFlow Lite
1
1

1

https://www.tensorflow.org/lite
.A representative dataset consisting of real-world images is used to calibrate the quantization process, minimizing possible accuracy degradation
[
11
]
. Both Part 1 and Part 2 models are quantized independently, with special care taken to ensure alignment of quantization parameters, specifically the scale and zero point between the output of Part 1 and the input of Part 2. This alignment is critical to preserve the semantic consistency of intermediate features.
The final result is a pair of quantized models (.tflite).
Following the model quantization, the resulting .tflite files were converted into C++ header (.h) and source (.cc) files suitable for integration into embedded firmware. Two separate Arduino sketches were then developed, where each one is embedding one of the model parts which were compiled into binary firmware files. The testbed consists of two ESP32-S3 IoT (10 cm apart) by joining an access point, such as a TP-Link router, in order to access the internet and reach an OTA update server. •

TinyML inference 1
:

The TinyML inference process begins with the allocation of the tensor arena, where a dedicated memory block is dynamically allocated of the microcontroller to serve as the working space for tensor operations. This is followed by model loading, in which a quantized TensorFlow Lite model is loaded from memory. Next, a MicroInterpreter instance is initialized by associating the loaded model with a global operator resolver and the pre-allocated tensor arena. This configuration prepares the system for runtime execution.

Subsequently, the interpreter performs tensor allocation, reserving memory within the arena for input, output, and intermediate layers. Once the environment is initialized, an image captured by the external camera module is preprocessed and loaded into the model’s input tensor. The inference phase is then triggered executing the convolutional operations of the first model partition on the input image. This results in the generation of intermediate feature activations, which are stored in the output buffer to be transmitted.

Once the intermediate activations are extracted from the output tensor, a transmission function call is responsible for forwarding this data to the next IoT node using one of several communication protocols, including UDP, TCP, ESP-NOW, or BLE. Due to the specific characteristics of each protocol particularly in terms of packet size (

Feedback prediction results

To complete the distributed inference workflow, the system incorporates a feedback mechanism implemented as a callback function that monitors the arrival of prediction results from the remote edge node 2.

IoT device 2:

•

Protocol communication setup
:
For the other IoT nodes, the same communication protocol setup is applied as implemented on IoT node 1.

•

Receive intermediate activations
:
On the receiving side, the edge node continuously listens for incoming packets containing intermediate activations from the transmitting IoT device. Each received packet is read directly into a pre-allocated buffer, with the data appended sequentially to reconstruct the full activation tensor.

•

TinyML inference 2
:
At the receiver side of the distributed inference pipeline, the second half of the TinyML model is executed on the microcontroller, following a similar TinyML configuration process as on the transmitting node. Once the intermediate activations for the layer are received from the first node, they are loaded into the input tensor of the n part of the model. Followed by the execution of the second stage of inference, completing the forward pass of the full neural network. The resulting output tensor contains classification probabilities, which are parsed to extract the top predicted classes along with their corresponding confidence scores.

V
Conclusion

This paper presents an experimental study of distributed TinyML inference using a pipelined MobileNetV2 model deployed across two ESP32-S3 devices. We evaluated the impact of model split points, transmission chunk sizes, and communication protocols on end-to-end delay.
Our measurements show that ESP-NOW offers overall the lowest round-trip time (RTT) due to its minimal setup time and direct MAC layer communication, making it well-suited for small-payload, low-latency data transfers. However, its small maximum packet size leads to increased transmission delay for larger data transfers.
UDP achieves the lowest transmission latency, but as well known lacks reliable transmission. TCP, while reliable, suffers from high setup overhead and retransmissions, especially when large payloads exceed the ESP32’s limited TCP buffer capacity.

In conclusion, ESP-NOW is the most efficient protocol for low-latency communication of small data transfers on ESP32 devices, though protocol choice should consider trade-offs in reliability, data size, and energy use. Our system is scalable and reproducible as it is possible to add more IoT devices using the same proposed firmware while adjusting the model split points. From a HW point of view, the solution can be reused with all other HW supporting TensorFlow Lite, and the latency performance might increase due to the HW change or the number of devices.
For future work, we plan to design and implement a dynamic and adaptive framework for split TinyML inference that takes into consideration communication protocol latency, energy constraints, and optimal model partitioning. This framework should support runtime decisions on activation chunk sizes and split points based on network conditions, device resources, and data characteristics.
