Impact of adjustable resistance clamps on the in-vitro hemolysis assessment of blood pumps
Authors: Krishnaraj Narayanaswamy, Xiangyu He, Leonie Schmitt, Martin Stoiber, Michael Röhrich, Daniel Zimpfer, Marcus Granegger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdjustable resistance clamps are essential to regulate flow and pressure in in-vitro test loops designed to evaluate hemolysis in blood pumps. However, their contribution to overall hemolysis remains unclear. This study investigated the hemolytic potential of adjustable resistance clamps across different resistance settings. Utilizing a single-pass test loop with heparinized bovine blood, the effect of resistance clamps on hemolysis was evaluated at three resistance settings: high (1.5 L/min, 90 mmHg), medium (5 L/min, 60 mmHg), and low (7.5 L/min, 30 mmHg). Two resistance clamps (sudden vs. gradual constriction) were compared at the medium resistance setting. Hemolysis was quantified by measuring plasma free hemoglobin (pfHb) and normalized index of hemolysis (NIH). Furthermore, computational fluid dynamics simulations were used to predict the hemolysis metric for each resistance setting with 3D-recreated replicas. Compared to hemolysis metrics reported for state-of-the-art blood pumps, resistance clamps may affect the hemolysis substantially, with up to 84% of the NIH at medium resistance settings and < 47% at low/high resistance settings, regardless of the resistance shape/profile. Numerically predicted hemolysis metric strongly correlated with experimental results for three resistance settings (r > 0.998). The findings underscore the importance of considering the influence of adjustable resistance clamps while investigating the hemolytic potential of blood pumps.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25380-y.Subject terms: Cardiology, Medical research, Engineering

Introduction

Blood pumps have become an integral part of modern medicine, providing circulatory support in various medical procedures such as cardiopulmonary bypass, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and ventricular assist devices (VADs). Ensuring the hemocompatibility of these blood pumps is paramount for the safety and success of these medical interventions.

To date, hemolysis, the device-induced damage of erythrocytes, serves as the primary design optimization criterion in blood pump development. Further, hemolysis assessments through in-vitro test loops are mandated for regulatory approval of these devices. Standard methods for evaluating hemolysis in continuous-flow blood pumps have been established by the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM). The methodologies outlined in ASTM F1841-19ɛ1 provide guidelines for conducting in-vitro tests using human/animal blood circulated through a defined bench-top test loop configuration1.

The recommended in-vitro test loop for hemolysis assessment consists of the blood pump under investigation circulating tempered blood for a defined duration from a reservoir/blood bag through an adjustable resistance clamp that mimics vascular resistance. Other components, such as tubing, connectors, sampling ports, and flow/pressure measurement devices, make up the complete test loop. The hemolysis assessment specified by the ASTM standard1 emphasizes the importance of various clinical scenarios, with the blood pump operated at different speeds and flow settings.

Ensuring the accuracy, reliability, and credibility of these in-vitro test setups is essential for both design optimization and regulatory approval of blood pumps. Therefore, it is important to consider the contribution made by individual components within the testing loop – and their importance while the pump is running at clinically relevant off-design operating conditions.

Besides the negligible contribution of shear stresses by the tube connectors and luer lock reported in previous studies2,3, the resistance clamps used in in-vitro test loops to regulate the pressure and flow of the loop may substantially contribute to hemolysis. The sudden change in the cross-sectional area of the tubing due to these adjustable clamps influences local velocities and thus may be reflected by elevated blood trauma.

Previous studies have shown the importance of these resistance clamps and their influence on the blood damage potential observed within an in-vitro test loop4,5. However, these studies utilized blood pumps in the test loop operating at the design point; hence, the sole effect of the adjustable resistance clamps on hemolysis remains unclear under the design and off-design operating conditions of a blood pump. Recent studies point towards substantial hemolysis contribution of an adjustable resistance clamp at different operating points6 but were limited by a purely numerical approach; a comprehensive experimental validation is yet to be performed.

The aim of this study was to identify the hemolytic potential of different adjustable resistance clamps under various resistance settings mimicking low flow, nominal flow, and high flow conditions in an in-vitro test loop in combination with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations.

Test loop design

The developed in-vitro test loop was modified from the previously established hybrid mock circulatory loop,7 comprising both hydraulic and pneumatic components. The closed hydraulic system consists of two polycarbonate reservoirs (Z1 and Z2 (Fig. 1A), inner diameter 84 mm, height 300 mm), connected through ½ inch polyvinyl chloride tubing. The distance between the reservoirs was 220 mm. An adjustable resistance clamp (either R1 or R2, Fig. 1B) was placed at the center of the tubing. R1 and R2 were chosen to represent a wide spectrum of resistance profiles commonly encountered in in-vitro hemocompatibility evaluation of blood pumps.

(A) Schematic diagram of the developed single-pass in-vitro test loop. Z1 and Z2 represent the two reservoirs connected by tubing; P1, P2, P3, and P4 represent the positions of pressure transducers (P3 and P4 were mounted on the reservoir via drilled port measuring internal blood pressure); R indicates the adjustable resistance clamp; F the flow sensor. The sampling port indicated through S, and the loop was filled through port X. (B) The adjustable resistance clamps R1 (easy-click closure, Bürkle GmbH, Bad Bellingen, Germany) and R2 (3D printed 50 × 40 mm gradual contraction/expansion) used in the experiments. The quantitative geometric differences between R1 and R2 are further details in the Supplementary Section 5.

A clamp-on flow sensor (Sonoflow CO55, Sonotec GmbH, Halle, Germany) was utilized to measure the flow rate. Four pressure transducers (TruWave, Edwards Lifesciences Corp., CA, USA) were used to monitor reservoir pressures. Two transducers (P1, P2) were positioned atop of the reservoirs to measure the air pressure above the blood surface. The transducers (P3, P4) mounted via drilled ports on the cylindrical reservoir walls, at the same height as the tubing to measure the blood pressure within each reservoir above the measurement location (Fig. 1A). The reservoirs were connected to the pneumatic system (air/vacuum supply) through pneumatic proportional valves (MPYE-5-1/4-010-B, Festo SE and Co. KG, Esslingen-am Neckar, Germany), which regulate the airflow in/out of the system. A dSpace MicroLabBox (dSpace GmbH, Paderborn, Germany) was employed to process the sensor signals, to control the pressure difference between the reservoirs and the fluid levels determined via the hydrostatic blood pressure. The control strategy was implemented in MATLAB Simulink (The MathWorks, MA, USA). The pressures within the reservoirs (Z1 and Z2) were controlled by individual PID controllers. PID controllers were tuned to achieve both rapid dynamic response and minimal steady state error (detailed in Supplementary Section 1). The controllers maintained the actual pressures within 1.9 mmHg of the desired target values and the overshoot pressure observed was less than 7 mmHg with a response time for flow reversal less than 0.52 s.

To maintain the temperature of the entire system at a physiological blood temperature, the system was placed inside a polycarbonate dome (800 mm × 800 mm × 800 mm) with a closable opening for monitoring and sample extraction. A heat gun (ELEKTRON, Leister Technologies AG, Kägiswil, Switzerland) was used to maintain the temperature at 37 °C by making use of a temperature controller (A-Senco TR-81, Pohltechnik.com GmbH, Essingen, Germany), which receives feedback from a temperature sensor (Pt100, HSWARBIT, Guangdong province, China) positioned inside the dome.

Working principle

The control system utilized two PID controllers to adjust the pressure difference (ΔP) between the reservoirs (Z1 and Z2). P3 and P4, measuring the hydrostatic pressure within each reservoir at the tubing height, served as feedback signals for the corresponding PID controllers. Once the target ΔP was defined, it was split into setpoints of +ΔP/2 for the PID controlling reservoir Z1 and −ΔP/2 for the PID controlling reservoir Z2. These controllers continuously adjusted the pneumatic proportional valves for air inflow and outflow to maintain the desired target pressure difference between P3 and P4. For fluid level tracking and automated flow reversal, the hydrostatic pressure in each reservoir was continuously computed by subtracting the static pressure measured by P3 and P4 from the corresponding air pressure measured by P1 and P2 at the top of the reservoir. Once the hydrostatic pressure in Reservoir Z2 reached a predefined threshold, the target inputs to the PID controllers were inverted, consequently reversing the pressure gradient and the direction of blood flow, leading to multiple passes through the system. The adjustable resistance clamp placed in between the reservoir was set to achieve the desired blood flow rate.

Operating conditions

Three operating conditions were chosen for this study, spanning the head-flow (HQ) curve of implantable VADs at a single operating speed, thereby covering a comprehensive range of resistance settings commonly encountered during in-vitro assessments of blood pumps: A high (1.5 L/min; pressure drop 90 mmHg), medium (5 L/min; pressure drop 60 mmHg), and low (7.5 L/min; pressure drop 30 mmHg) resistance setting representing the low, nominal, and high flow rate scenarios of an implantable VAD8,9. The switch volume between the reservoirs was maintained consistently (500 mL) over the three resistance settings, resulting in a switching time of 20 s, 6 s, and 4 s for high, medium, and low resistance, respectively.

Experimental procedure

The blood collection and preparation followed the procedures described in previous studies8,10 and are elaborated in Supplementary Section 2.

The blood contacting surfaces (reservoirs and connecting tube) were primed initially with saline solution. After the complete draining, 1700 mL of the prepared filtered blood filled the loop. The experiments were conducted over 8–12 h, with switching of resistance settings every 4 h. Furthermore, the sequence of conditions was altered over the course of the experiments. Following the completion of the blood filling procedure, the loop was de-aired, and the first resistance setting was maintained for a period of 10 min to ensure blood mixing prior to the collection of samples. A three-way stopcock was integrated into the test loop with an extended tube for enhanced sample extraction. Every 30-min interval, 1 mL of blood was discarded, and 1.5 mL of sample was collected in Eppendorf tubes.

Additionally, to rule out autolysis in the blood, a 500 mL static control was prepared and placed in a tempered incubator shaker (New Brunswick Scientific Innova 40, NJ, USA) and maintained for all the test days. A standard cleaning protocol was maintained throughout the study and detailed in Supplementary Section 3.

Hemolysis assessment

The hemolysis assessment was through the photometric determination of plasma free hemoglobin (pfHb) following the procedures described in the previous studies8,10 (Supplementary Section 4).

Statistics

Outliers were defined as the measured pfHb values of overall experiments for each time point exceeding twice the interquartile range. Upon verifying the normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test, all results were expressed in parametric form as mean and standard deviation. A paired t-test was performed to assess the significance of increase in pfHb levels over a 4-h duration. Further, a one-way ANOVA test was conducted to determine the significance of dfHb between the conditions across the experiments. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In-silico hemolytic characteristics

Geometry preparation

Following the experiments, the polyvinyl tubing and the adjustable resistance clamp corresponding to the three resistance settings were retrieved for the reconstruction of the internal geometries of the resistances. The extracted tubing with the clamps was filled with silicon material (Ecoflex 00-30, Smooth-ON, Inc., PA, USA). Upon curing, the solidified silicon was removed from the tubing. Subsequently, the three silicon replicas were subjected to 3D scanning (SmartSCAN HE-C8, Hexagon AG). The scanned surface model of the three replicas was post-processed in professional computer aided design (CAD) software NX CAD (Siemens, TX, USA), ensuring surface integrity. Figure 2 visualizes the procedures involved in reconstructing resistance profiles.

The reconstruction of the internal geometry for the three resistance settings. (A) The step-by-step process of the reconstruction starts with extracting the tube together with the clamp, followed by casting the silicone, the 3D-rendered model, and the reconstructed CAD model. (B) The post-processed final fluid domain.

Further, the surface model of the three resistance profiles (RP1-high, RP2-medium, and RP3-low resistance respectively) were converted into fluid domains, which were utilized for the CFD simulations. The length of the tubing was adjusted to the distance between the reservoirs (220 mm), with the stenosis at the middle for all three geometries, replicating the experimental scenario.

Numerical setup

The numerical evaluation was performed in StarCCM + solver (Siemens, Munich, Germany). To accurately capture the shear-thinning behavior, the non-Newtonian properties of blood were considered in this study, with a constant density of 1060 kg/m3 and the Carreau Yasuda viscosity model (Eq. 2)11. High-quality hexahedral mesh elements were utilized, with 15 prism layers resolving the near-wall flow. Upon performing the mesh convergence studies (Supplementary Section 6), the final mesh size for the recreated geometries representing high, medium, and low resistance settings (RP1, RP2, and RP3) were 4.99, 5.10, and 5.10 million, respectively, with a Y plus < 0.6. For all three recreated resistance profiles, 100% of the mesh elements met the mesh quality criteria prescribed by the solver’s guidelines12, including skewness angle < 85°, face validity = 1, cell quality > 0.00001, and volume change > 0.01.

where local shear rate (1/s), is the infinite-shear viscosity (0.00345 Pa.s), is the zero-shear viscosity (0.056 Pa.s), λ is relaxation time constant (3.313 s), a is shear thinning parameter (2), and n is power constant (0.3568)11.

Unsteady Reynolds averaged Navier Strokes simulation was performed by modeling turbulence using Menter’s k-ω shear stress transport model13. Additionally, Menter’s one equation gamma transition model14 was utilized to effectively capture the information on transitional flow in the turbulent boundary layer. Turbulent model selection was based on the calculated Reynolds number (Supplementary Section 7) and geometric properties. A mass flow inlet boundary condition was specified for the three resistance profiles with the respective flow rate obtained from the experiment (1.5 L/min, 5 L/min, and 7.5 L/min). The outlet boundary was prescribed as 0 Pa pressure outlet. All the simulations were performed for 10 flow-through cycles with a time step corresponding to a maximum fluid displacement of 2 mm of tube length per time step. The iterative convergence was achieved when the residuals for the continuity and momentum equations fell below 10− 2. The overall simulation was considered converged when the maximum deviation between the time averaged global quantities (pressure drop, wall shear stresses, and cross-sectional velocities) was less than 1% over the last three flow-through cycles. Further, the CFD model was validated through the correlation of the hydraulic parameters.

Hemolysis index (HI), which is the percentile rate of change in free hemoglobin release relative to the total hemoglobin concentration was computed using a power law model with the constants of Giersiepen et al.15 and correlated with the in-vitro calculated NIH. The power law implementation detailed in Supplementary Section 8 followed the procedures described by Garon et al.16 and Fraser et al.17 Upon correlation of HI, further simulations were performed with 10 different flow rates using the three recreated resistance geometries (RP1 – 1 L/min, 2 L/min, 2.5 L/min, RP2 – 3 L/min, 4 L/min, 6 L/min, 7 L/min, RP3 − 4.5 L/min, 6 L/min, 9 L/min) to extrapolate a contour map for HI as a function of hydraulic metrics.

Results

In-vitro hemolytic assessment

The baseline blood properties measured across all experiments were characterized by a pH value of 7.43 ± 0.098 and a measured hematocrit of 30.6 ± 1.4%. In all sets of experiments and for all individual operating conditions, a linear increase in pfHb (R2 > 0.80) was observed (Supplementary Fig. S4). In addition, the sufficient duration of the experiment for each condition was validated by the significant increase in pfHb release (p < 0.03) (Supplementary Fig. S5).

To evaluate the blood trauma potential of the developed test bench without an adjustable resistance clamp in the loop, three independent 8-h experiments were performed at a flow rate of 5 L/min. With 16 dfHb measurements per experiment, the test bench resulted in a dfHb30min of 0.14 ± 0.026 mg/dL.

To assess the hemolysis at different operating conditions, nine experiments were carried out for the three resistance settings with 6–8 dfHb measurements for each condition in each experiment. The dfHb calculated over a time interval of 30-minutes did not show any significant difference (p = 0.55) between the high (0.51 ± 0.32 mg/dL), medium (0.68 ± 0.44 mg/dL), and low (0.55 ± 0.29 mg/dL) resistance settings (Fig. 3A). An increased NIH was observed for the high resistance condition with 14.09 ± 7.57 mg/100L, followed by the medium resistance condition with 5.22 ± 3.38 mg/100L and the low resistance condition with 2.59 ± 1.61 mg/100L (Fig. 3B).

To evaluate the effect of two different adjustable resistance clamps (R1 and R2) under medium resistance condition (5 L/min at 60 mmHg), five experiments were performed. The dfHb30 min (R1: 0.52 ± 0.36 mg/dL, R2: 0.66 ± 0.37 mg/dL) and NIH (R1: 4.08 ± 2.83 mg/100 L, R2: 5.10 ± 2.91 mg/100 L) (Fig. 4A,B) showed no significant difference between the two resistance clamps (p = 0.58).

In-silico hemolytic characteristics

The numerically predicted pressure drops across the high, medium, and low resistance settings with the corresponding recreated resistance profiles (RP1, RP2 and RP3) were 97.12, 64.80, and 34.76 mmHg, respectively, showed a good agreement with the experiment (r = 0.999, RMSE < 7.12 mmHg). Discussion

Resistance clamps play an essential role in regulating pressure and flow in an in-vitro test loop by varying the cross-sectional area of the tubing. The aim of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the hemolytic potential of isolated adjustable resistance clamps in an in-vitro setting. In addition, using CFD, we attempted to understand and extrapolate the hemolysis induced by an adjustable resistance clamp for a wider range of flow conditions.

The main findings of our study revealed that adjustable resistance clamps substantially influence the hemolysis outcome in an in-vitro hemolysis evaluation of blood pumps. The dfHb observed in the three resistance settings indicated similar values. This observation can be explained by the power law formulation for hemolysis generation by shear stress and exposure time. In our study, a balance may exist between the shear stress and the exposure time across the three settings, resulting in similar dfHb. Upon considering the throughput of 100 L, the experimental NIH values are higher for the high-resistance settings, followed by the medium and low-resistance settings.

To assess the hemolytic contribution of resistance clamps, we compared the NIH results with those reported for the state-of-the-art VAD, HeartMate 3 (HM3, Abbott Inc., IL, USA)8, using the same animal species and adjustable resistance clamp (R1) in an ASTM-standard test loop. The literature demonstrated NIH outcomes of HM3 for three different flow-pressure characteristics, achieved by adjusting the resistance clamp at a fixed speed setting8. Upon comparison, at the pump’s main operating point (5600 rpm, 4.5 L/min), up to 84% of the NIH observed in the in-vitro experiments could be attributed to the resistance clamp. In contrast, during off-design flow conditions at the same speed (5600 rpm, 1 L/min, and 7 L/min), the resistance clamps contributed less than 47% of the hemolysis.

These results have important implications for previous findings on the off-design performance of rotary blood pumps (RBPs)8. The main factor contributing to hemolysis at the design point of pump may be pressure-reducing adjustable resistance clamps, while the contribution factor to hemolysis during the off-design operating conditions is mainly attributable to the pump itself. This distinction is especially relevant for next-generation VADs with very low hemolytic potential, where extrinsic test loop components such as clamps can disproportionately influence measured hemolysis, underscoring the vital role of this study for accurate device benchmarking, design refinement, and regulatory evaluation.

In addition, the comparative study on two different adjustable resistance clamps reveals that the hemolysis induced due to the sudden constriction and gradual constriction of the tubing is similar. Consistent with previous in-vitro studies,4,5 our study reinforces that the pressure-reducing resistance clamps impact the hemolysis outcomes irrespective of their shape and cross-sectional profiles.

The robustness of our results for different resistance settings and for different clamps depends on the developed in-vitro test loop. The developed test loop utilizes a hydraulic-pneumatic interface to drive blood, eliminating the blood pump from the loop. This made it possible to isolate the blood trauma potential of resistance clamps evaluated under a wider range of operating conditions. Further, the hemolytic potential of the developed test bench assessed through the experiments without a resistance clamp was comparable with the static control (Supplementary Section 9) maintained during the experiments, highlighting the atraumatic nature of the developed test bench. This indicates that, apart from the resistance clamp, the simplified loop components or the hydraulic-pneumatic interface did not substantially contribute to the hemolysis outcomes of this study. Overall, the versatile nature of the developed test bench can be utilized to test the isolated effect of flow-induced hemolysis in different loop components or other medical devices.

CFD provided a comprehensive overview of the flow dynamics inside the three resistance profiles, revealing substantially disturbed flow with flow detachment in the post-stenosis for all settings. The validated CFD models showed a strong correlation of HI with the corresponding experimental hemolysis metric (NIH), strengthening the accuracy of the CFD models in capturing the trend and relative changes in the hemolysis under different flow conditions.

Consistent with the previous studies on RBPs8, the extrapolated contour map of the HI for the resistance clamp exhibits similar trend across diverse flow rates and pressure drops. An increase in HI was evident with decrease in flow rates and increase in pressure drop. Therefore, the proportional hemolysis contribution of an adjustable resistance clamp to the overall hemolysis results of state-of-the-art RBPs does not change substantially. Notably, these similarities in the morphology of HI may apply to RBPs with similar HQ characteristics. However, the impact of resistance clamps may vary for pumps with different (e.g., steeper) HQ profiles.

Building on these findings, it is important to acknowledge that varying the tubing diameter and length can achieve similar pressure drops in an in-vitro setup without a resistance clamp; however, these variations also increase the priming volume of the standardized test loop prescribed by ASTM F1841-19 for hemolysis measurement of blood pumps. The previous study by Schima et al.4 demonstrated a similar hemolytic potential between tubing and clamp, but these findings may differ for off-design operating conditions of third-generation RBPs. Further, there may be potential to reduce the impact of resistance clamps on the in-vitro hemolysis assessment of blood pumps. For instance, an adjoint-based, shape-optimized design18 of the resistance profile may promote smoother blood flow, reducing disturbances in the post-stenosis region and potentially lowering shear stress. However, such a design may be challenging to manufacture or may increase the residence time of blood. Nonetheless, the hemolytic contribution of the pressure reducing resistance clamp remains a factor that cannot be overlooked.

Limitation

The priming volume of our in-vitro test loop was maintained at 1700mL, which exceeds the volume prescribed by the standard (500mL). ASTM F1841-19,1 being pump-centric standard, primarily focuses on evaluating the hemolytic potential of blood pumps. However, in our case, isolating the impact of the adjustable resistance clamps necessitated a larger blood volume to ensure effective circulation within the developed in-vitro loop. Despite the higher volume, the significant increase in pfHb (p < 0.03) (Supplementary Fig. S5) over the experimental duration indicates sufficient sensitivity. Additionally, considering the early studies on the influence of priming volume on NIH outcomes5,10,19,20, the priming volume of 1700 mL may not substantially affect the hemolytic contribution of adjustable resistance clamps in in-vitro hemolysis assessment of blood pumps. While the contribution of resistance clamps in the in-vitro blood pump test loop obtained through the comparison to literature-based NIH values for similar operating points of the HM3 provided valuable insight, they are not fully conclusive. The relative hemolytic contribution of resistance clamps may vary depending on the specific operating condition. However, the overall trend—with a higher contribution of clamps at the pump’s design point compared to the off-design pump operations likely be consistent.

While our CFD models provide valuable insights, they inherently involve certain modeling and discretization errors. Specifically, the geometry reconstruction does not account for the elastic deformation of the tubing under pressure, and the outlet boundary conditions were applied with the assumption that flow was sufficiently isolated rather than strictly fully developed. However, both aspects were mitigated by the strong agreement between numerical and experimental pressure drop data (RMSE 7.12 mmHg), indicating that the essential hydraulic behavior within the clamp, crucial for hemolysis prediction, was well-captured. Furthermore, while numerical power-law-based hemolysis prediction models have been a subject of ongoing debate due to the lack of universally calibrated coefficients for all conditions, our CFD simulations primarily aimed to capture relative differences and trends in hemolysis across the three resistance conditions, rather than focusing on comparing the absolute values with experiment.

Conclusion

The impact of an adjustable resistance clamp on hemolysis outcomes in the in-vitro setting is substantial, irrespective of the investigated shapes or profiles. These findings underscore the importance of considering the influence of clamps while investigating the hemolytic potential of blood pumps.
