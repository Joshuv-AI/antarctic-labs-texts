Analysis of the role of water condition on the friction performance of calcareous sand from the nano-scale
Authors: Kamal Al-Zaoari, Sha Lai, Wei-Qiang Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The strength of calcareous sand, which is closely related to the coastal stability, is controlled by the interparticle frictions. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the friction behavior of soil particles under different water conditions to predict their potential failure. This work aims to study the role of water on the friction behaviour of calcite, the main mineral in calcareous sand, at the nano-scale. Using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, the friction behaviour of calcite slabs under different water layers (WL) and sliding directions were compared and analysed. From the results, it is found that there is a nonlinear decrease in friction force as the water layers between calcite slabs increases. This is because that thin water films serve to weaken the direct attraction between the upper and bottom slabs, leading to the friction force reduces to a stable level. Meanwhile, the friction behaviour of calcite is affected by the sliding direction, particularly in low water layers (< 2WL), which is due to different atoms arrangement on the surfaces. Higher friction is observed when sliding in the x-direction ([100] crystal orientation) compared with that sliding in the y-direction ([010] crystal orientation). In addition, the stick-slip phenomenon is observed for the low water layers (< 2WL) and becomes less pronounced with increasing water layers which shows a smoother friction force evolution at relatively higher water layers (> 4WL). The results contribute to a deeper understanding of the role of water on the friction behaviour of calcite at the nano-scale.

Introduction

Recently, numerous structures have been built in coastal environments where calcareous sand commonly exists. Calcareous sand, an example of a typical marine sediment, differs from silica sands due to its large void ratio, crushable structure and angular composition1,2. These features make it susceptible to failure, which have significant impacts on the stability of beaches and coastlines3. It is widely acknowledged that the frictional behavior of calcareous sand exhibit sensitivity to water content4,5, and excessive moisture would lead to decreased friction and increased susceptibility to erosion6. This phenomenon highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of the frictional behavior of calcareous sands, particularly in offshore projects, where it is often used as the foundation material.

Calcite (CaCO3) is a prevalent crystalline form of calcium carbonate distributed across diverse geological formations. Through weathering or erosion processes, calcite can disintegrate into fine particles, playing a significant role in the genesis of calcareous sands. As a widely abundant mineral in geological formations and biological structures, calcite serves as the predominant mineral component in calcareous sand, constituting over 92% of its composition7,8. Its crystal structure, composed of calcium, carbon, and oxygen atoms, is characterized by closely packed layers of carbonate ions9. The charged surface of calcite interacts with water molecules10, contributing to the friction behaviour of carbonate sands. The water existing in soils normally classified two different forms : adsorbed water and free water11. It has been observed that the presence of water also affects the settlement behaviour12, shear strength13,14 and enhances the particle breakage of calcareous sands15. Kim and Choi13 measured the relationship of internal friction angle and cohesion with the water content through the direct shear test, and they found that water content plays a significant role in altering the friction behaviour of the sand, as the water content initially increases the cohesion and then decreases. Wu et al.14 found that the internal friction angle rises and then decreases with rising water content, while the cohesion increases significantly with water content. This observation conflicts with the idea that an increase in water content leads to a decrease in friction coefficient. Moreover, Liu et al.16 showed that the particle breakage of the calcareous sand decreases as the increase of water content initially and then increases once the water content reaches the saturated level of the specimen, indicating that the relationship between water and calcareous sand behaviour is more complex. Due to weather conditions and the foundation construction, the water in calcareous sands varies a lot, inducing the complex friction behavior. The complex behavior in macro-scale is closely related to the hydration state of soils in the nanoscale, which prompts a more in-depth investigation into the fundamental mechanisms that govern the frictional behaviour of calcite.

Nanotribology, a specialized field within tribology, studies friction, wear, and lubrication at the nanoscale17. Experimental and simulation techniques have been extensively advanced within the field of nanotribology. Several nanotribology studies have highlighted the influence of different factors on the frictional behaviour of surfaces18,19. For instance, the presence of water can significantly affect the frictional response of calcite and silica20. Previous research has suggested that the formation of water layers may contribute to changes in the friction coefficient of calcite surfaces21,22. These water layers act as a barrier, impeding the interaction between the mineral surfaces and consequently decreasing the friction coefficient23,24. Diao and Espinosa-Marzal25 have examined the frictional properties of calcite crystals in two brine solutions using AFM, NaCl and CaCl2, showing that the fluid composition, sliding velocity and normal stress all affect the frictional behavior of calcite, demonstrating that low stress levels would result in low friction coefficients. The results indicated that as the water content increases, the friction coefficients of calcite decrease, due to the lubricating effect of water between particles. However, the fundamental mechanisms of friction behaviour affected by water, particularly for calcite, are still not fully understood. Moreover, Yang et al.26 showed that sliding can induce surface wear, chemical changes, and deformation. These factors collectively influence the friction behaviour, posing challenges in terms of control and observation in experiments, where monitoring all these impact factors can be intricate.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulation is indispensable tool, employing computational methods to simulate the behaviour of molecules over time. It allows virtually exploring and understanding the atomic-scale interactions and force that govern friction and wear27,28. Moreover, MD simulation provides the advantages of tracing and calculating the properties of particular atoms29,30, as well as investigating the effects of individual factors, such as contact area and load, which cannot be achieved by experiments31,32. Together, these tools are pivotal in advancing the understanding of nanoscale tribological phenomena and contributing to developing innovative materials and technologies. Carpenter et al.33 showed that friction behaviour of calcite may be influenced by the chemical composition of the fluids, stress and the sliding velocity. Mutisya et al.34 found that as the number of water molecules between two hydrophilic surfaces increases, the water molecules exhibit a distinct layering behaviour. Bhamra et al.27 showed that the higher friction in wet diamond-calcite compared to diamond-quartz is attributed to increased interfacial bonding and the rate of interfacial bond increasing with pressure through experiments and MD. Luo et al.35 used atomistic simulations to investigate the directional dependence of calcite’s mechanical properties, revealing distinct anisotropies across crystallographic directions. Their results showed significantly higher fracture strength and elastic modulus along the [010] direction compared to [421] and [421̅], attributed to varying failure mechanisms such as dislocation activity, phase transitions, and void formation. Brekke-Svaland and Bresme36 observed that the (1014) surface of calcite and the (001) surface of aragonite have different adsorption properties, suggesting that the variations in hydration reflect as different intersurface forces. Tian et al.37 performed MD simulations on calcite and silica surfaces with geopolymer and evaluated the moisture effect in the interaction between geopolymer-aggregate surfaces based on a distance of approximately 10 Å, which showed that appropriate interfacial water promotes good interfacial interaction whereas excessive water may lead to interfacial failure. Although many related works have been conducted, little is known about how water layers and the surface structure affect the frictional response of calcite and what is the critical point of water roles that can affect the friction behaviour.

To investigate the interface friction of calcite, MD simulation is employed to simulate the friction behaviour of calcite in various water layers and different friction directions using a steered molecular dynamics (SMD) approach. Firstly, the MD simulation of calcite was compared with results from the direct shear test of calcareous sand to illustrate the reliability of the simulation. The relationship between the friction force and the change in the water content has been investigated. Moreover, the effects of sliding velocity and normal force on the friction forces were discussed. This work could provide new insight into calcite nanoscale friction behaviour using the atomistic approach, which will improve the understanding of the interfacial strength of carbonate sand and predict the risk of inter-particle failure.

Methodology and schedules

Molecular dynamic simulation

In this study, calcite minerals were considered and the unit cell was obtained from Graf38, the detailed information of unit cell is listed in Table 1. The model consists of upper and bottom slabs of calcite with water molecules (50–2000 W) between the two slabs, presenting different water films between calcite’s slabs. The unit cell was repeated in the x-, y-, and z-directions to create the slab model. After that the orthogonal slab was then created by cleaving the original slab to reveal their common surfaces. The relatively stable cleavage surface (104) of calcite was selected. The final calcite slab comprising of 6000 atoms with a size of x = 40.48 Å, y = 49.9 Å, z = 37.95 Å, as shown in Fig. 1.

The simulated model was divided into the rigid layers, thermostat layers, and free layers, as shown in Fig. 1a. The upper rigid layer was modelled as a rigid body to transfer the external vertical loads and sliding friction into the system without undergoing structural deformation, while the bottom rigid layer remained fixed throughout the simulation. The thermostat layers were employed to regulate the system temperature and minimize frictional heating during sliding31. The free layers, along with the interlayer water film, were unconstrained and allowed to move freely. This approach was adapted to simulate the friction behaviour of clay minerals and silica39–41.

All MD simulations were performed using the Large-scale Atomic Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS)42 and OVITO software43 was utilized for visualization. The force field developed by Xiao et al.44 was chosen to describe the atomic interaction of calcite. The simple point charge (SPC) water model was used to represent water molecules45. Both van der Waals and short-range Coulombic interactions were calculated with a cutoff distance of 10 Å. To eliminate interactions between periodic images along the z-axis, a vacuum slab twice the height of the simulation box was introduced, while periodic boundary conditions were applied in the x- and y-directions.

Simulation procedures

The simulation protocol involved three stages to prepare and analyse the system. Firstly, energy minimization was conducted using the Conjugate Gradient (CG) algorithm. Subsequently, the equilibration stage was performed in three steps: initial equilibrium, thermostat equilibrium, and equilibrium under normal load. In the initial equilibrium, the system was allowed to equilibrium with the isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) with P = 1 atm and T = 300 K for 500 ps and a time step of 1 fs. For the thermostat equilibrium, the thermostat layer was subjected to the Langevin thermostat while the free layer and water film were controlled under the constant volume and energy ensemble (NVE) for 100 ps. Further, the system was equilibrated under normal load (1 ~ 10 GPa) where the normal load was applied to each atom in the up rigid layer. The area of the applied normal load is equal to the area of the shear surface (xy plane). Lastly, after the system equilibrium was reached, the sliding friction was performed using steered molecular dynamics (SMD). An external load was applied to up rigid layer utilizing a virtual spring model with a spring constant of 150 N/m, and constant velocity allowing the sliding of the upper slab along a desired direction (x or y). Subsequently, the friction force was calculated to predict the friction behaviour of calcite with different hydration states.

Normally, it appears that the nonbonded interactions are the most dominant interactions, potentially influencing the friction force. Accordingly, both Coulombic and van der Waals interactions were taken into account to evaluate the interaction energies between the calcite slabs and the calcite–water system. Direct shear test

Direct shear tests were carried out under 200 kPa normal load and 0.017 mm/s shearing velocity. This study employs the MSS-1 A multifunctional submarine sediment shear system46, which is capable of measuring the frictional properties of the soil. In each test, 730 g of calcareous sand was put in the shear box after being papered with different water contents. In addition, the calcareous sand was placed into the shear box in layers and tested with a level gauge to avoid the disposition. After preparing the sample, the normal stress was applied and the direct shearing test was performed with the desired shear rate. For more details on sand property and test procedures the reader can refers to Feng et al.46. Furthermore, for the low water content test, the sand was dried in the oven for 24 h under 105 °C. For others water contents of 4.3–17.8%, the test samples were prepared under the desired water contents first and then three samples were taken from the shearing surface after the test to measure the water content.

Results and discussions

Effect of the water layer

To study the water layer effect on the friction behaviour of calcite, various water molecules (50–2000 W) were placed between the upper and the bottom slabs, representing different water layer thicknesses. Figure 2 shows the density profile of the water molecules between the two slabs after the equilibrium stage. It is observed that a layer structure of water molecules was formed on the surfaces with a density higher than 1 g/cm3, indicating the adsorbed water on the calcite surface. In this study, the term “water layers” refers to the number of distinct density peaks in the distribution of water molecules along the z-direction. Each peak corresponds to one hydration layer, and the exact number of water molecules in each layer may vary depending on the distribution of water molecules. For instance, a single water layer (1WL) was formed with 50–100 (1.5-2 Å) water molecules, as plotted in Fig. 2(a), while two and four water layers (2WL and 4WL) were formed, as presented in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c), with 150–300 (2.5–4.5 Å) and 400–450 (5.5–6.5 Å) water molecules, respectively. Furthermore, with the increase of water molecules up to 500 (7.5 Å) and beyond (> 4WL), the water layer between the two calcite slabs comprises the free water with a density close to 1 g/cm³, along with two adsorbed water layers at each surface, as shown in Fig. 2(d). Therefore, different water layers could be considered, including adsorbed water layers (1WL-4WL) as well as adsorbed water and free water (> 4WL), for the calcite.

Figure 3. shows the comparison of friction force during the sliding process with different water molecules numbers under 5e-4 Å fs− 1 sliding velocity and three normal loads (1, 5 and 10 GPa). The stick–slip friction behaviour is observed and it is obvious at low water molecules (≤ 200 W). At higher water molecules (≥ 400 W), the stick–slip friction behaviour declines and shows smoother friction. This is because the presence of water lowers the interaction between calcite surfaces and minimizing the stick-slip phenomenon. The stick–slip behaviour of calcite agrees with the trend of silica using MD simulation from Xu et al.47. Besides, non-monotonic trend was observed under low normal load and higher water molecules where the friction force for (1000 W) is slightly lower than that for (2000 W) under 1 GPa. This trend may due to the combined effects of week water layering and confinement at low normal load. At 1 GPa, the adsorbed layers for 1000 and 2000 W are same, and the addition of a water molecular can remain more fluid-like or disordered where normal load effect is minor.

Moreover, results from the direct shear test for calcareous sands with different water contents (0%~17.8%) were presented in Fig. 4. It can be seen that the stress-strain curves change with the increase of the water content, and the peak shear strength of calcareous sands decreases nonlinearly with the water content. This indicates that the water on the sand particle surfaces has the impact on the particle friction. These findings have a good consistency with related findings13. Figure 5 shows the evolution of the friction force with water molecules for calcite from MD simulation, and the shear strength with water content from the direct shear test of calcareous sands. It can be observed that water plays an important role in the shear strength of soils may be due to the increase of the adsorbed water layers and the occurrence of free water in the nanoscale. The critical point with 500 W is observed, suggesting that the friction force is less sensitive to the water after this transition point, where free water between calcite slabs at this higher hydration state may be starts to play the role. Thus, 1WL ~ 4WL water layers played a bonding role during the shear process, resulting in higher friction force, while > 4WL water layers shows a lubricating role with a constant friction force. It is worth noting that this study focuses on the fundamental inter-particle and sliding friction at the atomic level, which is a key but not the only component of the overall shear strength in geotechnical tests like direct shear test. The influences of surface roughness, particle shape, and rolling friction, which are important factors governing macroscopic shear behavior, were not considered in the present model and could be addressed in future studies.

Effect of the sliding direction

The friction behavior between calcite slabs is also influenced by the structure and surface properties and the sliding direction. Note that the friction force of each water layer is the average friction force of all the simulations with the range of water molecules for each water layer (see Fig. 5b). As presented in Fig. 6, the friction forces for the sliding in x- and y-directions are different, especially at < 4WL, in which the friction force sliding in the x-direction is significantly higher. The friction force differs slightly for different sliding directions at higher water layers and free water, (≥ 4WL) water layers, where the friction behavior is dominated by the interaction of water molecules on the calcite surface.

Figure 7 shows the calculated potential energy of the upper and bottom slab surface. To evaluate the surface potential energy, the oxygen atoms in the first layer were selected to investigation the energy at different lateral positions. The resulting potential energy profile represents the frictional forces acting on the surface during sliding. The (104) cleavage plane in calcite has a specific crystallographic orientation. The arrangement of atoms and the bonding in this plane is not uniform in all directions, leading to anisotropy in properties, including friction. There is a symmetry in the atom positions between the upper and bottom surfaces during sliding in the x-direction, which could account for the larger friction compared to sliding in the y-direction. Conversely, during sliding in the y-direction, there is an asymmetry in the atom positions, with atoms on one surface sliding in a zigzag pattern on the other surface. This asymmetry may offer less resistance compared to the sliding in the x-direction, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The effect of the sliding direction is limited to adsorbed water layers (< 4WL), as the water molecules increase this effect would be eliminated by the thick water layer (≥ 4WL). This provides insights into the oscillation of particles along the sliding direction, which would influence the friction force, especially at the macro scale where sand is randomly organized.

Effect of the shear velocity and the normal load

Figure 8 shows the friction force versus sliding velocity of calcite at various water layers between calcite slabs under the normal load of 5 GPa. It is found that the friction force exhibits a nonlinear logarithmic relationship with the sliding velocity. There are two regions for the velocity effect: the linear state which is prominent at the low velocity and the strengthening state at the higher velocity. This relationship agrees well with the thermally activated Prandtl-Tomlinson model48. For the hydration states from 2WL to larger than 4WL, the linear state is from 1e-5 to 1e-4 Å/fs-1, while the velocity-strengthening state shows with velocity above 1e-4 Å/fs-1. However, for the hydration state with 1WL, the velocity-strengthening regime was observed at 5e-4 Å/fs-1. This trend may be attributed to the dynamic movement of the water molecules between the calcite slabs. As shown in Fig. 8(b), the diffusion coefficient of water molecules rises notably with sliding velocity. At lower velocities, water molecules diffuse more slowly, providing sufficient time for water molecules to re-organize, resulting in weaker interaction and friction force. However, when the velocity increases to a certain value the friction force rises significantly due to the stronger interaction originates by the dynamic movement of the water molecules. This transition in frictional behaviour reflects the complex interplay between sliding velocity under different hydration states in calcite.

The peak shear stress values as a function of normal load under different water layers for the sliding in x-direction are represented in Fig. 9 (a). Generally, the shear stress gradually increases with the normal load, especially at 1WL and 4WL, indicating a strengthening bonding at these two water layers compared to 2WL. However, at a relatively higher water layers (> 4WL), the shear stress increases slightly with normal load, indicating smoothness friction of calcite at this hydration state. Previous studies that used AFM to study the friction of calcite have identified a similar relationship between the applied normal load and the resulting shear stress49. Furthermore, the increasing normal load decreases the distance between the calcite slabs and changes the distribution of water molecules between them. From Fig. 9 (b), It is evident that increasing the normal load reduces the thickness of the water film, leading to a more compressed layer and stronger interfacial interactions. Consequently, greater energy is required to overcome these interactions during sliding, which results in higher friction forces under larger normal loads.

Result discussions

Friction at the nanoscale is greatly affected by atomic interactions among the surfaces in contact. In our system, nonbond interactions are the most dominant interactions, which could affect the friction between calcite surfaces, as shown in Fig. 10. Besides, the bonded interactions are relatively small and can be negligible compared to the nonbonded interactions when analysing the atomic interactions between the system components (calcite and water).

To fully understand the role of water on the friction behaviour of calcite, it is crucial to consider atomic interactions between calcite slabs and water molecules. Figure 11 illustrates the average interaction energies between calcite slabs (Calcite-Calcite) and between calcite slabs and water molecules (Calcite-Water) under 5GPa normal load and under 5e-4 Å fs− 1 sliding velocity. Note that interaction energies for (> 4WL) hydration state are represented with 50, 1000 and 2000 W for clarity. The friction of the calcite-water system under different water layers have two phases: the interaction between the calcite slabs (Calcite-Calcite), which takes place at water layers (≤ 4WL), and (Calcite-Water) interaction which dominates the interaction at > 4WL. At 1WL, the strong direct atomic attrition between the two surfaces under an applied normal load leads to higher friction force. However, as water molecules increase, the distance between the slabs increases, weakens the slabs attraction, thus decreasing friction force. When free water is formed between calcite slabs at higher water layers (> 4WL), the interactions between the two surfaces disappear which (Calcite-Water) interactions control all the friction, and show a stable friction force. This indicates that the water molecules play an important role and lubricate the friction between the slabs. Although the direct atomic attrition between the two surfaces is eliminated in this phase (Calcite-Water), the friction is controlled by the pressure of confined water film14,50. Since the water film is compressed under the normal load, the attraction between calcite surfaces and water molecules leads to resistance to sliding.

Moreover, it is noted that the water layers on the surfaces can impact the stick–slip friction behaviour, which disappears with the rising of water molecules. It is suggested that the stick–slip friction is affected by the direct interaction between the surfaces where the effective interaction energy between the surfaces dissipate at a higher water layer with 500 W, as illustrated in Fig. 12. The stick–slip friction is attributed to energy barriers between two energy minima, where atoms must overcome these energy barriers to move to the next energy minima under the sliding force. This transition leads to the increase of friction force during sticking phases and subsequent reduction upon surpassing these energy barriers during slipping phases. Moreover, the presence of water molecules disrupts the strong interatomic interactions between calcite surfaces, resulting in less energy to be overcome during the sliding. Thus, the smooth friction force is observed at (> 4WL) water layers where no effective interaction energy between the surfaces of the calcite slabs.

Notably, there is an observed decline in the friction coefficient with the rising of water layers, as shown in Fig. 13a. The friction coefficient of calcite decreases by 61.36% and 60.46% from 1WL to > 4WL for the sliding in x- and y-direction, respectively. Furthermore, a peak of the friction coefficient is observed at 4WL, revealing unique friction behaviour under 4WL. This could be attributed to the change in the distribution of water molecules, indicating that the formation of 4 water layers adhere the two calcite’s slabs and results in a higher friction coefficient. This pattern is consistent with the findings of Wei et al.53, which indicate that the friction force of montmorillonite exhibits two peaks at interlayer water contents of 5% and 20%. Furthermore, the friction coefficient obtained in this study is compared with values reported in previous research in Table 2. The friction coefficients of calcite obtained from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations closely match those from atomic force microscopy (AFM) experiments, which confirms the validity of this MD simulation. The friction coefficient obtained from the MD simulations ranges from 0.051 to 0.132, aligning with the 0.003–0.07 values reported from AFM experiments on calcite21,49. However, the friction coefficients of MD and AFM are relatively smaller compared to the macroscale friction coefficient of calcite (0.6–0.8)54. Numerous studies have indicated a reduction in friction coefficient from the macroscale to the nanoscale for crystalline surfaces55, and the friction force is linearly dependent on the number of atoms that chemically interact across the contact56. At the nanoscale, as in MD simulations and AFM, the contact area is much smaller, leading to the different frictional behavior compared to that in macroscale tests. On the macroscale, surface roughness, imperfections, and larger contact areas significantly impact the friction coefficient. Thus, the close match between MD simulations and AFM experiments is their similar scale and interaction mechanisms, while the discrepancy with macroscale friction coefficients arises from differences in scale, surface conditions, and the complexity of interactions at larger contact areas.

With regard to the offset friction load, it is demonstrated that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_0}$$\end{document} decreases with an increase in water layers in both x- and y-directions, as illustrated in Fig. 13b. At the nanoscale, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_0}$$\end{document} represents the adhesive interaction between calcite slabs and interfacial water layers in the absence of an applied normal load, which reflects the structure stability of the calcite-water system. This force arises from the van der Waals and Coulomb interactions. For 1WL ~ 4WL, the structure stability of the calcite-water system is primarily governed by the interactions between calcite slabs, as well as between calcite slabs and water layers. However, for relatively higher water layers (> 4WL), the interaction between calcite slabs and water layers mainly provided the structure stability, aligning with the previously described trends in interaction energy Fig. 11c. Nevertheless, at the macroscale, the cohesion of sand is negligible because macroscopic friction does not account for van der Waals and Coulombic electrostatic interactions. As a result, the frictional behavior at the nanoscale may differ from that observed at. macroscales.

Conclusions

In this study, molecular dynamics simulations were utilized and performed to explore the impact of water on the friction characteristics of calcite. This work could provide insight into the complex relationship between water and friction behavior of calcite. The main findings are summarized as follows:
