Parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises in children with hereditary spherocytosis in the Czech Republic: multicentre retrospective study
Authors: Petr Birke, Veronika Fiamoli, Barbora Ludíková, Jana Zapletalová, Dagmar Pospíšilová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHereditary spherocytosis is the most common congenital anaemia in Europe. In patients with this disease, parvovirus B19 infection leads to aplastic crisis – a severe complication with significant morbidity and decrease in haemoglobin concentration and reticulocyte count.MethodsThis retrospective, multicentre study, conducted at the Department of Paediatrics, Faculty of Medicine and Dentistry Palacký University and University Hospital Olomouc, and the Department of Paediatric Haematology and Biochemistry, University Hospital Brno, Czech Republic, aims to describe the clinical features, diagnostic approaches, complications, and treatment of parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises in paediatric patients with a diagnosis of hereditary spherocytosis between January 1 and June 30, 2024.ResultsA total of 23 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of hereditary spherocytosis were hospitalised due to parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crisis. Their mean age was 6.9 years. The most frequent clinical symptoms were fever (p-value < 0.0001), pallor (p-value < 0.0001), fatigue (p-value 0.0001), vomiting (p-value 0.001), pain (p-value 0.004), and cough (p-value 0.009). Erythema infectiosum was rare. A mean haemoglobin concentration was 4.93 g/dL (range from 2.9 to 7.4 g/dL). Other abnormal haematological parameters included leukopenia (26.0%), thrombocytopenia (8.7%), or pancytopenia (26.0%). Mean transfusion requirements were 2.74 transfusion units of red blood cells. There was no significant difference between men and women in age, haemoglobin level, and number of transfusion units.ConclusionThis study provides a frequency of multiple clinical symptoms of parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises in children with hereditary spherocytosis during an epidemic, together with haematological parameters and transfusion requirements. Beginning in January 2024, we have observed more Parvovirus B19 aplastic crises than expected. Similar increase of incidence of parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises incidence has been reported across Europe and North America.

Introduction

Hereditary spherocytosis (HS), Minkowski-Chauffard syndrome [1], is the most common congenital haemolytic anaemia in Middle and Northern Europe. The estimated prevalence in these areas is 1/2000-1/5000 [2]. HS is caused by variants in genes encoding membrane proteins of the red blood cell (RBC) cytoskeleton: α-spectrin (SPTA1 gene), β-spectrin (SPTB gene), ankyrin (ANK1 gene), protein 4.2 (EPB42 gene) and band 3 (SLC4A1 gene). Deficiency of these proteins results in spherical-shaped RBC and haemolysis, which is compensated by accelerated erythropoiesis. Inheritance is autosomal dominant in approximately 75% of cases, in the remaining 25%, it is autosomal recessive or due to de novo variants [3, 4].

The clinical manifestations of HS are very variable. The diagnosis of HS is based on a combination of a positive family history (in about 75% of cases), clinical features (jaundice and enlarged spleen) and laboratory investigations (anaemia, presence of spherocytes on blood smear, reticulocytosis, biochemical markers of haemolysis: increased lactate dehydrogenase and unconjugated bilirubin, decreased haptoglobin). Severity of HS is classified as mild, moderate and severe according to haemoglobin concentrations, reticulocyte count, and bilirubin concentrations, see Table 1 [5]. Many individuals have a normal haemoglobin concentration, but with reticulocytosis due to compensation of haemolysis [6]. To confirm the diagnosis of HS, the eosin-5’-maleimide (EMA) binding test, acidified glycerol lysis test (AGLT), osmotic fragility test (OFT) or plasma membrane protein electrophoresis can be used. The molecular genetic testing is recommended when the diagnosis is difficult, and it is helpful for estimating of the development of HS and for finding optimal treatment options [2, 7].

Complications of HS include pigment gallstones (developing already in the first decade), haemolytic and aplastic crises. Haemolytic crises, often triggered by viruses, are typical in childhood; they are usually mild, and characterised by a transient increase in jaundice, reticulocytosis, and worsening of anaemia. Transient aplastic crises are caused by infection of Parvovirus B19, Epstein-Barr virus or cytomegalovirus [3].

Parvovirus B19 is a small, nonenveloped, single-stranded DNA virus that binds to the P antigen and is transmitted via the respiratory route, through blood or blood products, or during pregnancy to the foetus. Parvovirus B19 causes of childhood rash disease erythema infectiosum (fifth disease), arthritis or arthralgias, myocarditis, hydrops fetalis or intrauterine foetal death, transient aplastic crisis in individuals with chronic haemolytic anaemia, and pure RBC aplasia in individuals who are immunocompromised [8–10].

Parvovirus B19 infects RBC progenitors, which leads to transient inhibition of erythropoiesis and to severe anaemia with reticulocytopenia in individuals with chronic haemolytic anaemia who require hospitalisation [11]. The treatment of a parvovirus B-19 aplastic crisis involves RBC transfusions and symptomatic care.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, multicentre study conducted at Department of Paediatrics, Faculty of Medicine and Dentistry Palacký University and University Hospital in Olomouc, Czech Republic and Department of Paediatric Haematology and Biochemistry, University Hospital in Brno, Czech Republic, aims to describe clinical features, diagnostic methods, complications, and the treatment of parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises in paediatric patients with diagnosis of hereditary spherocytosis from January 1, 2024 to June 30, 2024.

Inclusion criteria were the age between 0 and 18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of HS according to the Guidelines for the diagnosis and management of hereditary spherocytosis [6] and proven by molecular-genetic testing, a diagnosis of the transient aplastic crisis with the need for red blood cell transfusion, and regular annual follow-up at the centre. Transient aplastic crisis is defined as a decrease in haemoglobin of 3 g/dL or more with reticulocytopenia [12]. Reticulocytopenia is defined as decrease in the number of reticulocytes under 25 × 109/L. Leukopenia is defined as decrease in the number of leukocytes under 4 × 109/L. Thrombocytopenia is defined as decrease in the number of thrombocytes under 150 × 109/L.

Exclusion criteria included incomplete clinical and laboratory data and records.

All research data were retrospectively obtained from electronic health records, including patient demographics (age, sex), the presence of clinical symptoms (fever, pain, vomiting, diarrhoea, cough, dyspnoea, fatigue, vertigo, rash, sore throat, heart murmur, tachycardia, enlarged spleen, dehydration), the presence of gallstones seen on ultrasound, and haematological parameters (haemoglobin concentrations, leukocyte count, and platelet count).

Serological tests for the detection of IgM and IgG antibodies against parvovirus B19, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein-Barr virus were evaluated. In case of cross-reactivity, real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for detecting parvovirus B19, cytomegalovirus (CMV), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) was used. Eventual coinfection detected by PCR was recorded.

Indication for transfusion of red blood cells was in case of haemoglobin level lower than 7.5 g/dL.

The number of transfusion units of leukocyte-depleted red blood cells was counted.

Blood count was performed daily. Patients were discharged from the hospital when they achieved both a stable condition and a haemoglobin concentration over 7.5 g/dL.

Descriptive statistics for baseline characteristics and outcomes were obtained. IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) was used to analyse the data. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare men and women in quantitative parameters, and the Fisher’s exact test was used to compare qualitative parameters. The significance of the occurrence of symptoms was assessed using the Fisher’s exact test. Tests were performed at a significance level of 0.05.

Results

During the study period from January 1, 2024, to June 30, 2024, a total of 23 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HS were hospitalised in the University Hospital in Olomouc and in Brno due to a parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crisis. The characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 2. The mean age was 6.9 years (median 6 years, range from 2 years to 13 years). Twelve of the patients (52.2%) were female. None of the patients underwent the splenectomy. Eight of the patients (34.8%) had present biliary sludge or gallstones previously to the aplastic crisis.

Positive IgM antibodies against parvovirus B19 were detected in all the patients. Due to parvovirus B19, CMV and EBV IgM cross-reactivity was found in 11 patients (47.8%), and PCR detection of parvovirus B19 was used. Four patients (17.4%) had a coinfection with other viral illnesses: EBV (1 patient), respiratory syncytial virus (2 patients), and influenza A (1 patient), confirmed by PCR.

Patients developed severe anaemia with a mean haemoglobin concentration of 4.93 g/dL (median 4,9 g/dL, range from 2.9 to 7.4 g/dL) and with reticulocytopenia. Other abnormal haematological parameters included the presence of leukopenia (with neutropenia and/or lymphopenia), thrombocytopenia, or pancytopenia, as reported in Table 4.

Discussion

Hereditary spherocytosis is one of the most common congenital anaemias in Europe. The clinical presentation of HS varies widely from symptomless or very mild to a severe RBC-transfusion-dependent form. Therefore, HS can be diagnosed in childhood up to adult age. Even complications such as gallstones, haemolytic or transient aplastic crises may be the first manifestation of HS [7]. HS was diagnosed in one patient of our study population at the age of six due to parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crisis. An additional finding was the presence of gallstones.

Parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crisis presents a serious complication of HS. It was first described in 1983, 8 years after the identification of parvovirus B19 by Cossart [13, 14]. Parvovirus B19 induces a cytopathic effect on erythropoietic progenitor cells, which can result in profound anaemia with reticulocytopenia (as a characteristic laboratory finding), congestive heart failure and cardiovascular collapse. Leukocyte and platelet counts can also be decreased, as we confirmed in our study. Pathophysiological explanation of leukopenia and thrombocytopenia remains still unclear. Parvovirus B19 may be cytotoxic also to non-erythroid cells, causes platelet destruction and suppress leukopoiesis and megakaryopoiesis [15].

In comparison with previous studies and case reports [16–20], we discovered that the most frequent clinical symptoms of parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crisis in patients with HS are fever, pallor, fatigue, vomiting, pain, and cough. Erythema infectiosum is rare, as is reported in studies mentioned above. Exact explanation for missing erythema infectiosum remains still unknown. The infectious cause can be confirmed by serological detection of IgM antibodies against parvovirus B19 and/ or PCR detection. Aplastic crisis usually lasts from 10 to 14 days [11] and does not occur in association with another complication of parvovirus B19 infection. Parvovirus B19 infection may lead to myocarditis [21]. Myocarditis with pleural effusions and hepatosplenomegaly was diagnosed in one patient of the study group. According to our results, there was no significant difference between men and women in age, haemoglobin level, and number of transfusion units.

Two patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HS and confirmed parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises were not enrolled in the statistics because they did not require the substitution of red blood cells. That means, that not all patients with parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises require RBC substitution. They were siblings of patients in the study group.

According to information from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, several European countries reported an increase in the circulation of parvovirus B19 during late 2023 and early 2024. An increase in parvovirus B19 positive plasma was seen [22, 23]. Severe adverse outcomes in pregnant women were observed [24]. We report an epidemic of parvovirus B19 in patients with underlying haematologic disease during the first half of 2024.

Conclusion

We report a retrospective multicentre study during an epidemic of parvovirus B19-induced aplastic crises in children with hereditary spherocytosis and the frequency of other multiple clinical symptoms, which have not been described yet, together with haematological parameters. Future research should include larger studies to support these findings, because of small cohort of patients vulnerable to selection bias and lack of a control group.
