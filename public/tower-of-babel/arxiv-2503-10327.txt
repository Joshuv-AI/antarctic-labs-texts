Structure groupoids of quiver-theoretic Yang–Baxter maps
Davide Ferri and Youichi Shibukawa

Abstract
. Solutions to the quiver-theoretic quantum Yang–Baxter equation are associated with structure categories and structure groupoids. We prove that the structure groupoids of involutive non-degenerate solutions are Garside. This generalises a well-known result about the structure groups of set-theoretic solutions, due to Chouraqui. We also construct involutive non-degenerate solutions from suitable presented categories. We then investigate the case of solutions of principal homogeneous type. Finally, we present some examples of this new class of Garside groupoids.



1.
Introduction

The Yang–Baxter Equation (YBE), germinated from the works of Yang
[
36
]
and Baxter
[
3
,
4
]
, has been studied for a long time in mathematical physics and representation theory, whereas its set-theoretic variant (proposed by Drinfeld
[
18
]
) has recently grown into a major field of research in algebra. A solution to the set-theoretic (quantum) Yang–Baxter equation, called a
set-theoretic Yang–Baxter map
, is a
braided set
—i.e., the datum of a set
X
and of a map
\sigma\colon X\times X\to X\times X
satisfying the
braid relation

(\sigma\times\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)(\sigma\times\mathrm{id})=(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma)(\sigma\times\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\times\sigma).

A generalisation is provided by the
quiver-theoretic quantum Yang–Baxter Equation (quiver-theoretic YBE). A solution to the quiver-theoretic YBE, called a
quiver-theoretic Yang–Baxter map
(
quiver-theoretic YBM
, or simply
YBM
), is a
braided quiver
—i.e., the datum of a quiver
{\mathscr{A}}
over a set of vertices
\Lambda
, and of a morphism
\sigma\colon{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}\to{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}
of quivers over
\Lambda
satisfying the braid relation

(\sigma\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes\sigma)(\sigma\otimes\mathrm{id})=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\sigma)(\sigma\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes\sigma)

in the monoidal category
\mathsf{Quiv}_{\Lambda}
of quivers over
\Lambda
. Here
\otimes
denotes the tensor product of quivers over
\Lambda
(see Definition
2.4
), and
{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}
is understood as the quiver
\Path_{2}({\mathscr{A}})
consisting of paths of length
2
on
{\mathscr{A}}
. A thorough study of the quiver-theoretic YBE was initiated by Andruskiewitsch
[
1
]
.

A set-theoretic YBM may be regarded as a quiver-theoretic YBM on a quiver
{\mathscr{A}}
with a single vertex
\lambda
, and with loops on
\lambda
being in a 1:1 correspondence with the elements of
X
. Much of the ‘‘one-vertex’’ theory of the set-theoretic YBE generalises almost
verbatim
to the ‘‘multiple-vertices’’ situation of the quiver-theoretic YBE, as we shall see.

A weaker version of the quiver-theoretic YBE, called the
set-theoretic dynamical Yang--Baxter equation
1
1

1

The original DYBE, in the context of Lie algebras, was introduced in mathematical physics by Gervais and Neveu
[
22
]
, then developed by Felder
[
20
]
, Etingof and Schiffmann
[
19
]
.
(
set-theoretic DYBE
), was first introduced in the framework of
dynamical sets

[
33
]
. Later, a connection between dynamical sets and quivers was established by Matsumoto and Shimizu
[
29
]
, although the core idea was already sketched by Matsumoto in
[
28
, §5]
. This provides further motivation for the study of the quiver-theoretic YBE.

Another motivation for studying the quiver-theoretic YBMs resides in the fact that they are
partial solutions
to the set-theoretic YBE. These have already raised the interest of other researchers, such as Chouraqui
[
12
,
13
]
.

Solutions to the quiver-theoretic YBE are associated with
structure categories
and
structure groupoids
, which are the main subjects of this paper.

The structure monoid
(resp. structure group) of a set-theoretic YBM
\sigma
on a set
X
is defined as the monoid
(resp. the group) generated by
X
modulo the relations

xy\sim x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\text{ for all }x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in X\text{ satisfying }(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\sigma(x,y).

Analogously, the structure category (resp. structure groupoid) of a YBM
\sigma
on a quiver
{\mathscr{A}}
is defined as the category (resp. the groupoid) generated by
{\mathscr{A}}
modulo the relations

x|y\sim\sigma(x|y)\text{ for all }x|y\in\Path_{2}({\mathscr{A}}).

More details and notations about presented categories and groupoids will be recalled later in §
2
.

Our viewpoint on the structure groupoid is preeminently Garside-theoretic. Garside theory is an approach to normal forms and the word problem in algebraic structures; it emerged from the work of Garside on braid groups
[
21
]
, and has been successfully applied to many other algebraic objects. Its interplay with the quiver-theoretic YBE is investigated here for the first time, although the main ideas were anticipated in the work of Dehornoy
et al.

[
16
]
.
Our results allow us to construct a class of examples of Garside groupoids. These objects commonly arise both in algebra and in geometry (see for instance
[
30
]
, for a discussion of Garside groupoids arising from hyperplane arrangements).

In the final section of this paper, we shall investigate the special case of
solutions of principal homogeneous type
. We shall prove in Corollary
8.15
that a
braiding
on a groupoid of pairs
{\mathscr{G}}
with a distinguished vertex is, in fact, tantamount to a group structure on the set
\Obj({\mathscr{G}})
of vertices of
{\mathscr{G}}
. This builds on an interpretation of
heaps
, defined by Prüfer
[
31
]
and Baer
[
2
]
, as an ‘‘affine notion’’ of groups; see also
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
35
]
. We exploit this class of YBMs to construct examples of Garside groupoids.

We hope that our viewpoint helps further advance Garside theory, by providing a class of concrete examples to work with.

1.1.
Scope and structure of the paper

In this paper, we prove that the structure groupoid of an involutive non-degenerate quiver-theoretic YBM is Garside. The approach we adopt is derived from
[
16
]
: we merge Chouraqui’s method with Dehornoy
et al.
’s investigation of weak RC-systems
[
16
, §XIV.2]
. Furthermore, we exploit our result to present some new examples of Garside groupoids.

The paper is structured as follows:

§
2

Preliminaries.
Here we survey some foundational concepts in the theory of quivers, presented categories, and Garside theory, and we set up our notations. We do this for making this paper self-contained. However, the entire content of this section can be found in the monograph
[
16
]
, which is going to be one of our main references.

§
3

Weak RC-systems and other cyclic systems.
Here we describe the notion of a weak RC-system, a weak LC-system and a weak RLC-system, and recall some useful results. We give the definition of the
structure category
of a weak RC-system, and prove that it is Garside under some assumptions. This section is mainly drawn from
[
16
, §XIV.2]
, although we fix some details.

§
4

Quiver-theoretic YBMs and their structure categories.
We recall the definition of quiver-theoretic YBMs, and the
involutivity
and
non-degeneracy
conditions. Then, we define the structure category of a YBM.

§
5

The interplay between weak RC-systems and YBMs.
In this section, from every left-non-degenerate involutive YBM, we shall construct a left-non-degenerate weak RC-system. As a converse connection, we shall construct YBMs from a suitable class of presented categories, whose relations contain the RC-law.

§
6

On the Garsideness of the structure category of YBMs.
We prove that, if
\sigma
is a non-degenerate involutive YBM, its structure category
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
is isomorphic to the structure category of a suitable weak RC-system. Under these assumptions, we prove that
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
is perfect Garside, and we give a description of the Garside family. Moreover, the structure groupoid
{\mathscr{G}}(\sigma)
of
\sigma
is the same as the enveloping groupoid of
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
, and
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
is embedded in
{\mathscr{G}}(\sigma)
, thus making
{\mathscr{G}}(\sigma)
into a Garside groupoid.

§
7

Examples of solutions and their structure categories.
Making use of the construction from §
5.2
, we present several examples of YBMs and Garside categories.

§
8

Solutions of principal homogeneous type.
We recall the definition of solutions of principal homogeneous (PH) type
[
29
,
34
]
. We moreover recall the definition of braided groupoids, of principal homogeneous groupoids, and prove that the category of braided principal homogeneous groupoids with a distinguished vertex is equivalent to the category of groups. We finally describe some examples of structure groupoids of involutive non-degenerate YBMs of principal homogeneous type, which turn out, by the previous discourse, to be examples of Garside groupoids.

2.1.
Quivers

A quiver is a directed multigraph with loops. More formally:

Definition 2.1
.

A
quiver
Q
over
\Lambda
is the datum of a set
\Lambda\neq\emptyset
, a set
Q
, and two set-theoretic functions
\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\colon Q\to\Lambda
, respectively called the
source
and
target
maps. The elements of
\Lambda
are called
vertices
, and the elements of
Q
are called
edges
or
arrows
.

We shall henceforth say that ‘‘
Q
is a quiver’’, implying that the data of
\Lambda
and of the maps
\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}
are understood. When we want to highlight that
\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}
are the source and target maps of a certain quiver
Q
, we write
\mathfrak{s}_{Q},\mathfrak{t}_{Q}
instead. We denote by
\Obj(Q)=\Lambda
the set of vertices of
Q
.

A
morphism

f\colon Q\to Q^{\prime}

of quivers over
\Lambda
is a set-theoretic map from
Q
to
Q^{\prime}
that preserves the sources and the targets: namely, such that
\mathfrak{s}_{Q^{\prime}}(f(x))=\mathfrak{s}_{Q}(x)
and
\mathfrak{t}_{Q^{\prime}}(f(x))=\mathfrak{t}_{Q}(x)
for all
x\in Q
.
2
2

2

The reader may have encountered a different definition, which is strictly milder, of morphisms between quivers that are allowed to have different sets of vertices. These are the
weak morphisms of quivers
; see Definition
8.1
.
The category of quivers over
\Lambda
is denoted by
\mathsf{Quiv}_{\Lambda}
.

Notation 2.2
.

Let
Q
be a quiver as above. If
x\in Q
has
\mathfrak{s}(x)=\lambda
and
\mathfrak{t}(x)=\mu
, we say that
x
is an arrow from
\lambda
to
\mu
, and we write
x\colon\lambda\to\mu
. The set of arrows with source
\lambda
and target
\mu
is denoted by
Q(\lambda,\mu)
. The set of arrows with source
\lambda
, and any possible target, is denoted by
Q(\lambda,\Lambda)
. Analogously,
Q(\Lambda,\mu)
denotes the set of arrows with target
\mu
, and any possible source.

Given a quiver
Q
, it is natural to consider
paths
of arrows in
Q
. These have, in turn, a quiver structure.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
Q
be a quiver over
\Lambda
. A
path of length
n
in
Q
(
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
) is a sequence
x_{1}|\dots|x_{n}
of elements
x_{i}\in Q
, where the target of each
x_{i}
equals the source of
x_{i+1}
(we say that such a sequence is
composable
). A
path of length
0
is tantamount to specifying a vertex
\lambda
, and it is also called the
empty path on
\lambda
. We denote it by
\varepsilon_{\lambda}
.

The source of
x_{1}|\dots|x_{n}
is, by definition,
\mathfrak{s}(x_{1})
, and the target is by definition
\mathfrak{t}(x_{n})
. With these source and target maps, the set
\Path_{n}(Q)
of paths of length
n
in
Q
is, in turn, a quiver over
\Lambda
.

We denote by
\Path(Q)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Path_{n}(Q)
the set of all paths in
Q
, of any possible length. This is also a quiver over
\Lambda
,
with
\mathfrak{s}(x_{1}|\dots|x_{n})=\mathfrak{s}(x_{1})
,
\mathfrak{t}(x_{1}|\dots|x_{n})=\mathfrak{t}(x_{n})
, and
\mathfrak{s}(\varepsilon_{\lambda})=\mathfrak{t}(\varepsilon_{\lambda})=\lambda
. Notice that, if we define the composition of two paths
x_{1}|\dots|x_{n}
and
y_{1}|\dots|y_{m}
as the usual concatenation
x_{1}|\dots|x_{n}|y_{1}|\dots|y_{m}
whenever
\mathfrak{t}(x_{n})=\mathfrak{s}(y_{1})
, then
\Path(Q)
becomes a category with set of objects
\Lambda
. For all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, the empty path
\varepsilon_{\lambda}
plays the role of the identity on
\lambda
.

Notice that
\Path(Q)
has a
graded structure
: the composition of a path of length
n
with a path of length
m
lies in
\Path_{n+m}(Q)
.

The category
\mathsf{Quiv}_{\Lambda}
of quivers over
\Lambda
is monoidal, with a monoidal product defined as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
Q,Q^{\prime}
be quivers over
\Lambda
. The tensor product
Q\otimes Q^{\prime}
is the quiver over
\Lambda
defined as follows:

i.

As a set,
Q\otimes Q^{\prime}
is the subset of
Q\times Q^{\prime}
consisting of the pairs
(x,y)
with
\mathfrak{t}_{Q}(x)=\mathfrak{s}_{Q^{\prime}}(y)
.

ii.

The source of
(x,y)
is defined to be
\mathfrak{s}_{Q}(x)
, and the target is defined to be
\mathfrak{t}_{Q^{\prime}}(y)
.

It is easy to verify (see
[
29
]
) that
Q\otimes(Q^{\prime}\otimes Q^{\prime\prime})
and
(Q\otimes Q^{\prime})\otimes Q^{\prime\prime}
are isomorphic. Therefore, it makes sense to define the tensor power
Q^{\otimes n}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Notice that
Q\otimes Q
is naturally identified with
\Path_{2}(Q)
. Analogously,
Q^{\otimes n}
is naturally identified with
\Path_{n}(Q)
.

Definition 2.6
.

The
opposite
of a quiver
Q
is the quiver
\bar{Q}
, with same vertices and reverted arrows. The
double

\Double(Q)
of a quiver
Q
over
\Lambda
is the quiver having
\Lambda
as a set of vertices and, as a set of arrows, the disjoint union
Q\sqcup\bar{Q}
.

2.2.
Generalities about categories

Here we report, for clarity, some standard definitions and results about categories. The reader may find the fundamentals in category theory covered in any textbook, such as
[

26

In the rest of this paper, we shall consistently consider categories as

Convention 2.7
.

When we say that

{\mathscr{C}}
, and we write

{\mathscr{C}}
—and

Notation 2.8
.

For a category

, we adopt the same conventions as in Notation

. Let

be the class of objects of
{\mathscr{C}}
and let

for an arrow

from
\lambda
to
\mu
, and we say that
\lambda
is the

is the
target
of

. The notations
{\mathscr{C}}(\lambda,\mu),{\mathscr{C}}(\lambda,\Lambda),{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda,\mu)
also have analogous meaning to those in Notation
2.2
. We denote by

the identity of the object

, and the subscript will be omitted whenever

is clear from the context.

Convention 2.9
.

Unless otherwise specified, here

denotes the composition of maps from
right to left
, while, from now on, the composition without the symbol ‘
\circ

For the following definitions and lemmas, we refer to

[

Definition 2.10
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a category, and let

We say that
x

. We denote the factoriality relation by

.

A

(resp.

) of

is an arrow
xu=yv
(resp.
ux=vy
) for some suitable
u,v\in{\mathscr{C}}

Given

.

We say that a category

admits

(resp.

.

A category

(resp.

(resp.

Suppose

(resp.

An element

2.3.
Presented categories and presented groupoids

We shall use the notion of
presented categories
extensively, in the rest of the paper. This is a way of describing categories with generators and relations, analogously to group presentations, monoid presentations, etc. However, since (unlike the case of groups) we do not have the notion of ‘‘a quotient of a category by a subcategory’’, some more care is needed in this context. Indeed, quotients of categories are defined with respect to
congruence relations
: this is analogous to quotients of monoids or semigroups. Our discourse closely follows
[
16
, Chapter II]
.

Definition 2.18
.

A
precategory

{\mathscr{P}}
is just the datum of objects and arrows. We do not require the existence of identities, nor the existence of a binary composition. A
small
precategory, endowed with its source and target maps, is a quiver.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a category. A
subfamily

{\mathscr{S}}
of
{\mathscr{C}}
is a precategory with same objects as
{\mathscr{C}}
, and arrows whose class is a subclass of the arrows of
{\mathscr{C}}
. Given a subfamily
{\mathscr{S}}
of
{\mathscr{C}}
, it makes sense to consider the
generated subcategory
—that is, the smallest subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}
including
{\mathscr{S}}
.

Definition 2.20
.

A
relation
on a category
{\mathscr{C}}
is a class of pairs
(x,y)
of elements
x,y\in{\mathscr{C}}
with
\mathfrak{s}(x)=\mathfrak{s}(y)
and
\mathfrak{t}(x)=\mathfrak{t}(y)
.

Typically, we will be interested in relations on a path category
\Path({\mathscr{P}})
on a given precategory
{\mathscr{P}}
. These will be the relations of our presentation.

We do not care so much about working with classes, as the rest of the paper will only involve
small categories
. For this reason, we prefer to assume directly, from now on, that our categories and precategories are small, although most of definitions and results may be generalised.

Definition 2.21
.

An equivalence relation
\equiv
on a (small) category
{\mathscr{C}}
is called a
congruence
if it is compatible with compositions: i.e., if
x_{1}\equiv x_{2}
and
y_{1}\equiv y_{2}
imply
x_{1}y_{1}\equiv x_{2}y_{2}
whenever the composition is defined.

Lemma 2.22

(
[
16
, Lemma II.1.37]
)
.

Let
R
be a relation on
{\mathscr{C}}
. There exists a unique minimal congruence
\equiv^{+}_{R}
on
{\mathscr{C}}
such that
x\equiv^{+}_{R}y
for all
(x,y)
in
R
. This is said to be the congruence generated by
R
.

Remark 2.23
.

For the path category
\Path({\mathscr{P}})
on a precategory
{\mathscr{P}}

\equiv^{+}_{R}
can be described in an alternative way which is often practically useful

2.4.
Noetherianity

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a category. A binary relation
\prec

{\mathscr{C}}
is

if every nonempty subfamily
{\mathscr{S}}
of
{\mathscr{C}}
has a
\prec
-minimal element—i.e., an element
x\in{\mathscr{S}}
such that, if
y\prec x
, then
y\notin{\mathscr{S}}
.

Definition 2.32
.

A category is left-Noetherian (resp. right-Noetherian) if the relation
\prec_{L}
(resp.
\prec_{R}
) is well-founded.
Here,
x\prec_{L}y
(resp.
x\prec_{R}y
) means that
xy^{\prime}=y
(resp.
y^{\prime}x=y
) for some
y^{\prime}
that is not invertible.

A category is Noetherian if the relation of proper factoriality
\subset
is well-founded.
Here,
x\subset y
means that
y^{\prime}xy^{\prime\prime}=y
and at least one of
y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}
is non-invertible.

Remark 2.33
.

If a left-cancellative category is both left- and right-Noetherian, then it is also Noetherian
[
16
, Proposition II.2.29]
. The proof relies on a characterisation of right-Noetherianity via increasing sequences of left-divisibility relations
[
16
, Proposition II.2.28]
, and this does not work without assuming left-cancellativity
[
16
, Exercise II.12]
.

A relation
x_{1}|\dots|x_{n}\sim y_{1}|\dots|y_{m}
is called
homogeneous
if
n=m
, and a
homogeneous presentation
is a presentation in which all relations are homogeneous. We refer to
[
16
, Propositions II.2.32 and II.2.33]
for the proof of the following:

Proposition 2.34
.

If a category
{\mathscr{C}}
admits a homogeneous presentation
{\mathscr{C}}\cong\langle{\mathscr{P}}\mid R\rangle^{+}

3.2.
Completions

If a weak RC-system
(Q,\star)
does not have a unit family (or possibly even if it already has one), we may extend

[
16

Let

, where
Q^{\prime}
is another precategory, whence we are going to pick up our additional units. For all objects
\lambda\in\Obj{Q}
we choose
\epsilon_{\lambda}\in Q^{\prime}(\lambda,\lambda)

Q^{\sharp}(\lambda,\lambda)=Q(\lambda,\lambda)\cup\{\epsilon_{\lambda}\}

\epsilon_{\lambda}
need not be in

We define

then modify the operation
\star

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathfrak{s}(y)}\star^{\sharp}y=y,

\displaystyle x\star^{\sharp}\epsilon_{\mathfrak{s}(x)}=\epsilon_{\mathfrak{t}(x)},

\displaystyle x\star^{\sharp}x=\epsilon_{\mathfrak{t}(x)},

\displaystyle x\star^{\sharp}y=x\star y\;\text{in all the remaining cases.}

on a weak RC-system. The loops

that we add to

There is a privileged way of choosing such an extension; namely, the case in which we pick

for all

by Dehornoy
et al.

[

]

5.

, and
({\mathscr{A}},\bullet)
is a left-non-degenerate weak co-RC-system.

Proof.

On the opposite quiver
\bar{{\mathscr{A}}}
, the morphism
\bar{\sigma}\colon\bar{{\mathscr{A}}}^{\otimes 2}\to\bar{{\mathscr{A}}}^{\otimes 2}
defined by

\bar{x}|\bar{z}\mapsto\overline{z\leftharpoonup x}|\overline{z\rightharpoonup x}

(where
\bar{\cdot}
denotes the reverse of the arrows in
{\mathscr{A}}
) is clearly a YBM; and
\overline{x\bullet y}=\bar{x}\,\bar{\star}\,\bar{y}
where the operation
\bar{\star}
is defined as in Proposition
5.1
, but with respect to the YBM
\bar{\sigma}
. One has that
({\mathscr{A}},\bullet)
is a left-non-degenerate weak co-RC-system if and only if
(\bar{{\mathscr{A}}},\bar{\star})
is a left-non-degenerate weak RC-system. The conclusion, then, follows from Proposition
5.1
.

∎

In force of the previous propositions, the proof of the following result becomes a straightforward verification:

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\sigma
be an involutive non-degenerate YBM on a quiver
{\mathscr{A}}
.
If we define
x\;\tilde{\star}\;y=y\bullet x
as in Proposition
5.2
,
then
({\mathscr{A}},\star,\tilde{\star})
is a weak RLC-system.

5.2.
YBMs from a class of presented categories

We now prove that categories with a suitable presentation produce involutive non-degenerate quiver-theoretic YBMs. This is analogous to Chouraqui’s result
[
11
, Theorem 1 (
ii
)]
, which allows to retrieve a YBM from every Garside group with a suitable presentation. However, unlike
[
11
, Theorem 1 (
ii
)]
, we do not assume the existence of a Garside structure: this will follow
a posteriori
from Theorem
6.4

(see also
[
16
, Proposition XIII.2.34]
).

Suppose that a category
{\mathscr{C}}
has a presentation
{\mathscr{C}}=\langle{\mathscr{A}}\mid R\rangle^{+}
, with
\Obj({\mathscr{A}})=\Lambda
. We assume
relations to be ordered (
q\sim r
and
r\sim q
are distinct relations),
and for a set of relations
R
, we let
R^{op}
be the set
\{r\sim q\mid q\sim r\in R\}
.
The set of redundant relations in
R
is defined as
R\cap R^{op}
.
We assume the following conditions:

i
.

Every relation in
R
has the form
a|v\sim b|w
(quadratic relations with
a,b,v,w\in{\mathscr{A}}
). If
x\sim y,\,x\sim y^{\prime}\in R
then
y=y^{\prime}
; if
x\sim y,\,x^{\prime}\sim y\in R
then
x=x^{\prime}
; and if
x\sim y,\,y^{\prime}\sim x\in R
, then
y=y^{\prime}
.

ii
.

For all
a,b\in{\mathscr{A}}
, with
\mathfrak{s}(a)=\mathfrak{s}(b)
and
a\neq b
, there exists a unique relation
a|v\sim b|w
or
b|w\sim a|v
in
R
.

ii
′
.

For all
a,b\in{\mathscr{A}}
, with
\mathfrak{t}(a)=\mathfrak{t}(b)
and
a\neq b
, there exists a unique relation
v|a\sim w|b
or
w|b\sim v|a
in
R
.

iii
.

For all
a\in{\mathscr{A}}
, there exists a unique
z_{a}\in{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(a),\Lambda)
such that:

iii
a.

if
v\in{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(a),\Lambda)\smallsetminus\{z_{a}\}
, then there exist
b\in{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{s}(a),\Lambda)\smallsetminus\{a\}
and
w\in{\mathscr{A}}
satisfying
(a|v\sim b|w)\in R\cup R^{op}
;

iii
b.

if
(a|z_{a}\sim b|w)\in R\cup R^{op}
for some
b,w\in{\mathscr{A}}
, then
b=a
and
w=z_{a}
.

iii
′
.

For all
a\in{\mathscr{A}}
, there exists a unique
z^{a}\in{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{s}(a))
such that:

iii
′
a.

if
v\in{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{s}(a))\smallsetminus\{z^{a}\}
, then there exist
b\in{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(a))\smallsetminus\{a\}
and
w\in{\mathscr{A}}
satisfying
(v|a\sim w|b)\in R\cup R^{op}
;

iii
′
b.

if
(z^{a}|a\sim w|b)\in R\cup R^{op}
for some
b,w\in{\mathscr{A}}
, then
b=a
and
w=z^{a}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

In the above hypotheses, the following conditions hold.

iv
.

If
(a|v\sim a|w)\in R
for some
a,v,w\in{\mathscr{A}}
, then
v=w=z_{a}
.

iv
′
.

If
(v|a\sim w|a)\in R
for some
a,v,w\in{\mathscr{A}}
, then
v=w=z^{a}
.

Proof.

We only prove
iv
, since the proof of
iv
′
is dual. If
(a|v\sim a|w)\in R
and
v\neq z_{a}
, then by
iii
a there exist
b\neq a
and
w^{\prime}
such that
a|v\sim b|w^{\prime}
; but by
i
the path
a|v
can only appear in one relation, thus we would have
a|w=b|w^{\prime}
, contradicting
b\neq a
. Hence
v=z_{a}
.
It follows from
iii
b and
(a|z_{a}\sim a|w)\in R
that
w=z_{a}
, which implies
iv
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Observe that the conditions
i
–
iii
′
do not imply that relations of the form
a|z_{a}\sim a|z_{a}
exist for all
a
.

The condition
ii
implies, for all
a\in{\mathscr{A}}
, the existence of a map

a\star\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\colon{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{s}(a),\Lambda)\smallsetminus\{a\}\to{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(a),\Lambda),\quad b\mapsto a\star b,

such that
(a|a\star b\sim b|w)\in R
for some
w
. We moreover assume:

v
.

The operation
\star
satisfies, for all pairwise distinct
a,b,c\in{\mathscr{A}}
,
\mathfrak{s}(a)=\mathfrak{s}(b)=\mathfrak{s}(c)
, the RC-law

(a\star b)\star(a\star c)=(b\star a)\star(b\star c).

Because of the conditions
i
and
iii
, the map
a\star\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}
defined on
{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{s}(a),\Lambda)\smallsetminus\{a\}
can be extended to a bijection
a\star^{\prime}\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\colon{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{s}(a),\Lambda)\to{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(a),\Lambda)
,
b\mapsto a\star^{\prime}b
, defined by

a\star^{\prime}b=\begin{cases}a\star b\text{ if }a\neq b,\\
z_{a}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Dually, using
i
,
ii
′
and
iii
′
, every
a\in{\mathscr{A}}
yields a map
a\bullet\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\colon{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(a))\smallsetminus\{a\}\to{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{s}(a))

satisfying
(a\bullet b)|a\sim w|b
for
b\in{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(a))\smallsetminus\{a\}
, which can be extended to a bijection
a\bullet^{\prime}\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\colon{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(a))\to{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{s}(a))
defined by

a\bullet^{\prime}b=\begin{cases}a\bullet b\text{ if }a\neq b,\\
z^{a}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The rest of this section is devoted to proving the following result, and to giving some examples of its application.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}=\langle{\mathscr{A}}\mid R\rangle^{+}
be a category presentation satisfying the above
conditions i–v, and let
\star^{\prime}
be defined as above. Then, the map

\sigma\colon{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}\to{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}
,
\sigma(a|b)=(a\rightharpoonup b)|(a\leftharpoonup b)
, defined by

a\rightharpoonup b=c\text{ if and only if }b=a\star^{\prime}c,\quad a\leftharpoonup b=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a

is an involutive non-degenerate YBM on
{\mathscr{A}}
, whose structure category is
{\mathscr{C}}
.

In particular, Theorem
5.6
will imply that such a category
{\mathscr{C}}
is perfect Garside, with Garside family as in Propositions
6.8
and
6.9
.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
5.6

Before proving the theorem, we need some preliminary results. The first one is a converse to Proposition
5.1
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
({\mathscr{A}},\star^{\prime})
be a left-non-degenerate weak RC-system, and let

a\rightharpoonup b=c\text{ if and only if }b=a\star^{\prime}c,\quad a\leftharpoonup b=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a.

Then,
\sigma\colon a|b\mapsto(a\rightharpoonup b)|(a\leftharpoonup b)
is a YBM on
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Proof.

We check (
YB1
), (
YB2
), and (
YB3
), for a path
a|b|c\in{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}\otimes{\mathscr{A}}
, by direct computation. Let
s=(a\rightharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup((a\leftharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup c)
and
t=a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c)
be the left- and right-hand side of (
YB1
), respectively. Then,
(a\leftharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup c=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}s
, thus
((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)\rightharpoonup c=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}s
, whence

c=((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)\star^{\prime}((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}s),

which by the RC-law is equal to
(a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b))\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}s)=b\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}s)
. Therefore,
s=a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c)=t
as desired.

Let now
s^{\prime}=(a\leftharpoonup b)\leftharpoonup c
and
t^{\prime}=(a\leftharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\leftharpoonup(b\leftharpoonup c)
be the left- and right-hand side of (
YB3
), respectively. Then

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\big((a\leftharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\rightharpoonup(b\leftharpoonup c)\big)\star^{\prime}(a\leftharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))

\displaystyle=\Big(\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\big)\rightharpoonup((b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b)\Big)\star^{\prime}\Big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\Big).

From the RC-law, one has

\displaystyle(b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b

\displaystyle=\Big(a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\Big)\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b))

\displaystyle=\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\big)\star^{\prime}\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)\big),

whence

\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\big)\rightharpoonup((b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b)=(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b),

and consequently

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Big(\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\big)\rightharpoonup((b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b)\Big)\star^{\prime}\Big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)\Big)\star^{\prime}\Big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\Big)

\displaystyle=\big((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\big)\star^{\prime}((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a).

From the proof of (
YB1
), we had

((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)\rightharpoonup c=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}s=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c)),

which we can plug into the previous expression of
t^{\prime}
, thus getting

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Big(((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)\rightharpoonup c\Big)\star^{\prime}((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)

\displaystyle=((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)\leftharpoonup c

\displaystyle=(a\leftharpoonup b)\leftharpoonup c

\displaystyle=s^{\prime},

and this concludes the proof of (
YB3
).

Finally, we let
s^{\prime\prime}=(a\rightharpoonup b)\leftharpoonup((a\leftharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup c)
and
t^{\prime\prime}=(a\leftharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\rightharpoonup(b\leftharpoonup c)
be the left- and right-hand side of (
YB2
), respectively.
Then, by using (
YB1
),

\displaystyle s^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\big((a\rightharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup((a\leftharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup c)\big)\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)

\displaystyle=(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b),

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\big)\rightharpoonup((b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b),

and from the RC-law

\displaystyle\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a\big)\star^{\prime}\big((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)\big)

\displaystyle=\big(a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\big)\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b))

\displaystyle=(b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=((a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}a)\rightharpoonup((b\rightharpoonup c)\star^{\prime}b)

\displaystyle=(a\rightharpoonup(b\rightharpoonup c))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)

\displaystyle=s^{\prime\prime},

as desired.
∎

We now prove the first part of Theorem
5.6
: namely, that
\sigma
is an involutive left-non-degenerate YBM.

It is clear that
\sigma
would be left-non-degenerate, because
a\star^{\prime}\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}
is bijective for all
a\in{\mathscr{A}}
. Involutivity is also easy (using (
I1
) and (
I2
)):
(a\rightharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup(a\leftharpoonup b)
is by definition the unique
c
such that
(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}c=a\leftharpoonup b=(a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a
, whence
c=a
. From (
I1
), one also obtains (
I2
): because
(a\rightharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup(a\leftharpoonup b)=a
, we get
(a\rightharpoonup b)\leftharpoonup(a\leftharpoonup b)=((a\rightharpoonup b)\rightharpoonup(a\leftharpoonup b))\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)=a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b)=b
.

We now need to prove the YBE for
\sigma
: we do so by proving that
({\mathscr{A}},\star^{\prime})
is a left-non-degenerate weak RC-system, thus
\sigma
is a YBM by Proposition
5.7
. We first need a couple of lemmas.

Lemma 5.8
.

In the hypotheses of Theorem
5.6
, one has
(a\star^{\prime}b)\bullet^{\prime}(b\star^{\prime}a)=a
for all
a,b\in{\mathscr{A}}
,
\mathfrak{s}(a)=\mathfrak{s}(b)
.

Proof.

We first suppose
a=b
. Because of the conditions
iii
and
iii
′
,
there is no
b\in{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(z_{a}))\smallsetminus\{z_{a}\}
such that
z_{a}\bullet b=a
. Because
z_{a}\bullet^{\prime}\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\colon{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(z_{a}))\to{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{s}(z_{a}))
is bijective, this implies
z_{a}\bullet^{\prime}z_{a}=a
. Since
z_{a}=a\star^{\prime}a
, we get the desired formula.

We now prove the formula when
a\neq b
. In this case,
(a\star^{\prime}b)\bullet^{\prime}(b\star^{\prime}a)=(a\star b)\bullet^{\prime}(b\star a)
. From the condition
iv
′
, one has
a\star b\neq b\star a
, and hence
(a\star b)\bullet^{\prime}(b\star a)=(a\star b)\bullet(b\star a)
, which equals
a
by condition
ii
′
.
∎

The proof of Lemma
5.8
never uses the condition
v
. Since the set of axioms
i
–
iii
′
is self-dual, the following result is also true:

Lemma 5.9

(dual of Lemma
5.8
)
.

In the hypotheses of Theorem
5.6
, one has
(a\bullet^{\prime}b)\star^{\prime}(b\bullet^{\prime}a)=a
for all
a,b\in A
,
\mathfrak{t}(a)=\mathfrak{t}(b)
.

Corollary 5.10
.

One has
b\bullet^{\prime}(a\leftharpoonup b)=a
for all
a,b\in{\mathscr{A}}
,
\mathfrak{t}(a)=\mathfrak{s}(b)
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
5.8
, we compute:

\displaystyle b\bullet^{\prime}(a\leftharpoonup b)

\displaystyle=b\bullet^{\prime}((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)

\displaystyle=(a\star^{\prime}(a\rightharpoonup b))\bullet^{\prime}((a\rightharpoonup b)\star^{\prime}a)

\displaystyle=a.

∎

Lemma 5.11
.

In the hypotheses of Theorem
5.6
,
z_{a\star b}=z_{b\star a}
implies
a\star b=b\star a
.

Proof.

One has

\displaystyle a\star b

\displaystyle{=}((a\star b)\star^{\prime}(a\star b))\bullet^{\prime}((a\star b)\star^{\prime}(a\star b))

\displaystyle=z_{a\star b}\bullet^{\prime}z_{a\star b}

\displaystyle=z_{b\star a}\bullet^{\prime}z_{b\star a}

\displaystyle=((b\star a)\star^{\prime}(b\star a))\bullet^{\prime}((b\star a)\star^{\prime}(b\star a))

\displaystyle{=}b\star a,

where we use Lemma
5.8
in each equality marked with
(\dagger)
.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

One has
(a\star b)\star z_{a}=z_{b\star a}
for all
a\neq b
with the same source.

Proof.

From the condition
ii
one has
{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(a\star b),\Lambda)={\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(b\star a),\Lambda)
, hence this set can be described in two equivalent ways:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(a\star b),\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\Big\{(a\star b)\star(a\star c),\;(a\star b)\star z_{a}\;\Big|\;c\in{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{s}(a),\Lambda)\smallsetminus\{a,b\}\Big\}\sqcup\{z_{a\star b}\}

\displaystyle={\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{t}(b\star a),\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\Big\{(b\star a)\star(b\star c),\;(b\star a)\star z_{b}\;\Big|\;c\in{\mathscr{A}}(\mathfrak{s}(a),\Lambda)\smallsetminus\{a,b\}\Big\}\sqcup\{z_{b\star a}\}.

Now, the condition
v
implies
(b\star a)\star(b\star c)=(a\star b)\star(a\star c)
; thus, for the two above sets to be equal, one either has

(9)

(a\star b)\star z_{a}=(b\star a)\star z_{b}\text{ and }z_{a\star b}=z_{b\star a},

or

(10)

(a\star b)\star z_{a}=z_{b\star a}\text{ and }z_{a\star b}=(b\star a)\star z_{b}.

The case (
9
) cannot occur: from Lemma
5.11
, if
z_{a\star b}=z_{b\star a}
then
a\star b=b\star a
, but then the relation
(a|(a\star b)\sim b|(b\star a))=(a|(a\star b)\sim b|(a\star b))
lies in
R
, which by
iv
′
would imply
a=b
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, one must have (
10
), the desired conclusion.
∎

We now prove that
({\mathscr{A}},\star^{\prime})
is a left-non-degenerate weak RC-system. The RC-law

(a\star^{\prime}b)\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}c)=(b\star^{\prime}a)\star^{\prime}(b\star^{\prime}c),

for
a,b,c
pairwise distinct, follows from the RC-law for
\star
. If
a=b
, then the RC-law holds trivially. If
a=c\neq b
, then

(a\star^{\prime}b)\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}c)=(a\star b)\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}a)=(a\star b)\star^{\prime}z_{a},

while, on the other hand,

(b\star^{\prime}a)\star^{\prime}(b\star^{\prime}c)=(b\star a)\star^{\prime}(b\star a)=z_{b\star a},

and these two are equal from Lemma
5.12
.

Finally, in case
b=c\neq a
, one has again from Lemma
5.12

\displaystyle(a\star^{\prime}b)\star^{\prime}(a\star^{\prime}c)

\displaystyle=(a\star b)\star^{\prime}(a\star b)

\displaystyle=z_{a\star b}

\displaystyle=(b\star a)\star^{\prime}z_{b}

\displaystyle=(b\star a)\star^{\prime}(b\star^{\prime}b)

\displaystyle=(b\star^{\prime}a)\star^{\prime}(b\star^{\prime}c),

as desired. Thus
({\mathscr{A}},\star^{\prime})
is a weak RC-system, and it is clearly left-non-degenerate. This concludes the proof that
\sigma
is an involutive left-non-degenerate YBM on
{\mathscr{A}}
.

We now observe that
\sigma
is also right-non-degenerate.
Indeed, suppose that
a,a^{\prime},b\in{\mathscr{A}}
(
\mathfrak{t}(a)=\mathfrak{t}(a^{\prime})=\mathfrak{s}(b)
) satisfy

a\leftharpoonup b=a^{\prime}\leftharpoonup b
. Then

a=b\bullet^{\prime}(a\leftharpoonup b)=b\bullet^{\prime}(a^{\prime}\leftharpoonup b)=a^{\prime},

from Corollary
5.10
. This proves that
\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\leftharpoonup b\colon{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{s}(b))\to{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(b))
is injective. We now show the surjectivity. It follows from the definition of
\rightharpoonup
and from Lemma
5.9
that

(b\bullet^{\prime}v)\rightharpoonup b=v\bullet^{\prime}b
for any
v\in{\mathscr{A}}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{t}(b))
, and

(b\bullet^{\prime}v)\leftharpoonup b=(v\bullet^{\prime}b)\star^{\prime}(b\bullet^{\prime}v)=v

as a consequence of Lemma
5.9
. Thus

v
is in the image of
\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}}\leftharpoonup b
. This concludes the proof that
\sigma
is non-degenerate.

We finally prove that
{\mathscr{C}}
is the structure category of
\sigma
. We denote by
\bar{R}
the set of all relations of the form
a|v\sim a|v
in
R
, and write

R^{\prime}=\{a|b\sim\sigma(a|b)\mid a|b\in\Path_{2}({\mathscr{A}})\}
, which is the relation that defines
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
.
We denote by
\bar{R}^{\prime}
the subset of
R^{\prime}
consisting of trivial relations of the form
a|b\sim a|b
.
Observe that
\bar{R}^{\prime}
may in principle be larger than
\bar{R}

6.
On the Garsideness of the structure category of YBMs

We investigate here the main focus of this paper. Namely, we establish a connection between involutive non-degenerate YBMs, and Garside groupoids. The proof of our result differs from the proof of Chouraqui’s theorem, in numerous details. However, the outline of the proof is similar. We shall establish our result by passing through the structure categories of some weak RC-systems.

Our starting point is Proposition
5.1
, saying that an involutive left-non-degenerate YBM

\sigma
on a quiver
{\mathscr{A}}
also provides a left-non-degenerate weak RC-system
({\mathscr{A}},\star)
. This weak RC-system need not be unital, nor even have a unit family. Indeed, the existence of a unit family requires
at least
that every vertex of the quiver has a loop; and this need not be true in general
(see examples in §
7
).

We would like to take the left-non-degenerate weak RC-system
({\mathscr{A}},\star)

in Proposition
5.1
, and consider its completion
(\hat{{\mathscr{A}}},\hat{\star})
.

Remark 6.1
.

Notice that
\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}
satisfies the additional condition (
4
). Indeed, if
x,y
lie in
{\mathscr{A}}=\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}\smallsetminus\mathcal{E}
and
x\neq y
, then
x\;\hat{\star}\;y=x\star y
is defined as
(x\rightharpoonup\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}})^{-1}(y)
which is an element of
{\mathscr{A}}
, and hence lies in
\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}\smallsetminus\mathcal{E}
.

As we have seen in the construction of
Q^{\sharp}
and
\hat{Q}
, the completion is not an extension: taking the completion
\hat{Q}
modifies the operation
\star
, hence possibly modifies the YBM.

The actual scenario is even worse. If
({\mathscr{A}},\star)
is a weak RC-system obtained by a YBM
\sigma
on
{\mathscr{A}}
, the weak RC-system
(\hat{{\mathscr{A}}},\hat{\star})
need not be associated with any quiver-theoretic YBM. In the following remark, we are going to see that in
almost all
cases, there is no possible non-degenerate YBM
\hat{\sigma}\colon\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}\otimes\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}\to\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}\otimes\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}
that can induce
\hat{\star}
.

Remark 6.2
.

Suppose that the
involutive left-non-degenerate
YBM
\sigma
is given by
\sigma(x,y)

=(x\rightharpoonup y,x\leftharpoonup y)
, as before. We define
x\star y=(x\rightharpoonup\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}})^{-1}(y)
. Then we consider the completion
(\hat{{\mathscr{A}}},\hat{\star})
and search for an operation
\hat{\rightharpoonup}
, defined on
\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}
, such that
x\;\hat{\star}\;y=(x\;\hat{\rightharpoonup}\;\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\text{---}})^{-1}(y)
.

What properties should this

satisfy? The relations
x\;\hat{\star}\;\epsilon_{\mathfrak{s}(x)}=\epsilon_{\mathfrak{t}(x)}
and
x\;\hat{\star}\;x=\epsilon_{\mathfrak{t}(x)}

yield, for
\hat{\rightharpoonup}

x\,\hat{\rightharpoonup}\,\epsilon_{\mathfrak{t}(x)}=\epsilon_{\mathfrak{s}(x)}
and

. It is apparent that these two relations are, in most of cases, inconsistent. Since they must hold for all

for all

{\mathscr{A}}
has no arrows at all, and
\hat{{\mathscr{A}}}
consists of one loop

for each vertex

Although the weak RC-system

is generally not induced by a YBM, this completion is useful to describe the structure category.

8.1.

Definition 8.1
.

A

Here,

A

of groupoids

Remark 8.2

Notice that a morphism in

Proof.

Let

\begin{split}\sigma(x^{-1}\;|\;x\rightharpoonup y)&=x^{-1}\rightharpoonup(x\rightharpoonup y)\;|\;x^{-1}\leftharpoonup(x\rightharpoonup y)\\

&\hskip-1.3pt\overset{(\dagger)}{=}y\;|\;(x\leftharpoonup y)^{-1},\end{split}

where the equality marked with

(x^{-1}\leftharpoonup(x\rightharpoonup y))(x\leftharpoonup y)\overset{\eqref{bg4}}{=}(x^{-1}x)\leftharpoonup y=1\leftharpoonup y\overset{\eqref{bg2}}{=}1.

Since (

On the other hand,

Corollary 8.15
.

Let
\Lambda
be a set,
{\mathscr{G}}=\hat{\Lambda}
the corresponding groupoid of pairs. The following data are equivalent:

Let

Proposition 8.16
.

Let

be a map of sets. We write two given group structures on

The map

ii.

The map

is a morphism of pointed heaps.

and

.

Proof.

Let
f
be a group homomorphism; then clearly
f\langle a,b,c\rangle=f(a*b^{-*}*c)=f(a)*f(b)^{-*}*f(c)=\langle f(a),f(b),f(c)\rangle^{\prime}

As for

, let

\displaystyle(\hat{f}^{1}\otimes\hat{f}^{1})\sigma([a,b,c])

\displaystyle=[f(a),f(\langle a,b,c\rangle),f(c)]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}([f(a),f(b),f(c)])

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}(\hat{f}^{1}\otimes\hat{f}^{1})([a,b,c]),

as desired. Finally, assuming

\displaystyle f(a*b)

\displaystyle=\langle f(a),f(u),f(b)\rangle^{\prime}

8.2.
Examples of structure groupoids in the principal homogeneous case

In this section, we present concrete examples of structure groupoids

, when
\sigma
is an involutive YBM of PH type.

Let
A
be an abelian group, and let
{\mathscr{A}}=\hat{A}
denote, as above, the groupoid of pairs on the set of vertices
A
. The ternary operation
\langle a,b,c\rangle=a-b+c
is an abelian heap structure, and the map
\sigma
sending
[a,b,c]
to
[a,a-b+c,c]
is an involutive braiding (and in particular a YBM) on
{\mathscr{A}}
, by Corollary
8.15
. By Lemma
8.12
, one has that
\sigma
is also non-degenerate.

The cardinality of
{\mathscr{A}}(a,A)
is constant for all
a\in A
, and equals the cardinality of
A
. Thus by Corollary
6.11
, the Garside family
E
of
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
has bounded length, where the upper bound is
|A|
. In particular,
E\subsetneq{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
.

Example 8.19
.

Let
A=\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
. The map
\sigma
defined above
acts as follows:

\displaystyle[a,b,b]\mapsto[a,a,b],\;[a,a,b]\mapsto[a,b,b]\text{ for all }a,b\in A,

\displaystyle[a,b,a]\mapsto[a,2a-b,a]\text{ for all }a,b\in A,

\displaystyle[a,b,c]\mapsto[a,b,c]\text{ if }a,b,c\text{ are all distinct.}

Therefore, the structure groupoid is generated by a complete quiver of degree
1
on three vertices, with the relations

\displaystyle[a,b,b]\sim[a,a,b]\text{ for all }a,b\in A,\qquad[a,b,a]\sim[a,2a-b,a]\text{ if }a\neq b.

Notice that
2a-b\neq b
if and only if
a\neq b
, thus the second class of relations contains no redundancies.

We now denote by
[[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]]
the equivalence class in
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
of a path
[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]
. The complementation
\star
is given by

\displaystyle[[a,b]]\star[[a,c]]=[[b,b-a+c]]\text{ when }b\neq c,

and it is easy to see by direct computation that
b-a+c=a
whenever
a,b,c
are distinct. From Proposition
6.8
,

E=\{\mathbf{1}_{a},[[a,a]],[[a,b]],[[a,a,b]],[[a,b,a]],[[a,a,b,a]]\mid a,b\in{\mathscr{A}}\text{ distinct}\}
. Observe that the Garside family
E
of
{\mathscr{C}}(\sigma)
is the union of the sets
\mathrm{Div}_{L}(\Delta_{a})
of left-divisors of
\Delta_{a}
, for
a\in A
; and if
a,b,c
are three distinct vertices, by the previous considerations one has
\Delta_{a}=[[a,b,b,a]]=[[a,a,b,a]]=[[a,a,c,a]]=[[a,c,c,a]]=[[a,b,a,a]]=[[a,c,a,a]]
. In particular,
\Delta_{a}
is a loop for all

.

Example 8.20
.

Let
A=(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}
. An element of

will be denoted by a row vector of

It is easy to see that

\sigma([a,b,c])_{i}=[a_{i},a_{i}-b_{i}+c_{i},c_{i}]=[a_{i},a_{i}+b_{i}+c_{i},c_{i}]

Thus, the structure groupoid of

vertices, modulo the relations

for the equivalence class of a path, the complementation

is

[[a,b]]\star[[a,c]]=[[b,a+\mathbf{1}+\delta_{b,c}]],

for

[[a,b]]\star[[a,c]]
equals
[[b,d]]
