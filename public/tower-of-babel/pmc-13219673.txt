Therapies for Infantile Haemangiomas: Current Practice and Evolving Perspectives
Authors: B Nolan, C O’Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfantile haemangiomas (IH) are the most common vascular tumours of infancy, characterised by a predictable trajectory of rapid proliferation, plateau, and slow spontaneous involution. Although most regress uneventfully, a significant minority risk ulceration, functional compromise, or disfigurement, and therefore require timely intervention. The therapeutic landscape was once dominated by corticosteroids, interferon, and cytotoxic chemotherapeutics of modest efficacy and considerable toxicity. Management was revolutionized in 2008 by the discovery that the beta-blocker propranolol induced prompt and sustained regression. Propranolol has since become the established first-line therapy for IH, with efficacy and safety confirmed by randomized controlled trials and corroborated by observational cohorts. Mechanistically there are multiple temporally stratified actions: immediate vasoconstriction, suppression of angiogenic signalling pathways, induction of endothelial apoptosis, and ultimately the promotion of adipogenic differentiation in keeping with natural involution. Safety is favourable under expert supervision, though hypoglycemia remains the principal preventable hazard, necessitating caregiver education on feeding regimens and “sick day” rules. Rebound growth, encountered in roughly one-fifth of cases, usually responds to re-treatment. Alternative beta-blockers such as atenolol and nadolol provide comparable efficacy with potential advantages in tolerability or dosing convenience. Topical timolol has limited effectiveness for small superficial lesions, while sirolimus, becaplermin gel, laser devices, and surgery may have circumscribed adjunctive roles. Corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents are now confined to salvage use. Contemporary perspectives extend beyond pharmacology to encompass caregiver experience and health-system burden. Recent work highlights the low burden of treatment with propranolol, while digital innovations such as photo-triage during the COVID-19 pandemic illustrate opportunities to reduce healthcare exposure and improve equity of access. This review describes the evolution of drug therapy for IH and integrates mechanistic insights, clinical pragmatism, and patient-centred innovations to chart the current trajectory of care.Keywords: Infantile haemangioma, Vascular anomaly, Propranolol, Beta-blocker

Key Summary Points

Infantile haemangiomas are common and most regress spontaneously, but 10–15% require treatment because of ulceration, functional compromise, disfigurement, or syndromic associations.Propranolol is very clearly the gold standard first-line therapy, with strong efficacy and an excellent safety profile when prescribed under the care of clinicians with expertise in vascular anomalies. Inpatient hospital initiation and vital sign monitoring is no longer routinely required.Atenolol and nadolol are evidence-based alternative beta-blockers that may reduce certain adverse effects or simplify dosing in selected infants. Introduction

Infantile haemangiomas (IH) are the most common benign vascular tumours of childhood, affecting 5–10% of white infants and fewer in other ethnic groups [1, 2]. IH occur more commonly in female infants and in infants born prematurely, of low birth weight, or of multiple gestation, highlighting respectively the influences of oestrogen and placental dysfunction on endothelial growth [3]. IH typically arise in the first weeks of life, proliferate rapidly over several months, plateau, and then involute over years, often leaving a fibrofatty residuum (Fig. 1) [4]. Although many are uncomplicated and resolve without intervention, approximately 10–15% require therapy due to ulceration, bleeding, functional compromise, or permanent disfigurement (Fig. 2) [2, 5]. The risk of complication or associated anomaly is strongly influenced by morphology and anatomic site (Table 1). Historically, management relied on systemic corticosteroids, interferon alfa, or cytotoxic chemotherapy, with limited efficacy and significant toxicity [5, 6]. The field changed abruptly in 2008 when Léauté-Labrèze and colleagues observed striking involution of IH in infants treated with propranolol for cardiac indications [7]. Clinical practice guidelines now strongly advocate propranolol as first-line monotherapy for problematic IH, emphasizing early identification of IH with a high risk of complications and prompt treatment to improve functional and cosmetic outcomes [3]. Randomized trials demonstrated that oral administration of propranolol at 3 mg/kg/day for 6 months achieved complete or near-complete resolution in a large proportion of cases, with outcomes superior to placebo and to historical steroid regimens [8–10]. Observational cohorts and meta-analyses further support high efficacy and acceptable safety, cementing propranolol’s role as the standard of care for IH [11, 12] (Fig. 3).

Pathophysiology of Infantile Haemangiomas and Role of Beta-Blockade

Understanding the pathophysiology of IH is imperative to understanding the current therapeutic landscape. IH are characterized by clonal endothelial proliferation, dysregulated angiogenesis, and a distinctive life cycle (Fig. 4) [4]. The most likely triggering factor in the development of IH is localized hypoxic stress in utero, which may be responsible for activation of the hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF-1α) pathway [4]. Segmental haemangioma development may be related to embryologic neural crest influence on local vasculogenesis, although the exact cellular and molecular mechanisms remain unclear [13]. After birth, proliferation and differentiation of progenitor cells are induced by angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). During the growth phase, endothelial cells predominate, eventually developing luminized capillary-like structures [4]. Eventually involution occurs as a result of replacement of the blood vessels with fibrofatty residuum due to the presence of mesenchymal cells with adipogenic potential [4]. Understanding the mechanism of action of propranolol has helped to explain the underlying etiology of IH, with multifactorial and temporally layered effects. Within hours, beta-2 blockade induces vasoconstriction in vascular smooth muscle, producing pallor and softening of lesions [14]. Over subsequent weeks, propranolol reduces angiogenesis by downregulating pro-angiogenic mediators such as VEGF and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), suppressing endothelial nitric oxide synthase, and attenuating PI3K/AKT/eNOS signalling [15, 16]. At the cellular level, propranolol promotes apoptosis of endothelial and other cells through caspase and p53-BAX pathways, while pushing progenitors toward adipogenic differentiation through changes that mirror the fibrofatty involution of untreated lesions [16, 17]. Data also suggest modulation of the renin–angiotensin axis and HIF-1α pathways, which may further curb angiogenesis and proliferation [18]. This convergent effect on vasomotor tone, angiogenic signalling, cell survival, and differentiation explains both the rapid and sustained therapeutic responses seen clinically [19].

Differential Diagnoses of Infantile Haemangiomas

IH should be distinguished from a range of vascular and non-vascular lesions that may present in early infancy [20]. Key vascular differentials include congenital haemangiomas (rapidly involuting, partially involuting, or non-involuting), which are fully formed at birth and follow a different growth pattern, as well as vascular malformations such as capillary, venous, lymphatic, or arteriovenous malformations, which typically grow proportionally with the child and do not undergo spontaneous involution [3, 4]. Some IH have only minimal or arrested growth [4]. These IH are more common on the lower limbs and can be confused with capillary malformations. Other considerations include pyogenic granuloma, which may appear as a rapidly growing friable lesion, and tufted angioma or kaposiform haemangioendothelioma, particularly when associated with thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy (Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon). Non-vascular mimics include dermoid cysts, epidermoid cysts, and, less commonly, malignant lesions such as infantile fibrosarcoma. Careful assessment of timing of appearance, growth characteristics, colour, compressibility, and associated systemic features is essential for accurate diagnosis. Histopathological [glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) positivity is seen in IH] or radiological investigations may be helpful in challenging cases [3].

When to Consider Treatment for Infantile Haemangiomas

The natural history of IH is rapid early proliferation followed by slow spontaneous involution. Therefore, management should balance reassurance and non-intervention for inconsequential IH against timely pharmacologic intervention for high-risk tumours. Propranolol should be considered for any IH which may compromise function or cosmesis (Table 1). Segmental haemangiomas which may point to associated problems, facial haemangiomas (especially on the nose or ears) interfering with cosmesis, periorbital haemangiomas risking amblyopia, perioral haemangiomas impeding feeding, airway haemangiomas causing respiratory compromise, chest haemangiomas obstructing breast development, and lumbosacral or perineal haemangiomas prone to ulceration or associated anomalies almost always merit early treatment [3]. Hepatic ultrasound should be performed if there are five or more infantile haemangiomas [3]. Validated tools for referral of infants with IH have also been devised e.g. the Infantile Haemangioma Referral Score (IHReS) [21].

Practical Prescription of Propranolol

Practical considerations in propranolol prescription include (1) optimal timing of initiation, (2) dosing, (3) monitoring, (4) practical administration, and (5) specific patient or tumour factors (Fig. 5). Guidelines recommend initiating propranolol in infants with problematic IH as soon as possible, ideally in the pre-proliferative or proliferative phases (generally before 6 weeks of age) [3, 22], although it is approved in Europe and the USA from 5 weeks. Initiation during the proliferative phase improves outcomes and can prevent critical sequelae. Regarding initiation dose and titration, many centres start therapy at 1 mg/kg/day in divided doses with subsequent up titration over several days [22], although initiation at 2–3 mg/kg/day divided twice or three times daily [23] can be considered. Titration may be performed more slowly in premature infants or infants with lower body weight or those with other risk factors for hypoglycemia. Twice daily prescription is preferable to three times daily because there is no significant difference in minimum and maximum concentrations between the two regimens, suggesting no difference in risk of adverse events [24]. In addition, twice daily dosing permits early administration of the second dose, which can be helpful in reducing any associated sleep disturbance. Serious cardiovascular events (symptomatic bradycardia, hypotension) are extremely rare [25, 26]. Baseline medical history should screen for family history of cardiac arrythmias or maternal connective tissue disease (due to the risk of congenital heart block associated with neonatal lupus), and feeding issues. Outpatient initiation is appropriate for the majority of infants, provided that cardiovascular examination is normal, and that families receive structured education, and have access to follow-up. Blood pressure and heart rate monitoring is no longer routinely required [27, 28]. Inpatient or day unit initiation may be preferred for neonates, infants with comorbidities (e.g. concerns for PHACES syndrome pending evaluation, significant cardiac disease, or a history suggestive of airway involvement), or for those with limited social supports [3, 22]. Hypoglycemia is the most important preventable adverse event. Propranolol can blunt adrenergic warning signs and impair gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, increasing risk during fasting or illness [29]. Younger age, intercurrent infection, and prolonged intervals between feeds are key risk factors [25, 26]. Education on dosing at the time of feeds, sick day rules, and prompt evaluation of lethargy or seizures is essential [22]. Propranolol should be withheld during poor oral intake, vomiting, or intercurrent illness, particularly in situations where fasting is prolonged, to reduce theoretical hypoglycemia risk [22]. Other adverse effects are uncommon and include sleep disturbance, irritability, diarrhoea, bronchospasm in predisposed infants, and cool extremities. These events are usually mild and self-limited [30]. A consistent dosing schedule helps mitigate sleep disturbance or ‘night terrors’, and administration of the second dose earlier in the afternoon (e.g. 7 am and 3 pm) should be considered to reduce nocturnal exposure to propranolol [24]. A patient information leaflet is advisable [22]. Beyond physiologic safety, caregiver burden should be minimized [31]. Night dosing, feed coordination, and ongoing monitoring can be challenging. Structured education, simplified schedules, and manageable follow-up improve adherence and satisfaction [31]. Families should be counselled about expected early colour/softness changes, the typical timeline for volume reduction, and the importance of adherence to dosing with feeds [22]. Early clinical change (colour, softening) typically occurs within days; volume reduction is most evident over weeks to months (and usually occurs centrifugally). Observational cohorts and meta-analyses corroborate high response rates across morphologic subtypes, including mixed and deep lesions [12, 14]. Clinical or parental photographic documentation can be helpful to assess response to therapy [32]. Treatment commonly continues for at least 6 months and until at least 12 months of age or until proliferation has ceased and clinically meaningful involution has been achieved [3, 8, 19]. Clinical follow-up mostly exists to facilitate up dosing of propranolol according to weight gain; as growth becomes less rapid after the first months of life, review intervals can be extended. The authors suggest, once target dosage has been achieved, expert review every 2–3 months in the first 6 months of life, every 3–4 months in the second 6 months of life, and every 4–6 months after 1 year of age if propranolol is being continued. Rebound growth occurs in approximately 20% after discontinuation, particularly with premature cessation before the age of 12 months, and in segmental or deep lesions [32]. If rebound growth occurs, propranolol can be immediately reinstituted at full dosage (2–3 mg/kg/day) to reverse rebound growth [32]. Some clinicians taper over 2–4 weeks to monitor for rebound, especially in segmental or deep lesions [33], although this may make it more difficult to appreciate rebound growth. Long-term outcomes for propranolol therapy are favourable—available cohort data have not shown adverse neurodevelopmental sequelae attributable to propranolol, and cosmetic/functional outcomes are excellent when treatment is initiated promptly [34]. Propranolol has few clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interactions in infants at standard doses. Importantly, beta-blockade may theoretically antagonise epinephrine in anaphylaxis; clinicians should use caution in infants who have been prescribed epinephrine auto-injectors and ensure that families understand emergency plans [35]. Ulceration can lead to significant pain, bleeding, and secondary infection and virtually always results in scarring [3]. Ulceration occurs most frequently in infants younger than 4 months, during the period of active IH proliferation [3]. Ulcerated IH often heal within weeks of starting propranolol [10, 11]. Some authors advocate for cautious initial dosing of infants with ulcerated IH at ≤ 1 mg/kg/day due to the risk of paradoxical worsening of the ulceration [36], as beta-blocker therapy may induce vasoconstriction, leading to tissue ischemia with resultant skin breakdown. Physicians should be cognisant of PHACES and LUMBAR syndromes. With multidisciplinary care and appropriate vascular imaging, propranolol has been used safely in PHACES, though individual risk assessment is critical [37, 38]. Consideration of introduction of propranolol at lower dosage for anogenital IH ulceration (whether part of LUMBAR syndrome or not) is prudent as these haemangiomas tend to be less proliferative than IH in other areas [39], but more likely to ulcerate.

Alternative Oral Beta-Blockers

Alternative beta-blockers such as atenolol and nadolol are occasionally used. While propranolol remains by far the most extensively studied agent, alternative beta-blockers can be valuable in selected contexts. Atenolol is a beta-1-selective antagonist with a longer half-life that facilitates once-daily dosing. Randomized trials demonstrate comparable efficacy to propranolol, with trends toward fewer bronchospasm and sleep-related adverse events, differences that may reflect reduced beta-2 activity in the lung and reduced central nervous system (CNS) penetration [40, 41]. Nadolol is a non-selective, long-acting beta-blocker with minimal CNS penetration. A multicentre noninferiority trial showed similar efficacy and safety to propranolol [42]. Once-daily dosing may ease adherence for families juggling feeds and sleep schedules, though careful counseling on hypoglycemia precautions remains essential. A death has been reported from nadolol toxicity in an infant with constipation [43], thought to be due to continued recirculation from feces. Propranolol has a shorter half-life, is hepatically metabolized and renally eliminated, allowing for less drug accumulation in healthy infants with variable stooling patterns. Choice among beta-blockers can be individualised according to comorbidities (e.g. reactive airway disease), adverse event profiles, family preference regarding dosing frequency, and local availability and experience. Where propranolol is poorly tolerated, contraindicated, or unavailable, atenolol or nadolol provide evidence-based alternatives [40–42].

Topical Beta-Blockers

Topical beta-blockers have limited utility for small, thin, superficial IH, particularly on cosmetically sensitive sites [44]. Timolol maleate (typically 0.5% gel-forming solution or drops applied twice daily) has strong evidence from randomised trials demonstrating significant improvement compared with placebo or observation, with a favourable safety profile [45, 46]. Clinicians should counsel families to avoid mucosal surfaces and large ulcerated areas. Although systemic absorption is generally low, it is measurable and can be clinically relevant in very young infants or with extensive application [47]; vigilance for lethargy, feeding difficulty, or bronchospasm is advised. Given the inevitability of systemic absorption and the inferiority to oral propranolol [48], oral beta-blockade is generally preferable.

Alternative Topical Therapies

Topical corticosteroids provide minimal benefit and are now seldom used even for thin, focal lesions [49], due to the superiority of beta-blockade. Intralesional corticosteroids have been used but should be avoided due to the complications of pain, necrosis, embolisation, and systemic exposure to corticosteroids [50]. Given the role of VEGF in IH development, intralesional bevacizumab (a monoclonal antibody against VEGFA) has been trialled, but was inferior to intralesional steroid injections [51]. Becaplermin gel (0.01%), a recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor, has been reported to accelerate healing of ulcerated perineal IH in a small case series, with favourable outcomes and no major safety signals [52]. Given limited evidence and off-label status, use should be restricted to select cases with close monitoring and wound care expertise.

Historic Therapies

Corticosteroids were once the mainstay drug therapy for IH and are now largely reserved for refractory cases or situations where beta-blockade is truly contraindicated. Oral prednisolone at 2–3 mg/kg/day suppresses proliferation but is limited by growth suppression, immunosuppression, hypertension, cushingoid features, and relapse after cessation [53]. Exploring efficacy and clinical outcomes with propranolol across randomized controlled trials, propranolol significantly improves rates of complete or near-complete resolution compared with placebo and performs at least as well as, and usually better than, systemic corticosteroids [8–10, 30]. Interferon alfa, previously used for life-threatening IH, is now avoided due to neurotoxicity, particularly spastic diplegia [54]. Vincristine and vinblastine have historical utility in refractory or syndromic cases; their use today is highly selective and generally confined to complex vascular tumour settings [55].

Device and Surgical Therapies

Device therapies have a circumscribed, adjunctive role. Pulsed dye laser (PDL) can hasten epithelialization of ulcerated IH and improve residual telangiectasia after involution; however, risks of scarring and dyspigmentation, particularly in darker skin types or with aggressive settings, limit primary use during proliferation [56]. Intense pulsed light (IPL) has overlapping indications but similar constraints. In current practice, devices complement pharmacologic therapy, e.g. PDL for painful ulcerations not rapidly improving on propranolol or for cosmetic refinement of telangiectasia after involution, rather than serving as standalone primary treatments. In rare instances, a cross-speciality approach with surgeons may be appropriate, with surgery reserved for focused indications. Excision in infancy may be considered for pedunculated lesions prone to recurrent bleeding or trauma, or for discrete lesions where medical therapy is unsuitable or ineffective [3]. More commonly, surgical intervention is deferred to the post-involution phase to correct residual deformities, redundant skin, contour irregularities, or persistent fibrofatty bulk, often timed for late childhood or early adolescence to optimise psychosocial benefits and scar quality and to allow for maximal natural involution [57]. Collaboration with plastic surgery or other surgical specialities ensures that functional and aesthetic goals are aligned with family priorities.

Emerging Therapies

One in silico study has highlighted the PI3K/AKT/MTOR pathway, RAS/MAPK pathway, and CGMP/PKG pathway as potential targets, with in vitro experiments validating the therapeutic promise of entinostat, sorafenib, dasatinib, and sirolimus [58]. Sirolimus (rapamycin), an mTOR inhibitor, has transformed care for complicated vascular anomalies and has been reported as a second-line option in difficult IH [59]. Sirolimus has anti-angiogenic effects, suppresses haemangioma-derived stem cells via mTOR-fatty acid-binding protein 4, disrupts phosphorylated AKT signaling, and modifies the expression and activity of pathologic PI3K/AKT/mTOR genes [60]. Given immunosuppressive risks and monitoring needs, it is reserved for specialist centres and atypical situations [59]. Itraconazole has shown anti-angiogenic activity in preclinical models and limited clinical experiences, but its role in routine IH management remains investigational [61].

Future Directions

Given the safety and efficacy of propranolol for IH, fewer candidate therapies are being investigated. Early recognition of high-risk IH by primary care physicians or paediatricians and prompt referral to a clinician experienced in vascular anomalies (usually a dermatologist) is crucial. Referral algorithms based on location (periorbital, nasal tip, lip, ear, airway), size, morphology, and growth kinetics have been devised [21]. While these rubrics provide a structured approach to triage, the multistep nature of these referral systems may introduce additional complexity and reduce usability in fast-paced settings, potentially affecting uptake in routine practice and timely access to expert care. Instead, the authors suggest using checklist-type systems such as Table 1 to decide whether or not an infant should be referred to a specialist for evaluation. IH should be managed under the care of clinicians with expertise in vascular anomalies, due to the risk of misdiagnosis, inadequate or excessive investigation, and mistreatment by clinicians without adequate training [3, 62, 63]. Other considerations regarding propranolol include equity, access, and the potential for digitally delivered care. Propranolol is generally inexpensive (especially when initiated as an outpatient) [64] and widely available internationally. Innovative approaches such as photo-triage and teledermatology have been piloted during the COVID-19 pandemic to streamline access and reduce unnecessary healthcare exposure, with promising results [65], although these methods have limitations of potential diagnostic uncertainty and inequities in digital access. In exploring the future of the therapeutic landscape of IH, key questions and future considerations in this landscape remain. Biomarkers that predict the optimal duration of therapy or rebound would refine the timing of discontinuation and reduce overtreatment. Comparative studies among beta-blockers, particularly head-to-head trials with long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up, could better define relative benefits in specific subgroups. Finally, research on endothelial biology of IH may yield adjunctive treatments for refractory IH.

In conclusion, the therapeutic landscape of IH has shifted from reliance on toxic and ineffective agents to a targeted, well-tolerated pharmacologic intervention. Propranolol is supported by robust randomised trials, mechanistic plausibility, and real-world effectiveness. Atenolol and nadolol extend options for infants if propranolol is poorly tolerated, ineffective, or unavailable. Topical timolol is an option for small, superficial lesions when there is reason or reluctance to pursue oral propranolol. Corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents are now salvage therapies for exceptional cases. Device-based approaches and surgery serve adjunctive or corrective roles. Early identification of high-risk IH, timely initiation of therapy by clinicians with expertise in vascular anomalies, and family-centred education grounded in consensus guidance optimize functional, cosmetic, and safety outcomes while minimizing caregiver burden. Continued refinement of pharmacologic strategies, coupled with innovations in care delivery and family-centred support, promise to sustain and extend these remarkable therapeutic gains for the next generation of children and families.
