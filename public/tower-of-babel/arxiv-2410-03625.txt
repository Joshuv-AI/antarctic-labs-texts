Lower Bounds for Book Ramsey Numbers
William J. Wesley

Abstract
We prove new bounds for Ramsey numbers for book graphs B n B_{n} . In particular, we show that R ⁡ ( B n − 1 , B n ) = 4 ​ n − 1 R(B_{n-1},B_{n})=4n-1 for an infinite family of n n using a block-circulant construction similar to Paley graphs. We obtain improved bounds for several other values of R ⁡ ( B r , B s ) R(B_{r},B_{s}) using different block-circulant graphs from SAT and integer programming (IP) solvers. Finally, we enumerate the number of critical graphs for R ⁡ ( B r , B s ) R(B_{r},B_{s}) for small r r and s s using SAT modulo symmetries (SMS).

Introduction

The
Ramsey number

R(r,s)
is the smallest
n
such that every graph on
n
vertices contains either a copy of the clique
K_{r}
or coclique
\overline{K}_{s}
. Ramsey numbers are among the most widely studied numbers in combinatorics, yet pinning down their precise values is remarkably difficult. Only nine nontrivial values are known, namely
R(4,4),R(4,5)
, and
R(3,s)
for
3\leq s\leq 9
.

A natural modification is to consider graphs other than
K_{r}
and
K_{s}
, and instead compute
R(G,H)
, the smallest
n
such that every graph on
n
vertices contains either a copy of
G
or
\overline{H}
. Note that
R(K_{r},K_{s})
is simply
R(r,s)
. For suitable choices of
G
and
H
, the problem of computing
R(G,H)
becomes much simpler. These generalized Ramsey numbers are of great interest, both for their asymptotics and exact values. A comprehensive dynamic survey of their known values is maintained by Radziszowski
[
26
]
.

In this work we restrict our attention to the
book graphs

B_{n}=K_{2}+\overline{K_{n}}
, where + denotes the graph join. That is,
B_{n}
consists of
n
triangles that share a common edge (see Figure
1
). The first systematic study of book Ramsey numbers was done in 1978 by Rousseau and Sheehan
[
27
]
. The authors are rather modest and claim that “the book
(B_{n})
does not have the same status in graph theory as does, for example, the complete graph
(K_{n})
, the path
(P_{n})
, cycle
(C_{n})
, or wheel
(W_{n})
”. However, in recent years Ramsey numbers involving book graphs have attracted significant attention
[
11
,
12
,
25
,
9
]
, and notably, book graphs are a key ingredient in the recent breakthrough proof that
R(s,s)\leq(4-\epsilon)^{s}

[
7
]
. Rousseau and Sheehan proved several exact bounds for families of book Ramsey numbers in
[
27
]
. In particular, they gave the following bounds for the diagonal and “almost diagonal” cases.

Theorem 1

(Rousseau-Sheehan)
.

For all
n
,

(i)

R(B_{n},B_{n})=4n+2\text{ if }q=4n+1\text{ is a prime power},

(ii)

R(B_{n-1},B_{n})\leq 4n-1
,

(iii)

R(B_{n-2},B_{n})\leq 4n-3\text{ if }n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
.

Book graphs
B_{3}
and
B_{4}
.

The upper bounds in Theorem
1
come from careful examination of Goodman’s bound for the number of monochromatic triangles in a 2-edge-coloring of a complete graph
[
17
]
. The lower bounds for
R(B_{n},B_{n})
come from Paley graphs. Further bounds on small Ramsey numbers for books and other graphs were found in
[
5
,
28
,
14
,
24
]
(see
[
26
]
for a detailed accounting).

In recent years, SAT solvers have been instrumental in solving difficult combinatorial problems. In particular, they have been used effectively to compute exact bounds in arithmetic Ramsey theory, namely in Schur, Rado, and van der Waerden numbers
[
18
,
22
,
21
,
19
,
6
,
8
]
. In 2016, Codish, Frank, Itzhakov, and Miller computed the second known three-color Ramsey number,
R(3,3,4)=30
, with SAT solvers
[
10
]
. More recent works have used SAT solvers to certify the bound
R(3,8)\leq 28

[
13
]
and compute the bound
R(5,5)\leq 46

[
2
]
.

The main contributions of our paper are improvements on book Ramsey number lower bounds using
block-circulant
graphs, which have yielded recent improvements for Ramsey numbers involving the graphs
K_{m,n},K_{n}-e,
and the wheel graphs
W_{n}

[
16
]
. In particular, we show that a certain generalization of the Paley graph gives the tight bound
R(B_{n-1},B_{n})=4n-1
for infinitely many
n
. We also produce block-circulant graphs that give new lower bounds for
R(B_{r},B_{s})
for several small values of
r
and
s
, some of which are tight as well. Moreover, we obtain additional lower and upper bounds for
R(B_{r},B_{s})
using SAT and integer programming (IP) solvers. Using the related SAT modulo symmetries (SMS) framework
[
20
]
, we are able to enumerate the graphs avoiding copies of
B_{r}
and
\overline{B}_{s}
for some small values of
r
and
s

We state our main results precisely in Section
2

3
we give more details on block-circulant graphs and our Paley-type constructions. Section
4
outlines the computational methods to achieve the improved bounds. Data for graphs that achieve the lower bounds is given in the Appendix.

3
Block-circulant Graphs

Recall that a
circulant matrix
is a matrix such that each row is a one-element right cyclic shift of the previous row. A
circulant graph
is a graph whose adjacency matrix is circulant. More generally, a graph is
block-circulant
on
k
blocks (or simply
k
-block-circulant
) if its adjacency matrix can be written in the form

A=\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}&\dots&C_{1k}\\
C_{21}&C_{22}&\dots&C_{2k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\\
C_{k1}&C_{k2}&\dots&C_{kk}\end{pmatrix},

where
C_{ij}
is a circulant matrix for all
i,j
. Observe that since
A
is symmetric, we have
C_{ij}=C_{ji}^{T}
for all
i
and
j
. Note also that
C_{ij}
must be symmetric for
i=j
, but
C_{ij}
need not be symmetric for
i\neq j
. A block-circulant graph, therefore, is determined completely by the first rows of
C_{ij}
for
i\leq j
. For all
i,j
, let
D_{ij}
denote the indices (using zero-indexing) of the columns of
C_{ij}
whose first entry is equal to 1. Since
A
is an adjacency matrix, we must have
0\not\in D_{ii}
for all
i
, but it is possible that
0\in D_{ij}
for
i\neq j
. We identify the tuple
(D_{ij})_{i\leq j}
with the corresponding block-circulant graph.

Circulant graphs often provide good (and in several cases, tight) lower bounds for the classical Ramsey numbers
R(s,t)
. The more general block-circulant graphs have also yielded improved lower bounds for other Ramsey numbers, in particular for
R(K_{s}-e,K_{t}-e)

[
16
]
. Here we show that block-circulant graphs give tight bounds for Ramsey numbers for book graphs.

We will use the following notation throughout this paper.

Definition 5
.

For subsets
X,Y
of an additive group
G
and
d\in G
, let

\displaystyle\Delta(X,Y,d)

\displaystyle:=|\{(x,y)\in X\times Y:x-y=d\}|,

\displaystyle\Sigma(X,Y,d)

\displaystyle:=|\{(x,y)\in X\times Y:x+y=d\}|.

Definition 6
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and two vertices
u,v\in V
, let

\Gamma(u,v):=\{w\in V:\{u,w\}\in E\text{ and }\{v,w\}\in E\}.

A useful property of 2-block-circulant graphs is that
|\Gamma(u,v)|
can be computed easily via the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
2
-block-circulant graph
(D_{11},D_{12},D_{22})
with
V=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
and
V_{1}=V_{2}=\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. That is, the vertex set is the disjoint union of two copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and
|V|=2m
. Then for all
u,v\in V
, we have

\displaystyle|\Gamma(u,v)|=\begin{cases}\Delta(D_{11},D_{11},v-u)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},v-u)\text{ if }{u,v\in V_{1}}\\
\Delta(D_{22},D_{22},v-u)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},v-u)\text{ if }{u,v\in V_{2}}\\
\Sigma(D_{11},D_{12},v-u)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},v-u)\text{ if }{u\in V_{1},v\in V_{2}}\end{cases}

Proof.

If
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
with
i\leq j
, then
u
and
v
are adjacent if and only if
v-u\in D_{ij}
.
Suppose
u,v
belong to
V_{1}
. Then they have a common neighbor
w
if and only if
w-u,w-v\in D_{11}
or
w-u,w-v\in D_{12}
. Then, letting
\chi_{S}
denote the indicator function of a set
S
, we have

\displaystyle|\Gamma(u,v)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V_{1}}\chi_{D_{11}}(w-u)\chi_{D_{11}}(w-v)+\sum_{w\in V_{2}}\chi_{D_{12}}(w-u)\chi_{D_{12}}(w-v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w+v\in V_{1}}\chi_{D_{11}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{11}}(w)+\sum_{w+v\in V_{2}}\chi_{D_{12}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{12}}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V_{1}}\chi_{D_{11}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{11}}(w)+\sum_{w\in V_{2}}\chi_{D_{12}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{12}}(w)

\displaystyle=\Delta(D_{11},D_{11},v-u)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},v-u).

The case where
u,v\in D_{22}
is similar. If
u\in V_{1},v\in V_{2}
, then
u
and
v
have a common neighbor
w
in
V_{1}
if and only if
w-u\in D_{11}
and
v-w\in D_{12}
, and a common neighbor
w\in V_{2}
if and only if
w-u\in D_{12}
and
w-v\in D_{22}
. The total number of neighbors of
u
and
v
is then
\Sigma(D_{11},D_{12},v-u)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},v-u)
.

Then we have

\displaystyle|\Gamma(u,v)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V_{1}}\chi_{D_{11}}(w-u)\chi_{D_{12}}(v-w)+\sum_{w\in V_{2}}\chi_{D_{12}}(w-u)\chi_{D_{22}}(w-v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w+v\in V_{1}}\chi_{D_{11}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{12}}(-w)+\sum_{w+v\in V_{2}}\chi_{D_{12}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{22}}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V_{1}}\chi_{D_{11}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{12}}(-w)+\sum_{w\in V_{2}}\chi_{D_{12}}(w+v-u)\chi_{D_{22}}(w)

\displaystyle=\Sigma(D_{11},D_{12},v-u)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},v-u).

∎

The following corollary gives a lower bound for
R(B_{r},B_{s})
. It follows quickly from the preceding lemma and the definition of book graphs.

Corollary 8
.

Let
G=(D_{11},D_{12},D_{22})
be a
2
-block-circulant graph on
2m
vertices. Let
\overline{D}_{ii},\overline{D}_{12}
denote, respectively, the complement of
D_{ii}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\setminus\{0\}
and the complement of
D_{12}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. If

\displaystyle\Delta(D_{11},D_{11},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)<r,\text{ for all $d\in D_{11}$}

\displaystyle\Delta(D_{22},D_{22},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)<r,\text{ for all $d\in D_{22}$}

\displaystyle\Sigma(D_{11},D_{12},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},d)<r,\text{ for all $d\in D_{12}$}

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{D}_{11},\overline{D}_{11},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{12},d)<s,\text{ for all $d\in\overline{D}_{11}$}

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{D}_{22},\overline{D}_{22},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{12},d)<s,\text{ for all $d\in\overline{D}_{22}$}

\displaystyle\Sigma(\overline{D}_{11},\overline{D}_{12},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{22},d)<s,\text{ for all $d\in\overline{D}_{12}$}

then
R(B_{r},B_{s})>2m
.

Proof.

A graph
G
does not contain a copy of
B_{r}
if and only if
|\Gamma(u,v)|<r
for adjacent
u,v
. The first three hypotheses correspond to the cases
u,v\in V_{1}
,
u,v\in V_{2}
, and
u\in V_{1},v\in V_{2}
, respectively. By Lemma
7
, this implies that
|\Gamma(u,v)|<r
for adjacent
u,v
. Similarly, the last three hypotheses and Lemma
7
imply that
G
contains no copy of
\overline{B_{s}}
, and the result follows.
∎

More generally, we can make the following construction, which is a “2-block” Cayley graph. Let
G
be an abelian group, and let
D_{11},D_{12},D_{22}\subseteq G
. Define a graph
\Gamma_{G}(D_{11},D_{12},D_{22})
with vertex set
V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
with
V_{1}=V_{2}=G
such that
x,y
are adjacent if and only if
x,y\in V_{1}
and
y-x\in D_{11}
,
x,y\in V_{2}
and
y-x\in D_{22}
, or
x\in V_{1},y\in V_{2}
and
y-x\in D_{12}
. A 2-block-circulant graph on
2m
vertices is the special case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
G
be an abelian group. Let
\overline{D}_{ii},\overline{D}_{12}
denote, respectively, the complement of
D_{ii}
in
G\setminus\{0\}
for
i=1,2
and the complement of
D_{12}
in
G
. If

\displaystyle\Delta(D_{11},D_{11},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)<r,\text{ for all $d\in D_{11}$}

\displaystyle\Delta(D_{22},D_{22},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)<r,\text{ for all $d\in D_{22}$}

\displaystyle\Sigma(D_{11},D_{12},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},d)<r,\text{ for all $d\in D_{12}$}

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{D}_{11},\overline{D}_{11},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{12},d)<s,\text{ for all $d\in\overline{D}_{11}$}

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{D}_{22},\overline{D}_{22},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{12},d)<s,\text{ for all $d\in\overline{D}_{22}$}

\displaystyle\Sigma(\overline{D}_{11},\overline{D}_{12},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{22},d)<s,\text{ for all $d\in\overline{D}_{12}$}

then
\Gamma_{G}(D_{11},D_{12},D_{22})
does not contain a copy of
B_{r}
or
\overline{B}_{s}
, hence
R(B_{r},B_{s})>2|G|
.

The proof of Lemma
9
is essentially the same as Lemma
7
and Corollary
8
; we omit the details here.

Recall that in the Paley graph of order
q
, vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent if and only if
x-y
is a nonzero quadratic residue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In particular, for prime
q
, Paley graphs are circulant. In Theorem
1
, the lower bound
R(B_{n},B_{n})\geq 4n+2
when
q=4n+1
is a prime power comes from the fact that the Paley graph of order
q
does not contain copies of
B_{n}
and is self-complementary. More precisely, two adjacent vertices share exactly
n-1
common neighbors. Our construction in the proof of Theorem
2
is a generalization of the Paley graphs using two blocks instead of one. The following lemma gives convenient properties of quadratic residues and nonresidues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
be a prime power. Let
Q
and
N
denote the set of nonzero quadratic residues and nonresidues, respectively, in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then

\displaystyle\Delta(Q,Q,d)=\begin{cases}\frac{q-1}{4}-1&d\in Q\\
\frac{q-1}{4}&d\in N\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Delta(N,N,d)=\begin{cases}\frac{q-1}{4}&d\in Q\\
\frac{q-1}{4}-1&d\in N\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Delta(Q,N,d)=\Delta(N,Q,d)=\begin{cases}\frac{q-1}{4}&d\neq 0\\
0&d=0\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
S,S^{\prime}\in\{Q,N\}
, and let
\chi_{A}
denote the indicator function of a set
A
. Observe that the analogue of Euler’s criterion holds in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, that is, for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

x^{(q-1)/2}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in Q\\
-1&\text{if }x\in N\\
0&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

Recall also that exactly half of the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
are squares, so
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}x^{(q-1)/2}=0
.

Let
\delta_{0}(x)=\chi_{\{0\}}(x)
. Let
\epsilon=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }S=Q\\
-1&\text{ if }S=N\end{cases}
, and similarly for
\epsilon^{\prime}
and
S^{\prime}
.

Then

\displaystyle\Delta(S,S^{\prime},d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi_{S}(x+d)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon(x+d)^{(q-1)/2}-\delta_{0}(x+d)}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1+\epsilon^{\prime}x^{(q-1)/2}-\delta_{0}(x)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(1-\delta_{0}(x)-\delta_{0}(x+d)+\epsilon^{\prime}x^{(q-1)/2}+\epsilon(x+d)^{(q-1)/2}-\epsilon\delta_{0}(x)(x+d)^{(q-1)/2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\epsilon^{\prime}\delta_{0}(x+d)x^{(q-1)/2}+\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}(x+d)^{(q-1)/2}x^{(q-1)/2}+\delta_{0}(x)\delta_{0}(x+d))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(q-2-\epsilon d^{(q-1)/2}-\epsilon^{\prime}(-d)^{(q-1)/2}+\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(x^{2}+xd)^{(q-1)/2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(q-2-(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})d^{(q-1)/2}+\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}}(x^{2}+xd)^{(q-1)/2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(q-2-(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})d^{(q-1)/2}+\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}}(1+x^{-1}d)^{(q-1)/2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(q-2-(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})d^{(q-1)/2}+\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{1\}}y^{(q-1)/2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(q-2-(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})d^{(q-1)/2}-\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}\Bigr).

∎

We can now prove Theorem
2
.

Proof.

Let
q=2n-1
be a prime power equivalent to 1 modulo 4. Let
Q
and
N
denote respectively the set of quadratic residues and nonresidues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Set
D_{11}=D_{12}=Q
, and
D_{22}=N
. We will show that the conditions in Lemma
9
hold for the graph
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q,Q,N)
.

Since
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, we have
-1\in Q
. It follows that
d\in Q
if and only if
-d\in Q
, and also
d\in N
if and only if
-d\in N
. Therefore
\overline{D}_{22}=D_{11}=Q
,
\overline{D}_{11}=D_{22}=N
, and
\overline{D}_{12}=N\cup\{0\}
.

By Lemma
10
, we have the following.

For all
d\in D_{11}=Q
,

\Delta(D_{11},D_{11},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)=2\Delta(Q,Q,d)=\frac{q-1}{2}-2=n-3.

For all
d\in D_{22}=N
,

\Delta(D_{22},D_{22},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)=\frac{q-1}{4}-1+\frac{q-1}{4}=n-2.

For all
d\in D_{12}=Q
,

\displaystyle\Sigma(D_{11},D_{12},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},d)

\displaystyle=\Delta(D_{11},-D_{12},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{22},d)

\displaystyle=\Delta(Q,Q,d)+\Delta(Q,N,d)

\displaystyle=\frac{q-1}{4}-1+\frac{q-1}{4}

\displaystyle=n-2.

For all
d\in\overline{D}_{11}=N
,

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{D}_{11},\overline{D}_{11},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{12},d)

\displaystyle=\Delta(N,N,d)+\Delta(N\cup\{0\},N\cup\{0\},d)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{q-1}{4}-1\Bigr)+\Bigl(\frac{q-1}{4}+1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=n-1,

For all
d\in\overline{D}_{22}=Q
,

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{D}_{22},\overline{D}_{22},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{12},d)

\displaystyle=\Delta(Q,Q,d)+\Delta(N\cup\{0\},N\cup\{0\},d)

\displaystyle=\frac{q-1}{4}-1+\frac{q-1}{4}=n-2

For all
d\in\overline{D}_{12}=N\cup\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\Sigma(\overline{D}_{11},\overline{D}_{12},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{22},d)

\displaystyle=\Delta(\overline{D}_{11},-\overline{D}_{12},d)+\Delta(\overline{D}_{12},\overline{D}_{22},d)

\displaystyle=\Delta(N,N\cup\{0\},d)+\Delta(N\cup\{0\},Q,d)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{q-1}{2}+0\text{ if }d=0\\
\frac{q-1}{4}+\frac{q-1}{4}\text{ if }d\neq 0\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq n-1.

By Lemma
9
,
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q,Q,N)
does not contain a copy of
B_{n-1}
or
\overline{B_{n}}
. Therefore
R(B_{n-1},B_{n})\geq 2q+1=4n-1
, and by Theorem
1
, this is tight.
∎

The above proof gives the bound
R(B_{n-1},B_{n})\geq 4n-1
for an infinite family of
n
. For the small values
n\geq 20
and the bounds for other Ramsey numbers in Theorem
3
, we outline the computational methods used to obtain them in the next section.

4
SAT and IP methods

There are natural encodings for computing Ramsey numbers using SAT solvers and IP solvers. For arbitrary graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, the following encoding gives a way to compute
R(G_{1},G_{2})
.

Encoding A
.

The Ramsey number
R(G_{1},G_{2})
is at most
n
if the formula
F_{n}(G_{1},G_{2})
is unsatisfiable, where and

F_{n}(G_{1},G_{2}):=\Bigl(\bigwedge_{H\subset K_{n},H\cong G_{1}}\Bigl(\bigvee_{e\in E(H)}\bar{x}_{e}\Bigr)\Bigr)\wedge\Bigl(\bigwedge_{H\subset K_{n},H\cong G_{2}}\Bigl(\bigvee_{e\in E(H)}x_{e}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Moreover, if
F_{n}(G_{1},G_{2})
is satisfiable, then
R(G_{1},G_{2})\geq n+1
.

However, for
(G_{1},G_{2})=(B_{r},B_{s})
, this formula becomes too large even for modest values of
r
and
s
because
K_{n}
contains
\binom{n}{2}(\binom{n-2}{r}+\binom{n-2}{s})
copies of
B_{r}
and
B_{s}
. To obtain a smaller encoding, we introduce new variables
y_{ijk}
and
y^{\prime}_{ijk}
for each triangle, letting

y_{ijk}:=x_{ij}\wedge x_{ik}\wedge x_{jk},\hskip 10.00002pty^{\prime}_{ijk}:=\bar{x}_{ij}\wedge\bar{x}_{ik}\wedge\bar{x}_{jk}.

(1)

(Note that here we ignore the order of the subscript indices, so that
y_{123}
and
y_{321}
are the same.)

Now let
L_{r}(i,j)
(respectively
L^{\prime}_{r}(i,j)
) denote the constraint that at most
r
of the variables
y_{ijk}
(respectively
y^{\prime}_{ijk}
) are set to true. Then we have the following encoding to bound
R(B_{r},B_{s})
.

Encoding B
.

With notation as above, the Ramsey number
R(B_{r},B_{s})
is at most
n
if and only if the formula

F_{n}(r,s):=\bigwedge_{1\leq j\leq n}L_{r}(i,j)\cap\bigwedge_{1\leq j\leq n}L_{s}^{\prime}(i,j)

is unsatisfiable.

This encoding can be converted to conjunctive normal form (CNF) by using a Tseitin transformation for the formulas in (
1
) and using a cardinality constraint encoding for the
L_{r}(i,j)
and
L^{\prime}_{s}(i,j)
. For this latter encoding we used the tree encoding from
[
32
]
, Section 2.4 (see also
[
3
]
).

Finally, we added symmetry breaking clauses to enforce that no simple vertex transposition (that is, swapping vertices
i
and
i+1
for some
i
) results in a lexicographically smaller adjacency matrix. This is the same set of clauses produced by the SAT symmetry breaking software
Shatter

[
1
]
. We could have introduced more symmetry breaking clauses, for instance other vertex transpositions, but in practice this usually does not provide any useful speedup.

Another popular method for computing Ramsey number bounds is integer programming (IP). For some of our lower bounds, this method was more efficient than SAT solving. In particular, IP solving worked well for finding 2-block-circulant graphs (though it is not difficult to enforce block-circulant constraints in the SAT encoding). For our book graphs, we can encode the problem as an integer program by converting the constraints in Corollary
8
into linear inequalities. For brevity, we will not give the encoding in full detail here, but as an example, the constraint

\Delta(D_{11},D_{11},d)+\Delta(D_{12},D_{12},d)<r,\text{ for all }d\in D_{11}

in Corollary
8
can be encoded as follows:

\displaystyle s_{i,d}

\displaystyle\leq x_{i},

\displaystyle u_{i,d}\leq z_{i},

\displaystyle s_{i,d}

\displaystyle\leq x_{i+d},

\displaystyle u_{i,d}\leq z_{i+d},

\displaystyle s_{i,d}

\displaystyle\leq x_{d},

\displaystyle u_{i,d}\leq z_{d},

\displaystyle s_{i,d}

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}+x_{i+d}+x_{d}-2,

\displaystyle u_{i,d}\geq z_{i}+z_{i+d}+z_{d}-2,\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\forall i,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}

\displaystyle s_{i,d}+u_{i,d}\leq r-1

\displaystyle\forall d\in\mathbb{Z}_{m},

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x_{i},

\displaystyle z_{i},s_{i,d},u_{i,d}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall i,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Here the variables
x_{i}
are set to 1 if
i\in D_{11}
, and similarly
z_{i}
is set to 1 if
i\in D_{12}
. Then
s_{i,d}=1
if and only if
d\in D_{11}
and
x_{i},x_{i+d}\in D_{11}
, and similarly for
z_{i,d}
.
