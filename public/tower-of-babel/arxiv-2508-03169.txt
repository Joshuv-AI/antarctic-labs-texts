Quantum Dynamics and Information Measures in 𝒫 \mathcal{P} 𝒯 \mathcal{T} and Anti - 𝒫 \mathcal{P} 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -Symmetric Systems
Amir Ahmadi, Roozbeh H. Asgari, Javad T. Firouzjaee

Abstract
In this study, we investigate qubit dynamics under 𝒫 \mathcal{P} 𝒯 \mathcal{T} and Anti - 𝒫 \mathcal{P} 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -symmetric non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, focusing on phase evolution, decoherence, quantum speed limits (QSL), and Rényi entanglement entropies. Using similarity transformations and Dyson maps, we analyze the reduced density matrix evolution in bosonic environments. Anti- 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric systems show enhanced robustness against decoherence, with slower entropy growth and longer coherence times compared to 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric counterparts. QSL behavior is non-monotonic, reflecting rapid initial evolution followed by gradual decrease. Higher-order Rényi entropies reveal that Anti - 𝒫 \mathcal{P} 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -symmetric qubits preserve quantum information more effectively, offering advantages for memory and cryptographic applications.

Introduction

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}

;

Recent research has unveiled a broad spectrum of applications for

\mathcal{T}

II.1
The Parity Operator
\mathcal{P}
and the Time-Reversal Operator
\mathcal{T}

The parity operator performs spatial inversion and acts on position and momentum operators as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}x\mathcal{P}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-x,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}p\mathcal{P}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-p.

(2)

It is a linear, Hermitian, and involutive operator:

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\dagger},\qquad\mathcal{P}^{2}=I.

(3)

The parity operator’s role in quantum systems extends beyond just spatial inversion; it also plays a fundamental part in the symmetry properties of the system’s Hamiltonian
Sakurai and Napolitano 1995
. The parity operation is critical in determining the system’s response to spatial transformations and in categorizing quantum states into even and odd parity states. The involutive property of the parity operator ensures that performing the parity operation twice yields the identity, reinforcing its fundamental symmetry nature.

The time-reversal operator reverses the sign of momentum and complex conjugates the imaginary unit:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}x\mathcal{T}^{-1}

\displaystyle=x,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}p\mathcal{T}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-p,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}i\mathcal{T}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-i.

(6)

Unlike
\mathcal{P}
, the operator
\mathcal{T}
is anti-linear and anti-unitary. It generally satisfies:

\mathcal{T}^{2}=I.

(7)

The time-reversal operator plays an essential role in reversing the flow of time in quantum systems, providing a symmetry that is particularly useful in studying systems out of equilibrium
Wigner 1959
. It leads to profound implications in the dynamics of quantum systems, especially in scenarios where time-reversal symmetry is spontaneously broken. This symmetry also plays a critical role in quantum information theory, where the invariance of the system under time-reversal is a key factor in ensuring certain information-theoretic properties
Viola and Knill 2022
.

II.2
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
Symmetric and Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
Symmetric Qubit

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-Symmetric Hamiltonians:

A Hamiltonian is said to be
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric if it commutes with the combined operator
\mathcal{PT}

Gardas et al. 2016
;
Cen and Saxena 2022
:

[\mathcal{PT},H]=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{PT}H(\mathcal{PT})^{-1}=H.

(8)

This condition does not imply Hermiticity:

H\neq H^{\dagger},

(9)

yet, under certain conditions,
H
can still have a real energy spectrum.

Unbroken and Broken
\mathcal{PT}
-Symmetry:

If an eigenfunction
\psi
of
H
is also an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{PT}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{PT}\psi=\lambda\psi,\qquad|\lambda|=1,

(10)

then
\psi
lies in the unbroken
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
phase, and the corresponding eigenvalue
E
is guaranteed to be real:

H\psi=E\psi,\qquad E\in\mathbb{R}.

(11)

In this unbroken phase, the system exhibits normal quantum mechanical behavior, with real eigenvalues corresponding to stable energy states. On the other hand, when
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is spontaneously broken, the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian become complex conjugates of each other, leading to a dramatic shift in the system’s behavior
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
;
Bender 2007
. This phase transition between broken and unbroken
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is a fascinating phenomenon observed in various physical systems, such as optical cavities and quantum dots
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
.

Anti-
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-Symmetric Systems:

Beyond
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems, recent work has introduced the notion of
Anti-
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry, where the Hamiltonian
H
satisfies an anti-commutation relation with the combined
\mathcal{PT}
operator
Cen and Saxena 2022
. Specifically, an Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Hamiltonian obeys:

\{\mathcal{PT},H\}=\mathcal{PT}H+H\mathcal{PT}=0.

(12)

The emergence of Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry offers a new paradigm for studying systems with non-Hermitian dynamics, as it leads to entirely different spectral characteristics from those seen in
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric systems.

II.3
Pseudo-Hermitian Systems and the Metric Operator

The notion of
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry was later connected to the broader class of
pseudo-Hermitian
Hamiltonians introduced by Mostafazadeh
Mostafazadeh 2005
. A non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
is called
pseudo-Hermitian
if there exists a linear, invertible, and Hermitian operator
\eta
such that:

H^{\dagger}=\eta H\eta^{-1}.

(13)

This relation implies that
H
is Hermitian with respect to a modified inner product:

\langle\psi|\phi\rangle_{\eta}:=\langle\psi|\eta|\phi\rangle.

(14)

If such a positive-definite
\eta
exists, then the time evolution governed by
H
is unitary in the
\eta
-inner product space
Mostafazadeh 2007
. This allows for a consistent quantum theory even when
H\neq H^{\dagger}
in the usual sense.

For many
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Hamiltonians, it is possible to construct such an
\eta
explicitly, showing that they are pseudo-Hermitian. In fact, the reality of the spectrum and the unbroken
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry condition correspond to the existence of a positive-definite
\eta

Mostafazadeh 2005
;
Mostafazadeh 2007
.

Conditions for Reality of the Spectrum:

Thus, we may summarize the key conditions for a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
to possess a real spectrum:

1.

H
is
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric:
[\mathcal{PT},H]=0
(see Eq. (
8
)),

2.

The
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is
unbroken
: each eigenfunction
\psi
satisfies
\mathcal{PT}\psi=\psi
(cf. Eq. (
10
)).

Then, according to
Gardas et al. 2016
, if the spectrum of the Hamiltonian is real, there exists a Hermitian, positive-definite operator
\eta
such that
H^{\dagger}=\eta H\eta^{-1}
(Eq. (
13
)).

Although
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
and Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
systems have different physical properties and definitions, their dynamics are governed by the same framework of open quantum systems, which we introduce in the following subsections.

II.4
The Spin-Boson Model

We consider the spin-boson model as our fundamental open quantum system
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
, which will later be extended to include
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
and Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric dynamics. In this model, the reduced system is ultra weakly coupled to a bosonic bath, thus allowing only quantum information flow and no heat exchange between the system and the environment. This constraint leads to the famous
pure decoherence
or
dephase
phenomena
Gardas et al. 2016
;
Cen and Saxena 2022
.

The total Hamiltonian is expressed as:

H=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{I},

(15)

where
H_{S}
is the Hamiltonian of the reduced system, and

H_{B}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b^{\dagger}_{k}b_{k},

(16)

is the bosonic bath Hamiltonian, and the bosonic creation and annihilation operators satisfy the canonical commutation relation:

[b_{k},b^{\dagger}_{k^{\prime}}]=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}.

(17)

The interaction Hamiltonian is generally taken as:

H_{I}=H_{S}\otimes V_{B},

(18)

with the bath operator defined as:

V_{B}=\sum_{k}\left(g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}+g_{k}^{*}b_{k}\right).

(19)

Here,
g_{k}
represents the system-bath coupling strength for the
k
-th mode of the environment.

III
Dynamics: Phase Evolution and Decoherence

In open quantum systems, phase evolution and decoherence are two fundamental yet distinct phenomena that arise from interactions with the environment
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Lindblad 1976
.

Decoherence
refers to the loss of quantum coherence between components of a superposition, which destroys interference without necessarily changing energy levels or populations. For instance, random fluctuations in a magnetic field or collisions with gas particles can turn a coherent quantum state into a classical mixture
Gardas et al. 2016
. It manifests in the suppression of off-diagonal terms (coherences), signaling a transition from quantum to classical behavior
Deffner and Lutz 2013
.
Physically, decoherence arises because the system becomes entangled with its environment, leaking quantum information into inaccessible degrees of freedom.

Phase evolution
also arises from the non-Hermitian, open system dynamics
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. We will show that it causes some phase amplification in the
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-Symmetric system, while projecting phase decay in the Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric case.

Understanding and controlling these processes is vital for quantum technologies, where environmental coupling sets the ultimate limits for coherence times, entanglement preservation, and computational fidelity. In the following subsection, we introduce the non-Hermitian Hamiltonians of our
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
and Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric qubit systems, their corresponding density matrices, and how phase evolution and coherence loss emerge within these dynamics.

III.1
Hamiltonians for
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
and Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-Symmetric Qubits :

The general non-Hermitian system Hamiltonian in matrix form can be defined as
Cen and Saxena 2022
:

H_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+i\theta&\xi+i\delta\\
-\xi+i\delta&-\alpha+i\theta\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{(Anti\,-\,$\mathcal{P}$\,$\mathcal{T}$-symmetric)}

(20)

H_{S}^{\mathcal{PT}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+i\theta&\xi+i\delta\\
\xi-i\delta&\alpha-i\theta\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{($\mathcal{P}$\,$\mathcal{T}$-symmetric)}

(21)

where
\alpha,\,\theta,\,\delta,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, with
\theta
representing the amplification rate in
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric systems and serving as a key parameter characterizing non-Hermitian behavior. In contrast, for Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric systems, the parameters
\delta
and
\xi
govern the system’s dynamical behavior.

If we take the parity operator
{\mathcal{P}}
as the Pauli matrix

\sigma_{x}
and the time reversal operator
{\mathcal{T}}
as its complex conjugate, we have both
[\mathcal{PT},H_{S}^{\mathcal{PT}}]
= 0  and
\{\mathcal{PT},H_{S}\}
=0  just satisfied.

Defining the transformation matrix:

T=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}-\alpha&-\xi-i\delta\\
\omega_{0}+\alpha&\xi+i\delta\end{pmatrix},

(22)

and using it for similarity transformation, we define:

\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{PT}}=\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\xi^{2}-\theta^{2}},

(23)

which leads to a diagonalized Hermitian Hamiltonian
h_{S}^{D}
such that:

h_{S}^{D}=TH_{S}^{\mathcal{PT}}T^{-1},

(24)

with resulting eigenvalues given by:

E_{\pm}=\alpha\pm\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{PT}}.

(25)

In this work, we focus on the regime where the eigenvalue splitting remains real. This corresponds to the parametric condition:

\delta^{2}+\xi^{2}\geq\theta^{2}.

(26)

To diagonalize the total system Hamiltonian
H
for the Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric case, we again employ the transformation from Eq. (
22
), with the resulting eigenvalue:

\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{APT}}=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\xi^{2}-\delta^{2}}.

(27)

The transformed diagonal Hamiltonian is:

H_{D}=THT^{-1}=(-\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{APT}}\sigma_{z}+i\theta)(1+V_{B})+H_{B}.

(28)

This diagonal form corresponds to the eigenvalue expansion:

H=\sum_{n}E_{n}\ket{n}\bra{n},

(29)

with
E_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus,
H
can be recast using a biorthonormal eigenbasis:

H=\sum_{n}E_{n}\ket{\psi^R_n}\bra{\psi^L_n},

(30)

where the right and left eigenvectors are defined as:

\ket{\psi^R_n}=T^{-1}\ket{n},\qquad\bra{\psi^L_n}=\bra{n}T,

(31)

and satisfy the normalization and completeness relations:

\bra{\psi^L_n}\ket{\psi^R_m}=\delta_{nm},\qquad\sum_{n}\ket{\psi^R_n}\bra{\psi^L_n}=I.

(32)

The two eigenvalues of the Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Hamiltonian (Eq. (
20
)) are given by:

E_{\pm}=i\theta\pm\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{APT}}.

(33)

In the general case of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
, prior studies
Mostafazadeh 2002
;
Bender 2007
;
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
have proposed expressing it as a sum of Hermitian and anti-Hermitian components:

H=H_{R}+iH_{I},

(34)

where

H_{R}=\frac{H+H^{\dagger}}{2},\quad H_{I}=\frac{H-H^{\dagger}}{2i}

(35)

are Hermitian operators representing the real and imaginary parts of
H
, respectively.

III.2
Dynamics of
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric qubit

Since the
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Hamiltonian (
21
) led to a diagonalized Hermitian one (
24
) under the similarity transformation (
22
), we can obtain its dynamics using the conventional Hermitian-valid formalism.

The reduced density matrix for the system is recovered as a partial trace over the environment of the overall system plus bath evolution. Subsequently, it can be expressed in terms of the reduced density matrix for the diagonalized system under normalization as

\rho_{S}(t)=\frac{T^{-1}\rho^{D}_{S}(t)(T^{-1})^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}_{S}\left[T^{-1}\rho^{D}_{S}(0)(T^{-1})^{\dagger}\right]}

(36)

\rho^{D}_{S}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{D}_{11}&\rho^{D}_{12}\\
\rho^{D}_{21}&\rho^{D}_{22}\end{pmatrix}

(37)

\rho^{D}_{S}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{D}_{11}&0\\
0&\rho^{D}_{22}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\rho^{D}_{12}(t)\\
\rho^{D}_{21}(t)&0\end{pmatrix}e^{-(\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{APT}})^{2}\gamma(t)}

(38)

\rho^{D}_{12}(t)=e^{2i\omega_{0}t}e^{-i\omega_{0}^{\mathcal{APT}}\Omega(t)},\quad\rho^{D}_{21}(t)=\left[\rho^{D}_{12}(t)\right]^{*}

(39)

where

\Omega(t)=4\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dw\,J(w)\frac{wt-\sin(w t)}{w^{2}}

(40)

and

\gamma(t)=4\int_{0}^{\infty}dw\,J(w)\frac{1-\cos(w t)}{w^{2}}\coth\left(\frac{\beta w}{2}\right)

(41)

and

J(w)=\sum_{k}|g_{k}|^{2}\delta(w-w_{k})

(42)

are respectively the function influencing phase evolution, the decoherence factor, and the spectral density of the bath
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Cen and Saxena 2022
.

Realizing that the similarity transformation does not turn the Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Hamiltonian into a Hermitian one, so we cannot use the same dynamics formalism as that of the
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
system, we utilize a different, more powerful approach to calculate the dynamics of that system.

Time-Dependent Dyson Map:

It has been shown that non-Hermitian Hamiltonians can be reformulated and re-investigated within the framework of Hermitian Hamiltonians using a time-dependent Dyson map as the key tool, as analyzed for example in
Cen and Saxena 2022
for general non-Hermitian systems, and previously in studies of
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric bosonic systems coupled to baths
Gardas et al. 2016
and
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonians
Cen and Saxena 2022
.

We will utilize the approach developed in
Cen and Saxena 2022
for general non-Hermitian systems and then specify it for our purposes in
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
and Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric systems.

First, consider a Hermitian system characterized by a diagonal Hermitian Hamiltonian
h_{D}
, with an associated density matrix
\rho_{Dh}
. Putting
\hbar
=1, the standard Liouville–Von Neumann equation for this setup is:

i\partial_{t}\rho_{Dh}=[h_{D},\rho_{Dh}],

(43)

which governs the unitary evolution of the Hermitian system
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

Turning to the non-Hermitian case, specifically the diagonalized non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{D}
, one obtains a modified Liouville–Von Neumann equation by combining the Schrödinger equation and its Hermitian conjugate:

i\partial_{t}\rho_{D}=H_{D}\rho_{D}-\rho_{D}H_{D}^{\dagger}.

(44)

This equation governs the evolution of the density matrix in the non-Hermitian framework
Mostafazadeh 2007
.

Assuming that
H_{D}
and
h_{D}
are connected through a time-dependent Dyson map
\eta
via the relation:

H_{D}=\eta^{-1}h_{D}\eta-i\eta^{-1}(\partial_{t}\eta),

(45)

where the map
\eta
connects the eigenstates of the Hermitian and non-Hermitian systems as
\ket{\phi_i}=\eta\ket{\psi_i}

Mostafazadeh 2002
;
Cen and Saxena 2022
.

Inserting this Dyson transformation into Eq. (
44
) and comparing with Eq. (
43
) yields a mapping between the density matrices:

\rho_{Dh}=\eta\rho_{D}\eta^{\dagger}.

(46)

With the spectral decomposition
\rho_{Dh}=\sum_{i}P_{i}\ket{\phi_i}\bra{\phi_i}
, it follows that

\rho_{D}=\sum_{i}P_{i}\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i},

(47)

indicating that the Dyson map preserves the probability distribution
\{P_{i}\}

Mostafazadeh 2007
.

Conversely, starting from the Hermitian Liouville–Von Neumann equation (
43
) and applying the Dyson relation (
45
) again, one obtains an alternative evolution equation:

i\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{D}=[H_{D},\tilde{\rho}_{D}],

(48)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{D}=\eta^{-1}\rho_{Dh}\eta=\rho_{D}M
, with the metric operator defined as
M=\eta^{\dagger}\eta
. This construction leads to a quasi-Hermitian Hamiltonian:

H_{Q}=H_{D}+i\eta^{-1}(\partial_{t}\eta),

(49)

which satisfies the condition

\bra{H_Q \psi_i}M\ket{\psi_i}=\bra{\psi_i}MH_{Q}\ket{\psi_i}=\bra{\phi_i}h_{D}\ket{\phi_i},

(50)

and also the quasi-Hermiticity relation
(H_{Q})^{\dagger}M=MH_{Q}

Mostafazadeh 2007
;
Cen and Saxena 2022
.

Density Matrix Evolution of the Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Qubit:

We now determine the equivalent Hermitian system using a time-dependent Dyson map. Starting from the diagonalized non-Hermitian Hamiltonian from Eq. (
28
), and choosing an ansatz for the Dyson map
Cen and Saxena 2022
:

\eta=\exp[\varphi(t)(1+V_{B})]

(51)

the transformed Hermitian Hamiltonian becomes:

h_{D}=(-\omega_{0}^{\mathrm{APT}}\sigma_{z}+i\theta)(1+V_{B})+H_{B}-\varphi\tilde{V}_{B}-\frac{\varphi^{2}}{2}\Omega_{k}+i\partial_{t}\varphi,

(52)

where

\tilde{V}_{B}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}(g_{k}a_{k}^{\dagger}-g_{k}^{*}a_{k}),\quad\Omega_{k}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}|g_{k}|^{2}.

To ensure that
h_{D}
remains Hermitian, we impose the constraint:

\partial_{t}\varphi=-\theta,

which allows us to take:

\eta=\exp[-\theta t(1+V_{B})]

(53)

The resulting Hermitian Hamiltonian is then:

h_{D}=-\omega_{0}^{\mathrm{APT}}\sigma_{z}+H_{B}-\omega_{0}^{\mathrm{APT}}\sigma_{z}V_{B}+\theta t\tilde{V}_{B}-\frac{\theta^{2}t^{2}}{2}\Omega_{k}.

(54)

We can alternatively represent
h_{D}
through a series expansion in terms of the quasi-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{Q}
:

h_{D}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}C_{G}^{(n)}(H_{Q}),

(55)

where
G=\theta t(1+V_{B})
and

C_{G}^{(n)}(O)=[G,[G,\ldots[G,O]\ldots]]

(56)

denotes the
n
-fold nested commutator between
G
and the operator
O
.

Similarly, in terms of the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{D}
, we can express
h_{D}
as:

h_{D}=-i\theta(1+V_{B})+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}C_{G}^{(n)}(H_{D})

(57)

To ensure the eigenvalue gap remains real, we constrain the system parameters to lie within the regime:

\alpha^{2}\geq\delta^{2}+\xi^{2}.

The final step is to compute the density matrix associated with Eq. (
54
), from which we can extract the decoherence characteristics of our qubit system.

We consider the qubit dynamics where the system is initially uncorrelated with the bath, which is in a thermal Gibbs state, i.e.,
Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\Omega_{B}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{B}}}{Z},\quad Z=\Tr_{B}\left(e^{-\beta H_{B}}\right),

so that the full system-bath density matrix at
t=0
is given by:

\rho^{Dh}(0)=\rho_{S}^{Dh}(0)\otimes\Omega_{B}

(58)

We assume a general form for the initial reduced density matrix of the qubit
Cen and Saxena 2022
:

\rho_{S}^{Dh}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(1+\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle)&\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle\\

(64)

\sigma_{z}(t)=\sigma_{z}

\langle\sigma_{z}(t)\rangle=\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle

(67)

IV.1
Quantum Speed Limit

Quantum Speed Limit, The Mandelstam–Tamm Bound:

In our investigation of the non-Hermitian dynamics, the quantum speed limit (QSL) is evaluated using the Mandelstam–Tamm-type bound. The Mandelstam–Tamm (MT) bound sets a fundamental lower limit on the time it takes for a quantum system to evolve between two distinguishable states. Originally derived for closed Hermitian systems, it connects the minimal evolution time to the inverse of the energy uncertainty in the system, thus reflecting a deep energy-time uncertainty relation.

As shown by Mandelstam and Tamm, and further generalized in various contexts
Mandelstam and Tamm 1945
;
Giovannetti et al. 2003
, the MT bound can be derived by bounding the time-dependent fidelity (or overlap) between the initial and evolved quantum states. Using trigonometric inequalities and spectral decomposition of the Hamiltonian, one obtains a bound on the square modulus of the survival amplitude, which leads to a minimal evolution time inversely proportional to the standard deviation of energy.

This framework has also been extended to mixed states and time-dependent (driven) dynamics using the Bures angle as a distance measure
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
. In these generalizations, the QSL is expressed in terms of the time-averaged energy variance, offering a rigorous limit even in open systems.

Non-Markovian Dynamics and Quantum Speed Limit:

Deffner and Lutz (2013) analyzed the quantum speed limit for non-Markovian systems, where the dynamics are influenced by memory effects of the environment. In non-Markovian dynamics, the rate of change of the system’s state is modified by the history of previous interactions, and as a result, the quantum speed limit can be slower than in Markovian systems. This work generalized the concept of quantum speed limits, incorporating these memory effects by modifying the expression for
\tau_{QSL}
and introducing a non-Markovian correction term that depends on the past evolution of the system’s state.

In their framework
Deffner and Lutz 2013
, the time evolution of the system state is characterized by a generalized Liouville superoperator, and the fidelity
F(\rho(0),\rho(t))
is a central quantity in computing the quantum speed limit. This fidelity captures both the instantaneous evolution and the influence of the system’s past states, thus reflecting the memory inherent in non-Markovian dynamics.

In our non-Markovian system, we adopt the Mandelstam–Tamm bound as derived from the time-local evolution of the reduced system’s coherence terms. This approach enables us to track how the intrinsic decoherence mechanisms—arising from
\mathcal{PT}
or Anti -
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{T}
-symmetry affect the minimal time scale of state evolution.
The quantum speed limit (QSL) provides an upper bound on the speed of a quantum system, evolving from one state to another. In the general framework suggested in
Deffner and Campbell 2017
, the quantum speed limit is given by the expression:

V_{QSL}=\frac{\left\|L(\rho(t))\right\|_{\text{op}}}{2\sin\mathcal{L}\cos\mathcal{L}}

(74)

\tau_{QSL}=\dfrac{(\sin\mathcal{L})^{2}}{\dfrac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,\left\|L(\rho(t))\right\|_{\text{op}}}

(75)

where
L(\rho(t))
is the Liouville superoperator that governs the time evolution of the quantum state, and
\|\cdot\|_{\text{op}}
denotes the operator norm.

The angle
\mathcal{L}
is defined as the arccosine of the fidelity between the initial state
\rho(0)
and the state at time
t
,
\rho(t)
, which is given by:

\mathcal{L}(\rho(0),\rho(t))=\arccos\sqrt{F(\rho(0),\rho(t))}

(76)

F(\rho(0),\rho(t))=\left[\text{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho(0)}\rho(t)\sqrt{\rho(0)}}\right)\right]^{2}

(77)

Here,
F(\rho(0),\rho(t))
is the fidelity function, which measures the overlap between the initial and final quantum states.

V
Entanglement Entropy of the Rényi Group

We begin by reviewing the Rényi group of entropies, a one-parameter family of generalized entanglement measures
Rényi 1961
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. These entropies are especially useful in characterizing different spectral properties of the reduced density matrix
\rho_{s}
.

V.1
Definition of the Rényi Entropy

For a normalized density matrix
\rho_{s}
, the Rényi entropy of order
q\in(0,\infty)-\{1\}
is defined as:

S_{q}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-q}\log\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^{q}].

(78)

which is a monotonically decreasing function of q.
This function reduces to the Von Neumann entropy in the limit
q\to 1

Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Müller-Lennert et al. 2013
:

S_{1}(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\log\rho].

(79)

Each value of
q
controls how sensitively the entropy measure reacts to large versus small eigenvalues of
\rho

Müller-Lennert et al. 2013
. For
q
-values between 0 and 1, it emphasizes on smaller eigenvalues of
\rho
, while
q
-values higher than 1 emphasize the larger eigenvalues.

We now explore the entropy at four distinguished values of
q
, ordered from
0
to
\infty
.

V.2
Rényi Entropy at Distinguished Orders

V.2.1
q=0
: Max-Entropy

The Rényi entropy at
q=0
captures the logarithmic rank of the density matrix:

S_{0}(\rho)=\log\mathrm{rank}(\rho).

(80)

This quantity is insensitive to the actual eigenvalues and simply counts the number of non-zero ones, weighting all of their corresponding eigenstates with equal probabilities. It reflects the maximum possible entropy or largest possible uncertainty supported by the system and is sometimes called the "max-entropy"
Müller-Lennert et al. 2013
.

V.2.2
q=1
: Von Neumann Entropy

The standard quantum entropy:

S_{1}(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\log\rho].

(81)

This measures the average uncertainty or entanglement entropy and is additive for independent systems, weighting all of their corresponding eigenstates linearly. It has deep connections to thermodynamics, quantum channel capacities, and entanglement theory
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
.

V.2.3
q=2
: Collision Entropy

At
q=2
, we find:

S_{2}(\rho)=-\log\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^{2}].

(82)

This entropy is directly related to the purity and discrimination between quantum states, and is frequently used to detect decoherence, quantify entanglement, and study complexity in many-body systems
Müller-Lennert et al. 2013
.

V.2.4
q\to\infty
: Min-Entropy

Taking the limit
q\to\infty
, the Rényi entropy becomes:

S_{\infty}(\rho)=-\log\lambda_{\max},

(83)

where
\lambda_{\max}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
. This form, called the min-entropy, has operational meaning in single-shot information theory and bounds the optimal probability of correctly guessing the outcome of a measurement
Tomamichel 2015
.
It represents the minimum surprise or maximum predictability.

In cryptography, min-entropy measures how much randomness remains even if an adversary knows everything except the most likely outcome.

V.3
Summary

Different orders of the Rényi entropy highlight different aspects of a quantum state’s spectral profile. To summarize:

These Rényi entropies form a hierarchy:
