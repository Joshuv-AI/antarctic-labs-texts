Evaluating a coaching intervention for Dementia Care Practice Recommendations in care communities: a cluster randomized controlled trial
Authors: Boeun Kim, Basia Belza, Shawn Johnson, Emily Waddington, Walter Moczygemba, Sam Fazio, Lorna Prophater
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesWithin care communities, including nursing home and assisted living settings, person-centered dementia care, outlined by the 2018 Alzheimer’s Association Dementia Care Practice Recommendations (DCPR), is foundational to quality care and improving staff outcomes. This study evaluates the effectiveness of a 6-month Care Community Coaching Program in enhancing person-centered dementia care and staff outcomes in alignment with the DCPR.Research Design and MethodsA cluster randomized controlled trial was conducted with 77 care communities and 434 staff members—227 from 38 intervention communities and 207 from 39 control communities. Outcomes included employee satisfaction (areas: job satisfaction, team building and communication, scheduling and staffing, training, and management and leadership), person-centered care practices (areas: workplace practices, individualized care and services, caregiver-resident relationships), and dementia care confidence, measured pre- and post-intervention and at 3-month follow-up. A generalized Estimating Equations model was used to estimate intervention effects.ResultsCare communities assigned to the coaching intervention showed statistically significant improvements in employee satisfaction and staff perceptions of workplace practices and individualized care. No statistically significant effects on staff perceptions of caregiver-resident relationships or on dementia care confidence were noted.Discussion and ImplicationsFindings provide direction for future research and intervention development, including examining coaching’s impact on resident quality outcomes, and incorporating skills training into future models. Collectively, findings provide evidence of the effectiveness of a Care Community Coaching Program in improving staff outcomes and person-centered practices, offering a practical path towards improving the lived experience of residents and staff in care communities.

As of 2025, an estimated 7.2 million adults aged 65 and older were living with Alzheimer’s disease in the United States, a number projected to increase in the coming years (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025). As the population of older adults living with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) grows, long-term care settings, also known as care communities, have become a key provider of dementia care. Today, 42% of residents in residential care communities (including assisted living), and 58% of long-stay nursing home residents are living with ADRD (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025). For these residents, the provision of person-centered dementia care, a philosophy of care built on cultivating relationships and tailoring care to the needs and preferences of the individual, is essential to quality care (Fazio et al., 2018a).

The realization of person-centeredness within care communities is a primary focus of the broader culture change movement, an ongoing effort to shift long-term care environments away from institutional and highly medicalized settings to more resident-driven and homelike models (White-Chu et al., 2009). The culture change movement, and the emphasis on resident choice, dignity, respect, self-determination, and purposeful living, has shown success in increasing resident psychological well-being, and, for residents living with ADRD, decreasing adverse behavioral symptoms and psychotropic medication use (Li & Porock, 2014). The benefits of embracing person-centered dementia care extend beyond residents to care staff. While empirical evidence is limited, by providing staff with effective tools to respond to the unique needs of residents living with ADRD, and aligning the provision of care with internal motivations, person-centered approaches have potential to increase staff’s sense of competence and knowledge, and reduce stress, burnout, and job dissatisfaction (Barbosa et al., 2015; Jeon et al., 2012; Spector et al., 2016).

Despite the benefits and the progress made by the culture change movement, there exists an ongoing theory-practice gap regarding person-centered dementia care in many care communities. Barriers to adopting person-centered practices include inadequate buy-in of leadership, persistent institutional task-based attitudes, ongoing resource limitations, and a general lack of knowledge surrounding person-centeredness (Danylyshen-Laycock et al., 2018; Güney et al., 2021). While training and educational materials exist, their implementation and effectiveness are limited by issues of sustainability, and an overreliance on providing time-limited education alone minus ongoing reinforcement (Caspar et al., 2016; Spector et al., 2016). The lack of sufficient and effective training to address the unique needs of persons living with ADRD impacts resident and staff well-being, contributing to the ongoing workforce crisis among long-term care communities (Pace et al., 2024).

Recognizing the need to enhance person-centered dementia care, the Alzheimer’s Association has outlined 56 specific care recommendations across ten domains of quality dementia care (Figure 1). These Dementia Care Practice Recommendations (DCPR), originally released in February of 2018 based on a comprehensive review of evidence, practices, and expert opinion, provide practical, dementia sensitive, person-centered tools adaptable across care settings (Alzheimer’s Association, n.d.a; Fazio et al., 2018b). To translate these recommendations into practice in care communities, and to move beyond time-limited transactional training approaches, the Alzheimer’s Association Care Community Coaching Program was developed. This strengths-based coaching model provides communities with the opportunity to assess their current adoption of best practices as outlined by the DCPR, and, through an action planning and implementation process, make organization-wide, measurable changes in areas of need. The effectiveness of coaching as a practical tool to implement and sustain evidence-based interventions has been previously described (Grover & Furnham, 2016; Gunderson et al., 2018; Jones et al., 2025), and the Care Community Coaching Program has shown both feasibility and acceptability within care communities (Welsch et al., 2025). However, the effectiveness of this intervention on staff outcomes has not yet been described.

To evaluate the effects of participation in the Care Community Coaching Program on staff outcomes, a cluster randomized controlled trial was conducted. Specifically, this study examined the effect of the Care Community Coaching Program on employee satisfaction, staff confidence in providing dementia care, and the delivery of person-centered practices in care communities across the state of Ohio in the United States.

Research design and methods

Study design

A cluster randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06249204) was conducted in long-term care communities located in Ohio. Each care community was assigned to either the intervention group (i.e., coaching) or the control group (i.e., training). Outcomes were assessed at pretest (T1) and at two posttests (T2 and T3) to examine the treatment effects immediately after the 6-month intervention and 3 months after the intervention was completed. This project received approval by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Washington, Seattle (STUDY00007668). All participants voluntarily consented to participate in this trial.

Randomization

Randomization was conducted using a block size of 10, stratified by care community type (nursing home vs. assisted living), to ensure balanced allocation of the two settings across the intervention and control group. This stratification helped control for potential differences between the two care environments, as nursing homes and assisted living facilities have distinct organizational structures, staffing patterns, and resident populations that could influence treatment outcomes. Given the program’s focus on organization-wide change, randomizing at the community level—rather than the individual level—was desirable. This approach was appropriate because the Care Community Coaching Program is designed to assess the adoption of best practices at the organizational level based on the DCPR. Assuming an effect size of 0.5 (Welsch et al. 2025) and an intraclass correlation coefficient ranging from 0.01 to 0.05 (reflecting correlation among staff members within a care community), a recruitment goal of 80 care communities was determined, with 4–6 staff members per care community. Accounting for an anticipated 30% attrition rate, this sample size was calculated to provide greater than 90% power. Randomization was completed prior to recruitment by a statistician who was not involved in the recruitment or intervention process. Coaches and participants were informed of their care community’s assignment only after the care community provided consent to participate.

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

Outreach for care communities was conducted by the research team via existing relationships with: local chapters of the Alzheimer’s Association; the Alzheimer’s Association Dementia Care Provider Round Table; state and national membership associations; care coaches; and through targeted outreach of nursing homes from publicly available databases, such as Definitive Healthcare and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) data. All licensed skilled nursing facilities and licensed assisted living communities in Ohio were eligible; however, 627 care communities (of 1,765 total) located in counties with low socioeconomic status, greater ethnic and racial diversity, and higher rural population density were prioritized. Participating care communities were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group and subsequently recruited an average of 4–6 staff members per community using convenience sampling, whereby each community self-selected the individual participants. Each community’s coaching team was required to include at least one: community leader (e.g., administrator, director, etc.); a team leader in memory care or dementia care; and a direct care staff member. The target of 4–6 staff members per care community was informed by prior pilot work. This mixed-group approach was intended to support the development of internal champions for practice change while minimizing additional burden on care communities experiencing workforce constraints. Individual participants were required to meet the following criteria: they were staff members (e.g., direct care staff, health and social services staff, and leadership staff) working in a licensed skilled nursing facility and/or licensed assisted living community located in the state of Ohio; were aged 18 years or older; and had documented care community leadership providing documented endorsement for staff participation. Recruitment began in November 2022 and ended in May 2024.

Intervention

Coaching group (intervention group)

Over a 6-month period, staff members from participating communities worked with a care community coach as part of the Care Community Coaching Program. The Care Community Coaching Program enables professional caregivers and organizations to assess their current adoption of best practices as related to the DCPR, and to prioritize areas of growth or focus. Each participating community had a designated team of 4–6 care team staff members who met with the coach in person for 1 hr each month over 6 months. The monthly coaching schedule was informed by prior pilot work and designed to balance intervention intensity with feasibility in long-term care settings (Welsch et al., 2025). Five coaching meetings, representing 2.3% of all coaching meetings, were conducted virtually when necessary. At the Kickoff Meeting, the coach introduced coaching; provided a project overview, outlined next steps; and obtained consent from all participants. The first coaching meeting succeeding the Kickoff Meeting, included a review of DCPR; together the coach and community team collaboratively reviewed current practices using a DCPR self-assessment tool and created action steps (goals) based on identified priorities. This tool tracks the implementation of specific practices and policies related to recommendations in each DCPR topic area. For this study, two DCPR topic areas were used, with coaches initially focusing on practice recommendations related to person-centered care—a foundational component of the DCPR—followed by workforce (Table 1). Over the next 6 months and through an action planning and implementation process, staff were guided to make organization-wide, measurable changes in the following three areas: (1) policies and procedures, (2) education and training, and (3) care practices to increase and maintain practices within each topic area as outlined by the DCPR. The coaching model employed a tailored approach that could be adaptable to each care community setting’s specific organizational context and care practices while maintaining fidelity to core intervention components.

This structure, based on the Institute for Healthcare Improvement Model for Quality Improvement (Institute for Healthcare Improvement, n.d.), enabled staff members to participate in an active planning meeting, complete action steps over the following month, and then review and adjust at the following meeting. Coaches provided ongoing support to each community via telephone and email, using strengths-based coaching techniques, and helped team members identify barriers, develop solutions, and embed the DCPR into their community’s culture. Participating staff engaged other staff through routine communication, modeling person-centered care practices, and discussing proposed practice changes during existing meetings or day-to-day care activities. Care coaching emphasizes staff members as experts in their communities’ needs, priorities, and culture. Also, the care team intentionally included both leaders who could support organizational change and frontline direct care providers who understood daily workflow challenges and had peer credibility, facilitating both leadership support and frontline engagement.

Selection and training of care coaches

Care coaches had prior experience in social work, group facilitation, working with individuals living with ADRD, and/or in long-term care settings. For this study, communities were coached by, or had the coach liaison for the control group, by the same coach who recruited them. Coaches were trained by a member of the research team on the DCPR content and the Care Community Coaching Program model, and attended an additional, intensive Coaching training held by an external organization that specializes in strength-based coaching. In addition, the strength-based coaching organization worked to develop a training specific to this type of group-coaching setting.

Coaching fidelity

Fidelity in intervention delivery was ensured through the use of the Care Community Coaching Program Fidelity Checklist, created by the Alzheimer’s Association and based in part on the Evidence-Based Program Fidelity Monitoring Checklists from the Colorado State University Prevention Research Center Implementation Toolbox (Colorado State University, n.d.). This checklist was used throughout the study and during coaching observations conducted by a member of the research team. The checklist monitors fidelity based on: qualifications of the Care Community Coaching Program coordinator (a member of the research team); qualifications and training of coaches; observed behaviors of care community coaches; and participation of care community team members in the coaching process. As required by the Care Community Coaching Program Fidelity Checklist, each coach observed at least one coaching session conducted by another coach. To additionally ensure consistent delivery, each coach performed regular check-ins throughout the project with the other coach, the coordinator, and during project team meetings.

Training group (control group)

Communities assigned to the training group received a monthly email from care coaches as a check-in, containing an Alzheimer’s Association newsletter, intended to minimize attrition. No other components of the intervention were provided by the research team during the 6-month period. Following study participation, including the 3-month post-survey, Training Group communities were provided access to the essentiALZ Alzheimer’s Association Dementia Care Training and Certification, an online training program developed with evidence from the DCPR for professional caregivers (Alzheimer’s Association, n.d.b).

Data collection and measurement

Outcomes included employee satisfaction, person-centered practices, and dementia care confidence. Outcome measures were collected from both the Coaching and Training groups at three time points: pre-intervention, immediately post-intervention, and 3 months post-intervention. Both an online and paper-based version of the survey were available, depending on participants’ preferences (e.g., technology familiarity and/or access to technology) and/or the community setting (e.g., Internet access), to increase survey response rates. There was no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups in the use of paper vs. online formats. Employee satisfaction was measured using the CMS’s Nursing Home Employee Satisfaction Survey (CMS, n.d.). This tool includes 5 topic areas: Job satisfaction (9 items), team building and communication (10 items), scheduling and staffing (4 items), training (6 items), and management and leadership (6 items). Participants responded on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Average scores overall and by topic area were calculated (range: 1–5), with higher scores indicating greater employee satisfaction. Internal consistency reliability was acceptable to good, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.85 for job satisfaction, 0.86 for team building and communication, 0.79 for scheduling and staffing, 0.90 for training, and 0.89 for management and leadership. Person-centered practices were measured using the Toolkit for Person-Centeredness in Assisted Living (Zimmerman et al., 2015). Three areas were assessed: workplace practices (23 items), individualized care and services (8 items), and caregiver–resident relationships (7 items). Participants responded on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Average scores overall and by area were calculated and then multiplied by 25. Total scores ranged from 25 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater person-centeredness. Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.91 for workplace practices, 0.85 for individualized care and services, and 0.70 for caregiver-resident relationship. Dementia care confidence was measured using the Confidence in Dementia Scale (CODE) (Elvish et al., 2014). This nine-item self-report questionnaire is scored on a 5-point scale with anchored ratings of “not able,” “slightly able,” “somewhat able,” “mostly able,” and “very able.” Total scores range from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating greater confidence in working with people with ADRD. Internal consistency reliability was excellent, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.95. In addition to these outcome measures, baseline data on care community characteristics—such as setting, number of licensed beds, and organization type—were obtained from an administrator at each care community. Demographic information (e.g., age, gender, educational attainment, and other relevant characteristics) was collected from participating staff members.

Data analysis

Participant and care community characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Baseline differences in participant and community characteristics were examined using a regression model or Chi-Squared Test, as appropriate. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) model was used to estimate the population-averaged changes in outcome measures, comparing the intervention and control groups while accounting for repeated measures (Liang & Zeger, 1986; Wang, 2014). Individual and community characteristics did not significantly differ between the two groups at baseline, except for care setting (urban, suburban, rural area) and the presence of a memory care unit within the care community. GEE models with an unstructured correlation structure and robust standard errors were fitted separately for each outcome and controlled for the presence of a memory care unit, given its potential differential influence on treatment effects. xtgee function in Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) was used to perform the analyses. All available data were included in the analysis, regardless of participants’ study completion status, using an intent-to-treat approach. Statistical significance level was determined at 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Participant characteristics and survey completion

A total of 77 care communities (39 assisted living facilities and 38 nursing homes) participated and were randomized. Five communities did not complete the study (2 from the control group and 3 from the intervention group; n = 26 participants), with an average participation period of 2.2 months (SD = 2.3) before leaving the program. Community-level withdrawal was primarily due to the time commitment required for participation, while individual non-completion was most commonly related to staff turnover. Characteristics of 76 care communities were reported in Table 2; one care community did not complete this survey. Care communities assigned to the control group were more likely to be located in rural areas, while those in the intervention group tended to be located in urban areas (p = .05). Care communities in the intervention group were also more likely to have a memory care unit compared to those in the control group (p = .02). Other characteristics, such as community type and the number of license beds, did not significantly differ between the intervention and control groups.

Discussion and implications

This study conducted a cluster randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effectiveness of the Care Community Coaching Program. A total of 434 staff members were successfully recruited across 77 care communities in Ohio, with approximately 70% retained at the 3-month follow-up. All 35 care communities assigned to the intervention group focused on implementing recommendations related to the DCPR topic area of person-centered care, of these, only 10 completed the topic area of person-centered care and then engaged with recommendations related to the DCPR topic area of workforce. Overall, data show that this coaching intervention can improve both employee satisfaction and person-centered practices in care community settings.

Following participation in the Care Community Coaching Program, care communities saw significantly positive improvements in employee satisfaction. Throughout the DCPR topic areas of person-centered care and workplace practices, DCPR recommendations emphasize fostering a supportive workplace environment, examples include implementing consistent and effective orientation and training protocols, and developing supportive leadership (Fazio et al., 2018a). The positive impact of care coaching on job satisfaction and the person-centered domain of workplace practices is therefore consistent with previous literature identifying workplace culture, supportive leadership, and adequate job orientation as contributing to job satisfaction (Aloisio et al., 2019; Chamberlain et al., 2016). Also, potentially increasing job satisfaction was the process of coaching itself. Throughout care coaching, team members with varying functional and leadership roles had the opportunity to voice their opinions and contribute personal experiences to organization decision-making processes. Such opportunities are necessary elements of empowerment (Rawson et al., 2024), a contributor to long-term care direct care worker satisfaction (Squires et al., 2015).

After the program, communities assigned to the care coaching intervention also showed statistically significant improvements in the staff-reported provision of individualized care and services, a domain of person-centered practices. Person-centered care is dependent on both organizational and individual factors (Güney et al., 2021), and person-centered interventions tailored to a community’s specific needs are known to be effective in increasing the individualization of care and the implementation of evidence-based practices (Brownie & Nancarrow, 2013; McGough & Thompson, 2024). The Care Community Coaching Program may be a particularly potent model, as the intervention involves coaches learning each community’s culture and then tailoring recommendations to expressed organizational needs over several meetings.

Care coaching had no statistically significant impact on the person-centered domain of caregiver-resident relationships. Staff perceptions of caregiver-resident relationships as assessed by the PC-PAL focus on residents’ relationships with both individual care staff and the broader community; the majority of questions focus on residents’ ability to influence their own care, schedule, and pace of care delivery (Zimmerman et al., 2015). Resident choice and self-direction in care are often in tension with the routinized environment of a care community (Palmer et al., 2018). While incorporating resident preferences into daily care is included in the DCPR topic area of person-centered care, specific recommendations regarding tailoring the daily care schedule are not, potentially limiting the impact of the intervention on caregiver-resident relationships. Future interventions could involve residents and families in coaching sessions or action planning to better center their voices and priorities. In addition, future studies should consider measuring observable relationship behaviors, such as the frequency of choice offered or personalized interactions, and incorporating qualitative interviews with residents and families rather than relying solely on staff self-report.

Furthermore, although the intervention group showed greater improvement in confidence in dementia care than the control group, the differences were not statistically significant. Staff in this study were highly experienced, with an average of 14 years of work in care communities, and their baseline confidence was already high; even small increases may reflect meaningful progress. Alternatively, the Confidence in Dementia Scale captured efficacy and general capabilities of staff when engaging with residents living with ADRD (Elvish et al., 2014). While the Care Community Coaching Program focuses on adapting organizational policies and processes to align with best dementia care practices, statistically significant improvements in staff confidence may not have been detectable by this study, as the adoption of best practices may not immediately translate to confidence in enacting said practices. Indeed, previous studies have shown that information on best dementia care practices by itself may not increase confidence, and that hands-on support and skills training being more immediately effective (Rivett et al., 2019). Other studies note the role of experience in driving dementia care confidence, and the cumulative effect of repeated training (Parveen et al., 2021; Scerri & Scerri, 2019). The absence of specific skills training in the Care Community Coaching Program, alongside ongoing staff turnover as observed by care coaches, may therefore have attenuated the ability of this coaching model to affect dementia care confidence. Future intervention designs to improve dementia care confidence should enhance the coaching model through tailored coaching based on baseline confidence levels, with foundational support for less confident staff and advanced problem solving for experienced staff, and protected time for coached practice, where coaches observe staff during actual care interactions and provide real-time feedback. This differentiated approach may better bridge the gap between knowledge of best practices and confident enactment, enabling coaches to support staff in translating organizational changes into skilled dementia care delivery.

Some coaching programs are intended to be self-sustaining interventions (Jones et al., 2025); a previous study of a consultation coaching program in long-term care describes the effects of coaching as a ripple effect, with changes in one part of an organizational system impacting the person-centeredness of others (Crandall et al., 2007). In this study, three indicators related to job satisfaction did not show significant improvement until the 3-month follow-up period, while all significant findings related to person-centered practices showed sustained improvement at both immediate and 3-month follow-up periods. While further research is needed to better understand the process outcomes of the Care Community Coaching Program, a hierarchical effect is theorized, with coaching initially impacting organizational policies and knowledge related to person-centered practices, and a delayed, secondary impact on certain elements of staff satisfaction. These results provide evidence that not only are the effects of the Care Community Coaching Program sustained beyond the intervention period, but seemingly increase in effect as the culture of a community continues to align itself with the DCPR.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, when compared to previous analyses of the long-term care workforce in the United States, staff members of communities participating in both the control and intervention arms were disproportionately white, held a college degree, and identified as full-time employees (Chidambaram et al., 2024), potentially impacting generalizability. An additional factor potentially affecting this study’s generalizability is that communities had the opportunity to self-select into the study prior to randomization, and leadership approval for staff participation was a requirement for enrollment. This may have resulted in communities and leadership in both arms being predisposed to engage in quality improvement and implement suggested changes impacting staff outcomes. Leadership investment in training and quality improvement is often a driver of program sustainability (Caspar et al., 2016; Danylyshen-Laycock et al., 2018). Future research is needed to understand the potential relationship between a community’s pre-disposition towards quality improvement and the impact of care coaching on staff, and if such a relationship exists, to explore potential mechanisms for increasing leadership investment in the process. Second, despite leadership endorsement, flexible scheduling, and hybrid coaching options, sustained staff participation was approximately 70%. This likely reflects common long-term care challenges including staffing shortages, competing demands, and high turnover. Although we oversampled by 30% to account for anticipated attrition, suboptimal engagement may have reduced intervention dose and attenuated effects. Staffing inconsistency resulting from scheduling conflicts or staff turnover may have affected the effectiveness of the Care Community Coaching Program. Care coaches reported that consistent attendance at meetings was sometimes difficult to achieve, with communities experiencing turnover and coaching team members being unable to attend sessions while filling gaps left by absent peers. These limitations are unsurprising given the ongoing long-term care workforce crisis (Pace et al., 2024), and the proximity of this project’s start to the COVID-19 pandemic, with recruitment beginning in 2022. Staffing issues may explain not only the lack of certain findings, but also the relatively slow pace of DCPR engagement, with less than a third (n = 10) of communities completing both DCPR topic areas of person-centered care and workplace practices over the course of 6 months. Future research should test strategies to improve retention and participation, including protected staff time, sustained leadership accountability, designated backup Core Team members to ensure continuity during turnover. Third, although both study groups received monthly contact from care coaches, there was a substantial dose imbalance between the groups. The coaching group received interactive coaching sessions with ongoing support available as needed, whereas the training group received informational newsletters only. This difference in intervention intensity limits our ability to attribute findings solely to the coaching approach vs. the overall dose of contact and support. Fourth, the person-centered practice measure was originally developed for assisted living settings. Although the selected domains and items were reviewed by the study team and considered relevant to both assisted living facilities and nursing homes, some aspects of person-centeredness that are particularly important in nursing home settings may not have been fully captured. Finally, given the nature of long-term care communities, the potential for cross-contamination between the control and intervention sites, such as a travelling nurse or direct care worker working at multiple communities, must be acknowledged. While a cluster randomized design was employed, and care coaches took active steps to avoid this by ensuring that coaching teams contained members with no prior exposure to the Care Community Coaching Program, the potential for this scenario remains.

Conclusion and directions for future research

This study provides the first evidence for the effectiveness of a Care Community Coaching Program in improving employee satisfaction and enhancing staff perceptions of person-centered practices. Other person-centered dementia care coaching interventions have reported positive impacts on resident experiences and outcomes (Crandall et al., 2007; Jones et al., 2025). Future research is necessary to understand the impact of this Care Community Coaching Program on resident outcomes, such as satisfaction, quality of life indicators, and resident and family perceptions of relationships with staff. Given the importance of tailored education in implementing health care practices, and previous successes in incorporating in-service training into coaching models (Crandall et al., 2007; McGough & Thompson, 2024), the feasibility of adapting the Care Community Coaching Program to include specific skills training is another potential area for future research. Suggested training includes EssentiALZ or ECHO, both designed to provide interactive and self-paced training based on best dementia care practices, including the DCPR (Alzheimer’s Association, n.d.b; Alzheimer’s Association, n.d.c). Further study may also examine both the feasibility and efficacy of a tele-coaching model; while care coaches anecdotally noted that the occasional use of virtual coaching meetings (2.3% of all visits) was effective, they raised concerns about the impact of a fully virtual model on coaches’ relationship with care communities. Future research should also examine the longer-term effects of the Care Community Coaching program beyond the 9-month follow-up period, particularly because the coaching model was intended to support sustained practice change. Longer follow-up is needed to determine whether improvements are maintained after active coaching ends and whether they translate into broader changes in organizational culture, staff practices, resident outcomes, and family engagement. In conclusion, evidence indicates that the Care Community Coaching Program improves employee satisfaction and enhances person-centered dementia care. By highlighting its promise and providing meaningful directions for future research, this study encourages the continued and expanded implementation of care coaching throughout long-term care communities.
