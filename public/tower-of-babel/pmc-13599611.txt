Child with genetically confirmed Prader-Willi syndrome
Authors: Menbere Gebreanania Kahssay, Katherine Oyieke, Charlotte Hoybye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare genetic disorder caused by loss of paternal genes on chromosome 15. It is characterised by hyperphagia, obesity, endocrine deficiencies and neurodevelopmental challenges. We report on a boy in his early teens presenting with short stature, early-onset obesity and clinical features consistent with PWS. Genetic testing confirmed maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15. Management included food restriction and increased physical activity, resulting in modest improvement. This case highlights the diagnostic and management challenges of PWS in resource-limited settings and underscores the value of comprehensive genetic testing in clarifying phenotypic variations.Keywords: Genetics, Pituitary disorders

Background

Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused by the absence of expression of paternally inherited genes in the chromosome 15q11–q13 region.1 It affects approximately 1 in 15 000 newborns2 3 and is characterised by hypotonia and failure to thrive in infancy, development of hyperphagia at age 4–6 years leading to risk for early-onset obesity, neurodevelopmental delays, behavioural challenges, short stature, small hands and feet and hormonal deficiencies.3 Three main genetic subtypes exist: paternal deletion of 15q11–q13 in 60%–70% of cases, maternal uniparental disomy (UPD) in 30%–40% and imprinting defects in 2%–5%.3 Behavioural challenges, including temper outbursts and autism features, occur frequently particularly in patients with UPD.3 4 Hormone deficiencies are common, with growth hormone deficiency and hypogonadism being almost universal.4 PWS is considered a hypothalamic disease, and the hypothalamus is highly involved in the complex regulation of hunger and satiety. Studies have examined genes in the PWS region of chromosome 15, as well as transcription factors, central and peripheral secreted hormones, neuropeptides and proteins without identifying a specific cause.5

Treatment of PWS consists of a restricted and supervised diet, regular physical activity and replacement of hormonal deficiencies.6 Recently, diazoxide choline extended release (DCCR) has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of hyperphagia in PWS.7 However, access of DCCR is limited to the USA.

In low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), many cases of PWS remain undiagnosed or unreported, resulting in limited awareness of the disorder and its associated complications. A helpful tool could be the Holm consensus diagnostic criteria.8 The Holm consensus diagnostic criteria were developed in 1993 before molecular genetic testing became widely available.8 The criteria were designed to recognise the characteristic features seen in PWS across different ages, and findings were divided into major and minor criteria, with major criteria counting as 1 point and minor criteria as 0.5 point. In children younger than 3 years, at least 5 points, including four major criteria, are required for diagnosis of PWS, whereas in older children and adults at least 8 points including five major criteria are required. The major criteria include severe neonatal hypotonia, feeding difficulties and failure to thrive in infancy, followed later by rapid weight gain and obesity, characteristic facial appearance, hypogonadism, developmental delay or intellectual disability, hyperphagia with food-seeking behaviour, and the presence of a chromosome 15q11–q13 abnormality. Minor criteria include decreased fetal movements, weak infant cry, behavioural rigidity, tantrums, obsessive-compulsive traits, sleep apnoea, short stature, hypopigmentation, small hands and feet, thick saliva, articulation problems and skin picking. Additional supportive findings include reduced vomiting, high pain threshold, scoliosis, osteoporosis and unusual proficiency with jigsaw puzzles.

The present case is noteworthy because it reports a child with PWS caused by maternal UPD and highlights the diagnostic and management challenges of rare genetic disorders in resource-limited settings.

Case presentation

A pre-pubertal boy in middle childhood was referred by a developmental specialist to a paediatric endocrinology clinic at a tertiary specialist unit in sub-Saharan Africa, with concerns of short stature, severe obesity, developmental delay and behavioural challenges. The patient had since early childhood a slow and very decreased growth velocity and increasing obesity. A rapid increase in weight noted at 5 years of age was exacerbated after becoming wheelchair-bound due to bilateral hip and knee pain for the past 2 years. He was delivered at term via caesarean section following a failed induction. Antenatal history was significant for polyhydramnios. His birth weight was 3.8 kg, with Apgar scores of 7, 9 and 10. He was fed on both breast milk and formula until 6 months of age, but his early feeding was poor and suboptimal. By the age of 3 years, his appetite had notably improved, and he would frequently wake up during the night requesting food. From this point, his weight gain progressed.

He achieved independent sitting at 6 months and walked independently at 18 months. He began speaking a few words around the age of 2 years. At 6 years, he developed generalised seizures confirmed by electroencephalography, requiring long-term antiepileptic therapy. Follow-up was inconsistent due to socioeconomic constraints.

Currently, his expressive language is limited to a few words; however, it has improved with recently initiated speech therapy. He has challenges with mobility owing to his obesity and he uses a wheelchair. His sleep pattern is highly irregular and inverse, sleeping only 2 hours at night and 5 hours during the day and snores while sleeping. His growth velocity has been poor. He has no polyuria or polydipsia. He is engaged in passive activities like watching television and playing with blocks during the night.

He had a 2-year history of bilateral hip and knee pain, progressing to non-ambulation. Pelvic X-ray and orthopaedic evaluation revealed bilateral avascular necrosis (AVN) of the hips.

There was no family history of obesity or genetic syndromes. His appetite is reported as good, with excessive demands for large breakfasts but occasional meal skipping. There was no reported visual loss, acanthosis nigricans or other significant skin changes.

On examination, he had weight for age >97th centile and height for age below the 3rd percentile, with a body mass index of 69 kg/m². Dysmorphic features included almond-shaped eyes, a short neck and small hands and feet. Genital examination showed a buried penis with sexual development appropriate for age (Tanner stage I). He had central obesity and hepatomegaly, but no acanthosis nigricans or skin hyperpigmentation. The patient uses a wheelchair and exhibited a waddling gait when attempting assisted standing. Liver function tests, including bilirubin, γ-glutamyl transferase, alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase, were within normal limits. Alkaline phosphatase was appropriate for age. Total protein was marginally elevated, with a normal albumin level. Renal function and serum electrolytes, including sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate, were normal.

Glycaemic assessment showed a normal fasting blood glucose and haemoglobin A1c, excluding diabetes mellitus. Morning serum cortisol was within the reference range, making adrenal insufficiency or hypercortisolism unlikely. Serum leptin concentration was within the expected range.

Thyroid function tests demonstrated a mildly elevated free triiodothyronine (FT3) with normal free thyroxine (FT4) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), a pattern not suggestive of overt thyroid dysfunction and therefore not requiring intervention. Comprehensive endocrine testing (including insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1) and growth hormone stimulation) could not be completed due to resource limitations.

Brain CT was normal. MRI was not performed due to anaesthesia risk. Overall, the investigations did not identify alternative endocrine or metabolic causes contributing to the clinical presentation and supported conservative management.

Genetic testing with chromosomal microarray identified maternal UPD of chromosome 15, consistent with PWS (ISCN nomenclature: arr[GRCh37] 15×2 hmz denoting two homozygous copies of chromosome 15). Further genetic testing included a monogenic obesity panel, including sequence and deletion/duplication analysis, aiming to identify pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants contributing to the patient’s phenotype. This test identified a BDNF c.172T>C p.Ser58Pro heterozygote variant on chromosome 11 of uncertain significance (WAGR (Wilms tumour, Aniridia, Genitourinary anomalies, and intellectual disability (formerly referred to as mental retardation))/WAGRO (WAGR syndrome with obesity) syndrome overlap?).

Retrospectively Holm’s diagnostic criteria for the clinical diagnosis of PWS8 was calculated. The patient had a clinical score of 11 points, exceeding the diagnostic threshold for children older than 3 years. Major criteria identified included neonatal hypotonia with poor feeding in infancy, rapid-onset obesity with hyperphagia, developmental delay/intellectual impairment, characteristic facial features, including almond-shaped eyes, hypogonadism with buried penis and short stature with poor growth velocity. Minor criteria included sleep disturbance with snoring, behavioural challenges, speech delay and small hands and feet.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis focused on causes of early onset hyperphagia, abnormal weight gain and associated endocrine features. Common endocrine disorders associated with obesity were considered first. Hypothyroidism was excluded by normal TSH and FT4 levels, and the isolated mild elevation in FT3 was not consistent with clinically significant thyroid disease. Cushing syndrome was considered; however, normal morning cortisol levels and the absence of cushingoid features made this diagnosis unlikely.

Hypothalamic and pituitary disorders were also evaluated, as these may present with hyperphagia, obesity and hormonal abnormalities. Normal electrolyte balance preserved adrenal function, and the absence of arginine vasopressin deficiency (formerly diabetes insipidus) or progressive neurological symptoms reduced the likelihood of a structural hypothalamic or pituitary lesion.

Monogenic and syndromic causes of obesity were considered, particularly those associated with hyperphagia. Leptin deficiency and leptin resistance syndromes were considered; however, serum leptin levels were within the expected range, making these diagnoses less likely. Other genetic obesity syndromes, such as Bardet–Biedl syndrome, were not supported due to the absence of characteristic clinical features such as polydactyly, retinal dystrophy or renal abnormalities.

The combination of early onset hyperphagia, behavioural difficulties, and evolving endocrine involvement raised suspicion for PWS. This was also more likely considering the clinical presentation than alternative metabolic or endocrine disorders and PWS was subsequently confirmed on genetic testing, establishing the final diagnosis.

Treatment

Multidisciplinary care was implemented, involving paediatric endocrinology, neurology, orthopaedics, nutrition and developmental paediatrics. Nutritional management included vitamin D supplementation and a structured dietary plan emphasising reduced carbohydrate intake and increased plant-based protein sources, developed by a dietitian. Adherence has been limited due to financial constraints, restricted access to recommended foods and behavioural challenges associated with hyperphagia.

Growth hormone therapy was not initiated due to cost constraints, as government insurance schemes did not cover treatment and no local pharmaceutical assistance programmes were available. Evaluation for sleep apnoea is ongoing, with planned polysomnography when feasible. Orthopaedic management includes regular follow-up for AVN of the hips, physiotherapy, hydrotherapy and occupational therapy to optimise mobility. Speech therapy and educational support services have been continued.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient has now been followed for 12 months since the PWS diagnosis. Nutritional management included vitamin D supplementation and a structured dietary plan. Adherence has been limited due to financial constraints, restricted access to recommended foods, and behavioural challenges associated with hyperphagia.

During the follow-up period, the patient has demonstrated meaningful improvements: sleep patterns have become more regular, he is now able to stand independently and mobilise short distances with support, remains seizure-free and shows improved expressive communication. Daily activities, including engagement in play and therapy sessions, have increased, though he remains largely dependent on wheelchair due to obesity and limited mobility.

Surveillance follows international guidelines for PWS, including periodic endocrine assessments, nutritional monitoring, growth and body composition evaluation and behavioural management. Laboratory monitoring includes lipid profile, fasting glucose and liver function tests, with intervals adjusted based on clinical status.

The patient remains alive, with ongoing multidisciplinary care. No complications related to the underlying diagnosis have resulted in hospitalisation during the follow-up period. Continued long-term monitoring and support are planned to address obesity-related complications, endocrine deficiencies, and developmental needs, consistent with current guideline recommendations for PWS.

Discussion

PWS is a complex, rare, genetic multisystem disease. Early diagnosis is important for initiating correct care, including detecting hyperphagia to avoid development of morbid obesity and associated comorbidities. A pronounced muscular hypotonia at birth is strongly associated with PWS and involvement of the neonatologist is key for an early diagnosis. Our patient was diagnosed in middle childhood, morbidly obese and with hepatic steatosis. In LMICs, limited awareness of rare genetic obesity syndromes and poor access to genetic testing frequently delays diagnosis and intervention. This can have serious consequences in conditions such as PWS. In the case described, late diagnosis of PWS led to uncontrolled hyperphagia, extreme early-onset obesity, and eventual dependence on a wheelchair.

The degree of intellectual disability varies widely among individuals with PWS and can hinder school achievements and social interactions and necessitate specialised educational strategies and support systems.9 Behavioural challenges, including obsessive-compulsive behaviours and social difficulties, are prevalent in individuals with PWS. Individuals may exhibit difficulties with impulse control, social interactions and emotional regulation. These behavioural issues can be exacerbated by cognitive deficits, leading to increased frustration and social isolation. Genotype–phenotype studies indicate that individuals with UPD are more likely to exhibit behavioural challenges, whereas those with paternal deletions generally demonstrate greater cognitive impairment.9 Many of these behavioural challenges were seen in this patient.

The estimated prevalence of seizures in PWS is 10%–30%, which is higher than in the general population where it is 1%.10 The epigenetic alterations affecting the imprinted genes on chromosome 15 may influence neuronal development and excitability, potentially increasing the risk of seizure activity.10 Additionally, structural brain abnormalities, such as alterations in cortical thickness or white matter integrity, have been observed in some individuals with PWS, which may further contribute to the risk of epilepsy.11 This patient had epilepsy, but available neuroimaging was not sufficient to ascertain the integrity of brain white matter.

Genetic causes should be considered in any child presenting with early-onset obesity, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, or developmental delay. The Holm’s diagnostic criteria are no longer considered the definitive diagnostic method because modern DNA methylation testing can identify more than 99% of cases of PWS.3 Today the main value of Holm’s criteria is helping clinicians recognise PWS and deciding when genetic testing should be performed. The criteria may also be helpful in settings where access to genetic testing is limited, as they support nutritional management, endocrine assessment and family counselling. However, affordable or subsidised genetic testing programmes should be advocated for in LMICs, alongside clinician education to improve early case identification and management. Children with genetic causes of obesity require highly individualised, multidisciplinary care involving endocrinologists, nutritionists, geneticists, orthopaedic surgeons and developmental paediatrics. Family and caregiver education on hyperphagia control strategies, structured food access and activity promotion is essential to reduce health risks. Early, coordinated and comprehensive management can prevent or delay irreversible complications and improve long-term outcomes.
