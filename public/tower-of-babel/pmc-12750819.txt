Autoimmune thyroiditis promotes breast cancer progression: exploratory role of thyroid hormone receptor beta signaling disruption
Authors: Sha Li, Qi-Lan Jiang, Hong-Zhen Gong, Yang Zeng, Jun Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between autoimmune thyroiditis (AITD) and breast cancer has been frequently reported in epidemiological studies. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, particularly regarding whether AITD-related antibodies contribute to breast cancer progression. We hypothesized that thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRβ) autoantibodies may mediate this association by disrupting THRβ signaling in breast cancer cells.MethodsAn AITD-breast cancer comorbidity model was established by simultaneously inducing EAT and orthotopically transplanting breast cancer cells in NOD/ShiLtJ mice. Histological evaluation (H&E staining), ELISA, and immunohistochemistry were used to assess thyroiditis induction and tumor characteristics. In vitro, 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU), Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), scratch, and Transwell assays were performed to evaluate the effects of THRβ-reactive antibody on the proliferation and migration of breast cancer cells. The antagonistic effect of the selective THRβ agonist Resmetirom was also examined. Additionally, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases were analyzed to investigate THRβ expression and its prognostic relevance in breast cancer tissues.ResultsIn vivo experiments demonstrated that the AITD mouse model exhibited significantly elevated serum levels, accompanied by increased tumor formation rate and proliferative activity of breast cancer cells. In vitro, directly promoted proliferation and migration of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, while the selective THRβ agonist Resmetirom effectively counteracted these effects. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that THRβ expression was downregulated in breast cancer tissues, and higher expression was associated with improved disease-free survival.ConclusionThis study suggests that THRβ-reactive antibody may promote breast cancer progression by disrupting THRβ signaling, indicating a mechanistic link between AITD and breast cancer comorbidity. Targeting THRβ signaling with agents such as Resmetirom may hold therapeutic potential in this immune-related oncologic context.Keywords: Autoimmune thyroiditis, Breast cancer, THRβ-reactive antibody, Tumor microenvironment, Mouse model

Introduction

The potential association between breast cancer and thyroid disorders has been recognized for decades, encompassing both non-autoimmune thyroid diseases (e.g., thyroid carcinoma) and autoimmune thyroid diseases (AITD), such as Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and Graves’ disease. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated a significantly increased risk of thyroid disorders among breast cancer patients, with a notably higher prevalence of AITD in particular [1–4]. Meta-analyses further revealed that serum levels of thyroid autoantibodies—such as anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab)—are generally elevated in breast cancer patients [5], along with an increased risk of developing hypothyroidism [6].

Interestingly, several studies have suggested that the presence of thyroid autoantibodies may be associated with more favorable clinical outcomes in breast cancer patients [7–11]. For instance, breast cancer patients with higher levels of TPO-Ab often exhibit lower tumor cell proliferation indices and a reduced risk of axillary lymph node metastasis [12]. One possible mechanism is that breast and thyroid epithelial cells may share certain antigens, such as the sodium/iodide symporter (NIS) and thyroid peroxidase (TPO) [13, 14], which may elicit cross-reactive immune responses in the context of AITD. These responses may, in turn, enhance immune-mediated clearance of breast cancer cells [10]. It is worth noting that different types of thyroid autoantibodies may exhibit functional heterogeneity, and their specific roles in breast cancer progression remain to be elucidated.

Beyond the comorbid association, thyroid functional status itself may influence breast cancer risk. Epidemiological studies have shown that hyperthyroidism is associated with an increased incidence of breast cancer, whereas hypothyroidism may exert a protective effect to some extent [15, 16]. These effects are mediated primarily through thyroid hormone receptors (THRs), particularly thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRβ), which functions as a tumor suppressor in normal breast tissue by regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis [17, 18]. Notably, THRβ expression is frequently downregulated in breast cancer, and this downregulation may contribute to the loss of its tumor-suppressive function [19, 20]. Moreover, patients with thyroid carcinoma also exhibit a significantly elevated risk of developing breast cancer [21, 22].

Among various thyroid disorders, autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD)—particularly Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT)—is considered a key immune-related risk factor for breast cancer [23, 24]. Previous studies have indicated that individuals with HT are at higher risk of developing breast cancer compared to the general population, with a markedly elevated prevalence [24–27]. Interestingly, however, some investigations have suggested that breast cancer patients who are positive for thyroid autoantibodies may experience improved clinical outcomes, including prolonged disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) [9].

Taken together, there exists a complex and multilayered association between autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) and breast cancer, the underlying mechanisms of which remain incompletely elucidated—particularly due to a lack of experimental evidence at both the cellular and animal levels. From an experimental standpoint, developing an animal model that simultaneously recapitulates features of both AITD and breast cancer remains technically challenging. On the one hand, immunocompetent mouse strains commonly used to model autoimmune diseases tend to reject xenogeneic tumor cells due to intact immune surveillance. On the other hand, immunodeficient mice, while widely used for tumor transplantation studies, lack the immunological context required for the induction of AITD. Therefore, establishing an animal model that combines AITD-associated immune background with the capacity for breast tumor formation is pivotal for investigating the pathophysiological link between these two diseases. In this study, we employed non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice, which are predisposed to autoimmune disorders, as a foundation to construct a comorbidity model by orthotopically implanting breast cancer cells. This in vivo approach was complemented by in vitro functional assays and public database analyses to systematically evaluate the impact of AITD on breast cancer initiation and progression. Furthermore, we explored a potential autoantibody-mediated mechanism, aiming to provide new experimental evidence for the immunological crosstalk between breast cancer and autoimmune thyroid disorders. Specifically, we hypothesized that THRβ-reactive antibodies generated in AITD may promote breast cancer progression by disrupting THRβ's tumor-suppressive signaling, and tested this hypothesis through integrated in vivo, in vitro, and bioinformatics approaches.

Materials and methods

Establishment of experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) mouse model

This study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval No. SWMU20231001). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China, 2001, Order No. 55) and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, 2011).

Female NOD/ShiLtJ mice aged 3–4 weeks and weighing 13 ± 2 g were purchased from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. Mice were randomly assigned to either the model group or the control group, with 30 mice in each group. The control group received regular drinking water, while the model group was provided with high-iodine water containing 0.1% sodium iodide to induce experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) [28]. Additionally, to establish a breast cancer xenograft model, 100 μL of cell suspension containing 1 × 10⁶ MDA-MB-231 or MCF-7 breast cancer cells was injected into the fourth mammary fat pad on the left side of each mouse. After 4 weeks of treatment, all animals were euthanized, and tissue samples were collected. During the experimental period, mice were monitored regularly for behavioral status, coat condition, food and water intake, and changes in body weight.

At the study endpoint, 10 mice were randomly selected from each group. Peripheral blood was collected for measuring serum levels of thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab) and thyroglobulin antibody (TG-Ab). Bilateral thyroid tissues were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess histopathological changes and validate the successful induction of the EAT model.

Cell culture and passaging

The human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Subculturing was performed at 80–90% confluence using standard trypsinization protocols. MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative) and MCF-7 (ER-positive) were selected to represent the major molecular subtypes of human breast cancer, enabling investigation of AITD-breast cancer comorbidity mechanisms with direct clinical relevance and database support (e.g., TCGA).

Establishment of breast cancer xenograft model in mice

MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and resuspended to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. Concurrently with the induction of the experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) model, both EAT model mice and control mice were randomly divided into three subgroups (n = 10 per subgroup): control group, MDA-MB-231 xenograft group, and MCF-7 xenograft group (Fig. 1). For tumor implantation, 100 μL of the prepared breast cancer cell suspension containing 1 × 10⁶ cells was injected subcutaneously into the left fourth mammary fat pad of each mouse to establish the breast cancer xenograft model.

Given the requirement to simultaneously establish experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) and breast cancer xenografts within a single animal model, we selected human breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7) based on established epidemiological evidence linking AITD with human breast cancer and the availability of comprehensive database resources for THRβ signaling analysis. We then selected NOD/ShiLtJ mice—which possess functional T and B lymphocytes—as the experimental foundation to balance immune competence with tumorigenic potential. This strain is widely used in autoimmune disease research and is capable of developing thyroid lesions resembling AITD. Although highly immunodeficient mice such as NOD/SCID and NSG offer clear advantages for xenograft tumor establishment, their lack of a functional immune system precludes the induction of AITD. Therefore, they are not suitable for modeling the comorbid state required in this study. In contrast, NOD/ShiLtJ mice exhibit moderate tumor-forming capacity during the early stages of breast cancer cell implantation. However, as their immune system matures, some tumors begin to regress spontaneously between weeks 4 and 8, suggesting that the host immune system is capable of mounting an effective response against xenogeneic tumor cells. To ensure that tumors remained in a stable growth phase and to obtain reliable experimental data, the study duration was limited to 4 weeks.

Starting from day 7 after cell implantation, tumor dimensions—including the longest diameter (L) and the shortest diameter (W)—were measured every 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × L × W2. Body weight, general condition, and tumor growth status of the mice were also monitored and recorded throughout the experiment. At 4 weeks post-implantation, all mice were euthanized; peripheral blood was collected, and thyroid glands along with breast tumor tissues were harvested for subsequent serological assays, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and immunohistochemical analyses.

Serological analysis

At the end of the animal experiment, peripheral blood was collected from the mice via cardiac puncture. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum, which was then stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Serum concentrations of thyroid-related autoantibodies—including anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab) and anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TG-Ab)—were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Fankew Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Additionally, we used a commercial ELISA kit from the same manufacturer with recombinant THRβ protein as the capture antigen to detect serum antibodies capable of binding to THRβ. For clarity, we refer to these as "THRβ-reactive antibodies" throughout this manuscript. It is important to note that this assay detects antibody reactivity to recombinant antigen but does not establish the presence of naturally occurring THRβ autoantibodies, as such antibodies have not been characterized in the published literature.

All ELISA assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, standard curves and serum samples (in duplicate) were added to ELISA plates. After incubation with enzyme-conjugated reagent (100 μL, 37 °C, 1 h) and washing, chromogenic solutions A and B (50 μL each) were added and incubated for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped, and optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations of TPO-Ab, TG-Ab, and THRβ-reactive antibody were calculated based on the standard curve.

Histological assessment of thyroid tissue and evaluation of lymphocytic infiltration

Following euthanasia, thyroid glands were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h. Tissues were then processed through standard dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding procedures. Paraffin blocks were sectioned consecutively at a thickness of 4–5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope to assess histopathological changes, with a focus on follicular structural integrity, epithelial cell arrangement, and the extent of lymphocytic infiltration.

The degree of lymphocytic infiltration was semi-quantitatively scored. For each section, five random high-power fields (× 400 magnification) were selected, and the proportion of the area infiltrated by lymphocytes relative to the total thyroid tissue area was estimated. The scoring criteria were as follows: 0 (no infiltration), 1 (< 10% infiltration), 2 (10–30%), 3 (30–50%), and 4 (> 50%). All slides were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists under blinded conditions, and the average score was used as the final infiltration index.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of breast cancer xenografts

At the end of the animal experiment, mice were euthanized, and breast cancer xenograft tissues were promptly dissected. Tumor formation in each mouse was recorded to calculate the tumor incidence rate. The longest diameter (L) and the shortest diameter (W) of each tumor were measured using a vernier caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × L × W2, to assess tumor growth.

Tumor specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h, followed by standard dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding procedures. Paraffin blocks were sectioned consecutively at a thickness of 4–5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope to evaluate histological characteristics, including cellular architecture, nuclear atypia, mitotic figures, and the invasive boundary between tumor and surrounding tissues.

To assess the proliferative activity of tumor cells, Ki-67 immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on paraffin-embedded tumor sections using standard protocols (deparaffinization, antigen retrieval with citrate buffer, and peroxidase blocking). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Ki-67 antibody (Abcam, UK; 1:200), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, DAB visualization, and hematoxylin counterstaining. Five random high-power fields (× 400) were analyzed per section to calculate the Ki-67 labeling index (positive cells/total cells × 100%). Evaluations were conducted independently by two experienced pathologists under blinded conditions.

In vitro assays: effects of thyroid-related autoantibodies on breast cancer cell behavior

To further investigate the potential impact of thyroid-related autoimmune factors on the biological behavior of breast cancer cells, a series of in vitro functional experiments were designed and conducted. Various thyroid-related antibodies were introduced into the cell culture system to assess their regulatory effects on cell viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion, aiming to preliminarily explore their potential immune-mediated mechanisms of action. MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 human breast cancer cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization and counted. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. After allowing the cells to adhere, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM, and different thyroid-related antibodies were added at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL. The antibodies used included: anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab; Proteintech, USA), anti-lactoperoxidase antibody (LPO-Ab; Santa Cruz, USA), anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TG-Ab; Solarbio, China), anti-thyroid hormone receptor beta antibody (THRβ-Ab; Abcam, UK), and anti-sodium/iodide symporter antibody (NIS-Ab; Proteintech, USA). The control group received equal volumes of antibody-free base medium. All experimental conditions were performed in parallel replicates. After 24 h of antibody treatment, a series of functional assays were performed: the CCK-8 assay was used to assess cell viability, the EdU incorporation assay was used to evaluate DNA synthesis and proliferation, the wound-healing assay was used to measure cell migration rate, and the Transwell assay was employed to evaluate cell migration and invasion capabilities.

The rationale for selecting these antibodies is as follows: Elevated levels of TPO-Ab and TG-Ab are the most characteristic immunological markers of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and are widely used in the clinical diagnosis and research of AITD. Lactoperoxidase (LPO), one of the major peroxidases expressed in breast tissue and breast cancer cells, shares high structural homology with thyroid-specific peroxidase (TPO), raising the possibility of antigenic cross-reactivity that may lead to immune-mediated interference [29, 30]. A growing body of evidence suggests that thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRβ) exerts tumor-suppressive effects in breast cancer, with high THRβ expression being significantly associated with favorable prognosis and prolonged disease-free survival [17, 31]. The sodium/iodide symporter (NIS), while predominantly expressed in thyroid follicular cells, is also highly expressed in lactating mammary tissue and in breast cancer cells [32], indicating a shared antigenic background between the thyroid and the breast. Taken together, these thyroid-related antibodies are not only involved in the pathogenesis of AITD, but may also act as immunological bridges linking thyroid disorders and breast cancer through antigen cross-recognition or interference with key signaling pathways.

It is important to note that, due to experimental constraints, all thyroid-related antibodies used in this study were commercially available products, most of which are derived from recombinant antigen fragments or non-native conformations. These antibodies may differ from naturally occurring autoantibodies in the serum of AITD patients in terms of structure and binding affinity. Therefore, in this study, these antibodies were employed as immunological intervention tools to preliminarily simulate the autoimmune background associated with AITD and to evaluate its effects on the behavior of breast cancer cells. Although this approach has certain limitations, it remains valuable for exploratory purposes under conditions of well-controlled experimental variables and good assay reproducibility.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization and counted. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well, with five replicates per group. After cells had adhered, they were treated with thyroid-related neutralizing antibodies or the drug Resmetirom for 24 h. Following treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 1 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader to quantify cell viability.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU (5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay. MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization and counted. Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, with three replicates per group. After cell adherence, thyroid-related neutralizing antibodies or the THRβ agonist Resmetirom were added to the wells for 24-h treatment.

Following treatment, EdU staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RiboBio, China). A working solution of EdU (final concentration: 10 μM) was added to each well and mixed with an equal volume of culture medium, followed by incubation for 2 h. After incubation, cells were fixed and permeabilized, and then incubated with Click reaction solution for fluorescent labeling. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342.

Cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73, Japan), and five random fields per well were selected for analysis. The number of EdU-positive cells was counted, and the EdU incorporation rate (percentage of EdU-positive cells) was calculated as an indicator of proliferative capacity.

Transwell invasion assay

To evaluate the effects of thyroid-related antibodies or Resmetirom on the migration and invasion abilities of breast cancer cells, a Matrigel-coated Transwell invasion assay was performed. MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were treated with antibodies or Resmetirom for 24 h, then harvested by trypsinization, counted, and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL.

Transwell inserts with 8 μm pore size (Corning, USA) were precoated with Matrigel (diluted 1:8; 50 μL per insert) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow solidification, thereby mimicking the extracellular matrix. Then, 200 μL of serum-free cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while 600 μL of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. The chambers were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 24 h.

After incubation, the inserts were removed and gently washed twice with PBS. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed using a cotton swab. The number of invaded cells on the lower surface was counted under a light microscope in five randomly selected high-power fields (× 400) to assess invasive capacity.

THRβ agonist intervention assay

The THRβ-specific agonist used in this study, Resmetirom (Cat. No. HY-12216, purity ≥ 99.9%; MedChemExpress, USA; trade name: Rezdiffra), was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2024, for the treatment of moderate to severe fibrosis in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), marking the first-in-class approval of such an agent [33].

In the experiment, MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization, counted, and seeded into either 96-well plates (for viability assays) or 6-well plates (for proliferation, migration, and invasion assays). Once cells had adhered, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM, and cells were assigned to either a control group (0 μM) or treatment groups receiving different concentrations of Resmetirom (25, 50, 75, and 100 μM). All conditions were tested in parallel replicates, and the treatment duration was uniformly set to 24 h.

Following treatment, a series of assays were conducted to evaluate the biological effects of Resmetirom on breast cancer cells: cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay; proliferative capacity was measured via EdU incorporation; migration rate was evaluated by wound healing assay; and invasive potential was assessed using the Transwell invasion assay.

By applying Resmetirom at various concentrations, we systematically analyzed its regulatory effects on the malignant phenotype of breast cancer cells and, through functional in vitro validation, further explored the potential mechanistic role of THRβ signaling in breast cancer progression.

Analysis of THRβ differential expression in breast cancer based on TCGA and GEO datasets

To investigate the expression pattern and prognostic relevance of thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRβ; gene symbol: THRB) in breast cancer, bioinformatic analyses were conducted using publicly available datasets. First, RNA-seq expression data in HTSeq-FPKM format were obtained from the “TCGA-BRCA” dataset via the TCGA portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), comprising 112 normal breast tissue samples and 1070 breast cancer samples. In addition, the GSE93601 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE93601), which includes 172 normal and 205 breast cancer tissue samples based on the GPL10558 platform. All expression data were normalized using log2(FPKM + 1) transformation in R (version 4.2.3). Differential expression analysis was performed using the “limma” package, and boxplots were generated with “ggplot2”. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to assess statistical significance of expression differences between groups. To further evaluate the prognostic value of THRβ expression, disease-free survival (DFS) analysis was conducted using TCGA breast cancer data. Patients were stratified into high and low THRβ expression groups based on the median expression value. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed using the “survival” and “survminer” packages, and hazard ratios (HRs) along with P values from the log-rank test were calculated to determine the statistical association between THRβ expression and DFS outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances by the Levene test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired t-test or Welch’s t-test when variances were unequal; comparisons among multiple groups employed one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Games–Howell post hoc tests as appropriate. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Repeated tumor-volume measurements were analyzed by a mixed-effects model (REML) with Geisser–Greenhouse correction. Correlations between variables were assessed by Pearson or Spearman methods depending on data distribution. All tests were two-tailed and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was performed with five independent biological replicates.

Results

High-iodine intake successfully induced experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) in mice

After four weeks of continuous administration of high-iodine water containing 0.1% sodium iodide, mice showed no significant difference in body weight compared to the control group. However, some animals exhibited mild abnormal signs such as coarse fur and reduced activity. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed that thyroid follicles in the EAT model group were disorganized, with partial follicular shrinkage or disappearance, accompanied by prominent lymphocytic infiltration and the formation of lymphoid follicle-like structures. In contrast, thyroid tissues from the control group displayed intact architecture, regular follicular morphology, and no evident inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 2A). Semi-quantitative scoring of lymphocytic infiltration further confirmed that the proportion of inflammatory cells in the thyroid tissue was significantly higher in the EAT group (Fig. 2B). Serological analysis showed that serum levels of thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab) and thyroglobulin antibody (TG-Ab) were markedly elevated in the EAT group and in EAT mice bearing breast cancer xenografts compared to controls (Fig. 2C, D). Moreover, the level of thyroid hormone receptor beta antibody (THRβ-Ab) was also significantly increased in the serum of EAT mice (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these results indicate that the experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) model was successfully established by high-iodine induction, characterized by typical histopathological features and elevated thyroid-specific autoantibodies.

Experimental autoimmune thyroiditis promotes tumorigenesis and growth of breast cancer xenografts

In the breast cancer xenograft model, EAT significantly enhanced both tumorigenic capacity and growth rate. Tumor formation rates increased from 16 to 45% (EAT + MDA-MB-231, P < 0.05) and from 20 to 27% (EAT + MCF-7) compared to respective controls. At 4 weeks, tumor volumes in EAT groups were significantly larger than controls (P < 0.01 for both cell lines) (Fig. 3A, n = 10 per group).

In preliminary pilot experiments, we observed that the subcutaneous tumor volume in mice peaked at the fourth week after cell inoculation, followed by spontaneous shrinkage or complete regression in some animals. This finding suggests that the host immune system may progressively eliminate xenografted tumor cells as it matures. Therefore, we set the experimental endpoint at 4 weeks to ensure data consistency.

At this 4-week endpoint, the average tumor volumes in the EAT-combined groups were significantly larger than those in their respective control groups (Fig. 3A, right). Histologically, tumors in the EAT groups exhibited more disorganized architecture, with irregular cell arrangement, pronounced nuclear atypia, increased mitotic figures, and evident invasive growth patterns (Fig. 3B). Ki-67 immunohistochemistry revealed significantly higher proliferative indices in EAT groups compared to controls (P < 0.01 for EAT + MDA-MB-231; P < 0.0001 for EAT + MCF-7), indicating accelerated tumor progression (Fig. 4, n = 5 per group).

Effects of thyroid-related antibodies on breast cancer cell viability and proliferation in vitro

EdU assays revealed that treatment with TPO-Ab and THRβ-reactive antibody significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, as indicated by a higher percentage of EdU-positive cells compared to controls (Fig. 5A and C). In contrast, lactoperoxidase antibody (LPO-Ab), thyroglobulin antibody (TG-Ab), and sodium/iodide symporter antibody (NIS-Ab) showed no effects on cell proliferation or migration, demonstrating the specificity of THRβ-reactive antibody effects.

Consistently, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that TPO-Ab and THRβ-reactive antibody significantly increased cell viability after 24 h of treatment, with OD values markedly elevated in both cell lines relative to controls (Fig. 5B and D). No significant differences were detected in the LPO-Ab, TG-Ab, or NIS-Ab groups.

Collectively, these findings suggest that TPO-Ab and THRβ-reactive antibody can significantly promote breast cancer cell viability and proliferation in vitro, whereas the other three antibodies exhibited no appreciable effects. Specifically, significantly increased EdU-positive cells (P < 0.01 for MDA-MB-231, P < 0.001 for MCF-7) and cell viability (P < 0.01 for both lines) after 24 h.

Effects of thyroid-related antibodies on the migration and invasion abilities of breast cancer cells in vitro

Transwell invasion assays revealed that treatment with TPO-Ab and THRβ-reactive antibody significantly enhanced the invasive capacity of breast cancer cells, as evidenced by an increased number of invaded cells compared to the control group (Fig. 6A and D), indicating a promotion of cell migration. No significant differences were observed in the LPO-Ab, TG-Ab, or NIS-Ab groups (data not shown).

THRβ agonist resmetirom inhibits proliferation and viability of breast cancer cells in vitro

To further investigate the functional role of the THRβ pathway in breast cancer cells, we treated MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cell lines with the THRβ-specific agonist Resmetirom and assessed its effects on cell proliferation and viability. EdU incorporation assays showed that Resmetirom significantly inhibited the proliferation of breast cancer cells at concentrations of 50 μM and above, with a dose-dependent reduction in the percentage of EdU-positive cells as drug concentration increased (Fig. 7A and C). Consistently, CCK-8 assays revealed that Resmetirom significantly reduced cell viability in both cell lines, with more pronounced inhibitory effects observed at 75 μM and 100 μM (Fig. 7B and D). These findings indicate that Resmetirom effectively suppresses the viability and proliferation of breast cancer cells. In addition, the aforementioned migration assays (Fig. 6) demonstrated that Resmetirom markedly impaired cell migratory capacity, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in targeting multiple malignant phenotypes of breast cancer.

THRβ is downregulated in breast cancer tissues and its low expression is associated with poor prognosis

Analysis of the TCGA-BRCA dataset (including 112 normal breast tissues and 1,070 breast cancer tissues) revealed that the expression level of the THRβ gene was significantly lower in breast cancer tissues compared to normal breast tissues. This finding was further validated by the GSE93601 dataset from the GEO database, which included 172 normal and 205 breast cancer samples (Fig. 8A). Moreover, survival analysis indicated that breast cancer patients with lower THRβ expression had shorter disease-free survival (DFS), suggesting that reduced THRβ levels may be associated with unfavorable prognosis (Fig. 8B).

Its downregulation or functional interference by autoantibodies may contribute to breast cancer initiation and progression.

Discussion

Recent epidemiological studies have reported that levels of thyroid autoantibodies are significantly elevated in patients with breast cancer compared to the general population [5], and the comorbidity between autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) and breast cancer has been increasingly recognized [24]. However, this association appears to exhibit a complex dual nature. On the one hand, individuals with AITD have a higher risk of developing breast cancer; on the other hand, breast cancer patients who are positive for thyroid autoantibodies often exhibit better prognoses, including improved disease-free survival and overall survival [9, 23]. This paradoxical phenomenon may be attributed to the co-expression of shared antigens (such as NIS and TPO) on the surface of both breast and thyroid cells [13, 14], potentially triggering cross-immune responses that enhance immune recognition and clearance of breast cancer cells [10].

Thyroid hormones (THs) and their receptors (THRs) exert complex regulatory effects in breast cancer. Both clinical and experimental studies have shown that hypothyroidism is associated with a reduced incidence of breast cancer, whereas hyperthyroidism correlates with a higher incidence, accelerated progression, and increased aggressiveness of the disease [15, 34, 35]. Triiodothyronine (T3) has been demonstrated to activate the MAPK/ERK pathway and promote phosphorylation of ERα, thereby enhancing the proliferation of breast cancer cell lines such as MCF-7 and T47D [36, 37]. In contrast, other studies have reported that T3 can induce apoptosis in breast cancer cells and suppress the expression of mitosis-related genes [38], suggesting a bidirectional regulatory role that may depend on hormone concentration and microenvironmental context. Notably, THs exert their effects primarily through two nuclear receptor subtypes, THRα and THRβ. Among them, THRβ is considered a tumor suppressor in several solid malignancies, including breast cancer, thyroid cancer, and lung cancer [39]. Decreased THRβ expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients [40]. The signaling pathways regulated by THRβ mainly include the AKT/PI3K and JAK/STAT pathways [39, 41, 42]. While T3 can exert context-dependent effects on breast cancer cells, our findings focus on antibody-mediated disruption of signaling. The pro-tumorigenic effects observed with and the inhibitory effects of the agonist Resmetirom together suggest that maintaining functional signaling is critical for tumor suppression, independent of systemic thyroid hormone status.

Our findings collectively support a novel mechanism by which AITD may promote breast cancer progression through THRβ signaling interference. In vivo, EAT mice with elevated THRβ-reactive antibody levels exhibited increased tumor formation, volume, and Ki-67 expression. In vitro, THRβ-reactive antibody enhanced proliferation, viability, and migration of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, effects that were reversed by the selective THRβ agonist Resmetirom. Complementary bioinformatics analysis confirmed downregulation of THRβ in breast cancer tissues and linked its high expression to better prognosis. These data establish THRβ-reactive antibody as a potential oncogenic factor and outline a mechanistic framework from AITD-induced antibody production to THRβ-dependent modulation of tumor behavior (Fig. 8C). Potential mechanisms include: antibody interference with hormone binding or receptor dimerization, antibody-induced receptor degradation, or disruption of THRβ-cofactor interactions. While direct molecular evidence of binding to THRβ and subsequent disruption of receptor signaling awaits further investigation, the convergent functional, pharmacological, and bioinformatics evidence collectively support this proposed mechanism.

The specificity of these findings is noteworthy. Although LPO-Ab, TG-Ab, and NIS-Ab are frequently elevated in autoimmune thyroid disease, they produced no detectable effects on breast cancer cell proliferation or migration in vitro. In contrast, THRβ-Ab consistently enhanced both proliferative (EdU) and migratory (scratch and Transwell) activity, whereas other antibodies—such as TPO-Ab—primarily increased cell viability in CCK-8 assays without promoting genuine proliferation. This divergence between metabolic activity and proliferative behavior suggests that certain antibodies may influence cellular metabolism without conferring tumor-promoting capacity. Collectively, these in vitro findings argue against non-specific immune activation and indicate that THRβ-targeted autoimmunity plays a distinctive role in the AITD–breast cancer association, likely through disruption of THRβ’s tumor-suppressive signaling.

The potential mechanisms underlying the downregulation of THRβ expression in experimental autoimmune thyroiditis (EAT) warrant further investigation. Several possible explanations have been proposed: First, the chronic inflammatory microenvironment may suppress THRβ transcription or translation [43]. Second, patients with AITD often present with low T3 levels, and thyroid hormone deficiency may reduce tissue expression of THRβ via negative feedback mechanisms. In contrast, supraphysiological levels of T3 or T4 may also induce self-suppression of THRβ through mechanisms such as ubiquitin-mediated degradation and transcriptional repression [44]. Chronic autoimmune stress may further contribute to increased methylation of the THRB gene promoter or dysregulation of histone modifications, leading to transcriptional silencing [45]. Structural damage to follicular epithelial cells may also reduce the expression of functional THRβ. Even in early-stage hyperthyroidism or during exogenous T4 therapy, compensatory downregulation of THRβ may occur as a feedback response.

This study represents an initial effort to establish a comorbidity animal model integrating autoimmune thyroiditis (AITD) and breast cancer. To simultaneously induce AITD and support xenograft tumor formation in the same host, we employed NOD/ShiLtJ mice, which retain functional T and B lymphocytes, as opposed to using fully immunodeficient NOD/SCID mice. This design provided a practical compromise between maintaining immune competence for AITD induction and enabling tumor engraftment. During the experiment, we observed that some tumors began to regress or even disappear spontaneously after week 4, suggesting the onset of immune-mediated tumor clearance as the mice matured. Informed by these findings and preliminary data, we predefined the experimental endpoint at 4 weeks to capture the peak of tumor progression while minimizing immunological interference. Although this model offers a feasible framework for studying the interplay between AITD and breast cancer, it also has limitations. Future refinements could include the use of humanized immune mouse strains with better immunological control to enhance model stability and translational relevance.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our functional assays and pharmacological rescue experiments support the hypothesis that THRβ-reactive antibodies interfere with THRβ signaling, direct molecular evidence demonstrating physical interaction between and THRβ (e.g., co-immunoprecipitation or immunofluorescence colocalization) and assessment of downstream signaling components (e.g., Western blot or qPCR of THRβ target genes) were not performed in this study and will be essential for future validation. Additionally, although Resmetirom treatment reversed the pro-tumorigenic effects observed in THRβ-Ab–treated cells, we did not perform combination experiments (Resmetirom + THRβ-Ab) to directly demonstrate competitive inhibition or functional rescue, which would provide stronger mechanistic evidence for THRβ signaling interference. Second, the specific molecular mechanisms—whether through direct receptor binding, suppression of transcriptional activity, or modulation of downstream pathways (AKT, JAK)—remain to be fully elucidated. Third, antibody concentrations used in vitro may not fully recapitulate the in vivo context, warranting future dose–response studies. Fourth, the use of human breast cancer cell lines in partially immunocompetent NOD mice limits the duration of tumor observation.

Future research directions include: (1) molecular validation using co-immunoprecipitation or surface plasmon resonance to confirm binding; (2) comprehensive signaling pathway analysis; (3) clinical validation exploring the correlation between serum levels and breast cancer features in AITD patients to assess its potential as a clinical biomarker; (4) therapeutic investigation of THRβ agonists such as Resmetirom for AITD-breast cancer comorbidity.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the potential mechanistic link between autoimmune thyroiditis (AITD) and breast cancer at both the animal and cellular levels. The experimental results demonstrated that in AITD model mice, THRβ-reactive antibody levels were significantly elevated and accompanied by enhanced tumorigenic capacity, increased proliferative activity, and augmented migratory potential of breast cancer cells. In vitro experiments further confirmed that THRβ-reactive antibody promotes the viability and aggressiveness of breast cancer cells, while the THRβ agonist Resmetirom could partially antagonize these effects at appropriate concentrations. Analysis of TCGA and GEO datasets revealed that THRβ expression is markedly downregulated in breast cancer tissues, and its higher expression is associated with better patient prognosis. Integrating findings from animal models, cell-based assays, and bioinformatics analyses, this study proposes that THRβ-reactive antibody may contribute to breast cancer progression in the context of AITD by interfering with the THRβ signaling pathway, offering novel insights into immune regulation and potential therapeutic targets in breast cancer.
