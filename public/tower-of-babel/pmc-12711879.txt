The interferon-inducible MxB large GTPase attenuates hepatitis B virus replication by activating the RIG-I innate immunity signaling pathway
Authors: Masazumi Onuki, Jun Inoue, Kotaro Doi, Masashi Ninomiya, Akitoshi Sano, Mio Tsuruoka, Kosuke Sato, Satoko Sawahashi, Keishi Ouchi, Kengo Watanabe, Mark A. McNiven, Atsushi Masamune
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The innate immune response has been reported to be perturbed by hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. We hypothesized that the induction of the IFN-inducible dynamin family member, myxovirus resistance protein 2 (MxB), might support an innate immune response against HBV via mitochondrial alterations. The objective of this study was to elucidate the mechanism by which MxB suppresses HBV replication. Knockdown of MxB in HBV-expressing HepAD38 cells induced morphological changes in mitochondria. These cells exhibited a substantial increase in the release of enveloped HBV particles, as determined by immunoprecipitation or sucrose density-gradient centrifugation. The expression of envelope proteins L/M/SHBs was increased in the cells, and HBsAg in the culture supernatant was also increased by 4–10 fold. When cells were stimulated with poly(I: C), the downstream signaling of RIG-I including the mRNA of type I and III IFNs was suppressed. Mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) clustering, which is required for this pathway, was inhibited by MxB knockdown. In conclusion, these findings provide support for a new model implicating MxB-directed HBV inhibition initiated by downstream RIG-I signaling that could promote the formation of MAVS clusters in the mitochondria. Enhancement of this pathway could be a potential therapeutic option for suppressing HBV infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27678-3.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major health concern worldwide because of the potential risk of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma despite the availability of effective vaccines1. According to the latest estimation by the World Health Organization (WHO), the global seroprevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) was 3.8%, and 296 million people were living with chronic HBV infection in 20192. Although effective anti-HBV agents, nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs), and interferons (IFNs) are available, HBsAg clearance, a goal of anti-HBV therapies, is difficult to achieve3and long-term administration of NAs is required for a substantial proportion of patients. While many clinical trials of novel drugs for HBV are ongoing4, there are still no clinically available drugs other than NAs and IFNs.

When HBV infects hepatocytes, the virus establishes a life cycle by utilizing cellular functions such as transport, transcription, and translation5. After HBV internalization through its receptor, sodium-taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP)6 covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) are formed in the nucleus. Several mRNAs are transcribed from cccDNA, and viral proteins such as envelope proteins, large/middle/small hepatitis B surface proteins (L/M/SHBs) that are detected as HBsAg, and hepatitis B core protein (HBc) are translated. The nucleocapsid is composed of HBc, and the viral genome is considered to be enveloped with L/M/SHBs on a multivesicular body. Dane particles, a mature form of HBV particles, are released from hepatocytes5.

Innate immunity is the first line of defense against many infectious pathogens7. For example, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) bind to specific receptors and trigger a signaling pathway that leads to IFN induction, that induces antiviral interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) and cytokines. In viral infections, viral RNA or DNA are common PAMPs that are detected by pathogen recognition receptors in the cytosol8. Detection of viral dsRNA by retinoic acid-inducible gene-I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs), such as RIG-I and melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5), causes conformational changes in these proteins, leading them to the RLR adaptor protein, the mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein9. The caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD) in RIG-I and MDA5 interacts with the CARD domain in MAVS. This interaction leads to aggregation of MAVS and activates downstream signaling10. In HBV infection, a lack of effective innate immunity is considered to have functional consequences promoting chronic infection11. HBV proteins including hepatitis B x (HBx), HBV polymerase, HBc, and hepatitis B e antigen have been reported to attenuate the innate immunity via different targets12. However, the mechanisms by which HBV evades innate immune recognition remain unclear.

Myxovirus resistance protein 2 (MX2, also designated MxB), a dynamin-like large GTPase, has been reported to have antiviral effects on some viruses such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)−1, herpesviruses, and hepatitis C virus (HCV)13–16. Recently, MxB has been shown by others to act as an IFN-inducible host restriction factor that decreases HBV RNA levels while indirectly impairing cccDNA formation17. Interestingly, MxB has also been reported to be associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane where it appears to play an important role in the morphology, function and genomic stability of hepatocellular mitochondria18. Based on its capacity to expel the mitochondrial genome into the surrounding cytoplasm, we hypothesized that IFN-induced MxB could act as a key factor in the mitochondria in the antiviral signaling cascade. This study investigates the potential role for MxB in HBV infection models in vitro and examined MxB expression in human samples. We find that MxB promotes the formation of MAVS clusters in the mitochondria, enhances RIG-I signaling, which leads to the inhibition of the Dane particle release.

Methods

SiRNA and plasmids

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting human MxB (siGENOME Human MX2, SMARTpool siRNA) and non-targeting (NT) control siRNA were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Another siRNA targeting MxB (siMxB #1)15, which was used for validation, was purchased from Nihon Gene Research Laboratories (Sendai, Japan). The MxB-pEGFP N1 construct (MxB-GFP) has been previously described18.

Antibodies

Anti-MxB polyclonal antibody was obtained at Mayo Clinic, MN, USA, as described previously18. Another anti-MxB polyclonal antibody for immunohistochemistry was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Anti-HBc polyclonal antibody was purchased from DAKO (Carpinteria, CA). The anti-HBs horse polyclonal antibody was kindly provided by the Institute of Immunology (Tokyo, Japan). The anti-MAVS monoclonal antibody was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA). Anti-interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), anti-TANK-binding protein (TBK1), anti-phospho-IRF-3, and anti-phospho-TBK1 polyclonal antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA).

Cell culture and transfection

Human hepatoma HepG2 cells were cultured as previously described19. The HBV-expressing stable cell lines HepG2.2.15 and HepAD38 (a gift from Christoph Seeger at Fox Chase Cancer Center) were incubated as previously described20,21. HepG2.2.15.7 cells, which were derived from HepG2.2.15 cells and stably express HBV at high levels, were kindly provided by Dr. Koichi Watashi of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases (Tokyo, Japan). The HBV-permissive cell line HepG2-hNTCP-C422 was kindly provided by Dr. Koichi Watashi. HepG2.2.15, HepG2.2.15.7, and HepAD38 cells were seeded on collagen-coated plates or coverslips. Transfection of siRNA was performed using 6 µl of RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) for a 6-well plate, and cells were washed the following day. Two days later, the culture supernatant and cells were harvested. Transient transfection of plasmids was performed using 7.5 µg of TransIT-LT1 (Mirus Bio LLC, Madison, WI) for a 6-well plate the day after cell seeding, and the cells were washed the following day. Two days later, the culture supernatants and cells were harvested. In vitro-transcribed RNA was transfected using a TransIT mRNA transfection kit (Mirus Bio LLC) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Clinical liver samples

Human liver samples were obtained from 97 patients who underwent liver biopsy between December 2013 and October 2022 at the Tohoku University Hospital. Patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB, n = 35), chronic hepatitis C (CHC, n = 39), or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, n = 19) were included. The inclusion criteria for CHB and CHC were serum HBsAg positivity and serum HCV RNA positivity, respectively, for at least six months. MASLD inclusion criteria were the presence of hepatic steatosis, as detected by any imaging tests, and the presence of metabolic complications, such as obesity, hyperglycemia, hypertension, or dyslipidemia. Patients with other liver diseases or HIV infection were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tohoku University (approval numbers 2016-1-280, 2020-1-1106, and 2024-1-012), and was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Western blotting

For Western blotting, cells were lysed in NP buffer (137 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 10% glycerol, 2mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-base [pH 8.0], and complete protease inhibitors [Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN]). Soluble protein (40 µg) was resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking with Amersham ECL Blocking Agent (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA), the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, washed, and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. Signals were detected using Amersham ECL Prime Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva).

Northern blotting

To detect transcripts of HBV, Northern blotting was performed as described previously23 with modifications, using digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled full-length HBV DNA probe synthesized with a PCR DIG probe synthesis kit (Roche Diagnostics).

Real-time PCR and CLIA using culture supernatant

Total DNA was extracted from 100 µl of the supernatant using a QIAamp Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and 1 µl of the extracted DNA solution was subjected to real-time PCR using StepOne Plus (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) to quantify HBV DNA in the culture supernatant, as previously described19. The HBsAg levels in the culture supernatant were quantified by chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) using the ARCHITECT HBsAg Reagent Kit (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL).

Immunoprecipitation of enveloped viral particles

To quantify the Dane particles, immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed prior to real-time PCR, as described previously24. Briefly, the culture supernatant was incubated with the polyclonal anti-HBs antibody at 4 °C for 20 h, and after the addition of Protein G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA), it was incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 0.1 M glycine HCl (pH2.2) was added. After centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 1 min, the supernatant was neutralized with NaOH and subjected to HBV DNA quantification as described above.

Real-time PCR for mRNA quantification

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or human liver samples using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A human liver sample was finely minced with a sterile needle in a tube before RNA extraction. cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HBV precore mRNA and pregenomic RNA were quantified using the Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and previously described primers24. Primers PCP (5’- GGT CTG CGC ACC AGC ACC-3’) and BC1 (5’- GGA AAG AAG TCA GAA GGC AA-3’) were used as precore mRNA, and primers PGP (5’- CAC CTC TGC CTA ATC ATC-3’) and BC1 were used to detect both precore mRNA and pregenomic RNA, and primers for glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were used as internal controls. MxB mRNA was quantified with primers MxB-F (5’-AAG CAG TAT CGA GGC AAG GA-3’) and MxB-R (5’-TCG TGC TCT GAA CAG TTT GG-3’), as described previously16. The primers used for the quantification of IFNA4, IFNB1, IFNL1, and IFNL2-3 were described previously25.

Immunofluorescence labeling and microscopy

Mitochondria in the cells on coverslips were labeled with CellLight Mitochondria-RFP, BacMam 2.0, (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Antibody labeling was performed as previously described18. Briefly, the cells were rinsed with PBS and fixed with 2.5% formamide in PIPES buffer for 20 min. After rinsing with PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS was added to permeabilize the cells for 2 min, followed by rinsing with PBS. After blocking for 1 h at 37 °C, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 2 h at 37 °C and rinsed with PBS. The cells were then incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in a blocking buffer for 1 h at 37 °C and rinsed with PBS and distilled water. Cells on coverslips were mounted on glass slides using ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence micrographs were captured using an LSM780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a 63× objective and Zen software (Carl Zeiss), as previously described26 and images were adjusted using Adobe Photoshop software (Adobe, San Jose, CA). The circularity (4π × area/perimeter^2) and the aspect ratio (major axis/minor axis) of mitochondria, and the number of MAVS clusters were quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) liver biopsy tissues were used for MxB staining. Antigen activation was performed using Bond Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) at 100 °C for 20 min. A polyclonal anti-MxB antibody (dilution 1:50) was used as the primary antibody and detected with Bond Polymer Refine Detection (Leica Biosystems). Images were captured using a BZ-X810 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) and the staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ/Fiji27.

Sucrose density gradient centrifugation

Sucrose density gradient centrifugation was performed to determine the buoyant density of the viral particles in the culture supernatants as previously described24. Centrifugation was performed using a CP80WX (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan) with 5 ml tubes. Fractions (100 µl) were collected from the top, and DNA was extracted from 10 of 100 µl and subjected to real-time PCR for HBV DNA. Two of the 100 µl fractions were subjected to dot blot using anti-HBs antibody, and the signal levels were quantified as HBsAg.

Semi-denaturing detergent gel electrophoresis

Semi-denaturing detergent gel electrophoresis (SDD-AGE) was performed to evaluate the aggregation of MAVS as previously described28. Briefly, the cells were scraped in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 100 mM N-ethylmaleimide. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in cell lysis buffer (PBS with 100 mM N-ethylmaleimide, 10 µg/mL aprotinin, 10 µg/mL leupeptin, 5mM NaF, 1 mM activated Na3VO4, 2 mM disodium 4-nitrophenyl phosphate, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate) and sonicated on ice. After centrifugation, an equal amount of cell lysis buffer containing 0.1% SDS was added. The samples were electrophoresed on a 1.5% agarose gel at 100 V and 4 °C. The proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane and the MAVS signal was detected using western blotting.

In vitro transcription of RNA

A stem-loop RNA structure, called “epsilon (ε)” in the 5’-end of the HBV pgRNA was transcribed in vitro from an HBV DNA fragment (nt 1818–1966) using a T7 promoter and a MEGAscript T7 transcription kit (Invitrogen). The RNA was then purified using a MEGAclear transcription clean-up kit (Invitrogen).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test unless otherwise indicated, and P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Effects of MxB knockdown on mitochondrial morphology and HBV replication

As a first step, the morphology of mitochondria was observed after MxB knockdown in both HBV-expressing cells HepAD38 (Fig. 1A, B) and non-expressing cells HepG2 (Fig. S1). In both cell types, the shape of mitochondria was altered after MxB knockdown. Similar to a previous report18, mitochondria in MxB-depleted cells were deformed and fragmented. The deformity and the fragmentation of mitochondria were quantified as circularity and aspect ratio. Then, the circularity was significantly higher in MxB-depleted cells, and the aspect ratio was significantly lower in those cells, showing that mitochondria became round-shaped and fragmented after MxB knockdown (Fig. S2). In control cells, MxB was localized to both mitochondria and the nucleus, while in MxB-depleted cells, some nuclear MxB staining remained but was absent from deformed mitochondria (Fig. 1A, B). These results support the premise that MxB in the mitochondria is required for the maintenance of mitochondrial morphology in both HBV-expressing and non-expressing cells.

Changes in HBV replication after MxB knockdown were analyzed using HepG2.2.15.7 and HepAD38 cells. HBsAg levels were significantly higher (4–10 fold) in both cell lines with reduced MxB levels compared to control cells (Fig. 2A) while HBV DNA levels were slightly higher in MxB knockdown cells (Fig. 2B) as well as HBeAg levels (Fig. S3A). The difference of HBV DNA level was significant in HepG2.2.15.7 cells, but not in HepAD38 cells. To address the off-target effects, we used another siRNA (siMxB #1) instead of siGENOME Human MX2. Although the knockdown efficiency was not strong, but we observed a similar tendency in L/M/SHBs in the cells (Fig. S4A) and in HBsAg in the culture supernatant (Fig. S4B) when using both HepG2.2.15.7 and HepAD38 cells. Importantly, we observed that in HBV infection models using HepG2-hNTCP-C4 cells (Fig. 2C), both HBsAg and HBV DNA levels were significantly higher in MxB knockdown cells on days 7–13 post infection (Fig. 2D, E). In addition, the significant increase of HBsAg was observed in PLC/PRF/5 cells, which express HBsAg from the integrated HBV DNA (Fig. S3B).

To observe changes in the buoyant density of HBV-associated particles in MxB altered cells, sucrose density gradient centrifugation was performed. In control cells, there were two peaks of HBV DNA: a lower density peak (fraction 7) corresponding to the mature Dane particles, and a higher density peak (fraction 13) corresponding to the naked core particles (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the HBV DNA level in the Dane particle peak was higher than that in the naked core particle peak (Fig. 3B), suggesting that MxB knockdown increased the number of Dane particles. To test this premise further, Dane particle levels were evaluated by real-time PCR for HBV DNA after IP with an anti-HBs antibody. Then, the HBV DNA levels were significantly higher in both HepG2.2.15.7 cells and HepAD38 cells following MxB knockdown (Fig. 3C). We observed similar tendency using siMxB #1 in HepAD 38 cells (Fig. S4C). Similarly, the HBV DNA levels after IP were significantly higher in the infection model using HepG2-hNTCP-C4 cells exposed to MxB knockdown (Fig. 3D). Western blotting showed that MxB knockdown increased the levels of L/M/SHBs but not HBc in both HepG2.2.15.7 cells and HepAD38 cells (Figs. 4A, B, S5 and S6). The overexpression of wild-type GFP-MxB decreased levels of L/M/SHBs, and the effect was smaller with the GTPase-defective K131A mutant of MxB (Fig. 4C and S7). This suggests that the GTPase activity of MxB is required for the HBV suppression, which is consistent with a previous report17. The increased levels of L/M/SHBs caused by MxB knockdown were partially rescued by the overexpression of the wild type GFP-MxB (Figs. 4C and S7). In addition, Northern blotting using total RNA from both HepG2.2.15.7 cells and HepAD38 cells showed an increase of 2.4/2.1 kb mRNA expressing L/M/SHBs, but not 3.5 kb mRNA (Fig. 4D).

Reduced MxB levels lead to changes in the RIG-I and MAVS signaling pathways

As MxB knockdown alters mitochondrial morphology, we tested for changes in mitochondrial-centric innate immune signaling upon HBV infection. We focused on the RIG-I signaling pathway because HBV has been reported to be sensed by RIG-I through its interaction with the double-stranded portion of HBV RNA25. In control cells treated with poly(I: C)-mimicking double-stranded RNA, the phosphorylation of TBK1 was observed and was markedly reduced in MxB knockdown cells (Figs. 5A and S8). Similarly, the phosphorylation of IRF3 induced by poly(I: C) stimulation was reduced in knockdown cells (Figs. 5B and S8). Furthermore, members of the downstream IFN signaling cascade was evaluated, the increased mRNA levels of type I and III IFNs after poly(I: C) stimulation were significantly reduced in MxB knockdown cells (Fig. 5C). In addition, we transfected a stem-loop RNA structure, called “epsilon (ε)” in the 5’-end of HBV pgRNA, which was reported to be recognized by RIG-I25, into HepG2 cells. Then, the e RNA increased the mRNA levels of type III IFNs, and the induction was significantly reduced by MxB knockdown (Fig. 5D).

To further define the role of MxB in RIG-I signaling pathway, the clustering of MAVS on mitochondria was analyzed. MAVS clustering has been reported to be required for the RIG-I signaling pathway9,10. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed that MAVS clustering could be resolved upon mitochondria in control HepAD38 cells (Fig. 6A), but appeared to be markedly reduced on mitochondria in MxB-knockdown cells (Fig. 6B). The decrease in the number of MAVS clusters was confirmed by counting the MAVS clusters per cell (Fig. 6C). In addition, MAVS aggregation was evaluated using SDD-AGE, which was performed under semi-denaturing detergent conditions. As depicted in Figs. 6D and S9, MAVS aggregation appeared reduced in MxB knockdown HepG2.2.15 cells and HepAD38 cells, whereas the total levels of MAVS detected by western blotting did not change (Figs. 6E and S9).

MxB induction was modest in HBV-infected patients

As an in vivo correlate to our studies using infected cultured hepatoma cells, the expression of MxB in the liver samples obtained from HBV-infected patients was evaluated. This was done using several different approaches. First, MxB mRNA expression were obtained from the GEO2R database, which contains data from patients with chronic HBV infection (GSE83148)29. A comparison of MxB mRNA levels between HBV-infected patients (n = 122) and healthy controls (n = 6) showed significantly higher levels in HBV-infected patients (Fig. 7A). Second, liver samples from patients with CHB (n = 35) were analyzed and compared with those from patients with MASLD (n = 19) and CHC (n = 39). The clinical characteristics are shown in Table S1. Compared to non-viral MASLD, the mRNA level of MxB was significantly higher in CHC (P = 0.001), but no significant increase was observed in CHB (Fig. 7B). The difference between CHC and CHB was significant (P = 0.015). These results show that HCV infection induces strong MxB expression, but its induction by HBV infection may not be as strong, suggesting that HBV may not induce enough or may inhibit the responsiveness of MxB.

When examining the correlation between MxB mRNA levels and clinical data in CHB patients, no correlation was observed with HBV-related markers (HBV DNA, HBsAg, and HBeAg) or with biochemical test data, including liver function, as shown in Table S1. However, a significant positive correlation was observed with platelet count (P = 0.017, R² = 0.160) (Fig. 7C). This finding suggests that MxB levels may be associated with liver fibrosis progression.

To validate the MxB mRNA results, immunohistochemistry was performed using FFPE liver tissues from CHB, CHC, and MASLD patients (n = 6 for each group). The representative images are shown in Fig. S10. MxB staining was significantly stronger in CHC than in CHB (P = 0.031) and MASLD (P = 0.013) (Fig. 7D), in consistent with the mRNA results.

Discussion

Clarifying the detailed mechanisms of immune responses to inhibit HBV replication is one of the major challenges in the elimination of this difficult-to-eradicate virus. In this study, we found that MxB inhibited Dane particle formation via suppression of the HBV envelope proteins LHBs/MHBs/SHBs (Figs. 2–4). It was also found that MxB, which maintains mitochondrial morphology, was required for the innate immunity pathway downstream of RIG-I by enhancing the formation of MAVS clusters (Figs. 1 and 5–6). Because type III IFN, which is induced by activation of the RIG-I pathway, was reported to suppress HBsAg production30,31, it was suggested that MxB might inhibit HBV replication through efficient induction of endogenous IFNs. However, MxB was not strongly induced in the livers of HBV-infected patients (Fig. 7). Sato et al. demonstrated that RIG-I recognizes the ε structure in the 5’-end of the 3.5 kb HBV mRNA, but not that in the 2.4/2.1 kb mRNA, which lacks the ε structure at the 5’ end25. Our data showing that the ε RNA induces type III IFN are consistent with the previous report, and importantly, the induction was reduced by MxB knockdown (Fig. 5D). We used poly(I: C) to stimulate the innate immune responses. Previous reports have shown that poly(I: C) is recognized by receptors including RIG-I, MDA5, and TLR3, and RIG-I has been reported to recognize short poly(I: C)32,33. Although TLR3 has been shown to be downregulated in hepatoma cell lines34, RIG-I is believed to recognize short poly(I: C) and the ε RNA, which has a short stem-loop structure.

Myxovirus resistance protein 1 (MX1, also designated MxA) and MxB are large GTPases known to suppress various viruses. One of the ISGs, MxB, has been reported to inhibit HIV-1 and herpesviruses through a process requiring and interaction between MxB and the viral capsids35. Additionally, MxB was shown to interact with the HCV protein NS5A, thereby inhibiting viral RNA replication16. Furthermore, MxB was found to inhibit other cyclophilin A-dependent viruses of the Flaviviridae family, such as Japanese encephalitis virus and dengue virus. This suggests a potential link between cyclophilin A dependence and susceptibility to MxB inhibition16. Mx genes have an IFN-regulated promoter region, and their expression is induced by type I and III IFNs but not by other cytokines36. The results of this study suggest that MxB can suppress the expression of HBV envelope proteins, resulting in the reduction of Dane particles. As a function of MxB expression, the mitochondrial structure was stabilized and the RIG-I signaling pathway was enhanced through MAVS clustering. This downstream signaling could lead to IFN expression that is further potentiated by MxB (Fig. 8). It is possible however that HBV expression could dampen a MxB response as has been shown to occur for other innate immune signaling37. The lack of innate immune responses in HBV infection38 and the regulatory roles of HBV proteins in the innate immune responses12,39 have been demonstrated. HBx protein has been reported to regulate pattern-recognition receptor signaling via multiple mechanisms40. HBx interacts with several proteins in the signaling40, and promotes MAVS ubiquitination to trigger its proteasome-mediated degradation41. In addition, epigenetic modification of HBV mRNA has been reported to inhibit the recognition by RIG-I42,43. Overcoming the stealthy nature of HBV is a major challenge. Actually, the induction of type I and III IFNs was not observed in our HBV infection model using HepG2-hNTCP-C4 cells, although an insufficient proportion of infected cells might affect the results (data not shown). Previous reports have shown that HBV infection or transfection induces type III IFN, but not type I or II IFN25,44. In contrast, other reports have shown that neither type I nor type III IFNs are induced in response to HBV infection38,45. The differences in IFN responses to HBV infection need further clarification.

In this study, MxB expression was shown to be induced in HBV-infected patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 7A), but it was lower than that in patients with HCV infection and similar to that in non-viral MASLD patients (Fig. 7B). HCV is known to attenuate IFN response, and as one of the mechanisms, NS3/4A protein of HCV was reported to cleave MAVS and inhibit RIG-I signaling pathway46. Therefore, it is considered that HCV may inhibit MxB induction to some extent, but the result in this study suggests that the disturbance of innate immune response including MxB induction may be greater in HBV infection. The underlying mechanisms have been unclear, but several previous reports have shown that HBV proteins suppress IFN signaling12. For example, HBeAg has been reported to inhibit the JAK-STAT pathway by stimulating the expression of suppressor of cytokine signaling 2 (SOCS2)47, and such suppression could lead to MxB unresponsiveness. Efficient MxB induction may control HBV replication, and we believe that combining of putative MxB inducers with Peg-IFNα could be an effective treatment strategy. The correlation with platelet count in CHB patients is noteworthy (Fig. 7C). A previous study of 677 patients with liver biopsy showed that platelet count correlates with liver fibrosis progression48. To our knowledge, no previous study has demonstrated differences in MxB expression in the context of liver fibrosis. It remains unclear whether low MxB expression is a cause or consequence of liver fibrosis. Notably, no such correlation was observed between platelet count and MxB in MASLD or CHC patients. A recent study examined an HBV-transgenic mouse model with CCl4-induced liver fibrosis and found that liver stiffness increased osteopontin levels, which limited ISG activation in hepatocytes and subsequently raised HBV markers49. This suggests that MxB might also decrease in the HBV-related fibrotic liver through a similar mechanism. A liver biopsy is an invasive procedure that is difficult to perform frequently. However, measuring serum MxB levels over time might clarify its association with the progression of liver fibrosis, a topic for future investigation.

Mitochondria are exceptionally dynamic organelles and HBV infection has been documented to alter these dynamics leading to immune and metabolic consequences50–52. Further Koshiba et al. reported that cells lacking the ability for mitochondrial fusion impaired the induction of IFNs and proinflammatory cytokines in response to viral infection53. It was also shown that mitochondrial membrane potential is required for MAVS-mediated antiviral response53. As MxB knockdown in Hep3B cells reduced mitochondrial membrane potential18, the reduction in MAVS aggregation by MxB knockdown found in this study may be a result of the reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential. HBx protein was reported to induce mitochondrial membrane depolarization under the inhibition of NF-κB through modulation of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore54, so a reduced membrane potential could be restored by MxB induction, and RIG-I signaling via MAVS aggregation could be maintained. Previous research has shown that MxB is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane18, while MAVS is anchored to the outer mitochondrial membrane through its C-terminal transmembrane domain10. Based on these differences in protein location, we speculate that MxB does not interact directly with MAVS. We observed an increase in total TBK1 expression after MxB knockdown (Fig. 5A). The detailed mechanism is still unknown, but, since MxB knockdown has been reported to induce the release of mtDNA18, it has been speculated that the released mtDNA in the cytosol stimulates the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to TBK1 induction.

The effect of MxB knockdown on HBsAg was greater than on intracellular protein levels, suggesting a potential role for MxB in HBsAg release (Figs. 2 and 4). The detailed mechanism should be clarified in a future study. MxB knockdown increased L/M/SHBs levels, but did not affect HBc levels (Fig. 4A). This is in accordance with northern blotting results showing that MxB knockdown increased 2.4/2.1 kb mRNA, but not 3.5 kb mRNA (Fig. 4D). It has been speculated that MxB may enhance the downstream signaling of RIG-I, which could inhibit the transcription of the 2.4/2.1 kb mRNA specifically. The effect of MxB knockdown on the released amount of Dane particle (Fig. 3) was thought to be mediated by the increase in HBsAg, and the change in total HBV DNA level was small (Fig. 2B). The discrepancy between the change in HBsAg and that in HBV DNA might be due to the large amount of naked nucleocapsid released from the cultured cells. Since the presence of naked nucleocapsids has not been found in human blood55, the effects of MxB on HBV DNA levels may be more direct in humans. Furthermore, the change in HBV DNA and Dane particle levels after MxB knockdown was greater in the HBV infection model using HepG2-NTCP-C4 cells than in the HBV-expressing cells (Figs. 2E and 3D). This may indicate that innate immune signaling is more important in the early response to infection.

Wang et al. demonstrated that MxB inhibits the formation of cccDNA and the transcription of mRNAs, including the 3.5 kb HBV mRNA17. However, our results showed that MxB knockdown altered the expression of 2.4/2.1 kb mRNA but not 3.5 kb mRNA. Additionally, Sato et al. demonstrated that RIG-I overexpression decreases 3.5 kb mRNA25. This finding is inconsistent with our data on MxB knockdown, which is expected to reduce the RIG-I pathway. The cGAS-STING pathway may be enhanced by the MxB knockdown due to increased mtDNA. This indirect modification of innate immunity could affect HBV mRNA responses. Alternatively, the difference in cell type may contribute to the discrepancy.

This study has some limitations. First, experiments using primary human hepatocytes and mouse models were not conducted. Although various cell lines were used to confirm the results, it is possible that signaling pathways may be altered in hepatoma cell lines. Thus, the results should be validated using models that maintain IFN responses. Second, we were unable to analyze the downstream signaling of MxB overexpression. Third, we were unable to quantify the HBV DNA replicative intermediates by Southern blot. Forth, efficient induction of MxB may lead to HBV suppression; however, specific methods have not yet been identified. If future studies can identify agents that efficiently induce MxB, the functional cure rates may be increased by monotherapy with such an agent or by combination therapy with existing treatments, including IFNs and NAs.

In conclusion, this study provides support for a novel model of MxB-directed HBV inhibition initiated by downstream RIG-I signaling that could promote the formation of MAVS clusters in the mitochondria. Enhancement of this pathway could be a potential therapeutic option for suppressing HBV infection.
