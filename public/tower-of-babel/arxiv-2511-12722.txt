On Robustness of Linear Classifiers to Targeted Data Poisoning
Nakshatra Gupta, Sumanth Prabhu S, Supratik Chakraborty, R Venkatesh

Abstract
Data poisoning is a training-time attack that undermines the trustworthiness of learned models. In a targeted data poisoning attack, an adversary manipulates the training dataset to alter the classification of a targeted test point. Given the typically large size of training dataset, manual detection of poisoning is difficult. An alternative is to automatically measure a dataset’s robustness against such an attack, which is the focus of this paper. We consider a threat model wherein an adversary can only perturb the labels of the training dataset, with knowledge limited to the hypothesis space of the victim’s model. In this setting, we prove that finding the robustness is an NP-Complete problem, even when hypotheses are linear classifiers. To overcome this, we present a technique that finds lower and upper bounds of robustness. Our implementation of the technique computes these bounds efficiently in practice for many publicly available datasets. We experimentally demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. Specifically, a poisoning exceeding the identified robustness bounds significantly impacts test point classification. We are also able to compute these bounds in many more cases where state-of-the-art techniques fail.



Introduction

Data poisoning
is a training-time attack wherein an adversary perturbs the training dataset to alter the predictions of the trained models
(
6
)
. Here, the perturbation is called poisoning. Manually determining whether a training dataset is poisoned or not is challenging due its size. Consequently, this attack affects the trustworthiness of machine learning models and hinders their deployment in industries
(
23
)
.

Targeted data poisoning
refers to an adversary manipulating the training data to alter the prediction of a single (or a small fixed subset of) test data. While targeted data poisoning can be sophisticated, such as backdoor attacks that use patterns called triggers, we focus on trigger-less attack. In such an attack, an adversary targets a specific test point and aims for the victim’s classifier to classify this point as a desired class. An example is a loan applicant adversary who poisons the training data so that the model trained on the poisoned data approves the adversary’s loan.

We consider a practical adversary who can poison the training dataset only by contaminating labels. While contaminating the features of a dataset can be difficult, labels can be easily manipulated as label errors are common, especially when they are collected from external sources
(
1
)
. Furthermore, we assume that the adversary has knowledge of only the hypothesis space of the victim’s classifier, but not the training procedure or the actual classifier. In other words, the victim’s training procedure is treated as a black-box. In this setting, we define robustness as:

The minimum number of label perturbations in the training dataset required for a classifier from the hypothesis space to classify the test point as desired.

For example, consider the dataset Pen-based Handwritten Digit Recognition
(
2

. In the dataset, a test point shown in Figure

The above notion of robustness is not only useful to measure confidence in the training data, but also in contesting a model’s predictions. When a model produces an undesirable decision, the user may want to identify the minimal set of training point labels that potentially influenced the outcome. If any of these labels are incorrect, the user can flag and contest the decision. This application is directly addressed by a solution to the robustness problem defined above.

Problem Setting

Preliminaries

Consider the task of binary classification from
\mathit{d}
dimensional input features
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathit{d}}
to binary output labels
\mathcal{Y}=\{+1,-1\}
. The goal of the classification task is to find a function (called classifier)
\mathit{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
that has a small generalization error. In machine learning,
\mathit{f}
is derived from a given set of hypotheses (called hypothesis space)
\mathcal{H}
and a set of labeled training data
\mathcal{D}
. We assume
\mathcal{H}
is a set of linear functions
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}
of the form
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x)=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^{T}x+b)
, where
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathit{d}}
is the weight parameter, and
b\in\mathbb{R}
is the bias parameter. The labeled training data
\mathcal{D}
is generated from an i.i.d over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Given
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{D}
, the classification task is to find a model
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}\in\mathcal{H}
that minimizes a loss function (
e.g.
, hinge loss) over
\mathcal{D}
.

Threat Model

We model the overall workflow via
causative targeted attack

(
3
)
, which is a game between a
victim
and an
attacker
, that proceeds as follows:

1.

The victim generates a clean training data
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in[\mathit{m}]\}
from an i.i.d over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

2.

The attacker
poisons

\mathcal{D}_{c}
by perturbing (a subset of) labels to get
\mathcal{D}_{p}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})\mid i\in[\mathit{m}],\;y_{i}^{\prime}=y_{i}\text{ or }y_{i}^{\prime}=-y_{i}\}
.

3.

The victim finds a model
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}\in\mathcal{H}
that minimizes a loss function over
\mathcal{D}_{p}
.

4.

The attacker has a target test point with label
(x_{t},y_{t})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
(x_{t},y)
is not in
\mathcal{D}_{c}
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The attacker wins the game if the victim learns a model
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}
from
\mathcal{D}_{p}
such that
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{t})=y_{t}
. Otherwise, victim wins the game.

We assume an attacker who can only perturb labels in
\mathcal{D}_{c}
, and is aware of the victim’s hypothesis space, but not the training procedure, the loss function, or the model’s weights.

Robustness

The robustness
\mathit{r}
of a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in[\mathit{m}]\}
with respect to a target test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
is the minimum number of label perturbations required for the attacker to win the above game. Sans knowledge of the victim’s loss function, we define robustness as follows. Let
\mathfrak{P}
be the set of all (possibly) poisoned datasets
\mathcal{D}_{p}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})\mid i\in[m],\;y_{i}^{\prime}=y_{i}\text{ or }y_{i}^{\prime}=-y_{i}\}
s.t.
there exists
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}\in\mathcal{H}
with:

1.

\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{i})=y_{i}^{\prime},\quad\forall i\in[m]

2.

\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{t})=y_{t}

Then
\mathit{r}=\min_{\mathcal{D}_{p}\in\mathfrak{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(y_{i}^{\prime}\neq y_{i})
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function.

Note that some authors prefer to define robustness as the
maximum
number of label perturbations in
\mathcal{D}_{c}
such that no classifier in
\mathcal{H}
learned from the poisoned data classifies the test point
x_{t}
as
y_{t}
. Clearly, this is
\mathit{r}-1
, where
\mathit{r}
is as defined above. For computational simplicity, we use the definition provided above.

Computing Robustness

A naive way to compute robustness is by perturbing labels of subsets of the training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
in increasing order of subset size, and finding whether there exists a classifier that satisfies all required conditions for the poisoned dataset. The time complexity of such an algorithm is clearly exponential in
|\mathcal{D}_{c}|
. Hence, a natural question is whether this algorithm can be improved. Theorem
1
shows that this is unlikely.

Theorem 1
.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in[m]\}
and a target test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
, deciding whether its robustness
\mathit{r}
is less than a threshold
\kappa
is NP-Complete, when
\mathcal{H}
is a set of linear binary classifiers.

Proof Sketch.

In NP
: Given a witness classifier
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}
, we can check whether (a)
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{t})=y_{t}
, and (b)
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{i})\neq y_{i})<\kappa
in polynomial time. Alternatively, given a witness poisoned dataset
\mathcal{D}_{p}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})\mid i\in[m]\}

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(y_{i}^{\prime}\neq y_{i})<\kappa
, and (b) there exists a linear classifier
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}
that satisfies
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{t})=y_{t}
and
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{i})=y_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in[m]
. The latter can be done by standard techniques for solving linear systems of equations, viz. Gaussian elimination.

: We show this by reduction from the vertex cover problem. Let

be an undirected graph, where

is the set of vertices, and

is the set of edges. A vertex cover of

is a subset

of

such that for every edge

is in

. Given

We give below a polynomial-time reduction from the vertex cover problem to the problem of deciding if the robustness of a dataset w.r.t. a target test point is less than a threshold. This proves NP-hardness of deciding if robustness is less than a threshold.

Since perturbing subsets results in an exponential algorithm, it does not scale even for a small dataset. To overcome this, we propose to compute an approximation of robustness
\mathit{r}
. Notice that the victim is interested in how low the robustness of their dataset is, to ensure that it is not susceptible to an attack, while the attacker’s interest lies in how high the robustness is, so that the attack is likely to succeed without detection. Considering these perspectives, we compute both a lower bound
\check{\mathit{r}}
and an upper bound
\hat{\mathit{r}}
of robustness
\mathit{r}
, such that it is guaranteed that
\check{\mathit{r}}\leq\mathit{r}\leq\hat{\mathit{r}}
.

Lower Bound Robustness
\check{\mathit{r}}
via Partitioning

We observe that the problem of finding robustness for linear classifiers can be formulated as an optimization problem. In particular, it can be encoded as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) using the big M method
(
4
)
, assuming a known bound on the range of the linear classifier function. A solution to this optimization problem determines the weights of the function that classifies the test point as required, while minimizing misclassifications on the training dataset. More formally, for a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
and a test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
, we encode the problem as the following optimization problem:

Variables:

•

w_{1},\dots,w_{\mathit{d}},\mathit{b}\in\mathbb{R}
as weights and bias, and

•

\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{\mathit{m}}\in\{0,1\}
as indicators for label perturbations.

Objective:

Minimize
\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{m}}\delta_{i}

Constraints:

\displaystyle y_{t}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{t}+\mathit{b}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon

\displaystyle y_{i}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}\right)+\mathit{M}\delta_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon,\quad\forall i\in[\mathit{m}]

\displaystyle y_{i}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}\right)-\mathit{M}(1-\delta_{i})

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon,\quad\forall i\in[\mathit{m}]

Parameters:

\mathit{M}\gg 0
is a large integer constant,
\epsilon\approx 0
, and
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{c}
.

A solution to the problem provides weights and bias (
w_{1},\dots,w_{\mathit{d}},\mathit{b}
) that classifies the test point
x_{t}
as
y_{t}
and minimizes the sum of perturbed labels in
\mathcal{D}_{c}
, denoted by
\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{m}}\delta_{i}
. The first constraint ensures that
y_{t}=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b})
. For each point
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{c}
, the next two constraints ensure the following:

\delta_{i}=\begin{cases}0&y_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b})\\
1&y_{i}\neq\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b})\end{cases}

For instance,
\delta_{i}=0
makes the second constraint
y_{i}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}\right)\geq\epsilon
, which in turn makes
y_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b})
. Similarly,
\delta_{i}=1
makes the third constraint
y_{i}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}\right)\leq\epsilon
, which makes
y_{i}\neq\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b})
. These constraints additionally adds the conditions
y_{i}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}\right)<\mathit{M}
and
y_{i}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}\right)>\mathit{-M}
, resp..

Hence, a solution gives the robustness
\mathit{r}
(where
\mathit{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{m}}\delta_{i}
), along with the labels that were perturbed (
y_{i}
for which
\delta_{i}
is
1
). A solution exists only when
\mathbf{w}\cdot x_{i}+\mathit{b}
is in the interval
(\mathit{-M},\mathit{M})
.

Since the complexity of solving such optimization problems is exponential, we can get robustness in this way for only small datasets. To scale this, we propose an optimization approach that finds a lower bound of robustness. In this approach, the dataset is partitioned into smaller size, then robustness is computed individually for each partition, and finally the results are summed. Although this sum provides a lower bound, it significantly improves the scalability as the optimization problem is on small sized sets.

Consider the partition of

into

contains

For each partition

we compute robustness

by encoding it as the optimization problem(as before). Finally, we compute the sum of these robustness
\check{\mathit{r}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathit{r}^{j}
.

For dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
and a test point
(x_{t},y_{t})

\check{\mathit{r}}\leq\mathit{r}

Upper Bound Robustness
\hat{\mathit{r}}
via Augmentation

In addition to the lower bound, we also compute an upper bound of robustness. For this purpose, we train a linear classifier on an augmented version of the training dataset

. The augmentation biases the learning to train a classifier that classifies the test point

y_{t}
. While theoretically this classifier may not minimize the label perturbations of
\mathcal{D}_{c}
, in practice we observe that it will give a tighter upper bound. Moreover, this procedure will be quick, as it requires only learning a classifier, hence scales for large datasets.

In order to find such a classifier, we restrict our objective to learn a classifier
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{t})=y_{t}
. Since not all classifiers can achieve this objective, we first introduce a targeted augmentation scheme, wherein
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
identical copies of the test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
are added to
\mathcal{D}_{c}
. Let the augmented set be
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}_{c}\bigcup\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}^{k^{\prime}}
, where
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}^{k^{\prime}}
is a multi-set of
k^{\prime}
copies of
(x_{t},y_{t})
.

We then train a classifier on
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{\prime}
by minimizing the empirical loss with respect to a loss function
\mathit{l}
:

\displaystyle\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}=\arg\min_{\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{\mathit{m}+k^{\prime}}\left(\begin{aligned} &\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{m}}\mathit{l}(\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{i}),y_{i})+\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}}\mathit{l}(\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{t}),y_{t})\end{aligned}\right)

Once a classifier
\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}
is learned on the augmented dataset, it is checked whether the classifier correctly classifies the test point
x_{t}
as
y_{t}
. If it does, then the number of misclassified points from the original dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
will be the the upper bound. In other words,
\hat{\mathit{r}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{m}}\mathbb{I}(\mathit{f_{\mathbf{w},\mathit{b}}}(x_{i})\neq y_{i})
.

Theorem 3
.

For dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
and a test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
,
\mathit{r}\leq\hat{\mathit{r}}
.

Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we have proven that the problem of finding robustness in targeted data poisoning is NP-complete in the setting considered and have introduced effective techniques for computing lower and upper bound of robustness. An interesting future direction is to extend the lower bound algorithm for non-linear classifiers.

Theorem 4
.

For dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
and a test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
,
\check{\mathit{r}}\leq\mathit{r}
.

Proof.

We prove by contradiction. Assume
\mathit{r}<\check{\mathit{r}}
. Consider the training points from
\mathcal{D}_{c}
that are witness to
\mathit{r}
(i.e., these training points results in
\mathit{r}
). Now, consider a partition of
\mathcal{D}_{c}
into
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{1}\dots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{k}
. Suppose our partition-based algorithm computes robustness of these partitions as
\check{\mathit{r}}^{1}\dots\check{\mathit{r}}^{k}
. Let
\mathit{r}^{i}
be the witness points with respect to partition
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{i}
(i.e, training points from
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{i}
). By our assumption, we have
\sum_{i}^{k}\mathit{r}^{i}<\sum_{i}^{k}\check{\mathit{r}}^{i}
. Furthermore, each
\mathit{r}^{i}<\check{\mathit{r}}^{i}
as there can not be overlaps as
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{1}\dots\mathcal{D}_{c}^{k}
are partitions. But,
\check{\mathit{r}}^{i}
is the minimal robustness for
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{i}
, which is ensured by the encoding. Hence, it is not possible to have
\mathit{r}^{i}<\check{\mathit{r}}^{i}
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 5
.

For dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
and a test point
(x_{t},y_{t})
,
\mathit{r}\leq\hat{\mathit{r}}
.

Proof.

Consider
\hat{\mathit{r}}
computed via augmentation. The method ensures that the test point classifies as desired. It is not possible to have
\hat{\mathit{r}}<\mathit{r}
, as
\mathit{r}
is the minimal perturbations required to classify the test point as desired.
Furthermore, an upper bound for
k^{\prime}
can also be computed by assuming certain properties on the loss function
\mathit{l}
. For instance, consider that
\mathit{l}
is bounded by an interval
[L,U]
. Then, we have
k^{\prime}\leq\frac{\mathit{m}U}{L}
.
∎
