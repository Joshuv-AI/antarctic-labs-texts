Differential complement pathways and components in the clinical spectrum of systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Haijun Ma, Qingfeng Yin, Huifang Li, Minghao Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe precise contributions of the classical (CP), lectin (LP), and alternative (AP) pathways to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to comprehensively investigate complement pathway components in SLE.MethodsSixty SLE patients and 20 healthy controls (HC) were recruited. Plasma C1q, C4, C4b, C2, C3, C5, Factor B (FB), Factor P (FP), Factor D (FD), Factor H (FH), Factor I (FI), and Mannose-binding lectin were measured by Multiplex Assay.ResultsCompared to HC and patients with low disease activity, active SLE showed decreased C1q, C4, C4b, C3, and FP, with increased C2, while FB was reduced only relative to HC. Patients with low C3 and C4 showed higher anti-dsDNA and C2, more newly diagnosed SLE, and lower C1q, C5, FP, FB, and FH. SLE patients with isolated low C3 exhibited reduced FP, FB, and FH, with a trend toward longer disease duration. Newly diagnosed SLE showed decreased C1q and C4b, while those with renal involvement had lower C1q and C3 but higher C2. The concurrent infection subgroup presented decreased C1q and increased C2. Regression analysis identified C2 as independently associated with concurrent infection and FD as the strongest negative correlation with renal function. Principal component analysis further delineated three patient clusters with distinct complement signatures.ConclusionThe CP is predominantly activated at disease onset, whereas the AP contributes to both initiation and progression of SLE. C2 and FD hold potential as biomarkers for SLE complications. Complement-driven stratification informs precision therapy in SLE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03721-6.Keywords: Complement dysregulation, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem involvement [1]. Although disease-specific autoantibodies are well-recognized hallmarks of SLE, therapeutic responses to B-cell–targeted agents vary considerably [2–4], and treatment failure is not uncommon. Accumulating evidence underscores the crucial role of complement dysregulation in the pathogenesis of SLE [5–11], with complement components consistently present in affected organs, indicating excessive activation of the complement cascade [9, 12, 13]. However, the mechanisms through which specific complement pathways and their individual components contribute to disease progression are not fully understood [14–16]. Furthermore, correlations between circulating complement levels and clinical manifestations of SLE are inconsistent, greatly complicating their interpretation [9, 10, 17, 18]. Therefore, clarifying the associations between distinct complement components and their activation pathways with disease activity, organ involvement, and comorbidities would enhance their utility in diagnosis, disease monitoring, and treatment decision-making.

The present study aims to systematically investigate the complement spectrum in patients with SLE and healthy subjects, and to explore its associations with disease activity and clinical phenotypes.

Subjects and methods

Sixty patients with SLE and 20 healthy blood donors were recruited. All patients with SLE fulfilled the 1997 revised classification criteria of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) [19]. Disease activity was assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K) [20]. The clinical SLEDAI (cSLEDAI) score was calculated by excluding the complement scoring items (C3 and C4) from the standard SLEDAI-2 K.

Data collection and definitions

The following demographic and clinical data were collected at the time of enrollment as part of standard of care, including age, gender, medical history, SLEDAI-2 K scores, and routine laboratory parameters. Patients were stratified according to the Asia-Pacific Lupus Collaboration criteria [21], with low disease activity (LDA) defined as a SLEDAI-2 K score of 0–4 and active disease defined as a SLEDAI-2 K score greater than 4. Among the SLE patients, 20 were classified as having LDA, while the remaining 40 were categorized as having active SLE.

Newly diagnosed patients were defined as those receiving the first-time definitive diagnosis of SLE at our institution before glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive treatment initiation. Follow-up patients had a prior SLE diagnosis confirmed at our institution or another hospital and were receiving ongoing treatments. SLE patients were also categorized into renal and non-renal subgroups according to the presence or absence of renal involvement, defined as persistent abnormal urinalysis or renal biopsy [22], after excluding alternative causes including uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes mellitus, urological conditions, or nephrotoxic drugs. Furthermore, concurrent infection of SLE was diagnosed based on clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, imaging examinations, pathogen identification, and assessment of response to antimicrobial therapy. Patients without evidence of infection were classified as non-infected.

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University (2018059). Informed consent was obtained from all of the participants prior to their enrollment in this study.

Measurement of complement components

Fasting venous blood was collected from all participants. Plasma was separated using EDTA-2 K tubes, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. For complement detection, all specimens underwent only a single freeze-thaw cycle, and plasma concentrations of C1q, C4, C4b, C2, C3, C5, Factor B (FB), Factor P (FP), Factor D (FD), Factor H (FH), Factor I (FI), and Mannose-binding lectin (MBL) were quantified using the Luminex MAGPIX® system (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA). The MILLIPLEX® Human Complement Panel 1 and 2 (EMD Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) were employed for the assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Two-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied for multiple comparisons. Comparisons among three groups were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey’s HSD or Dunn’s test with FDR correction.

Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations between complement components and clinical parameters. Multivariable linear regression models were fitted to evaluate the associations of individual complement proteins with cSLEDAI and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), respectively, with all models adjusted for potential confounders. Additionally, logistic regression was performed to identify complement components independently associated with concurrent infection, after adjusting for cSLEDAI. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated to identify the optimal cutoff values for distinguishing between SLE patients with and without concurrent infection.

To comprehensively evaluate whether clustered patterns of complement activation provide superior clinical insights compared to individual analytes, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the 12 complement proteins. Patient stratification was performed using k-means clustering of principal component scores. The optimal number of clusters was determined by the silhouette coefficient and the elbow method, and inter-cluster differences in clinical characteristics were subsequently evaluated.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0), and adjusted p (p.adj) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

As shown in Table 3, patients in the combined hypocomplementemia subgroup exhibited a significantly higher proportion of newly diagnosed cases, elevated cSLEDAI scores, and increased anti-dsDNA positivity compared to the comparison subgroup. They also demonstrated reduced plasma levels of C1q, C4b, FB, FP, C5, and FH, together with elevated C2. As expected, the isolated low C3 subgroup showed decreased FB, FP and FH levels, as well as higher cSLEDAI scores. Notably, this subgroup displayed increased C4b levels (p.adj < 0.05) and a trend toward longer disease duration (p.adj = 0.0662) compared to the combined hypocomplementemia subgroup.

These findings suggest that activation of CP and/or LP could trigger AP activation in SLE, particularly among newly diagnosed patients. However, the subsequent sustained AP activation, resulting from complement dysregulation, may contribute to the chronicity of SLE disease.

Differential complement profiles in renal Involvement, concurrent infection, and newly diagnosed SLE

Renal involvement is one of the most common manifestations of SLE. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment are crucial to prevent its progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). In this cohort, 31 patients showed evidence of renal involvement. Compared with both HC and SLE patients without renal involvement, those with renal involvement exhibited significantly reduced plasma levels of C1q and C3, along with elevated C2 (all p.adj < 0.05; Table S1). Moreover, plasma C4, C4b, FB, and FP were significantly lower in these two SLE subgroups than in HC. These findings suggest that both the CP and AP were activated in SLE patients with renal involvement, with CP activation potentially playing a more pronounced role in its pathogenesis.

Another major complication of SLE is infection, which is one of the leading causes of mortality in affected patients. It can occur either concurrently with disease flares or sequentially during the disease course. Distinguishing between infection and SLE flare is often challenging due to their overlapping clinical manifestations, highlighting the critical need for reliable biomarkers to differentiate these conditions. In the present study, we found that SLE patients with concurrent infection exhibited significantly decreased C1q and elevated C2 levels compared to both HC and non-infected SLE patients (both p.adj < 0.05; Table S2). Furthermore, both infected and non-infected SLE subgroups showed reduced levels of C4, C4b, FB, and FP compared to HC (all p.adj < 0.05; Table S2). These data suggest that decreased C1q and elevated C2 may serve as potential biomarkers for differentiating concurrent infections in SLE patients.

Considering the dynamic changes in complement components during the course of SLE, newly diagnosed patients represent a particularly informative subgroup for investigating early complement dysregulation in the present cohort. Compared with both HC and follow-up patients, they exhibited significantly decreased plasma levels of C1q and C4b (both p.adj < 0.05; Table S3). Similar to other subgroups, decreased levels of C4, FB, and FP, along with increased C2 (all p.adj < 0.05; Table S3), were also observed in both newly diagnosed and follow-up SLE patients. Taken together, these data suggest that although both the CP and AP are activated in the early phase of SLE, the CP appears to be the predominant pathway during this initial stage. In contrast, the chronic phase of the disease is characterized by sustained AP activation accompanied by diminished CP activity.

Potential of complement proteins in assessing infection and renal outcomes in SLE

Having identified significant differences in complement profiles across disease phenotypes, we next quantified their clinical and prognostic relevance. To this end, we employed correlation, regression, and ROC analyses.

As shown in Fig. 2A, Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated distinct patterns of association between complement components and clinical parameters. Plasma C1q (ρ = -0.71, p.adj < 0.001) was significantly inversely correlated with cSLEDAI, while C4 (ρ = -0.42, p.adj = 0.0515) showed a trend toward negative association. Conversely, C2 (ρ = 0.57, p.adj < 0.01) was positively correlated with disease activity. Notably, analysis of renal function revealed a significant negative correlation between FD and eGFR (ρ = -0.50, p.adj < 0.05).

As mentioned above, distinguishing between SLE flares and concurrent infections remains a critical clinical challenge due to their frequent co-occurrence or sequential development. Reliable biomarkers to differentiate between these conditions are therefore urgently needed to guide precise diagnosis and tailored therapeutic interventions. In the present study, after adjusting for clinical SLEDAI scores, logistic regression identified plasma C2 as the only factor independently associated with concurrent infections in SLE patients (adjusted OR = 7.648, 95% CI: 2.083–51.665, p.adj < 0.05). ROC analysis further revealed that C2 exhibited excellent diagnostic performance, showing an AUC of 0.876 (Fig. 2B and C). At an optimal cutoff value of 0.348 µg/mL, C2 yielded a sensitivity of 88.5% and a specificity of 76.5%.

Beyond the challenge of diagnosing concurrent infections, the identification of biomarkers for monitoring the risk of progression to ESRD is equally important in managing SLE patients. To assess the impact of complement proteins on renal outcomes, we performed multivariable linear regression analysis of eGFR adjusted for age, gender, disease duration, cSLEDAI, and renal involvement. As shown in Fig. 2D, plasma FD (β = -2.856, 95% CI: -4.984 to -0.729) and C2 (β = -2.199, 95% CI: -3.864 to -0.533) demonstrated significant independent associations with eGFR (both p.adj < 0.05). In contrast, plasma C1q (β = 0.142, 95% CI: 0.016 to 0.269) showed only a marginal association with eGFR.

These data collectively demonstrate the multifaceted roles of the complement system in SLE. C1q showed the strongest inverse correlation with disease activity, C2 contributed to the discrimination of infection, and FD was most strongly and independently associated with renal function decline.

Multivariate complement signatures and distinct patient clusters in SLE

While analyses of individual complement components revealed important component-specific associations, their power for patient stratification was limited. We therefore employed principal component analysis (PCA) to integrate the information from all 12 proteins quantified using a Luminex-based assay into multivariate signatures and to determine whether this approach could improve clinical stratification.

The first three principal components (PCs) collectively explained 67.56% of the total variance (PC1: 39.80%, PC2: 16.66%, PC3: 11.10%). Based on the scores from these PCs, k-means clustering stratified patients into three stable groups (k = 3), as supported by the elbow method and silhouette analysis, with high clustering stability (average adjusted Rand index = 0.748) (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B, each cluster was characterized by a distinct complement signature: a broad high-complement group (Cluster 1, N = 22), a low-complement group (Cluster 2, n = 34), and a small but distinct C2/FD-dominant group (Cluster 3, n = 4).

Patient clusters and complement signatures from multivariate analysis in SLE. A Three patient clusters identified by PCA-based complement signatures. B Complement protein expression patterns across patient clusters

These multivariate patterns captured clinically relevant heterogeneity. Notably, conventional C3/C4 hypocomplementemia was nearly universal in Clusters 2 and 3, while more than half of the patients in the high-complement Cluster 1 did not exhibit it (both p.adj < 0.05; Table S4), highlighting a discordance between traditional markers and broader complement profiles. Furthermore, the C2/FD-dominant Cluster 3 was associated with the highest disease activity, as assessed by clinical SLEDAI (p = 0.0472; Table S4). A trend toward increased infection comorbidity was also observed, with all patients in Cluster 3 presenting with concurrent infections, compared with approximately 40% in Clusters 1 and 2 (p = 0.0705; Table S4). All four patients in Cluster 3 also had renal involvement.

Although statistical power for clinical comparisons was limited by sample size and multiple-testing correction, these results suggest that complement analyte clusters may better capture disease heterogeneity than individual complement markers alone.

Discussion

Complement dysregulation is a critical immunopathogenic mechanism in SLE, driving organ damage and impairing immune defense. Notably, individual complement components display marked functional diversity and may exert opposing effects, reflecting a tightly regulated complement hierarchy. A deeper understanding of the regulation of this hierarchy is therefore crucial for elucidating SLE pathogenesis and developing novel therapeutic strategies. In the present study, we comprehensively profiled complement proteins from the CP, AP, and LP pathways by employing multiplex magnetic bead-based immunoassays in both SLE patients and healthy controls. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply this high-throughput approach in SLE patients.

Our findings demonstrate that plasma levels of C1q, C4, C3, FB, and FP are decreased in active SLE and those with renal involvement, suggesting activation of the CP and AP, which is consistent with previous studies [23–26]. Notably, although hypocomplementemia occurs in both newly diagnosed and follow-up patients, C1q and C4b levels are particularly reduced in newly diagnosed patients compared to their follow-up counterparts and health subjects, indicating pronounced CP activation at disease onset. In contrast, the AP is somewhat persistently activated in the development of SLE, which, together with the longer disease duration observed in our SLE patients exhibiting isolated AP activation, is particularly intriguing. Furthermore, a growing body of evidence indicates that excessive AP activation plays a more critical role in driving renal damage progression in SLE [16, 18, 27]. Collectively, our findings suggest that the CP and AP are differentially involved in SLE development, and the complement hierarchy shifts over the course of disease [15].

Unexpectedly, plasma C2 is elevated in our SLE patients. This finding challenges the traditional view that deficiency of early CP components are predisposing factors for SLE, although the penetrance of C2 is the lowest [28]. Notably, there have been preclinical studies demonstrating that therapeutic blockade of C2 using siRNA or monoclonal antibodies can ameliorate disease in CP-mediated autoimmune models [29–31], which stands in sharp contrast to the pathogenic consequences of C1q deficiency in SLE [15]. Given that the ‘lupus paradox’ may operate not only across the entire complement cascade but also within its individual pathways [32], these heterogeneous contributions of individual complement components warrant further investigation to elucidate their context-dependent and component-specific functions in autoimmune diseases, including SLE.

Conventionally, there are several mechanisms influencing the circulating levels of complement proteins. Firstly, over-activation of the complement cascade leads to hypocomplementemia [33]. This represents a fundamental mechanism in the pathogenesis of SLE, a prototypical type III hypersensitivity disease [34]. Secondly, acute-phase responses, triggered by infection or systemic inflammation, can enhance hepatic and extrahepatic synthesis of complement components. This synthesis counterbalances complement consumption and, in some patients, may become even more pronounced [35, 36]. Indeed, a substantial proportion of SLE patients do not develop hypocomplementemia even during the active phase [37, 38]. Additionally, upregulated C2 expression has been described in various cell types including hepatocytes, endothelial cells, monocytes, and astroglioma cells [39]. Notably, Bemis et al. also reported elevated C2 in patients with rheumatoid arthritis [40]. Thirdly, genetic polymorphisms contribute to the variation in complement protein levels. Multiple cis- and trans-acting protein quantitative trait loci, including rs9268149, rs9267673, and chr6:31902065 (GRCh37), have been identified as significant genetic regulators of C2 protein levels [41, 42]. These results suggest that individual differences in C2 protein levels are not solely attributable to inflammation or consumption but are also genetically determined.

Accurately distinguishing between concurrent infections and SLE flares is essential for guiding clinical management [43]. However, this diagnostic differentiation becomes particularly challenging when both conditions occur simultaneously or sequentially. In line with this clinical scenario, we observed that SLE patients with coexisting infections exhibited decreased levels of C1q, C4b, C4, FB, and FP, along with elevated C2. Furthermore, compared to patients without infections, those with infections showed more pronounced reductions in C1q and greater elevations in C2. These data suggest that infection may induce and amplify activation of the complement cascade in SLE, particularly leading to over-activation of the CP [44]. Given the distinct therapeutic strategies required for managing SLE flares and concurrent infections, the identification of eligible and reliable biomarkers to differentiate between these conditions is highly desirable. Interestingly, our study revealed that elevated plasma C2 distinguishes SLE patients with concurrent infections, demonstrating both high sensitivity and good specificity. Further studies involving larger cohorts of SLE patients with and without co-infections are required to validate the context-dependent role of C2 using standardized procedure.

Notably, our findings further revealed that elevated FD and C2 were independently associated with lower eGFR in SLE patients, whereas reduced C1q showed a borderline significant association. More importantly, FD demonstrated the strongest association among the complement markers and was the only factor showing a significant negative relationship with eGFR in Spearman analysis, suggesting its potential utility in evaluating renal functional deterioration. Although a prior study documented elevated FD in SLE patients with renal involvement [36], our results extend this observation by demonstrating its potential for evaluating renal outcomes in a broader SLE population. These findings advance our understanding of complement-mediated injury in SLE and highlight the clinical significance of FD, particularly considering the fact that renal involvement develops in 50–60% of patients within the first decade after diagnosis and nearly 25% of these progress to ESRD within 10 years [45]. As the rate-limiting enzyme in the AP amplification loop, elevated FD implies enhanced AP activation, which may contribute to the progression of renal damage in SLE. Therefore, clarifying the precise pathogenic role of FD in SLE is crucial for developing targeted strategies to prevent its progression to ESRD [46].

Although individual complement components correlate strongly with clinical features, multivariate profiling integrates all 12 components into coordinated patterns that offer deeper insight into SLE heterogeneity. Specifically, clustering based on principal components identified a C2/FD-dominant subgroup (Cluster 3) characterized by the highest disease activity and a distinct burden of co-morbidities. This phenotype is accessible exclusively through integrated profiling, not through any single component. Therefore, complement signatures delineate systemic dysregulation that goes beyond single-marker associations, supporting their use for patient stratification and guiding targeted complement therapies.

Despite highlighting differential roles of the complement system in SLE, this study has several limitations. First, complement proteins, especially their split products, might be sensitive to pre-analytical factors including sample type, handling, storage conditions, and repeated freeze-thaw (FT) cycles [47, 48]. However, performing the present study with fresh specimens was logistically not feasible. To minimize these impacts as much as possible, we used a pre-aliquoted EDTA-plasma sample that had undergone one FT cycle and employed a bead-based multiplex assay with internal controls and technical replicates for simultaneous multi-analyte measurement. Second, renal involvement was pragmatically defined by persistent urinary abnormalities or renal biopsy. Although a subset of patients underwent renal biopsy, most had documented chronicity index scores. Consequently, the analysis could not definitively determine whether the observed differences in complement profiles between groups were related to active nephritis or to chronic damage. This heterogeneity may have attenuated the findings and should be considered when interpreting them. Third, the cross-sectional, single-center design precludes longitudinal tracking of intra-individual complement dynamics. While including both newly diagnosed and follow-up patients offers some insights into stage-dependent differences, the modest sample size, largely due to single-center recruitment constraints and assay costs, reduces statistical power for subgroup analyses and limits generalizability. Therefore, larger multi-center longitudinal studies that include complotype analysis will be essential to validate the pathway-specific profiles and facilitate future mechanistic and therapeutic research.

In conclusion, distinct complement pathways are differentially engaged in SLE. The classical pathway plays a fundamental role in initial pathogenesis, whereas the alternative pathway contributes to early development and sustains disease progression. Complement C2 assists in distinguishing concurrent infections, with FD showing potential for evaluating renal function. Importantly, multivariate complement profiling identified three patient clusters with distinct complement signatures and clinical outcomes, which may inform the future development of stratified, complement-targeted therapies in SLE.
