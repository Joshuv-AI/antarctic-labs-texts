Bi-martingale optimal transport and its applications
Karol Bołbotowski

Abstract
We introduce a new non-linear optimal transport formulation for a pair of probability measures on ℝ d {\mathbb{R}^{d}} sharing a common barycentre, in which admissible transference plans satisfy two martingale-type constraints. This bi-martingale framework underlies and interconnects several variational problems on the space of probability measures. For the quadratic cost, it provides an optimal transport interpretation of the second Zolotarev distance on 𝒫 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) \mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}) . For a broader class of convex costs, it leads to optimization problems under convex order constraints, encompassing in particular the Zolotarev projection onto the cone of dominating probability measures. As a main application, we construct a Γ \Gamma -convergent bi-martingale approximation of the classical martingale optimal transport problem. This scheme robustly accommodates deviations from convex order between the marginal distributions and overcomes the well-known instability of MOT with respect to variations of the marginals in higher dimensions.

Introduction

1.1
Selected topics on the space of probabilities
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})

Let us open the introduction with a recapitulation of several issues revolving around the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
such as optimal mass transport, metrics, and stochastic ordering. The purpose of this paper is to propose a new optimization framework that bridges these topics and provides a springboard for computational methods.

Optimal transport and its martingale variant

With
\mu
and
\nu
being probability measures on closed subsets of
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,
X
and
Y
respectively, the Monge–Kantorovich formulation of optimal transport consists in finding a plan of minimum cost,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\inf\left\{\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}c(x,y)\,\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\right\}\qquad\qquad\qquad(\mathrm{OT}).

Above
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is a subset of probabilities on
X\times Y
whose marginal distributions are
\mu
and
\nu
. Optimal transport has countless applications and is a topic of ever growing interest, see the monographs
[
44
,
5
,
41
]
. Choosing the cost
c(x,y)={\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{p}
for
p\in[1,+\infty)
yields the Wasserstein metric
W_{p}(\mu,\nu)
on the space of probability measures of finite
p
-th moment, further denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.

Motivated by applications in finance, the authors of
[
8
]
have introduced the variation of
(\mathrm{OT})
called the
martingale optimal transport
,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\inf\left\{\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}c(x,y)\,\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)\right\}\qquad\qquad\quad(\mathrm{MOT}).

The set
\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)
consists of those transport plans
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
that satisfy the martingale condition,

\int_{Y}y\,\gamma_{x}(dy)=x\quad\text{for $\mu$-a.e. $x$},

where
x\mapsto\gamma_{x}
is the
transport kernel
, a Borel measure-valued map that constitutes the disintegration
\gamma(dxdy)=\mu(dx)\otimes\gamma_{x}(dy)
. As expounded in
[
8
]
, the minimum in
(\mathrm{MOT})
represents the lowest price of an exotic option with payoff
c
for which no arbitrage opportunity occurs in the market.

Unlike
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
, the set of martingale plans may be empty. Strassen theorem
[
43
]
states that
\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)
is non-empty if and only if
\nu
dominates
\mu
in the convex order, namely,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,d\mu\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,d\nu\qquad\text{for all convex }\varphi:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R},

written
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
for short.
A necessary condition, but by no means sufficient, is the agreement of the barycentres
[\mu]=[\nu]
, where
[\mu]:=\int x\,\mu(dx)
.

Zolotarev distance as an alternative for Wasserstein

In the paper
[
46
]
Vladimir M. Zolotarev introduced a family of metrics on the space of probabilities on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. The Zolotarev distance of order
s=1,2,\ldots
reads for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{s}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,

Z_{s}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{u\in C^{s-1,1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,d(\mu-\nu)\,:\,\Arrowvert D^{s-1}u(x)-D^{s-1}u(y)\Arrowvert\leq{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}\ \ \forall\,x,y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right\}

where
C^{k,1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
is the space of functions whose
k
-th derivative is Lipschitz continuous, and
\Arrowvert{\,\cdot\,}\Arrowvert
is the relevant operator norm. Strictly speaking,
Z_{s}
is an extended metric when
s\geq\nolinebreak 2
since
Z_{s}(\mu,\nu)<+\infty
implies equality of the mixed moments of order up to
s-1
. For instance, the distance
Z_{2}
should be defined on
\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):=\big\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\,:\,[\mu]=b\big\}
, where
b\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a generic but fixed barycentre.

The work
[
11
]
demonstrates that Zolotarev and Wasserstein distances
Z_{s}
and
W_{s}
control the same topology on the subspace of probabilities in
\mathcal{P}_{s}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
with prescribed mixed moments up to order
s-1
. Moreover,
Z_{s}
is an example of an
ideal metric
: it is scale-
s
-homogeneous and it is subadditive under convolution, cf.
[
39
, Chapter 14]
for more details. These properties make
Z_{s}
useful in obtaining sharp convergence rates in the Central Limit Theorem
[
40
,
26
]
.

The famous Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality result states that
Z_{1}=W_{1}
. It has an abundance of ramifications, one of which is the OT approach for the Beckmann problem
[
16
,
25
]
, a PDE formulation that is dual to
Z_{1}
. Few similar results are available for
s\geq 2
. One example is
[
32
]
where a family of optimal
transshipment
problems is considered, see also
[
38
]
. These, however, may suffer from the lack of solutions, unlike the transport formulations.

In a recent paper
[
14
]
, co-written by the present author, the Kantorovich–Rubinstein result has been brought to the second-order case
s=2
. For two measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
sharing the barycentre
b
, the distance

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\max\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,d(\mu-\nu)\,:\,u\in C^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \mathrm{lip}(\nabla u)\leq 1\right\}

(1.1)

was recast as a minimum with respect to transport 3-plans
\varpi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
with suitable marginal and martingale constraints (cf. Section
3.2
below). The motivation behind
[
14
]
comes from the optimal design of mechanical structures. The PDE dual of
Z_{2}
is a second-order counterpart of the Beckmann problem, and its solutions are symmetric-tensor-valued measures that encode configurations of beams constituting the stiffest bearing system of a ceiling. A partial characterization of optimal 3-plans was proposed in
[
20
]
.

The duality result in
[
14
]
paves the way to establishing new properties of the
Z_{2}
distance. It was exploited directly in the subsequent paper
[
15
]
, establishing the inequalities comparing the Zolotarev-2 and Wasserstein-2 metrics,

\frac{1}{4}\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\ \leq\ Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}\,(\sigma_{\mu}+\sigma_{\nu})\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\qquad\quad\forall\,\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),

(1.2)

where
\sigma_{\mu},\sigma_{\nu}
stand for the standard deviations. The above constants are sharp. In dimension one, the lower bound was proven in
[
40
]
. These estimates allow to retrieve the topological equivalence between
Z_{2}
and
W_{2}
established already in
[
11
]
. Thus, on the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
the convergence in
Z_{2}
enjoys the characterization that is well known for Wasserstein-2,

Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})\to 0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu,\qquad m_{2}(\mu_{n})\to m_{2}(\mu),

(1.3)

where
m_{2}(\mu)=\int{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\mu(dx)
is the second moment, and
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
stands for the weak convergence of measures, i.e.
\int\varphi\,d\mu_{n}\to\int\varphi\,d\mu
for every continuous bounded function
\varphi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.

Optimization with stochastic order constraints

The work
[
23
]
has initiated a new line of research on optimization in mathematical finance by incorporating constraints in the form of stochastic order between random vectors, see e.g.
[
24
,
31
]
for further developments. In particular cases, the problem can be written in terms of the laws only,

\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,d\rho\,:\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \ \rho\succeq_{\mathrm{st}}\mu_{i},\ \ i=1,\ldots,n\right\},

(1.4)

where
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Borel cost function, and
\mu_{i}
are given reference probabilities.
The stochastic order
\succeq_{\mathrm{st}}
means that
\int\varphi\,d\rho\geq\int\varphi\,d\mu_{i}
for every
\varphi
within a certain class
\mathcal{F}
of real functions
[
42
,
37
]
. The convex order is one example, subharmonic order is another. For instance, to implement risk-averse investing strategies,
\mathcal{F}
should be chosen as the set of increasing concave functions (
d=1
).

This paper will focus on the stochastic optimization for convex order only. The most important instance of this problem will involve two reference probabilities and a quadratic cost,

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\Big\{m_{2}(\rho)\,:\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\mu,\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\nu\Big\},

(1.5)

where
m_{2}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\left\lvert z\right\rvert}^{2}\rho(dz)
. Its significance stems from the connection with Zolotarev-2 distance, obtained as a by-product of the second-order Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality
[
14
]
,

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{m_{2}(\mu)+m_{2}(\nu)}{2}.

(1.6)

Projection onto the cone of probabilities dominating for convex order

As mentioned above, well posedness of martingale optimal transport entails convex order between the marginals. This calls for careful sampling techniques when tackling
(\mathrm{MOT})
numerically, as the convex order that holds for original probabilities can be lost upon discretization. The authors of
[
2
]
proposed the
Wasserstein projection
approach as a post-sampling fix of convex order, see also
[
28
,
35
]
. Assuming that the convex order is corrupted,
\mu\npreceq_{c}\nu
, it consists in solving the problem,

\displaystyle\min\Big\{W_{2}(\nu,\rho)\,:\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \rho\succeq_{c}\mu\Big\}.

Its solution
\hat{\rho}
is the Wasserstein-2 projection of
\nu
onto the convex cone of measures that dominate
\mu
, also called the
forward projection
. The idea is then to replace
\nu
with
\hat{\rho}
and subsequently solve
(\mathrm{MOT})
for the ordered measures
\mu\preceq_{c}\hat{\rho}
.
Alternatively, one can compute the
backward projection
: of
\mu
onto the backward cone
\{{\,\cdot\,}\preceq_{c}\nu\}
.

If, despite the lack of convex order, the probabilities share their barycentre, the aforementioned equivalence between Wasserstein-2 and Zolotarev-2 metrics inspires to investigate the projection technique with
W_{2}
replaced by
Z_{2}
. In this paper, we will study only the forward Zolotarev projection,

\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu):=\operatorname*{arg\,min}\limits_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu}Z_{2}(\nu,\rho).

The equality (
1.6
) gives away that the
Z_{2}
metric itself is intrinsically linked to convex order, a feature not
a priori
shared by
W_{2}
. This factor will prove to play into the properties of the Zolotarev projection.

with the least second moment. Notice the emerging symmetry: while enforcing e.g. only that
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu
, we obtain the second dominance
\rho\succeq_{c}\nu
as a result of optimization. As a by-product, we get the formulas for the normalized distance to the projections,

\displaystyle\frac{\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu}Z_{2}(\nu,\rho)}{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}=\frac{1-\alpha_{\succeq_{c}}(\nu\,|\,\mu)}{2},\qquad\frac{\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\nu}Z_{2}(\mu,\rho)}{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}=\frac{1+\alpha_{\succeq_{c}}(\nu\,|\,\mu)}{2},

(1.14)

where for distinct measures
\mu\neq\nu
we have introduced the
convex-order index
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\succeq_{c}}(\nu\,|\,\mu):=\frac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)}{2Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}\ \in\ [-1,1].

From the two foregoing equalities, it is clear that
\alpha_{\succeq_{c}}(\nu\,|\,\mu)=1
when
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
, and
\alpha_{\succeq_{c}}(\nu\,|\,\mu)=-1
when
\mu\succeq_{c}\nu
. The intermediate index values convey an information on how much the convex order, in one direction or the other, is violated in the sense of the
Z_{2}
metric. Observe that this information is essentially carried through the distance
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)
alone.

Remark 1.3
.

It must be emphasised that Proposition
1.2
asserts an equality between three sets which are not singletons in general, cf. Example
7.1
. Therefore, the term ’Zolotarev projection’ is a slight abuse of language. Note that a similar terminology is used in
[
2
,
35
]
for the forward Wasserstein projection, which in general is not uniquely defined either.

1.4
A robust approximation of the classical martingale transport

As observed above,
(\mathrm{MOT})
can be reset through
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
with the cost (A). However, it is a theoretical observation that is of no direct aid when tackling
(\mathrm{MOT})
, for instance numerically. For one of the main contributions of this work we shall propose a bi-martingale approximation of
(\mathrm{MOT})
.

In order that such an approximation facilitates a robust computational method, it must be stable with respect to the two marginals. Assume that we start with marginals in convex order,
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
, which are then approximated by convergent sequences
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu,\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\nu
, e.g. as a result of sampling or deterministic discretization. Two scenarios may occur:

1)

either
\mu_{n}\preceq_{c}\nu_{n}
for every
n
(e.g. thanks to the Wasserstein or Zolotarev projection),

2)

or the convex order is not preserved at the approximative level.

In the first case, assume that
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
is the exact solution of the well-posed
(\mathrm{MOT})
. Under relevant continuity assumptions on the cost
c
, one expects that every accumulation point
\gamma
of the sequence
\gamma_{n}
is optimal for
(\mathrm{MOT})
, and that the total transport cost converges as well. Results of this kind are well established for the classical optimal transport
(\mathrm{OT})
, but for its martingale variant stability was proven in 1D only
[
34
,
7
,
45
]
. Meanwhile, stability has been refuted for multiple dimensions in
[
18
]
with a simple counter-example.

It is thus clear that even in the scenario 1) an approximate
(\mathrm{MOT})
formulation is the only possible path to robustness, whist scenario 2) leaves no choice. Either way, the martingale condition must be induced approximatively. The paper
[
30
]
addresses this challenge by proposing the integral constraint
\int\big|[\gamma_{x}]-x\big|\mu(dx)\leq\varepsilon
. Through a careful control of
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}
the authors have established the stability result under the assumption that
c
is Lipschitz continuous.

Our bi-martingale approximation of
(\mathrm{MOT})
will serve as an alternative for the proposal of
[
30
]
. It will involve a penalty term: for
\varepsilon>0
the three-point cost function will read,

{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)=c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}{\left\lvert z\right\rvert}^{2}.

Under suitable conditions, the martingale optimal transport problem
(\mathrm{MOT})
will be identified as the
\Gamma
-limit of the following sequence of bi-martingale problems,

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\iint_{({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{2}}\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\right\}

\displaystyle(\mathrm{M^{2}OT}_{n})

The constant
\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
, see (
1.5
), affects the functional through a vertical shift. Hence, it is not required for solving the problem – it is merely added for the purpose of
\Gamma
-convergence analysis.

Theorem 1.4
.

With a generic but fixed barycentre
b\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, assume:

•

a continuous cost
{c}\in C({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
that satisfies the following growth condition: for
p\in[0,2)
and
D_{1},D_{2}>0
,

-D_{1}\leq c(x,y)\leq D_{2}(1+{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{p}+{\left\lvert y\right\rvert}^{p});

(1.15)

•

probabilities
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
in convex order
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
;

•

sequences
\{\mu_{n}\},\{\nu_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
(not necessarily in convex order) that converge to, respectively,
\mu,\nu
in the Zolotarev-2 metric (equivalently, in the Wasserstein-2 metric);

•

a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}>0
that converges to zero slowly enough so that,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})}{\varepsilon_{n}}=0.

Then, the problems
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT}_{n})
are well-posed and they
\Gamma
-converge
4
4

4

More accurately, the
\Gamma
-limit is the bi-martingale problem
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
with the cost (A), which is equivalent to
(\mathrm{MOT})
.
to
(\mathrm{MOT})
. Accordingly, for any
(\gamma_{n},q_{n})
being a sequence of minimizers for
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT}_{n})
, up to selecting a subsequence there holds,

\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu),

where
\gamma
solves
(\mathrm{MOT})
. Moreover,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\min\,(\mathrm{M^{2}OT}_{n})=\min\,(\mathrm{MOT}).

Remark 1.5
.

Comparison to the similar result
[
30
, Theorem 2.2]
is in order:

(a)

The contribution
[
30
]
assumes Lipschitz continuity of the cost function
c
, whilst here only the growth conditions are stipulated apart from continuity.

(b)

Our result has an extension to the limit data

which are not in convex order, see below.

(c)

The paper

covers the case of multiple marginals, whilst our strategy is currently confined to one-step martingale couplings only.

(d)

Proposition 1.6

Assume the prerequisites of Theorem

, except that

1.6
Additional remarks on the similarities with the weak optimal transport

A non-linear variant of optimal transport different than
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
has attracted considerable attention in the recent years.
Consider a cost function
\breve{c}:X\times\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
that is convex in the second argument. A formulation that today is known under the name of
weak optimal transport
has been proposed in
[
29
]
and later in
[
3
]
,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\inf\left\{\int_{X}\breve{c}(x,\gamma_{x})\,\mu(dx)\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\right\}\qquad\qquad(\mathrm{WOT}).

The primary difference between the formulations
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
and
(\mathrm{WOT})
is that the latter is inherently dissymmetric. The similarities are more apparent if the class of barycentric costs is considered in
(\mathrm{WOT})
, i.e. costs
\breve{c}(x,p)
that involve
[p]
.
Then, there is a resemblance between the roles played by
[\gamma_{x}]
and
\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)
in the respective problems. For instance, if
\breve{c}(x,p)=\int c(x,y)\,p(dy)+\chi_{\{x\}}([p])
, then
(\mathrm{WOT})
problem becomes the classical martingale optimal transport problem, in a similar fashion to
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
with the cost (A).
The most meaningful parallels can be discerned for the quadratic barycentric cost,
\breve{c}(x,p)={\left\lvert p-x\right\rvert}^{2}
, as we shall now outline.

An intimate connection between the backward Wasserstein projection and the quadratic barycentric variant of
(\mathrm{WOT})
has been reported independently in
[
2
]
and
[
28
]
,

\min\limits_{\rho\preceq_{c}\nu}W^{2}_{2}(\mu,\rho)=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}\big|[\gamma_{x}]-x\big|^{2}\mu(dx).

If
\gamma
solves the barycentric
(\mathrm{WOT})
as above, then the backward Wasserstein projection can be recast as
\rho=T\#\mu
for the transport map
T(x)=[\gamma_{x}]
. Despite the uniqueness of
\rho
, in general there is a large freedom in the choice of the optimal plan
\gamma
, cf. the characterization
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
. The same result recognizes
T
as the Brenier map
\nabla\varphi
for a convex function
\varphi
that is
C^{1,1}
.

Based on Theorem
1.1
in this paper, we can deduce links of similar flavour for the forward Zolotarev projection and the quadratic bi-martingale optimal transport,

\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu}Z_{2}(\nu,\rho)=\min_{(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\frac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)-y\Big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy).

The
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
problem above shares solution
(\overline{\gamma},\overline{q})=(\overline{\gamma},\overline{\zeta}\overline{\gamma})
with the two problems in Theorem
1.1
. Accordingly,
\overline{\rho}=\overline{\zeta}\#\overline{\gamma}
is a forward Zolotarev projection, where the coupling map
\overline{\zeta}
is expressed through the gradient of a
C^{1,1}
potential
\overline{u}
, see (
1.11
). Moreover, the set of optimal plans
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
offers a lot of flexibility. In particular, for ordered data
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
, solutions
\overline{\gamma}
are exactly martingale plans, which is also the case for the barycentric
(\mathrm{WOT})
. The analogies with the results of
[
28
]
become even more crisp upon analysing the proof of Proposition
1.7
(Proposition
6.3
in the text), where the 1-Lipschitz cyclically monotone functions
\overline{\zeta}(x,{\,\cdot\,})
serve as the Brenier maps from
\gamma_{x}
to
\breve{\gamma}_{x}
.

•

By

In the text, we will make use of the following fact which is an easy consequence of Strassen theorem and Jensen inequality.
If
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a strictly convex function, then for any ordered pair
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
such that
\int\varphi\,d\mu<+\infty
, the equivalence below holds true,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,d\mu=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\,d\nu\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\mu=\nu.

(2.3)

The admissibility condition in the Zolotarev problem (
1.1
) defining
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)
admits several equivalent forms. Indeed, it is a classical result that for a continuous function
u\in C({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
we have,

u\in C^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \ \mathrm{lip}(\nabla u)\leq 1\ \ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \ -\mathrm{Id}\preceq\nabla^{2}u\preceq\mathrm{Id}\ \ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \ \tfrac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}\pm u\ \ \text{are convex}.

(2.4)

The second condition can be understood in the sense of distributions. Yet another equivalent condition was devised in
[
36
]
:
u
is differentiable and,

\displaystyle\big({u}(x)+{\left\langle\nabla{u}(x),z-x\right\rangle}\big)-\big({u}(y)+{\left\langle\nabla{u}(y),z-y\right\rangle}\big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Big)

(2.5)

for every triple
(x,y,z)\in({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{3}
, see
[
14
, Lemma 3.4]
for an alternative proof.

3
Bi-martingale formulation for a general cost

Throughout this section
\mu
and
\nu
will be probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with finite first order moments and a common barycentre

, i.e.
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Their supports will be denoted by

and

.
The cost function
{\mathsf{c}}
will be assumed to satisfy the following conditions:

(H1)

{\mathsf{c}}
is jointly lower semi-continuous on
X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and bounded from below;

(H2)

z\mapsto{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)
is convex for every
(x,y)\in X\times Y
;

(H3)

z\mapsto{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)
is equi-superlinear on

; namely,
there exists a convex and superlinear function

such that for all
(x,y,z)\in X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,

{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)\geq h(z).

The superlinerity assumption is crucial to ensure that there is a solution
(\gamma,q)
of
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
for which

, see
[
4
, Example 2.36]
. Only then the bi-martingale perspective on the couple
(\gamma,q)
is valid. However, when the supports
X,Y
are not compact, the condition (H3) fails for some important costs like (A) or (C) in Section
1.2
. To address that, we could instead work with a weakened version of this condition, to which the proofs in this section could be easily adapted:

(H3’)

there exists a convex superlinear function
\tilde{h}
integrable with respect to
\mu
and
\nu
along with scalars
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
(x,y,z)\in X\times Y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
there holds,

{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)\geq\tilde{h}(z-\alpha x-\beta y).

3.1
Existence of optimal bi-martingale plans

In this subsection we employ the direct method of the calculus of variations to show the well-posedness

Proposition 3.1

If

4
Application to convex dominance with minimum cost

In this section
\mu,\nu
are again probabilities in
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, where
b\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a generic barycentre. For
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
we take any convex lower semi-continuous function that is superlinear. The problem of finding a convex dominant that minimizes the
f
-cost reads as follows,

\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)\,\rho(dz)\,:\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\mu,\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\nu\right\}.

(4.1)

It is an infinite dimensional linear programming problem.
The plan below is to show that minimizers in (
4.1
) can be found by
solving the problem
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
for the cost
{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)=f(z)
. Beforehand, we will show that, in dimension one, an explicit formula for the optimal dominant can be proposed by appealing to the join-semilattice structure of convex order on the real line.

4.1
Optimal convex dominance on the real line

In this short subsection we work in dimension one,
d=1
, where optimal dominance problem (
4.1
) attains a closed-form solution. This stems from the join-semilattice structure of the convex order on the real line
[
42
,
33
]
. Namely, for each pair
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
there exists a unique least upper bound
\mu\vee\nu
.

In order to construct
\mu\vee\nu
, for any measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})

define,

{G}_{\mu}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x-\xi)_{+}\mu(d\xi)\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R},

where
a_{+}=\max\{a,0\}
. The function
G:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is non-negative, finite valued, and convex. One can also check that the second distributional derivative
{G}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}
, being
a priori
a non-negative Borel measure, is equal back to the probability
\mu
, thus portraying
{G}_{\mu}
as a double primitive function of
\mu
.

Note that, for each
x
,
(x-{\,\cdot\,})_{+}
is a special case of convex function. Therefore, we must have
{G}_{\mu}(x)\leq{G}_{\nu}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
whenever the convex order
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
holds true. The converse turns out to be also true provided that the barycentres match.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let us be given two measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
on the real line sharing the barycentre:
[\mu]=[\nu]
. Then,
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
if and only if
{G}_{\mu}\leq{G}_{\nu}
.

See, for instance,
[
42
, Theorem 3.A.1]
or
[
33
, Proposition 2.4]
.
∎

The idea for building the least upper bound
\mu\vee\nu
takes its shape: it should be the second distributional derivative of the function
g(x)=({G}_{\mu}\vee{G}_{\nu})(x):=\max\{{G}_{\mu}(x),{G}_{\nu}(x)\}
.
The closed formula for the optimal convex dominant in 1D can be readily given.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let us be given two measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
on the real line with the same barycentre,
[\mu]=[\nu]
. Then,

\rho=\mu\vee\nu:=({G}_{\mu}\vee{G}_{\nu})^{\prime\prime}

Moreover, if

is strictly convex, then the solution
\rho=\mu\vee\nu
is unique.

The functions
{G}_{\mu}
,
{G}_{\nu}
are convex, and by
[

we have
\lim_{x\to-\infty}{{G}_{\mu}}(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to+\infty}(-{{G}_{\mu}}(x)+x)=[\mu]
. The same is true for
{G}_{\nu}
. Then,
g={G}_{\mu}\vee{G}_{\nu}
is clearly convex, and the convergences
\lim_{x\to-\infty}{g}(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to+\infty}(-{g}(x)+x)=[\mu]=[\nu]
are inherited from those for
{G}_{\mu},{G}_{\nu}
. Again thanks to

, we have
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})

, and

Let now

, therefore

. Using the same result once more, we see that
\breve{\rho}\succeq_{c}{\rho}
, and thus
\int_{\mathbb{R}}fd\breve{\rho}\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}fd{\rho}
, furnishing optimality of
\rho
. The moreover part of the statement now follows from (
2.3
).
∎

4.2
Link with the bi-martingale optimal transport

For a pair that is admissible for
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
, namely
(\gamma,q)=(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
, a candidate for a convex dominant of
\mu
and
\nu
can be found through the push-forward,

\rho=\zeta\#\gamma.

(4.3)

In the introduction we have deduced that
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu
and
\rho\succeq_{c}\nu
based on Strassen theorem. For a more direct argument, we can exploit the equations (
1.8
) holding for a feasible couple
(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)
. Accordingly, with the disintegration
\gamma(dxdy)=\mu(dx)\otimes\gamma_{x}(dy)
, we know that
\int_{Y}\zeta(x,y)\,\gamma_{x}(dy)=x
for
\mu
-a.e.
x
. Then, for any convex function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
Jensen’s inequality gives,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(z)\,\rho(dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\varphi\big(\zeta(x,y)\big)\,\gamma(dxdy)=\int_{X}\left(\int_{Y}\varphi\big(\zeta(x,y)\big)\,\gamma_{x}(dy)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{X}\varphi\left(\int_{Y}\zeta(x,y)\,\gamma_{x}(dy)\right)\mu(dx)=\int_{X}\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx),

thus establishing
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu
. The disintegration with respect to the other marginal,
\gamma(dxdy)=\gamma_{y}(dx)\otimes\nu(dy)
, and the second equality in (
1.8
) furnish
\rho\succeq_{c}\nu
.

With the change of variables (
4.3
) in mind, we can now propose the bi-martingale reformulation of the optimal convex dominant problem (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}f\Big(\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big)\gamma(dxdy)\ :\ (\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)\right\}.

(4.4)

To prove the equivalence between the two problems, one still have to show that the set of
\rho
generated from
(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
via (
4.3
) is rich enough to produce minimizers in (
4.1
). The desired result will follow smoothly once we pass through the 3-plan formulation presented in Section
3.2
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The equality
\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom}=\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom_gammaq}
holds true, and
an admissible pair
(\gamma,q)=(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
is a solution to (
4.4
) if and only if

\rho=\zeta\#\gamma

solves the optimal convex dominance problem (
4.1
).

If, in addition,
f
is strictly convex, then to every
\rho
solving (
4.1
) one can assign at least one pair
(\gamma,q)=(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
such that
\rho=\zeta\#\gamma
.

Owing to the existence result for
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
in Corollary
3.3
, existence of an optimal dominant follows as well.

Corollary 4.4
.

For every convex superlinear function
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup+\infty
, the optimal convex dominance problem (
4.1
) admits a (in general non-unique) solution if
\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom}<+\infty
.

Remark 4.5
.

Before giving the proof, let us make several comments on the result:

(a)

A key consequence of Proposition
4.3
can be deduced when
\mu,\nu
are discrete. Since the discreteness automatically propagates to plans
\gamma
, our result shows that there are solutions
\rho
for (
4.1
) which are discrete as well (all of them are if
f
is strictly convex). Contrarily, Proposition
4.2
shows that for generic data that are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure (even with smooth densities), solutions
\rho=(G_{\mu}\vee G_{\nu})^{\prime\prime}
contain atoms: maximum of two smooth functions is typically not smooth.

(b)

Related to the previous comment is the influence of Proposition
4.3
on the computational perspectives for the optimal convex dominance problem (
4.1
). Indeed, if
\mu,\nu
are discrete, then (
4.4
) is a finite dimensional convex problem. Meanwhile, attacking (
4.1
) directly would typically involve looking for
\rho
supported on a fixed grid of points, and the accuracy of the solution would depend on its resolution, even for data as simple as in Fig.
1
. See Section
7.2
, where this observation is reflected in practice.

(c)

The formula
\rho=\zeta\#\gamma
is reminiscent of
[
1
, Proposition 4.2]
, where the Wasserstein barycentre is found as the push-forward of an optimal multi-marginal plan
[
27
]
through the linear barycentre map
T(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
. Let us briefly note that adaptation of (
4.4
) to an
n
-martingale problem, that would underlie the optimal convex dominance for
n
marginals, is straightforward.

(d)

Non-uniqueness of solutions to (
4.1
) will be demonstrated in Example
7.1
for the quadratic cost
f={\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
. It is related to the lack of displacement convexity for the forward cone
\{{\,\cdot\,}\succeq_{c}\mu\}
as opposed to the backward cone
\{{\,\cdot\,}\preceq_{c}\mu\}
, see
[
35
, Example 8.2]
.

(e)

From the definition of convex order, we can easily see that the necessary condition for the finiteness of the total cost in (
4.1
) is that
\max\{\int f\,d\mu,\int f\,d\nu\}<+\infty
. It is also sufficient if the cost function satisfies a doubling condition. Assume that
[\mu]=[\nu]=0
and there exists
C>0
such that
f(2x)\leq Cf(x)
for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Then, we can use the fact that the convolution
\mu*\nu
dominates both probabilities for convex order to make the relevant estimate.
For instance, if
f={\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{p}
for
p>1
, existence of solutions with finite cost in (
4.1
) is equivalent to finiteness of the
p
-th moments of
\mu
and
\nu
.

(f)

The strict convexity assumption in the moreover part in Proposition
4.3
is essential. Indeed, consider the trivial data
\mu=\nu=\delta_{0}
and assume that
f
is affine on a convex set
A\ni\nolinebreak 0
. Then, any centred probability
\rho
that is supported on
A
solves (
4.1
), whilst
\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)=\{(\delta_{(0,0)},0)\}
.

Take any pair
(\gamma,q)=(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)\in\Gamma Q
and define
\rho=\zeta\#\gamma
. We have,

\displaystyle\iint f\Big(\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big)\,\gamma(dxdy)=\iint f\big(\zeta(x,y)\big)\,\gamma(dxdy)=\int f(z)\,\rho(dz)\geq\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom}.

(4.5)

Taking the infimum of the left hand side with respect to
(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
furnishes the inequality
\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom_gammaq}\geq\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom}
.

Conversely, take any admissible
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
such that
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu
and
\rho\succeq_{c}\nu
. By Strassen theorem, we can select martingale plans
\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\rho)
,
\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\nu,\rho)
. Next, by virtue of the gluing lemma (cf.
[
44
, Lemma 7.6]
), a probability
{\varpi}\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
can be constructed such that
\pi_{1,3}\#{\varpi}={\gamma}_{1}
,
\pi_{2,3}\#{\varpi}={\gamma}_{2}
, and
\pi_{1,2}\#\varpi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
. Therefore, clearly
\varpi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
. Accordingly, Proposition
3.4
for the cost
{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)=f(z)
yields,

\displaystyle\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom_gammaq}=\inf\eqref{eq:3plans}\leq\iiint f(z)\,\varpi(dxdydz)=\int f(z)\,\rho(dz),

(4.6)

since
\pi_{3}\#\varpi=\rho
. Minimizing the right hand side with respect to
\rho
dominating
\mu,\nu
gets us to the converse inequality
\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom_gammaq}\leq\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom}
.
With the equality
\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom}=\inf\eqref{eq:conv_dom_gammaq}
at hand, the equivalence between optimality of
(\gamma,q)=(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)
and optimality of
\rho=\zeta\#\gamma
now follows from (
4.5
).

Assume now that
f
is strictly convex and
\rho
is optimal in (
4.1
). Construct
\varpi
as above. From (
4.6
) we can deduce optimality of
\varpi
in (
3.4
), and, thanks to the strict convexity of
f
, Proposition
3.4
guarantees the form
\varpi(dxdydz)=\gamma(dxdy)\otimes\delta_{\zeta(x,y)}(dz)
. We can now see that
\zeta\#\gamma=\pi_{3}\#\varpi=\rho
, which ends the proof.
∎

5
The quadratic case and the Zolotarev-2 distance

The results of this sections are fuelled by the main result of the paper
[
14
]
, which can be referred to as the second-order Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, where in place of the Wasserstein-1 distance (equal to the Zolotarev-1 distance) we put the Zolotarev-2 distance, i.e.

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\max\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,d(\mu-\nu)\,:\,u\in C^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \mathrm{lip}(\nabla u)\leq 1\right\}.

(5.1)

In the entire Section
5
,
\mu,\nu
will be probability distributions with equal barycentre
b\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and of finite second moments, namely
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. For such data, the maximum in the
Zolotarev problem
(
5.1
) is always attained, cf.
[
14
, Proposition 2.1]
. Solutions are not unique. In particular, adding an affine function does not change optimality of
u
, but less trivial modifications are possible, for instance far enough from the convex hull of the union of supports of
\mu,\nu
.

5.1
Second-order Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality revisited

A convenient intermediate point on the path towards the second-order Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality is the optimal convex dominant problem for the quadratic cost
f={\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
,

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu):=\min\Big\{m_{2}(\rho)\,:\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\mu,\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\nu\Big\},

(5.2)

where
m_{2}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\left\lvert z\right\rvert}^{2}\rho(dz)
. Since
\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
is always finite for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
(cf. Remark
4.5
(e)), solutions to (
5.2
) exist by Corollary
4.4
. The dual problem entails maximizing with respect to a pair of convex potentials. Then, its non-trivial transformation founded on the fact that convexification preserves semi-concavity, has lead to the key equality of the paper
[
14
]
.

Theorem 5.1
(
[
14
]
, Theorem 1.3).

For any pair of probabilities
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
the following equality holds true,

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{m_{2}(\mu)+m_{2}(\nu)}{2}.

(5.3)

Expressing the Zolotarev-2 distance by means of the bi-martingale transport is readily at our disposal thanks to Proposition
4.3
which states that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)=\min\left\{\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)\ :\ (\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)\right\}.

(5.4)

It is the
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
problem for the cost
{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)={\left\lvert z\right\rvert}^{2}
. Passage to the cost
{\mathsf{c}}(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}
, advertised as cost (B) in the introduction, is a straightforward use of the properties of the set
\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
. Together with (
5.4
), the following result yields the first part of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 5.2
.

We have the equality,

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\!\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)-x\Big|^{2}\!\!\!+\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)-y\Big|^{2}\bigg)\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)\right\}

and the set of minimizers is identical to the set of minimizers in (
5.4
).

Remark 5.3
.

Note that this result could be recovered directly from
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
by translating the 3-plan framework used therein to the one of
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
via Proposition
3.4
.

Using the fact that
\pi_{1}\#\gamma=\mu
and
\pi_{1}\#q=x\mu
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\iint\frac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}-x\Big|^{2}d\gamma

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)-\iint{\left\langle x,q(dxdy)\right\rangle}+\iint\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)-\frac{m_{2}(\mu)}{2}.

(5.5)

Similarly,
\iint\frac{1}{2}|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}-y|^{2}d\gamma=\iint\frac{1}{2}|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}|^{2}d\gamma-\frac{m_{2}(\nu)}{2}
. We thus see that the cost functional in the asserted equality coincides with the one in (
5.4
) up to the additive constant
\frac{1}{2}\big(m_{2}(\mu)+m_{2}(\nu)\big)
. Readily, the equality follows by Theorem
5.1
.
∎

5.2
Characterizing optimal bi-martingale plans in the quadratic case

We will characterize the set of plans
(\gamma,q)
which are minimal for the quadratic case, that is
(\gamma,q)
belonging to the set,

\displaystyle\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu):=

\displaystyle\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\Big|\frac{d{q}}{d\gamma}\Big|^{2}d\gamma

(5.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)-x\Big|^{2}+\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)-y\Big|^{2}\bigg)\gamma(dxdy),

where the equality of the two sets of minimizers is due to Corollary
5.2
.
In the sequel the overbar
\overline{{\,\cdot\,}}
will stand for optimality in the context of the quadratic case.

To any
\overline{u}\in C^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
solving the Zolotarev problem (
5.1
) for a pair
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, we assign the Lipschitz continuous coupling map,

\overline{\zeta}(x,y):=\frac{x+y}{2}+\frac{\nabla\overline{u}(x)-\nabla\overline{u}(y)}{2},

(5.7)

and then the set of bi-martingale plans with respect to to that map (cf. (
1.12
) for an equivalent definition),

\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu):=\Big\{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\,:\,\overline{\zeta}\gamma\in Q(\mu,\nu)\Big\}.

(5.8)

The following result proves the equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) in Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The set of optimal bi-martingale plans in the quadratic case equals,

\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)=\Big\{(\gamma,q)\,:\,\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu),\ \ q=\overline{\zeta}\gamma\Big\},

(5.9)

where
\overline{\zeta}
and
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
can be defined for any solution
\overline{u}
of the Zolotarev problem (
5.1
). In particular, the set
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
is non-empty, and it does not depend on the choice of the possibly non-unique maximizer
\overline{u}
.

Moreover, for any
\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
,

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big|\overline{\zeta}(x,y)-x\big|^{2}+\big|\overline{\zeta}(x,y)-y\big|^{2}\Big)\gamma(dxdy),

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\!\int_{Y}\big|\overline{\zeta}(x,y)\big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy).

(5.11)

First we prove the inclusion
\subset
. By the admissibility in the Zolotarev problem, we have
\mathrm{lip}(\nabla\overline{u})\leq 1
. According to (
2.5
), it means that for all
(x,y,z)\in({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{3}
,

\displaystyle\overline{u}(x)-\overline{u}(y)+{\left\langle\nabla\overline{u}(x),z-x\right\rangle}-{\left\langle\nabla\overline{u}(y),z-y\right\rangle}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Big).

One checks easily that
z=\overline{\zeta}(x,y)
is the unique minimizer of the gap in this inequality. In particular, if
z\neq\overline{\zeta}(x,y)
, then the inequality must be strict. Take a pair
(\gamma,q)=(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
. Testing (
2.2
) with
\varphi=\overline{u}=-\psi
and
\Phi=\nabla\overline{u}=-\Psi
(which is valid since
\overline{u}
and
\nabla\overline{u}
are of at most quadratic and linear growth, respectively, while
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
\iint{\left\lvert\zeta\right\rvert}^{2}d\gamma<+\infty
), we get,

\displaystyle\int\overline{u}\,d(\mu-\nu)

\displaystyle=\iint\Big(\overline{u}(x)-\overline{u}(y)+{\left\langle\nabla\overline{u}(x),\zeta(x,y)-x\right\rangle}-{\left\langle\nabla\overline{u}(y),\zeta(x,y)-y\right\rangle}\Big)\,\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle\leq\iint\frac{1}{2}\Big({\left\lvert\zeta(x,y)-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert\zeta(x,y)-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Big)\,\gamma(dxdy).

The left hand side is equal to
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)
by maximality of
\overline{u}
. Thus, owing to Corollary
5.2
, the optimality
(\gamma,\zeta\gamma)\in\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
implies that the inequality above is an equality. This is possible only if
\zeta(x,y)=\overline{\zeta}(x,y)
for
\gamma
-a.e.
(x,y)
, which proves the inclusion
\subset
.

Conversely, take any
\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
and define
q=\overline{\zeta}\gamma
. By definition of the set
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
, we have
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
and
q\in Q(\mu,\nu)
. Recalling the definition of
\overline{\zeta}
, we compute,

\displaystyle\iint\big|\overline{\zeta}\big|^{2}d\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle x+\nabla\overline{u}(x),\overline{\zeta}(x,y)\big\rangle\,\gamma(dxdy)+\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle y-\nabla\overline{u}(y),\overline{\zeta}(x,y)\big\rangle\,\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle x+\nabla\overline{u}(x),q(dxdy)\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle y-\nabla\overline{u}(y),q(dxdy)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\big\langle x+\nabla\overline{u}(x),x\big\rangle\,\mu(dx)+\frac{1}{2}\int\big\langle y-\nabla\overline{u}(y),y\big\rangle\,\nu(dy),

(5.12)

where the last equality is owing to
q\in Q(\mu,\nu)
. We have thus showed that the value of the integral
\iint\big|\overline{\zeta}(x,y)\big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)
is independent of the choice of
\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
. Take now a pair
(\overline{\gamma},\overline{q})\in\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
, which always exists. By the already proven inclusion
\subset
, there must hold
\overline{q}=\overline{\zeta}\overline{\gamma}
, while
\overline{\gamma}
must be an element of
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
. Altogether, we obtain,

\iint\Big|\frac{d{q}}{d\gamma}\Big|^{2}d\gamma=\iint\big|\overline{\zeta}\big|^{2}d\gamma=\iint\big|\overline{\zeta}\big|^{2}d\overline{\gamma}=\iint\Big|\frac{d\overline{q}}{d\overline{\gamma}}\Big|^{2}d\overline{\gamma}=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu),

where the final equality is by optimality of
(\overline{\gamma},\overline{q})
. This renders
(\gamma,q)
an element of
\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
as well, and the inclusion
\supset
is established. The independence of
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
with respect to the choice of the optimal potential
\overline{u}
now follows by the independence of
\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Unlike for the set
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
, the possible non-uniqueness of the optimal potential
\overline{u}
can induce the non-uniqueness of the coupling map
\overline{\zeta}
, e.g. far away from the set
\spt(\mu)\otimes\spt(\nu)
. However, the characterization (
5.9
) implies that
\overline{\zeta}
is uniquely determined on the set
\bigcup_{\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)}\spt(\gamma)
.

In the following, we shall show that the set
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)
is exactly the set of martingale plans
\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)
once we have the convex order
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
. This characterization will rely on the fact that
\overline{u}=-\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
solves the Zolotarev problem in this case, which gives
\overline{\zeta}(x,y)=y=\pi_{2}(x,y)
.

Proposition 5.6
.

For a pair
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

the convex order relation
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
holds true;

(ii)

\overline{u}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}
solves the Zolotarev problem (
5.1
) or, equivalently,

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)}{2};

(5.13)

(iii)

\overline{\rho}=\nu
is the (unique) solution of the minimal convex dominance problem (
5.2
) or, equivalently,

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)=m_{2}(\nu);

(iv)

the set of optimal bi-martingale plans equals
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)=\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)
or, equivalently,

\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)=\Big\{(\gamma,q)\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu),\ q=y\gamma\Big\}.

(5.14)

To prove the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) assume the convex order
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
.
Take a competitor
u
in the Zolotarev problem (
5.1
). Then,

\varphi=u+\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
is a convex function, see (
2.4
), and so,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,d(\mu-\nu)=\frac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)}{2}+\int\varphi\,d(\mu-\nu)\leq\frac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)}{2}.

By taking the supremum on the left hand side with respect to admissible functions
u
we see that
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq\frac{1}{2}\big(m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)\big)
. Clearly, the inequality above is saturated for
\varphi=0
, which corresponds to
u=\overline{u}=-\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
. This gives the condition (ii).

Next, we show that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Assume that
\overline{\rho}
is a solution to (
5.2
), in particular
\overline{\rho}\succeq_{c}\mu
and
\overline{\rho}\succeq_{c}\nu
. Theorem
5.1
combined with (
5.13
) says that
\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)=m_{2}(\nu)
. Thus,
\int{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}d\overline{\rho}=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)=\int{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}d\nu
and, since
{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
is strictly convex, from (
2.3
) we deduce that
\overline{\rho}=\nu
. This necessary optimality condition turns sufficient if we take into account that (
5.2
) always has a solution. We have thus arrived at (iii).

Showing (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is straightforward: every competitor
\rho
in (
5.2
) dominates
\mu
in the convex order, and so this also applies to
\overline{\rho}=\nu
in the case when (iii) holds true.

We have so far proved the equivalences (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii). The implication (iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) is an easy consequence of Strassen theorem. Indeed, since
\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
is non-empty, (iv) implies that there exists a martingale plan from
\mu
to
\nu
. Finally, to show that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iv) we exploit Proposition
5.4
. If
\overline{u}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}
is the solution of (
5.1
), then the formula (
5.7
) yields
\overline{\zeta}(x,y)=y
. From the definition (

) of the set

, we see that the first condition, i.e.
\int\overline{\zeta}(x,y)\,\gamma_{x}(dy)=x
, is now the classical martingale condition

, whilst the second condition is void:
\int\overline{\zeta}(x,y)\,\gamma_{y}(dx)=\int y\,\gamma_{y}(dx)=y
for any kernel
\gamma_{y}(dx)
. We have thus showed that
\overline{\Gamma}(\mu,\nu)=\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)

∎

5.3
Zolotarev projection onto the cone of dominating probabilities

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
we define the
Zolotarev projection
, being a set-valued operator

\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}:\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\to 2^{\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}
,

\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu):=\operatorname*{arg\,min}\limits_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu}Z_{2}(\nu,\rho).

(5.15)

The well-posedness of the Zolotarev projection can be shown directly, and here is the outline of how to do it. If
\rho_{n}
is a minimizing sequence, then the estimate
2Z_{2}(\nu,\rho_{n})\geq m_{2}(\rho_{n})-m_{2}(\nu_{n})
yields tightness of the sequence
\{{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}\rho_{n}\}
. This is enough to ensure the stability of
\rho_{n}\succeq_{c}\nu
for the weak limit points
\overline{\rho}
, see e.g.
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
. The same tightness also allows to adapt the proofs of Section
3.1
to obtain the lower semi-continuity
\liminf_{n}Z_{2}(\nu,\rho_{n})\geq Z_{2}(\nu,\overline{\rho})
, thus proving that
\overline{\rho}\in\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
.

We can skip the details of this reasoning since, here, the non-emptiness of
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
will emerge as the consequence of an unexpected feature of the Zolotarev projection: swapping the roles of
\mu
and
\nu
does not change the resulting set, and this set equals to the common convex dominants of minimal second moment
6
6

6

The choice for the notation
\mu\curlyvee\nu
is to acknowledge that on the real line this set is equal to
\{\mu\vee\nu\}
. It must be emphasized that convex order in
d>1
does not enjoy the structure of semilattice, hence
\mu\curlyvee\nu
is not the least upper bound.
,

\mu\curlyvee\nu:=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu,\ \rho\succeq_{c}\nu}m_{2}(\rho),

which is a non-empty set.
The main step towards connecting the Zoloterev projection and optimal quadratic convex dominance consists in the following observation,

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=Z_{2}(\mu,\overline{\rho})+Z_{2}(\overline{\rho},\nu)\qquad\qquad\forall\,\overline{\rho}\in\mu\curlyvee\nu.

(5.16)

To see it, we first exploit the dominances
\overline{\rho}\succeq_{c}\mu
,
\overline{\rho}\succeq_{c}\nu
, which, by virtue of Proposition
5.6
, furnish the equalities

Z_{2}(\mu,\overline{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}\big(m_{2}(\overline{\rho})-m_{2}(\mu)\big)
and
Z_{2}(\nu,\overline{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}\big(m_{2}(\overline{\rho})-m_{2}(\nu)\big)
. By definition of the set
\mu\curlyvee\nu
we have
m_{2}(\overline{\rho})=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
, and so the desired identity follows from Theorem
5.1
.

The following result completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.7
.

For any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
we have the equalities between the three sets,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)=\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\nu}(\mu)=\mu\curlyvee\nu.

In one dimension the projection set always consists of exactly one element being the least upper bound (
4.2
) for convex order:
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)=\{\mu\vee\nu\}
.
In dimension
d>1
,
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
is a non-empty convex set that may contain more than one element.

Moreover, for any
\overline{\rho}\in\mu\curlyvee\nu
we have,

\displaystyle\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu}Z_{2}(\nu,\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{2}(\overline{\rho})-m_{2}(\nu)}{2}=\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{1}{2}\big(m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)\big)}{2},

(5.17)

\displaystyle\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\nu}Z_{2}(\mu,\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{2}(\overline{\rho})-m_{2}(\mu)}{2}=\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{1}{2}\big(m_{2}(\mu)-m_{2}(\nu)\big)}{2}.

(5.18)

Remark 5.8
.

The equalities (
1.14
) announced in the introduction, which involve the convex order index

\alpha_{\succeq_{c}}(\nu\,|\,\mu)=\frac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\mu)}{2Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}
, are now a simple corollary from the equalities (
5.17
), (
5.18
).

We will show the equalities
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)=\mu\curlyvee\nu
and (
5.17
), and the other two will follow by symmetric arguments. The non-emptiness and convexity of
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
, as well as its multiplicity in many dimensions, will be then a consequence of Corollary
4.4
, whilst the equality
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)=\{\mu\vee\nu\}
in 1D will follow from Proposition
4.2
.

Let us choose a measure
\overline{\rho}\in\mu\curlyvee\nu
, whilst by
\rho
denote any probability in
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
such that
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu
, i.e.
\rho
is a competitor in (
5.15
). Next, choose any optimal dominant
\breve{\rho}
for the pair
\nu
,
\rho
, that is
\breve{\rho}\in\nu\curlyvee\rho
.
By transitivity, we have,

\breve{\rho}\succeq_{c}\nu,\qquad\quad\breve{\rho}\succeq_{c}{\rho}\succeq_{c}\mu.

(5.19)

This renders
\breve{\rho}
a competitor for the minimal common convex dominant for the pair
\mu
,
\nu
as well. Since
\overline{\rho}
was an optimal one, we must have
m_{2}(\breve{\rho})\geq m_{2}(\overline{\rho})
.
Kicking off with the identity (
5.16
) written for the pair
\nu,\rho
and its optimal dominant
\breve{\rho}
, we are led to,

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\nu,{\rho})=Z_{2}(\nu,\breve{\rho})+Z_{2}(\rho,\breve{\rho})\geq Z_{2}(\nu,\breve{\rho})=\tfrac{m_{2}(\breve{\rho})-m_{2}(\nu)}{2}\geq\tfrac{m_{2}(\overline{\rho})-m_{2}(\nu)}{2}=Z_{2}(\nu,\overline{\rho}),

(5.20)

where we made use of the formula (
5.13
) for the Zolotarev distance between ordered measures: first for
\nu\preceq_{c}\breve{\rho}
, and then for
\nu\preceq_{c}\overline{\rho}
. Since
\rho
was an arbitrary competitor in the projection problem (
5.15
), the above chain of inequalities proves that
\overline{\rho}\succeq_{c}\mu
is a minimizer, i.e.
\overline{\rho}\in\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
.

The above establishes the inclusion
\mu\curlyvee\nu\subset\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
and the first equality in (
5.17
), whilst the second follows from Theorem
5.1
and the fact that
m_{2}(\overline{\rho})=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
.
To get the converse inclusion, assume that
\rho\in\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
. This is equivalent to saying that the two inequalities in (
5.20
) are equalities. The second of those equalities implies that
m_{2}(\breve{\rho})=m_{2}(\overline{\rho})
, which means that
\breve{\rho}\in\mu\curlyvee\nu
thanks to (
5.19
). The first equality requires that
Z_{2}(\rho,\breve{\rho})=0
, and so that
\rho=\breve{\rho}
. This furnishes the inclusion
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)\subset\mu\curlyvee\nu
and completes the proof.
∎

6
Approximation of the martingale optimal transport

This section focuses on employing the framework of bi-martingale optimal transport
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
to finding approximate solutions of the classical two-period martingale optimal transport problem
[
8
]
,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\inf\left\{\iint_{({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{2}}c(x,y)\,\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)\right\}.\qquad\quad(\mathrm{MOT})

In the whole section,
\mu
and
\nu
are elements of
\mathcal{P}^{b}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, i.e. they have finite second moments and a fixed barycentre

A priori

that converge to, respectively,
\mu,\nu
in the Zolotarev metric

(or equivalently in the Wasserstein

metric). For a continuous cost function

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\iint_{({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{2}}\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\right\}

is a sequence of positive numbers. In what follows, we show that

, the
\Gamma
-limit problem becomes

6.1
The
\Gamma
-convergence result

\displaystyle F_{n}(\gamma,q)

\displaystyle:=\iint_{({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{2}}\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)\gamma(dxdy)+\chi_{\Gamma Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}(\gamma,q).

Then, the sequence

Under the prerequisites of the theorem,

Proposition 6.3

For any plan

\displaystyle\int_{X}W_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{x},\gamma^{1}_{x})\,\mu(dx)=\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\mu}2Z_{2}(\nu,\rho)=2Z_{2}(\nu,\overline{\rho}),

\displaystyle\int_{Y}W_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{y},\gamma^{2}_{y})\,\nu(dy)=\min_{\rho\succeq_{c}\nu}2Z_{2}(\mu,\rho)=2Z_{2}(\mu,\overline{\rho}),

where the transport kernels constitute the following disintegrations:
\gamma(dxdy)=\mu(dx)\otimes\gamma_{x}(dy)

6.2
Proof of the lim inf inequality and compactness

In order to exploit the
\Gamma
-convergence result in Theorem

Firstly, we need to show the tightness of the sequence of positive measures

Owing to the convergences
\mu_{n}\to\mu
,

Readily, the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})=\min\left\{\iint\bigg(c+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)d\gamma\,:\,(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\right\}

. We can choose

as in Proposition

∎

(\varphi,\Phi)\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, and the same holds for
\nu_{n},\nu
. Accordingly, the weak convergences of
\gamma_{n}
and
q_{n}
furnishes
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
and
q\in Q(\mu,\nu)
, see (
2.2
). In order to conclude that
(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
, the absolute continuity
q\ll\gamma
will be shown in the sequel.

Upon fixing
\varepsilon>0
, we use the fact that
(\gamma_{n},q_{n})\in\Gamma Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
to show the following chain of inequalities,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}

\displaystyle F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})=\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\iint\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq_{n}}{d\gamma_{n}}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)\gamma_{n}(dxdy)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\iint\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq_{n}}{d\gamma_{n}}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)\gamma_{n}(dxdy)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\iint\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq_{n}}{d\gamma_{n}}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)\Big)\bigg)\gamma_{n}(dxdy)

\displaystyle\qquad+\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\iint\bigg(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big(\Big|\frac{dq_{n}}{d\gamma_{n}}(x,y)\Big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)\Big)\bigg)\gamma_{n}(dxdy)

\displaystyle\geq\begin{cases}\iint\Big(c(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\big(\big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)\big)\Big)\gamma(dxdy)&\text{if \ \ $\gamma\geq 0$ and $q\ll\gamma$,}\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The arguments behind the subsequent inequalities are as follows:

-

to pass to the second line we recall that
\iint\big|\frac{dq_{n}}{d\gamma_{n}}(x,y)\big|^{2}\gamma_{n}(dxdy)\geq\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
, see (
5.4
);

-

to pass to the third line we simply acknowledge that
\gamma_{n}
are probabilities;

-

to pass to the forth line we acknowledge the formula (
5.3
) to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})=Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)-Z_{2}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu)-m_{2}(\mu_{n})}{2}+\tfrac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\nu_{n})}{2}

which converges to zero since
\mu_{n},\nu_{n}
converge to
\mu,\nu
in the
Z_{2}
metric, cf. (
1.3
);

-

to pass to the last line we use the lower semi-continuity result in Proposition
3.1
.

Since the case when
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})<+\infty
is the only non-trivial scenario, from the chain of inequalities above we can deduce that indeed
q\ll\gamma
. This establishes that
(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
. With that information at hand, we know that
\iint\big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)\geq\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
, and sending
\varepsilon
to zero gives,

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})\geq\iint c(x,y)\,\gamma(dxdy)+\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\iint\big|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}(x,y)\big|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu),\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Considering the definition of the set
\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
, see (
5.6
), we recognize the right hand side above as
F(\gamma,q)
exactly. This concludes the proof of the
\liminf
inequality.
∎

6.3
Proof of the lim sup inequality

Let us now construct a recovery sequence
(\gamma_{n},q_{n})
for a pair
(\gamma,q)
. The task is trivial unless
F(\gamma,q)<+\infty
, thus we can assume that
(\gamma,q)\in\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
, cf. the definition (
5.6
).
In particular,
(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\nu)
and,

\iint|\zeta(x,y)|^{2}\gamma(dxdy)=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu),\qquad\text{where}\quad\zeta=\frac{dq}{d\gamma}.

(6.3)

For each
n
take any pairs
(\hat{\gamma}_{n},\hat{q}_{n})\in\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\mu_{n})
and
(\check{\gamma}_{n},\check{q}_{n})\in\overline{\Gamma Q}(\nu,\nu_{n})
. Namely,
(\hat{\gamma}_{n},\hat{q}_{n})\in\Gamma Q(\mu,\mu_{n})
and
(\check{\gamma}_{n},\check{q}_{n})\in\Gamma Q(\nu,\nu_{n})
, while

\displaystyle\iint\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big|\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big|^{2}+\big|\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-\xi\big|^{2}\Big)\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)=Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n}),\qquad\hat{\zeta}_{n}=\frac{d\hat{q}_{n}}{d\hat{\gamma}_{n}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\iint\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big|\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big|^{2}+\big|\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-\eta\big|^{2}\Big)\check{\gamma}_{n}(dyd\eta)=Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n}),\qquad\check{\zeta}_{n}=\frac{d\check{q}_{n}}{d\check{\gamma}_{n}}.

(6.5)

We will now perform a gluing of the measures
\gamma
,
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
, and
\check{\gamma}_{n}
. To that end, let us perform the disintegrations,

\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)=\mu(dx)\otimes\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n}(d\xi),\qquad\check{\gamma}_{n}(dyd\eta)=\nu(dy)\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n}(d\eta),

where
x\mapsto\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n}
and
y\mapsto\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n}
are Borel measurable measure-valued maps. The gluing is now achieved using the generalized product measure,

\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta):=\gamma(dxdy)\otimes\big(\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n}\big)(d\xi d\eta).

Its marginals satisfy
\pi_{1,3}\#\lambda_{n}=\gamma
,
\pi_{1,2}\#\lambda_{n}=\hat{\gamma}_{n}
,
\pi_{3,4}\#\lambda_{n}=\check{\gamma}_{n}
, whilst
\pi_{2,4}\#\lambda_{n}
belongs to
\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
. The latter is thus a candidate for the recovery sequence,

\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta):=\pi_{2,4}\#\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta).

In order to build
q_{n}
, we first define the map
z_{n}:({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,

z_{n}(x,\xi,y,\eta):=\zeta(x,y)+\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)+\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-x-y.

Finally, with
(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\lambda^{n}_{\xi,\eta}
being the Borel map in the disintegration
\lambda_{n}=\gamma_{n}\otimes\lambda^{n}_{\xi,\eta}
, we define

\displaystyle q_{n}:=\zeta_{n}\gamma_{n},\qquad\text{where}\quad\zeta_{n}(\xi,\eta):=\iint z_{n}(x,\xi,y,\eta)\,\lambda^{n}_{\xi,\eta}(dxdy).

In the next two lemmas we show that the sequence
(\gamma_{n},q_{n})
is admissible, i.e.
F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})<+\infty
, and that it weakly converges to
(\gamma,q)
.

Lemma 6.5
.

For every
n
there holds
(\gamma_{n},q_{n})\in\Gamma Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
.

We know that
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
, but the condition
q_{n}\in Q(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
must be verified. Taking a function
\Phi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, we check the first marginal. Observe that, since the Borel functions
\zeta,\zeta_{n},\hat{\zeta}_{n},\check{\zeta}_{n}
are square-integrable with respect to
\lambda_{n}
, all the integrals below are well defined and finite,

\displaystyle\iint\big\langle\Phi(\xi),q_{n}(d\xi d\eta)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint\big\langle\Phi(\xi),\zeta_{n}(\xi,\eta)\big\rangle\,\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta)

\displaystyle=\iint\Big\langle\Phi(\xi),\iint z_{n}(x,\xi,y,\eta)\,\lambda^{n}_{\xi,\eta}(dxdy)\Big\rangle\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta)

\displaystyle=\iiiint\big\langle\Phi(\xi),\zeta(x,y)+\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)+\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-x-y\big\rangle\,\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle=\iint\big\langle\Phi(\xi),\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)\big\rangle\,\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)+\iint\big\langle\hat{\Phi}_{n}(x),\zeta(x,y)-x\big\rangle\,\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle\qquad+\iiint\big\langle\hat{\Phi}_{n}(x),\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\,\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n}(d\eta)\,\gamma_{y}(dx)\,\nu(dy)

\displaystyle=\iint\big\langle\Phi(\xi),\hat{q}_{n}(dxd\xi)\big\rangle+\iint\big\langle\hat{\Phi}_{n}(x),\zeta(x,y)-x\big\rangle\,\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle\qquad+\iint\big\langle\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(y),\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\,\check{\gamma}_{n}(dyd\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\big\langle\Phi(\xi),\xi\big\rangle\,\mu_{n}(d\xi),

where we used the disintegration
\gamma(dxdy)=\gamma_{y}(dx)\otimes\nu(dy)
and we introduced the bounded Borel measurable vector functions
\hat{\Phi}_{n}(x)=\int\Phi(\xi)\,\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n}(d\xi)
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(y)=\int\hat{\Phi}_{n}(x)\,{\gamma}_{y}(dx)
. The last equality acknowledges that:
\hat{\zeta}_{n}\hat{\gamma}_{n}=\hat{q}_{n}\in Q(\mu,\mu_{n})
,
\zeta\gamma=q\in Q(\mu,\nu)
, and
\check{\zeta}_{n}\check{\gamma}_{n}=\check{q}_{n}\in Q(\nu,\nu_{n})
, cf. (
2.2
). Checking the second marginal is similar.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

The sequences
\gamma_{n},q_{n}
weakly converge to
\gamma,q
, respectively.

It is clear that sequences
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
and
\check{\gamma}_{n}
converge weakly to
(x,x)\#\mu
and
(y,y)\#\nu
, but we need a stronger result. We shall prove that we can always extract a subsequence
(\hat{\gamma}_{n_{k}},\check{\gamma}_{n_{k}})
such that,

\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n_{k}}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup\delta_{(x,y)}\qquad\text{for $\gamma$-a.e. $(x,y)$}.

(6.6)

To that end, we use (
6.4
), (
6.5
). By convexity we have
{\left\lvert c-a\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert c-b\right\rvert}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert a-b\right\rvert}^{2}
, hence

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\iint\frac{1}{4}|x-\xi|^{2}\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)+\iint\frac{1}{4}|y-\eta|^{2}\check{\gamma}_{n}(dyd\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\iiiint\Big(|x-\xi|^{2}+|y-\eta|^{2}\Big)\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\iint\left(\iint\Big(|x-\xi|^{2}+|y-\eta|^{2}\Big)(\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n})(d\xi d\eta)\right)\gamma(dxdy)

Since
Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})
and
Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})
converge to zero, the function
(x,y)\mapsto\iint\big(|x-\xi|^{2}+|y-\eta|^{2}\big)\,(\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n})(d\xi d\eta)
converges to zero in
L^{1}_{\gamma}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Thus, we can select a sequence of indices
n_{k}
for which the latter integral converges to zero for
\gamma
-a.e.
(x,y)
. This means that
W_{2}(\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n_{k}}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n_{k}},\delta_{(x,y)})\to 0
in the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{2})
for
\gamma
-a.e.
(x,y)
, and so, in particular, (
6.6
) holds true. With this convergence at hand, the fact that
\gamma_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup\gamma
follows easily. Indeed, for a function
\varphi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R})
, we have,

\displaystyle\iint\varphi(\xi,\eta)\gamma_{n_{k}}(d\xi d\eta)=\iiiint\varphi(\xi,\eta)\lambda_{n_{k}}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\iint\left(\iint\varphi(\xi,\eta)\,(\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n_{k}}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n_{k}})(d\xi d\eta)\right)\!\gamma(dxdy)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\ \iint\varphi(x,y)\gamma(dxdy),

where we used (
6.6
) and the dominated convergence theorem. Next, for a vector function
\Phi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
we have,

\displaystyle\iint\big\langle\Phi(\xi,\eta),q_{n_{k}}(d\xi d\eta)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\iiiint\big\langle\Phi(\xi,\eta),\zeta(x,y)+\hat{\zeta}_{n_{k}}(x,\xi)+\check{\zeta}_{n_{k}}(y,\eta)-x-y\big\rangle\,d\lambda_{n_{k}}

\displaystyle=\iint\bigg\langle\iint\Phi(\xi,\eta)\,(\hat{\gamma}_{x}^{n_{k}}\otimes\check{\gamma}_{y}^{n_{k}})(d\xi d\eta)\,,\,\zeta(x,y)\bigg\rangle\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle\qquad+\iiiint\big\langle\Phi(\xi,\eta),\hat{\zeta}_{n_{k}}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\lambda_{n_{k}}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\iiiint\big\langle\Phi(\xi,\eta),\check{\zeta}_{n_{k}}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\lambda_{n_{k}}(dxd\xi dyd\eta).

Using (
6.6
) again, we show that the first integral after the last equality sign converges to
\iint\big\langle\Phi(x,y),\zeta(x,y)\big\rangle\gamma(dxdy)
, being equal to
\iint\big\langle\Phi(x,y),q(dxdy)\big\rangle
. Meanwhile, using (
6.4
) we estimate the second integral, starting with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\iiiint\big\langle\Phi(\xi,\eta),\hat{\zeta}_{n_{k}}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\lambda_{n_{k}}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\iiiint{\left\lvert\Phi(\xi,\eta)\right\rvert}^{2}\lambda_{n_{k}}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint\big|\hat{\zeta}_{n_{k}}(x,\xi)-x\big|^{2}\hat{\gamma}_{n_{k}}(dxd\xi)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sqrt{2}\,\Arrowvert\Phi\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}}\Big(Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n_{k}})\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

Similarly, the third integral can be bounded by
\sqrt{2}\,\Arrowvert\Phi\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}}(Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n_{k}}))^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Since
Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n_{k}})\to 0
and
Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n_{k}})\to 0
, this establishes the convergence
q_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup q
.

We have showed that the desired convergence holds on a subsequence. However, a convergent sub-subsequence could be extracted from any subsequence of
(\gamma_{n},q_{n})
. Since the limits are always the same and equal
(\gamma,q)
, the convergence must hold for the whole sequence as well.
∎

The
\limsup
condition can be readily proven.

For every
n
we have,

\displaystyle F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})

\displaystyle=\iint\bigg(c(\xi,\eta)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\big|\zeta_{n}(\xi,\eta)\big|^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\Big)\bigg)\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta)

\displaystyle=\iint\bigg(c(\xi,\eta)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\left|\iint z_{n}(x,\xi,y,\eta)\,\lambda_{\xi,\eta}^{n}(dxdy)\right|^{2}\bigg)\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta)-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\iiiint\bigg(c(\xi,\eta)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\big|z_{n}(x,\xi,y,\eta)\big|^{2}\bigg)\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n}),

where to pass to the last line we used Jensen’s inequality. To continue, we expand the formula for
z_{n}
, but first we also make use of the formula (
5.3
) and the fact that
Z_{2}
is a metric in order to make the following estimate,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})

\displaystyle=Z_{2}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu_{n})+m_{2}(\nu_{n})}{2}

\displaystyle\geq Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)-Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})-Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu_{n})+m_{2}(\nu_{n})}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)-Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})-Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})-\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu)-m_{2}(\mu_{n})}{2}-\tfrac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\nu_{n})}{2}.

Carrying on, we obtain,

\displaystyle F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})\leq\iiiint\bigg(c(\xi,\eta)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\Big(\Big|\zeta(x,y)+\big(\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big)+\big(\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big)\Big|^{2}\Big)\bigg)d\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\bigg[-\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)+Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu)-m_{2}(\mu_{n})}{2}+\tfrac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\nu_{n})}{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=\iint c(\xi,\eta)\,\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta)+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\bigg[\iint{\left\lvert\zeta(x,y)\right\rvert}^{2}\gamma(dxdy)+\iint|\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x|^{2}\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\iint|\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y|^{2}\check{\gamma}_{n}(dyd\eta)+2\iiiint\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad+2\iiiint\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad\quad+2\iiiint\big\langle\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x,\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)+Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu)-m_{2}(\mu_{n})}{2}+\tfrac{m_{2}(\nu)-m_{2}(\nu_{n})}{2}\bigg].

Let us now compute or bound the integrals in the square brackets. From (
6.3
) we know that
\iint{\left\lvert\zeta(x,y)\right\rvert}^{2}\gamma(dxdy)=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
, while, following the steps of (
5.5
), we obtain by optimality of
(\hat{\gamma}_{n},\hat{\zeta}_{n}\hat{\gamma}_{n})
and
(\check{\gamma}_{n},\check{\zeta}_{n}\check{\gamma}_{n})
,

\displaystyle\iint|\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x|^{2}\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)=\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu_{n})-m_{2}(\mu)=Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\mu_{n})-m_{2}(\mu)}{2},

\displaystyle\iint|\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y|^{2}\check{\gamma}_{n}(dyd\eta)=\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu_{n})-m_{2}(\nu)=Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})+\tfrac{m_{2}(\nu_{n})-m_{2}(\nu)}{2},

where we also used the formula (
5.3
).
Next, we find that,

\displaystyle\iiiint\!\!\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle d\lambda_{n}=\!\!\iiiint\!\!\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\check{\gamma}^{n}_{y}(d\eta)\,\hat{\gamma}^{n}_{x}(d\xi)\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\!\!\iiint\!\!\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\hat{\gamma}^{n}_{x}(d\xi)\gamma(dxdy)=\!\!\iiint\!\!\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\hat{\gamma}^{n}_{x}(d\xi)\gamma_{x}(dy)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\!\iint\!\big\langle x,\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\hat{\gamma}^{n}_{x}(d\xi)\mu(dx)=\!\iint\!\big\langle x,\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big\rangle\hat{\gamma}_{n}(dxd\xi)=0.

Above we used the disintegration
\gamma(dxdy)=\mu(dx)\otimes\gamma_{x}(dy)
, and then we passed to the last line by recalling that
\int\zeta(x,y)\,\gamma_{x}(dy)=x
, see (
1.8
).
The last equality is by the fact that
\hat{\zeta}_{n}\hat{\gamma}_{n}\in Q(\mu,\mu_{n})
, see (
2.2
). By a similar token we get,

\iiiint\big\langle\zeta(x,y),\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\lambda_{n}(dxd\xi dyd\eta)=0.

Finally, starting with Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then using (
6.4
), (
6.5
), we estimate,

\displaystyle{\left\lvert\iiiint\big\langle\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x,\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big\rangle\,d\lambda_{n}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\iiiint\big|\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big|^{2}\,d\lambda_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iiiint\big|\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big|^{2}\,d\lambda_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=2\left(\iint\frac{1}{2}\big|\hat{\zeta}_{n}(x,\xi)-x\big|^{2}\,d\hat{\gamma}_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint\frac{1}{2}\big|\check{\zeta}_{n}(y,\eta)-y\big|^{2}\,d\check{\gamma}_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\big(Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n}),

where at the end we used Young’s inequality. Upon plugging the above equalities and estimates into the chain of inequalities, every term in the bracket, apart from
Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n}),\,Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})
, cancels, which allows to conclude that,

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})\leq\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\left\{\iint c(\xi,\eta)\,\gamma_{n}(d\xi d\eta)+\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big[Z_{2}(\mu,\mu_{n})+Z_{2}(\nu,\nu_{n})\Big]\right\}.

To prove the convergence of the integral
\iint c\,d\gamma_{n}
we note that
the convergences
\mu_{n}\to\mu
,
\nu_{n}\to\nu
in the metric
Z_{2}
guarantee that the second moments
m_{2}(\mu_{n})
,
m_{2}(\nu_{n})
converge, in particular they are bounded. In turn, this means that the sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
has uniformly bounded second moments as well. Then, the convergence of
\iint c\,d\gamma_{n}\to\iint c\,d\gamma
follows by weak convergence of
\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup\gamma
and the
p
-growth of
c
with
p<2
, see
[
5
, Section 5.1.1]
. Finally, the assumed rate of decrease of
\varepsilon_{n}
in (
6.1
) furnishes,

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}F_{n}(\gamma_{n},q_{n})\leq\iint c(x,y)\,\gamma(dxdy).

Since
(\gamma,q)\in\overline{\Gamma Q}(\mu,\nu)
, the latter integral is
F(\gamma,q)
exactly, which concludes the proof.
∎

7
Examples

7.1
Non-uniqueness of the Zolotarev projection

The purpose of the first example is to show that the Zolotarev projection can be non-unique, i.e. that the non-empty set
\Pi_{\succeq_{c}\mu}(\nu)
may not be a singleton for some pair of probability distributions
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{b}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Recall that Proposition
1.2
states that this set is identical to the set of convex dominants of minimum second moment. We can thus concentrate on proposing data such that (
1.5
) has non-unique minimizers instead.

The following is the only example in this work with exact solution, not counting the 1D case in which the optimal convex dominance problem has a universal solution
\mu\vee\nu

7.2
Optimal convex dominance in two dimensions – simulations

As advertised in the introduction, the bi-martingale formulation
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
paves ways to efficient numerical methods. The simplest strategy is to use off-the-shelf software for non-linear convex optimization. In this subsection we shortly demonstrate the computational potential of
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
for the optimal convex dominance problem with the power cost, i.e. in the scenario
c(x,y,z)=f(z)={\left\lvert z\right\rvert}^{p}
for
p\in(1,+\infty)
. Assuming that
\mu,\nu
are discrete, we can rewrite
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
as a finite dimensional convex conic optimization problem.

For
\mu=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
,
\nu=\sum_{j}\nu_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the variables in
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT})
are simply

\gamma=\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{j})}
and
q=\sum_{i,j}\big(q_{ij}^{(1)},q_{ij}^{(2)}\big)\delta_{(x_{i},y_{j})}
, where
\gamma_{ij},q_{ij}^{(1)},q_{ij}^{(2)}
are numbers. Let us add the fourth variable
r_{ij}
and, for each
i,j
, consider the convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
satisfying,

(r_{ij})^{1/p}(\gamma_{ij})^{1-1/p}\geq\sqrt{\big(q_{ij}^{(1)}\big)^{2}+\big(q_{ij}^{(2)}\big)^{2}},\qquad r_{ij}\geq 0,\ \ \gamma_{ij}\geq 0.

Note that this constraint guarantees that
q\ll\gamma
. Naturally, the condition
(\gamma,q)\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
becomes a linear system of algebraic equations. The total cost satisfies the inequality

\iint|\frac{dq}{d\gamma}|^{p}d\gamma\leq\sum_{i,j}r_{ij}
, which becomes an equality at optimality. This conic form of the problem is ready to be tackled by standard convex optimization algorithms. For our simulations below, we have used the MOSEK solver
[
6
]
. After we have found the optimal solution
\hat{\gamma}_{ij},\hat{q}_{ij}^{(1)}\!,\,\hat{q}_{ij}^{(2)}
, the optimal convex dominant for the cost
f={\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{p}
can be computed as,

\hat{\rho}=\hat{\zeta}\#\hat{\gamma}=\sum_{i,j}\hat{\gamma}_{ij}\,\delta_{\left(\hat{q}_{ij}^{(1)}\!,\,\hat{q}_{ij}^{(2)}\right)/\hat{\gamma}_{ij}}.

It must be stressed that, if
\mu,\nu
are discrete and the finite dimensional conic problem is solved exactly, then
\hat{\rho}
above is an exact solution of the optimal convex dominance problem (
1.5
), see also Remark
4.5
(b). Hence, in the numerical simulations below, the solutions are as accurate as is the MOSEK’s interior point algorithm.

Example 7.4
.

On the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we will consider the
(\mathrm{MOT})
problem for the distance cost
c(x,y)={\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}
and the data
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}\,\delta_{x_{i}}
,
\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{1}{4}\,\delta_{y_{j}}
, where
x_{1}=(-\frac{1}{2},0)
,
x_{2}=(\frac{1}{2},0)
and
y_{1}=(-\frac{3}{2},0)
,
y_{2}=(-\frac{1}{2},0)
,
y_{3}=(\frac{1}{2},0)
,
y_{4}=(\frac{3}{2},0)
.
The problem is essentially one dimensional. The convex order
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
is easily verified, and the set of martingale plans
\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)
contains more than one element. The unique martingale plan
\gamma
solving
(\mathrm{MOT})
for the distance cost is the one that does not move the shared mass, i.e.
\gamma=\frac{1}{6}\delta_{(x_{1},y_{1})}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{(x_{1},y_{2})}+\frac{1}{12}\delta_{(x_{1},y_{4})}+\frac{1}{12}\delta_{(x_{2},y_{1})}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{(x_{2},y_{3})}+\frac{1}{6}\delta_{(x_{2},y_{4})}
, cf.
[
18
]
for details. The minimal martingale transport cost equals
\iint{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}\,\gamma(dxdy)=\frac{2}{3}
.

After
[
18
]
, we will now build a sequence
\nu_{n}
that weakly converges to
\nu
(also in metrics
W_{2}
or
Z_{2}
), cf. Fig.
4
. With
R_{\theta_{n}}e_{1}
being the anti-clockwise rotation of
e_{1}=(1,0)
by the angle

, we define
\nu_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{1}{4}\delta_{y_{j}^{n}}
, where,

y_{1}^{n}=x_{1}-R_{\theta_{n}}e_{1},\quad y_{2}^{n}=x_{2}-R_{\theta_{n}}e_{1},\quad y_{3}^{n}=x_{1}+R_{\theta_{n}}e_{1},\quad y_{4}^{n}=x_{2}+R_{\theta_{n}}e_{1},

see Fig.
4
(a). We see that
y_{j}^{n}\to y_{j}
, hence
\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\nu
. Moreover, the following is a martingale plan,

\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(\delta_{(x_{1},y_{1}^{n})}+\delta_{(x_{1},y_{3}^{n})}+\delta_{(x_{2},y_{2}^{n})}+\delta_{(x_{2},y_{4}^{n})}\Big)\ \in\ \Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu_{n}).

The core idea behind this example lies in the following observation
[
18
, Lemma 1.1]
: for any
n\geq 1
the set of martingale plans between
\mu
and
\nu^{\theta}
is a singleton, namely
\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu_{n})=\{\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\}
, rendering the relevant martingale optimal transport problem trivial. The left column of Fig.
4

\tilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)

\gamma

\mu,\nu
. In fact,

Let us now jump to the sequence of bi-martingale problems
(\mathrm{M^{2}OT_{n}})
for the sequence of data

,
\nu_{n}
