Hydrolysis-Driven Compositional Modification of Flavonoids in the Aerial Parts of Lespedeza cyrtobotrya Miq. and Its Impact on Monoamine Oxidase A Inhibitory Activity
Authors: Hyoung-Geun Kim, Su-Ah Lee, Ji-Hong Kim, Su-Yeon Lee, Jongmin Ahn, Sei-Ryang Oh, Dongho Lee, Sung-Kyun Ko, Hyung Won Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to investigate processing-induced compositional modification of flavonoids in Lespedeza cyrtobotrya and its impact on food-relevant bioactivity using a quantitative and validated analytical approach. Extracts, fractions, and isolated compounds from the aerial parts were systematically evaluated for monoamine oxidase inhibitory activity as a functional indicator. Nine flavonoid glycosides were isolated and structurally characterized, including two newly identified acetylated derivatives that exhibited moderate inhibitory effects. To assess the influence of processing, acid hydrolysis was applied to convert flavonoid glycosides to aglycone forms. Quantitative analysis revealed a pronounced shift in flavonoid composition after hydrolysis, with kaempferol increasing from undetectable levels to 907.1 mg/g of the extract. This compositional transformation was accompanied by a significant enhancement of MAO-A inhibitory activity, which showed a strong correlation to kaempferol enrichment. Overall, the results demonstrate that controlled hydrolysis effectively modulates flavonoid composition and functionality, highlighting L. cyrtobotrya as a promising raw material for food chemistry and functional ingredient applications.

Introduction

Neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease, are among the most prevalent and debilitating conditions worldwide, imposing substantial medical and socioeconomic burdens. Dysregulation of monoamine metabolism has been recognized as a central pathogenic factor in these disorders. Monoamine oxidases (MAOs), comprising two isoforms, MAO-A and MAO-B, catalyze the oxidative deamination of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine. Excessive MAO-A activity is closely linked to depressive states through the depletion of serotonin and norepinephrine, whereas increased MAO-B activity contributes to dopamine deficiency and oxidative stress, particularly in the aging brain, thereby playing a critical role in neurodegenerative processes. Consistent with these mechanistic insights, MAO-A has been primarily associated with the regulation of serotonin and norepinephrine metabolism and mood-related neurochemical balance, whereas MAO-B is more closely linked to dopamine oxidation and age-related oxidative processes. However, existing inhibitors are limited by safety concerns and insufficient selectivity, highlighting the continuing need for novel scaffolds with improved efficacy and safety profiles.

Natural products have attracted increasing interest as promising candidates for the development of isoform-selective MAO inhibitors. Compared with synthetic compounds, plant-derived chemical constituents offer structural diversity and generally favorable safety profiles, which may help to overcome the limitations of currently available inhibitors. Numerous studies have demonstrated that polyphenolic constituents, particularly flavonoids, possess the ability to modulate MAO activity, , highlighting natural products as valuable resources for novel bioactive scaffolds. Among these flavonoids, kaempferol and its O-substituted derivatives have been identified as notable MAO modulators. Sloley et al. reported that pure kaempferol exhibits significant inhibitory activity toward both MAO-A and MAO-B. In addition, Chaurasiya et al. and Gidaro et al. reported that these aglycones exhibit significant inhibitory activity toward MAO-A and show a structure-dependent influence on isoform selectivity. These findings emphasize that naturally occurring flavonol scaffolds can serve as promising leads for the development of MAO-targeting agents. Nevertheless, the direct applicability of polyphenolic flavonoids as central nervous system agents may be limited by their generally low blood-brain barrier permeability. Therefore, in the present study, MAO inhibitory activity was used as a functional indicator to evaluate processing-induced changes in bioactivity, rather than to directly claim CNS efficacy. Given its wide distribution in edible and medicinal plants, kaempferol may still be considered a relevant dietary flavonoid for evaluating food-related functional bioactivity.

Recently, to address cost-effectiveness issues in mass production and to propose natural materials from an industrial perspective, new plant-derived raw materials were identified through screening for MAO inhibitory activity. This approach was driven by the need to develop low-cost, applicable production methods, such as acid hydrolysis, to enable food-related functional applications.

Therefore, in this study, the potential of Lespedeza cyrtobotrya, a plant traditionally used for the treatment of lumbago, arthritis, headaches, joint pain, inflammation, and skin whitening effects, was investigated as a functional raw material, with particular emphasis on processing-induced compositional modification of flavonoids and its impact on monoamine oxidase inhibitory activity. Rather than emphasizing the intrinsic activity of individual compounds, this study was designed to demonstrate how hydrolysis-driven conversion of flavonoid glycosides into aglycones can modulate the overall bioactivity in a food-relevant context.

In this study, a 70% aqueous ethanol extract of the aerial parts of L. cyrtobotrya and its fractions were isolated and characterized. Structural analysis was performed based on their inhibitory activities against MAO-A and MAO-B, together with Ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-QTOF/MS) profiling. The inhibitory activities of the isolated compounds were systematically evaluated against both MAO isoforms together with their corresponding aglycone standards. Furthermore, on the basis of previous evidence that the aglycone forms of these compounds exhibited stronger inhibitory activity than their glycosylated counterparts, hydrolysis was performed to increase the aglycone content of the extract, and the resulting changes in overall MAO-A inhibitory activity and isoform selectivity were investigated. To ensure analytical reliability, quantitative analysis of key compounds was conducted using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography photodiode array (UPLC-PDA). Collectively, this study provides new insights into the MAO-A inhibitory potential of L. cyrtobotrya and its kaempferol derivatives, highlights this species as a promising source for functional material development, and offers essential baseline information for its standardization.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

The aerial parts of L. cyrtobotrya Miq. were collected from Mount Cheonjusan, Changwon, Gyeongsangnam-do. The collected plant material was identified, and voucher specimens (Dr. Jin-Hyub Paik: NCB007-093) were deposited at the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology.

Reagents and Instruments

Methanol (MeOH) of high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analytical grade (Honeywell, Charlotte, NC) and acetonitrile (ACN) of HPLC analytical grade (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) were purchased. Water (H2O) was purified by a water purification system (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid (FA) and leucine enkephalin of liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analytical grade were used for instrumental analysis (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis). Pyridine-d 5 and Methanol-d 4 NMR solvents were purchased from Merck Millipore (Darmstadt, Germany). Tentative identification of extracts and compounds was conducted on an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) (ACQUITY UPLC, Waters, Milford, MA) column equipped with a PDA and a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Vion IMS-QTOF system, Waters) to obtain HRESIMS spectroscopic data. Fractionation was performed on a Multiple Preparative HPLC (MPLC, k-prep LAB 100S, YMC, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a DAD-50-700S column (700 × 50.0 mm, 10 μm, YMC) packed with YMC ODS-AQ HG resin. Isolation and purification of compounds were conducted on MPLC LC-Forte/R2 series (YMC, Kyoto, Japan) and Armen SPOT Prep 2-250 (Gilson, Middleton, WI) equipped with a X-bridge column (250 × 20.0 mm, 5.0 μm, Waters, Milford, MA) and Atlantis T3 column (250 × 20.0 mm, 5.0 μm, Waters). A preparative HPLC was used: PLC 2020 (Gilson) and LC Prep 150 System (Waters) with a Pack ODS-AQ column (250 × 21.2 mm, 5.0 μm, YMC) and Kinetex Biphenyl column (250 × 20.0 mm, 5.0 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) (Table ). NMR spectra were recorded on AVANCE II 600 MHz NMR Spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Quantification and method validation were performed on UPLC (ACQUITY UPLC, Waters) and UHPLC-TQ/MS 1290 Infinity II Multisampler (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) using a ACQUITY BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) (Waters).

UPLC-QTOF/MS Analysis

Mass spectrometric analysis of extracts using 70% aqueous ethanol extract of L. cyrtobotrya was carried out by UPLC-QTOF/MS with an electrospray ionization device operating in negative ion mode, using the following operation parameters, based on the conditions of several references: , Negative ion mode capillary voltage, 2300 V; cone voltage, 50 V; ion source temperature, 110 °C; desolvation temperature, 350 °C; flow rate of desolvation gas (N2), 800 L/h: mass scan range, 100–1500 Da; scan time, 0.25 s. The chromatographic analysis was performed on ACQUITY BEH C18 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters) column. The temperature of the column oven was kept at 35 °C. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min using distilled water with 0.1%/FA as a mobile phase A and acetonitrile with 0.1% FA as a mobile phase B. The gradient method was: 0.00–1.00 min 5% B; 1.00–20.00 min 100% B; 20.00–22.30 min 100% B; 22.30–22.40 min 5% B. The concentration of compounds was 0.1 mg/mL. The injection volume was 2 μL.

Extraction and Isolation

The aerial parts of L. cyrtobotrya (14.3 kg) were extracted during the day using 70% aqueous ethanol, repeated twice, and the extracts were fractionated on a Diaion HP-20 column using aqueous CH3OH as the eluent (20, 40, 60, 80, 100% CH3OH) step gradient protocol to generate eight fractions (F1–F8). The subsequent repeated preparative HPLC using appropriate packing materials yielded nine flavonoid glycosides, compounds C1–C9, including two new compounds (C6 and C8). Inhibition Activities against Monoamine Oxidase A and B

MAO activity was measured using the MAO-Glo Assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, Cat.# V1402) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 12.5 μL of MAO substrate and 12.5 μL of test compound were added to each well of a 96-well plate. The reaction was initiated by adding 25 μL of MAO-A or MAO-B enzyme solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Inc., St. Louis, MO, Cat.# M7316 and M7441, respectively) and incubating the plate at room temperature for 3 h. Subsequently, 50 μL of reconstituted Luciferin Detection Reagent was added, followed by incubation for 20 min to stabilize the luminescent signal. Luminescence was measured using a Victor X2 multilabel microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA).

Hydrolysis of Extracts from the Aerial Parts of Lespedeza Species

Acid hydrolysis of extracts from the aerial parts of Lespedeza species was performed according to methods reported in the literature with slight modification. Briefly, each crude extract (200 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of 2 M sulfuric acid containing 2 mL of ethanol and was heated at 90 °C for 2 and 4 h to induce hydrolysis. After cooling to room temperature, 50 mL of distilled water was added, and the solution was neutralized to pH 7 by the gradual addition of 2 M sodium hydroxide (20–27 mL). The neutralized mixture was partitioned three times with n-butanol (2:1, 2:1, 1:1 v/v). The combined organic layers were evaporated under reduced pressure to obtain the hydrolyzed fractions. The original extract, kaempferol standard (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, CAS: 520-18-3), and hydrolyzed samples (2000 mg/kg each) were analyzed by UPLC-PDA to evaluate the changes in aglycone content before and after hydrolysis. The acid hydrolysis experiment was independently performed in triplicate, and each hydrolyzed extract was analyzed in triplicate by UPLC-PDA under the selected analytical conditions.

Enzymatic hydrolysis using α-rhamnosidase and alkaline hydrolysis were performed according to the method presented in the literature. For enzymatic hydrolysis, α-rhamnosidase was used at pH 6.5 with an enzyme concentration of 6.5 U/mL, and the reaction was carried out for 2 h. For alkaline hydrolysis, 2% NaOH was used, and the reaction was conducted at 110 °C for 1 h (Figure S15).

Quantitative Analysis Using UPLC-PDA

Quantitative analysis was performed on UPLC-PDA, using the following operation parameters, based on the conditions of several references: [ACQUITY UPLC, corporation (Waters)/ equipped with ACQUITY BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters)/purified water +0.1% FA as a mobile phase A and ACN + 0.1% FA as a mobile phase B/linear gradient: B; 0.0–1.0 min, 5% B; 1.0–20.0 min, 100% B; 20.0–23.5 min, 100% B; 23.5–23.6 min, 5% B; 23.6–25.0 min, 5% B/flow rate: 0.4 mL/min]. The injection volume was 2 μL, and the temperature of the column oven was kept at 35 °C. The method for quantitation analysis was validated to determine the linearity (R 2 > 0.999), and the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ) were evaluated. All curves showed good linearity (R 2 ≥ 0.9934–0.9999) within the test ranges, and the overall LODs and LOQs were in the range of 0.0009–0.0999 μg/mL and 0.0028–0.3028 μg/mL, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Inhibitory Activities of Extract and Fractions from the Aerial Parts of L. cyrtobotrya against Monoamine Oxidase A and B

To identify the bioactive fractions responsible for the monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitory effects of L. cyrtobotrya, the 70% aqueous ethanol extract of the aerial parts was fractionated on a Diaion HP-20 column using a stepwise gradient of aqueous ethanol (20–100%) to yield eight fractions (F1–F8). Each fraction, along with the crude extract, was evaluated for its inhibitory activity against MAO-A and MAO-B (Figure A). In MAO-A assays, fractions F4 through F8 exhibited clear inhibitory effects. The inhibitory effect was particularly pronounced toward MAO-A, with F4 exhibiting the strongest activity, whereas F1–F3 showed no detectable inhibition against either enzyme. These results suggest that MAO-A inhibitory activity was concentrated in the mid-to-nonpolar ethanolic fractions (F4–F8), whereas the highly polar fractions (F1–F3) contributed little to no inhibition. In contrast, fractions F4, F5, and F8 exhibited only minimal inhibition toward MAO-B. Notably, the crude extract displayed stronger inhibition of MAO-B than its subfractions, suggesting that MAO-B inhibitory constituents were present in the extract but were diluted or distributed across multiple fractions during the fractionation process. This tendency is consistent with previous reports that flavonoid- or phenolic-rich fractions of leguminous plants exhibit notable MAO inhibitory properties. , Because these fractions represent chemically complex mixtures, their inhibitory profiles should be interpreted as the combined effects of multiple constituents rather than the activity of a single dominant compound. Based on these findings, the bioactive fractions (F4–F8) were selected for further metabolite profiling by UPLC-QTOF/MS.

Tentative Identification of Chemical Constituents in Extract and Fractions from the Aerial Parts of L. cyrtobotrya Using UPLC-QTOF/MS

The 70% aqueous ethanol extract and fractions LC-4 ∼ 8 were analyzed using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-QTOF/MS) to characterize their major compounds. Most of the dominant peaks appeared in the retention time (RT) range of 3–14 min (Figure B). Chemical profiling was carried out to identify compounds detected in the part of the RT range of 3–14 min from L. cyrtobotrya extract. As a result, 15 compounds were detected in the chromatogram, and these constituents were tentatively identified through MS profiling analysis (Table ). Listed as follows: catechin (detected for C15H14O5, 289.0744, t R = 3.07 min), schaftoside (calcd for C26H28O14, 563.1409, t R = 3.95 min), isoschaftoside (calcd for C26H28O14, 563.1409, t R = 4.13 min), quercetin-3-O-sambubioside (calcd for C26H28O16, 595.1306, t R = 4.27 min), veronicastroside (calcd for C27H30O15, 593.1509, t R = 4.43 min), kaempferol 3-vicianoside (calcd for C26H28O15, 579.1355, t R = 4.70 min), kaempferitrin (calcd for C27H30O14, 577.0445, t R = 4.79 min), astragalin (calcd for C21H20O11, 447.0948, t R = 5.21 min), kaempferol 3-(6’’-acetylglucoside)-7-rhamnoside (calcd for C29H32O16, 635.1605, t R = 5.45 min), sutchuenoside A (calcd for C29H32O15, 619.1678, t R = 5.64 min), kaempferol dirhamnoside derivative 1 (unknown) (calcd for C29H32O15, 619.1646, t R = 5.72 min), kaempferin (calcd for C21H20O10, 431.0993, t R = 5.74 min), kaempferol dirhamnoside derivative 2 (unknown) (calcd for C29H32O15, 619.1677, t R = 5.77 min), 3″-acetyl-kaempferitrin (calcd for C29H32O15, 619.1647, t R = 5.93 min), and nonioside C (calcd for C20H36O12, 467.2143, t R = 6.18 min), Most structures were tentatively assigned as flavonoid glycosides. Previous phytochemical studies of Lespedeza species have mainly reported isoflavones, chalcones, and isoflavanones as predominant constituents, whereas reports on flavonol glycosides have been relatively limited. , Therefore, these flavonol glycosides newly identified in L. cyrtobotrya were considered major bioactive candidates for further isolation and biological evaluation.

Furthermore, comparison of the chromatographic profiles obtained from UPLC-PDA (254 nm) analysis (Figure S1) with the MAO inhibitory activities of the fractions revealed that fractions LC-4, LC-6, and LC-7 contained the highest intensities of the flavonol glycoside group. Specifically, peaks 2–5 in LC-4 (46.8% of total peak intensity), peaks 6–12 and 14 in LC-6 (80.2%), and peaks 6–14 in LC-7 (76.53%) accounted for the majority of the detected flavonoid glycosides (Table S1). Although LC-6 and LC-7 did not exhibit markedly stronger MAO-A inhibition than LC-4, LC-5, or LC-8, they showed a distinctly richer accumulation of kaempferol-based flavonol glycosides. Given that these compounds were identified as major compounds of L. cyrtobotrya and were considered putative contributors to the overall MAO inhibitory activity of the active fractions, LC-4, LC-6, and LC-7 were selected as priority fractions for compound isolation and structural elucidation.

Isolation and Structural Characterization of Major Compounds in Bioactive Fractions from the Aerial Parts of L. cyrtobotrya

The aerial parts of L. cyrtobotrya were extracted in 70% aqueous ethanol, and the concentrated extracts were successively partitioned into nine fractions using a Diaion HP-20 column eluted by aqueous ethanol (20, 40, 60, 80, 100%) step gradient protocol. The fractions 4–8 were used for isolation of bioactive metabolites. Repeated preparative HPLC of the fractions yielded nine flavonol glycosides, including two new ones (C6 and C8). Their molecular weight and molecular formula were determined by high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HRESIMS) data in negative ion mode obtained from a Vion IMS-Qtof-MS (Waters). Comparison of NMR and MS data with reported values led to identification of known compounds schaftoside (C1), isoschaftoside (C2), quercetin-3-O-sambuvioside (C3), kaempferitrin (C4), sutchuenoside A (C5), kaempferin (C7), and 3′’-acetyl-kaempferitrin (C9) (Figures S2–S8). The structures of two novel compounds (C6 and C8), previously detected by MS analysis but tentatively unidentified, were elucidated through detailed NMR experiments, including 1H, 13C, HSQC, HMBC, and COSY. The carbon signals corresponding to the flavonol moiety were included one conjugated ketone [δC 179.6 (C-4)], six oxygenated olefinic quaternaries [δC 163.6 (C-2), δC 163.0 (C-7), δC 161.9 (C-4′), δC 159.8 (C-5), δC 158.1 (C-9), δC 135.9 (C-3)], two olefinic quaternaries [δC 122.2 (C-1′), δC 107.5 (C-10)], and six olefinic methines [δC 131.9 (2C, C-2′ and C-6′), δC 116.7 (2C, C-3′ and C-5′), δC 100.4 (C-6), δC 95.6 (C-8)]. In the case of the deoxyhexose moiety, it contains two hemiacetals [δc 103.0 (C-1‴), δC 99.9 (C-1″)], eight oxygenated methines [δC 75.3 (C-4‴), δC 73.6 (C-4″), δC 73.4 (C-3″), δC 73.4 (C-3‴), δC 72.2 (C-2‴), δC 72.1 (C-2″), δC 71.3 (C-5″), δC 70.5 (C-5‴)], and two methyls [δC 18.1 (C-6″), δC 17.7 (C-6‴)]. Based on the chemical shifts of the two deoxyhexose units, these sugar moieties were assigned as rhamnopyranosyl units. The L-configuration was further supported by comparison of the hydrolyzed sugar with an authentic standard using UPLC analysis (Figure S16). The anomeric proton signals appeared as broad singlets with very small or unresolved H-1/H-2 coupling, which is consistent with an α-rhamnopyranosyl configuration. In contrast, β-anomers typically exhibit larger coupling constants due to axial–equatorial relationships. In addition, the 1H and 13C NMR chemical shifts of the rhamnose moieties were in good agreement with those of previously reported α-L-rhamnopyranosyl flavonoids, including kaempferitrin. Accordingly, the sugar moieties were assigned as α-L-rhamnopyranosyl units. In addition, the presence of one acetyl group was observed as one ester δC 171.9 (C-1⁗) and one methyl δC 20.8 (C-2⁗). In the HMBC spectrum, two anomer proton signals δC 5.46 (H-1‴) and δC 5.44 (H-1″) showed cross-peaks with δC 163.0 (C-7) and δC 135.9 (C-3), respectively, suggesting that two α-rhamnopyranoses were attached to C-7 and C-3. Also, one oxygenated methine proton signal δC 5.01 (H-4‴) showed a cross-peak with one ester δC 171.9 (C-1⁗), suggesting that the acetyl group was attached to C-4‴ of 7-O-α-rhamnopyranose. By comparing with the data of compound C4 and the literature, the chemical structure of this compound was determined as a new compound, 4‴-acetyl-kaempferitrin (Figures S9–S11).

Compound C8 was obtained as a brown amorphous powder, and its molecular formula was predicted to be C29H32O15 from the HR-ESI-MS spectrum m/z 619.1667 [M – H]−. It was confirmed to have a similar structure to compound C6 through 1H NMR (600 MHz, Methanol-d4) and 13C NMR (150 MHz, Methanol-d4) spectra, excluding binding sites of two α- rhamnopyranose and acetyl group. In the HMBC spectrum, two anomer proton signals δH 5.56 (H-1‴) and δH 5.47 (H-1″) showed cross-peaks with δC 136.2 (C-3) and δC 71.9 (C-2″), respectively, suggesting that two α-rhamnopyranoses were attached to C-3 and C-2′′. Also, one oxygenated methine proton signal δH 5.00 (H-3‴) showed a cross-peak with one ester δC 172.7 (C-1⁗), suggesting that the acetyl group was attached to C-3‴ of 2″-O-α-rhamnopyranose. By comparing with the data of compound C4 and the literature, the structure of this compound was determined as a new compound, kaempferol 3-O-α-L-(3‴-acetyl)-rhamnopyranosyl-(1→2)-O-α-l-rhamnopyranoside (Figures S12–S14).

Inhibitory Activities of Isolated Compounds from the Aerial Parts of L. cyrtobotrya against Monoamine Oxidase A and B

The monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitory activities of the ethanolic extract and nine flavonol glycosides (C1–C9) isolated from L. cyrtobotrya were evaluated against MAO-A and MAO-B (Table and Figure ). Overall, the isolated compounds exhibited weak to moderate inhibition of MAO-A, with IC50 values ranging from 51.10 to 98.48 μM. In contrast, the inhibition of MAO-B was consistently stronger than that of MAO-A. Most compounds showed moderate inhibition of MAO-B, with IC50 values between 25.46 and 49.57 μM. Among them, sutchuenoside A (C5) displayed the greatest potency (IC50 = 25.46 μM), followed by quercetin-3-O-sambubioside (C3), kaempferitrin (C4), and their acetylated derivatives (C6 and C9). This trend is likely due to the larger and more hydrophobic substrate cavity of MAO-B. Structural features further contributed to the observed activity differences. Acetylated derivatives (C6 and C9) showed slightly improved MAO-B inhibition compared with their nonacetylated counterparts, likely due to increased hydrophobicity enhancing interactions within the MAO-B active site. Collectively, these findings indicate that although the isolated flavonol glycosides from L. cyrtobotrya exhibit dual inhibitory activity toward MAO-A and MAO-B, their overall potency remains moderate, with a slight preference toward MAO-B. This apparent difference between the fraction-level and compound-level activities may be explained by the chemical complexity of the active fractions. Fractions LC-4 ∼ LC-8 were complex mixtures containing multiple coeluting constituents; therefore, their overall MAO inhibitory profiles may have been influenced not only by the major isolated flavonol glycosides but also by the relative abundance of active constituents, minor components, matrix effects, and possible additive or antagonistic interactions among constituents. In contrast, C1–C9 were evaluated as purified compounds and therefore may not fully represent the combined activity of their corresponding fractions. Thus, the stronger MAO-A inhibition observed in LC-4 ∼ LC-8 and the relatively higher MAO-B inhibition of the isolated compounds should not be interpreted as contradictory, but rather as reflecting the difference between mixture-based and single-compound bioassays. In addition, the relatively higher MAO-B inhibition observed for several isolated flavonol glycosides may be partly associated with the bipartite and more hydrophobic active-site cavity of MAO-B, which can accommodate hydrophobic flavonoid scaffolds and substituents, as suggested by previous structural and docking studies. , Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that kaempferol, the aglycone backbone of the major flavonol glycosides (C4–C9), acts as a potent and selective inhibitor of human MAO-A. In addition, kaempferol has been reported to exert strong MAOs inhibition and neuroprotective effects. These observations support the interpretation that glycosylation significantly reduces MAO inhibitory potency and reinforce the hypothesis that increasing the aglycone (kaempferol) content through hydrolysis could markedly enhance MAO inhibition and modify isoform selectivity.

Quantitative Analysis of Major Flavonoids in Isolated Compounds from the Aerial Parts of L. cyrtobotrya Using UPLC-PDA

Among the nine isolated compounds, MS-based relative contents and MAO inhibitory activities were compared to select the major compounds. Although C1 and C9 were identified as major components based on their relative MS peak intensities (C9 = 19.42%, C1 = 10.39%), they were excluded due to their low inhibitory activity against MAOs. Compounds C4–C6 (MS peak intensities: C4 = 44.50%, C5 = 5.23%, C6 = 4.59%; data not shown) were selected as the final lead compounds because they showed both relatively high contents and confirmed biological efficacy. These compounds were therefore subjected to quantitative analysis. Ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) analysis was conducted to determine the content of compounds C4–C6 in the 70% aqueous ethanol extract from L. cyrtobotrya. Gradient elution using H2O (+0.1% formic acid) and acetonitrile (ACN) was performed using ACQUITY BEH C18 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). Peaks were detected at 280 nm. Peaks appeared at 4.79, 5.64, and 5.72 min, and were identified as those of kaempferitrin (C4), sutchuenoside A (C5), and 4‴-acetyl-kaempferitrin (C6), respectively. Each analyte was identified by matching its retention time and UV absorption spectrum with the corresponding reference standard prior to quantification. All calibration curves exhibited good linearity (R 2 > 0.999) within the six concentration ranges by diluting the components, and the overall LOD and LOQ were calculated to be 0.8334–4.9269 μg/mL and 2.5256–14.9301 μg/mL, respectively (Table ). Extrapolation from the calibration curve determined the contents of compounds C4–C6 in the extract from L. cyrtobotrya to be 23.6 mg/g (C4), 5.5 mg/g (C5), and 2.3 mg/g (C6), respectively (Table ). Among these, kaempferitrin (C4) was identified as the predominant constituent, showing the highest concentration (23.6 mg/g) among the quantified compounds.

Acid Hydrolysis and MAO Inhibitory Activity of Aerial Part Extracts from Lespedeza Species

To verify the hypothesis that hydrolysis could enhance MAO inhibitory activity by increasing the kaempferol content, the ethanolic extracts of four Lespedeza species were subjected to multiple hydrolytic approaches. Enzymatic hydrolysis using α-rhamnosidase cleaved only the terminal 7-O-rhamnose, yielding kaempferin but not kaempferol, likely due to the enzyme’s specificity toward terminal rhamnose residues and limited accessibility to inner glycosidic linkages. Alkaline hydrolysis was similarly ineffective; the dirhamnoside linkage was largely preserved, and in some cases only partial cleavage of the 3-O-rhamnose occurred, resulting in no detectable formation of kaempferol, as flavonoid glycosides are generally stable under basic conditions. In contrast, acid hydrolysis effectively converted the major kaempferol dirhamnosides into their aglycone form, kaempferol (Figure S15). The hydrolysis conditions were selected based on previously reported methods with slight modifications. To establish optimal hydrolytic conditions, different concentrations of sulfuric acid (1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 M) were examined, confirming that stronger acidic environments (2.0 M H2SO4) improved the conversion efficiency (Figure S15). Based on these findings, the ethanolic extracts of four Lespedeza species (200 mg each) were treated with 2 M sulfuric acid containing 2 mL of ethanol and heated at 90 °C for 4 h. After neutralization with 2 M sodium hydroxide and partitioning with n-butanol, the hydrolyzed fractions were analyzed using UPLC-PDA to quantify kaempferol contents.

As shown in Figure and Table , kaempferol (t R = 7.6 min) was not detected (below the detection limit) in any of the original (nontreated) extracts but appeared distinctly after hydrolysis, confirming the cleavage of glycosidic bonds in flavonol glycosides. The kaempferol peak intensity increased in a time-dependent manner, with the 4 h treatment showing the highest yield. Quantitative analysis revealed that the kaempferol content reached 907.1 mg/g in L. cyrtobotrya after hydrolysis, followed by L. maximowiczii at 16.0 mg/g. L. thunbergii and L. tomentosa were not detected (below the detection limit). Interestingly, L. cyrtobotrya exhibited the highest kaempferol yield among the tested species, demonstrating that acid hydrolysis can effectively induce kaempferol enrichment in its extract. This finding suggests that L. cyrtobotrya has strong potential as a bioactive material with enhanced MAO inhibitory activity through hydrolysis-driven aglycone conversion.

To determine whether this compositional change translated into improved enzyme inhibition, the MAO-A and MAO-B inhibitory activities of hydrolyzed (AH1–4) and untreated (raw materials) extracts were compared at concentrations of 10 and 20 μg/mL (Table ). As shown in Table , all hydrolyzed samples exhibited stronger inhibition than their corresponding controls, with a more pronounced enhancement observed for MAO-A. For L. cyrtobotrya (1H), MAO-A inhibition increased from 6.73 to 51.69% at 10 μg/mL and from 17.09 to 60.15% at 20 μg/mL, approaching the level of the kaempferol standard (83.06%). MAO-B inhibition showed comparable levels before and after hydrolysis (from 22.22 to 21.49% at 10 μg/mL and from 44.63 to 31.88% at 20 μg/mL), indicating that hydrolysis had little effect on MAO-B inhibition (kaempferol: 67.76% inhibition) (Table ). Other Lespedeza species exhibited little or no change in MAO-A or MAO-B inhibition after hydrolysis, reflecting their minimal kaempferol formation and low overall bioactivity. These results indicate that acid hydrolysis significantly enhances the MAO inhibitory capacity of L. cyrtobotrya, primarily through the conversion of glycosylated flavonols into the aglycone kaempferol. The greater increase in MAO-A inhibition compared with MAO-B is consistent with previous findings that kaempferol acts as a potent and selective MAO-A inhibitor. , Although the precise inhibition mechanism (e.g., competitive or noncompetitive) was not investigated in this study, the enhanced MAO-A inhibitory activity observed after hydrolysis can be attributed to processing-induced compositional modification, particularly the increased formation of kaempferol from flavonoid glycosides, which is consistent with its previously reported MAO-A inhibitory properties. This hydrolysis-driven compositional shift directly translates into improved MAO-A inhibitory efficacy of L. cyrtobotrya extracts, indicating that aglycone enrichment contributes to the modulation of monoamine oxidase activity. These results highlight L. cyrtobotrya as a promising candidate for the development of natural MAO-A-targeted functional agents and further demonstrate that compositional modification, rather than the intrinsic activity of individual compounds, can serve as an effective strategy to enhance functional bioactivity in plant-derived materials.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that L. cyrtobotrya is a promising plant-derived raw material whose functional potential can be substantially enhanced through processing-induced compositional modification. Systematic screening revealed that flavonoid glycosides isolated from the aerial parts exhibited moderate monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitory activity, whereas acid hydrolysis markedly increased the overall activity of the extract. This enhancement was closely associated with the conversion of glycosylated flavonoids to their aglycone form, particularly kaempferol, as supported by quantitative analysis. The strong correlation between kaempferol enrichment and increased MAO-A inhibition highlights hydrolysis as a key mechanism for modulating bioactivity through a chemical transformation. From a food chemistry perspective, these findings underscore the effectiveness of simple and broadly applicable processing approaches, such as acid hydrolysis, for enriching bioactive constituents in plant materials. Overall, L. cyrtobotrya represents a valuable botanical resource for the development of food-related functional ingredients and nutraceutical applications based on the processing-assisted bioconversion of flavonoids.
