Risk prediction models for depression in older adults with cancer
Authors: Martino Belvederi Murri, Guido Sciavicco, Michele Specchia, Marco Marozzi, Angela Muscettola, Barbara Zaccagnino, Goce Kalcev, Michela Atzeni, Clelia Madeddu, Federica Sancassiani, Mauro Giovanni Carta, Maria Giulia Nanni, Rosangela Caruso, Luigi Grassi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression in people with cancer is common and debilitating, but few instruments exist for early identification. We aimed at developing streamlined Risk Prediction Models (Arturo RPMs) for identifying individuals at higher risk for depression.MethodsPredictors of depression were identified from a review of available literature. Then, we used data from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) prospective study. SHARE recruited community residing adults aged 55 years or older, from which we selected those who reported having received a diagnosis of cancer. The outcome was the presence of depression (EURO-D score ≥ 4) in the Classification Approach (CA), and the severity of depression at a two-year follow up evaluation (EURO-D sum-score) in the Regression Approach (RA). Multiple RPMs were developed using combinations of sample balancing techniques (no balancing, under- or oversampling), learning methods (Generalized Linear Models (GLM), Decision Trees (DT), Random Forest (RF)) and variable selection methods (none, Backward (BW) and Forward (FW) sequential, Genetic Algorithm (GA)).ResultsWe identified 90 predictors of depression that were measured in the SHARE dataset and, combining waves 4 to 8, selected a sample of 4057 participants with cancer. Of these, 33.5% were depressed at 2 years follow-up. In the classification approach the RPM based on undersampling, GLM and GA variable selection used 34 predictors and reached satisfying accuracy (74.4%, AUC-ROC: 0.80; PPV: 84.7%; NPV: 60.1%). In the regression approach, the GLM model with Genetic Algorithm reached the best accuracy (75.1%, AUC-ROC: 0.81; PPV: 70.5%; NPV: 76.2%). The calibration curve suggested a satisfactory level of prediction, homogeneous at all levels of risk thresholds. Using a threshold of 50% risk, the model yields a PPV of 80% and an NPV of 75%.ConclusionsThe Arturo RPMs can identify older adults with cancer who are at higher risk of developing depression over the following two years. The model is freely available as a web-based calculator for use by individuals, clinicians, and policy makers. Introduction

Depression is prevalent and debilitating among cancer patients, significantly impacting their quality of life, treatment adherence, and overall survival. Beyond its psychological impact, depression in cancer patients is associated with immune dysfunction, faster disease progression, and premature mortality [1]. Identifying individuals at high risk to develop depression could enable earlier interventions and improve patient outcomes [2, 3]. Early risk stratification may also facilitate personalized prevention strategies [4] and support more efficient allocation of healthcare resources.

A Risk Prediction Model (RPM) uses multiple predictors to estimate an individual’s probability of developing a specific outcome, such as depression, within a defined time frame [5]. Despite advances in statistical modelling, predictive tools for depression in cancer patients remain scarce. A nomogram developed in elderly breast cancer patients, based on income, pain, family support, and physical activity [6] demonstrated good predictive performance (AUC: 0.824) but was cross-sectional and relied on data from two tertiary hospitals, possibly limiting its generalizability. Another model predicted anxiety after early-stage breast cancer treatment, and employed advanced statistical methods to develop a parsimonious model [7]. Baseline anxiety and depression scores, caring responsibilities, age, and homeownership explained 60% of the variance of anxiety outcomes. A further study examined the long-term onset of depression among prostate cancer survivors, identifying ethnicity, unemployment, income, comorbidities, treatment decisional regret, and nonadherence to exercise recommendations [4]. Notably, only few selected variables were primarily cancer-related, suggesting that general psychosocial and demographic factors may play a significant role. Together, these findings support the feasibility of risk models for depression in cancer populations, leveraging both clinical and psychosocial information for prediction across different cancer types.

Recently, we developed Manto, a Risk Prediction Model to identify depression among older adults in the general population [2], later translated into a freely available web-based calculator (manto.unife.it). Building on this approach, this study aims to develop RPMs specific to individuals with cancer, with the following objectives: (1) to develop and compare multiple RPMs using advanced machine learning methods; (2) to identify a smaller subset of predictors while retaining predictive power; and (3) to implement the model as a publicly accessible risk calculator.

Methods

We followed the TRIPOD statement for transparent reporting of prediction models, specifically the TRIPOD-AI extension for Artificial Intelligence methods [8].

Data, participants and data Preparation

The RPMs were developed and cross-validated using data from SHARE, a comprehensive multi-centre cohort study on aging in Europe [9]. SHARE surveys community-dwelling Europeans aged 50 years or older on a wide range of health, psychosocial and demographic factors. For this study, eligibility criteria were age older than 55 years, a history of cancer, availability of data on predictors at baseline, and depression data at follow-up. The presence of cancer was ascertained using relative pertinent items (supplement S4.1). We used data from wave 5–8 (2013–2019), excluding wave 4 and 9 due to missing predictors. Each participant contributed to a baseline block (BL) of predictors and a follow up (FU) outcome data, i.e. depression EURO-D score two years later (e.g. waves 5–6, 6–7 or 7–8). If multiple blocks were available, we retained the earliest, closest to cancer diagnosis. The supplement reports the flow of participants, highlighting those who were excluded due to death, loss to follow-up or missing data in four or more euro-d variables (Par S4.2).

The SHARE project has received ethical approval from the Ethics Council of the Max Planck Society (latest approval 8 June 2021, covering Waves 4–9), and previously from the Ethics Committee of the University of Mannheim (decisions 13 December 2004, 16 December 2008, 28 June 2010). Ethical reviews were also obtained in each participating country. All participants provided written informed consent. Our analyses used only anonymized, publicly available SHARE data. All procedures contributing to this work comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, revised in 2008.

Outcomes

Depression was assessed with the EURO-D scale [10] which rates the presence or absence of 12 depressive symptoms (depressed mood, pessimism, death wishes, guilt, sleep problems, loss of interest, irritability, loss of appetite, fatigue, concentration difficulties, lack of enjoyment, and tearfulness). The EURO-D has established psychometric validity [10, 11], with total scores ranging from 0 to 12. For the classification approach, a EURO-D score of 4 or higher indicated the presence of major depression, consistent with validation against interview-based diagnoses [10]. For the regression approach, the raw EURO-D score was used.

Predictors

We conducted a literature review to identify predictors of depression in cancer, starting from our previous review in the general population [2]. Cancer-specific predictors were identified from secondary literature, including systematic and narrative reviews of prospective and cross-sectional studies (in preparation). Predictor were included in the RPM if they (i) were associated with depression in the general or cancer population; (ii) could be assessed by questionnaire without instrumental or laboratory measures, and (iii) were available in the SHARE items, following a similar strategy used in the Manto RPM [2].

Sample size and missing data

We sought to maximize sample size within SHARE by including all eligible waves. Although cancer cases were a minority of the cohort, their depression rates were high. Based on established criteria, a sample of 1,000 events with 50 predictors meets the recommended threshold of minimum 20 Events Per Variable (EPV) [12]. Missing predictors values were coded as a separate “missing data” category. At follow-up, participants were classified as depressed if they endorsed ≥ 4 EURO-D items with ≤ 3 missing responses, non-depressed if they endorsed ≤ 4 EURO-D items with ≤ 3 missing responses, missing, otherwise. Participants without a valid depression status at follow-up were excluded.

Data analysis

Each predictor was first analysed descriptively. We compared distributions across three groups- participants deceased before follow-up, lost to follow-up or alive at follow-up- using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality. Among included participants, we assessed the variance of each predictor, as a crude indicator of predictive potential. No predictors were excluded at this stage.

Model construction and assessment, fairness

We approached the task in two ways: (i) as a binary classification problem (classification approach) using a EURO-D cut-off ≥ 4, and (ii) as regression problem, predicting the continuous EURO-D score (regression approach).

Three resampling methods, as recommended (Zhang et al., 2023): original, non-resampled dataset (NOBL), Balanced Undersampling dataset (BLU, capped at the cardinality of the less common class) and Balanced Oversampling (BLO, with artificial data generation). All approaches were evaluated with 10-fold cross-validation; in undersampling, folds were generated 10 times to yield 100-fold cross-validation.

The combination of these methods produced 36 models, each defined by a learning method (DT, RF, GLM), a resampling method (NOBL, BLU, BLO), and a feature selection method (ALL, FW, BW GA). We named the models accordingly (e.g., DT-BLU-FW-C is decision trees, with balanced undersampling and forward selection, classification approach).

Model performance was compared using accuracy, sensitivity/specificity ratio (optimal value close to 1), calibration performance and the number of predictors retained. The best-performing model was then used to extract cutoff rules. The most effective learning and feature selection methods identified in the classification analyses were applied in the regression approach to derive the clinical RPM. Rebalancing was not performed as as it is not meaningful for continuous outcomes. The regression approach has the advantage of predicting the estimated EURO-D score at follow up, allowing stratification of depression severity. Models were evaluated with 10 fold cross-validation.

Predictive accuracy was assessed using the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE, equivalent to the Brier score) and explained variance (R2). For comparability with prior studies, predicted EURO-D score were back-transformed into the dichotomous outcome (depression yes/no, cut-off ≥ 4). On this binary outcome we computed the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV) Negative Predictive Value (NPV), F1-score, and the Clinical Utility Index (CUI) [13]. To further characterize performance, we examined calibration plots to describe the agreement between the estimated and observed number of events at each threshold of risk, and precision–recall (PR) curves to complement ROC analyses. Calibration is particularly relevant to examining whether a model under- or overestimates risk among specific ranges of risk scores. In this regression approach, the model output consisted of the individual predicted EURO-D score at follow up, which was also converted into the likelihood of belonging to the “depressed” class. Finally, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the net clinical benefit of the models across different risk thresholds [14]. To assess fairness, model performance was compared between men and women.

Results

Participants

From SHARE waves 4–8, we identified 4057 participants with cancer (Supplement S4, Figure S4.2). At follow-up, 1360 met criteria for depression (33.5%). This sample size allows up to predictors within the 20 events-per-variable threshold.

Participants were mostly females, with a mean age of 69, and predominantly retied. Civil status and cancer location had high rates of missing data, although most participants were married and living with spouse. The most common cancer types were breast, followed by colon, skin and multiple sites (Table 1, S4.5, S4.6). Predictor distributions were largely similar across those with and without data at follow up (Table S4.5, Table S4.6). As expected, the largest difference by depression status appeared in EURO-D items and related factors: depressed participants reported more economic difficulties, lower energy levels, less confidence in the future, poorer computer skills, higher prevalence of long-term conditions and activity limitations, and less frequent physical activity.

Identification of predictors

Of 94 potential predictors identified from SHARE, four with low variance were excluded, leaving a final set of 90 (Table S4.5, Figure S4.7).

Model development and specification

Full details of model development are provided in the Supplement. The analytic code is available at https://github.com/aclai-lab/arturo-risk-prediction-model. All models are trained on the same dataset, with missing data coded consistently.

Model performance

Classification approach

Because of class imbalance (depressed, n = 1360 vs. non-depressed, n = 2697), we tested resampling strategies. Full results are provided in the Supplement (NOBL: supplement S5.1; BLU: S5.2; BLO: S5.3). Models using BLU were the most reliable, averaged over different feature selection and learning methods. Although BLU yielded slightly lower accuracy than oversampling (average accuracy BLU: 72.9%, BLO: 74.7%, NOBL: 75.8%), it achieved a superior sensitivity/specificity ratio (BLU: 1.09 average ratio, BLO: 1.49; NOBL: 1.73) (Tables S5.1.1, S5.2.1, S5.3.1), suggesting a more balanced representation of depressed and non-depressed cases.

Among learning methods, GLM performed slightly better than RF and DT in terms of mean accuracy, averaged over the different predictor selection methods (Table S5.2.1; GLM: 73.6%, RF: 73.1%, DT: 72.1%) and had a good sensitivity/specificity ratio (GLM: 1.04, RF: 1.02; DT: 1.21) (Tables S5.1.1, S5.2.1, S5.3.1).

Feature selection substantially reduced the number of predictors from 90, with GA retaining the best performance in terms of accuracy (GA: 74.6%, ALL: 71.9%, FW: 73.3%, BW: 74.4%, Table S5.2.1) and a good sensitivity/specificity ratio (GA: 1.04, ALL: 1.03; FW: 1.05, BW: 1.03) (Tables S5.1.1, S5.2.1, S5.3.1). This model had also the best CUI (0.64). Full details, including sensitivity, specificity, AUC-ROC, PPV, NPV, calibration curves, and confusion matrices, are reported in the Supplement (Sections S5.1–S5.3).

Decision curve analysis (Supplement S5.2.9) showed that GLM, RF, and DT models all yielded greater net benefit than the default strategies of treating all or treating none across clinically relevant thresholds (approximately 0.2–0.6). Within this range the models would identify more true cases of depression while reducing unnecessary interventions. At higher thresholds, net benefit declined toward or below zero, indicating limited additional value when only very high risks are considered. Overall, the GLM and RF models showed more stable curves than DT, consistent with their stronger overall performance.

Results of risk prediction models, regression approach

Based on these results, the GLM with Genetic Algorithm (GLM-GA-R) was selected for regression modelling. It achieved sufficient precision (MAE: 1.5, 95%CI: 1.4–1.6; MSE: 3.70, 95%CI: 4.10, 3.31) and explained 36.2% of EURO-D variance (R2 95%CI: 31.5% − 40.5%).

When EURO-D scores were dichotomized, GLM-GA-R reached 75.1% accuracy, with high mean specificity (90.7%) but lower mean sensitivity (43.8%). Predictive values were balanced (PPV: 70.5%; NPV: 76.2%), and discrimination was good (AUC-ROC: 0.81; Fig. 1). Calibration was satisfactory across risk thresholds, with only slight overestimation at higher risks (Fig. 2). Additional metrics, including confusion matrices, are available in the Supplement (Sections S6.1–S6.2). At a Using a 35% threshold, PPV was 82.7% and NPV 58.3%; at a more conservative threshold of 50% risk, PPV was 80% and NPV 75% (Table S5.1.5).

Sensitivity analyses confirmed GLM as the best learning method: DT-GA-R (AUC-ROC: 0.77, mean accuracy: 73.3%, PPV: 70.5%, NPV: 74%) and RF-GA-R (AUC-ROC: 0.81, mean accuracy: 75.2%, PPV: 69.9%, NPV: 76.8%) performed similarly but with slightly lower predictive values (Table S6.2.2). Calibration curves show that all methods showed a mild tendency to uniformly underestimate the risk of depression. GA consistently gave the best results. Overall, these models predicted depression at two-years follow-up in roughly three out of four cases.

GLM-GA-R retained 48 predictors (Table 2) spanning sociodemographic (e.g. age, marital and work status, urban vs. rural area, household composition, received help from others), economic (“Is household able to make ends meet”, “shortage of money stops doing things”), psychosocial (“Age prevents from doing things“, “Look back on life with happiness”, “Bothered by frailty”, loneliness), health-related factors (physical activity, ADLs, sensory abilities, oral health, difficulties accessing healthcare, number of medications, BMI, year of cancer diagnosis and years of other chronic medical illnesses), and the presence of depression at baseline plus few selected baseline EURO-D symptoms (death thoughts, sleep problems, irritability, lack of concentration).

Online risk calculator and code sharing

The final model (BLU-GLM-GA-C) was implemented as a multilingual, freely available web risk calculator [ https://dk.aclai.unife.it/Arturo ]. Data preparation and model code are openly available at the ACLAI-Lab GitHub page, University of Ferrara [ https://github.com/aclai-lab/arturo-risk-prediction-model ].

Discussion

This study presents Arturo, the first Risk Prediction Model designed to estimate the risk of depression among older adults with cancer. The model achieved satisfactory predictive accuracy and is freely accessible for public use (https://dk.aclai.unife.it/Arturo). Arturo may support risk stratification in psycho-oncology care and inform the prevention of depression in this vulnerable population.

Few RPMs exist for mental disorders [2, 15]; even fewer among patients with cancer, despite their clinical relevance [16, 17]. Previous models were restricted to breast cancer [6, 7]. One predicted anxiety after treatment, explaining 60% of variance with a small set of predictors selected by LASSO, but was restricted to early-stage disease and short-term follow-up [7]. Another cross-sectional model for depression achieved good discrimination (AUC 0.82; sensitivity 90.6%, specificity 72.1%), but relied on a relatively small hospital-based cohort and narrow set of clinical and social predictors [6]. The Arturo RPM extends these findings by using a large, multinational cohort including diverse cancer types, a two-year follow-up, and a broad range of health-related, psychological and social predictors. It builds on a similar strategy used in developing Manto [2] and DRAT-UP [15], RPMs for depression among the general population. The approach is based on the identification of robust predictors from literature reviews, use of wide public datasets, comparison of analytic strategies.

Arturo combines cancer-related with broader social, demographic and psychological factors, enabling stratification of depression risk in a clinically meaningful way. While not all patients remain depressed after cancer diagnosis, those who do experience worse prognosis and may even display higher mortality rates [1, 18]. Depression may either contribute causally to cancer outcomes, or reflect underlying processes influencing disease course [16, 19]. In both cases, it amplifies the impact of cancer on quality of life, psychosocial and work functioning. Evidence for prevention of depression in cancer remains limited, with mixed findings across pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy and other interventions [20]. Given the high heterogeneity of this population [16] and difficulties conducting research on prevention, targeted trials for high-risk groups identified by models such as Arturo may prove more effective. By providing individualized risk estimates, Arturo may support shared decision-making and help clinicians tailor preventive or therapeutic strategies. Decision curve analysis further suggested that the models provide clinical utility mainly at moderate thresholds (around 20–60%), which align with the range where clinicians would typically consider preventive interventions. The results from Arturo may guide discussions between patients and providers: exploring predicted risk scores may favour tailoring treatment or prevention efforts, or designing targeted strategies for reducing depression risk [21].

Limitations

This study is strengthened by a robust methodological approach and likely generalizability to the broad general population of older adults with cancer. The model represents a valuable tool for identifying individuals at higher risk of depression and has potential applications in both clinical settings and public health strategies, although further validation and refinement may be warranted. The study has also limitations. First, SHARE lacks key cancer-specific variables, such as stage and prognosis, and cancer site was often missing. This may have constrained performance, as these factors could influence depression risk [16]. However, the inclusion of multiple psychosocial predictors may have mitigated the impact [2] at the expense of a longer completion time of the online risk calculator. Second, external validation was not possible due to the absence of a suitable dataset with a comparable breadth of predictors included in the SHARE dataset. Third, the two-year prediction period may not apply to shorter or longer intervals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Arturo is the first freely available risk prediction model for depression in older adults with cancer. Its performance is comparable to cancer-specific models, but with broader scope and generalizability. Future iterations may improve accuracy by incorporating cancer-specific data and external validation. As a web-based tool, Arturo is readily accessible to patients, clinicians, and policymakers, with potential to guide preventive strategies and resource allocation.
