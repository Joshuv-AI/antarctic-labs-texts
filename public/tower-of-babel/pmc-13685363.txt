Efficiency and productivity of Mongolian subnational public hospital systems, 2014–2023: annual DEA, global Malmquist and double-bootstrap analysis
Authors: Buyantogtokh Batsukh, Ganzorig Batbaatar, Amarzaya Batchimed, Tumurbat Byamba, Gantsetseg Baljirsuren, Byambakhorloo Sukhbaatar, Byambatseren Boldbaatar, Dariimaa Ganbat

ABSTRACT

Background

Health systems facing constrained fiscal space must consider not only how much they spend, but also how effectively available resources are transformed into accessible and useful services. Efficiency evidence can support universal health coverage and resource planning, provided that production efficiency is not confused with allocative cost-effectiveness or social value [1, 2]. Hospital efficiency is particularly difficult to interpret because hospitals use several forms of labour and capital to produce heterogeneous services, while case mix, quality, referral roles and geography may differ substantially across providers [2, 3].

Data envelopment analysis (DEA) provides a non-parametric method for comparing multiple inputs and outputs without imposing a specific functional form [4]. Constant-returns and variable-returns models can distinguish overall technical performance from scale-related performance [5]. DEA has therefore been widely applied to hospitals, but reviews show substantial variation in production variables, orientation, reference technologies and second-stage methods [6, 7]. A DEA score is an estimated relative distance from an observed frontier; it is not a direct measure of clinical quality, welfare, budget adequacy or an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio.

Radial DEA may also overlook input- or output-specific slack. The slack-based measure (SBM) offers a complementary non-radial assessment [8]. Productivity change requires a separate intertemporal framework. The global Malmquist productivity index compares all observations with one global reference technology, avoids the circularity problems of chained adjacent-period indices and supports a consistent interpretation across the full panel [9]. Because frontier indices are estimated from finite samples, apparent changes may reflect sampling variation. Simar and Wilson developed a bootstrap procedure that reproduces the frontier data-generating process and provides inference for Malmquist indices and their components [10].

Second-stage regression creates an additional inferential problem. Estimated DEA scores are serially correlated through the common frontier, so conventional Tobit or ordinary regression standard errors are generally invalid. Simar and Wilson’s double-bootstrap truncated regression addresses the estimated-score process under a separability assumption [11]. In longitudinal applications, however, repeated observations from the same decision-making unit create a further dependence layer. Treating each region-year as independent can make time-invariant covariates, such as geographic area, appear much more precisely estimated than the number of genuinely independent regions permits.

Mongolia is an informative setting for this problem. Its population is distributed across a very large territory, while secondary and tertiary services are concentrated in regional centres and Ulaanbaatar [12–14]. Regional hospital systems differ markedly in population scale, remoteness and referral responsibilities. Recent systematic reviews emphasise the need for parsimonious production sets, uncertainty analysis and cautious policy translation in hospital efficiency research [15, 16].

The original submission used a broad five-input/five-output pooled frontier, an adjacent-period Malmquist index, under-five mortality as an undesirable hospital output and a random-effects panel Tobit model. Following peer review, we replaced rather than relabelled those analyses. The present study aimed to: (1) describe annual technical and scale efficiency using a source-audited three-input/three-output hospital production set; (2) estimate productivity change using a single global reference technology with frontier-bootstrap inference; and (3) assess population scale and geographic area using Simar-Wilson Algorithm 2 at the level of 22 independent regions.

Relationship to previous work

A related publication used selected years for 21 provincial systems and combined efficiency indicators with composite rankings and policy-oriented reform classifications [17]. The present manuscript has a different primary question and analytical design: it includes Ulaanbaatar, uses a balanced 2014–2023 panel, adopts an auditable three-input/three-output production set, estimates annual frontiers, applies a global Malmquist index with a smoothed frontier bootstrap, and conducts an independent-region double-bootstrap contextual analysis. The previous composite ranking and province-level reform groups are not reproduced as study outcomes here.

Methods

Study design, setting and analytical units

We conducted a retrospective longitudinal efficiency analysis of 22 aggregated subnational public hospital systems—21 aimags and Ulaanbaatar—from 2014 through 2023. The primary longitudinal analytical unit was the region-year hospital system, yielding a balanced panel of 220 observations. The unit does not represent an individual hospital and should not be interpreted as a facility-level judgement of managers or clinicians. For the contextual regression, the analytical unit was changed to one decade-average observation per region, yielding 22 independent decision-making units.

Data sources and audit

Hospital capacity and service data were transcribed from Ministry of Health statistical compendia and administrative regional tables. Population was obtained from the National Statistics Office of Mongolia [13]. During revision, the authors cross-walked 11 source variables across all 220 region-years (2,420 hospital-source cells) and all 220 population observations against the originating tables; no numerical mismatch was identified in the final analytical panel. Inpatient admissions were retained as reported and rounded to the nearest whole admission using half-up rounding. The 2021 increase in beds was retained because the source documents identify temporary COVID-19 public hospital capacity rather than a data-entry outlier. The dataset contains aggregated statistics and no individual-level or identifiable patient data.

Production variables and contextual information

Variables were selected for source traceability, continuity across all region-years, interpretability within a hospital production function and parsimony relative to 22 regions per annual frontier. The production set comprised three inputs—beds, physicians and mid-level personnel—and three desirable service outputs—inpatient admissions, operations and outpatient visits (Table 1). Nurses were not added to mid-level personnel because the source categories overlap. Financial variables were excluded because a harmonised, price-consistent regional expenditure measure was unavailable for all observations.

Production set and contextual information

Hospital days were excluded from the primary output set because beds already measure inpatient capacity and longer stays may reflect case complexity, delayed discharge or inefficient length of stay rather than greater desirable production. Population under-five mortality was removed from every DEA and SBM frontier. It is a population-level, non-discretionary contextual indicator influenced by primary care, socioeconomic conditions and other determinants; it was retained in the reproducibility dataset for descriptive use only and was not interpreted as hospital quality. Risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality was unavailable. Annual and pooled efficiency analysis

Input-oriented DEA was estimated under constant returns to scale (CRS) and variable returns to scale (VRS) [4, 5]. Annual frontiers were the primary description of contemporaneous efficiency, ensuring that an early-year observation was not judged against a technology observed only in later years. Scale efficiency was calculated as CRS efficiency divided by VRS efficiency. A decade-wide pooled VRS frontier was retained only as a supplementary common benchmark. A non-oriented VRS SBM using the same three inputs and three desirable outputs was also supplementary [8]. No mortality or other undesirable output was included.

Global Malmquist productivity and statistical inference

Productivity was measured with the output-oriented global CRS Malmquist productivity index (GMPI) [9]. All 220 observations defined one global reference technology. For each transition, the GMPI was decomposed into contemporaneous efficiency change and best-practice change; regional transition indices were aggregated using geometric means because productivity indices are multiplicative.

Uncertainty was estimated with a 400-replicate smoothed trajectory frontier bootstrap following the data-generating-process replication principle of Simar and Wilson [10], adapted to the global technology. First, annual CRS output distances were converted to radial inefficiencies and each observation was projected to its estimated annual frontier. Second, complete 10-year vectors of log-inefficiency were resampled across regions and smoothed with a multivariate Gaussian kernel. A Silverman-type bandwidth, variance correction and reflection at the zero boundary were applied. Third, pseudo outputs were generated along each observation’s output ray while inputs were held fixed. Fourth, both annual and global CRS technologies were re-estimated in every replicate before recalculating the GMPI and both components. Bias-corrected geometric-mean estimates and two-sided 95% log-ratio basic bootstrap intervals are reported. An interval excluding 1.000 was interpreted as evidence of productivity change at the 5% level.

Independent-region Simar-Wilson contextual analysis

The earlier 220-region-year second-stage model was removed because repeated observations from 22 regions are not independent and geographic area is repeated ten times. For the revised contextual analysis, each region’s inputs and outputs were averaged across 2014–2023, producing 22 independent long-run regional production observations. Input-oriented VRS DEA was then re-estimated on this 22-region cross-section. Reciprocal efficiency (δ = 1/θ; δ ≥ 1) was the dependent inefficiency measure.

Simar-Wilson Algorithm 2 used 200 model-based score-bias bootstrap replicates and 2,000 parametric regression bootstrap replicates [11]. The primary model was intentionally restricted to two prespecified covariates, standardised log mean population and standardised log geographic area, to limit overfitting. Initial and final left-truncated regressions used non-frontier observations; in each score-bootstrap replicate, reference inputs were rescaled, the original observations were re-evaluated against the pseudo reference set, and score bias was corrected before the final regression. Coefficients are associative, conditional on separability and the selected production set, and are not causal estimates.

Software, reproducibility and use of generative AI

Analyses were executed in Python 3 using NumPy and SciPy/HiGHS. The cleaned analytical panel, region-level contextual dataset, transition-level productivity results, bootstrap replicates and methods metadata are provided in Additional file 1; the executable Python analysis code is provided in Additional file 2; and the variable definitions, run specification, bootstrap description and file map are provided in Additional file 3. During revision, generative-AI tools were used for language editing, manuscript organisation and code review. All analyses were executed on the supplied data; the authors reviewed the code, checked the numerical outputs and references, interpreted the findings, and retain full responsibility for the final manuscript.

Results

Descriptive profile and annual efficiency

Across 220 region-years, mean beds were 1,187 (range 86–15,846), mean physicians 531 (52–9,856), mean mid-level personnel 952 (101–13,140), mean inpatient admissions 40,728 (4,337–584,495), mean operations 8,860 (347–217,564), and mean outpatient visits 827,525 (97,106–14,298,637). The wide ranges primarily reflect the inclusion of Ulaanbaatar and differences in regional scale; they do not represent case-mix-adjusted hospital comparisons.

Mean annual VRS technical efficiency was 0.931. Annual means ranged from 0.909 in 2017 to 0.950 in 2023 (Table 2). Mean annual CRS efficiency was 0.918, and mean annual scale efficiency was 0.986. Against the more demanding decade-wide pooled frontier, mean VRS efficiency was 0.845 and mean SBM efficiency was 0.604. These pooled values are supplementary common-benchmark diagnostics and are not annual trend estimates. The year-specific efficiency trajectories are shown in Fig. 2.

Independent-region contextual assessment

The 22-region decade-average DEA identified eight regions on the raw VRS frontier and 14 non-frontier regions. After Algorithm 2 score correction, the coefficient for standardised log population was −0.007 (95% bootstrap CI −0.107 to 0.094), and the coefficient for standardised log area was 0.120 (−0.093 to 0.201) (Table 4). Both intervals included zero. Accordingly, the revised independent-region analysis does not establish a clear association between either population scale or geographic area and long-run relative inefficiency. Discussion

This full reanalysis produced three main findings. First, regional systems generally operated near their own annual VRS frontiers, although the pooled benchmark and SBM revealed greater room for improvement when observations across the decade were compared with one common technology. Second, the global productivity analysis identified several statistically supported periods of decline, including 2019–2020, followed by a strong 2020–2021 recovery and a further improvement in 2022–2023. Third, once the contextual model was restricted to 22 independent regional observations, neither population nor geographic area had a clearly non-zero association with inefficiency.

The difference between annual and pooled efficiency is substantively important. Annual frontiers answer whether a region performed well relative to what was observed in the same year; the pooled frontier asks whether it performed well relative to the best combinations observed anywhere during the decade. The latter is a stricter diagnostic and can benchmark earlier observations against later technology. It should therefore not be plotted or interpreted as an annual efficiency trend. Hospital DEA studies often report high VRS scores, particularly when the number of decision-making units is modest relative to the number of variables [6, 7]. Our reduced three-input/three-output specification improves discrimination and traceability but does not eliminate the finite-sample nature of the frontier.

The global index provides a more coherent view of change than the former adjacent-period specification. The statistically supported declines from 2014 through 2017 indicate that productivity weakness predated the pandemic. The 2019–2020 decline, driven mainly by best-practice change, is compatible with disruption of the observable production frontier during the onset of COVID-19. The strong 2020–2021 rebound is compatible with service restoration and temporary capacity expansion. Similar pandemic-related disturbances have been reported in hospital efficiency studies, although institutional settings and variable definitions differ [18]. The finding should not be interpreted as proof that the pandemic caused each transition; the study has no counterfactual design.

The contextual result changes an important conclusion from the earlier revision. The pooled 220-row model repeated each region’s area ten times and produced an implausibly narrow confidence interval. Algorithm 2 corrects bias and serial correlation generated by estimated DEA scores under its data-generating assumptions, but it does not turn repeated values from 22 regions into 220 independent geographic observations. The independent-region model appropriately widens uncertainty and no longer supports a robust area effect. Geographic dispersion remains a plausible planning concern in Mongolia [12–14], but this dataset cannot quantify a precise causal or budgetary penalty for area.

International evidence shows that hospital efficiency depends on model choice, service mix, scale, institutional environment and quality adjustment [15, 16, 19, 20]. Studies from China, Eritrea and other middle-income settings demonstrate the usefulness of DEA for identifying potential input or scale imbalances, but direct numerical comparisons are inappropriate because the units, technologies and variables differ [19, 20]. Earlier CERA studies commonly used Tobit second stages [21, 22]; the present revision responds to subsequent econometric concerns by using double-bootstrap inference and by separating the longitudinal productivity question from the independent contextual question.

For policy, the results support transparent productivity monitoring rather than mechanical redistribution. A region with a low relative score may face case mix, referral obligations, geography or data-quality constraints that are absent from the model. Conversely, a high service-volume efficiency score does not demonstrate high clinical quality. Decisions about beds, staffing or budgets require marginal costs, health outcomes, equity weights and opportunity costs. The results are therefore not incremental cost-effectiveness ratios and do not justify punitive rankings, automatic funding cuts or replication of the previous composite reform groups.

Strengths and limitations

A principal strength is that the revision directly addresses the inferential and conceptual concerns raised during peer review. It uses an auditable, parsimonious production set; separates annual and pooled reference technologies; adopts a single global productivity technology; re-estimates frontiers in the productivity bootstrap; removes population mortality and hospital days from desirable production; and replaces the dependent region-year second stage with an independent-region Algorithm 2 analysis. The complete analytical panel, code and bootstrap outputs are supplied for reproducibility.

The study also has important limitations. First, each decision-making unit is an aggregated regional hospital system. Hospital-level governance, case mix, severity, referral flows and private-sector activity are unobserved. Second, service counts do not measure clinical outcomes or quality. Under-five mortality is population-level and cannot substitute for risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality. Third, no harmonised real expenditure series was available, so the study estimates technical production efficiency rather than allocative or cost efficiency. Fourth, Ulaanbaatar differs substantially from the aimags, and its inclusion expands variable ranges even though it is an essential part of the national subnational system.

Fifth, annual DEA uses only 22 regions; although the three-input/three-output model is parsimonious, efficiency remains sensitive to frontier composition and measurement error. Sixth, the GMPI bootstrap is a trajectory-preserving adaptation of the Simar-Wilson data-generating-process approach to a global reference technology; finite-sample coverage cannot be guaranteed in this specific panel. Seventh, the independent contextual model has only 22 observations and consequently limited power. Its null findings should be interpreted as absence of clear evidence, not evidence that population and geography are irrelevant. Finally, separability between contextual variables and the production technology is assumed rather than testable with these data.

Conclusions

Using a source-audited and methodologically narrower design, Mongolian subnational public hospital systems showed high contemporaneous VRS efficiency but several statistically supported episodes of productivity decline, followed by a substantial 2020–2021 recovery and further improvement in 2022–2023. The independent 22-region double-bootstrap analysis did not identify a clearly non-zero association of population or geographic area with long-run inefficiency. The findings support careful productivity monitoring, data improvement and investigation of service restoration, case mix, quality and costs. They do not support causal geographic budget formulas, composite rankings or claims of cost-effectiveness.
