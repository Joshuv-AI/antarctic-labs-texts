Time-Dependent Hamiltonian Simulation in the Low-Energy Subspace
Shuo Zhou, Zhaokai Pan, Weiyuan Gong, Tongyang Li

Abstract
Hamiltonian simulations are key subroutines in adiabatic quantum computation, quantum control, and quantum many-body physics, where quantum dynamics often happen in the low-energy sector. In contrast to time-independent Hamiltonian simulations, a comprehensive understanding of quantum simulation algorithms for time-dependent Hamiltonians under the low-energy assumption remains limited hitherto. In this paper, we investigate how much we can improve upon the standard performance guarantee assuming the initial state is supported on a low-energy subspace. In particular, we compute the Trotter number of digital quantum simulation based on product formulas for time-dependent spin Hamiltonians under the low-energy assumption that the initial state is supported on a small number of low-energy eigenstates, and show improvements over the standard cost for simulating full unitary simulations. Technically, we derive the low-energy simulation error with commutator scaling for product formulas by leveraging adiabatic perturbation theory to analyze the time-variant energy spectrum of the underlying Hamiltonian. We further discuss the applications to simulations of non-equilibrium quantum many-body dynamics and adiabatic state preparation. Finally, we prove a lower bound of query complexity for generic time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations.

Simulating the dynamics of quantum systems governed by an underlying Hamiltonian is one of the primary applications of quantum computers

.
Since the quantum simulation algorithms were first studied by Lloyd

However, simulations of time-dependent Hamiltonians have been significantly less studied, with techniques ranging from time-dependent product formulas
[

]
, Dyson series
[

]
to Magnus expansion
[

Given an initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
, the time evolution is governed by the Schrödinger’s equation:

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\ket{\psi(t)}=H(t)\ket{\psi(t)},~t\in[0,T].

(1)

Our goal is to compute the quantum state
\ket{\psi(T)}
at the final stage.
To achieve this, we construct a quantum circuit to approximate the exact evolution operator with the time-ordering operator
\mathcal{T}
:

\displaystyle V(T,0)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}H(\tau)\differential{\tau}\right).

(2)

The most common algorithm is Trotterization, which given a Hamiltonian in the form of summation
H(t)=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\Gamma}H_{\gamma}(t)
, decomposes
T
into equidistant steps
\delta=T/r
, and then implements the product of (time-ordered) exponentials of
H_{\gamma}(t)
by a quantum circuit
U
.
For large enough
r
, we have
V(T,0)\approx\Pi_{j=1}^{r}U(j\delta,(j-1)\delta)
.
We can further generalize the product formula to
(p\geq 2)
-th orders using the recursion rule.
There are two different common choices of product formula with similar properties in each time step in recent literature
[
22
,
40
]
, known as generalized and standard time-dependent product formulas.
The main challenge for implementing product formulas is to decide a proper
r
to guarantee that the simulation error is below the target threshold
\epsilon
.

The applications of time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations span across adiabatic quantum computation
[
17
]
, quantum control
[
14
]
, and quantum many-body physics
[
2
]
, where quantum dynamics often take place in the low-energy sector.
In the simpler case where the Hamiltonian is time-independent, this problem has attracted much attention from the community.
Former studies
[
34
,
20
,
21
]
have shown that when the initial state is only supported on a low-energy subspace, the simulation complexity of product formulas
[
26
,
9
,
11
,
39
]
can be significantly improved.
Ref.
[
41
]
further proposed low-energy simulation algorithms based on quantum singular value transformation
[
19
]
.
Recently, Ref.
[
33
]
substantially improved the low-energy simulation analysis for product formulas with commutator scaling
[
12
]
.
However, the understanding of quantum simulation algorithms for time-dependent Hamiltonians under the low-energy assumptions is limited.

Unlike the time-independent case, the exact evolution operator with time-ordered exponential no longer commutes with the low-energy subspace projector.
We cannot adopt the analysis framework in the time-independent case splitting the error into projected error and effective low-energy norm.
To address this issue, we introduce adiabatic perturbation theory as a key subroutine to compute the error terms in the analysis framework.
Our results provide a valuable tool for simulating non-equilibrium quantum many-body dynamics and adiabatic state preparation by product formula, which paves the way to practical time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations.

1.2
Techniques

Here, we provide a high-level discussion of the techniques to derive the theorems mentioned above.
In the following, we refer to the simulation error for each time step
\delta
as the short-time simulation error, and the whole simulation error over
T
as the long-time simulation error.

Short-time simulation error.
To derive an upper bound with commutator scaling for the short-time simulation error, we first recall the time-independent case
[
12
]
.
According to the variation-of-parameters formula, the
p
-th order time-independent product formula
S_{p}(\delta)
and the exact evolution operator
V(\delta)=e^{-iH\delta}
satisfy

\displaystyle S_{p}(\delta)-V(\delta)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\differential\tau e^{-iH(\delta-\tau)}\left(\frac{\differential}{\differential\tau}S_{p}(\tau)+iHS_{p}(\tau)\right)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\differential\tau e^{-iH(\delta-\tau)}S_{p}(\tau)\Delta(\tau),

where
\Delta(\tau)=S_{p}^{\dagger}(\tau)(\frac{\differential}{\differential\tau}S_{p}(\tau)+iHS_{p}(\tau))
can be written as the form
e^{\tau A_{1}}e^{\tau A_{2}}\cdots e^{\tau A_{q}}Be^{-\tau A_{q}}\cdots e^{-\tau A_{2}}e^{-\tau A_{1}}
for some Hermitian operators
A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{q}
, and can be further expanded as nested commutators.
Recently, Ref.
[
33
]
significantly improved the analysis of the low-energy simulation error by directly appending the low-energy projector
\Pi
at both sides of the above equation:

\displaystyle(S_{p}(\delta)-V(\delta))\Pi=\int_{0}^{\delta}\differential\tau e^{-iH(\delta-\tau)}S_{p}(\tau)\Delta(\tau)\Pi,

\displaystyle\norm{(S_p(\delta)-V(\delta))\Pi}\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}\differential{\tau}\norm{\Delta(\tau)\Pi}.

The order condition indicates that
\Delta(\tau)=O(\tau^{p})
, so we only need to reserve the remainder in its
(p-1)
-th order Taylor expansion.
However, in this way
\norm{\Delta(\tau)\Pi}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle\norm{e^{iH_\gamma}\cdot\text{commutator}\cdot e^{-iH_\gamma}\cdot\Pi}\neq\norm{\text{commutator}\cdot\Pi}

for some commutator, which forbids us to apply the projection lemmas to the nested commutator. As a simple illustrative example, we consider the
(p-1)
-th order Taylor expansion of
e^{\tau A}Be^{-\tau A}
:

\displaystyle e^{\tau A}Be^{-\tau A}=B+\tau\operatorname{ad}_{A}B+\frac{\tau^{2}}{2!}\operatorname{ad}_{A}^{2}B+\cdots+\frac{\tau^{p-1}}{(p-1)!}\operatorname{ad}_{A}^{p-1}B+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{\tau}_{1}\frac{\tau_{1}^{p-1}}{(p-1)!}e^{(\tau-\tau_{1})A}\operatorname{ad}_{A}^{p}Be^{-(\tau-\tau_{1})A}}_{O(\norm{[A,\dots,[A,B]]}\cdot\tau^{p})}.

It can be observed that all the lower-order terms only contain commutators.
However, the commutator in the integral remainder is sandwiched by
e^{\tau A}
if we only expand the Taylor series to the
(p-1)
-th order.
To address this issue, we expand the Taylor series to higher orders to calculate the benefit to the leading-order term from simulating only states in the low-energy subspace.

In this work, we leverage the Floquet theory
[
32
]
to calculate a time-dependent analogue to the residual term
\Delta(\tau)
above.
We append the low-energy projector to its high-order Taylor series, and compute upper bounds on different terms using a series of projection lemmas as tools.

Long-time simulation error.
To illustrate why we cannot simply decompose the long-time simulation error into sum of each step, we recall the proof of triangle inequality for telescoping operator products:
Given unitaries
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
,
\norm{U_1U_2\cdots U_r-V_1V_2\cdots V_r}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{U_i-V_i}
since

\displaystyle\norm{U_1U_2\cdots U_r-V_1V_2\cdots V_r}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{U_1U_2\cdots U_r-U_1V_2\cdots V_r}+\norm{U_1V_2\cdots V_r-V_1V_2\cdots V_r}

\displaystyle=\norm{U_1(U_2\cdots U_r-V_2\cdots V_r)}+\norm{(U_1-V_1)V_2\cdots V_r}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{U_1\cdots U_{i-1}(U_i-V_i)V_{i+1}\cdots V_r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{U_i-V_i}.

However, if we append an additional operator such as the projector
\Pi
, we can only derive

\displaystyle\norm{(U_1U_2\cdots U_r-V_1V_2\cdots V_r)\Pi}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{U_1\cdots U_{i-1}(U_i-V_i)V_{i+1}\cdots V_r\Pi}\neq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{(U_i-V_i)\Pi}.

In general, the last two expressions do not equal unless
[V_{i},\Pi]=0
or
[U_{i},\Pi]=0
.
On the other hand, note that in adiabatic perturbation theory, there are upper bounds for the form of
\norm{(I-\Pi)V\Pi}
. Therefore, intuitively, we can split the long-time simulation error into the sum of short-time simulation errors plus additional leakage errors, that is,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{U_1\cdots U_{i-1}(U_i-V_i)V_{i+1}\cdots V_r\Pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\norm{U_1\cdots U_{i-1}(U_i-V_i)(I-\Pi+\Pi)V_{i+1}\cdots V_r\Pi}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\norm{(U_i-V_i)\Pi}+\norm{(U_i-V_i)(I-\Pi)V_{i+1}\cdots V_r\Pi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\norm{(U_i-V_i)\Pi}+\norm{U_i-V_i}\cdot\norm{(I-\Pi)V_{i+1}\cdots V_r\Pi}\right).

Lower bound for query complexity.
Time-independent Hamiltonians can be regarded as a subset of time-dependent Hamiltonians.
Therefore, any generic quantum algorithm for time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations also applies to time-independent Hamiltonian simulations, and thus cannot violate the existing lower bound concerning the time-independent case
[
7
]
.

Among the parameters for sparse Hamiltonian simulations, we keep the sparsity
d
unchanged and consider the time-dependent variant of the max-norm
\norm{H(t)}_{\max}
, such that the derived lower bound is compatible with the above arguments. We take a linear time-dependent Hamiltonian as the hard instance, and construct a reduction from its simulation to string parity computation, which has a known lower bound on both probabilistic Turing machine and quantum Turing machine
[
3
,
16
]
.

1.3
Open questions

Our paper leaves several open questions for future investigation:

•

Interaction picture.
In this work, we focus on product formulas in the Schrödinger picture. It is natural to ask whether the low-energy assumption can also enhance interaction picture Hamiltonian simulations
[
30
]
as another frequently considered time-dependent scenario.

•

L^{1}
-norm scaling.
Our error derivation is based on
[
32
]
which bounds the nested commutator using max-norm. Is there possibility to improve our max-norm scaling to
L^{1}
-norm scaling as the continuous qDRIFT algorithm and the rescaled Dyson series algorithm
[
8
]
.

•

Non-smooth Hamiltonian simulation.
The time-dependent product formulas require appropriate smooth conditions to achieve the desired error scaling. On the other hand, the current qubitization approaches
[
31
]
for time-dependent Hamiltonians are also restricted to similar prerequisites. Inspired by this, can we prove a tighter lower bound for non-smooth Hamiltonian simulations, which may indicate that some algorithms are already optimal for generic cases?

Model and setup

In this work, we consider the following

\displaystyle H(t)=\sum_{X\subset\Lambda}f_{X}(t)h_{X},

\displaystyle H_{1}(t)=\sum_{i;\mathrm{odd}}h_{i,i+1}(t),\quad H_{2}(t)=\sum_{i;\mathrm{even}}h_{i,i+1}(t).

We will see later that the multi-linear structure (

For an arbitrary time-dependent Hamiltonian

Therefore, additional assumptions on derivatives are necessary to acquire improvements over full Hilbert space simulations. We thus assume that all the coefficients

The first application scenario is adiabatic state preparation, where

:

H(t)=-\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{2T}\right)\sigma_{x}-\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2T}\right)\sigma_{z}.

Even without the adiabatic condition, this model also simulates non-equilibrium quantum many-body phenomena.
For instance, consider an

For

2.3
Projection lemmas

To analyze the suppression of the operator norm in the subspace of time-dependent Hamiltonians, we first prove a fine-tuned version of the backbone lemma in
[
1
]
:

Lemma 2

(Time-dependent version of Theorem 2.1 of
[
1
]
)
.

For a
k
-local time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
as (
3
), we shift all the local interaction terms to positive semi-definite.
At an instantaneous time
t
, we can regard
H(t)
as a time-independent Hamiltonian with a fixed spectrum, the projector defined in (
6
) is equivalent to
\Pi_{\sigma}(t)=\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t}}
for some energy threshold depending on
t
.

Given an operator
A
, we denote
R
the sum of strengths of
f_{X}(t)h_{X}
that do not commute with
A
. Given energy threshold
\Lambda_{t}^{\prime}
and
\Lambda_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\Pi_{>\Lambda_t'} A\Pi_{\leq\Lambda_t}}\leq\norm{A}\cdot e^{-\lambda(\Lambda_{t}^{\prime}-\Lambda_{t}-2R)},

where
\lambda=(2gk)^{-1}
,
g
is an upper bound of the sum of strength acting on a single spin at
t
.

Proof.

For any
0\leq s<{(gk)}^{-1}
, according to Lemma 3.1 of Ref.
[
1
]
,

\displaystyle\norm{\Pi_{>\Lambda_t'} A\Pi_{\leq\Lambda_t}}

\displaystyle=\norm{\Pi_{>\Lambda_t'} e^{-sH(t)}e^{sH(t)}A e^{-sH(t)}e^{sH(t)}\Pi_{\leq\Lambda_t}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{e^{sH(t)}Ae^{-sH(t)}}\cdot e^{-s(\Lambda_{t}^{\prime}-\Lambda_{t})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-s(\Lambda_{t}^{\prime}-\Lambda_{t})}}{(1-sgk)^{R/gk}}.

To minimize the RHS, by simple calculus we pick
s=\frac{1}{gk}[1-\frac{R}{\Lambda_{t}^{\prime}-\Lambda_{t}}]
, then

2.4
Product formula for time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation

There are essentially two types of product formulas for time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations, which employ different exponential terms.
Huyghebaert and De Raedt first showed how to generalize the Trotter formula to ordered exponentials
[
22
]
, where the time integral over exponent and the time-ordering operator are reserved. We thus call this approach generalized product formulas and denote them by
U_{p}
in this paper.
Subsequently, Suzuki developed another decomposition scheme by merely substituting time-independent product formulas with midpoint times
[
38
]
, which we call standard product formulas and denote them by
S_{p}
in this paper. Besides, there is research
[
23
]
exploiting time-dependent product formulas with the minimum number of exponential terms. Furthermore, Ref.
[
10
]
provides a unifying framework for all these time-dependent product formulas.

Given
H(t)=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\Gamma}H_{\gamma}(t)
, to approximate the exact evolution operator
V(T,0)
, we first decompose
T
into equidistant steps
\delta=T/r
. The first- and second-order generalized product formulas are defined as

\displaystyle U_{1}(t+\delta,t)=\prod_{\gamma}^{\leftarrow}\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int_{t}^{t+\delta}H_{\gamma}(\tau)\differential{\tau}}\right],

\displaystyle U_{2}(t+\delta,t)=\prod_{\gamma}^{\rightarrow}\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int_{t+\delta/2}^{t}H_{\gamma}(\tau)\differential{\tau}}\right]\cdot\prod_{\gamma}^{\leftarrow}\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int_{t}^{t+\delta/2}H_{\gamma}(\tau)\differential{\tau}}\right],

while the standard product formula is defined by the midpoint rule as

\displaystyle S_{2}(t+\delta,t)=\prod_{\gamma}^{\rightarrow}e^{-iH_{\gamma}(t+\delta/2)\delta}\cdot\prod_{\gamma}^{\leftarrow}e^{-iH_{\gamma}(t+\delta/2)\delta},

where each (time-ordered) exponential term can be easily implemented by local quantum gates.

Ref.
[
40
]
formalized the higher-order standard product formulas and proved their error scaling, given the order of differentiability. Specifically,

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{2p}(t+\delta,t)&=S_{2p-2}\left(t+\delta,t+\left[1-u_{p}\right]\delta\right)S_{2p-2}\left(t+\left[1-u_{p}\right]\delta,t+\left[1-2u_{p}\right]\delta\right)\\
&\times S_{2p-2}\left(t+\left[1-2u_{p}\right]\delta,t+2u_{p}\delta\right)S_{2p-2}\left(t+2u_{p}\delta,t+u_{p}\delta\right)U_{2p-2}\left(t+u_{p}\delta,t\right),\end{split}

(8)

where
u_{p}=(4-4^{1/(2p-1)})^{-1}
. Although this recursive method in
[
40
]
is developed for standard product formulas, it can be verified that the vanishing of lower-order error terms still holds for generalized product formulas, given appropriate smooth conditions.
Given each
H_{\gamma}(t)\in C^{p}
, that is, the
0,1,\ldots,p
-th order derivatives exist and are continuous, then

\displaystyle U_{p}(t+\delta,t)=\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int_{t}^{t+\delta}H(\tau)\differential{\tau}}\right]+O(\delta^{p+1}),

\displaystyle S_{p}(t+\delta,t)=\mathcal{T}\left[e^{-i\int_{t}^{t+\delta}H(\tau)\differential{\tau}}\right]+O(\delta^{p+1}).

Recently, Ref.
[
32
]
derives explicit error bounds with commutator scaling for time-dependent product formulas. We review their Floquet theory details and list the important results as lemmas in
Appendix
A
. Given each
H_{\gamma}(t)\in C^{p+2}
, they first embed each
H_{\gamma}(t)
into a time-periodic Hamiltonian with
T^{\mathrm{ex}}=2T
using a bump function.
Then, by employing Floquet theory, they map the time-periodic Hamiltonian into a time-independent Hamiltonian on infinite-dimensional space, whose Trotter error can be bounded with commutator scaling for time-independent scenarios
[
12
]
.
As a result, they obtain a meaningful convergent bound back to the original Hilbert space as follows:

\|U_{p}(t+\delta,t)-V(t+\delta,t)\|\in{O}\left(\max_{\tau\in[t,t+\delta]}\alpha_{\mathrm{com}}^{p+1}(\tau)\cdot\delta^{p+1}\right),

where the nested commutator factor
\alpha_{\mathrm{com}}^{p+1}(t)
is given by

\alpha_{\mathrm{com}}^{p+1}(t)=\sum_{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p}=1}^{\Gamma+1}\sum_{\gamma_{0}=1}^{\Gamma}\left\|\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathcal{D}_{\gamma_{j}}(t)\right\}H_{\gamma_{0}}(t)\right\|,\quad\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(t)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{ad}_{H_{\gamma}(t)}&(\gamma=1,\dots,\Gamma)\\
2\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{~d}}{\mathrm{~d}t}&(\gamma=\Gamma+1)\end{cases}.

(9)

Given each
H_{\gamma}(t)\in C^{p+2}
,
Ref.
[
32
]
also proves that for standard product formulas,

\|S_{p}(t+\delta,t)-V(t+\delta,t)\|\in{O}\left(\max_{\tau\in[t,t+\delta]}\overline{\alpha}_{\mathrm{com}}^{p+1}(\tau)\cdot\delta^{p+1}\right),

where the alternative nested commutator factor
\overline{\alpha}_{\mathrm{com}}^{p+1}(t)
is given by

\overline{\alpha}_{\mathrm{com}}^{p+1}(t)=\sum_{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p}=1}^{\Gamma+1}\sum_{\gamma_{0}=1}^{\Gamma}\left\|\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathcal{\overline{D}}_{\gamma_{j}}(t)\right\}H_{\gamma_{0}}(t)\right\|,\quad\mathcal{\overline{D}}_{\gamma}(t)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{ad}_{H_{\gamma}(t)}&(\gamma=1,\dots,\Gamma)\\
\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{~d}}{\mathrm{~d}t}&(\gamma=\Gamma+1)\end{cases}.

\displaystyle\leq 3q^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}2^{n+1-j}\frac{j^{n+1}}{j!}F^{j}\sum_{X_{1},\dots,X_{j}}\big\|{\underbrace{[h_{X_{j}},\dots,[h_{X_{2}},h_{X_{1}}]]}_{\text{commutator}}\Pi_{\sigma}(0)}\big\|,

Note that in the above analysis, all the operators are considered to be at the same time
t=0
.
As we mentioned in

2
, at an instantaneous time, the spectral projector is equivalent to
\Pi_{\sigma}(0)=\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}}
for some energy threshold
\Delta_{t=0}
.
Similarly we define
\Pi_{>\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}
(
\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}\geq\Delta_{t=0}
) for the fixed spectrum of
H(0)
. We then use the techniques for the time-independent case
[

]
:

\displaystyle\norm{\text{commutator}\cdot\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}}}\leq\norm{\Pi_{>\Delta_{t=0}'}\cdot\text{commutator}\cdot\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}}}+\underbrace{\norm{\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}\cdot\text{commutator}\cdot\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}}}}_{\leq\norm{\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}\cdot\text{commutator}\cdot\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}}}.

The sum of terms of the first kind is directly bounded by

:

\displaystyle\norm{\Pi_{>\Delta_{t=0}'}A_n\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}}}\leq

\displaystyle 3q^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}2^{n+1-j}\frac{j^{n+1}}{j!}F^{j}\sum_{X_{1},\dots,X_{j}}\norm{\Pi_{>\Delta_{t=0}'}[h_{X_j},\dots,[h_{X_2},h_{X_1}]]\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3q^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}(2j)^{n}(kg)^{j-1}\cdot gN\cdot e^{-\lambda(\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}-\Delta_{t=0}-2g(k+(j-1)(k-1))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3(2eq(n+1)kg)^{n+1}(2k)^{-1}Ne^{-\lambda(\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}-\Delta_{t=0})},

where the second inequality follows that the commutator
[h_{X_{j}},\dots,[h_{X_{2}},h_{X_{1}}]]
is supported on at most
k+(j-1)(k-1)

For the sum of terms of the second kind, we alternatively assign the function coefficients into the nested commutator, there are in total
S(n+1,j)
tuples of
(m_{j},\dots,m_{1})
after split:

\displaystyle\sum_{X_{1},\dots,X_{j}}\sum_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j}=n+1-j}\Big|\frac{\differential^{n_{j}}}{\differential{t}^{n_{j}}}(f_{X_{j}}(0),\dots,\frac{\differential^{n_{1}}}{\differential{t}^{n_{1}}}f_{X_{1}}(0))\Big|\norm{\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}[h_{X_j},\dots,[h_{X_2},h_{X_1}]]\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{S(n+1,j)}\sum_{X_{1},\dots,X_{j}}\left\|\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}[f_{X_{j}}^{(m_{j})}(0)h_{X_{j}},\dots,[f_{X_{2}}^{(m_{2})}(0)h_{X_{2}},f_{X_{1}}^{(m_{1})}(0)h_{X_{1}}]]\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}\right\|

To use
Lemma
3
, we first note that by definition the layer of the nested commutator consisting of no derivatives already satisfies the requirement

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}\sum\nolimits_{X}f_{X}(0)h_{X}\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}\right\|=\left\|\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}H(0)\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}}\right\|\leq\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}.

Additionally, we need to introduce the following assumption concerning the layer of derivatives:

Assumption 2
.

For a projector
\Pi(t)
onto the low-energy spectrum of
H(t)
with energy threshold
\Delta_{t}^{\prime}
at any instantaneous time

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi(t)\sum\nolimits_{X}|f_{X}^{(m)}(t)|h_{X}\Pi(t)\right\|\leq\Delta_{t}^{\prime},\quad m=1,\dots,p,

where the inequality can be relaxed to
O(\Delta_{t}^{\prime})

A sufficient condition can be that the derivative is small enough such that
\|{\sum\nolimits_{X}|f_{X}^{(m)}(t)|h_{X}}\|\leq J\leq\Delta_{t}.

We will discuss the reasonability of

2

Section

we have

\displaystyle\norm{\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}A_n\Pi_{\leq\Delta_{t=0}'}}\leq

\displaystyle 3q^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}2^{n+1-j}S(n+1,j)(j-1)!(2kg)^{j-1}\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3q^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}(2j)^{n}(kg)^{j-1}\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3(n+1)^{n+1}(2qkg)^{n}q\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}.

Combining the two parts together, note that for
n
from
p
to
p_{0}-1
,
n+1\leq p_{0}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{n=p}^{p_{0}-1}\frac{\delta^{n+1}}{n+1}\norm{A_n\Pi_\sigma(0)}&\leq 3\sum_{n=p}^{p_{0}-1}(2eqp_{0}kg\delta)^{n+1}(2k)^{-1}Ne^{-\lambda(\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}-\Delta_{t=0})}+3\sum_{n=p}^{p_{0}-1}(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{n}q\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}\delta\\
&\leq 3\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}(2eqp_{0}kg\delta)^{n+1}(2k)^{-1}Ne^{-\lambda(\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}-\Delta_{t=0})}+3\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{n}q\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}\delta.\end{split}

(20)

For a sufficiently small time step
\delta
such that
2q(p_{0}+1)kg\delta\leq\frac{1}{2e}
, which can be achieved by multiplying

with a constant, the formula for the sum of geometric sequences gives:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}(2eqp_{0}kg\delta)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}<1;

\displaystyle\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{p}}{1-2qp_{0}kg\delta}<2(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{p}.

Respectively applying these to the two terms in (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=p}^{p_{0}-1}\frac{\delta^{n+1}}{n+1}\norm{A_n\Pi_\sigma(0)}\leq\frac{3}{2}k^{-1}Ne^{-\lambda(\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}-\Delta_{t=0})}+6(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{p}q\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}\delta.

Finally, the remainder
A_{p_{0}}(\tau)
in (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}\differential{\tau}\norm{A_{p_0}(\tau)}\leq\frac{3}{2}(2q(p_{0}+1)kg\delta)^{p_{0}+1}k^{-1}N\leq\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{e}\right)^{p_{0}+1}k^{-1}N.

Now we set the parameters as follows, such that the first summand and the remainder are both less than
\frac{\epsilon}{4r}
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t=0}+2gk\cdot\log(6k^{-1}Nr/\epsilon),

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\max\{\lceil\log(6k^{-1}Nr/\epsilon)\rceil-1,~p\}.

To sum up, the short-time simulation error (

\displaystyle\norm{(U_p(\delta, 0)-V(\delta, 0))\Pi_{\sigma}(0)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}k^{-1}Ne^{-\lambda(\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}-\Delta_{t=0})}+6(2qp_{0}kg\delta)^{p}q\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}\delta+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{e}\right)^{p_{0}+1}k^{-1}N

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2r}+3(kp_{0}g)^{p}\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}(2q\delta)^{p+1}.

(21)

Since
\Delta_{t=0}^{\prime}
completely gets rid of

\Delta_{t=0}
with the energy supremum
\Delta
over
[0,T]
.

We remark that the error of the standard product formula only differs from above by the absence of a constant
2
before
\Gamma
.
Therefore, the error bound (
21
) also applies to standard product formulas.

3.3
Long-time simulation error

Next, we address the long-time simulation problem (
7
).
We cannot take for granted that

\displaystyle\left\|(U_{p}(T,0)-V(T,0))\Pi_{\sigma}(0)\right\|\overset{?}{\leq}r\left\|(U_{p}(\delta,0)-V(\delta,0))\Pi_{\sigma}(0)\right\|.

We follow the decomposition in proving the triangle inequality for telescoping operator products:

\displaystyle\norm{(U_p(T,0)-V(T,0)) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}=\norm{\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}U_p(T,(j+1)\delta)\left(U_p((j+1)\delta,j\delta)-V((j+1)\delta,j\delta)\right)V(j\delta,0) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\norm{U_p(T,(j+1)\delta)\left(U_p((j+1)\delta,j\delta)-V((j+1)\delta,j\delta)\right)\cdot\underbrace{I}_{\text{split}}\cdot V(j\delta,0) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\norm{U_p(T,(j+1)\delta)\underbrace{\left(U_p((j+1)\delta,j\delta)-V((j+1)\delta,j\delta)\right)\Pi_{\sigma}(j\delta)}_{\text{short-time error}}V(j\delta,0) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\norm{U_p(T,(j+1)\delta)\left(U_p((j+1)\delta,j\delta)-V((j+1)\delta,j\delta)\right)\underbrace{(I-\Pi_\sigma(j\delta))V(j\delta,0) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}_{\text{leakage error}}}.

The above analysis shows that the long-time simulation error is the sum of short-time simulation errors plus additional leakage error terms, that is,

\displaystyle\norm{(U_p(T,0)-V(T,0)) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\norm{U_p((j+1)\delta,j\delta)-V((j+1)\delta,j\delta)\Pi_{\sigma}(j\delta)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\norm{U_p((j+1)\delta,j\delta)-V((j+1)\delta,j\delta)}\cdot\norm{(I-\Pi_\sigma(j\delta))V(j\delta,0) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}.

Each short-time simulation error can be bounded in a similar way to (
21
).
We only need to substitute the initial time
0
with
j\delta
.
Correspondingly, the energy threshold
\Delta_{t=0}
should be modified for the fixed spectrum of
H(j\delta)
with
\Pi_{\sigma}(j\delta)
, which is then bounded by the supremum
\Delta
.

Next, we use adiabatic perturbation theory with multiple eigenstates to bound the leakage error.
For each leakage error term, we define the scaled time
s_{j}=\frac{t}{j\delta}\in[0,1]
.
Note that the norm of derivative with respect to
s_{j}
is smaller than that with respect to
s=\tfrac{t}{T}
since
\derivative{s_j}=j\delta\cdot\derivative{t}
,
j\delta\leq T
.
Therefore, every
\dot{H}(s_{j})
and
\ddot{H}(s_{j})
can be respectively upper bounded by

\displaystyle\|{\dot{H}}\|\equiv\max_{s\in[0,1]}\norm{\dv{H}{s}},\quad\|{\ddot{H}}\|\equiv\max_{s\in[0,1]}\norm{\dv[2]{H}{s}}.

Additionally, with a uniform lower bound
\gamma
of spectral gap
\gamma(s)
, by
Lemma
1
we have

\displaystyle\norm{(I-\Pi_{\sigma}(j\delta))V(j\delta,0)\Pi_{\sigma}(0)}\leq\frac{1}{j\delta}\left(2\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+7\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}\right).

(22)

Finally, we conclude that the whole long-time simulation error is bounded by

\displaystyle\norm{(U_p(T,0)-V(T,0)) \Pi_{\sigma}(0)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(g^{p+1}N\delta^{p+1}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\delta}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots\frac{1}{r-1}\right)\left(2\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+7\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}\right)+\tilde{O}\left(g^{p}\Delta^{\prime}\frac{T^{p+1}}{r^{p}}\right)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(g^{p+1}N\frac{T^{p}}{r^{p}}\right)\cdot(1+\ln r)\left(2\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+7\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}\right)+\tilde{O}\left(g^{p}\Delta^{\prime}\frac{T^{p+1}}{r^{p}}\right)+\frac{\epsilon}{2},

(23)

where the last inequality follows that
\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{j}\leq\ln(r)+1.

Applications

We further demonstrate our results in (
23
) for different Hamiltonians.
In our analysis we take

and
p=O(1)
. The upper bound of interaction strength on a single qubit is set to (

\displaystyle g=F\|\hskip-1.00006pt|h|\hskip-1.00006pt\|_{1}=O(\|\hskip-1.00006pt|h|\hskip-1.00006pt\|_{1}).

For geometrically-local Hamiltonians,
\Gamma=O(1)

We have a constant degree

\displaystyle\quad g=O(1).

For power-law Hamiltonians, the recursive decomposition scheme in

Appendix F of
[

\displaystyle g=\begin{cases}{O}\left(N^{1-\alpha/D}\right),&\text{ for }0\leq\alpha<D,\\

{O}(1),&\text{ for }\alpha>D.\end{cases}

Next, we choose an appropriate Trotter number

to ensure the whole long-time simulation error is no more than
\epsilon
.
In the first case, if the sum of step errors is the dominant term, that is,

\displaystyle 3(2q)^{p+1}(p_{0}g)^{p}\Delta^{\prime}\frac{T^{p+1}}{r^{p}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

\displaystyle r=\tilde{O}\left(g\frac{(\Delta+g\log(N/\epsilon))^{1/p}T^{1+1/p}}{\epsilon^{1/p}}\right).

In the second case, if the error containing the leakage term is dominant, that is,

\displaystyle(2k(p+1))^{p}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}\Gamma^{p+1-j}g^{j}N\frac{T^{p}}{r^{p}}\right)(1+\ln r)\left(2\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+7\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

It suffices to choose

\displaystyle r=\tilde{O}\Bigg(g^{1+1/p}\frac{N^{1/p}T}{\epsilon^{1/p}}{\left(\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}\right)^{1/p}}\Bigg).

4.2
Adiabatic state preparation

In adiabatic state preparation, the Hamiltonian usually takes the form of

\displaystyle H(t)=\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)H^{(1)}+\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)H^{(2)}~~\text{or}~~H(t)=\cos{\left(\frac{\pi t}{2T}\right)}H^{(1)}+\sin{\left(\frac{\pi t}{2T}\right)}H^{(2)},

where
H^{(1)}
and
H^{(2)}
need to be further broken into
H_{\gamma}
’s, the exponentials of which are more convenient to be implemented on a quantum computer.
In such adiabatic scenarios,
t
is typically encoded into the scaled time

.
Then, the derivatives of
O(1)
-bounded coefficient functions
f_{X}
will scale inversely with
T
, that is,
|f_{X}^{(m)}(t)|\propto\frac{1}{T^{m}}
.
In the adiabatic regime where

\displaystyle T=\Omega\left(\frac{gN}{\Delta}\cdot\left(\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\right),

2
is naturally satisfied since

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi(t)\sum\nolimits_{X}|f_{X}^{(m)}(t)|h_{X}\Pi(t)\right\|\leq\left\|\sum\nolimits_{X}|f_{X}^{(m)}(t)|h_{X}\right\|=O\left(\frac{gN}{T^{m}}\right)=O(\Delta)=O(\Delta^{\prime}).

Besides, in the Trotter number of
Theorem
2
, only the first term induced by the sum of short-time simulation errors is dominant. We thus have the following simplified bound:

Theorem 3

(Trotter number for simulating adiabatic state preparation)
.

In the adiabatic regime where
T=\Omega(\frac{gN}{\Delta}\cdot(\sigma\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}+\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}\frac{\|{\dot{H}}\|^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}+\sigma\frac{\|{\ddot{H}}\|}{\gamma^{2}}))
, it suffices to choose

r=\tilde{O}\left(g\frac{(\Delta+g\log(N/\epsilon))^{1/p}T^{1+1/p}}{\epsilon^{1/p}}\right),

achieving an exponential reduction in dependence on the system size
N
compared to the full Hilbert space.

5
Lower Bound for Simulating Time-Dependent Hamiltonian

5.1
Full Hilbert space

We remark that the lower bound of query complexity concerning generic time-dependent sparse Hamiltonian simulations is implicitly mentioned in the discussion section of
[
8
]
.
Here, we provide a rigorous proof for the full Hilbert space.

Theorem 4

(Hard instance)
.

For any
\epsilon,T>0
, integer
d\geq 2
, and fixed function of
\|H(t)\|_{\max}
over
t\in[0,T]
, there exists a d-sparse time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
such that simulating
H(t)
for time
t\in[0,T]
within precision
\epsilon
requires query complexity

\displaystyle\Omega\left(d\int_{0}^{T}\norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}\differential{\tau}+\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}\right).

Proof.

We consider the following hard instance

\displaystyle{H}(t)=\|H(t)\|_{\max}\cdot\tilde{H}/\|\tilde{H}\|_{\max},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

where
\tilde{H}
follows the construction in Lemma 12 of
[
7
]
. Given a string
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, let
d^{\prime}=\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor
, we consider
2d^{\prime}
-sparse Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
acts on
\ket{i,j,\ell}
with
i\in\{0,\dots,N\},j\in\{0,1\}
, and
\ell\in[d^{\prime}]
, whose non-zero entries are

\langle i-1,j,\ell|\tilde{H}\left|i,j\oplus x_{i},\ell^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\langle i,j\oplus x_{i},\ell^{\prime}\right|\tilde{H}|i-1,j,\ell\rangle=\sqrt{i(N-i+1)}/N.

Note that
[H(t),H(t^{\prime})]=0
holds for any time over
[0,T]
due to linear scaling, the Dyson series or time-ordered exponential reduces to a case similar to the time-independent scenario, that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}{H}(\tau)\differential{\tau}\right)=\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}\norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}\differential{\tau}\cdot\tilde{H}/\|\tilde{H}\|_{\max}\right).

We start the simulation from the state
|0,0,*\rangle
, where
|i,j,*\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d^{\prime}}}\sum_{\ell}|i,j,\ell\rangle
denotes a uniform superposition over the third register.
The subspace
\operatorname{span}\{|i,j,*\rangle:i\in\{0,\dots,N\}
,
j\in\{0,1\}\}
is an invariant subspace of
\tilde{H}
.
Since the initial state lies within this subspace, the quantum walk remains confined to this subspace.
The non-zero matrix elements of
\tilde{H}
in this invariant subspace are

\displaystyle\langle i-1,j,*|\tilde{H}\left|i,j\oplus x_{i},*\right\rangle=\left\langle i,j\oplus x_{i},*\right|\tilde{H}|i-1,j,*\rangle=d^{\prime}\sqrt{i(N-i+1)}/N.

Note that
\|\tilde{H}\|_{\max}=\Theta(1)
, simulating
{H}(t)
over time
[0,T]
yields an unbounded-error algorithm determining the parity of string
p(x)=x_{1}\oplus x_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{N}
since

\displaystyle\bra{N, p(x), *}\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}{H}(\tau)\differential{\tau}\right)|0,0,*\rangle=\left(\sin(d'\int_0^T \norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}/(N\cdot\|\tilde{H}\|_{\max}))\right)^{N},

while

\displaystyle\bra{N, p(x)\oplus 1, *}\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}{H}(\tau)\differential{\tau}\right)|0,0,*\rangle=0.

If we measure
\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{T}{H}(\tau)\differential{\tau}}|0,0,*\rangle
using the computational basis for the first two registers and an orthogonal basis containing
\ket{*}
for the third register, then the third register must be exactly
\ket{*}
due to aforementioned invariant subspace.
If the first register is not
N
, we output
0
or
1
with equal probability. If the first register is
N
, we output the value of the second register. This is an unbounded-error algorithm of computing parity
p(x)
with success probability
>\frac{1}{2}
, and thus requires
\Omega(N)
queries
[
3
,
16
]
.

Since we allow that the implemented quantum circuit
U
can deviate from
\mathcal{T}\exp(-i\int_0^T {H}(\tau)\dd{\tau})
up to
\epsilon
, it suffices to require
[
5
]

\displaystyle\left(\sin(d'\int_0^T \norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}/(N\cdot\|\tilde{H}\|_{\max}))\right)^{N}\geq\epsilon.

By taking the logarithm on both sides and using the approximation
\sin x\approx x
, it suffices to set

N=\Theta\left(\max\left\{d^{\prime}\int_{0}^{T}\norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}\differential{\tau},\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}\right\}\right).

Above all, if we construct this kind of Hamiltonian with
N=\Theta(d\int_{0}^{T}\norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}\differential{\tau}+\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\log\log(1/\epsilon)})
, simulating it over
[0,T]
with error up to
\epsilon
at least requires query complexity

\displaystyle\Omega(N)=\Omega\left(d\int_{0}^{T}\norm{H(\tau)}_{\max}\differential{\tau}+\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}\right).

Thus, there is no generic quantum algorithm for time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations that can exceed this lower bound.
∎

We remark that the rescaled Dyson series
[

]
achieves query complexity as a product of the above two parts, just as the former truncated Taylor series algorithm for the time-independent scenario.
We hope that, in the future, a time-dependent analogue to quantum signal processing might match the lower bounds in all the above parameters.
