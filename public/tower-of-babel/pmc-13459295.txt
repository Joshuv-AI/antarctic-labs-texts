Demographic patterns among physician assistant/associate applicants and matriculants compared with U.S. population benchmarks, 2013–2022
Authors: Lucy W Kibe, Katrina M Schrode
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Physician Assistant/Associate (PA) profession offers an innovative solution to increase access and equitable outcomes in healthcare, especially for communities lacking primary care physicians. However, as of 2025, the PA workforce does not mirror the present and projected changes in the U.S. demographic composition. We examine nine-year race, ethnic, and gender trends of PA training programs- the pipeline to the profession.MethodsData on PA program applicants and matriculants from the 2012–2013 to the 2021–2022 application cycles were obtained from the Central Application Service for Physician Assistants (CASPA) and analyzed alongside United States (U.S.) Census data. Demographic categories included self-identified gender, racial and ethnic groups.ResultsOver ten years, the number of PA programs and applicants increased significantly, yet representation across gender, racial and ethnic groups remained uneven. White and Asian applicants and matriculants were generally overrepresented, while among females, Black, American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN), and Hispanic individuals were underrepresented, relative to their representation in the Census. Among males, all groups were underrepresented, relative to their representation in the Census. While some strides have been made in some historically underrepresented groups, Black men and AIAN individuals remained consistently underrepresented. These findings underscore persistent challenges in creating a PA workforce that reflects the nation’s demographics, with notable gaps among underrepresented groups.ConclusionsFor the last ten years, the racial and ethnic distribution of PA trainees has not matched the demographics of the U.S. population. Improvements among some historically underrepresented groups suggest areas of progress, but population-level disproportionality persists. Continued evaluation of applicant and matriculant trends may help inform evidence-based strategies to support broad access to PA education and strengthen the healthcare workforce.Keywords: Physician assistant, Physician associate, Applicant, Matriculant, Representation, Trends, Pipeline, Healthcare workforce

Background

Diversity within the healthcare sector is increasingly recognized as a crucial component for promoting the nation’s overall health and well-being [1–3]. Therefore, as the racial and ethnic composition of the U.S. changes, so must the healthcare workforce. The U.S. Census projections indicate that in the next 40 years, non-Hispanic whites will continue to be the single largest race group but will no longer make up the majority of the U.S. population by 2045 [4]. According to U.S. Census projections, the racial and ethnic makeup of the U.S. is expected to shift significantly between 2030 and 2060. The proportion of the population identifying as AIAN will increase slightly from 1.3% to 1.4%; Asian from 6.9% to 9.1%; Black from 13.8% to 15.0%; and Hispanic from 21.1% to 27.5%. In contrast, the proportion of non-Hispanic whites will decline from 55.8% to 44.3%, while the Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander (NHPI) group is expected to remain steady at 0.3%. Notably, individuals identifying as two or more races are projected to experience the fastest growth, with an estimated 200% increase [4].

Although the U.S. population is evolving, the healthcare workforce has not kept pace with these demographic shifts. Research shows that healthcare outcomes improve when providers and patients share similar demographic backgrounds [5, 6]. However, current trends suggest that without continued evaluation and deliberate action, this misalignment will persist [7]. Therefore, continued efforts to support broad access to health professions education may help ensure the workforce reflects the evolving population.

Established in the 1960s, the Physician Assistant/Associate (PA) profession has emerged as a strategic response to healthcare shortages, particularly in underresourced communities [8], underscoring its pivotal role in meeting the escalating demands for healthcare services. The current PA education model is a rigorous master’s-level, competency-based medical training designed to graduate generalist practicing providers ready to join the healthcare workforce in 2–3 years. PAs are licensed in every state and have more than 500 million patient interactions every year, working as part of the healthcare team, and often being the sole healthcare provider for many patients. PAs operate across a spectrum of medical subspecialties, geographical regions, and practice settings, consistently delivering exemplary patient care and satisfaction [9, 10]. Admission to PA training programs is competitive, with an overall 36.8% acceptance rate in 2021 [11].

Despite its popularity and widespread practice, the profession grapples with significant diversity deficits, marking it as one of the least diverse healthcare sectors in the nation [7, 11–13]. This deficiency is particularly evident among racial and ethnic minority groups. Data collected in 2022 showed that of all practicing PAs, < 1% identified as AIAH or NHPI, 3.3% as Black, 7% as Hispanic, and 2.3% of PAs identified as multi-racial [14]. Additionally, gender parity remains a notable challenge [15], as approximately 70% of PAs identify as female [14]. These inequalities are expected to worsen following the recent federal directives prohibiting targeted race and gender-based college admissions to increase representation of marginalized demographic groups [16, 17].

To gain insight into the trajectory of the PA workforce and to inform targeted interventions, it is imperative to closely examine patterns in the demographic distribution of PA applicants and matriculants. Without proportional growth in representation, demographic projections suggest that the misalignment between the PA workforce and the U.S. population may increase over time. By elucidating these trends, we can better understand the evolving landscape of PA education and workforce demographics, ultimately facilitating informed decision-making and strategic planning within the field. This study examined the demographics of applicants and matriculants, specifically within the age bracket most applicable to PA applicants: 20 to 29 years. The objective of our investigation was to describe patterns in the racial/ethnic and gender distribution of PA applicants and matriculants over a span of ten years. Through this analysis, we aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the evolving demographic landscape within the PA training pipeline, shedding light on potential disparities and areas for targeted intervention and improvement.

Methods

This study was a descriptive analysis of secondary data on applicants and matriculants to PA programs in the U.S. We also analyzed U.S. Census data to estimate numbers of potential and expected applicants and matriculants. This study was considered exempt from review by the Charles R. Drew University of Medicine and Science institutional review board.

Application and matriculation data

The PA Education Association (PAEA) administers the Central Application Service for Physician Assistants (CASPA™), which is the application system that most (~ 90%) U.S.-based PA programs use for admissions. Applicants enter their information directly into the system. De-identified individual-level CASPA data from the 2012–2013 to the 2021–2022 application cycles for all applications were obtained from PAEA. For simplicity, we will refer to the cycles by their matriculation year. Individuals who matriculated into a program were classified as matriculants. Variables included in our analysis were self-reported age, gender, racial and ethnic identity at the time of application. Individuals could decline to report any of these variables; therefore, those with missing data were excluded from the relevant calculations but included in all total estimates.

Applicants who self-identified with multiple racial identities were categorized by the identity least represented among PA applicants. E.g., those who identified as ‘Black or African American’ were categorized as ‘Black’, even if they also identified as ‘Asian’ or ‘white’. While we recognize inherent issues with this approach, we felt it was the simplest and most conservative way to compare demographic trends between applicant subgroups. Of individuals who reported a racial identity, approximately 4% reported more than one; there was an increase of 1.5% points in the number identifying as more than one racial identity over the study period. Hispanic ethnic identity was treated independently from racial identity, so individuals who identify as Hispanic may also be included in a racial category.

Census data

U.S. population annual estimates based on 2010 and 2020 decennial counts conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau were obtained from the Census Bureau website [18]. Based on CASPA data, the interquartile range of applicant ages most closely matched the combined U.S. Census age ranges of 20–24 and 25–29. Thus, the “potential applicant pool” for each racial/ethnic group for each application cycle was determined as the total number of individuals in the U.S. Census aged 20–29 in the corresponding year. To capture the total potential pool for each racial/ethnic group, we used the Census Bureau’s “alone or in combination” estimates which include all individuals who identify with a given racial group, including multi-racial individuals.

PA program data

We obtained the total number of operating, accredited programs for each year from 2013 to 2022 from the Accreditation Review Commission on Education for the Physician Assistant, Inc (ARC-PA). ARC-PA maintains a list of all accredited programs which is available on their website [19]. We counted the total number of programs operating under the assumption that each PA program has been open since it first received provisional accreditation. We did not exclude any programs based on probationary status.

Data analysis

We calculated frequencies and percentages of applicants and matriculants by gender and race/ethnicity for each application cycle. Standardized rates for PA applicants for each subgroup (i.e. gender and race/ethnicity) were calculated as the number of actual applicants divided by the estimated number of individuals with corresponding gender and race/ethnicity in the entire U.S. aged 20 to 29 years per 100,000. Standardized rates for PA matriculants were calculated following the same strategy and will be referred to as population-based matriculant rates. The matriculation rate (distinct from population-based matriculant rate) was calculated as the percentage of applicants that successfully matriculated into a PA training program each year.

Expected numbers were determined by calculating the percentage of the U.S. population aged 20 to 29 years in 2022 that was represented by a given subgroup and applying that percentage to the total number of applicants or matriculants from the CASPA data. Data from 2022 were used, because these were the most recent data we had available at the time of analysis. For example, AIAN males aged 20–29 represented 1.3% of the total U.S. population aged 20–29 in 2022, and thus could be expected to represent 1.3% of the 29,906 actual applicants in 2022. These expected number estimates were compared to the actual numbers of applicants or matriculants within each subgroup. All statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Between the 2012–2013 and 2021–2022 application cycles, applicant volume and demographic composition changed alongside the expansion of accredited PA programs. Over the ten-year period, the number of PA programs accredited by the ARC-PA expanded from 171 in 2013 to 300 in 2022 (Fig. 1). This increase in program capacity coincided with a 51.3% increase in the total number of applicants, growing from 19,761 in 2012 to 29,906 in 2022 (Fig. 1; Table 1).

American Indian and Alaska Native (AIAN) applicants

Despite some growth, both AIAN men and women remained underrepresented in PA program applications, with applicant counts falling below demographic expectations. AIAN men consistently represented just 0.3% of the total applicant pool (Table 1; Fig. 2a). Although the total number of applications from AIAN men increased by 33% between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 2a), their representation remained low relative to other demographic groups. From 2016 to 2022, AIAN men had the lowest application rates across all groups. In 2022, their application rate was 16 applicants per 100,000 individuals (Fig. 3a). In that year, the observed number of AIAN male applicants was 76% lower than expected based on U.S. Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4a), representing the largest gap between observed and expected counts among all groups.

Similarly, AIAN women comprised 0.8% of the applicant pool (Table 1) and experienced a 41% increase in applications between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 2a). Despite this growth, their representation remained below demographic expectations. Although the application rate for AIAN women reached 45 applicants per 100,000 by the end of the study period (Fig. 3a), the observed number of applicants in 2022 was 32% lower than expected based on U.S. Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4a).

Asian applicants

Application patterns for Asian applicants showed sustained growth and close alignment between observed and expected counts over time. Asian men consistently represented 3.8% of the applicant pool across the study period. Over this time frame, the population-based application rate for Asian men increased by 30% (Fig. 3a). In contrast to several other demographic groups, the observed number of Asian male applicants closely matched expectations based on U.S. Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4a).

Asian women showed a slight but steady increase in representation in the applicant pool, growing by an average of 0.1% annually, from 11% in 2012 to 12.1% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 2a). They consistently exhibited the highest population-based application rates per 100,000 individuals throughout the study period (Fig. 3a), with application rates increasing by 50% over the decade. By 2022, the observed number of Asian female applicants exceeded expected counts by 217% relative to Census estimates for the relevant age group (Fig. 4a).

Black applicants

For Black applicants, application patterns varied by gender, with increases in population-based rates occurring alongside differences in representation relative to demographic expectations. Black men consistently accounted for 2.3% of the applicant pool throughout the decade (Table 1; Fig. 2a). From 2013 to 2016, Black men held the third-lowest population-based application rate among male applicants, remaining steady at 13 applicants per 100,000 individuals (Fig. 3a). By 2022, this rate increased to 18 applicants per 100,000 individuals, representing a 38% increase over the ten-year period. Despite this growth, Black men continued to have the second-lowest application rate among men across all racial and ethnic groups (Fig. 3a). The observed number of Black male applicants remained 73% lower than expected based on U.S. Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4a), reflecting a substantial gap between observed and expected counts.

The share of Black women in the overall applicant pool increased gradually, rising from 5.2% in 2013 to 7.4% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 2a), with larger increases occurring in the latter part of the decade. Population-based application rates for Black women doubled from 29 to 58 applicants per 100,000 individuals between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 3a). Despite this increase, Black women continued to have the second-lowest application rate among female applicants (Fig. 3a), and the observed number of applicants was 14% lower than expected based on Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 5a).

Hispanic applicants

Population-based application trends among Hispanic individuals showed increases over time, with distinct patterns observed for men and women. While representation of male Hispanic applicants in the overall applicant pool increased modestly from 2.6% in 2013 to 3.6% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 2a), the population-based application rate nearly doubled, rising from 11 to 21 applicants per 100,000 individuals (Fig. 3a). Despite this growth, the observed number of Hispanic male applicants remained 69% lower than expected based on Census data for individuals of applicant age (Fig. 4a).

In contrast, Hispanic women showed larger increases in both applicant pool share and population-based application rates. Their share of the applicant pool rose from 6.4% in 2013 to 10.7% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 2a) accompanied by a 117% increase in application rates, from 30 to 65 applicants per 100,000 individuals (Fig. 3a). The observed number of Hispanic female applicants aligned with Census-based expectations, resulting in the smallest difference between projected and actual applicant counts among all demographic groups (Fig. 4a).

Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander (NHPI) applicants

NHPI applicants represented a small proportion of the applicant pool throughout the study period. Although both groups experienced growth in application rates, NHPI men and women differed in the degree to which observed applicant counts aligned with demographic expectations. Throughout the study period, NHPI men consistently accounted for 0.1% of the total applicant pool (Table 1; Fig. 2a). Over the same period, NHPI men experienced the largest increase in population-based application rates among all demographic groups, with rates increasing from 10 applicants per 100,000 individuals in 2013 to 30 applicants per 100,000 by 2022 (Fig. 3a). Despite this growth, the observed number of NHPI male applicants remained 55% lower than expected based on U.S. Census counts for individuals of applicant age (Fig. 4a), reflecting a substantial difference between observed and expected counts.

NHPI women maintained a steady 0.4% share of the applicant pool across the study period (Table 1; Fig. 2a) and had the second-highest increase in population-based application rates, with a 151% increase reaching 93 applicants per 100,000 individuals in 2022 (Fig. 3a). In contrast to NHPI men, the observed number of NHPI female applicants exceeded Census-based expectations by 40% (Fig. 4a).

White applicants

The number of applicants who were white women increased steadily over the study period, whereas the share of white men in the applicant pool declined gradually, decreasing by an average of approximately 0.6% points per year, from 20.2% in 2013 to 15.0% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 2a). White men exhibited the smallest increase in population-based application rates across all demographic groups, with a 13% increase over the study period (Fig. 3a). By 2022, the application rate for white men was 27 applicants per 100,000 individuals, ranking third among male applicants (Fig. 3a). Despite this growth, the observed number of white male applicants remained 59% lower than expected based on applicant-aged U.S. Census population estimates (Fig. 4a), reflecting a gap between expected and observed counts.

In contrast, white women consistently represented the largest share of the applicant pool. Their share increased modestly from 50.4% in 2013 to 51.5% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 2a). Over the same period, population-based application rates for white women increased by 56% (Fig. 3a), reaching 97 applicants per 100,000 individuals in 2022, which was the second-highest rate among all demographic groups (Fig. 3a). The observed number of applicants who were white women exceeded demographic expectations, with counts 46% higher than projected based on applicant-aged U.S. Census population estimates (Fig. 4a).

Demographic distribution and trends of matriculants (2013–2022)

Analysis of matriculant data from the 2012–2013 to 2021–2022 application cycles showed a substantial increase in the number of PA matriculants over time. The total number of matriculants nearly doubled, rising from 6,192 to 12,029, coinciding with the expansion of accredited PA programs during this period. Despite this growth, the demographic composition of matriculants changed only modestly, with the largest shifts reflecting a decrease in the share of matriculants who were white men and an increase in the share who were Asian women.

Over the same period, the proportion of applicants who matriculated increased from 31.3% in 2013 to 40.2% in 2022 (Table 1). This upward trend was observed across all racial and ethnic groups, although the magnitude of change varied across demographic groups (Table 1; Fig. 5).

American Indian and Alaska Native (AIAN) matriculants

Overall, AIAN men and women consistently accounted for less than 1% of the matriculant pool throughout the study period (Table 1; Fig. 2b). In 2022, the population-based matriculant rate for AIAN men was 4 per 100,000 individuals (Fig. 3b), which was 87% lower than expected based on U.S. Census counts for individuals in the relevant age group (Fig. 4b). The proportion of AIAN male applicants who matriculated increased by 33% over the study period. However, this proportion remained the lowest among all demographic groups (Table 1; Fig. 5a). Across groups, AIAN men showed the smallest change in matriculation proportions and the largest difference between observed and expected matriculant counts (Fig. 5a).

The percent of matriculants that were AIAN women remained below 1%, and the population-based matriculant rate for AIAN women also remained low throughout the study period. In 2022, the population-based matriculant rate for AIAN women was 16 per 100,000 individuals, the second-lowest among women (Fig. 3b), and the observed number of matriculants was 39% lower than expected based on Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4b). Over the same period, the proportion of AIAN female applicants who matriculated increased from 25.2% in 2013 to 36.0% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 5b).

Asian matriculants

The proportion of matriculants that were Asian men remained relatively stable across the study period, rising from 2.6% in 2013 to 3.4% in 2023 (Fig. 2b), but remaining 13% lower than expected based on U.S. Census population estimates for the relevant age group (Fig. 4b). Their population-based matriculant rates more than doubled, reaching 23 matriculants per 100,000 individuals in 2022 (Fig. 3b). Over the same period, the proportion of Asian male applicants who matriculated increased by 65% (Table 1; Fig. 5a).

Asian women saw the highest increase in representation among matriculants, with their share increasing from 7% in 2013 to 11.7% in 2023, an increase of 4.7% points (Fig. 2d). They had the highest population-based matriculant rates per 100,000 individuals throughout the study period (Fig. 3b). Their rates increased from 28 to 82 matriculants per 100,000 between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 3b). The observed number of Asian female matriculants in 2022 exceeded Census-based expectations by 206% (Fig. 4b). Over the same period, the proportion of Asian female applicants who matriculated increased from 20.0% in 2013 to 38.8% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 5b).

Black matriculants

Between 2013 and 2022, Black men saw an increase in their share of matriculants from 1.2% to 1.6% (Fig. 2b). They had the second-lowest population-based matriculant rates among all groups (Fig. 3b), despite seeing a 150% increase over time. This increase corresponded to a rise from 2 to 5 matriculants per 100,000 individuals. In 2022, the observed number of Black male matriculants was 81% lower than expected based on U.S. Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4b). Over the study period, the proportion of Black male applicants who matriculated increased from 16.8% in 2013 to 28.2% in 2022, yet remained lower than that observed for most other demographic groups (Table 1; Fig. 5a).

Representation of Black women among matriculants more than doubled to 4.9% in 2022 (Fig. 2d), and population-based matriculant rates increased by 275% over the study period (Fig. 3b). However, they continued to have the lowest population-based matriculant rate among women across all racial and ethnic groups, reaching 15 matriculants per 100,000 individuals in 2022 (Fig. 3b). The observed number of Black female matriculants was 43% lower than expected based on Census data for the relevant age group (Fig. 4b). Over the same period, the proportion of Black female applicants who matriculated increased from 14.4% in 2013 to 26.4% in 2022, but remained lower relative to other demographic groups (Table 1; Fig. 5b).

Hispanic matriculants

Matriculation patterns for Hispanic applicants showed trends similar to those observed in application data, with increases over time that were more pronounced among women. Between 2013 and 2022, the proportion of matriculants that were Hispanic men rose from 2.1% to 2.8% (Fig. 2f), and the population-based matriculant rates for Hispanic men increased by 133%, reaching 7 matriculants per 100,000 individuals in 2022 (Fig. 3b). Despite this increase, the observed number of Hispanic male matriculants remained 75% lower than expected based on U.S. Census data for the applicant-aged population (Fig. 4b). Over the same period, the proportion of Hispanic male applicants who matriculated increased from 25.0% in 2013 to 32.1% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 5a).

Hispanic women experienced a larger 79% increase in their share of the matriculant pool from 5.2% in 2013 to 9.3% in 2022 (Fig. 2f). They also saw substantial increases in population-based matriculant rates, which rose by 188% from 8 to 23 matriculants per 100,000 individuals between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 3b). Although the observed number of Hispanic female matriculants was 15% lower than expected, this difference was smaller than that observed for most other demographic groups (Fig. 4b). During the same period, the proportion of Hispanic female applicants who matriculated increased from 25.7% in 2013 to 35.0% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 5b).

Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander (NHPI) matriculants

NHPI matriculants represented a small fraction of the matriculant pool throughout the study period (Fig. 2b), but NHPI men exhibited the second-largest percent increase in representation among matriculants and the largest change in population-based matriculant rates over time (Fig. 3b). In 2013, the share of matriculants that was NHPI men was 0.03%, with an increase of 286% to 0.12% in 2023 (Fig. 2b). The population-based matriculant rate for NHPI men was 2 per 100,000 individuals, increasing to 11 per 100,000 by 2022 (Fig. 3b). Over the same period, NHPI men also showed the greatest increase in the proportion of applicants who matriculated, rising from 15.4% in 2013 to 36.6% in 2022 (Table 2; Fig. 5a). Despite these increases, the observed number of NHPI male matriculants in 2022 was 59% lower than expected based on U.S. Census data (Fig. 4b), reflecting a difference between observed and expected counts.

NHPI women also experienced increases in their share of the matriculant pool, from 0.2% in 2013 to 0.4% in 2022 (Fig. 2d). Similarly, their population-based matriculant rates rose from 11 per 100,000 individuals in 2013 to 37 per 100,000 by 2022 (Fig. 3b). During the same period, the proportion of NHPI female applicants who matriculated increased from 28.9% in 2012 to 39.7% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 5b). In 2022, the observed number of NHPI female matriculants exceeded Census-based expectations by 38% (Fig. 4b).

White matriculants

White applicants were consistently the largest proportion of matriculants and had the highest population-based matriculant rates from 2013 to 2022, exceeding those of other racial and ethnic groups throughout the study period (Fig. 3b). Among white men, the share of matriculants declined gradually over time (Fig. 2b), and they exhibited the second-smallest increase in population-based matriculant rates, with a 38% increase over the study period (Fig. 3b). Despite this decline in representation, white men still accounted for the second-largest share of matriculants in 2022, at 14.9% (Fig. 2b), and were tied for the second-highest population-based matriculant rate among men in 2022, at 11 matriculants per 100,000 individuals (Fig. 3b). Over the same period, the proportion of white male applicants who matriculated increased from 33.6% in 2013 to 39.4% in 2022 (Table 1; Fig. 5a).

In contrast, representation of white women among matriculants was the most stable, changing by less than 0.01% over the timeframe (Fig. 2d), while their population-based matriculant rates rose by 95% over the study period (Fig. 3b). In 2022, white women had the second-highest population-based matriculant rate among all demographic groups, at 43 matriculants per 100,000 individuals. The observed number of white female matriculants exceeded Census-based expectations by 62% (Fig. 4b). From 2013 to 2022, the percentage of white female applicants who matriculated increased steadily from 35.9% to 44.7% (Table 1; Fig. 5b).

Discussion

This descriptive study provides a longitudinal analysis of demographic trends among PA applicants and matriculants over a ten-year period. During this time, the overall number of applicants and matriculants increased substantially, reflecting continued interest in the PA profession and expansion of accredited training programs, respectively. Despite this growth, proportional representation across several racial, ethnic, and gender subgroups remained uneven relative to age-matched U.S. population benchmarks. These patterns mirror broader inequities observed across other healthcare professions, including medicine and nursing [7, 11, 20].

One particularly concerning trend is the persistently low representation of men of color among applicants. Black males, for example, comprised only 2.3% (N = 698) of PA applicants in 2022, despite comprising 8.7% of the U.S. population aged 20–29, similar to the 2.7% and < 1% reported among medical school and nursing applicants [21, 22], and much lower than the 4.7% of pharmacy school applicants [23] in proximate years. Proportions of PA applicants that were AIAN, Hispanic, and NHPI men were similarly low at 0.3% (N = 92), 3.6% (N = 1,067), and 0.1% (N = 41), respectively (compared to 1.3%, 11.5%, and 0.3% of the U.S. population aged 20–29). Underrepresentation is significant given that racial concordance between patients and providers has been shown to improve patient satisfaction and health outcomes [5, 6]. Increasing the number of male PAs of color could have a direct impact on reducing disparities in healthcare delivery, especially in communities that have historically lacked access to culturally congruent care. To address this gap, PA programs need to develop innovative new recruitment strategies and support systems.

Although white females continue to represent the largest demographic group among PA applicants, making up more than half of the applicant pool in 2022, there were encouraging increases in the representation of AIAN, Black, and Hispanic women among applicants over the ten years studied. Despite these increases, these groups remain underrepresented relative to their population proportions, in part due to lower matriculation rates among these groups compared to those of white applicants. This suggests that while interest in the profession is growing, further efforts are needed to ensure successful matriculation.

This study is descriptive and does not examine causal mechanisms; however, several theoretical frameworks help contextualize the observed disproportional representation. Structural opportunity theory highlights how differential access to financial, academic, and mentoring resources may influence professional entry [24]. Therefore, efforts to address the differences identified in this study must be multifaceted. Implementing holistic admissions practices that evaluate candidates beyond academic metrics can help identify applicants with the potential to succeed in diverse practice settings [24–26]. By evaluating attributes such as resilience, community service, and leadership potential alongside academic achievement, PA programs could similarly increase the diversity of their student body while adapting to the new legal landscape. Expanding pipeline programs that provide mentorship, application coaching, and exposure to the PA profession is also critical [11, 24–26]. For example, the EDGE-PA program at Charles R. Drew University of Medicine [27], the University of Southern California Keck School of Medicine PA program [28] and AAPA’s Pathways [29] program provide mentorship and admissions guidance, equipping aspiring PA students with success tools and confidence. Partnerships with historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs) and Hispanic-serving institutions (HSIs) could provide a pathway for more minority students to pursue PA education. Additionally, increasing the number of minority faculty members is crucial, as these individuals can serve as mentors and role models [30], creating a more inclusive environment for underrepresented students. This approach has shown promise in other fields, where diverse faculty have been linked to higher retention rates among minority students [31–33]. Finally, offering targeted financial incentives to students from underrepresented minority groups for whom PA education is out of reach due to economic burden would make the profession more accessible to a broader range of applicants.

A call for inclusive strategies

Our findings highlight both progress and persistent disparities in the representation of various demographic groups among PA applicants and matriculants, similar to broader trends observed across healthcare professions, including physicians, dentists, nurse practitioners, physical therapists, and pharmacists [7, 34].

The juxtaposition of these findings paints a complex picture of diversity within the PA profession. On one hand, there are encouraging signs of increased applications and matriculants among historically underrepresented demographics, suggesting effective outreach and inclusivity efforts. On the other hand, the persistent disparities in representation, particularly when analyzing the proportions within the profession, calls for a concerted effort to not only attract diverse applicants but also ensure their successful matriculation and progression through PA training. Addressing these challenges requires a combination of strategic admissions policies, community outreach, partnerships with minority-serving institutions, and a sustained research agenda to monitor progress and adjust strategies as needed. By fostering a more inclusive and diverse PA workforce, the profession can better meet the needs of an increasingly diverse patient population. Aligning the demographic makeup of the PA workforce with that of the broader U.S. population will improve the health of all Americans.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, we did not include incomplete applications, which may show a different demographic representation. We also did not include applicants or matriculants to the 2.5% of PA programs with in-house admissions processes. Finally, for analysis purposes, we defined discrete racial and gender categories that may not capture the full spectrum of racial and gender identities of applicants and matriculants to PA programs. Additionally, the annual U.S. populations used in our analyses were projected estimates based on previous Census counts and would not reflect unexpected changes in population. In particular, it has been noted that the number of people identifying as AIAN increased 160% between 2010 and 2020, increasing the potential pool; thus we likely overestimated the current representation of AIAN individuals in the PA training pipeline.

Conclusion

Between 2013 and 2022, applications and matriculations to PA programs increased overall; however, representation remained uneven relative to age-matched U.S. population benchmarks. Several racial and gender subgroups, including Black men and AIAN individuals, continued to demonstrate disproportional underrepresentation despite measurable improvements in matriculant percentages among some historically underrepresented groups. These findings underscore the value of examining both applicant representation and applicant-to-matriculant transitions to better understand where progress is occurring and where imbalances persist. Continued monitoring of demographic trends and evaluation of evidence-informed educational strategies will be important to promote proportional representation and support long-term workforce alignment with the U.S. population.
